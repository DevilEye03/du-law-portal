Part I — Introduction: Genesis, Objects and Scheme of the Act

1. Constitutional and international backdrop

Article 47 of the Constitution (DPSP) ordains that the State shall regard improvement of public health as a primary duty and, “in particular, the State shall endeavour to bring about prohibition of the consumption except for medicinal purposes of intoxicating drinks and of drugs which are injurious to health.” The NDPS Act is the principal legislative fulfilment of this mandate in respect of drugs. India is also a party to the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, 1961, the Convention on Psychotropic Substances, 1971, and the United Nations Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances, 1988. The Act was passed under treaty obligations, and the Prevention of Illicit Traffic in NDPS Act, 1988 (a preventive-detention law, enacted under Article 253) followed it.

2. Pre-1985 law and reasons for a new statute

The earlier regime consisted of the Opium Act, 1857, the Opium Act, 1878 and the Dangerous Drugs Act, 1930, supplemented by a few IPC provisions (ss. 272–276 on adulteration of food and drugs). These were found deficient to deal with psychotropic substances, transnational trafficking and organised drug mafias. The Statement of Objects and Reasons (reproduced in Karnail Singh) states that the new law was needed:

  • “to make the scheme of penalties sufficiently deterrent to meet the challenge of well-organised gangs of smugglers”;
  • to empower Central enforcement agencies (Narcotics, Customs, Central Excise, Revenue Intelligence) to investigate offences relating to new drugs of addiction — psychotropic substances — posing serious problems to national governments, in the manner envisaged by the 1971 Convention;
  • to consolidate and amend the existing laws, strengthen controls, enhance penalties for trafficking, control psychotropic substances, and implement the international conventions to which India had acceded.

The NDPS Act (61 of 1985) came into force on 14 November 1985; s. 82 repealed the Opium Acts and the Dangerous Drugs Act, 1930. The Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB) was constituted on 17 March 1986 under s. 4(3).

3. Amendment timeline

1985 — Parent Act (w.e.f. 14-11-1985): uniform minimum 10 years RI for most trafficking offences; Chapter VA (forfeiture) added in 1989.
1989 Amendment (Act 2 of 1989, w.e.f. 29-5-1989): inserted ss. 7A (National Fund), 25A, 27A (financing/harbouring), 31A (mandatory death on second conviction), 52A (disposal of seized drugs), 64A (immunity to addict-volunteers); Special Courts scheme.
2001 Amendment (Act 9 of 2001, w.e.f. 2-10-2001): the rationalising amendment. Introduced three-tier quantity-based sentencing (small / intermediate / commercial) via definitions s.2(vii-a) & (xxiii-a) and the S.O. 1055(E) Notification of 19-10-2001; rewrote ss. 15–32, 36A, 37, 41–43, 50 (adding sub-ss. (5) & (6)); narrowed s.31A; s.42(2) report time changed from “forthwith” to “within 72 hours”; s.27 confined to consumption; bail restrictions confined to ss. 19, 24, 27A and commercial-quantity cases.
2009 Notification (S.O. 2941/2942(E), 18-11-2009): added “Note 4” — notified quantities apply to the entire mixture/solution/dosage form, not merely pure drug content. Upheld in Hira Singh (2020).
2014 Amendment (Act 16 of 2014, w.e.f. 1-5-2014): gave statutory effect to Hira Singh-era and Indian Harm Reduction Network positions — s.31A death made optional (“punishment not less than s.31 or with death”); s.52A expanded to controlled substances & conveyances; essential narcotic drugs regime; small-quantity maximum raised to 1 year.
2021 Amendment (Act 48 of 2021): corrected cross-references (e.g. s. 27A definition clause renumbered to s. 2(viiib)).

5. Salient features (as catalogued in DU faculty notes)

  1. Two streams of substances — narcotic drugs (sleep-inducing; derived from the cannabis, coca and opium plants and their manufactured derivatives) and psychotropic substances (which act on the mind — mood, thought, emotion, behaviour, perception; natural or synthetic and listed in the Schedule).
  2. Reformative as well as deterrent — s.64A immunity for addicts volunteering for de-addiction (reformative) coexists with ss. 31/31A enhanced and capital punishment (deterrent).
  3. General prohibition of cultivation, production, manufacture, possession, sale, purchase, transport, warehousing, use, consumption, import/export and transhipment, except for medical or scientific purposes (s.8), with permit regimes of the Centre (s.9) and States (s.10).
  4. Quantity-based three-tier punishment post-2001 — small quantity; intermediate (more than small but less than commercial); commercial quantity.
  5. Reverse presumptions — s.35 presumes culpable mental state; s.54 presumes commission of the offence from unsatisfactorily accounted possession.
  6. Heightened standard of rebuttal — under s.35(2) the accused must disprove the presumption beyond reasonable doubt, not merely by preponderance of probability (contrast ordinary IPC accusatorial standards).
  7. Special Courts (s.36) for speedy trial; all offences cognizable; specified offences non-bailable with twin conditions (s.37).
  8. Strict procedural safeguards at search/seizure (ss.42, 50) balancing the stringent law against false implication; report of arrest/seizure within 48 hours (s.57); production before Magistrate within 24 hours (s.52 read with Art. 22(2)).
  9. Chain-of-custody statute — s.52A makes Magistrate-certified inventory, photographs and samples primary evidence.
  10. No probation (s.33 bars s.360 CrPC / Probation of Offenders Act except for under-18s and ss.26/27 cases); no remission/commutation (s.32A, except s.27).
  11. Pardon/immunity machinery (s.64); publication of convicts’ names (s.40); vexatious searches punished (s.58); confiscation (ss.60–63).
Why a special Act at all? The faculty notes record that ss. 272–276 IPC were grossly insufficient against drug trafficking; NDPS is a special/socio-economic criminal statute. Under s.51 NDPS, CrPC applies only “in so far as not inconsistent” with the Act — where there is a conflict, NDPS prevails. In State of Gujarat v. Mohanlal Jitamalji Porwal the Supreme Court characterised such socio-economic crimes as more heinous than murder: murder injures an individual, while narcotics destroy a whole generation and the national economy — they are committed with a cool mind, for avarice, by organised syndicates.

Part A — Definitions of Narcotic Drug, Psychotropic Substance and Controlled Substance

The entire sentencing architecture of the NDPS Act rests on correctly classifying the seized substance. The five prescribed cases repeatedly demonstrate that the court must first identify the drug and the entry under which it falls; only then can quantity, and therefore sentence, be determined. (Harjit Singh, para 24.)

1. “Narcotic Drug” — s. 2(xiv)

Section 2(xiv)“narcotic drug” means coca leaf, cannabis (hemp), opium, poppy straw and includes all manufactured drugs.

Narcotic — from the Greek narkos (numbness/sleep) — denotes substances inducing stupor. The definition is inclusive and exhaustive by enumeration: four plant-based materials plus the manufactured-drug family. The definitional chain is best understood visually:

Opium in detail — s. 2(xv) [the provision at the heart of Harjit Singh]

Clause (a) — “pure” opium
the coagulated juice of the opium poppy. “Coagulated” means solidified, clotted, curdled — something which has commenced in a curdled/solid form. The proviso (0.2% morphine cut-off) has no application to clause (a).
Clause (b) — mixture
any mixture, with or without neutral material, of the coagulated juice; but does not include any preparation containing not more than 0.2% morphine. The morphine-content question arises only here (and for opium derivatives under s.2(xvi)).

Botany noted by the Supreme Court in Harjit Singh: the poppy secretes juice (opium) containing alkaloids — morphine (the primary alkaloid), codeine, thebaine. Pure opium “once seen and smelt can never be forgotten” because of its characteristic appearance and scent; chemical analysis is essential under the NDPS Act to prove the case, but the morphine percentage is irrelevant for quantity classification where clause (a) applies.

Opium derivative — s. 2(xvi)

(a) medicinal opium; (b) prepared opium (product suitable for smoking, incl. dross/residue after smoking); (c) phenanthrene alkaloids — morphine, codeine, thebaine and salts; (d) diacetylmorphine = diamorphine = heroin; (e) all preparations containing more than 0.2% morphine or containing any diacetylmorphine.

Doctrinal consequence (settled in Harjit Singh, para 22): Heroin (diacetylmorphine) is an “opium derivative” under s.2(xvi)(d)/(e) and therefore a “manufactured drug” under s.2(xi)(a), punished under s.21 — not under s.18 which deals with opium. This is why E. Micheal Raj (a heroin case) could not govern an opium case.

2. “Psychotropic Substance” — s. 2(xxiii)

Section 2(xxiii)“Psychotropic substance” means any substance, natural or synthetic, or any natural material or any salt or preparation of such substance or material, included in the list of psychotropic substances specified in the Schedule.
  • The Schedule (originally Entries 1–76 with Entry 77 as residuary “salts and preparations”; renumbered post-2001, residuary becoming Entry 111) controls identification. The Central Government may add/omit entries under s.3 to meet 1971 Convention obligations.
  • Examples: LSD, MDMA (ecstasy), methamphetamine, amphetamine, methaqualone, diazepam, alprazolam, buprenorphine, fentanyl, zolpidem.
  • Punished under s.22; the 2001 Notification fixes small/commercial quantities for each entry.

3. “Controlled Substance” — s. 2(viid)

Section 2(viid)“controlled substance” means any substance which the Central Government may, having regard to the available information as to its possible use in the production or manufacture of narcotic drugs or psychotropic substances or to the provisions of any International Convention, by notification in the Official Gazette, declare to be a controlled substance.

These are precursor chemicals (e.g. acetic anhydride, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, anthranilic acid) — not themselves drugs of abuse but essential to illicit manufacture. They are regulated under s.9A and breach of a s.9A order is punished under s.25A (RI up to 10 years + fine up to ₹1 lakh). The controlled-substance concept also feeds search provisions (ss.41–43, 49, 50(5)) and forfeiture (s.60).

4. The quantity definitions that drive sentencing

s. 2(vii-a) — “Commercial quantity”
“in relation to narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances, means any quantity greater than the quantity specified by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette.”
s. 2(xxiii-a) — “Small quantity”
“means any quantity lesser than the quantity specified by the Central Government by notification in the Official Gazette.”

The operative thresholds are in Notification S.O. 1055(E), dated 19-10-2001 (239 entries), amended by S.O. 2941/2942(E), dated 18-11-2009 which added Note 4. The interval between the small-quantity threshold and the commercial threshold is colloquially the “intermediate/lesser quantity” band (expressly punished in ss.20(b)(ii)(B), 21(b), 22(b)).

Part B — Authorities and Officers (Sections 4, 6, 7; with ss. 5, 53)

1. Section 4 — Central Government’s functions and power to create agencies

Section 4(1)Subject to the provisions of the Act, the Central Government shall take all such measures as it deems necessary or expedient for preventing and combating abuse of narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances and the illicit traffic therein, and for ensuring their medical and scientific use.

Section 4(2) enumerates the measure-area (illustrative, not exhaustive):

ClauseMeasure
(a)Coordination of actions by various officers, State Governments and other authorities under the Act or under any other enforcement law.
(b)Obligations under the International Conventions.
(c)Assistance to foreign authorities and international organisations for coordinated, universal action against illicit traffic.
(d)Identification, treatment, education, after-care, rehabilitation and social re-integration of addicts.
(da)Availability of narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances for medical and scientific use (inserted 2014).
(e)All other matters necessary for effective implementation.
Section 4(3) — the NCB clauseThe Centre may, by Official Gazette order, constitute an authority or a hierarchy of authorities by such name(s) as specified, to exercise such powers/functions of the Centre under the Act as are mentioned in the order, subject to the Centre’s supervision and control.

Exercising this power, the Government of India constituted the Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB) on 17 March 1986, with the Director General NCB; s.50A later specifically empowered the DG-NCB (or authorised officer) to undertake controlled delivery. The NCB is the apex coordination/enforcement body and national nodal agency under the anti-drug conventions.

2. Section 5 — Officers of the Central Government

  • s.5(1): the Centre shall appoint a Narcotics Commissioner and may appoint other officers with such designations as it thinks fit.
  • s.5(2): the Narcotics Commissioner — by himself or through subordinate officers — exercises all powers/functions relating to superintendence of cultivation of the opium poppy and production of opium, plus other entrusted functions.
  • s.5(3): officers are under the general control of the Central Government (or the Central Board of Indirect Taxes & Customs/other authority if so directed).

3. Section 6 — The NDPS Consultative Committee

  • s.6(1): the Centre may constitute an advisory body named “The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Consultative Committee” to advise on matters of administration of the Act referred to it.
  • s.6(2): a Chairman + members not exceeding 20, appointed by the Centre.
  • s.6(3): meets when required; regulates its own procedure.
  • s.6(4): may constitute sub-committees and co-opt non-members (including non-officials).
  • s.6(5): term, filling of casual vacancies and allowances are rule-prescribed.

4. Section 7 — Officers of the State Government

  • s.7(1): the State Government may appoint officers with such designations as it thinks fit for the purposes of the Act (e.g. State Excise/ Drugs Control/Police empowered officers).
  • s.7(2): they work under the general control and direction of the State Government or any designated authority.

5. Related machinery: s. 53 — power to invest departmental officers with SHO powers

s.53(1): the Centre (after consulting the State Government) may, by notification, invest officers of central excise, narcotics, customs, revenue intelligence, para-military/armed forces with the powers of an officer-in-charge of a police station for investigation of NDPS offences. s.53(2): the State Government may similarly invest officers of drugs control, revenue or excise departments. This is why NCB/Customs/DRI officers can file charge-sheets and receive seized stock under s.52A(2).

Part C — National Fund for Control of Drug Abuse (Section 7A; s. 7B)

Chapter IIA was inserted by the 1989 amendment.

Section 7A(1) — constitution and creditsThe Central Government may constitute a Fund called the National Fund for Control of Drug Abuse. There shall be credited to it —
(a) moneys provided by Parliament after due appropriation;
(b) sale proceeds of property forfeited under Chapter VA;
(c) grants made by any person or institution;
(d) income from investment of the Fund.
Section 7A(2) — application of the FundThe Fund is applied to meet expenditure on measures for —
(a) combating illicit traffic in NDPS/controlled substances; (b) controlling drug abuse; (c) identifying and treating/rehabilitating addicts; (d) preventing drug abuse; (e) educating the public against drug abuse; (f) supplying drugs to addicts where medically necessary.
  • s.7A(3)–(5): a Governing Body (Chairman not below the rank of Additional Secretary; up to six members) advises and sanctions expenditure up to the notified limit; it regulates its own procedure.
  • s.7B: the Government shall prepare an annual report of activities financed under the Fund and cause it to be laid before Parliament.
Policy link: the Fund operationalises s.4(2)(d) (rehabilitation) and gives a statutory channel for ploughing forfeited trafficking assets back into demand-reduction and de-addiction — an early example of asset-forfeiture-funded social remediation.

1. Section 8 — Prohibition of certain operations (the core prohibition)

Section 8No person shall —
(a) cultivate any coca plant or gather any portion of it; or
(b) cultivate the opium poppy or any cannabis plant; or
(c) produce, manufacture, possess, sell, purchase, transport, warehouse, use, consume, import inter-State, export inter-State, import into India, export from India or tranship any narcotic drug or psychotropic substance,

  • Second proviso: s.8 does not apply to export of poppy straw for decorative purposes.
  • Breach of s.8 is the threshold illegality for every penalty section (ss.15–22): e.g. in Harjit Singh, possession of commercial opium “violated s.8” and attracted s.18(b).

Section 8A (inserted 2001) creates the domestic money-laundering-type offences over drug-derived property — conversion/transfer, concealment/disguise, or acquisition/possession/use of property known to be derived from an NDPS offence; breach is punished under s.27B (3–10 years RI).

Part E — Presumptions under the Act (Sections 35 and 54)

The NDPS Act is a classic socio-economic/welfare penal statute where the common law presumption of innocence is statutorily diluted. Two distinct presumptions operate: s.35 (guilty mind) and s.54 (possession → commission). Note the heightened rebuttal standard in s.35(2).

1. Section 35 — Presumption of culpable mental state

Section 35(1)In any prosecution for an offence under this Act which requires a culpable mental state of the accused, the court shall presume the existence of such mental state; but it shall be a defence for the accused to prove the fact that he had no such mental state with respect to the act charged.
Explanation: “culpable mental state” includes intention, motive, knowledge of a fact and belief in, or reason to believe, a fact.
Section 35(2) — standard of proofA fact is said to be proved only when the court believes it to exist beyond reasonable doubt and not merely when its existence is established by a preponderance of probability.
Nature of the presumption
Rebuttable presumption of law as to mens rea. Once the prosecution proves the actus (possession/transport etc.), knowledge/intention is presumed. The accused must positively prove lack of the mental state.
Contrast with ordinary criminal law
Under the IPC the prosecution proves mens rea beyond reasonable doubt; an accused acquits by creating a reasonable doubt. In socio-economic statutes the accused generally rebuts on preponderance (e.g. PMLA s.24). Under NDPS s.35(2) the accused must go beyond preponderance — up to beyond reasonable doubt.

The faculty notes tabulate: IPC — presumption of innocence, accused need only raise a probability/doubt; NDPS — presumption of commission of crime (ss.35, 54) and the accused must disprove beyond reasonable doubt. This is constitutionally saved because the offence is grave, the facts are specially within the accused’s knowledge, and licence/permit records are accessible to him (Illustrations (e)/(f) to s.106 Evidence Act logic). In Devendra @ Kalu v. State (2020) and a consistent line of decisions the Court has read s.35 as shifting only the evidential burden after the prosecution establishes possession; the prosecution still proves the prohibited act and seizure beyond doubt.

2. Section 54 — Presumption from possession of illicit articles

Section 54In trials under this Act, it may be presumed, unless and until the contrary is proved, that the accused has committed an offence under this Act in respect of —
(a) any narcotic drug/psychotropic substance/controlled substance;
(b) any opium poppy, cannabis plant or coca plant growing on any land which he has cultivated;
(c) any apparatus specially designed, or group of utensils specially adapted, for manufacture; or
(d) any materials which have undergone any process towards manufacture, or any residue left from manufacture,
for the possession of which he fails to account satisfactorily.

Section 54 is an evidentiary presumption triggered by unexplained possession. The prosecution must first prove possession of the article; if the accused gives no satisfactory account, the court may presume he committed the offence in respect of it — e.g. possession of manufacturing apparatus/partially processed material supports a manufacture charge. It is “may presume” (rebuttable), leaving the court discretion under s.4 Evidence Act.

Coupled with s.54, the statute compresses the prosecution’s burden after seizure, while the procedural safeguards of ss.42 and 50 and the high rebuttal standard keep the scheme Article 21-compliant.”

4. Sections 31 and 31A — recidivism and capital punishment

s.31 — enhanced punishment after previous conviction: a person previously convicted of (or attempt/abetment/conspiracy to commit) any NDPS offence, subsequently convicted of an offence with the same punishment, is punished for the second/subsequent offence with 1½ times the maximum term and 1½ times the maximum fine (post-2014; earlier it was 1½ of the minimum where minimum sentence exists). A conviction under a corresponding foreign law counts (s.31(3)).

s.31A — death penalty for certain repeat offences (inserted 1989; narrowed 2001; made optional in 2014):

  • First conviction must be under s.19, s.24 or s.27A, or for an offence involving commercial quantity (these are quantity-independent in ss.19/24/27A).
  • Second/subsequent conviction must be for specified production/manufacture/possession/transport/import/export/transhipment of table-listed drugs at or above the table quantity, or financing such activities.
  • Table quantities: opium 10 kg; morphine 1 kg; heroin 1 kg; codeine 1 kg; thebaine 1 kg; cocaine 500 g; hashish 20 kg; LSD 500 g; THC 500 g; methamphetamine/methaqualone/amphetamine 1,500 g; salts & preparations of (ix)–(xiii) 1,500 g; mixture — the lesser constituent threshold.
  • Pre-2014: “shall be punishable with death.” Post the Bombay High Court decision Indian Harm Reduction Network v. UOI (2011) and the 2014 amendment: “shall be punished with punishment which shall not be less than the punishment specified in s.31 or with death” — i.e. judicial discretion restored; death only on special reasons (s.354(3) CrPC) in the rarest-of-rare category (Bachan Singh).
  • Foreign previous convictions under corresponding laws count (s.31A(2)).

Part G — Procedure: Special Courts, Bail, Search, Seizure, Disposal and Immunity

Chapter V (ss.41–68) is the constitutional-nerve centre of the Act. Because the statute grants extraordinary powers (warrantless entry, arrest and search), the legislature embedded mandatory checks — recorded information (s.42), the Gazetted-Officer/Magistrate option in personal search (s.50), reporting time-limits (ss.42(2), 57), and Magistrate-supervised sampling (s.52A). Three of the five prescribed cases (Karnail Singh, Jadeja, Mohanlal) interpret these checks.

1. Special Courts — Sections 36, 36A–36D

  • s.36: the Central/State Government may, by notification, constitute Special Courts for speedy trial; each consists of a single Judge appointed with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court; only a Sessions/Additional Sessions Judge is qualified.
  • s.36A(1)(a): every offence punishable with imprisonment over 3 years is triable only by the Special Court of the area (thus all commercial and most intermediate offences); s.36A(5): offences punishable with not more than 3 years may be tried summarily.
  • s.36A(1)(b): remand powers — Judicial Magistrate up to 15 days custody in whole; Executive Magistrate up to 7 days; the Magistrate must forward the accused to the Special Court if detention becomes unnecessary.
  • s.36A(1)(c): Special Court exercises s.167 CrPC remand powers. s.36A(1)(d): cognizance may be taken on police report or a compliant of an authorised officer without committal.
  • s.36A(2): the Special Court may try non-NDPS offences with which the accused could be charged at the same trial under CrPC.
  • s.36A(3): the High Court’s bail powers under s.439 CrPC remain; “Magistrate” there includes a Special Court.
  • s.36A(4): for offences under ss.19, 24, 27A or commercial-quantity offences, the s.167 default-charge-sheet period of 90 days becomes 180 days, extendable by the Special Court up to one year on the Public Prosecutor’s report showing progress and specific reasons.
  • s.36B: appeals/revision lie to the High Court (Chapters XXIX–XXX CrPC applied; Special Court treated as Sessions Court). s.36C: CrPC applies (including bail/bonds); Special Court = Court of Session; prosecutor = Public Prosecutor. s.36D: transitional provision — pre-constitution, Sessions Courts try such cases.

2. Bail — Section 37: cognizable and non-bailable

s.37(1)(a): every offence under the Act is cognizable. s.37(1)(b): no person accused of an offence under ss.19, 24, 27A, or any offence involving commercial quantity, shall be released on bail/own bond unless —
(i) the Public Prosecutor has been given an opportunity to oppose; and
(ii) where opposed, the court is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds to believe he is not guilty and that he is not likely to commit any offence while on bail.
s.37(2): these are additional limitations to the CrPC restrictions.

Small/intermediate-quantity offences remain governed by the ordinary bail code, though all are non-bailable; the 2001 amendment confined the twin “super-bail” conditions to serious categories. In Rhea Chakraborty the Special Court stressed that s.27A requires no quantity, triggering s.37 conditions (bail was later granted by the High Court on merits).

3. Addict-friendly procedures — Section 39 (and s.64A)

s.39: where an addict is found guilty under s.27 or of an offence involving small quantity, the court may, having regard to age, character, antecedents, physical/mental condition, with his consent, instead of sentencing, direct de-toxification/de-addiction at a Government maintained/recognised institution on bond (report within ≤1 year); on the treatment report it may release him after admonition on bond for up to 3 years. Section 39 is the judicial-route counterpart of the pre-prosecution immunity under s.64A (addict charged under s.27 or small-quantity offences who voluntarily undergoes complete treatment gets immunity; withdrawable if treatment is incomplete).

4. Entry, search, seizure and arrest — Sections 41, 42, 43, 49

Section 41 — power by warrant or authorisation

  • s.41(1): a Metropolitan Magistrate, JMFC or specially empowered JM-II may issue warrant of arrest / for search (day or night) of building, conveyance or place on reason-to-believe.
  • s.41(2): a Gazetted officer of central excise/narcotics/customs/revenue intelligence/other central dept. (incl. para-military/armed forces) or of state revenue/drugs control/excise/police, empowered by general/special order, may on personal knowledge or information taken in writing authorise a subordinate (above peon/sepoy/constable rank) or himself arrest/search.
  • s.41(3): the warrant/addressee officer and the authorising officer have all powers of an officer acting under s.42.

Section 42 — warrantless power over building, conveyance or enclosed place

s.42(1): an empowered officer (superior in rank to peon/sepoy/constable) of the named departments, if he has reason to believe from personal knowledge or information given by any person and taken down in writing that drugs/controlled substance/articles/illegally-acquired-property documents are kept/concealed in any building, conveyance or enclosed place, may between sunrise and sunset: (a) enter & search; (b) break open doors/remove obstacles on resistance; (c) seize drugs, manufacturing materials, articles, animals, conveyances and documents; (d) detain, search and, if he thinks proper, arrest.
Proviso 1 (2014): for a licence-holder for manufacture of manufactured drugs/psychotropic/controlled substances, such power must be exercised by an officer not below sub-inspector rank.
Proviso 2 (night search): if a warrant cannot be obtained without affording opportunity for concealment/escape, he may enter between sunset and sunrise after recording grounds of belief.
s.42(2): where information is taken down or grounds recorded, he must send a copy within 72 hours (pre-2001: “forthwith”) to his immediate official superior.

Section 43 — public place/in-transit power (no written-information requirement)

Any officer of a s.42 department may (a) seize in any public place or in transit drugs/controlled substances, plus animals/conveyances/articles/documents; (b) detain and search any person he has reason to believe committed an offence, and if unlawful possession appears, arrest him and any person in his company.
“Public place” (Explanation) includes any public conveyance, hotel, shop or other place intended for use by, or accessible to, the public.
Key 42/43 distinction (Karnail Singh, para 12): s.42 requires recording reasons/information in writing before search of a building/conveyance/enclosed place; s.43 contains no such requirement for public-place seizures. Characterising the scene decides compliance.

Section 49 — stopping and searching conveyances

An officer authorised under s.42, suspecting an animal/conveyance is or is about to be used for transport in contravention, may at any time stop it (and compel aircraft to land), (a) rummage/search; (b) examine goods; (c) use all lawful means to stop it, and where such means fail, fire upon the animal/conveyance.

7. Section 52A text (as amended by Act 16 of 2014) — Mohanlal’s pivot

(2) Where seized items are forwarded to the SHO/s.53 officer, the officer must prepare an inventory with description, quality, quantity, packing, marks, numbers, identity particulars, country of origin, and apply to a Magistrate for — (a) certifying inventory correctness; (b) photographs in his presence certified true; (c) drawing representative samples in his presence and certifying the sample list.

8. Immunity and pardon — Section 64 (and s.64A)

(2): accepted tender makes him immune to the extent tendered.
(3): non-compliance/concealment/false evidence → recorded finding → immunity deemed withdrawn; he may then be tried.

Harjit Singh v. State of Punjab

(2011) 4 SCC 441 · Supreme Court of India · Author: Dr. B.S. Chauhan, J. (Division Bench with P. Sathasivam, J.) · Decided 30 March 2011
Charging sections.18(b) NDPS (opium, commercial quantity)
Key provisionsss.2(xv), 2(xvi), 2(xi), 8, 18; Notification Entries 56, 77, 92, 93
QuestionDoes morphine % fix the quantity band for pure opium?
OutcomeAppeal dismissed — conviction RI 10 yrs + ₹1 lakh affirmed

Facts (from the DU material, paras 3–7)

  • On 4-7-2003, a police party was going from Focal Point, Mandi Gobindgarh towards the G.T. Road on patrol in a government vehicle. Near a culvert in village Ambe Majra, they spotted the appellant coming on foot with a plastic bag in his right hand; on seeing the police he turned to the left. He was apprehended on suspicion.
  • One Ashok Kumar, an independent witness, arrived and joined the party. The appellant was apprised of his s.50 right to be searched before a Gazetted Officer and his statement was recorded. The ASP, Shri Dinesh Partap Singh, was summoned; in his presence Inspector Amarjit Singh (PW-3) searched the bag — it contained opium.
  • Two samples of 10 g each were drawn; the remaining opium weighed 7.10 kg. Samples and stock were sealed and taken into possession. ₹510 was recovered on personal search; arrest memo prepared; FIR registered.
  • The FSL report confirmed the substance was opium with 0.8% morphine. Charge-sheet under s.18 followed; the appellant claimed trial.
  • At trial the prosecution examined PW-1 Constable Manjinder Singh, PW-2 HC Jagdish Singh, PW-3 Inspector Amarjit Singh, PW-4 ASP Dinesh Partap Singh and PW-5 SI Dalip Singh. The independent witness Ashok Kumar was not examined as he had been “won over”. In his s.313 CrPC statement the appellant alleged false implication, claimed he was lifted from home, ₹6,000 snatched, and that he could barely walk after a 1999 accident; he examined six defence witnesses.

Procedural history

  1. Special Judge, Fatehgarh Sahib (2-9-2005): convicted under s.18; RI 10 years + ₹1,00,000 fine (6 months further RI in default).
  2. Punjab & Haryana High Court (19-5-2010): dismissed the appeal.
  3. Supreme Court: SLP/appeal by certificate; both courts had recorded concurrent findings of fact, leaving only the sentence question open.

Issue before the Supreme Court

Whether, where recovered opium weighs 7.10 kg but contains only 0.8% morphine (56.96 g), the sentence must be graded by the morphine content (which sat above the small-quantity threshold for morphine but below its commercial threshold), making the maximum 10-year sentence unwarranted — or by the weight of the opium itself.

Arguments on behalf of the appellant (Shri R.S. Suri, Senior Advocate)

  • Reliance on E. Micheal Raj v. Intelligence Officer, NCB, (2008) 5 SCC 161: when a drug is found mixed with neutral substance(s), only the content of the narcotic drug is taken into account for punishment.
  • 0.8% of 7.10 kg = 56.96 g morphine — above the 0.5 g minimum but below the 250 g commercial threshold for morphine: an intermediate band, hence the maximum sentence was illegal.

Arguments on behalf of the State (Shri Jayant K. Sud, Addl. Advocate General)

  • The recovered material was opium as such, not a mixture; it is independently offending material. Morphine percentage becomes relevant only for a mixture/derivative.
  • The Notification contains distinct entries: Entry 92 — opium (small 25 g; commercial 2.5 kg); Entry 77 — morphine (0.5 g / 250 g); Entry 93 — opium derivatives (5 g / 250 g). The case falls under Entry 92: 7.10 kg is nearly three times the commercial quantity.
  • E. Micheal Raj was a heroin case (an opium derivative/manufactured drug) and has no application to opium.
  • The Notification dated 18-11-2009 (Note 4) provides quantities apply to the entire mixture; the appellant being a mere carrier was irrelevant to the legal position.

Reasoning of the Court

  1. Retrospectivity (Art. 20(1)): the 2009 amendment/Note 4 created a procedure which could enhance sentence; a penal provision enhancing sentence cannot operate retrospectively. The 2009 Notification therefore did not apply to the July 2003 recovery.
  2. Nature of opium: opium is the coagulated juice of the opium poppy and contains alkaloids (morphine, codeine, thebaine — morphine primary). It has a characteristic appearance and strong scent identifiable without chemical analysis; analysis is needed only where it is so diluted in a mixture that its traits are lost. The old Opium Act decisions are not authorities under NDPS; under NDPS chemical analysis is essential to prove the case, yet the FSL here established “substance is opium, morphine 0.8%”.
  3. Two-clause definition s.2(xv): clause (a) covers the coagulated juice (pure opium); clause (b) covers mixtures with/without neutral material, with the cut-off that preparations containing ≤ 0.2% morphine are excluded. “Coagulated” means solidified/clotted/curdled. The 0.2% proviso can apply only to clause (b) mixtures.
  4. Application to facts: the material was opium in its natural coagulated form — no neutral material, no new preparation: clause (a) squarely applied. If morphine content decided punishment for opium, Entry 92 would be rendered redundant. Entry 92 exclusively fixes small/commercial quantities of opium; morphine percentage merely indicates potency.
  5. Classification is a pre-condition: the law first requires identifying and classifying the substance, then finding the entry. Entries: 56 heroin; 77 morphine; 92 opium; 93 opium derivatives. Morphine is a derivative but is separately notified.
  6. E. Micheal Raj distinguished: heroin = diacetylmorphine, an opium derivative under s.2(xvi)(d)/(e) and thus a manufactured drug under s.2(xi)(a), punished under s.21. Its ratio (pure content in a mixture) was irrelevant where there was no mixture.
  7. Amarsingh Ramjibhai Barot v. State of Gujarat, (2005) 7 SCC 550 explained: black liquid with 2.8% anhydrous morphine plus poppy flower pieces was treated as an opium derivative under s.2(xvi)(e) (the 0.2% threshold there classified the substance) — a different situation.
  8. Possession of 7.10 kg could not be for personal consumption; it violated s.8 and was rightly punished under s.18(b) (commercial: minimum 10, up to 20 years; ₹1–2 lakh fine).
DecisionThe appeal was devoid of merit and dismissed; the conviction (RI 10 years + ₹1 lakh fine) was affirmed.
Principle / ratio ① Classify before sentencing: identify the substance and apply its own Notification entry. ② For pure opium (coagulated juice, s.2(xv)(a)), the entire weight counts; morphine percentage is irrelevant to the small/commercial band (Entry 92). ③ Morphine assay matters only for mixtures under s.2(xv)(b) or to bring a preparation within the opium-derivative definition s.2(xvi) (e.g. >0.2% morphine). ④ A notification whose effect enhances punishment cannot apply retrospectively (Art. 20(1)).

Hira Singh and Another v. Union of India

(2020) 20 SCC 272 · Three-Judge Bench, Supreme Court · Author: M.R. Shah, J. · Reference answered 2020
NatureReference to 3-Judge Bench (order in (2017) 8 SCC 162)
Statutesss.2(xx), (xi), (xiv), (xxiii), (vii-a), (xxiii-a), 21, 22, 27; Notifications 19-10-2001 & 18-11-2009
OverruledE. Micheal Raj v. NCB (2008) 5 SCC 161
UpheldNote 4 of the 2009 Notification — valid, clarificatory

Genesis and the questions referred

Disagreeing with E. Micheal Raj, which held that where a drug is mixed with neutral substance(s) only the pure narcotic content counts for sentence, the Bench framed five questions on 3-7-2017:

  1. Does E. Micheal Raj need reconsideration for omitting Entry 239 and Note 2 of the 2001 Notification and the interplay of the Act with s.21?
  2. Does the impugned notification redefine the parameters of the offence/punishment?
  3. Does the NDPS Act permit the Central Government such a dispensation?
  4. Must the mixture be considered in totality (street weight), or only actual drug content?
  5. Is s.21 a standalone provision or intrinsically linked to the “manufactured drug”/“preparation” provisions?

Arguments for the Union (Shri Aman Lekhi, ASG)

  • E. Micheal Raj read only s.21 in isolation; the expression “offending material” appears nowhere in the Act; reliance on Ouseph v. State of Kerala, (2004) 4 SCC 446 was misplaced as that decision passed sub silentio.
  • Legislative history: the original s.2(xx) defined “preparation” (one or more drugs in dosage/solution/mixture); “mixture” = mechanical mixture of two or more substances; s.2(xxiii) psychotropic substance included preparations (Schedule Entry 77 residuary, now 111); narcotic drugs s.2(xiv) included coca leaf, cannabis, opium, poppy straw and manufactured drugs — the Act always covered preparations, not merely pure content.
  • Originally only s.27 recognised a “small quantity” (S.O. 827 of 14-11-1985, five drugs); the 1989 amendment did not alter the scheme; the 2001 amendment added s.2(vii-a) and (xxiii-a) to rationalise sentencing — emphasis remained on quantity of the drug/substance including preparations; s.37 was similarly amended and ss.27/64A reworked (possession left s.27; addict immunity narrowed).
  • Note 2 of the 2001 Notification expressly applied notified quantities to preparations; Note 4 (2009) merely stated the obvious (ex abundanti cautela) — even ignoring it, the Act reached the entire mixture/solution.
  • Chapman v. United States, US Supreme Court (LSD blotter paper; 50 mg LSD on 5.7 g paper): the sentence turned on the weight of the “mixture or substance containing a detectable amount” — the street weight, not pure content; “that is a rational sentencing scheme”.
  • Practical absurdity: small quantity of heroin is 5 g; pure-content theory would make 100 g of street heroin (~0.25 g/dose ≈ 400 doses) a small-quantity offence with six months — traffickers would escape; the object and Preamble demand a literal deterrent reading (Crawford on Statutory Construction; Maxwell via Directorate of Enforcement v. Deepak Mahajan, (1994) 3 SCC 440; Murlidhar Meghraj Loya; Reema Aggarwal; Rajinder Singh — fair, pragmatic, common-sense construction; ex visceribus actus). Thus s.21 is not standalone; E. Micheal Raj is per incuriam.

Arguments for the accused appellants (Shri R.K. Kapoor, Crl. A. No. 722/2017; Shri Manoj Swarup, Crl. A. No. 721/2017)

  • Ultra vires: ss.2(vii-a)/(xxiii-a) empower the Centre only to notify quantities of the narcotic drug/psychotropic substance — there is no power to count neutral material, which is neither in Columns 2/4 nor punishable. “What cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.”
  • The A/B illustration: A holds 4 g heroin (below 5 g small) → one year; B holds 1 g heroin + 250 g neutral material (251 g > 250 g commercial) → up to 20 years: i.e. “one year for heroin and nineteen years for neutral material”; more dilution/less potency would yield more punishment — arbitrary and destructive of the 2001 rationalisation.
  • The Government’s power is confined to raising/lowering thresholds (e.g. heroin 5 g → 1 g; 250 g → 100 g), never to add neutral weight. “Mixture” is referenced only through “preparation”, which speaks of drugs/substances, not neutral material.
  • (Swarup) The Act itself recognises neutral material only at defined places — ss.2(iii) cannabis, 2(vi) coca leaf, 2(xv)–(xvi) opium/opium derivative; Note 4 could validly clarify only there. Across-the-board application makes it legislation, expands the offence, and frustrates 2001 rationalisation; E. Micheal Raj had no need to discuss Entry 239 because a notification is not the source of power.

Arguments of the Intervenor — Indian Drug Manufacturers’ Association (Shri Anand Grover)

  • Column 4 uses the chemical name of each drug; chemical composition fixes quantity (except natural plant drugs — Entries 23 cannabis/charas, 26 coca leaf, 55 ganja, 92 opium, 110 opium straw, 111 Indian hemp tincture — whose definitions expressly include neutral material).
  • Section 2(xi) notifications legitimately exempt pharmaceutical preparations (e.g. codeine Entry 35 and Entries 36, 37, 48, 70, 76, 83, 87): Note 4 would drag exempt medicines into liability “through the back door”; licences run on actual drug quantity while punishment would run on bulk — absurd for the pharmaceutical industry.
  • Legislative acquiescence point: the Government introduced the NDPS (Amendment) Bill 2011 (No. 78 of 2011) adding “in terms of the pure drug content or otherwise” to both quantity definitions; the Parliamentary Standing Committee rejected “preparation/otherwise” as vague and arbitrary; the Bill was withdrawn in 2013 and the change was not enacted in 2014 — showing the Centre lacked the power it claimed by notification.
  • Entry 239 (two-or-more-drug mixtures, with */** markers) uses the lesser small/commercial threshold of the constituents — illustration: 200 g = 110 g charas + 90 g ganja → judged by charas (small 100 g) not ganja (small 1 kg), i.e. intermediate liability; like clause (viii) of the s.31A table; Entry 239 never aggregates neutral weight. Reliance also on Bangladesh Supreme Court, Mohd. Jafar Alam (8-5-2012).

Discussion and findings of the Court

  1. Before E. Micheal Raj the consistent view was that the entire weight of the manufactured drug/preparation/mixture including neutral substance was counted. E. Micheal Raj took the contrary view solely from the 2001 amendment and s.21.
  2. What was seized there was 4 kg (4.5 kg noted in one passage) of heroin — Entry 56 (5 g small; 250 g commercial); the Court wrongly treated it as opium derivative/manufactured drug for sentencing and never considered Note 2 or Entry 239; had Note 2 been applied, 4.5 kg heroin was plainly commercial.
  3. The Statement of Objects and Reasons, 2001 (quoted in full) aimed at rationalisation — deterrence for significant-quantity traffickers, leniency for addicts — but does not say pure content is to be extracted; E. Micheal Raj “read more than what was stated”.
  4. The Preamble and SOR make the Act a deterrent special statute (Deepak Mahajan; Maxwell on modifying construction to avoid absurdity; the court must not frustrate legislative purpose). Definitions of “manufacture”, “manufactured drug” and “preparation”, read conjointly, require the total weight including neutral material.
  5. Reality check (para 10.3): illicit drugs are seldom pure — heroin is cut with caffeine (aids vaporisation), aspirin, chalk powder, zinc oxide; “brown sugar/smack” is only ~20% heroin; the harm lies in the entire street-preparation; pure-content grading would collapse commercial-quantity prosecutions.
  6. History (paras 10.5): Opium Act 1857/1875(78), Dangerous Drugs Act 1930 and deficiencies led to the 1985 Act — “the guilty must be in and the innocent out”; prevention outweighs punishment; the statute must be interpreted literally to serve its object, protecting life and liberty of the innocent through procedural safeguards rather than by diluting quantity rules.
  7. For two-or-more-drug mixtures (Entry 239), the lesser threshold of the constituent drugs applies and the entire mixture weight, including neutral material, is counted (100 g heroin + methadone illustration).
  8. Validity of Note 4: it was inserted after E. Micheal Raj by way of abundant caution; it merely clarifies the existing scheme (preparations/mixtures always covered); it is not ultra vires ss.2(vii-a)/(xxiii-a) or the Act. The challenge failed.
Formal answers to the reference (para 12) 12.1 E. Micheal Raj is not good law: neutral-substance weight is not to be excluded.
12.2 On seizure of a mixture with neutral substance(s), the neutral weight shall be included along with actual drug content in determining small/commercial quantity.
12.3 s.21 is not standalone; it must be construed with the whole Act and the 2001/2009 Notifications.
12.4 The 2009 Notification (Note 4) is valid, not ultra vires; writ petitions and Civil Appeal No. 5218 of 2017 were dismissed; matters remitted for decision on merits in light of the reference.
Principle / ratio Sentencing quantity is the “street weight” — the total weight of the seized mixture/preparation/solution/dosage form, including neutral/cutting material — not the assay-pure narcotic content. This applies to manufactured drugs (s.21) and psychotropic substances (s.22); for a mixture of two or more notified drugs the lowest constituent threshold (Entry 239) governs. Note 4 is declarative of the pre-existing law. Government notifications cannot reduce thresholds inconsistently, but counting the full seized preparation is within ss.2(vii-a)/(xxiii-a) because the Act itself punishes preparations (s.2(xx)).
Harjit Singh vs Hira Singh reconciled: the two decisions are complementary, not conflicting. Harjit Singh asks “what is the substance?” — a distinct drug gets its distinct entry (pure opium → Entry 92; morphine/derivative → Entries 77/93); pure drug vs mixture classification is preserved and the 0.2% cut-offs remain definitional. Hira Singh asks “once the substance is a mixture/preparation, how much do you weigh?” — answer: the entire mixture including neutral material. Illustrations: (i) 7.10 kg coagulated opium → Entry 92, commercial (Harjit); (ii) 300 g brown sugar of 20% purity = 60 g heroin content → weigh all 300 g against Entry 56 → commercial (Hira Singh); (iii) 1 g heroin + 250 g lactose = 251 g → commercial (the Court’s rejected “absurdity” is now the law).

Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana

(2009) 8 SCC 539 · Constitution Bench (5 Judges), Supreme Court · Author: P. Sathasivam, J. · Decided 29 July 2009
FocusScope & mandatory character of s.42 (entry/search/arrest without warrant)
Conflict resolvedAbdul Rashid Ibrahim Mansuri (2000) vs Sajan Abraham (2001)
Key change2001 amendment: report time “forthwith” → “72 hours”
RuleTotal non-compliance impermissible; delayed explained compliance acceptable

Why the reference arose

A three-Judge Bench in Abdul Rashid Ibrahim Mansuri v. State of Gujarat, (2000) 2 SCC 513 held compliance with s.42 mandatory — failure to reduce information to writing and forthwith report to the superior causes prejudice. Another three-Judge Bench in Sajan Abraham v. State of Kerala, (2001) 6 SCC 692 took a pragmatic view and accepted substantial compliance in emergent patrol situations. The conflict was referred to the Constitution Bench, which placed the scheme in context: Chapter IV creates offences/penalties, Chapter V provides procedure (s.41 warrants; s.42 warrantless entry into building/conveyance/enclosed place; s.43 public-place powers; s.50 conditions of personal search). Stringent punishment requires a balance between enforcement and protection from oppression; Chapter V’s checks “are capable of being misused through arbitrary or indiscriminate exercise unless strict compliance is required”.

The two precursor decisions examined in detail

Abdul Rashid Ibrahim Mansuri
Facts: PW-2, Inspector, Dariapur PS, got information on 12-1-1988 that one Iqbal was transporting charas to Shahpur in an autorickshaw (~4 p.m.). They stopped the auto driven by the accused; four gunny bags yielded ten packets of charas worth ₹5.29 lakh. Under s.313 the accused did not dispute interception but claimed two unknown persons had loaded the bags into his auto (Chokha Bazar story).
Trial Court acquitted; the High Court reversed and convicted.
Held: PW-2 admitted acting on prior information precisely falling under s.42(1) yet neither reduced it to writing nor sent any copy to the superior — absolute non-compliance. Applying the Constitution Bench’s State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh, (1999) 6 SCC 172 s.50 principles to s.42, the action on unrecorded information becomes suspect; trial may not be vitiated solely on that score, but prejudice results; non-recording deprives the accused and the court of the earliest, most valuable material and may count as a circumstance in favour of the accused (the recollected “information” itself tended to exculpate). Acquittal restored. Followed in Koluttumottil Razak v. State of Kerala, (2004) 4 SCC 465.
Sajan Abraham v. State of Kerala
Facts: on 10-10-1993 (~7 p.m.) the Head Constable (PW-3) of a Special Squad learnt that a person was selling injectable “Tidigesic” near Blue Tronics Junction, Palluruthy. While going to report to the SI, PW-3 met the SI (PW-5) on patrol in a jeep; the party proceeded immediately, found the accused with a packet — 5 strips of 5 ampoules each, three syringes, ₹10 — seized on the spot (Ext. P-1 mahazar), took one ampoule sample and arrested.
Trial Court acquitted on discrepancies; High Court reversed and convicted under s.21.
Held: a pragmatic approach is required; provisions must not be construed so literally as to make compliance impossible. The information reached the SI while he was moving in a jeep on patrol; had he stopped to write and report, the accused would have escaped. He sent the FIR/arrest records immediately afterwards. No s.42 violation — substantial compliance. Strict mandates exist to balance stringency, but they should not hand offenders an escape route.

Statutory provisions analysed

  • s.42(1) (pre-2001 quoted): empowered officer (rank above peon/sepoy/constable) with reason to believe from personal knowledge or information given and taken down in writing that contraband is in a building, conveyance or enclosed place, may between sunrise and sunset (a) enter/search, (b) break open on resistance, (c) seize drugs, materials, conveyances/animals/articles/documents, (d) detain/search/arrest; proviso allows night entry where a warrant cannot be obtained without risk of concealment/escape, after recording grounds.
  • s.42(2): pre-2001 — send the written information/grounds “forthwith” to the immediate official superior; post-2001 — “within seventy-two hours”.
  • Balbir Singh dicta ((1994) 3 SCC 299, paras 2-C/3): prior information must be in writing; personal-knowledge daytime searches need no recorded reasons; night searches do; total failure to send the copy affects the prosecution case, while mere delay is a question of fact (explained or undue).
  • s.42 vs s.43: s.42 (building/conveyance/enclosed place) requires pre-search recording; s.43 (public places/in transit) does not.
  • s.50 and the 2001 sub-ss.(5)/(6): the Court noted that insertion of the emergency s.100-CrPC search route and 72-hour reporting “diluted” the strict Baldev Singh mandate without removing safeguards — flexibility only for genuine emergencies.

Additional reasoning — technology and pragmatism

With mobile phones and wireless communication, information often reaches officers on the move; insisting on station-register entries before acting would frustrate emergency interdiction. The provisions “should be taken as a discretionary measure which should check the misuse of the Act rather than providing an escape to hardened drug-peddlers”. The mandatory core was restricted to communicating the recorded information/grounds to the superior; non-compliance which causes prejudice vitiates, not every deviation.

Conclusions of the Constitution Bench (paras 17(a)–(d)) (a) In-station information: the officer must record the information in the concerned register and forthwith send a copy to his immediate official superior before acting under clauses (a)–(d) of s.42(1).
(b) On-move emergency information: where information is received while on patrol/move (mobile or otherwise), it calls for immediate action, and delay would result in removal/destruction of goods/evidence, it is not feasible to write first; he may act under clauses (a)–(d), then as soon as practical record the information and inform the superior.
(c) Sequence: writing/reporting should normally precede entry, search and seizure; in special emergent circumstances it may be postponed by a reasonable period — a question of urgency and expediency.
(d) Total non-compliance is impermissible; delayed compliance with satisfactory explanation is acceptable. If the information was received in the station with ample time and nothing is recorded/sent — a suspicious, clear violation; if nothing is recorded or communicated at all — likewise a clear violation. Adequacy of compliance is a question of fact in each case. The 2001 amendment (72 hours) strengthens this position.
Principle / ratioSection 42 is mandatory in its core (reduce information to writing; copy to superior) but admits temporally relaxed compliance in genuine on-the-spot emergencies where acting later would allow escape/destruction; total omission or in-station inaction is fatal to the prosecution’s credibility (recovery becomes suspect, prejudice presumed in practice), while explained delay is not.

Vijaysinh Chandubha Jadeja (and Another) v. State of Gujarat (and Others)

(2011) 1 SCC 609 · Constitution Bench, Supreme Court · Author: D.K. Jain, J. · Decided 29 October 2010
FocusTrue scope of s.50(1) — duty to “inform” vs mere enquiry
Conflict resolvedJoseph Fernandez / Prabha Shankar Dubey vs Krishna Kanwar
Anchored toState of Punjab v. Baldev Singh (1999) 6 SCC 172 (Constitution Bench)
RuleDuty to apprise the suspect of the right is mandatory & strict

Question referred

Does s.50 cast a duty on the empowered officer to inform the suspect of his right to be searched in the presence of a Gazetted Officer or Magistrate if he so desires — or is a mere enquiry (“would you like to be searched before a Magistrate/Gazetted Officer?”) due compliance?

The divergence that triggered the reference

  • Joseph Fernandez v. State of Goa, (2000) 1 SCC 707 (3 Judges): intimation in the words “if you wish you may be searched in the presence of a gazetted officer or a Magistrate” was treated as substantial compliance — a view taken without the benefit of the Baldev Singh interpretation.
  • Prabha Shankar Dubey v. State of M.P., (2004) 2 SCC 56: a notice (“we have information you carry opium; would you give search to me or to a gazetted officer/Magistrate?”) was also held substantial compliance, following Joseph Fernandez.
  • Krishna Kanwar (Smt.) alias Thakuraeen v. State of Rajasthan, (2004) 2 SCC 608: no specific form is needed, but the suspect must be made aware of the existence of the right; courts must see substance, not form.

Arguments on both sides

For the accused (Shri P.H. Parekh, Sr.)
Conjoint reading of s.50(1) & (3) shows the safeguard must be employed effectively and honestly; Baldev Singh unambiguously settled the duty to inform; Joseph Fernandez and Prabha Shankar Dubey wrongly imported “substantial compliance”.
Section 50 is the only safeguard against the statute’s extensive powers — a “due process” clause with minimum procedural limits; per Beckodan Abdul Rahiman v. State of Kerala, (2002) 4 SCC 229, the harshness of NDPS casts a heavier duty on the prosecution. Mere questioning can never amount to compliance; it would make the right illusory.
For the States / Union (Shri Siddharth Luthra; Shri P.P. Malhotra, ASG)
Section 50’s rigours do not apply to officers acting on a s.41(1) Magistrate’s warrant or to Gazetted Officers acting/authorising under s.41(2) — Parliament reposes higher trust in gazetted rank (reliance on M. Prabhulal (2003) 8 SCC 449; UOI v. Satrohan (2008) 8 SCC 313; State of Rajasthan v. Ram Chandra (2005) 5 SCC 151 — the right is to be taken before the GO/Magistrate, who then decides discharge/direction to search).
Balbir Singh, Saiyad Umar Saiyad, Ali Mustaffa cases wrongly “read the phrase ‘for making the search’ into s.50(1)”, making it over-rigorous; Baldev Singh needed reconsideration; the Court should accept substantial compliance.

The Court confined the reference to the scope of the words “if the person to be searched so requires” and did not adjudicate the s.41 applicability arguments (noting Baldev Singh had itself considered s.41).

Statutory framework quoted

Section 50 (with sub-ss.(5),(6) inserted by Act 9 of 2001) was set out in full: (1) production before nearest GO/Magistrate if the person so requires; (2) detention till production; (3) GO/Magistrate to discharge if no reasonable ground, else direct search; (4) females searched only by females; (5)/(6) emergency search under s.100 CrPC where production risks disposal, with recorded reasons sent within 72 hours.

Reasoning and the restatement of Baldev Singh

  1. The Court reproduced conclusions (1), (2), (3), (5), (6), (7) of Baldev Singh: it is imperative to inform the person of the s.50(1) right (information need not be in writing); failure causes prejudice; a search on prior information without informing, and (if opted) without conducting the search before the GO/Magistrate, may not vitiate the trial but renders recovery suspect and vitiates conviction where it rests solely on the personal recovery; compliance is a trial issue; the illicit article so recovered cannot be used as evidence of unlawful possession though other materials may.
  2. Though Baldev Singh did not label s.50 mandatory/directory, it made the duty to make the suspect aware of the existence of the right mandatory; no prescribed form or writing is required but awareness is. “Any other interpretation would make the valuable right conferred on the suspect illusory and a farce.”
  3. Sub-sections (5)/(6) (2001) do not obliterate sub-s.(1): the emergency route is available only in emergent and urgent situations, never as a matter of course, and the 72-hour reasoned report remains open to judicial scrutiny. The Bench (para 19) cited its own observations in Karnail Singh that the Baldev mandate was “diluted” but safeguards not removed.
  4. Joseph Fernandez and Prabha Shankar Dubey did not notice the Baldev Singh ratio; the phrase “substantial compliance” in them appeared against the unamended understanding and cannot be read in isolation. “Substantial compliance” is neither borne out by the language of s.50(1) nor consonant with Baldev Singh.
  5. The object of the safeguard: check misuse of power, avoid harm to innocents, minimise planting/foisting of false cases. The key is the animus imponentis (In Re Presidential Poll, (1974) 2 SCC 33) — the reason and spirit of the law.
  6. Where possible the suspect should in the first instance be produced before the nearest Magistrate rather than a departmental Gazetted Officer — greater public confidence, legitimacy and strengthened prosecution; but this is an endeavour, not a straitjacket.
DecisionThe reference answered: the obligation under s.50(1) to apprise/inform the person to be searched of his right to be searched before a Gazetted Officer or Magistrate is mandatory and requires strict compliance; a bare enquiry is insufficient. Whether the procedure was followed remains a question of trial; no absolute formula is possible. The appeals were remitted for disposal accordingly.
Principle / ratio“Inform, don’t merely ask.” The suspect must be made to understand that the choice to be searched before a GO/Magistrate is his right, not the officer’s concession. Non-information does not per se void the trial, but personal recovery in such a search becomes suspect evidence and cannot sustain a conviction based solely on it; emergency s.50(5) searches need recorded reasons + 72-hour report and are exceptional. Applied later in Arif Khan @ Agha Khan v. State of Uttarakhand (2018) to acquit where mere written consent was obtained without full compliance.

Union of India v. Mohanlal & Another

(2016) 3 SCC 379 · Division Bench, Supreme Court · Author: T.S. Thakur, J. · Judgment 28 January 2016 (interlocutory directions from 2012, (2012) SCC orders)
TriggerMP HC acquittal for failure to prove destruction/disposal of 3.36 kg seized opium
IssuesSeizure & sampling; storage; disposal/destruction; judicial supervision
Key provisionss.52A (as amended 2014); s.53, s.55; Standing Order 1/89; Notifications of 2007 & 16-1-2015
ReliefNationwide mandamus + monitoring by High Court Judges’ Committees

Facts and genesis

  • The Union appealed against an MP High Court acquittal of the respondents under ss.8/18(b) r/w s.29: the HC found the prosecution had produced no evidence that the 3.36 kg opium seized had been destroyed/disposed of under the prescribed procedure; failure to produce the contraband before the trial court implied failure to prove seizure/possession.
  • The Centre relied on Standing Order No. 1/89 (13-6-1989) governing seizure, sampling, safe-keeping and disposal, said to be followed nationwide, and a Department of Revenue Circular of 23-2-2011 exhorting Chief Secretaries/police chiefs to enforce it. Doubts persisted because pilferage and re-circulation of seized drugs is a major hazard.
  • The Court appointed Shri Ajit Kumar Sinha, Senior Advocate, as Amicus Curiae to review the working of the procedure and identify loopholes.
  • A Times of India report (“Bathinda’s police stores bursting at the seams…”) documented, in Bathinda alone, 39 lakh sedative/narcotic tablets, 1.10 lakh capsules, 21,000 drug syrups, 1,828 injections, 8 kg smack, 84 kg ganja awaiting disposal — indicative of nationwide accumulation, worst in border States.

The nation-wide fact-finding exercise (3 & 4 July 2012 orders)

Reports were called from every State/UT through Chief Secretaries → DGPs, routed via Registrars General of High Courts (appointed Nodal Officers), who also independently gathered District & Sessions Judges’ answers; central agencies (NCB, CBN, DRI, Customs & Central Excise, Coast Guard) reported similarly. Four query-heads were framed:

HeadIllustrative questions
12.1 SeizureYear-wise/district-wise seizures over 10 years (natural & synthetic); steps to prevent damage/loss/pilferage during seizure/transit; circulars/notifications/guidelines.
12.2 StorageNotified stores per district/taluka; withdrawal conditions/court orders; nature/quantity checks; anti-substitution/anti-pilferage measures; stock registers, periodic inspections, infrastructure deficiencies.
12.3 Disposal / destruction10-year destruction data; who applies for court permission and defaults; technical personnel at destruction; transit safeguards; designated destruction facilities; records and supervising ranks.
12.4 Judicial supervisionSessions Judges’ inspections of stores and destruction initiatives; reports to Administrative Judges; pending destruction applications and delays; judicial officers associated at each stage; rules and average trial duration.

Issue I — Seizure and sampling (paras 11–14)

  • Section 52A(1) empowers the Centre to notify the disposal procedure; Standing Order 1/89 para 2.2 required on-the-spot duplicate samples drawn before panchas and the person, noted in the panchnama. The survey showed no uniform practice — only the DRI claimed spot sampling; the NCB said it did not; most States did not.
  • The Court set out s.52A (as substituted in 2014): on seizure and forwarding to the SHO/s.53 officer, an inventory must be prepared and an application made to a Magistrate for (a) certification of inventory, (b) photographs certified true, (c) representative samples drawn in the Magistrate’s presence with certified sample lists; the Magistrate must allow “as soon as may be”; certified documents/samples are primary evidence under s.52A(4) notwithstanding the Evidence Act/CrPC.
  • Held: the statutory scheme does not contemplate sampling at seizure (which usually occurs without a Magistrate); sampling is to be under Magistrate supervision and certification. Where Standing Order 1/89 conflicts with the statute, the statute prevails; the Centre was asked to re-examine/recalibrate the Standing Order. Seizure must be followed by the s.52A(2) application without undue delay; Magistrates must act expeditiously (s.52A(3)); High Courts must supervise both Magistrates and agencies.

Issue II — Storage (paras 15–16)

  • Beyond s.55 (SHO to keep articles in safe custody pending Magistrate’s orders) the Act has no storage rules; Standing Order 1/89 Section III (paras 3.2–3.9) fills the gap: storage invariably in safes and vaults with double-locking in designated godowns chosen for security and proximity to courts, under a Gazetted Officer’s overall charge; deposit within 48 hours with a forwarding memo (crime number, accused, test memo, description, package count); acknowledgement in Annexure-I to the IO; godown-in-charge to verify seals/packing and maintain the Annexure-II register; frequent inspections recorded at column 15; periodic returns; pre-trial disposal via s.52A(2).
  • Ground reality: after 26 years no notified godowns existed; NCB used court malkhanas; Customs/DRI had no designated facilities; States dumped drugs in the common police maalkhana alongside weapons and IPC case property; no specific registers, no periodic inspections — “complete failure bordering on criminal negligence”.
  • Only 16% of contrabands seized between 2002–2012 had been disposed of; the fate of 84% was unknown. The Court lamented the neglect, noting the deep-rooted menace is sustained by money power, transnational links and, at times, collusion of enforcement agencies/politicians; it expressed hope (rather than a finding) that the failure was apathy, not an “unholy connect” with traffickers.

Issue III — Disposal/destruction (paras 17–19)

  • The Notification of 16 January 2015 (superseding the 10-5-2007 Notification): para 4 requires the SHO/specified officer to apply to the Magistrate under s.52A(2) within 30 days of the chemical analysis report (Annexure 2); after certification, preserve inventory/photos/samples as primary evidence and send details to the Drugs Disposal Committee (DDC) Chairman and godown in-charge; paras 5–6 constitute the DDC and define functions; para 7 procedure; para 8 quantity/value limits with reference to a high-level DDC by the departmental head for excess consignments; para 9 mode of destruction; para 10 intimation/surprise checks by the head of department; para 11 destruction certificate; paras 12–13 godown-register entries and NCB communication.
  • Differences from Standing Order 1/89 (enumerated substances vs all substances/conveyances; court approval required under 1/89 but not expressly under 2015; head-of-department presence vs high-level DDC). To the extent of inconsistency, the 2015 Notification was treated as superseding Standing Order 1/89.
Three categories of pending stock and their treatment (para 19) (1) Cases finally concluded before 29 May 1989: DDCs to take stock and destroy without further verification, testing or sampling — responsibility for ancient pilferage cannot now be fixed; head of department personally supervises.
(2) Seizures after May 1989 where trial/appeal/revision have attained finality: testing under para 5.5 of Standing Order would be a futile exercise after such lapse; DDCs stock and destroy under the direct supervision of the head of department.
(3) Pending cases (trial/appellate/Supreme Court): heads of department ensure competent officers move applications before the DDCs under the 2015 Notification without further loss of time.

Final mandamus (paras 20–21)

  1. Immediate forwarding of every seizure to the SHO/s.53 officer, followed by a s.52A(2) application; the Magistrate allows it under s.52A(3); sampling under Magistrate supervision (paras 13–14).
  2. Centre, its agencies and States to set up exclusive storage facilities with vaults and double-locking within six months; designate officers and adopt Standing Order safeguards; one facility per district or shared by several districts as warranted.
  3. Existing malkhana stocks to be disposed of by the DDCs under the three-category plan.
  4. Chief Justices of High Courts requested to appoint administrative-side Committees of Judges to supervise/monitor compliance and issue directions, judicial or administrative, in public interest.
Principle / ratio① The NDPS sampling event is Magistrate-centric: s.52A(2)–(4) makes his supervised, certified inventory/photographs/representative samples the primary evidence; spot sampling is not statutorily mandated and a subordinate Standing Order cannot override the statute. ② Seized narcotics are hazardous, theft/ substitution-vulnerable evidence: the State owes a chain-of-custody duty — timely application, secure double-lock vaults, registers, inspections, DDC destruction under s.52A/2015 Notification. ③ Courts have constitutional supervisory jurisdiction (Arts. 226/227/141–142-style continuing mandamus) to compel systemic reform in NDPS administration, monitored by High Court Judges’ Committees.

Indian Harm Reduction Network & Another v. Union of India & Others (with Gulam Mohammed Malik matters)

Bombay High Court, Writ/PIL jurisdiction (Art. 226) · Division Bench: A.M. Khanwilkar & A.P. Bhangale, JJ. · Decided 16 June 2011
ChallengeConstitutionality of mandatory death under s.31A (Arts. 14 & 21)
PetitionersIHRN (NGO) + Gulam Mohammed Malik (twice convicted, sentenced to death: Ahmedabad 2004, Mumbai 2008)
AuthoritiesManeka Gandhi; Bachan Singh; Mithu Singh (s.303 IPC); s.235(2), s.354(3) CrPC; Art. 6 ICCPR
Effect“Shall be punished with death” read down to “may”; 2014 amendment codified it

Issues and answers recorded by the High Court

  1. Does s.31A violate Article 14? — No. The classification (first-time offender vs repeat offender dealing in specified huge quantities; or offences under ss.19/24/27A) is founded on intelligible differentia with rational nexus to deterrence; the classification between s.31 and s.31A cases is also rational.
  2. Does it violate Article 21? — Yes, as a mandatory sentence. Maneka Gandhi requires procedure that is just, fair and reasonable. Standardised mandatory death: (a) eliminates the pre-sentence hearing under s.235(2) CrPC; (b) does away with special reasons under s.354(3) CrPC and the Bachan Singh “rarest of rare” norm; (c) destroys individualised sentencing (circumstances of offence and offender); (d) impinges on separation of powers and the judicial sentencing function; (e) is disproportionate — drug activities are licensed torts absent licence, not inherently heinous, and drugs serve medical/scientific needs; (f) offends Article 6 ICCPR (no arbitrary deprivation of life).
  3. Is s.31A void ab initio? — No. The provision was saved by reading it down prospectively.
  4. Can “shall” be read as “may”? — Yes. “Shall be punishable with death” is to be read as “may be punishable with death”: the court retains discretion to impose the s.31 enhanced term or, on special reasons, death.

Respondents’ contentions rejected/accepted

The Union argued death is not cruel/unusual; narcotic crimes are more heinous than murder (society/economy as victim); procedural safeguards exist; classification is valid; sentencing minimums are legislative policy and prerogative. The Court accepted the Art. 14 defences but held mandatory capital punishment unconstitutional under Art. 21.

Principle / ratio and aftermathNo mandatory death penalty for drug offences: sentencing discretion and the rarest-of-rare discipline are non-negotiable under Article 21. Parliament gave effect to the ruling via Act 16 of 2014 (w.e.f. 1-5-2014): s.31A now prescribes “punishment which shall not be less than the punishment specified in s.31 or with death” — death optional. Pre-2014, the same result followed by judicial reading-down. Parallels: Mithu Singh v. State of Punjab struck down s.303 IPC mandatory death (s.303 IPC has no counterpart in the BNS, 2023).

Paper 9121 · Q. 1(b) Constitutionality of s.31A death penalty (Problem)
Question: P has been convicted by the trial Court under Section 19 of the NDPS Act, 1985. As it was his second conviction, the Trial Court awarded death penalty. P challenges it as unconstitutional, being violative of Articles 14 and 21. Decide.
(Faculty answer key: Indian Harm Reduction Network v. Union of India.)
Answer —

I. Statutory framework. Section 31 (enhanced punishment after previous conviction) raises the sentence to 1½ times the maximum. Section 31A, inserted in 1989 and narrowed in 2001, prescribed death on a second/subsequent conviction where the first was under s.19 (embezzlement of opium by cultivator), s.24 (external dealings) or s.27A (financing/harbouring) or for commercial quantity, and the second involves the table-listed quantities (opium 10 kg; heroin/morphine/codeine/thebaine 1 kg; cocaine 500 g; hashish 20 kg, etc.) or financing thereof. As it stood at P’s trial the word used was “shall be punishable with death”.

II. Article 14 challenge fails. In Indian Harm Reduction Network v. UOI (Bombay High Court, 16-6-2011) the Court held the classification is reasonable — first-time vs repeat offenders dealing in massive quantities is an intelligible differentia with rational nexus to the object of deterrence; narcotic crimes are treated as more heinous than murder (they destroy society and the economy). Hence no Art. 14 violation.

III. Article 21 challenge succeeds against the mandatory element. (i) Article 21 (post-Maneka Gandhi) demands procedure that is just, fair and reasonable; standardised mandatory death eliminates the pre-sentence hearing under s.235(2) CrPC and the requirement of special reasons under s.354(3) CrPC; (ii) it violates Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (death only in the rarest of rare cases after individualised sentencing on circumstances of the crime and the criminal); (iii) it repeats the vice of s.303 IPC struck down in Mithu Singh v. State of Punjab (mandatory death unconstitutional); (iv) it removes judicial discretion, trenching upon separation of powers and the rule of law; and (v) it is disproportionate and offends Art. 6 ICCPR (no arbitrary deprivation of life; drugs also serve medical/scientific needs).

IV. Remedy — reading down, not striking out. The section was not declared void ab initio; “shall be punishable with death” was read as “may be punishable with death”. Parliament codified this by the 2014 amendment (w.e.f. 1-5-2014): s.31A now provides punishment “not less than the punishment specified in s.31 or with death”.

V. Decision in P’s case: The blanket mandatory death sentence cannot stand. P’s matter must be remitted for sentencing in discretion: the court must hear him on sentence (s.235(2) CrPC), apply the rarest-of-rare test and record special reasons; ordinarily it would impose the s.31 enhanced term, reserving death for the gravest repeat commercial trafficking. The challenge is thus partly allowed — Art. 14 plea rejected, Art. 21 plea accepted; death sentence set aside and sentencing discretion restored.

Paper 9121 · Q. 2(b) Non-compliance with s.42 (Problem — Ganja in a car)
Question: An Inspector of Police got information that accused M was trying to transport ganja in a car to a neighbouring city. Immediately thereafter the Inspector left the police station and reached the spot; after some time M came in the car. The Inspector stopped M, searched the car, which had many bundles of ganja. M was arrested and tried. M defends on the ground of non-compliance with s.42. Decide.
(Faculty answer key: Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana, (2009) 8 SCC 539 — s.42.)
Answer —

1. Section 42 requirements. Where an empowered officer has reason to believe from personal knowledge or information given by any person that contraband is kept/concealed in any building, conveyance or enclosed place, the information must, under s.42(1), be taken down in writing, and under s.42(2) a copy sent to the immediate official superior — pre-2001 “forthwith”, post-2001 “within 72 hours”. Night searches additionally require recorded grounds.

2. Law — Karnail Singh Constitution Bench. (a) Where information is received in the police station with sufficient time, the officer must record it in the register and send a copy to the superior before acting; (b) only where information arrives on the move/on patrol and immediate action is necessary to prevent escape/destruction may he act first and record/report as soon as practicable thereafter; (c) total non-compliance is impermissible; delayed compliance with satisfactory explanation is acceptable; whether compliance is adequate is a question of fact. The ruling drew on Abdul Rashid (total omission — benefit to accused; acquittal restored) and Sajan Abraham (jeep-patrol emergency — omission excused).

3. Application. The Inspector received the information at the police station, had time before leaving, and the target was a conveyance (car) — squarely s.42(1). The facts show he “immediately left” and neither reduced the information to writing nor sent the 72-hour report. There is no emergent on-move situation (contrast Sajan Abraham). This is the clear-violation/“suspicious circumstance” category in Karnail Singh para 17(d). Under Abdul Rashid, non-recording deprives the accused and the court of the earliest material (the precise tip) and counts as a circumstance in the accused’s favour; the recovery becomes suspect.

4. Possible counter-characterisation. If the evidence established the stop as a s.43 public-place/in-transit seizure, s.43 contains no writing precondition. But where the officer acted on prior, specific information about a conveyance and lay in wait, the Court treats s.42 as the source of power (as in Abdul Rashid’s autorickshaw), not s.43.

5. Consequence. Non-compliance does not per se automatically vitiate the trial (unlike s.50 personal-search jurisprudence, it makes the action suspect and causes prejudice); however, where as here the conviction rests essentially on the car recovery with the statutory safeguards wholly ignored and no satisfactory explanation, the Court must render the benefit of doubt. Decision: M is entitled to acquittal (or, at minimum, the conviction cannot be sustained on such recovery), following Karnail Singh and Abdul Rashid.

Paper 9121 · Q. 3(b) · (Repeated 2019 Q.5(b)) Measures of the Central Government (Essay)
Question: What measures does the Central Government have to take for preventing and combating abuse of, and illicit traffic in, narcotic drugs, etc.?
Answer —

Constitutional and treaty basis. Article 47 obliges the State to endeavour prohibition of drugs injurious to health except for medicinal purposes. India is party to the Single Convention 1961, the Psychotropic Substances Convention 1971 and the 1988 Illicit Traffic Convention; the Preamble and Statement of Objects and Reasons of the NDPS Act (see Karnail Singh) require deterrent, comprehensive controls and implementation of those conventions.

Section 4(1) — the general duty: the Central Government shall take all such measures as necessary or expedient to prevent and combat drug abuse and illicit traffic and to ensure medical/scientific availability. Section 4(2) specifies the measure areas:

  • (a) Co-ordination of actions of officers, State Governments and authorities under the Act and other enforcement laws (operationalised through the NCB and inter-agency coordination, mutual assistance duty s.56).
  • (b) International obligations under the Conventions — notifications, controlled deliveries (s.50A), reciprocal arrangements.
  • (c) International assistance/co-operation to foreign authorities and organisations for universal action against illicit traffic.
  • (d) Demand reduction: identification, treatment, education, after-care, rehabilitation and social re-integration of addicts (backed by de-addiction centres under s.71, court-supervised release under s.39, addict immunity under s.64A).
  • (da) ensuring availability of drugs for medical and scientific use (essential narcotic drugs regime added in 2014).
  • (e) all other matters for effective implementation and combating abuse/traffic.

Section 4(3) — institutional power: by Gazette order the Centre may constitute an authority or hierarchy of authorities with statutory powers. Exercising it, the Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB) was set up on 17-3-1986; the DG-NCB undertakes controlled delivery under s.50A.

Supporting machinery: appointment of the Narcotics Commissioner and officers (s.5) to superintend opium-poppy cultivation/opium production; the NDPS Consultative Committee (s.6, advisory, ≤20 members, sub-committees); licensing/regulatory rules under s.9 (poppy/opium, manufactured drugs, psychotropics, imports/exports) and precursor control under s.9A; the National Fund for Control of Drug Abuse (s.7A) financing enforcement, treatment, education, rehabilitation and medical supply to addicts, fed by forfeited-property sale proceeds; the forfeiture regime (Chapter VA); notifications of small/commercial quantities under ss.2(vii-a)/(xxiii-a); and oversight of the disposal chain (s.52A and the Mohanlal directions).

Conclusion: s.4 is a mandatory, plenipotentiary provision fusing supply-side enforcement (coordination, NCB, conventions, licensing, precursors, forfeiture), demand reduction (treatment, education, rehabilitation, s.7A Fund) and regulated medical access, making the Centre the pivot of India’s anti-drug architecture.

Paper 9121 · Q. 5(b) Non-compliance with s.50 (Problem — T on foot with plastic bag)
Question: A police party proceeding from Gokulpuri to Bhajanpura on patrol in a jeep spotted T coming on foot with a plastic bag. Apprehending him on suspicion, the Police Inspector immediately searched T without saying anything and recovered a large quantity of opium. At trial T pleads non-compliance with s.50. Decide.
(Faculty answer key: Vijaysinh Chandubha Jadeja v. State of Gujarat, with Baldev Singh and Arif Khan.)
Answer —

1. Section 50 is a “due process” safeguard in personal searches. Before searching any person under ss.41/42/43, the officer must, when the person so requires, take him without unnecessary delay to the nearest Gazetted Officer or Magistrate (sub-ss.(1)–(3)); sub-ss.(5)–(6) (2001) allow an immediate s.100 CrPC search only on recorded belief that production would risk disposal, with reasons sent within 72 hours; a female is searched only by a female (sub-s.(4)).

2. The Constitution Bench standards. In State of Punjab v. Baldev Singh (1999) and authoritatively restated in Vijaysinh Chandubha Jadeja (2011): (i) it is imperative to “inform” the suspect of the existence of his right (no writing/form prescribed, but awareness is essential); a mere enquiry (“do you wish…?”) that does not communicate that the choice is the suspect’s right is insufficient; the “substantial compliance” view in Joseph Fernandez and Prabha Shankar Dubey was overruled; (ii) failure to inform causes prejudice and renders the recovery suspect; (iii) where the conviction is based solely on the illicit article recovered from the person in such a search, the conviction is vitiated; (iv) the article cannot be used to prove unlawful possession, though other independent evidence may still be used; (v) emergency sub-ss.(5)/(6) cannot be invoked as a matter of course.

3. Application. The Inspector searched T “without saying anything” — there was no communication of the right, no option, no refusal recorded, and no sub-s.(5) emergency belief/reasons. The facts mirror the rejected conduct in Jadeja and are stronger than Arif Khan (2018), where even a written consent did not cure non-compliance and acquittal followed. The opium was recovered from T’s person on a public road — the case falls under s.43, but s.50 applies to searches under ss.41, 42 and 43, so the safeguard is attracted regardless of the place.

4. Decision. Recovery alone is the foundation of the charge; there is no independent evidence. The mandatory duty to inform having been breached, the recovery is suspect and cannot sustain conviction. T is entitled to acquittal following Baldev Singh–Jadeja–Arif Khan.

Paper 2071 · Q. 1(b) Procedure for disposal of seized drugs; Mohanlal (Essay)
Question: Discuss the provisions relating to the procedure to be followed by the Central and State Governments for disposal of seized drugs and psychotropic substances under the NDPS Act, referring to the law laid down in Union of India v. Mohanlal & Another, (2016) 3 SCC 379.
Answer —

1. Statutory base — s.52A (inserted 1989, substituted 2014): (1) having regard to hazardous nature, vulnerability to theft/substitution and storage constraints, the Centre may notify drugs/psychotropic/controlled substances or conveyances to be disposed of promptly after seizure by notified officers in the prescribed manner; (2) on receipt of the seizure, the SHO/s.53 officer prepares a detailed inventory and applies to a Magistrate for (a) certifying the inventory, (b) photographing in his presence and certifying photographs, (c) drawing representative samples in his presence and certifying the sample list; (3) the Magistrate must allow the application “as soon as may be”; (4) the certified inventory/photographs/sample list are primary evidence, overriding the Evidence Act and CrPC. Allied provisions: s.52 (forwarding), s.55 (safe custody), s.57 (48-hour report), ss.60–63 (confiscation).

2. Subordinate legislation: Standing Order No. 1/89 (seizure, duplicate spot sampling under para 2.2; godowns with safes/vaults, double-lock, Gazetted-Officer charge, 48-hour deposit, Annexures I–II registers); Notifications of 10-5-2007 (forms for inventory/application/destruction certificate) and 16-1-2015 (all substances/conveyances; application within 30 days of the FSL report; Drugs Disposal Committees (DDCs), quantity/value limits, high-level DDC for excess consignments, destruction certificates, godown and NCB communications).

3. Mohanlal findings. Originating from an MP acquittal for failure to prove disposal of 3.36 kg opium, and aided by Amicus Shri Ajit Kumar Sinha, the Court found: no uniform sampling practice (only DRI sampled at spot); no notified godowns even after 26 years; drugs dumped in common police malkhanas; only 16% of 2002–2012 seizures disposed of, 84% untraced; no registers/inspections — “criminal negligence”. It held:

  • Sampling is Magistrate-centric: there is no statutory duty to sample at the seizure spot; representative samples must be drawn in the Magistrate’s presence under s.52A(2)(c)–(4) and constitute primary evidence; the statute prevails over inconsistent Standing Order para 2.2; the application must be made without undue delay and the Magistrate must act promptly.
  • Storage: exclusive facilities with vaults/double-locking within a fixed timeline, designated officers, registers, inspection and 48-hour deposit per Standing Order Section III.
  • Disposal in three categories: (i) cases finally concluded before 29-5-1989 — destroy without verification/testing/sampling under personal supervision of the departmental head; (ii) seizures after May 1989 in finally concluded cases — stock and destroy under the head of department, dispensing with futile re-testing; (iii) pending cases — move applications before the DDCs immediately under the 2015 Notification.
  • Directions: immediate forwarding and s.52A applications; set up exclusive storage facilities with vaults and double-lock within six months (one per district or shared); DDCs to clear existing stocks; Chief Justices to constitute Committees of Judges to monitor compliance on the administrative and judicial sides.

Conclusion: Mohanlal converted s.52A into a judicially supervised, time-bound chain: seizure → immediate forward → inventory → Magistrate-certified samples/photos → FSL → secure vault → DDC destruction → primary evidence at trial, with High Courts as continuing monitors.

Paper 2071 · Q. 3(b) Search, seizure and arrest — patrol problem (Discussion)
Question: X, a constable, got information about Y selling narcotic drugs. On his way to the police station to furnish the information to the Sub-Inspector, he found the SI on patrol duty. On communication of the information, the SI with the party immediately proceeded and apprehended the accused. Discuss the procedure for search, seizure and arrest under the NDPS Act with decided cases.
Answer —

1. Threshold legality. Officers empowered are those of central excise/narcotics/customs/revenue intelligence or State revenue/drugs control/excise/police departments, of rank above peon/sepoy/constable, empowered by general/special order (ss.41–42). A constable cannot himself authorise a s.42 search/seizure, but he can communicate information; the Sub-Inspector is the competent empowered officer (and for licensed-manufacturer premises must be at least SI rank).

2. Section 42 and the on-move exception — Karnail Singh. Normally, information received must be taken down in writing and a copy sent to the superior within 72 hours before entry/search of a building/conveyance/enclosed place. But on facts materially identical to Sajan Abraham v. State of Kerala (Head Constable met the SI in a moving jeep on patrol; immediate action necessary to prevent the seller escaping), the recording may follow the action: the SI may proceed at once and, as soon as practicable, record the information and inform the superior; the FIR/arrest papers sent immediately constitute substantial compliance. Delayed but explained compliance is valid; total or in-station non-compliance is not (Abdul Rashid; Koluttumottil Razak).

3. Choice of power. If Y is dealt with at a public place/in transit (selling on the road), s.43 empowers seizure and arrest on reason-to-believe without a pre-search writing; any person in his company may also be arrested; hotels, shops, public conveyances are “public places”. A moving conveyance may be stopped/searched under s.49 (rummage; force including firing as last resort). For a building/conveyance/enclosed place, s.42 applies; a Magistrate’s/Gazetted officer’s warrant route is s.41.

4. Personal search — s.50 (Jadeja). Before searching Y’s person the SI must inform him of his right to be taken to the nearest Gazetted Officer/Magistrate; if Y opts, the SI must detain and produce him, and cannot search himself; the GO/Magistrate discharges or directs search. Only on a genuine belief that Y would part with contraband may the SI search per s.100 CrPC, recording reasons and reporting within 72 hours; a female is searched only by a female.

5. Post-arrest procedure. Inform grounds of arrest (s.52(1)); prepare seizure memo/panchnama with sealing and samples; produce the accused before a Magistrate within 24 hours (Art. 22(2); s.52); forward articles to the SHO/s.53 officer; full arrest/seizure report to the superior within 48 hours (s.57) and s.42(2)/50(6) copies within 72 hours; prepare inventory and move the Magistrate under s.52A for certification, photographs and representative samples (primary evidence); FSL examination; safe vault custody; DDC disposal (Mohanlal).

6. Conclusion. The apprehension is lawful if the SI was an empowered officer and the situation fits the Sajan Abraham limb of Karnail Singh (on-move, urgent → post-action recording promptly); s.50 must still be strictly observed at personal search; all reporting/certification timelines follow. Any total failure to record/report (Abdul Rashid) or failure to inform under s.50 (Jadeja; Arif Khan) makes recovery suspect and, where it is the sole basis, vitiates conviction.

Paper 2071 · Q. 6(b) s.31A — mandatory standardised death penalty (Essay)
Question: Section 31A makes the sentence provision mandatory and provides for standardised death penalty. Discuss with relevant case law whether s.31A is violative of Articles 14 and 21.
Answer —

Scope. Section 31A (1989; narrowed 2001) ordained death for a second conviction involving ss.19/24/27A or commercial-quantity predicates and table-quantity recidivism/financing. The answer tracks Indian Harm Reduction Network (Bom. HC, 2011).

Not violative of Article 14: the law classifies intelligibly (first-time vs repeat offenders; ordinary vs table-quantity commercial trafficking), with nexus to deterrence; classification between s.31 and s.31A categories is rational; sentencing minimums are legislative policy.

Violative of Article 21 as a mandatory provision, because it: denies a just-fair-reasonable procedure (Maneka Gandhi); displaces the s.235(2) CrPC pre-sentence hearing and s.354(3) “special reasons”; negates Bachan Singh’s rarest-of-rare and individualised-sentencing jurisprudence; repeats the vice of s.303 IPC struck down in Mithu Singh; eliminates judicial discretion (separation of powers/rule of law); is disproportionate where activities are licensed torts and drugs also have medical-scientific uses; and ignores Art. 6 ICCPR.

Remedy and legislative response: the Court read “shall” as “may” without voiding the section; Parliament’s 2014 amendment recast s.31A as “punishment not less than s.31 or with death”, restoring discretion. Death remains constitutionally available for the gravest repeat commercial trafficking with special reasons, but is never automatic.

Conclusion: s.31A as a mandatory capital statute violated Article 21 but not Article 14; as read down (and as amended 2014) it survives — the present position is “minimum enhanced sentence under s.31, with death only in the rarest of rare cases on special reasons”.

DU LL.B. 2019 · Q. 2(b) “Discuss the law laid down in Karnail Singh” (Essay)
Question: Discuss the law laid down in Karnail Singh v. State of Haryana (2009) 8 SCC 539. (Citation in the paper appears as (2009) 7 SCC 539.)
Answer —

Reference: Constitution Bench resolved the conflict between Abdul Rashid Ibrahim Mansuri (s.42 mandatory; unrecorded tip and absent report = prejudice; acquittal restored in a charas-autorickshaw case) and Sajan Abraham (pragmatic/substantial compliance where the SI received the tip in a moving jeep and had to act instantly in a Tidigesic case).

Statutory position: s.42(1) requires information for building/conveyance/enclosed-place searches to be taken down in writing and action between sunrise–sunset (night searches on recorded grounds); s.42(2) originally required the copy “forthwith”, amended in 2001 to “within 72 hours”. Section 43 (public places) has no recording requirement; s.50 separately protects personal searches (Baldev Singh).

Law laid down — four propositions: (a) information received in the station must be recorded and reported before action; (b) information received on the move/on patrol demanding immediate action to prevent escape/destruction may be acted upon first and recorded/reported as soon as practicable; (c) recording/reporting should normally precede entry/search, but may be postponed for a reasonable period on urgency/expediency; (d) total non-compliance is impermissible; delayed compliance with satisfactory explanation is acceptable — in-station inaction or no recording/communication at all is a clear, suspicious violation; adequacy is a question of fact. The Court also noted that sub-ss.(5)/(6) inserted in s.50 in 2001 diluted strictness for genuine emergencies without abolishing safeguards, and invoked the reality of wireless/mobile communication.

Significance: the decision balances the stringent anti-trafficking object (no escape route for hardened peddlers) against protection from arbitrary action (paper-trail enabling judicial scrutiny of the genesis of the search).

DU LL.B. 2019 · Q. 8(d) Short note: Offences triable by Special Courts
Question: Write a short note on the provisions related to offences triable by Special Courts under the NDPS Act, 1985.
Answer —
  • Constitution (s.36): the Government notifies Special Courts for specified areas for speedy trial; a single Judge appointed with the Chief Justice of the High Court’s concurrence; qualification — sitting Sessions/Additional Sessions Judge.
  • Jurisdiction (s.36A(1)(a)): all offences punishable with imprisonment more than 3 years triable exclusively by the area Special Court; offences punishable with up to 3 years may be tried summarily (s.36A(5)).
  • Remand (s.36A(1)(b)–(c)): Judicial Magistrate may remand up to 15 days in whole, Executive Magistrate up to 7 days, forwarding to the Special Court once detention unnecessary; the Special Court exercises s.167 CrPC powers.
  • Cognizance (s.36A(1)(d)): directly on police report or authorised officer’s complaint without committal; associated non-NDPS offences may be tried together (sub-s.(2)).
  • Investigation time (s.36A(4)): for ss.19, 24, 27A and commercial-quantity offences, the 90-day default period is 180 days, extendable to one year by the Special Court on the PP’s progress report with specific reasons.
  • Appeals/revision (s.36B): lie to the High Court as if the Special Court were a Court of Session; s.36C deems it a Court of Session and the prosecutor a Public Prosecutor (CrPC, including bail, applies); High Court retains s.439 CrPC bail powers treating the Special Court as a Magistrate (s.36A(3)); s.36D provides transitional trial by Sessions Courts.
  • Bail linkage (s.37): all offences cognizable; ss.19/24/27A and commercial-quantity cases are non-bailable on the twin conditions (PP heard; reasonable belief of non-guilt and no likelihood of offending on bail).

Examination writing tips

  1. For problem questions: write the issue → provision text (sub-section precision) → prescribed-case ratio → apply to facts → conclude (decide in favour of/against the accused). Faculty answer keys always name the anchor case — lead with it.
  2. Quote section numbers accurately and distinguish 42 vs 43 vs 50 vs 52A; examiners reward these distinctions.
  3. Quantity problems: state the threshold, show the arithmetic (e.g. 0.8% of 7.1 kg = 56.96 g), then explain why the law ignores or includes it depending on classification.
  4. Constitutional questions (s.31A): structure as Art. 14 (classification test — fails challenge) → Art. 21 (Maneka/Bachan/Mithu — mandatory death fails) → remedy (read down, 2014 amendment).
  5. Use the words of the statute: “reason to believe”, “taken down in writing”, “forthwith/72 hours”, “if the person so requires”, “as soon as may be”, “primary evidence”, “without undue delay”.
  6. For s.4 essays remember the five measure-heads (coordination, conventions, international assistance, addict treatment/rehabilitation, other matters) + s.4(3) NCB and the s.7A Fund.
Doctrinal cautions for accuracy:
  • E. Micheal Raj is overruled for neutral-substance mixtures — do not apply it today; but Harjit Singh remains good for pure opium classification.
  • Section 42’s 72-hour rule and s.50(6) came in 2001; for pre-2001 searches use “forthwith” (as Abdul Rashid/Balbir Singh did).
  • Section 27 post-2001 covers consumption only; personal possession of small quantity is punished under ss.18–22(a), with s.39/64A reformative routes.
  • Death under s.31A is optional since 2014 and requires special reasons; bail restrictions attach only to ss.19/24/27A and commercial-quantity offences.
  • Standing Order 1/89’s spot-sampling requirement yields to s.52A’s Magistrate-supervised sampling (Mohanlal).
