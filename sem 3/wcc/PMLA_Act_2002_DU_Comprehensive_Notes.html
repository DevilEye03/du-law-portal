The single organising idea for the whole Topic — learn this first The PMLA runs on three parallel tracks that students constantly confuse:
  1. The predicate / scheduled offence track — investigated by the ordinary agency (police/CBI/NIA/CBIC) and tried by the ordinary court under the IPC (now BNS), PC Act, NDPS, etc. The ED does not investigate this offence (Binod Kumar).
  2. The money-laundering offender track — the s.3 offence, investigated by the ED alone (ss.45(1A), 49(1)), cognizable & non-bailable (2019 Explanation to s.45), tried by the Special Court on the Director’s written complaint (s.44(1)(b)), punishable under s.4, with the stringent twin-conditions bail test of s.45(1).
  3. The property track — s.5 provisional attachment (180 days) → s.8 adjudication (show-cause, confirmation for 365 days / pendency) → Special Court confiscation on conviction (s.8(5)) → absolute vesting in the Central Government free of encumbrances (s.9) → management/disposal by Administrator (s.10); appeals to the Appellate Tribunal (s.26) and High Court on a substantial question of law (s.42).
The property track is civil even though it runs alongside a criminal trial; attachment/confiscation do not punish (Ramaraju), and the burden on the possessor is the preponderance of probabilities, while the s.4 conviction must be proved beyond reasonable doubt (Vijay Madanlal).

Part I — The Need for Combating Money-Laundering (Syllabus Head A)

Every profitable crime produces two problems for the criminal: the predicate offence (murder, drug sale, bribe, fraud) and the cash trail. Spending stolen money openly is itself evidence. Money-laundering is the “second crime” that conceals the first: it converts the illegal/tainted “black” money into apparently legal/“white” money. Because the laundering process runs through banks, stock markets, real estate and cross-border payment systems, it threatens not merely individual victims but the financial system, economic policy, national security and sovereignty of States — which is why, after 1988, the entire world community (and India under Article 253) built a treaty-based AML architecture culminating in this Act.

1. What “money-laundering” means in ordinary language

Etymology (faculty notes)

To launder means to wash and iron clothes; figuratively, to conceal the origins of money obtained illegally, typically through transfers involving foreign banks or legitimate businesses. The term gained currency in the United States in the 1980s when organised crime groups channelled illicit cash through coin-operated laundromats (cash-intensive laundries) to disguise its source. The person who manipulates the money is called the “launderer”.

INTERPOL definition

“Money laundering is concealing or disguising the identity of illegally obtained proceeds so that they appear to have originated from legitimate sources. It is frequently a component of other, much more serious crimes such as drug trafficking, robbery or extortion.” INTERPOL notes it is omnipresent — even in environmental crimes — and that cryptocurrencies such as bitcoins have exacerbated the phenomenon (faculty notes, citing interpol.int).

Standing Committee on Finance, 18th Report (2008–09) — quoted in the DU faculty notes

“The process of money laundering involves the cleansing of money earned through illegal activities like extortion, drug trafficking and gun running etc. The tainted money is projected as clean money through intricate processes of placement, layering and laundering.” The Committee recorded that “the serious threat posed by money laundering to the financial systems and sovereignty was being progressively realized by various countries of [the] world.”

Judicial statement of the mischief

The Statement of Objects and Reasons, quoted by the Supreme Court in Nikesh Tarachand Shah v. Union of India, (2018) 11 SCC 1 (para 7), and again in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2022), states: “It is being realised, world over, that money laundering poses a serious threat not only to the financial systems of countries, but also to their integrity and sovereignty.” In B. Ramaraju v. Union of India, (2011) 164 Comp Cas 149 (AP), the Enforcement Directorate’s counter (para 12(A)), adopted by the High Court, described the “scourge of laundering of money which destabilizes national and international economies, the sovereignty of several States and has adverse impact on law and order maintenance”.

2. Why the crime is “laundered”: the two-crime structure

Drug trafficking, gun-running, human trafficking, illegal wildlife trade, food adulteration, bribery, embezzlement, securities fraud, cyber-fraud and extortion all generate large cash surpluses. The holder cannot deposit, invest or spend these surpluses without attracting tax, bank-suspicion or police enquiry. He must therefore break the audit trail between the crime and the money. This produces the characteristic legal architecture the PMLA addresses:

3. The international context: treaty architecture that compelled the Act

The PMLA is a textbook Article 253 statute: Parliament enacted it “for giving effect to international conventions”. The timeline below (drawn from the preamble, the SOR, the 18th Report of the Standing Committee, and the treaty history judicially noticed in Ramaraju and Vijay Madanlal) is the answer to “why was a special Act needed at all?”

1988 — UN Convention against Illicit Traffic in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (the “Vienna Convention”). The first global treaty to criminalise money-laundering. Article 3 obliges parties to make laundering of drug proceeds a criminal offence; Article 5 mandates confiscation; it also provides mutual legal assistance. India signed 1988 and bound itself to legislate. (India already had NDPS Chapter VA, inserted in 1989.)
1988 — Basel Committee Statement of Principles (Basel Convention on Banking Supervision). The first banking-sector response: customer identification, compliance with law, co-operation with law enforcement, staff training and record-keeping — the embryo of today’s KYC rules (now ss.11A–12AA, PMLA).
14–16 July 1989 — G-7 Paris Summit creates the Financial Action Task Force (FATF). An inter-governmental policy-making body set “standards” to combat ML; it has no treaty status but enormous financial leverage.
April 1990 — FATF Forty Recommendations. The global AML rulebook: criminalise ML; enable seizure/confiscation; customer due diligence; record-keeping; STR reporting; international co-operation. Revised in 1996, 2003 and 2012 (the current integrated framework, with the Nine Special Recommendations on Terrorist Financing, 2001, folded in).
23 February 1990 — UNGA 17th Special Session: Political Declaration & Global Programme of Action (Resolution S-17/2). Expressly invoked in the faculty notes as the trigger for the PML Bills of 1998/99. It called on States to adopt national AML legislation by the year 2003.
1990 — Council of Europe Convention on Laundering, Search, Seizure and Confiscation (Strasbourg); 1995 Egmont Group of Financial Intelligence Units (FIUs). India’s FIU-IND (2004) is an Egmont member.
8–10 June 1998 — UNGA 20th Special Session (UNGASS) on Drugs: Political Declaration and Action Plan against Money-Laundering (the first prescribed reading; the uploaded UN document records the later 2009 Vienna review). It set deadlines for national AML regimes, widened predicate offences beyond drugs, and demanded bank-regulation, FIU and MLA machinery. The Indian PML Bill followed within weeks (introduced 4 August 1998).
9 December 1999 — International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism; UNSC Resolution 1373 (28 September 2001). After 9/11, AML machinery was expressly turned to counter-terror financing (CFT); FATF’s mandate expanded accordingly (the Explanation to s.2(1)(sa) and the PML Rules’ “high risk… terrorist financing” language reflect this).
15 November 2000 — UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime (“Palermo Convention”). Obliges States to criminalise laundering of proceeds of all serious crimes (punishable by 4+ years), not merely drugs — leading to the wide Part A Schedule.
31 October 2003 — UN Convention against Corruption (“Merida/UNCAC”). Chapter V creates a binding global asset-recovery regime (laundering of corruption proceeds, MLA, return of assets — directly underpinning Ch. IX, ss.55–61, PMLA).
2010 — India becomes a full FATF member (and member of the Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering, APG); India’s FATF Mutual Evaluation Reports (2010, 2013 follow-up, 2023 MER) have driven every amendment cycle — the amendments of 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2019 (and the 2023 notifications on crypto and professionals) were justified as fulfilling FATF commitments (Vijay Madanlal, paras on international obligations).
Connected law — how FATF compliance is enforced (the “grey list” sanction)

FATF maintains (i) the “grey list” (Jurisdictions under Increased Monitoring) and (ii) the “black list” (“Call for Action”, e.g. North Korea, Iran historically). Listed jurisdictions suffer “de-risking”: correspondent banks refuse wire channels, cross-border payments require enhanced scrutiny, foreign investment and trade finance shrink. Vijay Madanlal judicially noticed that the FATF recommendations and the risk of counter-measures are a legitimate ground for stringent domestic legislation. India’s compliance machinery: FIU-IND (receives STRs/CTRs), Enforcement Directorate (investigation/prosecution/asset recovery), sectoral regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, ICAI/ICSIs as notified in 2023), and the PML (Maintenance of Records) Rules, 2005 which translate FATF Recommendation 10/11/20 (CDD, record-keeping, STR) into Indian law.

4. Why India in particular needed the Act — black money and its “cancerous” effects

India’s domestic driver was black money: income/wealth illegally concealed from tax and regulation, whether generated by evasion, corruption or crime, and held at home or smuggled abroad. The Direct Taxes Enquiry Committee (the Wanchoo Committee, final report 1971, chaired by former Chief Justice K.N. Wanchoo) — reproduced in the uploaded H.P. Ranina articles — called black money “a cancerous growth in the country’s economy which, if not checked in time, will surely lead to its ruination”.

Wanchoo Committee (1971): causes of generation of black money — as extracted from the uploaded material
  1. High rates of direct taxation. The marginal rate on personal income rose as high as 97.75 per cent at one stage — “the maximum marginal rate of income-tax went up to 97.75% and this resulted in large scale tax evasion. More the tax rates, more the tax evasion.”
  2. The regime of controls, licences and permits. “The system of controls, licences and permits… gave rise to a lot of corruption and black money. The system of licence permit raj gave rise to many malpractices.” Rationing, controls on steel, cement, paper, foreign exchange, motor vehicles and the “bonus system” created premiums and illegal rents.
  3. Ban on company donations to political parties (1976 amendment, originally Ordinance 1969). Companies were prohibited from donating to political parties, “which resulted in political corruption and black money… it is the big business houses who give political donations to politicians and the same is responsible for the generation of black money and corruption.”
  4. Disallowance of legitimate business expenditure. Advertisement, entertainment and publicity expenses were disallowed as deductions — “many of these expenses were of such nature that the same had to be incurred for business purposes… the businessman was tempted to show such expenses out of black money.”
  5. High rates of sales tax and other indirect levies driving under-invoicing and parallel markets.
  6. Ineffective enforcement — tax authorities lacked information and manpower; few prosecutions; “hardly any person was prosecuted… People started taking the risk of tax evasion as the same was a paying proposition.”

Cures recommended: lower tax rates and moderation; reduction in controls/permits; legalised, regulated and transparent political donations by companies; allowing legitimate business deductions; stronger enforcement and deterrent penalties; and voluntary disclosure schemes. The long-run legislative harvest included the Direct Tax Laws Amendment 1987–89, reduced rates under the 1991 reforms, the Companies Act donations regime, the Black Money Act 2015, demonetisation (2016), and the PMLA itself.

The petition named Hassan Ali Khan (income-tax demand of ≈₹40,000 crore; ED-disclosed dealings of US$1.6 billion during 2001–2005) and the Tapurias (≈₹20,580 crore demand). No PMLA provision was construed, but the decision is the judicial charter for retrieving outbound black money and the backdrop for s.60 (attachment abroad) and the Black Money Act, 2015.

5. How the Act answers that need — objects and salient features

The Preamble is the short answer to syllabus Head A: the Act was made “to prevent money-laundering and to provide for confiscation of property derived from, or involved in, money-laundering, and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto”. The 2002 design (faculty: “salient features”, updated to 2019) answers each harm:

Part II — Magnitude of Money-Laundering; its Steps and Methods (Syllabus Head B)

The 2015 DU question asks exactly this: “Describe ‘Money Laundering’ with its various steps and methods.” The answer has three layers: (1) scale (magnitude at home and abroad); (2) the three processing steps — placement, layering, integration (adopted judicially in Ramaraju, Mohammad Arif and Vijay Madanlal); and (3) the methods/techniques through which each step is executed (shell companies, hawala, smurfing, round-tripping, trade manipulation, real estate, gold, casinos, crypto…), plus the five directional fund flows identified in Ramaraju.

Global (FATF/IMF estimate)≈ 2–5% of global GDP laundered annually — quoted in Vijay Madanlal (≈ US$ 800 bn–2 tn in the era when the estimate was made).
ED cases (Vijay Madanlal, Union data)≈ 4,700 PMLA cases registered since inception; 2,086 in the last five years against ≈33 lakh FIRs countrywide — showing the predicate-to-laundering filtering.
Annual registrationsRose from 111 (2015–16) to 981 (2020–21) ECIRs as FATF-driven amendments took effect.

Domestic black-money estimates placed before policy makers have ranged from the Wanchoo Committee’s “cancerous growth” (1971) to estimates of hundreds of billions of dollars of outbound Indian funds in secrecy jurisdictions (the Ram Jethmalani petitions; Global Financial Integrity studies on illicit financial flows; the SIT reports on black money chaired by Justice B.P. Jeevan Reddy/Justice M.B. Shah). Post-2016, demonetisation, the Black Money Act 2015, the Benami law 2016 and GST were all aimed at shrinking the cash-black economy. The precise figure is unknowable — the laundered money is, by design, hidden — which is why the statute regulates processes and records, not just outcomes.

Integration“The money is… integrated into the financial system in such a way that its original association with crime is totally obliterated and the money could be used… as untainted/clean money.” Faculty: after layering… when the property is purchased with the help of such money, it is called laundering [integration]. • Purchase of immovable property (with documented “white” + cash “black” component), farms, malls, land parcels; M3M/IREO-style realty groups (see Pankaj Bansal);
• Shares/business of legitimate companies; Satyam-style inflated revenue/investments (Ramaraju: SRSR Holdings shares and 287 immovable properties);
• “Loans from friends and relatives” from laundered funds; gifts; foreign-settled credit cards;
• Re-investment into the same or another criminal enterprise — completing the cycle that funds drugs, terror and corruption.
Exam drafting point: always note that the three stages are not statutorily indispensable in sequence. Section 3 (post-2019 Explanation, as interpreted in Vijay Madanlal) criminalises any one of the six activities — concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projection or claiming as untainted. The stages are an analytical description of the laundering economy (the “generic” definition used even for civil attachment, as Ramaraju para 112 explains), not additional ingredients the prosecution must prove in order.

Part III — The PMLA, 2002: Genesis, Constitutional Basis, Objects and Scheme (Syllabus Head C)

The Act is a special, self-contained, hybrid (regulatory + civil-cum-confiscatory + penal) statute enacted under Article 253 of the Constitution to implement international conventions. It is not a mere anti-crime law: it builds an entire parallel machinery — reporting entities, financial intelligence, attachment, adjudication, confiscation, special criminal courts and global mutual assistance — around a single policy: crime must not pay, and tainted property must lose its taint’s value.

2. Constitutional and international basis

Article 253, Constitution of India: “Notwithstanding anything in the foregoing provisions of this Chapter, Parliament has power to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing any treaty, agreement or convention with any other country or countries or any decision made at any international conference, association or other body.”

Supporting Union-List entries: Entry 10 (foreign affairs), Entry 12 (UN), Entry 13 (treaties), Entry 14 (entering into treaties), Entry 16 (foreign exchange), Entry 17/18 (institutions/banking), Entries 1–2 (defence/atomic energy, including terror-financing), Entry 93 (offences against laws on Union subjects). The law therefore operates on a firmer federal footing than a State-list police statute — important for the ED’s all-India jurisdiction and the s.45(1A) bar on State police investigating the s.3 offence.

Connected law — how Ramaraju treated the treaties (Issue A)

The petitioners argued the Act lacked domestic competence and that the Vienna/Palermo conventions could not alter municipal rights. The Andhra Pradesh High Court (L. Narasimha Reddy & P.V. Sanjay Kumar JJ.) held: (i) Parliament’s competence under Article 253 read with the Union List is unassailable; (ii) international conventions “enumerated measures for incorporation as… criminal offences under domestic law” — they catalyse legislation; (iii) consistent with the principle in Gramophone Company of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey and Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin, municipal courts will read domestic law in harmony with treaties wherever there is ambiguity or a void, but treaties “are not to be construed as” overriding clear domestic law. The preamble itself records the enactment as fulfilment of the Political Declaration and Global Programme of Action adopted by the UN General Assembly on 23 February 1990 and the special session of June 1998 calling on States to legislate.

The Preamble

“An Act to prevent money-laundering and to provide for confiscation of property derived from, or involved in, money-laundering, and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.” It records the Political Declaration/Global Programme of Action and “whereas” the resolution urged States to enact national laws. Two objects only — (a) prevention/punishment of the offence; (b) confiscation of the property — but the second object drives the Act’s distinctive two-track design.

The Court rejected the argument that ss.5/8 attachment was disguised punishment attracting criminal-procedure guarantees. Attachment is a preventive, provisional civil measure to secure property for possible confiscation (following Amratlal Prajivandas v. 4. A self-contained code

  • Section 71 (non-obstante): “The provisions of this Act shall have effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in any other law for the time being in force” — the Act prevails over CrPC and other special laws to the extent of inconsistency (Gautam Kundu: the s.45 bail conditions bind even bail under s.439 CrPC).
  • Section 65: the CrPC, 1973 applies to proceedings before a Special Court “so far as… not inconsistent” with the Act; s.46 deems the Special Court a Court of Session. Post 1-7-2024, references to CrPC are read as corresponding BNSS provisions (remand s.167 CrPC → s.187 BNSS; anticipatory bail s.438 → s.482 BNSS; inherent powers s.482 CrPC → s.528 BNSS) — see the mapping in Part XIII.
  • Section 66 enables sharing of information with other agencies (including contemporaneous intimation to the predicate police under the s.5 second-proviso regime, per Vijay Madanlal).
  • Section 67 bars suits/civil proceedings against authorities acting in good faith; s.14 gives reporting-entity staff good-faith protection.
  • Section 62 punishes vexatious search/arrest (up to 2 years, ₹50,000); s.63 penalises non-cooperation (₹500–₹10,000; deemed offences under IPC ss.175/179/180/228 → BNS ss.208, 211, 212, 267).
  • Section 64 requires sanction for prosecuting authorities; the appropriate authority must decide within 90 days.
  • Section 70 extends liability to companies/firms (2013 Explanation 2: a company may be prosecuted even without prosecution of the individual director/partner).
  • Section 72A (2019) sets up an inter-agency Co-ordination Committee.

Part IV — Definitions, the Offence of Money-Laundering (s.3) and Punishment (s.4) (Syllabus Heads D–E)

How “proceeds of crime” expands — the chain-of-taint principle (Ramaraju, paras 114–115)

“A property acquires a taint on account being a derivative of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence and includes the value of such property. Since placement, layering and integration are among the essential features of money-laundering, the proceeds of crime may not necessarily continue in the hands of the original malfeasant(s).” Where proceeds are layered through plural transactions, s.23 presumes the remaining transactions form part of the same inter-connected chain unless rebutted. The 2019 Explanation to (u) (“relatable to”) confirms that even assets not directly taken from the victim (e.g. bribes earned from a fraud, substitution assets, profits churned through the crime) are proceeds. Equivalent-value property (2015/18) defeats the launderer who has moved the actual asset abroad: property of equal value in India or abroad is attachable.

Real example — third-party and family property (Ramaraju / Satyam)

The ED attached not only assets standing in the company/accused names but also SRSR Holdings shares and 287 properties held through family/associate entities. The High Court held property of any person — not merely the accused — can be attached/confiscated if it is shown involved in laundering; the s.8 notice provisos and hearing given to third parties/co-holders supply natural justice. A bona fide transferee for value without knowledge may seek release; a sham nominee cannot.

2. Section 3 — the offence of money-laundering

Section 3 (as amended in 2013; Explanation inserted w.e.f. 1-8-2019)

“Whosoever directly or indirectly attempts to indulge or knowingly assists or knowingly is a party or is actually involved in any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime including its concealment, possession, acquisition or use and projecting or claiming it as untainted property shall be guilty of offence of money-laundering.”

Explanation. — (i) a person is guilty if he has directly or indirectly attempted/assisted/been party/been involved in one or more of: (a) concealment; (b) possession; (c) acquisition; (d) use; (e) projecting as untainted; or (f) claiming as untainted, in any manner whatsoever; (ii) the process or activity is a continuing activity and continues till such time a person is directly or indirectly enjoying the proceeds by concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projection or claiming.

2.1 The four modes of involvement

  1. attempts to indulge (attempt liability built into the section itself);
  2. knowingly assists;
  3. knowingly is a party; or
  4. is actually involved, in each case directly or indirectly.

Knowledge (mens rea) attaches to “assists” and “is a party”; “actually involved” and the activity-based Explanation broaden liability but Vijay Madanlal insists there must still be material linking the person to proceeds of crime — guilt cannot be predicated on a “notional” basis (paras 281–284). Knowingly “assisting” catches professionals, bankers, hawala operators and directors who facilitate; s.70 catches companies.

2.2 The six activities (Explanation i, clauses (a)–(f))

(a) Concealment
Hiding or disguising the nature, source, location, disposition or ownership of proceeds — layering in any manner.
(b) Possession
Holding proceeds, even without having committed the predicate crime (Mohammad Arif: subsequent participation suffices).
(c) Acquisition
Receiving/obtaining proceeds by purchase, gift, loan, share subscription, benami transfer.
(d) Use
Deploying proceeds — buying assets, running businesses, funding further crime.
(e) Projecting as untainted
Showing dirty money as clean (the classic integration claim).
(f) Claiming as untainted
Asserting a clean title — e.g. in accounts, tax returns, court proceedings.

2.3 “And” read as “Or” — Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India (2022)

Landmark interpretive ruling (SCC para in the DU case file; ratio at paras 40–44 in LiveLaw text)

The challenge: s.3 uses the phrase “concealment, possession, acquisition or use and projecting or claiming it as untainted”; petitioners argued the statute required the prosecution to prove BOTH concealment/possession/acquisition/use AND projection as untainted — making possession alone unpunishable. The Supreme Court held that “and” must be read as “or”, relying on Sanjay Dutt v. State through CBI (II), (1994) 5 SCC 410 (“arms and ammunition” under TADA read disjunctively), Ishwar Singh Bindra v. State of U.P., and Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam v. Essar Power. The Court’s illustrative absurdity: if one person merely possesses crime proceeds and a friend alone projects them as untainted, a conjunctive reading would punish neither — “the whole Act will be frustrated”. The 2019 Explanation was held clarificatory (not retroactive punishment): it lists the six activities disjunctively and confirms guilt for involvement in “one or more”.

2.4 Standalone yet dependent; continuing offence; retrospectivity

  • Standalone: the s.3 offence is independent of the trial or even the registration of the scheduled-offence case; a person who never committed the predicate crime but knowingly holds/layers its proceeds is guilty (Mohammad Arif: “two types of offences”; Vijay Madanlal). It is also a continuing offence (Explanation (ii)) — it continues as long as the person enjoys the proceeds; the limitation clock does not run while possession/enjoyment continues, and prosecution lies even where the predicate predates the Act if laundering continued after notification (the Court applied s.482-CrPC–saving principles and the general continuing-offence doctrine).
  • Dependent: there must be criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence generating proceeds; authorities cannot act “on a mere notional basis” (Vijay Madanlal, paras 281–284). The scheduled offence is the “backbone” — its final acquittal/discharge/quashing destroys the foundation for s.3 and for attachment of that person’s property (Parvathi Kollur, applying para 187(d) of Vijay Madanlal).
  • Attempts: “attempts to indulge” is within s.3 itself (separate from general attempt liability in IPC s.511 → BNS s.120).

2.5 Ingredients checklist (write this in problem answers)

  1. A scheduled offence (Part A; Part B ≥₹1 cr; Part C cross-border) has been/ is being committed — the predicate.
  2. Property has been derived or obtained (directly/indirectly) from criminal activity relating to it = proceeds of crime s.2(1)(u) (or equivalent value; property used in the offence, s.2(1)(v) Explanation).
  3. The accused, directly or indirectly, attempted/knowingly assisted/was party/was actually involved in a process or activity connected with such proceeds.
  4. The activity is one of the six: concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projection, claiming (any one suffices after Vijay Madanlal and the 2019 Explanation).
  5. Guilty knowledge for assistance/party modes; material in possession (not mere suspicion/notional basis).

Connected law — cognate sentencing models

The Orissa High Court in Mohammad Arif v. Directorate of Enforcement (13 July 2020) observed the Act is modelled on the UK Criminal Justice Act, which imposes criminal liability on those who know or suspect laundering and fail to report. Compare the forfeiture minimum-terms under NDPS Chapter VA and the 7–10 year band of serious predicate offences; unlike ordinary IPC theft/cheating (now BNS ss.311–318), the sentence attaches to the handling of proceeds, not the predicate itself, and runs in addition to any sentence for the scheduled offence (no merger; distinct acts).

Real example — subsequent participation is enough (Mohammad Arif, 2020)

The High Court held that even a person who did not commit the scheduled offence is culpable “upon subsequent participation in the laundering of such monies”; the offence is independent as well as continuing — “as long as [the] accused remains in possession, causes concealment of the nature of the money or continues to mask the tainted money as untainted”. This is why spouses, nominees, directors and employees receiving proceeds are arrayed — but subject always to the Vijay Madanlal “foundational material” and Parvathi Kollur backbone limitations, and (post-2024) the Pankaj Bansal arrest safeguards.

Exam traps around the Schedule
  • Predicates are not investigated by ED. The scheduled offence is investigated/tried by the competent police/CBI/NIA/revenue authority; ED investigates only the s.3 laundering and property tracks (Binod Kumar).

Part V — Enforcement Track 1: Attachment of Property (s.5) and Retention (s.20) (Syllabus Head F, first limb)

Attachment is a provisional, civil, preventive order that freezes property so it cannot be dissipated before adjudication and confiscation. It is the most-used weapon of the Act and was challenged in every prescribed case from Ramaraju to Vijay Madanlal. Learn its five sub-sections, the three provisos and the strict 180-day/30-day clock — problem questions commonly test whether an attachment is legal at the threshold.

1. Section 5(1) — conditions for a provisional attachment order (PAO)

s.5(1) (substituted w.e.f. 15-2-2013)

Where the Director, or any other officer not below the rank of Deputy Director authorised by the Director, has reason to believe (the reason for such belief to be recorded in writing), on the basis of material in his possession, that —

(a) any person is in possession of any proceeds of crime; and
(b) such proceeds are likely to be concealed, transferred or dealt with in any manner which may result in frustrating any proceedings relating to confiscation under Chapter III —

2. Sub-sections (2) to (5) — procedural safeguards

  • s.5(2): immediately after attachment, the officer forwards the order + the material in his possession to the Adjudicating Authority in a sealed envelope (prescribed manner; AA retains it for the prescribed period) — an independent check on arbitrary belief.
  • s.5(3): the PAO lapses on expiry of 180 days OR on the date of the AA’s order under s.8(3), whichever is earlier. If the AA does not confirm in time, attachment dies and the property is freed.
  • s.5(4): the attachment does not prevent the person interested from enjoying attached immovable property — he may continue in possession/use (no dispossession at the attachment stage; “person interested” includes anyone claiming an interest). Vijay Madanlal extended this spirit to the post-confirmation s.8(4) stage (possession is the exception, not the rule, until actual confiscation).
  • s.5(5): the attaching officer must, within 30 days of attachment, file a complaint stating the facts before the Adjudicating Authority — this triggers s.8 adjudication; without it the PAO cannot be confirmed.

4. Judicial treatment of s.5

B. Ramaraju (AP HC, 2011) — Issues B & C (validity of s.2(u), s.5)

(i) “Proceeds of crime” and s.5 are not vague or arbitrary: the material-in-possession, recorded-reasons, sealed-envelope, 30-day complaint, 30-day show-cause, reasoned confirmation and two-tier appeal (s.26 Tribunal; s.42 High Court) supply the “several layers of scrutiny” (rejecting the Chintamon Rao-void-for-vagueness attack). (ii) No mens rea is required for civil attachment/confiscation — the question is whether property is derived from scheduled criminality, not the holder’s state of mind; otherwise launderers would park property with innocent nominees. (iii) Third-party property is attachable; third-party/co-holder notice provisos and the s.8 proviso hearing protect genuine claimants. (iv) The second proviso (pre-FIR attachment) was held clarificatory/retrospective in its 2009 context because attachment is preventive, not punitive — Article 20 is not attracted.

Vijay Madanlal (SC, 2022) — s.5 re-validated on the 2013–2018 text

The Court held: (a) the power is conditioned on material in possession and recorded reasons; the sealed-envelope and AA adjudication are substantive safeguards; (b) the second proviso permits attachment even before the s.173 report, for the property track cannot be held hostage to the predicate investigation — but the ED must simultaneously share information with the predicate agency (s.66(2)) so the scheduled-offence process is not bypassed; (c) attachment in aid of “any proceeding under this Act” includes inchoate/investigative stages; (d) attachment does not create an irreversible consequence — release/restoration follows if laundering is not established (ss.8(6), 8(8)).

Part VI — Enforcement Track 2: Survey, Search, Seizure, Freeze and Arrest (ss.16–19); Summons and Statements (s.50); Penalties (ss.62–64)

Chapter V equips ED authorities with inquisitorial powers wider than the police’s: survey without prior process (s.16), search/seizure/freezing without a predicate FIR after 2019 (ss.17–18), and arrest on recorded “reason to believe” (s.19). Every power is hedged by (i) rank thresholds, (ii) written reasons, (iii) sealed-envelope transmission to the Adjudicating Authority, and (iv) witness/Magistrate/female safeguards. Vijay Madanlal (2022) upheld these powers; Pankaj Bansal (2024) read s.19 down with mandatory written-grounds and independent magisterial verification.

4. Section 19 — Power to arrest (the 2015/2019 Q.7 and 2019 Q.6 limb; now governed by Pankaj Bansal)

s.19(1)

If the Director, Deputy Director, Assistant Director or any other officer authorised by the Central Government by general/special order has, on the basis of material in his possession, reason to believe (recorded in writing) that any person has been guilty of an offence punishable under this Act, he may arrest such person and shall, as soon as may be, inform him of the grounds for such arrest.

s.19(2): immediately after arrest, the officer forwards a copy of the order + material to the AA in a sealed envelope.
s.19(3): every arrested person must, within 24 hours (excluding journey time), be taken before a Special Court or a Judicial/Metropolitan Magistrate having jurisdiction.

Circumstances in which arrest is lawful — the distilled test

  1. The officer is competent and authorised (s.19(1); G.S.R. 440(E) hierarchy; s.53 empowerments).
  2. There is material in his possession (not conjecture) connecting the person to an offence punishable under the Act (s.3/s.4 — not merely to the scheduled offence).
  3. A genuine, recorded “reason to believe”, formed at or before arrest, showing guilt — “guilty of an offence” is a stronger standard than the s.5/s.17 “proceeds” threshold.
  4. Grounds are furnished “as soon as may be” — per Pankaj Bansal (2024), written grounds (the Form III order) are supplied as a matter of course, without exception (sensitive material may be redacted); oral intimation is insufficient.
  5. Production before the Special Court/Magistrate within 24 hours (s.19(3)); remand follows s.167 CrPC → s.187 BNSS (police custody staggered; judicial custody; default-bail clock) read with s.46 PMLA, and the Court independently verifies s.19 compliance before authorising remand.
  6. Arrest must not be a colourable exercise: summoning a person under s.50 on one pretext and arresting him under s.19 on another undisclosed ground is fraud on power (Pankaj Bansal).
What is NOT a ground for arrest (Pankaj Bansal v. UOI, (2024) 7 SCC 576): (i) non-cooperation with questioning; (ii) evasive or unsatisfactory answers — there is no legal obligation to make self-incriminating statements (Article 20(3); Santosh Kumar Mehmooda v. State, (2017) 9 SCC 714, applied), and an investigator may not arrest merely because the suspect protests innocence; (iii) absence from home during a search; (iv) the desire to “custodially interrogate” without recording why non-custodial means fail. Remand by itself cannot cure an unlawful arrest (Madhu Limaye); non-compliance “enures to the benefit” of the arrestee and may attract s.62 PMLA. Detailed ratio is in the Case File for Case 16.

Part VII — Adjudication, Confiscation, Vesting and Appeals (ss.6–11, 25–26, 42) (Syllabus Head G, first limb)

Adjudication is the civil heart of the statute: a quasi-judicial, three-member Authority decides whether attached/seized property is “involved in money-laundering”, confirms the attachment through investigation and trial, and a Special Court then orders confiscation, whereupon the property vests absolutely in the Central Government free of encumbrances. The 2015/2019 amendments reallocated the confiscation order from the Authority to the Special Court and lengthened confirmation to 365 days — answers must use the current structure (and the DU cases refer to the older two-stage model).

Key sub-section points

  • s.8(1) notice: minimum 30 days; it shifts the practical burden — the holder must indicate the sources of income, earnings or assets with which he acquired the property and the evidence on which he relies (this is where the s.24 presumption operates); if notice names a benamidar/holder on behalf of another, that other is also served; joint property → all joint holders served.
  • s.8(2) finding: reasoned order after reply + hearing + materials; a third-party claimant (not the notice recipient) must be heard to prove non-involvement (proviso) — the natural-justice anchor for attachment of family/company property.
  • s.8(3)(a) durations: 2013 text tied confirmation to 90 days/investigation and trial of the scheduled offence; from 20-3-2019 the period became 365 days during investigation plus pendency of proceedings for the offence under this Act before a court (or corresponding foreign court); court-stay periods are excluded. Clause (b): finality after confiscation under s.8(5)/(7), s.58B or s.60(2A).
  • s.8(4) possession: possession after confirmation; Vijay Madanlal held possession before actual confiscation is the exception, not the rule: authorities should, as far as possible, permit enjoyment analogously to s.5(4) (leasing/running businesses etc.) unless possession is necessary; impracticable freezing deems possession for confiscation.
  • s.8(5)–(7) (substituted 2013; Special Court substituted for AA in 2015): confiscation is ordered by the Special Court — (5) on conviction for ML; (6) release if ML not made out/property not involved; (7) if trial cannot be conducted because of death or the accused being declared a proclaimed offender, or for any other reason, or trial commenced but could not conclude, the Special Court decides confiscation/release on the material (this closes the “absconder loophole”).
  • s.8(8) (2015): victim restoration — on conviction the Special Court may direct restoration of confiscated property (or part) to a claimant with a legitimate interest who suffered quantifiable loss, acted in good faith, took all reasonable precautions and is not involved in ML; proviso permits consideration during trial.
Amendment trap: pre-2013 s.8(5) provided attachment ceased if the accused was acquitted under the scheduled offence. The 2013/2015 re-enactment ties confiscation/release to the ML trial before the Special Court, not the predicate judgment. After Parvathi Kollur (2022), however, a final acquittal/discharge/quashing that destroys the scheduled-offence foundation still collapses the ML case and attached property follows — read the current s.8 with that limitation.

3. Section 9 — vesting in the Central Government; s.10 Administrator

s.9 — absolute vesting free of encumbrances

Where an order of confiscation has been made under s.8(5)/(7), s.58B or s.60(2A), all rights and title in the property vest absolutely in the Central Government, free from all encumbrances. Proviso: after hearing interested persons, the Special Court/AA may declare any encumbrance or lease-hold interest created with a view to defeating Chapter III to be void — then vesting is free of it (the encumbrance-holder retains a damages remedy against the creator). This defeats post-attachment mortgages, gifts, leases and benami transfers layered to shield assets.

  • s.10: the Central Government appoints Administrators (officers not below Joint Secretary rank, Gazette) to receive and manage confiscated property under prescribed conditions and to take measures for its disposal after vesting.
  • Compare the SAFEMA/NDPS Chapter VA administrator model and the FEOA s.12 management regime — the PMLA Administrator is the same administrative lineage.

5. Burden and standard of proof in adjudication

  • The AA proceeds on the preponderance of probabilities (civil standard); once the Director shows foundational facts (property held; scheduled-offence criminality; linkage), s.24(b) permits and ss.22/23 support the presumption that the property is involved; the holder must establish lawful source by credible materials.
  • Before the Special Court at the s.8(5)/criminal stage, the prosecution proves the s.3 offence beyond reasonable doubt; once the accused is charged, s.24(a) makes the presumption mandatory (“shall… unless the contrary is proved”). Ramaraju upheld this as a valid reverse onus akin to NDPS/Customs, and Vijay Madanlal harmonised it with Article 21 via the foundational-facts requirement.
  • Ramaraju (Issue D, s.8(4) of the old numbering): even the possessor/acquirer who is not the scheduled-offence accused is liable to confiscation once the property is proved proceeds; confiscation does not require mens rea and is not barred by Article 20(1) — it is not a prosecution/punishment.

Part VII (cont.) — Special Courts, Cognizance and the Law of Bail (ss.40–47); Companies (s.70)

1. Constitution and jurisdiction of Special Courts

s.43 — designation

(1) The Central Government, in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, notifies one or more Courts of Session as Special Courts for trial of offences punishable under s.4, for specified areas or cases/classes of cases. (2) While trying a PMLA offence, the Special Court shall also try any other offence with which the accused may, under the CrPC, be charged at the same trial — enabling the predicate scheduled offence to be tried together.

s.44 — offences triable by Special Courts; cognizance
  • (1)(a): the s.4 offence and any scheduled offence connected with it are triable by the Special Court of the area where the offence was committed (scheduled-offence trials begun before the Act commenced continue in that Court).
  • (1)(b): cognizance of the s.3 offence is taken without the accused being committed for trial, upon a complaint made by an authority authorised under the Act (the Director/authorised officer) — not on a police report (the words peritting cognizance on police report were deleted in 2005). If investigation concludes that no ML is made out, the authority files a closure report before the Special Court (2019 proviso).
  • (1)(c): where another court has taken cognizance of the scheduled offence, it shall, on the authorised authority’s application, commit that case to the Special Court, which proceeds from the committal stage.
  • (1)(d): trial follows the CrPC as applicable to a Court of Session trial.
  • Explanation (2019): (i) the Special Court’s jurisdiction during investigation/enquiry/trial is not dependent on orders in the scheduled-offence case, and trying both sets of offences is not a joint trial; (ii) the complaint is deemed to include complaints on further investigation against any accused, named or not.
  • (2): the High Court’s special bail powers under s.439 CrPC (→ s.483 BNSS) are preserved; “Magistrate” is read as including the Special Court.
  • s.40: Chairperson/Members and officers are public servants within s.21 IPC (→ BNS s.2(15)-type definition). s.41: no civil court jurisdiction over matters the Act commits to the AA/Authorities.
  • s.46: save as otherwise provided, CrPC (including bail/bond provisions) applies; the Special Court is deemed a Court of Session; the prosecutor is deemed a Public Prosecutor; Central Government may appoint Special Public Prosecutors; PP qualification — seven years’ practice as advocate.
  • s.47: the High Court exercises all powers under Chapters XXIX (appeals) and XXX (reference/revision) CrPC (→ appeal s.374→s.415 BNSS; revision s.397→s.438 BNSS) as if the Special Court were a Sessions Court.

2. Section 45 — cognizable, non-bailable and the “twin conditions”

s.45(1) current text (post-2018; Explanation added 2019)

“Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, no person accused of an offence under this Act shall be released on bail or on his own bond unless — (i) the Public Prosecutor has been given an opportunity to oppose the application; and (ii) where the PP opposes, the court is satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing that he is not guilty of such offence and that he is not likely to commit any offence while on bail.”

First proviso: release may be directed for a person under 16 years, a woman, sick or infirm, or accused (alone or with others) of laundering a sum less than ₹1 crore.
Second proviso: the Special Court cannot take cognizance of a s.4 offence except on a written complaint of (i) the Director or (ii) an authorised Central/State Government officer.
s.45(1A): no police officer shall investigate a PMLA offence unless specifically authorised by the Central Government by general/special order and subject to prescribed conditions.
s.45(2): these limitations are in addition to Code limitations.
Explanation (2019): all offences under the Act are cognizable and non-bailable notwithstanding the CrPC, and authorised officers may arrest without warrant, subject to s.19 and this section.

The bail sequence every answer must trace

Original s.45 (2005): twin conditions applied only where the scheduled offence was punishable with more than three years under Part A; non-cognizable flavour at investigation.
Nikesh Tarachand Shah v. Union of India, (2018) 11 SCC 1 (judgment 23-11-2017, Chandrachud J.): struck the twin-condition classification down as violative Articles 14 and 21 on two grounds: (i) classification based on the predicate sentence (>3 years Part A) had no rational nexus to the object (same laundering of proceeds attracted the conditions or not depending on the predicate’s sentence — including the anomaly that laundering terrorism/Naxal proceeds could escape while laundering a 4-year property offence attracted them); (ii) the conditions did not apply to equally serious Part C cross-border offences — an arbitrary distinction between same-class offenders. The Court reaffirmed the trinity Sanjay Chandra, Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre: personal liberty is precious; bail not jail is the rule.
Money (Amendment) Act 13 of 2018 (w.e.f. 19-4-2018): Parliament re-enacted the twin conditions for every “offence under this Act” (removing both defects), added the <₹1 crore / under-16 / woman / sick-infirm carve-outs; 2019 added the cognizable/non-bailable Explanation.
Vijay Madanlal (27-7-2022): uphbold the re-enacted s.45 against the Nikesh-based challenge: economic/“grave” offences form a class apart (Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab on TADA; Usmanbhai Dawoodbhai under NDPS; State of M.P. v. Mohanlal Jitamalji Porwal); stringent bail is a valid regulatory response to FATF obligations, sovereignty and Directive Principles (Articles 38–39); the conditions are not a complete bar — they guide discretion and apply equally to anticipatory bail applications; the court still records “reasonable grounds” satisfaction and the vulnerable-class provisos soften rigour.

3. Section 45(1A) — the police-investigation bar (exam favourite; Binod Kumar)

State police cannot register/investigate the s.3 offence without Central authorisation; the ED is the statutorily designated investigator (G.S.R. 440(E) exclusive powers). Conversely, the ED cannot investigate the scheduled offence: the CBI/police retain the predicate. Binod Kumar (2011) settled the division: overlap in allegations permits parallel probes; sharing material is obligatory; s.43(2)/s.44(1)(a) permit connected trials; only the Central Government may authorise another agency for s.3 under s.45(1A).

4. Liability of companies and individuals — s.70

  • s.70(1): where an offence is committed by a company (includes firm/association), every person in charge/responsible to the company for conduct of its business and the company itself are guilty and liable.
  • Consent/connivance/neglect of directors/officers attracts personal liability.
  • Explanation 1 (2013): a nominated/identified director is liable unless he proves the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised due diligence.
  • Explanation 2 (2013): where an offence is committed by a company and it is proved committed with the consent/connivance of, or attributable to neglect of, any director/manager/secretary/officer, such person is deemed guilty and the company may be prosecuted even without prosecuting that individual — removing the vicarious-punishment obstacle (important for shell-company prosecutions).
Principle consolidation — the offender-track test

Before conviction under s.4 the prosecution must establish: (1) a scheduled offence (living predicate — not quashed/acquitted on the merits for the accused); (2) proceeds derived/obtained directly/indirectly; (3) one of the six s.3 activities (“and” = “or”); (4) the accused’s attempt/knowing assistance/party-hood/involvement; (5) material-based recorded satisfaction where powers are used; (6) trial on the Director’s complaint before the Special Court. Upon conviction: 3–7 years (up to 10 for NDPS proceeds) + unlimited fine + confiscation under s.8(5).

Real examples

(a) Cooperative banks, payment aggregators and jewellery houses have faced s.13 penalties for failing to detect/flag structuring; FIU-IND’s published orders sanction banks for delayed/lump-sum CTRs and failure to file STRs on “smurfed” deposits. Part IX — Preventive Mechanism 2: Reciprocal Arrangements with Foreign Countries (Chapter IX, ss.55–61)

Because placement/layering/integration are often transnational (the outbound, inbound, returning and flow-through routes), the Act creates a complete mutual legal assistance architecture: treaties/notifications define “contracting States”; letters of request gather evidence and execute summonses, warrants, attachment and confiscation orders across borders; foreign trials trigger Indian release/confiscation; and prisoners/accused can be transferred. This Chapter implements Articles of the Vienna, Palermo and UNCAC Conventions (asset recovery) and is the domestic counterpart of extradition treaties and the FEOA, 2018.

DU Case File 12 — Binod Kumar (Sinha) v. State of Jharkhand & Ors., (2011) 11 SCC 463

Case 12 · DU Case Material pp. 149–157 · Supreme Court of India

Binod Kumar (Sinha) v. State of Jharkhand & Ors.

(2011) 11 SCC 463 · decided 29 March 2011 · Dalveer Bhandari and Deepak Verma, JJ. · SLP(Crl.) from Jharkhand HC · Madhu Koda coal/iron-ore scam — who investigates what: CBI vs Enforcement Directorate; s.45(1A)

Facts

  • The allegation was amassing of illicit wealth running into several hundred crores by former Jharkhand Ministers, including former Chief Minister Madhu Koda, by abuse of official position in the grant of iron-ore mining leases and coal blocks and related allotments.
  • Vigilance P.S. Case No. 09/2009, Ranchi, dated 2-7-2009 was registered on the directions of the Special Judge, Vigilance, Ranchi exercising powers under s.156(3) CrPC, alleging IPC ss. 409, 420, 423, 424, 465, 120B (now BNS ss. 316, 318, 322, 323, 336/339, 61(2)) and PC Act ss. 7, 10, 11, 13(2) r/w 13(1)(d)/(e); it named Koda and former Ministers Kamlesh Singh, Bhanu Pratap Shah and Bandhu Tirkey; the appellant Binod Kumar Sinha’s involvement surfaced during investigation.
  • The amounts unearthed at that stage were ≈ ₹1.5 crore in one matter and ≈ ₹6.5 crore in another — “merely the tip of the iceberg”. The affidavit identified 32 companies to investigate and investments allegedly made in Bangkok, Dubai, Jakarta, Sweden and Libya — a multi-State, multi-national trail.
  • There was “no clear allegation so far about… laundering in the [PMLA] sense”, but an allegation of investment of illicit money in properties/shares in India and abroad.
  • The Jharkhand High Court Division Bench, recording an “area of overlap” and the need for a “systematic, scientific and analysed investigation by an expert investigating agency”, referred the matter to the CBI and observed the Centre should consider s.45(1A) to transfer the PML investigation to the CBI; failing such an order, the CBI would share material suggesting ML with the ED from time to time.
  • Binod Kumar (an absconder who had evaded arrest) appealed under Article 136; during notice (1-9-2010) the Supreme Court had allowed the CBI investigation to continue. The CBI chargesheet was filed 12-11-2010 (ss.120B IPC; ss.9, 13(2) r/w 13(1)(d) PC Act).

Issues (three substantial questions of law formulated by Mr. K.K. Venugopal, senior counsel for the appellant)

  1. Whether the PML Act is a self-contained code while the statute constituting the CBI (DSPE Act, 1946) is limited — ousting CBI investigation?
  2. Whether, in light of s.45(1A) read with ss.43 and 44, the CBI has any authority to investigate offences which are the “sole domain” of the Enforcement Directorate?
  3. Whether the High Court was right in brushing aside the allegations and directing a CBI investigation (and in commenting on s.45(1A))?

Arguments for the appellant (K.K. Venugopal, learned senior counsel)

  • Only ED can investigate the s.4 offence and only the Special Court can try it; the PMLA is a self-contained code enacted after the 1998 UNGASS Political Declaration and under Article 253; it prevails over inconsistent State enactments (Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel; S. Jagannath).
  • The DSPE Act limits the CBI (a police agency) territorially to Delhi and UTs and to consent/notification areas; the CBI has neither the multi-disciplinary composition (police, revenue, tax, specialists) nor the international tracing powers PMLA gives ED through s.54 (assistance), s.55(c) and Chapter IX; reliance on CBI v. State of Rajasthan, (1996) 9 SCC 735 (FERA a complete code; police not clothed with authority), and ED v. M. Samba Siva Rao, (2000) 5 SCC 431.
  • Convicting under s.4 requires ED to first establish the scheduled offence, then the property derived, then its projection as untainted — a special procedure CBI cannot operate; s.43/44 trial provisions and the second proviso to s.45(1) (written complaint) were said to reserve the whole field for ED.
  • Prayer: quash the CBI investigation of Vigilance P.S. 09/2009 and release the appellant from “illegal detention”.

Arguments for the respondents (H.P. Raval, ASG for CBI; written submissions of CBI and ED)

  • Money-laundering is a standalone offence, and its investigation alone is ED’s exclusive domain (subject to Central authorisation under s.45(1A)); nothing in the Act empowers ED to investigate the scheduled offences — IPC, PC Act or other predicate crimes.
  • s.5(1) attachment rests on reason to believe a person is in possession of proceeds — not a concluded investigation; the second proviso to s.45(1) bars Special Court cognizance only of the s.4 offence absent a written complaint — it does not regulate investigation of predicates.
  • Trial ≠ investigation: s.44 (Special Court trying the connected scheduled offence) and s.43(2) (joint charge) deal with trial, not investigation; no provision restricts the competent investigating agency of the predicate offence.
  • The CBI was “investigating… these offences alone and is not investigating any offence under the PML Act”; the High Court had recorded cogent reasons (multi-State/multi-national scale, tip-of-iceberg); the appellant was an absconder disentitled to discretionary relief; a chargesheet had already been filed and further chargesheets may follow.
Decision

The Court (Bhandari J., with Verma J.) held “no interference is called for”: the appeal was devoid of merit and dismissed, with parties bearing their own costs. The CBI could continue investigating the IPC/PC Act scheduled offences; ED retained exclusive investigation of the s.3/s.4 money-laundering offence (subject to s.45(1A) Central authorisation); the overlap “cannot be allowed to form a tool in the hands of the accused to scuttle the investigation”; sharing of material between CBI and ED was endorsed. (The judgment also reproduces the then ss.2(p), 2(ra), 2(u), 2(x), 2(y), 3 and 4 — with a ₹5 lakh fine ceiling and ₹30-lakh Part B threshold — a useful snapshot of the pre-2013/2015 law.)

Principles evolved
  1. Two-track jurisdiction: the ordinary agency (police/CBI/NIA/revenue agency) investigates/tries the scheduled offence; the ED alone investigates the money-laundering offence and the property tracks, unless the Central Government specifically authorises another agency under s.45(1A).
  2. Overlap is not a bar: where allegations overlap (illicit wealth simultaneously evidencing corruption and laundering), parallel proceedings must proceed — overlap cannot be exploited to stall investigation; agencies must share material (now reinforced by s.66(2)).
  3. The PMLA is a special, self-contained code enacted under Article 253; its machinery (s.54 assistance; Chapter IX cross-border powers) is specialist and national/multi-national in reach.
  4. An absconder will ordinarily be denied discretionary relief (Article 136).
  5. Jurisdiction of the High Court under Articles 226/227 and CrPC s.156(3) to refer a grave, complex economic scam to the CBI is not displaced merely because the facts may later support an ECIR.
Connected law & developments

(i) DSPE Act, 1946 ss.3–6 (CBI constitution; consent jurisdictions); (ii) s.45(1A) PMLA and the G.S.R. 440(E) empowerment; (iii) s.43(2) joint trial; s.44(1)(c) committal of the predicate to the Special Court; (iv) the Central Bureau of Investigation Act debates and the Vineet Narain statutory-status directives; (v) Madhu Koda was later convicted (2017 coal-allotment case) and the ED attached assets across India and overseas through s.57 letters of request — demonstrating the division of labour in practice. Note the post-2015 figures: Part B threshold is now ₹1 crore, PAO 180 days, and ss.17/18 searches no longer await a predicate FIR/chargesheet.

  6. Issue F: Is the s.24 reverse burden arbitrary; does it apply only to the s.3 trial, not Chapter III proceedings, and never against a person not accused of s.3?

Arguments of the petitioners (summary)

  • s.2(u): catches property in the hands of persons with no knowledge or nexus to the crime — grossly unreasonable, violates Articles 14/20(1)/21/300A; the Vienna Convention (Art. 3, conversion/concealment/use requires knowledge) limits confiscation to knowing handlers.
  • s.5: vague and internally inconsistent (“such property” of a charged person in the body vs “property of any person” in the second proviso); “involved in money-laundering” defines neither nature nor degree of involvement; no guidelines. The second proviso, if applied to pre-2009 property/offences, retrospectively inflicts penal consequences (Articles 14, 20, 300A).
  • s.8: no clear standard (civil or criminal?), no defined nexus test, no mens rea/knowledge requirement; s.8(4) permits dispossession of immovables before guilt is concluded — unconstitutional given confiscation’s “penal” character.
  • ss.23/24: the inter-connected-transactions presumption and the shifted burden punish the innocent; s.24 at most applies at a s.3 criminal trial and only to the accused.
  • Authorities: US void-for-vagueness line (Thornhill; Harriss; Papachristou; Grayned); Canadian Nova Scotia Pharmaceutical; Indian Romesh Thapar, A.K. Roy, Kartar Singh, Khemka & Co., A.X. Roy; Vienna Convention Art. 3; Palermo Convention (UNGA Res. 55/25).

Arguments of the respondents (ED/Union)

  • The Act implements the 1988 Vienna Convention, FATF recommendations and the 1998 Political Declaration under Article 253; it is a special, self-contained code with non-obstante override (s.71).
  • ML is a “complex web” through placement/layering/integration and five directional flows; proceeds leave the original malfeasant’s hands by design; attachment/confiscation run with the property, not the conviction.
  • Attachment/confiscation are civil, preventive proceedings, not punishment: Article 20(1) is not attracted; knowledge/mens rea is irrelevant to in-rem confiscation (line of Amratlal Prajivandas on SAFEMA; Heena Kausar under NDPS); the safeguards (material, recorded reasons, sealed envelope, 30-day complaint, 30-day notice, hearing, reasoned order, s.5(4) possession, Tribunal and High Court appeals) meet Articles 14/21/300A.
  • The second proviso is clarificatory/remedial (preventive, not punitive) and can apply to existing attachments; s.23 is a rebuttable rule of evidence (Izhar Ahmad; Best on Evidence) reflecting the reality of layered transactions; s.24 places the source-of-means burden on the accused who alone knows his income (s.106 Evidence Act-style).
  • Economic legislation attracts diffused/deferential scrutiny (R.K. Garg v. UOI, (1981) 4 SCC 675); civil/administrative statutes may be drafted with less precision than criminal ones.
Decision — all six issues answered against the petitioners; writs dismissed (merits left to statutory appeals)

(i) Issue A: property of any person — charged or not — is attachable/confiscatable if it is proceeds of crime/involved in laundering; s.2(u) is valid (third parties get notices/hearing under the s.8 provisos). (ii) Issue B: the second proviso applies even to property acquired before 6-3-2009; it is clarificatory, procedural and preventive — not penal — so no unconstitutional retrospectivity. (iii) Issues C & D: s.8 is not vague; it fixes the trigger, notice, source-of-income disclosure, hearing, reasoned confirmation and the nexus test; mens rea/knowledge is not required for civil confiscation; s.8(4) possession orders are constitutionally valid subject to the balance-safeguards. (iv) Issue E: s.23 is a valid rebuttable presumption (presumptio pro tantum) — a rule of evidence for inter-connected transactions (paras 111–124). (v) Issue F: s.24 applies to the accused both at the s.3 trial and in Chapter III proceedings; it never places the burden on a non-accused — against a non-accused connected through inter-connected transactions, the s.23 presumption (not s.24) operates.

Principles evolved
  1. In-rem character: attachment and confiscation attach to tainted property whoever holds it; criminal conviction is not the foundation of civil confiscation; third-party/benami holdings are reached, with natural-justice notices.
  2. No mens rea for the property track; the s.8(1) notice obliges the holder to disclose the sources of income, earnings or assets — the practical reverse onus.
  3. Two independent parallel actions (counter para 12(F)): s.4 prosecution after complaint/trial, and s.5–s.8 attachment/confiscation; neither is barred by the other — no double jeopardy against civil confiscation.
  4. Procedural/amending provisos aiding prevention (pre-police-report attachment) are clarificatory and retrospective in operation because they do not punish.
  5. The legislative definitions in ss.2(p)/2(u)/2(v) interlock to cover concealment, possession, layering and integration; s.23 presumption — rebuttable; s.24 burden — on the accused only.
  6. Economic legislation enjoys constitutionality-deference; treaties (Vienna/Palermo) guide construction of ambiguous or void municipal law but do not override it.
  7. “Bitter medicine is not bad medicine”: stringent remedies are proportionate where the mischief (placement-layering-integration across jurisdictions) is sophisticated and sovereignty-threatening.
Superseded details (exam caveat): Ramaraju was decided when PAO was for 150 days, the s.5(1) body referred to a person “charged” of the scheduled offence, and the second proviso referenced “clause (b)”. The 2013 substitution recast s.5 (180 days; “possession of proceeds”) and s.8 (Special Court confiscation under 2015; 365-day confirmation from 2019); the second-proviso reference was corrected in 2015. Vijay Madanlal (2022) re-validated the framework on the amended text; Pankaj Bansal (2024) tightened the arrest, not the attachment, track. Always cite Ramaraju for validity principles, not current time-limits.

DU Case File 14 — Vijay Madanlal Choudhary & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.

Case 14 · DU Case Material pp. 205–255 · Supreme Court of India

Vijay Madanlal Choudhary & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.

SLP(Crl.) No. 4634 of 2014 and batch · decided 27 July 2022 · (2022) SCC OnLine SC 950 · A.M. Khanwilkar, Dinesh Maheshwari and C.T. Ravi Kumar, JJ. · the “PMLA Magna Carta”: “and”=“or”; standalone/continuing offence; pre-FIR attachment and search; s.8(4) possession; ss.24/45 valid; ECIR internal; ED officers not police; review petitions part-heard

Facts and procedural setting

  • A batch of writ petitions, SLPs and bail matters (originating from the 2014 Special Director/CBI-type disputes and later High Court bail decisions) challenged (i) the PMLA amendments of 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2019 — several moved as Money Bills; (ii) the ED’s ECIR procedure, summons under s.50, search/seizure under ss.16–18, arrest under s.19, attachment under s.5, adjudication under s.8, the reverse burden under s.24 and the twin bail conditions under amended s.45 — as violating Articles 14, 20 and 21 and the CrPC scheme.
  • The Union’s case (led by the Solicitor General) was built on the FATF Mutual Evaluation process: India’s 2010 MER deficiencies, the commitments remedied by the 2012/2013 amendments (including the then Government’s assurance that Indian courts would read “and” as “or” where necessary, citing Sanjay Dutt), the 8th follow-up, and the 2015/2018/2019 strengthening.

Issues (consolidated)

  1. Must s.3 be read conjunctively — requiring both handling of proceeds AND projection/claiming as untainted — and is the 2019 Explanation an unconstitutional enlargement of the offence?
  2. Is the s.3 offence standalone/independent; can it proceed without registration/chargesheet of the scheduled offence; is it a continuing offence with retrospective reach?
  3. Is the second proviso to s.5 (pre-police-report attachment) valid; are s.5 and s.8(3)/(4) (180-day/365-day regimes; possession before confiscation) constitutional?
  4. Was the 2019 deletion of the ss.17/18 provisos (search without predicate FIR/report) valid; are the inbuilt safeguards enough?
  5. Is s.24 reverse burden constitutional, and on what foundational facts?
  6. Is the re-enacted s.45 twin-conditions bail regime (2018) valid after Nikesh Tarachand Shah; does it cover anticipatory bail?
  7. Must the ED register and supply an FIR-like ECIR; are s.50 statements (confessions to ED officers) barred by s.25 Evidence Act?
  8. Money Bill challenge to the amendments.

Arguments of the private parties (Kapil Sibal, Abhishek Singhvi, and other senior counsel)

  • The ECIR process is opaque and arbitrary: an accused can be summoned and his statements used without ever knowing the allegations; CrPC Chapter XII safeguards (registration, s.41A notice, witness/accused distinction under ss.41A/160) are ignored; there is no judicial check at initiation (Art. 14/21).
  • s.3 requires a definitive determination and prima facie quantification that proceeds were actually generated and projected/claimed as untainted; “and” cannot be read as “or”; the 2019 Explanation cannot enlarge a penal section (void as larger punishment/retrospective); Vienna Convention Art. 3 targets conversion/transfer for concealment, not mere possession/use.
  • The second proviso to s.5 de-links attachment from the scheduled offence and is invoked even before the predicate is registered — enabling attachment on suspicion; s.8(4) dispossesses immovable property before trial; the 365-day continuation is disproportionate.
  • Deletion of the ss.17/18 provisos permits warrantless, FIR-less searches by revenue officers, unlike CrPC s.165 safeguards (which bind even police stations); s.50 coerces self-incriminating statements.
  • s.24 forces the accused to prove innocence; s.45 twin conditions amount to incarceration before conviction, contrary to Nikesh Tarachand Shah, the bail trinity (Sanjay Chandra, Mhetre, Siddharam) and Article 21; anticipatory bail is rendered illusory.
  • The ED registers ECIRs mechanically on receipt of FIRs, collapsing the distinct offences; Money Bill amendments violate Articles 110/107.

Arguments of the Union / respondents

  • PMLA is a hybrid special statute — regulatory, preventive (civil confiscation) and penal — implementing FATF/Vienna/Palermo/UNCAC obligations under Article 253; economic offences are a class apart (Mohanlal Jitamalji Porwal; Kartar Singh on TADA; Usmanbhai under NDPS); economic legislation merits deferential scrutiny (R.K. Garg).
  • “And” between the activities is disjunctive in effect — possession by one person and projection by another would otherwise both escape (the Sanjay Dutt logic); the Explanation is clarificatory; ML is independent of the predicate trial yet dependent on existence of scheduled criminality; it is a continuing offence as long as proceeds are enjoyed.
  • Attachment is a civil, preventive, provisional measure, not punishment (Article 20 inapplicable): safeguards include rank (Director/Deputy Director), material in possession, written reasons, sealed-envelope to the three-member AA (chaired by a District-Judge-qualified member), 30-day complaint, 30-day notice, reasoned confirmation, Tribunal (45-day appeal, 6-month target) and High Court appeal; s.5(4) preserves enjoyment; the second proviso is paired with s.66(2) contemporaneous intimation to the predicate agency.
  • Search powers exceed CrPC protection in stringency: only senior officers may authorise, written reasons, sealed-envelope transmission, 30-day retention application with notice (ss.17(4), 20), witnesses and the s.18 Magistrate/Gazetted Officer route; vexatious search is punishable (s.62).
  • s.24 mirrors s.106 Evidence Act (facts within special knowledge), NDPS s.35/54 and Customs precedents; it triggers only after foundational facts (scheduled offence, proceeds, linkage) and is rebuttable; s.23 is rebuttable inter-connected-transactions presumption.
  • s.45’s twin conditions cured the two Nikesh defects by covering all offences under the Act; vulnerable classes and <₹1 crore cases are carved out; conditions are not a complete bar; statistics demonstrated calibrated use (≈2,086 ECIRs in five years vs 33 lakh FIRs; ₹19,111.20 cr attached of ₹22,585.83 cr fraud; terror/Naxal data).
  • The ECIR is an internal document (like a police case-diary/ad hoc report); non-supply is cured by supply of grounds of arrest and the Special Court’s remand scrutiny; ED officers are not police (no FIR powers; cognizance on complaint), so s.25 Evidence Act does not bar s.50 statements.
Decision — the 2013–2019 amendments and ED procedure upheld; writs/disposed of in terms of the declaration

(1) s.3 — “and” = “or”: each activity (concealment, possession, acquisition, use, projection, claiming) is independently punishable; the possessor and the projector cannot both escape; the 2019 Explanation is clarificatory, not retroactive punishment. (2) Standalone but dependent + continuing: s.3 is an independent offence triable irrespective of the fate/pace of the predicate trial, and continues until enjoyment ceases; but authorities cannot act on a notional basis — scheduled criminality must be registered/enquired/tried, and final discharge/acquittal/quashing destroys the ML case and linked property claims (the ratio later applied in Parvathi Kollur). (3) s.5: the second proviso permits immediate attachment before the s.173 report with contemporaneous s.66(2) information to the predicate agency; the 2015 textual correction was valid; civil character upheld. (4) s.8: the AA regime valid; under s.8(4), possession before confiscation is the exception, not the rule, with the s.5(4) enjoyment principle extended until confiscation wherever practicable. (5) ss.17/18: deletion of the FIR/report provisos valid; inbuilt safeguards adequate (Baldev Singh on searches). (6) s.24: valid after the three foundational facts; “shall” for the charged accused, “may” for others; standards differ (preponderance before AA; BRD before Special Court). (7) s.45: the 2018 twin conditions are constitutional for all PMLA offences, including at the anticipatory-bail stage; not an absolute bar; Special Court remains satisfied on reasonable grounds. (8) ECIR: internal, non-statutory, need not be supplied; grounds of arrest must be supplied and the Special Court may peruse the material at remand. (9) s.50 statements: ED officers are not police officers; statements are not excluded by s.25 Evidence Act, subject to voluntariness safeguards. The Money Bill question was left to the pending larger-benatch reference (Rojer Mathew line).

Principles evolved (exam-standard list)
  1. Hybrid statute doctrine: preventive-civil confiscation and penal prosecution coexist; each track carries its own forum, standard and safeguards; CrPC applies only insofar as consistent (ss.46, 65, 71).
  2. Disjunctive activities: one or more of the six s.3 activities completes the offence — projection as untainted is not essential; conspiracy/assistance/attempt “directly or indirectly” suffices.
  3. Backbone limitation: no PML action on a notional scheduled offence; final failure of the predicate for the accused extinguishes ML against him and his property-linked claimants (para 187(d)).
  4. Pre-FIR powers: attachment (s.5 second proviso) and search (ss.17/18 post-2019) may precede the predicate report, paired with sealed-envelope oversight and s.66(2) intimation.
  5. Reverse burden sequencing: foundational facts first — then presumption; civil preponderance vs criminal BRD.
  6. Liberty baseline: ECIR need not be supplied but grounds of arrest must be; 24-hour production; Special Court independently examines remand material; non-bailable status valid with carve-outs.
  7. Soft-state/sovereignty rationale: ML funds terror, Naxalism, corruption and capital flight; stringent laws implement Directive Principles (Arts. 38, 39) and international obligations.
  8. Bail conditions mirror/sit beside TADA, NDPS, Companies Act s.447-type special laws; “bail is rule, jail exception” operates through the statutory conditions, not outside them.

DU Case File 15 — Parvathi Kollur & Anr. v. State by Directorate of Enforcement

Case 15 · DU Case Material pp. 256–257 · Supreme Court of India

Parvathi Kollur & Anr. v. State by Directorate of Enforcement

SLP(Crl.) No. 4258 of 2021 · decided 2022 · Dinesh Maheshwari and Krishna Murari, JJ. · acquittal in the predicate offence destroys the money-laundering case — the “backbone” rule; family members discharged

Facts

  • Accused No.1, a Deputy Revenue Officer, was alleged to have amassed assets disproportionate to his known sources to the tune of ₹42,25,859; Lokayukta Police registered a case under ss.13(1)(e) r/w 13(2), PC Act, 1988.
  • During the trial, the ED registered a PMLA case against accused No.1 and his wife (Parvathi Kollur) and son (the appellants), and filed a complaint on 8-6-2016 before the Special Court under s.3.
  • The Special Judge (Lokayukta) acquitted accused No.1, finding the prosecution evidence insufficient. Accused No.1 and the appellants then sought discharge in the PMLA case under s.277 CrPC; before the application was decided, accused No.1 died on 8-5-2018.
  • By order 4-1-2019, the trial court discharged the appellants, holding that occurrence of a scheduled offence is the basic condition for “proceeds of crime” and its commission is a pre-condition to PML proceedings.
  • The Karnataka High Court (17-12-2020) allowed ED’s revision and set aside discharge, finding the complaint material prima facie sufficient.

Issue

Whether a money-laundering prosecution against family members can survive the acquittal of the sole predicate accused (and his death), when the family members were not even accused of the scheduled offence.

Arguments

  • Appellants: the position is settled by the three-Judge Bench in Vijay Madanlal, para 187(d): ML is dependent on illegal gain from criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence; authorities cannot prosecute on a notional basis; on final discharge/acquittal/quashing, there can be no ML against the person or anyone claiming property through him linked to that scheduled offence.
  • Respondent (ASG): with “all fairness… does not dispute the above position of law”.
Decision

Appeal allowed; the High Court order set aside and the trial court’s discharge order of 4-1-2019 restored; pending applications disposed of. The predicate accused had been acquitted and the appellants were never accused of any scheduled offence — the foundation for s.3 and for “proceeds of crime” did not exist.

Principle evolved

The scheduled offence is the backbone of the money-laundering offence and the property track. A final acquittal/discharge/quashing that negates the scheduled criminality for an accused collapses (i) the s.3 prosecution against him; (ii) prosecutions against persons claiming only through him (e.g. spouse/child recipients); and (iii) attachment/confiscation of property linked to that stated scheduled offence. Distinguish: this concerns final merits-based failure of the predicate; it does not bar PML proceedings where the predicate is pending, where the accused absconds/proceeds under s.8(7), or where the property derives from another independent scheduled offence. Read with Vijay Madanlal para 187(d) (no notional basis) and the current s.8(5)–(7) scheme.

Connected-law note

The discharge application was under s.277 CrPC (trial-stage discharge of an accused absent in pending/abated cases); under the BNSS, 2023 (in force 1-7-2024), the corresponding trial-chapter discharge provisions apply; the ED complaint route is s.44(1)(b) read with s.200 CrPC (→ s.223 BNSS), as used here. Accused No.1’s death would independently attract s.8(7)-type orders for property and abatement of criminal proceedings under s.394 CrPC (→ BNSS equivalent).

DU Case File 16 — Pankaj Bansal v. Union of India & Ors.

Case 16 · DU Case Material pp. 258–275 · Supreme Court of India

Pankaj Bansal & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors.

(2024) 7 SCC 576 · P.V. Sanjay Kumar and A.S. Bopanna, JJ. (judgment by Sanjay Kumar J.) · s.19 arrest: genuine recorded “reason to believe”; evasive answers are no ground; remand cannot cure illegal arrest; written grounds of arrest to be furnished as a matter of course (Form III); colourable exercise by ED condemned; Moin Akhtar Qureshi and Chhagan Bhujbal overruled

Facts and chronology

  • FIR No. 0006 dated 17-4-2023, Anti-Corruption Bureau, Panchkula, under PC Act ss.7, 8, 11, 13 r/w s.120B IPC (→ BNS s.61(2)), named (i) Sudhir Parmar, then Special Judge (CBI/ED), Panchkula; (ii) his nephew Ajay Parmar (Dy. Manager-Legal, M3M); (iii) Roop Bansal (M3M promoter); and unknown persons — alleged judicial favouritism towards IREO’s Lalit Goyal and the M3M owners.
  • First ECIR GNZO/10/2021 (15-6-2021): IREO Group/Lalit Goyal, based on 13 (later 30) home-buyer FIRs; M3M and the Bansals were not named. Prosecution Complaint COMA/01/2022 filed 14-1-2022 against seven accused — again without them.
  • ED summons to M3M India (12-5-2023); raids/seizures on 1-6-2023; Roop Bansal arrested 8-6-2023 in the first ECIR.
  • Pankaj and Basant Bansal obtained interim anticipatory bail protection from the Delhi High Court on 9-6-2023 in respect of the first ECIR; the High Court recorded that they were neither named nor summoned nor implicated in any scheduled offence. (ED’s SLPs against that order were pending.)
  • Second ECIR GNZO/17/2023 recorded on 13-6-2023 on the basis of the 17-4-2023 FIR — four days after the Delhi High Court protection. Yet at 6:15 p.m. on 13-6-2023, summons (ostensibly under the first ECIR) called both men to Rajokri ED office at 11:00 a.m. on 14-6-2023.
  • On 14 June, while complying, Pankaj was served a fresh summons at 4:52 p.m. for 5:00 p.m. before another IO (second ECIR); Basant Bansal was arrested at 6:00 p.m. and Pankaj Bansal at 10:30 p.m. under s.19(1).
  • Produced at Panchkula, the Vacation Judge remanded them to 5 days’ ED custody on 15-6-2023 without recording independent satisfaction with the s.19 grounds; extended 20-6-2023; judicial custody from 26-6-2023. The Punjab & Haryana High Court dismissed their writs (20/26-7-2023), holding s.19 validity was settled by Vijay Madanlal despite the pending review.
  • ED’s stated arrest reasons: the Bansals were evasive/non-cooperative in questioning under s.50 summons and did not divulge information; written grounds were only read out/allowed to be read (Basant allegedly refused signature).

Issues

  1. What must s.19(1) record before arrest, and may non-cooperation/evasive answers justify arrest?
  2. Must the Magistrate/Special Court independently verify s.19 compliance before remand; can remand validate an unlawful arrest?
  3. Must the written grounds of arrest be physically furnished to the arrestee (as against merely read out), and what is the status of the contrary High Court rulings?
  4. Were these arrests a colourable exercise/fraud on power?

Arguments (resumed)

  • Arrestees: s.19 requires a genuine, pre-arrest, recorded reason to believe of guilt on material; they were summoned under one ECIR and arrested under another — a retaliatory response to anticipatory bail; the second ECIR (recorded months after the FIR) was created to bypass the Delhi High Court and was suppressed there; the remand court applied no mind; mere evasion/non-confession cannot ground arrest (Article 20(3)); oral reading of grounds violates Article 22(1) and the Arrest Rules.
  • ED/Union: s.19 was upheld in Vijay Madanlal; the officers had formed satisfaction of non-cooperation and evasion; Form III was followed and grounds were explained; the remand orders were proper; Moin Akhtar Qureshi and Chhagan Bhujbal held that written service is not mandatory.
Decision — appeals allowed; arrest/remand orders set aside; release forthwith unless required in another case

(a) Magistrate’s duty (paras 15–18): following V. Senthil Balaji v. State, 7-8-2023, the court remanding an ED arrestee under s.167 CrPC must independently peruse the s.19(1) recorded reasons and verify compliance; s.167 gives effect to s.19; non-compliance enures to the benefit of the arrestee and may trigger s.62 PMLA action. Per In re Madhu Limaye, (1969) 1 SCC 292 (3-Judge Bench), a remand order cannot cure an arrest violating Article 22(1). The Panchkula remand orders reflected “total failure” of this duty.

(b) Evasion is not a ground (paras 24–25): s.19 requires reason to believe the person is guilty of an offence, formed within the statutory time; mere non-cooperation with a s.50 summons is insufficient; there is no duty to confess — Article 20(3), Santosh Kumar Mehmooda (Fafat) v. State of Maharashtra, (2017) 9 SCC 714 (custodial interrogation is not for obtaining confession; non-confession is not non-cooperation); absence during searches, when presence was not insisted on, cannot be held against the person. Statutory authorities must act within the four corners of the statute (Devinder Singh v. State of Punjab, (2008) 1 SCC 728).

(c) Written grounds mandatory (paras 26–35): s.19 uses “inform”; the Arrest Rules, 2005, Rule 6 and Form III prescribe a signed arrest order addressed to the arrestee recording the grounds. Henceforth a copy of the written grounds must be furnished as a matter of course and without exception, under acknowledgment. Two reasons: (i) avoiding swearing contests between the arrestee and officer (the Basant Bansal signature dispute proved the point; non-compliance mandates release per Senthil Balaji); (ii) enabling legal advice and an effective s.45 bail application — a newly arrested person is not in a calm state and cannot remember voluminous grounds read aloud (the Senthil Balaji grounds ran six pages). Sensitive material may be redacted; grounds are personal to the arrestee and ordinarily risk no leakage. Moin Akhtar Qureshi v. UOI (Del) and Chhagan Chandrakant Bhujbal v. UOI (Bom) are overruled as not laying down the correct law.

(d) Colourable exercise (paras 20–24): though mala fides was not expressly pleaded, the chronology showed the ED “summoning them on one pretext and arresting them on another”; the second ECIR was recorded immediately after interim protection despite the FIR being two months old, without credible time for a genuine opinion, and its existence was suppressed from the Delhi High Court; the ED’s Written Submissions claimed May 2023 inquiries absent from its replies — conduct that “reeks of arbitrary exercise of power”. Applying State of Punjab v. Gurdial Singh, (1980) 2 SCC 471; Collector, Allahabad v. Raja Ram Jaiswal, (1985) 3 SCC 1; Ravi Yashwant Bhoir v. Collector, (2012) 4 SCC 407, the arrests were fraud on power — power used for an end foreign to the statute.

Principles evolved (post-2024 arrest checklist)
  1. Pre-arrest material & recorded satisfaction: the authorised officer must have material and must genuinely record reason to believe of guilt (not mere suspicion/non-cooperation) before arrest; the timing and quality of that opinion are justiciable.
  2. No arrest for silence: evasive/unsatisfactory answers, refusal to confess, or absence at a search cannot constitute s.19 grounds; Article 20(3) applies to s.50 questioning.
  3. Independent magisterial verification: the remand court must peruse the s.19 order and grounds itself; mechanical remand is invalid; remand cannot resurrect an illegal arrest.
  4. Written grounds as of course: Form III written grounds served under acknowledgment (redaction allowed); non-service mandates release.
  5. Anti-circumvention: summons under one ECIR cannot be used to arrest under a freshly created ECIR designed to defeat anticipatory-bail protection; suppression from the issuing High Court is itself a fraud on power.
  6. Accountability: non-compliance enures to the arrestee’s benefit and may attract s.62 (vexatious search/arrest) and s.63/64 processes.
Relationship with Vijay Madanlal (do not confuse in exams): Pankaj Bansal did not strike down s.19 (upheld in Vijay) or hold the ECIR must be supplied (Vijay held it need not be). It read s.19 down: the grounds of arrest — distinct from the ECIR — must be furnished in writing, and the subjective satisfaction must be genuine and non-colourable. Senthil Balaji (where written grounds were supplied and custody was upheld on facts) is the contrasting application. Remand mechanics now operate under s.187 BNSS (formerly s.167 CrPC), including staggered police custody and default-bail guarantees.

DU Previous Year Questions with Best Model Answers

2015 — Q.7: Describe “Money Laundering” with its various steps and methods. In what circumstances can a person be arrested under the PMLA, 2002?
Answer strategy: Part A meaning + steps/methods (Parts I–II above); Part B s.19 arrest (Parts VI above). Model answer:

1. Meaning. Money-laundering is the process by which the illicit origin of criminal proceeds is disguised so that the proceeds appear legitimate: “concealing or disguising the identity of illegally obtained proceeds so that they appear to have originated from legitimate sources” (INTERPOL). The 18th Standing Committee Report describes “cleansing of money” from extortion, drugs and gun-running. Under the PMLA, s.2(1)(p) borrows the s.3 definition: whosoever directly or indirectly attempts to indulge, knowingly assists, knowingly is a party, or is actually involved in any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime, including its concealment, possession, acquisition, use and projection/claiming as untainted property, commits the offence. The 2019 Explanation lists six disjunctive activities and declares it a continuing offence; Vijay Madanlal read “and” as “or”.

2. Steps. Placement (dirty cash entering the financial system — deposits, cash businesses, gold); layering (multiple transfers through accounts/shell companies/jurisdictions to sever the trail — s.23 inter-connected-transactions presumption); integration (clean-looking money returning as assets — real estate, shares, businesses). Flows: domestic, returning (round-tripping), inbound, outbound (capital flight), flow-through (Ramaraju para 12(C)).

3. Methods. Shell/paper companies and fake invoicing; hawala/hundi; round-tripping through tax havens; smurfing/structuring; trade-based ML (over/under-invoicing); cash smuggling; real estate/benami; gold/precious stones; casinos/lotteries; insurance and penny stocks/LTCG abuse; façade businesses; loans from friends/relatives; professional enablers (CAs/CSs notified 2023) and virtual digital assets (notified 2023).

4. Punishment. s.4: 3–7 years RI + fine; up to 10 years for NDPS-related proceeds (para 2 Part A).

5. Arrest — s.19. The Director, Deputy/Assistant Director or Central-Government-authorised officer may arrest where, on material in his possession, he has recorded-in-writing reason to believe the person is guilty of an offence punishable under the Act; grounds must be supplied as soon as may be — per Pankaj Bansal, written grounds (Form III) as of course and without exception, with redaction permitted. The arrest order/material goes to the AA in a sealed envelope (s.19(2)); the person is produced before the Special Court/Judicial or Metropolitan Magistrate within 24 hours (journey excluded), and remand follows s.167 CrPC (→ s.187 BNSS) after independent verification (Senthil Balaji; Pankaj Bansal). Mere non-cooperation, evasive answers, non-confession or absence during search are not grounds (Article 20(3); Santosh Kumar Mehmooda); colourable arrests (summoned under one ECIR, arrested under another) are void; vexatious arrest is punishable under s.62. All offences are cognizable and non-bailable (s.45 Explanation, 2019).

2015 — Q.8(c) / 2017 Q.7(a) / Unknown year Q.5(a): Explain in detail “Adjudication” under the PMLA, 2002 (and the procedure under s.8)

Concept: Adjudication is the civil, in-rem process by which the three-member Adjudicating Authority (s.6: Chairperson + two members with law/administration/finance/accountancy experience; law member District-Judge-qualified; natural justice; civil-court powers under s.11) determines whether attached/seized property is “involved in money-laundering” and confirms its attachment for confiscation.

Procedure (s.8):

  1. Initiation (s.8(1)): on receipt of the Director’s complaint under s.5(5) (filed within 30 days of PAO) or applications under s.17(4)/s.18(10), the AA, on reason to believe that an s.3 offence was committed or the person holds proceeds, issues a show-cause notice of not less than 30 days requiring the person to indicate the sources of income, earnings or assets by which the property was acquired, his evidence and particulars, and to show cause why it should not be declared involved in ML and confiscated. Benamidar and co-holders must also be served (two provisos).
  2. Hearing (s.8(2)): after the reply, hearing both sides and considering all material, the AA records a reasoned finding; third-party claimants get a hearing (proviso).
  3. Confirmation (s.8(3)): if involved, the AA confirms attachment/retention/freeze, which continues during investigation up to 365 days and for the pendency of court proceedings (Indian/foreign), and becomes final on confiscation; court-stay time excluded.
  4. Possession (s.8(4)): Director takes possession in the prescribed manner; per Vijay Madanlal pre-confiscation possession is the exception, not the rule (s.5(4) enjoyment principle).
  5. Confiscation/release (ss.8(5)–(7)): now by the Special Court — (5) on conviction, confiscation to the Centre; (6) acquittal/non-involvement → release; (7) death, proclaimed offender or inconclusive trial → appropriate order on the material.
  6. Restoration (s.8(8)): good-faith victims with quantifiable loss may be restored.

Vesting (s.9): confiscated property vests absolutely in the Central Government free of encumbrances; sham encumbrances/leases are declared void; the s.10 Administrator (Joint-Secretary rank) receives, manages and disposes.

Appeals: to the Appellate Tribunal (s.26) within 45 days (sufficient cause condonable; 6-month disposal target), and to the High Court under s.42 within 60 (+60 condonable) days on questions of law or fact.

Burden/standard: preponderance of probabilities; ss.22/23 record/inter-connected transaction presumptions; s.24 mandatory presumption against the accused; mens rea is not required for civil confiscation (Ramaraju Issues C–F; Vijay Madanlal safeguards).

2017 — Q.7(b): Discuss the law relating to search and seizure, including search of persons, under the PMLA, 2002

Survey (s.16): on material and recorded reason to believe that an s.3 offence has been committed, the authority may enter premises within/allocated area (including declared record-keeping places), inspect records, verify proceeds, obtain information, mark records, inventory property and record statements; reasons/material go to the AA in a sealed envelope; s.17(3) allows an immediate warrant-free search after survey where evidence may be concealed/tampered.

Search and seizure (s.17): the Director or an officer not below Deputy Director, on information and recorded reason to believe that a person committed ML, holds proceeds, records or crime-related property, may authorise a subordinate to enter/search any building, place, vessel, vehicle or aircraft; break open locks; seize; mark/inventory; and examine on oath. s.17(1A): freezing where seizure is impracticable, with service of the order. s.17(2): reasons/material to the AA immediately in a sealed envelope. s.17(4): retention application within 30 days; s.20 caps retention/freeze at 180 days unless the AA extends on prima facie satisfaction. The proviso requiring a predicate FIR/report was deleted w.e.f. 1-8-2019; Vijay Madanlal upheld deletion given rank safeguards, sealed-envelope oversight and ss.62/20 accountability.

Search of persons (s.18): a Central-authorised authority with recorded reason to believe that records/proceeds are secreted about a person may search and seize; reasons to the AA (s.18(2)); on demand, the person must, within 24 hours (journey excluded), be taken before a superior Gazetted Officer or Magistrate, who discharges him if no reasonable ground appears (ss.18(3)–(5)); search before two or more independent witnesses, with a signed inventory (ss.18(6)–(7)); no female searched except by a female (s.18(8)); statement of the person searched recorded (s.18(9)); retention application within 30 days (s.18(10)). The FIR-chargesheet proviso was also deleted in 2019.

Relationship with the CrPC/BNSS: the PMLA is a self-contained code (s.71) but CrPC applies save inconsistency (ss.46, 65): occupant rights, search witnesses, AV-recording (BNSS s.185), and BNSS forensic mandates supplement; vexatious search is an offence under s.62 (up to 2 years/₹50,000).

Unknown year — Q.6(a): Explain in detail the law relating to attachment of property involved in money-laundering

Basis: s.5, Chapter III. The Director or authorised officer not below Deputy Director rank, on material in possession and written reason to believe that (a) a person holds proceeds of crime, and (b) those proceeds are likely to be concealed, transferred or dealt with so as to frustrate confiscation, may by written order provisionally attach the property for up to 180 days in the prescribed manner. Property of any person, including third parties/companies, is covered (Ramaraju Issue A).

Provisos: first — ordinarily a s.173 CrPC report (→ s.193 BNSS) or a cognizance complaint must have been made for the scheduled offence (or equivalent foreign process); second — immediate attachment is permitted even before that report where non-attachment would frustrate proceedings under the Act, with contemporaneous information to the predicate agency under s.66(2) (Vijay Madanlal upheld); third (2018) — High Court stay periods plus 30 days after vacation are excluded from the 180 days.

Procedure: s.5(2) sealed-envelope transmission to AA immediately; s.5(3) lapse at 180 days or on the s.8(3) order, whichever earlier; s.5(4) the person interested may continue enjoying attached immovable property; s.5(5) complaint to AA within 30 days; then the s.8 process, Special Court confiscation (s.8(5)–(7)), s.9 absolute vesting free of sham encumbrances, s.10 Administrator; appeals ss.26/42. Freezing of property that cannot be seized is governed by s.17(1A) and retention by s.20 (180 days + AA extension; 90-day withholding for appeals).

Character: attachment is civil, preventive and provisional — not punishment; no mens rea is required; the 2009 second proviso was held clarificatory and retrospective in operation by Ramaraju (Issue B), and the amended regime was re-validated in Vijay Madanlal.

Unknown year — Q.7(a): What are the obligations of banking companies, financial institutions and intermediaries under the PMLA, 2002?

Chapter IV (ss.11A–15) implements the FATF Recommendations and Basel principles through “reporting entities” (s.2(1)(wa)): banking companies, financial institutions, intermediaries, and persons carrying on designated businesses/professions (s.2(1)(sa): casinos, IG of Registration, real-estate agents, precious-metal/stone dealers, safekeeping services; plus CAs/CSs/CWAs in specified financial transactions and VDA service providers notified on 3-5-2023).

  1. Identity verification (s.11A): verify clients and beneficial owners — Aadhaar authentication for banks, offline Aadhaar, passport or OVD; voluntary choice; no denial for want of Aadhaar; no storage of core biometrics.
  2. Record-keeping (s.12): maintain records reconstructing every transaction; furnish prescribed transaction information (CTR/STR/NTR via FIU-IND); keep identity documents and account files; confidentiality; retention of transaction records 5 years and of identity records 5 years after the relationship ends/account closes.
  3. Access (s.12A): furnish any record/additional information the Director calls for, in the specified time; confidentiality otherwise.
  4. Enhanced due diligence (s.12AA): before specified high-value cash/forex/import/remittance or high-risk/terror-financing transactions — enhanced identity verification, examination of ownership/financial position and source of funds, recording purpose; non-compliant client cannot transact; increased monitoring of suspicious relationships.
  5. Enforcement (s.13): the Director may inquire/order independent CA audits (at Central cost), warn, issue instructions/progress-report directions, and impose ₹10,000–₹1,00,000 per failure on the entity, its designated director or employees; appeal to the Tribunal under s.26(2).
  6. Protection (s.14): no civil/criminal liability for good-faith reporting (no “tipping off”); s.15/ss.73 rules (PML Maintenance of Records Rules 2005, amended 2023) prescribe forms; FIU-IND (Egmont member) analyses and disseminates intelligence.

These duties are the preventive limb of the Act and directly police the placement and layering stages.

Unknown year — Q.8(b) (Problem, on Binod Kumar): Illicit wealth amassed by Chief Ministers, unprecedented in scale, invested in property etc. in India and abroad, with no clear allegation of “laundering”; referred to CBI — accused plead ED exclusivity. Decide.

Answer on the basis of Binod Kumar v. State of Jharkhand, (2011) 11 SCC 463.

(1) Two distinct offences/agencies: The PMLA presupposes a scheduled offence (s.2(1)(y); Parts A/B/C) generating proceeds of crime (s.2(1)(u)), which are then handled by concealment/possession/acquisition/use/projection/claiming (s.3). The scheduled offences — here PC Act ss.7, 10, 11, 13(2) r/w 13(1)(d)/(e) and IPC ss.409, 420, 423, 424, 465, 120B → BNS ss.316, 318, 322, 323, 336/339, 61(2) — are investigated by the ordinary agency (State Vigilance, CBI) and tried by the ordinary court (or committed to the Special Court under s.44(1)(c)). Only the s.3 offence is the ED’s exclusive domain, subject to Central authorisation of another agency under s.45(1A).

(2) The plea of ED exclusivity fails: In Binod Kumar, the Supreme Court held that the ED does not investigate IPC/PC Act offences; the CBI’s investigation of the predicates is lawful; the PMLA being a self-contained code does not oust police/CBI jurisdiction over scheduled offences. The CBI/ED written submissions conceded this division, and the Court dismissed the challenge. Where the High Court refers a multi-State/multi-national scam to the CBI (s.156(3) CrPC → BNSS s.173/175 line; inherent powers), the referral is a proper use of supervisory jurisdiction, particularly where 32 companies and overseas investments require expert multi-jurisdictional investigation.

(3) Overlap is no defence: the “area of overlap” between illicit wealth (predicate) and its laundering “cannot be allowed to form a tool in the hands of the accused to scuttle the investigation”; agencies proceed in parallel and share material (s.66(2); s.54 duty to assist). The ED may register an ECIR and attach under s.5 (even pre-FIR via the second proviso) once material of laundering emerges, while the CBI files chargesheet; the Special Court may try connected offences together (ss.43(2), 44(1)(a)).

(4) Decision: the CBI investigation stands; the accused’s objection is rejected (as in Binod Kumar, appeal dismissed); the absence of a clear “laundering” allegation at the preliminary stage does not bar investigation of the scheduled offences, which must be proved first — “you have to first rule that the scheduled offence is committed… then how much money or property was derived… and lastly how it was projected as untainted.” An absconding accused additionally lacks discretionary relief.

2019 — Q.4(a): Whether the CBI has any authority to investigate offences which are the sole domain of the Enforcement Directorate? Discuss with provisions and decided cases.

(1) The ED’s exclusive domain is limited to the s.3 offence. G.S.R. 440(E), 1-7-2005, issued under s.49(1), vests the Director with exclusive powers under ss.5, 8, 16–21, 26(1), 45, 50, 57, 60, 62 and 63; s.45(1A) bars police investigation of “an offence under this Act” without Central authorisation. The s.3 case is cognizable, non-bailable, and tried only by the Special Court on the Director’s written complaint (ss.44–45).

(2) Scheduled offences are not ED’s domain. Nothing empowers the ED to investigate IPC/PC/NDPS/SEBI/Customs predicates; these belong to the police/CBI/NIA/revenue authorities under their own statutes (Binod Kumar; the Court contrasted CBI v. State of Rajasthan (1996) on FERA, where the special enactment itself covered the foreign-exchange offence — unlike predicates under the PMLA).

(3) CBI authority: under the DSPE Act, 1946 (ss.3–6) with Central/State consent/notification, the CBI may investigate scheduled offences within its mandate; Binod Kumar expressly approved CBI investigation of IPC/PC Act charges in the coal scam even while recognising ED exclusivity over s.3. Section 43(2)/s.44(1)(c) permit connected predicate cases to be tried by the Special Court, but trial is not investigation.

(4) Overlap and sharing: parallel proceedings are permissible; s.54 obligates police/customs/tax/RBI/SEBI officers to assist ED; s.66 mandates information-sharing (contemporaneous intimation when the ED attaches before the predicate report). The Central Government may, by general/special order, additionally authorise police/CBI for PML offences (s.45(1A)).

Conclusion: the CBI cannot investigate the s.3 offence absent Central authorisation, but it has full authority — indeed the primary role — over the scheduled predicate offences; the two agencies operate on separate, coordinated tracks.

2019 — Q.6: Discuss the term “money-laundering” and its punishment, with relevant provisions. In what circumstances can a person be arrested under the Act?

Definition. s.2(1)(p) borrows s.3: direct or indirect attempt to indulge, knowing assistance, knowing party-hood, or actual involvement in any process/activity connected with proceeds of crime — concealment, possession, acquisition, use, or projection/claiming as untainted; the 2019 Explanation enumerates the six activities disjunctively and declares the activity continuing until enjoyment of the proceeds ceases. “Proceeds of crime” (s.2(1)(u)) mean any property derived or obtained directly or indirectly by criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence, its value, equivalent property abroad, and property relatable to such criminality (2019 Explanation); “scheduled offence” is in Parts A (no threshold), B (Customs s.132, ≥₹1 crore) and C (cross-border implications, IPC Chapter XVII property crimes, Black Money Act s.51).

Interpretation. In Vijay Madanlal (2022), “and” before “projecting or claiming” is read as “or” (Sanjay Dutt logic); the offence is standalone but dependent on scheduled criminality — no prosecution on a notional basis, and final acquittal/discharge/quashing of the predicate destroys the ML case (Parvathi Kollur, 2022). It is independent and continuing (Mohammad Arif, Orissa HC, 2020).

Punishment (s.4). RI 3–7 years + fine (fine ceiling deleted in 2013); up to 10 years for proceeds relating to para 2 Part A (NDPS offences). Companies are liable under s.70 (2013 Explanations); offences are cognizable and non-bailable (s.45 Explanation, 2019); Special Court sentence + s.8(5) confiscation.

Arrest (s.19). Competent officers (Director, Deputy/Assistant Director or Central-authorised officer) may arrest on the basis of material, recorded reason to believe the person is guilty of an offence under the Act; grounds must be informed as soon as may be — Pankaj Bansal (2024) mandates written Form III grounds furnished as of course and without exception (redaction allowed), independent magisterial verification at remand, and rules out arrest merely for evasive answers/non-cooperation; sealed-envelope transmission (s.19(2)); production within 24 hours before the Special Court/Judicial or Metropolitan Magistrate (s.19(3)); remand under s.167 CrPC → s.187 BNSS; colourable arrests set aside and vexatious arrest punishable under s.62.

Unknown year — Q.8(c): Special Courts and their powers under Chapter VII of the PMLA, 2002
  1. Constitution (s.43): Central Government, in consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court, notifies one or more Courts of Session as Special Courts for areas/cases; a Special Court may also try other offences charged at the same trial (s.43(2)).
  2. Jurisdiction/cognizance (s.44): the s.4 offence and connected scheduled offences are triable there; cognizance is taken only on the authorised authority’s complaint (no police report route), without committal; closure report if no ML is made out (2019); connected predicate cases are committed to it; Sessions-trial procedure applies; its jurisdiction does not depend on predicate-case orders and joint trial is not a “joint trial” (2019 Explanation).
  3. Bail (s.45): cognizable, non-bailable; no release unless the PP was heard and, where opposed, the court finds reasonable grounds for believing the accused not guilty and not likely to commit offences while on bail; carve-outs for persons under 16, women, sick/infirm and laundering below ₹1 crore; cognizance requires the Director’s/authorised officer’s written complaint; s.45(1A) bars unauthorised police investigation; the re-enacted 2018 conditions were upheld in Vijay Madanlal after Nikesh Tarachand Shah struck down the old classification; conditions also govern anticipatory bail; default bail under s.167 CrPC → s.187 BNSS remains available.
  4. Procedure (s.46): CrPC applies save inconsistency; Special Court deemed a Court of Session; prosecutors deemed Public Prosecutors (7-year qualification); Special Public Prosecutors may be appointed.
  5. Appeals/revision (s.47): High Court exercises Chapters XXIX/XXX CrPC powers (appeals/revisions → BNSS ss.415/438); High Court s.439 powers preserved (s.44(2)).
  6. Confiscation: the Special Court itself orders confiscation/release on conclusion of the ML trial (s.8(5)–(8)) and decides foreign-trial requests (ss.58A, 58B, 60(2A)); s.40 deems Members public servants and s.41 bars civil-court jurisdiction.

Case anchors: Gautam Kundu (s.45 binds s.439 bail); P. Chidambaram (rule/jail balancing in economic offences); Vijay Madanlal (constitutional validity of the whole Chapter); Binod Kumar (agency domains).

Likely problem — cross-border: An Indian exporter over-invoices machinery exported to a UAE shell firm; the excess proceeds are parked in Dubai and round-tripped as FDI into his Indian real-estate project. Which provisions apply?

Predicate candidates: Customs Act s.135 (smuggling/prohibition evasion — Part A para 12) and s.132 false declaration (Part B if value ≥ ₹1 crore); IPC cheating/forgery (ss.420/467/471 → BNS ss.318(4)/338/340); Companies Act s.447 (para 29); FEMA breaches (not scheduled, but revenue intelligence feeds the ED). The export-then-round-trip is an offence of cross-border implications under s.2(1)(ra)(ii) → Part C. Layering through the UAE entity is s.3 conduct (concealment/acquisition/use/projection), a continuing offence. Enforcement: ECIR → s.16 survey / s.17 search, freeze (17(1A)) → s.5 attachment of the Indian project and equivalent-value assets (s.2(1)(u)) → s.8 adjudication; s.57 letter of request to the UAE contracting State for evidence and s.60(1) for execution of attachment/freeze/confiscation; s.60(7) asset-sharing; prosecution by Director’s complaint before the Special Court under ss.44/4; bail under s.45; the bank handling the export receipts is a reporting entity with CTR/STR and EDD obligations (ss.11A, 12, 12AA), and the IREO-style real-estate layer engages notified real-estate agents/IG Registration.

4. Answer-writing strategy

  1. Open every answer with the trichotomy (predicate offence → s.3 ML → property track) — it demonstrates mastery of the hybrid statute.
  2. Statutory chain order: definition (2(1)) → offence (s.3, six activities) → punishment (s.4) → power (s.5/16–19/50) → safeguards (written reasons, sealed envelope, witnesses, 24 hours, Form III) → forum (AA → Tribunal → HC; Special Court) → cases.
  3. Give current law + the case-era law (150 vs 180 days; old s.45 vs Nikesh vs 2018 vs Vijay; 90 vs 365 days; pre/post-2019 FIR provisos) — examiners reward noting amendments.
  4. Use case names as hooks: Binod (domains), Ramaraju (validity/property/civil character), Vijay (interpretive charter), Parvathi (backbone), Pankaj (arrest safeguards), Nikesh (bail sequence), Kundu (bail override), Chidambaram (rule/exception), Senthil Balaji (remand duty), Mohammad Arif (stages/continuing).
  5. Draw the diagrams from Figs. 1–9: two-crime structure, placement/layering/integration, attachment clock, arrest sequence, two courts, MLA flow.
  6. Mention connected instruments beside the concept: Rules 2005/FIU for KYC; Form III for arrest; FEOA/Black Money Act for cross-border; Benami/SAFEMA/NDPS VA for property; FEMA for the ED’s other hat; FATF grey-list leverage for “need”.
  7. Renumber post-2024 correctly: CrPC 167 → BNSS 187; CrPC 438 → BNSS 482; CrPC 482 → BNSS 528; IPC 467 → BNS 338; IPC 420 → BNS 318(4); IEA 25 → BSA 23; and cite Nagani for the dynamic-reference principle.
  8. Conclude critically: PMLA = bitter but proportionate medicine for sovereignty-threatening laundering (Ramaraju), now conditioned by Vijay (validity) + Parvathi (predicate backbone) + Pankaj (arrest fairness), with review/Money Bill questions pending — the Act’s constitutionality is “settled but not static”.
