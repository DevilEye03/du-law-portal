Part I — Genesis, the Santhanam Committee and the Scheme of the Act

1. Corruption as a crime — from Kautilya to the special statute

The problem of corruption has ancient roots in Indian governance discourse. Kautilya’s Arthashastra famously compares the corruption of government servants to fish moving under water: “just as it is impossible not to taste the honey or the poison that finds itself at the tip of the tongue, so it is impossible for a government servant not to eat up, at least, a bit of the king’s revenue; just as fish moving under water cannot possibly be found out either as drinking or not drinking water, so government servants employed in the government work cannot be found out while taking money.” The modern law developed in recognisable stages:

IPC, 1860 — Chapter IX (“Of Contempts of the Lawful Authority of Public Servants”) contained the basic bribery and public-servant offences: s.161 (public servant taking gratification other than legal remuneration), ss.162–163 (gratification to influence a public servant / by exercise of personal influence), s.164 (punishment for abetment by public servant of ss.162/163), s.165 (public servant obtaining valuable thing without consideration from a person concerned in proceeding/business), s.165A (abetment), plus ss.166–171 (disobedience, unlawful trade, personation, elections) and the property offences s.409 (criminal breach of trust by public servant). These were widely regarded as inadequate: low sentences, no investigative specialisation, no machinery for attaching ill-gotten wealth.
Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance, 1944 (Ord. 38 of 1944) — a wartime measure under the Government of India Act, 1935, enabling attachment and confiscation of property procured by scheduled offences (including corruption), through District Judges. It survives today and is applied to PC Act cases via s.18A (inserted 2018), with the Special Judge treated as District Judge.
Prevention of Corruption Act, 1947 (Act 2 of 1947) — the first independent anti-corruption statute; s.5 criminalised “criminal misconduct in discharge of official duty” (the disproportionate-assets offence); s.6 required previous sanction; amended in 1952 and again in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee’s recommendations.
Santhanam Committee (1962–1964) — the Report of the Committee on Prevention of Corruption (Chairman: Shri K. Santhanam, MP), submitted in 1964, produced the institutional and doctrinal blueprint (detailed below).
Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (Act 49 of 1988; assent 9-9-1988) — a consolidating and strengthening statute: it widened the public-servant definition, absorbed ss.161–165A IPC, enhanced sentences, created Special Judges, codified the s.20 presumption and tied in attachment of property.
2014 amendment (Act 1 of 2014) — enhanced the sentence for s.13(2) criminal misconduct from a minimum of 1 year to a minimum of four years (maximum raised from 7 to 10 years); added the Lokpal cross-reference.
Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018 (Act 16 of 2018; enforced 26-7-2018) — the most sweeping recast, aligned to the UN Convention against Corruption, 2003: new ss.7, 7A, 8, 9, 10; criminalisation of the bribe giver and of commercial organisations; narrowed s.13 criminal misconduct; inserted s.17A prior-approval, s.18A attachment, the two-year trial target, and an expanded s.19 (sanction even for retired/transferred public servants).

2. The Santhanam Committee (the answer to Syllabus Head A)

The committee was born out of a Lok Sabha debate in June 1962 (Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru was Prime Minister; Lal Bahadur Shastri was Home Minister). Shri K. Santhanam chaired it; the Report was delivered in 1964. Its diagnostic premise (paras 2.1 and the socio-economic offences chapter) was that the IPC was “dominated by the notion that almost all major crimes consist of offences against person, property or State” and failed against white-collar and socio-economic offences by “powerful sections of modern society” — offences more dangerous because the financial stakes are high and they cause “irreparable damage to public morals”.

2.1 The eight categories of socio-economic offences identified by the Committee

  1. Offences calculated to prevent or obstruct the country’s economic development and endanger its economic health;
  2. Evasion and avoidance of taxes lawfully imposed;
  3. Misuse of position by public servants in making contracts, disposal of public property, issue of licences and permits and similar matters;
  4. Delivery by persons/undertakings of goods not according to agreed specifications in fulfilment of public contracts;
  5. Profiteering, black-marketing and hoarding;
  6. Adulteration of foodstuffs and drugs;
  7. Theft and misappropriation of public property and funds; and
  8. Trafficking in licences, permits, etc.

The Committee’s illustrative list of white-collar crime included tax evasion/avoidance, share-pushing and company-administration malpractices, monopolistic controls, usury, under-/over-invoicing, hoarding, profiteering, sub-standard construction and supply contracts, evasion of economic laws, bribery and corruption, and election offences. This list was repeatedly adopted by the Law Commission (29th Report, 1966; 47th Report, 1972).

3. Why the 1988 Act was needed — the “need of the Act”

The Statement of Objects and Reasons (appended to the 1987 Bill, piloted by Minister P. Chidambaram, 20 February 1987) records that the legislation was “intended to make the existing anti-corruption laws more effective by widening their coverage and by strengthening the provisions.” The faculty notes catalogue four drivers:

  1. The Bofors context (1986–87): on 18 March 1986 India signed a ₹1,437-crore contract with Swedish arms maker AB Bofors for 400 155 mm howitzers; on 16 April 1987 Swedish radio alleged that the company had paid bribes to top Indian politicians and defence personnel. The scandal exposed the weakness of the 1947 Act against high-level political corruption and middlemen.
  2. The 1947 Act was too narrow: the 1988 Act replaced the 1947 Act and the scattered IPC provisions, widened “public servant”, incorporated ss.161–165A IPC with enhanced punishment, provided for day-to-day trial, and created Special Judges.
  3. The Santhanam Committee’s reform agenda (above) remained partially unimplemented.
  4. Judicial articulation of purpose: in State of Madhya Pradesh v. Shri Ram Singh (decision 1 February 2000), the Supreme Court explained that Act 49 of 1988 was enacted to deal with “the circumstances, contingencies and shortcomings which were noticed in the working and implementation of the 1947 Act”; it is a social legislation to curb illegal activities of public servants and is to be liberally construed so as to advance its object, and not liberally in favour of the accused. In CBI, Bank Securities & Fraud Cell v. Ramesh Gelli (2016), the Court read the Objects & Reasons with s.46A of the Banking Regulation Act, 1949 to bring private-bank functionaries within “public servant”.
⚖ Connected law — how the 1988 Act re-engineered the earlier statutes
  • s.30 — repeal: the PC Act, 1947 and the Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1952 were repealed, with savings (actions taken under them deemed taken under the 1988 Act; Special Judges under the 1952 Act deemed Special Judges under s.3 — s.26).
  • s.31 (later repealed by the Repealing and Amending Act, 2001): omitted ss.161–165A IPC. Corruption bribery offences have since lived only in the PC Act — which is why the BNS, 2023 does not re-enact them (see the IPC↔BNS table later).
  • s.28: the Act is “in addition to, and not in derogation of, any other law” — a public servant may additionally face IPC/BNS offences (cheating, CBT s.409→BNS s.316(5)), departmental proceedings, PMLA prosecution and asset actions, all in parallel.
  • s.1(2): extends to the whole of India and applies to all citizens of India outside India (extra-territorial reach — relevant to bribery by Indians abroad and the unimplemented UNCAC Art. 16 foreign-bribery obligation).

4. Objects and salient features

From the name of the Act, the Statement of Objects and Reasons and the decisions in Ram Singh and Ramesh Gelli, the objects are: (i) prevention of bribery and corruption among public servants; (ii) wider coverage of who is a “public servant”; (iii) higher, minimum-sentence punishment; (iv) specialised investigation and trial; (v) protection of honest officers through sanction; and (vi), since 2018, punishing the supply side (bribe givers and businesses) and meeting UNCAC obligations.

🌎 Real-world example — why every layer exists: the 2G and CWG scandals and the CPI context The KPMG analysis accompanying the 2018 amendment notes that India sank to a historic low of 95th rank in Transparency International’s Corruption Perception Index in 2011, after the 2G spectrum and Commonwealth Games revelations of 2010 (the Comptroller and Auditor General quantified presumptive 2G losses at ₹1.76 lakh crore; both matters produced CBI prosecutions under the PC Act). Reform moved on two tracks simultaneously: (i) institutional — the CVC Act 2003, the Lokpal Act 2013, and court-monistered CBI investigations; and (ii) statutory — the 2018 amendment criminalising bribe-giving and businesses (demand side alone had failed). Digital public-infra reform (e-procurement, e-tenders, online passport/rail bookings, DBT) attacked the “interface” corruption the 2018 law still punishes; India’s CPI ranking improved to 81st of 180 by 2017, though state/department/municipal-level corruption remains the stated concern. The ecosystem answer for “need of the Act” is therefore: general code (IPC/BNS) → special code (PC Act) → institutions (CBI/ACB/CVC/Lokpal) → asset regime (1944 Ordinance/PMLA) → treaty discipline (UNCAC).

Part A — The Anti-Corruption Machinery (Syllabus Head B): ACB, CVC and CBI

The PC Act is enforced by a three-tier institutional architecture: State Anti-Corruption Bureaus / Vigilance Departments inside each State, the Central Bureau of Investigation (the Delhi Special Police Establishment) for central employees and notified cases, and the Central Vigilance Commission as the apex integrity watchdog which also superintends the CBI in corruption cases. Above them sits the Lokpal (and State Lokayuktas) since 2014.

1. Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB) / State Vigilance

  • Each State maintains a Police Vigilance & Anti-Corruption Bureau (the names vary: ACB, Vigilance Bureau, Lokayukta police) under the State Government’s Home/Vigilance department, staffed by State police officers and registered under the Police Acts.
  • It registers traps and FIRs under the PC Act against State Government employees and local/State public functionaries, lays traps, conducts searches, investigates and prosecutes before the State’s Special Judge(s). The Telangana ACB trap in K. Shanthamma (Deputy Superintendent of Police, ACB, Hyderabad) is the prescribed-case example of the entire trap workflow.
  • Officers below DSP rank cannot investigate PC Act cases without a Magistrate’s order (s.17); State ACBs operate their own trap-teams with mediators/panch witnesses and chemical tests.

2. Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) and the DSPE Act, 1946

Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946 — key provisions s.3: the Central Government may constitute a special police force (the DSPE) for investigation of notified offences in Union territories; s.4: superintendence of the DSPE vests in the Central Government — but, by virtue of the CVC Act, 2003, superintendence over corruption cases under the PC Act vests in the CVC, while the Director/CBI administration remains with the Government (the Vineet Narain settlement); s.5: the Centre may by notification extend DSPE powers to other areas (railways, States); s.6: no member may exercise powers in a State area (not being a UT or railway area) without the State Government’s consent; s.8: the Centre may specify other offences/officers the DSPE may investigate.
  • Origins: the Special Police Establishment was set up in 1941 (War Department) to check wartime corruption; it became the DSPE under the 1946 Act and was renamed the Central Bureau of Investigation by a Ministry of Home Affairs resolution dated 1 April 1963. The CBI is not created by statute (the DSPE Act never uses the initials “CBI”) — it is the DSPE under its better-known name.
  • Divisions: Anti-Corruption Division (PC Act cases against central public servants, PSUs, nationalised banks); Special Crimes Division (serious/economic crimes on notification/State consent/High Court or Supreme Court orders); Economic Offences Division (bank frauds, cyber, securities); Directorate of Prosecution; Central Forensic Science Laboratory linkages.
  • Consent regime under s.6: States give either general consent (a standing notification, e.g. the Maharashtra consent of 22-2-1989) or case-specific consent. Withdrawal of general consent (West Bengal, Chhattisgarh, Rajasthan, Maharashtra, Kerala, Jharkhand, Mizoram and others did so at various points after 2015) means CBI officers lose police powers within the State for new local cases; pending investigations and cases registered elsewhere whose trail enters the State are unaffected, and the Supreme Court/High Courts can still order a CBI probe without consent (judicial direction overrides s.6).
  • Single Directive → s.6A → s.17A: the “Single Directive” required prior Government sanction to investigate senior officers (Joint Secretary and above). The Supreme Court in Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1998) struck down the executive directive as unsupported by law; Parliament reintroduced statutory prior approval in DSPE s.6A via the CVC Act 2003; the Constitution Bench in Subramanian Swamy v. CBI (2014) struck down s.6A as violative of Article 14; the 2018 amendment then inserted the present, decision-focused s.17A of the PC Act (below).
  • Director’s tenure/selection: post-Vineet Narain and the CVC/DSPE amendments, the CBI Director is appointed by a high-powered committee (Prime Minister, Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha, Chief Justice of India or his nominee) with a fixed two-year tenure.
⚖ Connected law — the uploaded 1949–1952 Enquiry Committee material The Special Police Establishment Enquiry Committee (1949–1952) (the report included in the project materials) examined the working of the DSPE after a decade of operation, reviewed its charter, strength and procedures, and supplied the institutional bridge between the wartime SPE and the permanent national investigating agency that became the CBI — the first formal post-independence stocktaking of specialist anti-corruption policing, preceding the Santhanam Committee by a decade. For the essay on institutions, the sequence is: 1941 SPE → 1946 DSPE Act → 1949–52 Enquiry Committee → 1963 CBI resolution → 1964 CVC (resolution) → 1998 Vineet Narain → 2003 CVC Act → 2013 Lokpal Act → 2018 s.17A.

3. Central Vigilance Commission (CVC) — the CVC Act, 2003

The CVC was created by Government resolution in February 1964 on the Santhanam recommendation and became a statutory body under the Central Vigilance Commission Act, 2003. It consists of a Central Vigilance Commissioner and up to two Vigilance Commissioners, appointed by the President on a committee recommendation (Prime Minister, Home Minister, Leader of the Opposition). 4. Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013

  • Creates the Lokpal at the Centre (Chairperson + up to 8 members; 50% judicial; quota for SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women) with an Inquiry Wing and Prosecution Wing, and mandates State Lokayuktas within 365 days.
  • Jurisdiction reaches the Prime Minister (with safeguards), Ministers, MPs and Groups A–D public servants; entities financed by the Government; and (under amendments) certain recipient entities of foreign contributions.
  • The chapeau to s.19(1) PC Act begins “save as otherwise provided in the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013”: where the Lokpal directs prosecution, the sanction procedure is governed by the Lokpal regime (Lokpal may itself grant or seek sanction through the competent authority).

Part B — Definitions: ‘Public Servant’, ‘Public Duty’ and ‘Undue Advantage’ (Syllabus Head C)

1. “Public servant” — Section 2(c)

Section 2(c) — twelve inclusive categories (i) any person in the service or pay of the Government or remunerated by fees/commission for performance of any public duty;
(ii) any person in the service or pay of a local authority;
(iii) any person in the service or pay of a statutory corporation, Government-aided/controlled/owned body or Government company;
(iv) any Judge, including any person empowered to discharge adjudicatory functions;
(v) any person authorised by a court in connection with administration of justice (liquidator, receiver, commissioner);
(vi) any arbitrator or person to whom a cause/matter is referred for decision/report by a court or competent public authority;
(vii) any person holding office empowering him to prepare electoral rolls or conduct elections;
(viii) any person holding an office by which he is authorised/required to perform any public duty;
(ix) office-bearers of aided registered co-operative societies engaged in agriculture, industry, trade or banking;
(x) chairmen/members/employees of Service Commissions/Boards and selection-committee members for examinations/selections;
(xi) Vice-Chancellors, governing-body members, teachers and employees of Universities, and persons whose services are availed for examinations;
(xii) office-bearers/employees of aided educational, scientific, social, cultural or other institutions.

Explanation 1: persons falling in these categories are public servants whether appointed by the Government or not.
Explanation 2: a person in actual possession of the situation of a public servant is covered, whatever legal defect may exist in his title to hold it.
⚖ Connected law — the general-code definition: IPC s.21 → BNS s.2(28) The PC Act’s s.2(c) is much wider than IPC s.21 (now re-enacted as the definition of “public servant” in clause 2(28) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, in force from 1-7-2024). The IPC/BNS definition uses descriptions (commissioned officers, judges, court officers, officers with revenue/property duties, etc.); the PC Act definition uses relationships to public power (service/pay, aid, office carrying public duty) and, through Explanation 1, reaches office-bearers regardless of Government appointment. Where the PC Act applies it prevails as the special law (s.28 is “in addition to, not in derogation of” other laws); BNS s.2(28) remains relevant for offences charged under the general code.

2. “Public duty” — Section 2(b)

“Public duty” means a duty in the discharge of which the State, the public or the community at large has an interest.
Explanation: “State” includes a corporation established by or under a Central/Provincial/State Act, or an authority or body owned or controlled or aided by the Government, or a Government company (now under the Companies Act, 2013 successor provision).

The definition is the gateway clause: it is the performance (or pretence of performance) of a public duty that makes an office-holder a public servant under s.2(c)(viii)—(xii). The Ramesh Gelli Court read it with the Objects & Reasons and with banking law: a private bank’s directors “perform a public duty for the reason that the activities of the banks have public character” — depositors’ money is held and the bank is regulated “for the welfare of the public”. Public duty also fixes the constitutional s.197/s.218-BNSS analysis (was the act “in discharge of official duty”? — corruption never is, per Kalicharan).

3. “Undue advantage” — Section 2(d) (inserted 2018)

Section 2(d)“Undue advantage” means any gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration.
Explanation: (a) “gratification” is not limited to pecuniary gratifications or to gratifications estimable in money; (b) “legal remuneration” is not restricted to remuneration paid to the public servant but includes all remuneration which he is permitted by the Government or the organisation he serves to receive.
  • This replaces the phrase “valuable thing or pecuniary advantage” and the old wording “gratification other than legal remuneration” in pre-2018 s.7. It covers non-monetary benefits: gifts, foreign trips, jobs for relatives, club memberships, shares, property below market price, sexual favours, medals/referral benefits — anything that satisfies or pleases the recipient.
  • Gratification is not reward: per Madhukar Bhaskarrao Joshi v. State of Maharashtra (2000) 8 SCC 571 (para 12), once payment/acceptance of gratification is proved, the s.20 presumption supplies the “motive or reward” element; “gratification” in that sense means any payment giving satisfaction to the recipient.
  • Legal remuneration is the complete answer when money is received which the officer is actually permitted to receive (e.g. honorarium lawfully allowed, exam-invigilation dues) — that is why an unauthorised private-practice fee, though not “legal remuneration” from the official employer, was still held in Kakkar not to be an illegal gratification for an official act (professional remuneration for a private medical act, not corruption — see Case 10).
🌎 Real-world examples — “undue advantage” beyond cash
  • Below-market flats / gifts: a municipal commissioner allotted himself/relatives prime plots at circle rates far below market value — the difference in value is a non-cash undue advantage; the s.11 (obtaining valuable thing without/inadequate consideration from a person concerned in business) and s.7 offences capture it.
  • Jobs and admissions: a selection-board member securing an appointment for a candidate against donations to a favoured trust — s.2(c)(x) already declares him a public servant; the seat/“recommendation” is the gratification.
  • Foreign junkets and sponsorships: regulators/PSU officials accepting overseas travel and hospitality from vendors bidding for contracts — “gratification not estimable in money” squarely within s.2(d)(a).
  • The trap example: a ration-card applicant being asked ₹5,000 “to process his routine application on time” — this is the statutory Illustration to s.7 itself: even though timely processing is the officer’s duty, the demand is bribery.

Part C — Offences and Penalties: Public Servant and Bribe Giver (Syllabus Head D; Sections 7–14) and Attempt (s.15, Head E)

The 2018 amendment replaced the old ss.7–10 and narrowed s.13. The prescribed cases (Shanthamma, Kakkar, Kalicharan, Chautala) were decided under the old sections 7 and 13(1)(d)/(e), so an exam answer must know both numbering schemes. This Part gives the current law first and a complete old-law comparison table at the end.

1. Section 7 — offence relating to a public servant being bribed (post-2018)

Section 7A public servant who —
(a) obtains or accepts or attempts to obtain from any person an undue advantage, with the intention to perform/cause performance of public duty improperly or dishonestly, or to forbear/cause forbearance, by himself or another public servant; or
(b) obtains/accepts/attempts to obtain an undue advantage as a reward for the improper/dishonest performance or forbearance of a public duty (by himself or another); or
(c) performs or induces another public servant to perform improperly/dishonestly, or to forbear, a public duty in anticipation of or in consequence of accepting an undue advantage,
shall be punished with imprisonment not less than three years, extendable to seven years, and fine.
  • Explanation 1: the obtaining/accepting/attempt to obtain is itself an offence even if the public duty is not, in fact, performed improperly. Illustration (statutory): “A public servant ‘S’ asks a person ‘P’ to give him five thousand rupees to process his routine ration-card application on time. S is guilty under this section.” The duty need not be bent; demanding for doing the duty suffices.
  • Explanation 2: covers obtaining “for himself or another” by abuse of position or personal influence or any corrupt/illegal means; and it is immaterial whether the advantage is received directly or through a third party (middlemen, agents, family members).
  • The three clauses cover the demand, the receipt and the corrupt act respectively; “attempts to obtain” makes even an unsuccessful demand punishable at the same scale.

2. Section 7A — taking undue advantage to influence a public servant by corrupt/illegal means or personal influence

Whoever accepts/obtains/attempts to obtain an undue advantage for himself or another, as motive or reward to induce a public servant (by corrupt/illegal means or exercise of personal influence, through that or another public servant) to perform/forbear a public duty improperly — punishment 3–7 years + fine. This is the “influence peddling / middleman” offence and implements UNCAC Article 18 (trading in influence). The fixer who never meets the official but trades on access is caught even though he holds no office.

3. Section 8 — the bribe giver (supply-side criminalisation)

Section 8(1)Any person who gives or promises to give an undue advantage to another, with intention (i) to induce a public servant to perform public duty improperly, or (ii) to reward such improper performance — imprisonment up to 7 years or fine or both.
Proviso 1 (coerced bribe-giver): the section does not apply where a person was compelled to give the undue advantage;
Proviso 2 (7-day report): the compelled person must report the matter to a law-enforcement/investigating agency within seven days of giving the advantage;
Proviso 3: where the offence is committed by a commercial organisation, it is punished with fine.
(2): no liability for a person who, after informing the authorities, gives/promises an advantage to assist an investigation (legitimate trap/sting cooperation).
Explanation: immaterial whether the recipient is the same person who performs/was to perform the duty, and whether given directly or through a third party.
Statutory illustration: P gives S ₹10,000 to secure a licence over other bidders — P is guilty.
Doctrine note: pre-2018, the bribe giver was primarily caught as an abettor under s.12 and could be a witness; old s.24 protected a bribe giver from prosecution if he made a statement (immunity). Section 24 was omitted in 2018 and replaced by the narrow compelled-giver + seven-day-report safe harbour — deliberate reporting, not after-the-fact cooperation, now protects the harassed citizen.

4. Sections 9–10 — commercial organisations and persons in charge

  • s.9(1): a commercial organisation (Indian-incorporated body doing business anywhere; foreign body doing business/part-business in India; Indian or foreign partnership/association — s.9(3)(a)) is punishable with fine if a person associated with it (employee, agent, subsidiary — Explanations 1–3; an employee is presumed associated unless contrary proved) gives or promises an undue advantage to a public servant intending to (a) obtain/retain business for the organisation or (b) obtain/retain an advantage in the conduct of business. Defence: the organisation proves it had adequate procedures in compliance with prescribed guidelines to prevent such conduct. Under s.9(5) the Central Government prescribes those guidelines (rule-making power in s.29A).
  • s.9(4): ss.7A, 8 and 9 are cognizable — arrest is possible without warrant in the manner of the Code (a deliberate departure for supply-side offences).
  • s.10: where the s.9 offence is committed with the consent/connivance of a director, manager, secretary or other officer, that person is guilty too — 3–7 years + fine (“director” includes a firm’s partner).
⚖ Connected law — UNCAC and the comparative corporate-bribery regime Section 9 implements UNCAC Articles 15–16 and 26 (bribery and liability of legal persons). It closely tracks the UK Bribery Act, 2010, s.7 (“failure of a commercial organisation to prevent bribery”; the statutory “adequate procedures” six-principle defence: proportionate procedures, top-level commitment, risk assessment, due diligence, communication/training, monitoring/review). The older US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, 1977 instead regulates issuers/US persons through anti-bribery prohibitions plus books-and-records and internal-controls requirements; India’s Companies Act, 2013 (s.447 fraud; s.177 audit committee/vigil mechanism; s.184 conflicts) supplies the books-and-records side domestically. The one gap honestly recorded in the faculty notes: India has still not legislated UNCAC Article 16 (bribery of foreign public officials) — the Law Commission’s 258th Report (August 2015) drafted amendments through the Prevention of Bribery of Foreign Public Officials Bill.

5. Section 11 — undue advantage without consideration from a person concerned in a proceeding/business

A public servant who accepts/obtains/attempts to obtain, for himself or another, an undue advantage without consideration, or for a consideration he knows to be inadequate, from a person whom he knows to be concerned, or likely to be concerned, in any proceeding or business transacted or about to be transacted by him, or having a connection with the official functions/public duty of himself or a superior, or from an interested/related person — punishment minimum 6 months, extendable to 5 years + fine. This is the “benami gifts / dealings” provision (old s.11; it corresponds to former s.165 IPC). It requires no proof that any official act was done or promised; the relationship between the giver and the officer’s work plus want of consideration suffices.

6. Section 12 — abetment

Whoever abets any offence under the Act, whether or not the offence is committed in consequence, is punishable with 3–7 years + fine. Like NDPS s.29, this is stricter than the general code (IPC s.116 / BNS s.56 gives reduced punishment where the offence abetted is not committed).

7. Section 13 — criminal misconduct by a public servant (the sentencing centrepiece)

Section 13(1) (post-2018) — only two forms of criminal misconduct (a) he dishonestly or fraudulently misappropriates or otherwise converts for his own use any property entrusted to him or under his control as a public servant, or allows any other person so to do; or
(b) he intentionally enriches himself illicitly during the period of his office.
Explanation 1 — illicit enrichment presumed: a person is presumed to have intentionally enriched himself illicitly if he (or anyone on his behalf) is, or at any time during office has been, in possession of pecuniary resources or property disproportionate to his known sources of income which he cannot satisfactorily account for.
Explanation 2: “known sources of income” means income from lawful sources.
s.13(2): imprisonment not less than four years, extendable to ten years + fine (minimum raised from 1 to 4 years by the 2014 amendment).
Narrowing in 2018 — a frequently asked reform point: old s.13(1) contained five clauses: (a) habitual gratification; (b) gratification by corrupt or illegal means; (c) gratification by exercise of personal influence; (d) obtaining valuable thing/pecuniary advantage by abuse of position without public interest; (e) disproportionate assets. Clauses (a)–(d) were deleted as duplicative of new ss.7/7A/8/11 (or are now covered by them), and clause (e) was re-cast as clause (b) (“illicit enrichment”); old clause (a) (misappropriation/breach of trust type conduct) survives as new clause (a). The practical effect: the broad “abuse of position” charge in old (d) — used in Kakkar and many DA/“benefit” cases — must now be fitted to ss.7/11, and legitimate-decision protection is additionally provided by s.17A.

8. Sections 14, 15 and 16

  • s.14 (substituted 2018) — habitual offender: whoever, having been convicted of an offence under the Act, subsequently commits an offence under it — minimum 5 years, extendable to 10 years + fine. (Old s.14 punished the public servant who committed the s.13(a)–(d) misconduct after having previously accepted gratification.)

Pre-2018, s.15 also punished attempts under old s.13(1)(c)/(d); the 2018 amendment confined it to misappropriation. Attempts to bribe/take bribes are now covered in the language of ss.7–8 themselves (“attempts to obtain”; “promises to give”).
  • s.16 — fine fixation: the court must consider the amount/value obtained by the offence — or, for s.13(1)(b), the value of unaccounted resources/property — in fixing fine.

Part D — Special Judges and Investigation (Syllabus Head F: Sections 17 and 17A)

1. Special Judges (Sections 3–6)

  • s.3: the Central/State Government may, by Official Gazette notification, appoint as many Special Judges as necessary for specified areas/cases to try (a) offences under the Act and (b) conspiracy, attempt or abetment of them. Qualification: a Sessions, Additional Sessions or (earlier) Assistant Sessions Judge.
  • s.4: notwithstanding anything in the CrPC/BNSS or other law, these offences are triable by Special Judges only; territorial allocation; co-trial of connected offences; and, as substituted in 2018, trial “as far as practicable on a day-to-day basis” with an endeavour to conclude within two years, reasons recorded for delay, extensions of six months at a time, ordinarily not exceeding four years in aggregate.
  • s.5: the Special Judge takes cognizance without committal, follows warrant-case procedure, may tender pardon (deemed tender under s.307 CrPC → s.344 BNSS; the approver regime of ss.306–308 → ss.343–345 BNSS applies), and the court is deemed a Court of Session with the prosecutor deemed a Public Prosecutor; s.5(6) confers District Judge powers under the 1944 Ordinance (attachment).
  • s.6: narrow summary-trial provision for public-servant contraventions tied to Essential Commodities Act special orders (cap of one year’s imprisonment on summary conviction).
  • s.27: appeal/revision to the High Court as if the Special Judge were a Sessions Court (appeal now follows s.415 BNSS, replacing s.374 CrPC).

2. Section 17 — persons authorised to investigate

Notwithstanding the CrPC, no police officer below the rank — (a) in the Delhi Special Police Establishment (CBI): Inspector of Police; (b) in the metropolitan areas of Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, Ahmedabad and other notified metropolitan areas: Assistant Commissioner of Police; (c) elsewhere: Deputy Superintendent of Police or equivalent — shall investigate an offence under the Act without the order of a Metropolitan Magistrate/first-class Magistrate, or arrest without a warrant.
Proviso 1: an Inspector authorised by the State Government by general/special order may investigate without a Magistrate’s order and arrest without warrant.
Proviso 2: the s.13(1)(b) illicit-enrichment offence (old 13(1)(e)) shall not be investigated without the order of a police officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police.

Consequence of breach: investigation by an officer below the prescribed rank is not a nullity per se but, absent proper authorisation, is an illegality; courts assess whether it caused failure of justice and permit ratification by a competent officer taking over. The rank check is a recurring ground in quash petitions (compare H.N. Rishbud v. State of Delhi (1955) and State of M.P. v. B.B.S. Thimaya principles for cognizance despite procedural lapses).

3. Section 17A — previous approval before enquiry/inquiry/investigation (inserted 2018)

Section 17A(1)No police officer shall conduct any enquiry, inquiry or investigation into an offence alleged to have been committed by a public servant under this Act, where the alleged offence is relatable to any recommendation made or decision taken by such public servant in discharge of his official functions or duties, without the previous approval of — (a) the Central Government for Union employees; (b) the State Government for State employees; (c) in any other case, the authority competent to remove him at the time of commission of the alleged offence.
  • Proviso 1 (spot-arrest exception): no approval is needed “for cases involving arrest of a person on the spot on the charge of accepting or attempting to accept any undue advantage for himself or for any other person” — every ACB/CBI trap case falls in this exception.
  • Proviso 2 (time-limit): the competent authority must convey its decision within three months, extendable by one further month for recorded reasons in writing.
  • Scope (Supreme Court reading, e.g. Anil Kumar v. M.K. Aiyappa, (2023) and the 2021–24 line): approval is a post-FIR safeguard against harassment for bona fide decisions: it is not required to register an FIR or conduct a preliminary enquiry, but is required before actual investigation involving coercive steps into a decision/recommendation; it is not required for offences unrelated to official decisions (e.g. trap-based s.7 acceptance; private assets/DA cases of the s.13(1)(b) type are governed by s.17 proviso 2 SP-level authorisation).
⚖ Connected law — the Single Directive lineage and the BNSS complaint checks s.17A is the statutory successor to the “Single Directive” (executive sanction for probing senior officers) struck down in Vineet Narain, and to DSPE s.6A (inserted by the CVC Act 2003) struck down in Subramanian Swamy v. CBI (2014) as Article-14-violative (no basis to shield senior officers categorically). The 2018 drafting survived challenge by being decision-focused rather than rank-focused. Alongside it, BNSS s.223(2) (new; replaces s.200 CrPC complaint procedure) now requires a Magistrate taking cognizance on a private complaint against a public servant relating to official functions to give the accused an opportunity of explanation and seek a report from his superior — a parallel pre-cognizance filter. CVC s.8A/8B supplies the preliminary-enquiry track (90+90 days; then investigation, departmental action or closure with action against a false complainant under Lokpal Act s.46).

4. Sections 18 and 18A — bankers’ books and attachment of property

  • s.18: an empowered officer who suspects an offence may inspect bankers’ books relating to the suspect’s accounts (or third-party accounts suspected to be held for him) and take certified copies; the bank is bound to assist. Below SP rank, specific written authorisation of an SP is needed. “Bank”/“bankers’ books” carry the meanings from the Bankers’ Books Evidence Act, 1891 (now succeeded by the BSA-compatible certified-copies regime).
  • s.18A (inserted 2018): save as otherwise provided in PMLA, the Criminal Law Amendment Ordinance, 1944 applies, as far as may be, to attachment, administration and execution of attachment/confiscation of money or property procured by a PC Act offence; references to “District Judge” are read as the Special Judge. This is the domestic asset-forfeiture track; the Enforcement Directorate’s PMLA track (PC offences are scheduled predicate offences) runs independently.
🌎 Real-world example — the trap workflow the ACB actually follows (see Shanthamma for a failed trap)

Part E — Sanction for Prosecution (Syllabus Head G: Section 19 read with s.197 CrPC → s.218 BNSS)

1. Section 19 (current text, post-2018)

Section 19(1)No court shall take cognizance of an offence punishable under sections 7, 11, 13 and 15 alleged to have been committed by a public servant, except with the previous sanction save as otherwise provided in the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013 —
(a) of the Central Government for a person employed, or who was at the time of commission employed, in connection with the affairs of the Union and removable only by that Government;
(b) of the State Government on the same footing for State affairs;
(c) in any other case, of the authority competent to remove him from office.
Private-complainant provisos (2018): a private person (not police/law-enforcement) may seek sanction only after filing a court complaint which has not been dismissed under s.203 CrPC (now the BNSS analogue) with a direction to obtain sanction; the competent authority must give the public servant an opportunity of being heard before granting such sanction.
Time-limits: decision within three months, extendable by one month for recorded reasons (legal consultation); guidelines may be prescribed.
Explanation: “public servant” for s.19(1) includes a person (a) who has ceased to hold the office during which the offence is alleged, or (b) who has ceased to hold that office and holds another office.
  • s.19(2): if any doubt arises whether sanction should be by the Centre, a State or another authority, it is given by the Government/authority competent to remove the public servant at the time the offence was allegedly committed.
  • s.19(3): absence of, or error/omission/irregularity in, sanction does not by itself reverse/alter a finding, sentence or order or stay proceedings unless a failure of justice has in fact been occasioned; no revision lies against interlocutory orders. s.19(4): in judging failure of justice the court asks whether the objection could and should have been raised earlier (delay/waivability). Explanation: “error” includes competency of the sanctioning authority.
  • Nature of sanction: per R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay and the long line of cases, sanction is not an idle formality but a solemn and sacrosanct act: the competent authority must apply its mind to the evidence collected and the facts; a mechanically granted sanction is invalid.
  • Sections covered: pre-2018 the list was “7, 10, 11, 13 and 15”; post-2018 it is “7, 11, 13 and 15” (old s.10 disappeared in the re-enactment; ss.7A, 8, 9, 10 giver/middleman offences deliberately need no sanction against the private party).

2. Section 197 CrPC → Section 218 BNSS — the general-code counterpart

⚖ Connected law — general vs special sanction s.197 CrPC 1973 (now s.218 BNSS, 2023) bars cognizance of an offence alleged to have been committed by a Judge/public servant while acting or purporting to act in discharge of official duty, without sanction of the Government/competent authority; the words “who is or was” expressly cover retired officials. BNSS s.218 first proviso adds a 120-day limit: the competent authority must decide a sanction request within 120 days, failing which sanction is deemed accorded. Differences from s.19 PC Act (as explained in Kalicharan):
  • s.197/s.218 protects official acts (“acting or purporting to act in discharge of duty”); the PC Act offences cannot be characterised as official acts even purportedly — hence Parliament deliberately left s.19’s wording unchanged in 1988 despite adding “or was” to s.197 in 1973.
  • s.19 is a special provision keyed to enumerated sections and the authority competent to remove; where it applies it excludes the general provision.
  • Certain offences are declared outside sanction protection (the CrPC explanation lists ss.166A/166B, 354 series, 370, 376 IPC type offences); BNSS s.223(2) separately guards private complaints against public servants at the cognizance stage.

Part F — Presumption, Trap Evidence and Burden of Proof (Syllabus Head H: Section 20)

1. Section 20 — presumption where a public servant accepts undue advantage

Section 20 (post-2018)Where, in any trial of an offence punishable under section 7 or section 11, it is proved that a public servant accused of an offence has accepted or obtained or attempted to obtain for himself or another any undue advantage from any person, it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that he accepted/obtained/attempted to obtain that advantage as a motive or reward under section 7 for performing/causing performance of a public duty improperly or dishonestly (by himself or another), or, as the case may be, the advantage without consideration/inadequate consideration under section 11.
  • This is a rebuttable presumption of law (“shall presume”) cast in terms of the old s.4, Evidence Act 1872, now the “shall presume” concept in s.4, para (2) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA).
  • Foundation first: the presumption switches on only after the prosecution proves the acceptance/obtaining (and, under the section’s interplay with case law, in a trap case the demand). Once the foundation is laid, the burden shifts to the accused to show the money was not a motive or reward (e.g. it was due, legally receivable, a loan, returned, planted). Under the evidence code the accused’s burden is generally discharged by preponderance of probability (contrast the NDPS s.35(2) “beyond reasonable doubt” anomaly); he need not prove his innocence to the hilt.
  • Pre-2018, s.20 applied on proof of acceptance in trials under ss.7 and 11 and old s.13(1)(a)/(b); the present text keys it to new s.7 and s.11. Madhukar Bhaskarrao Joshi and B. Noha (below) remain the governing interpretations of the premise-and-inference structure.

2. The two indispensable facts — demand and acceptance

Settled principle (P. Satyanarayana Murthy, (2015) 10 SCC 152, para 23)“The proof of demand of illegal gratification is the gravamen of the offence under ss.7 and 13(1)(d)(i)/(ii), and in absence thereof unmistakably the charge fails. Mere acceptance of any amount allegedly by way of illegal gratification or recovery thereof, dehors the proof of demand, ipso facto would not be sufficient to bring home the charge…. Failure of the prosecution to prove the demand would be fatal and mere recovery of the amount from the accused would not entail conviction.” This is the exact rule applied to acquit in K. Shanthamma (Case 9).
CaseRule
Madhukar Bhaskarrao Joshi v. State of Maharashtra (2000) 8 SCC 571 (para 12)The premise for the presumption is payment or acceptance of gratification; once that is established, the inference is that it was accepted “as motive or reward”; “gratification” need not mean “reward” — reward is the outcome of the presumption; gratification means any payment giving satisfaction. The official-act nexus is thus presumed, not independently proved.
B. Noha v. State of Kerala (2006) 12 SCC 277 (paras 10–11)“When it is proved that there was voluntary and conscious acceptance of the money, there is no further burden cast on the prosecution to prove by direct evidence the demand or motive; it has only to be deduced from the facts and circumstances.” But Noha applies on a reliable foundation — it does not dispense with proof of demand where acceptance itself is doubtful (Shanthamma reconciles the two).
Raj Rajendra Singh Seth (R.R.S. Seth) v. State of Jharkhand (2008) 11 SCC 681Where money demanded for proper treatment of a patient in a government hospital (₹500, payable even to the ward boy) is proved to have passed to the doctor, the burden is on him to show it was not illegal gratification; not discharged → conviction upheld. Cited contrast in Kakkar: hospital-linked extraction is corruption; unauthorised private-practice fee is not.
State of Punjab v. Madan Mohan Lal Verma; Vishnu Kumar Bhargava lineThe complainant’s evidence in trap cases requires careful scrutiny with the assistance of independent witnesses; where the bribe-giver turns hostile and other circumstances do not establish demand, acquittal follows.

3. The trap case — evidence architecture

Prosecution must establish
1. Demand by the accused for an undue advantage linked to an official function (oral/writing; prior demands + demand at trap).
2. Acceptance — conscious, voluntary receipt of the tainted money/advantage (hand-to-hand, or through a third party/diary/agent).
3. Identity and continuity — pre-trap note serial numbers, sealing, panch witnesses, the complainant’s version, shadow witness, post-trap recovery and chemical test.
4. Official nexus — the advantage related to the public duty (then presumed under s.20 unless rebutted).
Defence commonly raised
No demand — complainant’s version is an improvement/after-thought (s.161 CrPC → s.180 BNSS statement vs. court statement).
Money planted / kept unknowingly (diary, drawer); no conscious possession — negative chemical test on fingers.
Independent shadow witness did not witness the transaction; sole interested witness.
Amount legally due (fees, dues, loan, returned advance); enmity/memo motive of witnesses; no pending favour (order already passed/no tax due).

4. Accused as witness; charge particulars; other evidentiary provisions

  • s.21 — accused a competent defence witness: he may give evidence on oath in disproof of charges against himself/co-accused, only on his own request; his failure to testify cannot be commented upon by the prosecution or raise a presumption; cross-examination on prior offences/bad character is tightly restricted.
  • s.22 — modified Code procedure before the Special Judge (witness lists, absence of accused, no automatic stay on revision, restricted record-calling). s.23: for a s.13(1)(a) charge (misappropriation), it suffices to describe the property and the date-range (not exceeding one year) without item/date particulars, and the charge is deemed one offence for s.219 CrPC (now BNSS analogue) — practical for DA/embezzlement prosecutions.
  • s.25: armed-forces/paramilitary statutes and their courts are unaffected; the Special Judge is nonetheless a court of ordinary criminal justice for those laws. s.27: appeal/revision to the High Court.

5. Burden of proof and the new evidence code

⚖ Connected law — presumptions and burdens under the BSA, 2023 (in force 1-7-2024)
  • “May presume / shall presume / conclusive proof” (old s.4 IEA) → s.4, paras (1)–(3), BSA (definitions in s.2). Section 20 PC Act is a “shall presume”: the court must hold the presumed fact proved until disproved.
  • Burden of proving exceptions/defences (old s.105 IEA) → s.108 BSA — the accused’s case of legal entitlement, coercion, repayment etc.
  • Burden as to facts within special knowledge (old s.106 IEA) → s.109 BSA — why money was in the drawer, how assets were acquired: facts pre-eminently within the accused’s knowledge.
  • Accomplice/approver evidence (s.133 + ill.(b) to s.114 IEA) → s.307 BSA, relevant to co-accused/pardons under s.5 read with BNSS ss.343–345.
  • Trap witnesses: police/accomplice witnesses are not automatically untrustworthy; courts assess reliability, with independent witnesses preferred where available (the Shanthamma failure to let the shadow witness enter the chamber was fatal precisely because no independent corroboration of demand survived).
🌎 Real-world example — phenolphthalein traps and the “diary problem” in Shanthamma In a standard cash trap the tainted notes are dusted with phenolphthalein powder; when the recipient handles them and dips his fingers into sodium-carbonate water, the solution turns pink — corroborating physical contact. In Shanthamma the officer allegedly asked the complainant to place notes inside a diary in her drawer; her fingers did not turn pink (she never touched the notes), the independent shadow witness remained outside the chamber, and the complainant’s repeated-demand story was an “improvement” over his s.161/s.164 statements — recovery from the diary proved custody but neither demand nor conscious acceptance as bribe. Contrast the modern practice where trap teams insist on hand-to-hand passing, continuous audio/video recording and two independent panch witnesses — reforms directly responsive to acquittals on these gaps.

Prescribed Case 8 · DU Case Material pp. 121–124

Kalicharan Mahapatra v. State of Orissa

AIR 1998 SC 2595 · (1998) 6 SCC 411 · Supreme Court of India · M.M. Punchhi, C.J.I., and K.T. Thomas, J. · Judgment delivered by K.T. Thomas, J. (4 August 1998)
Forum chainSpecial Judge, Bhubaneswar (Orissa Special Courts Act, 1990) → High Court → Supreme Court
Charges.13(2) r/w s.13(1)(e) PC Act — disproportionate assets
Key questionCan a retired public servant be prosecuted without sanction?
AnswerYes — and no sanction is needed after he demits office

Facts

  • The appellant, Kalicharan Mahapatra, was an IPS officer who rose to Superintendent of Police in the Orissa State Police Service.
  • 12 May 1990: on “sleuth information”, a raid was conducted at his residence and a good amount of cash and jewellery was recovered. A case was registered under s.13(2) of the PC Act.
  • 31 December 1990: the appellant retired from service while investigation continued.
  • 30 September 1992: the Vigilance Department filed a charge-sheet under s.13(2) read with s.13(1)(e) (criminal misconduct — pecuniary resources/property disproportionate to known sources of income).
  • The case went to the Special Judge at Bhubaneswar; the appellant raised a preliminary objection that without previous sanction the court could not take cognizance. The Special Judge and the High Court rejected it; the matter reached the Supreme Court.

Issues framed (faculty summary)

  1. Whether the PC Act, 1988 applies to a retired public servant? — Yes.
  2. Whether previous sanction under s.19 is required to try a retired public servant? — No.
  3. Whether s.19 must be interpreted in the light of the amended s.197 CrPC 1973 (“is or was”)? — No; the two provisions are distinct.

Arguments on behalf of the appellant

  1. The Act does not cover a retired person: the legislature did not include a retired public servant; there is no mention of a person who has ceased to be a public servant; s.2(c) does not include him, and Chapter III (offences/penalties) points to no non-public-servant offender for the public-servant offences.
  2. The s.19(1) structure breaks down after retirement: clause (a) covers a person employed in Union affairs removable by the Centre; clause (b) likewise for the States; clause (c) covers “any other person” removable by a competent authority. A retired person cannot be removed from any office — so, it was argued, none of the three clauses applies and prosecution is impossible.
  3. The CrPC changed the rule: s.197 of the 1973 Code expressly covers “any person who is or was… a public servant”, whereas the old CrPC 1898 did not protect retired officials. Since Parliament knew how to say “is or was” but omitted such words from s.19 PC Act, the older decisions (under the 1947 Act) could no longer be relied upon.

Arguments for the respondent (accepted by the Court)

  • Sections 8, 9, 12 and 15 deal with offenders who need not be public servants, while the other sections deal with public servants; nothing anywhere says an offence “vanishes” when the offender demits office.
  • Public-servant status is required at the time of commission, not at trial; the offence cannot be committed after demitting office, but an offence committed in office remains triable.
  • The s.19 wording was carried over materially unchanged from s.6 of the PC Act 1947 despite the 1973 CrPC change — a deliberate distinction: s.197 protects acts done in purported discharge of official duty; PC offences can never be official acts even purportedly.

Statutory provisions and precedents analysed

  • s.2(c) (public servant); s.13(1)(e) and s.13(2) (old); s.19(1)(a)/(b)/(c); s.6 PC Act 1947 (pari materia); ss.197 CrPC 1898 and 1973; 41st Law Commission Report (1969), para 15.123.
  • S.A. Venkataraman v. The State [1958 SCR 1040] (3-Judge Bench): s.6(1) of the 1947 Act did not require sanction for a person who had ceased to be a public servant when cognizance was taken; clause (c) covers “any other public servant whom a competent authority could remove”.
  • C.R. Bansi v. State of Maharashtra [1971(3) SCR 236]; State of W.B. v. Manmal Bhutoria [1977(3) SCR 758]; and the Constitution Bench in K. Veeraswami v. UOI (1991) 3 SCC 655 — all to the same effect (no sanction after retirement).
  • R. Balakrishna Pillai v. State of Kerala (1996) 1 SCC 478 — recorded the Law Commission reasoning (41st Report, para 15.123): protection after retirement is needed under s.197 so a private complainant cannot wait out the officer’s tenure to harass him — but that rationale is specific to official-act prosecutions.
Decision (paras 14–15)A public servant who committed an offence under the Act while in office can be prosecuted with the s.19 sanction if he continues in office when the court takes cognizance; if he has ceased to be a public servant by then, the court can take cognizance without any sanction. In other words, he is liable whether or not he remains in office at trial or during prosecution. Accepting the appellant’s argument would produce the absurd result that an officer could offend just before retirement and escape, or protract trial until superannuation. The appeal was dismissed; the Special Court and High Court were upheld.
Principle / ratio
  1. Status at the time of offence, not trial: the PC Act applies to retired public servants for offences committed while in office.
  2. No sanction post-retirement under the unamended Act: s.19 protects the sitting public servant; once the office is demitted there is no authority “competent to remove” and the protection disappears (s.6 1947 Act precedents applied).
  3. s.19 PC Act ≠ s.197 CrPC: the former concerns non-official corrupt acts; the latter concerns acts done or purported to be done in official duty — Parliament’s 1988 decision not to import “is or was” was deliberate.
  4. Superseded by the 2018 amendment: the faculty notes expressly record that this ratio has been legislatively overruled — the Explanation to amended s.19(1) now includes the person who has ceased to hold the office; post-2018, sanction is required even after retirement (see the Akhilesh illustration and Katti Nagaseshanna, 2018 on prospectivity).

K. Shanthamma v. State of Telangana

Facts and prosecution case

  • The appellant, K. Shanthamma, was Commercial Tax Officer at Secunderabad. PW1, R. Seetharamulu @ Sharma, was a supervisor at the Farmers’ Service Co-operative Society and filed its commercial-tax returns. Assessment for 1997–98 was complete but the 1996–97 returns were pending.
  • On 14 February 2000 the appellant issued notice to the Society to produce the cash book, general ledger and purchase/sales statements for 1996–97. PW1 produced the records; she directed PW4 (Ahmed Moinuddin, ACTO) to verify them.
  • The prosecution case: on 24 February 2000 she demanded ₹3,000 for issuing the assessment order; reiterated the demand on three consecutive days; on 29 February refused to issue the final order without payment; on 23 March scaled the demand to ₹2,000.
  • 27 March 2000: PW1 and the Society’s Managing Director went to the ACB, Hyderabad; PW1 gave a written complaint to the DSP, ACB, and a trap was laid. When PW1 tendered the tainted ₹2,000, the appellant (allegedly) did not take the money directly but opened a diary from her table drawer, asked him to place the notes inside, closed and locked the drawer, and kept the key in her handbag. She then signed the ledger/cash book (dating them 26 February 2000), had the attender affix the official rubber stamp, and signed the final assessment office note. PW1 collected the books, came out and gave the signal; the trap party entered.
  • The DSP questioned her; she opened the right-side drawer with the key from her handbag and produced the diary; the wad of notes matched the pre-trap serial numbers. Seizure formalities followed. The Special Court convicted under ss.7 and 13(1)(d)/13(2); the Telangana High Court dismissed the appeal.

Issues

  1. Was the demand of illegal gratification proved?
  2. Was acceptance of the tainted notes by the appellant proved (recovery from the diary; negative chemical test)?
  3. If recovery was proved, did the s.20 presumption nevertheless fail because the foundational demand/acceptance was not established?

Arguments — appellant (Mrs. V. Mohana, Senior Advocate)

  • Demand not proved — PW1’s evidence was an improvement: the 24 February and three-day demands did not figure in the Ex.P3 complaint, the s.161 CrPC statement or the s.164 statement; PW1 admitted these omissions in cross-examination.
  • Independent witness LW8 never entered the chamber: the DSP (PW8) had directed R. Hari Kishan (LW8) to accompany PW1 inside and observe; both PW7 and PW8 admitted LW8 waited in the corridor. The prosecution offered no explanation — PW1 was therefore the sole witness to demand and acceptance.
  • Negative chemical test: her fingers did not turn pink in the sodium-carbonate test — she never handled the notes; recovery from the diary was not recovery from her person. Her defence under s.313 CrPC: PW1 slipped the notes into the diary on her table when she was in the attached washroom; the officers admitted the chamber had an attached washroom.
  • No possible motive: the notice dated 26 February 2000 (served 15 March 2000) recorded that the Society’s net turnover was nil and allowed the claimed exemption — no tax was due; the final assessment order was a mere procedural formality, making the demand “highly doubtful”.
  • PW4 (ACTO) had a grudge: she had served a memo on him on 21/22 March 2000 for defaults in duty.
  • She relied on the line of authority that without proof of demand and acceptance, the s.20 presumption does not arise.

Arguments — respondent (Ms. Bina Madhavan)

  • PW1 had no reason to falsely implicate her; his evidence of continuous demands was trustworthy.
  • The tainted notes were in her diary, in her locked drawer; she had the key, opened it herself and produced the diary — conscious possession/acceptance.
  • The assessment order (as distinct from the 26 February notice) was still pending and was in fact issued on the trap day — the demand for issuing it was the official favour.
  • Both courts had recorded concurrent findings of fact on evidence; under Article 136 the Supreme Court should not interfere.

Law applied

The Court quoted P. Satyanarayana Murthy v. District Inspector of Police, State of A.P., (2015) 10 SCC 152, para 23 in extenso: proof of demand is the gravamen under ss.7 and 13(1)(d); mere acceptance/recovery dehors proof of demand is insufficient; failure to prove demand is fatal. It then examined the evidence:

  • Doubtful demand on 23 March: by 15 March the Society had been served with the nil-turnover/exemption notice; the final order was only formal — a bribe demand then was “highly doubtful”.
  • Sole, improved witness: LW8’s failure to enter the chamber was unexplained; PW1 alone spoke to demand/acceptance; his examination-in-chief narrative (“I informed AO that I brought the bribe amount”) showed the officer did not reiterate any demand at the trap; the earlier dates were admitted improvements over Ex.P3, s.161 and s.164 statements.
  • Corroboration absent: PW2 had no personal knowledge; PW3 (godown in-charge) was not told the money was for any favour; PW4 was not a witness to any demand (and had been memoed).
Decision (paras 15–16)The demand of illegal gratification — the sine qua non for the s.7 offence — was not conclusively proved; without it the charge under s.7 and s.13(1)(d) could not survive despite diary recovery. The judgments of the Special Court and High Court were set aside; the appeal allowed; the appellant acquitted.
Principle / ratio
  • Demand + acceptance are the two indispensable elements of the s.7/old s.13(1)(d) offence; recovery of tainted money alone, without proof of demand, cannot convict (Satyanarayana Murthy restated).
  • The s.20 presumption arises only after the foundational facts (acceptance, with the demand context) are proved; the prosecution cannot start from the recovered notes.
  • Where the independent shadow witness does not witness the transaction without explanation, and the complainant’s demand-version is an admitted improvement, the uncorroborated interested testimony is unsafe.
  • Absence of any pending official favour (nil assessment already decided) and a negative finger test powerfully support the defence that the notes were planted — the accused’s s.313 explanation must be considered in the totality.
Note the post-2018 framing: today the same facts are charged under new s.7 (and, for the giver, s.8). Explanation 1 to s.7 makes demand for doing a duty owed anyway (the ration-card illustration) an offence — but Shanthamma’s evidentiary lesson is untouched: the demand must still be proved by credible, preferably independent, evidence.

Kanwarjit Singh Kakkar & Another v. State of Punjab

(2011) 13 SCC 158 · (2011) 6 SCR 895 · Supreme Court of India · Markandey Katju and Gyan Sudha Misra, JJ. (judgment by Misra, J.; 28 April 2011)
AccusedTwo Punjab Government Medical Officers (Dr. Chawla & Dr. Kakkar)
FIRNo.13 dated 9-4-2003, PS Vigilance Bureau, Ludhiana
Chargess.13(1)(d) r/w s.13(2) PC Act & s.168 IPC
ResultFIR quashed — departmental misconduct, not corruption

Facts

  • Dr. Rajinder Singh Chawla (government doctor at Dhanasu) and Dr. Kanwarjit Singh Kakkar (government doctor at Koom Kalan, Ludhiana) were alleged to be doing private practice in the evening at Metro Road, Jamalpur, charging ₹100 cash per patient as prescription fee. Chawla checked blood pressure; Kakkar examined patients, issued prescription slips and medicines.
  • The informant, Raman Kumar, claimed he himself obtained medicines/treatment for ₹100 and complained that government doctors cannot charge patients contrary to government instructions. A raid was conducted and the doctors were allegedly trapped receiving ₹100 consultation charges.
  • FIR No.13 of 9 April 2003 was registered at PS Vigilance Bureau, Ludhiana under s.13(1)(d) r/w s.13(2) PC Act and s.168 IPC (public servant unlawfully engaging in trade).
  • The High Court (single Judge, 2 April 2009) declined to quash, relying on Rule 15 of the Punjab Civil Medical (State Service Class I) Rules, 1972; the doctors appealed by special leave and sought quashing.

Issues

  1. Can a government doctor alleged to be doing private practice be booked under the PC Act (s.13(1)(d))?
  2. Does the conduct amount to an offence under s.168 IPC (unlawfully engaging in trade)?
  3. Or is it merely misconduct under the service rules (Rule 15 / Punjab Civil Services Punishment & Appeal Rules)?

Arguments — appellants

  • No apparatus of practice: no medical instrument, BP machine or even a thermometer was recovered; Kakkar’s house was under renovation; the complainant requested treatment and on humanitarian grounds the doctor scribbled a prescription on plain, unsigned paper.
  • Rule 15 is a conduct regime, not a criminal prohibition: the government may, by general/special order, permit private practice subject to conditions and may withdraw permission without cause; deviation from the rule warrants departmental action under the Punjab Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules — not the IPC, much less the PC Act.
  • Section 7’s concept of corruption: corruption means acceptance/demand of illegal gratification for doing an official act. A private consultation fee is professional remuneration, not gratification for official duty; the s.13(1)(d) ingredients (corrupt/illegal means; abuse of position to obtain valuable thing/pecuniary advantage; obtaining for another without motive) were absent.
  • s.168 IPC inapplicable: medical treatment is a professional duty, not “trade”; fee acceptance does not involve profit-making of a trade character (relying on State of Gujarat v. Maheshkumar Dheerajlal Thakkar).
  • The State Vigilance Department’s own memo (23 December 2004, acted on 19 January 2005) directed withdrawal of cases against government teachers giving private tuition — tuition fees are neither corruption nor remuneration for an official act; the same logic applies.

Arguments — respondent State

  • Government doctors in Punjab were specifically prohibited from private practice by departmental rules; the act of charging patients was therefore illegal and the PC Act’s prohibition on abuse of position applied.
  • The Rule 15 permission regime showed private practice was controlled public business; unauthorised practice was a breach punishable under the special statute.

The Court’s analysis

  • On a critical reading of the Preamble and s.7, “corruption” is acceptance/demand of illegal gratification for doing an official act; the receipt of a fee in private practice “by itself cannot be held to be an illegal gratification as the same obviously is the amount charged towards professional remuneration”. It would be “preposterous” and against plain common sense to criminalise charging a fee for medical help rendered as a professional.
  • Contrast — Raj Rajendra Singh Seth @ R.R.S. Seth v. State of Jharkhand, (2008) 11 SCC 681: a government doctor demanded ₹500 for proper treatment of the complainant’s admitted father, even directing payment to his ward boy Nag Narain; the CBI-tried conviction was upheld — there was no claim the money was legally receivable; once money passed to the public servant the burden was on him to show it was not illegal gratification (relying on B. Noha v. State of Kerala, (2006) 12 SCC 277 and Madhukar Bhaskarrao Joshi v. State of Maharashtra, (2000) 8 SCC 571). Demanding payment for admission/“proper” treatment in a government hospital, or prescribing unnecessary surgery to extract money, would make out the PC Act/IPC — but nothing of that kind was alleged.
  • The ingredients of the offence must be deduced from the facts and circumstances; here, even per the FIR, the money was a professional fee for examining/treating private patients, not gratification for an official favour; no presumption of motive/reward could be drawn.
  • Rule 15 itself contemplated permitted private practice; breach of the bar was, at its highest, misconduct under the Service Rules (a professional discharging his professional duty commits no crime merely because a circular forbids the arrangement — the act becomes a service-code violation, not a criminal offence).
  • s.168 IPC was not made out: treating patients is discharge of professional duty and is not engaging in “trade”.
Decision (paras 16–17)The High Court orders were set aside and FIR No.13 dated 9-4-2003 was quashed: no prima facie case under s.168 IPC or s.13(1)(d) r/w s.13(2) PC Act; proceeding would abuse the process of court and waste judicial time. The State was left free, if so advised, to initiate departmental proceedings under the Punjab Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules. Both appeals allowed.
Principle / ratio
  • Definition of corruption: corruption is the demand/acceptance of illegal gratification for an official act; professional fees for a private professional act are not illegal gratification.
  • Service-rule breach ≠ crime: unauthorised private practice by a government doctor/teacher is departmental misconduct (with compulsory retirement/pension sanctions available), not PC Act corruption or s.168 IPC unlawful trade.
  • The dividing line: money extracted for performing the official/public function (admission to a government hospital, proper ward treatment, government certificates) is bribery (R.R.S. Seth); money for genuine private professional service outside official duty is at most a service violation.
  • Burden under s.20 logic: once money for an official act is proved to have passed, the burden shifts to the public servant to explain it (B. Noha; Madhukar Joshi) — but that presumption never starts where the foundational official-nexus fact is absent.
  • Post-2018 mapping: the conduct would now fall to be tested against new ss.7/11; nothing changes the core holding that a genuine professional fee lacks the official-act nexus.

Abhay Singh Chautala & Another (Ajay Singh Chautala) v. C.B.I.

(2011) 7 SCC 141 · Supreme Court of India · V.S. Sirpurkar and T.S. Thakur, JJ. (judgment by Sirpurkar, J.; 4 July 2011) · Two SLPs disposed by common judgment
ContextJBT teachers recruitment scam; SC-ordered CBI probe (W.P.(Crl.) 93/2003)
Chargesss.13(1)(e) & 13(2) r/w s.109 IPC — disproportionate assets
Abhay’s DA₹119.69 crore = 522.79% of known income (check period 7-6-2000 to 8-3-2005)
Ajay’s DA₹27.74 crore = 339.26% (check 24-5-1993 to 31-5-2006)

Facts and genesis

  • The Supreme Court, by order in Writ Petition (Crl.) No.93 of 2003 (the JBT Teachers Recruitment Scam matter), directed the CBI to investigate the scam (illegal recruitment of 3,208 JBT teachers with forged documents, allegedly under the Om Prakash Chautala Government). The offences were registered on 24 May 2004.
  • Searches reportedly showed the family had acquired movable/immovable properties valued at ₹1,467 crores; a notification dated 22 February 2006 under ss.5 & 6 of the DSPE Act, with Haryana’s consent, extended CBI jurisdiction into Haryana for the disproportionate-assets case, and an FIR was registered against Shri Om Prakash Chautala and family.
  • Abhay Singh Chautala (MLA Haryana, Rori constituency during the check period 7-6-2000 to 8-3-2005) allegedly amassed ₹1,19,69,82,619 — 522.79% of known sources. Ajay Singh Chautala (MLA Rajasthan 2-3-1990–15-12-1992 and 28-12-1993–31-11-1998; Lok Sabha MP from Bhiwani 10-10-1999–6-2-2004; Rajya Sabha MP 2-8-2004–3-11-2009; later MLA from Dabwali) allegedly amassed ₹27,74,74,260 — 339.26% of known sources over 24-5-1993 to 31-5-2006.
  • Separate charge-sheets were filed under ss.13(1)(e) and 13(2) r/w s.109 IPC. No sanction under s.19 had been obtained. The Special Judge, CBI (common order 2 February 2010), held the charge-sheets did not allege abuse of the current office (MLA at cognizance), so no sanction was necessary. The High Court dismissed the s.482 CrPC petitions on 8 July 2010. Hence the SLPs.

Issue

Whether sanction under s.19 PC Act was necessary where the accused were admittedly public servants when cognizance was taken/charges framed, but the office alleged to have been abused during the check period was an entirely different office from the one held at cognizance — and whether R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay was correctly decided / required reconsideration.

Arguments — appellants (Shri Mukul Rohatgi and Shri U.U. Lalit)

  1. Plain language of s.19(1): on the cognizance/charge date both accused were admittedly public servants; the embargo is complete — no cognizance of ss.7, 10, 11, 13, 15 offences without sanction.
  2. R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay (1984) 2 SCC 183 and the line following it (Habibullah Khan (1995) 2 SCC 437; Balakrishnan Ravi Menon (2007) 1 SCC 45; K. Karunakaran (2007) 1 SCC 59; Prakash Singh Badal (2007) 1 SCC 1) were wrongly decided and merited a larger-bench reference: the Court ignored the plain meaning and the observations were obiter dicta (once Antulay was held not a public servant as MLA, the sanction discussion was unnecessary).
  3. Per incuriam of s.6(2) of the 1947 Act (pari materia s.19(2)): the sub-section contemplates doubts about the sanctioning authority where the accused held different offices — implying the Act itself recognises a person may hold a different office at cognizance and still need sanction.
  4. K. Veeraswami undermined Antulay; reading Antulay’s gloss amounts to adding an unwritten proviso (“provided nothing applies where the accused holds a different post”), which rewrites s.19(1) and violates strict construction in favour of the accused; the whole class of public servants would be exposed to frivolous prosecutions and career damage.
  5. Two terms, two times: “public servant” in the chapeau looks to the past (commission); “a person” in clauses (a)/(b)/(c) looks to the present (sanction stage); they must mean different things — “person” covering the present office-holder.
  6. Limited-tenure public servants (arbitrators, election officers, co-operative office-bearers under s.2(c)(vi)–(x)) would be in a “hazardous” position if the Antulay view prevailed; plural offices create doubt which only s.19(2) can resolve.

Arguments — respondent CBI (Shri Gopal Subramanium, Solicitor General)

  • The law was settled for 25+ years by the Constitution Bench in Antulay and consistently followed: where the public servant abused office in one capacity and holds a different office (or no office) at cognizance, no sanction is needed. The principle of stare decisis counsels no disturbance.
  • Sanction is tied to the authority competent to remove from the office abused; once that office is not held, there is no such authority and s.19(2)’s “doubt” machinery never arises.

Reasoning

  1. Antulay ratio, not obiter: the Constitution Bench specifically interpreted s.6 as a whole (including s.6(2)) and decided the sanction question; its reasoning was necessary to the decision. Reconsideration refused.
  2. Not per incuriam: s.6(2)/s.19(2) was neither ignored nor missed — once the Court held no sanction at all was required where the abused office was not held, there was no occasion to invoke the doubt-resolving sub-section. s.19(2) deals with a genuine doubt as between Centre/State/another competent authority; it does not sanction prosecutions where no sanction is necessary. To use (2) to create the need in (1) is “putting the cart before the horse”.
  3. Stare decisis: the rule settled for over 25 years should not be disturbed — per Lord Coke, “those things which have been so often adjudged ought to rest in peace”.
  4. The added-proviso and literal arguments rejected: the suggested proviso speaks of a different “post”, but the Act speaks of an “office” — distinct concepts; clauses (a)/(b)/(c) all turn on the power to remove from the office. The section operates in praesenti but by necessary implication excludes a person who abused some other office. Literal construction would lead to absurd results already condemned in Antulay.
  5. “Public servant” vs “a person”: the court takes cognizance of the offence, not the offender; “public servant” identifies the offender-capacity in ss.7, 10, 11, 13 and 15; “a person” in the clauses denotes the accused as an employee removable by the named authority. No conflicting meanings result.
  6. Limited-tenure offices: no hazard — if the person is not a public servant at trial, no sanction is required; the section is clear.
  7. Plurality of offices: s.19(2) doubt arises only where sanction is necessary; if the abused office is not held at cognizance, there is no necessity and no doubt. The relevant time is the date of cognizance.
Decision (para 34)The High Court was “absolutely right” to hold that the appellants had abused entirely different office(s) from the ones held at cognizance, so no s.19 sanction was necessary. The appeals were dismissed; the trials could proceed.
Principle / ratio
  • Office-specific sanction: the competent sanctioning authority is the one competent to remove the public servant from the office alleged to have been abused. If at cognizance he holds neither that office nor an office whose holder is removable by the same authority, no sanction is required — whether he has retired (Kalicharan) or merely changed office (Antulay; Chautala).
  • s.19(2) resolves genuine doubts between sanctioning authorities; it does not manufacture a sanction requirement.
  • Antulay is binding ratio (Constitution Bench), not obiter or per incuriam, and is not to be reconsidered lightly (stare decisis).
  • Legislative reversal in 2018: the Explanation added to s.19(1) now expressly includes (a) persons who have ceased to hold the abused office and (b) persons holding another office — statutory reversal of both Kalicharan and Chautala for the post-amendment regime (prospective per Katti Nagaseshanna).
  • Epilogue (connected trial): in the parent JBT recruitment case, Om Prakash Chautala v. CBI, the Special CBI court (January 2013) sentenced Om Prakash Chautala, Ajay Singh Chautala and officials to 10 years; the High Court (6 March 2015) and Supreme Court (3 August 2015) upheld the convictions — 55 accused convicted.

DU 2015 · Question 6 — What actions of a public servant amount to misconduct? Is it necessary to obtain previous sanction for prosecution under the PC Act, 1988?
Question: What actions of a public servant amount to misconduct? Is it necessary to obtain previous sanction for prosecution under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988?
(1) “Criminal misconduct” — s.13

Under the pre-2018 s.13(1), a public servant committed criminal misconduct if he: (a) habitually accepted/obtained gratification from persons dealing with him; (b) accepted gratification by corrupt or illegal means; (c) accepted gratification by exercise of personal influence; (d) obtained any valuable thing/pecuniary advantage by abusing his position (with the benefit/public-interest provisos); or (e) held resources/property disproportionate to known sources of income which he could not satisfactorily account for. Punishment under s.13(2): after Act 1 of 2014, 4–10 years + fine (earlier 1–7 years).

Under the amended s.13 (26-7-2018), only two forms remain: (a) dishonest or fraudulent misappropriation/conversion of entrusted property, and (b) intentional illicit enrichment during office (Explanation 1 — presumption from unaccounted disproportionate assets; Explanation 2 — known sources = lawful sources). The deleted conduct migrated to ss.7, 7A and 11.

Other misconduct/offences: taking bribery (s.7), influence-peddling (s.7A), receiving gifts/undue advantage from persons with business before the officer (s.11), abetment (s.12), attempt (s.15), habitual re-offending (s.14). Kakkar cautions that a pure service-rule breach (e.g. unauthorised private practice/medical fee) is departmental misconduct, not criminal misconduct.

(2) Previous sanction — s.19

Yes, as a rule: no court can take cognizance of offences under ss.7, 11, 13 and 15 (pre-2018: 7, 10, 11, 13, 15) alleged against a sitting public servant without sanction of the authority competent to remove him (Central Government, State Government, or other competent authority under s.19(1)(c)). Sanction is a “solemn and sacrosanct act” requiring application of mind (Antulay); absence/irregularity does not upset a completed trial unless it caused failure of justice (s.19(3)), and the objection must be raised early (s.19(4)).

Exceptions as the cases stood: (i) Kalicharan Mahapatra (1998) — no sanction needed if the officer has retired/ceased to hold office before cognizance; (ii) Antulay/Chautala — no sanction if at cognizance he holds a different office than the one abused; (iii) private-person offences (bribe giver ss.8–10, middleman s.7A) need no sanction. The 2018 amendment changed (i) and (ii): the Explanation to s.19(1) now covers retired/transferred officers, with a 3+1-month decision period; Katti Nagaseshanna holds the change prospective.

DU 2015 · Question 8(b) — Short note: Public servant under the PC Act, 1988
Question: Write a short note on “Public Servant” under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988.

Section 2(c) defines “public servant” by twelve inclusive clauses: (i) persons in service/pay of Government or remunerated by fees/commission for public duty; (ii) employees of local authorities; (iii) employees of statutory corporations, Government-aided/controlled bodies and Government companies; (iv) Judges/adjudicatory functionaries; (v) court-authorised persons (receiver, liquidator, commissioner); (vi) arbitrators; (vii) election functionaries; (viii) any office-holder authorised/required to perform a public duty; (ix) office-bearers of Government-aided co-operative societies; (x) members/employees of Service Commissions and selection/examination committees; (xi) university officers, teachers and examination personnel; (xii) office-bearers/employees of aided educational, scientific, social, cultural institutions.

Explanation 1: coverage irrespective of Government appointment. Explanation 2: de facto possession of the situation suffices despite defect in title. The general-code definition is IPC s.21, now BNS s.2(28); the PC Act definition is wider and special. Case contours: Ministers/CM (Karunanidhi, 1979); superior-court judges with CJI-consultation/President-sanction safeguards (Veeraswami, 1991); MPs/MLAs with no Article 105 immunity for bribery (P.V. Narasimha Rao, 1998); officers of private banks performing public duty (Ramesh Gelli, 2016). Status is determined at the time of the offence (Kalicharan), and s.1(2) extends the Act to Indian citizens abroad.

DU (May be 2016) · Question 5(a) — Define ‘Public Servant’ and ‘Illegal Gratification’
Question: Define ‘Public Servant’ and ‘Illegal Gratification’ under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988.

Public servant: as s.2(c) (twelve clauses + Explanations 1–2; detailed in the 2015 answer above) — organised around three relationships: service/pay, holding of an office carrying public duty, and State-funded/statutory institutional position. “Public duty” (s.2(b)) is a duty in the discharge of which the State, public or community has an interest, and its Explanation sweeps in statutory corporations, aided/controlled bodies and Government companies.

Illegal/undue gratification: pre-2018 the Act punished acceptance of “gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration” (old s.7) and “valuable thing or pecuniary advantage” (old s.13(1)(d)). The 2018 amendment codified this as “undue advantage” in s.2(d): any gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration; the explanation adds that gratification is not limited to pecuniary benefits and that legal remuneration includes anything the officer is permitted by his organisation to receive. Per Madhukar Bhaskarrao Joshi (2000), “gratification” means any payment giving satisfaction — it need not be “reward”; reward is the conclusion drawn by the s.20 presumption once acceptance is proved. It covers cash, gifts, property below market price, jobs, travel, favours and non-monetary benefits. Kakkar holds a genuine professional consultation fee is not illegal gratification for an official act; R.R.S. Seth holds a demand tied to a government-hospital function is.

DU (May be 2016) · Question 6(b) — Problem: MP “M” with disproportionate assets; sanction objection
Question: M was an MP in Lok Sabha from Karol Bagh, Delhi. He had earned money and property highly disproportionate to his known sources. An FIR was lodged and he was put on trial. M objected that no previous sanction had been taken though he is a public servant representing South Delhi Lok Sabha. Decide.

Step 1 — public servant: MPs are public servants under s.2(c) (read with s.2(b) public duty), held in P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI) (1998), which also ruled there is no Article 105 immunity from bribery prosecutions.

Step 2 — charge: disproportionate assets fall under old s.13(1)(e) (criminal misconduct) r/w s.13(2); after 2018, s.13(1)(b) illicit enrichment with the Explanation 1 presumption.

Step 3 — sanction: s.19 requires sanction of the authority competent to remove. No authority is competent to remove an MP (the House can expel its member but that is not “removal from office” by an appointing authority). P.V. Narasimha Rao therefore held the court can take cognizance without s.19 sanction; however, until Parliament legislates, the prosecuting agency must obtain the permission of the Speaker, Lok Sabha / Chairman, Rajya Sabha before filing the charge-sheet — a procedural protection.

Step 4 — the different-constituency point: M’s objection that he “represents South Delhi” while the assets relate to the Karol Bagh tenure mirrors the Antulay/Chautala situation: where the check period relates to an office no longer held, pre-2018 law required no sanction at cognizance (Abhay Singh Chautala v. CBI, (2011) 7 SCC 141). Post the 2018 Explanation to s.19, the competent authority for the office abused at the time of commission is to be approached; for an MP that constitutional gap still leaves the Speaker-permission route.

Conclusion: M’s objection fails — the trial is valid without s.19 sanction, subject to Speaker/Chairman permission for the charge-sheet; the assets are to be assessed check-period-wise against known lawful sources, and unaccounted excess triggers the statutory presumption.

DU (May be 2016) · Question 8(b) — Problem: illicit wealth of former CM/Ministers — CBI vs Enforcement Directorate
Question: There was amassing of illicit wealth by a former Chief Minister and former Ministers of a State; the amount was unprecedented but there was no clear allegation of laundering. It led to investment in property. The matter was referred to the CBI, opposed as being the ED’s exclusive domain. Decide.

(1) Distinct regimes. The PC Act, 1988 punishes criminal misconduct/illicit enrichment (old s.13(1)(e); new s.13(1)(b)) by public servants — accumulation of assets disproportionate to known lawful sources. The Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA) punishes “projection” or claiming of tainted property as untainted (s.2(1)(u) “proceeds of crime”; s.3), which presupposes a scheduled predicate offence and a process/activity connected with the proceeds; mere possession/investment of disproportionate assets without such projection is the PC Act offence, not laundering.

(2) Who investigates. PC Act DA cases are investigated by the CBI/ACB under s.17 (SP-level authorisation for the DA clause); attachment of PC Act property follows the 1944 Ordinance route via s.18A (Special Judge as District Judge), “save as otherwise provided in PMLA”. The ED’s PMLA jurisdiction arises only if laundering is made out; it is not an exclusive forum and does not oust the PC Act machinery.

(3) Application. On the facts there is no clear allegation of laundering, only amassing and property investment by a former CM/Ministers — the CBI is the correct and competent agency (a Supreme Court direction or State consent under DSPE s.6 resolves territorial issues where needed, as in the JBT/Chautala probe). The ED may act later/parallelly if evidence of projection/concealment emerges; the statutes operate in addition (s.28 PC Act; PMLA is in addition to other law). The opposition fails; the CBI reference is valid and must proceed; nothing bars parallel ED proceedings if a scheduled offence + laundering is later disclosed.

DU 2017 · Question 6(a) — Define ‘Public Servant’ with special reference to decided cases
Question: Define ‘Public Servant’ under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 with special reference to decided cases.

Open with s.2(c) (twelve clauses; Explanations 1–2) and s.2(b) “public duty” (State/public/community interest; deemed-State Explanation). Then the case map:

  • M. Karunanidhi v. UOI (1979): Chief Minister and Ministers are in the pay of Government and are public servants.
  • K. Veeraswami v. UOI (1991) 3 SCC 655: High Court and Supreme Court judges are public servants; CJI consultation/assent precedes registration of an FIR; President grants sanction — judicial independence safeguards.
  • P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (CBI) (1998): MPs (and by parity MLAs) are public servants; no Article 105 privilege for bribe-voting; absent a removing authority, cognizance without s.19 sanction but with Speaker/Chairman permission before charge-sheet.
  • Kalicharan Mahapatra v. State of Orissa, AIR 1998 SC 2595: status is tested when the offence is committed; a retired officer remains triable (though pre-2018 he needed no s.19 sanction).
  • CBI, Bank Securities & Fraud Cell v. Ramesh Gelli, (2016) 3 SCC 788: chairman/directors/officers of a private bank are public servants under s.2(c)(viii) read with s.2(b) — banking is a regulated public function over depositors’ money (s.46A BR Act, 1949).

Conclude with the functional test: “service/pay” or “office carrying public duty” — not the employer’s label — decides; aided institutions, cooperatives, universities, arbitrators and election staff are covered expressly; de facto officers by Explanation 2.

DU 2017 · Question 6(b) — Problem: Joint Secretary (Home) bribed while in Finance Ministry; sanction objection
Question: Sh. Rajender is working as Joint Secretary in the Home Ministry. An FIR is lodged by Sh. Sanjeev for taking bribe when Rajender was working in the Finance Ministry. The trial is opposed for want of previous sanction from the competent authority. Decide.

Pre-2018 answer — R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay (1984) and Abhay Singh Chautala v. CBI (2011): sanction is tied to the office alleged to have been abused and to the authority competent to remove the accused from that office. The offence arose in the Finance Ministry posting; at cognizance Rajender holds a different office (Joint Secretary, Home) whose removing authority is different in the sanction chain. Where the officer abused one office and holds another at cognizance, the Constitution Bench held no sanction is required — the current office’s authority cannot be asked to protect/assess conduct in a past office, and requiring plural sanctions would be absurd. The trial was therefore valid without sanction.

Post-2018 answer — amended s.19: clauses (a)/(b) now read “employed, or as the case may be, was at the time of commission of the alleged offence employed”, and the Explanation includes a person who “has ceased to hold the office… and is holding an office other than the office during which the offence is alleged”. Sanction is therefore now required even on transfer, sought from the authority competent to remove him at the time of commission (Finance Ministry/Central Government — for a Joint Secretary, the Appointments Committee/Central Government), to be decided within 3+1 months.

Decision: for an exam answer give both regimes: under the law considered in the prescribed cases the objection fails and the trial proceeds (Chautala); under the 2018 amendment, cognizance requires the Central Government’s sanction relatable to the Finance-tenure office. Absence of sanction for a sitting officer without the exception is a bar to cognizance, though s.19(3) protects completed trials unless failure of justice resulted.

DU 2018 · Question 3(a) — Criminal misconduct + changes in s.19 by amendment
Question: What actions of a public servant amount to criminal misconduct? Enumerate the changes made in Section 19 by the amendment to the PC Act, 1988.
Criminal misconduct (s.13)

Old five clauses (habitual gratification; gratification by corrupt/illegal means; personal influence; abuse of position for valuable thing/pecuniary advantage; disproportionate assets) — all punishable under s.13(2), raised to 4–10 years by Act 1 of 2014. New s.13 (2018): only (a) dishonest/fraudulent misappropriation or conversion of entrusted property and (b) intentional illicit enrichment; Explanation 1 creates the presumption from disproportionate assets unaccounted for; Explanation 2 limits known sources to lawful income. Deleted conduct now sits under ss.7, 7A and 11.

Changes in s.19 by the 2018 Amendment
  1. Sections list narrowed: “sections 7, 10, 11, 13 and 15” became “sections 7, 11, 13 and 15” (old s.10 disappeared in re-enactment).
  2. Retired/transferred officers covered: clauses (a)/(b) changed to “who is employed, or was at the time of commission employed”; new Explanation includes (a) those who ceased to hold the abused office and (b) those holding a different office — a legislative reversal of Kalicharan/Chautala.
  3. Lokpal saving: chapeau “save as otherwise provided in the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013” (added 2014).
  4. Private-complainant route regulated: prior court complaint not dismissed under s.203 CrPC; opportunity of hearing to the public servant before private-party-initiated sanction.
  5. Time-bound decision: endeavour to decide within three months, extendable by one month for recorded reasons (legal consultation); Central guidelines permitted.
DU 2018 · Question 4(a) — Border of ‘public servant’ and ‘illegal gratification’ with reference to Kalicharan Mahapatra
Question: Define the border of ‘public servant’ and “illegal gratification’ with reference to the law laid down in Kalicharan Mahapatra v. State of Orissa, AIR 1998 SC 2595.

Candour about the case: the faculty answer-key itself notes that the expression “illegal gratification” was not discussed — nor even used — in Kalicharan Mahapatra; the case is a sanction/status authority. The “border” each concept draws must therefore be stated from the statute and other cases, with Kalicharan supplying the temporal boundary of public-servant status.

Border of “public servant” (from Kalicharan): (i) the outer temporal border is the date of commission of the offence — the accused must be a public servant when he commits the act; he cannot commit the offence after demitting office, but remains triable for what he did in office; (ii) the s.19 protection border is the date of cognizance — pre-2018, if he has ceased to hold office by then, no sanction; (iii) the Court rejected the absurdity of an officer offending just before retirement to escape; (iv) s.2(c)’s conceptual border is public duty/service-pay (clauses i–xii; Explanations 1–2), refined by Ramesh Gelli (functional public duty in private banks).

Border of “illegal gratification”: any gratification other than legal remuneration (old s.7; new s.2(d) “undue advantage”), not limited to money. Its border with lawful receipts was fixed in Kakkar — a professional fee for a private professional act is not illegal gratification for an official act, even if received in breach of service rules; whereas money tied to an official function (R.R.S. Seth; ration-card illustration to s.7) crosses the border. “Gratification” need not mean reward (Madhukar Joshi); once acceptance is proved, s.20 presumes the motive/reward.

DU 2018 · Question 4(b) — Discuss the law laid down in Kanwarjit Singh Kakkar v. State of Punjab
Question: Discuss the law laid down in Kanwarjit Singh Kakkar v. State of Punjab, (2011) 6 SCC 895, referring to the PC Act, 1988.

(Answer as the full case analysis above; in exam form state:) Two government doctors were FIR-bound for evening private practice charging ₹100 consultation, under s.13(1)(d) r/w s.13(2) PC Act and s.168 IPC; the High Court refused to quash relying on Rule 15 of the Punjab Civil Medical (State Service Class I) Rules, 1972. The Supreme Court (Katju & Misra JJ.) quashed the FIR, laying down:

  1. Definition of corruption: corruption is acceptance/demand of illegal gratification for an official act; a consultation fee is professional remuneration, not illegal gratification; treating medical fees as bribery would be preposterous and against common sense.
  2. Service breach ≠ crime: if unpermitted private practice violates Rule 15/service rules, the remedy is departmental proceedings under the Punjab Civil Services (Punishment & Appeal) Rules — not the IPC or PC Act.
  3. s.168 IPC (now BNS s.202) not attracted: treating patients is professional duty, not “trade” (cf. Maheshkumar Thakkar).
  4. Contrast R.R.S. Seth (2008) 11 SCC 681: demanding ₹500 for proper treatment of an admitted government-hospital patient (even via a ward boy) is PC Act bribery; once money passes for the official act, the burden shifts to the officer to explain it (B. Noha; Madhukar Joshi).
  5. The State may proceed departmentally; criminal process for mere professional service would abuse court process.
DU 2019 · Question 2(a) — What actions amount to criminal misconduct?
Question: What actions of a public servant amount to criminal misconduct under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988? Discuss.

Discuss s.13 in both regimes (old five clauses (a)–(e); new two-clause s.13(1): misappropriation/conversion and illicit enrichment with the disproportionate-assets presumption), with s.13(2) sentence (4–10 years post-2014). Add the boundaries: (i) Kakkar — rule-breaching professional fees are service misconduct, not criminal; (ii) the presumption in new Explanation 1 shifts the evidential burden on accounting for assets; (iii) connected offences ss.7, 7A, 11, 12, 14, 15; (iv) attempt to s.13(1)(a) under s.15 (2–5 years); (v) investigation of the enrichment clause requires SP-level order (s.17 proviso 2), sanction s.19, Special Judge trial with day-to-day hearing and the 2-year/4-year target; (vi) attachment of the illicit property under s.18A/1944 Ordinance and parallel PMLA. Conclude with the policy shift: 2018 narrowed the elastic “abuse of position” clause and paired the statute with s.17A decision-protection to reduce harassment while sharpening enrichment and bribery liability.

DU 2019 · Question 7 — Problem: police officer raided 12-5-1990; charge-sheet after retirement — sanction?
Question: A raid was conducted at the residence of a police officer on 12.05.1990 and a good amount of cash and jewellery was recovered. The charge-sheet under the PC Act was submitted after retirement of the accused police officer. Whether sanction is required for his prosecution? Discuss with provisions and supporting cases.

The facts are on all fours with Kalicharan Mahapatra v. State of Orissa, AIR 1998 SC 2595 (raid 12-5-1990; retirement 31-12-1990; charge-sheet 30-9-1992 under s.13(2) r/w s.13(1)(e)).

Position under the unamended 1988 Act: (i) the Act applies — public-servant status is required at the time of the offence, not trial; offences do not “vanish” on retirement; (ii) no previous sanction is needed if the officer has ceased to be a public servant when cognizance is taken — s.19(1)(c)’s authority “competent to remove him” cannot exist post-retirement, following S.A. Venkataraman (1958), C.R. Bansi (1971), Manmal Bhutoria (1977) and K. Veeraswami (1991); (iii) Parliament deliberately kept s.19 wording unlike s.197 CrPC (“is or was”) because PC offences are not official acts. Answer: no sanction; trial valid.

Position after 26-7-2018: the Explanation to amended s.19(1) includes a person who “has ceased to hold the office”, and clauses (a)/(b) say “is or was employed”; sanction is now required even after retirement, from the authority competent at the time of commission, decided within 3+1 months (Katti Nagaseshanna, Telangana HC 16-11-2018 — prospective operation). Note the distinction with s.197 CrPC → s.218 BNSS which protected retired officials from 1973 onwards (41st LCI Report, para 15.123).

Conclusion: on the 1990 facts, the officer’s sanction objection fails (Kalicharan); a post-2018 cognizance on a post-2018 offence would require sanction notwithstanding retirement — the 2018 amendment is the legislative overruling of Kalicharan’s sanction ruling, while its holding that retired officers remain triable is untouched and strengthened.

Examination writing tips

  1. Always state both regimes for sanction and s.13 problems: the four prescribed cases interpret the pre-2018 sections; conclude each answer with the 2018 amendment position (“Kalicharan/Chautala no longer govern sanction after 26-7-2018; Katti Nagaseshanna is prospective”).
  2. For trap problems lead with demand → acceptance → recovery → presumption, and quote Satyanarayana Murthy/Shanthamma: recovery alone is fatal to a conviction.
  3. For definition questions quote the clause and attach one case per category (CM; judge; MP; retired; private banker).
  4. For doctor/teacher private-practice problems apply Kakkar: professional remuneration ≠ illegal gratification; contrast R.R.S. Seth.
  5. Use statutory language: “undue advantage”, “legal remuneration”, “public duty”, “authority competent to remove”, “at the time when the offence was alleged to have been committed”, “failure of justice”.
  6. In institutional answers give the full chain: SPE 1941 → DSPE Act 1946 → CBI 1963 → Santhanam/CVC 1964 → Vineet Narain → CVC Act 2003 → Lokpal 2013 → s.17A in 2018.
  7. Mention UNCAC article mapping (15→ss.7/8; 18→s.7A; 17→s.13(1)(a); 20→s.13(1)(b); 26→s.9; Art.16 not yet codified — LCI 258th Report) for the “prescribed reading” marks.
Doctrinal cautions for accuracy:
  • The prescribed cases cite old s.7 (taking gratification other than legal remuneration) and old s.13(1)(d)/(e); do not quote new s.7/clause (b) numbering when narrating them.
  • Old s.24 (bribe-giver statement immunity) was omitted in 2018; today only the compelled + 7-day-report rule protects the harassed giver.
  • s.17A approval is for recommendations/decisions, not traps with on-spot acceptance; it does not bar FIR registration/preliminary enquiry (Anil Kumar line).
  • BNSS s.218 has a 120-day deemed-sanction proviso; PC Act s.19 has the 3+1-month formula — do not conflate.
  • Private-bank employees are covered by Ramesh Gelli on public-duty reasoning; not every private employee is a public servant.
