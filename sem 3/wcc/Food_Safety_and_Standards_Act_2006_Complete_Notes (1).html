1 Introduction, Object & Constitutional Foundation

The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (FSS Act) is a comprehensive, consolidated and integrated law that replaced a fragmented multi-departmental regime of food regulation in India. It was published in the Official Gazette on 23 August 2006; its provisions were brought into force on different dates by different notifications. It extends to the whole of India, including the Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh.

📜 The Preamble

"An Act to consolidate the laws relating to food and to establish the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India for laying down science-based standards for articles of food and to regulate their manufacture, storage, distribution, sale and import, to ensure availability of safe and wholesome food for human consumption and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto."

🗂️ What it repeals (Second Schedule → S.97)

  • The Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954
  • Fruit Products Order, 1955
  • Meat Food Products Order, 1973
  • Vegetable Oil Products (Control) Order, 1947
  • Edible Oils Packaging (Regulation) Order, 1998
  • Solvent Extracted Oil, De-oiled Meal & Edible Flour (Control) Order, 1967
  • Milk and Milk Products Order, 1992
  • Any other order under the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 relating to food

📚 Related Penal Law

  • Section 272, IPC — adulteration of food/drink intended for sale.
  • Section 273, IPC — sale of noxious food/drink.
  • Law Commission Report No. 264 (2017) — recommended amending Ss. 272–273 IPC (submitted in light of Swami Achyutanand Tirth).
💡 Reasons for enactment (LCI Report 264, para 4.1–4.2) There were numerous pre-constitutional and post-constitutional laws, orders and rules on food safety that complemented and supplemented each other — but because of multiplicity of specifications/standards and administration by different departments/agencies, there were implementation problems and safety standards lost importance over time. The FSS Act therefore established a single reference point and moved from multi-level, multi-departmental control to a single line of command.

Salient Features of the Act

✔️ Movement to an integrated line of command

From multi-level, multi-departmental control to a single integrated command (FSSAI).

✔️ Self-compliance regime

Shift from a purely regulatory regime to self-compliance through Food Safety Management Systems.

✔️ Responsibility on Food Business Operators

FBOs must ensure food processed, manufactured, imported or distributed complies with domestic food laws.

✔️ Graded penalties

Graded penalties according to gravity of offence — civil penalties for minor offences; punishment for serious violations.

🔎 Key statutory facts • The Act contains 101 sections and two Schedules.
• First Schedule → relates to S.5(1)(e); divides States/UTs into five zones (for rotation of State-representative members).
• Second Schedule → relates to S.97 (Repeal & Saving).
• The Central Government issued the Food Safety and Standards Rules, 2011 under the Act.

2 Definitions (Section 3)

The FSS Act, 2006 is an interpretation-heavy statute. Its definitions are wider, more inclusive and more "pervasive" than the corresponding definitions in the repealed Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 — a difference repeatedly emphasised by the courts in the decided cases.

A. “Food” — Section 3(1)(j)

The definition of "food" is the most important definition in the Act. It has three parts — (1) the main clause, (2) the inclusion clause, and (3) the exclusion clause.

“food” means any substance, whether processed, partially processed or unprocessed, which is intended for human consumption, and includes primary food to the extent defined in clause (zk), genetically modified or engineered food or food containing such ingredients, infant food, packaged drinking water, alcoholic drink, chewing gum, and any substance, including water used into the food during its manufacture, preparation or treatment — but does not include any animal feed, live animals unless they are prepared or processed for placing on the market for human consumption, plants prior to harvesting, drugs and medicinal products, cosmetics, narcotic or psychotropic substances.

① Main clause

Any substance — processed, partially processed or unprocessed — intended for human consumption.

② Includes…

  • Primary food [to extent defined in cl.(zk)]
  • Genetically modified / engineered food
  • Infant food
  • Packaged drinking water
  • Alcoholic drink
  • Chewing gum
  • Any substance incl. water used in manufacture/preparation/treatment

③ But does not include…

  • Animal feed
  • Live animals (unless prepared/processed for human market)
  • Plants prior to harvesting
  • Drugs & medicinal products
  • Cosmetics
  • Narcotic / psychotropic substances
📌 Proviso to S.3(1)(j) The Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, declare any other article as food for the purposes of the Act, having regard to its use, nature, substance or quality.

Related definition — “Primary Food”, S.3(1)(zk)

“primary food” means an article of food, being a produce of agriculture or horticulture or animal husbandry and dairying or aquaculture in its natural form, resulting from the growing, raising, cultivation, picking, harvesting, collection or catching in the hands of a person other than a farmer or fisherman.

Related definition — “Food Safety”, S.3(1)(q)

“food safety” means assurance that food is acceptable for human consumption according to its intended use.
⚖️ How the courts read the definition of “food” (decided cases) Pyarali K. Tejani v. Mahadeo Ramchandra Dange (1974): "…the word food is a very general term and applies to all that is eaten by man for nourishment and takes in subsidiaries. Is supari eaten with relish by man for taste and nourishment? It is." And so it is food.

State of T.N. v. R. Krishnamurthy (1980): To be "food", an article need not be fit for consumption, nor described as intended for consumption — it is enough if it is generally or commonly used for human consumption or in preparation of human food.

Bishan Das Mehta v. UoI (1970, Del HC): It is not essential that the article be consumed as such — if it enters into the composition/preparation of human food (e.g., katha used in paan), it is food. The definition is worded very widely.

Ramlal v. State of Rajasthan (2001): An article which is food does not lose its character as food merely because it is also used or sold for other purposes (e.g., camel's milk).

“adulterant” means any material which is or could be employed for making the food unsafe or sub-standard or misbranded or containing extraneous matter.

C. “Contaminant” — Section 3(1)(g)

“contaminant” means any substance, whether or not added to food, but which is present in such food as a result of the production (including operations carried out in crop husbandry, animal husbandry or veterinary medicine), manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packing, packaging, transport or holding of such food or as a result of environmental contamination — and does not include insect fragments, rodent hairs and other extraneous matter.
🧪 Key points on “contaminant” • It is present whether or not added — i.e., it need not be intentionally added.
• It arises from production, manufacture, processing, packing, transport, holding, or from environmental contamination.
• It excludes insect fragments, rodent hairs and extraneous matter (which are separately covered by S.3(i) "extraneous matter").

D. “Food Business” (S.3(1)(n)) and “Food Business Operator” (S.3(1)(o))

Food Business — S.3(1)(n)

"food business" means any undertaking, whether for profit or not and whether public or private, carrying out any of the activities related to any stage of manufacture, processing, packaging, storage, transportation, distribution of food, import and includes food services, catering services, sale of food or food ingredients.

Note the deliberate breadth — "whether for profit or not" and "whether public or private" bring charitable, NGO, temple and household-scale food ventures within the net.

Food Business Operator (FBO) — S.3(1)(o)

"food business operator", in relation to food business, means a person by whom the business is carried on or owned and is responsible for ensuring the compliance of this Act, rules and regulations made thereunder.

The FBO is the central duty-holder — the Act imposes primary responsibility for food-safety compliance squarely on the FBO.

E. “Misbranded Food” — Section 3(1)(zf)

The definition operates through three limbs (A), (B) and (C):

Limb (A) — Misrepresentation / deceptive selling

An article is misbranded if it is purported, or represented to be, or is being —

  • (i) offered or promoted for sale with false, misleading or deceptive claims either (a) upon the label of the package, or (b) through advertisement; or
  • (ii) sold by a name which belongs to another article of food; or
  • (iii) offered or promoted for sale under the name of a fictitious individual or company as the manufacturer or producer borne on the package/label.

Limb (B) — Deceptive packaging by a manufacturer/producer

Where the article is sold in packages sealed/prepared by or at the instance of the manufacturer or producer bearing his name and address, but —

  • (i) the article is an imitation of, or substitute for, or resembles in a manner likely to deceive another article of food, and is not plainly and conspicuously labelled so as to indicate its true character; or
  • (ii) the package/label bears any false or misleading statement, design or device regarding ingredients or substances, or is otherwise deceptive as to contents; or
  • (iii) the article is offered for sale as the product of a place or country which is false.

Limb (C) — Labelling deficiencies

Where the article contained in the package —

  • (i) contains any artificial flavouring, colouring or chemical preservative and the package is without a declaratory label stating that fact, or is not labelled per the Act/regulations or is in contravention; or
  • (ii) is offered for sale for special dietary uses, unless its label bears the specified information concerning vitamins, minerals or other dietary properties; or
  • (iii) is not conspicuously or correctly stated on the outside within the limits of variability laid down under the Act.
💰 Penalty for misbranded food — Section 52 (1) Any person who manufactures for sale, stores, sells, distributes or imports any misbranded article of food for human consumption shall be liable to a penalty up to ₹3 lakh.
(2) The Adjudicating Officer may direct the person found guilty to take corrective action to rectify the mistake, or the article of food shall be destroyed.

Duties & Functions of the Food Authority — Section 16

S.16(1) — Primary duty

To regulate and monitor the manufacture, processing, distribution, sale and import of food so as to ensure safe and wholesome food.

S.16(2) — Power to make regulations (specific matters)

The Food Authority may by regulations specify —

  • (b) limits for food additives, crop contaminants, pesticide residues, veterinary drug residues, heavy metals, processing aids, mycotoxins, antibiotics, pharmacological active substances, irradiation
  • (c) mechanisms & guidelines for accreditation of certification bodies (food safety management systems)
  • (d) procedure & quality control for imported food
  • (e) procedure for accreditation of laboratories & notification of accredited labs
  • (f) method of sampling, analysis & exchange of information
  • (g) surveys of enforcement
  • (h) food-labelling standards incl. health/nutrition claims
  • (i) manner/procedure of risk analysis, assessment, communication & management

  • Assist in crisis management and draw up a general plan for crisis management.
  • Establish a network of organisations for scientific cooperation.
  • Improve cooperation with international organisations.
  • Ensure public/consumers/Panchayats receive rapid, reliable, objective information.
  • Provide training programmes in food safety for food business personnel.
  • Undertake tasks assigned by Central Government.
  • Contribute to development of international technical standards (sanitary & phytosanitary).
  • Promote coordination of work on food standards & consistency between international and domestic standards.
  • Promote general awareness of food safety and standards.

Central Advisory Committee — Sections 11 & 12

The Food Authority shall, by notification, establish a Central Advisory Committee (S.11); its functions (coordination & advice) are in S.12.

Scientific Committee & Scientific Panels — Sections 13 & 14

Scientific Panels (S.13)

The Food Authority establishes Scientific Panels of independent scientific experts to develop standards and give scientific opinions. There are currently 21 panels — vertical (e.g., milk & milk products) and horizontal (e.g., pesticide residues).

Scientific Committee (S.14)

A statutory body of 6 independent experts (nominated by the Food Authority, not belonging to any panel) plus the Chairpersons of all Scientific Panels. It acts as the link between the Panels and the Food Authority, examining the panels' recommendations and recommending them for approval — the highest scientific body advising the Food Authority.

2. State Food Safety Authorities — Commissioner of Food Safety (Section 30)

Section 30 — Commissioner of Food Safety

For the purpose of this Act, the State Government shall appoint a Commissioner of Food Safety for the State. He is the chief enforcement authority at the State level and is bound by the directions of the Food Authority [S.16(5)].

  • The Commissioner may, by order, appoint the Designated Officer (S.36) and Food Safety Officers (S.37) and Food Analysts (S.45).
  • Under S.30, in cases of food risk to health, the Commissioner shall take appropriate steps to inform the general public of the nature of the risk [read with S.18(1)(f)].
  • He may issue guidelines for exercise of powers by Food Safety Officers, which are binding [S.38(10)].
📌 CAG Audit Report (Dec 2017) — Implementation shortcomings (1) FSSAI failed to identify "grey areas" where standards are yet to be framed/revised. (2) Licences issued on incomplete documents in >50% of cases checked. (3) Failure to ensure customs authorities keep unsafe foods out. (4) 65 of 72 state food laboratories lack NABL accreditation. (5) Acute shortage of staff — recruitment rules not framed even a decade after enactment.

4 Food Safety Officer — Power, Functions & Liabilities (Ss. 37–42)

Appointment & Qualifications — Section 37

“Food Safety Officer” [S.3(1)(t)] means an officer appointed under Section 37. S.37(1): The Commissioner of Food Safety shall, by notification, appoint such persons as he thinks fit, having the qualifications prescribed by the Central Government, as Food Safety Officers for such local areas as he may assign to them. S.37(2): The State Government may authorise any officer of the State Government having the prescribed qualifications to perform the functions of a Food Safety Officer within a specified jurisdiction.

Qualifications (Food Safety & Standards Rules, 2011, para 2.1.3 — made under S.91(2)(b)): a whole-time officer holding a degree in Food Technology / Dairy Technology / Biotechnology / Oil Technology / Agricultural Science / Veterinary Sciences / Bio-Chemistry / Microbiology, or a Master's degree in Chemistry, or a degree in Medicine from a recognised university (or other notified equivalent qualification).

Enforcement role — Section 29(4) & (5)

The Food Safety Officers enforce and execute the provisions of the Act within their area where the duty is not expressly/implicitly imposed on some other authority (S.29(4)); the regulations specify which officers enforce which provisions (S.29(5)).

Powers of the Food Safety Officer — Section 38

Liability of the Food Safety Officer — Section 39

S.39: Any Food Safety Officer who — (a) vexatiously and without reasonable ground seizes any article of food or adulterant; or (b) commits any other act to the injury of any person without having reason to believe that such act is necessary for the execution of his duty, shall be guilty of an offence under this Act and shall be liable to a penalty up to ₹1 lakh. PROVISO: where a false complaint is made against a Food Safety Officer and it is proved, the complainant shall be punishable with fine not less than ₹50,000, extendable to ₹1 lakh.

This creates a two-way accountability — it penalises the officer for abuse of power, and simultaneously deters false complaints against officers.

Procedure for launching prosecution — Section 42

(1) The Food Safety Officer shall be responsible for inspection of food business, drawing samples and sending them to the Food Analyst for analysis. (2) Where the Food Analyst's report shows contravention, the Food Analyst forwards the report to the Designated Officer to follow the prosecution procedure (read with S.40(2)).

🔗 Liabilities summarised The FSO is liable to a penalty up to ₹1 lakh (S.39) for vexatious seizure or injurious acts without reasonable belief; and a false complainant is liable to a fine of ₹50,000–₹1 lakh (proviso to S.39). The FSO is also under a duty to follow the CrPC procedure for search/inspection (S.38(5)) and must have witnesses present (S.38(7)).

5 Food Analyst — Section 45

Appointment — Section 45

S.45(1): The Commissioner of Food Safety shall, by notification, appoint such persons as he thinks fit, having the qualifications prescribed by the Central Government, to be Food Analysts for such local areas as may be assigned to them. S.45(2): The Food Analyst shall exercise the powers and perform the functions conferred on him under this Act (namely Ss.40, 41, 43, 44, 46 & 47) and the rules/regulations.

Qualifications (FSS Rules 2011, para 2.1.4)

A whole-time officer holding a Master's degree in Chemistry / Food Technology / Microbiology / Bio-Chemistry / Dairy Technology / Veterinary Science / Agricultural Science or equivalent, with prescribed experience (or such other notified qualifications).

Where must analysis be done?

A "food laboratory" [S.3(1)(p)] must satisfy a twin test: (i) accredited by NABL, and (ii) recognised & notified by the Food Authority under S.43. Section 43(1) mandates that the Food Analyst analyse food only in such a laboratory.

Functions of the Food Analyst — Section 46 (and related Ss. 40, 41, 43, 44, 47)

Receives sample from FSO / purchaser
→
Analyses in NABL-accredited + notified laboratory (S.43)
→
Furnishes a written report in prescribed form
→
Report → Designated Officer (S.42) for prosecution if contravention found

Sampling & analysis procedure — Section 47

Section 47 prescribes the procedure of sampling and analysis. Under S.47(1), when a Food Safety Officer takes a sample he must — divide it into parts, seal and mark them, send one part to the Food Analyst, and send two parts to the Designated Officer for further reference/appeal; the Food Safety Officer must also send the sample "in the prescribed manner" so that the vendor/manufacturer gets an opportunity to have the sample tested. Section 46(4) gives the right of appeal to the referral food laboratory.

⚠️ Why S.47 and S.43 were decisive in the Maggi case The Bombay High Court held that S.43(1) is mandatory, not directory: if food is tested in a laboratory not accredited by NABL / not recognised & notified by the Food Authority, the analysis cannot be relied upon. It also held the mandatory sampling procedure under S.47(1) had not been followed. (Discussed in detail in the case-law section below.)
📎 Relationship to S.40 The Food Analyst also analyses samples submitted by a purchaser under Section 40; and if the sample is found non-compliant, the Food Analyst forwards the report to the Designated Officer to follow the S.42 prosecution procedure.

6 General Principles to be Followed for Food Safety — Section 18

Section 18 is the philosophical and operational compass of the entire Act. It binds the Central Government, State Governments, the Food Authority and other agencies. It has three clauses.

Fig. 4 — The three clauses of Section 18.

Section 18(1) — Principles during implementation

(a) Appropriate level of protection

Endeavour to achieve an appropriate level of protection of human life and health and protection of consumers' interests, including fair practices in all kinds of food trade, with reference to food safety standards and practices.

(b) Risk management

Carry out risk management, taking into account the results of risk assessment and other relevant factors, to achieve the general objectives of the regulations.

(c) Precautionary principle

Where harm is identified but scientific uncertainty persists, adopt provisional risk-management measures pending further scientific information for a more comprehensive risk assessment.

(d) Proportionality

Measures under cl.(c) must be proportionate and no more restrictive of trade than required, having regard to technical/economic feasibility and other reasonable factors.

(e) Review of measures

Measures adopted shall be reviewed within a reasonable period, depending on the nature of the risk and the type of scientific information needed.

(f) Duty to inform the public

Where there are reasonable grounds to suspect a food may present a risk to health, the Food Authority and the Commissioner of Food Safety shall inform the general public of the nature of the risk, the food/type of food involved, and measures taken/proposed.

(g) Presumption re: batch/lot/consignment

Where any food which fails to comply with food-safety requirements is part of a batch, lot or consignment of the same class/description, it shall be presumed, until the contrary is proved, that all the food in that batch/lot/consignment fails to comply.

Section 18(2) — Principles while framing regulations / specifying standards

(a) Take into account prevalent practices & international standards

Consider (i) prevalent practices and conditions in the country (agricultural practices, handling, storage, transport) and (ii) international standards/practices — unless they would not be effective/appropriate, or there is scientific justification, or they would result in a different level of protection.

(b) Risk-analysis basis

Determine food standards on the basis of risk analysis, except where such analysis is not appropriate to the circumstances or nature of the case.

(c) Independent, objective, transparent risk assessment

Undertake risk assessment on the basis of available scientific evidence, in an independent, objective and transparent manner.

(d) Open & transparent public consultation

Ensure open and transparent public consultation (directly or through representative bodies incl. Panchayats) during preparation, evaluation and revision of regulations — except in urgency, where consultation may be dispensed with; such urgent regulations remain in force not more than six months.

(e) Protection of consumers' interests

Protect consumers' interests and provide a basis for informed choices.

(f) Prevention of unfair practices & unsafe food

Ensure prevention of (i) fraudulent, deceptive or unfair trade practices which may mislead/harm the consumer, and (ii) unsafe, contaminated or sub-standard food.

Section 18(3) — Non-application of the Act

The provisions of this Act shall not apply to any farmer or fisherman or farming operations or crops or livestock or aquaculture, and supplies used or produced in farming or products of crops produced by a farmer at farm level or a fisherman in his operations.
⚖️ Judicial interpretation of Section 18 The Association of the Traders v. Union of India (Bom. HC, 16.09.2015): "A perusal of Section 18 reveals that the foundation is preservation of human life and health while protecting the consumer's interest and by having fair practices in all kinds of food trade… The authorities are not merely to perform the negative duty of preventing food adulteration, but are required to play a very pro-active role for ensuring safe and wholesome food and to prevent and eliminate risk to health… FSS Act, 2006 is the comprehensive single special legislation for all food products on the subject of safety and standards."

Nestlé India Ltd. v. FSSAI: "Sub-section (1) of Section 18 enumerates the guiding principles to be followed while implementing the provisions; sub-section (2) lays down principles to be kept in mind by the Food Authority while framing regulations and specifying standards. This section cannot be said to be a source of power since it only lays down guidelines."

7 Licensing & Registration of Food Business — Section 31

S.31(1): No person shall commence or carry on any food business except under a licence. S.31(2): Nothing contained in sub-section (1) shall apply to a petty manufacturer who manufactures or sells any article of food himself or a petty retailer, hawker, itinerant vendor or a temporary stall holder or small scale or cottage or such other industry relating to food business or tiny food business operator; but they shall register themselves with such authority and in such manner as may be specified by regulations, without prejudice to the availability of safe and wholesome food for human consumption. S.31(3): Any person desirous to commence or carry on any food business shall make an application for grant of a licence to the Designated Officer in such manner containing such particulars and fees as may be specified by regulations. S.40(1): Nothing contained in this Act shall be held to prevent a purchaser of any article of food (other than a Food Safety Officer) from having such article analysed by the Food Analyst on payment of such fees, and receiving from the Food Analyst a report of his analysis within such period as may be specified by regulations. Case 1 — M. Mohammed v. Union of India

Citation: W.A. No. 1491 of 2014 and M.P. Nos. 1 & 2 of 2014  ·  High Court of Judicature at Madras  ·  Bench: Satish K. Agnihotri, J. and M. Venugopal, J.  ·  Reserved on: 04.02.2015 | Decided on: 12.02.2015  ·  Nature: Letters Patent Appeal (Clause 15) against order dated 10.10.2014 in W.P. No. 24999 of 2014

Parties

  • Appellant: M. Mohammed (importer/wholesaler of raw areca/betel nuts)
  • Respondents: (1) Union of India, Secretary, Ministry of Health & Family Welfare; (2) Authorised Officer, FSSAI, Chennai; (3) Commissioner of Customs, Chennai
A. Statutory and Regulatory Framework

Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (FSSA)

  • Section 3(j): "Food" means any substance, whether processed, partially processed or unprocessed, intended for human consumption, and includes primary food, genetically modified/engineered food, and any substance including water used in the food during manufacture/preparation/treatment; excludes animal feed, live animals (unless prepared for human consumption), plants prior to harvesting, drugs, and narcotic/psychotropic substances.
  • Section 3(zk): "Primary food" means an article of food, being produce of agriculture/horticulture/animal husbandry/dairying/aquaculture in its natural form, resulting from growing, raising, cultivation, picking, harvesting, collection, or catching, in the hands of a person other than a farmer/fisherman.
  • Section 48(2)(b): Where the quality/purity of a primary food article falls below the specified standard, or its constituents fall outside specified limits, solely due to natural causes beyond the control of human agency, it shall not be deemed unsafe, sub-standard, or containing extraneous matter.
  • Section 97(1): Repeals the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 — only the FSS Act now governs food imports/exports.

Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 (repealed — cited comparatively)

  • Section 2(v): "Food" means any article used as food/drink for human consumption (excluding drugs/water); includes articles ordinarily entering into or used in composition/preparation of human food, flavouring matter/condiments, and any article notified by the Central Government.
  • Section 2(xiia): "Primary food" means any article of food being agricultural/horticultural produce in its natural form.
  • Section 10: Food Inspector's power to draw samples.

FSS (Food Product Standards & Food Additives) Regulations, 2011

  • Regulation 2.3(5) ("Nuts and Raisins"): Prescribes standards for dry fruits/nuts — moisture ≤ 7%, damaged kernel (incl. slightly damaged) ≤ 5% by weight, aflatoxin ≤ 30 ppb; authorises laboratory analysis of imported betel nut/supari samples.

Other Instruments

  • Plant Quarantine (Regulation of Import into India) Order, 2003, Clause 3(16) — PQ Test requirement (Destructive Insects and Pests Act, 1914).
  • Plant, Fruits and Seeds (Regulation of Import into India) Order, 1989.
  • ITC (HS) 2012, Schedule I, Import Policy, Chapter 8, Tariff Heading 0802 80 (Areca Nuts) — CIF-value-based import conditions.
  • Constitutional provisions invoked: Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g), 21.
B. Facts

The appellant imported 688 bags of Sri Lankan betel nuts (50,942 kg) from M/s Commodities Importers, Sri Lanka, under Bill of Entry No. 5853707 dated 19.06.2014 — the Bill of Entry described the goods simply as "Areca Nuts (Betel Nuts)," not "Ungarbled Betel Nuts" (that characterisation appeared only later, in the appellant's affidavit).

On 19.06.2014, the Commissioner of Customs, Chennai passed an Examination Order requiring a test report and NOC from FSSAI before clearance. The appellant applied via FICS, contending the raw betel nuts were not "food" since they required extensive processing (washing, 10-day soaking, boiling, drying, shell removal) before becoming consumable/marketable.

The FSSAI's referral laboratory found the sample non-conforming: (i) mould growth on split areca nut, and (ii) damaged/discoloured units exceeding the prescribed limit. The Authorised Officer declined the NOC. The Single Judge dismissed the appellant's writ petition (W.P. No. 24999/2014) on 10.10.2014, holding betel nut is an agricultural product falling within "primary food," covered by "food" under Section 3(2) FSSA. The appellant filed this intra-court appeal.

C. Issues
  1. Whether "Ungarbled Betel Nuts" fall within "Food" under Section 3(j) FSSA, given they aren't commonly consumed raw.
  2. Whether Regulation 2.3(5) (framed for dry fruits/nuts) validly applies to raw betel nuts, argued to be a distinct/heterogeneous class.
  3. Whether the consignment qualifies as "primary food" under Section 3(zk), attracting the Section 48(2)(b) natural-variance exemption.
  4. Whether Al Marwa Traders (decided under the repealed PFA Act) continues to bind interpretation of the differently-worded FSS Act.
  5. Whether the Single Judge's dismissal, upholding FSSAI's NOC refusal, was legally sustainable.
D. Appellant's Contentions
  • Ungarbled betel nuts aren't meant/commonly used for human consumption in raw form; no food-safety concern in clearing the shipment.
  • Al Marwa Traders v. Assistant Commissioner of Imports [2007 (1) KLT 381] (Kerala HC DB) squarely applies: betel nuts are a "heterogeneous class" distinct from dry fruits/nuts; Regulation 2.3(5) standards inapplicable.
  • The change from PFA Act to FSS Act doesn't alter the settled principle since the dry-fruit/nut standard is materially the same under both statutes.
  • Even if "food," the nuts are "primary food"; under Section 48(2)(b), defects "solely due to natural causes beyond human control" cannot render the article unsafe/sub-standard.
  • Single Judge erred on Section 3(1)(j): the essential element — "intended for human consumption" in that form — was missing.
  • Relied on an unreported Kerala HC order (W.P.(C) No. ___ of 2014(L), dated 15.10.2014) reiterating betel nuts are heterogeneous from dry fruits/nuts, and suggesting Customs could ensure non-adulteration via the Plant Quarantine Order, 2003 / Plant, Fruits and Seeds Order, 1989 instead, without delaying clearance of perishables.
E. Respondents' Contentions
  • Regulation 2.3(5) expressly provides for lab analysis of imported betel nut/supari samples.
  • Section 3(j) FSSA is "all inclusive and pervasive" — covers processed/partially processed/unprocessed substances intended for human consumption, including "primary food" under Section 3(zk) — materially wider than the PFA Act.
  • Betel nuts, being agricultural produce, are automatically "primary food" and hence "food."
  • Relied on Pyarali K. Tejani v. Mahadeo Ramchandra Dange [1974 AIR 228]: supari is "food" because "eaten with relish by man for taste and nourishment."
  • Sampling under Regulation 2.3(5) revealed real defects — mould and excess damaged/discoloured units — justifying NOC refusal.
  • Single Judge correctly recognised the "vast difference" between "food"/"primary food" definitions under the PFA Act, 1954 vs. the FSS Act, 2006.
F. Court's Observations and Reasoning

The Bench relied on a purposive line of precedent (detailed in Section H below) to hold that "food" legislation must be construed widely to serve its protective, social-defence object.

On the central issue: Al Marwa Traders interpreted the PFA Act's narrower Section 2(v) definition; the present case falls under the FSS Act's "all inclusive and pervasive" Section 3(j), which expressly includes primary food under Section 3(zk). Since Section 97(1) FSSA fully repealed the PFA Act, only the FSS Act now governs food imports — so Al Marwa Traders "is to no avail to the Petitioner."

On the factual mischaracterisation: The Bill of Entry itself described the goods as "Areca Nuts (Betel Nuts)," not "Ungarbled Betel Nuts" — undermining the claim that the imported product was, on its face, an unprocessed raw item outside "food."

On Section 48(2)(b): The referral lab's findings (mould growth, excess damaged/discoloured units) were treated as genuine non-conformity, not mere natural variability beyond human control — so the exemption didn't assist the appellant.

G. Held

The Writ Appeal was dismissed. The Single Judge's finding — that the appellant "miserably failed to make out any case for granting the relief claimed" — suffered from no material irregularity or patent illegality. FSSAI's NOC refusal and the requirement of lab conformity before customs clearance were sustained.

💡 Ratio The FSS Act, 2006's definition of "food" (S.3(j)) is deliberately broader than the repealed PFA Act's definition (S.2(v)); since S.97(1) fully repealed the PFA Act, precedents interpreting the older, narrower definition don't automatically bind interpretation of the new, differently-worded statute. Agricultural produce like betel nut, even raw/ungarbled, falls within "primary food" under S.3(zk) and thus "food" under the FSSA — irrespective of whether further processing is needed before direct consumption.
H. Precedents Relied Upon (Paragraph Summaries)
  1. Pyarali K. Tejani v. Mahadeo Ramchandra Dange (1974 AIR 228 / (1974) 1 SCC 167) — Constitution Bench of SC. Dealer prosecuted under S.16(1)(a)(i) PFA Act for selling scented supari mixed with prohibited sweeteners (saccharin, cyclamate), violating S.7(i)(ii) and Rule 47 PFA Rules. Held: "supari is food within the meaning of Section 2(v)" — items like pan masala/gutka/supari are eaten "for taste and nourishment," qualifying as food despite not being a staple meal. Upheld strict liability irrespective of seller's knowledge; appeal dismissed, conviction affirmed, deterrent sentencing emphasised.
  2. Dineshchandra Jamnadas Gandhi v. State of Gujarat (1989 AIR 1011 / (1989) 1 SCC 420) — Tradesman convicted for selling "Kesari-coloured sweet supari sali" adulterated with yellow basic coal-tar dye under S.16(1)(a)(i) PFA Act. Gujarat HC restored conviction; SC upheld it, observing (paras 16-17) that the PFA Act's object is "to eliminate the danger to human life from the sale... of adulterated food," constituting "a legislative measure for social defence" requiring broad, purposive interpretation of its provisions, including the definition of food.
  3. Bishan Das Mehta v. Union of India (AIR 1970 Delhi 267) — Delhi HC considered whether "Katha" (used in preparing Pan) is "food" despite not being eaten alone. Held yes — the test is "whether the article... is usable and enters into the composition or preparation of food which is taken by human beings," not whether it's independently consumable. Supports the view that raw ingredients (like unprocessed betel nut) need not be directly edible to qualify as "food."
  4. Ramlal v. State of Rajasthan (AIR 2001 SC 47) — SC held camel's milk falls within "food" under the PFA Act, clarifying "an article which is food does not lose its character as food by the fact that it was also used or sold for other purposes." Used to reject the argument that betel nuts used in trade/pre-processing before consumption should escape "food" classification.
  5. Al Marwa Traders v. Assistant Commissioner of Imports (2007 (1) KLT 381) — Kerala HC DB held betel nuts form a class "heterogeneous" and distinct from "dry fruits and nuts," so dry-fruit/nut quality standards couldn't be mechanically applied to betel-nut imports. Appellant's central precedent; distinguished by the Madras HC as decided under the repealed PFA Act's narrower definition, rendered inapplicable post-repeal.
  6. Unreported Kerala HC Order (W.P.(C) No. ___ of 2014(L), dated 15.10.2014) — Followed Al Marwa Traders, holding Regulation 2.3(5) standards shouldn't apply to betel nuts, and suggesting Customs could verify non-adulteration via the Plant, Fruits and Seeds Order, 1989, and Plant Quarantine Order, 2003, instead. Rejected by the Madras HC for the same reason as Al Marwa Traders.

Case 2 — M/S Nestle India Limited v. Food Safety and Standards Authority of India & Ors.

Citation: Writ Petition (L) No. 1688 of 2015  ·  High Court of Judicature at Bombay, Ordinary Original Civil Jurisdiction  ·  Bench: V.M. Kanade, J. and B.P. Colabawalla, J. (per V.M. Kanade, J.)  ·  Reserved on: 31.07.2015 | Decided on: 13.08.2015
Maggi NoodlesLead (Pb) 2.5 ppmMSGProprietary food (S.22)Natural justiceS.43 & S.47Arts. 14 & 19S.30 & S.34

Parties

  • Petitioner: M/S Nestle India Limited
  • Respondents: (1) FSSAI; (2) Chief Executive Officer, FSSAI; (3) State of Maharashtra; (4) Commissioner of Food Safety, Maharashtra
A. Statutory and Regulatory Framework

Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006

  • Section 3(1)(zz) — misbranding, read with penal provisions.
  • Section 10(5) — power of Designated Officer/Food Authority to direct action on food articles.
  • Section 16(1), (2), (5) — powers/functions of Food Authority, including framing Regulations; Regulations acquire force of law only after being laid before both Houses of Parliament under Section 93.
  • Section 18(1)(a),(b),(c),(f),(g) — general principles governing Food Authority's exercise of functions.
  • Section 22 — regulation of genetically modified/novel/proprietary foods; requires risk assessment/product approval before marketing.
  • Section 26 — obligations of food business operators to ensure food safety.
  • Section 28 — power to prohibit non-compliant food business.
  • Section 29 — general enforcement powers.
  • Section 30 — Commissioner of Food Safety's emergency powers (State level).
  • Section 43 — accreditation/notification of food laboratories.
  • Section 46(4) — appellate remedy against a Food Analyst's report.
  • Section 47(1) — mandatory sampling/testing procedure.
  • Section 53 and related penal Sections 20, 23, 24, 48, 50, 52, 58, 59 — penalties for sub-standard/unsafe food.
  • Section 97 & Second Schedule — repeal of PFA Act, 1954, and six other food-control orders (Fruit Products Order 1955, Meat Food Products Order 1973, Vegetable Oil Products Order 1947, Edible Oils Packaging Order 1998, Solvent Extracted Oil Order 1967, Milk and Milk Products Order 1992).

Constitutional Provisions

Articles 14 (equality), 19(1)(g) (right to trade/business), 226 (writ jurisdiction).

Key Precedents Applied

Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trade Marks (AIR 1999 SC 22); Aamir Khan Productions Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India; Pepsico India Holdings v. Food Inspector; Dharampal Satyapal Ltd. v. Dy. Commissioner of Central Excise; Godawat Pan Masala Products v. Union of India; E.P. Royappa v. State of T.N.; Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India; A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (AIR 1970 SC 150).

B. Facts

Nestle had manufactured/sold Maggi Noodles in India for 30+ years — first under a 1983 PFA-era license, later under FSS Act product approvals (8 of 9 variants approved; the 9th, "Maggi Oats Masala Noodles," was under a stay in Vital Nutraceuticals v. Union of India).

In January 2015, a Food Inspector in Barabanki, UP, suspected the "No added MSG" label; the State Food Laboratory, Gorakhpur, detected MSG. The sample went to the Referral Laboratory, Kolkata, at Nestle's request; that lab (reporting ~3 months later, 29.03.2015) found MSG and lead at 17 ppm against the permissible 2.5 ppm. Nationwide follow-up testing of only 3 of the 9 variants across states found 30 of 72 samples exceeding the lead limit (42 compliant) — excess lead in Delhi, UP, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Punjab, Meghalaya; compliant in Goa and Kerala.

On 4 June 2015, amid media coverage, Nestle voluntarily announced withdrawal, asserting the product was safe. On 5 June, at a meeting with FSSAI's CEO, Nestle was informed of the excess-lead/mislabeling findings; the same day, the CEO passed the impugned order (Exhibit-A) under Sections 10(5), 16(1), 18(1)(a),(b),(c),(f),(g), 26, 28, 29 — directing withdrawal/recall of all 9 variants, cessation of production, plus a show-cause notice on cancelling product approval. The next day, Maharashtra's Commissioner passed a parallel order (Exhibit-B) under Section 30, based on 5 of 20 samples (only 4 of 9 variants) showing excess lead.

Nestle challenged both orders under Article 226.

C. Structure of the Judgment

The judgment is organised into: Challenge (paras 3-5), Facts (paras 6-21), Scheme of the Act (paras 22-34), Issues (para 34), and Reasons/Findings (para 35 onward).

D. Issues (Twelve, framed at para 34)
  1. Maintainability of the writ petition given the alternative remedy under S.46(4).
  2. Whether Nestle suppressed material facts / destroyed evidence.
  3. Whether the Food Authority could ban based on lead exceeding Nestle's own product-approval representation (below 1 ppm) though below the Regulation's 2.5 ppm ceiling.
  4. Whether the Food Authority had unfettered discretion over "proprietary food" standards, unbound by the Regulations.
  5. Whether natural justice was violated (no show-cause notice, no shared findings before the ban).
  6. The true source of statutory power for the impugned orders.
  7. Whether the testing laboratories were NABL-accredited/notified under S.43, and whether their reports were reliable.
  8. Whether lead beyond the permissible limit and misbranding were actually established.
  9. Whether banning all 9 variants (when only 3-4 were tested) was arbitrary/disproportionate and violative of Articles 14 and 19(1)(g).
E. Nestle's Contentions
  • Complete violation of natural justice — no show-cause notice, no particulars shared; the 5 June meeting was purely informational.
  • Testing labs were unaccredited/unnotified under S.43; even accredited labs lacked specific lead-testing accreditation.
  • Product should have been tested "according to intended use" (prepared with tastemaker/water as consumed) — not done.
  • Nestle's own accredited lab showed lead within limits.
  • No viable S.46(4) appeal since the Food Authority had already finally decided the issue via the ban itself — Article 226 was the only real recourse.
  • Banning all 9 variants when only 3 were tested was disproportionate and unconstitutional.
F. Respondents' Contentions
  • Alternative remedy under S.46(4) existed; petition premature (relied on Aamir Khan Productions).
  • A show-cause notice had in fact been issued (on product-approval cancellation); Nestle rushed to court instead of replying.
  • Accreditation objection raised only in rejoinder — an afterthought; Nestle suppressed Pune lab results and destroyed stock to avoid prosecution.
  • Food Authority has unfettered discretion on proprietary-food standards, unbound by Regulation limits.
  • Nestle's own product-approval application represented lead below 1 ppm; exceeding even that (despite being under 2.5 ppm) justified the ban.
  • Even a single non-compliant sample justifies prohibition in the public interest.
  • Orders validly passed under Ss. 10(5), 16(1), 16(5), 22, 26, 28 (FSSAI) and S.30 (Maharashtra).
G. Court's Findings, Issue-by-Issue

Issue I — Maintainability (paras 35-41): Held maintainable. Para 38 quotes para 7 of the impugned order directing withdrawal/recall/cessation "with immediate effect" — proving it was a completed ban, not a show-cause notice (that clause was merely consequential). Para 39 applies Whirlpool Corporation's three exceptions to the alternative-remedy bar: fundamental-rights enforcement, natural-justice violation, or orders wholly without jurisdiction. Aamir Khan Productions distinguished (that case involved only a show-cause notice).

Issue II — Suppression/Destruction of Evidence (paras 42-44): Rejected both allegations. Postal tracking showed Pune lab reports were dispatched 10.06.2015 and received 15-17 June — after the petition was filed (11.06.2015) — so no suppression. Stock destruction was carried out under signed, mutually agreed minutes with the Food Authority itself (preserving a specified sample quantity, reduced from 2,000 to 750 cases) — done in coordination with, not defiance of, the regulator.

Issues III-V — Proprietary Food Discretion & Section 22: Rejected the "unfettered discretion" argument — Regulations bind only after Parliamentary approval under S.93; the Food Authority cannot informally override its own notified 2.5 ppm ceiling merely because Nestle's application represented a lower figure.

Issue VI — Natural Justice: Held that even absent an express statutory hearing requirement, one is impliedly required before orders with adverse civil consequences (citing Dharampal Satyapal and Godawat Pan Masala Products). Informing Nestle of adverse findings without a genuine opportunity to respond before the same-day ban breached natural justice.

Issue VII — Source of Power: Held the orders weren't properly sustainable under Ss.10(5), 16(1), 16(5), 18, 22, 26, 28, 29 — more properly governed by Ss.30/34, both requiring natural-justice compliance. Wrong statutory basis was invoked for a peremptory, hearing-free ban.

Issues VIII-X — Laboratory Accreditation (decisive finding): Samples must be analysed only in NABL-accredited labs recognised under Ss.3(p) and 43 (relying on Pepsico India Holdings v. Food Inspector). Since the Food Authority's labs weren't fully accredited for lead-testing, their reports couldn't be relied upon; by the same logic, Nestle's own lab reports were equally procedurally unreliable — neither side's results were conclusive.

Issues XI-XII — Misbranding & Disproportionality: Misbranding ("No added MSG") held insufficient standalone ground for an indefinite nationwide ban given the narrower statutory penalty framework for misbranding. Applying E.P. Royappa, Maneka Gandhi, and Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta on arbitrariness under Article 14, banning all 9 variants when only 3-4 were tested was held arbitrary, unreasonable, unconstitutional, and lacking a transparent, reasoned basis.

H. Held

Both impugned orders (FSSAI's 05.06.2015 order and Maharashtra's 06.06.2015 order) were set aside for breach of natural justice, reliance on unaccredited lab findings, and disproportionality. However, the Court did not permit unconditional resumption — it directed fresh samples from every batch to be tested at three NABL-accredited laboratories (Hyderabad, Mohali/Punjab, Jaipur) within a stipulated period; resumption of manufacture/sale was conditional on these fresh tests confirming lead within the 2.5 ppm limit.

Swami Achyutanand Tirth & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.

Supreme Court of India · AIR 2016 SC 3626 · Coram: T.S. Thakur (CJI), R. Banumathi, U.U. Lalit, JJ. · Decided 05.08.2016
📋 Facts
  • Writ petitions were filed in the Supreme Court relying on the FSSAI report dated 02.01.2011, "Executive Summary on National Survey on Milk Adulteration, 2011", which concluded that at the national level 68.4% of milk sold is adulterated.
  • The worst performers were Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal, Mizoram, Jharkhand and Daman & Diu (up to 100% adulteration); in Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh, 88% of milk samples were found adulterated.
  • The petitioners (residents of Uttarakhand, U.P., Rajasthan, Haryana & NCT of Delhi) alleged that State Governments and the Union of India had failed to combat adulteration of milk with hazardous substances (urea, detergent, refined oil, caustic soda, etc.), violating Article 21.
⚖️ Decision / Guidelines
  • The Court disposed of the petitions with 10 directions, including: implement the FSS Act more effectively; inform dairy owners/retailers of stringent action for chemical adulterants; identify high-risk areas/times (near festivals) and increase sampling; ensure NABL-accredited laboratory infrastructure; undertake spot testing through Mobile Food Testing Vans; conduct periodic snap-shot surveys; constitute State-level and District-level Committees; set up websites & toll-free complaint mechanisms; create public awareness (per S.18(1)(f)); and evolve a complaint mechanism against corruption of food authorities/officers.
💡 Significance

The case read Article 21 (right to life) as encompassing the right to safe food, and led to Law Commission Report No. 264 (2017) recommending amendment of Ss.272–273 IPC. It also recognised the pro-active duty (not merely a negative duty) of food authorities.

The Association of the Traders & Ors. v. Union of India

Bombay High Court · Decided 16.09.2015
💡 Principle (relevant to Section 18)

The Court observed that the foundation of S.18 is preservation of human life and health while protecting consumers' interests and ensuring fair practices in food trade. The authorities under the Act are not merely to perform the negative duty of preventing adulteration but must play a pro-active role in ensuring safe and wholesome food and eliminating risk to health. The Court reiterated that Parliament enacted the FSS Act under Entry 18, List III in the interest of public health, safeguarding the constitutional guarantee under Article 21; the FSS Act is the comprehensive single special legislation on food safety and standards.

Ram Nath v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors. (2024 LiveLaw SC 160)

💡 Principle

The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 overrides the Indian Penal Code in respect of food adulteration; simultaneous prosecution under both the FSS Act and IPC (Ss.272/273) is not permissible.

The questions below have been compiled from the DU Case Material (2015, 2017, 2018, 2019 and "Unknown Year" papers). Each model answer follows the IRAC-style structure expected in the exam.

Q. What are the principles to be followed for food safety under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006? 2015 · 2017 · Unknown
Answer (Section 18): Section 18 lays down the general principles binding the Central Government, State Governments, the Food Authority and other agencies. (1) During implementation: (a) achieve an appropriate level of protection of human life/health and consumers' interests, including fair practices in food trade; (b) carry out risk management based on risk assessment; (c) adopt provisional precautionary measures where harm is possible but scientific uncertainty persists; (d) measures must be proportionate and no more trade-restrictive than necessary; (e) review measures within a reasonable period; (f) inform the public where there are reasonable grounds to suspect a risk to health; (g) presumption that if one item in a batch/lot/consignment fails, the whole batch/lot fails until the contrary is proved. (2) While framing regulations/standards: consider prevalent practices and international standards (Codex); base standards on risk analysis; undertake independent, objective, transparent risk assessment; ensure open public consultation (dispensable only in urgency, max 6 months); protect consumers' interests; and prevent fraudulent/deceptive trade practices and unsafe/contaminated/sub-standard food. (3) Non-application: the Act does not apply to farmers, fishermen, farming operations, crops, livestock, aquaculture and farm-level supplies. (Judicial gloss: Nestlé — S.18 is a guideline, not a source of power; Association of Traders — S.18's foundation is preservation of human life and health.)
Q. Define "food". Whether it can be analysed by the purchaser? 2015 · 2017 · 2019
Answer: "Food" [S.3(1)(j)] means any substance, whether processed, partially processed or unprocessed, intended for human consumption, and includes primary food [S.3(1)(zk)], GM/engineered food, infant food, packaged drinking water, alcoholic drink, chewing gum and any substance including water used in its manufacture/preparation/treatment — but excludes animal feed, live animals (unless processed for market), plants prior to harvesting, drugs, cosmetics and narcotic/psychotropic substances (with a proviso empowering the Central Government to notify other articles as food). The definition is wider than S.2(v) of the PFA Act, 1954 (see M. Mohammed; Pyarali K. Tejani — supari is food).
Yes, the purchaser may have food analysed — Section 40: a purchaser (other than a Food Safety Officer) may have any article of food analysed by the Food Analyst on payment of fees, and receive a report within the prescribed period; provided he informs the food business operator at the time of purchase of his intention. If the report shows non-compliance, the purchaser is entitled to a refund of fees; and the Food Analyst shall forward a contravention report to the Designated Officer to follow the S.42 prosecution procedure.
Q. Write a short note on the Food Safety and Standards Authority (of India). 2015
Answer: The FSSAI is the apex statutory body established under S.4 by the Central Government, functioning under the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare. It is a body corporate with perpetual succession, a common seal and power to sue/be sued; its head office is at Delhi. Under S.5 it consists of a Chairperson and 22 members (⅓ women) — 7 ex-officio members (Ministries), 2 industry representatives, 2 consumer representatives, 3 food technologists/scientists, 5 State/UT representatives (rotating across 5 zones), 2 farmers' representatives and 1 retailers' representative, appointed on the recommendation of the Selection Committee (S.6). It has a CEO (S.9) who is its legal representative (S.10). Its duties and functions (S.16) include regulating/monitoring food business; framing regulations on standards, additives, contaminants, residues, heavy metals, irradiation, labelling, risk analysis; accrediting laboratories; advising governments; risk communication; training; and international cooperation. It is assisted by the Central Advisory Committee (Ss.11–12) and Scientific Committee (S.14) / Scientific Panels (S.13), and is bound by the principles in S.18.
Q. What are the duties and functions of the Food Authority under the FSS Act, 2006? 2017
Answer (Section 16): (1) The primary duty is to regulate and monitor the manufacture, processing, distribution, sale and import of food so as to ensure safe and wholesome food. (2) It may make regulations specifying: standards & guidelines; limits for food additives, contaminants, pesticide/veterinary residues, heavy metals, mycotoxins, antibiotics, irradiation; accreditation of certification bodies; import quality control; laboratory accreditation & notification; sampling & information exchange; enforcement surveys; labelling standards; and risk-analysis procedures. (3) It shall also: advise Central/State Governments; collect scientific/technical data (consumption, biological risk, contaminants, residues, emerging risks, rapid alert); issue risk-assessment guidelines; assist crisis management; build scientific networks; promote international cooperation; disseminate information to the public/Panchayats; run training programmes; undertake assigned tasks; contribute to international standards; and promote awareness. (4)–(6) It must make public its scientific opinions and annual reports, may direct the Commissioner of Food Safety (who is bound), and must not disclose confidential information.
Q. Discuss the power, functions and liabilities of the Food Safety Officer under the FSS Act, 2006. 2017 · 2019
Answer: A Food Safety Officer is a person appointed by the Commissioner of Food Safety under S.37 (definition S.3(1)(t)), with prescribed qualifications (FSS Rules 2011, para 2.1.3). Powers (S.38): (1) take samples of any food/substance intended for sale or found on premises and seize offending articles, keeping them in the safe custody of the FBO (with a bond/sureties if required), and send samples to the Food Analyst; (2) enter and inspect any place of manufacture/storage/sale and take samples of food or adulterant; (3) pay the cost of the sample at market rate; (4) destroy perishable, deteriorated food after written notice to the FBO; (5) follow CrPC search procedure; (6) seize adulterants and books of account (with prior approval of the superior authority for books); (7) have witnesses present; (8) return seized books within 30 days; (9) upon seizure of an adulterant, the burden of proving it is not for adulteration lies on the possessor; (10) act under the Commissioner's binding guidelines. Functions (Ss.29(4), 42): enforce the Act in his area; inspect food businesses, draw samples and send them for analysis; launch prosecution through the Designated Officer. Liabilities (S.39): penalty up to ₹1 lakh for vexatious seizure or injurious acts without reasonable belief; and (proviso) a person making a false complaint against an officer is punishable with fine of ₹50,000–₹1 lakh.
Q. Discuss in detail the definition of "mis-branded food" and the penalty for the same in the context of decided cases. Unknown · 2019 (short note)
Answer: "Misbranded food" [S.3(1)(zf)] has three limbs: (A) the article is purported/represented/being offered or promoted for sale with false, misleading or deceptive claims (on the label or through advertisement), or sold under a name belonging to another food, or under a fictitious manufacturer; (B) where sold in sealed packages by the manufacturer, it is an imitation/substitute resembling another food without conspicuous labelling, or bears false/misleading statements about ingredients, or is falsely claimed to be from a place/country; (C) it contains artificial flavouring/colouring/preservative without a declaratory label, or is offered for special dietary uses without the prescribed label, or is not correctly stated on the package within variability limits. Penalty (S.52): up to ₹3 lakh, with power of the Adjudicating Officer to direct corrective action or destruction of the food. Decided case: In Nestlé India v. FSSAI, the Court held that even if the "No added MSG" declaration amounted to misbranding, the maximum penalty is only ₹3 lakh (S.52) and cannot justify an indefinite, nationwide ban; the petitioner's agreement to remove the declaration ended the misbranding allegation.
Q. N was engaged in the business of import, processing and wholesale of raw areca nut ("betel nuts") in ungarbled form. N imported 700 bags of Sri Lankan betel nuts under a Bill of Entry. The authorities passed an "Examination Order" directing that for customs clearance N must obtain a test report and a No Objection Certificate from the Authorised Officer. N objected. Decide. 2017 · 2018
Answer: This problem is based on the ratio of M. Mohammed v. Union of India. N will not get any remedy. "Ungarbled betel nut" is an unprocessed/partially processed substance intended for human consumption (after processing) and therefore falls within the wide definition of "food" under S.3(1)(j), which also includes primary food [S.3(1)(zk)]. The definition under the FSS Act, 2006 is wider than that under the repealed PFA Act, 1954, and by S.97(1) only the FSS Act applies. Hence the FSSAI Authorised Officer was justified in requiring a test report and NOC; the authority has a constitutional duty to protect public health and may take precautionary measures (SPS Agreement / Codex) even where a specific standard is not prescribed. The Examination Order is valid and N's objection will be rejected.
Q. Explain the process of licensing and registration of food business under the FSS Act, 2006. Unknown
Answer (Section 31): (1) No person shall carry on a food business except under a licence. (2) However, a petty manufacturer, petty retailer, hawker, itinerant vendor, temporary stall holder, small-scale/cottage or tiny food business operator need not take a licence but must register with the prescribed authority in the prescribed manner ("without prejudice to the availability of safe and wholesome food"). (3) An application for licence is made to the Designated Officer with prescribed particulars and fees. (4) The Designated Officer may grant the licence, or refuse it — after giving an opportunity of being heard and recording reasons in writing — if necessary in the interest of public health. (5) Every licence is in the prescribed form with prescribed conditions. (6) A single licence may cover one or more articles of food and different premises in the same area. (7) If premises are in more than one area, separate applications and separate licences are required.
Q. In the light of M/s Nestlé India Ltd. v. FSSAI, W.P.(L) No. 1688 of 2015, discuss the procedure of food analysis prescribed by the FSS Act, 2006. Unknown
Answer: The FSS Act prescribes a strict, mandatory procedure of sampling and analysis: a "food laboratory" [S.3(1)(p)] must satisfy a twin test — NABL accreditation and recognition/notification by the Food Authority under S.43; S.43(1) mandates that the Food Analyst analyse food only in such a laboratory. Section 47(1) prescribes the sampling procedure (division into parts, sealing, marking, sending parts to the Food Analyst and to the Designated Officer). Section 46(4) gives a right of appeal to the referral food laboratory. In Nestlé, the Bombay High Court held S.43(1) mandatory, not directory: reports from laboratories that were neither NABL-accredited nor notified for lead testing could not be relied upon, and the S.47(1) sampling procedure had not been followed — a key ground for setting aside the ban. It therefore directed re-testing of samples in three named NABL-accredited and notified laboratories following the S.47 procedure.
Q. Discuss the powers and functions of the Designated Officer under the FSS Act, 2006. Unknown
Answer: The Designated Officer [S.3(1)(h); S.36] is appointed by the Commissioner of Food Safety by order; he shall be not below the rank of Sub-Divisional Officer, and there shall be one for each district. Functions [S.36(3)]: (a) issue or cancel licences of FBOs; (b) prohibit sale of food in contravention of the Act/rules; (c) receive reports and samples from Food Safety Officers and get them analysed; (d) recommend to the Commissioner sanction for prosecution in offences punishable with imprisonment; (e) sanction/launch prosecutions in cases punishable with fine; (f) maintain records of inspections and actions taken; (g) get investigated written complaints of contravention; (h) investigate written complaints against Food Safety Officers; (i) perform other duties entrusted by the Commissioner. Under S.29(6), the Designated Officer (like the Commissioner) exercises the same powers and follows the same procedure as a Food Safety Officer. (FSS Rules 2011, para 2.1.2 add qualifications, functioning under the District Collector/DM, refund of fees under S.40, and administrative powers of suspension/cancellation/revocation of licence on a threat to public health.)
Q. Discuss in detail the definition of "Food" under the FSS Act, 2006 in the context of decided cases. 2017 · 2019
Answer: S.3(1)(j) defines "food" in three parts — main clause (any substance, processed/partially processed/unprocessed, intended for human consumption), inclusive clause (primary food, GM food, infant food, packaged drinking water, alcoholic drink, chewing gum, water used in manufacture, etc.), and exclusion clause (animal feed, live animals, pre-harvest plants, drugs, cosmetics, narcotic/psychotropic substances), with a proviso for Central Government notification. Decided cases: M. Mohammed v. UoI — "ungarbled betel nut" is food; the FSSA definition is wider and more pervasive than S.2(v) PFA Act, 1954. Pyarali K. Tejani v. Mahadeo Ramchandra Dange (1974) — supari is food ("eaten with relish for taste and nourishment"). State of T.N. v. R. Krishnamurthy (1980) — the article need not be fit or described as food; it is enough that it is generally/commonly used for human consumption or in preparation of food. Bishan Das Mehta v. UoI (1970) — an article entering into the composition/preparation of food (katha in paan) is food. Ramlal v. State of Rajasthan (2001) — an article does not lose its character as food if also used for other purposes.
