🔑 The four sentences that unlock Topic 8

  1. Art 301 is a limitation on legislative power, not a freestanding right of traders — it secures that "trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free", subject to the rest of the Part (Jindal: "not absolute").
  2. Taxes are not restrictions unless discriminatory — post-Jindal (2017): "'Free' does not mean 'free from taxation'"; only a discriminatory tax trips Art 301 via Art 304(a).
  3. Fiscal discrimination is tested ONLY under Art 304(a); procedural cure under 304(b) cannot rescue it (Jindal proposition 4) — and Art 304(a) & (b) are disjunctive gates.
  4. Parliament restricts in "public interest" (302) but may never prefer one State over another (303), except by a scarcity law; States may tax imports only equally (304(a)) and restrict only reasonably with the President's prior sanction (304(b) proviso).

§1 Art 301 · Concept & Scope of the Freedom syllabus: "Concept of trade and commerce; scope of freedom…"

Art 301 is the negative image of Partition: the framers had just watched custom-posts and permits shatter a subcontinent, and Part XIII is the pledge that India would remain one economic territory.

Article 301 — Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse. "Subject to the other provisions of this Part, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free." Verbatim · Constitution of India, Part XIII (Wikisource, Constitution (2020) text)

1.1 The three words of the guarantee

Trade & Commerce

Buying, selling, manufacture, transport, hiring — the business of exchange. In Krishnan-era doctrine the Court even distinguishes the essential attributes of trade from mere incidents (hours, lights, load-size) — laws governing incidents "regulate" trade rather than restrict it.

Intercourse

The widest word: movement and communication of persons, goods, and even ideas across the territory — migration, passage, transit. "Intercourse" is what turns a trade guarantee into a national-market guarantee (Atiabari: freedom of "movement").

Throughout the territory of India

Horizontal, not vertical: it binds every legislature (Union, State, local) territorially — no part of India may be fenced off from another. It protects inter-State AND intra-State flow (both are "throughout the territory").

1.2 "Subject to the other provisions of this Part" — freedom with valves

Art 301 opens with "Subject to", which means the guarantee is always read together with Arts 302–305. Jindal (2017) put it plainly: "Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse in terms of Article 301 is not absolute but is subject to the provisions of Part XIII." The architecture works as a cascade:

Fig 2 — The "Subject to" cascade: no valve, no restriction.

1.3 What does "free" mean? Two tempting wrong answers

❌ Not "free from regulation"

G.K. Krishnan (Mathew J): "The word 'free' does not mean freedom from regulation." Laws laying down rules of proper conduct for the activity — traffic rules, hours, weight, safety — are regulations that facilitate trade, not barriers. The line: does the law touch the essential attributes of the transaction (movement/sale), or merely its incidents?

❌ Not "free from taxation" either

Jindal (2017) proposition 1: "The word 'Free' used in Article 301 does not mean 'free from taxation'" — taxes simpliciter lie outside Part XIII. Only a discriminatory tax (hostile, protectionist sense) is an "impediment" under Art 301 (prop. 2, 8). The State's sovereign tax power is limited only by express constitutional limits (prop. on Art 245/246/265 read with 304(a)).

Scope check — Art 301 vs Art 19(1)(g): Art 19(1)(g) protects the individual's business against unreasonable restrictions; Art 301 protects the flow of trade itself (movement, intercourse) against any restriction not authorized by Part XIII's valves — no "reasonableness" gateway except where 304(b) demands it. A law can be perfectly reasonable under 19(6) yet still violate Art 301 (no valve invoked), and vice-versa. G.K. Krishnan had to satisfy both routes (301/304(b) and 19(1)(g)) because the challenge was pleaded on both.

§2 Fiscal Measures · Direct & Immediate Restrictions · Regulatory & Compensatory Taxes syllabus: "fiscal measures; direct and immediate restrictions; regulatory measures; compensatory taxes"

This is the battleground of Topic 8 — half a century of case-law on one question: when does a State levy become a "restriction" on free trade? The exam wants the four labels, the tests, and who invented/killed each one.

🧾 Fiscal measures

Any exaction by the State in its taxing power — sales tax, entry tax, vehicle tax, octroi, surcharges. Two distinct complaints arise: (i) the burden itself impedes movement (triggering 301/304(b)), or (ii) the burden discriminates against out-State goods (triggering 304(a)). Jindal resolved (i): a non-discriminatory tax never triggers 301; (ii) is the only live fiscal question — tested solely under 304(a).

⚡ Direct and immediate restrictions

Atiabari's test (1961): does the impugned measure operate "directly and immediately on trade or its movement"? Only such restrictions fall within Art 301 — remote/indirect burdens do not. Designed to save Assam's goods-carry tax, it instead created the "only movement-based taxes are caught" doctrine — and, ironically, threatened every State's Entry-56 levies (Krishnan's critique).

🛡 Regulatory measures

Laws that govern the manner of trading — traffic rules, licensing, inspection, toll rules for the use of facilities — per Krishnan, "rules of proper conduct". Automobile Transport (7 JJ): "Regulatory measures or measures imposing compensatory taxes for the use of trading facilities do not come within… Article 301… need not comply with… Article 304(b)." Post-Jindal, "regulatory" survives as a label but the compensatory-tax route is dead; the live distinction is regulation vs hostile discrimination.

💰 Compensatory taxes

A levy measuring the user's charge for trading facilities (roads, markets) — "service more or less commensurate with the tax" (Krishnan). Born in Automobile (1962) as an Art 301 exception, stretched by Bhagatram/Bihar Chamber ("some connection"), tightened back in Jindal(2) (2006) — and rejected as having "no juristic basis" by the 9-Judge Bench in Jindal (2017), prop. 5. Syllabus still lists the label — you must be able to narrate its life and its death.

§3 The Doctrine's Rise & Fall · 1961–2017 flow of all three cases through time

If you learn this timeline, every question on "compensatory taxes" or "direct and immediate restrictions" answers itself — the examiners are really asking: which era's rule applies?

1961 — Atiabari Tea Co. v. Assam (4:1; Sinha CJ dissenting). Assam Taxation (on Goods carried by Roads & Inland Waterways) Act, 1954 struck. Test born: only restrictions "directly and immediately" operating on trade's movement fall in Art 301. Sinha CJ: taxation simpliciter ≠ Art 301 restriction; Shah J: Art 301 freedom is in "widest amplitude".
1962/63 — Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) (7 JJ, 4:3). Rajasthan Motor Vehicles Taxation Act upheld. Compensatory theory created: taxes for use of trading facilities = outside Art 301, need not satisfy 304(b). "Working test": is the trader paying "patently much more" than facilities cost?
1974/75 — G.K. Krishnan v. Tamil Nadu (case 66). Contract-carriage tax ₹30→₹100/seat/quarter upheld: compensatory on facts (road spend ₹19.51 cr > receipts ₹16.38 cr); motive irrelevant; Art 14 classification valid. RESEARCHED Also sowed doubt: Mathew J questioned whether 304(b) even covers non-discriminatory taxes.
1980s–90s — Sales-tax line: Nataraja Mudaliar, H. Anraj, Indian Cement, Weston, Video Electronics (3 JJ) — 304(a) policing of rate discrimination: total local exemptions void (Anraj); varying inter-State rates bad (Indian Cement); but limited-period, class-specific new-unit incentives upheld (Video Electronics) — the exception Mahavir later polices.
1996 — Shree Mahavir Oil Mills (case 67) (2 JJ). J&K's blanket SRO exemption for local small-scale units while out-State makers paid 8% = "hostile discrimination" void vs Arts 301 + 304(a); Art 14 route rejected; relief moulded under Art 142 (effective 1-4-1997).
1995/96 — Bhagatram & Bihar Chamber of Commerce (3 JJ) stretch the theory: compensatory need only "some connection" with facilities, direct or indirect — even incidental public benefit suffices. Chaos in entry-tax litigation follows.
2003 — Jindal Stripe (2 JJ) refers the Atiabari-vs-Bhagatram conflict; 2006 — Jindal(2) (5 JJ): overrules Bhagatram + Bihar Chamber; re-affirms Atiabari/Automobile direct-and-immediate + working test.
2009–10 — Jaiprakash (2 JJ) frames 10 questions; Jindal(2010) (5 JJ) refers Atiabari/Automobile for reconsideration, noting the doubt "as early as 1975 in G.K. Krishnan".
2016/17 — Jindal Stainless (case 68) (9 JJ, 7:2). Compensatory theory rejected as juristically baseless; Atiabari, Automobile & Jindal(2) overruled to that extent; non-discriminatory taxes outside Art 301; entry-tax laws of 14+ States revived; answers referred back to regular benches.

Case 66 · G.K. Krishnan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975) 1 SCC 375 — the compensatory-tax high-water mark

Case 66 · DU case-list · syllabus pp. — (citation only)

G.K. Krishnan etc. v. State of Tamil Nadu & Anr. etc.

(1975) 1 SCC 375 = AIR 1975 SC 583 = 1975 SCR (2) 715 · decided 12-11-1974 · Bench: A.N. Ray CJ, K.K. Mathew & A. Alagiriswami JJ · judgment by Mathew J · RESEARCHED — DU case-book has no excerpt for this case (citation listed at serial 66 only)

📜 Facts

The Madras Motor Vehicles Taxation Act, 1931 (Act 3 of 1931) was enacted to abolish tolls in the Presidency of Madras and motor-vehicle levies of local bodies. S. 4(1) empowers the State Government, by notification in the official gazette, to levy "a tax on every motor vehicle using any public road"; s. 4(2) requires the notification to specify rates and quarter, not exceeding the maxima in Schedule II (amendable by rules under s. 17, laid on the Assembly's table). Schedule II entry 4(iii) covers stage carriages, entry 4(iv) contract carriages.

Chronology of the levy:

  • Original rate ₹10 per seat per quarter → raised to ₹30 when regional transport authorities began issuing permits.
  • G.O.Ms. 923-Home, 19-4-1969: ₹30 → ₹50 w.e.f. 1-7-1969. Avowed object: "to avoid unhealthy competition between omnibuses and regular stage carriage buses and to put down the misuse of omnibuses". Challenged (WP 1412/1969 etc.).
  • Pending those writs, G.O.Ms. 434-Home, 27-2-1970: ₹50 → ₹100 w.e.f. 1-9-1970 — same avowed object; more writs.
  • Madras HC common judgment 29-1-1971: quashed both notifications as "a device to eliminate the operation of contract carriages" and not an exercise of the power of taxation → rate reverted to ₹30. Appeals to the SC followed.
  • Meanwhile G.O. No. 2044-Home, 20-9-1971: again ₹30 → ₹100 per seat per quarter w.e.f. 1-7-1971 — the notification under challenge (leading WP 253/1973; CAs 2415/1972 & 128–132/1973 from the HC's 14-2-1972 orders).

The lead petitioner owned a 54-passenger omnibus with a contract-carriage permit (16-5-1968), paying ₹30/seat/quarter. Counter-affidavit of the State: in 1970-71 the Government spent ₹19.51 crores on maintenance and construction of roads (excluding grants to local bodies; "road costs" incl. traffic-control devices, safety measures, improvements, enforcement staff) while vehicle-tax receipts were only ₹16.38 crores. Reasons for taxing contract carriages higher: they run more miles (no fixed route/schedule — free to ply any road, certified-fit or not, day or night, unlimited miles), cause greater wear (higher speeds → higher impact stresses), carry more load, and pay no surcharge — whereas stage carriages run fixed routes/times, are capped (rule: 250 miles), must use certified roads, and their owners also pay a surcharge of 10 paise per rupee of fare (compounding ₹25/seat/quarter) under the Tamil Nadu Motor Vehicles (Taxation of Passengers and Goods) Act, 1952. Under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1939: "contract carriage" = s. 2(3) (whole-vehicle hire, no picking up en route), "stage carriage" = s. 2(29) (separate fares); permits governed by ss. 42, 46–51; fares by s. 43; speed by s. 71.

⚖️ Issues

1. Is the notification a colourable device — not bona fide taxation but a means to eliminate competition between omnibuses (contract carriages) and Government-run stage carriages?
2. Is the levy a restriction on the freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse under Art 301 — neither compensatory nor regulatory — and therefore invalid because it is an executive notification, not "law" made with the previous sanction of the President under Art 304(b)?
3. Is the enhanced rate confiscatory/excessive — an unreasonable restriction under Art 19(1)(g) read with 19(6)?
4. Does the higher rate on contract carriages vs stage carriages violate Art 14 — are they similarly situated for vehicle-taxation purposes (road use)?

🗣 Arguments

For the petitioners/appellants (S.V. Gupte, K.S. Ramamurthi, A.T.M. Sampath, A.K. Sen, K. Doraiswamy etc.):

  • Colourable device: the notification "was not a measure of taxation but a device to eliminate the competition of omnibuses with stage carriages run by Government" — the stated object of G.Os. 923 and 434 was exactly that; motive betrays a pretence.
  • Art 301 route: the tax is neither compensatory nor regulatory, hence a "restriction" on free trade; since it is issued under delegated power (s. 4), it is not a "law made by the legislature", and the President's previous sanction could never have been obtained for it → it cannot be saved by Art 304(b) → void.
  • Art 19(1)(g): the rate (₹100/seat/quarter) is confiscatory — an unreasonable restriction on the right to carry on business.
  • Art 14: (Gupte) stage and contract carriages "are similarly situated with respect to the purpose of vehicle taxation, namely, the use of the road" — taxing one higher has "no reasonable relation to the purpose of the Act" → manifestly discriminatory.

For the State of Tamil Nadu (S. Govind Swaminathan, Adv. Gen., A.V. Rangam, A. Subhashini):

  • Power, not motive: s. 4 expressly authorises the levy; a legislature competent to pass the Act + Government competent to notify = no colourable exercise, even if competition-elimination was the true purpose ("If an authority has power to impose a tax, the fact that it gave a wrong reason… would not derogate from the validity of the tax").
  • Compensatory facts: road expenditure (₹19.51 cr) exceeds vehicle-tax receipts (₹16.38 cr); "working test" of Automobile satisfied — traders get facilities, pay not "patently much more"; rough approximation suffices; no separate fund needed.
  • Classification is factual: contract carriages run more miles on any road at any time → greater wear; stage carriages are route/time/mileage-capped and pay surcharge — reasonable relation to road-usage; presumption of validity in taxing statutes; mileage data lies with the operators, who placed nothing on record.

🧾 Decision (Mathew J for the Court)

(i) Colourable device — rejected [para 9]. "As the State Legislature was competent to pass the Act and as the Government is authorised under s. 4 to levy the tax, the question of the motive with which the tax was imposed is immaterial… there can be no plea of a colourable exercise of power to tax if the Government had power to impose the tax, and the fact that the imposition of the tax was for the purpose of eliminating competition would not detract from its validity."

(ii) Art 301/304(b) — the doctrinal core. The Court reviewed the line of authority itself:

  • Atiabari (1961) (4:1): restrictions "directly and immediately" restricting/impeding the free flow or movement of trade fall within Art 301 — taxes "may and do amount to restrictions, but… only such taxes as directly and immediately restrict trade" are caught; Sinha CJ dissented ("taxation simpliciter… not within Article 301"); Shah J read Art 301 in the "widest amplitude". Krishnan's critique: the test "had great adverse effect upon the financial autonomy of states" — an Entry-56 List II road-tax would be bad, rendering the entry "otiose".
  • Automobile Transport (7 JJ, 4:3) practically overruled that insofar as a State may tax to raise money to maintain roads without Art 304(b) sanction; per Khelbari Tea (1964) 5 SCR 975, Automobile affirmed Atiabari "with a clarification that regulatory measures or measures imposing compensatory tax do not come within… Article 301 and… need not comply with… Article 304(b)". "In whatever way one may choose to put it, the effect of the majority decision in the Automobile Case is that a compensatory tax is not a restriction upon the movement part of trade and commerce."
  • Textual scheme: Art 301 = general limitation on all legislative power; Art 302 = Parliament's public-interest restriction power; Art 303(1) = preference/discrimination ban; "Each of the clauses of Article 304 operates as a proviso to Articles 301 and 303" — 304(a) puts imported goods "on a par" with local goods in State taxation; 304(b) = State analogue of 302 but with reasonableness additionally.
  • "Free" ≠ freedom from regulation. Distinction between laws interfering with the freedom to carry out trade-activities and laws imposing "rules of proper conduct" (regulation). "Regulations like rules of traffic facilitate freedom of trade and commerce whereas restrictions impede that freedom." Toll/road-tax collections "do not operate as barriers". Held: the notification's tax is compensatory in character and therefore does not restrict Art 301 freedom — need not comply with 304(b) [per Automobile].

Compensatory test applied: compensatory tax = charge for "nature and extent of the use made of the roads" (mileage/ton-mileage type), proceeds devoted to upkeep, no substantial interference with movement; no burden "beyond a proper recompense for the actual use"; users owe "a special contribution… over and above their general contribution as taxpayers". Working test (Automobile): are traders having the use of facilities "and not paying patently much more than what is required for providing the facilities"? "Rough approximation, rather than mathematical accuracy is all that is required" (Aristotle: precision "just so far as the nature of the subject admits"). Road-cost figures (19.51 > 16.38) pass it; even excluding capital cost of new roads, receipts wouldn't cover old-road maintenance; even the Rajasthan HC's figures in Automobile showed tax ≈ 50% of road costs. Supporting authorities: Armstrong v State of Victoria (99 CLR 28) and Commonwealth Freighters v Sneddon (102 CLR 280) on upkeep-vs-capital (Court: cannot say capital expenditure actually entered the levy); US: Interstate Transit v Lindsey (283 US 183), Capital Greyhound Lines v Brice (339 US 542), Morf v Bingaman, Aero Mayflower (result-based review, proper purpose + discretion); Freightlines v NSW [1968 AC 625] (charge to adversely affect trade = restriction).

(iii) The famous dictum (later vindicated by Jindal!): "Whether the restrictions visualized by Article 304(b) would include the levy of a non-discriminatory tax is a matter on which there is scope for difference of opinion. Article 304(a) prohibits only imposition of a discriminatory tax… Article 304 itself makes a distinction between tax and restriction." A discriminatory tax against outside goods "is not a tax simpliciter but a barrier". Taxing powers of Union and States are "separate and mutually exclusive" — "it is rather strange that power to tax given to States… should depend upon the goodwill of the Union executive"; a contrary reading would make intra-State sales tax bad without Presidential sanction. "It is unnecessary to pursue the matter further, as we think the tax imposed by the notification is compensatory in character…"

(iv) Art 19(1)(g): no material to show the levy confiscatory; "a tax which is compensatory cannot operate as an unreasonable restriction… for, the very idea of a compensatory tax is service more or less commensurate with the tax levied. No citizen has a right to engage in trade or business without paying for the special services he receives from the State."

(v) Art 14: presumption that a classification — especially in a taxing statute — is valid; challenger bears the burden (Amalgamated Tea Estates, Murthy Match Works). Art 14 offends "only if the classification rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the objective"; a statutory discrimination stands "if a state of facts may reasonably be conceived… to justify it" (McGowan v Maryland). Following State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills ("courts have only the power to destroy, not to reconstruct"; judicial self-restraint in tax/economic regulation) and San Antonio School District v. Rodriguez (Stewart J: courts "lack both the expertise and the familiarity with local problems" for raising revenue; no tax scheme "free of all discriminatory impact"), and Carmichael v. Southern Coal & Coke (local-conditions rationale): contract carriages' freedom to run more miles was known only to the operators — State's inference that they ran more miles "cannot be said to be a purely speculative assessment"; petitioners, holding the data, placed none. Classification reasonable → enhanced rate not hit by Art 14.

Disposition: "We dismiss the writ petitions and appeals without any order as to costs."

📌 Principle

Ratio — and its shelf life

1. A levy that is compensatory (charge for trading facilities ≈ cost of the facility) or regulatory is not a "restriction" under Art 301 and need not satisfy Art 304(b) — following Automobile. SUPERSEDED — the compensatory-tax theory was rejected in Jindal (2017), prop. 5–6; do not cite this as current law.

2. Motive is irrelevant to colourable-exercise objections against a tax where both the statute and the notification are within power — a wrong reason does not invalidate a competent levy (also bolsters Mahavir's later point: laudable objects don't cure discriminatory rates, and vice-versa).

3. In Art 14 challenges to taxing classifications: presumption of validity + burden on the challenger; local-conditions-based classifications get judicial deference.

4. Hidden gift to the future: the Court's hesitation to treat non-discriminatory taxes as 304(b) restrictions prefigures Jindal's holding that 304(a) is the only express fiscal limitation in Part XIII — a great "judicial development" point for a 20-marker.

Procedural history note (exam-worthy): the doubt sown here was formalised in Jindal Stainless (2010) 4 SCC 595 (5 JJ), which referred Atiabari/Automobile for reconsideration noting correctness "had been doubted as early as in the year 1975 in G.K. Krishnan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975) 1 SCC 375" — the DU syllabus's three cases are literally the arc of that reference.

Case 67 · Shree Mahavir Oil Mills v. State of J. & K. (1996) 11 SCC 39 — DU pp. 357–367

Case 67 · DU case-book excerpt · pp. 357–367

Shree Mahavir Oil Mills & Anr. v. State of Jammu & Kashmir & Ors.

1996 (11) SCC 39 · Bench: B.P. Jeevan Reddy & Suhas C Sen JJ · judgment by B.P. Jeevan Reddy J · DU EXCERPT ✓ (all facts/arguments/ratio below from the DU material, book pp. 357–367)

📜 Facts

Setting. "The State of Jammu and Kashmir seeks to encourage and promote the industrialisation of the State — like every other State in the country" (para 2, DU). Edible oil manufacture was the chosen vehicle. The appellant = a manufacturer of edible oil outside J&K, selling in the State.

The impugned SROs (the whole case is about two notification-streams):

  • SRO 93, dated 7-3-1991 — issued under s. 5 of the J&K General Sales Tax Act, 1962: total exemption from sales tax on goods manufactured by local small-scale units (investment ceiling raised from ₹10 lakh to ₹30 lakh) — for five years, extendable by five more (i.e., potentially a decade), covering virtually every local edible-oil unit.
  • Edible oil's own rate: shifted from Schedule D (4%) to Schedule C (8%) — first by SRO 213/1993 (later rescinded), then by SRO 124/1994. Result: out-State makers of edible oil: 8%; local units: nil (exempt).
  • Procedural history: Himachal Pradesh traders (out-State sellers facing the 8% + nil-local asymmetry) moved the HP High Court; the Single Judge and then the Division Bench dismissed their writs — the Division Bench relying on Video Electronics v. State of Punjab (1990) 3 SCC 87 (3 JJ). The out-State manufacturers appealed to the Supreme Court (Salve leading).
Fig 5 — Mahavir: how two SROs built an unconstitutional wall (chronology).

⚖️ Issues

1. Does the blanket, total tax exemption to all local small-scale edible-oil units while out-State makers pay 8% violate Art 301 (freedom of trade) and Art 304(a) (no discrimination against imported goods by taxation)?
2. Can such a discrimination be upheld as a classification under Art 14 (promoting local industry = laudable object)?
3. Does Video Electronics (limited-period new-unit incentives upheld) protect these SROs? Did out-State makers acquiesce (no protest at the earlier 4% rate; rising turnover)?
4. What relief is appropriate — straight striking-down, or moulding (dates, refunds) under Art 142?

🗣 Arguments

For the appellants (out-State edible-oil makers) — Mr Harish Salve:

  • The SRO scheme is textbook discrimination under Art 304(a): local goods "nil", imported goods 8% — "so, however, as not to discriminate" is flatly breached; also offends the Art 301 guarantee of free trade throughout the territory.
  • States cannot erect fiscal barriers — Part XIII exists precisely to keep India one economic unit; the only constitutional route for a State to impose differential fiscal burdens is the President's-assent machinery of Art 304(b) (and 304(a)'s equality command) — neither was complied with.
  • Video Electronics is distinguishable: that case upheld incentives for a specified class of new units for a limited period; here the exemption is total and unconditional for the whole local industry (old and new), indefinitely renewable (5+5 years).

For the State of J&K / HP respondents — Mr Verma (as recorded in DU):

  • Video Electronics applies directly: limited-period exemptions to promote infant industry were upheld there — the same industrialisation logic governs SRO 93.
  • Art 14 classification is read into Art 304(a): "discrimination" means unreasonable differentiation; a classification fostering investment and employment in a backward State is reasonable, not hostile.
  • No prejudice / acquiescence: out-State makers did not protest when the rate was merely 4%; their turnover in the State kept rising — they accepted the field with the tax.
  • Laudable object: industrialisation and small-scale promotion of a disturbed, backward State — courts should not strike down fiscal policy enacted in furtherance of Art 39-type goals.

🧾 Decision

The Part XIII scheme (para 8 — the DU exam's favourite paragraph):

  • Art 301 makes trade free "subject to the other provisions of this Part" — and among them Art 304(a) is the taxes valve: a State "may impose on goods imported from other States any tax to which similar goods manufactured or produced in that State are subject, so, however, as not to discriminate".
  • 304(a) is the negative aspect — "tax you may… but do not discriminate"; a "ringing declaration against tax/fiscal barriers". The Court observed that 304(a) is arguably "not really an exception [to Art 301]… but a restatement of a facet of 301".
  • Art 304(b) (reasonable restrictions, public interest + proviso's previous Presidential sanction, read with Art 255) is the positive valve; Art 302 = Parliament's public-interest valve; 303 = no inter-State preference (scarcity exception). Underlying theme: oneness of the territory.

Case-law survey in the DU excerpt: Atiabari ("Does the impugned restriction operate directly or immediately on trade or its movement?" — flow of trade "smooth and unhampered"); Automobile Transport (7 JJ 4:3 — regulatory/compensatory measures outside 301/304(b)); Mehtab Majid (higher tax on imported hides = discriminatory → struck); N.K. Nataraja Mudaliar (CST s. 3 violation of 301 saved by 302; s. 2A rates ≠ 303(1); 304(a) inapplicable there; Shah J's flow-of-trade observations didn't help the State); H. Anraj (1986) 1 SCC 414 (Tamil Nadu's own-lottery exemption = unfavourable discriminatory tax → struck); Indian Cement (1988) 1 SCC 743 (challenge UPHELD: (i) 304(b) restrictions need a law, not executive orders; (ii) varying inter-State sales-tax rates = local preference bad under Part XIII); Weston Electroniks (lower rate for local electronics = discriminatory); Video Electronics (3 JJ) (UP new-units limited-period exemption + Punjab's 1% for specified electronic goods upheld — pro-industry observations on industries in "limping or infancy", relied on by the State); W.B. Hosiery; comparative: West Lynn Creamery (Australia) and Bacchus Imports (US Commerce Clause) against protectionism.

Ratio (para 25): Art 304(a) prohibits discrimination only by way of taxation — States remain free to promote local industry by any means except discriminatory tax rates: "It matters not how this discrimination is brought about" — form is irrelevant; the Video Electronics exception survives only within its limits: specified/new industries + limited period + conditions.

Answers to the defences:

  • Art 14 rejected: "Art 14 speaks of equality; Art 301 speaks of freedom and Art 304(a) speaks of uniform taxation" — Part XIII's own text governs; you cannot smuggle a reasonableness/classification gateway into a flat non-discrimination command.
  • No acquiescence: one cannot acquiesce in a constitutional right; the earlier 4% common rate proves nothing.
  • Turnover immaterial: rising sales do not legalise a discriminatory fiscal structure.
  • Laudable object: industrialisation is commendable, but the Constitution prescribes the machinery (304(a)/304(b)) — good ends through wrong machinery still fail.

Holding: blanket exemption to ALL local small-scale edible-oil units (with out-State makers at 8%, even for ten years) is violative of Arts 301 and 304(a).

Relief (paras 26–27, moulded under Art 142): declaration of invalidity with two accommodations: (a) no refunds to the appellants (who had paid at the applicable rate); (b) invalidity effective from 1-4-1997 — the notifications remain operative till 31-3-1997, giving the State time to restructure. "Appeal allowed in these terms."

Fig 6 — Mahavir's decision tree: the Art 14 defence fails; 304(a) governs; the Video Electronics exception is narrow.

📌 Principle

Ratio of Mahavir

A State may subsidise, incentivise and promote local industry by every means — except by taxing outside goods more than its own. Art 304(a) strikes discriminatory taxation irrespective of motive, object or label; total/broad local exemptions paired with rates on imported goods = hostile discrimination void vs Arts 301 + 304(a); Video Electronics saves only class-specific, limited-period, conditional incentives; Art 14 "reasonableness" is no gateway to Part XIII; relief may be moulded (prospective dates, no refunds) under Art 142.

How Jindal reads Mahavir: in Jindal (para 130–134, DU p. 378) the majority relies on Mahavir for the total-and-unconditional exemption being the vice, and distinguishes it through Video Electronics — "discrimination" in 304(a) involves an element of "intentional and unfavorable bias"; mere differentiation ≠ discrimination. Mahavir remains good law — it is the live test for exemption/notification challenges (e.g. State industrial-policy SROs).

Case 68 · Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2017) 12 SCC 1 — DU pp. 368–391 — the 9-Judge reset

Case 68 · DU case-book excerpt · pp. 368–391

Jindal Stainless Ltd. & Anr. v. State of Haryana & Ors.

(2017) 12 SCC 1 · Constitution Bench answering a 9-Judge reference, pronounced 11-11-2016 · Majority: T.S. Thakur CJ (for himself, A.K. Sikri & A.M. Khanwilkar JJ) with separate concurring judgments of S.A. Bobde, Shiva Kirti Singh, N.V. Ramana & R. Banumathi JJ; Dissent: Dr D.Y. Chandrachud & Ashok Bhushan JJ — 7:2 bench composition researched (DU order line lists Thakur, Sikri, Bobde, S.K. Singh, Ramana) · DU EXCERPT ✓ (ORDER + paras 1–11, 66–72, 79, 126–143 per DU pp. 368–391)

📜 Facts & procedural history (the longest runway in Indian tax law)

  • The levies: at least 14 States, using Entry 52, List II (taxes on the entry of goods into a local area), enacted laws taxing "entry of goods into local areas comprising the States". Assessors challenged them in High Courts under Arts 301, 304(a) and for want of Presidential sanction under 304(b).
  • Haryana: WP 8700/2000 (P&H HC) challenged the Haryana Local Development Act, 2000. A Division Bench dismissed it — the levy was compensatory, hence outside Art 301 — relying on Atiabari (AIR 1961 SC 232), Automobile (AIR 1962 SC 1406), Bhagatram Rajeev Kumar ((1995 Supp [1] SCC 673) and Bihar Chamber of Commerce ((1996) 9 SCC 136).
  • 2003 — Jindal Stripe (2 JJ) refers: apparent conflict between pre-1995 law (Atiabari/Automobile: compensatory ≈ commensurate with cost of facility) and Bhagatram/Bihar Chamber ("some connection", direct or indirect, even incidental public benefit suffices) — "the parameters of this judicial concept are blurred" → Constitution Bench under Art 145(3) ((2003) 8 SCC 60).
  • 2006 — Jindal Stainless (2) (5 JJ) ((2006) 7 SCC 241): overrules Bhagatram + Bihar Chamber; the Automobile working test is incompatible with "some connection"; Atiabari's "direct and immediate effect" + Automobile's compensatory test "will continue to apply" (para 53). Remand: HCs must decide compensatory-ness on data.
  • After remand: High Courts held the levies neither compensatory nor passed with 304(b) procedure. States appealed. The appeals went to a 2-Judge Bench (Pasayat & Kapadia JJ) → Jaiprakash Associates (2009) 7 SCC 339 framed ten questions (assessees argued entry levies are "essentially fees"; transport cases differ from entry-of-goods cases) → Jindal (2010) 4 SCC 595 (5 JJ) referred Atiabari and Automobile themselves for reconsideration, noting the doubt existed "as early as in the year 1975 in G.K. Krishnan v. State of Tamil Nadu (1975) 1 SCC 375".
  • Before the 9-Judge Bench: counsel reframed everything into four questions (below). Mr Harish Salve (with Arvind Datar, Laxmi Kumaran, Ravindra Shrivastava, N. Venkataraman) led for the assessees: the compensatory-tax theory "had no legal basis or constitutional sanction and was neither acceptable nor workable" — States had taken "umbrage" under it, self-certifying levies as compensatory to dodge Art 301. The States — also unhappy with Atiabari/Automobile — wanted them revisited "to restore and protect the sovereign power of legislation of the States and the Federal character of our polity". Net: general consensus that the compensatory theory deserves rejection — "except a feeble attempt made by some Counsel".

⚖️ Issues — the four reframed questions (verbatim, DU p. 371)

Q1. Can the levy of a non-discriminatory tax per se constitute an infraction of Art 301?
Q2. If yes, can a compensatory tax also fall foul of Art 301?
Q3. What are the tests for determining whether a tax is compensatory?
Q4. Are the States' entry taxes violative of Art 301 — and must they be tested under both 304(a) and 304(b)?

🗣 Arguments

Assessees / traders (Salve, Datar, Kumaran, Shrivastava, Venkataraman):

  • Compensatory-tax theory = judicial invention with no constitutional sanction; unworkable in practice (who decides the "facility cost"?), routinely self-certified by States — neither acceptable nor workable.
  • Taxes on entry into a local area are direct impediments to inter-State movement → at minimum they fail 304(a) (discrimination) and 304(b) (no Presidential sanction) — both tests must apply.
  • Incentives/exemptions for local goods are "one form of insidious discrimination" — relying on Mahavir (total local exemption while outsiders paid 8% was void).
  • Traditional position: taxes "may and do amount to restrictions" (Atiabari majority framing) — Part XIII was meant to prevent fiscal barriers between erstwhile princely States.

States (14+ party States):

  • Equally attack Atiabari & Automobile: the compensatory theory is bereft of legal basis — revisit them to protect State sovereign taxing power and the federal design (taxation = "attribute of sovereignty"; limitations must be express).
  • Art 304(a) itself presupposes and permits a tax on imported goods — "the legislature of a State may by law impose…" is an empowerment, not a restriction; source = Arts 245–246 + Entry 52 List II.
  • Equalisation defence (para 141): exemptions, adjustments or credits designed to make the fall of fiscal burden equal on local and outside goods are not hostile discrimination.
  • Entry levies are for local-area development — incidental benefit to traders suffices (the old Bhagatram logic, even while disowning the label).

🧾 Decision — the ten propositions (ORDER, DU pp. 368–369)

By majority (7:2), the reference is answered as follows:

  1. Taxes simpliciter are not within Part XIII. The word 'Free' in Art 301 does not mean "free from taxation".
  2. Only discriminatory taxes are prohibited by Art 304(a) — a non-discriminatory tax is no infraction of Art 301.
  3. Clauses (a) and (b) of Art 304 must be read DISJUNCTIVELY.
  4. A levy violating 304(a) CANNOT be saved even if 304(b)/its proviso is fully satisfied.
  5. The compensatory tax theory (Automobile, modified in Jindal (2)) has no juristic basis — REJECTED.
  6. Atiabari, Automobile and Jindal (2), and all judgments following them, are overruled to that extent.
  7. An entry tax is permissible although similar goods are not produced within the taxing State.
  8. Art 304(a) "frowns upon discrimination (of a hostile nature in the protectionist sense) and not on mere differentiation" — therefore incentives, set-offs etc. granted to a specified class of dealers for a limited period, in a non-hostile fashion to develop economically backward areas, do NOT violate 304(a) (applicability to the batch's levies left to regular benches).
  9. States may design fiscal legislation so that the tax burden on goods imported from other States and goods produced within the State falls EQUALLY — no 304(a) breach (again, application left to regular benches).
  10. Left open: whether the entire State can be notified as a "local area"; and whether entry tax may be levied on goods entering India from another country.

Reasoning spine (paras 66–79):

  • With the compensatory theory gone, only two historic views compete: Gajendragadkar J's (Atiabari majority — direct & immediate) vs B.P. Sinha CJ's (Atiabari dissent — taxation simpliciter is not within Art 301). Sinha CJ wins.
  • 301: plain reading — freedom "subject to" Part XIII, not absolute. 302: Parliament may restrict in public interest → absolute character lost. 303(1): notwithstanding 302, no Parliament/State law may give preference or discrimination between States via any Seventh-Schedule trade entry; 303(2): unless the law declares it necessary for scarcity of goods.
  • 304 (para 69): does 304(a) treat taxes as restrictions so every levy falls foul of 301? "The answer… is in the negative." 304(a) specifically recognises the State's power to tax imported goods; "may by law impose" is not a restriction — but the power has two express conditions: (i) similar local goods must also be taxed; (ii) no discrimination between imported and local. "Levy of taxes on import of goods from other States is not by itself an impediment under… Article 301."
  • 304 disjunctive (para 70): (a) and (b) are "two distinct subjects… independent of each other" — a tax alone, a restriction alone, or both, are all permitted; "'and' can mean 'or' as well as 'and'"; taxes are not restrictions except discriminatory ones, so 304(b) applies to non-fiscal restrictions only → "for levy of taxes prior Presidential sanction… will be wholly unnecessary" (proviso confined to clause (b) bills).
  • Para 72 — the nine-point summary (verbatim structure): 301 not absolute → 302 Parliament's public-interest power → but no inter-State preference (303) → unless scarcity declaration (303(2)) → 304(a) recognises State tax power (Arts 245–246) → conditioned on similar-local-taxation + no discrimination → 304(a) "exhausts the universe" of State taxing limits → only a discriminatory import-tax "works as an impediment… within the meaning of Article 301" → 304(b) reasonableness does not comprehend taxes ("taxes are presumed to be both reasonable and in public interest").
  • Para 79 — sovereignty of the tax power: a Constitutionally vested taxing power may be controlled only by specific constitutional limitations; the only one here is 304(a) → non-discriminatory entry taxes are free to be levied (Arts 245–246 + Entry 52 List II undisputed).
  • Intra-State discrimination argument rejected ("more in despair than substantial"): Part XIII contained no bar on intra-State discriminatory taxation because the framers targeted princely-State fiscal barriers (Constituent Assembly Debates) — intra-State cases are judged case-to-case on Art 14.
  • Q1 answered NEGATIVE (para 126): "a non-discriminatory tax does not per se constitute a restriction" under 301 → Atiabari overruled (and with it Automobile's tax-as-restriction premise); Jindal (2) (2006) overruled. Q2 & Q3 do not arise (paras 127–128).
  • Q4 (paras 129–141): entry taxes are tested only under 304(a) — 304(b) "does not deal with taxes as restrictions… restrictions referred to in Article 304(b) are non-fiscal in nature"; fail 304(a) → struck "even if it had gone through the process stipulated by Article 304(b)" — "what is constitutionally impermissible in terms of Article 304(a) cannot be validated… through the medium of Article 304(b)". On incentives: Mahavir distinguished (total & unconditional exemption vice; Video Electronics = specific class + limited period; 304(a) discrimination carries an element of "intentional and unfavorable bias", per Video Electronics); para 141: equalising exemptions/set-offs/credits are acceptable if their intent is to equalise the fall of fiscal burden — actual operation left to regular benches.
  • Para 143: reference answered; matters to regular benches for expeditious disposal in light of the above.

The dissent (Chandrachud & Bhushan JJ — researched): defended the compensatory-tax doctrine as constitutionally grounded — judicially evolved in Automobile to exclude regulatory measures and facility-reimbursement levies from Part XIII, withstood decades of application, and could not be discarded by a later Bench "prejudic[ing] the interest of the parties"; they would have retained Atiabari's direct-and-immediate test and the compensatory exception. 📌 Principle

Ratio of Jindal (2017) — write these five lines in any answer citing it

1. Art 301 freedom ≠ freedom from tax; taxes simpliciter lie outside Part XIII; only discriminatory taxes are "impediments" (via 304(a)).
2. 304(a) and 304(b) are disjunctive: taxes → 304(a) alone; non-fiscal restrictions → 304(b) alone (+ Presidential sanction for bills).
3. 304(a) is a hard floor: procedural compliance of 304(b) can never cure a 304(a) discrimination.
4. Compensatory-tax theory is dead — Atiabari (to the extent taxes = restrictions), Automobile (compensatory exception) and Jindal (2) 2006 overruled; constitutional limitations on the sovereign tax power must be express.
5. Hostile, protectionist discrimination ≠ differentiation: class-specific, limited-period incentives and genuine equalising measures survive (304(a) "frowns upon" hostile bias only) — the Mahavir/Video Electronics line, now constitutionalised as the majority test.

Aftermath (context for 20-markers): the judgment revived entry-tax enactments across States (e.g. West Bengal's entry-tax law, previously struck on compensatory grounds, survived because "the foundation… no longer exists in law"); pending batch matters went to regular benches for 304(a) compliance under propositions 8–9. It remains the last great word on Arts 301–307 — and the endpoint of the DU syllabus arc Krishnan → Mahavir → Jindal.

§4 Arts 302–303 · Parliament's Power to Restrict syllabus: "restrictions… power of Parliament"

Parliament sits at the top of the restriction hierarchy: it may restrict freely in public interest — but even Parliament may not play favourites between States, unless the goods are scarce.

Article 302 — Power of Parliament to impose restrictions… "Parliament may by law impose such restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse between one State and another or within any part of the territory of India as may be required in the public interest."
Article 303 — Restrictions on the legislative powers of the Union and of the States…
(1) "Notwithstanding anything in article 302, neither Parliament nor the Legislature of a State shall have power to make any law giving, or authorising the giving of, any preference to one State over another, or making, or authorising the making of, any discrimination between one State and another, by virtue of any entry relating to trade and commerce in any of the Lists in the Seventh Schedule."
(2) "Nothing in clause (1) shall prevent Parliament from making any law giving, or authorising the giving of, any preference or making, or authorising the making of, any discrimination if it is declared by such law that it is necessary to do so for the purpose of dealing with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of the territory of India."

302 — the public-interest valve (Parliament only)

  • Only Parliament gets this free-restriction power — no matching State power (States go through 304).
  • Covers both inter-State AND intra-State trade ("between one State and another or within any part of the territory of India").
  • "by law" — legislation only, not executive order; standard = public interest (no express "reasonableness" word — contrast 304(b)).
  • Jindal (para 67): 301+302 together = "the absolute character of the freedom… is lost by reason of Article 302 itself empowering Parliament to impose such restrictions".

303 — the anti-preference rule (+ scarcity key)

  • 303(1): notwithstanding 302, neither Parliament nor a State may legislate preference/discrimination between States "by virtue of any entry relating to trade and commerce" in the Seventh Schedule — this is the constitutional ban on "buy-Bharat-first" style legislation.
  • 303(2): the only exception — Parliament may, if the law itself declares that it is necessary to deal with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of India (e.g. famine-time allocation laws: think the Essential Commodities framework during shortages).
  • Jindal (para 68) conjoint reading: "while the Parliament may impose restrictions in public interest under Article 302, the restriction so imposed shall not be in the nature of giving preference… except when the law declares that scarcity… necessitates such preference or discrimination."
  • Note 303 binds States too — a State cannot prefer its own goods via a "trade and commerce" entry either (feeds directly into 304(a) litigation).
Fig 8 — Parliament's lanes: 302 gate → 303 filter → 303(2) scarcity key.

§5 Art 304 · State Legislatures — Tax & Restrict Among States syllabus: "restrictions among states… power of… state legislatures"

Art 304 is where 90% of Part XIII litigation lives: every sales-tax, entry-tax, exemption-SRO and permit-ban case runs through its two disjunctive clauses.

Article 304 — Restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse among States. "Notwithstanding anything in article 301 or article 303, the Legislature of a State may by law—
(a) impose on goods imported from other States [or the Union territories] any tax to which similar goods manufactured or produced in that State are subject, so, however, as not to discriminate between goods so imported and goods so manufactured or produced; and
(b) impose such reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse with or within that State as may be required in the public interest:
Provided that no Bill or amendment for the purposes of clause (b) shall be introduced or moved in the Legislature of a State without the previous sanction of the President."
Verbatim · "[or the Union territories]" inserted by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956

5.1 Anatomy of the two clauses

304(a) — the EQUAL-TAX clause (no sanction needed)

  • Empowerment with two express conditions (Jindal para 69): (i) similar local goods must be taxed too ("tax you may — but tax your own too"); (ii) no discrimination between imported and local goods.
  • Covers goods "imported from other States or the Union territories" (7th Amdt 1956).
  • Jindal: this clause "exhausts the universe" of State taxing limits vis-à-vis Part XIII; hostile/protectionist discrimination only — differentiation (incentives, set-offs) survives (props 8–9).
  • No Presidential sanction under 304(a) — sanction machinery belongs only to clause (b) (Jindal prop 3 + para 70).
  • Test for a violating levy: Mahavir — total/local-only exemptions + outside-goods rates = void vs 301+304(a); relief moulded, no refunds, prospective cut-off.

304(b) — the REASONABLE-RESTRICTION clause (President at the gate)

  • Non-fiscal restrictions only post-Jindal (licensing bans, route bans, entry bans, stock limits…).
  • Triple test: (1) by law (not executive order — Indian Cement, per DU Mahavir); (2) reasonable + required in public interest; (3) proviso: previous sanction of the President before the Bill is introduced or moved (Art 255 deems a Governor's recommendation/existing Presidential recommendation as the required recommendation; Mahavir reads 304(b) "with Article 255").
  • Non-obstante opening: "Notwithstanding anything in article 301 or article 303" — a 304(b)-compliant State restriction overrides both the freedom (301) and the preference ban (303) — but not 304(a): Jindal prop 4 says a 304(a) breach can never be cured by 304(b) compliance.
  • Classic invalidity pattern: discriminatory notifications issued under executive power without law/sanction (Indian Cement route); classic validity: permit/ban laws for public order or road safety with sanction.

§6 Arts 305–307 · Savings & State Monopoly syllabus: "state monopoly (Articles 301-307)"

The last valve: Art 305 keeps pre-Constitution law and State-monopoly machinery alive despite 301/303; 306 is a fossil; 307 is Parliament's unused enforcement pen.

Article 305 — Saving of existing laws and laws providing for State monopolies. "Nothing in articles 301 and 303 shall affect the provisions of any existing law except in so far as the President may by order otherwise direct; and nothing in article 301 shall affect the operation of any law made before the commencement of the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, in so far as it relates to, or prevent Parliament or the Legislature of a State from making any law relating to, any such matter as is referred to in sub-clause (ii) of clause (6) of article 19."
Verbatim · subs. by the Constitution (Fourth Amendment) Act, 1955, s. 4 (w.e.f. 27-4-1955)
Article 306 (original: "Power of certain States in Part B of the First Schedule to impose restrictions on trade and commerce") — Omitted by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956, s. 29 & Sch. (w.e.f. 1-11-1956). Part B States ceased to exist as a class — the provision went with them. Exam one-liner: "Art 306 is spent — a relic of the integrated States reorganisation."
Article 307 — Appointment of authority for carrying out the purposes of articles 301 to 304. "Parliament may by law appoint such authority as it considers appropriate for carrying out the purposes of articles 301, 302, 303 and 304, and confer on the authority so appointed such powers and such duties as it thinks necessary."

6.1 How the monopoly valve works

Fig 10 — Art 305: the exemption from the free-trade promise. (The 305 shield displaces 301/303 review — but Art 14 / Art 19(6) review of the monopoly itself continues.)

📜 "Existing law"

Laws in force when the Constitution began (26-1-1950) — saved against 301/303 unless the President by order directs otherwise (the classic subject of Presidential "otherwise-direct" orders under Art 305 first limb).

🏛 State monopoly = Art 19(6)(ii)

The 1955 Fourth Amendment inserted Art 19(6)(ii): the right to practise a profession/trade/business does not prevent the State from carrying on any trade (i.e., a State monopoly). Art 305's second limb says 301 won't touch laws relating to that matter — monopoly statutes are free of the free-trade guarantee.

🇮🇳 Where you see it

State liquor monopolies (TASMAC-style retail in TN/Kerala models; state liquor vend acts), certain States' wine/spirit wholesaling, older salt/excise monopolies — all defended via 305 + 19(6)(ii); validity still judged under Art 14 & 19(6) reasonableness, not Art 301.

Art 307 in practice: Parliament has never appointed a general "free trade authority" under Art 307 — enforcement of 301–304 has remained entirely judicial (Arts 226/32 → Supreme Court references). But the power remains live: a Parliament could, e.g., create a cross-State trade facilitation authority with investigatory teeth under Art 307. Exam line: "307 is an unused but residually potent enforcement provision — the Constitution anticipated an administrative referee for Part XIII and settled for the courts instead."

§7 DU PYQs + Model Answers

Two papers have hit this topic: December 2024 Q6(a) (short note, 10 marks) and the Centenary paper's Q6 (Article 301). Both model answers below are written to exam length and marking rhythm.

📝 December 2024 · Q6(a) · 10 marks — Short note on any two of: (a) "Freedom of Trade, Commerce and Intercourse" (b) Separation of Powers (c) locus standi
📜 Q6(a): Freedom of Trade, Commerce and Intercourse — write a short note (10 marks).
Model answer (≈ 450–500 words plan)

1. Meaning & text (2 marks). The guarantee of economic freedom across India is contained in Article 301, the heart of Part XIII (Arts 301–307): "Subject to the other provisions of this Part, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free." It means India is to remain one common market — no customs, check-posts or fiscal walls between States. "Trade and commerce" cover the business of exchange; "intercourse" widens it to movement and communication of persons and goods.

2. Scope (3 marks). (i) Horizontal and territorial: binds every legislature — Union, State, local — throughout the territory (inter-State and intra-State flow). (ii) Not absolute: it is expressly "subject to the other provisions of this Part" — Jindal (2017): "not absolute". (iii) Not freedom from regulation — G.K. Krishnan: "the word 'free' does not mean freedom from regulation"; traffic rules etc. facilitate trade. (iv) Not freedom from taxation — Jindal: taxes simpliciter lie outside Part XIII; only discriminatory taxes are "impediments" (Art 304(a)). (v) Distinct from Art 19(1)(g): 301 protects the flow of trade against unvalved restrictions, not the individual's business against unreasonable ones.

3. Architecture of restrictions (3 marks). Freedom yields to the Part's valves: Art 302 — Parliament may restrict by law in public interest; Art 303 — no preference/discrimination between States by any trade entry, except a scarcity declaration by Parliament (303(2)); Art 304 — State power, disjunctive clauses: (a) tax imported goods at the rate of similar local goods, no discrimination, no sanction needed; (b) reasonable restrictions in public interest, by law, with the President's previous sanction (proviso; Art 255); Art 305 — savings for existing laws and State monopolies (Art 19(6)(ii)); Art 306 omitted (1956); Art 307 — Parliament may appoint an enforcement authority.

4. Key doctrines & cases (2 marks). Atiabari (1961): only restrictions "direct and immediate" on trade's movement are caught. Automobile (1962): compensatory/regulatory taxes outside 301 — theory rejected in Jindal Stainless (2017, 9 JJ). Mahavir Oil Mills (1996): blanket local exemption + 8% on outsiders = hostile discrimination void vs 301 + 304(a); Art 14 is no gateway. Post-Jindal: non-discriminatory tax valid; 304(a)&(b) disjunctive; 304(b) compliance can never cure a 304(a) breach.

5. Close (1 mark). Hence Art 301 is a qualified but justiciable promise of free trade — qualified by 302–305, justiciable because every restriction must show its valve, with Jindal marking the current boundary.

Model answer (20-mark structure)

Introduction (2 marks). Article 301 is the constitutional pledge that Partition would not be repeated economically: "Subject to the other provisions of this Part, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free." It converts the federal compact into a single internal market, and its history — from princely-State tariff walls to GST's abolition of check-posts — is the history of Indian economic integration.

I. Scope of the guarantee (6 marks). (a) Three-fold subject-matter: trade, commerce and intercourse — the last adding movement/transit of persons and goods (Atiabari's "flow of trade" reading; freedom of movement is "a very important facet" — G.K. Krishnan). (b) Territorial amplitude: "throughout the territory of India" — catches inter-State and intra-State barriers; it is a limitation on all legislative power (Mathew J: "a general limitation on all legislative power"). (c) "Subject to" — internal qualifiedness: the freedom always coexists with Arts 302–305; Jindal: "not absolute". (d) Negative content: "free" = free from protectionist barriers — not from regulation (Krishnan: free ≠ "freedom from regulation") and not from non-discriminatory taxation (Jindal: "'Free' … does not mean 'free from taxation'"). (e) Institutional reach: binds Union, States and (via Art 245-style analysis) all plenary legislatures; Art 301 vs Art 19(1)(g) distinguished — Part XIII tests authority (is there a valve?), Art 19 tests reasonableness.

II. The restrictions architecture — Arts 302–307 (7 marks). Art 302: Parliament may impose by law such restrictions as are required in the public interest. Art 303(1): notwithstanding 302, neither Parliament nor a State may give preference or make discrimination between States under any Seventh-Schedule trade entry; 303(2) — the sole key is a law declaring necessity for scarcity of goods. Art 304 — the States' dual valve, read disjunctively (Jindal prop 3): (a) equal taxation of imported goods ("tax you may, but do not discriminate" — Mahavir calls it a "ringing declaration against tax/fiscal barriers", arguably "a restatement of a facet of 301"); (b) reasonable restrictions in public interest, imposed by law (Indian Cement: not executive orders), with previous Presidential sanction before introduction (proviso; read with Art 255); proviso + 304(a) interplay: a levy failing 304(a) can never be validated through 304(b) (Jindal prop 4). Art 305: existing laws and State-monopoly laws (Art 19(6)(ii), added by the Fourth Amendment 1955) are saved against 301/303 — the constitutional basis of State liquor/trade monopolies (President may by order direct otherwise). Art 306: omitted by the Seventh Amendment 1956. Art 307: Parliament may appoint an authority to carry out 301–304 (never yet used; courts enforce Part XIII).

III. Doctrinal evolution — the fiscal cases (4 marks). (1) Atiabari (1961, 4:1): Assam's goods-carry tax struck — only restrictions direct and immediately operating on trade's movement fall under 301 (Sinha CJ dissenting: taxation simpliciter outside 301). (2) Automobile Transport (1962, 7 JJ, 4:3): compensatory/regulatory measures outside 301, need not satisfy 304(b). (3) G.K. Krishnan (1974/75): contract-carriage tax ₹30→₹100 upheld as compensatory; motive irrelevant; Art 14 classification presumed valid — (superseded on the compensatory point). (4) Sales-tax line: Nataraja Mudaliar, Anraj, Indian Cement, Weston policed rate discrimination; Video Electronics (1990) upheld limited-period new-unit incentives (differentiation ≠ discrimination; "intentional and unfavorable bias"). (5) Mahavir (1996): total local exemption + 8% outsiders struck vs 301 + 304(a); Art 14 defence rejected; relief moulded under Art 142 (effective 1-4-1997, no refunds) — still good law. (6) Jindal (2017, 9 JJ, 7:2): compensatory theory "has no juristic basis" — Atiabari (tax limb), Automobile and Jindal(2) overruled; entry taxes of 14+ States revived; non-discriminatory taxes outside 301; 304(a) alone tests fiscal measures; incentives/set-offs lawful if non-hostile and equalising.

Conclusion (1 mark). Art 301 thus stands as a disciplined, not absolute, freedom: open in principle, closeable only through the Part's specified valves — with the modern position being that the wall is built out of discrimination, not taxation as such.

§8 Rapid Revision Kit

🧠 One-map memory: "1 promise · 3 hands · 1 shield · 2 relics"

  1. 1 promise — Art 301 (free throughout India, subject to the Part).
  2. 3 hands — 302 (Parliament: public interest), 303 (nobody: preference; scarcity key), 304 (State: (a) equal tax ⟂ (b) reasonable + President).
  3. 1 shield — 305 (existing laws + State monopoly via 19(6)(ii)).
  4. 2 relics/pens — 306 (omitted 1956), 307 (unused enforcement authority).

🎯 Case trio in three sentences

Krishnan (1974): compensatory tax saves it — theory since buried; motive irrelevance + Art 14 presumption survive.
Mahavir (1996): total local exemption + 8% outsiders = discrimination → struck (301 + 304(a)), Art 14 route closed, relief moulded — living law.
Jindal (2017): compensatory theory dead; non-discriminatory taxes free; 304(a)&(b) disjunctive; 304(b) can't cure 304(a) — final word.
