Section 0 · OrientationWhat this Topic Covers & How Examiners Test It

Part V Chapter I (Arts 52–78) and Part VI Chapter II (Arts 153–167) create a parliamentary executive at the Union and the States: a constitutional head (President/Governor) + an elected, collectively responsible Council of Ministers — and the courts' difficult job of policing that machinery without running it.

The 60-second core of the whole topic

  • Parallel architecture — Arts 52/53 ↔ 153/154 (vesting); 74/75 ↔ 163/164 (CoM); 77/78 ↔ 166/167 (business & information); 72 ↔ 161 (clemency); 111 ↔ 200–201 (assent). Learn one side, mirror it.
  • Normal rule — the President/Governor is a constitutional head: every executive (and legislative-character) function is exercised on the aid and advice of the CoM — Samsher Singh, U.N.R. Rao. "Satisfaction" required by the Constitution = constitutional satisfaction of the Council, not personal.
  • Exceptions — (i) where the Constitution expressly requires discretion (Arts 239(2), 371A, Sixth Schedule, 356 report, Art 200 2nd proviso; Art 163(1)–(2)); (ii) rare, principled situations — choice of CM, dismissal of a government that refuses to quit, dissolution (Samsher para 154); sanction to prosecute a CM/Minister where the CoM disables itself / is biased / its order is ex facie irrational (M.P. SPE).
  • Judicial review — Art 74(2)/163(3) bar inquiry into what advice was tendered — but not review of the action itself: courts test executive action for malafide, extraneous considerations, non-application of mind, arbitrariness (Epuru Sudhakar, B.P. Singhal, M.P. SPE); Art 74(1) is mandatory — "shall" is "shall" (U.N.R. Rao).
  • Delhi twist — the LG is bound by aid & advice except when he exercises the proviso to Art 239AA(4) (reference to the President); "communication ≠ concurrence" (NCT Delhi 2018).

Section 1 · Part V, Chapter I — Articles 52 to 72The President and the Vice-President

"There shall be a President of India" (Art 52) — a mandatory office, but not a personal ruler: Art 53(1) vests executive power in him to be exercised "in accordance with this Constitution", and Art 74 routes that exercise through the Council of Ministers.

1.1 The office and its constitutional limits — Arts 52 & 53

Art 52. There shall be a President of India.
Art 53(1). The executive power of the Union shall be vested in the President and shall be exercised by him either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with this Constitution.
Art 53(2). Without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing provision, the supreme command of the Defence Forces of the Union shall be vested in the President and the exercise thereof shall be regulated by law.
Art 53(3). Nothing in this article shall— (a) be deemed to transfer to the President any functions conferred by any existing law on the Government of any State or other authority; or (b) prevent Parliament from conferring by law functions on authorities other than the President.Constitution of India, Articles 52–53
  • The last five words of Art 53(1) are the whole case. In U.N.R. Rao (DU excerpt, para 6): "The last five words are important inasmuch as they control the President's action under Article 53(1). Any exercise of the executive power not in accordance with the Constitution will be liable to be set aside." — i.e., judicial review of Presidential action is written into the vesting clause itself.
  • Formal head, real authority elsewhere. Ram Jawaya Kapur (Topic 1) and Samsher Singh establish that the President is a constitutional/formal head; the government is "the President aided by his Ministers".
  • Art 53(2) — supreme command of the armed forces vests in the President but "regulated by law": e.g., the Army Act 1950, Navy Act 1957, Air Force Act 1950 — the President exercises command through the civilian political executive (defence minister/Cabinet), consistent with Samsher (no personal exercise).
  • Art 53(3) — saves existing-law functions and Parliament's power to confer functions on others (basis for statutory authorities/tribunals exercising "executive" functions).

1.2 Election, qualifications, conditions, term — Arts 54–59, 62, 70–71

Electoral college & manner — Arts 54–55

  • Art 54: elected members of both Houses of Parliament + elected members of the State Legislative Assemblies. Explanation: "State" includes NCT of Delhi and Puducherry (added by the Constitution (Seventieth Amendment) Act, 1991).
  • Excluded: nominated members of Parliament (Rajya Sabha's 12 nominated members; LS's 2 Anglo-Indian nominees — nomination to LS abolished by 104th Amdt 2020), nominated members of Assemblies, and members of UT legislatures other than Delhi & Puducherry (which have Assemblies).
  • Art 55: (1) uniformity of scale of representation of the States; (2) value of an MLA's vote = (State population ÷ total elected MLAs) ÷ 1000, plus 1 if the remainder ≥ 500; value of an MP's vote = total value of all States' MLA votes ÷ total elected MPs; (3) proportional representation by single transferable vote, secret ballot. Census proviso: 1971 census till the first census after 2026 is published.
  • Mini-example (worked): if a State has 10,00,000 population and 200 MLAs → 10,00,000/200 = 5,000 → /1000 = 5 votes per MLA. The design gives weight to States (MLAs) while keeping parity States ↔ Union.

Qualifications & conditions — Arts 58–59

  • Art 58: (a) citizen of India; (b) completed 35 years; (c) qualified for election as a member of the House of the People; (2) not holding any office of profit (exception: President/VP/Governor/Minister are not deemed to hold an office of profit by reason only of those offices).
  • Art 59: (1) shall not be a member of either House of Parliament/State Legislature — vacates seat on entering office; (2) no other office of profit; (3) official residence + emoluments fixed by Parliament (until then Second Schedule); (4) emoluments shall not be diminished during term.
  • Art 56: 5-year term from entry; resignation to the VP (who forthwith communicates it to the Speaker, proviso (a) + Art 56(2)); removal only by impeachment for violation of the Constitution (Art 61); holdover until successor enters (no caretaker gap).
  • Art 57: eligible for re-election (unlimited times). Art 62: election for full-term vacancy completed before expiry; casual vacancy — as soon as possible, within 6 months; elected person serves full 5 years.
  • Art 70: Parliament may provide for discharge of President's functions in contingencies not covered by the Chapter (gap-filler for "any other contingency").
  • Art 71: all doubts/disputes about Presidential/VP election → Supreme Court, final; if election void, prior acts not invalidated (71(2)); Parliament may regulate by law (71(3)); vacancy among electors no bar (71(4)) — invoked in Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) 2 SCC 1 (Bihar HC dissolution case) where a member's election was set down but Art 71(4) protected his acts.
Oath of the President (Third Schedule, Art 60): sworn before the Chief Justice of India (or, in his absence, the senior-most available SC Judge): "…I will faithfully execute the office of President … and will to the best of my ability preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law and that I will devote myself to the service and well-being of the people of India." U.N.R. Rao quotes this oath (para 6) to show the President is "bound by the provisions of the Constitution" — the oath is a justiciable limit on discretion.

1.3 Impeachment of the President — Article 61 (and how it differs from removal of the VP)

Art 61 — the only mode of removing a President before expiry of term (besides resignation):

  1. Charge "for violation of the Constitution" preferred by either House.
  2. Not less than 14 days' written notice, signed by not less than 1/4 of the total membership of that House, with the proposal to move the resolution.
  3. The charge must be passed by majority of not less than 2/3 of the total membership of the House in which it is preferred.
  4. Then the other House investigates (or causes the charge to be investigated) — the President has the right to appear and to be represented.
  5. If that House passes a resolution by 2/3 of total membership declaring the charge sustained → removal from office with effect from that date.
  • Ground is limited: "violation of the Constitution" — not policy, not loss of majority (a President has no political majority to lose). No impeachment has ever been moved in India.
  • Art 361(1) proviso 1: the President's conduct may be brought under review by any court/tribunal appointed by either House for Art 61 investigation — the single exception to immunity.
  • Contrast VP removal — Art 67(b): a resolution of the Council of States passed by majority of all the then members and agreed to by the House of the People, after 14 days' notice; ground not specified (any ground); emoluments cannot be reduced (67 read with 97). Never used either.

President vs Vice-President — quick diff

  • Who elects: People's reps (Parliament + Assemblies) vs Parliament alone.
  • LS qualification: President must be LS-qualified; VP must be RS-qualified.
  • Removal: impeachment for constitutional violation (Art 61, double 2/3) vs RS resolution + LS agreement (Art 67(b), simple majority of members present? — majority of all then members of RS, simple in LS).
  • Oath: before CJI vs before President.
  • Resignation: to VP vs to President.
  • Extra office: none vs ex-officio RS Chairman.

1.5 Powers of the President — an organised map

Fig. 3 — Presidential powers: every box is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Samsher, para 57), except the narrow referral/finality roles the Constitution itself assigns (Art 103(2): EC opinion; Art 61 process; Art 71 is judicial).

1.5.1 Clemency — Article 72 (vs Governor's Art 161)

Art 72(1). The President shall have the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of any offence—
(a) in all cases where the punishment or sentence is by a Court Martial;
(b) in all cases where the punishment or sentence is for an offence against any law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the Union extends;
(c) in all cases where the sentence is a sentence of death.
Art 72(3): nothing in (c) affects power to suspend/remit/commute death sentences exercisable by the Governor under any law for the time being in force (i.e., CrPC remission provisions).Constitution of India, Article 72
  • Scope differences: President = court-martial + Union-law matters + all death sentences; Governor = matters of State executive power (including death sentences under State/central laws applicable in the State, via CrPC s.432/433 powers) but never court-martial.
  • Judicial review of mercy: Epuru Sudhakar (case 20) — limited review on mala fide, extraneous considerations, non-application of mind, suppression of relevant material (worked out in §6.7).
  • Art 72 vs statutory remission: Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) 1 SCC 385 and Kehri Lal line — remission schemes (CrPC 432/433) must be exercised reasonably, on notice to the State/public prosecutor where the convict's release affects victims (see Epuru — the victims' sons were the petitioners).

1.5.2 Assent to Bills — Article 111 (preview; full treatment in §5)

  • President shall declare either that he assents or withholds assent; may (if not a Money Bill) return the Bill once with a message for reconsideration; if passed again with/without amendment and re-presented, "the President shall not withhold assent therefrom".
  • No time-limit on assent → the famous pocket veto (President Zail Singh, Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill 1986 — never returned, lapsed with the 8th Lok Sabha in 1989). Contrast: on a returned-and-repassed Bill the veto disappears (mandatory assent).
  • Assent is normally exercised on CoM advice — refusal would require the advice of the Council (de Smith's rule, quoted in Samsher para 154 via M.P. SPE: withholding assent "on the ground that the Monarch strongly disapproved … would nevertheless be unconstitutional").

Also in Articles 60–79 worth remembering: Art 60 oath (above); Art 76 Attorney-General (appointed by President, qualified to be SC Judge, holds office during pleasure, right of audience — duties on Art 77(3) rules); Art 79 — Parliament = President + two Houses (so the President is part of Parliament but has no vote in either House).

Section 2 · Part VI, Chapter II — Articles 153 to 161 (+ Article 361)The Governor of a State

The Governor mirrors the President — same constitutional-head logic, same pleasure-tenure, same clemency chapter — but with three twists: (i) he is appointed by the President (Art 155), (ii) he alone has a constitutionally carved-out discretion channel (Art 163), and (iii) no impeachment — only "pleasure" removal (Art 156(1)), policed in B.P. Singhal.

2.1 Text and structure — Arts 153–160

Art 153. There shall be a Governor for each State: Provided that nothing in this article shall prevent the appointment of the same person as Governor for two or more States.
Art 154. (1) The executive power of the State shall be vested in the Governor and shall be exercised by him either directly or through officers subordinate to him in accordance with this Constitution. …
Art 155. The Governor of a State shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal.
Art 156. (1) The Governor shall hold office during the pleasure of the President. (2) May resign by writing addressed to the President. (3) Otherwise a term of five years, with holdover until successor enters.
Art 157. No person shall be eligible … unless he is a citizen of India and has completed the age of thirty-five years.
Art 159. Oath before the Chief Justice of the High Court of the State (or senior-most available Judge) — same oath-substance as the President's (preserve, protect and defend).
Art 160. The President may make provision for discharge of the Governor's functions in any contingency not provided for in the Chapter.Constitution of India, Articles 153–160 (verbatim)

Art 158 — Conditions of office

  • (1) Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State — a legislator appointed Governor vacates the seat on entering office.
  • (2) Shall not hold any other office of profit.
  • (3) Official residence rent-free + emoluments/allowances/privileges determined by Parliament by law (until then Second Schedule).
  • (3A) Same person as Governor of 2+ States → emoluments allocated as the President may by order determine.
  • (4) Emoluments shall not be diminished during term.

Appointment & removal — the practical law

  • Who appoints: the President (Art 155) — i.e., on the Union CoM's advice; in practice, the Prime Minister's Cabinet chooses the Governor (the Constitution has no election, no fixed eligibility pool, no residency/State-connection requirement — unlike the US state governors).
  • Removed how?: only "during the pleasure of the President" (156(1)) — no impeachment procedure exists for Governors; resignation is the only personal exit (156(2)).
  • Term: 5 years (156(3)) — but pleasure can cut it short at any time: B.P. Singhal — remove without reason/notice, but not arbitrarily; "loss of confidence/out of sync with Union policy" is no ground.
  • Convention check: Art 157 + political convention — an outsider/retired figure of the ruling party is usually picked; the Sarkaria & Punchhi Commissions recommended consultation with the CM and fixed tenure in practice (recommendations, not law).

2.2 Article 161 — Governor's clemency (the Epuru power)

Art 161. The Governor of a State shall have the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of any offence against any law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the State extends.Constitution of India, Article 161
  • Coverage: offences under State laws (and central laws applied in the State) within the State's executive power — including death sentences (subject to Art 72(3) saving & CrPC ss. 431–434), but not court-martial matters (exclusive to the President).
  • Reviewability: Epuru Sudhakar (2006) — Art 161 clemency is subject to limited judicial review for mala fide/extraneous grounds/no application of mind/suppression of material; quashed G.O.Ms. No. 170 (2005) and remitted the matter for fresh consideration. Full facts in §6.7.
  • Delay as illegality: Maru Ram guidelines; in Perarivalan v. State of Tamil Nadu (2022) 9 SCC 1 the Supreme Court, noting inordinate delay in deciding the mercy petition (filed 1998, pardon petition pending), used Art 142 to commute the death sentence — a reminder that executive clemency is answerable to constitutional delay-doctrine as well as mala fide.
  • Who advises the Governor: normally the State CoM through the Home Department; the "satisfaction" is the constitutional satisfaction of the Council (Samsher, para 30: Arts 123, 213, 311(2), 317, 352(1), 356, 360 — all cabinet satisfaction; Art 161 falls in the same family of executive acts).

2.3 Article 361 — Immunity of the President and Governors

Art 361(1): The President, or the Governor … shall not be answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of his office or for any act done or purporting to be done … — proviso 1: conduct of the President may be brought under review for an Art 61 impeachment investigation; proviso 2: nothing restricts the right of any person to bring appropriate proceedings against the Government of India or the Government of a State.
361(2): No criminal proceedings shall be instituted or continued against the President/Governor during his term.
361(3): No process for arrest or imprisonment during term.
361(4): No personal-capacity civil proceedings during term until two months after written notice stating nature of proceedings, cause of action and relief.Constitution of India, Article 361 (verbatim)
  • Why suits are against the Union/State, never the office-holder: Samsher (argument adopted, extract lines on Arts 300 & 361) — "Articles 300 and 361 indicate that neither the President nor the Governor can be sued for any executive action of the State" — hence writs/claims lie against the Union/State Government (the "Government" in the name of whom business is transacted under Arts 77(1)/166(1)).
  • Immunity is personal, not institutional: B.R. Kapur — Art 361 did not bar quo warranto against the person appointed Chief Minister (the appointee, not the Governor, was the respondent); "the Governor cannot be made immune from the consequence of an unconstitutional appointment".
  • But the action is still reviewable: immunity from being a party ≠ immunity of the order: orders are challenged against the State/Union, and courts examine them for malafide/arbitrariness (Epuru: G.O. quashed though issued by the Governor — relief ran against the order/Government).
Exam trap — "Can a Governor be removed by impeachment?" No. Art 61 is for the President only; Governors hold "during the pleasure of the President" (156(1)). The real question examiners want is what limits the pleasure → B.P. Singhal: valid constitutional grounds + no arbitrariness + limited judicial review. And there is no fixed 5-year immunity — the term in 156(3) is "subject to the foregoing provisions" (i.e., subject to pleasure).

Section 3 · Articles 73, 162, 77 & 166Executive Powers of the Union and the States

Arts 73/162 allocate the subject-matter reach of executive power in a federation built on the three Lists; Arts 77/166 provide the form in which government acts — and the doctrinal key that everything in the President's/Governor's name is really the government's act.

3.1 Article 73 — extent of the Union's executive power

Art 73(1). Subject to the provisions of this Constitution, the executive power of the Union shall extend—
(a) to the matters with respect to which Parliament has power to make laws; and
(b) to the exercise of such rights, authority and jurisdiction as are exercisable by the Government of India by virtue of any treaty or agreement:
Provided that the executive power referred to in sub-clause (a) shall not, save as expressly provided in this Constitution or in any law made by Parliament, extend in any State to matters with respect to which the Legislature of the State has also power to make laws.
Art 73(2). Until otherwise provided by Parliament, a State … may continue to exercise … such executive power or functions as the State … could exercise immediately before the commencement of this Constitution.Constitution of India, Article 73 (verbatim)
  • Follows legislation: Union executive power tracks the Union List + residuary matters (Parliament's legislative competence) — "executive power follows legislative power" as the default federal rule.
  • The proviso is the federal brake: on Concurrent List subjects inside a State, the Union's executive power does not extend unless expressly conferred by the Constitution (e.g., Art 258A? — no: Art 258 is Union→State delegation; express conferments e.g. Arts 249/250 emergency, 253 treaties, Entry 14 Union List treaties) or by a law of Parliament (e.g., a law conferring powers on Union officers). Practical illustration: CBI investigations in States depend on State consent under s. 6 of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946 (police being State List Entry 2) — the classic tug-of-war over "consent" (blanket vs case-specific) that shows Art 73's proviso at work.
  • Art 73(2): preserves pre-1950 State executive functions over matters later made Union-legislative until Parliament provides — a transitional federal compromise.
  • Art 73(1)(b): treaty/executive-agreement rights — e.g., obligations under treaties (passport/extradition/consular functions), dovetailing with Entry 14 Union List (discussed in Topic 2 for cession).

3.2 Article 162 — extent of the State's executive power

Art 162. Subject to the provisions of this Constitution, the executive power of a State shall extend to the matters with respect to which the Legislature of the State has power to make laws:
Provided that in any matter with respect to which the Legislature of a State and Parliament have power to make laws, the executive power of the State shall be subject to, and limited by, the executive power expressly conferred by this Constitution or by any law made by Parliament upon the Union or authorities thereof.Constitution of India, Article 162 (verbatim)
  • Mirror image of Art 73: State executive power = State List + (concurrently) legislative competence, but on Concurrent matters it yields to the Union's express executive power.
  • Federal overlap: in a Concurrent field with no express Union conferment, both governments may act — until repugnancy rules (Art 254) or a law of Parliament displaces State action; executive conflict is resolved by the same priority the provision states.
  • "Conterminous" idea: NCT Delhi (para 204) applied the structure by analogy — the NCT's executive power is conterminous with the legislative power of the Delhi Assembly under Art 239AA(3) (all State-List subjects except Entries 1, 2, 18 — public order, police, land — plus all Concurrent subjects): a ready-made sentence for any question on the shape of executive power.
  • All executive power may partake of legislative/judicial character: Samsher (para 29) — "executive power may also partake of legislative or judicial actions. All powers and functions of the President except his legislative powers … are executive powers" — hence removal of a probationer, sanction to prosecute, etc., are executive acts even when statute-styled.

3.3 Articles 77 & 166 — conduct of government business

Art 77 (Union) — text

  • (1) All executive action of the Government of India shall be expressed to be taken in the name of the President.
  • (2) Orders authenticated as per presidential rules — validity of a so-authenticated order shall not be called in question on the ground that it is not an order made or executed by the President (an authentication bar only — the order's legality on other grounds remains reviewable).
  • (3) The President shall make rules for convenient transaction of business and for allocation among Ministers → Government of India (Transaction of Business) Rules, 1961 + Allocation of Business Rules 1961 (§7).
  • Not delegation: Samsher para 30 — "Neither Article 77(3) nor 166(3) provides for any delegation … the decision of any Minister or officer under the Rules … is the decision of the President or the Governor respectively."

Art 166 (State) — mirror

  • (1) All executive action of the State Government expressed in the name of the Governor.
  • (2) Same authentication bar.
  • (3) Rules for transaction of business + allocation among Ministers — except business in which the Governor is required to act in his discretion (the Art 163 carve-out written into the business-rules power).
  • Implemented by each State's Transaction of Business Rules; NCT Delhi has its own — Transaction of Business of the Government of NCTD Rules, 1993 (Chapter V on difference of opinion — central to NCT Delhi 2018).
  • Art 78 / Art 167 — the PM's/CM's duties to the President/Governor: (a) communicate all CoM decisions and legislative proposals; (b) furnish information called for; (c) submit for CoM consideration any matter decided by a Minister alone if the head so requires. U.N.R. Rao (para 10): with Arts 74(1), 75(2)-(3) read harmoniously, "other articles of the Constitution also have full play, e.g. Article 77(3) … and Article 78". These are the information lifelines that make the ceremonial head's office workable — usually enforced politically, but cited by courts as part of the constitutional scheme (see §5 matrix).
  • Consequences of the "name of" rule: every gazette notification, ordinance, appointment, sanction and mercy order legally runs through the President/Governor; yet responsibility lies with the Council (Ram Jawaya: "the Governor is the Government, aided by Ministers"; Sanjeevi Naidu quoted in U.N.R. Rao: "the Cabinet is responsible to the Legislature for every action taken in any of the ministries. That is the essence of joint responsibility").
Real-world anchor: When the Delhi Government's file says " Lieutenant Governor's despatch/communication", or when a Governor's Order under Art 200 "reserves" a Bill, the citizen is seeing Arts 77/166 + 200 machinery — the government acting in the head's name. The 2015–2018 Delhi tussle (§8) was, at bottom, a fight over whose "aid and advice" controlled that machinery under Art 239AA.

Section 4 · Articles 74–75 (Union) & 163–164 (States)The Council of Ministers — Composition, Appointment & Responsibility

The CoM is the real executive. Arts 74/163 make it mandatory and binding ("aid and advise"); Arts 75/164 govern its composition, tenure and responsibility. Almost every case in §6 turns on how these clauses interact with the ceremonial head's residual roles.

4.1 Article 74 — aid and advise (the spine of the executive)

Art 74(1). There shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President who shall, in the exercise of his functions, act in accordance with such advice:
Provided that the President may require the Council of Ministers to reconsider such advice, either generally or otherwise, and the President shall act in accordance with the advice tendered after such reconsideration.
Art 74(2). The question whether any, and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the President shall not be inquired into in any court.Constitution of India, Article 74 (verbatim; as it stands after the 42nd and 44th Amendments)
  • Mandatory: U.N.R. Rao — "Article 74(1) is mandatory in form … we must read the word 'shall' as meaning 'shall' and not 'may'. The Constituent Assembly did not choose the Presidential system of Government." (full holding in §6.2).
  • One reconsideration, then binding: the proviso gives the President a single constitutional courtesy — return advice for reconsideration; after reconsideration the advice is final (no second refusal). (Compare the Governor: no such proviso exists in Art 163(1) — S.P. Anand, para 12.)
  • Art 74(2) is a shield for advice, not for action: courts cannot ask "what did the Ministers advise?" — but they can and do review the resulting order for legality, mala fide, extraneous grounds and arbitrariness (Epuru, B.P. Singhal, M.P. SPE; and even for Art 356 proclamations — S.R. Bommai line: judicial review of the action survives the 74(2) bar). Working formula for exams: review of the act yes, review of the advice's content no — unless the advice's vice is inferable from the act's own record (malafide/extraneous).
  • Textual history (memory aid): the mandatory clause + 74(2) bar came with the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976; the reconsideration proviso was added by the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978. Quote only "as it stands today".

The two classic "non-member" questions

1. Non-legislator as PM/CM — allowed (6 months)

  • Art 75(5)/164(4) presume you can appoint a non-member — otherwise the 6-month clause is senseless (S.P. Anand, para 14; Constituent Assembly: Ambedkar spurned the "only elected members as Ministers" amendment — draft Arts 62 & 144).
  • Chain: Har Sharan Verma (T.N. Singh, non-member CM UP) → (1985) 2 SCC 48 (16th Amdt oath irrelevant) → Harsharan Verma (Kesri, Union Minister) → HC parallels (Dr Janak Raj Jai WP 2408/1996 Delhi; Ashok Sen Gupta CO 1336(w)/1996 Calcutta).
  • English convention (Halsbury: PM "invariably a member") = not our scheme (S.P. Anand, para 15/17).

2. Convicted/disqualified as CM — not allowed

  • B.R. Kapur: appointment of a person disqualified under s. 8(3) RPA 1951 (conviction + ≥2 years' sentence, conviction not stayed) as CM was quashed by quo warranto.
  • Suspension of sentence ≠ suspension of conviction.
  • Art 164(1) read with 164(4): the 6-month non-member window is for the otherwise qualified; disqualification under Art 191/RPA is a hard bar.
  • Governor must decline where disqualification appears on the face of the record — that is a constitutional mandate, not an "impossible burden"; where facts are disputed, the Governor's Art 164 choice is his discretion (and the appointee's authority is court-testable).

Collective responsibility — how it actually works

  • Majority device: loss of majority → resignation/dismissal (75(3)/164(2)); money Bill/confidence vote; the Cabinet speaks with one voice (individual dissent = resign or accept the majority — S.P. Anand, para 16: "If any Minister does not agree … his option is to resign or accept the majority decision").
  • Tenure device: "pleasure" (75(2)/164(1)) exercised by the head through the PM/CM's advice — the head cannot drop a Minister on his own whim (the S.P. Anand/UNR Rao logic; contrast Singhal where President's pleasure over Governors stands unmediated).
  • Defection device: Tenth Schedule + 75(1B)/164(1B) + Art 361B — a disqualificationee cannot be a Minister or hold remunerative political posts.
  • Statutory device: 15% cap (91st Amdt); tribal-welfare Minister mandate (164(1) proviso); oath before entering office.
  • Failure modes: government refuses to quit after losing majority → Governor may dismiss (Samsher para 154(b)); House dissolved → CoM continues in office (U.N.R. Rao) until a new government assumes office.

5.3 The three "finality vs review" patterns in one box

Pattern A — Advice-bar (74(2)/163(3))

Protects deliberative content. Verified by courts: the act is still tested (Epuru; Singhal: court may call for the material behind an order if prima facie arbitrary). Bommai line: even Art 356's "satisfaction" is reviewable for extraneousness/mala fide.

Pattern B — Express finality (103(1), 163(2), 217(3))

Bars merits re-appreciation but presupposes the decision-maker acted within jurisdiction: obtained the EC opinion (103(2)), genuinely was in a discretionary zone (163(2)), genuinely had a "question" (217(3)). Defect in precondition = reviewable (Anisminic-style: jurisdictional error survives every ouster).

Pattern C — Constitutional mandate (74(1), 75, 111, 163(1))

Positive duties ("shall act in accordance"; "shall not withhold assent"; CoM "shall be" constituted): courts enforce the existence and form of compliance (U.N.R. Rao: quo warranto against a PM with no CoM) and police delay/arbitrariness (T.N. Governor 2023).

5.4 Relationship of President/Governor with the CoM — the four governing propositions

  1. Constitutional head, not chief executive: all executive (and legislative-character) functions are exercised on aid & advice — Samsher paras 57 & 88; U.N.R. Rao (mandatory CoM even after dissolution).
  2. Constitutional satisfaction: wherever the text requires the P/G's "satisfaction" (Arts 123, 213, 311(2)(c), 317, 352(1), 356, 360), it is the Council's satisfaction — Samsher para 30 (the citation list examiners love).
  3. Discretion is the narrow exception: only where the Constitution (expressly or by necessary implication) requires it; even then the Governor must act in harmony with the CoM — "The Constitution does not aim at providing a parallel administration" (Samsher para 55); plus the rare Samsher-154 situations and M.P. SPE's principled cases.
  4. Information dialogue: Arts 78/167 keep the head informed; Arts 77(3)/166(3) allocate business; the head's only formal lever is one reconsideration (74(1) proviso) — and, in States, the discretion channel and (for Governors) Art 356 reporting.
"…the President as well as the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in executive action and is not required by the Constitution to act personally without the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers or against the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. Where the Governor has any discretion the Governor acts on his own judgment. The Governor exercises his discretion in harmony with his Council of Ministers."Samsher Singh, para 88 (DU case material) — the single most quotable paragraph of this topic

5.5 Art 163 vs Art 163(2) — the "discretion" reading that fills 10-mark answers

  • Textual route (appellants in Samsher, rejected): 163(1)'s "except … discretion" + 163(2)'s finality → claim that all non-justiciable? Court: discretion exists only where the Constitution requires it — express instances listed (239(2), Sixth Schedule 9(2), 371A four pockets; 356 report even against advice because failure "may be because of the conduct of the Council of Ministers" — Samsher para 55; Art 200 second proviso — reserve bills derogating from HC powers).
  • Interpretive route (M.P. SPE, Sorabjee's argument accepted): "If that were so then sub-clause (2) of Article 163 would be redundant" — 163(2) can only be doing work for matters not expressly marked discretionary; therefore implied/occasion-based discretion exists (the four M.P. SPE situations).
  • Boundary rule: 163(2) says the validity of an act cannot be attacked "on the ground that he ought or ought not to have acted in his discretion" — i.e., no merits review of the exercise; it does not bar (a) deciding the characterisation (was it discretionary at all?), (b) malafide/arbitrary exercise, (c) non-compliance with the Constitution's conditions. (B.P. Singhal shows the same "limited review" design for the parallel pleasure-clause in Art 156(1).)

Case 14 · DU syllabus list

B.P. Singhal v. Union of India, (2010) 6 SCC 331

Constitution Bench (5 Judges) · decided 7-4-2010 · Art 32 PIL · Arts 156(1), 154, 163 read with Arts 53/155

Facts

In 2004, within months of the change of government at the Centre, the Union Government caused the transfer and/or termination of several State Governors (the orders and the file material described the incumbents as having lost confidence of the Union Council of Ministers / being out of sync with the policy of the Union Government). A writ petition under Art 32 was filed challenging these actions as unconstitutional — contending that a Governor holds a constitutional office for a five-year term (Art 156(3)) and cannot be removed for political dissonance with the party in power at the Centre.

Issues

1. Whether "loss of confidence of the Union Council of Ministers" / "out of sync with the Union Government's policy" is a valid ground for removing a Governor under Art 156(1)?
2. Whether Art 156(1)'s "pleasure of the President" is (a) wholly unreviewable, or (b) subject to constitutional limitations — and whether reasons/notice are required before removal?
3. Whether such an order is amenable to judicial review, and what the locus standi of the petitioners is?

Arguments

For the petitioners (challenging removal):
  • A Governor is not an employee, agent or delegate of the Union Government; he holds a constitutional office with a prescribed term (Art 156(3)) — removal must therefore have a constitutional, not political, foundation.
  • "Loss of confidence" of the ruling party at the Centre is a concept unknown to the Constitution for Governors — confidence matters only for the elected ministry vis-à-vis the Assembly/House (Arts 164(2)/75(3)).
  • Unlimited, reasonless removals would reduce the office to a pliable instrument — arbitrary and violative of Arts 14/163 (constitutional morality).
  • If removal orders are arbitrary/mala fide, courts must review; otherwise Art 156(1) becomes a licence.
For the Union of India (defending removal):
  • Art 156(1) is unqualified: the Governor holds office "during the pleasure of the President" — no reasons, no notice, no inquiry is constitutionally required; the pleasure can be withdrawn at any time.
  • Art 156(3)'s five-year term is expressly "subject to the foregoing provisions" — i.e., subject to pleasure; so there is no fixed tenure at all.
  • The question of whether the President still "pleases" is non-justiciable; no court can sit in judgment over the President's pleasure; the petitioners lack locus as to individual Governors' removals.

Decision (Constitution Bench — holdings)

  1. "Loss of confidence / out of sync with Union policy" is NOT a valid ground. The Governor is not an employee or agent of the Union Government; government-policy dissonance cannot ground removal — the office serves the Constitution, not the party in power.
  2. Pleasure without reasons is permissible — a Governor may be removed at any time without assigning reasons or notice (Art 156(1)) — but pleasure is not licence: the power must be exercised within constitutional limits and not arbitrarily, capriciously or for mala fide purposes. The grounds for compulsion must be rare and exceptional/compelling (the Court's framing allows reasons wider than corruption/disability but insists they be genuine constitutional reasons).
  3. The order is subject to limited judicial review. If a prima facie case of arbitrariness, mala fide or caprice is made out, the court may call for the material/record on which the order rests and examine it for constitutional infirmity. (This is how the advice-bar of Arts 74(2)/163(3) coexists with review: the court reviews the act and its record, not the ministers' inner deliberation.)
  4. Locus standi: the petitioners were not entitled to individual relief in respect of particular Governors' removals; but the general legal question on the scope of Art 156(1) was decided in the PIL — no Governor was reinstated.
Principle evolved

Article 156(1) "pleasure" is a constitutionally bounded power: removal of a Governor needs no reasons and no notice, yet must rest on a constitutionally relevant, non-arbitrary, non-mala fide ground — political disagreement with the Union Council is not such a ground — and the order is amenable to limited judicial review (call for record + test for arbitrariness/mala fide).

→ This is the template answer for "how far is the pleasure clause justiciable": yes, on the narrow grounds of malafide/arbitrariness/extraneousness; no, on merits/second-guessing policy. Pair with Epuru Sudhakar (Art 161 — same design) and M.P. SPE (Art 163 — review of reasons where discretion used).

Case 15 · DU case material — excerpt pp. 75–77

U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi, AIR 1971 SC 1002

S.M. Sikri, C.J. + G.K. Mitter, K.S. Hegde, A.N. Grover, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, JJ. · quo warranto · Arts 74, 75, 53, 60, 77, 78

Facts

The House of the People was dissolved by the President on 27-12-1970 under Art 85(2). The respondent, Indira Gandhi, continued as Prime Minister after the dissolution. The appellant filed a writ of quo warranto questioning her continuation, contending that on dissolution of the LS, the Council of Ministers (PM + Ministers) ceased to hold office because Art 75(3) makes the CoM collectively responsible to the House of the People — and "how can the CoM be collectively responsible to a House which has already been dissolved?" It was further contended that no void would be created because the President could exercise the executive power of the Union directly or through officers subordinate under Art 53(1) (i.e., a President-direct rule interregnum).

Issues

1. Is it essential to have a Council of Ministers under Art 74(1) even at a time when the House of the People has been dissolved or its term has expired?
2. Does Art 75(3) ("collectively responsible to the House of the People") compel the PM and Ministers to quit on dissolution — or can the President then act under Art 53(1) on his own?

Arguments

Appellant's contention (verbatim structure from DU excerpt):
  • Art 75(3) must be interpreted "according to its own terms, regardless of the conventions that prevail in the United Kingdom": collective responsibility to a non-existent House is impossible → CoM must cease on dissolution.
  • Consequently the President may rule directly through officers subordinate under Art 53(1) without ministers — without creating any constitutional void.
Respondent's position (upheld by the Court):
  • Art 74(1) is mandatory: there "shall be" a CoM with the PM at the head, and the President "shall" act in accordance with its advice — this is the basic structure of the parliamentary executive the Constituent Assembly chose (not a Presidential system).
  • Art 75(2) (Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President) continues to operate; the President has not withdrawn his pleasure.
  • Harmonising construction: Art 75(3) applies when the House is not dissolved/prorogued; the dissolution-expiry contingency is otherwise covered by s. 14, RPA 1951 (five-year term machinery).

Decision

  • Article 74(1) is mandatory — "shall" is "shall", not "may" (paras 7–8): "The Constituent Assembly did not choose the Presidential system of Government. If we were to give effect to this contention of the appellant we would be changing the whole concept of the Executive." Without a CoM there would be no one responsible to the House — the President "with the aid of advisers … would be able to rule the country at least till he is impeached under Article 61" — a result the Constitution does not permit.
  • Art 53(1) is controlled by its last words "in accordance with this Constitution": "Any exercise of the executive power not in accordance with the Constitution will be liable to be set aside" (para 6) — direct Presidential rule without a CoM is not "in accordance with this Constitution".
  • Art 60 oath (quoted in full at para 6) binds the President to "preserve, protect and defend the Constitution" — he cannot govern without the constitutionally mandated Ministers.
  • Art 75(3) harmonised (para 9): "Article 75(3) only applies when the House of People does not stand dissolved or prorogued." While the House exists, 75(3) has full operation; on dissolution, Ministers continue under Art 75(2) — "The President has not said that it is his pleasure that the respondent shall not hold office." The expiry-of-term contingency (Art 83(2)) is covered by s. 14 of the RPA 1951.
  • Arts 77(3) and 78 have full play even post-dissolution (para 10) — business rules for allocation and the PM's information-duties continue, proving the machinery is designed to run.
  • The Court quoted Ram Jawaya Kapur (paras 13–14) and A. Sanjeevi Naidu ("the Governor is essentially a constitutional head, the administration … run by the Council of Ministers … the Cabinet is responsible to the Legislature for every action taken in any of the ministries. That is the essence of joint responsibility") — U.N.R. Rao appeal dismissed.

Arguments of both sides — the dialogue worth reproducing

Sikri, C.J.: "If the words of an Article are clear, notwithstanding any relevant convention, effect will, no doubt, be given to the words. But it must be remembered that we are interpreting a Constitution and not an Act of Parliament, a Constitution which establishes a Parliamentary system of Government with a Cabinet. In trying to understand one may well keep in mind the conventions prevalent at the time the Constitution was framed."U.N.R. Principle evolved

(i) A Council of Ministers under Art 74(1) is constitutionally indispensable at all times — including during dissolution/expiration of the Lok Sabha; the President may not exercise executive power personally or through officers bypassing the CoM. (ii) Art 75(3) is not a self-executing dissolution-trigger for ministerial exit; it is read harmoniously with Arts 74(1) and 75(2). (iii) Art 74(1) is mandatory — the parliamentary executive is non-negotiable (a precursor to the basic-structure doctrine).

Case 16 · DU case material — excerpt pp. 78–83

S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda, (1996) 6 SCC 734

A.M. Ahmadi, C.J. and Sujata V. Manohar, J. · Art 32 · Arts 14, 21, 74, 75, 88, 163, 164

Facts

Shri H.D. Deve Gowda, not being a member of either House of Parliament, was appointed Prime Minister of India. The petitioner challenged the appointment as violative of Arts 14, 21 and 75, contending that the President committed a grave constitutional error in swearing him in — the petition was argued in person "with great passion, zeal and emotion". The Court narrated the litigation history: the same petitioner had earlier unsuccessfully challenged (i) T.N. Singh's appointment as CM of U.P. (non-member) — Har Sharan Verma v. Tribhuvan Narain Singh, (1971) 1 SCC 616; (ii) K.P. Tiwari's ministership after the 16th Amendment oath-rule — Har Sharan Verma v. State of U.P., (1985) 2 SCC 48; (iii) Sita Ram Kesri's ministership — Harsharan Verma v. UOI, 1987 Supp SCC 310; and that two High Courts had rejected the identical challenge to Deve Gowda's appointment (Dr Janak Raj Jai, WP 2408/1996, Delhi HC; Ashok Sen Gupta, CO 1336(w)/1996, Calcutta HC).

Issues

1. Can a person who is not a member of either House of Parliament be appointed Prime Minister of India (and, correspondingly, a non-legislator as Chief Minister under Art 164)?
2. If so, for how long, and subject to what constitutional conditions?

Arguments

Petitioner:
  • A PM who is not the elected representative of the people, "in whom the people have not placed confidence", is a great risk "which the country can ill afford to take" — construe the provisions so as to relieve the country of that risk.
  • Art 75(5) permits appointment of a "Minister" for six months — but a Prime Minister is a distinct office from a Minister; nothing in 75(5) suggests a non-member can be PM.
  • Halsbury's Laws of England (3rd Edn.) p. 347, para 745: "By conventional usage the Prime Minister is invariably a member of either House of Commons or House of Lords" — the English convention should inform Art 75.
  • The three Supreme Court decisions relied on are old and need reconsideration in changed circumstances; national interest would be jeopardised otherwise.
Respondent (Attorney General + Court's own survey):
  • The settled line of authority: Har Sharan Verma (1971 — T.N. Singh as non-member CM upheld: Art 164(4) "had an ancient lineage and there was no reason to whittle down the plain thrust" of the provision); (1985) 2 SCC 48 (16th Amdt oath rule irrelevant to appointment); Harsharan Verma 1987 Supp (Kesri — appointing a non-member as Minister "did not militate against the constitutional mechanism nor … against the democratic principles" — Art 75(5) read with Art 88).
  • Delhi HC: "when Article 75(5) speaks of a 'Minister' it takes within its embrace that Minister also who is described in the Constitution as Prime Minister"; Calcutta HC: 75(5) permits a non-member PM "subject to the possibility of his commanding the support of the majority of members of the Lok Sabha".

Decision

  • Section 8 of the DU excerpt (the holding in one line): "a person who is not a member of either House of Parliament or of either House of a State Legislature can be appointed a Minister in the Central Cabinet (which would include a Prime Minister) or a Minister in the State Cabinet (which would include a Chief Minister), as the case may be."
  • 74↔163 and 75↔164 juxtaposition (paras 11–13) — the Court tabulated both pairs and found them materially identical, differing only in: (a) the "except … discretion" clause in 163(1) (absent in 74(1)); (b) the reconsideration proviso in 74(1) (absent in 163(1)); (c) 163(2) discretion-finality (absent in Art 74); (d) 74(2) ≡ 163(3) verbatim; 75(1)-(2) ≡ 164(1); 75(3) ≡ 164(2) (House vs Assembly); 75(5) ≡ 164(4) (six months).
  • Textual reasoning for 75(5) (para 14): the Constitution-makers desired to permit non-member Ministers for six consecutive months — reading the debates (draft Arts 62 & 144), an amendment "No person should be appointed a Minister unless at the time of his appointment, he is elected member of the House" was moved on the ground that it would "cut at the very root of democracy" — and was spurned by Dr Ambedkar.
  • The PM is not a species apart (para 16): the caption of Art 75 is "Other provisions as to Ministers"; no separate provision deals with appointment of the PM; the same oath (Third Schedule) serves PM and Minister; the PM is "chosen by the elected representatives" and can occupy the position "only if he enjoys the confidence of the majority"; collective responsibility does the democratic work.
  • English convention rejected (paras 15, 17): "The English convention … is not our constitutional scheme since our Constitution clearly permits a non-member to be appointed a Chief Minister or a Prime Minister for a short duration of six months. That is why in such cases when there is any doubt in the mind of the President, he normally asks the person appointed to seek a vote of confidence of the House of the People within a few days of his appointment." Practice in India "has been just the opposite" of the British convention.
  • Life-span of the appointment: up to six consecutive months under 75(5)/164(4); within that period the person must be elected to either House or vacates office. Petition dismissed (para 19); interim stays in pending proceedings vacated with direction to dispose of them in light of the judgment.
Principle evolved

The Constitution of India permits a non-legislator to be appointed Prime Minister or Chief Minister (and any Minister), for a maximum of six consecutive months (Arts 75(5)/164(4), reinforced by Arts 75(1), 88 and the absence of any membership qualification in Art 75(1)/164(1)), provided the appointee is otherwise qualified (Arts 84/173) and not disqualified (Arts 102/191, RPA s. 8 — see B.R. Kapur) and commands majority confidence (conventionally proved by a floor test within days). English conventions on PM's membership are not part of our constitutional scheme.

Case 17 · DU case material — excerpt pp. 84–97 (the longest DU extract)

Samsher Singh v. State of Punjab, AIR 1974 SC 2192

Seven Judges · A.N. Ray, C.J. + Palekar, Mathew, Chandrachud, Alagiriswami, Bhagwati, Krishna Iyer, JJ. · Arts 234, 235, 311(2)(c), 74, 75, 77, 163, 166 · overrules Sardari Lal

Facts

Both appellants joined the Punjab Civil Service (Judicial Branch) and were on probation:

  • Samsher Singh — Subordinate Judge on probation: terminated 27-4-1967 by order: "The Governor of Punjab is pleased to terminate the services of Shri Samsher Singh, Subordinate Judge, on probation under Rule 9 of the Punjab Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules, 1952 with immediate effect…"
  • Ishwar Chand Agarwal — PCS(Judicial) : terminated 15-12-1969: "On the recommendation of the High Court of Punjab and Haryana, the Governor of Punjab is pleased to dispense with the services … under Rule 7(3) in Part 'D' of the Punjab Civil Services (Judicial Branch) Rules, 1951…"

The appeals were placed before a larger Bench to decide one referred question: whether the decision in Sardari Lal v. UOI [(1971) 3 SCR 461] correctly lays down the law that where the President or the Governor is to be satisfied, the satisfaction is his personal satisfaction (Sardari Lal concerned Art 311(2) proviso (c) — "in the interest of the security of the State" satisfaction).

Issues

1. (Referred) Is the satisfaction required by the Constitution — under Art 311(2)(c) and similar clauses — the personal satisfaction of the President/Governor (Sardari Lal), or the constitutional satisfaction of the Council of Ministers?
2. Must the Governor exercise powers of appointment/removal in the subordinate judicial services (Arts 234/235) personally, or on aid and advise?
3. Were the two probationer terminations valid (Rule 9 vs Rule 7(3) and Art 311)?

Arguments

Appellants (Governor must act personally):
  • Several functions are conferred on the Governor eo nomine (by name) — appointment/removal of judicial officers, oath, pleasure, discretion clauses — and are not "executive powers of the State" within Art 154/162.
  • Art 163 itself distinguishes functions exercised on aid and advise from functions the Governor must exercise "in his discretion" — extracting "in his discretion", the Governor personally appoints/removes judicial officers.
  • Sardari Lal: where the Constitution says the President/Governor "is satisfied", it means personal satisfaction — otherwise the words are spent.
  • Art 311(2)(c)-type powers cannot be subordinated; they are personal.
State/Union (aid and advise):
  • The Governor exercises constitutional powers as the constitutional/formal head on aid and advise of his CoM, as held in Ram Jawaya Kapur, A. Sanjeevi Naidu and U.N.R. Rao — which were neither referred nor considered in Sardari Lal (para 47).
  • Arts 77(3)/166(3) business rules show the machinery: a Minister's/officer's decision under the rules is the P/G's decision — no delegation required.
  • Arts 300 & 361: neither the President nor the Governor is ever sued for executive action — because the Government acts (para on line 140-144 of the excerpt).

Decision — the grand doctrine (paras 27–33, 48, 54–57, 88)

  • Constitutional or formal head (paras 27–28): "Under this system the President is the constitutional or formal head of the Union and he exercises his powers and functions conferred on him by or under the Constitution on the aid and advice of his Council of Ministers." Same for the Governor — "save in spheres where the Governor is required by or under the Constitution to exercise his functions in his discretion".
  • Art 103 is an exception (para 27): "Article 103 is an exception to the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers because it specifically provides that the President acts only according to the opinion of the Election Commission." — the express exception carved by the text itself.
  • Executive includes legislative/judicial-character functions (para 29): "executive power may also partake of legislative or judicial actions. All powers and functions of the President except his legislative powers as for example in Article 123 … and all powers and functions of the Governor except his legislative power as for example in Article 213 … are executive powers."
  • Business rules ≠ delegation (paras 30–31): "Wherever the Constitution requires the satisfaction of the President or the Governor … as for example in Articles 123, 213, 311(2) proviso (c), 317, 352(1), 356 and 360 the satisfaction required by the Constitution is not the personal satisfaction … but the satisfaction … in the constitutional sense under the Cabinet system of Government. … the decision of any Minister or officer under the Rules of Business … is the decision of the President or the Governor respectively. Neither Article 77(3) nor 166(3) provides for any delegation."
  • English constitutional inheritance (paras 32–33): "In England the Sovereign never acts on his own responsibility … The Indian Constitution envisages a Parliamentary and responsible form of Government … not a Presidential form."
  • Paras 45–53 — overruling Sardari Lal & Babu Ram Upadhya: Sardari Lal's "personal satisfaction" theory is not good law — the decisions it relied on (Moti Ram Deka; Jayantilal Shodhan on Art 258) do not support it; State of U.P. v. Babu Ram Upadhya Prop. 2 (pleasure delegable to subordinates) is superseded by Moti Ram Deka (Art 311 "authority subordinate" language) — "the majority view in Babu Ram Upadhya case is no longer good law after the decision in Moti Ram Deka".
  • Discretion islands enumerated (para 54): Art 239(2) (Governor as administrator of an adjoining UT — "independently of his Council of Ministers"); Sixth Schedule paras 9(2) and 18(3) (18(3) omitted w.e.f. 21-1-1972); Arts 371A(1)(b), (1)(d), (2)(b), (2)(f); plus Art 356 report and Art 200 second proviso as instances.
  • Art 356 report even against advice (para 55): "In making a report under Article 356 the Governor will be justified in exercising his discretion even against the aid and advice of his Council of Ministers. The reason is that the failure of the constitutional machinery may be because of the conduct of the Council of Ministers. … The Constitution does not aim at providing a parallel administration within the State by allowing the Governor to go against the advice of the Council of Ministers." (In all other discretionary matters he must act "in harmony" with the CoM.)
  • Para 57 — dispositive on the referred question: "the President or the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers … in all matters which vest in the Executive whether those functions are executive or legislative in character. Neither the President nor the Governor is to exercise the executive functions personally. … Appointment or dismissal or removal of persons belonging to the Judicial Service of the State is not a personal function but is an executive function of the Governor exercised in accordance with the rules in that behalf."
  • On the two terminations: Rule 9 (Samsher) required the probationer to be apprised of grounds and given an opportunity to show cause — the termination order "was one of punishment … concerned themselves with matters which were really trifle … The order of termination is in infraction of Rule 9" → set aside (paras 86–87). Agarwal: termination under Rule 7(3) on the HC's recommendation was, in substance, a punishment without Art 311 safeguards; HC should not have abdicated its Art 235 control — Agarwal declared a member of PCS(Judicial) (para 89).
  • Para 88 (the exam paragraph): "the President as well as the Governor acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in executive action and is not required by the Constitution to act personally without … or against the aid and advice. Where the Governor has any discretion the Governor acts on his own judgment. The Governor exercises his discretion in harmony with his Council of Ministers. … That is why any action by any servant of the Union or the State in regard to appointment or dismissal is brought against the Union or the State and not against the President or the Governor."

Exceptional situations recognised — the celebrated "para 154" (quoted in the M.P. SPE DU excerpt)

"We declare the law of this branch of our Constitution to be that the President and Governor, custodians of all executive and other powers under various articles shall, by virtue of these provisions, exercise their formal constitutional powers only upon and in accordance with the advice of their Ministers save in a few well-known exceptional situations. Without being dogmatic or exhaustive, these situations relate to (a) the choice of Prime Minister (Chief Minister), restricted though this choice is by the paramount consideration that he should command a majority in the House; (b) the dismissal of a Government which has lost its majority in the House, but refuses to quit office; (c) the dissolution of the House where an appeal to the country is necessitous, although in this area the head of State should avoid getting involved in politics and must be advised by his Prime Minister (Chief Minister) who will eventually take the responsibility for the step. … We have no doubt that de Smith's statement … regarding royal assent holds good for the President and Governor in India: 'Refusal of the royal assent on the ground that the Monarch strongly disapproved of a Bill or that it was intensely controversial would nevertheless be unconstitutional…'"Samsher Singh, para 154 (reproduced in the DU case material for M.P. SPE, pp. 99–100) — note: the exceptions are "not exhaustive" (M.P. SPE, para 12)
Principle evolved

(i) The President and the Governor are constitutional/formal heads who exercise all executive — and legislative-character — functions on the aid and advice of their Councils; wherever the Constitution requires their "satisfaction", it is the constitutional satisfaction of the Council (list: Arts 123, 213, 311(2)(c), 317, 352(1), 356, 360). (ii) Business rules under Arts 77(3)/166(3) are not delegation — a Minister's/officer's decision is the P/G's decision. (iii) Sardari Lal overruled; Babu Ram Upadhya Prop. 2 no longer good law. (iv) Discretion exists only where the Constitution requires it — and even then must be exercised in harmony with the Council (no parallel administration), with the recognised exceptional situations (choice of head, refusal-to-quit dismissal, dissolution; Art 356 report against advice).

Case 18 · DU case material — excerpt pp. 98–107

M.P. Special Police Establishment v. State of M.P., (2004) 8 SCC 788

Five Judges · N. Santosh Hegde, S.N. Variava, B.P. Singh, H.K. Sema, S.B. Sinha, JJ. (Variava, J. for the Bench) · referred 12-9-2003 · Art 163, CrPC s. 197, PC Act 1988 s. 13(1)(d)+(2), IPC s. 120B

Facts

  1. Rajendra Kumar Singh and Bisahu Ram Yadav were Ministers in the Government of Madhya Pradesh (respondents in the appeals).
  2. A complaint was made to the Lokayukta, M.P. that they had released 7.5 acres of land illegally to its earlier owners though it had been acquired by the Indore Development Authority. An FIR was lodged on 31-3-1998.
  3. The Lokayukta held there were sufficient grounds to prosecute the two Ministers under s. 13(1)(d) read with s. 13(2) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 and for criminal conspiracy under s. 120-B IPC, directing the DG (SPE) under s. 4(1) of the M.P. Special Police Establishment Act, 1970 to register and investigate (by an officer not below SP rank). By the time of the report, the Ministers had already resigned — hence the sanction question arose for the s. 120B offence.
  4. Sanction sought from the CoM: the Council held there was "not an iota of material" showing criminal conspiracy and refused sanction — saying no prima facie case was made out.
  5. The Governor considered the request keeping in view the CoM's decision, opined that the available documents and evidence were enough for a prima facie case, and granted sanction under s. 197 CrPC on 24-9-1998 — i.e., against the advice of the CoM.
  6. The Ministers challenged the Governor's order under Arts 226/227. A Single Judge held the sanction was not a matter in which the Governor could act "in his discretion" under Art 163; he could not act contrary to aid and advise; bias could not be imputed to the entire CoM; and doctrine of necessity could not apply. The Division Bench upheld this (distinguishing State of Maharashtra v. Ramdas Shrinivas Nayak [(1982) 2 SCC 463] as applicable only to a Chief Minister). The matter was referred to Five Judges.

Issue

Can a Governor act in his own discretion and against the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in granting sanction for prosecution of Ministers (former Ministers) under the PC Act/IPC? (I.e., what is the scope of Art 163 discretion — express only, or also occasion-based?)

Arguments

Appellants' side — Mr Soli J. Sorabjee (sanction valid; discretion exists):
  • Normally the Governor acts on aid and advise — but "merely because the Constitution of India expressly provides, in some cases, for the Governor to act in his discretion, can it be inferred that the Governor can so act only where the Constitution expressly so provides? If that were so then sub-clause (2) of Article 163 would be redundant" — 163(2) postulates discretionary matters not expressly provided for (para 11).
  • Relied on Samsher Singh: normal rule = aid and advise; exceptions are not exhaustive (para 12) — situations of "peril to democracy … not amenable to Ministerial advice", including bias inherent and/or manifest in the advice of the CoM.
  • Ramdas Shrinivas Nayak: where bias "is well apparently inherent in the proposed action of the Ministry", it would be justified for the Governor to act on his own, independently and without reference to any Ministry — the Bombay HC judges' concession (para 13).
Respondent Ministers' side — Mr Tankha:
  • Sanction-to-prosecute is not within Art 163 discretion; the Governor is not an appellate body and cannot sit in appeal over the CoM's decision (para 20); the CoM's decision could only be challenged in a court.
  • The CoM examined all material and applied its minds; presumption of regularity applies to high authorities (V.C. Shukla; State of Punjab v. V.K. Khanna — fairness/malice needs cogent evidence).
  • Bias cannot be presumed against a collective body; the real doctrine requires a "real danger of bias" on collated circumstances (Kumaon Mandal Vikas Nigam) — each member has his own say, majority prevails (paras 21, 23).
  • Doctrine of necessity: where a collective statutory decision is required, the CoM (not the Governor) must decide — supported by J. Mohapatra, ICAI v. L.K. Ratna, Charan Lal Sahu, Badrinath, ECI v. Subramaniam Swamy, Ramdas Shrinivas Nayak, Dr Yashwant Trimbak (para 33).

Decision

  • Normal rule reaffirmed, exceptions opened (para 19): "Undoubtedly, in a matter of grant of sanction to prosecute, the Governor is normally required to act on aid and advice of the Council of Ministers and not in his discretion. However, an exception may arise whilst considering grant of sanction to prosecute a Chief Minister or a Minister where as a matter of propriety the Governor may have to act in his own discretion. Similar would be the situation if the Council of Ministers disables itself or disentitles itself."
  • Bias analysis (paras 22–25): if facts indicate bias "the conclusion becomes inescapable"; taking a cue from Antulay, "a Council of Ministers may not take a fair and impartial decision when their Chief Minister or other members of the Council face prosecution" — but for former Ministers, "the doctrine of 'apparent bias' … may not be applicable in a case where a collective decision is required to be taken … in relation to former Ministers" — no material was shown of actual malice; presumption of bona fide applies first.
  • Two competing orders tested on the record (paras 26–31): the CoM said "no iota of material" for conspiracy, while the Governor's order of 24-9-1998 found complicity disclosed. The Lokayukta's report — by a former SC Judge — was "replete with the materials"; the report's conclusion was quoted in full (fit case to register offence; officers of DSP-rank investigation). "It may not be possible in a given case even to prove conspiracy by direct evidence … Such a relevant consideration apparently was absent in the mind of the Council of Ministers … refusal to take into consideration a relevant fact or acting on the basis of irrelevant and extraneous factors … would vitiate an administrative order." Power is vitiated by "manifest error of record", arbitrariness, or "non-consideration or non-application of mind to relevant factors" (para 30).
  • The famous finding (para 31): "We … have no hesitation to hold that the decision of the Council of Ministers was ex facie irrational whereas the decision of the Governor was not."
  • Why refusal is dangerous (para 32): "If, on these facts and circumstances, the Governor cannot act in his own discretion there would be a complete breakdown of the rule of law … it would then be open for Governments to refuse sanction in spite of overwhelming material … If, in cases where a prima facie case is clearly made out, sanction to prosecute high functionaries is refused or withheld, democracy itself will be at stake. It would then lead to a situation where people in power may break the law with impunity…"
  • Doctrine of necessity — rejected on facts, framework accepted (para 33): "Certainly, the Council of Ministers has to first consider grant of sanction. We also presume that a high authority like the Council of Ministers will normally act in a bona fide manner… However, on those rare occasions where on facts the bias becomes apparent and/or the decision of the Council of Ministers is shown to be irrational and based on non-consideration of relevant factors, the Governor would be right, on the facts of that case, to act in his own discretion and grant sanction."
  • Disposal (para 34): appeals allowed; HC judgments set aside; Ministers' writ petitions dismissed; the Governor's sanction to be given effect to and the CoM's refusal set aside; trial court directed to dispose expeditiously.
Principle evolved

Under Art 163, the Governor's discretion is not confined to express constitutional texts — Art 163(2) itself presupposes occasion-based discretion. In sanction-matters the normal rule is aid and advise, but the Governor may act in discretion in rare, principled situations: (1) propriety demands it when the sanction is about the Chief Minister/Minister themselves (Ramdas Shrinivas Nayak concession approved); (2) the CoM disables or disentitles itself; (3) apparent bias (real-danger test of A.K. Kraipak); (4) the CoM's decision is ex facie irrational / non-arbitrary analysis shows non-consideration of relevant material. The CoM decides first (presumption of bona fide), the Governor acts on the record — and courts review both orders for reasons, arbitrariness and extraneousness (the advice-bar of 163(3) does not immunise a decision whose vice is evident on its face).

Case 19 · DU case material — excerpt pp. 108–115

State (NCT of Delhi) v. Union of India, (2018) 8 SCC 501

Constitution Bench (5 JJ) · Dipak Misra, C.J. (for self, Sikri, Khanwilkar, JJ.) · Dr D.Y. Chandrachud, J. (concurring) · Ashok Bhushan, J. (concurring) · Art 239AA, GNCTD Act 1991, TBR 1993

Facts

  1. The Delhi High Court, in Government of NCT of Delhi v. UOI, W.P.(C) 5888/2015, order dated 8-9-2016 (sub-para (iv) of para 304), held that "it is mandatory under the constitutional scheme to communicate the decision of the Council of Ministers to the Lt. Governor even in relation to the matters in respect of which power to make laws has been conferred on the Legislative Assembly … and an order thereon can be issued only where the Lt. Governor does not take a different view, and no reference to the Central Government is required in terms of the proviso to clause (4) of Article 239AA read with Chapter V of the Transaction of Business of the Government of NCT of Delhi Rules, 1993."
  2. A 2-Judge Bench referred the matter to the Constitution Bench (order dated 15-2-2017) as substantial questions on interpretation of Art 239AA arose.
  3. Background painted by the Court (para 16): 7th Amendment (19-10-1956) abolished Part A/B/C/D States — Delhi became a UT; Union Territories Act 1963 (not applied to Delhi); Delhi Administration Act 1966 (Metropolitan Council); Balakrishnan Committee (1987) — Delhi should remain a UT but with an Assembly and Council of Ministers responsible to it, quoting (para 6.7.21): "The Administrator should be expressly required to perform his functions on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. The expression 'to aid and advice' is a well understood term of art … the general rule is that the exercise of executive functions by the Administrator has to be on the aid and advice of his Council of Ministers which means that it is virtually the Ministers that should take decisions on such matters."

Issues

1. Is the Lieutenant Governor of the NCT bound by the aid and advise of the Council of Ministers of the Government of NCT Delhi — and to what extent? (Dec-2024 Q7 asks this in almost the same words.)
2. Does the proviso to Art 239AA(4) (difference of opinion → reference to the President) require prior concurrence of the LG before the government can act? Is "communication" of decisions to the LG the same as "concurrence"?
3. What is the scope of the LG's discretion (s. 41, GNCTD Act) and the reach of the Assembly's executive power (Art 239AA(4) vs (3)(a))?

Arguments

Appellant NCT Government:
  • Art 239AA adopts the Westminster model; the LG is bound by aid and advise — any other reading "shall run contrary to the very concept of Parliamentary democracy, which is basic feature of the Constitution" (Ashok Bhushan, J. summarising, para 576).
  • Delhi is sui generis with a constitutionally entrenched Assembly + CoM (69th Amendment) — a government "that enjoys the confidence of the people" must be able to usher in policies (para 209); treating every decision as needing LG's concurrence would reduce elected representatives "to a cipher".
Respondent Union of India:
  • The proviso to Art 239AA(4) is an exception to the aid-and-advice mandate: where the LG decides to refer a matter, aid and advice "is not to be adhered to" (Ashok Bhushan, J., para 576 — the provision's own text); the scheme mirrors s. 44 of the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963.
  • Union interests in the national capital require the LG's protective reference power; Delhi remains a Union territory under Part VIII with Parliament's overriding legislative authority (Art 239AA(3)(b), (c)).

Decision — the five holdings (majority + concurring)

  • Headnote (verbatim): "Held — the Lieutenant Governor act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except when he decides to refer the matter to the President for final decision."
  • Sui generis status (Misra C.J., para 284.12, relying on the 9-Judge New Delhi Municipal Corporation case): "by no stretch of imagination, NCT of Delhi can be accorded the status of a State … The status of NCT of Delhi is sui generis, a class apart, and the status of the Lieutenant Governor … is not that of a Governor of a State, rather he remains an Administrator, in a limited sense, working with the designation of Lieutenant Governor."
  • Conterminous executive power (para 204): "the executive power of the Government of NCT of Delhi is conterminous with the legislative power of the Delhi Legislative Assembly … which extends over all but three subjects in the State List [public order, police, land — Entries 1, 2, 18] and all subjects in the Concurrent List."
  • No independent decision-making power (para 284.17): "the Lieutenant Governor of NCT of Delhi is bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers and this position holds true so long as the LG does not exercise his power under the proviso … The Lieutenant Governor has not been entrusted with any independent decision-making power. He has to either act on the 'aid and advice' … or he is bound to implement the decision taken by the President on a reference being made by him."
  • Communication ≠ concurrence (para 284.21): the Rules' phrases ("send a copy thereof", "forwarded to", "submitted to", "cause to be furnished to") lead "to the only possible conclusion that the decisions of the Council of Ministers must be communicated to the Lieutenant Governor but this does not mean that the concurrence of the Lieutenant Governor is required. The said communication is imperative so as to keep him apprised in order to enable him to exercise the power conferred upon him under Article 239AA(4) and the proviso thereof."
  • "Any matter" ≠ "every matter" (Chandrachud, J., para 468): "If the expression 'any matter' were to be construed as 'every matter' or every trifling matter that would result in bringing to a standstill the administration … Every conceivable difference would be referred to the President. The elected representatives would be reduced to a cipher. … The forms of the Constitution would remain but the substance would be lost."
  • No prior concurrence (para 469): "Neither the Constitution nor the enabling legislation … contemplate that every decision of the executive government must receive the prior concurrence of the Lieutenant Governor before it can be implemented."
  • Dialogue first, escalate last (para 475.19): "Before the Lieutenant Governor decides to make a reference … the course of action mandated in the Transaction of Business Rules must be followed. The Lieutenant Governor must, by a process of dialogue and discussion, seek to resolve any difference of opinion with a Minister and if it is not possible … to attempt it through the Council of Ministers. A reference … is contemplated by the Rules only when the above modalities fail…"
  • When the proviso is for (para 475.21): "The proviso … is in the nature of a protector to safeguard the interests of the Union on matters of national interest … Every trivial difference does not fall under the proviso. The proviso will, among other things, encompass substantial issues of finance and policy which impact upon the status of the national capital or implicate vital interests of the Union." (No exhaustive list possible — courts cannot exhaustively indicate circumstances; the LG must follow the judgment's principles.)
  • Parliament's supremacy in the UT (para 470): Delhi continues under Part VIII; the Assembly's legislation "is subject to the overriding power of Parliament"; Art 254 repugnancy is "extended by Article 239AA [3(b) and 3(c)]" over State and Concurrent List subjects.
  • Ashok Bhushan, J. (separate): aid and advise binding "except when he decides to exercise his power given in proviso of clause (4)" (paras 576, 582); and (para 604.8): "The power given in proviso to clause (4) to LG is not to be exercised in a routine manner rather it is to be exercised by the LG on valid reasons after due consideration, when it becomes necessary to safeguard the interest of the Union Territory."
  • Section 41 discretion zone (quoted by Chandrachud, J., para 398): the LG acts in his discretion in (i) matters outside the Assembly's powers but entrusted/delegated by the President (Art 239), and (ii) matters where required by/under law or to exercise judicial/quasi-judicial functions; para 399: such matters "lie outside the realm of the aid and advice mandate".
Fig. 7 — The Delhi decision-flow after NCT Delhi (2018): advise → communicate → dialogue → (only if needed) reference → President's decision binds; no prior concurrence, no routine references.
Principle evolved

In the NCT of Delhi, the Council of Ministers is the real executive; the Lieutenant Governor is bound by its aid and advise on all subjects within the Assembly's legislative competence, except when he exercises the proviso to Art 239AA(4) — a protective power reserved for substantial matters of Union/national-capital interest, to be used after dialogue (TBR), not routinely, and always followed by abiding by the President's decision. Communication of decisions is mandatory; concurrence is not. The LG has no independent decision-making power; his s. 41 discretion is a separate, narrow zone. Parliament's legislative supremacy over Delhi (239AA(3)(b)-(c)) stands untouched.

Case 20 · researched authority (no DU excerpt)

Epuru Sudhakar v. Govt. of Andhra Pradesh, (2006) 8 SCC 321

Pasayat, J. for the Court; Kapadia, J. concurring · W.P.(Crl.) 284–285/2005, decided 11-10-2006 · Art 32 · Arts 72 & 161 · CrPC ss. 432/433

Facts

  1. Gowru Venkata Reddy (respondent No. 2) was convicted of murder (a case arising from the killing of a political worker) and sentenced to ten years' rigorous imprisonment.
  2. His wife (respondent No. 3) was elected an MLA in May 2004. During the pendency of her husband's clemency petition, he was released on parole on 18-10-2004 for one month.
  3. On the State Government's recommendation, the Governor of Andhra Pradesh, in exercise of powers under Art 161, issued G.O.Ms. No. 170 dated 11-8-2005, remitting the unexpired balance of the sentence (about seven years), and the convict was released on 12-8-2005 from the Central Prison, Cherlapally.
  4. The petitioners — sons of the victims — challenged the remission under Art 32 as resting on extraneous and irrelevant considerations (the file reportedly recorded that the convict was "a good Congress worker" and referred to his being "defeated by political conspiracy" in elections) and on material not disclosed to the Governor.
  5. The Andhra Pradesh High Court dismissed their challenge; they came to the Supreme Court under Art 32. (Soli Sorabjee was appointed amicus curiae.)

Issues

1. Is the Governor's power of remission under Art 161 (and the President's under Art 72) subject to judicial review — and on what grounds?
2. Whether the remission in G.O.Ms. No. 170/11-8-2005 was vitiated by extraneous political considerations, non-application of mind, or suppression of relevant material?
3. What is the relationship between constitutional clemency (Arts 72/161) and statutory remission (CrPC ss. 432/433)?

Arguments

Petitioners (victims' sons):
  • The remission order rested on statements wholly irrelevant to clemency — the convict's political party work and election grievances — which do not appear in any permissible consideration of "remission of punishment".
  • Pending criminal matters/material were not placed before the Governor; the order shows non-application of mind to the crime's gravity and its effect on the victims.
  • Clemency is not immune from review where the record shows mala fide or extraneous factors — otherwise Art 161 becomes a license.
Respondent State/convict:
  • Pardoning/remeasure of sentences is a prerogative executive act under Art 161 — courts cannot sit in appeal over its merits; the Governor is the constitutional authority and the advice received cannot be scrutinised (Art 163(3)/74(2) logic).
  • The Governor considered the Government's recommendation and the material on file — the exercise of discretion is final; a convict's political affiliations were only part of the narrative, not the sole ground.

Decision

  1. G.O.Ms. No. 170 dated 11-8-2005 was QUASHED.
  2. Executive clemency under Arts 72/161 is subject to limited judicial review. The Court may examine whether the order suffers from: (i) mala fide; (ii) extraneous/wholly irrelevant considerations; (iii) non-application of mind; (iv) suppression of relevant material. Political considerations ("good Congress worker", electoral grievance) are not permissible grounds for remitting a murder sentence.
  3. Remitted back for fresh consideration on all relevant, correctly ascertained material — i.e., the correct remedy is not rewriting mercy but ensuring a lawful process (the State may treat the clemency petition as pending and place the complete record before the Governor).
  4. Pardon vs remission distinction affirmed: Art 72 (President) reaches court-martial sentences, Union-law offences and all death sentences; Art 161 (Governor) covers offences against laws within the State's executive power; neither authorises acting on irrelevant grounds. Statutory remission under CrPC ss. 432/433 (appropriate Government; now carried in the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023) runs alongside constitutional powers — both are reviewable where the record shows illegality. (Pardon = extinguishes conviction; remission = reduces the sentence — but both are acts of the executive subject to constitutional limits.)
  5. Earlier precedent noted: in Swaran Singh-type cases the Court had earlier quashed remission for absence of opportunity/material — the law has consistently denied absolute immunity to mercy.
Principle evolved

The pardoning/remission powers of the President (Art 72) and the Governor (Art 161) are not beyond judicial review: they are exercised within the Constitution and are struck down if the record shows mala fide, extraneous or wholly irrelevant considerations, non-application of mind, or suppression of relevant material; the remedy ordinarily is remission of the matter back for lawful reconsideration. Mercy is a constitutional power, not an executive whim — "pleasure/licence" logic of B.P. Singhal applied to Art 161.

Bonus · DU case-book case 22 (Topic 4 list, p. 106) — needed for Centenary Q5(a)

B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu, (2001) 7 SCC 231

Constitution Bench · S.P. Bharucha, J. (for himself, Y.K. Sabharwal & Ruma Pal, JJ.) · quo warranto · Arts 164(1) & (4), 173, 191, 361 · RPA 1951 s. 8(3)

Facts

Jayalalithaa had been convicted and sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years in corruption cases (TANSI and others); her conviction was not stayed — only the sentence was suspended (she was on bail). Her nomination for election was rejected under s. 8(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (disqualification for conviction + sentence ≥ 2 years). Nevertheless, on her party's (AIADMK's) overwhelming electoral victory, she was appointed Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu on 14-5-2001. The appointment was challenged by way of quo warranto. The High Court/quashing proceedings led to the Constitution Bench.

Issues

1. Can a person who has been convicted and sentenced to imprisonment for two years or more (conviction not stayed; only sentence suspended) and is consequently disqualified under s. 8(3) RPA 1951 be appointed Chief Minister?
2. Is the Governor bound to decline such an appointment (Arts 164(1), 173, 191) — or is it his unfettered discretion? Is Art 361 immunity a bar to testing the appointee's authority?

Arguments

Petitioners (quo warranto):
  • Art 164(1) permits appointment of a CM who is a member of the Legislative Assembly; Art 164(4) gives a non-member at most six months — but nothing can cure a person disqualified under law; Arts 84/173 (qualification) and 102/191 (disqualification) + s. 8(3) RPA bar the appointee entirely.
  • Suspension of sentence ≠ suspension of conviction: disqualification under s. 8(3) operates from conviction; the Governor cannot legitimise an unlawful appointment; the court's writ of quo warranto lies against the appointee.
Respondents / State:
  • Appointment of the CM is the Governor's discretion under Art 164(1); the elected majority's choice should not be disturbed; requiring the Governor to adjudicate disqualification imposes an "impossible burden".
  • Art 361 bars proceedings against the Governor; the will of the people (a massive mandate) ought to prevail over technicalities.

Decision

  • A convicted person disqualified under s. 8(3) RPA cannot be appointed Chief Minister. "Qualified to be a member" and "not disqualified" are twin conditions of Arts 173/191/164 — a person whose nomination was rejected for disqualification plainly fails them.
  • Where disqualification is apparent on the face of the record, the Governor MUST decline the appointment — declining is a constitutional mandate, not an impossible burden. Where the disqualification depends on disputed facts, the Governor may appoint (that is his Art 164 discretion) — but the appointee's authority remains testable by the court.
  • Quo warranto lies against the appointee — Art 361 protects the Governor personally, not the unlawfully occupied office; immunity is not vicariously available to the appointee.
  • Other acts done by the de facto CM remain valid (to avoid administrative chaos) — but the office itself must be vacated.
  • "The Constitution prevails over popular mandate" — a brute majority cannot licence a disqualified person's appointment.
Principle evolved

A person disqualified for membership (e.g., conviction + ≥2 years' sentence under s. 8(3) RPA 1951, conviction not stayed) cannot hold office as Chief Minister; the Governor must refuse such an appointment where disqualification appears on the record; the appointee's authority is justiciable through quo warranto notwithstanding Art 361, though his past acts stand. Read with S.P. Anand: non-member ✔ (6 months) · disqualified ✘ (never).

Section 8 · Currency & illustrationReal-World Examples

🗳️ Governor churn at every change of government → B.P. Singhal

In 2004, within months of the change of government at the Centre, several Governors were transferred or replaced — the file-notations echoing "lost confidence" / "out of sync with the Union government's policy". The PIL against those orders (arising from the 2004 churn) was decided in 2010: those political grounds struck down, pleasure held bounded, limited review allowed. A similar churn followed in 2014 (Governors of West Bengal, Gujarat, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh among those changed) — which is why the case is the standard answer whenever an exam asks "is the Governor's removal justiciable?"

🧳 Presidents who used the pocket veto

President Zail Singh (1985–89) neither assented to nor returned the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill, 1986 — it simply lapsed with the 8th Lok Sabha in 1989: the textbook pocket veto possible because Art 111 sets no time limit. Contrast the mandatory assent after a returned-and-repassed Bill — the asymmetry examiners love (§1.5.2, §5 matrix).

⚖️ Governors & sanction decisions

The M.P. SPE pattern — a CoM refusing sanction for prosecution of its own ex-ministers and a Governor granting it — recurs in Indian politics (sanction/withholding controversies under CrPC s. 197 whenever incumbent or former ministers are accused). The 2004 judgment tells you exactly how a court will approach it: CoM first (presumed bona fide), Governor only in rare situations, both orders scrutinised on the record. (Post-2018, s. 17A of the PC Act adds a parallel sanction-to-investigate layer for serving public servants.)

🏛️ The Delhi LG tussle (2015–2018)

After the 2015 Delhi election, the elected CM government and the LG (Najeeb Jung, then Anil Baijal) clashed over files, the Anti-Corruption Branch and "who is the head of the administration". The Delhi HC (2016) backed the LG; the Constitution Bench of 2018 reversed that reading (aid & advice + narrow proviso). Follow-up: the Constitution Bench decision of 11 May 2023 (Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India) held the power over "services" (Entry 41, State List) vests in the NCT Government — exercisable by the LG on the CoM's aid and advise — except matters of public order, police and land. The trilogy (HC 2016 → SC 2018 → SC 2023) is the ideal "current developments" paragraph for Dec-2024 Q7.

🕊️ Mercy: delay, politics and the courts

Epuru (extraneous political grounds → quashed) and Perarivalan (1998 mercy petition; 2022 SC commutation using Art 142 on delay) bracket modern clemency law; earlier, Governors' remissions of death sentences (e.g., under CrPC s. 433) have themselves been challenged — remission must satisfy Maru Ram-type fairness. Article 161 remains the most exercised — and most litigated — Governor's power after assent.

👮 CBI consent & the federal line

The recurring "CBI needs our consent" disputes between States and the Centre (blanket vs case-specific consent under DSPE Act s. 6) are an Art 73-proviso/Entry 2 story: police being a State subject, Union executive action inside a State needs an express constitutional or statutory footing — exactly the allocation Arts 73/162 codify.

CENTENARY CHANCE EXAMINATION · Q5(a) · ≈10 marks (attempt any two of a–d)
"Can a person convicted for an offence and disqualified to be a member of the Legislature be appointed as a Chief Minister?" — Answer with the relevant provisions of the Constitution and case laws.
Model answer

No. A person convicted of an offence who stands disqualified for membership of the Legislature cannot be validly appointed Chief Minister of a State.

Provisions. Art 164(1) empowers the Governor to appoint the Chief Minister; read with Arts 173 (qualification for assembly membership), 191 (disqualifications) and 84-side requirements, the appointee must at least be qualified to be a member and free from disqualification. Under s. 8(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, a person convicted of an offence and sentenced to imprisonment of not less than two years stands disqualified from the date of conviction until the expiry of six years after release. Art 164(4) — which gives a non-member up to six consecutive months to become a member — presupposes an otherwise qualified, undisqualified person; it cannot cure a statutory disqualification. Suspension of the sentence (bail) is not suspension of the conviction.

Case law. B.R. Kapur v. State of T.N. (2001) 7 SCC 231 (Constitution Bench): Jayalalithaa, convicted and sentenced to ≥2 years (conviction not stayed; only sentence suspended), whose nomination was rejected under s. 8(3) RPA, was appointed CM on 14-5-2001 — appointment quashed by quo warranto. The Court held: (i) a disqualified person cannot hold the office; (ii) where the disqualification appears on the face of the record the Governor must decline the appointment — that is a constitutional mandate, not an impossible burden; (iii) Art 361 does not protect the appointee; the writ lies against the person unlawfully holding office (though acts already done remain valid); (iv) "the Constitution prevails over popular mandate". Contrast S.P. Anand (1996): a mere non-member (not disqualified) can be CM for six months under Art 164(4) — non-member ✔, disqualified ✘.

Conclusion. The appointment would be void and liable to be quashed; the Governor's discretion under Art 164 does not extend to appointing a person the law has disabled.

CENTENARY CHANCE EXAMINATION · Q5(b) · ≈10 marks
"Can a person who is not a member of the Parliament be appointed as the Prime Minister of India?"
Model answer

Yes — for a maximum of six consecutive months, provided the person is otherwise qualified and not disqualified, and commands the confidence of the House of the People.

Provisions. Art 75(1): the President appoints the Prime Minister — the article imposes no membership qualification. Art 75(5): "A Minister who for any period of six consecutive months is not a member of either House of Parliament shall … cease to be a Minister" — a clause that only makes sense if non-members may be appointed; it applies to the PM also (caption of Art 75: "Other provisions as to Ministers"). Art 88 gives every Minister (PM included) the right to speak and take part in Parliament even while a non-member. Arts 84/173 qualifications (35 years, citizen, oath) still apply, and Arts 102/191 + RPA s. 8 disqualifications must be absent (B.R. Kapur).

Case law. S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda (1996) 6 SCC 734: Deve Gowda, a non-member, was appointed PM; petition dismissed. The Court relied on the chain of precedents — Har Sharan Verma (1971) (T.N. Singh as non-member CM; Art 164(4) has "ancient lineage"), (1985) 2 SCC 48 (16th Amendment oath rule irrelevant), Harsharan Verma 1987 Supp (Kesri as non-member Union Minister upheld on 75(5) + 88) — and on the Delhi and Calcutta High Courts' decisions in the very Deve Gowda matter. Constituent Assembly history: the amendment to bar non-member Ministers ("cut at the very root of democracy") was spurned by Dr Ambedkar (draft Arts 62 & 144). The English convention (Halsbury's: PM "invariably a member") is not our constitutional scheme. Normally, where there is doubt, "the President … asks the person appointed to seek a vote of confidence … within a few days".

Conclusion. Appointment is constitutional; failure to become a member within six months terminates the office under Art 75(5).

CENTENARY CHANCE EXAMINATION · Q5(c) · ≈10 marks
"Is it essential to have a Council of Ministers under Art. 74(1) even at a time when the House of the People has been dissolved or when its term has expired?"
Model answer

Yes — absolutely essential. This is the precise question decided in U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi, AIR 1971 SC 1002 (Sikri, C.J. + 4 JJ.), where quo warranto was sought against Indira Gandhi's continuation as PM after the LS was dissolved on 27-12-1970.

Reasoning (use the DU excerpts' paragraphs). (1) Art 74(1) is mandatory — "shall" must be read as "shall", not "may": "The Constituent Assembly did not choose the Presidential system of Government." Without a CoM nobody would be responsible to the House, and the President "with the aid of advisers … would be able to rule the country at least till he is impeached under Article 61" — a result the Constitution does not contemplate (paras 7–8). (2) Art 53(1) is controlled by "in accordance with this Constitution" — the President cannot act directly through officers bypassing the CoM (para 6), and the Art 60 oath binds him to the Constitution. (3) Art 75(2) continues: Ministers hold office during the President's pleasure — "The President has not said that it is his pleasure that the respondent shall not hold office" (para 8). (4) Art 75(3) is harmonised: collective responsibility to the LS operates when the House is not dissolved or prorogued; on dissolution the CoM continues in office to carry on administration (para 9); the expiry-of-term case is dealt with by s. 14, RPA 1951. (5) Arts 77(3) and 78 have full play — business allocation and the PM's information-duties continue during the interregnum (para 10). The Court quoted Ram Jawaya Kapur (paras 13–14) and A. Sanjeevi Naidu ("the essence of joint responsibility").

Conclusion. A Council of Ministers is a permanent constitutional requirement of the parliamentary system; dissolution suspends the responsibility mechanism (75(3)), not the existence of the Council — appeal dismissed, government legally continues until a new House is constituted.

CENTENARY CHANCE EXAMINATION · Q5(d) · ≈10 marks
"Can a Governor of a State overrule the advice of the State Council of Ministers refusing sanction to prosecute an ex-Minister for corruption?"
Model answer

Yes — but only in rare, principled situations; normally he must follow the advice. This is exactly the question decided by the Constitution Bench (5 Judges) in M.P. Special Police Establishment v. State of M.P. (2004) 8 SCC 788.

Provisions. Art 163(1): the Governor is aided and advised by the CoM except where required to act in discretion; Art 163(3): advice-content is not inquirable in court. Sanction to prosecute for offences connected with official duty = s. 197 CrPC (here, for IPC s. 120B — no sanction was needed for the PC Act offences themselves). The normal rule: sanction is an executive function exercised on aid and advise (Samsher Singh, paras 57, 88: no act "against the aid and advice").

The exception (M.P. SPE). The Governor may act in his own discretion where — (i) propriety demands it because the sanction is about the Chief Minister/Minister themselves (Ramdas Shrinivas Nayak concession approved — bias inherent in ministerial advice); (ii) the CoM disables/disentitles itself (para 19); (iii) apparent bias on the real-danger test (A.K. Kraipak); (iv) the CoM's refusal is ex facie irrational — here the Council said there was "not an iota of material" for conspiracy while the Lokayukta's (a former SC Judge's) detailed report showed a prima facie case: "the decision of the Council of Ministers was ex facie irrational whereas the decision of the Governor was not" (para 31). The Court added the rule-of-law rationale (para 32): otherwise "people in power may break the law with impunity" and "democracy itself will be at stake". Doctrine of necessity rejected on facts: the CoM decides first (bona fide presumed), but "on those rare occasions where … bias becomes apparent and/or the decision … is shown to be irrational and based on non-consideration of relevant factors, the Governor would be right … to act in his own discretion" (para 33).

Procedure & conclusion. The Governor does not sit in appeal: he considers the record, acts within discretion only in the above situations, and his order (like the CoM's) is judicially reviewable for reasons/arbitrariness (Arts 226/136/142). Disposal: the Governor's sanction was given effect to; the CoM's refusal set aside. So: overrule-able — yes, exceptionally, on this four-point test; routinely — no.

DECEMBER 2024 EXAMINATION (LB-301) · Q5(b) · 10 marks
"When can a non-legislator be made a Chief Minister or Minister under Article 164 of the Constitution of India? Support your answer with relevant legal provisions and judgments."
Model answer

Whenever the Governor so appoints — for a maximum of six consecutive months — if the person is qualified (Art 173: citizen, 35 years, assembly-qualification side) and not disqualified (Arts 191, Tenth Schedule, RPA s. 8), and (politically) he commands the House's confidence.

Provisions. Art 164(1): CM appointed by the Governor, other Ministers on CM's advice — no membership requirement. Art 164(4): "A Minister who for any period of six consecutive months is not a member of the legislature of the State shall at the expiration of that period cease to be a Minister" — the provision presupposes appointment of non-members; CM is a "Minister" for this purpose. Union parallels: Arts 75(1), (5) + Art 88 (participation rights while a non-member). The 15% cap (164(1A)) and oath (164(3)) apply alike.

Judgment. S.P. Anand v. H.D. Deve Gowda (1996) 6 SCC 734 — "a person who is not a member of either House of Parliament or of either House of a State Legislature can be appointed a Minister in the Central Cabinet (which would include a Prime Minister) or a Minister in the State Cabinet (which would include a Chief Minister)". Line of authority: Har Sharan Verma (1971) (T.N. Singh non-member CM of U.P. — 164(4) "ancient lineage"); (1985) 2 SCC 48 (16th Amdt oath not a condition of appointment); Harsharan Verma 1987 Supp (Kesri — 75(5) with 88); High Courts in Deve Gowda's case (Delhi WP 2408/1996; Calcutta CO 1336(w)/1996). Constituent Assembly: the amendment barring non-member Ministers was rejected (draft Arts 62/144) — the English convention is not our scheme. Practical safeguard: President/Governor normally asks the appointee to prove confidence within days; failure to be elected within six months ends the appointment.

Two caveats with case support. (1) B.R. Kapur (2001): a person disqualified (conviction + ≥2 years, s. 8(3) RPA) can never be appointed — the six-month window is for the qualified non-member only. (2) Defection disqualification (75(1B)/164(1B)) blocks appointment for the disqualification period.

Conclusion. Non-legislator CM/Minister: permissible on appointment, self-terminating at six months, subject to qualification, non-disqualification and confidence.

DECEMBER 2024 EXAMINATION (LB-301) · Q7 · 20 marks
"To what extent is the Lieutenant Governor of the NCT of Delhi bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers of the government of NCT Delhi? Answer with the help of relevant constitutional law principles, legal provisions and judgments."
Model answer — 20-mark architecture (intro → text → principles → exceptions → conclusion)

I. Textual foundation (Arts 239AA(3)–(6), GNCTD Act 1991, TBR 1993). The 69th Amendment (1991) created the NCT with an elected Assembly and a CoM. Art 239AA(4) requires a CoM (≤10% of MLAs, CM at head) "to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor in the exercise of his functions in relation to matters with respect to which the Legislative Assembly has power to make laws, except in so far as he is, by or under any law, required to act in his discretion" — with a proviso: on difference of opinion, the LG shall refer the matter to the President and act on his decision; pending it, urgent action may be taken if the matter is so urgent. Art 239AA(5)-(6): CM appointed by the President (on whom? — on the strength of majority; other Ministers on CM's advice) and CoM collectively responsible to the Assembly. The enabling statute is the GNCTD Act, 1991 — s. 41 (LG's discretion), s. 44 (aid and advise) — plus the Transaction of Business Rules, 1993 (Ch. V on references).

II. The extent — four propositions from State (NCT of Delhi) v. UOI (2018) 8 SCC 501 (Constitution Bench):

  1. Bound on everything within the Assembly's legislative field. Executive power of the NCT Government is conterminous with the Assembly's legislative power (para 204): all State-List subjects except Entries 1, 2, 18 (public order, police, land) + all Concurrent subjects. On these, the LG "is bound by the aid and advice … so long as he does not exercise his power under the proviso" — he has "not been entrusted with any independent decision-making power" (284.17); he either acts on advice or implements the President's decision on a reference (284.17, 475.20).
  2. Communication ≠ concurrence. The Rules' vocabulary ("send a copy", "forward", "submit") means decisions must be communicated to keep him informed "but this does not mean that the concurrence of the Lieutenant Governor is required" (284.21). Neither the Constitution nor the statute contemplates that "every decision … must receive the prior concurrence of the Lieutenant Governor" (469).
  3. The proviso is narrow. "Any matter" cannot mean "every matter" — otherwise "the forms of the Constitution would remain but the substance would be lost" and "the elected representatives would be reduced to a cipher" (468). The proviso is "a protector to safeguard the interests of the Union": it "will … encompass substantial issues of finance and policy which impact upon the status of the national capital or implicate vital interests of the Union" — "[e]very trivial difference does not fall under the proviso" (475.21).
  4. Process discipline. Before a reference, the TBR dialogue-first process must be exhausted — "dialogue and discussion" with the Minister, then the Council (475.19); on reference the LG must abide by the President's decision (475.20); urgent-action power only where "so urgent" (proviso, second limb). Ashok Bhushan, J.: the proviso power "is not to be exercised in a routine manner rather … on valid reasons after due consideration" (604.8); the scheme equals s. 44, GOUT Act 1963 (576, 582).

III. The LG's own zones (the "extent" boundaries). (a) s. 41 GNCTD Act discretion: matters outside the Assembly's powers entrusted by the President (Art 239) + judicial/quasi-judicial functions — these "lie outside the realm of the aid and advice mandate" (399); (b) the 239AA(3)(b)-(c) supremacy zone: Parliament may legislate on any matter for Delhi; repugnancy applies to State and Concurrent subjects (470) — executive power follows this allocation; (c) the three excluded entries (public order, police, land) — LG/Union domain.

IV. Status reasoning (score-marker paragraphs). Delhi is sui generis, "a class apart" — not a State; the LG "remains an Administrator, in a limited sense" (284.12, per the 9-Judge NDMC case); the Balakrishnan Committee (1987) 6.7.21 already stated the "aid and advise" mandate; the Court traced Shamsher's draft-article history (draft 143 → 163) to show discretion was meant to be rare. Samsheer's rule applies mutatis mutandis: no parallel administration; 163(2)-style finality has no room against an elected government except in the proviso zone.

V. Conclusion (one paragraph). The LG is bound by aid and advise for all subjects of the NCT Government's competence — i.e., almost everything — except (i) matters within s. 41/Art 239 discretion, (ii) urgent interim action pending a bona fide proviso reference, and (iii) matters properly referred under the proviso (substantial finance/policy touching the national-capital status or vital Union interests, after TBR dialogue) where the President's decision then binds. No prior concurrence; communication is mandatory; references are exceptional. (Currency sentence: the 2023 Constitution Bench on "services" — power under Entry 41 vests with the NCT Government except public order, police, land — confirms the same allocation.)

Mark-distribution hint (20 marks): provisions 4–5 marks; the 2018 five propositions with paragraph numbers 8–9 marks; s. 41/proviso boundaries 3–4 marks; conclusion + currency 2–3 marks.

DECEMBER 2024 EXAMINATION (LB-301) · Q8(a) · 10 marks
"As per the Constitution of India, when can a Governor exercise discretionary power? Discuss with the help of relevant legal provisions and judgments."
Model answer

The Governor's discretion is an exception to the aid-and-advice rule, arising only where the Constitution (expressly or by necessary implication) requires him to act in his own judgment — and even then in harmony with the Council of Ministers.

Provision. Art 163(1): CoM aids and advises the Governor "except in so far as he is by or under this Constitution required to exercise his functions or any of them in his discretion"; Art 163(2): whether a matter is discretionary is the Governor's final call — but validity cannot be attacked on the ground that he ought/ought not to have acted in discretion. (Art 74 has no counterpart — S.P. Anand para 12; Samsher para 16.)

Express islands (Samsher, para 54): Art 239(2) (Governor as administrator of an adjoining UT — "independently of his Council of Ministers"); Sixth Schedule paras 9(2) and 18(3) (18(3) since omitted); Art 371A(1)(b), (1)(d), (2)(b), (2)(f) (Nagaland special provisions); Art 356 — the Governor's report on failure of constitutional machinery may be made even against ministerial advice "because the failure … may be because of the conduct of the Council of Ministers" (para 55); Art 200 second proviso — a Bill derogating from the High Court's position must be reserved for the President (para 56); Art 160 (contingencies), Art 192/103-type referrals.

Recognised exceptional situations (Samsher, para 154): (a) choice of Chief Minister (paramount consideration: majority in the House); (b) dismissal of a government that has lost its majority but refuses to quit; (c) dissolution of the House where an appeal to the country is necessitous — "action must be compelled by the peril to democracy".

Judicial occasions (M.P. SPE, 2004 — Art 163 discretion is not exhausted by express texts): Sorabjee's argument — "If that were so then sub-clause (2) of Article 163 would be redundant" — was accepted: the Governor may act in discretion in rare cases of sanction where (i) propriety so demands (CM/Ministers accused), (ii) the CoM disables itself, (iii) apparent bias (Kraipak real-danger test), (iv) the CoM's order is ex facie irrational (para 19, 31, 33); para 32: otherwise impunity for high functionaries. Also NCT Delhi 2018: for Delhi's LG the zones are s. 41 GNCTD Act + the proviso to Art 239AA(4) (604.8: valid reasons, due consideration, not routine).

Limits. Discretion must be exercised in harmony with the CoM — "no parallel administration" (Samsher 55, 88); 163(2) bars merits-review of a genuine exercise but not jurisdictional review (was it discretionary at all?) nor malafide/arbitrary exercise (B.P. Singhal design); advice given on other matters remains under the 163(3) bar.

Conclusion. When — and only when — the Constitution/law requires it (express pockets), or in the narrow class of situations where ministerial advice is structurally compromised (Samsher-154 trio; M.P. SPE's four sanction situations), acting always within constitutional propriety.

  • One current reference (2018 Delhi, 2023 services/assent, Perarivalan) for currency marks.

Common traps

  • ✗ "Art 74(2) makes executive action non-justiciable" — no: only advice content is shielded (Epuru/Singhal review the act).
  • ✗ "Governor can be impeached" — no: only pleasure (Art 156(1)) — bounded by Singhal.
  • ✗ "Non-member can be CM forever" — no: six months (164(4)) + must be qualified/undisqualified (Kapur).
  • ✗ "Governor's discretion = whatever he thinks" — no: Constitution-required situations + harmony rule (Samsher 55/88) + MPSPE rarity.
  • ✗ "LG of Delhi = a Governor" — no: Administrator in a limited sense, sui generis (284.12).
  • ✗ "President's pardon is unreviewable" — no: Epuru grounds.
  • ✗ "Art 75(3) forces resignation on dissolution" — no: UNR Rao harmonisation.
  • ✗ "Art 77(3) delegates power to Ministers" — no: it is the President's own decision channel (Samsher 30).
