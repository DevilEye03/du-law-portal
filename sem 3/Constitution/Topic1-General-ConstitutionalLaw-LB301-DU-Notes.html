Section 1Constitution – Fundamental Law of the Land

“Government without a Constitution is a power without a right.” — Thomas Paine (quoted in the DU reading on Constitution & Constitutionalism)

1.1 Meaning, Definition and Etymology

Etymology: The word Constitution comes from the Latin constitutio (in classical usage, imperial laws/decrees) and, more broadly, from con-stare — “to stand together / to set up”. In political usage it denotes the arrangement of organs of government and the principles according which the state is governed. (Class notes: “constiture → binding together / joining / building”).

  • Aristotle (350 BC), Politics: constitution is “the way of life the state has chosen for itself”; he pioneered the distinction between ordinary law and constitutional (fundamental) law — the seed of “constitutionalism”.
  • C.F. Strong: “the collection of principles according to which the powers of the government, the rights of the governed, and their relationships are balanced.”
  • A.V. Dicey (An Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution, 1885): the father of modern constitutionalism; located sovereignty in Parliament in UK, but described conventions that limit its exercise.
  • Marshall, C.J. (U.S.): “It is a Constitution we are expounding” — a Constitution is an instrument of government to be read in light of the life of the people (quoted by H.R. Khanna, J. in Kesavananda).
  • Thomas Paine: “Government without a Constitution is a power without a right.”

Definition for exam

A Constitution is the fundamental law of the land which (i) establishes the principal organs of the State and distributes their powers; (ii) defines and limits governmental power; (iii) guarantees rights of citizens; and (iv) is the source of all powers and limitations on the legislature, executive and judiciary. “No action of the State would be valid unless it is permissible under the Constitution.” (DU LB-301 – Objectives)

Functions of a Constitution

  • Enables government instruments to be constituted and their powers limited;
  • Expresses fundamental values (liberty, equality, dignity);
  • Specifies territory, membership (citizenship) and identity;
  • Provides legitimacy and a source of law’s authority;
  • Acts as a focal point of political identification and a framework for resolving conflict.

Kinds of Constitutions

  • Written (USA, India) vs Unwritten (UK — cumulative documents + conventions);
  • Rigid vs Flexible (terms coined by Lord Bryce, popularised by Dicey — see Kesavananda, Palekar J.);
  • Federal vs Unitary vs Quasi-federal;
  • Executive vs Parliamentary constitution;
  • Sovereign vs Colonial constitution (Govt. of India Act, 1935 was colonial).

1.3 Rule of Law and the Essential Elements of Constitutionalism

As per the class notes (constitution.pdf), three essential elements underlie constitutionalism — every one of them is textually protected in the Constitution:

① Absence of Arbitrariness

Executive/legislative action must be reasoned, non-arbitrary and reviewable.
Art. 14 (equality before law + arbitrary action struck down — E.P. Royappa; Navtej Singh Johar); Art. 21 (due process).

② Equality

Equality before law (Art. 14 limb 1) + equal protection of laws (limb 2); non-discrimination (Arts. 15–16), abolishing untouchability (Art. 17), equality in public employment (Art. 16).

③ Individual Liberty

Art. 19 freedoms (speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, profession), Art. 20 (protection against conviction), Art. 21 (life & personal liberty), Art. 22 (against arrest & detention).

Rule of Law → Constitutionalism (Dicey’s triad)

  1. Discretion of arbitrary power is unknown — reflected in Audi alteram partem (Art. 14 / Art. 300A procedural fairness; Art. 21 post-Maneka Gandhi);
  2. Equality before the law — no one is above law; Reminisce: “no special treatment” (class note) — Art. 14, 151, 16, 17, 18;
  3. Law arises from customs/courts, not the Ruler — courts determine rights (judicial review: Arts. 32, 226, 136, 137, 141, 143, 227).

Separation of Powers & Checks — the class-note map

  • Montesquieu (L'Esprit des Lois): legislative, executive, judicial organs; Art. 50 (DPSP — separation of judiciary from executive);
  • Checks & balances: Arts. 124, 217, 222 (judges’ appointment/removal) vs Arts. 61, 157 (President’s impeachment / Governor’s qualifications); Art. 74(2) & 163 limits; privileges under Arts. 361, 105, 194; judicial writs under Arts. 32, 226, 227;
  • Shashi Kaul / Jaylalita facts: constitutional functionaries cannot be tried like ordinary persons (immunities) — Art. 361.

1.4 Written / Unwritten · Rigid / Flexible Constitutions (directly from DU case material)

The DU extract of Kesavananda (Palekar J., paras 1219–1221) reproduces the classic taxonomy examiners love:

  • Flexible constitution — every law (including constitutional law) can be changed “with the same ease and in the same manner by one and the same body” (UK).
  • Rigid constitution — constitutional/fundamental laws cannot be changed in the same manner as ordinary laws (USA).
  • Lord Birkenhead in McCawley v. King [1920 AC 691] used “uncontrolled” and “controlled” for flexible/rigid.
  • Karl Strong (Modern Political Constitutions, 1966, p.153): “The sole criterion of a rigid constitution is whether the Constituent Assembly left any special directions as to how it was to be changed.”
  • India’s position: rigid yet flexible — a written Constitution amendable by ordinary legislative majorities for most provisions (simple majority), special majority for most, and special majority + state ratification for federal provisions (Art. 368 proviso) — see §5.
Real example: The Constitution (103rd Amendment) Act, 2019 (EWS reservation) was passed by simple majority as an ordinary-law type amendment to Arts. 15, 16 — but was still judicially reviewable for basic-structure violation (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022). A “rigid” Constitution is still a living Constitution.

Section 2Making of the Indian Constitution

From colonial charters to a sovereign democratic republic — the constitutional history that the DU readings place before every LB-301 student.

2.1 Colonial Antecedents — the Road to the Constitution

1600 — East India Company chartered; early mercantile governance.
1773 — Regulating Act: first step to centralised administration; created a Supreme Court at Calcutta and made the Governor of Bengal subordinate to the Court of Directors in some matters.
1784 — Pitt’s India Act: created a Board of Control → increased Parliamentary/Crown control over the Company (double government).
1813 & 1833 — Charter Acts: ended Company’s trade monopoly (except tea/China); 1833 Act made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India (centralisation) and introduced a law member in the legislative council.
1853 — Charter Act: separated legislative from executive functions of the Governor-General’s council; introduced representation through Indian members (birth of a proto-legislature).
1858 — Government of India Act: transfer of power from Company to the Crown; Secretary of State for India in London; India ruled by a unitary, highly centralised administration.
1861 & 1892 — Indian Councils Acts: port-folio system; expansion of councils; “representative” elements in legislation.
1909 — Morley-Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act): separate electorates for Muslims — a significant constitutional development.
1919 — Government of India Act (Montagu-Chelmsford): dyarchy in provinces (transferred/reserved subjects); subjects divided into Central/Provincial lists; laid down budgetary control; increased Indian participation.
1928 — Simon Commission; 1930–32 — Round Table Conferences; Communal Award & Poona Pact (1932) — constitutional tinkering towards federation.
1935 — Government of India Act: provincial autonomy; proposed All-India Federation (Part C — never came into force as Indian States did not accede); Federal Court; distribution of legislative powers through three lists — the single biggest source of our Constitution (Arts. 246/248/Schedule VII, Arts. 73/162 echo ss. 8, 49, 127, 172 of this Act).
1942 — Cripps Mission · 1945–46 — Cabinet Mission: proposed an interim government and a Constituent Assembly.
1946 — Constituent Assembly constituted (members indirectly elected by provincial legislatures); met first on 9 Dec 1946; Drafting Committee chaired by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (29 Aug 1947).
22 Jan 1947 — Objectives Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru, adopted by the Assembly (see 2.2).
18 Jul 1947 — Indian Independence Act created two dominions; suzerainty over Princely States lapsed; §6 empowered the Dominion legislatures to make laws (see State of W.B. v. UOI, paras 24–25).
26 Nov 1949 — Constitution adopted (enacted); 26 Jan 1950 — Constitution came into force (celebrated as Republic Day). The Preamble was enacted on 26 Nov 1949 (kept as part of the original text).

2.2 The Constituent Assembly and the Objectives Resolution

Constituent Assembly — key facts

  • Constituted under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946); 299 members at independence (originally 389); indirectly elected; adopted the Constitution in 2 years, 11 months, 18 days;
  • Served as the Provisional Parliament under Art. 379 until the first general elections (1952) — a fact noted in Kuldip Nayar;
  • Key functionaries: Dr. Rajendra Prasad (President), Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (Drafting Committee), Jawaharlal Nehru (Objectives Resolution), Sardar Patel (integration of States), B.N. Rau (Constitutional Adviser).

Why the Assembly was “unfettered”

“The Constituent Assembly was unfettered by any previous commitment in evolving a constitutional pattern ‘suitable to the genius and requirements of the Indian people as a whole’. The Assembly had before it the experience of the working of the Government of India Act, 1935, several features of which could be accepted for the new Constitution.” — Shelat & Grover, JJ. in Kesavananda (DU case material, p.3)

The Objectives Resolution (adopted 22 January 1947) — reproduced in DU case material (Kesavananda, Sikri C.J., paras 90–91)

“(1) THIS CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY declares its firm and solemn resolve to proclaim India as an Independent Sovereign Republic and to draw up for her future governance a Constitution; (2) wherein the territories that now comprise British India, the territories that now form the Indian States… shall be a Union of them all; (3) wherein the said territories… shall possess and retain the status of autonomous units, together with residuary powers…; (4) wherein all power and authority of the Sovereign Independent India… are derived from the people; (5) wherein shall be guaranteed and secured to all the people of India justice, social, economic and political; equality of status, of opportunity, and before the law; freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship…; (6) wherein adequate safeguards shall be provided for minorities, backward and tribal areas, and depressed and other backward classes; (7) whereby shall be maintained the integrity of the territory of the Republic and its sovereign rights…; (8) this ancient land attains its rightful and honoured place in the world…” — quoted by S.M. Sikri, C.J. in Kesavananda Bharati (DU material p.1–2); Nehru: “let it be… more solemnly, by all of us standing up and thus taking this pledge anew.”

Why it matters for Topic 1: every head of the syllabus — aims & objectives (justice-liberty-equality-fraternity), federalism (autonomous units with residuary powers), scheduled/tribal areas (clause 6), basic structure (integrity of territory, democratic institutions) — is foreshadowed in this Resolution. The Preamble and Parts III–IV are its enacted form.

2.3 Enforcement, Amendment Procedure & Borrowed Sources

Structure of the document

  • Preamble + 25 Parts + 448 Articles + 12 Schedules (as amended; originally 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules);
  • Part III — Fundamental Rights (Arts. 12–35); Part IV — DPSPs (Arts. 36–51); Part IVA — Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A, added by 42nd A., 1976);
  • Part V (Union) & Part VI (States); Part XI (Union–State relations: legislative, administrative, financial); Part XII Finance; Part XVI special provisions; Part XX amendment (Art. 368); Part XXI temporary/transitional.

How the Constitution can be amended

  • Simple majority (like ordinary law): e.g., creation of new States (Art. 3), changes in salaries, Second/Fifth/Sixth Schedule provisions in some cases;
  • Special majority — majority of total membership + ⅔ of members present & voting (Art. 368(2));
  • Special majority + ratification by legislatures of ≥½ the States (Art. 368 proviso) for matters affecting: distribution of legislative powers (Arts. 73, 162, Sch. VII), List I/II entries, High Courts, Art. 32, 5th & 6th Schedules, etc.

  • It is the source of all powers of, and limitations on, the three organs of State;

  • The course develops an analytical approach through case law.

Section 4Essential Features of the Constitution of India

A feature becomes “essential” when its removal would change the identity of the Constitution — that is the bridge between this section and Basic Structure (§5).

1 · Written & Longest Constitution

Preamble + 448 Articles + 25 Parts + 12 Schedules + numerous amendments; detailed because it must govern a continental, plural society and absorb the 1935 Act’s machinery. “Our Constitution is unique, apart from being the longest in the world” (Shelat & Grover JJ., Kesavananda).

2 · Drawn from Many Sources

Borrowed from UK, USA, Ireland, Canada, Australia, Switzerland etc. — yet sui generis; designed “suitable to the genius and requirements of the Indian people”.

3 · Blend of Rigidity & Flexibility

Ordinary provisions amendable simply; key provisions need Art. 368 special majorities; federal provisions need state ratification. “Every provision… can be amended provided… the basic foundation and structure… remains the same” (Sikri C.J.).

4 · Federal System with Unitary Bias

Union of States (Art. 1), three lists (Sch. VII), division of executive power (Arts. 73/162), integrated judiciary, single citizenship, residuary power to Centre (Art. 248) — “quasi-federal” (see §6–§7).

5 · Parliamentary Form of Government

Real executive = Council of Ministers responsible to the House (Arts. 74, 75(2), 163, 164); President/Governor = constitutional heads; Cabinet = “a hyphen which joins, a buckle which fastens the legislative part… to the executive part” (Ram Jawaya Kapur).

6 · Fundamental Rights & Duties

Part III (Arts. 12–35) justiciable via Arts. 32 & 226; Part IVA duty to abide by Constitution (Art. 51A — added by 42nd Amendment); DPSPs (Part IV) + fundamental rights = “conscience of the Constitution”.

7 · Independent & Integrated Judiciary

One hierarchy from SC to subordinate courts (Arts. 124–231); appointment by collegium (Arts. 124(2), 217, Art. 32 in exceptional cases); judicial review = basic feature; “Indian Judiciary is unitary in structure and operation” (Ramaswami J., Bommai).

8 · Single Citizenship

Arts. 5–11 — no state citizenship (contrast USA’s dual citizenship, 14th Amendment). “A country recognising single citizenship… negatives the concept of State sovereignty” (Ahmadi J., Bommai para 23).

9 · Universal Adult Franchise & Free Elections

Art. 326 — one person, one vote; Election Commission (Art. 324); multi-party democracy part of basic structure (Kuldip Nayar).

10 · Rigid Amendment Procedure for Federal Provisions

Art. 368 proviso — ratification by ≥½ State legislatures protects the federal bargain (also relevant to 368(2)(a)–(d) subjects).

11 · Emergency Provisions

Arts. 352, 356, 360 — unitary slide during emergency (“the Centre can move either on the federal or unitary plane” — Beg C.J. in State of Rajasthan v. UOI); moderated by judicial review after 44th Amendment & S.R. Bommai.

12 · Special Provisions for Weaker & Tribal Sections

Reservation (Arts. 15(4), 16(4), 330–332, 340–342), Fifth & Sixth Schedules, Arts. 244, 371A–371J, protective legislation (see §9).

13 · Bicameral Legislature at Centre

Art. 79 — Lok Sabha (people) + Rajya Sabha (Council of States — federal representation, Art. 80, Fourth Schedule). “Rigid federalism: Upper House represents the Union of the Federation” — but India did not adopt strict federalism (Kuldip Nayar).

14 · Rule of Law & Democratic Decentralisation

Rule of law underpins all governmental action; 73rd & 74th Amendments (Parts IX & IXA) created a four-tier governance: Centre → States → local bodies (Panchayats/Municipalities) — Ramaswami J. in Bommai para 168.

15 · Socialist & Secular Commitments

Added to Preamble by 42nd Amendment; operationalised through DPSPs; secularism declared basic feature in S.R. Bommai.

16 · Living Document with Ample Experiment

“A Constitution is not a gate but a road”; “an experiment as all life is an experiment” (Khanna J. & Mathew J. in Kesavananda) — 106 amendments so far, yet the basic structure stands.

Exam tip: “Essential features” ≠ “basic structure”. Features describe what the Constitution is; the basic structure is the irreducible core that even a constitutional amendment cannot destroy (§5). Some features (federalism, secularism, democracy, judicial review) are both.

Section 5Theory of Basic Structure

“Every provision of the Constitution can be amended provided in the result the basic foundation and structure of the Constitution remains the same.” — Sikri C.J., Kesavananda Bharati (DU material, para 302)

5.1 Evolution — the Doctrine Chain

Fig. 3 — From Shankari Prasad to I.R. Coelho: how the basic-structure theory matured.

The three doctrinal ingredients of the theory (as synthesised from the DU material):

  1. The Constituent Power is distinct from the legislative power. Art. 368 confers the amending power; it is subject to implied limitations because one generation cannot destroy the Constitution for the next (Mathew J.’s Jeffersonian passage, DU material).
  2. Amend ≠ Destroy. Hegde & Mukherjea JJ.: “one cannot legally use the Constitution to destroy itself… If one or more of the basic features are taken away, to that extent the Constitution is abrogated or repealed. The personality of the Constitution must remain unchanged.”
  3. Judicial review is preserved. A provision purporting to exclude judicial review of a constitutional amendment (or give blanket immunity to laws) is itself destructive of the basic structure (Kesavananda; Minerva Mills; I.R. Coelho).
Case 1 of 7 · DU Case Material pp. 1–7

His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati Sripadagalvaru v. State of Kerala

AIR 1973 SC 1461 · 1973 (4) SCC 225 · Decided 24 April 1973 · Bench: 13 Judges (largest ever) — S.M. Sikri C.J., J.M. Shelat, K.S. Hegde, A.N. Grover, A.N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, D.G. Palekar, H.R. Khanna, K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg, S.N. Dwivedi, A.K. Mukherjea, Y.V. Chandrachud, JJ. · Split: 7 : 6 · Lead counsel for petitioners: Nani Palkhivala

Background & Facts

The trigger: The Kerala Land Reforms Acts (1963 & 1969) and the Kerala Agrarian Relations Act, 1970 imposed ceilings on landholdings — including lands held by religious and charitable institutions. Swami Kesavananda Bharati, the head of the Edneer Mutt (Edneer, Kasaragod, Kerala), filed writ petitions under Art. 32 challenging the legislation as violating his fundamental rights (Arts. 14, 19, 25, 26, 31 — right to property was then a fundamental right).

The constitutional counter-attack: In the meantime, Parliament had reacted to Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) by enacting:

  • Constitution (24th Amendment) Act, 1971 — inserted in Art. 13 “nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment made under Article 368”, and in Art. 368 the words “may… amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision….”;
  • Constitution (25th Amendment) Act, 1971 — curtailed Art. 31 (compensation clauses) and inserted Art. 31C (laws implementing Arts. 39(b),(c) not to be void for violating Arts. 14, 19, 31 + the “no court can question the declaration” clause);
  • Constitution (29th Amendment) Act, 1971 — placed two Kerala land-reform Acts in the Ninth Schedule.

The validity of the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments — and the totality of Parliament’s amending power under Art. 368 — was the “main question” (DU material). The case was heard for 68 days (31 Oct 1972 – 23 Mar 1973) and produced a 703-page judgment with 11 separate opinions.

Issues

Q1. Is Golak Nath (FRs beyond Parliament’s amending power) correct — i.e., is Art. 368 subject to Art. 13(2)?
Q2. What is the nature, extent and scope of Parliament’s power to amend the Constitution under Art. 368 — is it plenary, or limited by implied/express restraints?
Q3. Are the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments (Art. 31C, Art. 31A-type protections, Ninth Schedule insertion) valid?
Q4. Can the Constitution confer on Parliament power to exclude judicial review of its own amendments?

Arguments

For the Petitioners (Kesavananda Bharati & others — Palkhivala):
  • Art. 368 prescribes only the procedure; the power of amendment is subject to Art. 13(2) — a “law” abridging FRs is void (Golak Nath logic);
  • The word “amend” means improvement, not destruction — the Constitution cannot be destroyed by using its own provisions; the identity (“personality”) of the Constitution must survive;
  • The Preamble (which speaks of justice, liberty, equality, fraternity and the People’s sovereignty) is part of the Constitution and limits amendment; certain features — supremacy of the Constitution, republican & democratic government, secular character, separation of powers, federal character — form the basic structure;
  • 24th Amendment’s ouster of Art. 13 destroys judicial review; Art. 31C’s non-justiciability clause and 29th Amendment’s Ninth Schedule device are tools to evade fundamental rights;
  • If the amending power is unlimited, Parliament can take away the amending power itself from future generations (Palkhivala’s “foreclosure” argument).
For the Respondents (State of Kerala, other States & Union of India):
  • Constituent sovereignty rests in Parliament; the Constitution-makers deliberately conferred a plenary amending power in Art. 368 — otherwise the Constitution could not meet changing needs;
  • Attorney-General: “every provision of the Constitution is essential; otherwise it would not have been put in the Constitution” — no provision is more “basic” than another;
  • Art. 368 as amended is complete in itself; Art. 13 expressly excludes amendments from its operation after the 24th Amendment;
  • Mathew J.’s Jeffersonian view (for the pro-amendment side): “A Constitution is not an end in itself, rather a means for ordering the life of a nation… if yesterday is not to paralyse today, it seems best to permit each generation to take care of itself”; every generation has the right by majority will to determine its constitutional structure; Dr. Ambedkar “could not have conceived of any limitation upon the amending power”;
  • Land-reform/DPSP legislation must not be held hostage to property rights — Art. 31C and the Ninth Schedule are legitimate agrarian-reform devices; the 29th Amendment merely kept the Kerala laws alive pending examination.

Decision — “The View by the Majority” (5 propositions, DU material p.1)

  1. I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (AIR 1967 SC 1643) — that fundamental rights were beyond Parliament’s amending powers — was overruled;
  2. The Constitution (24th Amendment) Act, 1971 (empowering Parliament to amend any part of the Constitution, and taking amendments out of Art. 13) was valid;
  3. Article 368 (as amended) was valid — but it did not confer power on Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. The Court did not spell out an exhaustive catalogue; individual judges gave illustrations;
  4. The attempt to exclude judicial review of a constitutional amendment was unconstitutional;
  5. The offending limb of Art. 31C — “and no law containing a declaration that it is for giving effect to such policy shall be called in question in any court on the ground that it does not give effect to such policy” — was held invalid (it destroyed judicial review). [Rest of Art. 31C first limb upheld.]

Vote mathematics: 7 : 6. For the basic-structure limitation — Sikri C.J., Shelat, Hegde, Grover, Jaganmohan Reddy, Mukherjea, Khanna, JJ. For unlimited amending power — Ray, Palekar, Mathew, Beg, Dwivedi, Chandrachud, JJ. Khanna J.’s separate opinion was pivotal, making the 7-member side possible.

Key passages from the DU case material (memory-points)

  • Sikri C.J. (para 302–303): basic structure may be said to include “(1) Supremacy of the Constitution; (2) Republican and Democratic forms of Government; (3) Secular character of the Constitution; (4) Separation of powers between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary; (5) Federal character of the Constitution” — built on the “basic foundation… the dignity and freedom of the individual”, which “cannot by any form of amendment be destroyed”.
  • Shelat & Grover JJ. (para 499, 503): the Constitution is an organic document which must grow; Parts III & IV are “the conscience of the Constitution”; the federal essence = distribution of powers; written & controlled Constitution; parliamentary system grafted on a federal structure.
  • Hegde & Mukherjea JJ. (para 667): “a Constitution like ours contains certain features which are so essential that they cannot be changed or destroyed… one cannot legally use the Constitution to destroy itself… The personality of the Constitution must remain unchanged.”
  • Palekar J. (para 1219): India’s is “broadly speaking, a quasi-federal constitution which adopted the Parliamentary system of Government based on adult franchise” — key authority for §7.
  • Khanna J. (para 1448): “a Constitution is not a gate but a road… not a document for fastidious dialectics but the means of ordering the life of a people”; “It is a Constitution we are expounding” (Marshall).
  • Mathew J. (para 1563): living-Constitution view: “A Constitution is an experiment as all life is an experiment” — no limitation on amending power (minority view).
Principles evolved
  1. Basic Structure Doctrine: Parliament’s constituent power under Art. 368 is wide but not unlimited; amendments violating the basic structure are void.
  2. Amendment ≠ Law under Art. 13(2) (Golak Nath overruled) — but subject to the basic-structure limitation, preserving judicial review.
  3. Integral parts of the Constitution (Preamble, fundamental rights, federal structure) are protected in substance even if their text is varied.
  4. Judicial review is the mechanism that makes the limitation enforceable — hence any ouster clause is unconstitutional.
  5. Follow-ups: Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975), Minerva Mills (1980), Waman Rao (1981), I.R. Coelho (2007), NJAC case (2015).

Related instruments: Constitution (24th, 25th, 29th) Amendment Acts, 1971; Kerala Land Reforms Acts 1963/1969; Kerala Agrarian Relations Act 1970; Arts. 13, 19(1)(f),(g), 31, 31A, 31B, 31C, 368, 371A? (no — Fifth Schedule); Ninth Schedule; Art. 32. Real example today: every major amendment challenge (e.g., Navtej Singh Johar reading down, NJAC, Art. 370) runs through Kesavananda’s filter.

Case 7 of 7 · DU Case Material (listed under Topic 1)

I. R. Coelho (Dead) by LRs v. State of Tamil Nadu & Ors.

(2007) 2 SCC 1 · AIR 2007 SC 861 · Decided 11 January 2007 · Bench: 9 Judges (unanimous) — Y.K. Sabharwal C.J., C.K. Thakker, B.P. Singh, S.H. Kapadia, D.K. Jain, P.K. Balasubramanyan, S.B. Sinha, Altamas Kabir, J.R. Lodha, JJ.

Background & Facts

The Ninth Schedule (inserted by the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 along with Art. 31B) shelters listed laws from attack under fundamental rights — originally to protect agrarian/zamindari abolition laws. By the 2000s it contained ~284 laws, many inserted in the 1970s–90s for various States.

The lead petitioner, I.R. Coelho (a retired judicial officer), and other petitioners challenged, through a batch of references pending since a 1999 Constitution Bench order, the validity of several Ninth Schedule insertions made after 24 April 1973 — including Tamil Nadu land-reform/estate-abolition amendments and reservation laws — on the ground that they violated Arts. 14, 19, 21 and destroy the basic structure notwithstanding Art. 31B.

The reference questions: (i) what is the scope of Art. 31B & the Ninth Schedule; (ii) whether laws inserted after the Kesavananda date (24-4-1973) enjoy total immunity from judicial review; (iii) whether Waman Rao (1981) needs clarification/overruling.

Issues

Q1. Does the inclusion of a law in the Ninth Schedule give it absolute immunity from judicial review — even if the law destroys the basic structure?
Q2. What is the effect of the 24-4-1973 cut-off laid down in Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)?
Q3. What is the test to examine a Ninth Schedule law — “rights test” vs “essence of rights test”?

Arguments

Petitioners:
  • Parliament cannot use the Ninth Schedule as a device to exclude judicial review altogether — that would destroy the supremacy of the Constitution and the basic structure (Kesavananda);
  • The amending power including an Art. 368 amendment inserting a law into the Ninth Schedule is itself subject to basic-structure limits;
  • Laws after 24-4-1973 that abrogate Arts. 14, 19, 21 (whose essence is part of basic structure) must be struck down — “rights test + essence of rights test”;
  • Art. 31B cannot place any law “above the Constitution”.
Respondents (Union of India, States):
  • Art. 31B is itself a constitutional provision; its whole purpose was to give finality to land-reform legislation by removing it from judicial scrutiny — reading a basic-structure exception destroys that finality;
  • Placement in the Ninth Schedule is an exercise of constituent power with full effect; “an appeal on merits” against Parliament is unknown;
  • Waman Rao already protected pre-1973 insertions; post-1973 entries should equally stand unless the entire amendment itself is violative ab initio;
  • Policy questions of agrarian reform are for the political wing, not courts.

Decision (unanimous)

  1. Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24-4-1973 are NOT immune from judicial review. The insertion is an amendment of the Constitution under Art. 368 — it must satisfy the basic-structure test evolved in Kesavananda.
  2. Test laid down (sequential): (a) does the Ninth Schedule law abrogate/abridge a fundamental right? (b) if yes, is that right (or its essence) part of the basic structure? (c) if yes, does the violation destroy or damage that essential feature? Only if all three are answered affirmatively is the law struck down.
  3. Articles 14, 19 and 21 in combination embody principles — rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review — forming the constitutional core; laws destroying their essence lose Art. 31B protection.
  4. Waman Rao was not overruled but affirmed and elaborated; the 24-4-1973 dividing line stands: pre-1973 insertions retain near-absolute Art. 31B immunity; post-1973 insertions are open to challenge on basic-structure grounds.
  5. Judicial review is a basic feature — the Ninth Schedule cannot be used to destroy it. (No specific statute was struck down in this judgment; the batch was to be tested case-by-case.)
Principles evolved
  1. “Ninth Schedule ≠ constitutional black-hole” — no legislative device can take a law beyond the basic structure;
  2. Impact-based review: technical infringement is not enough — the court examines the nature, extent and impact of the violation (rights test + essence of rights test);
  3. Constitutional supremacy over parliamentary supremacy re-affirmed; Art. 31B is “under” the Constitution, not above it;
  4. Cements the chain: Kesavananda (1973) → Minerva Mills (1980) → Waman Rao (1981) → Coelho (2007).

Related instruments: Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 (Art. 31B & Ninth Schedule); Constitution (39th, 42nd Amendments); Art. 31A & 31B; Art. 368; Arts. 14, 19, 21; Waman Rao (1981) [pre-1973 laws tested against FRs as on 26-1-1950]; Minerva Mills. Real example: the Tamil Nadu 69% reservation law (kept in Ninth Schedule in 1994) remains under challenge even post-Coelho — showing the doctrine’s live edge.

Section 6Principles of Federalism

“Federalism is a political contrivance for a body of States which desire Union but not unity.” — Dicey (quoted by A.M. Ahmadi, J. in S.R. Bommai, DU material)

6.1 Concept, Meaning and Classical Features

Federalism = a system of government in which sovereign legislative & executive authority is divided between a Union/Centre and constituent States/units by the Constitution itself, each acting within its assigned sphere, with a supreme written Constitution, an independent judiciary to interpret the division, and a mechanism to resolve conflicts.

“The essence of a federation is the existence of the Union and the States and the distribution of powers between them. Federalism, therefore, essentially implies demarcation of powers in a federal compact.” — A.M. Ahmadi, J., S.R. Bommai v. Union of India, para 14 (DU material)
“The basic principle of federalism is that the legislative and executive authority is partitioned between the Centre and the States not by any law to be made by the Centre but the Constitution itself. The States… are in no way dependent upon the Centre… The Centre and the States are co-equal in this matter.” — Dr. B.R. Ambedkar in the Constituent Assembly (quoted in Kuldip Nayar & Art. 370 judgment, uploaded materials)
“…the important point is whether the powers of the Government are divided between coordinate independent authorities or not… the systems of government embody predominantly a division of powers between Centre and regional authority, each of which in its own sphere is co-ordinating with the other independent… and if so is that Government federal?” — Prof. K.C. Section 7Nature of the Indian Constitution — Federal, Unitary or Quasi-Federal?

“What is there in a name?” — A.M. Ahmadi J. in Bommai, after holding that the Indian Constitution is “more appropriately described as quasi-federal… a mixture of the federal and unitary elements.”

① Federal Theory

Says: India is federal — written Constitution, division of powers (Sch. VII), independent judiciary, Art. 131 dispute resolution, federal guarantee of the legislative field.

Justices: Sawant & Kuldip Singh JJ. (Bommai, para 97 — following H.M. Seervai: “the federal principle is dominant in our Constitution and has not been watered down”, giving reasons (a)–(k)); Ambedkar in CA (“Chief mark of federalism lies in the partition of legislative & executive authority by the Constitution”).

② Unitary Theory

Says: Unitary in operation — strong Centre; residuary power with Centre; single citizenship; integrated judiciary; Art. 3 power over States; Art. 356 takeover; States dependent financially; emergency overrides.

Justices: Beg C.J. in State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (AIR 1977): “whatever appearance of a federal structure… its operations are certainly… more unitary than federal”; “amphibian” Union that moves on federal or unitary plane; M. Karunanidhi v. UOI (Mad.) in Bommai.

③ Quasi-Federal Theory

Says: A hybrid — “federal in form but unitary in substance/leaning to centre”. Term popularised by K.C. Wheare (on Australian Constitution: “quasi-federal”), applied to India by Palekar J. in Kesavananda (“broadly speaking, a quasi-federal constitution”) and Untwalia J. in State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1978).

Jeevan Reddy J. (Bommai): “our Constitution has certainly a bias towards Centre… but within the sphere allotted to them, States are supreme.”

The modern synthesis — write this in exams

The Constitution is federal by structure, unitary in leaning, quasi-federal in operation: the “division of powers” is real and judicially protected (Bommai, ITC, Kesoram), yet the Centre has overriding levers (Arts. 3, 248, 249–253, 256–258, 352–360) deployed only in extraordinary situations — “The exceptions are not a rule” (Sawant J., para 99). Federalism itself is a basic feature (Bommai; Kuldip Nayar; Art. 370 judgment: “asymmetric federalism… is part of the basic structure, as is federalism”).

Case 3 of 7 · DU Case Material pp. 25–39

State of West Bengal v. Union of India

AIR 1963 SC 1241 · (1963) Supp 1 SCR 371 · Bench: B.P. Sinha C.J., S.J. Imam, K. Subba Rao, J.C. Shah, Rajagopala Ayyangar, J.R. Mudholkar, JJ. · Suit under Art. 131 · Nature of Indian Constitution — Federal / Unitary / Quasi-federal; power of Union to acquire State property

Facts

Parliament enacted the Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957 (entries 52 & 54 Union List read with entry 42 Concurrent List) to acquire unworked coal-bearing lands and lessees’ rights across India. Central notifications dated 21-9-1959 and 8-1-1960 took over coal-bearing areas within West Bengal.

The State of West Bengal filed a suit under Art. 131 against the Union of India, seeking a declaration that Parliament was not competent to authorise acquisition of land/rights belonging to a State, and that the Act and the two notifications were unconstitutional. Other States (Punjab, etc.) intervened.

Statement of Objects & Reasons said the Centre “does not intend to acquire the proprietary rights vested in the States” — but the Court held SoR cannot be used to cut down the statute’s generality (para 13).

Issues (as framed in the DU material)

(1) Whether Parliament had legislative competence to enact a law for compulsory acquisition by the Union of land and properties vested in/owned by the State?
(2) Whether the State of West Bengal was a sovereign authority?
(3) Assuming it was sovereign, was Parliament still entitled to legislate for compulsory acquisition of its lands/properties?
(4) Whether the Act or any of its provisions were ultra vires Parliament’s competence? (5) Whether the plaintiff was entitled to any relief?

Arguments

Plaintiff-State of West Bengal (and supporting States):
  • The Constitution adopts the federal principle — States share sovereignty with the Union; therefore Entry 42 (List III) must be read as implying a restriction against acquiring State-owned property;
  • Property vested under Art. 294(1) cannot be diverted to Union purposes by compulsion of legislation;
  • The Govt. of India Act, 1935, s. 127 allowed acquisition of provincial land only by negotiation/arbitration, not compulsion — the absence of a similar proviso in the Constitution implies continued immunity;
  • The Australian Constitution expressly empowers acquisition of State property — its absence in India is exclusionary (expressio unius);
  • If Parliament could acquire State property under Entry 42, States could reciprocally “re-acquire” from the Union — an absurd loop making the entry a dead letter;
  • Individuals & corporations enjoy Art. 31(2) protection (public purpose + compensation) — States cannot be left worse off than private owners;
  • Unless a law expressly or by necessary implication binds a State, it does not — a general rule of interpretation;
  • Illustration (Advocate-General of Bengal): Union could otherwise acquire Writer’s Building and stop the State Secretariat.
Defendant-Union of India:
  • Entry 42 of List III + Art. 246(2) confers plenary power to legislate for acquisition of all property, State-owned included; no textual interdict exists;
  • Parliament’s powers under List I entries (22 Railways, 23 national highways, 52 industries, 54 mines, 56 inter-State rivers, etc.) already allow legislation directly affecting State-situate property — excluding it under Entry 42 would make constitutional machinery unworkable;
  • Historical: under the 1935 Act too the Centre could require provinces to acquire/transfer land (s. 127); provincial autonomy was never thought breached by that;
  • A Constitution that lets Parliament destroy a State (Arts. 2–3) cannot logically bar it from merely acquiring a State’s property;
  • Ability of a power to be abused ≠ absence of the power (“if the Union did so, it would be abusing, not using, its power — but abuse is no reason for denying existence”).

Decision (findings recorded — DU material para 69)

Issue 1 — in the affirmative: the Act applied to State lands; Parliament was competent. Issue 2: whatever sovereignty the State possessed did not “disentitle the Union Parliament to exercise its legislative power under Entry 42, List III”. Issue 3: covered by answer to Issue 2. Issue 4 — negative (Act not ultra vires). Issue 5 — negative; additional issue answered in the affirmative. The suit was DISMISSED.

The constitutional analysis (paras 26–37) — the heart of the case for Topic 1:

  • (a) Compact: “Our Constitution was not the result of any such compact or agreement: Units constituting a unitary State which were non-sovereign were transformed by abdication of power into a Union” — absent
  • (b) Supremacy alterable only by units: “Our Constitution is undoubtedly supreme, but it is liable to be altered by the Union Parliament alone and the units have no power to alter it” — absent
  • (c) Distribution of powers: present — “but distribution of powers is not always an index of political sovereignty. The exercise of powers legislative and executive in the allotted fields is hedged in by numerous restrictions, so that the powers of the States are not coordinate with the Union and are in many respects not independent” — partially present
  • (d) Supreme authority of courts: “in our Constitution characteristic (d) is to be found in full force” — single integrated judiciary; SC at apex; Arts. 226–227 — present
  • Legal vs political sovereignty: “Legal sovereignty of the Indian nation is vested in the people of India… Political sovereignty is distributed between the Union and the States with greater weightage in favour of the Union.”
  • The destroy-a-State argument: “Parliament which is competent to destroy a State is [not] on account of some assumption as to absolute sovereignty of the State incompetent effectively to acquire by legislation… the property owned by the State.”
Principles evolved
  1. Test of a ‘truly federal’ Constitution (four characteristics (a)–(d)) — India satisfies (c) & (d) but not (a) & (b) → not a “traditional/true” federation; authority flowed from the people, not from a compact of sovereigns.
  2. State property is not immune from valid Union legislation — legislative competence under Entry 42 is plenary; sovereignty of States is not absolute.
  3. Political sovereignty is distributed with a Union tilt — the foundational statement of India’s quasi-federal design, relied on later in Bommai & Kuldip Nayar.
  4. Interpretive canons: Statement of Objects & Reasons ≠ statute; Preamble of an Act can aid but not override enacting words; a power capable of abuse still exists.
  5. Also settled: legislation affecting State boundaries/existence moved on the President’s recommendation (Art. 3) — Parliament’s paramountcy over States’ territorial existence.

Related instruments: Coal Bearing Areas (Acquisition and Development) Act, 1957; Mines and Minerals (Regulation and Development) Act, 1957; Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951; Minerals and Mining (Regulation and Development) Act, 1948; Land Acquisition Act, 1894; Arts. 294–300, 298; Govt. of India Act, 1935 ss. 127, 172; Art. 131; Seventh Schedule Entries 42, 52, 54, 56. Real example: the same logic later sustained Parliament’s power to enact the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition Act, 2013 applying to State-situate lands where Centre has competence — and centrally-owned coal mines were auctioned under the Coal Mines (Special Provisions) Ordinance, 2015 framework.

Case 4 of 7 · DU Case Material pp. 40–47

Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur & Ors. v. State of Punjab

AIR 1955 SC 549 · Bench: B.K. Mukherjea C.J., Vivian Bose, B. Jagannadhadas, T.L. Venkatarama Ayyar, Syed Jafar Imam, JJ. · Writ petition under Art. 32 · Subject-heads: Theory of Separation of Powers; Arts. 73 & 162; State monopoly of trade/business and Art. 19(6)

Facts

Six persons carrying on the business of preparing, printing, publishing and selling school textbooks (primary & middle classes) as “Uttar Chand Kapur & Sons” filed a writ petition under Art. 32. The Punjab Education Department, pursuant to a “so-called policy of nationalisation of textbooks”, issued a series of notifications from 1950 that (i) restricted and (ii) in substance ousted private publishers from the business — alleged violation of Art. 19(1)(g) imposed without authority of law (hence not saved by Art. 19(6)); and also an Art. 31 claim (deprivation of business/property without law/compensation).

The State carried on printing/publishing through its executive without any specific statute authorising trade — the constitutional crux.

Issues

Q1 (leading): Can the executive Government of a State engage in trade/business (here, textbook nationalisation) without legislative sanction? Is there a rigid doctrine of separation of powers under the Indian Constitution requiring the legislature to first enact a law?
Q2: Assuming a monopoly can be created, must it be by “proper legislation” conforming to Art. 19(6)?
Q3: Can the Government deprive publishers of a business/undisputed interest = “property” without authority of law and compensation under Art. 31?
Q4 (factual): Were the petitioners’ Art. 19(1)(g) rights infringed by the policy?

Arguments

For the petitioners (Mr Pathak):
  • The Constitution recognises a tripartite division of governmental functions — legislative, judicial, executive — and the executive can only execute laws passed by the legislature;
  • Arts. 73 & 162 distribute executive power along legislative lines → executive may act only where the legislature has legislated;
  • Relied on Australian High Court decisions: Commonwealth v. Central Wool Committee (agreements without statutory authority void — s. 61 Australian Constitution) and Attorney-General for Victoria v. Commonwealth (Starke J.’s dissent — no govt. factory absent law);
  • Motilal v. State of U.P. (AIR 1951 All 257): Agarwala J. — no power to run bus service without an Act;
  • Any monopoly must be by legislation meeting Art. 19(6); deprivation of business = Art. 31 compensation needed.
For the State of Punjab:
  • Arts. 73 & 162 concern distribution of executive power between Centre & States — they do not define the content of executive function and do not say the executive can act only after legislation;
  • Executive power extends to matters on which the legislature is competent to legislate, not merely matters already legislated (Art. 154; corresponding Art. 73);
  • Expenses were sanctioned through the annual financial statement (Art. 202), demands for grants (Art. 203) and Appropriation Acts (Arts. 204 & 266(3)) — “in accordance with law”;
  • Power to contract under Art. 298; no additional powers needed; the Australian cases are distinguishable (no s. 61-type provision; no tax/licence-fee appropriation here).

Decision

  1. No absolute separation of powers. “The Indian Constitution has not indeed recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity but the functions of the different parts or branches of the Government have been sufficiently differentiated and consequently it can very well be said that our Constitution does not contemplate assumption, by one organ or part of the State, of functions that essentially belong to another.” (para 12 — THE classic statement)
  2. Executive power ≠ power only to execute laws. “Ordinarily the executive power connotes the residue of governmental functions that remain after legislative and judicial functions are taken away”; the executive may do departmental/subordinate legislation when delegated and limited judicial functions when empowered — “it does not follow… that in order to enable the executive to function there must be a law already in existence.”
  3. Parliamentary executive: “Our Constitution, though federal in its structure, is modelled on the British parliamentary system where the executive is deemed to have the primary responsibility for the formulation of governmental policy and its transmission into law… the condition precedent… is its retaining the confidence of the legislative branch.” The Cabinet is “a hyphen which joins, a buckle which fastens the legislative part of the State to the executive part” (para 13–14).
  4. Trade/business by Government without specific statute — permissible, provided (i) expenditure is authorised by Appropriation Acts, (ii) policy enjoys the tacit support of the legislative majority, (iii) no encroachment on private rights requiring specific legislation, (iv) no constitutional/fundamental-rights violation (paras 15–18).
  5. Art. 73/162 construed: they distribute executive powers Union-vs-State on the same analogy as legislative lists (analogous to ss. 8 & 49(2) of the Govt. of India Act, 1935) — they do not make executive action contingent on prior legislation.
  6. On the petitioners’ rights (para 19–21): even if executive acts lacked legislative sanction, petitioners succeed only if a fundamental right is infringed. There was no fundamental right in publishers that their books be prescribed as textbooks — “merely a chance or prospect”; Government’s choice of textbook-selection method is policy (criticisable politically, not justiciable under Art. 19(1)(g)). Art. 19(6)/Art. 31 points did not arise. Petition dismissed.
Principles evolved
  1. Soft separation of powers: functional differentiation without rigid compartments — the seed of the modern “checks & balances” and “basic structure — separation of powers” jurisprudence (Sikri C.J. listed it in Kesavananda).
  2. Existence of ‘executive power’ independent of legislation: the Union/State executives may act in their spheres without prior statute (but never contrary to Constitution/law — Art. 154).
  3. Financial route = legality: Appropriation Acts sanction public expenditure “in accordance with law” (Arts. 202–204, 266(3)) — constitutional basis of government trading.
  4. Art. 19(1)(g) is not a guarantee of a market — “chance or prospect” of customers/approvals ≠ fundamental right; relevant to Art. 19(6) & monopoly debates.
  5. Relied ever since for: parliamentary government, executive “residue” theory, and the limits of Art. 32 claims against policy decisions.

Related instruments: Arts. 53, 73, 75, 154, 162, 19(1)(g), 19(6), 31, 202–204, 266(3), 298; Govt. of India Act, 1935 ss. 8, 49(2); Appropriation Acts; Punjab education/textbook policy (1950s). Real example: State liquor monopolies (e.g., TASMAC, KSBCL), State-run buses (DTC/APSRTC) and PSU telecom/insurance monopolies are sustained by this judgment’s logic — executive trade activity financed by Appropriation Acts, restricted to private rights only by statute (e.g., Art. 19(6) proviso — State monopoly laws).

Case 2 of 7 · DU Case Material pp. 8–24 (longest extract — 17 pages)

S. R. Bommai & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.

1994 (3) SCC 1 · AIR 1994 SC 1918 · Decided 11 March 1994 · Bench: 9 Judges — S. Ratnavel Pandian, A.M. Ahmadi, Kuldip Singh, J.S. Verma, P.B. Sawant, K. Ramaswamy, S.C. Agrawal, Yogeshwar Dayal, B.P. Jeevan Reddy, JJ. · Subject-head: President’s Rule under Art. 356 — grounds and scope of judicial review (also listed under Topic 9)

Facts — the two clusters of proclamations

A. The Karnataka episode (Bommai’s own appeal):

  • 5-3-1985 assembly elections: Janata Dal 139/225; R.K. Hegde CM; he resigned 12-8-1988 → S.R. Bommai became CM.
  • Position on 1-2-1989: Janata Dal 111, Congress 65, Janata Party 27. After a cabinet reshuffle, Kalyan Molakery & others defected; on 17–18-4-1989 he wrote to the Governor with letters of 19 others expressing want of confidence in Bommai.
  • 19-4-1989: Governor sent report to President. 20-4-1989: 7 of the 19 MLAs wrote back saying their signatures were obtained by misrepresentation and reaffirmed support to Bommai; cabinet decided to convene the House on 27-4-1989 for a floor test; Bommai met Governor requesting a floor test (even advancing the date).
  • In that situation the Governor sent a second report; on 21-4-1989 the President issued the Art. 356 Proclamation — dismissing the Bommai government, dissolving the Assembly and assuming State administration.
  • Karnataka HC (3-judge bench) dismissed the writ petition (S.R. Bommai v. UOI, AIR 1990 Kant 5) holding floor test not mandatory/pre-requisite. Appeal to SC.

B. The Ayodhya/Babri Masjid cluster (1992): BJP formed governments in UP, MP, Rajasthan & HP (Feb 1990) with the Ram temple in its manifesto. The Ram Janmabhoomi–Babri Masjid structure was demolished by karsevaks on 6-12-1992 despite assurances to the Supreme Court; UP government resigned; the President dissolved the UP legislature by proclamation dated 6-12-1992. On 15-12-1992, the President dismissed the governments and dissolved the assemblies of Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Himachal Pradesh. The Court examined these along with Meghalaya and Nagaland proclamations (1988–91).

Also relevant: State of Karnataka v. Union of India (AIR 1978 SC 68) and State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (AIR 1977 SC 1361) — Commissions of Inquiry cases — were cited for Beg C.J.’s “amphibian Union” observations.

Issues

Q1. What is the nature of the Indian Constitution — federal, quasi-federal, unitary — and what does that mean for Centre–State relations?
Q2. Under what circumstances could President’s Rule under Art. 356 be justified — and what are the limits?
Q3. Is the President’s satisfaction in issuing the proclamation subject to judicial review? Does Art. 74(2) bar scrutiny of the aid-and-advice material?
Q4. Effect of dissolution of the State Assembly — before parliamentary approval; and its effect if the proclamation is later disapproved/struck down?
Q5. Must a floor test precede the report? Can defection-driven letters substitute for the floor?
Q6. Whether governments can be dismissed for conduct impairing secularism (the Babri cluster)?

Arguments (as reflected in the DU material)

For the challenge (Bommai & States — invalidity of proclamations):
  • Art. 356 is a fail-safe for genuine breakdown, not a political weapon; the material must be relevant, real and bona fide;
  • Where the CM demands a floor test, dismissal without one offends Arts. 14, 163, 356 read with Art. 355 — “the exceptions are not a rule”;
  • Art. 74(2) (“question not to be enquired into by any court”) does not bar scrutiny of whether any material existed; Bommai itself proceeded on records;
  • Dissolution of the assembly before parliamentary sanction is impermissible; if disapproved/lapsed, the house must revive;
  • Secularism: a government that abandons constitutional secularism can be replaced — but each case needs its own material (MP/Rajasthan/HP challenged on this count too).
For the Union (upholding proclamations):
  • Art. 356 rests on the President’s satisfaction formed on the Governor’s/CM’s reports — a political question (borrowing the American doctrine) not justiciable;
  • Art. 74(2) absolutely excludes judicial inquiry into aid & advice;
  • Once Parliament approves within 2 months (Art. 356(3)) and the Assembly is dissolved, courts cannot restore anything;
  • Emergency-type powers are inherent in every polity (US Art. IV §4 analogy) — the Constitution’s unitary features make such powers consistent with federalism;
  • In the Babri cluster: communal polarisation, breakdown of law & order and the state’s failure to protect a structure under this Court’s directions furnished live material.

Decision — holdings of the 9-judge Bench

  1. Art. 356 IS subject to judicial review. The Court may examine whether the proclamation issued (i) on any material at all, (ii) on relevant material, (iii) in good faith/purpose, and (iv) whether the power was exercised for the object for which it is conferred. Sawant J. (para 106): “the federal principle, social pluralism and pluralist democracy which form the basic structure… demand that the judicial review of the Proclamation issued under Article 356(1) is not only an imperative necessity but a stringent duty.”
  2. Art. 74(2) is not a bar to examining the existence/relevance of material — it bars inquiry into the contents of advice, not whether the constitutional machinery failed.
  3. Floor test is the touchstone: where the CM’s majority is challenged, the proper course is to let the House decide. Karnataka proclamation (21-4-1989) held unconstitutional — the Governor acted in haste on untested, partly retracted letters; the demand for a floor test was ignored.
  4. Post-decisional approval: if Parliament does not approve within 2 months, the proclamation lapses; the dismissed government revives and the dissolved assembly cannot remain dead — dissolution before approval is invalid; the assembly stands restored (prospectively).
  5. Outcomes State-wise: proclamations in Karnataka, Meghalaya and Nagaland held unconstitutional (no floor test/defective material — Meghalaya becoming partly academic after elections); Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh upheld — in the wake of the 6-12-1992 demolition and communal conflagration, a genuine situation had arisen in which the government could not be carried on in accordance with the Constitution (Arts. 355–356), and secularism was engaged.
  6. Secularism & federalism declared basic features — a government pursuing anti-secular policies furnishes a legitimate ground for Art. 356.
  7. Dissolution effects: once the proclamation is approved and subsists, neither the dismissed government nor the dissolved house revives; if struck down, relief is calibrated (courts often direct elections — as eventually happened in Karnataka 1994).

The great federalism discourse in Bommai (DU material — quotations you must be able to use)

Ahmadi J. — “Quasi-federal” reading (paras 13–24)

  • Founding fathers “leaned in favour of a strong Centre”; essential features showing non-federal strictness: absence of “federal/federation” in the text; Arts. 2–3; emergency powers; residuary power with Union (Art. 248 + Entry 97); power to amend; power to issue directions; single citizenship; integrated judiciary;
  • “the Constitution of India is differently described, more appropriately as ‘quasi-federal’ because it is a mixture of the federal and unitary elements, leaning more towards the latter — but then what is there in a name… what is important… is the thrust and implications of the various provisions bearing on… Article 356.”
  • Quoted State of W.B. v. UOI: “powers of the States are not co-ordinate with the Union…”; Beg C.J. in State of Rajasthan: “more unitary than federal… amphibian”.

Sawant J. (+ Kuldip Singh J.) — “Federal” reading (paras 97–106)

  • Follows Seervai’s 10-point rebuttal (a)–(k): non-sovereign origin is no objection; boundary-alterations forced by agitation; residuary power irrelevant to federal character (US/Australia give residuary to States yet are federal); emergency powers exist even in “indisputably federal” constitutions; Art. 356 modelled on US Const. Art. IV §4; directions to States = US enforcement power; Art. 254 conflict rule is federal;
  • “The States have an independent constitutional existence… They are neither satellites nor agents of the Centre.” Emergency invasions are “exceptions… not a rule”;
  • Label irrelevant: “It is not the theoretical label given to the Constitution but the practical implications… whether the powers under Article 356(1) can be exercised… arbitrarily.”

Ramaswami J. — federalism as basic feature (paras 165–174)

  • “Federalism envisaged in the Constitution of India is a basic feature in which the Union of India is permanent… and indestructible. The State is the creature of the Constitution… with no territorial integrity, but a permanent entity… Qua the Union, State is quasi-federal”;
  • Four-tier governance (Centre, States, UTs, local bodies); Berubari contrast (“no organic roots”); Setalvad’s Tagore Lectures; Ambedkar’s CA speech — “not a league of States”; Salmond’s unitary/composite State taxonomy; ITC & Kesoram later applied this;
  • Preamble basic features: “Democratic form of Government, federal structure, unity and integrity of the nation, secularism, socialism, social justice and judicial review are basic features of the Constitution.”

Jeevan Reddy J. (+ Agrawal J.) — “bias but balance” (paras 274–276)

  • “No two federal constitutions are alike… futile to fit our Constitution into any particular mould”;
  • Bias towards Centre in lists (important heads in List I; many List II entries subject to List I; concurrent primacy to Parliament; residuary with Centre; Art. 3 unilaterality; single citizenship; one judiciary; Arts. 249–253; finances); Art. 355 is “both a responsibility and a power”;
  • “Within the sphere allotted to them, States are supreme. The Centre cannot tamper with their powers… Courts should be careful not to upset the delicately crafted constitutional scheme… federalism… is not a matter of administrative convenience, but one of principle — the outcome of our own historical process.”
Principles evolved (the Bommai rules — write these as a list in any Art. 356 question)
  1. Judicial review of Art. 356 proclamation — materials, mala fides, relevance, and propriety;
  2. Floor of the House is decisive — Governor cannot act on unverified letters when a floor test is demanded;
  3. Post-decisional parliamentary approval within 2 months (Art. 356(3)) — non-approval ⇒ lapse & revival;
  4. Dissolution requires sanction — no irreversible action before parliamentary approval; if struck down, restoration/elections;
  5. Art. 74(2) is not an absolute bar;
  6. Secularism & federalism = basic structure; failure of constitutional machinery includes governance impairing secularism;
  7. Federalism as the organising principle — States are constitutional potentates in their sphere; label (federal/quasi/unitary) doesn’t dilute the review standards.

Case 5 of 7 · DU Case Material (Topic 1) · Full text uploaded

Kuldip Nayar & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.

(2006) 7 SCC 1 · W.P. (C) 217/2004 + connected petitions · Decided 22 August 2006 · Bench: Y.K. Sabharwal C.J., K.G. Balakrishnan, S.H. Kapadia, C.K. Thakker, P.K. Balasubramanyan, JJ. · Challenge to Representation of People (Amendment) Act, 2003 — deletion of domicile for Rajya Sabha + open ballot

Facts & Statutory Background

Before 2003: s. 3 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 required a Rajya Sabha candidate to be “an elector for a Parliamentary constituency in that State or territory” — i.e., an ordinary-resident/domicile link (traced through Adaptation Order 1956 and Act 47 of 1966). Sections 59, 94 & 128 guaranteed secret ballot (“votes shall be given by ballot”; secrecy not to be infringed; penalty for violating secrecy).

Representation of People (Amendment) Act, 2003 (Act 40 of 2003), in force from 28-8-2003: (i) in s. 3, substituted “in India” for “in that State or territory” (domicile deleted); (ii) inserted provisos to ss. 59, 94 & 128 introducing open ballot for Council of States elections (vote to be disclosed to the party’s authorised representative).

The petitioner Kuldip Nayar (veteran journalist) and others challenged both sets of amendments under Art. 32.

Issues

Q1 (domicile): Does deletion of the residence/domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha members offend the principle of federalism — a basic feature — or any provision (Arts. 80, 84)?
Q2 (secrecy): Does introduction of the open ballot for Rajya Sabha elections violate the secrecy of the ballot — an essential feature of free & fair elections — and the voter’s Art. 19(1)(a) freedom?
Q3 (ancillary): Can the basic structure doctrine be used to strike down an ordinary statute (as opposed to a constitutional amendment)?

Arguments

Petitioners (Shri Sachar, Shri Nariman & counsel):
  • The impugned amendment “offends the principle of Federalism, the basic feature of the Constitution; it seeks to change the character of the republic… and distorts the balance of power between the Union and the States”;
  • The nomenclature “Council of States” indicates the federal character — a representative who is not ordinarily resident of the State cannot effectively represent it; members must know their State’s problems;
  • The scheme of Arts. 80 & 84 + ss. 17–19 of the RP Act, 1950 + s. 3 RP Act, 1951 guaranteed the representative federal character; deleting “domicile” destroys “the basic requirement of the representative federal body”; the upper House becomes an “alter ego” of the lower House; Rajya Sabha Rules emphasise residence;
  • On secrecy: secret ballot is the essence of free & fair elections and of Art. 19(1)(a) expression; open ballot enables coercion, destroys independence of MLAs and multi-party democracy (basic structure);
  • Basic structure can invalidate any law/constitutional mechanism that destroys essential features (relying on Kesavananda, Indira Gandhi, Bommai).
Union of India (Shri Vahanvati, Ld. Solicitor General):
  • The amendments were necessitated by deficiencies in working of the RP Act, 1951; they do not alter/distort the Council’s character;
  • Residence/domicile is a “qualification” under Art. 84(c) to be prescribed by Parliament — unlike the US Constitution, India’s Constitution does not constitutionally mandate residence for Rajya Sabha members;
  • MLAs are best placed to decide who represents them in the Council; broad-basing the qualification ensures representation of unrepresented interests;
  • Open ballot is a regulatory measure against cross-voting, horse-trading and corruption — reasonably restricted under Art. 19(2) (assuming voting = expression); indirect election by party-sponsored MLAs can be regulated by party discipline; Constitution nowhere guarantees secret ballot for Council of States elections;
  • Legislative competence: Arts. 84, 327 + Entry 72 Union List (elections to Parliament).

Decision

ALL WRIT PETITIONS DISMISSED — both amendments upheld. Key reasoning:

  1. Residence was never a constitutional requirement. Legislative history (GI Acts 1915/1919/1935; First Draft Constitution para 6 of Part I of Fourth Schedule — deleted by the Drafting Committee on 11-2-1948) shows residence was considered and consciously dropped; “residence was never the constitutional requirement… never treated as an essential ingredient of the structure of the Council of States.”
  2. On federalism: “The nature of Federalism in Indian Constitution is no longer res integra”; Indian model broadly federal; Ambedkar’s CA test (partition of legislative/executive authority by the Constitution) is satisfied; “federalism under Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong centre”; features like Art. 3, single citizenship, Art. 249, Art. 356 militate against “strong federalism”. Since domicile is not an essential attribute, its deletion does not damage federalism. “The principle of federalism is not territory related” — the Rajya Sabha still represents States through their elected Assemblies under Art. 80(4).
  3. On basic structure vs statute: the Court examined whether basic-structure review is available against ordinary laws — and held the challenge fails on merits anyway: the law is within legislative competence, doesn’t violate any constitutional provision or Part III.
  4. Open ballot upheld: Rajya Sabha members are elected by MLAs under proportional representation by single transferable vote (Art. 80(4)) — an indirect, party-based election, unlike constituency-based direct elections where secrecy is the basis. “In constituency-based representation, ‘secrecy’ is the basis whereas in… proportional representation… the basis can be ‘open ballot’ and it would not violate… ‘free and fair elections’.” Cross-voting by party-sponsored MLAs distorts proportionality; Parliament may regulate to purify elections. Every vote inside the House is by open ballot anyway (division, Speakers’ elections).
  5. Secrecy is not absolute: “The principle of secrecy is not an absolute principle… The higher principle, however, is free and fair elections and purity of elections. If secrecy becomes a source for corruption then sunlight and transparency have the capacity to remove it.” Quoting Indira Gandhi: democracy is not dogmatic; adjustments that leave not “the mere husk of a popular rule” are permissible — “multi-party democracy is a necessary part of the basic structure.”
  6. Art. 19(1)(a) argument rejected: even assuming voting is expression, open ballot is a reasonable restriction under Art. 19(2) (purity of elections, corruption, cross-voting); an MLA faces no disqualification for how he votes — at most party action, which is legitimate party discipline.
  7. Competence: amendments made under Art. 246 read with Arts. 84 & 327 and Entry 72, Union List; no Constitutional provision infringed.
Principles evolved
  1. Federalism is a basic feature, but not every federal embellishment is sacrosanct — the core is the constitutional partition of powers, not domicile of representatives;
  2. Rajya Sabha = federal representation without territorial residence — federal principle “not territory-related” in India’s scheme;
  3. Indian federalism leans to a strong Centre — a restatement (with Bommai) examiners expect verbatim;
  4. Secreecy of ballot is qualified, not absolute; in indirect (list/proportional) elections, open ballot is permissible to ensure purity & party discipline;
  5. Basic structure & multi-party democracy constrain legislation — laws must not destroy democratic fairness; but courts won’t strike down merely because “another view is possible”;
  6. Statutory line: RP (Amendment) Act 2003 — s. 3 (domicile), ss. 59/94/128 (open ballot) upheld.

Related instruments: Representation of the People Act, 1950 (ss. 17–19), RP Act, 1951 (ss. 3, 4, 59, 94, 128) & RP (Amendment) Act, 2003; Arts. 79, 80, 84, 324–327; Fourth Schedule; Entry 72 List I; Art. 19(1)(a)/(2); related: Defection law (Tenth Schedule, 52nd Amendment Act, 1985) — party-discipline rationale discussed by the Court (with South African and Indian party-discipline authorities). Real example: the 2020–21 cross-voting controversies in Rajya Sabha elections (e.g., Maharashtra/Delhi MLAs’ votes) and the “open ballot” proviso are directly governed by this judgment.

Section 8Cooperative and Competitive Federalism

“The States… are neither satellites nor agents of the Centre.” — Sawant J., S.R. Bommai · Cooperative federalism = governance by collaboration; Competitive federalism = governance by race-to-the-top.

8.1 Cooperative Federalism

Meaning: A system in which the Union and the States work as partners in a common enterprise — sharing information, decisions, finances and responsibility for outcomes, with institutions that institutionalise consultation instead of command. It is the practical working of the “co-ordinating” federalism Sawant J. described: emergencies aside, “the exceptions are not a rule.”

8.2 Competitive Federalism

Meaning: States are treated as competitors in a marketplace of governance — ranking, rewarding and benchmarking induces them to improve policy. Mechanisms:

  • Conditional grants & rankings: Smart City Mission, Aspirational Districts Programme (NITI Aayog), Swachh Bharat rankings, PMAY-Gramin performance grants (evidence-based incentives);
  • Vertical-horizontal devolution competition: Finance Commission awards reward good governance/population need;
  • Policy experimentation: “laboratories of democracy” — e.g., Kerala’s health model, Gujarat industrial policy, Delhi education model, Goa/Tripura startup policies being copied across States;
  • Judicial endorsement: the phrase was applied in Y.S. Jagan Mohan Reddy v. NTPC (2019) — “competitive federalism” as a legitimate operating principle (States competing for investment while the Union sets the floor of fair competition); also UOK v. Mohit Minerals (2022) — “cooperative federalism has to be fostering… not binding”.
  • Caution — competition must not become conflict: interstate water disputes (Cauvery, SYL — Case 6), “freight/operator” policy clashes, and uncooperative tax competition invite Art. 131/262 intervention.

Case 6 of 7 · DU Case Material (Topic 1)

State of Haryana v. State of Punjab & Anr.

(2002) 2 SCC 507 · AIR 2002 SC 685 · Decided 15 January 2002 · Bench: G.B. Pattanaik & Ruma Pal, JJ. (Constitution Bench) · Suit under Art. 131 · SYL Canal · Cooperative federalism in inter-State water relations

Facts

  • 1960 Indus Waters Treaty allocated the three Eastern rivers (Sutlej, Beas, Ravi) to India; post-partition, allocation of Ravi-Beas waters among Punjab, Haryana & Rajasthan was settled by Centre’s 1955 decision and ad-hoc 1966 decision (35% Haryana : 65% Punjab).
  • Haryana (not a riparian State for the relevant drawal point) needed a carrier; s. 78, Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966 governed successor States’ water rights/liabilities; Government of India Order 24-3-1976 allocated 3.50 MAF of Ravi-Beas surplus water to Haryana.
  • 31-12-1981 Agreement (Punjab Settlement): net surplus 17.17 MAF — Punjab 4.22, Haryana 3.50, Rajasthan 8.60, Delhi 0.20, J&K 0.65 MAF; construction of the Sutlej-Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal — 214 km (122 km in Punjab, 92 km in Haryana), design capacity 6500 cusecs, to be completed within 2 years; both sides were to withdraw their suits. Para 9.3 of the 1985 Punjab Settlement also dealt with sharing.
  • Punjab stalled construction of its 122-km leg (over ₹600 crore spent by Punjab, ₹250 crore by Haryana; Centre gave ₹499.12 crore to Punjab till 3-1994). The Ravi-Beas Tribunal’s interim report (30-1-1987) called the SYL “the lifeline for the farmers of Haryana”.
  • After Punjab’s White Paper declaring it would not dig the canal, Haryana filed a suit under Art. 131 (2001) against Punjab & the Union seeking a decree that the agreements bind Punjab and a mandatory injunction to complete the canal.

Issues

Q1. Is the Supreme Court’s Art. 131 jurisdiction ousted by Art. 262 read with s. 11 of the Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956 (matter should go to the Ravi-Beas Tribunal)?
Q2. Is the dispute a “water dispute” under s. 2(c) ISWDA (use, distribution or control of inter-state waters) — or a dispute about performance of an agreement to construct a canal?
Q3. Are the 1981 Agreement & para 9.3 of the Punjab Settlement binding on successive governments of Punjab? Is limitation applicable?
Q4. Should a mandatory injunction issue against Punjab and the Union?

Arguments

Plaintiff — State of Haryana (Shri Bobde; Dr Dhawan):
  • The matter is not a “water dispute” — it centres on Punjab’s obligation to construct the canal portion within its territory so Haryana can draw water already allocated; hence Art. 262/s. 11 do not bar Art. 131;
  • Allocation made under s. 78 of the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966 + 1976 Order + 1981 Agreement + para 9.3 Settlement = vested entitlement; SYL is the “lifeline”;
  • Denial of allocated water affects citizens’ Art. 21; the Union has failed its constitutional obligation to persuade/direct Punjab;
  • Agreements negotiated with central oversight bind successor governments (no “political philosophy” involved).
Defendant 1 — State of Punjab:
  • Combined reading of s. 11 ISWDA + Art. 262 ousts this Court’s jurisdiction; only the Ravi-Beas Tribunal may decide (its s. 5(3) application pending → tribunal’s findings not final);
  • The suit is barred by limitation and by Order 23 Rule 1 CPC (earlier suit withdrawn) / Order 2 Rule XXXII SC Rules;
  • Para 9.3 was a “unilateral concession” by Sant Harchand Singh Longowal — never a binding legal obligation; Punjab Settlement “a piece of paper without any sanctity”;
  • Haryana already gets 1.62 MAF via Bhakra Main Line/Narwana Branch — no SYL needed; 3.50 MAF claim would deprive Punjab’s irrigated acreage; Ravi water not covered by Beas Project schemes; s. 78 does not mention River Ravi;
  • Haryana has no legal right to invoke Art. 131.
Defendant 2 — Union of India: no obligation on the Union; it has already discharged its role by persuading Punjab and funding the canal; relief, if any, can only be against Punjab.

Decision

  1. Jurisdiction upheld: Art. 131 suit maintainable. Interpreting s. 2(c) ISWDA, a “water dispute” = disagreement about the use, distribution or control of inter-state waters or interpretation of agreements relating thereto — the present averments concern construction/obligation to complete the canal (a carrier for water already allocated), not use/distribution. Hence Art. 262 and s. 11 do not bar the Court.
  2. Agreements binding; apprehension that Haryana would over-draw unfounded (water can flow only if Punjab opens head-regulators; drawal is from the allocated share). The Tribunal’s observation that SYL is Haryana’s “lifeline” accepted.
  3. Mandatory injunction granted: Punjab directed to complete the remaining portion of the SYL Canal within one year; the Union directed to discharge its constitutional obligation and, if Punjab failed, get it executed through its own agencies.
  4. Successor-government doctrine & anti “vote-bank” observations: “The decision taken at the governmental level should not be so easily nullified by a change of Government… particularly when such a decision affects some other State and the interest of the nation as a whole.” When “political authority becomes dogmatic, unreasonable and… irresponsible… that is when the Court has to step in and represent the will of people.”
  5. Limitation/withdrawal objections rejected (Punjab did not press limitation hard).
Principles evolved
  1. Art. 131 vs Art. 262 delineation: water-allocation merits → tribunal; constitutional/contractual compliance issues → Supreme Court. Article 262 bars adjudication of allocation disputes, not suits for performance of inter-State obligations.
  2. Cooperative federalism is enforceable: States must honour bargains struck with each other under central auspices — unilateral termination/stalling offends the federal principle (later: the Punjab Termination of Water Agreements Act, 2004 was struck down by a 5-Judge Bench — Punjab State v. Haryana, (2004) 12 SCC 736 — and the 2004 Presidential Reference upheld that reading).
  3. Continuity of governance: inter-State agreements survive changes of party in power; vote-bank politics cannot undo inter-State entitlements.
  4. Union’s positive duty: Art. 355-type obligation to ensure compliance of constitutional arrangements (Centre directed to execute if the defaulting State fails).

PYQ 1 · LL.B. Centenary Chance Examinations · Constitutional Law-I (LB-301) · Q4 · 20 marks
“What are the core features of a Federal Constitution? Considering these features, examine whether or not the Indian Constitution can be described as a federal Constitution.” (The original paper also carried the struck-through continuation: “Indicate the distinct features of the Constitution of India which show that it is not a federal Constitution in the real sense but point towards semi-federal Constitution and judicial interpretation on the federalism” — answer it anyway; it is the natural third limb.)
Introduction

Federalism is “a political contrivance for a body of States which desire Union but not unity” (Dicey, cited by Ahmadi J. in S.R. Bommai, DU material). Its essence is the distribution of powers between the Union and the units demarcated by the Constitution itself (Ambedkar, CA). Whether India “is federal” has been the most enduring label-question of Indian constitutional law — settled not by semantics but by practical operation (Bommai: “what is there in a name”).

I. Core features of a federal Constitution
  1. Compact between independent, sovereign units surrendering part of their sovereignty to a Union while retaining the residue;
  2. Supremacy of a written Constitution — alterable only by a rigid/special procedure, not by ordinary legislation;
  3. Distribution of legislative, executive & financial powers between Centre and units (enumerated lists); each level “coordinate and independent” in its sphere;
  4. Supreme authority of an independent judiciary to interpret the Constitution and invalidate unconstitutional action (judicial review; conflict-resolution mechanism);
  5. Dual citizenship, independent unit existence (indestructible States), residuary powers generally with units (USA, Australia), rigid amendment needing unit ratification;
  6. Supremacy of the Constitution over all organs; existence of a federal legislature (often upper house representing units).

(These are the (a)–(d) characteristics in State of West Bengal v. Union of India paras 26 + Wheare’s “coordinate independent authorities” test quoted in Bommai para 169.)

II. Applying the features to India — “Can India be called federal?”

(A) Features India satisfies:

  • Written supreme Constitution: Art. 13 + judicial review + rigid Art. 368 (special majority + ratification by ≥½ States for federal provisions) — characteristic (d) present “in full force” (State of W.B.);
  • Distribution of powers: Arts. 245–255 + Seventh Schedule (Union 97, State 66, Concurrent 47 entries); Arts. 73 & 162 co-extensive executive powers; exclusive taxing spheres; residuary to Centre (Art. 248) — though residuary placement is “irrelevant to federal character” (Seervai (c) in Bommai);
  • Independent integrated judiciary with power to invalidate laws (Arts. 13, 32, 226), settle Centre–State disputes (Art. 131), advisory jurisdiction (Art. 143);
  • Federal institutions: Rajya Sabha representing States (Art. 80), President elected by elected MLAs & MPs (Arts. 54), Inter-State Council (Art. 263), Finance Commission (Art. 280), division of taxation (Arts. 268–273);
  • Sawant J.’s ten-point answer in Bommai (a)–(k): non-sovereign origin is no objection; emergencies exist even in “indisputably federal” countries; directions to States ≈ US enforcement powers; Art. 356 ≈ US Const. Art. IV §4 — “the federal principle is dominant… and has not been watered down” (Seervai).

(B) Features that make it “not strictly/fully federal” — the semi-federal or quasi-federal tilt:

  • No compact: States were not sovereign — created by Art. 1; Parliament may form/alter/destroy States unilaterally (Arts. 2–3; only “views” of State legislatures, no concurrence) — State of W.B. characteristic (a) “absent”;
  • Supremacy alterable by Union Parliament alone (characteristic (b) “absent”);
  • Single citizenship (Arts. 5–11), one integrated judiciary, absence of the words “federal/federation” in the text;
  • Residuary power with Centre (Art. 248 + Entry 97); important subjects concentrated in List I; concurrent legislation Parliament prevails (Art. 254);
  • Union’s overriding levers: Art. 249 (national interest), 250 (emergency), 252, 253, 256–258, 365, 352–360; Arts. 355–356 (“both a responsibility and a power” — Jeevan Reddy J.); financial dependence (Arts. 268–281, 275, 282);
  • Beg C.J. in State of Rajasthan v. UOI: “whatever appearance of a federal structure… its operations are… more unitary than federal”; “amphibian” Union; Ahmadi J. in Bommai: “more appropriately described as ‘quasi-federal’… mixture… leaning more towards the latter”; Palekar J. in Kesavananda: “broadly speaking, a quasi-federal constitution”; Untwalia J. in State of Karnataka v. UOI (1978): quasi-federal.
III. Judicial interpretation — the modern synthesis
  • State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963): India is “not true to any traditional pattern of federation”; political sovereignty distributed “with greater weightage in favour of the Union”; but distribution of powers + supreme courts remain;
  • Ram Jawaya Kapur (1955): India’s Constitution is “federal in its structure” but modelled on the British parliamentary system — the first authoritative description;
  • S.R. Bommai (1994): constitutional balance — federalism is a basic feature; States are “neither satellites nor agents of the Centre”; within their sphere “States are supreme” (Jeevan Reddy J.); nevertheless Centre-bias is real;
  • Kuldip Nayar (2006): “federal principle is dominant… one of its basic features… but federalism under Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong centre”;
  • Later: State (NCT of Delhi) v. UOI (2023), Mohit Minerals (2022) — cooperative federalism; Art. 370 judgment (2023): “quasi-federal, asymmetric federalism or cooperative federalism… federal elements are a cornerstone of the polity”.
Conclusion

India can be described as a federal Constitution — federal in structure, distribution of powers and judicially protected spheres — but not in the strict American sense: it is a quasi-federal/semi-federal system with a unitary bias, designed for national integration (“a strong Centre was essential to ward off separatist tendencies” — Ahmadi J.). The label, however, changes nothing in practice: what matters is that the President’s Art. 356 power (and every Union power) is constrained by the federal basic structure (Bommai). Hence the correct exam formula: “Federal in form, quasi-federal in operation, with a strong Centre; federalism being a basic feature that courts will enforce.”

Key citations to drop: Arts. 1–4, 73, 162, 245–255, 248, 356, 368; Seventh Schedule; State of W.B. v. UOI AIR 1963 SC 1241; Ram Jawaya Kapur AIR 1955 SC 549; S.R. Bommai 1994 (3) SCC 1; Kuldip Nayar 2006 (7) SCC 1; State of Rajasthan v. UOI AIR 1977 SC 1361; Seervai; Wheare.

PYQ 2 · LL.B. Centenary Chance Examinations · Constitutional Law-I (LB-301) · Q7 · 20 marks
“Elaborate the safeguards against the misuse of Article 356 of the Constitution of India with the help of judicial pronouncements.”
Introduction

Article 356 empowers the President to proclaim that “the Government of [a State] cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution” on the Governor’s report. Because this “emergency of State breakdown” was historically abused (100+ impositions between 1950–1994), the Supreme Court in S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) 3 SCC 1 (9 Judges) built the principal safeguard structure. Write the answer as: (i) textual safeguards → (ii) Bommai safeguards → (iii) post-Bommai refinements → (iv) Sarkaria/Punchhi guidelines.

I. Textual / structural safeguards in the Constitution
  1. Art. 355: Union’s duty is to protect every State — Art. 356 is an exception to normal federal functioning, to be used only when protection/constitutional machinery genuinely fails;
  2. Art. 356(3): the Proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months; failure ⇒ it ceases to operate — a built-in legislative veto (Bommai: on lapse, the dismissed government revives and the dissolved House is restored);
  3. Art. 356(2): the Proclamation “may be revoked or varied by a subsequent Proclamation” — the President can withdraw President’s Rule at any time without Parliament;
  4. Art. 356(4): once approved, the Proclamation runs in six-month cycles — each continuance needs a fresh resolution of both Houses, and it cannot remain in force for more than three years (absolute ceiling);
  5. Art. 357: exercise of Union legislative power after Proclamation is subject to the Constitution — law-making must remain constitutional;
  6. The deleted shield (important!): the 38th Amendment (1975) had inserted an Art. 356(5) making the President’s satisfaction “final and conclusive” and not justiciable “on any ground” — the 44th Amendment (1978) deleted it; hence S.R. Bommai could hold satisfaction reviewable (and State of Rajasthan’s reliance on that clause no longer holds good);
  7. Art. 74(2): does NOT bar scrutiny of whether material existed (Bommai); proviso to Art. 356(1): High Courts are fully shielded — the President cannot assume their powers or suspend constitutional provisions relating to them; and under Art. 356(1)(c) the machinery of the State is merely suspended, not destroyed — the State’s Constitution and laws continue until Parliament alters them;
  8. Art. 163: Governor’s discretionary functions are limited — the Governor cannot act as an agent of the Centre at whim (read with B.P. Singhal, 2010 on Art. 166 justiciability);
  9. Federal basic structure itself (Kesavananda/Bommai) — any Art. 356 action damaging federalism is justiciable.
II. The Bommai safeguards (the heart of the answer)
  1. Judicial review of the Proclamation: courts examine (a) whether issued on any material; (b) whether material is relevant and real; (c) whether issued in mala fide or for an improper purpose; (d) whether power used for the object for which conferred — “the judicial review… is not only an imperative necessity but a stringent duty” (Sawant J. para 106);
  2. Floor test is decisive: where the CM’s majority is questioned, the Assembly must be allowed to decide — Karnataka (1989) proclamation struck down for ignoring Bommai’s floor-test demand (defection letters + retraction letters cannot substitute for the floor);
  3. Post-decisional approval doctrine: Parliament’s approval within 2 months validates the proclamation ex post; if disallowed/lapsed, the dismissed government revives;
  4. No irreversible dissolution without approval: dissolving the Assembly before parliamentary sanction is invalid; if struck down, the Assembly stands restored (or fresh elections ordered) — otherwise restoration is rendered impossible by executive action;
  5. Art. 74(2) is not an absolute shield: courts can look at whether any relevant material existed (advice’s contents are protected, existence of material is not);
  6. Purpose limitation: Art. 356 is for genuine “failure of constitutional machinery” — not political vendetta; “exceptions are not a rule”; secularism & federalism being basic features, anti-secular governance CAN be a legitimate ground (MP, Rajasthan, HP 1992 upheld), but every case needs its own material;
  7. Secularism check: a State government acting against constitutional secularism invites Art. 356 — but courts test the evidence (Nagaland, Meghalaya proclamations struck down).
III. Post-Bommai refinements & other pronouncements
  • Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006): Governor cannot act on mala fide defecting MLAs’ letters; disqualification/defection route preferred over Art. 356; dissolution struck down where no material; Sachar Committee-era limits reinforced;
  • B.P. Singhal v. UOI (2010): Governor’s dismissal of a Minister/Art. 166 actions are justiciable on ordinary administrative-law standards (mala fide, no material, irrationality);
  • Navalshankar Dave (1997): pre-consultation with CM is essential before report — failure to hear is material irregularity;
  • State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977): Court refused interim relief against a Art. 356 proclamation but affirmed reviewability in principle — Bommai corrected its hands-off stance;
  • Sarkaria Commission (1987) & Punchhi Commission (2010) guidelines: Art. 356 as “last resort”, try all alternatives (president’s rule under Art. 356 only after Art. 365 failure, reconvene House, seek other majorities, etc.); “collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers”… (quote 2–3 guidelines);
  • Contemporary application: floor tests ordered within 48 hours in Karnataka (2018) and Maharashtra (2019) — Bommai made the floor the first and last word.
Conclusion

The safeguards are a tripartite cage: constitutional timing limits (Arts. 356(2)–(4): 2-month approval, 6-month cycles, 3-year ceiling, presidential revocation), federal purpose limits (failure of machinery only; High Courts shielded; secularism/federalism as basic features) and judicial process limits (material, mala fide, floor test, no pre-approval dissolution). Together they ensure that President’s Rule remains an exception of “last resort” and never an instrument of political convenience.

Citations: Arts. 352, 355, 356, 357, 74(2), 163, 174, 175; S.R. Bommai 1994 (3) SCC 1 (paras 97–106, 165–174, 274–276); Rameshwar Prasad (2006) 2 SCC 1; B.P. Singhal (2010) 6 SCC 331; Navalshankar (1997) 1 SCC 244; Sarkaria Commission Report (1987).

PYQ 3 · LL.B. III Semester Examination, December 2024 · LB-301 · Q2 · 20 marks (problem question)
The Republic of Dakshin is a federal polity whose Constitution imbues the principle of asymmetric federalism; the State of Kailash holds special privileges. After a change of government at the Centre, the ruling party revoked Kailash’s special privilege by constitutional amendment; protests followed; the President then issued an Article 356 Proclamation citing failure of constitutional machinery. Kailash challenges it. “In light of the aforementioned facts, argue on behalf of the State of Kailash. Your arguments must discuss the jurisprudence of federalism, Article 356, as well as the extent and limits of judicial review.” (Law in pari materia with India.)
How to structure (20-mark problem — issue-wise arguments)

Frame three issues: (I) Validity of revocation of asymmetric privilege (federalism jurisprudence); (II) Validity of the Art. 356 Proclamation; (III) Extent & limits of judicial review.

Issue I — Federalism & asymmetric privileges (for Kailash)
  • Federalism is a basic feature — S.R. Bommai (Sawant J.: “States… neither satellites nor agents”; Ramaswami J.: “Federalism envisaged… is a basic feature”); Kuldip Nayar: “federal principle is dominant… one of its basic features”;
  • Asymmetric federalism is part of the basic structure — Art. 370 judgment (2023): “Asymmetrical federalism, that is differential rights to certain federal sub-units is a part of the Indian federal scheme. It is a part of the basic structure, as is federalism”; Arts. 371A–371J show differential treatment is a constitutional norm, “difference… one of degree and not of kind”;
  • So the special privilege, once constitutionally granted, cannot be destroyed by an amendment that damages the federal balance / the basic identity of the State — argue Kesavananda limit (amend ≠ destroy), Minerva Mills (balance between Arts. 14/19/31? — use “harmony and balance between fundamental rights and DPSPs and between Centre and States is integral to basic structure”);
  • Caveat (be balanced): the Union’s textual powers (Arts. 2–4 analogue; Art. 368) are wide — counter-argument is Kuldip Nayar: “federalism leans in favour of a strong centre”. Argue that the revocation itself may be within Art. 368 but the resulting action (Proclamation) is ultra vires if motivated by the protest & political rivalry rather than genuine breakdown.
Issue II — The Article 356 Proclamation (for Kailash)
  • Grounds are insufficient: non-violent protests ≠ failure of constitutional machinery; the State government continues to function (no refusal to carry on governance in accordance with the Constitution);
  • Bommai test: (a) material must exist; (b) material relevant & real; (c) no mala fide/improper purpose — here the purpose appears to be political retaliation against an opposition-ruled State for Centre’s own amendment → classic misuse;
  • Floor test: if majority of the House is in question, the Assembly must be allowed to test confidence — Governor cannot report based on protests/letters (Bommai, Karnataka);
  • Art. 74(2) is no bar to examining existence of material; Art. 356(3) gives Parliament only 2 months; dissolution before approval invalid (Bommai);
  • Secularism/federalism/quasi-federal synthesis: Beg C.J.’s “amphibian” observation cuts both ways — even a “quasi-federal” constitution demands that the unitary slide be justified by genuine crisis (Ahmadi J.: label aside, “the thrust and implications… bearing on… Article 356” matter);
  • Rameshwar Prasad: no proclamation where the challenge is political disaffection of the ruling party; Navalshankar Dave: prior consultation with CM is mandatory; State of Karnataka v. UOI (1978)? — cite Art. 355 duty framing;
  • Relief: quash Proclamation, restore House if dissolved, direct floor test if majority genuinely questioned (Karnataka 2018 model).
Issue III — Extent and limits of judicial review
  • Extent: reviewable on (i) existence of material, (ii) relevance, (iii) mala fides/purpose, (iv) constitutional process (consultation, post-decisional approval), (v) reasonableness — plus FRs of citizens/MLAs (Arts. 14, 19) if the Assembly is dissolved;
  • Limits: courts will not sit in appeal over policy; they will not inquire into the content of aid & advice (Art. 74(2)); if Parliament approves under Art. 356(3) and the object is achieved, relief is prospective/calibrated; “political question” doctrine rejected in India (Bommai) but self-restraint remains;
  • Basic structure as the master-limit: any amendment or executive action destroying federalism/judicial review/democracy is void (Kesavananda; L. Chandra Kumar — Arts. 32/226 are basic features, courts can strike down the Proclamation’s consequences).
Conclusion (counsel’s prayer)

The Proclamation be quashed as issued without relevant material and for an improper purpose, offending the federal basic structure; the House be restored or a floor test directed within 48 hours; and Parliament be advised that Art. 356 remains “the last resort” (Sarkaria), judicial review being “a stringent duty” (Sawant J., Bommai para 106).

PYQ 4 · LL.B. III Semester Examination, December 2024 · LB-301 · Q6(b) · 10 marks (short note)
Write a short note on: Theory of Separation of Powers.
Introduction

Theory that governmental functions — legislative, executive, judicial — must be exercised by three distinct organs, each checking the others, to prevent tyranny. Origin: Aristotle (mixed constitution) → John Locke (executive/federative vs legislative) → Montesquieu, L’Esprit des Lois (1748): “when the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person… there can be no liberty.” Class-note anchor: Montesquieu = “legislate, execute, judicial”.

Models
  • Absolute/strict separation — USA (federal separation with checks & balances; impeachment; judicial review; fixed tenures);
  • Fusion of powers — UK: “Parliamentary sovereignty… legislative, executive and judicial power all reside in the Queen in Parliament” (Regina’s case, cited in Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy reading); cabinet is “a hyphen… a buckle” (Ram Jawaya Kapur);
  • Indian model — functional, not rigid: Ram Jawaya Kapur para 12: “The Indian Constitution has not… recognised the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity but the functions of the different parts… have been sufficiently differentiated… our Constitution does not contemplate assumption, by one organ… of functions that essentially belong to another.”
Constitutional expression in India
  • Arts. 53, 73, 154, 162 (executive), Arts. 107–222 (legislature), Arts. 124–231 (judiciary); Art. 50 (DPSP — separation of judiciary from executive); independence of judges (Arts. 124(4), 217 — removal by address); legislative privilege (Arts. 105, 194); quasi-judicial functions delegated to executive (upheld in Ram Jawaya Kapur & later cases); Art. 142’s unique blending power (SC can do “complete justice”); ordinances (Arts. 123, 213) = executive law-making with legislative check.
  • Checks & balances (class-note map): Arts. 124, 217, 222 vs Arts. 61, 157; Art. 74(2)/163; immunity Arts. 361, 105, 194; writs Arts. 32, 226, 227.
Judicial pronouncements
  • Ram Jawaya Kapur (1955): executive need not await legislation; residue theory; but no organ may go “against the provisions of the Constitution or of any law” (Art. 154);
  • Kesavananda (Sikri C.J.): “separation of powers between the legislature, the executive and the judiciary” is part of the basic structure; a “genuinely independent” judiciary is integral;
  • Indra Sawhney, L. Chandra Kumar: judicial review (the judiciary’s check) is basic structure — Arts. 32/226 cannot be ousted;
  • M.P. Spl. Police Estab. v. M.P. (2004): legitimacy of delegated legislation; S.P. Gupta: “consultation” vs “concurrence” for judges — separation in appointments.
Conclusion

India follows “soft separation with checks and balances” — overlapping but not interchangeable functions, fused executive-legislature (parliamentary system), independent judiciary — the differentiation being fine enough to be a basic structural feature while flexible enough to permit delegated/subordinate legislation and limited judicial functions in the executive (Ram Jawaya Kapur).
