Section 0 · OrientationWhat this Topic Covers & How Examiners Test It

The Constitution lets the executive legislate — but only provisionally, only in a recess, and only on sufferance of the legislature. This Topic is the story of that leash: where it is drawn (Arts 123 & 213), what happens when the executive pulls it too hard (Wadhwa), how the courts got their grip on it (A.K. Roy), and what happens when the same ordinance is promulgated over and over forever (Krishna Kumar Singh).

🎯 The five propositions that answer 80% of Topic 5 questions

  1. Ordinance = legislation, not executive action. It is a law-making power of a limited, conditional, provisional kind — exercised in the recess of the Houses on the executive's subjective satisfaction of "immediate action" (A.K. Roy; DU material: "no distinction in principle between a law made by the legislature and an ordinance … both equally subject to constitutional limitations").
  2. It has the "same force and effect as if enacted" by the legislature (Art 123(2) / 213(2)) — therefore it is equally a "law" under Article 13(3)(a) and equally subject to fundamental rights (A.K. Roy).
  3. It is a lease, not a freehold. Maximum natural life ≈ 7½ months: six weeks from re-assembly + a fresh session that must be summoned within ~3 months (Arts 85/174) + six weeks again (Wadhwa, DU para: "emergency power of limited life").
  4. Re-promulgation is the constitutional fault-line. Serially re-promulgating the same ordinance to keep a law alive without facing the legislature is a colourable exercise of power / fraud on the Constitution (Wadhwa); when done mechanically, without fresh satisfaction, it is "fundamentally at odds with the scheme of Arts 123/213" and the ordinances are bereft of legal effects (Krishna Kumar Singh).
  5. On lapse: no automatic blanket revival AND no automatic obliteration. An ordinance that ceases to operate is not void ab initio; what survives is decided by construction + public interest & constitutional necessity (and irreversibility), with power to mould relief (Krishna Kumar Singh). But an ordinance that is void under Art 123(3)/213(3) (or unconstitutional under Art 13) was void from the start.

Section 1 · The InstrumentThe Ordinance Power — Articles 123 & 213 Side by Side

An ordinance is a formal, general legislative instrument promulgated by the executive head (President at the Union, Governor in a State) when the legislature is not in session, which has the force of an Act. It is the Constitution's emergency valve for legislation — used when circumstances "render it necessary … to take immediate action" and there is no time to wait for a Bill.

1.1 Article 123 — Ordinances promulgated by the President (verbatim)

123. Power of President to promulgate Ordinances during recess of Parliament.— (1) If at any time, except when both Houses of Parliament are in session, the President is satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action, he may promulgate such Ordinances as the circumstances appear to him to require. (2) An Ordinance promulgated under this article shall have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, but every such Ordinance— (a) shall be laid before both Houses of Parliament and shall cease to operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament, or, if before the expiration of that period resolutions disapproving it are passed by both Houses, upon the passing of the second of those resolutions; and (b) may be withdrawn at any time by the President. Explanation.—Where the Houses of Parliament are summoned to reassemble on different dates, the period of six weeks shall be reckoned from the later of those dates for the purposes of this clause. (3) If and so far as an Ordinance under this article makes any provision which Parliament would not under this Constitution be competent to enact, it shall be void. (4)* * * * *Text: Constitution of India (Part V, Chapter III — Legislative Powers of the President). Note: Article 123 has no proviso — the only bar in clause (1) is the session bar; and clause (4) survives only as "* * * * *" — the old clause making the President's satisfaction "final and conclusive and … not … questioned in any court", expressly deleted by s. 16 of the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978 (DU material).
Session bar — the clean reading (a favourite exam trap):
  • Art 123(1): the President may promulgate "at any time, except when both Houses of Parliament are in session". So the power is available in a recess of Parliament — when at least one House is not sitting (including when the Lok Sabha is dissolved and only the Rajya Sabha sits — a classic fact-pattern). An ordinance promulgated while both Houses are in session falls outside the power altogether.
  • Art 213(1): unavailable when the Legislative Assembly is in session (unicameral), or when both Houses of the State Legislature are in session (bicameral State). A bicameral State's Council sitting alone does not bar promulgation.
  • Art 123 has no proviso; Art 213 has one — the Governor needs presidential instructions for ordinances in the three President-connected cases (a)–(c). The design logic: the Governor cannot do by ordinance what he could not do by Bill without engaging the President (Arts 197/200-type screens).
  • Never write: "an ordinance may be promulgated only when either House is not in session" or invent a Union proviso — both are standard answer-killers.

1.2 Article 213 — Ordinances promulgated by the Governor

213. Power of Governor to promulgate Ordinances during recess of Legislature.— (1) If at any time, except when the Legislative Assembly of a State is in session, or where there is a Legislative Council in a State, except when both Houses of the Legislature are in session, the Governor is satisfied that circumstances exist which render it necessary for him to take immediate action, he may promulgate such Ordinances as the circumstances appear to him to require: Provided that the Governor shall not, without instructions from the President, promulgate any such Ordinance if— (a) a Bill containing the same provisions would under this Constitution have required the previous sanction of the President for the introduction thereof into the Legislature; or (b) he would have deemed it necessary to reserve a Bill containing the same provisions for the consideration of the President; or (c) an Act of the Legislature of the State containing the same provisions would under this Constitution have been invalid unless, having been reserved for the consideration of the President, it had received the assent of the President. (2) An Ordinance promulgated under this article shall have the same force and effect as an Act of the Legislature of the State assented to by the Governor, but every such Ordinance— (a) shall be laid before the Legislative Assembly of the State, or where there is a Legislative Council in the State, before both the Houses, and shall cease to operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of the Legislature, or if before the expiration of that period a resolution disapproving it is passed by the Legislative Assembly and agreed to by the Legislative Council, if any, upon the passing of the resolution or, as the case may be, on the resolution being agreed to by the Council; and (b) may be withdrawn at any time by the Governor. Explanation.—Where the Houses of the Legislature of a State having a Legislative Council are summoned to reassemble on different dates, the period of six weeks shall be reckoned from the later of those dates for the purposes of this clause. (3) If and so far as an Ordinance under this article makes any provision which would not be valid if enacted in an Act of the Legislature of the State assented to by the Governor, it shall be void: Provided that, for the purposes of repugnancy under the Concurrent List, an Ordinance promulgated under this article in pursuance of instructions from the President shall be deemed to be an Act of the Legislature of the State which has been reserved for the consideration of the President and assented to by him. (4)* * * * *Text: Constitution of India (Part VI, Chapter IV — Legislative Power of the Governor). Three exam-relevant features: (i) the session bar distinguishes unicameral/bicameral States; (ii) the proviso is not an absolute ban — the Governor may promulgate with presidential instructions in cases (a)–(c), protecting the Art 197/200 assent-and-reservation channels; (iii) clause (4) is deleted ("* * * * *") — the DU material records that "by a similar amendment, clause 4 was introduced into Article 213 … expressly deleted by Section 16 of the Forty-fourth Amendment".

🔑 The three-move grammar common to both articles

  1. Entry condition — recess + satisfaction of necessity for immediate action (Arts 123(1)/213(1)).
  2. Life-cycle condition — same force and effect as an Act; laid before the House(s); ceases after six weeks from re-assembly, or earlier on a disapproval resolution (Arts 123(2)/213(2)(a)); or withdrawn at will (123(2)(b)/213(2)(b)).
  3. Competence condition — void if it legislates on a subject needing the President's assent without it (Arts 123(3)/213(3)); screened by the provisos.

1.3 Nature of the power — legislative, not executive

A.K. Roy (syllabus case 32) — settled law

  • The ordinance power is legislative in nature — the executive makes law (general, binding, prospective) though the instrument is issued by the executive.
  • It is not confined to "virgin land" — Parliament may amend, repeal or alter existing laws by ordinance (para 30 of the judgment; cf. R.K. Garg v. UOI, cited in the DU material: ordinances may amend tax laws).
  • No American strict separation of powers in India (para 23): the Constitution blends the streams — Parliament itself delegates rule-making to the executive throughout the statute-book, and the executive legislates by ordinance in recess.
  • DU quotation of its ratio (reproduced in the Krishna Kumar material): "no distinction in principle between a law made by the legislature and an ordinance … both equally subject to constitutional limitations … [for] extraordinary situations and not [to] be perverted to serve political ends."

Why "legislative" matters doctrinally

  • Art 13 applies — an ordinance is a "law" (13(3)(a)); it can take away fundamental rights only within the limits of 13(2) (see §3).
  • Political control substitutes for legislative control — the classic check is not prior consent but (i) the six-week disapproval mechanism, (ii) the confidence/no-confidence power, (iii) the Governor's/President's veto points — Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly speech on legislative control over ordinances is quoted in the DU material (via R.K. Garg).
  • But the initiator is the executive — exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Arts 74/163; cross-ref Topic 3 §5, Samsher Singh): the "satisfaction" is ministerial satisfaction, formed by the Cabinet and communicated to the President/Governor.
  • Provisional by design — B.N. Rau's memorandum and the colonial pedigree (below) show it was drafted as temporary, recess legislation, pending the House's return.

1.4 Historical pedigree — from the Indian Councils Act to Krishna Kumar

1861 — Indian Councils Act, s. 23: the Governor-General could make ordinances without the legislature's assent — the colonial seed of Art 123 (DU, KKS history).
1915 — Government of India Act: the ordinance power continued in the colonial scheme (DU, KKS history).
1935 — Government of India Act, s. 42: the fully developed colonial ordinance regime; its "immediate action" formula was transplanted into Arts 123 & 213 (DU, KKS history).
1949 — Constituent Assembly: B.N. Rau's memorandum treated the ordinance as provisional legislation; the Assembly rejected B. Bocket Sahib's amendment to Art 13 — leaving "law" expressly inclusive of an "Ordinance" (CA Debates Vol. 8, p. 203; DU/KKS and A.K. Roy material).
1950 — Constitution: Arts 123 & 213 enacted with an immunity clause making the President's satisfaction "final and conclusive and … not … questioned in any court".
1976–78 — 42nd & 44th Amendments: the satisfaction-immunity clause expressly deleted by s. 16 of the 44th Amendment Act, 1978 — reopening judicial review (DU material; Art 123(4) now "* * * * *").
1980 — National Security Ordinance: promulgated (Ord. 2 of 1980) after MISA's lapse — the litigation becomes A.K. Roy.
1982 — A.K. Roy v. UOI: ordinance = law; power legislative; satisfaction reviewable but not justiciable as to adequacy; Arts 14/19/21 fully apply.
1987 — D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar: serial re-promulgation = colourable exercise / fraud on the Constitution; "There must not be ordinance-raj".
2017 — Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar: seven-Judge Bench — successive promulgation fundamentally at odds with Arts 123/213; laying mandatory; survival test = public interest + constitutional necessity; relief can be moulded; two old cases overruled.

Section 2 · Syllabus Item (a)Essential Conditions for Promulgation

Syllabus item (a) asks for the conditions precedent and concurrent that make an ordinance constitutionally valid. Ten conditions — three entry conditions, four life-cycle conditions, and three exit/conduct conditions — plus Art 213's presidential-instructions proviso (Art 123 has none). Frame any answer as: validity = entry satisfied + life-cycle observed + competence intact + conduct non-abusive.

⏱️ The "7½ months" arithmetic (Wadhwa, DU)

The maximum natural life of a single ordinance:

  1. 6 weeks running while the Houses reassemble/recess — an ordinance may be promulgated just after a session ends;
  2. the next session must be convened within ~3 months (Art 85: Parliament summoned so as to meet at least twice a year / gap ≤ 6 months; Art 174: State Assembly — same rhythm; and the Constitution expects prompt reassembly after a recess);
  3. then another 6 weeks from re-assembly before cessation.

≈ 7½ months — which is why the DU material calls it an "emergency power of limited life". An ordinance kept alive for 1–14 years (Bihar 1967–81 facts) is ipso facto evidence of abuse.

🚫 The Governor's screen — Art 213 proviso (Art 123 has none)

Text: "Provided that the Governor shall not, without instructions from the President, promulgate any such Ordinance if—

  • (a) a Bill containing the same provisions would under this Constitution have required the previous sanction of the President for its introduction into the Legislature; or
  • (b) he would have deemed it necessary to reserve a Bill containing the same provisions for the consideration of the President (Art 200(2)); or
  • (c) an Act with the same provisions would have been invalid unless reserved and assented to by the President.

Reading it precisely: it is not an absolute ban — the Governor may promulgate in cases (a)–(c) if the President instructs him. Its purpose: the Governor cannot do by ordinance what he could not do by Bill without engaging the President — no circumvention of the Art 200/201 assent-and-reservation channel (and, through the repugnancy proviso to 213(3), a President-instructed ordinance is deemed reserved & assented for Art 254 purposes).

Exam tip: when the question says "essential conditions", mention (i) the session bar, (ii) the satisfaction, (iii) the Art 213 proviso + instructions, (iv) laying, (v) the six-week/disapproval machinery, (vi) withdrawal, (vii) competence — most answers miss the proviso entirely.

Condition 2 unpacked — what counts as "immediate action"?

"[The ordinance power is] meant to be used in unusual and exceptional circumstances and not perverted to serve political ends."Ratio of A.K. Roy v. UOI, as quoted in the DU case material (Krishna Kumar Singh excerpt)
  • Positive view: urgency need not be an emergency — legislative urgency (a lapsed statute, a budget cycle, a calamity) suffices; the satisfaction is the executive's to form.
  • Negative view (the abuse test): using the ordinance for ordinary, recurring, or perpetually renewable legislation — "governing by ordinance" — negates the very condition of promulgation. Wadhwa: the power is "emergency power of limited life"; serial re-promulgation shows there was never "immediate action" necessity — only a desire to avoid the legislature.
  • Judicial posture: courts will not re-form the satisfaction (no adequacy review), but will ask whether a satisfaction could exist at all on the material — and whether the pattern of promulgation reveals a colourable device (§4).

2.3 The session rhythm that disciplines the power (related provisions)

Art 85 — Parliament

President summons each House so that it meets at least twice a year and the gap between sittings ≤ 6 months; the ordinance's six-week clock runs from re-assembly — the Constitution's design keeps recesses short.

Art 174 — State Assemblies

Same rhythm for the Governor's ordinance: the session bar (213(1)) and the six-week clock (213(2)) are anchored to Assembly sittings. Wadhwa's maximum-life calculation runs through Art 174.

Arts 200 / 201 — Governor's Bill routes

Assent / withhold / reserve / return — the channel the Art 213 proviso protects: an ordinance may not be used to achieve what reservation to the President would have blocked.

Cross-ref: during President's rule under Art 356(1)(b), the powers of the State Legislature become exercisable "by or under the authority of Parliament", and Art 357(1)(a) lets Parliament confer on the President the State Legislature's power to make laws (with Art 357(2) preserving such laws after the Proclamation ceases) — the legislative machinery that replaces the ordinary Art 213 channel when the Governor's functions have been assumed.

Section 3 · Syllabus Item (b)'Ordinance' under Article 13

Article 13 is the fundamental-rights filter through which every law must pass — and it expressly names the ordinance. This is why an ordinance can destroy lives (detention), salaries, or status, but can never destroy a fundamental right: the ordinance itself is "law" made by "the State".

3.1 Article 13 verbatim (so far as it concerns ordinances)

13. Laws inconsistent with or in derogation of the fundamental rights.— (1) All laws in force in the territory of India immediately before the commencement of this Constitution, in so far as they are inconsistent with the provisions of this Part, shall, to the extent of such inconsistency, be void. (2) The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void. (3) In this article, unless the context otherwise requires,— (a) "law" includes any Ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having in the territory of India the force of law; (b) "laws in force" includes laws passed or made by a Legislature or other competent authority … before the commencement of this Constitution and not previously repealed … (4) Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under article 368.Constitution of India (Wikisource, Part III) — Clause (3)(a) is the hinge of syllabus item (b): the Framers deliberately put "Ordinance" first in the list of instruments having "the force of law".

Why (3)(a) exists — the Constituent Assembly link

  • The Assembly rejected B. Bocket Sahib's amendment to keep "Ordinance" out of "law" (CA Debates Vol. 8, p. 203; recorded in A.K. Roy and in the DU KKS material) — a deliberate choice that the ordinance is fully justiciable under Part III.
  • Art 13(1) ("laws in force … before commencement") is not the ordinance hook — ordinances are post-Constitution instruments; the hook is 13(2) + 13(3)(a): "the State shall not make any law" … and "law includes any Ordinance".
  • The President/Governor is part of "the State" (Art 12 for Part III; Art 367 for general construction) — so the promulgator is squarely inside 13(2).
  • Art 13(4) closes the back door: no "law" (hence no ordinance) can amend the Constitution under Art 368 — an ordinance can never touch the basic structure or any constitutional provision; constitutional amendment requires the Art 368 procedure alone.

The syllogism examiners love

P1. Art 123(2)/213(2): an ordinance has "the same force and effect as if it were an Act".
P2. Art 13(3)(a): "law" includes "any Ordinance".
P3. Art 13(2): the State shall not make any law taking away/abridging Part III rights; such law is void to that extent.
C. Therefore an ordinance violating Arts 14/19/21 is void to the extent of the violation — enforceable in writ jurisdiction exactly like an Act (Art 32/226). A.K. Roy: Arts 14, 19, 21 "must not be reduced to dead letters" (para 31).

Corollary used in A.K. Roy: the National Security Ordinance, keeping preventive detention alive after MISA, had to satisfy Art 21's "procedure established by law" (post-44th Amdt wording) — and it survived only because read down with safeguards.

Section 4 · Syllabus Item (c)Judicial Review of Ordinances

The old text said the President's satisfaction was "final and conclusive and … not … questioned in any court". The Forty-fourth Amendment tore that clause out (Art 123(4) now reads "* * * * *"), and A.K. Roy + Krishna Kumar Singh built the modern review doctrine in its place: the satisfaction is reviewable, but the court reviews the existence and legality of the satisfaction — not its adequacy.

4.1 The evolution — five steps

Step 1 — Original text: Art 123(4)/corresponding Art 213 clause: "the satisfaction of the President mentioned in clause (1) shall be final and conclusive and shall not be questioned in any court on any ground" (quoted in DU material; encountered in R.C. Cooper debate at p. 295 of the reported discussion).
Step 2 — Deletion: "This amendment was expressly deleted by Section 16 of the Forty-fourth Amendment" (DU material). With the immunity clause gone, the door reopened for review of ordinance-making.
Step 3 — A.K. Roy (1982), paras 26–27: after deletion, the President's satisfaction is not a purely political question; judicial review is not totally excluded — but the petitioners must lay a foundation showing that no circumstances could have justified the satisfaction (they failed on facts).
Step 4 — Standard fixed (KKS, following Bommai / Indra Sawhney): the court does not substitute its view on the adequacy of satisfaction; it asks (i) was there any relevant material? (ii) was there fraud on the power / oblique purpose? (iii) was there no satisfaction at all (actuated by mala fides or total non-application of mind)?
Step 5 — Structural review (Wadhwa → KKS): beyond satisfaction, courts review the existence and proper exercise of the power itself: competence (123(3)/213(3)), procedural conditions (recess, laying, six weeks), Art 13/Arts 14-19-21 conformity, and the re-promulgation pattern — "the Court cannot examine the satisfaction, but it can examine whether the power existed and was properly exercised" (Wadhwa, DU).

4.3 Doctrinal toolkit — the propositions to quote

What courts CAN review

  • Existence of the power — subject-matter competence (123(3)/213(3), Lists); "the Court cannot examine the satisfaction, but it can examine whether the power exists" (Wadhwa).
  • Procedural conditions — recess condition, laying, six-week clock, withdrawal (Wadhwa: no savings clause can keep a ceased ordinance alive).
  • Fundamental rights — full Art 13 review (A.K. Roy; Arts 14/19/21 not dead letters, para 31).
  • Existence of any satisfaction / relevant material / absence of fraud or oblique purpose (A.K. Roy paras 26–27; KKS adopting the Bommai–Indra Sawhney standard).
  • The re-promulgation pattern as a colourable device (Wadhwa) and as "fraud on constitutional power" (KKS).
  • Locus — any citizen may challenge in public interest; no need for a specially affected party (Wadhwa: locus standi relaxed for ordinance abuse).

What courts will NOT review

  • Adequacy of the satisfaction — how urgent is "urgent enough" is the executive's call, formed on advice;
  • Wisdom/policy of the ordinance's content — legislation is for the political process (disapproval resolution + no-confidence being the designed checks — Ambedkar, per R.K. Garg in DU material);
  • Compelling legislation — courts cannot order Parliament/Assembly to pass a Bill; the remedy is strike-down + political accountability ("There must not be ordinance-raj" — a constitutional-verdict, not an injunction);
  • Pre-1978 style blanket immunity — dead: the final-and-conclusive clause is deleted; review exists but is calibrated (the three limits above).

📝 How to open a 10-mark answer on judicial review of ordinances

"Judicial review of ordinances operates at two levels: review of the power (competence, procedural conditions, fundamental rights) — which is broad and unqualified; and review of the satisfaction — which is narrow and calibrated. The Forty-fourth Amendment's deletion of the 'final and conclusive' clause (Art 123(4) now '* * * * *') shifted the balance: A.K. Roy held judicial review 'not totally excluded', while Krishna Kumar Singh fixed the standard as relevance-and-fraud, not adequacy — and D.C. Wadhwa added that the courts control the life-span of ordinances through the colourable-device doctrine."

Case 31 · DU syllabus · DU case material (book p. 159)

D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987) 1 SCC 378

Supreme Court of India · Public-interest writ jurisdiction · Bhagwati C.J. speaking for the Court · The "ordinance-raj" case

1 · Facts DU MATERIAL

  • The petitions: four writ petitions under Article 32 challenging ordinances promulgated by the Governor of Bihar. The DU extract opens with the Court's treatment of locus standi (paras 2–3): citizens who are not personally affected may still challenge an ordinance in the public interest — where the question is abuse of a constitutional power, the interest of every citizen is engaged.
  • The three ordinances in issue:
    1. Bihar Forest Produce (Regulation of Supply) (Third) Ordinance, 1983;
    2. Bihar Intermediate Education Council (Third) Ordinance, 1983;
    3. Bihar Bricks Supply (Third) Ordinance, 1983 — note the telling "(Third)": each subject already had earlier ordinances in the same series.
  • The statistical indictment (DU facts): in the period 1967–1981 the State of Bihar promulgated 256 ordinances; many remained alive for 1 to 14 years; 69 ordinances were re-promulgated with the President's permission; promulgations came in herds — e.g., 54 ordinances on a single day, 26-8-1973.
  • The documentary proof: the DU extract reproduces verbatim a Bihar Government circular — PA/MISC.1040/80-872, dated 29-7-1981 — evidence of the institutionalised, departmentalised practice of re-promulgating ordinances as a routine alternative to legislation.
  • Outcome on the facts: in the same litigation the Supreme Court struck down the Bihar Intermediate Education Council Ordinance, 1985 (BIEC Ordinance).

2 · Issues

I1. Whether a citizen may move the Supreme Court to challenge ordinances in which he is not personally interested (public-interest locus).
I2. Whether the ordinance-making power may be used to keep a law in force indefinitely by successive re-promulgation without ever allowing the legislature to consider it — i.e., what is the constitutional shelf-life of an ordinance?
I3. Whether the Court can examine the President's/Governor's satisfaction under Art 123(1)/213(1), or whether that satisfaction is beyond judicial review.
I4. Whether any safeguard (e.g., a savings clause keeping rights alive) can extend an ordinance beyond its constitutional life; and what relief the Court may grant against the abuse.

3 · Arguments of both sides

🏛️ For the petitioners (citizens challenging the ordinances):

  • The three ordinances — part of endless "(Third)" series — show that the government was governing by ordinance, using Arts 123/213 as a standing substitute for legislation; the legislature was never given a chance to vote.
  • Re-promulgation defeats the Constitution's design: the six-week clock, the laying requirement and the disapproval resolution are the only legislative checks — serial re-promulgation makes them a dead letter.
  • An ordinance is an emergency power of limited life; a device that keeps laws alive for years (1–14 years) is by definition not the exercise of a limited power — it is a colourable exercise: power used for a purpose beyond the power (frustrating legislative control).
  • No savings clause can save it: Art 123(2)/213(2) make cessation automatic — the Constitution attaches no permanence to an ordinance; the executive cannot extend its own lease by drafting trickery.
  • The Court should look at the power, the pattern and the documentary record — the circular of 29-7-1981 is proof enough.

🏛️ For the State of Bihar (respondent):

  • Each ordinance was independently and validly promulgated — the Governor's satisfaction was formed each time; the President's permission was obtained where the practice required it (the DU facts note 69 re-promulgations "with the President's permission").
  • The satisfaction is subjective — under the constitutional text (and the then-understood final-and-conclusive clause) it is not for the Court to say that circumstances did not require immediate action.
  • Whether to legislate, when to legislate, and how long a subject needs an ordinance are policy questions for the political branches; the Court cannot compel the legislature to sit or to pass laws.
  • Each ordinance ceased in due course and was replaced by a fresh act of promulgation — there is no single law that has run without legislative interruption for 14 years; every individual step was intra vires.

4 · Decision DU MATERIAL — Bhagwati C.J., paras 4–8

  1. Public-interest locus allowed (paras 2–3): citizens may challenge ordinances that they allege are being used to subvert the legislative process.
  2. Character of the power: the ordinance power is an "emergency power of limited life". Its maximum natural life is about 7½ months (six weeks + a session that must be convened within roughly three months under Art 174/85 + six weeks). Any device that stretches it to years, decades or "1–14 years" is outside the power's nature.
  3. Colourable exercise / fraud on the Constitution: repeated re-promulgation of the same or substantially the same ordinances — keeping a law alive while systematically avoiding the legislature — is a colourable exercise of the power: because the power cannot achieve that result directly (indefinite life), achieving it indirectly through repetition is a fraud on the Constitution. Ordinance-making cannot be used to do what only the legislature could do: enact permanent law.
  4. Scope of review: the Court cannot examine the satisfaction itself (no reweighing of urgency), but it can examine whether the power existed and was properly exercised — the existence of the legislative competence, the conditions of promulgation, and whether the device used was colourable.
  5. No savings possible: under Art 123(2)/213(2) the ordinance ceases automatically — no savings clause can keep it alive; the Constitution does not let the executive draft itself perpetual life.
  6. Executive practice contrasted: the President of India has never re-promulgated an ordinance since 1950 — the abuse is an executive-government (State) phenomenon, which shows it is a failure of political accountability, not of constitutional design.
  7. Verdict: the re-promulgated ordinance including the BIEC Ordinance, 1985, was struck down; and the Court's ringing direction to the system: "There must not be ordinance-raj."

5 · Principle evolved

Ratio of D.C. Wadhwa
  • The ordinance power is a temporary, limited-life emergency power; its constitutional shelf-life is measured in months (≈7½), not years.
  • Successive re-promulgation used to keep a law alive without legislative consideration is a colourable exercise of power — a fraud on the Constitution — and is strike-downable by the Court.
  • Justiciability is split in two: the executive's satisfaction of urgency is not justiciable; the existence, conditions and legitimate exercise of the power (including the re-promulgation device) are fully justiciable.
  • Citizens may sue in the public interest to enforce these limits — ordinance abuse is a matter of constitutional concern for every citizen.
  • Shorthand to quote: "There must not be ordinance-raj."
Case 32 · DU syllabus · no DU excerpt — researched externally + DU's quotation of its ratio in the Krishna Kumar material

A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982) 1 SCC 271

AIR 1982 SC 710 · Five-Judge Constitution Bench: Chandrachud C.J., Bhagwati, A.C. Gupta, Tulzapurkar and D.A. Desai JJ. · The foundational ordinance case

1 · Facts EXTERNAL RESEARCH

  • The National Security Ordinance (Ordinance No. 2 of 1980) was promulgated in January 1980 as the statutory life of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, 1971 (MISA) was running out (MISA's non-permanent provisions lapsed on 31-3-1980) — the Ordinance provided for preventive detention on grounds connected with the security of the State, with an advisory-board mechanism, and was followed by the National Security Act, 1980.
  • Writ petitions under Art 32 followed, including by A.K. Roy — then a sitting Member of Parliament who was himself detained under the National Security Act, 1980 on the order of the District Magistrate, Dhanbad — challenging (i) the ordinance and the Act made thereon, (ii) specific detention provisions, and (iii) associated constitutional provisions (including a challenge to s. 1(2) of the 44th Amendment, on which the majority declined the mandamus sought).
  • The litigation forced the Supreme Court to answer, for the first time at Constitution-Bench strength, the three foundational questions about ordinances: Is an ordinance a "law" for Arts 21/13? Is the ordinance power legislative or executive? Is the President's satisfaction justiciable after the Forty-fourth Amendment deleted the final-and-conclusive clause?
  • DU note: there is no extract of this case in the DU case material; the DU Krishna Kumar Singh material nevertheless quotes its ratio (used below), and the rest of this card is researched from the reported judgment.

2 · Issues

I1. Is an ordinance promulgated under Art 123 a "law" within the meaning of Article 13(3)(a) and "procedure established by law" under Article 21 (post-44th Amendment)?
I2. Is the ordinance-making power legislative or executive? Is it confined to legislating only on a "virgin field" / fresh subject, or may it amend, alter, repeal existing law?
I3. After the Forty-fourth Amendment deleted the clause making the President's satisfaction "final and conclusive", is the satisfaction under Art 123(1) justiciable, or is it a political question beyond judicial review?
I4. Are Arts 14, 19 and 21 reduced to "dead letters" when the State chooses to legislate by ordinance instead of by Act?
I5. (Beyond ordinances) Are the detention provisions of the National Security framework valid — preventive or punitive — and are the constitutional challenges (incl. s. 1(2) of the 44th Amendment) made out?

3 · Arguments of both sides

🏛️ For the petitioners (including A.K. Roy):

  • An ordinance is an executive act, not a law made by the legislature — hence not "law" under Art 13/21; the Constitution's guarantees were framed for legislative laws, and the Founders had rejected proposals to constitutionalise ordinances (the B. Bocket Sahib episode runs the other way).
  • Even if it is law, the ordinance power is at most a power to fill a temporary, novel gap — it cannot be a parallel legislature sustaining detention statutes indefinitely; using it to perpetuate MISA-like detention by an ordinance that keeps being replaced defeats legislative scrutiny (the Wadhwa objection already visible).
  • The satisfaction is justiciable — more so after the Forty-fourth Amendment deleted the final-and-conclusive clause; where fundamental rights (Art 21 liberty) hang on it, the Court must examine whether any material existed for "immediate action".
  • Arts 14/19/21 must bite the ordinance: detention on vague grounds, without the safeguards of a legislative enactment, cannot survive Part III.

🏛️ For the Union of India:

  • Art 123(2) is conclusive: an ordinance has "the same force and effect as if enacted by Parliament" — a thing with the force of an Act is by definition a law; Art 13(3)(a) expressly includes "any Ordinance" in "law". The Assembly rejected the amendment (B. Bocket Sahib) that would have excluded it.
  • The power is legislative in nature and undelimited as to "new" vs "old" subject-matter — competence runs on the Lists, not on novelty; ordinances throughout independent India have amended taxation and regulatory codes.
  • India follows no rigid separation of powers; the Constitution deliberately lets the executive legislate in recess, on the aid and advice of the Council, with the legislature's six-week check as the designed control.
  • The satisfaction is the President's, formed on ministerial advice; urgency is not justiciable — courts lack the information and the constitutional warrant to re-form it. The petitions lay no foundation showing that no circumstances could have existed.
  • The National Security framework is preventive, not punitive, with an advisory board and statutory safeguards — it satisfies Art 21 read with Arts 14/19; s. 3(2) can be read down.

4 · Decision

  1. Ordinance = law (I1): an ordinance promulgated under Art 123 (and 213) is "law" for Arts 21 and 13(3)(a). The Court relied on Art 123(2)'s "same force and effect as if enacted by Parliament" and the Constituent Assembly's rejection of B. Bocket Sahib's amendment to Art 13 (CA Debates, Vol. 8, p. 203). Detention by ordinance must therefore satisfy Art 21's "procedure established by law".
  2. Legislative power, full field (I2, para 30): the ordinance power is legislative and is not confined to a "virgin field" — it may alter, repeal and re-enact existing law (consistent with R.K. Garg, cited in the DU material: an ordinance may amend tax laws).
  3. No American separation (I3, para 23): the American strict-separation doctrine does not apply; India's Constitution mixes legislative and executive streams with legislative control by disapproval resolution and no-confidence (Ambedkar's analysis, per R.K. Garg in DU).
  4. Satisfaction reviewable but calibrated (I3, paras 26–27): after the Forty-fourth Amendment deleted the final-and-conclusive clause, the President's satisfaction is not a purely political question and judicial review is not totally excluded — but the Court will not substitute its own view of adequacy; the petitioners must show that no circumstances could have justified the satisfaction, and they failed on facts (no such foundation was laid).
  5. Arts 14/19/21 alive (I4, para 31): choosing the ordinance route cannot reduce Part III to dead letters — constitutional limitations apply "equally" to ordinances and Acts.
  6. NSA upheld with safeguards (I5): the National Security framework is preventive, not punitive, upheld subject to advisory-board and humane-treatment safeguards; s. 3(2) read down (Art 14); the challenge to s. 1(2) of the 44th Amendment failed (mandamus refused by majority).

5 · Principle evolved

Ratio of A.K. Roy — as quoted in the DU case material (Krishna Kumar Singh extract)
"[There is] no distinction in principle between a law made by the legislature and an ordinance … both are equally subject to constitutional limitations … [the power exists for] extraordinary situations and not [to] be perverted to serve political ends."Ratio of A.K. Roy, quoted in the DU material for Krishna Kumar Singh
  • Doctrinal package: (1) ordinance = law (Art 13(3)(a) + Art 123(2)); (2) power is legislative, unrestricted as to novelty; (3) no strict separation of powers in India; (4) Part III applies to ordinances in full; (5) satisfaction is reviewable for existence/foundation — not for adequacy; (6) the power exists for unusual and exceptional circumstances, never as a perversion for political ends.
  • These propositions are the spine of every Topic 5 answer: Wadhwa builds the life-span doctrine on them; Krishna Kumar builds the re-promulgation and survival doctrines on them.
Case 33 · DU syllabus · DU case material (book p. 165)

Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) 3 SCC 1

Decided 1-2-2017 · Seven-Judge Constitution Bench (per DU material: Chandrachud, Thakur, Lokur, Bobde, Goel, Lalit and Nageswara Rao JJ.) · Majority 5:2 (DU) · The re-promulgation & survival case

1 · Facts DU MATERIAL

  • The scheme: the State of Bihar took over the management and control of 429 government-aided Sanskrit schools. The takeover — and everything that followed — was done only by ordinance, never by an Act, over roughly 1989 to 1992.
  • The chain: the first ordinance — Ord. 32 of 1989 — was promulgated on 18-12-1989; it was followed by five successive ordinances; then a fresh ordinance dated 13-8-1990 whose clauses 3 and 4 were materially altered (the DU extract sets out the differing clause text — the series was not a mechanical roll-over; the scheme kept being restructured while never facing the Assembly); the chain continued to Ord. 2 of 1992, which lapsed in 1992.
  • Not one ordinance in the series was ever laid before the Legislative Assembly — so the House was never engaged: no scrutiny, no possibility of disapproval, each ordinance quietly left to run out or be replaced by the next. (This is the factual centre of the "laying is mandatory" holding.)
  • The human stakes: teachers and employees of the 429 schools claimed that under the ordinance regime (especially the absorption clauses) they had acquired permanent rights — government-employee status and continuing salaries — and management committees claimed continuing control of the schools.
  • Past Hierarchy at Patna (High Court): (i) the ordinances created no permanent vesting of management — committees' control ended with the ordinances; (ii) the successive promulgation was contrary to D.C. Wadhwa; (iii) the 4th ordinance was void for violation of Article 14; (iv) salaries were payable to employees up to 30-4-1992 (i.e., through the cessation window of the last ordinance); (v) management of the schools reverted to the committees/State structure in accordance with law. Appeals and cross-appeals reached the Supreme Court, which restored the reference to a seven-Judge Bench.

2 · Issues

I1. Is the successive (serial) promulgation of the same ordinance constitutional — is it per se impermissible, or permissible when justified by fresh necessity (Wadhwa's colourable-device rule refined)?
I2. Is the obligation to lay the ordinance before the House mandatory? What follows from never laying it — invalidity, or automatic cessation at six weeks?
I3. When an ordinance ceases to operate (by lapse of six weeks or by a resolution of disapproval), does the pre-ordinance state of affairs revive ipso jure? Do the ordinance's legal effects — rights, status, salaries — stand obliterated as a matter of law? (The exact Dec-2024 Q5(a) question.)
I4. What is the difference between an ordinance that is void (Art 213(3)) and one that ceases to operate (Art 213(2)) — and does an ordinance ever become void ab initio by lapse?
I5. What is the correct relief — can the Court mould it (e.g., protect salaries already paid while denying prospective rights)?
I6. Do the older decisions holding that an ordinance's effects can "endure" irreversibly after lapse — State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy v. State of A.P. — still represent the law?

3 · Arguments of both sides

🏛️ For the employees/committees asserting rights under the ordinances ("effects survive" side):

  • For nearly three years the State itself governed the 429 schools under an unbroken chain of ordinances; the employees performed service, drew salaries, and were treated as government employees under the scheme — their rights and status relied upon and acted upon cannot evaporate the moment the last ordinance lapses.
  • Where an ordinance creates concluded transactions and irreversible consequences (salaries paid, services rendered, management exercised), the law recognises that such effects endure — the older line of authority (Bhupendra Kumar Bose; T. Venkata Reddy) so held; a repeal/cessation does not undo completed acts.
  • On re-promulgation: each promulgation was a fresh act of the Governor based on a fresh satisfaction; the material alterations (clauses 3 and 4 changed in the 13-8-1990 ordinance) show genuine legislative reworking, not mechanical roll-over; necessity continued because the Assembly had not legislated.
  • The State is estopped from denying the consequences of its own three-year scheme — public interest requires continuity of pay and status for teachers who served under it.

🏛️ For the State / opposing position ("cessation ends effects" side):

  • An ordinance by constitutional design is provisional: Art 213(2) says it "shall cease to operate" — it was never meant to confer permanence. No ordinance can legislate its own immortality: the Constitution attaches no degree of permanence to actions or transactions during its currency, absent a valid savings clause — and a device that achieves permanence by re-promulgation is itself the fraud Wadhwa condemned.
  • Non-laying is fatal to survival: an ordinance that is never laid was never before the House at all — no scrutiny, no disapproval possible; the executive simply ran out the clock repeatedly — each series member died at six weeks (or earlier), and the High Court was right to treat the schemes as having no ongoing force.
  • Permanent absorption of hundreds of teachers into government service by ordinance alone — bypassing the Assembly's service-legislation competence and the President's-assent screens — would let a Governor do by ordinance what a Bill could not easily do.
  • The re-promulgation pattern is the tell: the very need to re-promulgate five, six, eight times proves there was no "immediate action" necessity — only a strategy to avoid the legislature (Wadhwa; and the Art 14 vice found by the High Court in the 4th ordinance).

4 · Decision

(A) The separate opinion of Lokur J. (reproduced in the DU material under "Dissenting Opinion"; he agreed with the majority on the outcome):

  1. Successive promulgation is not per se a fraud — but it must never be mechanical: each promulgation requires genuine, fresh satisfaction of immediate necessity and real deliberation; re-promulgation used as a mere relay to keep a law alive without legislative consideration is impermissible.
  2. The three Art 213(2) scenarios: (i) ordinance laid but not disapproved → it still ceases at six weeks — the text contains no approval mechanism; silence is not approval and cannot ratify (contrast Arts 352(4), 356(3) and 360(2), which keep proclamations alive only on affirmative approval); (ii) laid and disapproved → it ceases immediately on the resolution (Assembly's resolution + Council's agreement, if any); (iii) never laid → the House never had it — it could never be scrutinised or disapproved, and it must also cease at six weeks, while the omission itself breaches an obligatory constitutional duty. Running through: the laying requirement is obligatory; the majority held this for three reasons.
  3. Void vs ceased: an ordinance is void under Art 213(3) when it legislates beyond competence (assent-required subject) — that defect goes to birth; an ordinance that simply ceases under Art 213(2) (six weeks/disapproval/withdrawal) is not void at inception — cessation operates prospectively. "The ordinance is not void at its inception."
  4. Enduring-rights theory rejected: an ordinance cannot create an enduring or irreversible right in a citizen; consequently the contrary views in State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy v. State of A.P. are overruled.

(B) Chandrachud J. for the Court (the majority "Judgement"):

  1. Constitutional history: the power descends from the Indian Councils Act, 1861 (s. 23) → 1915 Act → Government of India Act, 1935 (s. 42); B.N. Rau's memorandum treated the ordinance as provisional legislation; in the Constituent Assembly the rejection of B. Bocket Sahib's amendment fixed ordinances as "law" under Art 13.
  2. Framework: R.K. Garg (ordinances may amend tax laws; legislative control = disapproval resolution + no-confidence, per Ambedkar); A.K. Roy (no distinction in principle between Act and ordinance; both subject to constitutional limitations); R.C. Cooper (the satisfaction-immunity debate); the Forty-fourth Amendment's deletion of the immunity clause (Art 123(4) now "* * * * *") — after which review of satisfaction is governed by the Bommai/Indra Sawhney standard: no review of adequacy; review for fraud on power, oblique purpose, or no satisfaction at all.
  3. Laying is mandatory (three reasons): the text is in mandatory terms; laying is the mechanism of legislative control without which the six-week and disapproval machinery cannot operate; and permitting omission would let the executive keep laws alive unseen by the legislature — a government cannot bypass the House by never laying the ordinance.
  4. Re-promulgation: serial re-promulgation is "fundamentally at odds with the scheme of Arts 123 and 213" — the six-week clock exists precisely so that the ordinance must either die or face the legislature; using fresh promulgations to defeat that clock is a fraud on the constitutional power (confirming and extending Wadhwa).
  5. The survival test: whether rights, privileges, obligations and liabilities survive a ceased ordinance must be decided as a matter of construction, guided by public interest and constitutional necessity — with irreversibility a relevant consideration; there is no blanket rule of automatic obliteration and no blanket rule of automatic survival. English authorities on the effects of lapse/repeal (Wicks; Warren v. Windle; Steavenson) were considered.
  6. Relief may be moulded: in a suitable case the Court may shape the remedy rather than declare an all-or-nothing position.
  7. The declaration on these facts: every ordinance in the series — Ord. 32/1989 through Ord. 2/1992 — is a fraud on the constitutional power; the ordinances are bereft of any legal effects and consequences; they do not create rights or confer the status of government employees on the teachers; the High Court's core conclusions were affirmed. Moulded relief: the Court did not order recovery of salaries already paid — concluded payments stand; no prospective absorption/status follows.

5 · Principle evolved

Ratio of Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar — nine propositions
  1. Ordinance-ages are finite and conditional: the constitutional scheme of Arts 123/213 — recess condition, six-week clock, laying, disapproval — is an integrated machinery of legislative control; defeating it by serial re-promulgation is a fraud on constitutional power.
  2. Successive promulgation is "fundamentally at odds" with Arts 123/213 — legitimate only where a genuinely fresh necessity and real deliberation exist (Lokur: not per se invalid; the majority: structurally suspect, abuse strike-downable).
  3. Laying is mandatory — silence of the executive to lay = illegality; the ordinance cannot survive six weeks un-laid, and non-laying is itself a constitutional default.
  4. Silence is not approval — nothing the House does (short of disapproval) can save an ordinance past six weeks: it simply dies (proclamations, by contrast, survive only on affirmative approval — Arts 352(4)/356(3)/360(2)).
  5. Cease ≠ void ab initio: cessation (Art 213(2)) operates prospectively; voidness (Art 213(3)/Art 13) goes to birth — the classification controls every "what survives" question.
  6. Survival is decided, not assumed: construction of the instrument + public interest & constitutional necessity (+ irreversibility as a guiding consideration) — no ipso jure blanket revival of the pre-ordinance state of affairs; no automatic obliteration of all effects.
  7. Relief can be moulded — deny the claimed status/rights prospectively while protecting concluded, irreversible consequences (salaries paid) in public interest.
  8. Overruled: State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy v. State of A.P. — to the extent they allowed ordinances to create enduring, irreversible rights.
  9. Ordinances cannot self-perpetuate: permanence must come from the legislature — the only body whose Bills face assent, reservation and debate.

Section 6 · LinkagesRelated Acts, Constitutional Provisions & Amendments

Ordinances do not float free — they sit inside a web of colonial statutes, constitutional amendments, session machinery and emergency provisions. Examiners reward the student who draws these threads in.

📜 Colonial ancestry (DU material — KKS history)

  • Indian Councils Act, 1861, s. 23 — the Governor-General's ordinance power: the original template (power to make laws without the legislature's assent).
  • Government of India Act, 1915 — the power carried forward in the colonial scheme.
  • Government of India Act, 1935, s. 42 — the mature colonial ordinance regime; its "immediate action" formula, laying and cessation machinery were transplanted into Arts 123 & 213.
  • Constituent Assembly — B.N. Rau's memorandum treated the ordinance as provisional legislation; the Assembly rejected B. Bocket Sahib's amendment to keep "Ordinance" out of Art 13 (CA Debates Vol. 8, p. 203).

🛠️ Amendments that reshaped the doctrine

  • Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978, s. 16 — deleted the clause making the President's/Governor's satisfaction "final and conclusive and … not … questioned in any court" (Art 123(4) and Art 213(4) now read "* * * * *" — DU material). This single deletion re-opened judicial review: A.K. Roy paras 26–27; KKS standard.
  • 42nd (1976) & 44th (1978) Amendments — Art 21: the 42nd substituted "due process"; the 44th restored "procedure established by law" — the exact wording A.K. Roy construes when holding that detention by ordinance must satisfy Part III.
  • Art 13(4) + Art 368 — an ordinance can never amend the Constitution; constitutional amendment requires the Art 368 procedure exclusively.
  • Art 123(3) / 213(3) — the built-in voidness clauses (competence / validity-as-an-Act defects) plus the 213(3) repugnancy proviso (President-instructed ordinance deemed reserved & assented — Art 254 interaction).

🗓️ Session & assent machinery the clock depends on

  • Art 85 (Parliament's sessions; gap ≤ 6 months) and Art 174 (State Assemblies) — fix the outer rhythm of the six-week clock (Wadhwa's 7½-month calculation runs through Art 174).
  • Arts 200/201 — Governor's assent/withhold/reserve/return: the channel protected by the Art 213 proviso (a)–(c) (and by the reservation-ordinances of the DU facts).
  • Arts 352(4), 356(3), 360(2) — the contrast set Lokur J. uses: proclamations survive only on affirmative approval; ordinances have no such mechanism (silence ≠ approval).
  • Arts 356(1)(b), 357 — during President's rule the State Legislature's powers become exercisable "by or under the authority of Parliament", and Parliament may confer the State's law-making power on the President (Art 357(2) preserves such laws after the Proclamation ends) — the machinery that replaces the ordinary channel when the Governor's functions are assumed.

⚖️ Executive-accountability provisions read into the power

  • Arts 74/163 + Samsher Singh (Topic 3 §5): promulgation and withdrawal are exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers — the "satisfaction" is ministerial satisfaction.
  • Political control (Ambedkar, per R.K. Garg in DU): the designed checks are the disapproval resolution and the no-confidence motion — because the ordinance never faces a legislative vote.
  • General Clauses Act, 1897, s. 6 (background analogy): statutory repeals ordinarily do not disturb accrued rights "unless a different intention appears" — the classic repeal-savings logic by which older decisions explained surviving effects; Krishna Kumar severed that analogy for ordinances: lapse is governed by Arts 123/213 and the construction/public-interest test — an ordinance cannot borrow statutory savings to engineer permanence.
  • Ordinance practice instruments: Gazette publication + laying of a copy before the House (routine executive practice); the DU Wadhwa facts show the darker side — the Bihar circular PA/MISC.1040/80-872 dated 29-7-1981 institutionalising re-promulgation.

Section 7 · ContextReal-World Examples to Drop into Answers

Examiners reward specificity. These five chains — one from the DU material, four from public record — turn abstract doctrine into memorable argument.

1️⃣ Bihar 1967–81 — the original "ordinance-raj" DU FACTS

256 ordinances in fourteen years; 54 promulgated on a single day (26-8-1973); lives stretching 1–14 years; 69 re-promulgated with the President's permission; the government's own circular (29-7-1981) on record; the BIEC Ordinance, 1985 struck down; Bhagwati C.J.: "There must not be ordinance-raj." Contrast recorded in the same judgment: the President has never re-promulgated an ordinance since 1950.

2️⃣ Krishna Kumar's 429 Sanskrit schools DU FACTS

A management scheme running only on ordinances from 18-12-1989 (Ord. 32/1989) to Ord. 2/1992 — five successive ordinances plus a materially altered fresh ordinance of 13-8-1990, never once laid — ending in the seven-Judge declaration that the whole series was a fraud on constitutional power, bereft of legal effects, with relief moulded (salaries paid not recovered). The perfect fact-pattern for any question on re-promulgation or post-lapse effects.

3️⃣ Triple talaq — ordinances as a bridge to legislation

Shayara Bano (22-8-2017) struck down instant triple talaq; the government promulgated the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Ordinance (10-9-2017), re-promulgated it on 6-1-2018 (it lapsed), re-promulgated again on 19-7-2018 (it lapsed), and finally Parliament enacted the Act of 2019 in July 2019. A textbook illustration of justified repeated promulgation (fresh necessity each time, crossed with eventual legislative cover) — pair with Lokur J.'s "not per se fraud" line, and with the majority's insistence that the bridge must not become a residence.

4️⃣ Enemy Property — the re-promulgation chain

The Enemy Property (Amendment and Validation) Ordinance was promulgated four times between January 2016 and February 2017 (each time bridging the gap after the previous one lapsed or an Act was challenged), twice replaced by an Act (2016 and 2017). A post-Wadhwa example commentators invoke for the "serial promulgation" critique — useful to show the doctrine's continuing bite.

5️⃣ Farm laws — full lifecycle: ordinance → Act → repeal

Three reform ordinances (June 2020) → the three Farm Acts, 2020 (September 2020) → repeal by Parliament (November 2021). Shows both faces: the executive using the recess power for structural reform, and the legislature — the only body that can give permanence — ultimately deciding. Also frames the KKS point: permanence is Parliament's business.

6️⃣ The never-repeated Presidency

Du Wadhwa facts: since 1950 no President has re-promulgated an ordinance — re-promulgation is a disease of governments without legislative accountability, which is exactly why the courts policed it first in Bihar and then, seven Judges deep, in Krishna Kumar Singh.

📘 PYQ 1 — LL.B. (Hons.) III Semester Examination, December 2024 · Q5(a) · 10 marks
"'Where an ordinance has ceased to operate, would it result ipso jure in a revival of the state of affairs which existed before the ordinance was promulgated? Would the legal effects created by the ordinance stand obliterated as a matter of law upon the lapsing of an ordinance or passing of a resolution of disapproval?' Answer this question in the light of the judgment Krishna Kumar v. State of Bihar [(2017) 3 SCC 1]."
Model answer (structure = the KKS framework itself)

Introduction — why the question is asked. The two questions are mirror-images: does cessation restore the pre-ordinance world automatically (ipso jure revival), and does it erase the ordinance's world automatically (ipso jure obliteration)? The seven-Judge Constitution Bench in Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) 3 SCC 1 answered neither question in the absolute. It first classified the kind of legal exit, then replaced both blanket theories with a principled, case-by-case test — while overruling the older blanket theory in State of Orissa v. Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy v. State of A.P.

Step 1 — Classify before you consequences (the decisive move).

Kind of exitTextEffect on the ordinance
Void at birthArt 213(3)/123(3) (provision the legislature could not validly enact); Art 13(2) + Arts 14/19/21Void ab initio — never valid law; acts under it are a nullity; the pre-ordinance state of affairs was never lawfully displaced — automatic reversion follows from nullity, not from lapse
Cease to operateArt 213(2)/123(2): six weeks from re-assembly · disapproval resolution · withdrawalProspective cessation only. Lokur J.: "the ordinance is not void at its inception" — it was valid law while it stood; the clock stops from cessation onward

Step 2 — Answer to Question 1 (automatic revival?). NO — not as a blanket rule. Because the ordinance was valid law during its currency, the world it created does not spring back ipso jure in all respects the moment it lapses or is disapproved. What survives is decided by construction of the ordinance guided by public interest and constitutional necessity, with irreversibility as a relevant consideration, and the Court retains power to mould the relief (Chandrachud J. for the Court). Caveat: where the ordinance was void ab initio (constitutional invalidity or Art 213(3)), reversion is immediate and automatic — but that is the consequence of invalidity, not of cessation.

Step 3 — Answer to Question 2 (automatic obliteration?). NO — equally not as a blanket rule. Cessation does not wipe the ordinance's completed legal effects: rights acquired, acts done and obligations discharged during the currency remain prima facie intact — and no new effect can accrue after cessation. What the KKS Court rejected is the opposite absolutism: an ordinance cannot create enduring, irreversible rights that bind the State forever — the surviving-effects theory of Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy is expressly overruled. An ordinance cannot legislate its own immortality.

Step 4 — Lapse and disapproval are the same box. Under Arts 123(2)(a)/213(2)(a) a resolution of disapproval and expiry of the six weeks both mean one thing: the ordinance ceases to operate. (Disapproval merely ends it sooner; and note the mechanics: both Houses at the Union — effective on "the passing of the second of those resolutions"; the Assembly's resolution with the Council's agreement, if any, at the State. Silence saves nothing — there is no approval mechanism; contrast Arts 352(4)/356(3)/360(2).) The consequences analysis in Steps 2–3 therefore applies identically to both routes — precisely what the question's "or" invites you to say.

Step 5 — Apply to the facts of KKS. The chain of ordinances from Ord. 32 of 1989 to Ord. 2 of 1992 (429 Sanskrit schools; never laid; serially re-promulgated) was held a fraud on the constitutional power — the ordinances were bereft of any legal effects and consequences: they did not create rights or confer government-employee status on the teachers. Yet the Court moulded the relief: salaries already paid were not recovered. That single disposition is the whole answer: no prospective rights from a fraudulently sustained scheme; but concluded, irreversible, publicly-beneficial consequences are protected — neither blanket revival nor blanket obliteration.

Conclusion (the two sentences examiners look for): (1) Lapse or disapproval does not result ipso jure in revival of the pre-ordinance state of affairs — cessation is prospective, and survival of effects is decided by construction + public interest + constitutional necessity (unless the ordinance was void ab initio, where reversion flows from nullity). (2) Nor do the ordinance's legal effects stand obliterated as a matter of law — they endure where public interest and constitutional necessity require it and the Court so moulds its relief — but they can never harden into enduring rights, the Bhupendra Bose–Venkata Reddy theory having been overruled.

📎 30-second exam skeleton (memorise)
  1. Classify: void ab initio (213(3)/13) vs ceased (213(2)) — "not void at its inception" (Lokur).
  2. Cease = prospective; no ipso jure revival.
  3. Effects survive/die per construction + public interest + constitutional necessity (+ irreversibility); court may mould relief.
  4. Disapproval = lapse (same box; mechanics; silence saves nothing).
  5. KKS application: fraud → bereft of effects → no absorption; but salaries paid not recovered.
  6. Overruling: Bhupendra Bose & Venkata Reddy (enduring rights) — the middle path is the KKS path.
📘 PYQ 2 — Centenary Chance Examination (LL.B. III Year) · Q8(b) · 10 marks ("Attempt any two of the following")
"Ordinance making power of the executive."
Model answer — short note

Meaning & constitutional basis. The ordinance is the Constitution's emergency channel of legislation: the executive (President under Art 123, Governor under Art 213) may make general law when the legislature cannot act immediately. Its pedigree runs from s. 23 of the Indian Councils Act, 1861 through the Government of India Acts of 1915 and 1935 (s. 42); the Framers transplanted it as provisional legislation (B.N. Rau's memorandum) and, by rejecting B. Bocket Sahib's amendment (CA Debates Vol. 8, p. 203), made an ordinance expressly a "law" under Art 13(3)(a).

Nature: legislative, not executive. A.K. Roy v. UOI (1982) settles that the power is legislative in nature, not confined to a "virgin field" (it may amend and repeal existing law — cf. R.K. Garg: ordinances may amend tax laws); India follows no rigid American separation; but it exists for "unusual and exceptional circumstances and not [to] be perverted to serve political ends". It is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Samsher), and an ordinance has "the same force and effect as an Act" (Arts 123(2)/213(2)).

Essential conditions (syllabus item (a)). (1) Recess: unavailable "when both Houses of Parliament are in session" (123(1)); unavailable when the Assembly sits — or both Houses where a Council exists (213(1)). (2) Satisfaction of circumstances requiring immediate action — subjective but bonâ fide. (3) Competence: within the Lists; Art 123(3) voids provisions "Parliament would not … be competent to enact"; Art 213(3) voids provisions invalid as a Governor-assented Act. (4) The Art 213 proviso: the Governor needs instructions from the President in cases (a)–(c) (previous-sanction Bills; Art 200(2) reservation; assent-required invalidity). (5) Laying before both Houses/Assembly — mandatory (KKS, three reasons). (6) Six-week clock from re-assembly (Explanation: later date), ended by lapse — no approval mechanism exists; disapproval shortens life (both Houses/"second of those resolutions" at the Union; Assembly + Council at the State; silence saves nothing). (7) Withdrawal at any time (123(2)(b)/213(2)(b)). (8) No abuse of re-promulgation — a genuinely fresh satisfaction is required for each fresh promulgation. Maximum natural life ≈ 7½ months (Wadhwa) — an emergency power of limited life.

'Ordinance' under Article 13 (syllabus item (b)). Art 13(2): "The State shall not make any law" taking away or abridging Part III rights; Art 13(3)(a) defines "law" to "include any Ordinance". Hence an ordinance violating Arts 14/19/21 is void to that extent — writ-reviewable exactly like an Act (A.K. Roy: Arts 14/19/21 must not become "dead letters"; detention by ordinance must satisfy Art 21's "procedure established by law"). Art 13(4) with Art 368: an ordinance can never amend the Constitution.

Judicial review (syllabus item (c)). The old clause making the satisfaction "final and conclusive … not … questioned in any court" was deleted by s. 16 of the 44th Amendment (1978) (Arts 123(4)/213(4) now "* * * * *"). After deletion, A.K. Roy (paras 26–27) held review "not totally excluded" — but courts test only for relevant material, fraud/oblique purpose, or absence of satisfaction (the Bommai–Indra Sawhney standard adopted in KKS), never for adequacy. Alongside, courts review the power itself: competence, procedural conditions, fundamental rights — and, decisively, the re-promulgation pattern (Wadhwa: "the Court cannot examine the satisfaction, but it can examine whether the power exists"). Remedies: strike down (Wadhwa — BIEC Ordinance 1985; KKS — whole series), read down (A.K. Roy s. 3(2)), or mould relief (KKS — no recovery of salaries paid). Locus: any citizen in public interest (Wadhwa, paras 2–3).

Successive promulgation (syllabus item (d)). Serially re-promulgating the same ordinance to keep a law alive while dodging the legislature is a colourable exercise / fraud on the Constitution ("There must not be ordinance-raj" — D.C. Wadhwa (1987); Bihar's 256 ordinances, 69 re-promulgated, lives of 1–14 years). In Krishna Kumar Singh (2017, 7 Judges) re-promulgation was held "fundamentally at odds with the scheme of Arts 123 and 213"; mechanically re-promulgated, never-laid ordinances (Ord. 32/1989–Ord. 2/1992) were a fraud on constitutional power, bereft of legal effects — but relief was moulded; and the enduring-rights theory of Bhupendra Kumar Bose and T. Venkata Reddy was overruled (survival = construction + public interest + constitutional necessity).

Conclusion. The ordinance power is a constitutional necessity with a constitutional leash: recess-only, satisfaction-bound, competence-bound, rights-bound, six-weeks-bound — and now, after Wadhwa and Krishna Kumar Singh, re-promulgation-bound. As the DU material's A.K. Roy ratio puts it, ordinance and Act are "equally subject to constitutional limitations"; when the leash is pulled too hard, courts strike down — while leaving the legislative clock, not the executive, the last word.

  3. Conditions: recess · satisfaction · competence/proviso · laying · 6 weeks/disapproval · withdrawal · anti-abuse; 7½ months.

陷阱 Common traps — do NOT write these

  • "Power available when either House is not in session" — wrong; the bar is both Houses in session.
  • Inventing a proviso to Art 123 — there is none.
  • "If both Houses approve, the ordinance becomes an Act" — no such mechanism; it dies at six weeks regardless.
  • Saying lapse/disapproval makes the ordinance void ab initio — cessation is prospective (KKS).
  • Saying satisfaction is totally non-justiciable — true only before the 44th Amendment; today: calibrated review.
  • Saying ordinances cannot touch existing law — A.K. Roy para 30 says otherwise ("virgin field" rejected).
  • Citing Art 13(1) for ordinances — the hooks are 13(2) + 13(3)(a).

  • Forgetting that disapproval needs both Houses at the Union ("second of those resolutions" ≠ either House alone).
