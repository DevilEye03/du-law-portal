The 40-second core of the whole topic

  • Art. 1 — India is a Union of States; territory = States + Union Territories + territories that may be acquired (sovereignty lives outside the text).
  • Art. 2 — Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new States “on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” (dealing with territory outside the existing States).
  • Art. 3 — Parliament may by law form/alter/divide/rename the internal constituents (a–e), subject to a procedural proviso (President’s recommendation + reference of the Bill to the affected State legislature for views).
  • Art. 4 — laws made under Art. 2/3 are not “amendments” under Art. 368; they may add/alter the First & Fourth Schedules; simple majority.
  • Ceding land abroad? Berubari: neither executive action nor an Art. 3 law — only a constitutional amendment under Art. 368 (amending Art. 1 + First Schedule), as done by the Ninth Amendment, 1960.

Section 1 · Article 1Name and Territory of the Union — Article 1(1)–(3)

“India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States” — a phrase chosen deliberately over “Federation”, and the reason the Union is called indestructible.

1.1 Text

Art. 1. (1) India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.
(2) The territory of India shall comprise—
   (a) the territories of the States;
   (b) the Union territories specified in the First Schedule; and
   (c) such other territories as may be acquired.Constitution of India, Article 1 (as amended by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956)

1.2 “Union of States” vs “Federation” — why the word matters

Deliberate drafting choice

The Drafting Committee (Dr. Ambedkar) rejected the expression “Federation of India”. As Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly, the phrase “Union of States” carries two implications: (i) the Union is indestructible — no State has the right to secede; and (ii) the constituent units are alterable in territory (via Arts. 2–4) though the Union itself is not.

The DU case material states the point squarely: Article 1 is a recognition of the history that the Union of India’s territorial limits are unalterable and the States are creatures of the Constitution — territorially alterable constituents, with a single citizenship for all, and no right of secession.

States ≠ mere administrative units

“…dual polity under the proposed Constitution will consist of the Union at the Centre and the States at the periphery each endowed with sovereign powers to be exercised in the field assigned to them respectively by the Constitution. … the Indian Constitution proposed in the Draft Constitution is not a league of States nor are the States administrative units or agencies of the Union Government.” Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Constituent Assembly Debates, Vol. 7, 4 November 1948 (quoted in Art. 370 Judgment, para 482)

The 2019 Art. 370 judgment (uploaded case material) builds its Article-3 analysis on this: Art. 1(1) indicates that the States are essential and indispensable — “the Union cannot exist without the existence of the States” (para 483). Democracy + federalism are basic features (para 481).

1.3 Clause (3): the three-layered territory — and the sovereignty clause

1(3)(a) States

Territories of the 28 States listed in the First Schedule. Altered only through Arts. 3–4 machinery or constitutional amendment.

1(3)(b) Union Territories

The UTs specified in the First Schedule (e.g., Delhi, Puducherry, J&K, Ladakh, Andaman & Nicobar). Administered by the Union (Arts. 239–241).

1(3)(c) “such other territories as may be acquired”

The open door for sovereignty: foreign territory acquired by India automatically becomes part of India. Berubari (para 30): Art. 1(3)(c) does not confer the power to acquire — it formally provides for absorption/integration of territory acquired by virtue of India’s inherent sovereign right.

The symmetry point examiners love: the Constitution expressly accommodates acquisition (1(3)(c)) but is silent on cession. In Berubari Chatterjee argued from this silence (expressio unius est exclusio alterius) that India can never cede. The Court rejected it — cession, like acquisition, is an attribute of sovereignty outside the Constitution, exercisable in India only through the constitutional route of Art. 368 amendment of Art. 1 + First Schedule (paras 27–30, 46).

1.4 Evolution of Article 1

1950 original — Art. 1 listed territories of States in Parts A, B, C of the First Schedule, the Part D territory (Andaman & Nicobar), and “such other territories as may be acquired”. A separate Art. 243 (Part IX) administered Part D and newly acquired territories (Berubari, para 33).
1956 — Seventh Amendment Act — abolished the A/B/C/D classification; replaced it with a two-fold scheme: territories of the States + Union territories specified in the First Schedule; Art. 243 deleted. Basis: States Reorganisation Commission Report (1955) → States Reorganisation Act, 1956 (Art. 370 Judgment, paras 479, 486).
1959–60 — Berubari debate — whether 1(3)(c)/Preamble impliedly or expressly barred cession → Court: no bar in principle, but cession demands Art. 368 (amending Art. 1 + First Schedule).
1960 — Ninth Amendment Act — First Schedule amended to give effect to the Indo-Pakistan (Berubari/enclaves) Agreement — the working template for cession.
2015 — Hundredth Amendment Act — First Schedule re-aligned w.e.f. 1-8-2015 for the India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement (exchange of 111 Indian enclaves for 51 Bangladeshi enclaves + citizenship rights to enclave residents) — the modern repeat of the Berubari template (DU case material, “Additional Readings”).

Section 2 · Article 2Admission or Establishment of New States

Art. 2. Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.Constitution of India, Article 2

2.1 Anatomy of the power

  • Two modes: (i) admit a territory already existing as a political unit into the Union (e.g., Sikkim, Goa), or (ii) establish a new State that did not previously exist as such (e.g., carving a State out of acquired/union territory).
  • “On such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” — widest legislative discretion: Parliament can attach special conditions (special representation, protective clauses, transitional provisions). Berubari para 34: Art. 2 enables foreign territory acquired under 1(3)(c) to be assimilated by law — “admitted into the Union or constituted into new States”.
  • Constitutional limit (R.C. Poudyal): the “terms and conditions” must be consistent with the basic structure of the Constitution — DU’s Additional-Reading note states this as the operative proposition of case 12 (AIR 1993 SC 1804).
  • Route: a law under Art. 2 is not a constitutional amendment (Art. 4) — but when admission also changes Art. 1/First Schedule membership of the Union itself (Sikkim, 1975), Parliament has used the Art. 368 route: Constitution (Thirty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1975.

Art. 2 in action — Sikkim (1975)

Sikkim, a protectorate with the 36th Amendment: admitted as the 22nd State; Art. 371F inserted (special safeguards — capacity of the Assembly to protect backward classes, representation, laws prevailing at admission). Upheld in R.C. Poudyal — challenges to the admission (reservations in the Assembly for Bhutia-Lepchas/Sangha) unsuccessfully attacked the 36th Amendment.

Art. 2 vs Art. 3 — the classic distinction

Art. 2 → deals with territory outside the existing States (admission of new units; establishment of brand-new States). Art. 3 → deals with the internal re-arrangement inter se the existing constituents (“broadly stated it deals with the internal adjustment inter se of the territories of the constituent States of India” — Berubari, para 38). Territory acquired abroad enters by 1(3)(c) automatically, then is assimilated by law under Art. 2 or Art. 3(a)/(b).

Section 3 · Articles 3 & 4Alteration of Areas, Boundaries & Names — Procedure and Machinery

Article 3 is the most used — and most litigated — provision of Part I: it is the engine of every reorganisation since 1950.

3.1 Text of Article 3 (current), with the 1966 Explanations

Art. 3. Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States. — Parliament may by law—
(a) form a new State by separation of territory from any State or by uniting two or more States or parts of States or by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
(b) increase the area of any State;
(c) diminish the area of any State;
(d) alter the boundaries of any State;
(e) alter the name of any State:
Provided that no Bill for the purpose shall be introduced in either House of Parliament except on the recommendation of the President and unless, where the proposal contained in the Bill affects the area, boundaries or name of any of the States, the Bill has been referred by the President to the Legislature of that State for expressing its views thereon within such period as may be specified in the reference or within such further period as the President may allow and the period so specified or allowed has expired.
Explanation I. —In this article, in clauses (a) to (e), “State” includes a Union territory, but in the proviso, “State” does not include a Union territory.
Explanation II. —The power conferred on Parliament by clause (a) includes the power to form a new State or Union territory by uniting a part of any State or Union territory to any other State or Union territory.Constitution of India, Article 3 — proviso substituted in 1955; Explanations I & II added in 1966 (Art. 370 Judgment, para 497 & fn. 347)

Clause-wise coverage

ClauseOperation
3(a)Form new State: by separation from a State; by uniting 2+ States/parts; by uniting any territory to part of a State. Explanation II extends this to uniting parts of UTs too (e.g., Dadra & Nagar Haveli + Daman & Diu merged as one UT, 2020).
3(b)Increase area — from another State or from Art. 1(3)(c) territory absorbed into a State (Berubari para 39; Chandernagore added to West Bengal).
3(c)Diminish area — postulates the diminished area continues in India; not cession abroad (Berubari paras 41–42).
3(d)Alter boundaries — consequence of adjustments under (a)/(b)/(c) (West Bengal’s boundary changed for Chandernagore: Berubari para 35).
3(e)Alter name — Mysore→Karnataka, Madras→Tamil Nadu, Uttaranchal→Uttarakhand, Orissa→Odisha (Art. 370 Judgment, para 498).
Explanation IIn clauses (a)–(e), “State” includes UTs (so UTs can be altered/formed); in the proviso, “State” excludes UTs (so no reference of the Bill to a UT legislature).

The proviso — two conditions only

  1. Presidential recommendation is a condition precedent for introducing any Bill under Art. 3 (in either House).
  2. If the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President must refer the Bill to that State’s Legislature to express its views within the specified/further period.
Babulal Parate (case 13): (i) views of the State Legislature are recommendatory, not binding; (ii) a fresh reference is not required if Parliament subsequently modifies the Bill — only “the proposal contained in the Bill” at the time of reference is to be sent; requiring re-reference for every amendment would create an “interminable process”. No veto for the State.

3.2 Article 4 — why Art. 3 is “ordinary legislation”

Art. 4. (1) Laws made under Article 2 or Article 3 shall not be deemed to be amendments of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368 notwithstanding that any such law amends the First Schedule or the Fourth Schedule.
(2) Such laws may also make such consequential amendments (e.g., in Articles 4(1), 366…) as may be necessary or proper.Constitution of India, Article 4
  • Effect (Berubari para 36): “laws relatable to Article 2 or Article 3 are not to be treated as constitutional amendments for the purpose of Article 368 — which means that if legislation is competent under Article 3 … it would be unnecessary to invoke Article 368. On the other hand … if legislation … is not competent under Article 3, Article 368 would inevitably apply.”
  • Mechanics: simple majority of each House (not “special majority” of Art. 368), no ratification by State Legislatures — Berubari para 48: neither Art. 1 nor Art. 3 is in the entrenched list in the proviso to Art. 368 (so even an Art. 368 amendment altering the First Schedule for cession needs no state ratification).
  • Art. 368’s special majorities do apply when Art. 368 itself is used (as in cession): majority of total membership of each House + ⅔ of members present & voting — the “safeguard” noted in Berubari para 47.

Doctrinal guard-rails on Article 3 (all from uploaded material)

  • Internal adjustment only — Art. 3 broadly deals with internal adjustment inter se of constituents; no power to cede abroad (Berubari paras 38, 41–44).
  • UTs outside the proviso — pre-1966, Berubari para 40 noted Art. 3 “in terms does not refer to the Union territories”, so cession of a UT’s part anyway had to use Art. 368; Explanation I later clarified UTs are included in clauses (a)–(e) but not in the proviso.
  • Art. 3 cannot re-arrange the Seventh Schedule — “under Article 3, Parliament cannot take away the powers of the State executive or the State legislature in respect of matters enumerated in List II” (State of Himachal Pradesh v. UOI, 2011 (13) SCC 344, quoted in Art. 370 Judgment para 501). Altering Union–State division of powers needs Art. 368 with ratification by half the States (Art. 368(2), para 502).
  • Extinguishing statehood? — “A constituent unit loses its character as a State only if it is converted into a Union territory in full, with no part of it retaining statehood” (para 499). Whether Parliament may do that under Art. 3 was left open in the Art. 370 case (para 504) — a live question (see §5).

Section 4 · Cession DoctrinePower to Cede Indian Territory to a Foreign Nation

The single most important proposition of Topic 2 — In re Berubari Union (DU case 8): cession of Indian territory to a foreign State requires a constitutional amendment under Article 368; executive action alone is unconstitutional, and an ordinary law under Article 3 is incompetent.

4.1 Cession in international law — the concept

“Cession of national territory in law amounts to the transfer of sovereignty over the said territory by the owner State in favour of another State. … a sovereign State can exercise its right to cede a part of its territory to a foreign State. This power … is subject to the limitations which the Constitution of the State may either expressly or by necessary implication impose … the treaty-making power would have to be exercised in the manner contemplated by the Constitution.” Gajendragadkar J., In re Berubari Union, DU case material, para 31 (citing Oppenheim’s International Law, Lauterpacht, Vol. I, pp. 551, 553, 8th Edn.)
  • Two essential attributes of sovereignty under international law: power to acquire foreign territory and power to cede national territory (Berubari para 30).
  • Hardship of cession — inhabitants lose old citizenship “whether they like it or not”; mitigable by an option to emigrate (Oppenheim, ibid., cited in para 31). Later rejected as compensation-claim basis in Ram Kishore Sen (see case 9).
  • In India, sovereignty-attributes are exercised through the Constitution: acquisition automatically integrates (1(3)(c)); cession runs only via Art. 368.

4.2 The Berubari ruling in one board

The wrong routes (rejected)

  • Executive agreement alone — rejected: the agreement itself is a cession; implementation needs legislation (Q1: “Yes”); the Court did not even reach the AG’s “executive power” defence once cession was found (para 26).
  • Art. 3 law — rejected: 3(c) “diminution” postulates the area stays in India (para 41); anomaly — cession of a UT would need 368 anyway (para 42); Assam–Bhutan instance (Act 47 of 1951) is no legislative practice (para 43). Q2(a): incompetent.
  • Preamble / 1(3)(c) prohibition (Chatterjee) — rejected: Preamble is not a source of substantive power or prohibition; 1(3)(c) recognizes acquisition, and Art. 368’s power to amend includes Art. 1, hence the power to cede exists (paras 27–30).

The right route (upheld)

  • Art. 368 constitutional amendment altering Art. 1 + First Schedule (entry for West Bengal), passed by special majority — Q2(b): competent and necessary.
  • Alternative: Parliament may first amend Art. 3 (by Art. 368) to expressly cover cession, then pass the implementing law under the amended Art. 3 — Q2(c)/Q3(c): both laws only in that sequence.
  • Actually done: Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960 — the canonical cession instrument.

Section 5 · Creation & Extinction of StatesPower to Create or Extinguish a State

States are “creatures of the Constitution” — they can be born (Art. 2/3(a)), divided, merged, renamed, and (possibly) extinguished; but their statehood character and the Seventh-Schedule distribution are protected by higher thresholds.

5.1 Modes of creating a State

Mode A — Art. 2 (new unit enters)

Admission/establishment “on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit”. Classic: Sikkim (1975) admitted via 36th Amendment; terms tested for basic-structure compatibility in Poudyal.

Mode B — Art. 3(a) (internal carving)

(i) separation of territory from a State (Telangana from AP); (ii) uniting two or more States or parts of States (the composite Bombay State assembled in 1956; Punjab reorganisation 1966); (iii) uniting any territory to part of a State (Chandernagore to West Bengal, 1954).

Mode C — 1(3)(c) + assimilation

Acquired foreign territory enters India automatically, then a law under Art. 2/3(a) constitutes it as a State or merges it (Berubari paras 34–35; Goa: UT 1961 → State 1987).

5.2 Modes of extinguishing a State

Extinction in fact

A State “ceases to exist” when it is divided to create two or more new States — e.g., Bombay divided into Gujarat & Maharashtra (Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960); the old composite Hyderabad State dissolved in 1956 (its territories shared among Andhra, Maharashtra and Karnataka under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956); the original Part A/B/C States themselves (1956). The entity’s name disappears — but statehood as a character of the polity survives in the successors.

Extinction of the character of statehood

“A constituent unit can be said to lose its character as a State only if it is converted into a Union territory in full, with no part of it retaining statehood. A change in boundaries or name does not change that character — it derives not from name/boundaries but from the relationship with the Union” (Art. 370 Judgment, para 499). Conversion of a State wholly into UTs: J&K 2019 — question whether Art. 3 permits it was expressly LEFT OPEN (para 504).

Three constitutional speed-bumps:
  1. Seventh Schedule entrenchment — Art. 3 cannot strip a State’s List II powers (State of Himachal Pradesh v. UOI, quoted at para 501); division of powers changes require Art. 368 + ratification by half the States (Art. 368(2), para 502).
  2. Federalism & representative democracy are basic features (paras 481–484; Bommai, Kuldip Nayar, Delhi, Swaraj Abhiyan cited) — animate any reading of Art. 3’s outer limits.
  3. Art. 2 terms must fit the basic structure (Poudyal) — Parliament’s discretion is wide but not unreviewable.

5.3 The J&K laboratory (2019) — Art. 3 at its most stressed

  • Proclamation under Art. 356 (19 Dec 2018) vested the State Legislature’s powers in Parliament and suspended both provisos to Art. 3 as applied to J&K (para 505).
  • Second proviso to Art. 3 (as applied to J&K) — inserted by CO 48 of 1954: no Bill to increase/diminish J&K’s area or alter its name/boundary could be introduced without the consent of the J&K legislature (para 512) — an asymmetry unique to J&K.
  • First proviso compliance: President referred the Reorganisation Bill to Lok Sabha & Rajya Sabha themselves (5 Aug 2019) because Parliament exercised the State Legislature’s powers under the Proclamation; both Houses “resolved to accept” the Bill (paras 507, 510–511 — Babulal Parate: views are recommendatory, so Parliament substituting its views was valid, not mala fide).
  • CO 272 (5 Aug 2019) — Art. 370 rendered inoperative: all “exceptions and modifications” (including CO 48’s second proviso) ceased the moment the Bill was introduced; the second-proviso issue became spent (para 513).
  • Held: Ladakh UT upheld — Art. 3(a) read with Explanation I permits forming a UT by separation of territory from a State (para 503); elections directed by 30 Sep 2024; restoration of J&K statehood promised “at the earliest”; the deeper question (may Parliament extinguish statehood altogether?) left open.

Section 6 · Name, Area & BoundaryAlteration of Name, Area and Boundary of Existing States — Procedure

The bread-and-butter of Article 3: renaming States, adjusting areas, redrawing boundaries — always by ordinary law, always with the proviso’s procedural choreography.

6.1 The three-step procedure in detail

Step 1 — Presidential recommendation

“No Bill for the purpose shall be introduced in either House … except on the recommendation of the President.” A Bill introduced without it is constitutionally dead on arrival (proviso, first limb). The recommendation is an executive act of the Union (Art. 74 — aid & advice).

Step 2 — Reference & views

If the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State → President refers the Bill to that State’s Legislature for expressing views within the period specified/allowed. Explanations I: a UT legislature gets no reference. Failure to respond within time does not block Parliament (implied; views advisory — Parate).

Step 3 — Passage & aftermath

Simple majority in both Houses; Presidential assent; under Art. 4 the law amends the First Schedule (territorial descriptions, names) and makes consequential amendments — without being an Art. 368 amendment and without state ratification (Berubari para 48).

Border disputes & Parliament’s slow sword

Assam–Arunachal (1986 onwards), Maharashtra–Karnataka (Marathi-speaking areas), UP–Uttarakhand, Assam–Meghalaya — Art. 3 altercations remain pending; Parliament is the only alterer of inter-State boundaries (no State may keep or acquire territory against an Art. 3 statute). The Supreme Court’s Constitution Bench in State of Assam v. State of Meghalaya (2024) dealt with the decades-old inter-State reference — reminding that until Parliament legislates under Art. 3, the proviso’s consultative machinery is the constitutional path, and views under it remain advisory.

Name changes ≠ jurisdictional chaos

Renaming under Art. 3(e) changes the First Schedule entry only; laws, High Court jurisdiction (Art. 214+), pending litigation and citizenship tie-ups continue seamlessly (Art. 4 consequential powers). Examples: Madras → Tamil Nadu (1973) also renamed the Madras High Court’s territorial references; Orissa → Odisha (2011) with all Central/State laws read accordingly.

DU Case 8 · Full text from DU case material (pp. 48–65) · Presidential Reference

In re Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves — AIR 1960 SC 845

(B.P. Sinha C.J., S.K. Das, P.B. Gajendragadkar, A.K. Sarkar, K. Subba Rao, M. Hidayatullah, K.C. Das Gupta and J.C. Shah JJ.) · Decided 14 March 1960 · Headnote in DU book: “Power to cede away Indian territory in favour of a foreign country — whether permissible; Procedure”

1. Facts & Background

The Reference. With a view to removing causes of tension between India and Pakistan on account of the boundary dispute, the Prime Ministers of both countries entered into the Indo-Pakistan Agreement (10-9-1958) settling the boundary dispute. A doubt arose whether implementation of the Agreement relating to Berubari Union required legislative action — a law under Article 3, or an amendment under Article 368, or both — and a similar doubt arose about the Agreement relating to the exchange of Enclaves. The President, in exercise of powers under Article 143(1), referred three questions to the Supreme Court for advice (verbatim from DU material):

  1. Is any legislative action necessary for the implementation of the Agreement relating to Berubari Union?
  2. If so, is a law of Parliament relatable to Article 3 sufficient, or is an amendment under Article 368 necessary, in addition or in the alternative?
  3. Is a law under Article 3 sufficient for the Exchange of Enclaves agreement, or is an Art. 368 amendment necessary, in addition or in the alternative?

The two agreement items in dispute (quoted in DU material):

  • Item 3 (para 2 of the Agreement): “Berubari Union 12: This will be so divided as to give half the area to Pakistan, the other half adjacent to India being retained by India. The Division … will be horizontal, starting from the north east corner of Debiganj Thana. The Division should be made in such a manner that the Cooch-Behar Enclaves between Pachagar Thana of East Pakistan and Berubari Union 12 of Jalpaiguri Thana … will remain connected as at present with Indian territory and will remain with India. The Cooch-Behar Enclaves lower down between Boda Thana of East Pakistan and Berubari Union 12 will be exchanged along with the general exchange of enclaves and will go to Pakistan.”
  • Item 10: “Exchange of old Cooch-Behar Enclaves in Pakistan and Pakistan Enclaves in India without claim to compensation for extra area going to Pakistan, is agreed to.”

Historical facts (paras 4–13): Berubari Union No. 12 — area 8.75 sq. miles, part of Jalpaiguri district — stayed with India after the Radcliffe Award (1947); the award’s map (Annexure B) and description (Annexure A) diverged, but the Chairman directed that the description prevails over the map (para 21). Boundary disputes went to the Indo-Pakistan Boundaries Disputes Tribunal (Lord Justice Bagge), whose Award (26-1-1950) never touched Berubari (para 8). Pakistan first raised the Berubari claim in 1952; correspondence continued until the Nehru–Noon Agreement of 10-9-1958 (para 9).

Enclave background through colonial legislation (paras 10–13 — the GoI Act 1935 chain):

  • s. 290, Government of India Act, 1935 — His Majesty may by Order-in-Council increase or diminish the area of any Province or alter its boundary (prescribed procedure). The GoI was authorised to exercise such powers through the Extra-Provincial Jurisdiction Act, 1947.
  • 12-1-1949 — GoI Act 1935 amended: ss. 290-A and 290-B inserted. s. 290-A: where full and exclusive authority for governance of any Indian State/Group is exercisable by the Dominion Government, the Governor-General may order the State(s) to be administered as a Chief Commissioner’s Province or as part of a Governor’s/Chief Commissioner’s Province. s. 290-B(1): Governor-General may direct that an acceding area within a Province be administered by a neighbouring acceding State as if it formed part of that State.
  • States Merger (Governors’ Provinces) Order, 1949 (27-7-1949); merger agreement with the Ruler of Cooch-Behar (28-8-1949), takeover 12-9-1949 (Cooch-Behar = Part C State No. 4); States Merger (West Bengal) Order, 1949 (31-12-1949) → from 1-1-1950 Cooch-Behar administered as part of West Bengal; First Schedule entry for West Bengal amended accordingly.
  • Post-partition, former Cooch-Behar areas became enclaves in Pakistan and vice-versa — a constant source of tension → Item 10’s exchange (para 13).

2. Issues

Q1. Is legislative action necessary to implement the Berubari Agreement?
Q2. If so — (a) is a law relatable to Article 3 sufficient; or (b) is an Article 368 amendment necessary (in addition or alternatively)?
Q3. Same question for the Exchange of Enclaves agreement.

Underlying wider issues: (i) does the Agreement amount to cession or mere ascertainment of boundary? (ii) can the Union executive implement it alone? (iii) does the Preamble / Art. 1(3)(c) bar cession altogether (Mr. Chatterjee’s extreme plea)?

3. Arguments — Attorney-General (for the Union of India)

  1. No legislative action needed — “ascertainment, not cession” (paras 14–16): the Agreement merely ascertains/delineates the exact boundary already fixed by the Radcliffe Award; yielding possession of some land to Pakistan is “merely a mode of settling the boundary”, not alienation or cession; no alteration of West Bengal’s First Schedule entry follows. Similarly, the enclave exchange is incidental to the broader settlement (para 15). Settlement of a boundary dispute by agreement in light of a binding award “cannot be treated as cession” — and can be effected by executive action alone (para 16).
  2. Legislative & executive toolkit (paras 17–18): Entry 14, List I (treaties & implementing them); Art. 253 (Parliament may legislate to implement treaties); Arts. 245(1)–(2), 246, 248 (residuary); executive power under Arts. 53(1), 73(1)(a)–(b), 74 is co-extensive with legislative power — relying on Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab AIR 1955 SC 549 (Mukherjea C.J.: executive function includes “the direction of foreign policy”).
  3. Art. 3 wide enough (para 41): the words of Art. 3(c) (“diminish the area of any State”) are wide enough to cover cession of national territory causing diminution of a State’s area.
  4. “Special features” argument (para 32): India’s basic structure mirrors the GoI Act 1935; constituent units were created without organic roots; emphasis on territorial integrity of States is absent; Art. 3 was designed exactly for redistribution after integration of princely States — so reading Art. 3 broadly is consistent with the Constitution’s design.
  5. No First Schedule change (para 24): Berubari was never legally included in West Bengal’s territorial description, so implementation needs no Schedule change.
  6. Assam–Bhutan precedent (para 43): Parliament itself passed Act 47 of 1951 under Art. 3 to cede ~32 sq. miles of Dewangiri (Kamrup district, Assam) to Bhutan — s. 2 ceded the strip & altered boundaries, s. 3 amended the First Schedule.
  7. Art. 3 proviso benefit (para 47): using Art. 368 would deprive the West Bengal Legislature of its chance to express views — an argument for the Art. 3 route.

4. Arguments — Mr. Chatterjee (intervener) & reply of the Court

Chatterjee’s “even Parliament cannot cede” plea (para 27): neither ordinary legislation nor even constitutional amendment can cede Indian territory; the Agreement is void absolutely. Grounds: (i) the Preamble (“sovereign democratic republic”) makes India’s territory inviolable & eternally democratic-republican — Parliament cannot touch it; (ii) Art. 1(3)(c) expressly provides for acquisition but is silent on cession → expressio unius est exclusio alterius bars cession.

Court’s rejection (paras 28–30):

  • Preamble is “a key to open the mind of the makers” (Story) but not a source of substantive power — nor of prohibitions/limitations (Willoughby); at most an aid where articles are ambiguous. No limitation on the sovereign attribute of cession is imported.
  • Art. 1(3)(c) does not confer the power to acquire — it formally provides for absorption/integration of territory acquired by India’s inherent right. Acquisition and cession are both essential attributes of sovereignty outside the text.
  • Art. 368 answers the silence: “The power to amend the Constitution must inevitably include the power to amend Article 1, and that logically would include the power to cede national territory.” Hence no constitutional impossibility — but also no route short of Art. 368.

5. Decision — the Court’s reasoning (paras 19–49)

  1. The Agreement is a cession, not boundary-ascertainment (paras 19–23): “There is no trace in the Agreement of any attempt to interpret the award… it had been decided that India should give half the area to Pakistan”; the agreement was “reached independently of the award and for reasons and considerations which appeared to the parties to be wise and expedient.” → “It is an Agreement by which a part of the territory of India has been ceded to Pakistan” (para 22); the enclave exchange is a fortiori cession (para 23).
  2. First Schedule is affected (para 24): Berubari was in Indian possession and treated as part of West Bengal → it falls within the territories comprised in the Province of West Bengal immediately before the Constitution → implementation alters West Bengal’s boundary and Entry 13 in the First Schedule.
  3. Executive-power defence not reached (para 26): since the Agreement is cession, it is unnecessary to examine whether the Union executive could have acted alone — the AG conceded that argument only on the “mere ascertainment” assumption.
  4. Article 4 sets the frame (para 36): laws under Arts. 2/3 are not Art. 368 amendments — so if competent under Art. 3, no Art. 368 needed; if not competent under Art. 3, “Article 368 would inevitably apply.” Crux: can Parliament legislate on the Agreement under Art. 3?
  5. Article 3 = internal adjustment (paras 38–40): Art. 3 “broadly … deals with the internal adjustment inter se of the territories of the constituent States of India”; 3(a) modes include uniting any territory (thus acquired foreign territory) to part of a State; 3(b) increase may come from 1(3)(c) territory; 3(d) consequential boundary alteration; 3(e) name — “no material bearing”. Art. 3 in terms did not refer to Union territories → UTs outside 3(b)–(e)’s purview (para 40).
  6. Article 3(c) cannot bear cession (paras 41–42): “The diminution of the area of any State to which it refers postulates that the area diminished … should and must continue to be a part of the territory of India.” Attributes of sovereignty (acquire & cede) are outside the Constitution; reading cession into 3(c) is “unduly straining the language”. Anomaly test: cession of a Union territory’s part would need Art. 368 anyway — illogical to allow cession of State territory by Art. 3 law.
  7. Assam–Bhutan instance rejected (para 43): a single statute is not “legislative practice”; cannot illuminate Art. 3’s scope.
  8. Conclusion (para 44) + AG’s concession: “it would not be competent to Parliament to make a law relatable to Article 3 … for … implementing the Agreement. It is conceded by the learned Attorney-General that this conclusion must inevitably mean that the law necessary … has to be passed under Article 368.”
  9. The two lawful paths (para 46): (i) Parliament may make a law under Art. 368 altering Art. 1 & the First Schedule (diminution of Union territory); or (ii) Parliament may first amend Art. 3 (via Art. 368) to cover cession, then pass the implementing law under the amended Art. 3.
  10. Proviso-to-Art. 368 safeguard & its limits (paras 47–48): Art. 368 route demands special majority in each House — “the consent of the major part of the parties … a safeguard”. But the state-ratification proviso does not apply: neither Art. 1 nor Art. 3 is in the entrenched list — “It is not for us to enquire … whether it would be appropriate to include the said two articles … That is a matter for Parliament to consider and decide.”
  11. Answers (para 49): Q1: Yes (legislative action necessary). Q2(a): Art. 3 law incompetent; Q2(b): Art. 368 law competent and necessary; Q2(c): laws under both only if Parliament first amends Art. 3. Q3: same as Q2(a)–(c) (exchange of enclaves likewise).
Principles evolved (learn verbatim-ready)
  1. Cession = transfer of sovereignty; an agreement of cession cannot be consummated by executive action — legislative action is indispensable (Q1).
  2. Art. 3 is for internal adjustment only; its “diminution” postulates the area remaining in India — Parliament cannot cede national territory by an Art. 3 law (paras 41–44).
  3. Cession requires an Art. 368 amendment of Art. 1 + First Schedule (Q2(b)); route used: Ninth Amendment, 1960.
  4. Preamble & Art. 1(3)(c) impose no absolute bar on cession — sovereignty includes cession; Art. 368’s amending power includes amending Art. 1 (paras 27–30).
  5. Acquisition is automatic under 1(3)(c); Art. 2/3 assimilates acquired territory by law (paras 30, 34–35) — Chandernagore illustration.
  6. Art. 1/3 lie outside Art. 368’s ratification proviso — no State gets a veto over territorial diminution (para 48).
  7. Facts control characterization: a political half-and-half settlement is cession, not “ascertainment” (paras 19–22).
★ Bonus: Chandernagore Merger Act, 1954 — the Court’s own worked example (para 35)

Chandernagore (French possession) — declared a free city; June 1946 France intimated intent to let the people decide; referendum, 1949: people voted for merger with India; 2-5-1950: de facto transfer of administration to India; GoI assumed control under s. 4 of the Foreign Jurisdiction Act, 1947 (notification applying Indian laws; French laws to cease); treaty of cession signed at Paris; de jure transfer on ratification, 9-6-1952 (Foreign Jurisdiction Act ceased to apply); then Art. 243(1) applied; the President promulgated a regulation under Art. 243(2) (in force 30-6-1952); a Commission of Inquiry found near-unanimous favour for merger with West Bengal; Parliament passed the Chandernagore Merger Act, 1954 (Act 37 of 1954) — enacted under Article 3: West Bengal’s boundaries altered under Art. 3(d), First Schedule modified by s. 4; merger effective 2 October 1954. → “significantly illustrates the operation of Article 1(3)(c) as well as Article 3(b) and (d).”

★ What happened after: Ninth Amendment & the DU “Additional Readings” trail

Parliament passed the Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960 to give effect to the Indo-Pak Agreement. Its validity was attacked (confusing/impossible language re Berubari Union No. 12; deprivation of citizenship and property without compensation) → dismissed in Ram Kishore Sen (case 9), Gajendragadkar C.J. holding the advisory opinion binding. The enclave-exchange limb of the Ninth Amendment was never fully worked out with Bangladesh-successor Pakistan issues — leading to the Tin Bigha litigation (case 10) and, eventually, the Land Boundary Agreement implemented by the Hundredth Amendment Act, 2015 (111 Indian enclaves ↔ 51 Bangladeshi enclaves; citizenship rights to enclave residents).

DU Case 9 · DU “Additional Readings” note

Ram Kishore Sen v. Union of India — AIR 1966 SC 644 : (1966) 1 SCR 430

Constitution Bench · P.B. Gajendragadkar C.J. speaking for the Court · Articles 1, 3, 368, 141 · Challenge to the Ninth Amendment, 1960

1. Facts

  • After the Berubari advisory opinion (1960), Parliament passed the Ninth Amendment Act, 1960: its Second Schedule divided Berubari Union No. 12 (half to Pakistan, half retained by India — division “horizontal, starting from the north east corner of Debiganj Thana”) and also dealt with transfer of the village of Chilahati (~512 acres, part of Debiganj Thana, allotted to Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award but not then physically transferred).
  • Aggrieved residents of the affected belt moved litigation (starting in the Calcutta High Court, carried to the Supreme Court) seeking to restrain survey, demarcation and transfer — arguing that (i) the Amendment Act’s language re Berubari Union No. 12 was confusing and incapable of implementation (no mathematically “horizontal” line possible), (ii) official maps were withheld/suppressed and a “wall map” showed a different position, (iii) the transfer would deprive citizens of citizenship and property without compensation, (iv) the Berubari advisory opinion was not binding (advisory jurisdiction opinions bind nobody), and (v) adverse possession of the land by Indian residents.
  • Respondents (Union/State) urged: the relevant portions of the Ninth Amendment are clear and implementable; “horizontal” division is not a mathematical line but a direction to divide half-and-half beginning from the NE corner of Debiganj Thana; Chilahati lies within Thana Debiganj per the Radcliffe Award and was always due to Pakistan; onus to show implementability lay on them — and they discharged it with official maps/records.

2. Issues

(i) Is the advisory opinion in In re Berubari binding on courts — or merely advice with no precedential force?
(ii) Is the Ninth Amendment’s provision for division of Berubari Union No. 12 capable of implementation, or so vague as to be a nullity?
(iii) Can transfer be stopped on the ground of deprivation of citizenship/property without compensation, or on adverse possession?

3. Arguments

Appellants: Art. 143 opinions are consultative only — they do not bind anybody; the Ninth Amendment’s wording (“horizontal … north east corner of Debiganj Thana”) demands a mathematically exact division which is impossible on the ground; official maps deliberately withheld; the taking-away of land and citizenship without compensation is unconstitutional; adverse possession over the villages; mandamus should restrain respondents from any survey/demarcation/transfer of Berubari Union No. 12 and Chilahati.
Respondents (UOI/State): Berubari was decided by eight Judges and Parliament legislated precisely in terms of it — the opinion is binding as the constitutional position (Art. 141); “horizontal” denotes direction (east–west division beginning at the NE corner), not geometric precision; the amendment means “half-and-half”, starting from the stated corner; Chilahati is part of Debiganj Thana per the award and had always been meant for Pakistan; the onus of proving unimplementability is on the challengers — official records/maps displace the private “wall map”; adverse possession has no place in international law.

4. Decision

  • Appeal dismissed — Gajendragadkar C.J. speaking for the Court (5-Judge Bench): the advisory opinion in In re Berubari is binding (DU note); it states the constitutional position on cession and was the express foundation of the Ninth Amendment.
  • The relevant portions of the Ninth Amendment (Second Schedule) are clear and capable of implementation: Berubari Union No. 12 to be divided half-and-half, starting from the north-east corner of Debiganj Thana — a substantive, not mathematical, direction; “horizontal” does not require a precise geometric line (the division runs as stated from the east/west directions agreed).
  • Chilahati transfer upheld — part of Debiganj Thana allotted to Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award; ~512 acres due for transfer; no valid objection (private wall map rejected against official maps/records).
  • Deprivation of citizenship/property without compensation — rejected; adverse possession — no defence under international law; theory that maps were “deliberately withdrawn” not accepted.
  • Respondents were entitled to proceed with survey/demarcation and implementation of the transfer; prayer for mandamus refused.
Principle evolved
  1. A Presidential Reference opinion under Art. 143(1) on the Constitution’s meaning binds courts and grounds legislative action — the Ninth Amendment’s validity is tested against it, not re-opened.
  2. Constitutional-amendment language implementing an international settlement is read purposively and practically — “half-and-half” is workable; mere difficulty of demarcation doesn’t invalidate cession legislation.
  3. Reaffirms Berubari’s cession doctrine and closes the door on Preamble-based impossibility arguments: citizenship/property consequences of cession do not render the amendment unconstitutional.
DU Case 10 · DU “Additional Readings” note

Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta — AIR 1990 SC 1692 : 1990 Supp SCC 545

Judges (per report): K. Jagannatha Shetty, K.N. Saikia and S.C. Agrawal JJ. · Articles 1(3)(c), 3, 368 · Tin Bigha corridor — “lease in perpetuity” to Bangladesh

1. Facts

  • The Ninth Amendment’s enclave-exchange was never fully given effect: old Cooch-Behar enclaves (Dahagram–Angarpota and others) remained as Pakistani/Bangladeshi pockets; after 1971, East Pakistan became Bangladesh.
  • To connect the Bangladeshi enclave of Dahagram–Panbari Mouja to Bangladesh proper, the two Governments signed the Agreement of 16 May 1974, by which India agreed to lease in perpetuity the area at Tin Bigha (Indian territory); the Agreement of 1982 spelled out the terms: Bangladesh would have undisturbed possession and use of the leased area for the stated purposes — but sovereignty over the area would remain with India; Bangladesh’s rights were expressly restricted.
  • Challengers invoked the Berubari template and obtained directions from the Calcutta High Court — including a direction that the Constitution be amended before giving effect to the arrangements (and further legislative steps regarding Berubari-type transfers). The Union of India appealed to the Supreme Court.

2. Issues

(i) Does implementation of the 1974/1982 agreements — labelled “lease in perpetuity” — amount to cessation of Indian territory requiring a constitutional amendment (Berubari)?
(ii) Does the arrangement transfer sovereignty (in whole or part) to Bangladesh?
(iii) Is mere executive action sufficient to implement it — or is parliamentary legislation needed?

3. Arguments

Respondents (challengers): the Tin Bigha area is Indian territory; handing “perpetual” possession and use to a foreign State is cession or at least transfer of sovereignty in substance; on the Berubari reasoning, any diminution of Indian territory’s availability demands Art. 368 amendment; the High Court was right to insist on constitutional/legislative action.
Union of India: the agreements expressly retain sovereignty with India; Bangladesh gets only defined rights of access/use for specified purposes (clause 3 of the 1982 Agreement is purpose-limited); no territory is “given away”; the Ninth Amendment itself was premised on exchange arrangements never consummated for these enclaves; therefore no cession and no amendment is required — executive implementation is lawful.

4. Decision

  • Implementation of the 1974 and 1982 agreements “did not amount to cession of any Indian territory which would require any constitutional amendment” (headnote) — the High Court’s directions requiring amendment/legislation were modified/reversed.
  • “There was no question of transfer of sovereignty, wholly or partially, in respect of the said area. What had merely been done was to enable the Government of Bangladesh and its nationals to exercise certain rights … which otherwise they would not have been entitled to do” (paras 5–6).
  • The “lease in perpetuity” does not amount to a lease or surrender of sovereignty as understood in international law (para 25): India’s rights are merely restricted to serve Bangladesh’s interests → in international-law terms this is a “servitude” — not cession.
  • Therefore the agreements could be implemented by mere executive action — no parliamentary law, no Art. 368 amendment.
Principle evolved
  1. The sovereignty test, not the label: everything turns on whether sovereignty is transferred. “Perpetual lease” on paper ≠ cession if sovereignty is retained — servitudes (access, use, corridor rights) may be created by executive agreement.
  2. Berubari confined but not shaken: Berubari applies to transfers of sovereignty; arrangements short of that (Tin Bigha corridor; border-access packages) need no constitutional amendment.
  3. Provides the constitutional template for India’s cross-border corridor/enclave arrangements — the very logic later used for the 2015 Land Boundary Agreement (implemented by the Hundredth Amendment where the First Schedule did change).

N.M. Sahib v. Chief Commissioner, Pondicherry — AIR 1962 SC 797

Constitution Bench (majority; Sarkar and Das Gupta JJ. contra) · Article 1(3)(c) “territories as may be acquired” · French India’s integration

1. Facts

  • France’s Indian Establishments (Pondicherry, Karaikal, Mahe, Yanam) were de facto transferred to Indian administration under the Agreement of 21 October 1954 between the French Republic and the Government of the Indian Union; Indian authorities exercised full administrative control thereafter (a Chief Commissioner administered the territory), and Indian laws were extended to the Establishments by executive notifications — the Foreign Jurisdiction Act, 1947 route used for Chandernagore (Berubari, para 35).
  • Residents of Pondicherry faced enforcement/action under Indian laws (including preventive-detention style actions by the Chief Commissioner’s administration); litigation reached the Supreme Court by way of appeals and writ petitions.
  • Critically, the treaty of cession had not yet been ratified (French parliamentary ratification came only in 1962); the Constitution had not yet listed Pondicherry in the First Schedule (that came with the Constitution (Fourteenth Amendment) Act, 1962).
  • The Supreme Court, exercising powers under Art. 142 (and having regard to Evidence Act, s. 57(10)), forwarded a question to the Union Government about the status of Pondicherry; the answer received was treated as binding on the Court — the Government’s position was that only administrative control had passed, not territory.

2. Issues

(i) Is Pondicherry part of the “territory of India” — is it territory “acquired” within Article 1(3)(c)?
(ii) Does the Indian Constitution apply automatically to a territory taken over de facto under an international agreement, without formal legal steps?
(iii) Consequently, do appeals/writ petitions from Pondicherry lie to the Supreme Court?

3. Arguments

Appellants/petitioners: Indian administration, laws and courts have been operating in Pondicherry since 1954; the territory is part of India; Indian fundamental rights and constitutional machinery apply; the Supreme Court should entertain appeals/writs from there.
Union Government (via the forwarded reply) & respondents: the 1954 Agreement transferred power of Government/administration, not territory — the common intention of the parties was not to transfer territory at that stage; unless and until the treaty is ratified there is legally no transfer of territory; without transfer of territory there can be no “acquisition of territory” under Art. 1(3)(c); formal constitutional steps (First Schedule integration) are still awaited.

4. Decision

  • Majority (Sarkar and Das Gupta JJ. contra): where the exercise of power/administration “is referable to an agreement between two States”, whether the territory has integrated depends wholly on the terms on which the new Government is invited to exercise control (para 13).
  • Under the Agreement dated 21-10-1954, though complete administrative control passed to India over the French Establishments, “this transfer of control cannot be equated to a transfer of territory, that being the common intention of the parties” (para 13).
  • “Unless a ratification takes place there will legally be no transfer of territory and without a transfer of territory there will not be … an ‘acquisition of territory’, with the consequence that at present Pondicherry has to be treated as not part of the territory of India” (para 13).
  • Hence the Supreme Court had no jurisdiction to entertain the appeals, and (per the majority) the writ petitions also could not be entertained (para 15) — while reaffirming that once a fundamental right to a writ is established, the Court has no discretion to refuse it ex debito justitiae (the general principle stated in the judgment).
Principle evolved
  1. De facto ≠ de jure: administrative takeover under an international agreement does not, by itself, integrate territory into India. Constitutional integration requires formal legal steps — ratification of the cession treaty and/or constitutional action (First Schedule).
  2. Art. 1(3)(c) is not self-executing for negotiated takeovers: “acquisition” presupposes a completed transfer under the agreement’s terms; the Constitution does not automatically stretch over newly administered lands.
  3. Practical consequence: Parliament cured the gap through the Constitution (Fourteenth Amendment) Act, 1962, adding the four French Establishments to the First Schedule as Union territories (Art. 240 framework) — after which Indian laws, courts and fundamental rights applied fully. (Compare Chandernagore: de facto 1950 → treaty ratified 9-6-1952 → Merger Act 1954.)
DU Case 12 · DU “Additional Readings” note

R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India — AIR 1993 SC 1804 : 1994 Supp (1) SCC 324

Five-Judge Constitution Bench (leading opinion of Venkatachaliah J., per case commentaries) · Article 2 · Constitution (Thirty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1975 · Article 371F

1. Facts

  • Sikkim (monarchy under the Chogyal) was admitted into India in 1975 following a referendum, through the Constitution (Thirty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1975 — which added Sikkim as a State in the First Schedule and inserted Art. 371F (special provisions for Sikkim in view of its unique history, including savings for laws prevailing at admission and Assembly provisions protecting backward classes).
  • The Sikkim Legislative Assembly (32 seats) had special arrangements: 12 seats reserved for Bhutia-Lepchas, 1 seat for Buddhist Sanghas (elected by a separate roll of monastery representatives), 2 for Scheduled Castes, 17 general.
  • The petitioner — a Nepali Sikkimese — challenged the reservation scheme and the conditions of Sikkim’s admission: the Bhutia-Lepcha and Sangha reservations were based on ethnicity/religion, violated Arts. 14, 15(1), 325 (no religion-based electoral distinctions), the one-person-one-vote and secular-democratic basic structure, and produced disproportion (e.g., ~38% of seats for ~20% of the population); Parliament’s “terms and conditions” power under Art. 2 could not license such departures; the Courts could review admission terms.
  • The Union defended the scheme as necessary to preserve Sikkim’s distinctive demographic-cultural identity and polity — the basis on which admission was negotiated.

2. Issues

(i) What is the scope of “such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” in Art. 2 — are they justiciable?
(ii) May Parliament reserve seats on ethnic/religious criteria while creating/admitting a State — violative of Arts. 14/15/325 and secularism?
(iii) Are the special provisions of Art. 371F and the 36th Amendment contrary to the basic structure (democracy, secularism, equality)?

3. Arguments

Petitioners: reservations for Bhutia-Lepchas (12) and Sangha (1) discriminate against the Nepali Sikkimese majority; ethnic/religion-based seats violate Art. 15(1) and the secular common roll of Art. 325; the ratio between seats and population (as in Arts. 330(2), 332(3)) is the constitutional norm — “excessive representation” creates unequal status; the conditions attached to admission under Art. 2 cannot exceed permissible constitutional limits; courts must be able to review terms of admission, else Parliament’s discretion is absolute.
Respondents (Union & State of Sikkim): Sikkim’s unique history and the need to protect its indigenous minorities justified the arrangement; Art. 2 expressly lets Parliament impose “terms and conditions as it thinks fit”; Art. 371F itself is a constitutional affirmation of the special settlement; the classifications rest on intelligible differences tied to Sikkim’s social reality, furthering political stability and genuine representation — a legitimate objective; conditions of a newly admitted State may legitimately differ from those of other States.

4. Decision

  • DU’s operative proposition (must quote): “Under Article 2, Parliament has power to admit into the Union or establish new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. The terms and conditions must be consistent with the basic structure of the Constitution. The admission of Sikkim by Constitution (Thirty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1975 was unsuccessfully challenged.”
  • The Court treated Art. 2’s power as flexible but reviewable: Parliament’s admission terms are subject to judicial review against the basic-structure standard — but the terms here passed that test.
  • Art. 371F upheld: the special provisions (and the reservation scheme) rest on historical and demographic classifications amounting to intelligible differentia, serving Sikkim’s integration; they do not destroy democracy or secularism.
  • A newly admitted State may differ from pre-existing States in its internal arrangements, provided the arrangement is not incompatible with the constitutional institutions the Constitution envisages.
Principle evolved
  1. Article 2 discretion is wide, not unlimited: “terms and conditions” are subject to basic-structure review — the admission of a State is a justiciable constitutional act.
  2. Parliament may craft special safeguards for vulnerable regional communities as part of admission terms; such schemes are protected if reasonably related to the State’s unique history (intelligible differentia).
  3. The 36th Amendment (Sikkim) is valid — completing the trilogy: Berubari (territory out: no cession without 368) · Poudyal (territory/state in: terms within basic structure) · Parate (internal changes: advisory procedure).
DU Case 13 · DU “Additional Readings” note

Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay — AIR 1960 SC 51 : (1960) SCR 605

Five-Judge Bench · Article 3 proviso · States Reorganisation Act, 1956 · Art. 226 writ

1. Facts

  1. The States Reorganisation Bill, 1956, introduced on the President’s recommendation under the proviso to Art. 3, proposed three separate units: (i) a Union Territory of Bombay, (ii) State of Maharashtra (including Marathwada & Vidarbha), (iii) State of Gujarat (including Saurashtra & Cutch).
  2. The Bill was referred to the Legislature of Bombay for expressing its views (proviso requirement satisfied on the original Bill), and also to a Joint Select Committee of both Houses.
  3. The Joint Select Committee recommended a composite State of Bombay instead of three units; the amended Bill was passed by both Houses and received assent → States Reorganisation Act, 1956, s. 8(1) formed a single (Part A) State of Bombay.
  4. Babulal Parate filed a petition under Art. 226 in the Bombay High Court contending that the composite-State formation violated the proviso to Art. 3: the Bombay Legislature had expressed views only on the original three-unit Bill, not on the changed proposal — a fresh referral was mandatory. The High Court dismissed the petition.
  5. He carried the matter to the Supreme Court.

2. Issues

(i) Does the proviso to Art. 3 require the modified Bill (composite Bombay instead of three units) to be referred afresh to the State Legislature?
(ii) Does “the Bill” in the proviso include subsequent substantial amendments made by Parliament?
(iii) Are the views of the State Legislature binding on Parliament?

3. Arguments

Appellant (Parate): “State” in Art. 3 means not merely a territory but the people living in it; the proviso embodies a democratic-process safeguard — a change as fundamental as converting a three-unit scheme into one composite State is a “new proposal” affecting the area/boundaries of the State; justice and fair play demand the altered proposal go back to the Bombay Legislature; otherwise the referral right is illusory.
State/Union: the proviso’s plain words require referring “the Bill” — the proposal contained in the Bill at the time of reference; there is no textual warrant for re-referencing amendments; the views are consultative only; requiring fresh referrals for every amendment would produce an “interminable process” (different Legislatures giving divergent views, endless re-referencing); the composite-State change was germane to, and discussed in, the Bill’s subject-matter of reorganisation.

4. Decision

  • The Court set out two conditions in the proviso: (1) introduction only on the President’s recommendation; (2) where the proposal affects area/boundaries/name of a State, the President must refer the proposal contained in the Bill to that State’s Legislature for its views within the prescribed period.
  • “On a true construction, the proviso does not contemplate that if Parliament subsequently modifies that proposal, there must be a fresh Bill or a fresh reference” — what the President must refer under the second condition is the proposal contained in the Bill, not later modifications. (DU note: “A Bill referred by the President … need not be referred again … if changes are made by Parliament in the original Bill.”)
  • Views are recommendatory, not binding: “Nor is there anything in the proviso to indicate that Parliament must accept or act upon the views of the State Legislature. Indeed, two State Legislatures may express totally divergent views …” (para 5 of the judgment, quoted in the Art. 370 Judgment, para 510); a contrary construction would create an “interminable process”.
  • No “special or recondite doctrine of ‘democratic process’” is embedded in the proviso; “State” means the States in the First Schedule — the provision is about legislative consultation, not about the populace at large.
  • Appeal dismissed: formation of the composite State of Bombay under s. 8(1) of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 did not contravene Art. 3; the Act stands.
Principle evolved
  1. One-shot reference: the Art. 3 proviso requires an initial reference of the original Bill; Parliament may amend the Bill thereafter — even substantially — without re-referral (changes must remain germane to the Bill’s subject of state-reorganisation).
  2. Consultation ≠ veto: State legislatures have no binding say over Art. 3 reorganisation — Parliament is supreme in the internal rearrangement of the Union’s constituents.
  3. Applied repeatedly since — most visibly in 2014 (AP Reorganisation Act: views sought, not awaited) and 2019 (J&K: Parliament itself furnished the “views” under the first proviso during President’s Rule — upheld in the Art. 370 Judgment, paras 505–511, relying on this case).

Section 9 · Advisory JurisdictionArticle 143 — Presidential Reference Procedure

Berubari came to the Court not as litigation but as a question from the President — the procedure itself is examinable.

9.1 Constitutional scheme

Art. 143(1) — discretionary reference

  • If the President is of opinion that a question of law or fact of public importance has arisen (or is likely to arise) which it is expedient to obtain the opinion of the Supreme Court, he may refer it; the Court may report its opinion.
  • Discretionary at both ends (“may”); opinion delivered by such bench as the Court determines — minimum five Judges (Art. 145(3) read with Art. 143).
  • Examples: Re Berubari Union (1960, 8 JJ), Re Kerala Education Bill (1958), Delhi Laws Act (1951), Kesavananda Bharati (1973, 13 JJ) — the biggest constitutional questions travelled this route.

Art. 143(2) — compulsory reference

  • Arises in a dispute (pre- or post-occurrence) involving a question on which the President requires opinion (e.g., inter-State water disputes routed to the Court if Parliament so provides; “ex officio” disputes).
  • Here the Court shall report its opinion (subject to Art. 143(1)’s exceptions — e.g., opinion actually decided by law? proviso: if the matter has been decided by the Court already or is pending before it, no report).
  • Art. 144: all authorities — civil, judicial, military — must act in aid of the Supreme Court’s opinion.
  • Art. 145: rules of procedure (bench, practice) made with Presidential approval.

PYQ 1 · LL.B. III Semester Examination, December 2024 · Paper Code LB-301 · Q1 · 20 marks (compulsory question)
“On the basis of an agreement between two sovereign nations, the Republic of Warli and the Republic of Banasthali, it was decided that 1000 square kilometres of territory belonging to Warli would be transferred to Banasthali. This transfer of territory was implemented through an executive action by the Republic of Warli. Discuss the constitutionality of this executive action. The constitution and laws of the Republic of Warli are in pari materia with the Constitution and laws of India. You are required to rely on relevant legal provision and judgments to support your answer.”
Model Answer — how to structure 20 marks
  1. Identify the issue (2–3 marks): inter-State cession of 1000 sq km implemented only by executive action — constitutional validity under a Constitution in pari materia with India’s (i.e., apply Arts. 1, 3, 4, 368, 73, 253 of the Indian Constitution).
  2. The governing framework (4 marks):
    • Art. 1(2)–(3): territory of India = States + UTs + territories that may be acquired; Art. 1 defines the Union’s territorial extent — any transfer diminishes Art. 1 + the First Schedule.
    • Cession as sovereign attribute: Berubari (AIR 1960 SC 845), Gajendragadkar J.: acquisition and cession are essential attributes of sovereignty (para 30) — but in a written Constitution they operate only through constitutional routes; treaty-making power is “subject to the limitations which the Constitution … impose[s]” (para 31).
    • Executive competence: Arts. 53, 73(1) executive power extends to matters of legislation (Entry 14, List I; Art. 253) — but Berubari Q1: even a valid agreement of cession cannot be implemented without legislation; the Court refused to bless executive-only implementation (para 26: the AG’s executive-power defence was expressly left undecided because the agreement was cession).
  3. Application to Warli (6–7 marks):
    • The agreement transfers sovereignty over 1000 sq km → it is a cession in international law (Berubari para 31), not a boundary “ascertainment” — on facts, a negotiated package between two sovereigns is exactly what the Berubari Court held to be cession, whatever its label (paras 19–22).
    • Executive action alone = unconstitutional: (i) legislative action is necessary (Q1: Yes); (ii) an ordinary law under Art. 3 is incompetent — Art. 3(c)’s “diminution” postulates the area stays within India, and cession of State/UT territory alike falls outside Art. 3 (paras 41–44); (iii) the only route is an Art. 368 amendment of Art. 1 + First Schedule by special majority (Q2(b); para 46) — India’s instance: Ninth Amendment, 1960.
    • Preamble/1(3)(c) arguments (Chatterjee) are unavailable/irrelevant — the Constitution does not forbid cession; it channels it through Art. 368 (paras 27–30).
    • Even the enclave-like alternatives: had the arrangement merely granted access/use while Warli retained sovereignty (as in UOI v. Sukumar Sengupta, AIR 1990 SC 1692 — Tin Bigha “perpetual lease” = servitude), executive action would suffice. But a transfer of territory to Banasthali crosses that line: cessation of territory → Berubari route.
    • Post-implementation consequences even if amended: inhabitants lose/change citizenship (Oppenheim hardship, Berubari para 31) — but compensation/citizenship claims do not invalidate the amendment (Ram Kishore Sen, AIR 1966 SC 644); the advisory opinion route itself binds (Art. 141 — same case).
  4. Counter-view & reply (2 marks): Warli’s executive may argue treaty-making is inherent in sovereignty and Art. 253 lets Parliament implement treaties. Reply: Berubari concedes legislative competence for treaties generally, yet holds that cession specifically requires constitutional amendment — ordinary legislation cannot shrink Art. 1 (paras 44, 46).
  5. Conclusion (2 marks): The executive action is unconstitutional. Valid implementation requires (a) an Art. 368 constitutional amendment (special majority; no ratification of States — Berubari para 48) altering Art. 1/First Schedule, as in the Ninth Amendment 1960 and Hundredth Amendment 2015; or (b) re-engineering the arrangement as a sovereignty-retaining servitude/lease implementable by executive agreement (Sukumar Sengupta). A bare executive transfer stands on no constitutional footing.
Marks magnet — quote these: “Yes” (Q1) · “A law … relatable to Art. 3 … would be incompetent” (Q2(a)) · “A law … relatable to Art. 368 … is competent and necessary” (Q2(b)) · “the power to cede national territory cannot be read in Article 3(c) by implication” (para 41) · Sukumar Sengupta’s servitude test · Ram Kishore Sen’s binding-opinion/validity holding.
PYQ 2 · Centenary Chance Examination (Jan 2023) · Q8(a) — answer any two of Q8(a)–(c)
“Procedure for creation of new State in India”
Model Answer (10-mark version)
  1. Opening line: Creation of new States is governed by Part I — Arts. 2, 3 and 4 — a self-contained code (Berubari para 33); Parliament is the sole organ; states are creatures of the Constitution.
  2. Route A — Article 2 (external/new units): “Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.” Used for units outside the existing State structure — e.g., admission of Sikkim by the Constitution (Thirty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1975; Parliament’s terms must be consistent with the basic structure (R.C. Poudyal, AIR 1993 SC 1804 — conditions justiciable, admission upheld). Art. 4: such a law is not an Art. 368 amendment.
  3. Route B — Article 3(a) (internal carving): Parliament may by law “form a new State by separation of territory from any State or by uniting two or more States or parts of States or by uniting any territory to a part of any State” — Explanation II extends the power to uniting parts of UTs. Examples: Nagaland (1962), Haryana (1966), Meghalaya (1971), Chhattisgarh–Uttarakhand–Jharkhand (2000), Telangana (Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014).
  4. Step-by-step procedure (Art. 3 proviso + Art. 4):
    1. The Bill is introduced in either House only on the President’s recommendation.
    2. If it affects the area, boundaries or name of an existing State, the President must refer it to that State’s Legislature to express its views within the specified/further period (Explanations I: no such reference for UTs; UTs count as “States” in clauses (a)–(e)).
    3. Views are consultative only — not binding; no fresh reference for later amendments (Babulal Parate, AIR 1960 SC 51).
    4. Passage by simple majority in both Houses; Presidential assent; Art. 4 permits amendment of the First/Fourth Schedules and consequential changes without Art. 368 procedure and without ratification by States (Berubari para 48).
  5. Special cases: territory acquired abroad enters automatically under Art. 1(3)(c) and is then constituted/absorbed by law under Art. 2 or Art. 3 (Berubari paras 34–35, Chandernagore); creating a Union territory from a State is covered by Art. 3(a) + Explanation I (Art. 370 Judgment para 503, Ladakh) — though whether statehood itself can be extinguished is left open (para 504).
  6. Illustrations to name-drop: Sikkim (1975, Art. 2 + 36th Amendment), Telangana (2014), the 2000 creations, Goa (1987), Bombay’s division (Bombay Reorganisation Act, 1960 — as validated through Parate’s framework), and the negative: no State may secede (Union of States; Art. 1).
  7. Closing sentence: Thus, creation of a new State = a political decision executed as ordinary legislation by Parliament with presidential recommendation, consultative reference, simple majority, and Schedule consequentialism — wide discretion, narrow procedure, judicially policed at the edges (Poudyal).

“…the agreement does not purport to be, and has not been, reached as a result of any interpretation of the award … It is an Agreement by which a part of the territory of India has been ceded to Pakistan.”Berubari, para 22 — kills the “boundary ascertainment” defence
“The diminution of the area of any State … postulates that the area diminished from the State in question should and must continue to be a part of the territory of India.”Berubari, para 41 — kills the Art. 3(c) route
“The power to amend the Constitution must inevitably include the power to amend Article 1, and that logically would include the power to cede national territory…”Berubari, para 30 — kills Chatterjee’s absolute bar
“…if the law … is to be passed under Article 368 … the Bill has to be passed … by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the House present and voting — … a safeguard provided by the Article in matters of this kind.”Berubari, para 47 — the procedural silver lining
“Nor is there anything in the proviso to indicate that Parliament must accept or act upon the views of the State Legislature. Indeed, two State Legislatures may express totally divergent views…”Babulal Parate, para 5 (quoted in Art. 370 J. para 510)

12.5 Examiner’s checklist — tick before you submit

In every long answer

  • Name the Article (1/2/3/4/368/143) and clause for every proposition.
  • Anchor on Berubari with its three Q&A formulation.
  • Distinguish at least one: cession vs lease/servitude (Sengupta), or Art. 2 vs Art. 3, or consultation vs veto (Parate).
  • Mention one instrument (9th/100th Amendment, SRA 1956, Foreign Jurisdiction Act, Chandernagore Act).
  • Cite one post-2000 example (Telangana/J&K/2000 States) for currency.

Common traps

  • ✗ “Art. 3 can cede territory” — no (Berubari 41–44).
  • ✗ “State legislature’s consent needed for Art. 3” — no; views only (Parate).
  • ✗ “Art. 368 ratification needed for Art. 1/First Schedule” — no (Berubari 48).
  • ✗ “Preamble forbids cession” — rejected (paras 28–29).
  • ✗ “Tin Bigha was cession” — no; servitude (Sengupta).
  • ✗ “Art. 1(3)(c) grants the power to acquire” — it only integrates (Berubari 30).
