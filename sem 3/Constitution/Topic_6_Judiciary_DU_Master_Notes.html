Part V • Chapter IV

The Union Judiciary: Supreme Court of India (Articles 124 – 147)

An exhaustive clause-by-clause constitutional and jurisprudential analysis of the composition, appointment, removal, conditions of service, and procedural regulation of the Supreme Court of India.

Core Institutional Anchor

Article 124: Establishment and Constitution of the Supreme Court

Foundational charter of India's apex judicial organ

§ Clause-by-Clause Detailed Breakdown:

  • Article 124(1) – Composition & Numerical Strength: There shall be a Supreme Court of India consisting of a Chief Justice of India and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, of not more than seven other Judges.
    Parliamentary Expansion: Parliament has enlarged the strength through the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Acts: 10 (1956), 13 (1960), 17 (1977), 25 (1986), 30 (2008), and currently 33 Judges + 1 CJI = 34 Judges via the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019.
  • Article 124(2) – Appointment & Constitutional Consultation: Every Judge of the Supreme Court shall be appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal on the recommendation of the National Judicial Appointments Commission referred to in Article 124A (struck down in 2015; restored to original text: "after consultation with such of the Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary for the purpose").
    Mandatory Proviso: In the appointment of a Judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted.
    Judicial Reconstruction: The term "consultation" has been judicially interpreted in the Three Judges Cases and NJAC Case to mean "concurrence", through a participatory collegiate body (CJI + 4 senior-most Judges).
  • Article 124(2A) – Determination of Age: Inserted by the 15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963. The age of a Judge of the Supreme Court shall be determined by such authority and in such manner as Parliament may by law provide. Under the Supreme Court Judges (Salaries and Conditions of Service) Act, 1958, the date of birth declared at the time of appointment is final. A Judge holds office until attaining the age of 65 years.
  • Article 124(3) – Eligibility Qualifications: A person shall not be qualified for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court unless he is a citizen of India and:
    1. (a) has been for at least 5 years a Judge of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession; OR
    2. (b) has been for at least 10 years an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession; OR
    3. (c) is, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist (academician/scholar category – an innovative feature of the Indian Constitution inspired by the US Supreme Court practice, though historically never utilized).
  • Article 124(4) – Removal (Impeachment Mechanism): A Judge of the Supreme Court shall not be removed from his office except by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of Parliament supported by a special majority:
    • A majority of the total membership of that House; AND
    • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.
    • Presented to the President in the same session for such removal.
    • Sole substantive constitutional grounds: "proved misbehaviour" or "incapacity".
  • Article 124(5) – Parliamentary Regulation of Investigation: Parliament is empowered by law to regulate the procedure for the presentation of an address and for the investigation and proof of the misbehaviour or incapacity of a Judge under clause (4). Parliament enacted the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 and framed the Judges (Inquiry) Rules, 1969.
  • Article 124(6) – Oath or Affirmation: Before entering upon office, a Judge must subscribe an oath before the President, or some person appointed in that behalf by him, according to Form IV of the Third Schedule (to bear true faith and allegiance to the Constitution, uphold the sovereignty and integrity of India, perform duties without fear or favour, affection or ill-will, and uphold the Constitution and the laws).
  • Article 124(7) – Absolute Post-Retirement Bar: No person who has held office as a Judge of the Supreme Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India. (Unlike High Court judges who can practice in the Supreme Court or other High Courts under Art. 220, SC judges are subject to a complete bar on advocacy).
Statutory Link: The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 (Procedure for Removal)

Under Article 124(5), the statutory removal mechanism operates in 5 stringent stages:

  1. Motion of Impeachment: Signed by not less than 100 members of Lok Sabha or 50 members of Rajya Sabha submitted to the Speaker or Chairman.
  2. Discretion to Admit & Constitution of Inquiry Committee: If admitted, the Speaker/Chairman constitutes a 3-member judicial committee consisting of: (i) a Supreme Court Judge, (ii) a High Court Chief Justice, and (iii) a Distinguished Jurist.
  3. Judicial Investigation: Committee frames charges, serves notice, conducts inquiry with cross-examination, and submits report. If the committee exonerates the judge, the proceedings terminate instantly.
  4. Parliamentary Consideration & Voting: If proved misbehaviour/incapacity is reported, each House considers the motion and must pass the address by special majority (majority of total strength + 2/3rd present and voting) in the same session.
  5. Presidential Warrant: President issues an executive order removing the judge. (Notable Precedents: Justice V. Ramaswami in 1993 – motion fell due to ruling party abstention; Justice Soumitra Sen of Calcutta HC in 2011 – passed by Rajya Sabha, resigned before Lok Sabha vote; Justice P.D. Dinakaran – resigned before committee report).
Institutional Independence & Organization

Articles 125 – 130: Institutional Safeguards, Ad-Hoc Judges & Court of Record

Structural guarantees ensuring independence from legislative and executive interference
Article Constitutional Subject Legal Scope, Components & Operational Rules
Article 125 Salaries, Allowances & Pensions of Judges Salaries determined by Parliament by law (Second Schedule). Proviso ensures that neither privileges nor allowances, nor rights in respect of leave of absence or pension, shall be varied to a Judge's disadvantage after appointment. The only exception is a Proclamation of Financial Emergency under Article 360(4)(b), during which salaries may be temporarily reduced. Charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India (non-votable under Art. 112(3)(d)(i)).
Article 126 Appointment of Acting Chief Justice When the office of CJI is vacant, or when the CJI is by reason of absence or otherwise unable to perform duties, the duties shall be performed by one of the other Judges of the Court as the President may appoint for the purpose. Convention dictates appointing the senior-most sitting judge.
Article 127 Appointment of Ad-Hoc Judges If at any time there is a lack of quorum of Supreme Court Judges to hold or continue any session, the CJI may, with the previous consent of the President and after consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned, request in writing the attendance of a High Court Judge duly qualified to be appointed an SC Judge, to sit and act as an ad-hoc Judge of the Supreme Court for such period as necessary. The requested judge has all jurisdiction, powers, and privileges of an SC Judge.
Article 128 Attendance of Retired Judges The CJI may at any time, with previous consent of the President, request any retired SC Judge or retired HC Judge qualified for SC appointment, to sit and act as a Judge of the Supreme Court. Requires the consent of the retired Judge. He is entitled to allowances determined by the President, but is not deemed to be a permanent Judge.
Article 129 Supreme Court to be a Court of Record The Supreme Court is a Court of Record and has all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself. As a court of record: (1) its judgments and proceedings have evidentiary value and cannot be questioned in subordinate courts; (2) it has inherent summary power to punish for civil and criminal contempt. In Delhi Judicial Service Association v. State of Gujarat (1991) 4 SCC 406, the SC held that its contempt power under Art. 129 extends to punishing contempt of subordinate courts across India, not restricted by the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971. In In re Vinay Chandra Mishra (1995) 2 SCC 584, the SC suspended an advocate's license under Art. 129/142, but this was corrected in Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998) 4 SCC 409, holding contempt power cannot usurp statutory disciplinary powers of the Bar Council under the Advocates Act, 1961.
Article 130 Seat of the Supreme Court The Supreme Court shall sit in Delhi or in such other place or places as the Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, from time to time, appoint. This power is enabling and discretionary with the CJI; no writ of mandamus can be issued to establish circuit benches (Union of India v. S.P. Anand (1998) 6 SCC 466).
Jurisdictional Spectrum

Articles 71, 131 – 144: The Vast Jurisdictional Architecture of the Supreme Court

Original Exclusive, Original Concurrent, Appellate, Special Leave, Review, Advisory & Inherent Powers

1. Original Exclusive Jurisdiction (Articles 71 & 131)

A. Article 71 – Presidential & Vice-Presidential Elections: All doubts and disputes arising out of or in connection with the election of a President or Vice-President shall be inquired into and decided by the Supreme Court whose decision shall be final. Petition can only be filed after the election concludes, by a candidate or 20 electors (under the Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952).

B. Article 131 – Federal Inter-Governmental Disputes: The Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction in any dispute between:

  • (a) the Government of India and one or more States; or
  • (b) the Government of India and any State or States on one side and one or more other States on the other; or
  • (c) two or more States.
Essential Ingredients of Article 131 Suit:
  1. Parties must be Constitutional Units: Must be Union vs State(s) or State vs State. A suit cannot be filed by or against a private citizen, corporation, or statutory body (e.g., State of Bihar v. Union of India (1970) 1 SCC 67 – Hindustan Steel Ltd. cannot be co-plaintiff).
  2. Legal Right in Dispute: The dispute must involve any question (whether of law or fact) on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends. Political questions without legal rights are barred. In State of Karnataka v. Union of India (1977) 4 SCC 608, challenge to a Commission of Inquiry against the State Chief Minister was held maintainable under Art. 131 as affecting the legal powers of the State.
  3. Express Constitutional Exclusions:
    • Proviso to Art. 131: Disputes arising out of pre-Constitution treaties, covenants, or agreements.
    • Article 262(2): Inter-state water disputes barred from court jurisdiction if Parliament enacts law (Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956).
    • Article 280: Finance Commission recommendations.
    • Article 290: Adjustment of expenses between Union and States.

2. Original Concurrent Jurisdiction: Article 32 vs Article 226

Under Article 32, the Supreme Court has original concurrent jurisdiction with the High Courts (under Article 226) for the enforcement of the Fundamental Rights guaranteed in Part III. Key distinctions:

  • Nature of Right: Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right in Part III; the SC cannot refuse to entertain an Art. 32 petition where a violation of a Fundamental Right is established (Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras, AIR 1950 SC 124). In contrast, Article 226 is a constitutional power, and High Courts exercise discretion.
  • Substantive Scope: Article 32 is invoked strictly for the enforcement of Part III rights. Article 226 can be invoked for Fundamental Rights "and for any other purpose" (enforcement of non-fundamental legal rights, statutory rights, or administrative excesses). Thus, High Court's jurisdiction under Art. 226 is substantively wider.
  • Territorial Reach: SC has pan-India territorial jurisdiction. HC has jurisdiction within its state territory or where the cause of action arises in whole or in part (Art. 226(2)).

3. Appellate Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (Articles 132 – 135 & 134A)

Article Category of Matter Mandatory Conditions, Thresholds & Procedure
Article 132 Constitutional Questions (Civil, Criminal, or Other) An appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, decree, or final order of a High Court if the High Court certifies under Article 134A that the case involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of this Constitution.
Article 133 Civil Matters Appeal lies from any judgment, decree, or final order in a civil proceeding of a High Court if the HC certifies under Art. 134A: (a) that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance; AND (b) that in the opinion of the High Court, the said question needs to be decided by the Supreme Court. (30th Amendment Act, 1972 removed monetary valuation thresholds).
Article 134 Criminal Matters Appeal lies to SC in criminal proceedings under three heads:
(a) As of Right (cl. 1(a)): High Court on appeal has reversed an order of acquittal and sentenced the accused to death.
(b) As of Right (cl. 1(b)): High Court has withdrawn for trial before itself any case from any subordinate court and convicted the accused and sentenced him to death. (Extended to 10 years imprisonment/life imprisonment via Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970).
(c) Certificate of Fitness (cl. 1(c)): High Court certifies under Art. 134A that the case is a fit one for appeal to the Supreme Court.
Article 134A Certificate for Appeal to Supreme Court Inserted by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978. Requires the High Court to determine the grant of certificate suo motu immediately after passing judgment, or on an oral application made by or on behalf of the party aggrieved immediately following judgment delivery.
Article 135 Federal Court Jurisdiction Supreme Court exercises all jurisdiction and powers in relation to any matter to which the provisions of Art. 133 or 134 do not apply, if jurisdiction in that matter was exercisable by the Federal Court immediately before the commencement of the Constitution.

4. Article 136: Special Leave to Appeal (SLP)

Article 136(1): "Notwithstanding anything in this Chapter, the Supreme Court may, in its discretion, grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India."

  • Extraordinary & Plenary Power: Non-obstante clause overrides all limitations in Articles 132-135. It is not an ordinary right of appeal, but a discretionary reserve power to prevent gross miscarriage of justice (Pritam Singh v. The State, AIR 1950 SC 169).
  • Comprehensive Sweep: Applies to "any cause or matter" – civil, criminal, taxation, labour, revenue, constitutional, electoral, or administrative. Applies to final, preliminary, or interlocutory orders.
  • Tribunals Covered: Applies to any adjudicatory body possessing the trappings of a court (CAT, NCLAT, Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, etc.; Bharat Bank Ltd. v. Employees, AIR 1950 SC 188).
  • Express Exception (Art. 136(2)): Does not apply to any judgment, determination, sentence or order passed by any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces (Court Martial).
  • Two-Stage Process: (1) Petition for Special Leave – Court exercises discretion to grant leave; (2) Once leave is granted, it converts into a regular Civil or Criminal Appeal (Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala (2000) 6 SCC 359).

5. Article 137: Power of Review

Article 137: "Subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament or any rules made under article 145, the Supreme Court shall have power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it."

Regulated by Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013:

  • Civil Grounds (analogous to Order 47 Rule 1 CPC):
    1. Discovery of new and important matter or evidence which was not within knowledge after exercise of due diligence;
    2. Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record (patent, manifest, self-evident error without requiring a lengthy process of re-arguing);
    3. Any other sufficient reason (analogous to the first two).
  • Criminal Grounds: A review in criminal proceedings is permitted strictly on the ground of an error apparent on the face of the record. A review is not an appeal in disguise (Northern India Caterers v. Lt. Governor of Delhi (1980) 2 SCC 167).
  • Limitation & Chamber Procedure: Review petition must be filed within 30 days. Ordinarily disposed of by circulation in chambers without oral arguments, unless the Court directs otherwise.
  • Constitutional Exception in Death Penalty Cases: Modified by 5-Judge Bench in Mohd. Arif v. Registrar, Supreme Court (2014) 9 SCC 737 – mandatory open-court oral hearing for 30 minutes before a Bench of at least 3 Judges.

6. Articles 138, 141 & 142: Enlargement, Binding Precedent & Plenary Justice

Article Doctrine / Concept Constitutional Scope, Limits & Landmark Jurisprudence
Article 138 Enlargement of Jurisdiction Parliament may by law confer further jurisdiction on SC with respect to matters in the Union List (Entry 77, 95 List I), or by special agreement between Union and State Governments.
Article 141 Law Declared by SC Binding on All Courts "The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India."
• Ratio Decidendi: The principle or rule of law on which the judgment is founded is binding law.
• Obiter Dicta: Observations not essential to the determination carry high persuasive value, especially of Constitution Benches.
• Does SC bind itself?: No. In Bengal Immunity Co. v. State of Bihar, AIR 1955 SC 661, SC held that it is not bound by its own previous decisions and can overrule them to correct manifest errors.
• Per Incuriam & Sub-Silentio: Decisions rendered in ignorance of binding statutes or binding higher precedents do not constitute binding precedent.
• Prospective Overruling: Evolved in Golak Nath v. State of Punjab, AIR 1967 SC 1643 – overruling operates only for the future without disturbing past settled actions.
Article 142 Enforcement of Decrees & "Complete Justice" "The Supreme Court in the exercise of its jurisdiction may pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it..."
• Plenary & Inherent: Enables the Court to fashion innovative remedies, fill legislative voids (e.g., Vishaka guidelines (1997) on sexual harassment), and dissolve deadlocked disputes (e.g., Bhopal Gas Leak / Union Carbide (1991) 4 SCC 584).
• Constitutional Limitations: In Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India (1998) 4 SCC 409, a Constitution Bench clarified that Art. 142 cannot be used to supplant substantive statutory law or bypass express legislative prohibitions; it supplements, not supplants, statutory provisions. In Shilpa Sailesh v. Varun Sreenivasan (2023), SC held it can grant divorce on irretrievable breakdown bypassing the 6-month cooling period under Sec 13B Hindu Marriage Act using Art. 142.

7. Article 143: Advisory Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Enables the President to seek the advisory opinion of the Supreme Court on questions of law or fact:

  • Article 143(1) – General Questions of Law or Fact: If it appears to the President that a question of law or fact has arisen, or is likely to arise, of such public importance that it is expedient to obtain the opinion of the Supreme Court, he may refer it. The Court "may, after such hearing as it thinks fit, report to the President its opinion thereon." The word "may" gives the Supreme Court discretion to decline answering (e.g., Ayodhya Reference / M. Ismail Faruqui (1994) 6 SCC 360 – declined to answer whether a Hindu temple existed beneath the Babri Masjid).
  • Article 143(2) – Pre-Constitution Treaties: Disputes arising out of pre-Constitution treaties/agreements excluded from Art. 131. The text states the Court "shall... report to the President" (mandatory obligation).
  • Legal Nature of Advisory Opinion:
    • Not Binding on the President: It is purely advisory counsel; the Government is not legally bound to implement it.
    • Status under Article 141: In In re Special Courts Bill, 1978 (1979) 1 SCC 380, the SC held that while an advisory opinion under Art. 143 does not strictly fall within the phrase "law declared" under Art. 141, it carries the highest persuasive authority and is entitled to the utmost respect, binding on all subordinate courts across India.

8. Article 145 & The "Master of the Roster" Doctrine

Article 145: Empowers the Supreme Court, with the approval of the President, to make rules for regulating generally the practice and procedure of the Court (Supreme Court Rules, 2013). Mandatory requirements:

  • Article 145(3) – Minimum Bench of Five Judges: The minimum number of Judges who are to sit for the purpose of deciding any case involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of this Constitution or for hearing any reference under Article 143 shall be five (Constitution Bench).
  • Master of the Roster Doctrine: Under Order VI Rule 1 of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013, the Chief Justice of India is the administrative head of the Supreme Court and exercises exclusive authority over the constitution of benches and allocation of judicial business. In State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand (1998) 1 SCC 1, Campaign for Judicial Accountability and Reforms (CJAR) v. Union of India (2018) 2 SCC 196, and Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India (2018) 8 SCC 396, the SC authoritatively settled that the CJI is "first among equals" judicially, but is the exclusive Master of the Roster administratively. No judge can assign a case to himself or direct the CJI to constitute a specific bench.
Part VI • Chapter V

The State Judiciary: The High Courts (Articles 214 – 231)

Constitutional framework, appointment, conditions of service, transfers, and supervisory powers of the High Courts in the States.

State Constitutional Judiciary

High Courts Architecture & Key Articles

Articles 214, 215, 216, 217, 218, 222, 224, 226, 227 & 228
Article Constitutional Subject Provisions, Standards & Principles
Article 214 & 215 Establishment & Court of Record There shall be a High Court for each State. Every High Court is a Court of Record and has all powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself (Art. 215).
Article 216 & 217 Composition & Appointment of Judges Appointment (Art. 217(1)): Every Judge of a High Court is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State, and (in case of appointment of a Judge other than the Chief Justice) the Chief Justice of the High Court.
Tenure: Holds office until attaining the age of 62 years (raised from 60 to 62 by 15th Amendment, 1963).
Qualifications (Art. 217(2)): Citizen of India who has for at least 10 years held a judicial office in India, OR has for at least 10 years been an advocate of a High Court. (Note: The "distinguished jurist" ground is NOT available for High Court appointments).
Article 218 Removal of High Court Judges Applies the provisions of Article 124(4) and 124(5) to High Court Judges. A High Court Judge can be removed only on proved misbehaviour or incapacity via the parliamentary impeachment address and the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.
Article 222 Transfer of Judges from One High Court to Another The President may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, transfer a Judge from one High Court to any other High Court. Entitled to compensatory allowance.
Judicial Milestones:
• Sankalchand Sheth (1977): Non-consensual transfer valid only in "public interest" after effective consultation; not punitive.
• S.P. Gupta (1981): Reaffirmed public interest; consent not required.
• Second Judges (1993) & Third Judges (1998): Initiated solely by CJI in consultation with 4 senior-most SC judges + Chief Justices of both transferor and transferee High Courts. Judicial review limited to non-compliance with consultative norms.
Article 224 & 224A Additional, Acting & Retired Judges Article 224: President may appoint duly qualified persons as Additional Judges for temporary increase of business or arrears, for a period not exceeding two years (max age 62). (S.P. Gupta: Additional Judges have legitimate expectation to be considered for permanency).
Article 224A: Chief Justice of HC with previous consent of President may request a retired Judge to sit and act as a High Court Judge.
Article 226 Power to Issue Prerogative Writs High Courts have power to issue directions, orders or writs (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, certiorari) to any person or authority, including Government:
(a) for the enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights; AND
(b) for any other purpose.
Territorial Nexus (Art. 226(2)): Can issue writs if the cause of action, wholly or in part, arises within its territorial jurisdiction, even if the seat of Government or authority is outside.
Article 227 Power of Superintendence over All Courts & Tribunals Every High Court has superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction (except military courts).
• Judicial & Administrative: Dual superintendence. Judicial superintendence is exercised to keep subordinate courts and tribunals within the bounds of their authority, preventing grave dereliction of duty or flagrant violation of law (Waryam Singh v. Amarnath, AIR 1954 SC 215).
• Held to be part of the Basic Structure in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) 3 SCC 261.
Article 228 Transfer of Constitutional Cases to High Court If the High Court is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution whose determination is necessary for disposal, it shall withdraw the case and either dispose of the case itself or determine the constitutional question and return it with its judgment.
Articles 230 & 231 Common High Courts & Extension to UTs Parliament may by law extend the jurisdiction of a High Court to Union Territories, or establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and a Union Territory (e.g., Punjab and Haryana High Court; Bombay High Court for Maharashtra, Goa, Dadra and Nagar Haveli & Daman and Diu; Gauhati High Court for Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh).

Case No. 34 • Judicial Independence & Transfer

Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth

(1977) 4 SCC 193 | AIR 1977 SC 2328 | 5-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: Y.V. Chandrachud, P.N. Bhagwati, V.R. Krishna Iyer, N.L. Untwalia, and S. Murtaza Fazal Ali, JJ.

► Background & Material Facts:

During the Internal Emergency imposed in June 1975, the Central Government issued mass transfer orders transferring 16 High Court Judges without their consent. On May 27, 1976, the President of India issued an order under Article 222(1) transferring Justice Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth, a sitting permanent Judge of the Gujarat High Court, to the Andhra Pradesh High Court. The transfer was effected without obtaining Justice Sheth's consent, and without meaningful, full consultation with the Chief Justice of India (CJI A.N. Ray).

Justice Sheth filed a writ petition under Article 226 before the Gujarat High Court challenging the transfer order as unconstitutional, null, and void. A Special Three-Judge Bench of the Gujarat High Court allowed the petition, holding that a Judge could not be transferred without his consent. The Union of India appealed to the Supreme Court.

► Constitutional Provisions in Issue:

Article 222(1): "The President may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, transfer a Judge from one High Court to any other High Court."

► Core Constitutional Questions Framed:

  1. Whether the power of the President under Article 222(1) to transfer a High Court Judge is conditioned upon obtaining the prior consent of the Judge concerned?
  2. What is the nature, scope, and constitutional content of the requirement of "consultation with the Chief Justice of India" under Article 222(1)?
  3. Whether an order of transfer under Article 222(1) is subject to judicial review?
Arguments on Behalf of Respondent (Justice Sheth):
  • Independence of Judiciary as Inviolable: The High Court is a coordinate constitutional court, not a subordinate department of the Union. Judges take an oath to discharge duties without fear or favour. Non-consensual transfer destroys judicial fearlessness.
  • Transfer as an Instrument of Executive Coercion: If consent is not read into Article 222(1), the executive can weaponize transfers to punish inconvenient judges and reward compliant ones, undermining the separation of powers.
  • Colonial Precedent: Under the Government of India Act, 1935, there was no provision for non-consensual transfer. The Constituent Assembly intended transfers to be rare exceptions based on consensual arrangements.
  • Vitiated Consultation: The consultation with CJI Ray was perfunctory, superficial, and lacked full disclosure of facts and material.
Arguments on Behalf of Appellant (Union of India):
  • Literal Interpretation: The plain grammatical text of Article 222(1) contains no requirement of "consent". Reading words into the text amounts to judicial legislation.
  • National Integration: Transfer of judges is an essential administrative instrument to foster national integration, prevent parochial bias, and strengthen High Court benches.
  • Sufficient Safeguard: The requirement of "consultation with the Chief Justice of India" is the sole and sufficient constitutional check against arbitrary executive action.
  • Non-Justiciability: Matters of judicial administration and transfer are high policy matters outside the purview of ordinary judicial review.

► Judgment of the Court (Majority: Chandrachud, Untwalia, Fazal Ali, JJ.):

  1. Consent Cannot be Read into Article 222(1): The Court held by majority that consent of the Judge is not mandatory. The word "consent" is conspicuously absent in Article 222(1), whereas the framers explicitly used words requiring consent in Articles 127, 128, and 224A. Therefore, reading consent into Article 222(1) would amount to rewriting the Constitution.
  2. Strict Constitutional Safeguards on Transfer: The majority ruled that non-consensual transfer power is strictly hedged by two non-negotiable conditions:
    • (a) Public Interest Requirement: The transfer must be strictly in the public interest. It cannot be used as a weapon of punishment, victimisation, or executive displeasure.
    • (b) Full, Effective & Meaningful Consultation: "Consultation" with the CJI is not an empty formality. The President must place all relevant facts, data, reasons, and materials before the CJI. The CJI must apply his independent judicial mind and express an informed opinion. A transfer made without full consultation is void ab initio.
  3. Judicial Review Available: An order of transfer is open to judicial review if it is shown that it was passed without full and effective consultation with the CJI, or was actuated by collateral purpose/mala fides, or was not in the public interest.
  4. Concurring Opinion of Bhagwati and Krishna Iyer, JJ.: Emphasized that non-consensual transfer inherently poses a grave threat to judicial independence. Bhagwati J. observed that "transfer" must ordinarily involve the willingness of the judge, and even if power exists without consent, it must be hedged with the strictest scrutiny to preserve judicial independence.
"Consultation is not a casual or perfunctory dialogue. It must be full, effective and meaningful. All relevant data must be placed before the Chief Justice of India so that he may express his opinion. A judge cannot be transferred by way of punishment." — Y.V. Chandrachud, J. (AIR 1977 SC 2328 at 2338)

► Principle Evolved & Jurisprudential Legacy:

Article 222 transfer does not require the judge's consent, but is subject to: (1) demonstrable public interest, (2) full and effective consultation with the CJI, and (3) limited judicial review against punitive or mala fide transfers. This established the foundational framework that was later built upon in the First, Second, and Third Judges cases.

Case No. 35 • The First Judges Case (1981)

S.P. Gupta & Others v. President of India & Others

1981 Supp SCC 87 | AIR 1982 SC 149 | 7-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: P.N. Bhagwati, A.C. Gupta, S. Murtaza Fazal Ali, V.D. Tulzapurkar, D.A. Desai, R.S. Pathak, and E.S. Venkataramiah, JJ.

► Detailed Material Facts (As per DU Case Material):

In 1981, following the return of the Congress government, Union Law Minister P. Shiv Shankar issued a confidential circular letter dated March 18, 1981 (D.O. No. 66/10/81-Jus.) addressed to the Chief Ministers of all States and High Court Chief Justices. The circular requested them to:

  • (a) Obtain written advance consent from all Additional Judges working in their High Court to be appointed as permanent Judges in any other High Court in the country, requesting them to name three preferred High Courts;
  • (b) Obtain similar consent from all prospective candidates proposed for initial judicial appointment.

Simultaneously, the Central Government engaged in granting short-term extensions (for 3 months, 4 months) to sitting Additional Judges of the Delhi High Court (Justices O.N. Vohra, S.N. Kumar, and S.B. Wad). Ultimately, Justice S.N. Kumar was not granted a further extension and was dropped despite the recommendation of CJI Y.V. Chandrachud, because Delhi High Court Chief Justice Prakash Narain had made unverified integrity complaints in confidential correspondence with the Law Minister. Furthermore, Chief Justice K.B.N. Singh was transferred from the Patna High Court to the Madras High Court, and CJ M.M. Ismail of Madras was transferred to Kerala.

Practicing advocates (S.P. Gupta of Allahabad HC, Iqbal Chagla and others of Bombay HC, V.M. Tarkunde, J.C. Kalra) filed public interest writ petitions challenging the validity of the Law Minister's circular, the short-term extensions, the dropping of Justice S.N. Kumar, and the transfer of CJ K.B.N. Singh.

► Key Constitutional & Statutory Issues:

  1. Locus Standi: Whether practicing lawyers have standing to maintain a writ petition challenging executive orders affecting the higher judiciary when the affected judges themselves (except Justice Kumar) had not approached the court?
  2. Crown / State Privilege (Article 74(2) & Section 123 Evidence Act): Whether the Union of India could claim immunity from producing the confidential correspondence between the Law Minister, CJI Chandrachud, and Delhi HC CJ Prakash Narain?
  3. Constitutional Meaning of "Consultation" (Articles 124(2) & 217(1)): Does "consultation" mean "concurrence"? Does the opinion of the CJI enjoy primacy over the executive or other constitutional consultees?
  4. Status & Rights of Additional Judges (Article 224): Does an Additional Judge have a right to be appointed as a permanent Judge upon expiry of his term? What are the grounds on which an Additional Judge can be dropped?
  5. Constitutionality of Policy Transfers (Article 222): Was the Law Minister's circular valid, and was the transfer of CJ K.B.N. Singh lawful?
Arguments of Petitioners (Advocates & Bar):
  • Lawyers as Officers of Court: Advocates are integral partners in the administration of justice. Executive coercion of the judiciary damages the rule of law, directly giving lawyers standing.
  • Open Society & Disclosure: Executive accountability demands disclosure. Article 74(2) only protects ministerial advice, not the factual materials and correspondence.
  • Judicial Primacy Essential for Independence: If executive can override the CJI, judicial independence becomes an illusion. Consultation must mean concurrence or CJI's view must prevail.
  • Abuse of Art. 224: Additional Judges were treated as temporary probationary officers, creating executive subservience.
Arguments of Respondent (Union of India & Law Minister):
  • No Legal Injury to Lawyers: Third-party interlopers cannot file petitions where the judges themselves have not sought redress.
  • Article 74(2) Absolute Bar: Ministerial advice and high-level correspondence regarding judicial selection are protected by state secrecy under Sec. 123 of Evidence Act and Art. 74(2).
  • No CJI Veto in Constitution: Framers deliberately selected "consultation", not "concurrence". Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explicitly rejected giving CJI a veto. Ultimate appointing authority is the President (Council of Ministers).
  • No Tenure Beyond 2 Years: Additional Judges under Art. 224 have no legal right to permanency; executive has discretion not to renew.

► Decision & Findings of the 7-Judge Bench:

  1. Revolution in Locus Standi (Birth of PIL): Bhagwati J. delivered the watershed pronouncement establishing that any member of the public acting bona fide, having sufficient interest, can maintain an action for judicial redress of public injury caused by breach of public duty. Lawyers have a vital interest in the independence of the judiciary and cannot be "told off at the gates".
  2. Rejection of State Privilege (Article 74(2) & Section 123 Evidence Act): The Court ruled (6:1) that Article 74(2) bars inquiry into the actual advice tendered by Ministers to the President, but DOES NOT protect the factual material, letters, and correspondence forming the basis of that advice. Applying the balancing test under Section 123 Evidence Act, the public interest in open justice and disclosure far outweighed the plea of administrative secrecy. The files were inspected and made public.
  3. "Consultation" does NOT mean "Concurrence" (Executive Supremacy): The majority held that consultation means full and effective discussion, but not concurrence. All three constitutional functionaries under Art. 217(1) (Governor, High Court CJ, and CJI) stand on equal footing; the CJI has no constitutional primacy. The ultimate decision on appointment vests in the Central Government. The executive has the final say and can override the CJI.
  4. Additional Judges (Article 224): An Additional Judge has no automatic legal right to be appointed as a permanent Judge. However, he has a right to be considered for appointment in preference to fresh appointees. Dropping of Justice S.N. Kumar was upheld as valid since there was a divergence of opinion between CJI Chandrachud and HC CJ Prakash Narain, giving the Central Government justification to choose between conflicting views.
  5. Transfer under Article 222: Upheld the transfer of CJ K.B.N. Singh as being in the public interest and not punitive. The Law Minister's circular was held not to be coercive as it merely sought consent.
"The concept of independence of the judiciary is not a mere bar-room slogan. But consultation does not mean concurrence. It is impossible to hold that the opinion of the Chief Justice of India has primacy over the opinions of the other two constitutional functionaries. The power of appointment ultimately rests with the Central Government." — P.N. Bhagwati, J. (1981 Supp SCC 87)

► Principle Evolved & Criticism:

S.P. Gupta established Executive Supremacy in judicial appointments. While it democratized access to justice through PIL and open governance, it led to immense political interference, supersessions, and delay in judicial appointments throughout the 1980s, creating the impetus for its eventual overruling in the Second Judges Case (1993).

Case No. 36 • The Second Judges Case (1993)

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India

(1993) 4 SCC 441 | AIR 1994 SC 268 | 9-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: J.S. Verma (writing for majority), S. Ratnavel Pandian, A.M. Ahmadi, Kuldip Singh, P.K. Ray, B.P. Jeevan Reddy, S.P. Bharucha, M.M. Punchhi, and G.N. Ray, JJ.

► Factual Catalyst & Context:

In the decade following S.P. Gupta (1981), the executive routinely disregarded recommendations of the Chief Justice of India. Long delays, judicial vacancies, and allegations of political packing plagued the higher judiciary. In Subhash Sharma v. Union of India (1991) Supp (1) SCC 574, a Three-Judge Bench observed that S.P. Gupta required reconsideration by a larger bench of nine judges to insulate the judiciary from political executive interference.

► Core Issues Framed:

  1. Whether the majority judgment in S.P. Gupta declaring executive supremacy in judicial appointments was correct in law?
  2. What is the true constitutional meaning of "consultation" under Articles 124(2) and 217(1)? Does the Chief Justice of India have primacy?
  3. Whether the appointment of the CJI is governed strictly by the seniority convention?
  4. What is the nature and scope of judicial review in matters of appointment and transfer?
Arguments of Petitioners (Bar & Advocates):
  • Basic Structure Violation: Independence of the judiciary is an inviolable part of the Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati). Executive control over appointments destroys this independence.
  • Purposive Interpretation: The phrase "consultation" in the context of judicial appointments cannot mean subordinate advice to the executive; it requires judicial primacy to ensure that appointments are based on merit, not political favour.
  • Constitutional Intent: The Constitution intended the judiciary to assess legal acumen and character, matters on which the CJI is best equipped to judge.
Arguments of Respondent (Union of India):
  • Doctrine of Textualism: The framers consciously selected "consultation" and rejected "concurrence". Reading "concurrence" creates an unconstitutional judicial veto.
  • Democratic Accountability: In a constitutional democracy, the executive is responsible to Parliament and the electorate; transferring absolute appointing power to the judiciary creates a self-appointing oligarchy.
  • Stare Decisis: S.P. Gupta was decided by a 7-Judge Bench and had governed appointments for over a decade.

► Landmark Holding of the 9-Judge Bench (7:2 Majority – J.S. Verma, J.):

  1. Overruling of S.P. Gupta: The Court decisively overruled the majority opinion in S.P. Gupta, holding that executive supremacy in judicial appointments is contrary to the constitutional scheme and violates the Basic Structure.
  2. Primacy of the Chief Justice of India: In the appointment of Judges to the Supreme Court and High Courts, and in the transfer of High Court Judges, the opinion of the Chief Justice of India has PRIMACY. No appointment can be made by the President unless it is in conformity with the opinion of the CJI.
  3. Invention of the "Collegium System": The Court held that the opinion of the CJI is not his individual opinion alone, but a collegiate opinion formed in consultation with his senior-most colleagues. For Supreme Court appointments, CJI must consult his two senior-most puisne judges.
  4. Executive's Limited Power of Reconsideration: If the executive has strong, cogent, and verifiable reasons against a recommended candidate (e.g., adverse background checks), it may return the recommendation to the CJI for reconsideration. However, if the CJI and the collegium unanimously reiterate the recommendation, the executive is constitutionally bound to make the appointment.
  5. Seniority Rule for CJI: The appointment of the Chief Justice of India must strictly follow the rule of seniority – the senior-most sitting Judge of the Supreme Court must be appointed CJI, eliminating executive discretion and supersession.
  6. Dissent (A.M. Ahmadi & M.M. Punchhi, JJ.): Dissenting, held that "consultation" cannot be rewritten as "concurrence" or "primacy". The word was chosen advisedly by the framers to avoid giving a veto to either organ.
"The role of the CJI cannot be reduced to that of a mere consultant whose opinion can be bypassed by the executive. The process of appointment of judges is a participatory consultative process, but the primacy must necessarily lie with the judiciary headed by the Chief Justice of India." — J.S. Verma, J. (writing for majority in AIR 1994 SC 268)

► Principle Evolved:

Replaced Executive Supremacy with Judicial Primacy. Formulated the nascent Collegium System (CJI + 2 senior judges) and mandated the seniority convention for CJI appointment as an indispensable safeguard of judicial independence.

Case No. 37 • The Third Judges Case (1998)

In Re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998

(1998) 7 SCC 739 | AIR 1999 SC 1 | 9-Judge Constitution Bench (Advisory Jurisdiction)
Coram: S.P. Bharucha (author), M.K. Mukherjee, S.B. Majmudar, Sujata V. Manohar, G.T. Nanavati, S. Saghir Ahmad, K. Venkataswami, B.N. Kirpal, and G.B. Pattanaik, JJ.

► Factual Background & Presidential Reference under Article 143:

Following the Second Judges Case (1993), serious practical controversies arose regarding the consultation process. In 1998, CJI M.M. Punchhi made recommendations to the Government for SC appointments without consulting his senior colleagues. The Union Government (through President K.R. Narayanan) was reluctant to accept recommendations made unilaterally without collegial consensus.

To resolve constitutional doubts without reopening the conflict, the President made a Reference under Article 143(1) formulating nine specific questions regarding the consultation process, the composition of the Collegium, the role of High Court conversant judges, judicial review of transfers, and the relevance of seniority.

► The Nine Questions Referred & Authoritative Answers of the 9-Judge Bench:

Q. No. Presidential Question Referred Authoritative Answer / Ruling of the 9-Judge Bench
Q. 1 Does "consultation with CJI" require consultation with a plurality of Judges, or does the sole individual opinion of CJI suffice? Plurality is Mandatory: "Consultation with the CJI" requires consultation with a plurality of Judges. The sole individual opinion of the CJI does NOT constitute consultation within Articles 217(1) and 222(1).
Q. 2 Is the transfer of High Court Judges judicially reviewable? Strictly Limited Judicial Review: Reviewable ONLY to the extent that the transfer recommendation was not made in consultation with the 4 senior-most SC judges and the Chief Justices of both transferor and transferee High Courts. The transferred judge alone can challenge.
Q. 3 What is the exact size of the Collegium for SC and HC appointments? Expanded Collegium:
• For Supreme Court appointments: CJI + 4 senior-most puisne Judges of SC.
• For High Court appointments: CJI + 2 senior-most puisne Judges of SC.
• For High Court transfers: CJI + 4 senior-most puisne Judges of SC + views of both HC Chief Justices.
Q. 4 Can CJI act solely in individual capacity regarding adverse government material? No Unilateral Action: The CJI cannot act in an individual capacity. Materials and adverse information conveyed by the Government must be placed before and evaluated by the Collegium.
Q. 5 Who qualifies as "conversant judges" for HC appointments? Includes not only SC judges whose parent HC is the court concerned, but also judges who have occupied the office of Judge or CJ of that High Court on transfer.
Q. 6 Must "strong cogent reasons" be recorded for superseding senior HC judges for SC appointment? "Strong cogent reasons" do not need to be recorded for each bypassed senior judge; what must be recorded is the positive reason for recommending the candidate selected on merit.
Q. 7 & 8 Must opinions of consulted judges be in writing and conveyed to the Government? Mandatory Written Opinions: Views of all consulted judges must be in writing and conveyed to the Union Government along with the CJI's recommendation. The CJI is legally obliged to comply with these consultative norms.
Q. 9 Are recommendations made without complying with these norms binding on the Government? Non-Compliant Recommendations NOT BINDING: Recommendations made by the CJI without complying with the collegium norms are NOT BINDING on the Government of India!

► Internal Consensus & Veto within the Collegium:

The Court ruled that the CJI must form recommendations based on consensus. If the majority of the Collegium disagrees with the CJI, the CJI cannot make the recommendation. Specifically: "If two or more Judges of the Collegium give an adverse opinion regarding a candidate, the CJI shall not press the recommendation."

► Principle Evolved:

The Third Judges Case institutionalized the Full Collegium System (CJI + 4 for SC appointments/transfers, CJI + 2 for HC appointments), converting the nascent Verma J. formula into a detailed, structured, rule-bound consultative code governing judicial appointments across India.

Case No. 38 • The Fourth Judges Case / NJAC Verdict (2016)

Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India

(2016) 5 SCC 1 | AIR 2016 SC 117 | 5-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: Jagdish Singh Khehar (author for majority), J. Chelameswar (dissenting), Madan B. Lokur, Kurian Joseph, and Adarsh Kumar Goel, JJ.

► Legislative Background & Constitutional Challenge:

To dismantle the opaque collegium system, Parliament unanimously passed the Constitution (Ninety-Ninth Amendment) Act, 2014 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014. The amendment inserted Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C, replacing the collegium with a 6-member Commission comprising:

  • (1) The Chief Justice of India (Chairperson, ex officio);
  • (2) Two senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court (Members, ex officio);
  • (3) The Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice (Member, ex officio);
  • (4) Two "eminent persons" nominated by a committee consisting of the Prime Minister, the CJI, and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha. (Proviso: one eminent person must belong to SC/ST/OBC/Minorities or women).

The Section 6(6) Veto: Under Section 6(6) of the NJAC Act, the Commission could not recommend any person if any two members of the Commission disagreed with the recommendation. Writ petitions were filed by the SC AOR Association and senior jurists challenging the 99th Amendment and NJAC Act as destructive of the Basic Structure.

► Core Constitutional Issues:

  1. Whether judicial primacy in appointments is an integral, non-negotiable component of the Independence of the Judiciary and part of the Basic Structure?
  2. Whether the inclusion of the Union Law Minister and two "eminent persons" with veto power violates the independence of judiciary and separation of powers?
  3. Whether the striking down of the 99th Amendment automatically revives the Collegium System (status quo ante)?
Arguments on Behalf of Petitioners (Fali Nariman, Anil Divan, Ram Jethmalani):
  • Executive is the Biggest Litigant: The Union Government is the single largest litigant in superior courts. Giving the Law Minister a seat and veto power over appointments directly compromises judicial impartiality and independence.
  • Unqualified "Eminent Persons" with Veto: Non-judicial persons with no legal qualifications could combine with the Law Minister to block any meritorious judicial nomination, neutralizing the judicial component.
  • Subversion of Basic Structure: Judicial independence is impossible without judicial primacy in appointments. Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C subvert the institutional insulation established in Second and Third Judges cases.
Arguments on Behalf of Union of India (Attorney General Mukul Rohatgi):
  • Judges Appointing Judges is Alien to Democracy: No major constitutional democracy in the world permits judges to select themselves without executive and democratic participation.
  • Checks and Balances: Absolute judicial monopoly creates unaccountability, opacity, and nepotism. NJAC was enacted by unanimous parliamentary will and ratified by 20 state legislatures.
  • Judicial Independence ≠ Absolute Primacy: Judicial independence pertains to fearless post-appointment adjudication, not exclusive control over recruitment.

► Landmark Decision (4:1 Majority):

  1. 99th Amendment & NJAC Act Struck Down: The Constitution Bench (4:1) declared both the Constitution (99th Amendment) Act, 2014 and the NJAC Act, 2014 UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND VOID in their entirety.
  2. Judicial Primacy is Part of the Basic Structure: The majority (Khehar, Lokur, Kurian Joseph, Goel, JJ.) ruled that the primacy of the judiciary in the selection and appointment of judges is an essential component of the Independence of the Judiciary, which is part of the inviolable Basic Structure.
  3. Fatal Flaws of Article 124A:
    • Law Minister's Presence: Violates separation of powers. A minister representing the biggest litigant sitting on the selection board compromises institutional independence.
    • Eminent Persons & Section 6(6) Veto: Two lay persons, without defined qualifications, armed with a veto power, could override the unanimous recommendation of the CJI and two senior-most SC judges.
  4. Automatic Revival of the Collegium System: The Court rejected the Union's contention that striking down the amendment would create a constitutional vacuum. It held that the invalidation of an unconstitutional amendment automatically restores the status quo ante – the pre-amendment Collegium System sprang back into full operation.
  5. Concession of Flaws & MoP Reform: The Court acknowledged criticisms of opacity in the collegium and held subsequent hearings to reform the Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) regarding eligibility criteria, transparency, a permanent secretariat, and complaints mechanism.
  6. Dissent of Justice J. Chelameswar: Chelameswar J. vigorously dissented, holding that the Collegium system was opaque, lacked accountability, and fostered trade-offs. He held that checks and balances are fundamental to constitutionalism and the presence of the Law Minister and eminent persons did not destroy judicial independence.
"The sensitivity of selecting judges is so enormous, and the consequences of making a bad appointment so grave, that the primacy of the judiciary cannot be surrendered to a commission where the judicial component can be vetoed by the executive or outside nominees." — Jagdish Singh Khehar, J. (2016) 5 SCC 1

► Constitutional Significance:

The Fourth Judges Case entrenched the Collegium System as a constitutional reality protected under the Basic Structure doctrine, solidifying India as the only major democracy where judges have the exclusive decisive power in appointing their peers.

Case No. 39 • Master of the Roster

Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India through its Registrar

(2018) 8 SCC 396 | 2-Judge Bench
Coram: A.K. Sikri and Ashok Bhushan, JJ.

► Context & Material Facts:

In January 2018, four senior-most puisne judges of the Supreme Court (Justices J. Chelameswar, Ranjan Gogoi, Madan B. Lokur, and Kurian Joseph) held an unprecedented press conference, expressing grave concern that cases of far-reaching national consequence were being assigned arbitrarily to select junior benches by then CJI Dipak Misra. Soon thereafter, former Union Law Minister and Senior Advocate Shanti Bhushan filed a writ petition under Article 32.

The petitioner prayed for a declaration that the administrative power of the Chief Justice of India to allocate cases and constitute benches ("Master of the Roster") under Article 145 and Order VI Rule 1 of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013, should not be exercised by the CJI alone in his individual discretion, but by the Collegium of five senior-most judges, drawing an analogy to the interpretation of "CJI" in the Second and Third Judges cases.

► Core Legal Issues:

  1. Whether the expression "Chief Justice of India" in the context of the administrative power of allocating cases and constituting benches means the CJI individually or the Collegium of senior judges?
  2. Whether the power of the CJI as Master of the Roster is absolute and unguided, or subject to institutional collegial oversight?
Arguments on Behalf of Petitioner (Dushyant Dave & Shanti Bhushan):
  • Equal Judicial Authority: In judicial capacity, all Supreme Court judges are co-equals; the CJI is merely primus inter pares (first among equals).
  • Erosion of Public Confidence: Concentrating untrammeled administrative discretion in one individual creates the risk of bench-hunting, forum shopping, and political manipulation of sensitive cases.
  • Harmonious Analogy: Since "CJI" in Articles 124(2) and 217(1) was interpreted as the Collegium, the same democratic, collegiate principle must apply to roster allocation under Article 145.
Arguments on Behalf of Respondent (Attorney General K.K. Venugopal):
  • Administrative Impracticability: Roster allocation requires day-to-day administrative dispatch. Involving five judges in daily roster allocation would create friction, delays, and institutional paralysis.
  • Specific Scope of Judges Cases: The Collegium formula was strictly evolved for appointments and transfers, not for daily court administration.
  • Exclusive Roster Authority: Under Order VI Rule 1 of Supreme Court Rules, 2013 and settled conventions, the CJI alone is the Master of the Roster.

► Judgment & Holding of the Court:

  1. CJI is the Sole Master of the Roster: The Court decisively held that the Chief Justice of India is the individual administrative head of the Supreme Court and possesses the exclusive authority to allocate cases and constitute benches.
  2. Distinction between Judicial and Administrative Functions:
    • Judicially: The CJI is primus inter pares (first among equals). A judgment of a bench headed by the CJI has no greater judicial authority than a bench of co-equal strength headed by any other puisne judge.
    • Administratively: The CJI occupies a pre-eminent, unique institutional position as the leader of the court. The expression "CJI" in Supreme Court Rules cannot be interpreted to mean the Collegium.
  3. Rejection of Collegial Roster: Subdividing roster allocation among five judges would lead to chaotic administrative friction and compromise the functioning of the court.
  4. Presumption of Bona Fides & High Conventions: The power must be exercised bona fide, in accordance with established traditions, subject expertise of judges, and sound administrative principles to maintain public confidence.

► Principle Evolved:

Reaffirmed the exclusive authority of the Chief Justice of India as the Master of the Roster, while cementing the vital constitutional distinction between judicial parity and administrative leadership.

Case No. 40 • Critical Monograph

The National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC): Design, Working & Critique

99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 & NJAC Act, 2014 | DU Case Material Excerpt (pp. 243-244)

► Institutional Architecture & Legislative Background:

Passed almost unanimously by both Houses of Parliament in August 2014 and ratified by 20 State Legislatures, the NJAC sought to dismantle the judicial monopoly over appointments established by the 1993 Second Judges verdict. It introduced a structural shift towards a broad-based, multi-stakeholder appointment mechanism modeled loosely on judicial commissions in the United Kingdom, Canada, and South Africa.

► Structural Composition under Struck Down Article 124A:

Component Designation / Membership Constitutional / Democratic Rationale
Chairperson Chief Justice of India (ex officio) Ensures institutional headship of the apex judiciary.
Judicial Members Two senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court (ex officio) Provides high-level judicial representation and professional evaluation.
Executive Member Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice (ex officio) Ensures democratic accountability to the elected representatives of the people.
Civil Society Members Two "Eminent Persons" nominated by Committee (PM + CJI + Leader of Opposition/Single largest party) Brings public perspective, diversity, and representation of marginalized sections (one member mandatorily SC/ST/OBC/Minority/Woman).

► Fatal Statutory Flaws That Led to its Judicial Demise:

  • The Section 6(6) Veto Trap: Any two members could veto any candidate. Thus, the Law Minister and one eminent person could veto the unanimous choice of all three senior Supreme Court judges, completely eroding judicial primacy.
  • Vagueness of "Eminent Persons": No legal, constitutional, or professional criteria were specified in the Constitution or the Act to define who qualified as an "eminent person".
  • Article 124C Blank Cheque: Empowered Parliament to regulate the procedure for appointments through ordinary legislation or delegated rules, subordinating constitutional judicial selection to parliamentary majorities.

► Current Stalemate & The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP):

Following the invalidation of the NJAC, the Supreme Court directed the Central Government to finalize a revised Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) in consultation with the CJI. However, deep disagreements have persisted between the Executive and the Collegium over key clauses – specifically the Government's proposed "National Security" clause (enabling executive rejection on grounds of national security without disclosing reasons) and the establishment of a formal Search and Evaluation Committee.

Case No. 41 • Judicial Review as Basic Structure

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India

(1997) 3 SCC 261 | AIR 1997 SC 1125 | 7-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: A.M. Ahmadi, C.J., M.M. Punchhi, K. Ramaswamy, S.P. Bharucha, S. Saghir Ahmad, K. Venkataswami, and K.T. Thomas, JJ.

► Historical Background & Material Facts:

By the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976, Parliament inserted Part XIV-A into the Constitution containing Articles 323A and 323B. These articles empowered Parliament and State Legislatures to establish administrative and subject-specific tribunals. Crucially, Article 323A(2)(d) and Article 323B(3)(d) enabled Parliament to totally exclude the jurisdiction of all courts, except the Supreme Court under Article 136.

Pursuant to Article 323A, Parliament enacted the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985. Section 28 of this Act excluded the jurisdiction of all courts, including the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226/227. In S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987) 1 SCC 124, a 5-Judge Bench upheld Section 28, holding that Parliament could exclude High Court jurisdiction if an equally efficacious alternative institutional mechanism was provided.

However, subsequent developments demonstrated that Tribunals lacked judicial independence, were overburdened, and in J.B. Chopra v. UOI (AIR 1987 SC 357), single administrative members were deciding constitutional validity of laws. A 7-Judge Bench was constituted to re-evaluate the constitutionality of Part XIV-A and the exclusion of judicial review.

► Core Questions Framed by Chief Justice Ahmadi:

  1. Whether the power conferred upon Parliament/Legislatures by Article 323A(2)(d) and Article 323B(3)(d) to totally exclude the jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Article 32 violates the Basic Structure of the Constitution?
  2. Whether Tribunals constituted under Articles 323A/323B possess the competence to test the constitutional validity of statutory provisions and rules?
  3. Whether these Tribunals can be regarded as effective substitutes for the High Courts in discharging judicial review?
  4. Whether Section 5(6) of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (allowing single-member benches) is constitutional?

► Landmark Holdings of the 7-Judge Bench (Unanimous):

  1. Judicial Review is Inviolable Basic Structure: The Court unanimously held that the power of judicial review over legislative action vested in the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is an integral and essential feature of the Constitution, constituting an inviolable part of the Basic Structure. It cannot be ousted even by a constitutional amendment!
  2. Exclusion Clauses Declared Unconstitutional: Clause 2(d) of Article 323A, Clause 3(d) of Article 323B, and Section 28 of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 (along with all similar exclusion clauses in other tribunal statutes), to the extent they excluded the jurisdiction of High Courts and Supreme Court, were struck down as UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND VOID.
  3. Tribunals are NOT Substitutes, but Supplemental Bodies: Tribunals cannot act as full substitutes for the superior judiciary because their members lack the constitutional insulation, security of tenure, and independence enjoyed by High Court judges. Tribunals perform a purely supplemental role.
  4. Competence to Test Vires of Subordinate Rules: Tribunals possess the competence to test the constitutional validity of statutory rules and subordinate legislation. However, they CANNOT question the validity of their own parent statute (e.g., CAT cannot decide the validity of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985).
  5. Tribunal as Court of First Instance & Division Bench High Court Review:
    • The Tribunal will act as the court of first instance in respect of areas for which it is constituted. Litigants cannot bypass the Tribunal and rush directly to the High Court.
    • ALL decisions of Tribunals are subject to judicial scrutiny before a DIVISION BENCH of the respective High Court under Articles 226/227.
    • Direct appeal to the Supreme Court under Article 136 bypassing the High Court was firmly discouraged.
  6. Harmonious Construction of Section 5(6): Upheld Section 5(6) by reading it down – if a question involving constitutional validity arises before a Single Member bench, it must be referred to a Division Bench consisting of at least two members, one of whom must be a Judicial Member.
  7. Independent Nodal Ministry: Recommended that all tribunals be placed under a single independent nodal ministry (Ministry of Law) rather than parent ministries against whom they adjudicate.
"The power of judicial review over legislative action vested in the High Courts under Article 226 and in this Court under Article 32 is an integral and essential feature of the Constitution, constituting part of its basic structure. The constitutional safeguards ensuring the independence of the Judges of superior courts are not available to those who man tribunals... They can never be full substitutes for the superior judiciary." — A.M. Ahmadi, C.J. (1997) 3 SCC 261 at 301

► Jurisprudential Impact:

Overruled Sampath Kumar on the exclusion of High Court writ jurisdiction. Re-established the High Court as the paramount constitutional sentinel in the state and established the mandatory "Tribunal → HC Division Bench (Art. 226) → Supreme Court (Art. 136)" hierarchy.

Case No. 42 • Independence of Judiciary & NTT

Madras Bar Association v. Union of India

(2014) 10 SCC 1 | AIR 2015 SC 1571 | 5-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: R.M. Lodha, C.J., J.S. Khehar (author for majority), J. Chelameswar, A.K. Sikri, and R.F. Nariman, JJ.

► Legislative Background & Material Facts:

Parliament enacted the National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005 (NTT Act) to establish the National Tax Tribunal (NTT). The NTT was established to divest High Courts of their jurisdiction to hear appeals on substantial questions of law arising from orders of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) and Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT). The NTT Act allowed non-judicial "technical members" (former civil servants/accountants) to sit on NTT benches and adjudicate complex substantial questions of law, with appointment committees heavily dominated by executive nominees.

The Madras Bar Association challenged the NTT Act as violative of the Basic Structure – specifically the Independence of the Judiciary and Separation of Powers.

► Core Constitutional Questions:

  1. Can Parliament transfer core judicial functions of the High Court – specifically the determination of substantial questions of law – to a quasi-judicial tribunal?
  2. Can non-judicial "technical members" be empowered to adjudicate substantial questions of law?
  3. Does the NTT Act violate the basic structure by compromising judicial independence and separation of powers?

► Judgment & Holding:

  1. NTT Act Struck Down as Unconstitutional: The Constitution Bench unanimously struck down the National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005 in its entirety as unconstitutional and void.
  2. Core Judicial Powers Cannot be Transferred to Substandard Tribunals: The Court ruled that while Parliament has legislative competence to create tribunals to supplement courts, it cannot transfer core constitutional judicial powers historically exercised by High Courts to tribunals unless the new forum possesses identical standards of independence, security of tenure, and judicial capability.
  3. Adjudication of Substantial Questions of Law is Exclusively Judicial: Deciding "substantial questions of law" is an intrinsic judicial function. Non-judicial technical members (civil servants/accountants) lack judicial training and cannot be permitted to adjudicate substantial questions of law.
  4. Defective Selection Mechanism: Selection committees where executive representatives hold parity or dominance over judicial representatives violate the independence of the judiciary.

► Principle Evolved:

Set definitive constitutional boundaries on tribunalisation: Core adjudicatory functions involving pure questions of law cannot be transferred from High Courts to executive-dominated tribunals lacking judicial character and independence.

Case No. 43 • Article 137 Review & Article 21

Mohd. Arif @ Ashfaq v. The Registrar, Supreme Court of India

(2014) 9 SCC 737 | AIR 2014 SC 3517 | 5-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: R.M. Lodha, C.J., J.S. Khehar, J. Chelameswar (concurring), A.K. Sikri, and R.F. Nariman (author for majority), JJ.

► Material Facts & Challenge:

Mohd. Arif was convicted and sentenced to death for the sensational 2000 Red Fort attack. His regular appeal and SLP were dismissed by the Supreme Court. Under Order XL Rule 3 of the Supreme Court Rules, 1966 (now Order XLVII of 2013 Rules), review petitions under Article 137 are ordinarily disposed of by "circulation in chambers" – meaning the judges consider the written paper-book in private chambers without any open-court oral hearing by counsel.

Arif's review petition was dismissed by circulation. He filed a writ petition under Article 32, joined by the People's Union for Democratic Rights (PUDR), arguing that where a person's life is at stake, the absolute denial of an oral hearing violates the right to procedural due process and fair trial under Article 21 of the Constitution.

► Core Constitutional Questions:

  1. Whether a condemned prisoner sentenced to death has a fundamental right under Article 21 to an oral hearing in open court of his review petition under Article 137?
  2. Whether the Supreme Court Rules prescribing chamber disposal by circulation for review petitions are unconstitutional in capital cases?

► Landmark Judgment & Holding (4:1 Majority – R.F. Nariman, J.):

  1. Mandatory Open Court Oral Hearing in Capital Reviews: The Constitution Bench held that in ALL cases where the death penalty has been awarded, review petitions under Article 137 MUST be heard in OPEN COURT by a bench of at least THREE Judges.
  2. Article 21 Primacy over Article 145 Rules: While the Supreme Court has procedural rule-making power under Art. 145, procedural rules must bow down to the fundamental right to life and procedural fairness under Article 21 (Maneka Gandhi doctrine). Death is irrevocable and unique; an oral hearing affords counsel the last opportunity to persuade the court on mitigating factors and judicial errors that could save human life.
  3. Reasonable Time Limit: To prevent misuse, the Court limited the duration of oral hearing in review petitions to a maximum of 30 minutes.
  4. Retrospective Operation: The benefit of open-court review hearing was extended retrospectively to all death row convicts whose review petitions had previously been dismissed in chambers and who had not yet been executed.
"Death penalty is irreversible. A judicial error in such cases is irreparable. Article 21 demands that before a man is sent to the gallows, the procedure established by law must be just, fair and reasonable. An oral hearing in open court, even if limited to 30 minutes, is an indispensable requirement of procedural fairness." — Rohinton F. Nariman, J. (2014) 9 SCC 737

► Principle Evolved:

Harmonized Article 137 review powers and Article 145 procedural rules with Article 21 procedural due process, creating an indelible human rights safeguard in Indian capital sentencing jurisprudence.

Case No. 44 • Origin of Curative Petition

Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra & Another

(2002) 4 SCC 388 | AIR 2002 SC 1771 | 5-Judge Constitution Bench
Coram: S.P. Bharucha, C.J., Syed Shah Mohammed Quadri (author), U.C. Banerjee, S.N. Variava, and S.V. Patil, JJ.

► Material Facts:

In a bitter matrimonial dispute, a joint petition for mutual consent divorce was filed under Section 13B of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. Before the statutory 18-month period expired, the wife (Rupa Hurra) withdrew her consent. Despite the withdrawal, a Two-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court granted divorce decree under Article 142 on the ground of irretrievable breakdown of marriage. The wife's review petition under Article 137 was dismissed.

The wife then filed a writ petition under Article 32 challenging the final Supreme Court judgment as a nullity, arguing it violated principles of natural justice and her fundamental rights. A 5-Judge Constitution Bench was convened to decide whether a final judgment of the Supreme Court could ever be reopened or challenged under Article 32.

► Core Legal Questions:

  1. Whether an Article 32 writ petition is maintainable to challenge or set aside a final judgment or order rendered by the Supreme Court itself?
  2. Whether the Supreme Court, to prevent abuse of its process and cure gross miscarriage of justice, has inherent power to reconsider its final judgment even after dismissal of a review petition?
  3. What are the parameters, grounds, and procedural safeguards governing such a remedy?

► Landmark Judgment & Holding (Unanimous):

  1. Article 32 Cannot Lie Against Supreme Court Judgments: Reaffirming Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra (AIR 1967 SC 1), the Court held that a writ petition under Article 32 cannot be maintained against a final judgment of the Supreme Court. One bench of the Supreme Court cannot issue a writ to another bench; judicial orders of constitutional courts do not violate Part III rights for the purpose of writ jurisdiction.
  2. Invention of the "Curative Petition": However, balancing the maxim interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium (it is in public interest that litigation must end) with the duty to prevent manifest injustice, the Court held that under its inherent plenary powers (Articles 137 and 142), it possesses the power to cure defects in its own final orders through a "CURATIVE PETITION".
  3. Extremely Narrow Substantive Grounds: A Curative Petition can ONLY be entertained on two strict grounds:
    • (a) Violation of Principles of Natural Justice: The petitioner was not served with notice or was not given a hearing before the order was passed.
    • (b) Apparent Bias of the Judge: The judge failed to disclose a conflict of interest, connection with the parties, or personal bias.
  4. Four-Tier Procedural Filters to Prevent Frivolous Petitions:
    • Filter 1: The petition must aver that the grounds mentioned were raised in the review petition and that the review petition was dismissed by circulation.
    • Filter 2: Senior Advocate Certificate: Must be certified by a Senior Advocate with specific averments that the strict grounds laid down in Rupa Hurra are satisfied.
    • Filter 3: Collegiate Chamber Screening: Circulated first to a Bench of the three senior-most Judges of the Supreme Court AND the Judges who delivered the original judgment (if still available).
    • Filter 4: Exemplary Costs: If at any stage the petition is found frivolous or without merit, exemplary costs shall be imposed.
"The concern of the court for doing complete justice must be balanced with the need for finality of litigation. A curative petition is not a matter of course. It is an extraordinary remedy available only in cases of manifest injustice where the foundational principles of natural justice were breached or the adjudicator was biased." — Syed Shah Mohammed Quadri, J. (2002) 4 SCC 388

► Procedural Codification:

Subsequently codified into Order XLVIII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013. Serves as the ultimate, rarest-of-rare constitutional safety valve in Indian judicial hierarchy.

Case No. 45 • Limits of Article 32 Post-Curative

Zakarius Lakra & Others v. Union of India

(2005) 3 SCC 161 | 2-Judge Bench
Coram: Y.K. Sabharwal and P.P. Naolekar, JJ.

► Material Facts & Context:

Zakarius Lakra and co-convicts were awarded the death penalty for gruesome murders. Their appeals, review petitions under Article 137, and curative petitions under the Rupa Ashok Hurra doctrine had all been considered and dismissed by the Supreme Court. The President of India had also rejected their mercy petitions under Article 72.

Subsequently, human rights organizations and activists filed a fresh writ petition under Article 32 on behalf of the convicts, seeking to stay the execution of the death sentence and re-examine the trial evidence and sentence on grounds of alleged delays and procedural irregularities.

► Core Legal Issues:

  1. Whether an Article 32 petition is maintainable to reopen criminal convictions and sentences after regular appeals, reviews, and curative petitions have been dismissed?
  2. What is the scope of third-party locus standi under Article 32 in matters where the convict has already exhausted all constitutional remedies?

► Holding & Jurisprudential Rationale:

  1. Reaffirmation of Absolute Finality: The Supreme Court dismissed the writ petition, holding that once regular appeals, reviews, and curative petitions are dismissed, the judicial verdict attains irrevocable finality. Litigants cannot repeatedly invoke Article 32 to reopen settled criminal convictions. Doing so amounts to gross abuse of the process of the court.
  2. Article 32 Cannot Act as a Second Appeal in Disguise: The Court reiterated that Article 32 cannot be used as an endless appellate forum. No third party or activist can be permitted to derail execution by filing repetitive petitions challenging the merits of conviction.
  3. Narrow Exception for Supervening Events: The Court clarified that the only post-curative ground maintainable under Article 32 is a challenge founded on supervening circumstances (such as inordinate, unexplained executive delay by the President/Governor in deciding mercy petitions causing dehumanizing agony, as later authoritatively settled in Shatrughan Chauhan v. UOI (2014) 3 SCC 1). In the absence of genuine supervening grounds, the court will not interfere.

► Principle Evolved:

Reinforced the doctrine of finality of judicial pronouncements post-Rupa Hurra and established that Article 32 cannot be transformed into an endless collateral challenge to reopen capital convictions, preserving institutional closure in the criminal justice system.

Part III & Part VI • Judicial Review in Action

The Law of Prerogative Writs (Articles 32 & 226)

Exhaustive doctrinal analysis of the five extraordinary constitutional writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari.

Constitutional Genesis: The Prerogative Writs

Originating in English Common Law as royal prerogative remedies issued by the King's Bench, Articles 32 and 226 democratized these writs as constitutional guarantees against arbitrary state action. While Article 32 is restricted exclusively to the enforcement of Fundamental Rights (Part III), Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights "and for any other purpose" (enforcement of ordinary legal rights and statutory obligations).

Writ 1 • Personal Liberty Safeguard

1. Writ of Habeas Corpus ("Have the Body")

Latin: "Habeas Corpus ad subjiciendum" – The Great Writ of Liberty

§ Meaning, Scope & Legal Nature:

A judicial order in the nature of a command directing the person or authority who has detained another to produce the body of the detenu before the court, so that the court can examine the validity, legality, and jurisdiction of such detention. If the detention lacks legal justification, the court immediately sets the prisoner at liberty.

§ Grounds for Issuance:

  • Detention without authority of law or in violation of statutory procedure.
  • Violation of constitutional guarantees under Article 21 (right to personal liberty) and Article 22 (safeguards against arrest and preventive detention).
  • Detention by executive authority actuated by mala fides or collateral purpose.
  • Detention under an ultra vires or unconstitutional law.

§ Against Whom Available & Locus Standi:

  • Both Public and Private Detentions: Available against the State, police authorities, jail superintendents, AND private individuals (e.g., illegal custody of a child, wrongful confinement by family members).
  • Liberal Locus Standi: The petition may be filed by the detenu himself, or by any relative, friend, or public-spirited person on his behalf (Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration, AIR 1980 SC 1579 – prisoner's letter converted into habeas corpus to protect fellow inmate from torture).

§ When Habeas Corpus CANNOT be Issued (Exceptions):

  1. Where the detention is in execution of a sentence or order of a competent court having jurisdiction.
  2. Where the person is in custody pursuant to an order of remand by a Magistrate.
  3. Where the detention is for contempt of court or breach of parliamentary privilege.
  4. Where the detenu is outside the territorial jurisdiction of the High Court (under Art. 226).
Overruling ADM Jabalpur: In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) 10 SCC 1, a 9-Judge Bench formally and decisively overruled the infamous Emergency ruling in ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) 2 SCC 521, holding that the right to life and liberty under Article 21 cannot be suspended even during an Emergency and Habeas Corpus remains inviolable.
Writ 2 • Enforcement of Public Duties

2. Writ of Mandamus ("We Command")

The Command to Perform a Public Legal Duty

§ Meaning & Substantive Elements:

A judicial command issued by the Supreme Court or High Court to any public authority, statutory corporation, subordinate tribunal, or government official directing them to perform a mandatory public legal duty imposed upon them by the Constitution or statute, which they have failed or refused to perform.

§ Mandatory Pre-Conditions for Mandamus:

  1. Public Duty of Imperative Character: The respondent must have a duty imposed by law which is not merely discretionary, but mandatory and ministerial.
  2. Clear Legal Right in Petitioner: The applicant must possess a corresponding legal right to enforce the performance of that duty.
  3. Demand and Refusal: The petitioner must have made a formal demand upon the authority to perform the duty, and the authority must have refused or failed to act within a reasonable time (Saraswati Industrial Syndicate v. UOI, AIR 1975 SC 460).

§ Against Whom Mandamus DOES NOT Lie:

  • Against the President of India or Governor of a State for the exercise and performance of official duties (absolute constitutional immunity under Article 361).
  • Against private individuals or purely private companies lacking any statutory public character (Praga Tools Corporation v. C.A. Imanual, AIR 1969 SC 1306).
  • To enforce contractual obligations without any statutory backing (Radhakrishna Agarwal v. State of Bihar, AIR 1977 SC 1496).
  • To compel the legislature to enact a law, or executive to frame rules (State of J&K v. A.R. Zakki, AIR 1992 SC 1546).
Writ 3 • Preventive Jurisdictional Check

3. Writ of Prohibition ("To Forbid")

Preventive Writ to Restrain Inferior Courts and Tribunals

§ Meaning & Preventive Character:

An extraordinary judicial writ issued by a superior court to an inferior court, statutory tribunal, or quasi-judicial body to prevent it from usurping or exceeding jurisdiction that is not legally vested in it, or from violating principles of natural justice.

Fundamental Maxim: "Prevention is better than cure." While Certiorari is curative (issued after the order is passed to quash it), Prohibition is preventive – issued while proceedings are still pending to stop the lower forum from proceeding further.

§ Grounds for Issuance:

  • Absence of Jurisdiction: The court or tribunal has no legal authority to entertain the matter.
  • Excess of Jurisdiction: The tribunal has jurisdiction over the subject, but exceeds its statutory powers.
  • Violation of Natural Justice: Clear bias of the adjudicator (nemo judex in causa sua) or denial of fair hearing during pending proceedings.
  • Infringement of Fundamental Rights: Where the law under which proceedings are initiated is ultra vires.

§ Limitations:

Cannot be issued against legislative bodies, executive officials performing purely administrative functions, or private individuals. Lies strictly against judicial and quasi-judicial authorities.

Writ 4 • Curative Jurisdictional & Legal Review

4. Writ of Certiorari ("To Be Certified")

The Curative and Corrective Writ to Quash Illegal Decisions

§ Meaning & Function:

A judicial order issued by a superior court commanding an inferior court, tribunal, or quasi-judicial/administrative authority to certify and transmit its records of a completed proceeding to the superior court, so that the legality of its decision can be reviewed and, if found illegal, quashed.

§ Classical Grounds for Issuing Certiorari (The Golden Quadrant):

  1. Defect of Jurisdiction: Lack of initial jurisdiction, excess of jurisdiction, or failure to exercise jurisdiction.
  2. Violation of Principles of Natural Justice:
    • Audi alteram partem (denial of opportunity of being heard).
    • Nemo judex in causa sua (rule against pecuniary, personal, or subject-matter bias).
  3. Error of Law Apparent on the Face of the Record: An error that is patent, manifest, and self-evident on a mere inspection of the record without requiring elaborate arguments or re-examination of evidence (Syed Yakoob v. K.S. Radhakrishnan, AIR 1964 SC 477). A mere error of fact cannot be corrected under Certiorari.
  4. Fraud, Collusion, or Mala Fides.

§ Modern Expansion to Administrative Actions:

In A.K. Kraipak v. Union of India (AIR 1970 SC 150) and Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (AIR 1978 SC 597), the Supreme Court abolished the rigid distinction between quasi-judicial and purely administrative acts. Today, Certiorari lies against any administrative authority whose decision affects civil rights and violates fairness, reasonableness (Art. 14), or natural justice.

Writ 5 • Scrutiny of Public Office Usurpation

5. Writ of Quo Warranto ("By What Warrant?")

Judicial Challenge to Unauthorized Usurpation of Public Office

§ Meaning & Constitutional Purpose:

A writ issued to enquire into the legality of the claim which a party asserts to an office or franchise, and to oust him from its enjoyment if the claim is not well founded. It protects the public from being deprived of public offices by usurpers lacking the statutory or constitutional qualifications.

§ Four Essential Conditions for Quo Warranto (University of Mysore v. C.D. Govinda Rao, AIR 1965 SC 491):

  1. The office in question must be a public office (an office created by the Constitution or statute with duties involving the public).
  2. The office must be of a substantive character (an independent office with permanent tenure, not an employment at will or subordinate servant).
  3. The respondent must be in actual physical occupation/possession of the office.
  4. The appointment was made in flagrant violation of statutory or constitutional qualification requirements.

§ Locus Standi – Anyone May Challenge:

Unlike other writs where a personal aggrieved right is necessary, any member of the public (relator) can apply for Quo Warranto, even if he has no personal interest or rivalry in the office. Every citizen has a legitimate interest in ensuring that public offices are occupied only by persons legally qualified (B.R. Kapur v. State of Tamil Nadu (2001) 7 SCC 231 – Quo Warranto issued declaring J. Jayalalithaa unconstitutionally appointed as Chief Minister due to criminal conviction).

PYQ 1 • Judicial Appointments Architecture (20 Marks) Centenary Chance Exam (LB-301) Q.2
Question: "Examine in detail the provisions of the Constitution of India regarding the appointment of Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts with case laws." (20 Marks)

1. Introduction & Constitutional Philosophy

The appointment of Judges to the superior judiciary is the foundational bulwark of the Independence of the Judiciary, which has been authoritatively recognized as an inviolable component of the Basic Structure of the Indian Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, AIR 1973 SC 1461). The Constitution of India avoids both extremes – unbridled executive patronage (as historically seen in England prior to 2005) and direct election by the populace or legislature (as in certain US states). Instead, the Framers envisioned a participatory, consultative process under Articles 124(2) and 217(1). Over seven decades, judicial interpretation has transformed the textual term "consultation" into "concurrence" through the four historic Judges Cases.

2. Constitutional Provisions Governing Appointments

  • Article 124(2) – Appointment of Supreme Court Judges: Every Judge of the Supreme Court is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal after "consultation with such of the Judges of the Supreme Court and of the High Courts in the States as the President may deem necessary". Under the mandatory Proviso, in the appointment of a Judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of India shall always be consulted.
  • Article 217(1) – Appointment of High Court Judges: Every Judge of a High Court is appointed by the President by warrant after consultation with: (i) the Chief Justice of India, (ii) the Governor of the State, and (iii) in case of appointment of a Judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the High Court.
  • Article 222(1) – Transfer of High Court Judges: The President may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, transfer a Judge from one High Court to any other High Court.

3. Judicial Evolution Through the Landmark Cases

  1. Union of India v. Sankalchand Himatlal Sheth (1977) 4 SCC 193: A 5-Judge Bench held that "consultation" under Article 222(1) cannot be a casual or perfunctory dialogue. It must be full, effective, and meaningful, with all relevant materials placed before the CJI. While transfer does not require the judge's consent, it can only be made in the "public interest" and cannot be punitive.
  2. The First Judges Case – S.P. Gupta v. President of India (1981 Supp SCC 87): A 7-Judge Constitution Bench ruled by a majority that "consultation does not mean concurrence". The Court held that all constitutional consultees stand on coordinate footing and the CJI has no primacy. The ultimate deciding power rests with the Central Government/Executive. This established Executive Supremacy, leading to extensive political interference throughout the 1980s.
  3. The Second Judges Case – Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993) 4 SCC 441: A 9-Judge Constitution Bench decisively overruled S.P. Gupta (7:2 majority, per J.S. Verma, J.). The Court held that judicial independence is a basic feature, requiring Judicial Primacy in appointments. "Consultation" was interpreted as requiring agreement. The Court birthed the Collegium System, holding that the CJI's opinion represents a collective view formed in consultation with his two senior-most puisne colleagues. The executive can only request reconsideration once on strong, cogent reasons; if reiterated unanimously, the appointment is mandatory. The senior-most judge of the SC must be appointed CJI.
  4. The Third Judges Case – In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 (1998) 7 SCC 739: Answering a 9-question reference under Article 143(1), a unanimous 9-Judge Bench expanded and institutionalized the Collegium:
    • SC appointments & HC transfers require a Collegium of CJI + 4 senior-most SC Judges.
    • HC appointments require CJI + 2 senior-most SC Judges.
    • Plurality is mandatory: the sole individual opinion of the CJI does not constitute "consultation".
    • If two judges in the Collegium give an adverse opinion, the CJI cannot recommend the candidate.
    • Recommendations made without following collegium norms are not binding on the Government.
  5. The Fourth Judges Case / NJAC Case – SC AOR Association v. Union of India (2016) 5 SCC 1: Struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 and the NJAC Act, 2014 (4:1 majority). Held that judicial primacy in appointments is part of the Basic Structure. Inclusion of the Law Minister and two eminent persons with veto power undermined judicial insulation. Revived the Collegium System (status quo ante).

4. Critical Assessment & Conclusion

The contemporary appointment framework presents a constitutional paradox: while the text of Articles 124(2) and 217(1) vests appointing authority in the President after consultation, judicial interpretation has vested de facto appointing authority in the judicial collegium. While this has effectively shielded the higher judiciary from executive subjugation and political packing (safeguarding the Basic Structure), it has attracted criticism for opacity, lack of objective evaluation criteria, and delays in vacancy filling. A revised Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) ensuring transparency, institutional secretariats, and structured consultation remains an urgent necessity to balance judicial independence with democratic accountability.

PYQ 2 • Constitutionality of Articles 323A(2)(d) & 323B(3)(b) (20 Marks) December 2024 Exam (LB-301) Q.4
Question: "Articles 323 A and 323 B were inserted in the Constitution of India through the Constitution (Forty-second amendment) Act 1976. Discuss the constitutionality of Article 323A (2)(d) and Article 323 B(3) (b). Answer in light of relevant constitutional law principles, legal provisions and judgments." (20 Marks)

1. Introduction & Legislative Genesis

By the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976, Parliament introduced Part XIV-A into the Constitution, comprising Articles 323A and 323B. The ostensible objective of these provisions was to reduce the crushing docket explosion in the High Courts by setting up specialized administrative and subject-matter tribunals. However, embedded within these provisions were sweeping exclusion of jurisdiction clauses:

  • Article 323A(2)(d): Empowered Parliament to enact a law for "excluding the jurisdiction of all courts, except the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 136, with respect to the disputes or complaints referred to in clause (1)" (service matters).
  • Article 323B(3)(d) / (3)(b): Empowered Parliament and State Legislatures to exclude the jurisdiction of all courts, except the Supreme Court under Article 136, with respect to specified subjects (taxation, foreign exchange, industrial disputes, land reforms, ceiling on urban property, elections to legislatures, food-stuffs, rent and tenancy).

Pursuant to Article 323A, Parliament enacted the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, wherein Section 28 expressly ousted the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227.

2. The Shift from Sampath Kumar to L. Chandra Kumar

In S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987) 1 SCC 124, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench initially upheld the constitutional validity of Section 28 of the 1985 Act. The Court propounded the "Alternative Institutional Mechanism" doctrine, holding that while judicial review is a basic feature, it does not require that judicial review must always be exercised by regular constitutional courts; Parliament could exclude High Court jurisdiction if it created an equally efficacious alternative forum, preserving an ultimate appeal to the Supreme Court under Article 136.

However, the functioning of tribunals over the next decade revealed severe systemic pathologies: tribunals were treated as appendages of the executive ministries against which they adjudicated; members lacked security of tenure and constitutional insulation; and in J.B. Chopra v. Union of India (AIR 1987 SC 357), single administrative members were deciding the constitutional vires of parliamentary statutes. This necessitated a comprehensive reconsideration by a 7-Judge Constitution Bench in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) 3 SCC 261.

3. Doctrinal Holdings in L. Chandra Kumar (1997)

Delivering the unanimous verdict of the 7-Judge Bench, Chief Justice A.M. Ahmadi held:

  1. Judicial Review is Inviolable Basic Structure: The power of judicial review over legislative and executive action vested in the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is an essential and integral feature of the Constitution, forming part of the inviolable Basic Structure. Relying on Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's speech in the Constituent Assembly characterizing draft Article 25 (now Art. 32) as the "very soul and heart of the Constitution", the Court held that this power can never be ousted even by a constitutional amendment.
  2. High Court Judicial Superintendence (Article 227): The power of judicial superintendence vested in High Courts over all courts and tribunals in their territory is also an integral component of the Basic Structure. Divesting High Courts of their judicial supervisory role damages the constitutional architecture.
  3. Unconstitutionality of Exclusion Clauses: The Court declared Clause 2(d) of Article 323A and Clause 3(d) of Article 323B UNCONSTITUTIONAL AND VOID to the extent they excluded the jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Article 32. Consequentially, Section 28 of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 and all identical exclusion clauses in tribunal enactments were struck down.
  4. Tribunals as "Supplemental" Adjudicatory Bodies: Tribunals cannot be regarded as full or effective substitutes for the High Courts because tribunal members lack the constitutional safeguards, security of tenure, and institutional independence enjoyed by High Court Judges under Articles 217-221. Tribunals can only play a supplemental role.
  5. The Two-Tier Architecture Established:
    • Courts of First Instance: Tribunals constituted under Arts. 323A and 323B continue to act as courts of first instance. Litigants cannot directly bypass tribunals and approach High Courts (except where the vires of the parent Act creating the tribunal itself is challenged).
    • Mandatory Division Bench High Court Review: All decisions rendered by Tribunals are mandatorily subject to judicial review before a DIVISION BENCH of the respective High Court under Articles 226/227. Direct recourse to the Supreme Court under Article 136 without approaching the High Court is barred.
  6. Competence over Constitutional Validity: Tribunals possess the jurisdiction to test the constitutional vires of statutory rules and subordinate legislation, provided the matter is heard by a Bench of at least two members, one of whom must be a Judicial Member (harmonious construction of Section 5(6)).

4. Subsequent Reinforcement: Madras Bar Association (2014)

The doctrine was further entrenched in Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2014) 10 SCC 1, where the Supreme Court struck down the National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005. The Court reaffirmed that core judicial functions of High Courts – such as deciding substantial questions of law – cannot be transferred to tribunals lacking judicial character, security of tenure, and independence.

5. Conclusion

The unconstitutionality of Article 323A(2)(d) and Article 323B(3)(d) established that while Parliament can create specialized forums to assist the judiciary, it cannot create judicial enclaves insulated from constitutional scrutiny. The writ jurisdiction of High Courts under Articles 226/227 remains the supreme constitutional safeguard preserving the Rule of Law in the Indian federation.

PYQ 3 • Critical Evaluation of NJAC & Fourth Judges Case (20 Marks) Standard DU LL.B. Question
Question: "Critically examine the constitutional validity of the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 and the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) Act, 2014 in light of Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2016). Did the Supreme Court prioritize judicial independence over democratic accountability?" (20 Marks)

1. Introduction

The verdict in Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2016) 5 SCC 1 (popularly known as the Fourth Judges Case or the NJAC Verdict) represents the high-water mark of judicial assertiveness in India. By a 4:1 majority, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench invalidated a constitutional amendment passed unanimously by both Houses of Parliament and ratified by 20 States, striking down the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) and reviving the judge-led Collegium system.

2. The NJAC Scheme & The Constitutional Challenge

The 99th Amendment inserted Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C. Article 124A established a 6-member Commission comprising: (i) CJI (Chairperson), (ii) Two senior-most SC Judges, (iii) Union Minister of Law and Justice, and (iv) Two "eminent persons" selected by a committee of PM, CJI, and Leader of Opposition. Section 6(6) of the NJAC Act provided that if any two members disagreed, the person could not be recommended.

3. Rationale of the Majority (Khehar, Lokur, Kurian Joseph & Goel, JJ.)

  • Judicial Primacy as Basic Structure: The majority ruled that the primacy of the judiciary in appointments is an indispensable component of the Independence of the Judiciary. Without judicial primacy, independence cannot survive executive encroachment.
  • Executive as Litigant: The Union Government is the single largest litigant before superior courts. Giving the Law Minister a seat and veto power creates an insurmountable conflict of interest, eroding the separation of powers.
  • The Veto of "Eminent Persons": Non-judicial persons with undefined qualifications could team up with the executive to veto meritorious judicial candidates unanimously endorsed by the CJI and senior judges.
  • Automatic Revival: The Court ruled that striking down an unconstitutional amendment automatically restores the status quo ante, reviving the Collegium system.

4. The Powerful Dissent of Justice J. Chelameswar

Justice J. Chelameswar delivered a trenchant dissent, arguing that:

  • Judicial independence does not require an absolute judicial monopoly over appointments. The Collegium system is completely absent from the text of the Constitution and operates in total secrecy, prone to nepotism and subjective preferences.
  • Checks and balances are the bedrock of constitutionalism; executive and civil society participation in selection enhances democratic legitimacy.

5. Critical Assessment: Independence vs. Accountability

The Court undoubtedly prioritized institutional independence over accountability. While the judgment successfully shielded the judiciary from the executive's "spoils system", it left the higher judiciary as a self-appointing body unique in global constitutional democracies. Even the majority conceded flaws in the collegium and directed the framing of a new Memorandum of Procedure (MoP). A truly balanced constitutional model requires structured, transparent selection criteria rather than opaque informal consultations.

PYQ 4 • Review (Art. 137) vs. Curative Petitions & Death Penalty Reviews (20 Marks) DU LL.B. Semester Exam Core Question
Question: "Explain the evolution, scope, and grounds of Curative Petitions as established in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002). How does it differ from the power of Review under Article 137? Discuss how Mohd. Arif v. Registrar, Supreme Court (2014) modified review procedure in death penalty cases." (20 Marks)

1. Power of Review under Article 137

Under Article 137, the Supreme Court has the power to review any judgment or order pronounced by it, subject to parliamentary legislation and rules under Article 145. Under Order XLVII of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013, review in civil matters is guided by Order 47 Rule 1 CPC (discovery of new evidence, error apparent on face of record, or other sufficient reason). In criminal matters, review is confined strictly to an error apparent on the face of the record. A review is filed within 30 days and is ordinarily disposed of by circulation in chambers without oral hearing.

2. Genesis & Scope of Curative Petition: Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002)

In Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002) 4 SCC 388, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench settled that an Article 32 writ petition cannot lie against a final judgment of the Supreme Court. However, to prevent abuse of process and gross miscarriage of justice, the Court invoked its inherent powers under Articles 137 and 142 to evolve the "Curative Petition" as a rare, exceptional remedy post-dismissal of a review petition.

Substantive Grounds for Curative Petition:
  1. Violation of Natural Justice: The petitioner was not served with notice or was denied a hearing.
  2. Bias of the Judge: The adjudicator had an undisclosed interest or bias in the outcome.
Mandatory Procedural Safeguards (Order XLVIII SC Rules 2013):
  • Grounds must have been taken in the review petition, and review must have been dismissed by circulation.
  • Must contain a certificate by a Senior Advocate confirming fulfillment of Rupa Hurra grounds.
  • Circulated first to a Bench of 3 senior-most Judges + the Judges who passed the original order.
  • Listed for open hearing only if majority concludes there is genuine merit; exemplary costs if frivolous.

3. Review vs. Curative Petition: Comparative Distinctions

Feature Review Petition (Article 137) Curative Petition (Rupa Hurra)
Constitutional Basis Express textual provision under Article 137. Inherent power under Articles 137, 142 & natural justice.
Stage of Invocation First challenge after pronouncement of judgment. Last resort: filed ONLY AFTER review petition is dismissed.
Permissible Grounds Discovery of evidence; Error apparent on face of record. Strictly limited: (1) Breach of natural justice; (2) Judicial bias.
Senior Counsel Filter Not mandatory. Mandatory certificate signed by a Senior Advocate.
Bench Composition Heard by the same Bench that delivered judgment. Heard by 3 senior-most SC Judges + surviving original Judges.

4. Open Court Hearing in Death Penalty Reviews: Mohd. Arif (2014)

In Mohd. Arif v. Registrar, Supreme Court (2014) 9 SCC 737, a 5-Judge Constitution Bench created a vital constitutional exception to chamber circulation. The Court held that in all cases where the death penalty has been awarded, review petitions under Article 137 MUST be heard in OPEN COURT by a bench of at least THREE Judges, with oral arguments permitted up to 30 minutes. The Court grounded this in Article 21, holding that procedural due process demands the highest degree of fairness when irrevocable capital punishment is involved.

5. Limits Post-Curative: Zakarius Lakra (2005)

In Zakarius Lakra v. Union of India (2005) 3 SCC 161, the Court reiterated that once review and curative petitions are dismissed, litigants cannot invoke Article 32 to reopen convictions. The only post-curative ground is a challenge founded on supervening events (such as inordinate, unexplained executive delay in deciding mercy petitions, per Shatrughan Chauhan).

PYQ 5 • The Master of the Roster Doctrine (15 Marks) DU LL.B. Semester Exam Question
Question: "'The Chief Justice of India is first among equals judicially, but Master of the Roster administratively.' Discuss the scope of the administrative powers of the CJI regarding bench constitution and allocation of matters in light of Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India (2018)." (15 Marks)

Answer Structure & Analysis

1. Judicial Parity vs. Administrative Leadership: Judicially, all Supreme Court judges possess coordinate constitutional authority. The CJI is primus inter pares (first among equals) – his judicial vote carries the exact same weight as any puisne judge. However, administratively, the CJI is the institutional head and the sole authority empowered to regulate business under Article 145 and Order VI Rule 1 of the Supreme Court Rules, 2013.

2. The Ruling in Shanti Bhushan v. Supreme Court of India (2018) 8 SCC 396: Following controversies regarding bench allocation, Senior Advocate Shanti Bhushan sought a declaration that the Master of the Roster power should be exercised by the 5-judge Collegium. The Supreme Court rejected this plea:

  • Collegium Inapplicable to Roster: The Collegium concept established in the Second and Third Judges cases was evolved strictly for appointments and transfers under Articles 124, 217, and 222. It cannot be imported into day-to-day administrative roster allocation.
  • Administrative Paralysis: If bench constitution required consensus among five senior judges, daily court management would descend into chaotic friction and institutional paralysis.
  • Exclusive Discretion: The CJI alone constitutes benches and allocates rosters. No individual judge can assign a matter to himself or alter roster allocations (reaffirming State of Rajasthan v. Prakash Chand (1998) 1 SCC 1 and CJAR v. UOI (2018) 2 SCC 196).

3. Conclusion: The Court held that the CJI's power as Master of the Roster is an administrative necessity, grounded on high constitutional trust and conventions. While absolute, it must be exercised bona fide, transparently, and in alignment with judges' subject expertise to preserve institutional credibility.

PYQ 6 • Writ Jurisdictions & Certiorari vs. Prohibition vs. Mandamus (20 Marks) DU LL.B. Semester Exam Core Question
Question: "Compare and contrast the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 32 with that of the High Courts under Article 226. Examine the scope, essential conditions, and landmark precedents for Certiorari, Prohibition, and Mandamus." (20 Marks)

Answer Structure & Analysis

1. Article 32 vs. Article 226 Comparison:

  • Status of Right: Article 32 is itself a guaranteed Fundamental Right under Part III. The Supreme Court cannot refuse relief where a Part III violation is established (Romesh Thappar). Article 226 is a constitutional power, and High Court relief is discretionary.
  • Substantive Scope: Article 32 is exercisable solely for the enforcement of Part III Fundamental Rights. Article 226 is exercisable for Fundamental Rights "and for any other purpose" (ordinary legal and statutory rights). Hence, the High Court's substantive jurisdiction is wider.
  • Territorial Scope: The Supreme Court has pan-India territorial jurisdiction. High Courts have jurisdiction within their State territories, or where the cause of action arises in whole or in part (Art. 226(2)).

2. Mandamus ("We Command"): An order commanding a public authority to perform a mandatory statutory duty. Conditions: (i) imperative public legal duty; (ii) clear legal right in petitioner; (iii) prior demand and refusal. Does not lie against the President/Governor (Art. 361) or private bodies without public duties.

3. Prohibition vs. Certiorari:

  • Prohibition (Preventive): Issued to an inferior court or tribunal to stop pending proceedings that are in excess of jurisdiction or in breach of natural justice. It arrests the trial before a decision is reached.
  • Certiorari (Curative): Issued after the inferior court or tribunal has rendered its decision, commanding the transmission of records to quash the illegal order. Grounds: (i) defect of jurisdiction; (ii) violation of natural justice; (iii) error of law apparent on the face of the record (Syed Yakoob). Extended to administrative actions in A.K. Kraipak and Maneka Gandhi.
PYQ 7 • Advisory Jurisdiction under Article 143 (15 Marks) DU LL.B. Semester Exam Core Question
Question: "Analyze the nature, scope, and legal effect of the Supreme Court's Advisory Jurisdiction under Article 143. Is the Supreme Court bound to answer every reference? Is the President bound by the Court's opinion?" (15 Marks)

Answer Structure & Analysis

1. Dual Dimensions of Article 143:

  • Article 143(1) – General Questions: If the President considers that a question of law or fact has arisen, or is likely to arise, of public importance, he may refer it to the Supreme Court. The Court "may... report to the President its opinion thereon".
  • Article 143(2) – Pre-Constitution Treaties: Questions excluded from Article 131 proviso. Here, the Court "shall... report to the President" (mandatory obligation).

2. Discretion of the Supreme Court to Decline Answering: Under Article 143(1), the word "may" confers judicial discretion. The Supreme Court is not bound to answer every reference. It may decline to answer if the reference is hypothetical, speculative, academic, or politically partisan. Landmark Precedent: In M. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994) 6 SCC 360 (Ayodhya Reference), the 5-Judge Bench declined to answer whether a Hindu temple originally existed at the site of the Babri Masjid, holding that the question was historical, contentious, and intended to bypass civil court adjudication.

3. Legal Effect & Binding Nature:

  • On the President: The advisory opinion is purely advisory counsel. The President/Central Government is not legally bound to act upon it.
  • Under Article 141: In In re Special Courts Bill, 1978 (1979) 1 SCC 380, the Supreme Court held that while an advisory opinion does not strictly amount to "law declared" under Article 141 (as there are no contesting parties or lis), it carries the highest persuasive authority and is binding on all High Courts and subordinate courts throughout India.
