§ How to read these notes

Topic 3 is really two chapters of the Partnership Act bolted together, and the DU paper treats them as one unit. Chapter V (ss. 31–38) answers a question of status: how does a person become a partner, and by how many doors can he stop being one? Chapter VII (ss. 56–72) answers a question of proof and procedure: is the firm on the Register, and if it is not, what happens to its lawsuit?

The golden rule of this topic: every one of the eight sections in Chapter V carries, expressly or impliedly, the words "subject to contract between the partners" — s. 31(1) says it, s. 32(1)(b) says it, s. 33(1) says it, s. 34(2) and s. 35 say it, s. 36(1) and (2) say it, and s. 37 says it. So the partnership deed governs, and the Act only fills the gaps. Chapter VII is the opposite: it is machinery, it is mandatory in form, and s. 69 in particular is a procedural bar which the court must enforce — even suo motu — regardless of what the partners agreed.

A

Incoming and Outgoing Partners — Chapter V, ss. 31 to 38

One door in (s. 31), six doors out (ss. 32–35, plus dissolution and transfer), and three sections that govern what happens afterwards (ss. 36–38).

§ The two ideas that run through the whole chapter
  1. Partnership is a personal relationship built on delectus personae — the choice of persons. That is why no one can be brought in without the consent of all (s. 31(1)), and why a partner cannot be thrown out except by a power the partners have themselves agreed to (s. 33(1)).
  2. Outsiders are entitled to rely on appearances. A man who has dealt with a firm as a partner cannot quietly slip out and leave the creditor without a remedy: until public notice is given he remains liable to the world at large (s. 32(3)), and until actual notice reaches those who dealt with him before, he remains liable to them (Scarf v. Jardine, Topic 2 §2.7).

§1.2 — Section 31: Introduction of a partner

§ The section, verbatim

31. Introduction of a partner.— (1) Subject to contract between the partners and to the provisions of section 30, no person shall be introduced as a partner into a firm without the consent of all the existing partners.

(2) Subject to the provisions of section 30, a person who is introduced as a partner into a firm does not thereby become liable for any act of the firm done before he became a partner.

✱ Element-by-element: s. 31(1)
  1. "Subject to contract between the partners" — the partners may, by the deed, provide that a new partner may be brought in by a majority, or by a named partner, or on the happening of an event. Where they do, that provision prevails.
  2. "and to the provisions of section 30" — a minor cannot be introduced as a partner at all (s. 30(1)); he can only be admitted to the benefits, and that too only with the consent of all.
  3. "no person shall be introduced … without the consent of all the existing partners" — the default rule is unanimity. Contrast s. 12(c), where ordinary matters are decided by majority: bringing in a partner is not an ordinary matter, because it changes the delectus personae and it changes who is liable.
  4. Consent may be express or implied — s. 11(1) allows variation "by the consent of all the partners, which consent may be express or implied by a course of dealing". A person who is held out and acquiesces is the classic illustration.
✱ Element-by-element: s. 31(2)
  1. The rule: an incoming partner is not liable for the firm's past debts.
  2. What "act of the firm done before he became a partner" means — every obligation incurred before the date of his admission: money borrowed, goods bought, torts committed, rent accrued.
  3. The date is the date of admission, not the date of the deed, and not the date of registration.
  4. This is "subject to contract" — he may expressly assume the past liabilities, and a deed very often does provide that the incoming partner takes over a stated share of the firm's debts. He then becomes liable to the firm inter se; but he cannot, by a private contract, become liable to the creditor without the creditor's consent — see s. 32(2) for the converse case, and the general rule that novation requires the consent of all three (s. 62, ICA 1872).
  5. Contrast s. 25 — for acts done after he becomes a partner his liability is joint and several and unlimited.
Fig. 3 — The s. 31(2) bright line. Everything turns on the date of admission.
◈ Real-life illustration

Mehta, Nair and Oliveira run "MNO Interiors" with a cash-credit limit of ₹40 lakh from a bank, of which ₹31 lakh is drawn. On 1 April 2025 they admit Pereira, who brings in ₹25 lakh as capital. The bank's sanction letter had been signed only by the three original partners.

  • The ₹31 lakh drawn before 1 April — Pereira is not liable (s. 31(2)). If the deed says he takes over a share of it, he owes that money to the firm, but the bank cannot sue him on it without a novation under s. 62, ICA 1872.
  • Every rupee drawn after 1 April — Pereira is fully liable, jointly and severally (s. 25).
  • The bank's guarantee — if the guarantee was given "in respect of the transactions of the firm", then the change in the firm's constitution on 1 April revokes it as to future transactions under s. 38 unless the deed of guarantee says otherwise. Banks therefore always take a fresh guarantee letter on admission of a partner. This is the practical reason s. 38 matters.
  • Registration — within 90 days the firm must send an intimation under s. 63 or the Register will not show Pereira, and the firm will be unable to sue on any contract (s. 69(2)).

! The three traps examiners set on s. 31
  1. "The deed says new partners may be admitted by majority, so A and B admitted C over D's objection." Valid — s. 31(1) is "subject to contract between the partners". Say that first.
  2. "A, B and C agreed that on A's death his widow would automatically become a partner." Not valid against the surviving partners' objection if they withhold consent: the agreement is a contract to admit, and a contract to admit is not admission. The widow's remedy is a suit for specific performance or damages, not a declaration that she is a partner. (DU 1992 Q.1(a); DU 1995 Q.2(a); DU 1997 Q.1(b).)
  3. "The new partner is liable for the old debts because he got the firm's assets." Wrong. He may have to indemnify the firm inter se under the deed, but the creditor's remedy is against the old partners (s. 31(2)).

§1.3 — Section 32: Retirement of a partner

§ The section, verbatim

32. Retirement of a partner.— (1) A partner may retire—

(a) with the consent of all the other partners,

(b) in accordance with an express agreement by the partners, or

(c) where the partnership is at will, by giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to retire.

(2) A retiring partner may be discharged from any liability to any third party for acts of the firm done before his retirement by an agreement made by him with such third party and the partners of the reconstituted firm, and such agreement may be implied by a course of dealing between such third party and the reconstituted firm after he had knowledge of the retirement.

(3) Notwithstanding the retirement of a partner from a firm, he and the partners continue to be liable as partners to third parties for any act done by any of them which would have been an act of the firm if done before the retirement, until public notice is given of the retirement:

Provided that a retired partner is not liable to any third party who deals with the firm without knowing that he was a partner.

(4) Notices under sub-section (3) may be given by the retired partner or by any partner of the reconstituted firm.

Note: in a firm at will, mode (c) is available because s. 7(1) defines a firm at will as one where "no provision is made by contract between the partners for the duration of their partnership, or for the determination of their partnership". Where the deed does provide for duration or determination, mode (b) governs. Do not confuse s. 32(1)(c) with s. 43 — s. 43 is the notice that dissolves the whole firm; s. 32(1)(c) is the notice by which one partner steps out while the firm continues.

✱ s. 32(2) — the only way to wipe out the PAST liability

s. 32(3) keeps a retiring partner liable for future acts until public notice. But for acts already done before retirement he is liable anyway, under s. 25 — and the only escape is s. 32(2). Its four elements:

  1. an agreement — a tripartite arrangement, not a unilateral act;
  2. between three sets of people — the retiring partner, the third party (creditor), and the partners of the reconstituted firm;
  3. discharging the retiring partner from liability for pre-retirement acts; and
  4. the agreement may be implied by a course of dealing between the third party and the reconstituted firm after the third party had knowledge of the retirement.

This is a novation (s. 62, ICA 1872) dressed in partnership language. Example. A supplier who, after learning of A's retirement, goes on supplying goods to the reconstituted firm, renders bills in the new firm's name alone and accepts payments from the continuing partners for a year without demur — a court may infer the s. 32(2) agreement from that course of dealing.

◈ Real-life illustration — the s. 32(2) trap

"Ravi Traders" (Ravi, Sanjay, Tina) owe ₹12 lakh to a paper mill for stock delivered in March. Ravi retires on 1 June; public notice is given on 15 June. In September the firm (now Sanjay and Tina) orders more paper and the mill supplies it. The mill's ledger still shows "Ravi Traders" and its statements are still addressed to the old name. When the firm defaults, the mill sues all three.

  • The March debt: Ravi is liable (s. 25). He escapes only if he proves an s. 32(2) agreement — and the mill's continued use of the old name is evidence against any such agreement.
  • The September debt: Ravi is not liable — public notice was given on 15 June, before the September contract (s. 32(3)). But if the mill had known Ravi as a partner and had never been told, and no public notice had been given, he would be.
  • Moral: the retiring partner's checklist is — retirement deed; Gazette and vernacular newspaper notice (s. 72); intimation to the Registrar under s. 63 if registered; individual circular letters to every existing creditor and customer; and an express written release where the creditor will give one.

! The four traps examiners set on s. 32
  1. Using s. 32(1)(c) for a fixed-term firm. The notice route is available only where the partnership is at will. In a fixed-term firm he needs consent (a) or a clause in the deed (b).
  2. Saying public notice cures everything. It does not cure an existing dealer's claim: he is entitled to actual notice (Scarf v. Jardine).
  3. Thinking s. 32(3) is about acts done before retirement. It is not: it is about acts done after retirement which "would have been an act of the firm if done before". Pre-retirement liability comes from s. 25, and is released only under s. 32(2).
  4. Forgetting the proviso. A partner who was dormant throughout owes no notice to anybody, because nobody dealt with the firm on the faith of his being a partner. This is the same idea as s. 36(1) proviso (b) to the English s. 36(3) explained in Tower Cabinet.

§1.4 — Section 33: Expulsion of a partner

§ The section, verbatim

33. Expulsion of a partner.— (1) A partner may not be expelled from a firm by any majority of the partners, save in the exercise in good faith of powers conferred by contract between the partners.

(2) The provisions of sub-sections (2), (3) and (4) of section 32 shall apply to an expelled partner as if he were a retired partner.

✱ s. 33(1) — read the negatives carefully

The section is drafted as a prohibition with a single exception. It therefore contains four requirements:

  1. There must be a power to expel — "powers conferred by contract between the partners". Without a clause in the deed, no expulsion is possible, not even unanimous.
  2. The power must be exercised by a majority — the clause will normally say what majority (simple, two-thirds, special). A single partner cannot expel another unless the deed says so.
  3. The power must be exercised in good faith — this is the statutory Wednesbury-type control. The majority must act honestly, for the purpose for which the power was given, and not for a collateral advantage (e.g. to grab the expelled partner's share at an undervalue).
  4. The partner must, in fairness, be heard — although the section does not say so in terms, the requirement of good faith has been held to import the principles of natural justice. An expulsion in secret, without notice of the charges or an opportunity to answer, is not in good faith.

Note the contrast with s. 31(1): admission needs the consent of all; expulsion needs only a majority — but only where the contract confers the power, and only in good faith. The asymmetry is deliberate: the law makes it hard to force a person into a firm and hard to throw him out without agreement.

✱ s. 33(2) — the fiction that does the work

By a statutory fiction the expelled partner is treated as if he were a retired partner, and three provisions of s. 32 are lifted across:

  • s. 32(2) — he may be discharged from liability for acts done before the expulsion by an agreement with the third party and the partners of the reconstituted firm, which agreement may be implied by a course of dealing after the third party learned of the expulsion.
  • s. 32(3) — he continues liable to third parties for acts done by any of them which would have been acts of the firm, until public notice is given of the expulsion; with the proviso that he is not liable to a third party who dealt with the firm without knowing he was a partner.
  • s. 32(4) — the notice may be given by him or by any partner of the reconstituted firm. This matters: an expelled partner who has been thrown out against his will can protect himself by giving the notice himself.

Note the one thing s. 33(2) does not import — s. 32(1). The modes of voluntary retirement are irrelevant to expulsion, because expulsion is involuntary. And s. 37 (right to share subsequent profits) applies to him independently, since it speaks of a member who has "died or otherwise ceased to be a partner".

◈ Real-life illustrations
  • A valid expulsion. A four-partner chartered-accountancy firm's deed provides: "Any partner who is guilty of gross negligence or of conduct bringing the firm into disrepute may be expelled by a three-fourths majority." One partner is found to have forged a client's signature. The other three hold a meeting, give him written notice of the charge, hear his explanation, and resolve by 3:1 to expel him. That is within the power, by the required majority, and in good faith — valid.
  • An invalid one. The same three partners want the fourth out so that his 25% share can be taken over at book value rather than on a goodwill-inclusive valuation. They contrive a charge of "insubordination", give him no notice of the meeting, and expel him. That is a power exercised for a collateral purpose — not in good faith; the expulsion is void.
  • The aftermath in both cases. Whoever wins, the third parties are protected: until public notice is given under s. 72 — and notice to the Registrar under s. 63 if the firm is registered — the expelled partner remains liable for what the firm does (s. 33(2) → s. 32(3)).

§1.5 — Sections 34 and 35: Insolvency of a partner, and liability of the estate of a deceased partner

§ The sections, verbatim

34. Insolvency of a partner.— (1) Where a partner in a firm is adjudicated an insolvent, he ceases to be a partner on the date on which the order of adjudication is made, whether or not the firm is thereby dissolved.

(2) Where under a contract between the partners the firm is not dissolved by the adjudication of a partner as an insolvent, the estate of a partner so adjudicated is not liable for any act of the firm and the firm is not liable for any act of the insolvent, done after the date on which the order of adjudication is made.

35. Liability of estate of deceased partner.— Where under a contract between the partners the firm is not dissolved by the death of a partner, the estate of the deceased partner is not liable for any act of the firm done after his death.

§ The one idea behind both sections

The default rule is that death and insolvency dissolve the firm — s. 42(c) (death) and s. 42(d) (adjudication as an insolvent), both "subject to contract between the partners". Where the partners have contracted out of that (or where the surviving partners simply carry on, which raises ss. 35 and 37), the firm survives — and then the statute has to answer one question: is the departed partner's estate on the hook for what happens next? Sections 34(2) and 35 give the same answer: no, not for acts done after the triggering date. The line is drawn at the date of the adjudication order and at the date of death respectively.

Fig. 6 — Death and insolvency. Note that s. 34(1) fixes the date of cessation (the date of the adjudication order), which is what makes s. 34(2) workable.
✱ Points to state for s. 34
  1. Cessation is automatic and dated. "He ceases to be a partner on the date on which the order of adjudication is made" — no notice, no deed, no consent.
  2. "Whether or not the firm is thereby dissolved" — the words that make s. 34(1) independent of s. 42(d).
  3. s. 34(2) is mutual. Two shields: the estate is not liable for the firm's later acts, and the firm is not liable for the insolvent's later acts. Students almost always state only the first.
  4. The insolvent's share vests in the Official Assignee or Receiver, who steps into his shoes for the purposes of accounts.
  5. s. 44(e) is the majority's remedy — where the share is transferred, charged or sold in execution, the others may sue for dissolution.
✱ Points to state for s. 35
  1. It applies only where the firm continues. The opening words — "Where under a contract between the partners the firm is not dissolved by the death of a partner" — are a condition precedent. If the firm is dissolved (the default under s. 42(c)), s. 35 has nothing to operate on; the estate's position is then governed by s. 25 (for pre-death acts) and s. 48 (on settlement).
  2. The shield is limited to acts done after the death. Pre-death debts remain recoverable from the estate.
  3. Read with s. 37. Where the survivors carry on with the firm's property without a final settlement, the estate may claim either a share of the profits attributable to the use of the deceased's share of the property, or 6% per annum interest on it — at the estate's option.
  4. Read with s. 17. s. 17(2) makes the same "subject to contract" arrangement for the deceased partner's estate after a change in the firm.
  5. Read with s. 42(c) and s. 55. On dissolution the goodwill is an asset (s. 55(1)) and the estate gets its share of it.

§ s. 35 in one sentence — and why DU asks it

The DU paper asks s. 35 almost always through the "widow / eldest son automatically becomes a partner" problem (1992 Q.1(a), 1995 Q.2(a), 1997 Q.1(b), 1994 Q.7(a)). The answer is always the same three steps: (1) an agreement that a person shall be admitted is not an admission — s. 31(1) requires the consent of all the existing partners, and that consent must exist when the introduction happens; (2) if the deed does not provide otherwise, the death dissolves the firm (s. 42(c)), so there is no firm for anyone to be introduced into; and (3) even where the deed keeps the firm alive, s. 35 protects the estate against everything done after the death — which is exactly why the survivors cannot simply treat the heir as a partner with full liability. The heir's remedy is a suit on the agreement, not a declaration of partnership.

§1.7 — Section 36: Rights of an outgoing partner to carry on a competing business

§ The section, verbatim

36. Rights of outgoing partner to carry on competing business.— (1) An outgoing partner may carry on a business competing with that of the firm and he may advertise such business, but, subject to contract to the contrary, he may not—

(a) use the firm name,

(b) represent himself as carrying on the business of the firm, or

(c) solicit the custom of persons who were dealing with the firm before he ceased to be a partner.

(2) Agreement in restraint of trade.— A partner may make an agreement with his partners that on ceasing to be a partner he will not carry on any business similar to that of the firm within a specified period or within specified local limits; and, notwithstanding anything contained in section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, such agreement shall be valid if the restrictions imposed are reasonable.

§ The three-way balance s. 36 strikes

The general law (s. 27, ICA 1872) says every agreement in restraint of trade is void. s. 11(2) of the Partnership Act carves out one exception during partnership; s. 36(2) carves out a second one after it ends; s. 55(3) carves out a third on the sale of goodwill. But — and this is the point — s. 36(1) is the rule and s. 36(2) is the exception. An outgoing partner is presumed free to compete; he is restrained only if he has validly agreed to be restrained. The three prohibitions in (a), (b) and (c) are not restraints of trade at all — they are restraints on misrepresentation and poaching, and they apply whether or not there is a restraint-of-trade clause.

Fig. 7 — s. 36. The presumption is freedom; the three prohibitions and the restraint clause are the exceptions.
✱ "Solicit" — the exam word

The distinction that carries the marks:

  • Permitted: a general advertisement in a newspaper, a hoarding, a website, a listing in a directory, an announcement to the trade at large. Nobody is being asked.
  • Prohibited: a circular, letter, e-mail, telephone call or personal visit addressed to a person who was a customer of the old firm before the partner left. That is solicitation.
  • The difficult case: an old customer who reads the general advertisement and comes of his own accord. That is not solicitation and is not prohibited — the initiative came from the customer.
  • Note the timing requirement — the customer must have been "dealing with the firm before he ceased to be a partner". A customer acquired after his departure is not within (c).
✱ s. 36(2) and the reasonableness test

Section 27 of the ICA makes restraint of trade void, subject to the Exception (sale of goodwill). s. 36(2) adds a fourth statutory exception. Its elements:

  1. There must be an agreement between the partners (usually in the deed).
  2. It must bite on ceasing to be a partner — retirement, expulsion, insolvency; probably also dissolution.
  3. The business restrained must be "similar" to that of the firm.
  4. The restraint must be bounded — "within a specified period or within specified local limits". Note the disjunctive: a period alone will do; a local limit alone will do.
  5. The restriction must be reasonable — the court asks whether it is no wider than reasonably necessary to protect the firm's goodwill and connection.

Compare s. 55(3), which permits the same kind of restraint on a partner on the sale of the goodwill of the firm after dissolution — and s. 11(2), which permits an agreement that a partner will not carry on any other business while he is a partner. The three together cover the whole life cycle.

◈ Real-life illustration

Dr. Iyer and Dr. Jha run "City Dental Studio" in Connaught Place. Iyer retires on 31 March and opens "Iyer Dental Care" 400 metres away. The deed is silent about competition.

  • A full-page advertisement in the Hindustan Times announcing the new clinic — permitted (s. 36(1)).
  • Calling it "formerly of City Dental Studio" in that advertisement — borderline. If it is a bare statement of historical fact it is not (b); if it suggests he is still carrying on the old firm's business it is — and it will also be a passing off.
  • Sending a personalised letter to the 900 patients on the old firm's register — prohibited by s. 36(1)(c). The remedy is an injunction.
  • Continuing to use the old firm's letterhead and appointment cards — prohibited by (a), and it will also expose him to liability to third parties by holding out under s. 28 (see Tower Cabinet Co. v. Ingram).
  • If the deed had contained a clause "Iyer shall not practise dentistry within 5 km of Connaught Place for three years after ceasing to be a partner" — that is a s. 36(2) restraint, valid if reasonable. Three years and five kilometres for a professional practice is very likely reasonable.

§1.8 — Section 37: Right of an outgoing partner to share subsequent profits

§ The section, verbatim

37. Right of outgoing partner in certain cases to share subsequent profits.— Where any member of a firm has died or otherwise ceased to be a partner, and the surviving or continuing partners carry on the business of the firm with the property of the firm without any final settlement of accounts as between them and the outgoing partner or his estate then, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, the outgoing partner or his estate is entitled at the option of himself or his representatives to such share of the profits made since he ceased to be a partner as may be attributable to the use of his share of the property of the firm or to interest at the rate of six per cent per annum on the amount of his share in the property of the firm.

Provided that where by contract between the partners an option is given to surviving or continuing partners to purchase the interest of a deceased or outgoing partner, and that option is duly exercised, the estate of the deceased partner or the outgoing partner or his estate, as the case may be, is not entitled to any further or other share of profits; but if any partner assuming to act in exercise of the option does not in all material respects comply with the terms thereof, he is liable to account under the foregoing provisions of this section.

Fig. 8 — s. 37. Four conditions first; then the option of the outgoing partner or his estate — not of the continuing partners.
◈ Worked numbers

A, B and C are equal partners. C retires on 1 April 2020. His share of the firm's property, valued as on that date, is ₹30 lakh. No accounts are settled. B and A carry on with the same premises, plant and stock and, for the year 2020-21, make a profit of ₹18 lakh, of which the court finds ₹4.2 lakh attributable to the use of C's share of the property. C may claim ₹4.2 lakh (profits) or 6% of ₹30 lakh = ₹1.8 lakh (interest). He will obviously elect profits. Had the year been a lean one, with only ₹60,000 attributable to his share, he would elect the ₹1.8 lakh interest. That is exactly why the option is his.

✱ Five points that fetch the extra marks on s. 37
  1. The option belongs to the outgoing partner (or his estate), not to the continuing partners. They cannot force 6% on him because it is cheaper.
  2. The claim is an alternative, not cumulative — "or", not "and".
  3. It is a statutory quasi-fiduciary duty: the continuing partners use another man's capital in their business at their own risk. They must therefore keep accounts and be ready to render them.
  4. The proviso is strict — the option to purchase must be "duly exercised", i.e. in all material respects in compliance with its terms. A partner who purports to exercise it but underpays or delays is thrown back on s. 37 and "is liable to account under the foregoing provisions".
  5. Interest is on the amount of his share in the property of the firm, i.e. on the value of his net interest after discharging his share of the debts — not on the gross value of the assets.

§1.9 — Section 38: Revocation of a continuing guarantee by change in the firm

§ The section, verbatim

38. Revocation of continuing guarantee by change in firm.— A continuing guarantee given to a firm, or to a third party in respect of the transactions of a firm, is, in the absence of agreement to the contrary, revoked as to future transactions from the date of any change in the constitution of the firm.

✱ The four elements
  1. A continuing guarantee — defined in s. 129, ICA 1872: a guarantee which extends to a series of transactions. A one-off guarantee is not a continuing guarantee and s. 38 has nothing to say about it.
  2. Given to a firm or to a third party in respect of the firm's transactions — s. 38 covers both directions: the firm's own security, and the security a customer gives a third party (such as a bank) for the firm's dealings with that third party. Example: a cash-credit guarantee given by S to the firm's bank.
  3. A change in the constitution of the firm — admission (s. 31), retirement (s. 32), expulsion (s. 33), insolvency (s. 34) or death (s. 35) all qualify. A mere transfer of a partner's interest under s. 29 does not, because the transferor remains a partner.
  4. No agreement to the contrary — in practice every bank guarantee contains a clause to the contrary ("the guarantee shall continue notwithstanding any change in the constitution of the firm"), and that clause displaces s. 38.
✱ What "revoked as to future transactions" means
  • Prospective only. The surety remains liable for everything already owed at the date of the change. Revocation wipes the slate only for transactions entered into after that date.
  • Automatic. No notice is required. It operates "from the date of any change", whether or not the surety knew of it.
  • Contrast s. 130, ICA 1872 — revocation by notice, and s. 131 — revocation by the surety's death as to future transactions. s. 38 is a distinct, partnership-specific head of revocation.
  • Contrast s. 133, ICA 1872 — any variance in the terms of the contract between the principal debtor and the creditor discharges the surety as to transactions after the variance. s. 38 is narrower and cleaner: any change in the constitution revokes, without proof of prejudice.
  • And ss. 139–141, ICA — discharge by the creditor's act or omission impairing the surety's eventual remedy, or by loss of security; these apply to partnership guarantees too.
◈ Real-life illustration — why banks insist on the clause

In 2022, Bose gives a continuing guarantee to a bank for the cash-credit account of "Saha & Bose", then a two-partner firm of Saha and Bose. In 2024, Dutta is admitted. From that date s. 38 revokes the guarantee as to future drawings unless the deed of guarantee says otherwise. The bank's standard form does say otherwise — it provides that the guarantee "shall not be affected by any change in the constitution of the firm, and shall be available as security for the balance due at any time". That is the "agreement to the contrary" s. 38 expressly contemplates. This is the single most common transaction in which s. 38 is litigated.

B

Registration of Firms — Chapter VII, ss. 56 to 72

Registration is optional, but the price of not registering is that the firm cannot walk into a civil court to enforce a contract. That single disability — section 69 — has produced more reported litigation than the rest of the Act put together, and all four of the prescribed cases for this topic are s. 69 (or s. 59) cases.

§ The five ideas that explain the whole chapter
  1. Registration is optional, not compulsory. There is no penalty for not registering. The sanction is a disability to sue (s. 69), not a fine. s. 56 lets a State Government exempt areas altogether; s. 58 lets the firm register "at any time".
  2. The Registrar is a recording officer, not an adjudicator. The Special Committee's Report said so, and s. 59 makes it plain: "When the Registrar is satisfied that the provisions of section 58 have been duly complied with he shall record an entry". That is why the Kerala High Court and (later) the Supreme Court could say there is no discretion — and yet, as Jayalakshmi holds, registration still takes place only on the entry.
  3. The Register is conclusive against the signatories, useless against strangers. s. 68(1) makes a statement "conclusive proof" of its contents "as against any person by whom or on whose behalf such statement was signed" — and no one else. So a third party may still prove partnership by other means (see Snow White Food Products, Topic 2 §2.7).
  4. The bar in s. 69 is about contracts, not about torts or statutory rights. That is Haldiram Bhujiawala.
  5. The bar is procedural and curable, in one sense only. Registration after the suit is filed does not cure the defect — but the plaintiff may withdraw with leave and file a fresh suit, and s. 14 of the Limitation Act protects him (that is Haldiram's paragraph 26, relying on Surajmal Dagduramji Shop v. Shrikisan Ramkisan).

§2.1 — Sections 56 and 57: Exemption and the Registrar

§ s. 56 — Power to exempt

"The State Government of any State may, by notification in the Official Gazette, direct that the provisions of this chapter shall not apply to that State or to any part thereof specified in the notification."

Why it matters. Chapter VII has a territorial reach, and s. 69(4)(a) expressly preserves suits by firms whose places of business are "situated in areas to which, by notification under section 56, this Chapter does not apply". So the first question in a registration problem is always: where does the firm carry on business?

§ s. 57 — Appointment of Registrars

"(1) The State Government may appoint Registrar of firms for the purposes of this Act, and may define the areas within which they shall exercise their powers and perform their duties. (2) Every Registrar shall be deemed to be a public servant within the meaning of section 21 of the Indian Penal Code."

Why it matters. (i) Jurisdiction is territorial — s. 58(1) requires the application to go to the Registrar "of the area in which any place of business of the firm is situated or proposed to be situated". (ii) The "public servant" deeming provision supports s. 70 and exposes the Registrar to the penal provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, s. 218 (public servant) for misconduct. (iii) Under s. 71 the State Government makes rules, and most States have framed Partnership (Registration of Firms) Rules prescribing the forms (Form A for the statement under s. 58, Form C for the intimation under s. 63, and so on), the fees and the manner of verification.

§2.2 — Section 58: Application for registration

§ The section, verbatim

58. Application for registration.— (1) The registration of a firm may be effected at any time by sending by post or delivering to the Registrar of the area in which any place of business of the firm is situated or proposed to be situated a statement in the prescribed form and accompanied by the prescribed fee stating—

(a) the firm name,

(b) the place or principal place of business of the firm,

(c) the names of any other places where the firm carries on business,

(d) the date when each partner joined the firm,

(e) the names in full and permanent addresses of the partners, and

(f) the duration of the firm.

The statement shall be signed by all the partners, or by their agents specially authorised in that behalf.

(2) Each person signing the statement shall also verify it in the manner prescribed.

(3) A firm name shall not contain any of the following words, namely— "Crown", "Emperor", "Empress", "Empire", "Imperial", "King", "Queen", "Royal", or words expressing or implying the sanction, approval or patronage of Government, except when the State Government signifies its consent to the use of such words as part of the firm name by order in writing.

✱ "At any time"

The three words that start s. 58(1) are the reason registration is called optional. There is no deadline. A firm may be registered on the day it is formed, or ten years later, or after a suit has been filed — but see s. 69: registration after suit does not retrospectively validate that suit.

✱ Who must sign

All the partners — or their specially authorised agents. A statement signed by only some of the partners is not a statement under s. 58, and the Registrar cannot act on it. This is the direct link to s. 69(2): "the persons suing are or have been shown in the Register of Firms as partners" — a partner who never signed and was never shown cannot sue, and his presence in the array of plaintiffs sinks the firm's suit.

✱ Six particulars, all mandatory

Name · principal place of business · other places · the date each partner joined · the full names and permanent addresses of the partners · the duration. The "date when each partner joined" is what makes s. 31(2) provable from the Register, and "duration" is what makes s. 7 (firm at will) provable.

§2.3 — Section 59: Registration — and the exact moment at which it happens

§ The section, verbatim — nine lines that generated a Supreme Court case

59. Registration.— "When the Registrar is satisfied that the provisions of section 58 have been duly complied with he shall record an entry of the statement in a register called the Register of Firms, and shall file the statement."

! The question this section answers

Does the firm become registered when the application is presented (s. 58) or when the entry is made (s. 59)? The Supreme Court answered it in CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills Contractor Co., AIR 1971 SC 1015 — case 11 in the DU reader. The entry. Everything else in Chapter VII — and every s. 69 problem question — follows from that answer.

✱ Why this matters outside income-tax law
  1. s. 69. The plaintiff must be able to say, at the date of the suit, that the firm is registered. "My application is pending" is not enough. Registration after the suit does not cure it — a proposition Grover J. noted had been "consistently held" under s. 69 itself.
  2. s. 68. Conclusive proof under s. 68(1) attaches to a statement "recorded or noted in the Register of Firms" — again, the entry.
  3. s. 72(a). Public notice for a registered firm requires notice to the Registrar and Gazette and newspaper. Whether the firm is "registered" on a given date decides which limb of s. 72 applies.
  4. Limitation. Where a suit is dismissed for the formal defect of non-registration, the plaintiff may withdraw with leave and re-file; s. 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 then excludes the time spent, because the defect is an "other cause of like nature" (Haldiram, ¶26; Surajmal Dagduramji Shop v. Shrikisan Ramkisan).

§2.4 — Sections 60, 61 and 62: keeping the Register current (I) — alterations and branches

§ s. 60 — Recording of alterations in firm name and principal place of business

(1) "When an alteration is made in the firm name or in the location of the principal place of business of a registered firm, a statement may be sent to the Registrar accompanied by the prescribed fee specifying the alteration, and signed and verified in the manner required under section 58."

(2) "When the Registrar is satisfied that the provisions of sub-section (1) have been duly complied with he shall amend the entry relating to the firm in the Register of Firms in accordance with the statement, and shall file it along with the statement relating to the firm filed under section 59."

Note the two-step structure, identical to ss. 58–59: a statement, then a ministerial act. The same "may send / shall amend" pattern runs through ss. 61, 62 and 63. And note what is not here: a change in the constitution of the firm (a partner in or out) is dealt with by s. 63, not s. 60.

§ s. 61 — Noting of closing and opening of branches

"When a registered firm discontinues business at any place or begins to carry on business at any place, such place not being its principal place of business, any partner or agent of the firm may send intimation thereof to the Registrar, who shall make a note of such intimation in the entry relating to the firm in the Register of Firms, and shall file the intimation along with the statement relating to the firm filed under section 59."

Note "any partner or agent" — unlike s. 58 (all partners) and s. 60 (the same signing requirements as s. 58), an intimation under s. 61 can be sent by a single partner. This is deliberate: branches open and close frequently and the Act does not want a unanimity requirement to clog the Register.

§ s. 62 — Noting of changes in names and addresses of partners

"When any partner in a registered firm alters his name or permanent address, an intimation of the alteration may be sent by any partner or agent of the firm to the Registrar, who shall deal with it in the manner provided in section 61."

Practical point. s. 58(1)(e) requires "names in full and permanent addresses". If a partner moves house, s. 62 is the section that keeps the Register accurate — and accuracy matters because s. 66 opens the Register to inspection by anyone on payment of a fee. A creditor who looks up a firm and finds an outdated address may be misled; that is the commercial purpose of ss. 60–62.

§2.5 — Section 63: Recording of changes in, and dissolution of, a firm

§ The section, verbatim

63. Recording of changes in and dissolution of a firm.— (1) When a change occurs in the constitution of a registered firm any incoming, continuing or outgoing partner, and when a registered firm is dissolved any person who was a partner immediately before the dissolution, or the agent of any such partner or person specially authorised in this behalf, may give notice to the Registrar of such change or dissolution, specifying the date thereof, and the Registrar shall make a record of the notice in the entry relating to the firm in the Register of Firms, and shall file the notice along with the statement relating to the firm filed under section 59.

(2) Recording of withdrawal of a minor.— When a minor who has been admitted to the benefits of partnership in a firm attains majority and elects to become or not to become a partner, and the firm is then a registered firm, he, or his agent specially authorised in this behalf, may give notice to the Registrar that he has or has not become a partner, and the Registrar shall deal with the notice in the manner provided in sub-section (1).

✱ Who may give the notice — the widest list in the chapter
  • On a change in constitution: any incoming partner, any continuing partner, or any outgoing partner. One is enough.
  • On a dissolution: any person who was a partner immediately before it, or the agent of such a person, or a person "specially authorised in this behalf".
  • On a minor's election: the minor himself, or his specially authorised agent.

Why the list is so wide. An outgoing partner has the strongest possible incentive to get himself off the Register — his liability under s. 32(3) continues until public notice, and s. 72(a) makes the notice to the Registrar one of the three components of that public notice for a registered firm. The Act therefore lets him do it himself, without needing the continuing partners' co-operation. This is the exact counterpart of s. 32(4): "Notices under sub-section (3) may be given by the retired partner or by any partner of the reconstituted firm."

✱ s. 63(2) and the minor — the bridge back to Topic 2

Read s. 63(2) together with s. 30(5):

  1. The minor attains majority (18, or 21 where a guardian was appointed under the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, s. 3).
  2. Within six months of that — or of his obtaining knowledge of the admission, whichever is later — he must give public notice of his election (s. 30(5)).
  3. If the firm is registered, that public notice must, by s. 72(a), include notice to the Registrar under s. 63 plus Gazette and vernacular newspaper publication.
  4. s. 63(2) is the machinery by which the Register then records whether he has or has not become a partner.
  5. If he gives no notice at all, the proviso to s. 30(5) makes him a partner — and, by s. 30(7)(a), personally liable with retroactive effect for acts done since his admission. (But only if a firm exists to be a partner of — Shivgouda Ravji Patil v. Chandrakant, AIR 1965 SC 212.)
◈ Real-life illustration — the s. 63 trap

"Krishna Electricals" is registered in 2019 with partners A, B and C. In 2022 A retires and D is admitted. Nobody sends an intimation under s. 63, so the Register still shows A, B and C. In 2023 the firm (B, C, D) supplies goods worth ₹18 lakh to a builder who defaults, and sues.

  • The plaint is filed by B, C and D, "partners of Krishna Electricals".
  • s. 69(2) requires that "the persons suing are or have been shown in the Register of Firms as partners". D is not shown — so the suit is barred. This is precisely the 1994 Q.8(b) problem (Vahida retired, Juhi admitted but not registered).
  • The defect is curable only by withdrawing with leave and re-filing after the s. 63 intimation is recorded — and s. 14 of the Limitation Act saves the period.
  • And A: although he has in fact retired, he remains liable to third parties until public notice (s. 32(3)), of which s. 72(a) makes the s. 63 notice a component. His failure to send it is his own loss.

§2.6 — Sections 64 and 65: Rectification of mistakes and amendment by order of court

§ s. 64 — Rectification of mistakes

(1) "The Registrar shall have power at all times to rectify any mistake in order to bring the entry in the Register of Firms relating to any firm into conformity with the documents relating to that firm filed under this Chapter."

(2) "On application made by all the parties who have signed any document relating to a firm filed under this Chapter, the Registrar may rectify any mistake in such documents or in the records or notes thereof made in the Register of Firms."

Two distinct powers. Sub-section (1) is administrative — the Registrar acts of his own motion to make the entry match the documents already on his file (a copying error, a misspelt name, a transposed date). Sub-section (2) is on application by all the signatories and reaches the documents themselves. Limit: the Registrar cannot adjudicate a dispute about who is or is not a partner — that is for the civil court, and then s. 65 is the route by which the Register is brought into line.

§ s. 65 — Amendment of Register by order of Court

"A court deciding any matter relating to a registered firm may direct that the Registrar shall make any amendment in the entry in the Register of Firms relating to such firm which is consequential upon its decision, and the Registrar shall amend the entry accordingly."

The word "consequential" is the limit. The court directs only what follows from its own decision — if it declares that X ceased to be a partner on 1 April 2022, the Register must be amended to say so. The court does not, under s. 65, conduct a general review of the Register. Compare the court's power to try a suit for a declaration of partnership or of dissolution under the general law, and O. 7 r. 7, CPC (relief to be specifically asked for) — a plaintiff who wants the Register corrected should ask for it.

§2.7 — Sections 66 and 67: Inspection of the Register and grant of copies

§ s. 66 — Inspection of Register and filed documents

(1) "The Register of Firms shall be open to inspection by any person on payment of such fee as may be prescribed."
(2) "All statements, notices and intimations filed under this Chapter shall be open to inspection, subject to such conditions and on payment of such fee as may be prescribed."

The commercial purpose of the whole chapter is in these two lines. Any member of the public, for a small fee, can find out who the partners of a firm are. That is the "strong protection to persons dealing with firms against false denials of partnership" which paragraph 18 of the Special Committee's Report (quoted in Haldiram Bhujiawala) identified as the object of the scheme. Contrast the position of a company: the Registrar of Companies' records are likewise public (s. 399, Companies Act, 2013), but the company's internal books are not.

§ s. 67 — Grant of copies

"The Registrar shall, on application, furnish to any person, on payment of such fee as may be prescribed, a copy, certified under his hand, of an entry or portion thereof in the Register of Firms."

Then s. 68(2) gives the certified copy its evidentiary value: it "may be produced in proof of the fact of the registration of such firm, and of the contents of any statement, intimation or notice recorded or noted therein". Compare s. 76(2) of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now s. 76, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023), which makes certified copies of public documents admissible without producing the original, and s. 74(1)(iii) (documents forming the acts or records of the acts of public officers) — the Registrar being a public servant under s. 57(2).

§2.8 — Section 68: Rules of evidence

§ The section, verbatim

68. Rules of evidence.— (1) Any statement, intimation or notice recorded or noted in the Register of Firms shall, as against any person by whom or on whose behalf such statement, intimation or notice was signed, be conclusive proof of any fact therein stated.

(2) A certified copy of an entry relating to a firm in the Register of Firms may be produced in proof of the fact of the registration of such firm, and of the contents of any statement, intimation or notice recorded or noted therein.

! The single most important word in s. 68 — "signed"

Conclusive proof under s. 68(1) operates only against the signatory (or the person on whose behalf he signed). It is a shield for the public and a trap for the partner: a man who signs a statement saying he is a partner cannot later deny it. But it is not a shield for the firm against the public — a third party who is not a signatory is not bound by the Register and may prove partnership (or its absence) by any other evidence. That is exactly what the Calcutta High Court held in Snow White Food Products Co. Ltd v. Sohan Lal, AIR 1964 Cal 239 (Topic 2, case 9-b): the Register is conclusive only against the signatory, and a stranger to the notice may still be proved a partner by holding out under s. 28. Cite s. 68 and Snow White together — examiners reward that pairing.

§2.9 — Section 69: Effect of non-registration

§ The section, verbatim — every word of it matters

69. Effect of non-registration.— (1) No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract or conferred by this Act shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of any person suing as a partner in a firm against the firm or any person alleged to be or to have been a partner in the firm unless the firm is registered and the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner in the firm.

(2) No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of a firm against any third party unless the firm is registered and the persons suing are or have been shown in the Register of Firms as partners in the firm.

(3) The provisions of sub-sections (1) and (2) shall apply also to a claim of set-off or other proceeding to enforce a right arising from a contract, but shall not affect—

(a) the enforcement of any right to sue for the dissolution of a firm or for accounts of a dissolved firm, or any right or power to realise the property of a dissolved firm, or

(b) the powers of an official assignee, receiver or Court under the Presidency-Towns Insolvency Act, 1909, or the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920, to realise the property of an insolvent partner.

(4) This section shall not apply—

(a) to firms or to partners in firms which have no place of business in the territories to which this Act extends, or whose places of business in the said territories are situated in areas to which, by notification under section 56, this Chapter does not apply, or

(b) to any suit or claim of set-off not exceeding one hundred rupees in value which, in the presidency towns, is not of a kind specified in section 19 of the Presidency Small Cause Courts Act, 1882, or outside the presidency towns, is not of a kind specified in the Second Schedule to the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act, 1887, or to any proceeding in execution or other proceeding incidental to or arising from any such suit or claim.

§ The anatomy of the bar — six ingredients
  1. A "suit", a "claim of set-off", or an "other proceeding".
  2. "Instituted in any court" — the phrase that decides the arbitration question in Jagdish Chander Gupta and Umesh Goel.
  3. "To enforce a right arising from a contract" — plus, in sub-section (1) only, "or conferred by this Act". Note the asymmetry: sub-section (2) does not include "conferred by this Act", which is why a firm's claim for a statutory or common-law right is not caught (Haldiram).
  4. Proper parties — sub-section (1): a person suing as a partner against the firm or a person alleged to be a partner. Sub-section (2): the firm against a third party (defined in s. 2(d) as a person who is not a partner of the firm).
  5. The firm must be registered — as at the date of institution.
  6. And the person suing must be shown in the Register — "is or has been shown", which is why a retired partner who was once on the Register can still sue.

The numbering was different when Jagdish Chander Gupta was decided in 1964: Hidayatullah J. describes sub-section (1) as barring suits "between partners inter se or between partners and the firm" and sub-section (2) as barring suits "by or on behalf of the firm against a third party". ✱ "Is or has been shown in the Register" — the relaxing words

The phrase "or has been shown" is doing real work:

  • A partner who retired before the suit, but whose name was on the Register while he was a partner, can still sue the firm for his share — even though he is no longer shown.
  • The estate of a deceased partner can sue, because the deceased "has been shown".
  • But an incoming partner who has never been shown cannot — and because the word is "persons suing" (plural, in sub-section (2)), his joinder as a co-plaintiff defeats the whole suit. This is the Vahida–Juhi problem (DU 1994 Q.8(b)).

! The five traps examiners set on s. 69
  1. "Registration is compulsory." It is not — it is optional (ss. 56, 58). Say this in the first line of every answer and then explain that the sanction is a disability to sue.
  2. Forgetting the second condition. It is not enough that the firm is registered; the person suing must be or have been shown in the Register. Two conditions, cumulative.
  3. Applying s. 69 to a tort or a statutory right. Haldiram is directly against that.
  4. Treating any contract mentioned in the plaint as fatal. Haldiram ¶25: the contract must be with the defendant and in the course of the firm's business.
  5. Assuming the suit is dismissed. The proper course is a stay (Jagdish Chander Gupta), leaving the plaintiff free to register, withdraw with leave and re-file within the time saved by s. 14 of the Limitation Act.

§2.10 — Sections 70, 71 and 72: Penalty, rules, and public notice

§ s. 70 — Penalty for furnishing false particulars

"Any person who signs any statement, amending statement, notice or intimation under this Chapter containing any particulars which he knows to be false or does not believe to be true, or containing particulars which he knows to be incomplete or does not believe to be complete, shall be punishable with imprisonment which may extend to three months, or with fine, or with both."

Read it carefully — there is only one offence here, and it is not failure to register. Three elements: (i) a person signs; (ii) a statement under Chapter VII; (iii) containing particulars known to be false / not believed to be true, or known to be incomplete / not believed to be complete. Note that omission is as much an offence as falsehood — a statement that leaves out a partner is "incomplete". Compare Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, ss. 336–340 (forgery, making a false document, using a false document) and s. 229 BNS (false evidence), which may be attracted on the same facts; and s. 58(2), under which each signatory must verify the statement — the verification is what makes the prosecution straightforward.

§ s. 71 — Power to make rules

"(1) The State Government may make rules prescribing the fees which shall accompany documents sent to the Registrar of Firms, or which shall be payable for the inspection of documents in the custody of the Registrar of Firms, or for copies from the Register of Firms.
(2) The State Government may also make rules — (a) prescribing the form of statement submitted under section 58 and of the verification thereof; (b) requiring statements, intimations and notices under sections 60, 61, 62 and 63 to be in prescribed form and prescribing the form thereof; (c) prescribing the form of the Register of Firms and the mode in which entries relating to firms are to be made therein, and the mode in which such entries are to be amended or notes made therein; (d) regulating the filing of documents received by the Registrar; (e) prescribing conditions for the inspection of original documents; (f) regulating the grant of copies; and (g) generally carrying out the purposes of this Chapter."

Why it matters in practice. Every State has framed Partnership (Registration of Firms) Rules under s. 71 — prescribing Form A (s. 58 statement), Form B (s. 60), Forms C/D/E (ss. 61, 62, 63), the scale of fees, the manner of verification, the form of the Register and the conditions of inspection. Do not confuse them with the Indian Partnership Act's own sections; the Rules are subordinate legislation, and a defect in form is curable.

§ s. 72 — Mode of giving public notice (reproduced here because it is the hinge of Chapter V)

The full text is at §1.10. For quick reference: (a) for retirement or expulsion from a registered firm, dissolution of a registered firm, and a minor's election in a registered firm — notice to the Registrar under s. 63 + publication in the Official Gazette + at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district; (b) in every other case — Gazette + vernacular newspaper only. § The whole chapter in four sentences

CASE 11 · CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills Contractor Co.
AIR 1971 SC 1015 : (1971) 1 SCC 280 · Supreme Court (A.N. Grover J.) · DU reader p. 49
[Date of registration of a firm] — on what date does registration "take place": the date of the application under s. 58, or the date of the entry under s. 59?
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The assessee firm was constituted under a deed of partnership, dated October 6, 1955. It was to come into existence with effect from November 5, 1954. The assessee filed an application under Section 26-A of the (Income-tax) Act for registration of the firm for the assessment year 1956-57. The 'previous year' of the firm was shown as the year ending October 26, 1955. This application was received by the Income-tax Officer on October 14, 1955.

On October 20, 1955, the assessee filed before the Registrar of Firms a statement under Section 58 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. On November 2, 1955, the Registrar of Firms filed the statement of the assessee and made entries in the register of firms.

On March 23, 1961, the Income-tax Officer passed an order refusing to register the firm under Section 26-A, inter alia, for the reason that the application had not been made in time. The appeal taken to the Appellate Assistant Commissioner by the assessee failed. The Income-tax Appellate Tribunal also upheld the order of the Income-tax Officer and the Appellate Assistant Commissioner. On that a reference was sought and the High Court answered the question referred in favour of the assessee on the ground that the application had been filed in time.

The statutory frame (income-tax side). Section 26-A of the Income-tax Act provides that an application may be made to the Income-tax Officer on behalf of any firm constituted under an instrument of partnership specifying the individual shares of the partners for registration for the purposes of the Act. The application has to be made by such person or persons and at such time and has to contain such particulars as may be prescribed. Rules 2 to 6(6) of the Rules made under Section 59 of the Act deal with registration of firms. The material portion of Rule 2 reads:

"Such application shall be made.......
(a) Where the firm is not registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (IX of 1932) or where the deed of Partnership is not registered under the Indian Registration Act, 1908 (XVI of 1908) and the application for registration is being made for the first time under the Act.
  (i) Within a period of six months of the constitution of the firm or before the end of the 'previous year' of the firm whichever is earlier, if the firm was constituted in that previous year,
  (ii) before the end of the previous year in any other case,
(b) Where the firm is registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (IX of 1932) or where the deed of partnership is registered under the Indian Registration Act (XVI of 1908) before the end of the previous year of the firm......"Rule 2 of the Rules framed under s. 59, Income-tax Act

It was common ground that the application for registration was not made within the period prescribed by Rule 2(a).

Issue

As the Supreme Court framed it: "The real question which has to be determined is whether the registration of a firm under the Partnership Act takes place with effect from the date on which the application for registration is made in accordance with Section 58 of that Act." In short — application or entry?

For the assessee (the argument the High Court accepted)
  • The application was governed by Rule 2(b), not Rule 2(a). The firm should be deemed to have been registered not on the date on which it was actually registered by the Registrar of Firms, but with effect from the date on which the application for registration was presented to the Registrar — i.e. October 20, 1955, the date on which the statement under s. 58 was filed.
  • Reliance on the language of s. 58(1). Section 58(1) provides that "the registration of a firm may be effected at any time by sending by post or delivering to the Registrar … a statement in the prescribed form and accompanied by the prescribed fee". Read literally, registration is effected by the sending — so once an entry is later made under s. 59 pursuant to it, registration should relate back to the date of presentation.
  • Reliance on the Report of the Special Committee which had examined the Bill before it was passed: the statement relating to clause 58 (corresponding to s. 59) was to the effect that the Registrar was a mere recording officer and that he had no discretion but to record the entry in the register of firms. If the Registrar has no discretion, the argument runs, there is no reason to treat the date of his ministerial act as the date of registration.
  • Requirement of Rule 2(b) would then be satisfied: if the firm became registered under the Partnership Act on 20 October 1955, it was registered "before the end of the previous year of the firm", even though the application to the ITO had been made on 14 October 1955.
For the Revenue (the argument the Supreme Court accepted)
  • Section 59 is the operative provision. "Under Section 59 when the Registrar is satisfied that the provisions of Section 58 have been duly complied with he shall record an entry of the statement in a register called the 'register of firms' and shall file the statement." Until that is done there is no entry, and without an entry there is no registration.
  • The point is settled by authority. In Ram Prasad v. Kamla Prasad [AIR 1935 All. 898] "it was laid down that the registration of a firm under the Partnership Act takes place only when the necessary entry is made in the register of firms."
  • Section 69 confirms it. "Even under Section 69 of the Partnership Act which deals with the effect of non-registration it has been consistently held that the registration of a firm subsequent to the filing of the suit did not cure the defect. Thus under the Partnership Law it can be taken to have been settled by decisions of High Courts from a long time that the registration of a firm takes place only when the necessary entry is made in the register of firms under Section 59."
  • A recent, directly parallel decision. The Kerala High Court in Kerala Road Lines Corporation v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Kerala [51 ITR 711] "clearly expressed the view that reading Sections 58 and 59 of the Indian Partnership Act together a firm cannot be said to be registered when the statement prescribed by Section 58 and the required fee are sent to the Registrar and that the registration of the firm is effected only when the entry of the statement is recorded in the register of firms and the statement is filed by the Registrar as provided in Section 59." An identically similar question had arisen there under s. 26-A.
  • The Committee Report is not an aid here. The Report cannot be used to contradict the scheme of the enacted provisions.
Decision (A.N. Grover J.) — appeal allowed

The Supreme Court accepted the Revenue's case in full and reversed the High Court.

"Now it is common ground that the application for registration was not made within the period prescribed by Rule 2 (a). What has been urged throughout on behalf of the assessee is that the application to the Income-tax Officer was governed by Rule 2(b) and was in time as the firm should be deemed to have been registered not on the date on which it was actually registered by the Registrar of Firms but with effect from the date on which the application for registration was presented to the Registrar."¶ 3
"Section 58(1) provides that the registration of a firm may be effected at any time by sending by post or delivering to the Registrar of the area in which any place of business of the firm is situated or proposed to be situated a statement in the prescribed form and accompanied by the prescribed fee stating...... Under Section 59 when the Registrar is satisfied that the provisions of Section 58 have been duly complied with he shall record an entry of the statement in a register called the 'register of firms' and shall file the statement. In Ram Prasad v. Kamla Prasad [AIR 1935 All. 898], it was laid down that the registration of a firm under the Partnership Act takes place only when the necessary entry is made in the register of firms. Even under Section 69 of the Partnership Act which deals with the effect of non-registration it has been consistently held that the registration of a firm subsequent to the filing of the suit did not cure the defect. Thus under the Partnership Law it can be taken to have been settled by decisions of High Courts from a long time that the registration of a firm takes place only when the necessary entry is made in the register of firms under Section 59 of the Partnership Act by the Registrar."¶ 4
"It is true that sub-section (1) of Section 58 employs language which without anything more may lend support to the view that the registration of a firm may be effected merely by sending an application which would mean that as soon as an application is sent and if entry is made under Section 59 pursuant to it the registration would be effective from the date when the application was presented. But Section 58(1) is not to be read in isolation and has to be considered along with the scheme of the other provisions of the Act, namely Section 59 and Section 69. The latter section may not have a direct bearing on the point under our consideration but it throws light on what was contemplated by the Legislature with regard to the point of time when the firm could be regarded as registered."¶ 4
"The High Court in the judgment under appeal referred to the statement extracted from the report of the Special Committee … to the effect that the Registrar was a mere recording officer and that he had no discretion but to record the entry in the register of firms. We are unable to see how that statement can be taken into consideration for the purpose of interpreting the relevant provisions of the Partnership Act. We also cannot concur with the other reasoning of the High Court … For the reasons given above the appeal is allowed. The answer to the question referred must be given in the affirmative and against the assessee."¶ 5
Principles evolved
  1. Registration of a firm takes place only when the entry is made in the Register of Firms under s. 59 — not when the statement under s. 58 is sent or delivered, and not on any notional relation back to the date of the application.
  2. s. 58(1) is not to be read in isolation. It must be read with the scheme of s. 59 and s. 69. This is the classic statement of the rule that a provision is construed in its statutory context, not literally and alone.
  3. Registration after the event does not cure the defect. Registration "subsequent to the filing of the suit did not cure the defect" under s. 69 — a proposition the Court treated as settled. This is the bridge to every DU problem question on registration during the pendency of a suit.
  4. The Registrar is a recording officer, but that does not make his act a nullity. He has no discretion to refuse once s. 58 is complied with — but the Act still makes the entry the constitutive act.
  5. Obiter on Committee Reports: the Court declined to use the Special Committee's Report to construe the Act. Note the later development — in Haldiram Bhujiawala (¶ 16) Jagannadha Rao J. said this view "is no longer good law as it was clearly dissented on this aspect in the judgment of the Constitution Bench in R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay (1984) 2 SCC 183", and went on to rely on the very same Report to construe s. 69(2).
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

Whenever the question is "the firm registered while the suit was pending — is the suit maintainable?" (DU 1992 Q.5(a), 2000 Q.6(b), 2004 Q.6(ii), 2013 Q.5), write: "Under s. 59, registration takes place only when the Registrar records the entry — the application under s. 58 is not registration (CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills). It has consistently been held under s. 69 that registration subsequent to the filing of the suit does not cure the defect. The suit must therefore fail as framed; the proper course is a stay (Jagdish Chander Gupta), withdrawal with leave and a fresh suit, with s. 14 of the Limitation Act excluding the time (Haldiram)." Note also that the DU reader's headnote prints "[Date of registration of a partnership firm — section 60]"; the section in issue is section 59 (s. 60 deals with alterations in the firm name and principal place of business).

CASE 12 · Jagdish Chandra Gupta v. Kajaria Traders (India) Ltd.
AIR 1964 SC 1882 · Supreme Court (M. Hidayatullah J.) · DU reader p. 52
[Reference of a dispute to arbitration as envisaged in the partnership agreement — whether barred by s. 69] — what does "other proceeding" in s. 69(3) mean?
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

By a letter dated July 30, 1955, M/s. Kajaria Traders (India) Ltd. and M/s. Foreign Import and Export Association (a sole proprietary firm owned by the appellant Jagdish C. Gupta) entered into a partnership to export between January and June 1956, 10,000 tons of manganese ore to Phillips Brothers (India) Ltd., New York. Each partner was to supply a certain quantity of manganese ore. The Court was "not concerned with the terms of the agreement but with one of its clauses which provided":

"That in case of dispute the matter will be referred for arbitration in accordance with the Indian Arbitration Act."the arbitration clause

The company alleged that Jagdish C. Gupta failed to carry out his part of the partnership agreement. After some correspondence, the company wrote to him on February 28, 1959 that they had appointed Mr R.J. Kolah (Advocate, O.S.) as their arbitrator and asked him either to agree to Mr Kolah's appointment as sole arbitrator or to appoint his own arbitrator. On March 17, 1959 the company informed him that, as he had failed to appoint an arbitrator within 15 clear days, they were appointing Mr Kolah as sole arbitrator. Jagdish C. Gupta disputed this, and the company filed on March 28, 1959 an application under Section 8(2) of the Indian Arbitration Act, 1940 for the appointment of Mr Kolah or any other person as arbitrator.

Jagdish Chander Gupta appeared and objected. Two grounds were urged: (i) that s. 8(2) of the Indian Arbitration Act was not applicable, as it was not expressly provided in the arbitration clause that the arbitrators were to be by consent of the parties; and (ii) that s. 69(3) of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 afforded a bar to the petition because the partnership was not registered.

The litigation before it reached the Supreme Court

The petition was referred by the Chief Justice to a Divisional Bench of Mudholkar J. (as he then was) and Naik J. The two learned Judges agreed that an application under s. 8 of the Arbitration Act was competent and that the court had power to appoint an arbitrator. They disagreed on the second point:

  • Mudholkar J. — s. 69(3) barred the application.
  • Naik J. — it did not.
  • The case was then referred to K.T. Desai J. (as he then was), who agreed with Naik J., "with the result that the application was held to be competent".
  • In the Supreme Court appeal it was not contended that the conclusions on s. 8(2) were erroneous. "The decision was challenged only on the ground that Section 69(3) was wrongly interpreted and the bar afforded by it was wrongly disallowed."
Issues
  1. Can an application under s. 8(2) of the Arbitration Act, 1940 be regarded as a proceeding "to enforce a right arising from a contract", and therefore within the bar of s. 69 of the Partnership Act?
  2. Do the words "a claim of set-off", which precede "other proceeding" in s. 69(3), cut down the meaning of "other proceeding" by the rule of ejusdem generis or noscitur a sociis?
For the respondent company (the wider reading — accepted)
  • The proceeding has its genesis in the arbitration clause. Without an agreement to refer the matter to arbitration, s. 8 of the Arbitration Act "cannot possibly be invoked".
  • The arbitration clause is part of the agreement constituting the partnership. "Whether we view the contract between the parties as a whole or view only the clause about arbitration, it is impossible to think that the right to proceed to arbitration is not one of the rights which are founded on the agreement of the parties."
  • "Other proceeding" must receive its full and natural meaning. The words used are not "any proceeding" nor "any other proceedings" — but that is a matter of drafting; the substance is that the legislature meant to catch every proceeding of any kind for the enforcement of a contractual right.
  • The scheme of s. 69 proves it. Sub-section (4)(b) speaks of "any proceeding in execution or other proceeding incidental to or arising from any such suit or claim" — "this clearly shows that the word 'proceeding' is not limited to a proceeding in the nature of a suit or a claim of set-off". "It would hardly have been necessary to be so explicit if the words 'other proceeding' in the main section had a meaning as restricted as is suggested by the respondent."
For the appellant (the narrower reading — rejected)
  • Naik J.'s reasoning (adopted by the appellant). The words are not "any proceeding" nor "any other proceedings" but "other proceeding"; and because they are juxtaposed with "a claim of set-off" they indicate "a proceeding of the nature of a claim in defence".
  • This is not a proceeding to enforce a right arising from the contract. Naik J. held it was "a claim for damages and such a claim could be entertained because it was based on something which was independent of the contract to supply ore" — "the right which was being enforced was a right arising from the Arbitration Act and not from the contract of the parties."
  • K.T. Desai J.'s variant. The words preceding "other proceeding", namely "a claim of set-off", had a "demonstrative and limiting effect"; he ascertained the meaning of "other proceeding" by reference to the meaning of "a claim of set-off", which he considered were associated with it.
  • And the drafting argument: if all proceedings were meant to be excluded, why was it not sufficient to speak of proceedings along with suits in sub-sections (1) and (2) instead of framing a separate sub-section coupling "other proceeding" with "a claim of set-off"?
Decision (M. Hidayatullah J.) — appeal allowed

On issue 1 — the proceeding is to enforce a right arising from a contract.

"The proceeding under the eighth section of the Arbitration Act has its genesis in the arbitration clause, because without an agreement to refer the matter to arbitration that section cannot possibly be invoked. Since the arbitration clause is a part of the agreement constituting the partnership it is obvious that the proceeding which is before the Court is to enforce a right, which arises from a contract. Whether we view the contract between the parties as a whole or view only the clause about arbitration, it is impossible to think that the right to proceed to arbitration is not one of the rights which are founded on the agreement of the parties. The words of Section 69(3), 'a right arising from a contract' are in either sense sufficient to cover the present matter."¶ 5

On issue 2 — "other proceeding" is not cut down by "a claim of set-off".

"When in a statute particular classes are mentioned by name and then are followed by general words, the general words are sometimes construed ejusdem generis i.e. limited to the same category or genus comprehended by the particular words but it is not necessary that this rule must always apply. The nature of the special words and the general words must be considered before the rule is applied. In Allen v. Emersons [(1944) 1 KB 362], Asquith, J., gave interesting examples of particular words followed by general words where the principle of ejusdem generis might or might not apply. We think that the following illustration will clear any difficulty. In the expression 'books, pamphlets, newspapers and other documents' private letters may not be held included if 'other documents' be interpreted ejusdem generis with what goes before. But in a provision which reads 'newspapers or other document likely to convey secrets to the enemy', the words 'other document' would include document of any kind and would not take their colour from 'newspapers'. It follows, therefore, that interpretation ejusdem generis or noscitur a sociis need not always be made when words showing particular classes are followed by general words. Before the general words can be so interpreted there must be a genus constituted or a category disclosed with reference to which the general words can and are intended to be restricted."¶ 6
"Here the expression 'claim of set-off' does not disclose a category or a genus. Set-offs are of two kinds — legal and equitable — and both are already comprehended and it is difficult to think of any right 'arising from a contract' which is of the same nature as a claim of set-off and can be raised by a defendant in a suit. Mr B.C. Misra, whom we invited to give us examples, admitted frankly that it was impossible for him to think of any proceeding of the nature of a claim of set-off other than a claim of set-off which could be raised in a suit such as is described in the second sub-section. In respect of the first sub-section he could give only two examples. They are (i) a claim by a pledger of goods with an unregistered firm whose goods are attached and who has to make an objection under Order 21 Rule 58 of the Code of Civil Procedure and (ii) proving a debt before a liquidator. The latter is not raised as a defence and cannot belong to the same genus as a 'claim of set-off'. The former can be made to fit but by a stretch of some considerable imagination. It is difficult for us to accept that the legislature was thinking of such far-fetched things when it spoke of 'other proceeding' ejusdem generis with a claim of set-off."¶ 6
"It is significant that in the latter part of clause (b) of that section the words are 'or to any proceeding in execution or other proceeding incidental to or arising from any such suit or claim' and this clearly shows that the word 'proceeding' is not limited to a proceeding in the nature of a suit or a claim of set-off. Sub-section (4) combines suits and a claim of set-off and then speaks of 'any proceeding in execution' and 'other proceeding incidental to or arising from any such suit or claim' as being outside the ban of the main section. It would hardly have been necessary to be so explicit if the words 'other proceeding' in the main section had a meaning as restricted as is suggested by the respondent."¶ 7
"In our judgment, the words 'other proceeding' in sub-section (3) must receive their full meaning untrammelled by the words 'a claim of set-off'. The latter words neither intend nor can be construed to cut down the generality of the words 'other proceeding'. The sub-section provides for the application of the provisions of sub-sections (1) and (2) to claims of set-off and also to other proceedings of any kind which can properly be said to be for enforcement of any right arising from contract except those expressly mentioned as exceptions in sub-section (3) and sub-section (4)."¶ 9 — the ratio, as later quoted in full by Umesh Goel

¶ 10: "The appeal is, therefore, allowed."

Principles evolved
  1. "Other proceeding" in s. 69(3) is given its full and natural meaning. It is not confined to proceedings of the nature of a suit or of a claim of set-off. It covers every proceeding "of any kind which can properly be said to be for enforcement of any right arising from contract".
  2. Ejusdem generis is not automatic. Before general words following particular words are cut down, there must be a genus constituted or a category disclosed with reference to which the general words can be restricted. "A claim of set-off" discloses no such genus.
  3. A right is none the less "arising from a contract" because the immediate source of the proceeding is a statute. An arbitration petition under s. 8 of the 1940 Act has "its genesis in the arbitration clause"; without the agreement, the section cannot be invoked.
  4. The ban is not limited to suits — and the scheme of the section (the express reference to execution proceedings in sub-s (4)(b)) confirms it.
  5. But the ban is limited to rights arising from a contract — and that limitation is what Haldiram later exploited, and what Umesh Goel later used to distinguish this very case.
  6. Practical consequence: the proper order when an unregistered firm's proceeding is hit by s. 69 is to stay it, not to dismiss it — so that the party may register and proceed. This is the standard DU note attached to this case.
! Two cautions before you cite this case
  1. The sub-section numbering in the judgment does not match the current Act. Hidayatullah J. describes sub-s (1) as barring suits "between partners inter se or between partners and the firm" and sub-s (2) as barring suits "by or on behalf of the firm against a third party". In the Act as it stands, sub-s (1) is the partner-v-firm bar and sub-s (2) the firm-v-third-party bar. State the principle, then apply the current numbering.
  2. This case was decided under the Arbitration Act, 1940, whose scheme gave the civil court pervasive control over arbitration at every stage. Umesh Goel (2016) holds that the ratio "can have no application to a proceeding which emanated under the 1996 Act". Do not state Jagdish Chander Gupta as the law on arbitration today without adding Umesh Goel.
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

DU 2010 OC Q.7 asks in terms: "If the partnership agreement, inter alia, includes reference of future disputes to arbitration, can the court entertain a petition by a partner of that firm to appoint an arbitrator if the firm is unregistered?" Answer: Under the 1940 Act, no — because the petition under s. 8 is a proceeding in a civil court to enforce a right arising from the arbitration clause, and "other proceeding" in s. 69(3) receives its full meaning (Jagdish Chander Gupta). Under the 1996 Act, yes — because s. 69 requires a suit instituted in a court as the foundation, and an arbitrator is not a court (Umesh Goel). Then add the standard note: the proper course in a court is to stay, not dismiss.

CASE 13 · Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar
(2000) 3 SCC 250 · Supreme Court (M. Jagannadha Rao J.) · DU reader p. 56 · [Sections 69(2), 2(d)]
What kind of "right arising from a contract" does s. 69(2) bar — only a contract entered into by the plaintiff firm with the defendant in the course of its business, or any contract referred to in the plaint as the source of title?
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The two defendants, M/s Haldiram Bhujiawala and Shri Ashok Kumar, appealed against the judgment of the Delhi High Court in FAO No. 365 of 1999 dated 30-11-1999, by which the High Court summarily dismissed their appeal against the order of the learned Single Judge dated 2-11-1999 in IA No. 5996 of 1999 in Suit No. 635 of 1992. The IA was filed under Order 7 Rule 11 CPC for rejection of the plaint filed by two plaintiffs, Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar trading as Haldiram Bhujiawala and Shiv Kishan Aggarwal, on the ground that the 1st plaintiff was a partnership not registered with the Registrar of Firms on the date of the suit i.e. on 10-12-1991, and that the subsequent registration of the firm on 29-5-1992 would not cure the initial defect.

The suit was filed by the plaintiffs (1) for permanent injunction restraining the defendant appellants, their partners, servants etc. from infringing Trademark No. 285062 and from using the trademark/name "HALDIRAM BHUJIAWALA" or any identical name/mark deceptively similar thereto, (2) for damages in a sum of Rs 6 lakhs, and (3) for destruction of the material etc.

Because the matter arose under Order 7 Rule 11 CPC, the Court went to the plaint allegations. The family history is worth following in full, because it is the whole case:

  • One Ganga Ram (sic Bishan) alias Haldiram carried on business in the name "Haldiram Bhujiawala" since 1941.
  • In 1965 he constituted a partnership with his two sons Moolchand, Shiv Kishan and his daughter-in-law Kamla Devi (wife of another son R.L. Aggarwal) to carry on business under the same name.
  • In December 1972 the firm applied to the Registrar of Trade Marks for registration of the name "Haldiram Bhujiawala - Chand Mal - Ganga Bishan Bhujiawala, Bikaner". The Registrar granted registration with No. 285062.
  • On 16-11-1974 the partnership was dissolved, and under the terms of the dissolution deed the trademark fell exclusively to the share of Moolchand, son of Ganga Bishan and father of the plaintiffs, for the whole country except West Bengal. Smt Kamla Devi was given ownership of the trademark rights for West Bengal.
  • Lala Ganga Bishan Haldiram executed his last will dated 3-4-1979, reiterating the rights conferred by the dissolution deed. He died in 1980; his will was later acted upon.
  • Moolchand died in 1985, leaving four sons — Shiv Kishan, Shiv Ratan, Manohar Lal and Madhusoodan. All of them got their names recorded as subsequent joint proprietors. The latter three formed a partnership in 1983 and ran a shop in Chandni Chowk, New Delhi under the trademark.
  • Meanwhile, on 10-10-1977, Moolchand's brother R.L. Aggarwal (husband of Kamla Devi) and his son Prabhu Shankar of Calcutta applied for registration in the very same name at Calcutta, without disclosing the dissolution deed dated 16-11-1974.

The plaint then alleged that the defendants had no right to use the said trademark beyond Calcutta; that the plaintiffs' registered trademark was renewed on 29-12-1986 till 28-12-1993; that the plaintiffs had also acquired a right on account of prior adoption and long user; that the 1st plaintiff firm (three sons of Moolchand) and the 2nd plaintiff (the fourth son) are joint owners of the trademark except in West Bengal; that the 1st defendant firm is a newly-constituted firm formed by Ashok Kumar, son of Kamla Devi, and the 2nd defendant is Ashok Kumar himself; and that the plaintiffs came to know of the violation in December 1991 when the defendants opened a shop at Arya Samaj Road, Karol Bagh, New Delhi.

The cause of action, as pleaded, was that the defendants acted "in violation of the common law and contractual rights of the plaintiff".

Issues (as framed by the Court, ¶ 8)
  1. (i) Whether Section 69(2) bars a suit by a firm not registered on the date of suit where permanent injunction and damages are claimed in respect of a trademark as a statutory right or by invoking common law principles applicable to a passing-off action?
  2. (ii) Whether the words "arising from a contract" in Section 69(2) refer only to a situation where an unregistered firm is enforcing a right arising from a contract entered into by the firm with the defendant during the course of its business, or whether the bar can be extended to any contract referred to in the plaint, unconnected with the defendant, as the source of title to the suit property?
For the appellant-defendants (Shri Ashok Desai and Shri R.F. Nariman, Senior Counsel)
  • The 1st plaintiff firm was not registered on the date of suit.
  • The plaint repeatedly referred to the proprietary right of the late Moolchand as having arisen out of the dissolution deed dated 16-11-1974, "and that without reference to the said document — which was a contract — the plaintiffs could not prove their right to the trademark through Moolchand and the suit was barred since Section 69(2) referred to a right 'arising from a contract'." The plaintiffs' right was based on the contract dated 16-11-1974.
  • The words "arising from a contract" are akin to the words "arising out of a contract" used in Ruby General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pearey Lal Kumar [AIR 1952 SC 119], where, while construing those words in relation to an arbitration clause, this Court held that the said words had to be construed widely.
  • "On the facts of this case and as stated in the plaint at several places, the 1st plaintiff was compelled to rely on the contract of dissolution dated 16-11-1974 to prove title to the trademark and thereby for an injunction and hence it was not a right claimed under common law or under any statute, like the Trade Marks Act."
For the respondent-plaintiffs (Shri Gopal Subramanium, Senior Counsel)
  • The suit for injunction was based upon two rights: (a) a statutory right under the Trade Marks Act arising out of prior registration of the trademark; and alternatively (b) the common law right available in a passing-off action.
  • "The suit was not based on any contract between the plaintiffs and the defendants. The provision in Section 69(2) did not apply if the right sought to be enforced did not arise out of a contract between the plaintiffs' firm and the defendants."
  • The reference in the plaint to the dissolution deed dated 16-11-1974 was merely a reference to a historical fact — that it was the source of the right of Moolchand, and that on his death the right devolved on his sons.
  • "The plaintiffs were not parties to the deed of dissolution. The defendants too were not parties to the dissolution deed though their mother was." Hence the bar under s. 69(2) did not apply.
Decision (M. Jagannadha Rao J.) — appeal dismissed

Point 1 — statutory and common law rights are outside the bar (¶¶ 9–12).}

"The question whether Section 69(2) is a bar to a suit filed by an unregistered firm even if a statutory right is being enforced or even if only a common law right is being enforced came up directly for consideration in this Court in Raptakas Brett Co. Ltd. v. Ganesh Property [(1998) 7 SCC 184]. In that case, Majmudar, J. speaking for the Bench clearly expressed the view that Section 69(2) cannot bar the enforcement by way of a suit by an unregistered firm in respect of a statutory right or a common law right. On the facts of that case, it was held that the right to evict a tenant upon expiry of the lease was not a right 'arising from a contract' but was a common law right or a statutory right under the Transfer of Property Act. The fact that the plaint in that case referred to a lease and to its expiry, made no difference. … it must be held in the present case too that a suit is not barred by Section 69(2) if a statutory right or a common law right is being enforced."¶ 9
"It is well settled that a passing-off action is a common law action based on tort. Therefore, in our opinion, a suit for perpetual injunction to restrain the defendants not to pass off the defendants' goods as those of the plaintiffs by using the plaintiffs' trademark and for damages is an action at common law and is not barred by Section 69(2). The decision in Virendra Dresses v. Varinder Garments [AIR 1982 Del 482] states that Section 69(2) does not apply to a passing-off action as the suit is based on tort and not on contract. In our opinion, the above decisions were correctly decided. … The learned Senior Counsel for the appellants no doubt relied upon Ruby General Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Pearey Lal Kumar. That was an arbitration case in which the words 'arising out of a contract' were widely interpreted but that decision, in our view, has no relevance in interpreting the words 'arising from a contract' in Section 69(2) of the Partnership Act."¶ 10 — note the SLP against Virendra Dresses was dismissed on 28-1-2000
"Likewise, if the reliefs of permanent injunction or damages are being claimed on the basis of a registered trademark and its infringement, the suit is to be treated as one based on a statutory right under the Trade Marks Act and is, in our view, not barred by Section 69(2)."¶ 11

Point 2 — the meaning of "arising from a contract" (¶¶ 13–26). This is the heart of the judgment, and the Court travelled a long way to get there:

"In our view, it will be useful in this context to refer to the Report of the Special Committee (1930-31) which examined the Draft Bill and made recommendations to the legislature."¶ 15
"Before going into the above Report … it will be necessary to refer to the case in CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills Contractor Co. (1971) 1 SCC 280, where this Court refused to refer to this very Report for construing Section 59 of the Partnership Act. But, in our view, that decision is no longer good law as it was clearly dissented on this aspect in the judgment of the Constitution Bench in R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay (1984) 2 SCC 183. … A restricted view was no doubt expressed in P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998) 4 SCC 626 (at pp. 691-92) that such reports can be looked into for the purpose of knowing the historical basis or mischief sought to be remedied, but not for construing the provision unless there is ambiguity. Even going by this restricted view, we find that there is considerable ambiguity in Section 69(2) (unlike the English Statute of 1916 and 1985) as to what is meant by the words 'arising out of a contract' inasmuch as the provision does not say whether the contract in Section 69(2) is one entered into by the firm with the defendant or with somebody else who is not a defendant, nor to whether it is a contract entered into with the defendant in business or unconnected with business. Hence, in our view, it is permissible to look into the Report even for purpose of construing Section 69(2)."¶ 16 — the explicit, reasoned departure from Jayalakshmi

The Committee and the Report (¶¶ 17–19). The Committee consisted of Sir Brojendra Lal Mitter, Sir Dinshah F. Mulla, Sir Alladi Krishnaswamy Iyer and Mr Arthur Eggar. The Court quoted paras 16 to 19:

"17. The outlines of the scheme are briefly as follows. The English precedent insofar as it makes registration compulsory and imposes a penalty for non-registration has not been followed, as it is considered that this step would be too drastic for a beginning in India, and would introduce all the difficulties connected with small or ephemeral undertakings. Instead, it is proposed that registration should lie entirely within the discretion of the firm or partner concerned; but, following the English precedent, any firm which is not registered will be unable to enforce its claim against third parties in the civil court; and any partner who is not registered will be unable to enforce his claims either against third parties or against fellow partners."¶ 17 of the Report
"18. Once registration has been effected the statement recorded in the register regarding the constitution of the firm will be conclusive proof of the facts therein contained against the partners making them and no partner whose name is on the register will be permitted to deny that he is a partner … This should afford a strong protection to persons dealing with firms against false denials of partnership and the evasion of liability by the substantial members of a firm."¶ 18 — the object behind both s. 68 and s. 69
"19. … On the other hand, a third party who deals with a firm and knows that a new partner has been introduced can either make registration of the new partner a condition for further dealings, or content himself with the certain security of the other partners and the chance of proving by other evidence, the partnership of the new but unregistered partner. A third party who deals with a firm without knowing of the addition of a new partner counts on the credit of the old partners only and will not be prejudiced by the failure of the new partner to register."¶ 19 — the protection is for DEALERS, not for strangers

The English precedent (¶¶ 19–21). The Court identified it as the Registration of Business Names Act, 1916, s. 8 of which speaks of

"(T)he rights of that defaulter under or arising out of any contract made or entered into by or on behalf of such defaulter in relation to the business in respect to the carrying on of which particulars were required to be furnished" (see Halsbury Statutes, 3rd Edn., Vol. 37, p. 867).

"The above provision clearly signifies that the right that is sought to be enforced by the unregistered firm and which is barred must be a right arising out of a contract with a third-party defendant in respect of the firm's business transactions."¶ 19

The Business Names Act, 1985 replaced the 1916 Act; s. 4 provides for dismissal of the action "to enforce a right arising out of a contract made in the course of a business" (¶ 20). Hence ¶ 21:

"The above Report and provisions of the English Acts, in our view, make it clear that the purpose behind Section 69(2) was to impose a disability on the unregistered firm or its partners to enforce rights arising out of contracts entered into by the plaintiff firm with the third-party defendants in the course of the firm's business transactions."¶ 21

The two elements, and the motor-car illustration (¶¶ 22–25).

"In Raptakos Brett and Co. it was clarified that the contractual rights which are sought to be enforced by the plaintiff firm and which are barred under Section 69(2) are 'rights arising out of the contract' and that it must be a contract entered into by the firm with the third-party defendants. Majmudar, J. stated as follows: 'A mere look at the aforesaid provision shows that the suit filed by an unregistered firm against a third party for enforcement of any right arising from a contract with such a third party would be barred….' (emphasis supplied) From the above passage it is firstly clear that a contract must be a contract by the plaintiff firm not with anybody else but with the third-party defendant."¶ 22
"The further and additional but equally important aspect which has to be made clear is that the contract by the unregistered firm referred to in Section 69(2) must not only be one entered into by the firm with the third-party defendant but must also be one entered into by the plaintiff firm in the course of the business dealings of the plaintiff firm with such third-party defendant."¶ 23 — element two
"It will also be seen that the present defendants who are sued by the plaintiff firm are third parties to the 1st plaintiff firm. Section 2(d) of the Act defines 'third parties' as persons who are not partners of the firm. The defendants in the present case are also third parties to the contract of dissolution dated 16-11-1974. Their mother, Kamla Devi was no doubt a party to the contract of dissolution. … The real crux of the question is that the legislature, when it used the words 'arising out of a contract' in Section 69(2), is referring to a contract entered into in course of business transactions by the unregistered plaintiff firm with its defendant customers and the idea is to protect those in commerce who deal with such a partnership firm in business. Such third parties who deal with the partners ought to be enabled to know what the names of the partners of the firm are before they deal with them in business."¶ 24
"Further, Section 69(2) is not attracted to any and every contract referred to in the plaint as the source of title to an asset owned by the firm. If the plaint referred to such a contract it could only be as a historical fact. For example, if the plaint filed by the unregistered firm refers to the source of the firm's title to a motor car and states that the plaintiff has purchased and received a motor car from a foreign buyer under a contract and that the defendant has unauthorisedly removed it from the plaintiff firm's possession, it is clear that the relief for possession against the defendant in the suit does not arise from any contract which the defendant entered into in the course of the plaintiff firm's business with the defendant but is based on the alleged unauthorised removal of the vehicle from the plaintiff firm's custody by the defendant. In such a situation, the fact that the unregistered firm has purchased the vehicle from somebody else under a contract has absolutely no bearing on the right of the firm to sue the defendant for possession of the vehicle. Such a suit would be maintainable and Section 69(2) would not be a bar, even if the firm is unregistered on the date of suit. The position in the present case is not different."¶ 25 — the illustration every DU answer should quote

And the part of the judgment that every DU paper-writer wants (¶ 26):

"In fact, the Act has not prescribed that the transactions or contracts entered into by a firm with a third party are bad in law if the firm is an unregistered firm. On the other hand, if the firm is not registered on the date of suit and the suit is to enforce a right arising out of a contract with the third-party defendant in the course of its business, then it will be open to the plaintiff to seek withdrawal of the plaint with leave and file a fresh suit after registration of the firm subject of course to the law of limitation and subject to the provisions of the Limitation Act. This is so even if the suit is dismissed for a formal defect. Section 14 of the Limitation Act will be available inasmuch as the suit has failed because the defect of non-registration falls within the words 'other cause of like nature' in Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963. (See Surajmal Dagduramji Shop v. Shrikisan Ramkisan.)"¶ 26

¶¶ 27–28: "…it is clear that the suit is based on infringement of statutory rights under the Trade Marks Act. It is also based upon the common law principles of tort applicable to passing-off actions. The suit is not for enforcement of any right arising out of a contract entered into by or on behalf of the unregistered firm with third parties in the course of the firm's business transactions. The suit is therefore not barred by Section 69(2). … the appeal fails and is dismissed without costs. We should not be understood as having said anything on the merits of the case for we have confined ourselves to the allegations in the plaint as we are here only dealing with an application filed by the appellants under Order 7 Rule 11 CPC."

Principles evolved
  1. s. 69(2) bars only the enforcement of a right arising from a contract. Statutory rights (Trade Marks Act, T.P. Act) and common law rights (passing off — an action in tort) are outside the bar (Raptakos Brett followed; Virendra Dresses approved).
  2. Two cumulative elements for the contract limb: (a) the contract must be one entered into by the plaintiff firm with the third-party defendant — not with a stranger; and (b) it must be entered into in the course of the business dealings of the plaintiff firm with that defendant.
  3. A contract pleaded merely as the source of title is a "historical fact" and does not attract the bar (the motor-car illustration).
  4. "Third party" = s. 2(d): a person who is not a partner of the firm. The defendants here were third parties to the firm and to the 1974 dissolution deed.
  5. Ejusdem generis and the English statutes. The Registration of Business Names Act 1916 s. 8 and the Business Names Act 1985 s. 4 both confine the disability to contracts made in the course of the business — and s. 69(2) was modelled on them.
  6. Reports of the Special Committee ARE admissible to construe the Act; Jayalakshmi's contrary view "is no longer good law" after R.S. Nayak v. A.R. Antulay. Even on the narrow P.V. Narasimha Rao view, s. 69(2) is ambiguous enough to permit it.
  7. Non-registration does not invalidate the contract. It only bars the remedy. The plaintiff may withdraw with leave and sue afresh after registration, and s. 14 of the Limitation Act 1963 ("other cause of like nature") saves the period (Surajmal Dagduramji Shop).
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

This is the case to cite whenever the question is "what is the effect of non-registration?" (DU 2017 Q.8, 2015 Q.4(b), 2014 Q.4, 2013 Q.5, 2011 Q.5–6, 2010 Q.7, 2000 Q.6(a), 1998 Q.7(a), 1997 Q.5(a), 1996 Q.7, 1992 Q.5(a)). The four-point closing paragraph writes itself: (1) the contract is not void — only the remedy is barred; (2) the bar reaches only rights arising from a contract with the defendant, in the course of business; (3) statutory and tortious rights are outside it; and (4) even where the bar applies the plaintiff may withdraw with leave and re-file after registration, with s. 14 Limitation Act excluding the time.

CASE 14 · M/s Umesh Goel v. Himachal Pradesh Cooperative Housing Society Ltd.
(2016) 11 SCC 313 · Supreme Court (F.M. Ibrahim Kalifulla J.) · DU reader p. 63
"An interesting but very important legal question arises for consideration in this appeal relating to interpretation of Section 69(3) of the Indian Partnership Act with reference to its applicability to Arbitral proceedings."
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The respondent, a Cooperative Group Housing Society, invited tenders for construction of 102 dwelling units with basement at Plot No. 21, Sector 5, Dwarka, New Delhi. Tenders were invited in May 1998. The appellant, an unregistered partnership firm, submitted its bid on 06.05.1998, was the successful bidder, and the contract was awarded at an estimated cost of Rs. 9.80 crores. A letter of intent was issued. On 09.08.1998 the appellant submitted its first bill. The agreement was entered into on 02.02.1999. There was delay in getting the plan sanctioned which, according to the appellant, was not his responsibility.

A dispute arose, and the appellant moved the Delhi High Court under Section 9 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (filed 22.05.2005) to restrain the respondent from dispossessing him from the worksite till the work executed was measured by a Commissioner. A Commissioner was appointed. A second s. 9 application was filed on 29.01.2003.

On the dispute itself, the respondent appointed Smt. Sangeeta Tomar, Advocate, as arbitrator on 17.03.2003. Though the appellant had earlier moved Arbitration Application No. 145 of 2003 on 09.07.2003 under Section 11(5) for appointment of an independent arbitrator, that application was subsequently withdrawn, and the appellant participated in the arbitration before the arbitrator appointed by the respondent. Claims and counter-claims were exchanged. The arbitrator passed an award on 05.05.2005 allowing the appellant's claim to the extent of Rs. 1,36,24,886.08 with interest at 12% from 01.06.2002 till the award and 18% thereafter.

Crucially: "While resisting the claim of the appellant, the respondent did not specifically raise any plea under Section 69 of the Partnership Act." The respondent challenged the award under Section 34 (A.A. No. 188 of 2005, filed 02.08.2005); the learned Single Judge dismissed it on 01.09.2005 and also dismissed Review Application No. 26 of 2005 on 03.10.2005. The respondent appealed in FAO (OS) No. 376 of 2005 on 14.11.2005, and by the impugned order dated 20.11.2007 the Division Bench allowed the appeal — hence this appeal by the firm.

Issue

Whether Section 69(3) of the Partnership Act applies to arbitral proceedings under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — i.e. whether the expression "other proceedings" in s. 69(3) includes arbitration before an arbitrator.

For the appellant firm (Shri Dhruv Mehta, Senior Counsel) — accepted
  • Sub-sections (1) and (2) are bodily lifted into sub-section (3). "When we read sub-section (3) … the provisions of sub-sections (1) and (2) have been impliedly incorporated … the entirety of the said two sub-sections should be held to be bodily lifted and incorporated in sub-section (3). It is difficult to state that any one part of sub-sections (1) and (2) alone should be held to be incorporated." Therefore "other proceedings" must be proceedings connected with a suit in a court and "cannot be read in isolation".
  • An arbitrator is not a court. Applying the golden rule of construction, "an arbitrator by himself is not a court for the purpose of Section 69". There is "a vast difference between an arbitrator and the Court": an arbitrator may exercise judicial powers but he does not derive such powers from the State; he derives them by the agreement of the parties under a contract.
  • Section 36 of the 1996 Act is only a limited statutory fiction. "It is only a statutory fiction by which for the purpose of enforcement, the award is deemed to be a decree and it cannot be enlarged … to mean that by virtue of the said award to be deemed as a decree, the arbitrator can be held to be a Court."
  • Three mandatory conditions must coexist before s. 69(3) can be invoked: (a) there should be a suit and the other proceedings should be intrinsically connected to the suit; (b) such suit should have been laid to enforce a right arising from the contract; and (c) such a suit should have been filed in a Court of law.
For the respondent Society (Shri Amarendra Saran, Senior Counsel) — rejected
  • "Other proceedings" includes arbitral proceedings, and the only foundation needed is "a right in a contract".
  • Arbitral proceedings are on a par with civil proceedings. Reliance on decisions interpreting Section 14 of the Limitation Act to that effect.
  • Under Section 2(a) of the Interest Act, "Court" has been defined to include a Tribunal and an Arbitrator; therefore "the arbitrator should be held to be a Court and the proceedings pending before it are to be treated as a suit and consequently other proceedings".
  • Sections 35 and 36 of the 1996 Act equate an award to a decree of the Court and apply the CPC for execution; therefore "the arbitral proceedings should be held to be civil proceedings before a Court".
  • Reliance also placed on Consolidated Engineering Enterprises and P. Sarathy.
Decision (F.M. Ibrahim Kalifulla J.) — appeal allowed; Division Bench set aside

Step 1 — sub-ss (1) and (2) are wholly incorporated into (3).

"When the opening set of expression in sub-section (3) states that the provisions of sub-sections (1) and (2) shall apply, there is no difficulty in accepting the said submission of learned Senior Counsel for the appellant that the entirety of the said two sub-sections should be held to be bodily lifted and incorporated in sub-section (3). … Therefore, we are convinced that when we read sub-section (3) it is imperative that all the ingredients contained in sub-sections (1) and (2) should be read into sub-section (3) and thereafter apply the said sub-section when such application is called for in any matter."¶ 12

Step 2 — the condition precedent is a suit in a court.

"If sub-sections (1) and (2) are virtually lifted whole hog and incorporated in sub-section (3), it must be stated that it is not the mere ban that is imposed in sub-sections (1) and (2) and that alone is contemplated for the application of sub-section (3). … the ban can operate in respect of an unregistered firm even relating to a set off or other proceedings only when such claim of set off or other proceedings are intrinsically connected with the suit that is pending in a Court. … To be more precise, the condition precedent for the operation of ban under sub-section (3) is that the launching of a suit in a Court of law should be present and it should be by an unregistered firm or by a person claiming to be partner of an unregistered firm either to a claim for set off in the said suit or any other proceedings intrinsically connected with the said suit. In the event of the above ingredients set out under sub-sections (1), (2) and (3) being fulfilled then and then alone the ban prescribed against an unregistered firm under Section 69(1), (2) and (3) would operate and not otherwise."¶¶ 15–17

Step 3 — the 1940 Act and the 1996 Act are structurally different. The Court catalogued the 1940 Act's provisions (ss. 8, 11, 12, 14–21, 23–25, 28–41, 43, 47) and concluded:

"The scope and ambit of the power and jurisdiction of Court defined under Section 2(e) of the 1996 Act is circumscribed to certain specified extent as set out in Sections 8, 9, 14, 27, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42, 43, 47, 48, 49, 50, 56, 58 and 59. A comparative consideration of the 1940 Act and 1996 Act disclose that the extent of control and operation of a Court under the former Act was far more intensive and elaborate than the latter Act. The more significant distinction … is that the former Act does not merely stop with the initiation and enforcement of an Arbitration and its award, but effectively provides for intervention at every stage of the Arbitral proceedings upto its final consideration and enforcement as if it were a regular civil suit, whereas under the 1996 Act, the scope of intervention is not that of a Civil Court as it could do in the matter of a suit."¶ 19

Step 4 — Jagdish Chander Gupta is distinguished, not overruled.

"…in Jagdish Chander case (supra), in as much the initiation of the proceedings were under Section 8 of the 1940 Act before a Civil Court having jurisdiction to decide the question forming the subject matter of suit and the respondent therein being an unregistered Partnership Firm, the ingredients set out in Section 69(1) to (3) of the Partnership Act applied in all force. … We only wish to add that though in the said decision, this Court did not specifically mention as to the requirement of pendency of a proceeding in the nature of a suit in a Civil Court as the basic ingredient … this Court was fully aware of the fulfillment of those mandatory requirement having regard to the nature of proceedings that existed under the provisions of the 1940 Act. … We have therefore no hesitation to hold that the ratio laid down in Jagdish Chander case does not in anyway conflict with the view which we have taken herein, having regard to the advent of the 1996 Act … and that it can have no application to a proceedings which emanated under the 1996 Act."¶¶ 19–20

Step 5 — Kamal Pushp Enterprises approved. Quoting ¶ 9 of Kamal Pushp Enterprises v. D.R. Construction Co. [(2000) 6 SCC 659]:

"The prohibition contained in Section 69 is in respect of instituting a proceeding to enforce a right arising from a contract in any Court by an unregistered firm, and it had no application to the proceedings before an Arbitrator and that too when the reference to the Arbitrator was at the instance of the appellant itself. … The Award in this case cannot either rightly or legitimately said to be vitiated on account of the prohibition contained in Section 69 of the partnership Act, 1932 since the same has no application to proceedings before an Arbitrator. At the stage of enforcement of the award by passing a decree in terms thereof what is enforced is the award itself which crystallise the rights of parties under the Indian Contract Act and the general law to be paid for the work executed and not any right arising only from the objectionable contract."Kamal Pushp ¶ 9, quoted at ¶ 21

Step 6 — the respondent's three answers all fail.

  • The Limitation Act s. 14 argument: "it is always well settled that a judgment can be a binding precedent on a question of law, which was canvassed before it and decided. … a reading of Section 69 as a whole does not permit of any interpretation that would cover Arbitral proceedings, de hors, filing of a suit in a Court … Therefore, any interpretation made under the Limitation Act while construing Section 14 to treat Arbitral proceedings on par with civil proceedings cannot be applied to the case on hand." Consolidated Engineering Enterprises and P. Sarathy not applied.
  • The Interest Act s. 2(a) argument: "If such a specific provision has been incorporated in the Partnership Act, there can be no difficulty in accepting the argument … In the absence of such a specific provision, it will not be appropriate to import the definition clause under Section 2(a) of the Interest Act to the Partnership Act in order to apply Section 69(3)."
  • The ss. 35–36 argument: "by referring to Sections 35 and 36, it is difficult to draw an inference that based on the deeming provision specifically meant for the enforcement and execution of an Award, the Arbitral Proceedings can be equated to a Civil Court proceedings. As rightly contended by Mr. Dhruv Mehta … Section 36 only creates a statutory fiction which is limited for the purpose of enforcement of the Award. The deeming fiction is specifically restricted to treat the Award as a decree of a Court, exclusively for the purpose of execution. … it is a settled proposition, that a statutory provision will have to be construed from the words that are expressly used and it is not for the Court to add or substitute any word to it."

Step 7 — the law on legal fictions. The Court drew support from Sadan K. Bormal ¶ 25 — "the Court must ascertain the purpose for which the fiction is created … and having done so must assume all those facts and consequences which are incidental or inevitable corollaries … In construing a fiction it must not be extended beyond the purpose for which it is created or beyond the language of the Section by which it is created. It cannot be extended by importing another fiction" — quoting Lord Asquith in East End Dwelling Co. Ltd. v. Finsbury Borough Council [(1951) 2 All ER 587]:

"If you are bidden to treat an imaginary state of affairs as real, you must surely, unless prohibited from doing so, also imagine as real the consequence and incidents which, if the putative state of affairs had in fact existed, must inevitably have flowed from or accompanied it. The statute says that you must imagine a certain state of affairs; it does not say that having done so, you must cause or permit your imagination to boggle when it comes to the inevitable corollaries of that state of affairs."Lord Asquith, East End Dwelling (1951) 2 All ER 587

And from Paramjeet Singh Patheja v. ICDS Ltd. [(2006) 13 SCC 322] ¶ 42:

"The words as if demonstrate that award and decree or order are two different things. The legal fiction created is for the limited purpose of enforcement as a decree. The fiction is not intended to make it a decree for all purposes under all statutes, whether State or Central."Paramjeet Singh Patheja ¶ 42

Conclusion: "Having regard to our conclusion that Arbitral Proceedings will not come under the expression other proceedings of Section 69(3) of the Partnership Act, the ban imposed under the said Section 69 can have no application to Arbitral proceedings as well as the Arbitration Award. Therefore, the appeal stands allowed, the impugned judgment of the Division Bench is set aside and the judgment of the learned Single Judge stands restored. No costs."

Principles evolved
  1. Sub-sections (1) and (2) of s. 69 are bodily lifted into sub-section (3). The ban in (3) therefore inherits every ingredient of (1) and (2) — including the requirement of a suit in a court.
  2. Condition precedent: the launching of a suit in a court of law, by or on behalf of an unregistered firm or a person suing as its partner, to enforce a right arising from a contract; a claim of set-off or "other proceeding" is caught only if it is intrinsically connected with that suit.
  3. An arbitrator is not a court for the purposes of s. 69. He exercises judicial power, but derives it from the agreement of the parties, not from the State.
  4. Arbitral proceedings under the 1996 Act are outside s. 69(3) — the Court's role is confined to ss. 8, 9, 14, 27, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42, 43, 47–50, 56, 58, 59, unlike the 1940 Act where the court intervened at every stage "as if it were a regular civil suit".
  5. Jagdish Chander Gupta survives but is confined to 1940-Act proceedings initiated before a civil court under s. 8.
  6. Kamal Pushp Enterprises approved: post-award proceedings are outside s. 69, and at the enforcement stage what is enforced is the award, which crystallises rights under the Contract Act and the general law — not "any right arising only from the objectionable contract".
  7. A statutory fiction is not extended beyond the purpose for which it is created (Sadan K. Bormal ¶ 25; East End Dwelling; Paramjeet Singh Patheja ¶ 42).
  8. A definition in one statute cannot be imported into another. The Interest Act s. 2(a) definition of "Court" cannot be read into the Partnership Act.
! Two things DU examiners love to trip you on here
  1. Do not say "Jagdish Chander Gupta is bad law." It is distinguished, not overruled. It still governs arbitral proceedings that were initiated before a civil court under the 1940 Act. The precise holding in Umesh Goel is that the ratio "can have no application to a proceedings which emanated under the 1996 Act".
  2. The firm in Umesh Goel was the claimant, and it won. The award was in its favour for ₹1.36 crore. The respondent Society never pleaded s. 69 before the arbitrator — yet the Division Bench allowed the s. 34 appeal on that basis. The Supreme Court's restoration of the Single Judge's order is therefore also a quiet rebuke to raising a s. 69 objection for the first time in appeal.
Diagram 11 — The same words, two different statutes, two opposite results. Jagdish Chander Gupta remains good law for 1940-Act arbitrations initiated before a civil court; Umesh Goel holds that under the 1996 Act there is no "suit" for s. 69(3) to attach to.
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

This is the modern answer to DU 2010 OC Q.7 and to every question that couples arbitration with s. 69. Write it as a two-regime answer: (1) under the 1940 Act the court superintended arbitration at every stage, so a s. 8 petition before a civil court was an "other proceeding" within s. 69(3) (Jagdish Chander Gupta); (2) under the 1996 Act the court's role is confined to ss. 8, 9, 14, 27, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42 and the arbitrator derives his authority from the agreement and not from the State — so, since sub-ss (1) and (2) are bodily lifted into (3), the ban requires a suit in a court and arbitral proceedings are outside it (Umesh Goel); (3) Kamal Pushp adds that even at the enforcement stage what is enforced is the award, not a contractual right. Always add that the 1996 Act is the operative statute today.

SET AIntroduction / admission of a partner — s. 31Recurs 3×
Question "How can a new partner be introduced into a firm? State the rights, duties and liabilities of a person who is admitted as a partner into an existing firm." (Asked as: 2011 Q.1(c); 2010 Q.1(c); 2003 Q.3. Variant wording: "Who is an incoming partner? What are his rights and liabilities — in particular, is he liable for the debts of the firm incurred before his admission?")
Model Answer

1. The governing provision — s. 31(1). "Subject to contract between the partners and to the provisions of section 30, no person shall be introduced as a partner into a firm without the consent of all the existing partners." Three features of that sentence are exam-worthy: (i) it opens subject to contract, so the deed may itself prescribe a majority or any other mechanism; (ii) the reservation of s. 30 is what lets a minor be admitted to the benefits of partnership without his "consent" being meaningful; and (iii) consent of all is the default, not a majority. s. 31(2) then adds that, subject to s. 30 and to contract, "any person may retire from a firm" — the other half of the same subsection.

2. What "introduction" actually is — s. 31(2), second limb. This is the most-missed part of the topic:

  • He is a partner from the date of the agreement to admit him — not from the date of any registration, intimation under s. 63, or public notice.
  • He has no liability for anything done before that date.
  • He becomes liable for everything done on or after that date.

Note the deliberate contrast with s. 32(3) (a retiring partner remains liable until public notice) and s. 31(2): an incoming partner needs no public notice at all to become liable. The Act makes the incoming partner's liability depend on the agreement alone, and the outgoing partner's continued liability depend on notice. That asymmetry is the single best sentence you can write in a comparative answer.

3. Rights of the incoming partner. On admission he acquires, by force of the Act and not by any special grant: (i) the right to take part in the conduct of the business (s. 12(a)); (ii) the right to be consulted on every matter and the right to veto a change in the nature of the business (s. 12(c)); (iii) the right of access to, and inspection and copying of, the books (s. 12(d)); (iv) the right to share equally in profits and to contribute equally to losses (s. 13(b)) — subject to the deed; (v) the right to interest at 6% on advances beyond agreed capital (s. 13(d)); (vi) the right to be indemnified for payments made and liabilities incurred in the ordinary and proper conduct of the business (s. 13(e)); (vii) the right to render true accounts from his co-partners (s. 9); and (viii) the right to implied authority to bind the firm (s. 19) — so that third parties may deal with him without further enquiry. Contrast: he has no right to the profits of a period before admission unless the deed so provides, and he may, by contract with the firm, share in the existing goodwill and assets — which is why a "premium" is often paid on admission.

4. Duties. s. 9 (greatest common advantage, just and faithful, true accounts); s. 10 (indemnify the firm against loss caused by his fraud); s. 12(b) (diligence); s. 13(b) (contribute equally to losses); s. 13(f) (indemnify for wilful neglect); s. 15 (firm property to be used exclusively for the business); s. 16 (account for personal profits, including profits of a competing business).

5. Liabilities — the three questions examiners ask.

  1. Is he liable for pre-admission debts? No — s. 31(2) says so expressly. But note two qualifications: (a) if he holds himself out as a partner, s. 28(1) makes him liable to anyone who gave credit on the faith of that representation; and (b) if he expressly assumes the old debts by agreement with the creditors, he is liable on that agreement (and under s. 31(2) he may be made liable to his co-partners by the terms of admission).
  2. Is he liable to the creditors of the firm for post-admission debts? Yes — ss. 25 and 27 make every partner jointly and severally liable for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner.
  3. Is he liable to the firm and to his co-partners? Yes, on the terms of the deed and of Chapter III — indemnity under ss. 10 and 13(f), contribution under s. 13(b).

6. Related provisions to cite in-place. s. 30 (minor admitted to benefits — reserved by s. 31); s. 63(1) (notice of a change in constitution to the Registrar — the incoming partner himself may give it); s. 69(2) (the firm must be registered and he must be shown in the Register before he can sue a third party on behalf of the firm); s. 72(a) (public notice rules); s. 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963; s. 2(d) ("third party"). Also IBC 2016 s. 79 and Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 s. 79(2) where the answer concerns the firm's property.

7. Real-life illustration. Mehta, Nair & Oberoi, a firm of three, take in Ms. Rao on 1 April 2025, the deed recording her admission from that date. The firm owes ₹40 lakh to a supplier for goods delivered in January 2025. Rao is not liable for that ₹40 lakh (s. 31(2)). In May 2025 the firm buys ₹12 lakh of steel on credit from the same supplier. Rao is jointly and severally liable for that ₹12 lakh (ss. 25, 27). If the firm's quotation sheets continue to name only the three old partners, Rao is nonetheless a partner as between the partners and as to the world at large — the omission affects only the supplier's ability to prove he gave credit on the faith of her being a partner.

Exam technique. Quote s. 31(1) and s. 31(2) verbatim — the subsection numbers matter and the second limb of s. 31(2) is where most scripts go silent. Then draw the date line: everything before it is not his, everything after it is. Then add the s. 31(2) v. s. 32(3) contrast (incoming needs no notice; outgoing needs public notice). Close with s. 28(1) holding out as the one route by which an incoming partner can be made liable for an old debt.

SET BRetirement of a partner — s. 32Recurs 9×
Question "State the various modes of retirement of a partner from a firm. What are the rights and liabilities of a retiring partner?"

Or, in its problem form (1999 Q.7(a)): "A, B and C are partners of a firm. They borrowed ₹2 lakh from a bank for the business of the firm. Later on A retired from the firm without giving public notice of his retirement. The firm failed to repay the loan. The bank filed a suit against the firm and A. Is A liable? Decide." (Asked as: 2017 OC Q.1(c) and Q.5; 2015 OC Q.1(c); 2013 Q.1(c); 2012 Q.1(c); 2011 Q.1(d); 2000 Q.4(a); 1999 Q.7(a))
Model Answer

1. The provision — s. 32(1). A partner may retire only in three ways: (a) with the consent of all the other partners; (b) in accordance with an express agreement by the partners; or (c) where the partnership is at will, by giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to retire. Note the structure: (a) and (b) apply to every firm; (c) only to a firm at will. A partner in a fixed-term firm who walks out without consent or an enabling clause has not retired — he has repudiated, and ss. 45 and 46 and the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (s. 39) govern what follows.

2. When does the retirement take effect — s. 32(2). In case (a) and (b), from the date of the agreement; in case (c), from the date of the notice. The retirement is complete inter se on that date; notice to the outside world is a different question and is governed by s. 32(3).

3. The three liabilities of a retiring partner — and this is the heart of the answer.

  1. As to acts done BEFORE retirement — absolute liability (s. 32(2), first limb). "Notwithstanding the retirement … he continues to be liable … for the acts of the firm done before his retirement." Public notice is irrelevant to this limb. A retiring partner cannot escape a pre-retirement debt by advertising in the Gazette.
  2. As to acts done AFTER retirement — liability until public notice (s. 32(3), first part). "…he also continues to be liable … for the acts of the firm done until public notice is given of his retirement." The liability here is not because the act was his; it is because the outside world is entitled to assume the firm's constitution is unchanged. Public notice is the cut-off.
  3. As to third parties who did not know — liability without limit of time (s. 32(3), second part). The proviso to s. 32(3) protects a person who dealt with the firm without knowing that he was a partner: as against such a person, the retired partner remains liable notwithstanding the public notice. This is the Scarf v. Jardine (1882) 7 App Cas 345 rule, and it is the reason the answer to a problem question is never complete on "he gave notice" alone.

4. How the liability is discharged — s. 32(2), second half, and s. 32(4). A retiring partner is discharged as to future acts in one of two ways: (i) by an agreement between himself and the continuing partners and the third party — a genuine tripartite novation under s. 62 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which requires the consent of the creditor; or (ii) by the creditor's conduct — where a dealing with the reconstituted firm is inconsistent with his continued liability. Note that s. 32(4) — the deeming of public notice — applies in addition to s. 32(3): notice to a registered firm is to be given under s. 63(1) to the Registrar and published in the Gazette and a vernacular newspaper, and it is deemed complete on the filing of the notice with the Registrar.

5. Rights of a retiring partner. (i) s. 36(1) — to carry on a competing business and to advertise it, subject only to the three prohibitions; (ii) s. 37 — where the surviving partners carry on with the firm's property without any final settlement of accounts, to elect profits or 6% interest; (iii) s. 32(2)/s. 46 — to have his share in the firm's property ascertained and paid; (iv) s. 26 read with s. 32 — to be indemnified by the continuing partners against the firm's debts, subject to contract; (v) s. 55 — on a sale of goodwill after dissolution, to restrain the seller from using the firm name (and see s. 36(1)(a)–(b)); (vi) s. 63(1) — he may himself give notice of the change to the Registrar; (vii) s. 7 Explanation 2 — the firm may take insurance on his life for the benefit of the others; and (viii) he is no longer bound by the implied authority of the continuing partners.

6. Answer to the 1999 problem. A is liable. Step 1: the ₹2 lakh was borrowed before A's retirement, so s. 32(2) makes him liable for the acts of the firm done before his retirement — notwithstanding his retirement, and whether or not public notice was given. Step 2: his liability is joint and several with B and C under s. 25, so the bank may sue A alone. Step 3: the absence of public notice is therefore not the reason he is liable — it would only have been relevant had the borrowing happened after his retirement (s. 32(3)). Step 4: the only way A could have escaped is a novation under s. 32(2)/s. 62 ICA — an agreement between A, B, C and the bank that the bank would look only to the reconstituted firm. None is pleaded. Held: A is liable to the bank. Add for extra marks: after paying, A is entitled to be indemnified by B and C subject to the terms of the retirement deed.

7. Related provisions to cite in-place. ss. 25, 27 (joint and several liability); s. 28(1) (holding out); s. 62 ICA 1872 (novation); ss. 124–125 ICA (contract of guarantee — the retiring partner is in substance a surety); s. 63(1) and s. 72(a) (notice); s. 36 and s. 37 (aftermath); s. 42(c) (dissolution on a partner ceasing to be one, if the deed so provides); s. 14, Limitation Act 1963; O. 30, CPC 1908 (suing a firm); s. 7 Explanation 2 (insurance).

Exam technique. Never answer a retirement problem with one sentence. Build the three-step ladder: (1) was the act before or after the retirement? → s. 32(2) or s. 32(3); (2) if after — was public notice given, and how (s. 72 / s. 63)?; (3) even if public notice was given — did this third party deal with the firm without knowing he was a partner? If yes, Scarf v. Jardine keeps him liable. Only then ask whether there was a novation. Candidates who stop at step (2) lose the problem.

SET CExpulsion of a partner — s. 33Recurs 4×
Question "No partner can be expelled from a firm by a majority of partners except in exercise, in good faith, of powers conferred by contract between the partners." Comment.

Or: "Can a partner be expelled from a firm? If so, on what grounds and subject to what conditions? What is the effect of expulsion?" (Asked as: 2011 Q.1(d); 2004 Q.6(i); 1999 Q.5(b); 1998 Q.8(a))
Model Answer

1. The provision — s. 33(1). "A partner may not be expelled from a firm by any majority of the partners, save in the exercise in good faith of powers conferred by contract between the partners." This is a prohibition with an exception, and the exception is itself hedged. Note also: (i) s. 33 is not subject to contract in its opening words — the prohibition is absolute; what the contract supplies is the power, not a licence to dispense with good faith; and (ii) expulsion is not dissolution — the firm survives and only he goes.

2. The four conditions, all cumulative (s. 33(1)).

  1. The expulsion must be in the exercise of a power conferred by contract between the partners — usually a clause in the partnership deed. Without such a clause, expulsion is void altogether, however overwhelming the majority and however grave the misconduct.
  2. The power must be exercised by the prescribed majority — i.e. in the manner the clause states. A power given to "all the other partners" cannot be exercised by two out of four.
  3. It must be exercised in good faith — this is an independent, judicially enforceable requirement, and it survives even a clause drafted in subjective terms.
  4. Principles of natural justice — though not named in the section, the courts read them into the good-faith requirement: notice of the charge, a reasonable opportunity to answer it, and an honest decision on the material. An expulsion on grounds not stated in the charge, or one decided before the partner was heard, is not in good faith.

3. What "good faith" is not. Good faith is not the same as correctness. The other partners may honestly get it wrong and still act in good faith. But good faith is incompatible with (a) an expulsion designed to appropriate the expelled partner's share cheaply; (b) an expulsion for an ulterior motive — e.g. to prevent him from insisting on accounts; (c) an expulsion without any enquiry; and (d) an expulsion of a partner for conduct the other partners themselves participated in or connived at.

4. The effect of expulsion — s. 33(2). It is identical to retirement for liability purposes, with one reversal of the burden.

  • s. 33(2) first limb: the expelled partner is liable for the acts of the firm done before his expulsion (his liability is not affected by the expulsion).
  • s. 33(2) second limb: he ceases to be liable for the acts of the firm done after his expulsion.
  • s. 33(3): these rules are subject to contract.

Now the contrast that wins marks. A retiring partner needs public notice to stop being liable for future acts (s. 32(3)). An expelled partner does not — s. 33(2) gives him a clean cut without any notice requirement. The explanation is that expulsion is not a voluntary act; he cannot be expected to advertise his own disgrace, and third parties who deal with the firm after his expulsion cannot say they relied on his continued membership when the firm itself put him out. Practical note: a prudent firm will publish notice anyway (s. 72(a) / s. 63(1)) to prevent disputes about whether the expulsion was valid.

5. Remedies of a wrongfully expelled partner. (i) a declaration that the expulsion is void and that he continues to be a partner; (ii) an injunction restraining the other partners from interfering with his right to take part in the business (s. 12(a)) or from holding him out as no longer a partner; (iii) a suit for accounts — and note that s. 44(g) makes "the business of the firm cannot be carried on save at a loss" and the court's view that it is "just and equitable" grounds for dissolution, so where relations have broken down he may seek dissolution instead; (iv) damages; and (v) if the firm is registered, an application under s. 63 read with s. 65 to correct the Register. Note also that a suit for accounts of a dissolved firm is expressly outside the s. 69 bar (s. 69(3)(a)).

6. Related provisions to cite in-place. s. 23 (authority of majority, and its limits under s. 12(c)); s. 9 (good faith); s. 32 (retirement — the contrast); s. 44(g) (just and equitable dissolution); s. 69(3)(a) (accounts of a dissolved firm not barred); s. 63(1) & s. 72(a) (notice); O. 30, CPC 1908; s. 14, Limitation Act, 1963; and Constitutional law — art. 19(1)(c) (freedom of association) and, where a professional body is involved, art. 14. Also NAI v. Union of India, AIR 1990 SC 1028 in the wider discipline-and-natural-justice context.

7. Real-life illustration. Dr. Fernandes, Dr. Ghosh, Dr. Iyer and Dr. Jain run a diagnostic centre. Clause 17 of the deed: "If any partner is guilty of conduct likely to injure the firm's reputation, the other partners may, by unanimous decision, expel him." Dr. Iyer is accused of inflating a tender; Ghosh and Jain — but not Fernandes — hold a meeting he is not invited to and expel him. The expulsion is bad — the power is not unanimous in exercise. Had all three met and heard him, the expulsion would be within the power, and would stand unless he showed want of good faith. If, instead, the other three expel him and then sell the centre at an undervalue to a company they control, the inference of bad faith is almost irresistible.

Exam technique. State s. 33(1) verbatim and split it into its four conditions — power by contract, manner of exercise, good faith, and natural justice. Then write the s. 32(3) v. s. 33(2) contrast in one crisp sentence: retirement needs public notice to cut off future liability; expulsion does not. Finally, add s. 33(3) — all of it is subject to contract — and give the remedies in a numbered list. Candidates who omit the good-faith requirement, or who treat expulsion as a species of retirement, rarely score above 6/10.

Model Answer — Part II: s. 36, the competing business

1. The rule — s. 36(1). "An outgoing partner may carry on a business competing with that of the firm and he may advertise such business, but, subject to contract to the contrary, he may not — (a) use the firm name; (b) represent himself as carrying on the business of the firm; or (c) solicit the custom of persons who were dealing with the firm before he ceased to be a partner."

2. Read the structure carefully — this is where marks are won and lost.

  • The permission has two limbs: he may compete, and he may advertise the competing business. Advertising is expressly allowed — it is not merely conduct that is not prohibited.
  • The three prohibitions are all "subject to contract to the contrary" — so the deed may widen or narrow them, and a clause forbidding solicitation is enforceable through s. 36(2).
  • The three prohibitions are independent and each is a separate wrong. Using the firm name is not the same as representing himself as carrying on the firm's business, and neither is the same as soliciting.
  • The word "solicit" does real work. A general advertisement addressed to the public at large is permitted by the opening limb even if old customers read it and come across. What is forbidden is reaching out to identified former customers of the firm — a letter, a call, a personal visit, a targeted message. English authority in the same vein: Trego v. Hunt [1896] AC 7 and Cruttwell v. Lye (1810) 17 Ves 335 — a retiring partner may not solicit but may deal with customers who come to him unprompted.
  • The time anchor is "before he ceased to be a partner". The prohibition is aimed at the firm's existing customers at the moment of exit. A person who becomes a customer afterwards may be solicited freely.

3. s. 36(2) — the restraint clause, and why it survives s. 27 ICA. "Agreements in restraint of trade — Notwithstanding anything contained in section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, such partners may, upon or in anticipation of (a) a dissolution of the firm, or (b) the retirement of a partner, make an agreement that some or all of them will not carry on a business similar to that of the firm within a specified period or within specified local limits; and notwithstanding anything contained in section 27, such agreement shall be valid if the restrictions imposed are reasonable." Note the points of detail: (i) the clause may be made "upon or in anticipation of" the event — so a restraint agreed before the retirement or dissolution is good; (ii) the restraint may be temporal or territorial or both; (iii) the reasonableness test is the touchstone, and the burden lies on the party relying on the clause; (iv) it is not limited to a retiring partner — partners who continue may bind themselves too; and (v) read it with s. 11(2) (restraint on a partner carrying on any other business while he is a partner) and s. 55(3) (restraint on the seller of goodwill after dissolution). Modern cross-reference: s. 27 of the ICA as read in Niranjan Shankar Golikari v. Century Spinning & Mfg. Co., AIR 1967 SC 1098 and Superintendence Co. of India v. Krishan Murgai, AIR 1980 SC 1717.

4. His other rights after ceasing to be a partner. (i) s. 37 — the profits-or-6%-interest election; (ii) s. 46 — payment of what is due to him out of the firm's property on dissolution; (iii) s. 48 — the mode of settlement of accounts; (iv) s. 26/s. 13(e) — indemnity for what he has paid on the firm's account; (v) s. 38 — his continuing guarantee is revoked as to future transactions by the change in constitution; (vi) s. 55 — on a sale of goodwill he may be restrained from using the firm name; (vii) s. 63(1) — he may himself notify the Registrar; and (viii) s. 72(a) — public notice, where he needs it.

5. Related provisions to cite in-place. s. 27, ICA 1872; s. 11(2); s. 55(3); s. 28(1) (holding out as a partner); ss. 129, 130, 131, 133, 139–141, ICA (continuing guarantees, in the s. 38 context); Trade Marks Act, 1999 — ss. 27 and 135 (passing off and infringement, where the outgoing partner uses the firm name); Specific Relief Act, 1963 — ss. 38 and 41 (injunction to enforce a s. 36(2) restraint); s. 14, Limitation Act, 1963; Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950; Competition Act, 2002 — s. 3(4) (non-compete between enterprises — a different regime entirely, worth one sentence of contrast).

6. Real-life illustration. Dr. Iyer leaves Fernandes, Ghosh, Iyer & Jain Diagnostics on 30 June. On 1 July she opens "Iyer Diagnostics" 400 metres away. She (a) puts a full-page advertisement in the local paper — permitted (s. 36(1) opening limb, read with the express right to advertise); (b) uses the old firm's name in her billboards — prohibited (s. 36(1)(a)); (c) tells new walk-in patients she is "still the same team" — prohibited (s. 36(1)(b)); and (d) writes to the 210 patients who were on the firm's books on 30 June offering them a discount — prohibited (s. 36(1)(c)). If her retirement deed contains a clause that she will not run a diagnostics lab within 5 km for two years, that clause is enforceable under s. 36(2) notwithstanding s. 27 ICA, provided the court finds the period and the radius reasonable.

Exam technique. Always reproduce the opening permission and the three prohibitions as a two-part structure — the permission is not subject to contract, the prohibitions are. Then explain "solicit" with the advertisement-v-targeted-approach contrast. Then add s. 36(2) with the two events, the two kinds of limit and the reasonableness test. And if the question says "retired partner", remember to ask whether the firm still exists — s. 36 applies to an outgoing partner, which includes one whose firm has been dissolved.

SET EInsolvency, death, the estate of a deceased partner, s. 37 · who may be a partner · goodwillRecurs 6×
Question "A partner of a firm dies. His son claims to be admitted as a partner in place of his deceased father. Is he entitled to be so admitted? Is the estate of the deceased partner liable for the acts of the firm done after his death?"

Variants: "What is the effect of the insolvency of a partner on the firm?"; "State the rule as to the liability of the estate of a deceased partner where the business is continued without final settlement of accounts" (s. 37); "Whether a karta of a Joint Hindu Family can become a partner in a firm?" (1995 Q.2(b); 1992 Q.1(b)); "Is goodwill an asset of a partnership firm?" (2015 OC Q.5(a); 2011 Q.5(b)).
Model Answer

Part I — Insolvency: s. 34. "Where a partner in a firm is adjudicated an insolvent, he ceases to be a partner on the date on which the order of adjudication is made, whether or not the firm is thereby dissolved." Three points: (i) the cessation is automatic — no notice, no resolution, no deed; (ii) the date is the date of the adjudication order, not the date of the petition or of the act of insolvency; and (iii) whether the firm also dissolves depends on s. 41(a) and s. 42(c) — s. 41(a) dissolves the firm on the insolvency of any partner if the contract so provides; s. 42(c) dissolves it compulsorily where a partner is adjudicated insolvent subject to contract between the partners. Under s. 34(3), where the firm is not dissolved, the insolvent partner's estate is not liable for acts done after the date of the adjudication order — but only if public notice is given; until then, the estate remains exposed (s. 34(3) read with s. 72). Modern cross-reference: the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 — s. 78 (all debts to which the bankrupt is subject provable), s. 79 (definition of "bankruptcy debt" for an individual partner), and ss. 95–100 (the application for bankruptcy by creditors); the Presidency-Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 continue to apply where the IBC does not, and s. 69(3)(b) expressly preserves the powers of the Official Assignee or Receiver from the s. 69 bar.

Part II — Death: s. 35, and the son's claim. "Where a partner in a firm dies, his estate is not liable for any act of the firm done after his death." Two conditions are built in by the structure of the Act: (i) the firm continues — s. 35 begins with the implicit premise that the surviving partners carry on; and (ii) the survivors actually carry on, whether with the firm's property or with their own. If the firm is dissolved by his death under s. 42(c), then s. 35 has no work to do and ss. 46–48 govern instead.

The son's claim — the answer is No. There is no right of inheritance of partnership. Partnership is a personal contract founded on delectus personae — the choice of the person — and s. 31(1) requires the consent of all the existing partners to any introduction. The son therefore has no entitlement to be admitted; he can be admitted only if all the surviving partners agree. What he does have is a right to the deceased partner's share in the firm's property and profits, to be ascertained under s. 46 and s. 48, and he takes it under the Indian Succession Act, 1925 (for non-Hindus) or the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 — ss. 8 and 30 (for Hindus), by testament or intestacy as the case may be. Note two further points for extra marks: (a) the survivors are bound to account and cannot keep the share without payment; (b) if the son is subsequently admitted and the firm is registered, the change must be notified under s. 63(1).

Part III — s. 37: the profits-or-interest election. "Where any member of a firm has died or otherwise ceased to be a partner, and the surviving or continuing partners carry on the business of the firm with the property of the firm without any final settlement of accounts as between them and the outgoing partner or his estate, then, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, the outgoing partner or his estate is entitled at the option of himself or his representatives to such share of the profits made since he ceased to be a partner as may be attributable to the use of his share of the property of the firm or to interest at the rate of six per cent per annum on the amount of his share in the property of the firm." The proviso: where there is a contract for the option to purchase the outgoing partner's share and it is duly exercised, the outgoing partner is not entitled to any further share; but if the purchaser does not comply with its terms in material respects, he is liable to account under s. 37. The four conditions are cumulative: (1) death or cessation; (2) survivors carry on; (3) with firm property; (4) without a final settlement of accounts. The election belongs to the outgoing partner or his estate — not to the continuing partners. Worked numbers: share in the property ₹30 lakh; profits attributable to the use of that share in the year ₹4.2 lakh; 6% of ₹30 lakh = ₹1.8 lakh. He elects the profits — ₹4.2 lakh. If the business had a bad year and the attributable profit were ₹90,000, he would elect interest — ₹1.8 lakh. That is the point of the election.

Part IV — Can a karta be a partner? Yes — and this is settled law. A Hindu Undivided Family is not a person in the ordinary sense, so the family cannot itself be a partner; but the karta (or other manager) of the family may enter into a valid partnership with strangers, on behalf of and for the benefit of the family. The leading authorities to cite are Rashiklal & Co. v. CIT, AIR 1998 SC 553, Commissioner of Income-tax, Madhya Pradesh v. Sir Hukamchand & Mannalal & Co., 1970 (2) SCC 566, Deccan Wine and General Stores v. CIT, AIR 1977 SC 2213 and N.V. Shanmugam & Co., AIR 1971 Mad 289. The incidents of such a partnership are these: (i) the karta is the partner in the eyes of the Act; (ii) the family members do not become partners merely by reason of their interest in the family; (iii) the karta's liability is personal and unlimited as to third parties, though as against his own family he acts within his powers; (iv) the family property may be reached for the firm's debts only to the extent of the karta's power of alienation for the benefit of the family; (v) for income-tax purposes, s. 184 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 requires the family to be separately represented in the firm; and (vi) a Explanation to s. 4 of the Partnership Act, 1932 deals with the position of persons who have entered into partnership with one another in a carrying-on-a-business-with-a-view-to-profit arrangement. Contrast s. 5 — partnership arises from contract, not from status — which is exactly why a joint family cannot be a partner as such.

Part V — Is goodwill an asset of the firm? Yes. Goodwill is the benefit and advantage of the good name, reputation and connection of a business together with the incidental rights that add value to it — the probability that old customers will resort to the old place. It is property, and on dissolution it falls to be dealt with as part of the firm's assets under s. 46 and s. 48. The authorities to cite are IRC v. Muller & Co.'s Margarine Ltd. [1901] AC 217 (Lord Macnaghten's classic definition), CIT v. B.C. Srinivasa Setty, AIR 1981 SC 972 and S.C. Cambatta & Co. v. CIT, AIR 1961 SC 1016. The Act's own treatment is in s. 55: on the sale of goodwill after dissolution, the seller may carry on a competing business and advertise it, but not use the firm name, represent himself as carrying on the firm's business, or solicit the old customers — subject to an agreement to the contrary which, by s. 55(3), is valid if reasonable notwithstanding s. 27 ICA. On the admission of a new partner, the goodwill of the existing firm is what the premium is paid for. And on the death of a partner, goodwill is part of the assets to be valued under s. 48 unless the deed excludes it — which is why well-drawn deeds say so expressly.

Exam technique. This question is really five questions. Answer them in the order asked and never merge s. 34 with s. 42(c) — the first deals with the partner's exit, the second with the firm's dissolution. In s. 37, reproduce all four conditions and say who has the option. On the son's claim, say "no right of inheritance" and explain it by delectus personae and s. 31(1). On the karta, give the case names — a bare assertion earns half the marks.

SET FIs registration of a firm compulsory? Effects of non-registration — s. 69Recurs 15× · the single most-asked question in this topic
Question "Is the registration of a partnership firm compulsory under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932? What are the disabilities that attach to a firm which is not registered?" (Asked as: 2017 Q.8; 2015 Q.4(b); 2015 OC Q.6; 2014 Q.4; 2014 Q.6(a); 2013 Q.5; 2011 Q.5; 2011 Q.6; 2010 Q.7; 2003 Q.7; 2002 Q.7; 2001 Q.4(d); 2000 Q.6(a); 1998 Q.7(a); 1997 Q.5(a); 1996 Q.7; 1992 Q.5(a))
Model Answer

Part I — Is registration compulsory? No. This is the answer most candidates get wrong, because they reason backwards from the disabilities. Registration is optional — and the Supreme Court has said so on the express authority of the framers' own report:

  • s. 58(1) begins: "The registration of a firm may be effected at any time …" — it is permissive, not mandatory.
  • s. 56 allows the State Government to exempt any State or any part thereof from Chapter VII altogether — which would be meaningless if registration were compulsory.
  • s. 69(4)(a) says the section "shall not apply" to firms which have no place of business in the territories to which this Chapter extends or whose places of business are in areas exempted by notification under s. 56.
  • The Act does not penalise non-registration as such. s. 70 punishes only the furnishing of false or incomplete particulars — not the failure to register. This is the clearest textual proof that registration is voluntary.
  • ¶ 17 of the Report of the Special Committee (1930-31), quoted with approval by the Supreme Court in Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar, (2000) 3 SCC 250 at ¶ 17: "The English precedent insofar as it makes registration compulsory and imposes a penalty for non-registration has not been followed, as it is considered that this step would be too drastic for a beginning in India … Instead, it is proposed that registration should lie entirely within the discretion of the firm or partner concerned; but, following the English precedent, any firm which is not registered will be unable to enforce its claim against third parties in the civil court; and any partner who is not registered will be unable to enforce his claims either against third parties or against fellow partners."

So the accurate formulation is this: registration is not compulsory, the firm may be formed without it, its contracts are valid, and it may sue and be sued generally — but it is disabled from enforcing, in a civil court, rights arising from a contract. Registration is optional in creation but compulsory in enforcement. That single sentence is the whole of Part I.

Part II — How registration is obtained (brief, for completeness). s. 58(1): a statement in the prescribed form, with the prescribed fee, by post or delivery to the Registrar of the area, containing the six particulars (firm name; place or principal place of business; other places of business; the date each partner joined; the names in full and permanent addresses of the partners; the duration of the firm), signed by all the partners or by their agents specially authorised. s. 58(2): each person signing must also verify the statement. s. 58(3): the firm name may not contain "Crown", "Emperor", "Empress", "Empire", "Imperial", "King", "Queen", "Royal" or words importing Crown patronage — subject to the Emblems and Names (Prevention of Improper Use) Act, 1950. s. 59: when the Registrar is satisfied, he shall record an entry in the Register of Firms and file the statement — and registration takes effect on that entry, as CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills Contractor Co., AIR 1971 SC 1015 holds. The Register is then kept current by ss. 60–65, is open to inspection by ss. 66–67, and is conclusive evidence against the signatories by s. 68.

Part III — The disabilities under s. 69. Set them out in four gates.

Gate 1 — the kind of proceeding (s. 69(1) and (2)).

  • s. 69(1) — no suit in any court by or on behalf of any person suing as a partner in a firm against the firm or any person alleged to be or to have been a partner in the firm, to enforce a right arising from a contract or conferred by this Act, unless the firm is registered and he is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner.
  • s. 69(2) — no suit in any court by or on behalf of the firm against any third party to enforce a right arising from a contract, unless the firm is registered and the persons suing are or have been shown in the Register of Firms as partners.

Gate 2 — the kind of right. Only a right arising from a contract (and, under s. 69(1), a right conferred by the Act). Statutory rights and common law/tortious rights are outside the bar — Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar, (2000) 3 SCC 250 (trade-mark infringement is statutory; passing off is a tort) and Raptakos Brett & Co. Ltd. v. Ganesh Property, (1998) 7 SCC 184 (ejectment of a tenant on expiry of the lease is a common law/statutory right under the Transfer of Property Act, not a contractual one). And two further elements, both from Haldiram ¶¶ 22–24: the contract must be one entered into by the plaintiff firm with the defendant, and in the course of the business dealings of the firm with that defendant. A contract pleaded merely as the source of title is a "historical fact" and does not attract the bar.

Gate 3 — which contract. See Gate 2. Add that the contract is not void — Haldiram ¶ 26: "the Act has not prescribed that the transactions or contracts entered into by a firm with a third party are bad in law if the firm is an unregistered firm". Only the remedy is suspended.

Gate 4 — registered and shown. Two cumulative requirements: the firm must be registered, and the person suing must be or have been shown in the Register as a partner. The words "or has been" mean that a person who was once shown but has since ceased to be a partner may still sue — which is how a retired partner's suit for accounts survives.

Part IV — s. 69(3): the extension to set-off and "other proceedings", and its exceptions. s. 69(3): the provisions of sub-ss (1) and (2) shall also apply to a claim of set-off or other proceedings to enforce a right arising from a contract — but not to (a) the enforcement of any right to sue for the dissolution of a firm or for accounts of a dissolved firm, or any right or power to realise the property of a dissolved firm; or (b) the powers of an Official Assignee, Receiver or Court under the Presidency-Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 or the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 to realise the property of an insolvent partner. On the meaning of "other proceeding": Jagdish Chandra Gupta v. Kajaria Traders (India) Ltd., AIR 1964 SC 1882 held that the words receive their full meaning untrammelled by the words "a claim of set-off" — and ejusdem generis does not apply because "a claim of set-off" discloses no genus (Asquith J.'s Allen v. Emersons [(1944) 1 KB 362] examples were adopted). M/s Umesh Goel v. Himachal Pradesh Cooperative Housing Society Ltd., (2016) 11 SCC 313 then held that sub-ss (1) and (2) are bodily lifted into sub-s (3), so the ban requires a suit in a court — and arbitral proceedings under the 1996 Act are outside s. 69, while Kamal Pushp Enterprises v. D.R. Construction Co., (2000) 6 SCC 659 held the same for post-award proceedings.

Part V — s. 69(4): the four escape hatches. (a) firms with no place of business in the territories to which Chapter VII extends, or whose places of business are in areas exempted under s. 56; and (b) any suit or claim of set-off not exceeding ₹100 in value which is not of a kind specified in s. 19 of the Presidency Small Cause Courts Act, 1882 or in the Second Schedule to the Provincial Small Cause Courts Act, 1887.

Part VI — The other disabilities, outside s. 69. A complete answer lists these too:

  1. s. 68(1) — the conclusive-proof rule operates only against signatories; an unregistered firm loses the evidentiary advantage of the Register altogether.
  2. O. 30 r. 1 and r. 2, CPC 1908 — a firm may sue in its firm name, and a defendant-firm may be sued in the firm name; but the procedural convenience is coupled with the s. 69 bar on the plaintiff's side.
  3. s. 44 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 is not attracted, but s. 76 of the Evidence Act / s. 76 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 governs how the Register is proved — by certified copy under s. 67.
  4. Income-tax Act, 1961 — s. 184: registration of the firm for tax purposes is a separate requirement; and the Income-tax Rules, 1962 build on the Partnership Act registration — the very point on which Jayalakshmi turned under s. 26-A of the 1922 Act.
  5. s. 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 and the withdrawal-with-leave route (Haldiram ¶ 26; Surajmal Dagduramji Shop) — this is the remedy for the disability.
  6. Trade Marks Act, 1999 — ss. 27, 135: unaffected by s. 69 (Haldiram) — a statutory right.

Part VII — The remedy, and the practical advice. Because the contract is valid and only the remedy is barred: (i) the plaintiff may withdraw the suit with leave under O. 23 r. 1, CPC, register the firm, and file a fresh suit; (ii) s. 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 excludes the time spent, because the defect of non-registration falls within "other cause of like nature" (Haldiram ¶ 26); (iii) alternatively, the court may stay rather than dismiss — the standard note to Jagdish Chander Gupta; and (iv) the defendant may rely on s. 69 as a preliminary objection under O. 7 r. 11, CPC, which is exactly what the Haldiram defendants did — unsuccessfully.

Exam technique — the seven-line answer that gets full marks. (1) Registration is not compulsory (s. 58(1) "may"; s. 56; s. 70 penalises only false statements; Special Committee ¶ 17 quoted in Haldiram). (2) But an unregistered firm is disabled from enforcing contractual rights in a civil court (s. 69(1), (2)). (3) The bar extends to set-off and other proceedings (s. 69(3)). (4) Exceptions: dissolution, accounts of a dissolved firm, realisation of its property; insolvency powers; exempted areas/foreign firms; suits up to ₹100. (5) Statutory and tortious rights are outside the bar (Haldiram, Raptakos Brett). (6) The contract remains valid. (7) The remedy is withdrawal with leave + registration + a fresh suit, with s. 14 Limitation Act. Then add the cases: Jayalakshmi (s. 59), Jagdish Chander Gupta (s. 69(3)), Haldiram (s. 69(2)), Umesh Goel (arbitration).

SET GSuit by an unregistered firm, registered during the pendency of the suitRecurs 5× · the most-likely problem question
Question "A suit was filed on 20-04-2004 by a firm which was not registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. The firm got itself registered on 22-05-2004. The suit came up for hearing on 13-06-2004. Is the suit maintainable? Give reasons." (2004 Q.6(ii))

Variants: "A firm entered into a contract on 10.10.1944 and filed a suit on the contract. It was not registered on the date of the suit but was registered on 16.03.1945. Decide." (2000 Q.6(b)); "An unregistered firm files a suit for ₹10,000. During the pendency of the suit the firm is registered. Can the suit be decreed?" (1992 Q.5(a)); and the recurring 2011/2012 form. (Asked as: 2012; 2011; 2004 Q.6(ii); 2000 Q.6(b); 1992 Q.5(a))
Model Answer

The short answer: the suit is not maintainable as framed. Registration subsequent to the institution of the suit does not cure the initial defect. But the plaintiff is not without a remedy: the proper order is to stay the suit, or to permit withdrawal with leave to file a fresh suit, and s. 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963 will exclude the time taken.

Step 1 — the text of s. 69(2). "No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of a firm against any third party unless the firm is registered and the persons suing are or have been shown in the Register of Firms as partners in the firm." The word that decides the case is "instituted". The requirement is antecedent to the institution: it must be satisfied on the day the plaint is presented, not on the day it is heard.

Step 2 — the authority. CIT v. Jayalakshmi Rice and Oil Mills Contractor Co., AIR 1971 SC 1015 (A.N. Grover J.): "Even under Section 69 of the Partnership Act which deals with the effect of non-registration it has been consistently held that the registration of a firm subsequent to the filing of the suit did not cure the defect. Thus under the Partnership Law it can be taken to have been settled by decisions of High Courts from a long time that the registration of a firm takes place only when the necessary entry is made in the register of firms under Section 59 of the Partnership Act by the Registrar." Note the two holdings in that one passage, and do not confuse them: (a) registration takes effect on the s. 59 entry, not on the s. 58 application; and (b) registration after the filing of the suit does not cure the defect. Supporting authority: Ram Prasad v. Kamla Prasad, AIR 1935 All 898 (registration takes place only when the entry is made); Kerala Road Lines Corporation v. CIT, 51 ITR 711 (reading ss. 58 and 59 together, a firm cannot be said to be registered when the statement and fee are sent). On the converse — that registration before institution is enough: Firm Ashwin Works v. Girdharlal M. Shah, AIR 1965 Guj 156 and M/s Shreeram Finance Corporation v. Yasin Khan, AIR 1989 SC 1769 are the cases usually cited for the proposition that what matters is registration before the institution of the suit.

Step 3 — apply it to the 2004 problem.

  • Suit instituted 20-04-2004. On that date the firm was not registered. → the bar of s. 69(2) attached the moment the plaint was presented.
  • Firm registered 22-05-2004. → irrelevant to the maintainability of this suit. "Subsequent registration does not cure the defect."
  • Hearing on 13-06-2004. → nothing that happens at the hearing can validate a suit that was not maintainable when filed.
  • Conclusion: the suit is not maintainable and must fail as framed.

The same reasoning disposes of 2000 Q.6(b) (suit on a contract of 1944; registration on 16.03.1945 — if the suit was filed before that date it fails) and of 1992 Q.5(a) (₹10,000 suit — note that the ₹100 threshold in s. 69(4)(b) is not remotely met, so no escape hatch applies).

Step 4 — what the court should do, and what the plaintiff should do. Three courses are recorded in the authorities:

  1. Stay, rather than dismiss. This is the standard note attached to Jagdish Chander Gupta. Because the defect is curable, the court stays the proceeding to allow the plaintiff to register and then proceed. A stay preserves the plaintiff's position without overriding the statute.
  2. Withdrawal with leave and a fresh suit. Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar, (2000) 3 SCC 250 at ¶ 26: "it will be open to the plaintiff to seek withdrawal of the plaint with leave and file a fresh suit after registration of the firm subject of course to the law of limitation and subject to the provisions of the Limitation Act. This is so even if the suit is dismissed for a formal defect. Section 14 of the Limitation Act will be available inasmuch as the suit has failed because the defect of non-registration falls within the words 'other cause of like nature' in Section 14 of the Limitation Act, 1963. (See Surajmal Dagduramji Shop v. Shrikisan Ramkisan.)"
  3. Rejection of the plaint under O. 7 r. 11, CPC. Where the bar is apparent on the face of the plaint, the defendant may apply for rejection — that is what happened in Haldiram itself. But because the plaint there disclosed a statutory and tortious claim, the application failed.

Step 5 — the three defences a plaintiff can still run (and the order to test them in). Before conceding, always check whether the suit is in truth outside s. 69 altogether:

  1. Is the right statutory or tortious rather than contractual? If the plaint is founded on trade-mark infringement, passing off, or ejectment on expiry of a lease, s. 69(2) does not apply at all (Haldiram; Raptakos Brett).
  2. Is the contract one with the defendant in the course of business? If the contract is pleaded merely as the source of title, it is a "historical fact" (Haldiram ¶ 25 — the motor-car illustration).
  3. Is the suit for dissolution, or for accounts of a dissolved firm, or to realise the property of a dissolved firm? If so, s. 69(3)(a) expressly exempts it. And is the claim not exceeding ₹100? If so, s. 69(4)(b). And remember the converse: a suit by one partner against the firm is caught by s. 69(1), not s. 69(2).

Step 6 — related provisions to cite in-place. s. 58(1) ("may be effected at any time" — so registration during the pendency is lawful; it simply does not retroact); s. 59 (the entry is the registration); s. 63(1) and s. 72(a) (notice); s. 68 (evidence); O. 7 r. 11, CPC (rejection of plaint); O. 23 r. 1, CPC (withdrawal with leave); O. 30 r. 1–2, CPC (suit by or against firms); s. 14, Limitation Act, 1963; s. 9, CPC (jurisdiction of civil courts); s. 151, CPC (inherent powers, for a stay); s. 2(d), Partnership Act ("third party"). And note the Uniform Partnership Acts in the United States, which contain a comparable provision, and the English Registration of Business Names Act, 1916 s. 8 and Business Names Act, 1985 s. 4, on which s. 69 was modelled (Haldiram ¶¶ 19–20).

Exam technique. This is a date question, so put the three dates in a column first and see which is which. Then write the one sentence that decides it: "s. 69 speaks of institution, and registration subsequent to the filing of the suit does not cure the defect — Jayalakshmi." Then, and only then, give the stay / withdrawal-with-leave / s. 14 Limitation Act sequence, and close with the three defences. Candidates who stop at "the suit fails" lose the marks for the remedy; candidates who start with the remedy lose the marks for the principle.

SET HCan a partner compel registration? · Dissolution of an unregistered firm · arbitration and s. 69Recurs 8×
Question (i) "A and B are partners of an unregistered firm. A wants the firm to be registered. B refuses. Can A file a suit for a direction to B to join him in getting the firm registered?" (2003 Q.7(b); 1997 Q.5(b); 1995; 1993 Q.5(a))

(ii) "Can a suit for dissolution of an unregistered partnership firm be maintained?" (2003 Q.6(b); 2013 Q.3(b))

(iii) "If the partnership agreement inter alia includes reference of future disputes to arbitration, can the court entertain a petition by a partner of that firm to appoint an arbitrator if the firm is unregistered?" (2010 Q.7)
Model Answer — (i) Suit to compel registration: NOT maintainable

The answer is No, and the reasoning has five steps.

1. Registration is a statutory act performed by the partners, not a right enforceable inter se. s. 58(1) requires the statement to be "signed by all the partners or by their agents specially authorised in that behalf". The Act provides no machinery by which one partner can compel another to sign, and no power in the court to dispense with the signature of a partner who refuses. Where a statute creates a right and prescribes a mode of exercising it, that mode must be followed.

2. s. 69(1) itself bars the suit. The proposed suit would be a suit by a person suing as a partner against the firm or against a person alleged to be a partner, to enforce a right conferred by this Act — namely the right to have the firm registered under s. 58. That is squarely within the words of s. 69(1), which bars suits founded both on contract and on any right conferred by this Act, unless the firm is registered and the plaintiff is shown in the Register. An unregistered firm cannot be the vehicle of a suit whose very purpose is to procure its registration. The provision would be circular if it were otherwise.

3. The specific relief analysis. A mandatory injunction to compel a partner to sign an application is not available: (a) it is not an "obligation arising from contract" in the sense of s. 14 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 unless the partnership deed itself contains an express covenant to register — and even then the relief is refused because the statutory scheme supplies a different, exclusive consequence for non-registration, namely the s. 69 disability; (b) s. 41(e) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 bars an injunction the granting of which would be inconsistent with or would defeat the provisions of any law — and the provision it would defeat is s. 69; (c) the supervision of an application under s. 58, with its verification under s. 58(2), is not a matter the court can execute (s. 14(1)(d), Specific Relief Act — a contract the performance of which involves the performance of a continuous duty which the court cannot supervise).

4. The scheme of Chapter VII shows the remedy is elsewhere. The Act's only sanction for non-registration is the disability under s. 69; the only punishment is s. 70, for false particulars; and the only cure is voluntary registration "at any time" under s. 58(1). A court-ordered registration would invert that scheme: s. 58(1) says registration may be effected by the partners, not shall be procured by a decree.

5. What A should do instead. (i) If the deed contains an express covenant to register, A may sue for damages for its breach — damages, not specific performance; (ii) A may dissolve the firm — and note that s. 69(3)(a) expressly excludes a suit for dissolution from the bar, so this route is open even though the firm is unregistered; (iii) A may retire under s. 32 and, if the firm is at will, do so on notice in writing; (iv) A may give public notice under s. 72 to protect himself from future liability; (v) A may sue for accounts of a dissolved firm — also excluded by s. 69(3)(a); and (vi) if the partnership is at will, A may dissolve it by notice under s. 43(1). Practical advice: the real answer is commercial, not litigious — a partner who will not sign the s. 58 statement is a partner with whom the business cannot safely be continued.

Model Answer — (ii) Dissolution of an unregistered firm: MAINTAINABLE

Yes. The authority is the plain text of s. 69(3)(a): the ban in s. 69 "shall not apply to the enforcement of any right to sue for the dissolution of a firm or for accounts of a dissolved firm, or any right or power to realise the property of a dissolved firm."

Why the legislature carved this out. The object of s. 69 is to induce registration by withholding the aid of the civil court from those who enforce contractual rights while unregistered. It is not to trap partners inside an unregistered firm for ever. A firm that cannot be dissolved would be a firm whose members' capital is locked up with no lawful exit, and whose creditors could never be satisfied out of its assets — a result the Act plainly does not intend. The exception in sub-s (3)(a) is therefore remedial, and is construed generously.

Four points to add for full marks. (1) The exception covers three distinct matters: dissolution; accounts of a dissolved firm; and realisation of the property of a dissolved firm. (2) It is not limited to unregistered firms — the words are general, and a registered firm may of course sue for dissolution too. (3) s. 44(g) supplies the grounds when the court is asked to dissolve — "the business of the firm cannot be carried on save at a loss" or any other ground which renders it just and equitable that the firm should be dissolved; and s. 43 supplies dissolution by notice where the partnership is at will. (4) A partner suing for dissolution is not caught by s. 69(1) either, because the right to dissolve is conferred by the Act but is expressly excepted by s. 69(3)(a) — the specific exception prevails over the general words of sub-s (1).

Related provisions. ss. 39–44 (dissolution); ss. 46–48 (consequences of dissolution, settlement of accounts, return of premium); s. 55 (sale of goodwill); s. 63(1) (notice of dissolution to the Registrar); s. 72 (public notice); s. 69(3)(b) (Official Assignee / Receiver); O. 30 r. 1–2, CPC; s. 14, Limitation Act, 1963; and — where the firm is a going concern in substance — the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 as an alternative route.

Model Answer — (iii) Arbitration and s. 69

Answer it in two regimes, because the statute changed.

Under the Arbitration Act, 1940 — NO, the court cannot entertain the petition. Jagdish Chandra Gupta v. Kajaria Traders (India) Ltd., AIR 1964 SC 1882 (M. Hidayatullah J.). A petition under s. 8(2) of the 1940 Act is an "other proceeding" within s. 69(3): (i) the proceeding "has its genesis in the arbitration clause, because without an agreement to refer the matter to arbitration that section cannot possibly be invoked"; (ii) "whether we view the contract between the parties as a whole or view only the clause about arbitration, it is impossible to think that the right to proceed to arbitration is not one of the rights which are founded on the agreement of the parties"; (iii) the words "other proceeding" receive their full meaning "untrammelled by the words 'a claim of set-off'"; and (iv) ejusdem generis has no application, because "a claim of set-off" discloses no genus — the Court adopted Asquith J.'s examples in Allen v. Emersons [(1944) 1 KB 362] and noted that counsel, invited to produce a counterexample, "admitted frankly that it was impossible for him to think of any proceeding of the nature of a claim of set-off other than a claim of set-off". The Court also drew support from s. 69(4)(b), whose words "any proceeding in execution or other proceeding incidental to or arising from any such suit or claim" "clearly show that the word 'proceeding' is not limited to a proceeding in the nature of a suit or a claim of set-off".

Under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — YES, arbitration is not barred. M/s Umesh Goel v. Himachal Pradesh Cooperative Housing Society Ltd., (2016) 11 SCC 313. Sub-sections (1) and (2) of s. 69 are "bodily lifted and incorporated" into sub-section (3); therefore "the condition precedent for the operation of ban under sub-section (3) is that the launching of a suit in a Court of law should be present", and a claim of set-off or "other proceeding" is caught only where it is "intrinsically connected with the suit". An arbitrator is not a court: he may exercise judicial powers but "he does not derive such powers from the State but by the agreement of the parties under a contract". And the 1996 Act confines the court's role to ss. 8, 9, 14, 27, 34, 36, 37, 39, 42, 43, 47–50, 56, 58, 59 — unlike the 1940 Act, which "effectively provides for intervention at every stage of the Arbitral proceedings … as if it were a regular civil suit". Hence "Arbitral Proceedings will not come under the expression other proceedings of Section 69(3)". Kamal Pushp Enterprises v. D.R. Construction Co., (2000) 6 SCC 659 adds the post-award stage: at enforcement "what is enforced is the award itself which crystallise the rights of parties under the Indian Contract Act and the general law … and not any right arising only from the objectionable contract". And s. 36 of the 1996 Act creates only a limited fiction — Paramjeet Singh Patheja v. ICDS Ltd., (2006) 13 SCC 322 ¶ 42: "The fiction is not intended to make it a decree for all purposes under all statutes, whether State or Central."

Three additions that earn extra marks. (1) The 1996 Act is the operative statute today, so the practical answer to the question is yes, the arbitrator may be appointed and the award will stand. (2) Even so, if the award is challenged under s. 34 or enforced under s. 36, the Civil Court is not converted into a suit — the fiction is limited to execution. (3) If the party goes to court under s. 9 for interim measures, or under s. 11 for appointment, the court is acting under the 1996 Act and not as a court of first instance in a suit — the reasoning of Umesh Goel applies.

Exam technique. Part (iii) is a two-regime question. Never write only Jagdish Chander Gupta, and never say it has been overruled — say "distinguished, and confined to proceedings under the 1940 Act initiated before a civil court". Then give the Umesh Goel ratio in its own words — "bodily lifted", "condition precedent", "intrinsically connected" — and close with Kamal Pushp and Paramjeet Singh Patheja on the scope of the s. 36 fiction.

SET IHolding out by a retired partner — s. 28, s. 32(3), s. 72Recurs 6× · the classic problem question
Question "A and B were partners in a firm. A retired from the firm. B continued the business under the same name and the letterhead of the firm continued to carry the names of A and B. C, who had been dealing with the firm even before A's retirement, supplied goods to the firm after A's retirement on credit. The firm failed to pay. C sued A and B. Is A liable? Discuss." (Asked, with the same letterhead fact-pattern, as: 2015 Q.3(b); 2012 Q.4(b); 1999 Q.7(b); 1996 Q.4; 1995 Q.5; 1994 Q.5)
Model Answer

The short answer: A is liable — and on three independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient. That is the structure to use, because a candidate who gives only one ground scores about half.

Ground 1 — s. 32(3), first part: no public notice. A retiring partner "also continues to be liable … for the acts of the firm done until public notice is given of his retirement". On the facts, the retirement was never published at all — on the contrary, the letterhead continued to carry A's name. The goods were supplied after the retirement, so this is precisely the situation s. 32(3) governs. Public notice is defined by s. 72: for a registered firm, notice to the Registrar under s. 63(1) plus publication in the Official Gazette plus at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district (three cumulative requirements); in any other case, Gazette plus vernacular newspaper. Nothing of the sort happened here.

Ground 2 — s. 32(3), proviso: the person who dealt without knowing. Even if public notice had been given, the proviso keeps A liable to any person who dealt with the firm without knowing that he was a partner. C had been dealing with the firm even before A's retirement — so C knew A was a partner, and the question becomes whether C knew of the retirement. On the facts, the letterhead positively represented that A was still a partner, so C plainly did not know. This is Scarf v. Jardine (1882) 7 App Cas 345 (House of Lords): a retiring partner was held liable to a customer of long standing who had not been told of the retirement, even though notice had been published in the Gazette. Lord Blackburn drew the two-tier distinction that decides this problem: as regards those who had dealt with the firm before the change, notice in the Gazette is not enough — actual notice is required; as regards those who had not previously dealt with the firm, notice in the Gazette (or a circular) is sufficient. C is an old dealer. Only actual notice would have released A, and the letterhead did the opposite.

Ground 3 — s. 28(1): holding out. "Any one who by words spoken or written or by conduct represents himself, or who knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner in a firm, is liable as a partner in that firm to any one who has on the faith of any such representation given credit to the firm, whether the person representing himself or represented to be a partner does or does not know that the representation has reached the person so giving credit." Apply it element by element:

  • A representation. The letterhead carrying the names of A and B is a written representation — and it is the classic illustration used in the textbooks and in the Delhi University papers year after year.
  • By whom. B made it; but s. 28(1) catches A if he "knowingly permits himself to be represented". A retired partner who allows his name to remain on the firm's letterhead, order forms, invoices, signboard, website or bank mandate permits the representation. Silence with knowledge is enough.
  • Giving of credit on the faith of it. C supplied goods on credit after the retirement. The representation need not be the sole inducement — it is enough that C gave credit on the faith of it.
  • Deemed communication. The closing words of s. 28(1) — "whether … he does or does not know that the representation has reached the person so giving credit" — remove any argument that A did not know C had seen the letterhead.
  • No partnership by estoppel — a point often worth one line: s. 28(2) and the authorities make clear that holding out creates liability, not partnership. A is liable as a partner to C, but he does not thereby become a partner, and he acquires none of the rights of one.

Supporting authority: Snow White Food Products Co. Ltd. v. Sohan Lal Bagai, AIR 1964 Punj 355 (FB) — a Full Bench decision on the credibility and proof of partnership and holding-out, and on the use of the Register of Firms and certified copies under s. 68 in such disputes. Tower Cabinet Co. Ltd. v. Ingram [1949] 2 KB 397 — a retiring partner whose name remained on the firm's notepaper and who did not inform a supplier with whom he had personally dealt was held liable. Rhodes v. Moules [1895] 1 Ch 236 — the representation may be by conduct as well as by words.

Conclusion. A is liable to C for the price of the goods. His liability is joint and several with B (s. 25), so C may sue A alone. After payment, A is entitled to be indemnified by B — the debt is in truth the firm's, and B has continued the business and taken its benefit. If B put A's name on the letterhead without A's knowledge, A may in addition claim against B; but as against C the knowledge or consent of A is not required once he knowingly permitted the representation.

Four defences A might raise — and why three of them fail.

  1. "I gave notice to B." — Ineffective. Notice to a co-partner is notice inter se only; s. 32(3) requires public notice.
  2. "The partnership deed was varied and the deed of retirement executed." — Ineffective against C. A private document binds the partners; it does not bind a third party who has no notice of it.
  3. "The firm was at will and I retired by written notice." — Correct as to the retirement itself (s. 32(1)(c)), but it says nothing about liability for future acts, which is governed by s. 32(3) and s. 28(1).
  4. "C did not actually rely on the letterhead — he supplied goods because of our long relationship." — This one may succeed in fact. s. 28(1) requires credit to have been given "on the faith of" the representation. If C knew perfectly well that A had retired and dealt with B alone, the causal link is broken. But on the facts as stated — the letterhead still carrying A's name — the burden is on A, and it is a heavy one.

Related provisions to cite in-place. s. 28(1) and (2) (holding out); s. 32(1)–(4) (retirement); s. 72(a) and (b) (public notice); s. 63(1) (notice to the Registrar, deemed complete on filing); s. 25 (joint and several liability); s. 26 (indemnity); s. 18 (a partner is the agent of the firm); s. 19 (implied authority); s. 29 (transfer of interest); s. 2(d) ("third party"); s. 69 (registration — note that C's suit is not barred: the bar in s. 69 affects the unregistered firm as plaintiff, not a third party suing it); O. 30 rr. 1–3, CPC 1908; s. 114, Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (presumption as to the ordinary course of business — a person receiving a letterhead would presume what it states); s. 14, Limitation Act, 1963; and s. 2(1)(e), Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 if the supply contract contains an arbitration clause (Umesh Goel).

Exam technique. Answer in three numbered grounds — s. 32(3) no-notice, s. 32(3) proviso / Scarf v. Jardine, and s. 28(1) holding out — and say explicitly that any one of them is sufficient. Quote the Scarf v. Jardine two-tier rule (Gazette enough for new dealers, actual notice needed for old dealers) — it is the sentence examiners look for. Then deal with the four defences and cite s. 28(2): holding out creates liability, not partnership. Close with the indemnity against B.

Retirement (s. 32) · Expulsion (s. 33) · Insolvency (s. 34) · Death (s. 35) · Dissolution (ss. 39–44) · Transfer (s. 29 — the one that is not an exit).
Say it as: "Really Expensive Ideas Demand Deep Thought."

★ s. 36 — the outgoing partner's "3 NOs"

He MAY compete and MAY advertise. But he may NOT: (a) use the firm Name, (b) represent himself as Now carrying on the firm's business, (c) Notice old customers — i.e. solicit them.
Three Ns: Name · Now · Notice.

★ s. 37 — "P or 6"

Four conditions: Out · Carry on · with Firm property · Without final settlement → the outgoing partner elects a share of Profits or 6% interest.
Mnemonic: "Out, Carry on, Firm property, Without — O.C.F.W."

★ s. 69 — the four gates

Kind of proceeding (suit? set-off? other?) · Kind of right (contract — or statute/tort?) · which Contract (with the defendant, in business?) · Registered and shown.
Mnemonic: "Kind, Kind, Contract, Registered."

★ s. 69 exceptions — "D.A.R.E."

Dissolution / accounts of a dissolved firm / realising its property — s. 69(3)(a) · Assignee, Receiver, Court under the insolvency Acts — s. 69(3)(b) · Regions exempted under s. 56 — s. 69(4)(a) · Exceeds? No — suits not exceeding ₹100 — s. 69(4)(b).

★ s. 72 public notice — "R.G.P."

For a registered firm, three cumulative steps: Registrar (s. 63 notice) + Gazette + one vernacular Paper. In any other case, Gazette + Paper only.
Note: it is deemed complete on filing with the Registrar (s. 32(4)).

★ The four cases — "J.J.H.U."

Jayalakshmi → s. 59 (the entry is the registration) · Jagdish Chander Gupta → s. 69(3) under the 1940 Act (barred) · Haldiram → s. 69(2) (only contracts with the defendant, in business) · Umesh Goel → s. 69(3) under the 1996 Act (not barred).

★ s. 58(1) — the six particulars

Name of the firm · Place or principal place of business · Other places of business · Date each partner joined · Names in full and permanent addresses of the partners · Duration of the firm.
Mnemonic: "Names, Places, Other places, Dates, Names, Duration."

★ Registration — "O.C."

Registration is Optional in Creation but Compulsory in Enforcement. s. 58(1) says "may"; s. 70 punishes only false particulars; the Special Committee expressly rejected compulsion (Haldiram ¶ 17).
One line: "Valid contract, suspended remedy."

AIR 1964 SC 1882 The words "other proceeding" in s. 69(3) receive their full meaning untrammelled by the words "a claim of set-off", which disclose no genus for ejusdem generis. §E.4 — Three answer templates you can write from memory

▤ Template 1 — "Effects of non-registration" (10 marks)
  1. Registration is not compulsory — s. 58(1) "may"; s. 56; s. 70 punishes only false particulars; Special Committee ¶ 17 in Haldiram.
  2. But s. 69(1) and (2) bar suits to enforce contractual rights unless registered and shown.
  3. s. 69(3) extends this to set-off and other proceedings, with two exceptions.
  4. s. 69(4) adds two more escape hatches — exempted areas and suits up to ₹100.
  5. The bar reaches only contractual rights with the defendant in the course of business (Haldiram, Raptakos Brett).
  6. The contract remains valid — only the remedy is suspended.
  7. Remedy: withdraw with leave → register → re-file, with s. 14 Limitation Act.
  8. Cases: Jayalakshmi (s. 59) · Jagdish Chander Gupta (s. 69(3), 1940) · Haldiram (s. 69(2)) · Umesh Goel (arbitration, 1996).
▤ Template 2 — "Is the retired partner liable?" (10 marks)
  1. Has he gone out at all? Which of the six modes — s. 32, 33, 34, 35, ss. 39–44, or s. 29?
  2. Before or after? Acts before → s. 32(2), absolute. Acts after → s. 32(3).
  3. Was public notice given? s. 72 — Registrar + Gazette + vernacular paper.
  4. Even if it was — did this third party deal without knowing? s. 32(3) proviso; Scarf v. Jardine (Gazette enough for new dealers; actual notice needed for old).
  5. Was there a novation? s. 32(2) / s. 62 ICA — the creditor must consent.
  6. Is he held out? s. 28(1) — and s. 28(2): liability, not partnership.
  7. After paying — indemnity against the continuing partners.
  8. Then s. 36 (compete), s. 37 (profits or 6%), s. 38 (guarantee).
▤ Template 3 — "The firm registered after the suit was filed" (10 marks)
  1. Put the dates in a column. Date of contract · date of institution · date of registration · date of hearing.
  2. s. 69 speaks of institution. The requirement is antecedent; it must be satisfied on the day the plaint is presented.
  3. Jayalakshmi: "registration of a firm subsequent to the filing of the suit did not cure the defect"; and registration takes place only on the s. 59 entry.
  4. Therefore the suit is not maintainable as framed.
  5. But three defences first: statutory or tortious right? contract only a source of title? dissolution / accounts / ₹100?
  6. Then the remedy: stay, or withdraw with leave under O. 23 r. 1 CPC, register, and re-file.
  7. s. 14 Limitation Act — "other cause of like nature" — excludes the time (Haldiram ¶ 26; Surajmal Dagduramji Shop).
  8. Order: principle first, remedy second, defences third. Never the other way round.

! The five errors that cost the most marks
  1. Writing that registration is compulsory. It is not. Optional in creation, compulsory in enforcement.
  2. Answering a retirement problem with one sentence. The ladder has four rungs: before/after → public notice → did this party know → novation.
  3. Treating expulsion as a species of retirement. It is not: s. 33(2) needs no notice.
