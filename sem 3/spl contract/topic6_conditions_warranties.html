§1.0 to §1.10 · express stipulations, implied stipulations, and how the parties may exclude them

§1.0 · The two boxes — why this Topic matters

Every contract of sale contains promises. Some are important and some are not. The Act's answer to ‘how important is this promise?’ is a sorting exercise into two boxes, and the consequence of the sorting is everything:

C Box 1 — CONDITION (s. 12(2))

A stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract. Breach gives the buyer a right to treat the contract as repudiated — i.e. reject the goods, refuse to pay, and walk away — and (if he chooses) sue for damages.

W Box 2 — WARRANTY (s. 12(3))

A stipulation collateral to the main purpose. Breach gives damages only — not a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated.

☕ The one example that fixes it forever

You order a filter coffee machine online. It arrives.

  • It is a juicer. The seller has broken a condition (s. 15 — sale by description). You may reject it and get your money back.
  • It is the right machine, but the drip tray is scratched. That is a warranty. You keep the machine and claim the cost of a new tray.

Same contract, same seller, same delivery — completely different remedies. The difference is made by ss. 12, 13, 15 and 16. That is Topic 6.

§1.1 · Section 11 — Stipulations as to time

Section 11 — verbatim
“Stipulations as to time.—Unless a different intention appears from the terms of the contract, stipulations as to time of payment are not deemed to be of the essence of a contract of sale. Whether any other stipulation as to time is of the essence of the contract or not depends on the terms of the contract.”

This is a two-limb section, and the two limbs work in opposite directions. Read them side by side:

LimbTextDefault ruleHow to displace it
Limb 1“stipulations as to time of payment” NOT of the essence Show a different intention from the terms of the contract
Limb 2“any other stipulation as to time” No default at all — it “depends on the terms of the contract” The court simply construes the contract on each occasion

So: when the buyer must pay is presumptively not fatal; when the seller must deliver, or when the goods must be inspected, is a pure question of construction with no presumption either way. That asymmetry is deliberate. A day's delay in payment causes a measurable loss (interest), and the Act has a ready remedy for it; a day's delay in delivery may destroy the entire commercial point of the contract — the uniforms for a wedding, the medicines for an epidemic, the paint for the Army's tents.

☕ Real examples
  • Payment: A traders' association buys stock on 30 days' credit. Payment arrives on day 33. Under s. 11 limb 1, time of payment is not of the essence, so the seller cannot repudiate; he can recover interest (s. 61(1) SOGA read with the Interest Act, 1978) and, if he has suffered a special loss within the second limb of Hadley v. Baxendale, damages under s. 73 ICA.
  • Delivery: A wedding caterer orders 500 gulab jamun boxes for delivery “by 6 p.m. on 14 February”. They arrive at 9 p.m. on 15 February. Delivery dates are ‘other stipulations as to time’; on the terms of this contract the date is plainly of the essence; the buyer may reject.
  • Inspection (the British Paints fact-pattern): Goods must be offered for inspection. The seller offers them on the last day of the delivery period, leaving the buyer no time to test them. The buyer is entitled to say that the tender is bad — this is exactly what the Calcutta High Court held in Case 30.
What makes time ‘of the essence’? The three routes
  1. Express stipulation. The contract says so. In Gomathi Nayagam v. Palaniswami (AIR 1967 SC 868), cited with approval in Case 30, the Supreme Court held that the intention to make time of the essence “may be evidenced by either express stipulations or by circumstances which are sufficiently strong to displace the ordinary presumption”.
  2. Necessarily implied from the nature of the contract. Where the subject-matter is perishable, or prices are volatile, or the goods are needed for a fixed event, time is of the essence though the contract is silent. This is the route taken in mercantile contracts generally.
  3. Notice making time of the essence. Where time was originally not of the essence and the other party is in unreasonable delay, the innocent party may serve a notice fixing a reasonable new date, after which time becomes of the essence. What is reasonable is a question of fact (s. 63).
! The trap: waiver by extension

In Case 30 Mr. Mukherjee argued that because the Union of India had extended the delivery date four times, time had ceased to be of the essence and had become “a warranty and nothing more”, so that the cancellation was itself an anticipatory breach entitling the plaintiff to damages. The Court rejected this. Two reasons, both worth memorising:

  1. An intention to make time of the essence “must be expressed in explicit and unmistakable language in the agreement itself, and if by any means such an intention is not explicit, it may be inferred from the antecedent conduct of the parties and surrounding circumstances but not from the subsequent conduct of the parties after the contract was made.”
  2. Where the seller fails to deliver in time and the buyer grants an extension, the effect of s. 55 of the Indian Contract Act is that “the buyer is entitled to damages computed in the ordinary way, if the seller fails to deliver within the extended time” (Md. Habidullah v. Bird & Co., AIR 1922 PC 178). An extension is a concession, not a variation into a warranty.

§1.2 · Section 12 — Condition and warranty

Section 12 — verbatim
“Condition and warranty.—(1) A stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to goods which are the subject thereof may be a condition or a warranty.
(2) A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to treat the contract as repudiated.
(3) A warranty is a stipulation collateral to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for damages but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated.
(4) Whether a stipulation in a contract of sale is a condition or a warranty depends in each case on the construction of the contract. A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract.”

Four sub-sections; four different jobs. s. 12(1) sets up the binary. s. 12(2) and s. 12(3) define the two boxes — and note that each is defined twice: once by substance (essential / collateral to the main purpose) and once by consequence (right to repudiate / damages only). s. 12(4) then gives the rule of construction and kills the label argument.

★ The one sentence to learn by heart

“A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract.” — s. 12(4). The converse is also true by the same logic: a stipulation labelled ‘condition’ may, on construction, be only a warranty. This single sentence is the answer to three separate DU questions (2013 CT OC Q7, 2011 CT OC Q2(b) and part of 2017 Q4(b)).

The classic judicial definition of ‘condition’
“There are some [obligations] which go so directly to the substance of the contract, or, in other words, are so essential to its very nature, that their non-performance may fairly be considered by the other party as a substantial failure to perform the contract at all.”
— Fletcher Moulton L.J., Wallis v. Pratt [1910] 2 KB 1003, 1012 (Court of Appeal), approved on this point by the House of Lords at [1911] AC 394.WEB

Compare the statutory formula in s. 12(2): “essential to the main purpose of the contract”. The two are the same test. Where they differ in emphasis, the statute governs — remember Cozens-Hardy M.R. in Bristol Tramways v. Fiat Motors [1910] 2 KB 831, 836: “I rather deprecate the citation of earlier decisions… in so far as there is an express statutory enactment, that alone must be looked at and must govern the rights of the parties, even though the section may to some extent have altered the prior common law.”WEB

How a court actually decides — the six signposts
  1. The label is irrelevant (s. 12(4)). Calling something a ‘warranty’ does not make it one.
  2. Does the breach deprive the buyer of substantially the whole benefit he contracted for? If yes, condition.
  3. Would damages be an adequate remedy? If yes, warranty. (This is the functional test, and it is why a scratched drip tray is a warranty.)
  4. Is the stipulation part of the description of the goods? If yes, it is normally a condition (s. 15) — see Wallis v. Pratt, where ‘common English sainfoin’ was held to be a condition.
  5. Is the contract severable? If the contract is divisible into instalments, a condition attaches to each instalment separately (s. 13(2) carves out non-severable contracts).
  6. Trade usage and course of dealing may annex a warranty or condition (s. 16(3); s. 62).

§1.3 · Section 13 — When condition to be treated as warranty

Section 13 — verbatim
“When condition to be treated as warranty.—(1) Where a contract of sale is subject to any condition to be fulfilled by the seller, the buyer may waive the condition or elect to treat the breach of the condition as a breach of warranty and not as a ground for treating the contract as repudiated.
(2) Where a contract of sale is not severable and the buyer has accepted the goods or part thereof, or where the contract is for specific goods the property in which has passed to the buyer, the breach of any condition to be fulfilled by the seller can only be treated as a breach of warranty and not as a ground for rejecting the goods and treating the contract as repudiated, unless there is a term of the contract, express or implied, to that effect.
(3) Nothing in this section shall affect the case of any condition or warranty fulfilment of which is excused by law by reason of impossibility or otherwise.”

The struck-out words are not a typo. By s. 3 of Act 33 of 1963, with effect from 22 September 1963, Parliament deleted the phrase “or where the contract is for specific goods the property in which has passed to the buyer,”. Before 1963, the mere fact that property in specific goods had passed to the buyer barred him from rejecting. After 1963 it does not: property passing and acceptance are now separate questions. Mentioning this amendment in an answer is an easy way to show you have read the statute and not a guide.

The four routes by which a condition becomes a warranty
1 Waiver — s. 13(1)

The buyer, with knowledge of the breach, intentionally abandons the right to treat the contract as repudiated. He may do so expressly, or by conduct — e.g. by asking for a repair instead of a refund.

2 Election — s. 13(1)

The buyer chooses to treat the breach of condition as a breach of warranty. He keeps the goods and claims the difference in value (s. 59(1)(b)).

3 Acceptance — s. 13(2)

In a non-severable contract, once the buyer has accepted the goods or part of them, the condition can only be treated as a warranty. Acceptance is defined in s. 42.

4 Impossibility — s. 13(3)

Where performance of the condition is excused by law (frustration, supervening impossibility), s. 13 does not apply at all. See ICA ss. 32 and 56.

What ‘acceptance’ means — s. 42, the gate to s. 13(2)
Section 42 — verbatim (the definition s. 13(2) depends on)
“Acceptance.—The buyer is deemed to have accepted the goods when he (a) intimates to the seller that he has accepted them, or (b) when the goods have been delivered to him and he does any act in relation to them which is inconsistent with the ownership of the seller, or (c) when, after the lapse of a reasonable time, he retains the goods without intimating to the seller that he has rejected them.”
☕ Acceptance in three real situations
  • You buy a laptop, use it for three weeks and then notice the screen is not the contracted 4K panel. Under s. 42(c) you have retained it beyond a reasonable time without intimating rejection → you have accepted → s. 13(2) reduces the condition to a warranty → damages only.
  • You buy curtains, take them to a tailor and have them cut and stitched. Cutting is an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership → s. 42(b) → acceptance → warranty only.
  • You buy 1,000 tiles deliverable in ten instalments of 100, each separately paid for. The first two instalments are the wrong shade. Because the contract is severable (s. 38(2)), s. 13(2) does not apply automatically; you may still reject those instalments, and whether you may repudiate the whole contract depends on the ratio of defective to sound instalments and the likelihood that future ones will also be bad (s. 38(2)).
! The sentence DU asks about — ‘once a condition, always a condition’

Two DU questions turn on this (2017 Q4(b); 2011 CT OC Q3). The answer is no, the statement is not correct as a matter of remedy, and yes as a matter of classification. Get the distinction right:

  • Classification is fixed. A stipulation that is a condition is a condition. s. 13 does not ‘convert’ it into something else.
  • The remedy is what changes. Once one of the four routes operates, the buyer ‘can only treat’ it as a breach of warranty — he loses the right to reject but keeps the right to damages.
“That does not mean that it was really a breach of warranty or that what was a condition in reality had come to be degraded or converted into a warranty. It does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio, but the injured party may treat it as if it had become so, and he becomes entitled to the remedies which attach to a breach of warranty.”
— Lord Loreburn L.C., Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt & Haynes [1911] AC 394, 396.WEB

Lord Alverstone C.J. put the same point from the draftsperson's side: the exclusion clause said the sellers gave ‘no warranty’. The House held that a clause negativing warranties does not negative conditions. “It is quite impossible to suggest that… when they used the word ‘warranty’ they intended that they were not making themselves liable in regard to any condition as to the goods.” And the reason the point mattered: once the buyers had accepted the seed, s. 11(1)(c) (the English equivalent of s. 13(2)) meant the breach of condition could only be treated as a breach of warranty — so the sellers argued there was no warranty left to sue on. The House rejected that: the term remained a condition; only the remedy had been confined. That is precisely the ratio you must state for 2015 Q6(a).

§1.4 · Section 14 — Implied undertaking as to title, etc.

Section 14 — verbatim
“Implied undertaking as to title, etc.—In a contract of sale, unless the circumstances of the contract are such as to show a different intention, there is—
(a) an implied condition on the part of the seller that, in the case of a sale, he has a right to sell the goods and that, in the case of an agreement to sell, he will have a right to sell the goods at the time when the property is to pass;
(b) an implied warranty that the buyer shall have and enjoy quiet possession of the goods;
(c) an implied warranty that the goods shall be free from any charge or encumbrance in favour of any third party not declared or known to the buyer before or at the time when the contract is made.”

Three implied terms, in a particular order, and with a particular distribution of remedies. Only (a) is a condition; (b) and (c) are warranties. That distribution is deliberate and is examined constantly.

★ Why (a) is a condition and (b) is only a warranty

Because if the seller has no title, the buyer has received nothing that he bargained for — his whole purpose fails, and he must be able to give the goods back and reclaim the full price. But if the buyer has title and is merely later disturbed, or the goods turn out to carry an undisclosed charge, the loss is quantifiable in money and damages are adequate. Niblett is the case that shows both limbs operating on the same facts.

The three elements of a breach of s. 14(a)
  1. The seller purported to transfer the general property in the goods (s. 2(11)).
  2. At the relevant moment he had no right to sell them — because he was not the owner, or because his own title was defeasible, or because a third party's right (trade mark, charge, lien) prevented a lawful sale.
  3. The buyer suffered loss as a result — in Niblett, by being forced to strip the labels and sell at a reduced price.
☕ Three real examples
  • Stolen phone. You buy a second-hand phone from a shop; the police trace it to a theft and seize it. s. 14(a) is broken: the seller never had a right to sell. Because it is a condition, you recover the whole price, not merely the phone's depreciated value (Rowland v. Divall, (1923) 2 KB 500 — the buyer recovered the full price even after months of use, because he had never received title at all).
  • Financed car. You buy a car that is still hypothecated to a bank. Title may pass, but the bank's charge is an undisclosed encumbrance → warranty (c) → damages equal to the amount needed to discharge the charge.
  • Infringing labels. 1,000 cases of condensed milk branded ‘Nissly’ infringe Nestlé's registered trade mark; Customs detain them. The seller had no right to sell goods that could not lawfully be sold → condition (a); and the buyer could not enjoy quiet possession → warranty (b). That is Niblett, in full.

§1.5 · Section 15 — Sale by description

Section 15 — verbatim
“Sale by description.—Where there is a contract for the sale of goods by description, there is an implied condition that the goods shall correspond with the description; and, if the sale is by sample as well as by description, it is not sufficient that the bulk of the goods corresponds with the sample if the goods do not also correspond with the description.”

Eleven lines of statute, and one of the widest implied conditions in the whole Act. Three points make it wide:

  1. ‘By description’ is not confined to goods the buyer has never seen. Lord Wright said so in Case 32: “there is a sale by description even though the buyer is buying something displayed before him on the counter: a thing is sold by description, though it is specific, so long as it is sold not merely as the specific thing but as a thing corresponding to a description, e.g., woollen undergarments, a hot water bottle, a second-hand reaping machine, to select a few obvious illustrations.”DU p. 222
  2. It is a condition, not a warranty. So breach gives the right to reject — subject to s. 13.
  3. It survives a sample match. The second limb exists precisely to stop a seller from saying ‘but the bulk is identical to the sample’. Both must be satisfied.
★ The two-limb test on any s. 15 fact pattern

Ask (i) what description did the buyer contract for, then (ii) do the goods answer it? If the sale is also by sample, ask two more: (iii) does the bulk match the sample (s. 17(2)(a)), and (iv) is there a latent defect in the bulk that reasonable examination of the sample would not have revealed (s. 17(2)(c))? A ‘yes’ to (iii) does not cure a ‘no’ to (ii).

§1.6 · Section 16 — Implied conditions as to quality or fitness

Section 16 — verbatim
“Implied conditions as to quality or fitness.—Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, there is no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale, except as follows:—
(1) Where the buyer, expressly or by implication, makes known to the seller the particular purpose for which the goods are required, so as to show that the buyer relies on the seller's skill or judgment, and the goods are of a description which it is in the course of the seller's business to supply (whether he is the manufacturer or producer or not), there is an implied condition that the goods shall be reasonably fit for such purpose:
Provided that, in the case of a contract for the sale of a specified article under its patent or other trade name, there is no implied condition as to its fitness for any particular purpose.
(2) Where goods are bought by description from a seller who deals in goods of that description (whether he is the manufacturer or producer or not), there is an implied condition that the goods shall be of merchantable quality:
Provided that, if the buyer has examined the goods, there shall be no implied condition as regards defects which such examination ought to have revealed.
(3) An implied warranty or condition as to quality or fitness for a particular purpose may be annexed by the usage of trade.
(4) An express warranty or condition does not negative a warranty or condition implied by this Act unless inconsistent therewith.”
! Read the opening words first — they are the doctrine

Before the four exceptions, s. 16 states the rule: “there is no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale”. That is caveat emptor — let the buyer beware — enacted. Everything that follows is an exception. The DU syllabus lists ‘Doctrine of Caveat Emptor’ as a separate head immediately before Jones v. Just, and this sentence is why. Part B of these notes takes the doctrine on its own.

Exception (1) — s. 16(1): fitness for a particular purpose

Four elements must all be present. Miss one and the exception does not operate:

① Purpose made known

The buyer must make known the particular purpose, expressly or by implication. Where goods have only one normal use, asking for them by name is enough — Priest v. Last.

② Reliance on the seller

The buyer must rely on the seller's skill or judgment, and that reliance must be brought home to the mind of the seller — usually by implication. Grant, per Lord Wright.

③ Seller's business

The goods must be of a description which it is in the course of the seller's business to supply. Whether he is the manufacturer is irrelevant.

④ Goods not fit

The goods must not be reasonably fit for that purpose. ‘Reasonably’ — not perfect, not ideal.

The proviso — sale under a patent or trade name

“Provided that, in the case of a contract for the sale of a specified article under its patent or other trade name, there is no implied condition as to its fitness for any particular purpose.”

This proviso is where Baldry v. Marshall [1925] 1 KB 260 does its work. Baldry told a motor dealer he wanted ‘a comfortable car suitable for touring’; the dealer recommended a Bugatti and Baldry bought it by that trade name; it proved unsuitable for touring. The dealer argued the proviso applied. The Court of Appeal (Bankes, Scrutton and Atkin L.JJ.) held for the buyer:

The test is: did the buyer himself, in purchasing the goods by name, form the judgment that the goods would be suitable for his own purpose, without relying on the seller? If so, no term as to fitness is implied. If there is still an indication of purpose and reliance, such a term may be implied. The mere fact that goods are described by trade name does not necessarily exclude the implied term.WEB
★ The two propositions DU wants from Baldry
  1. A sale under a trade name excludes the implied condition of fitness (s. 16(1) proviso) — but only where the buyer did not rely on the seller's skill.
  2. It does not exclude the implied condition of merchantable quality (s. 16(2)), which has no such proviso. Where goods are sold under their trade name, merchantability still applies although fitness is excluded.
Exception (2) — s. 16(2): merchantable quality
a Bought by description

Same wide meaning as s. 15 — including goods picked off a counter.

b From a dealer in that description

A seller who deals in goods of that description — manufacturer or not. A private one-off sale is outside the section.

c Not merchantable

The goods must fail the merchantability standard — and the proviso removes defects the buyer's examination ought to have revealed.

What ‘merchantable quality’ means
“The phrase in s. 14, sub-s. (2), is, in my opinion, used as meaning that the article is of such quality and in such condition that a reasonable man acting reasonably would after a full examination accept it under the circumstances of the case in performance of his offer to buy that article, whether he buys for his own use or to sell it again.”
— Farwell L.J., Bristol Tramways & Carriage Co. Ltd. v. Fiat Motors Ltd. [1910] 2 KB 831.WEB

Lord Wright, in Case 32, gave the gloss that DU actually examines:

“Whatever else merchantable may mean it does mean that the article sold, if only meant for one particular use in ordinary course, is fit for that use; merchantable does not mean that the thing is saleable in the market simply because it looks all right. It is not merchantable in that event if it has defects unfitting it for its only proper use but not apparent on ordinary examination: that is clear from the proviso…”DU p. 222

And Bankes L.J. supplied the point that ties merchantability to the packaging, in Niblett: “Quality includes the state or condition of the goods… The labels were as much part of the state or condition of the goods as the tins were. The state of the packing affected the merchantable quality of the goods.” Goods can be physically perfect and still unmerchantable, if they cannot lawfully be sold.

! An important statutory contrast with England

The United Kingdom replaced ‘merchantable quality’ with ‘satisfactory quality’ by the Sale and Supply of Goods Act 1994 (now s. 14(2) of the Sale of Goods Act 1979, with the s. 14(2A) ‘reasonable person’ test). India has not followed. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 still uses the 1893 expression ‘merchantable quality’ in s. 16(2). For a DU answer, therefore, rely on Bristol Tramways, Grant v. AKM and Niblett — not on the modern English case-law on ‘satisfactory quality’, which is not authority on the Indian text.

§1.7 · Section 17 — Sale by sample

Section 17 — verbatim
“Sale by sample.—(1) A contract of sale is a contract for sale by sample where there is a term in the contract, express or implied, to that effect.
(2) In the case of a contract for sale by sample there is an implied condition—
(a) that the bulk shall correspond with the sample in quality;
(b) that the buyer shall have a reasonable opportunity of comparing the bulk with the sample;
(c) that the goods shall be free from any defect, rendering them unmerchantable, which would not be apparent on reasonable examination of the sample.”

s. 17(1) is a definitional gate. The sale is by sample only where the contract so provides — expressly or by implication. The classic implication is the trade practice of the showroom: you pick from a swatch, a chip, a handful of grain, a test strip, and the goods are later delivered in bulk.

s. 17(2) then imposes three implied conditions, and note that all three are conditions — s. 17 does not use the word ‘warranty’ at all. Each does different work:

a Bulk = sample in quality

The delivered goods must match the sample. This is the sample analogue of s. 15's description condition. It is not enough by itself where the sale is also by description (s. 15, second limb).

b Opportunity to compare

The buyer must be given a reasonable opportunity of comparing bulk with sample. The seller who tenders the bulk at the very end of the last delivery day breaches this — that was held in Case 30. What is reasonable is a question of fact (s. 63).

c No latent unmerchantability

The goods must be free of defects making them unmerchantable that reasonable examination of the sample would not have revealed. This is the overlap with s. 16(2) — and it is the condition that catches defects present in both the sample and the bulk.

★ Why s. 17(2)(c) exists at all

Without (c), a seller could hand over a defective sample, deliver a bulk that faithfully reproduces the defect, and say ‘the bulk corresponds with the sample’. Clause (c) closes that door: matching the sample is no answer where the defect makes the goods unmerchantable and reasonable examination of the sample would not have shown it. It is the sample-sale counterpart of the proviso to s. 16(2).

☕ Three real examples
  • Carpet. You choose a carpet from a 20 cm swatch. The roll delivered is a noticeably different shade. Breach of s. 17(2)(a) — reject.
  • Army paint (the British Paints fact). The contract requires the stores to be inspected against the accepted sample before dispatch. The seller tenders 248 cwt on the last day of the delivery period, when the buyer's inspectors at Kanpur need at least three days to test. There was, on the facts, “no sufficient time to inspect”.   • Tea. You taste a sample; it is clean. The bulk is chemically identical but contains a pesticide residue that no tasting would reveal and that makes the tea unsaleable. Breach of s. 17(2)(c).

§1.8 · Section 62 — Exclusion of implied terms and conditions

Section 62 — verbatim
“Exclusion of implied terms and conditions.—Where any right, duty or liability would arise under a contract of sale by implication of law, it may be negatived or varied by express agreement or by the course of dealing between the parties, or by usage, if the usage is such as to bind both parties to the contract.”

Three modes of exclusion, one precondition, and one important limit.

1 Express agreement

An exclusion clause in the contract. This is the mode used — and defeated — in Wallis v. Pratt and Baldry v. Marshall.

2 Course of dealing

A regular and consistent course of dealing between the same parties may import an exclusion even where the particular contract is silent.

3 Usage

A trade usage — but only “if the usage is such as to bind both parties”. A usage the buyer did not know is no answer.

The precondition: the term must arise ‘by implication of law’

s. 62 reaches only terms the law implies — i.e. ss. 14, 15, 16, 17, and terms annexed by usage under s. 16(3). It does not touch terms the parties have expressly agreed. So where the parties have written an express promise into the contract, s. 62 is simply not engaged: the express term governs, read with s. 16(4) (an express term does not negative an implied one unless inconsistent).

The limit: the clause must actually reach conditions

This is the entire point of Wallis v. Pratt and it is worth stating precisely, because it is 2015 Q6(a):

“It is admitted that the language of the contract creates the obligation to deliver common English sainfoin, and that this has the status of a condition. It cannot, therefore, be affected or limited by a clause which only negatives the existence of warranties.” — Fletcher Moulton L.J.; and in the House of Lords, Lord Alverstone C.J.: “when the sellers said that they gave no warranty they [did not mean] that they would not be responsible for any breaches of condition”.WEB

The clause read: “Sellers give no warranty, express or implied, as to growth, description, or any other matters.” The House held it did not exclude the implied condition under s. 13 of the 1893 Act (our s. 15) — even though, because the buyers had accepted the seed, their only remedy was in damages for breach of warranty. The condition survived as a condition; the remedy was confined. Contrast Andrews v. Singer [1934] 1 KB 17, where the draftsman had expressly included the word ‘conditions’ in the exclusion clause, and the exclusion succeeded.

! The three limits on s. 62 that DU likes
  1. Construction. A clause negativing ‘warranties’ does not negative ‘conditions’ (Wallis v. Pratt).
  2. Consistency. Under s. 16(4), an express term negatives an implied one only where the two are inconsistent.
  3. Fairness. Where the exclusion is part of a standard-form contract and there is a gross inequality of bargaining power, the general law may still strike it down — ICA s. 23 (unlawful object or consideration; opposed to public policy) and s. 28 (agreements in restraint of legal proceedings void). And for consumers the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 now supplies a statutory overlay.

Part B — The Doctrine of Caveat Emptor

§2.1 to §2.5 · the rule, its origin, its exceptions, and the 2015 question answered

§2.1 · The rule and where it came from

Caveat emptor — “let the buyer beware”. The maxim expresses the common-law position that, in a sale of goods, the seller is not answerable for the quality or fitness of what he sells unless he has expressly promised something about it. The buyer must look after himself: he may inspect, he may ask for an express warranty, and if he does neither he takes the risk.

In India the maxim is not merely a judge-made rule — it is enacted. The opening words of s. 16 are its statutory form: “Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, there is no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose of goods supplied under a contract of sale, except as follows…”. So s. 16 is a code: rule first, exceptions after.

“S. 14 begins by a general enunciation of the old rule of caveat emptor and proceeds to state by way of exception the two implied conditions by which it has been said the old rule has been changed to a rule of caveat venditor; the change has been rendered necessary by the conditions of modern commerce and trade.”
— Lord Wright, Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills, AIR 1936 PC 34. DU p. 221

§2.2 · Why the rule made sense — and why it stopped making sense

The 2015 question puts the history in the examiner's own words, and the passage is worth knowing:

“The exceptions to the rule of Caveat Emptor have now become more prominent than the rule itself. The rule owes its origin to the times, when nearly all sales took place in the market. The buyer and the seller came face to face, the seller exhibited his wares, the buyer examined them and bought them if he liked. But as trade grew and assumed global dimensions, it became difficult for buyers to examine goods beforehand, most transactions being concluded by correspondence. Further on account of the complex structure of modern goods, it is only the sellers who can assure the co[ntents] and quality of goods.”WEB DU question paper, 2015 Q3

That is the whole argument in four sentences, and Jones v. Just (Case 31) is the case that decided it. The buyer bought Manila hemp “to arrive” from Singapore. Nobody had seen it — not the buyer, not the seller, not even the shippers. The hemp had been wetted by seawater in a shipwreck between Manila and Singapore, unpacked, dried and repacked. It fetched 75% of the sound price. The seller's counsel, Mr. Brett (later Lord Esher M.R.), argued that the only term was that the bales should answer the description “bales of Manilla hemp”, which they did, and that “as to quality or condition there was no warranty; that consequently the maxim caveat emptor applied”.DU p. 210

Mellor J., delivering the judgment of the Court of Queen's Bench, rejected that, and the decisive sentence is the one to quote in every caveat-emptor answer:

“We are aware of no case in which the maxim, caveat emptor, has been applied where there has been no opportunity of inspection, or where that opportunity had not been waived.” DU p. 213

§2.4 · From caveat emptor to caveat venditor

The Latin tag for the modern position is caveat venditor — let the seller beware. Lord Wright used it in Grant and described the transition as “rendered necessary by the conditions of modern commerce and trade”. In Indian law today the shift has three statutory layers:

  1. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — s. 16's exceptions, which are the original shift.
  2. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 — ‘defect’, ‘deficiency’, ‘unfair trade practice’ and product liability (Chapter IV), which give a consumer remedies that do not depend on the SOGA at all and, in the product-liability action, do not depend on privity.
  3. Sectoral quality statutes — the Food Safety and Standards Act 2006, the Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940, the Legal Metrology Act 2009 and the Bureau of Indian Standards Act 2016, each of which fixes a statutory floor below which goods are simply not merchantable.

§3.1 · Priest v. Last WEB

Priest v. Last
(1903) 2 KB 148 · King's Bench Division (Walton J., with a jury) · English · Cited in the DU syllabus immediately above Case 30
Where an article has only one ordinary use, buying it under that name makes the purpose known ‘by implication’ — s. 16(1) needs no express statement of purpose.
Facts

The plaintiff was a draper of Hereford. He went into the shop of the defendant, a retail chemist, and asked for a ‘hot-water bottle’. An article was shown to him as such and he examined it.

According to the defendant's own evidence, the plaintiff said he did not want an expensive article, that he wanted it for a special case (which he did not specify), and that he asked whether the article shown would stand boiling water. The defendant replied that the bottle was “meant for hot water, but would not stand boiling water”.

The plaintiff bought it. Some days later, while it was being used by the plaintiff's wife for the relief of cramp, the bottle burst and she was scalded.

Issues
  1. Was the plaintiff's particular purpose made known to the seller, “expressly or by implication”, within s. 14(1) of the Sale of Goods Act 1893 (our s. 16(1))?
  2. Did the plaintiff rely on the seller's skill or judgment?
  3. If so, was there a breach of the implied condition of reasonable fitness?
Arguments
For the plaintiff (buyer)
  • A ‘hot-water bottle’ has one purpose. Asking for it by that name is itself a statement of the purpose, so the purpose was made known by implication.
  • A draper buying from a chemist necessarily relies on the chemist's skill — the buyer cannot test the rubber himself.
  • The bottle was, on the jury's finding, not fit for use as a hot-water bottle when sold, and that was the cause of the bursting.
For the defendant (seller)
  • The buyer examined the article before buying it, and the seller gave an honest answer about boiling water; there was no express warranty.
  • The purpose was never stated: the plaintiff expressly declined to specify “the special case” for which he wanted it.
  • Accordingly the maxim caveat emptor applied, and the buyer took the risk.
Decision

Judgment for the plaintiff. The jury found that the defendant did not tell the plaintiff the bottle was fit for boiling water, that the bottle when sold was not fit for use as a hot-water bottle, and that this caused the bursting; damages were assessed at £40. Walton J., to whom power was given by consent to draw inferences of fact, found that the plaintiff had made known the particular purpose so as to show that he relied on the defendant's skill and knowledge, and held the case fell within s. 14(1) with the result that there was an implied warranty [condition] that the bottle was fit for holding hot water, and that it had been broken. An application for judgment or a new trial was dismissed.

“The particular purpose for which an article purchased is required may, under the Sale of Goods Act, 1893, s. 14, sub-s. 1, be made known to the seller by the recognised description by which the article is purchased.” Priest v. Last, (1903) 2 KB 148, headnote
Principles evolved
  1. Purpose may be communicated by description alone. Where goods have a single ordinary use, naming them is enough to make the purpose known by implication. No express statement of purpose is required.
  2. Examination is not fatal. The buyer's inspection of the article did not displace the implied condition. (Under the Indian Act the equivalent point is the proviso to s. 16(2), which removes only those defects the examination ‘ought to have revealed’ — and no examination could reveal that the rubber would burst.)
  3. The seller's own words can create the reliance. Answering a technical question about boiling water is an assumption of skill that the buyer is entitled to rely on.
  4. Reliance is usually inferred, not stated. This is the seed of Lord Wright's later formulation in Grant: reliance “will seldom be express; it will usually arise by implication from the circumstances”.
★ How to use Priest v. Last in a DU answer

It is the authority for the first element of s. 16(1) — ‘makes known … expressly or by implication’. Cite it whenever the facts show goods with one obvious use (a hot-water bottle, a raincoat, a pressure cooker, a pair of woollen undergarments). Then cite Baldry v. Marshall for the trade-name proviso and Grant for reliance, and your s. 16(1) answer is complete in three authorities.

30. British Paints (India) Ltd. v. Union of India
AIR 1971 Cal. 393 · Calcutta High Court (Appellate Side) · S.K. Chakravarti J. · DU Reader p. 204
Time expressly made of the essence; an extension is a concession, not a downgrade to a warranty; and a tender that leaves no reasonable time to inspect against the sample is a bad tender.
Facts

The Union of India invited tenders for the supply of paint described as “compound recolouring Olive Green Scamic 314 for faded tents”, to Specification Ind/32/7037. The plaintiff company submitted a tender. The laboratory did not consider the sample up to the mark, but the higher authorities of the Defence Department accepted the tender and placed an order for 500 cwt. at Rs. 256 F.O.R. Calcutta per cwt.

Under the contract the goods were to be inspected by the Inspector at Calcutta, and only if he was satisfied that they were up to the mark could the plaintiff dispatch them on receipt of the inspection notes. The original delivery date was 15 October 1952; because some ingredients had to be indented from the United Kingdom, time was extended on the plaintiff's successive applications, finally to 30 April 1953.

What happened to the five lots was this:

Before that letter arrived, Mr. Bogh, the plaintiff's Technical Director, had gone to Kanpur — where the tests were actually carried out — and was given every opportunity to see how the testing was done. Afterwards the plaintiff asked the Kanpur authorities to allow its chemist, Mr. I.B. Ghosh, to see why the goods were rejected. Mr. Ghosh went in the third week of May 1953 with a reconditioned sample; on 30 May 1953 the Kanpur authorities wrote to the Inspector in Calcutta, with a copy to the plaintiff, that the reconditioned sample was “found to conform to the quoted particulars in all respects and is therefore acceptable”.

There was further correspondence, but by letter dated 30 September 1953 the defendant intimated that its decision of 1 May 1953 was final and that the stores offered “were not in accordance with the terms of the Contract for quality.” The plaintiff served notices, and the matter went to arbitration; the arbitrators held that the Inspector's decision was final and binding and that they therefore had no jurisdiction over the rejection.

The plaintiff then sued for Rs 88,496 as damages (including storage charges) plus Rs 5,228 interest — a total claim of Rs 93,724. The learned Subordinate Judge at Alipore dismissed the suit on all points. This was the appeal.

Issues
  1. Is the plaintiff bound by the Inspector's reports, the contract providing that “the Inspector's decision as regards rejections aforesaid shall be final and binding on the parties”?
  2. Were the tests at Kanpur carried out in accordance with the contract — in particular, were they done against the accepted sample 30/100, or against a ‘tinted slip’ prepared months earlier; and had sample 30/100 been destroyed?
  3. Was time of the essence, and had the extensions converted it into a warranty?
  4. Had the defendant waived quality by the Kanpur authorities' letter of 30 May 1953?
  5. Does s. 23 of the Sale of Goods Act apply?
  6. Does the fact that the defendant reserved a claim to liquidated damages prevent it from cancelling the contract?
Arguments
For the plaintiff / appellant — Mr. Rabindra Mohan Mukherjee
  • The Inspector did not apply his mind and merely “dittoed” what the Kanpur authorities wrote; his reports are therefore perverse and arbitrary and cannot bind the parties.
  • The Kanpur tests were not in accordance with the contract: they compared the supplied material with “a tinted slip prepared some months ago with the paint from the said sample No. 30/100”, not with the sample itself at the time of examination.
  • Sample 30/100 had already been destroyed — so no valid comparison was possible at all.
  • The goods were specially manufactured for this tender and could not be resold in the market.
  • An independent expert, Mr. Monk, tested the rejected supplies and reported that they were of the same quality as the tender sample 30/100, a duplicate of which the plaintiff firm had kept.
  • Time was not of the essence. It had been extended again and again, which shows the Union of India treated the date as a warranty and nothing more; its unilateral cancellation was therefore itself an anticipatory breach entitling the plaintiff to damages.
  • The Kanpur authorities found the reconditioned sample acceptable in May, so the defendant was not entitled to cancel — and s. 23 of the Sale of Goods Act applies.
  • The defendant's own pleading relies on liquidated damages, which is inconsistent with cancelling the contract.
  • Reliance was placed on Burn & Co. v. Morvi State (AIR 1925 PC 188) and particularly on Hindusthan Construction Co. v. State of Bihar (AIR 1963 Pat 254).
For the Union of India / respondent — Mr. P.K. Sengupta, Government Advocate
  • Time was expressly made of the essence by the contract itself.
  • The tests at Kanpur were carried on in accordance with the rules; the Inspector's reports were not arbitrary; the supplies were rejected because they were not of the requisite quality.
  • Further, the plaintiff did not give sufficient time for inspection even. Under s. 17(2) of the Sale of Goods Act, where the purchase is on the basis of a sample, some reasonable time must be given to the purchaser to find out whether the goods offered accord with the sample. Here the plaintiff knew perfectly well that samples had to go from Calcutta to Kanpur and that “the test alone would take at least three days” — yet it offered the reconditioned second supply and the fourth and fifth instalments by letters dated 29 April, for a delivery date of 30 April.
  • The contract condition (Ext. 3-C) defined ‘delivery’ as “Delivery by the dates specified in the acceptance of tender of stores which are found acceptable by the Inspector” — not the mere submission of stores. There was therefore no delivery by 30 April 1953.
  • Under Nune Siwayya v. Maddu ((1935) 62 IA 89, 98 (PC)), a defendant who repudiates may rely on any ground existing at the time of repudiation, not only the ground he then stated.
Decision — per S.K. Chakravarti J.

The appeal was dismissed — “the defendant, therefore, was within its rights to repudiate the contract for the supply of the remaining portion of the goods, and this appeal therefore, must fail” — but, on the special facts, each party was directed to bear its own costs.

(1) The Inspector did apply his mind. Colonel Pillay gave evidence that he “waited for 3 to 4 days to make up his mind after obtaining the reports from Kanpur” and took other factors into account. There were no proper testing facilities in Calcutta; the procedure was to send samples to Kanpur and decide on their reports; and the Kanpur tests were done by experts, Dr. Ranganathan and Dr. Balakrishnan. “If the Inspector acted on the basis of such reports, it cannot be said that he did not apply his mind thereto.”

(2) The tests were in accordance with the contract. Drs. Ranganathan and Balakrishnan deposed that they tested every sample against sample 30/100, including the three rejected supplies, and emphatically denied that sample 30/100 had been destroyed. Mr. Bogh, who visited on 1 May 1953, never asked whether it had been destroyed; he only wanted to see the method. No complaint was made in writing. The trial judge preferred the testimony of the two responsible officers to that of Mr. Ghosh, and the appellate court saw no reason to differ. “Under the rules so long as the Contract is alive they are bound to keep it”.

Two further facts told against the plaintiff: it had itself written offering the 59½ cwt. after reconditioning, which “would also disclose that the supplies which had been made and rejected did not conform to the required specification”; and the contract required the test to be made by applying the sample “to a piece of cotton dosootie or sheeting used in the manufacture of tents” — which the plaintiff never did. As for the grievance that Kanpur used scoured rather than unscoured dosootie: “Scoured cotton dosootie is also cotton dosootie, and in the circumstances it cannot be said that the test carried on by the Kanpur authorities on scoured cotton dosootie would be inconsistent with the terms of the contract.”

(3) Mr. Monk's evidence was rejected, for four reasons: he tested in the absence of the defendant; he tested more than three years after the articles were made and himself said they were not exactly of the same quality as before, something having evaporated; he did not apply the paint to any scoured dosootie or tent sheeting; and “what is worse, he took samples from each of the rejected barrels and made a hotchpot of the same, and then made the comparison.” The Inspector's report is “final and conclusive”.

(4) No waiver of quality. “At no stage was there any waiver of the quality.” The contract was cancelled on 1 May 1953 by the Director-General of Supply; “the Kanpur authorities cannot extend the time of delivery, and therefore, this point also fails.”

(5) Time was of the essence — the central holding.

“It would appear from the Contract itself that time was specifically made of the essence of the Contract. … In this particular case, as we have already pointed out, there was an express stipulation that time would be of the essence of the Contract.” British Paints, para 9 (DU p. 208)

The Court then met the ‘extension = warranty’ argument head-on. It accepted the general principle from Gomathi Nayagam v. Palaniswami (AIR 1967 SC 868) that an intention to make time of the essence “may be evidenced by either express stipulations or by circumstances which are sufficiently strong to displace the ordinary presumption”, but held that the intention must appear from the agreement or from antecedent conduct:

“an intention to make time of the essence of the Contract must be expressed in explicit and unmistakable language in the agreement itself, and if by any means such an intention is not explicit, it may be inferred from the antecedent conduct of the parties and surrounding circumstances but not from the subsequent conduct of the parties after the Contract was made.” Ibid.

And the extensions themselves did no work for the appellant. In its telegram and letter of 2/3 March 1953 the defendant made it clear that “there would be no further extension of time”. Moreover, applying Md. Habidullah v. Bird & Co. (AIR 1922 PC 178):

“when after the seller of goods has failed to deliver them at the agreed time the buyer has agreed to an extension of time for delivery, the effect of Section 55 of the Indian Contract Act is that the buyer is entitled to damages computed in the ordinary way, if the seller fails to delivery within the extended time.” Ibid.

The two authorities the appellant relied on were distinguished. In Burn & Co. v. Morvi State the Privy Council had found, on the terms, that time was of the essence. In Hindusthan Construction Co. there was a provision for daily damages and the State of Bihar had not availed itself of its option to determine — “The facts in that case are entirely different from the facts of the present case wherein the plaintiff had asked for extension of time again and again and the defendant had reluctantly to agree thereto.” The conclusion:

“This term in the agreement was a condition precedent and not a mere warranty.” Ibid.

(6) The s. 17(2) / s. 63 point — the plaintiff never had a reasonable opportunity to have the goods inspected. This is the ground the Government Advocate argued and the Court accepted:

“It would appear from Section 17(2) of the Indian Sale of Goods Act that if the purchase was being made on the basis of a sample, some reasonable time must be given to the purchaser to find out if the goods offered were in accordance with the sample. It would further appear from the evidence that after the goods were manufactured, the plaintiff was to send an intimation to the Inspector in Calcutta and he would take samples and then send the same to Kanpur and there it must be tested and the test alone would take at least three days. All these were within the knowledge of the plaintiff. The plaintiff, however, offered the reconditioned second supply and the 4th and 5th instalments on the 30th April by its letter dated the 29th and the delivery date being the 30th April there was not sufficient time to inspect.” British Paints, para 8 (DU p. 209)

(7) The s. 23 argument failed. Mr. Mukherjee relied on s. 23 of the Sale of Goods Act on the footing that the Kanpur authorities had found the reconditioned sample acceptable in May. The Court held this could not help: the contract had been cancelled on 1 May 1953, and the Kanpur authorities had no power to extend the time for delivery.

(8) The repudiating party may rely on a ground not stated at the time. Following Nune Siwayya v. Maddu ((1935) 62 IA 89, 98 (PC)):

“in a suit for damages for breach by repudiation of the Contract for the sale of goods, the defendant can rely upon any grounds for repudiation which existed when he repudiated; he is not confined to the ground which he then stated. After the Inspector's reports were made available and showed that the goods were not in accordance with the tender, it was up to the Union of India to take up that ground as well.” British Paints, para 10 (DU p. 209)

(9) The liquidated-damages point failed. Condition 3-C expressly gave the Secretary three liberties where stores are rejected: (a) allow resubmission within a time specified, (b) buy the rejected quantity elsewhere at the contractor's risk and cost, or (c) terminate the contract and recover the loss. “Therefore, it was within the rights of the defendant to terminate the contract.” No claim for liquidated damages was actually made — the defendant had merely reserved the right, and the subsequent letter did not even refer to it.

(10) A note of sympathy, and costs. “we do not find that there has been any deliberate negligence on the part of the plaintiff. They had difficulties of their own, inasmuch as they had to import some of the ingredients, and the defendant itself was also responsible for some delay, inasmuch as, in the month of January it suddenly directed [the plaintiff] to supply the goods in galvanized sheets.” But the goods were urgently required for military purposes. Appeal dismissed; each party to bear its own costs.

Note on the text: the DU reader prints “30th of September 1953” in para 10 where the sense requires “30th April 1953”, and “directed the defendant” in para 13 where the sense requires “the plaintiff”. These are printing errors in the reader; the reasoning is unaffected.

Principles evolved
  1. Express stipulation wins. Where the contract says time is of the essence, it is. s. 11 limb 2 sends you to “the terms of the contract”, and that is where you go.
  2. Extensions do not dilute the term. Granting an extension is a concession. Under s. 55 ICA the buyer who extends still recovers damages if the extended date is missed. An extension is not an election to treat the date as a warranty.
  3. Intention must be antecedent. You read the intention from the agreement or from conduct before the contract — never from what parties did afterwards.
  4. Hindusthan Construction is not a general licence. A liquidated-damages clause plus a failure to exercise an option may show time was not of the essence on those facts; it does not do so where the seller begged for extensions and the buyer “reluctantly” agreed.
  5. s. 17(2) is a real obligation. A sale by sample carries an implied condition that the buyer will have a reasonable opportunity of comparing bulk with sample; and by s. 63 what is reasonable is a question of fact. A tender on the last day, where the test alone takes three days, is not a tender at all.
  6. A repudiating party is not locked into his stated ground. Nune Siwayya: he may rely on any ground that existed at the time.
  7. An expert clause is a clause like any other. Where the contract makes an Inspector's decision final, the courts will not interfere unless the decision is perverse or arbitrary — i.e. unless he did not apply his mind or acted outside the contract.
  8. A rejection does not require the buyer to choose only one path. The contract may give cumulative options (resubmission, risk purchase, termination); reserving liquidated damages does not waive the right to terminate.

The Faculty has placed British Paints at the head of Topic 6, immediately under ‘Stipulations as to time’, and that is where it belongs: it is the s. 11 case. But it is also the s. 17(2) case and the s. 63 case, and it is the only Indian decision in your list that shows all three sections operating in one contract. Use it whenever a question mentions a delivery date, an inspection clause, or a sample.

§3.3 · Niblett Ltd. v. Confectioners' Materials Co. Ltd. WEB

Niblett Ltd. v. Confectioners' Materials Co. Ltd.
(1921) 3 KB 387 · Court of Appeal (Bankes and Atkin L.JJ.; Scrutton L.J. dissenting) · English · Named in the DU syllabus between Cases 30 and 31
Goods can be physically perfect and still unmerchantable — if they cannot lawfully be sold. And a seller who cannot lawfully sell them has no ‘right to sell’ under s. 14(a).
Facts

The defendants, a firm dealing in confectioners' materials, agreed in writing to sell the plaintiffs a quantity of condensed milk in tins, of a stated standard, at a price that included insurance and freight from New York to London. Payment was to be made in cash against the shipping documents. The buyers received the documents and paid.

When the goods arrived, 1,000 cases bore the brand ‘Nissly’. The Nestlé and Anglo-Swiss Condensed Milk Co. considered that ‘Nissly’ infringed their registered trade mark ‘Nestlé’ and objected to the goods being dealt with in any way. At their instance the Commissioners of Customs detained the goods, writing on 26 May that they did so because the labels bore marks regarded as an infringement, while indicating they would deal favourably with an application for release.

Neither party wished to fight Nestlé. The sellers admitted the infringement and gave an undertaking not to sell condensed milk under the title ‘Nissly’. The buyers made an unsuccessful attempt to export the cases, and then removed the labels and sold the goods as unlabelled condensed milk at a reduced price.

Issues
  1. Were the goods of merchantable quality within s. 14(2) of the Sale of Goods Act 1893 (our s. 16(2))?
  2. Did the sellers have a right to sell the goods within s. 12(1) (our s. 14(a))?
  3. Were the buyers entitled to quiet possession within s. 12(2) (our s. 14(b))?
Arguments
For the buyers (plaintiffs)
  • They claimed damages for breach of three terms: merchantable quality, right to sell, and quiet possession.
  • They also alleged an implied condition or warranty that the milk would not, by its mark, label or name, infringe any trade mark or brand of condensed milk already on the market, or be a colourable imitation of one.
  • The practical effect was that the goods could not be sold as delivered — if they had sold them they would have been liable to an injunction in the terms of the undertaking the sellers themselves had given. That is the measure of the loss.
For the sellers (defendants)
  • The milk itself was perfectly good. Its quality was never in question; only the label was.
  • If the buyers chose not to fight Nestlé — as the sellers themselves had not — that was their own commercial decision, not a defect in the goods.
  • In any event, the goods were saleable: the buyers did sell them, unlabelled, and recovered a substantial part of the price.
Decision

Judgment for the buyers. The Court of Appeal held, by a majority, that the sellers had broken the implied condition as to right to sell and the implied warranty as to quiet possession; and Bankes L.J. held in addition that the goods were not of merchantable quality. Scrutton L.J. dissented on the right-to-sell point.

“Quality includes the state or condition of the goods. The state of this condensed milk was that it was packed in tins bearing labels. The labels were as much part of the state or condition of the goods as the tins were. The state of the packing affected the merchantable quality of the goods.” Bankes L.J., Niblett, at p. 395
“The seller had not the right to sell these goods … having admitted that there being an infringement of the Nestle Company's trade mark they were liable to an injunction restraining the sale. Therefore, they had no right to sell these goods at the time when the property was to pass.” Atkin L.J., Niblett
Principles evolved
  1. ‘Merchantable’ means legally saleable, not merely usable. Goods that expose the buyer to an injunction are not merchantable, however sound they are physically. This is the single most quotable proposition from the case, and it works through s. 2(12) (‘quality’ includes state or condition) into s. 16(2).
  2. Packing and get-up are part of the goods. Labels, tins, cartons and instructions are as much part of the ‘state or condition’ as the contents.
  3. ‘Right to sell’ is not the same as ownership. A seller may own the goods and still have no right to sell them, because a third party's trade mark, patent or charge stands in the way. That is a breach of the condition in s. 14(a), so the buyer may reject.
  4. Quiet possession is forward-looking. s. 14(b) protects the buyer's enjoyment after delivery; a threatened injunction disturbs it even before anyone is actually dispossessed.
  5. Trade-mark compliance is now also a statutory matter. Under the Trade Marks Act, 1999 (ss. 27–29) the registered proprietor has the remedy; the buyer's contractual remedy against his own seller is s. 14.
☕ The modern Indian version

A Delhi trader imports a container of branded trainers bearing a mark confusingly similar to a registered Indian mark. Customs detain the consignment; the registered proprietor obtains an injunction. On Niblett principles the trader can reject the whole consignment and recover the price from his supplier — breach of s. 14(a) (condition: no right to sell), s. 14(b) (warranty: no quiet possession) and s. 16(2) (condition: not merchantable, because the goods cannot lawfully be sold). All three, on one set of facts.

§3.4 · Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt & Haynes WEB

! This is the single most examined English case in Topic 6

DU set a question on it in 2015 Q6(a): “Discuss the observations of House of Lords in Wallis Son and Well v/s Pratt (1911) A.C. 394 that when a condition is reduced to warranty, the effect is not that the condition becomes warranty.” The passage is set out verbatim below. Learn it.

Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt & Haynes
[1911] AC 394 · [1911] UKHL 620 · House of Lords, 5 May 1911 · Lord Loreburn L.C., Lord Alverstone C.J., Lord Atkinson, Lord Gorell · Reversing the Court of Appeal (Vaughan Williams and Farwell L.JJ.; Fletcher Moulton L.J. dissenting)
A clause excluding ‘warranties’ does not exclude ‘conditions’. And when s. 13 confines a condition to a warranty remedy, the term remains a condition — only the remedy changes.
Facts

The appellants (buyers, seed merchants) bought seed from the respondents (sellers) described in the written contract as ‘common English sainfoin’. The contract carried the now-famous proviso:

“Sellers give no warranty, express or implied, as to growth, description, or any other matters.”

What was delivered was ‘giant sainfoin’ — a different and inferior seed. The buyers did not discover this; they accepted the seed and resold it to third parties as common English sainfoin. They were then obliged to pay damages to their sub-buyers, and sought to recover that amount from the sellers.

A first-instance judgment in the buyers' favour was reversed by the Court of Appeal by a majority (Vaughan Williams and Farwell L.JJ., with Fletcher Moulton L.J. dissenting). The buyers appealed to the House of Lords. As Lord Loreburn observed at the outset, “two Judges have been in favour of the appellants and two in favour of the respondents, and therefore it is impossible to doubt that there must be room for controversy”.

Issues
  1. Was the description ‘common English sainfoin’ a condition or a mere warranty of the contract?
  2. Did the exclusion clause — which mentioned only warranties — relieve the sellers of liability for breach of that condition?
  3. Where a buyer has accepted the goods, so that the breach of condition can only be treated as a breach of warranty, what exactly happens to the term?
Arguments
For the buyers (appellants)
  • It was admitted — and the House recorded it as admitted — that the contract required common English sainfoin to be delivered, and that this obligation “has the status of a condition”.
  • A clause which only negatives warranties “cannot… be affected or limited by a clause which only negatives the existence of warranties” (Fletcher Moulton L.J.).
  • Statutorily, s. 13 of the 1893 Act (our s. 15) uses the word condition, not warranty: “Where there is a contract for the sale of goods by description, there is an implied condition that the goods shall correspond with the description.”
  • The seeds delivered were “a thing as distinct in agricultural knowledge from common English sainfoin as in ordinary commerce a silver watch would be distinct from a gold watch”.
For the sellers (respondents)
  • They had expressly stipulated that they gave ‘no warranty, express or implied, as to growth, description, or any other matters’. The word ‘description’ appears in the clause, and the buyers' complaint is precisely about description.
  • Once the buyers had accepted the seed, s. 11(1)(c) of the 1893 Act (our s. 13(2)) meant the breach could only be treated as a breach of warranty — and the contract had excluded warranties. Nothing was left to sue on.
  • In the Court of Appeal the majority accepted that the word ‘warranty’ was used by these commercial men in an “ambulatory sense”, covering whatever remedy the buyer happened to be left with.
Decision — House of Lords

Appeal allowed. The respondents' failure to supply common English sainfoin amounted to a breach of condition, which, notwithstanding the terms of the contract, entitled the appellants to recover the amount of their loss. The judgment of the Chief Justice was restored. The petition for leave to adduce further evidence was dismissed without costs.

Lord Loreburn L.C. gave the passage that DU asks about. It must be quoted in full:

“If a man agrees to sell something of a particular description he cannot require the buyer to take something which is of a different description, and a sale of goods by description implies a condition that the goods shall correspond to it. But if a thing of a different description is accepted in the belief that it is according to the contract, then the buyer cannot return it after having accepted it; but he may treat the breach of the condition as if it were a breach of warranty — that is to say, he may have the remedies applicable to a breach of warranty. That does not mean that it was really a breach of warranty or that what was a condition in reality had come to be degraded or converted into a warranty. It does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio, but the injured party may treat it as if it had become so, and he becomes entitled to the remedies which attach to a breach of warranty.” Lord Loreburn L.C., Wallis v. Pratt, [1911] AC 394, 396

Lord Alverstone C.J. added the drafting point. He walked through the statute — s. 62 (the definition of ‘warranty’), s. 11(1)(a) and (b) (our s. 13(1) and s. 12(4)) and s. 13 (our s. 15) — and concluded:

“it is quite impossible to suggest that in the year 1906, when these parties made a contract whereby they required that the goods should be common English sainfoin, and the sellers put in a stipulation that they would not give any warranty, express or implied, it was intended that it was always to be understood that they were not making themselves liable in regard to any condition as to the goods or for the consequences of a breach.”

And on the sellers' ‘ambulatory’ reading of the word: “I do not think that these two English commercial men meant ‘warranty’ in a sense of any greater refinement than the breadth of the definition in the Sale of Goods Act.”

Fletcher Moulton L.J. in the Court of Appeal had supplied the definition of ‘condition’ that Indian textbooks still use, and the House approved it:

“There are some [obligations] which go so directly to the substance of the contract, or, in other words, are so essential to its very nature, that their non-performance may fairly be considered by the other party as a substantial failure to perform the contract at all.” Fletcher Moulton L.J., [1910] 2 KB 1003, 1012
Principles evolved
  1. Condition and warranty are different categories with different remedies. A clause that names one does not automatically reach the other.
  2. Exclusion clauses are construed against the party relying on them, and against the statutory background. Where the Act calls something a ‘condition’, a clause speaking only of ‘warranties’ will not exclude it. (Contrast Andrews v. Singer [1934] 1 KB 17, where the draftsman expressly included ‘conditions’ and the exclusion succeeded.)
  3. s. 13 changes the remedy, not the term. This is the answer to ‘once a condition, always a condition’: the classification is permanent; the remedy is what is confined.
  4. Description goes to the root. Delivering a different article is not a collateral matter, and it is emphatically not ‘collateral to the main purpose of such contract’ within the statutory definition of a warranty.
  5. The sub-buyer's damages are recoverable. The buyers recovered what they had to pay their own customers — a straightforward application of the first limb of Hadley v. Baxendale (ICA s. 73).
★ Answering 2015 Q6(a) in five moves
  1. Set the scene in two sentences: sale of ‘common English sainfoin’; ‘giant sainfoin’ delivered; clause excluding warranties only.
  2. Quote Lord Loreburn — the “does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio” passage. This is the heart of the answer.
  3. Explain the statutory scheme: s. 12(2) condition / s. 12(3) warranty / s. 13(1) waiver and election / s. 13(2) acceptance. Point out that s. 13 says the buyer ‘may treat’ and ‘can only be treated as’ — verbs of remedy.
  4. Add the drafting consequence: because the condition survives, an exclusion clause directed only at warranties fails (contrast Andrews v. Singer).
  5. Conclude with the one-liner: “a condition does not become a warranty; only the buyer's remedy is reduced to the warranty remedy.”

§3.5 · Baldry v. Marshall WEB

Baldry v. Marshall
(1925) 1 KB 260 · Court of Appeal (Bankes, Scrutton and Atkin L.JJ.), affirming the county court · English · Named in the DU syllabus immediately below Wallis v. Pratt
Buying under a trade name does not by itself disapply s. 16(1). The question is whether the buyer relied on the seller — and the trade-name proviso never excludes merchantable quality.
Facts

The plaintiff, Mr. Baldry, went to the defendant, Marshall, a motor-car dealer, and told him that he wanted a comfortable car suitable for touring. He made his purpose plain: he needed a fast, easily managed and comfortable car for long-distance travel.

Marshall recommended an eight-cylinder Bugatti. A contract was concluded for “an eight cylinder Bugatti car fully equipped and finished to standard specification as per the car inspected”. The written contract contained a clause disclaiming “any guarantee or warranty, statutory or otherwise”.

The car, when delivered, proved uncomfortable and unsuitable for touring. Baldry claimed to reject it and recover his money.

Issues
  1. Did the buyer make known a particular purpose and rely on the seller's skill or judgment, within s. 14(1) of the Sale of Goods Act 1893 (our s. 16(1))?
  2. Did the fact that the car was bought under its trade name (Bugatti) bring the proviso into play and exclude the implied condition of fitness?
  3. Did the exclusion clause assist the dealer?
Arguments
For the buyer (plaintiff)
  • He stated his purpose in terms: a comfortable car suitable for touring. That is a particular purpose, expressly made known.
  • He bought on the dealer's recommendation. He knew nothing of Bugattis; the dealer did.
  • The proviso cannot apply where the buyer did not form his own judgment but relied on the seller's.
For the dealer (defendant)
  • The car was bought by its trade name — ‘Bugatti’ — and the proviso to s. 14(1) therefore excludes any implied condition as to fitness for a particular purpose.
  • In any event the contract expressly excluded “any guarantee or warranty, statutory or otherwise”.
  • The specification was “as per the car inspected”, and the car delivered conformed to it.
Decision

Judgment for the buyer. The Court of Appeal (Bankes, Scrutton and Atkin L.JJ.) unanimously affirmed the county court's ruling, holding that the car's unsuitability breached the implied condition under s. 14(1), and that Baldry was entitled to reject the car and recover his payment.

The test is: “Did the buyer themselves, in purchasing the goods by name, form the judgment that the goods would be suitable for their own purpose, without reliance on the seller?” If so, no term as to fitness is implied. If there was still an indication of purpose and reliance, then such a term may be implied. The mere fact that goods are described by trade name does not necessarily exclude the implied term regarding suitability of purpose. Baldry v. Marshall, [1925] 1 KB 260

On the exclusion clause, the court followed Wallis v. Pratt: a clause that spoke of ‘guarantee or warranty’ did not reach the statutory condition. Scrutton L.J. added the point about objective assessment: fitness is to be judged against the stated purpose, not by what the seller believed he was selling.

Principles evolved
  1. Trade name is not a magic word. The proviso to s. 16(1) applies only where the buyer, buying by name, formed his own judgment and did not rely on the seller.
  2. The gravamen of s. 16(1) is reliance, not naming. If the buyer asks for a car for touring and the dealer recommends one, reliance is present even though the car is identified by brand.
  3. The proviso reaches only fitness, never merchantability. Where goods are sold under their trade name, s. 16(2) still applies. So even a buyer who fails on s. 16(1) may succeed on s. 16(2) — a valuable fall-back in a problem question.
  4. Exclusion clauses are read against the statutory categories. ‘Guarantee or warranty’ does not catch a condition.
  5. Fitness is objectively assessed — against the purpose the buyer disclosed, not against the seller's intentions.
☕ The same problem in an Indian showroom

You tell a dealer you need a bike for daily commuting in Delhi traffic with a pillion rider and ask for mileage above 55 km/l. He recommends a ‘Splendour X-Pro 120’ by name. It gives 38 km/l and the pillion seat is unusable. On Baldry, naming the model does not disapply s. 16(1): you disclosed the purpose and relied on him. And even if it did, s. 16(2) merchantability — fit for the ordinary purpose such bikes are bought for — survives the proviso.

§3.6 · Bristol Tramways & Carriage Co. Ltd. v. Fiat Motors Ltd. WEB

Bristol Tramways and Carriage Co. Ltd. v. Fiat Motors Ltd.
[1910] 2 KB 831 · Court of Appeal (Cozens-Hardy M.R. and Farwell L.J.) · English · Last authority named in the DU syllabus for Topic 6
The classic statement of what ‘merchantable quality’ means — and a warning not to decide a codifying statute by citing pre-code cases.
Facts

The plaintiffs, a tramway and carriage company operating in Bristol, bought a Fiat omnibus chassis from the defendants. The chassis was bought ‘for the road’ — to be used for the conveyance of passengers around Bristol, in heavy and hilly traffic conditions.

The chassis proved unfit for that purpose: it broke down too often, being too slightly built for heavy city traffic.

Issues
  1. Was there an implied condition that the omnibus should be reasonably fit for the declared purpose?
  2. Were the chassis of merchantable quality?
  3. What is the proper approach to construing a codifying statute?
Arguments
For the buyers (plaintiffs)
  • The purpose was expressly declared — a bus chassis for heavy passenger work in a hilly city — and the buyers relied on Fiat's judgment.
  • A chassis that repeatedly breaks down in that use is neither reasonably fit for the purpose nor merchantable as a bus chassis.
For the sellers (defendants)
  • The chassis was a standard Fiat product, well made, and the defects complained of were minor.
  • Goods may be reasonably fit for a purpose despite minor defects; the buyers were asking for perfection.
  • The pre-1893 common law, properly understood, did not imply the term contended for, and the older authorities should govern.
Decision

Judgment for the buyers. There was an implied condition that the omnibus should be reasonably fit for the declared purpose. Buses manufactured by the defendants which were too slightly built for heavy city traffic were both not fit for purpose and not of merchantable quality. The Court added the important qualification, however, that goods may be reasonably fit for a purpose despite minor defects — the standard is reasonableness, not perfection.

“The phrase in s. 14, sub-s. (2), is, in my opinion, used as meaning that the article is of such quality and in such condition that a reasonable man acting reasonably would after a full examination accept it under the circumstances of the case in performance of his offer to buy that article, whether he buys for his own use or to sell it again.” Farwell L.J., Bristol Tramways, [1910] 2 KB 831, 839

And on method, Cozens-Hardy M.R. delivered the warning that every student of a codified Act should remember:

“I rather deprecate the citation of earlier decisions… The object and intent of the statute… was, no doubt, simply to codify the unwritten law applicable to the sale of goods, but in so far as there is an express statutory enactment, that alone must be looked at and must govern the rights of the parties, even though the section may to some extent have altered the prior common law.” Cozens-Hardy M.R., at p. 836
Principles evolved
  1. The merchantability formula. Farwell L.J.'s ‘reasonable man acting reasonably would after a full examination accept it’ is still the working definition used in India, because the 1930 Act never replaced the phrase.
  2. It covers resale as well as use. ‘Whether he buys for his own use or to sell it again’ — so a trader can rely on s. 16(2) as much as a consumer.
  3. Fitness and merchantability can be broken together. Goods too lightly built for the declared purpose were both.
  4. Minor defects do not destroy merchantability. The standard is reasonable fitness — an important limit, and the reason a scratched drip tray is a warranty and not a condition.
  5. Codified law is construed from the code. Where the Act speaks, pre-Act common law yields.
🔗 Two lines that connect the four English cases
  • Priest gives you purpose by implication; Baldry gives you reliance despite a trade name; Bristol Tramways gives you the merchantability standard. Together they are the complete s. 16 answer.
  • Wallis v. Pratt gives you condition ≠ warranty and exclusion clauses; Niblett gives you title and legal saleability. Together they are the complete ss. 14–15 and s. 62 answer.

§3.7 · Case 31 — Jones v. Just DU p. 210

31. Jones v. Just
(1868) 3 Q.B. 197 · Court of Queen's Bench (Mellor J., delivering the judgment of the Court; Blackburn J. at nisi prius) · English · DU Reader p. 210 · headnote under the syllabus rubric “Doctrine of Caveat Emptor”
“Where there is no opportunity to inspect the commodity the maxim, caveat emptor, does not apply” — and the measure of damages gives the buyer the benefit of a rising market.
Facts

The plaintiffs, through their brokers Messrs. Beneke & Co., contracted with the defendant for the purchase of a quantity of Manila hemp, ‘to arrive’. The shipping documents were duly delivered and the price was paid.

All the vessels named in the contract arrived in due course, with the respective numbers of bales having marks corresponding to those specified in the contract on board, and the bales were delivered to the plaintiffs.

On examination it was found that the whole of those marked J.H.V. were in such a state as to afford strong evidence that they had at some time — probably from a shipwreck when on the voyage from Manilla to Singapore — been wetted through with salt water, had afterwards been unpacked and dried, and then repacked in the bales which were afterwards shipped at Singapore.

Manila hemp is divided into several qualities. The hemp in the bales in question, if in good condition, would have been what is called “fair current Manilla hemp” — not the lowest quality. In all the bales the hemp was damaged to some extent, though not so far as to make it lose the character of hemp.

After correspondence, the hemp was sold by auction by the plaintiffs' orders as “Manilla hemp, with all faults”, and realised about 75 per cent of the price which similar hemp would have fetched if undamaged. The price of hemp had risen considerably since the contract, so that the proceeds of the sale were very nearly equal to the invoice price.

There was no attempt to show that the defendant knew of the state in which the hemp had been shipped at Singapore.

Issues
  1. In a contract to supply goods of a specified description which the buyer has no opportunity to inspect, is there an implied term that the goods shall be not merely of that description but merchantable under it?
  2. Does caveat emptor apply to such a contract?
  3. If there was a breach, what is the measure of damages?
Arguments
For the plaintiffs (buyers)
  • The sale was of goods to arrive, and neither party had any opportunity of inspecting them before shipment. That is the whole commercial premise of the contract.
  • Where goods are supplied under a particular description and cannot be inspected, the cases establish an implied term that they must be saleable or merchantable under that description: Gardiner v. Gray (waste silk — “a right to expect a saleable article answering the description in the contract”); Laing v. Fidgeon; Shepherd v. Pybus; Bigge v. Parkinson.
  • The fifth class of case identified by the Court is precisely this one: a seller who supplies goods the buyer has had no opportunity of inspecting must supply a merchantable article.
  • The defendant sold to arrive but purchased from the shippers, and “must at least have purchased from them, and had recourse against them for not supplying an article reasonably merchantable”. There is no hardship in passing that liability down the chain.
  • On damages: the true measure puts the plaintiffs “in the position in which they would have been if the contract had been fulfilled” — i.e. the difference between the value on arrival and the value the hemp would have had if shipped sound, which gives them the benefit of the rise in the market.
For the defendant (seller) — Mr. Brett
  • At the close of the plaintiffs' case, Mr. Brett contended that in point of law, under this written contract, “there was no further condition or warranty than that the bales on their arrival should answer the description of bales of Manilla hemp, which they did, as was proved by the fact that the hemp, though sold with a stigma upon it, fetched a price only 25 per cent below that of sound hemp”.
  • “as to quality or condition there was no warranty; that consequently the maxim caveat emptor applied.”
  • There was no fraud; both parties were equally ignorant of the past history and actual condition of the article; neither had the opportunity of inspecting it. It was therefore the duty of the vendees to stipulate for a merchantable article if that was what they intended to contract for.
  • On the authorities, this was simply the sale of a specific article by a person who was not the manufacturer or producer, in which even a latent defect not discoverable on examination is the buyer's risk: Parkinson v. Lee.
  • Turner v. Mucklow — a sale of ‘spent madder’ where the buyer had not inspected before delivery and could not make marketable garancine from it — was exactly this case, and the direction there had been held right.
  • The Scottish statute 19 & 20 Vict. c. 60, s. 5 showed the legislature's own view of the law: without knowledge of a defect, and absent express warranty or an express sale for a specified purpose, “the goods with all faults shall be at the risk of the purchaser”.
  • Chancellor Kent's opinion (Commentaries, vol. II) supported the seller.
  • On damages, he contended the measure should at most be the difference between the value of the article actually delivered (fair average Manilla hemp in a damaged state) and the value of sound Manilla hemp of the lowest quality which might have been supplied at Singapore.
Decision — per Mellor J.

Rule discharged. The Court held that “Blackburn J.'s direction was substantially correct”, that “the maxim of caveat emptor cannot apply”, and that the damages had been correctly measured.

(a) The trial direction. Blackburn J. had told the jury that the question was “whether what was supplied under this contract was, when shipped at Singapore, such as to answer the description of reasonably merchantable Manilla hemp, that being the warranty which, I think, the law implies in a contract to supply, as this is: though it would be different in a sale of specific things which the purchaser might examine, or of things sold by sample.”DU p. 211

(b) The five-fold classification. Mellor J. said that “The cases which bear upon the subject do not appear to be in conflict, when the circumstances of each are considered. They may, we think, be classified as follows”:

(c) The step the Court then took. Having classified the cases, Mellor J. asked why the fifth class should stop at manufacturers:

“If, therefore, it must be taken as established that, on the sale of goods by a manufacturer or dealer to be applied to a particular purpose, it is a term in the contract that they shall reasonably answer that purpose, and that on the sale of an article by a manufacturer to a vendee who has not had the opportunity of inspecting it during the manufacture, that it shall be reasonably fit for use, or shall be merchantable, as the case may be, it is difficult to understand why a similar term is not to be implied on a sale by a merchant to a merchant or dealer who has had no opportunity of inspection.” DU p. 213

The answer, supplied by Bigge v. Parkinson (31 L.J. Ex. 301), was that it is so implied.

(d) The decisive sentence.

“We are aware of no case in which the maxim, caveat emptor, has been applied where there has been no opportunity of inspection, or where that opportunity had not been waived.” Jones v. Just, (1868) 3 Q.B. 197 (DU p. 213)

(e) The holding on the facts.

“It appears to us that, in every contract to supply goods of a specified description which the buyer has no opportunity to inspect, the goods must not only in fact answer the specific description, but must also be saleable or merchantable under that description. In the words of Lord Ellenborough in Gardiner v. Gray (4 Camp. 145), ‘without any particular warranty this is an implied term in every such contract’.” Ibid.

(f) Distinguishing Turner v. Mucklow. In that case the plaintiffs were calico printers who sold the defendant, a drysalter and dye-extract manufacturer, a boat-load of ‘spent madder’ from the refuse heap in their yard. The garancine made from it was unmarketable. Martin B. had said “no direction was ever more correct”. Mellor J. distinguished it on three facts:

“In that case it is to be observed that the defendant had the opportunity, if he had chosen to avail himself of it, to inspect the heap of spent madder; he knew that it was the refuse madder after it had gone through the plaintiffs' processes, and that it was not manufactured for sale. These circumstances entirely distinguish that case from the present.” Ibid. (DU p. 214)

(g) The Scottish statute and Chancellor Kent. On 19 & 20 Vict. c. 60, s. 5, Mellor J. observed that it “applies only to Scotland” and that on examination “it does not appear to bear out that view” — all it declares is that a seller without knowledge of a defect “shall not be held to have warranted their quality or sufficiency”. As to Kent: “as the American cases which he cites are generally adverse to his opinion, it can at most be said that the opinion of an eminent writer is opposed to the authority of the cases which he cites”.

(h) Damages. The jury had been told to measure damages “by the rate which the hemp was worth when it arrived compared with the rate which the same hemp would have realised, had it been shipped in the state in which it ought to have been shipped: thus, in effect, giving the plaintiffs the benefit of the rise in the market”. The Court upheld this: “if the contract had the effect which the direction stated it to have, the true measure of the damages was given, as it put the plaintiffs in the position in which they would have been if the contract had been fulfilled”. Mr. Brett's proposed measure — sound hemp of the lowest quality — was rejected.

(i) The reason at the root of it.

“The buyer bought for the purpose of sale, and the seller could not on any other supposition than that the article was merchantable have found a customer for his goods, and the buyer must be taken to have trusted to the judgment, knowledge, and information of the seller, as it is clear that he could exercise no judgment of his own; and this appears to us to be at the root of the doctrine of implied warranty, and… it makes no difference, whether the sale is of goods specially appropriated to a particular contract, or to goods purchased as answering a particular description.” Ibid. (DU p. 215)
Principles evolved
  1. Caveat emptor requires an opportunity to inspect. This is the master-principle, and it is the sentence the Faculty has put in your reader for a reason. “Where there is no opportunity to inspect the commodity the maxim, caveat emptor, does not apply” (Lord Ellenborough, quoted at p. 213).
  2. Description carries merchantability with it. In every contract to supply goods of a specified description which the buyer cannot inspect, the goods must both answer the description and be merchantable under it. That is the ancestor of India's ss. 15 and 16(2) read together.
  3. The five-fold classification is still the map. It tells you exactly when caveat emptor applies (classes 1–3) and when an implied term arises (classes 4–5).
  4. No opportunity of inspection is the organising idea, not the identity of the seller. Mellor J. expressly extended the manufacturer's implied term to “a sale by a merchant to a merchant”.
  5. Reliance is inferred from helplessness. “the buyer must be taken to have trusted to the judgment, knowledge, and information of the seller, as it is clear that he could exercise no judgment of his own” — the direct ancestor of Lord Wright's reliance reasoning in Grant.
  6. Damages put the buyer in the position of performance. Where the market has risen, the buyer gets the benefit; the measure is not limited to the cheapest conforming substitute.
  7. Ignorance is no defence for the seller. “There was no attempt to shew that the defendant knew” — and it made no difference. The implied term is strict, not fault-based. (Contrast the manufacturer's liability, which is in tort and which Grant held did require negligence.)
★ The one-line value of Jones v. Just

It is the Faculty's chosen authority for the Doctrine of Caveat Emptor — a separate rubric in your syllabus. Use it for two things and nothing else: (i) the sentence that ‘caveat emptor has never been applied where there was no opportunity of inspection’; and (ii) the five-fold classification, which is the cleanest statement in the books of when the rule applies and when it does not.

§3.8 · Case 32 — Richard Thorold Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills, Ltd. DU p. 216

§ Why this is the most important case in Topic 6

The Faculty has placed Grant last in the list, and it is the capstone. It is the only case in the syllabus that decides both implied conditions in s. 16 on one set of facts; it states the Indian meaning of ‘merchantable’; it settles that goods picked off a shop counter are still sold by description; it construes the trade-name proviso narrowly; and it is the bridge from contract to tort through Donoghue v. Stevenson. If you learn one case from Topic 6, learn this one.

32. Richard Thorold Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills, Ltd.
AIR 1936 PC 34 · [1936] AC 85 · Privy Council (Lord Wright, delivering the advice of the Board) · On appeal from the High Court of Australia, restoring Sir George Murray C.J. · DU Reader p. 216 · marginal note in the reader: “Section 16 — Reliance by buyer on seller's skill”
Woollen underwear carrying hidden sulphites: both implied conditions broken; the retailer liable in contract without negligence, the manufacturer liable in tort for it.
Facts

The plaintiff. The appellant was “a fully qualified medical man practising at Adelaide in South Australia”.

The purchase. On 3 June 1931 he bought from the shop of the respondents John Martin & Co., Ltd. (the retailers, who dealt in such goods) two pairs of underpants and two singlets. The retailers had in ordinary course bought them with other stock from the respondents Australian Knitting Mills, Ltd. (the manufacturers). The garments were of the manufacturers' make known as ‘Golden Fleece’.

The onset. He put one suit on the morning of Sunday, 28 June 1931. By the evening he felt itching on the ankles; no objective symptoms appeared until the next day, when redness appeared on each ankle, in front, over an area of about 2½ inches by 1½ inches. He treated himself with calamine lotion but scratched until he bled.

The second suit. On Sunday, 5 July he changed into the other set; the first was washed. He changed again the following Sunday into the washed set and sent the others to the wash; he changed again on 12 July.

The diagnosis. His skin trouble got worse but he did not attribute it to the underwear until 13 July, when he consulted a dermatologist, Dr. Upton, who advised him to discard the underwear. He did so, returning the garments to the retailers with the intimation that they had given him dermatitis. By then one set had been washed twice and the other once.

The illness. He got worse and worse: confined to bed from 21 July for seventeen weeks; the rash became generalised and very acute. In November he became convalescent and went to New Zealand to recuperate; he returned the following February and resumed practice, but soon relapsed; by March his condition was so serious that in April he went into hospital, where he remained until July. Dr. Upton was his medical attendant throughout; Dr. de Crespigny also attended from 22 July 1931. “The illness was most severe, involving acute suffering and at times Dr. Upton feared that his patient might die.”

The litigation. He commenced the action in April 1932; it was tried in and after November 1932, and the trial lasted twenty days. Sir George Murray, Chief Justice of South Australia, gave judgment against both respondents for £2,450 and costs. The High Court of Australia set that judgment aside by a majority: Evatt J. dissented (agreeing with the Chief Justice); Dixon J., with whom McTiernan J. concurred, thought the evidence was “not sufficient to make it safe to find for the appellant” (not that there was none); Starke J. accepted substantially all the Chief Justice's detailed findings but differed from him on his general conclusions of liability.

The cause of action pleaded was that the disease “was caused by the presence in the cuffs or ankle ends of the underpants … of an irritating chemical, viz., free sulphite, the presence of which was due to negligence in manufacture, and also involved on the part of the retailers a breach of the relevant implied conditions under the Sale of Goods Act.”

Issues
  1. Was the dermatitis of external origin, or was it dermatitis herpetiformis (generally regarded as of internal origin)?
  2. Was the appellant's skin normal, or was he abnormally sensitive — so that the mischief was “attributable to the appellant's own physical defect and not to any defect in the garments”?
  3. Was there in the garments “a detrimental quantity of any mischievous chemical”?
  4. Were the retailers liable in contract under s. 14 of the South Australian Sale of Goods Act, 1895 (in identical terms with s. 14 of the English Act of 1893 — which is our s. 16)?
  5. Were the manufacturers liable in tort, there being no privity of contract between them and the appellant?
Arguments
For the appellant (the doctor / buyer)
  • The disease was external in origin. Dr. Wigley, “a very eminent dermatologist”, was “confident that in the appellant's case the origin of the disease was external”; and the symptoms and course of the disease, described by the two doctors who attended him, were decisive against dermatitis herpetiformis — that disease “present[s] in general certain characteristic features, in particular bullae or blisters and symmetrical grouping of the inflammatory features, which were never present in the appellant”.
  • The place and time of the original infection point to the garments: the inflammation began “at the front of the shins where the skin is drawn tight over the bone, and where the cuff of the pants presses tightly under the socks against the skin, and began about nine or ten hours after the pants were first put on”.
  • It was admitted in the respondents' Answers to Interrogatories that the garments, when delivered by the manufacturers to the retailers, contained sulphur dioxide — and the presence of sulphur dioxide indicated the presence of free sulphites. Sweat “combines with the free sulphites to form successively sulphur dioxide, sulphurous acid and sulphuric acid” — “an irritant which would produce dermatitis in a normal skin if applied in garments under the conditions existing”.
  • Professor Hicks, “a very eminent scientist”, gave his opinion that the garments before washing must have had sulphites in considerably greater quantity; and Mr. Anderson's tests were of each garment as a whole, whereas the relevant parts were the ankle ends.
  • His skin was normal: he “had habitually up to the material time worn woolen undergarments without inconvenience”, and his doctors' experiment — placing a piece of scoured wool on a clear area of his skin — produced “no trace of irritation”.
  • The retailers are liable under both exceptions to s. 14: the garments were “naturally intended and only intended to be worn next the skin”, so the purpose was made known by implication; and they were not merchantable.
For the respondents — Mr. Greene
  • The dermatitis was “initially produced and was of the type described as herpetiformis, which is generally regarded as of internal origin” — a contention “strenuously advanced”.
  • Alternatively the disease “may have been contracted by the appellant from some external irritant the presence of which argued no imperfection in the garments but which only did harm because of the appellant's peculiar susceptibility”: mechanical irritation of the wool, or a chemical “something in itself harmless … so that the mischief was attributable to the appellant's own physical defect and not to any defect in the garments”. “the respondents … could not be held responsible for anything in the garments which would not be harmful in normal use.”
  • The appellant had suffered from tuberculosis, which “weakens the resistance of the skin and lowers the patient's vitality” — though “this contention did not appear to be established”.
  • It was “crucial” for the appellant to prove by positive evidence that the garments he wore contained an excess of free sulphites. His case “involved arguing in a circle”: the garments must have caused the dermatitis because they contained excess sulphites, and must have contained excess sulphites because they caused the dermatitis — “nought added to nought still is no more than nought”.
  • Mr. Anderson's analyses found only 0.0070, 0.0082 and 0.0201 per cent sulphur dioxide, and a nil return in one singlet — “not such as to show quantities likely to cause irritation”. Dr. Hargreaves, for the manufacturers, found “only negligible quantities”.
  • The manufacturers' secretary deposed that in the previous six years they had treated by a similar process 4,737,600 garments, sold to drapers throughout Australia, “and he had no recollection of any complaints”.
  • As to the manufacturers: there is no privity of contract between them and the appellant. “the manufacturers … parted once and for all with the garments when they sold them to the retailers”; “at no time … had they any knowledge of the existence of the appellant”; the contractual relationships “covered the whole field and excluded any question of tort liability”.
  • Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932 AC 562) is distinguishable: it was “a case of food or drink to be consumed internally”; and there the makers “had retained ‘control’ over it in the sense that they had placed it in stoppered and sealed bottles, so that it would not be tampered with”, whereas these garments “were merely put into paper packets, each containing six sets”.
  • If Donoghue were “extended even a hairsbreadth, no line could be drawn” — he instanced a foundry that had cast a rudder for a liner with a latent defect, which broke years later, wrecking the vessel: “the maker of the rudder would be held liable for damages of an indefinite amount, after an indefinite time and to claimants indeterminate until the event”.
Decision — per Lord Wright

Appeal allowed with costs here and in the Courts below; the judgment of the Chief Justice restored against both respondents; the petition for leave to adduce further evidence dismissed without costs.

(1) External origin — yes. “their Lordships are of opinion that the disease was of external origin.” The herpetiformis contention “may now be taken to have failed: it has been rejected by the Chief Justice at the trial and in the High Court, by Starke and Evatt, JJ., and, in effect also, by Dixon and McTiernan, JJ.”

(2) Normal skin — yes. The Chief Justice's finding was upheld: he habitually wore wool next to the skin without inconvenience; the scoured-wool experiment produced no irritation; the tuberculosis contention “did not appear to be established”; and the admitted fact that his skin had since become ‘allergic’ “could throw no light on the original skin condition”.

(3) Free sulphite — yes, and on circumstantial evidence. Mr. Greene's ‘nought added to nought’ argument was rejected in one of the most quotable passages in the reader:

“This, however, does not do justice either to the process of reasoning by way of probable inference which has to do so much in human affairs or to the nature of circumstantial evidence in law Courts. Mathematical, or strict logical demonstration is generally impossible: juries are in practice told that they must act on such reasonable balance of probabilities as would suffice to determine a reasonable man to take a decision in the grave affairs of life. Pieces of evidence, each by itself insufficient, may together constitute a significant whole, and justify by their combined effect a conclusion.” Grant, DU p. 220

And: “it is admitted … that some sulphites were present in the garments, and there is nothing to exclude the possibility of a quantity sufficient to do the harm”. The onus was on a defendant who contends on appeal that a judgment should be upset: “he has to show that it is wrong”. The Board was “not satisfied … that the Chief Justice was wrong”.

(4) The retailers — liable in contract, under BOTH exceptions. Mr. Greene had conceded liability under exception (ii) (merchantable quality) but not (i) (fitness). Lord Wright held both, “out of deference to the views expressed in the Court below”.

On reliance — exception (i):

“The reliance will seldom be express; it will usually arise by implication from the circumstances; thus to take a case like that in question of a purpose from a retailer the reliance will be in general inferred from the fact that a buyer goes to the shop in the confidence that the tradesman has selected his stock with skill and judgment; the retailer need know nothing about the process of manufacture; it is immaterial whether he be manufacturer or not; the main inducement to deal with a good retail shop is the expectation that the tradesman will have brought the right goods of a good make… there is no need to specify in terms the particular purpose for which the buyer requires the goods; which is nonetheless the particular purpose within the meaning of the section because it is the only purpose for which anyone would ordinarily want the goods.” Grant, DU p. 221–222

On the trade-name proviso:

“The proviso does not apply to a case like the sale of Golden Fleece make such as is here in question, because Golden Fleece is rather a patent or trade name within the meaning of the proviso to Excep. (i). With great deference to Dixon J. their Lordships think that the requirements of Excep. (i) were complied with.” Ibid., p. 221

(The word ‘rather’ in the reader is a printing slip for ‘not’ — “Golden Fleece is not a patent or trade name” is what the sentence means, and is what the Board held.)

On merchantability — exception (ii):

“whatever else merchantable may mean it does mean that the article sold, if only meant for one particular use in ordinary course, is fit for that use; merchantable does not mean that the thing is saleable in the market simply because it looks all right. It is not merchantable in that event if it has defects unfitting it for its only proper use but not apparent on ordinary examination… The appellant was satisfied by the appearance of the underpants; he could not detect and had no reason to suspect the hidden presence of the sulphites; the garments were saleable in the sense that the appellant or anyone similarly situated and who did not know of their defect, would readily buy them but they were not merchantable in the statutory sense because their defect rendered them unfit to be worn next the skin. The proviso to Excep. (ii) does not apply where, as in this case, no examination that the buyer could or would normally have made would have revealed the defect.” Ibid., p. 222

And on the overlap:

“In effect the implied condition of being fit for the particular purpose for which they are required and [the] implied condition of being merchantable produce in cases of this type the same result.” Ibid.

On sale by description:

“there is a sale by description even though the buyer is buying something displayed before him on the counter: a thing is sold by description, though it is specific, so long as it is sold not merely as the specific thing but as a thing corresponding to a description, e.g., woolen undergarments, a hot water bottle, a second-hand reaping machine, to select a few obvious illustrations.” Ibid.

Finally, on the standard of liability: “so far as [the retailers] are concerned, no question of negligence is relevant to the liability in contract.”

(5) The manufacturers — liable in tort. “between them the liability, if any, must be in tort, and the gist of the cause of action is negligence.”

“According to the evidence, the method of manufacture was correct; the danger of excess sulphites being left was recognised and was guarded against: the process was intended to be fool proof. If excess sulphites were let in the garment, that could only be because someone was at fault. The appellant is not required to lay his finger on the exact person in all the chain who was responsible or to specify what he did wrong. Negligence is found as a matter of inference from the existence of the defects taken in connexion with all the known circumstances: even if the manufacturers could by apt evidence have rebutted that inference they have not done so.” Ibid.

The manufacturing process itself mattered: of the six processes, the second (shrinking, with calcium hypochloride and hydrochloric acid), the third (removal with a solution of bisulphite of soda) and the fourth (neutralisation with bicarbonate of soda) were decisive. “If the fourth process did not neutralise the added bisulphite, free sulphites would remain, which the subsequent washing might not entirely remove.” The foreman scourer Smith was not called; the scourer Ashworth said “they had to be very careful that there was no excess of one chemical or the other”. Professor Hicks's experiment — agitating a singlet in unheated water for two minutes and finding 0.11 per cent sulphite — showed that “however well designed the manufacturers' proved systems may be to eliminate deleterious substances it may not invariably work according to plan. Some employee may blunder.”

On Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932 AC 562). The Board applied it, quoting Lord Atkin at p. 599 — a manufacturer who sells products “in such a form as to show that he intends them to reach the ultimate consumer in the form in which they left him with no reasonable possibility of intermediate examination.” Every one of Mr. Greene's attempts to distinguish it failed:

  • Food versus external application: “No distinction, however can be logically drawn for this purpose between a noxious thing taken internally and a noxious thing applied externally… indeed Lord Atkin specifically puts as examples of what is covered by the principle he is enunciating things operating externally, such as ‘an ointment, a soap, a cleaning fluid or cleaning powder’.”
  • Control / sealed bottle: “The decision in (1932) AC 562 did not depend on the bottle being stoppered and sealed: the essential point in this regard was that the article should reach the consumer or user subject to the same defect as it had when it left the manufacturer.” And “Nothing happened between the making of the garments and their being worn to change their condition” — they were “made by the manufacturers for the purpose of being worn exactly as they were worn in fact”, and “it was not contemplated that they should be first washed”.
  • ‘Privity’ and ‘proximity’: “the use of the word ‘privity’ in this connexion is apt to mislead because of the suggestion of some overt relationship like that in contract… proximity can only properly be used to exclude any element of remoteness”. “Equally also may the word ‘control’ embarrass” — it is used only “to emphasise the essential factor that the consumer must use the article exactly as it left the maker, that is in all material features, and use it as it was intended to be used”.
  • Indefinite liability: the rudder hypothetical was met with remoteness and directness: “So many contingencies must have intervened between the lack of care on the part of the makers and the casualty that it may be that the law would apply… the rule that cause and effect must not be too remote: in any case the element of directness would obviously be lacking.” And “It is enough now to say that their Lordships hold the present case to come within the principle of (1932) AC 562.”
  • Two causes of action, one damage: “It is immaterial that the appellant has a claim in contract against the retailers, because that is a quite independent cause of action, based on different considerations, even though the damage may be the same. Equally irrelevant is any question of liability between the retailers and the manufacturers on the contract of sale between them. The tort liability is independent of any question of contract.”
Principles evolved
  1. Reliance is usually inferred, and inferred from the shop itself. “A buyer goes to the shop in the confidence that the tradesman has selected his stock with skill and judgment.” There is no need to prove an express statement of reliance.
  2. Goods with one ordinary use disclose the purpose by implication. Underwear is “naturally intended and only intended to be worn next the skin”, and that is the ‘particular purpose’ within s. 16(1). This is Priest v. Last applied.
  3. The trade-name proviso is narrow. A brand or make name such as ‘Golden Fleece’ is not enough to exclude the implied condition where the buyer relied on the seller. This is Baldry v. Marshall applied.
  4. ‘Merchantable’ means fit for the purpose, not merely saleable. “merchantable does not mean that the thing is saleable in the market simply because it looks all right” — the sentence to quote whenever s. 16(2) is in issue.
  5. The proviso to s. 16(2) protects only what examination could have revealed. A hidden chemical is outside it.
  6. There is a sale by description even over the counter. Goods displayed in a shop are still sold as ‘a thing corresponding to a description’ — so s. 15 and s. 16(2) both apply to ordinary retail purchases.
  7. Fitness and merchantability overlap and usually produce the same result in the single-use-goods case.
  8. Contractual liability under s. 16 is strict. “no question of negligence is relevant to the liability in contract”. The retailer is liable though he could not possibly have detected the defect.
  9. The manufacturer's liability is in tort and does require negligence — but negligence may be inferred from the existence of the defect taken with all the known circumstances; the plaintiff need not identify the individual at fault.
  10. The contract and tort claims are independent and may be pursued together for the same damage, without double recovery.
  11. Circumstantial evidence suffices. “Pieces of evidence, each by itself insufficient, may together constitute a significant whole” — a proposition about evidence that travels well beyond sale of goods.
  12. A latent defect is the gateway to Donoghue. The principle applies “only where the defect is hidden and unknown to the consumer, otherwise the directness of cause and effect is absent”.
☕ 2017 Q5(b) — the dermatitis problem, answered

The question: “R purchased a coat and caught dermatitis by using it. This disease he caught due to abnormal sensitivity of his skin. Can he claim damages from the seller under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? Refer case law.”

This is Grant with one fact reversed. In Grant the Board was at pains to find, as a fact, that “the appellant's skin was normal”; the respondents' whole alternative case was that the mischief was “attributable to the appellant's own physical defect and not to any defect in the garments”. So where the hypersensitivity is established, the buyer's claim fails — or, more precisely, it fails on causation: the goods were merchantable (fit for the ordinary purpose) and reasonably fit for the purpose made known; the harm came from the buyer, not the coat. The plea of hypersensitivity is therefore a defence the seller must raise and prove. Note the contrast with Griffiths v. Peter Conway Ltd. [1939] 1 All ER 685, where a buyer with abnormally sensitive skin failed because she had not made her susceptibility known so as to show reliance on the seller's skill. And note that if the coat contained an irritant that would harm a normal skin, the hypersensitive buyer still recovers in full — a defendant takes his victim as he finds him.

! Two traps in writing up Grant
  1. Do not treat it as a negligence case only. The retailer's liability is contractual and strict; the manufacturer's is tortious and fault-based. DU questions that cite Grant are almost always about s. 16, not about Donoghue v. Stevenson.
  2. Do not say the trade-name proviso applied. It was argued and rejected: “The proviso does not apply to a case like the sale of Golden Fleece make.”

Set A — Condition and warranty: the distinction

2013CT · OC Q7 · the fullest version of the question
Question Define the term ‘Condition’. How does it differ from ‘Warranty’? A stipulation may be a condition though called a warranty in the contract. Explain bringing out the distinction between conditions and warranties.
Model answer

1. The statutory frame. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 classifies every stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to the goods into one of two boxes. s. 12(1) sets up the binary: “A stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to goods which are the subject thereof may be a condition or a warranty.”

2. Condition — s. 12(2), verbatim. “A condition is a stipulation essential to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a right to treat the contract as repudiated.” Two elements, therefore: substance (essential to the main purpose) and consequence (repudiation). The classic judicial gloss is Fletcher Moulton L.J.'s in Wallis v. Pratt [1910] 2 KB 1003: an obligation which “goes so directly to the substance of the contract … that their non-performance may fairly be considered by the other party as a substantial failure to perform the contract at all”.

3. Warranty — s. 12(3), verbatim. “A warranty is a stipulation collateral to the main purpose of the contract, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for damages but not to a right to reject the goods and treat the contract as repudiated.”

4. The distinction, in ten rows.

5. The heart of the question — s. 12(4). “Whether a stipulation in a contract of sale is a condition or a warranty depends in each case on the construction of the contract. A stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract.”

This does three things:

  • The label is not conclusive. Parties routinely misuse the words. A clause headed ‘Warranty’ that goes to the root of the contract is a condition.
  • The converse follows by the same logic — a stipulation the parties call a ‘condition’ may, on construction, be only a warranty.
  • Construction is objective. The court asks whether the breach deprives the buyer of substantially the whole benefit; whether damages would be an adequate remedy; whether the term is part of the description (s. 15); whether the contract is severable (s. 38); and whether trade usage or a course of dealing bears on it (ss. 16(3), 62).

6. Illustrations. (i) In Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt & Haynes [1911] AC 394 the contract called the correspondence-with-description obligation a matter of description and excluded only warranties; the House of Lords held the obligation was a condition and the exclusion failed. (ii) In Baldry v. Marshall [1925] 1 KB 260 the contract disclaimed “any guarantee or warranty, statutory or otherwise”; the Court of Appeal held the statutory condition of fitness survived. (iii) In Re Moore & Co. and Landauer & Co. [1921] 2 KB 519 canned fruit contracted in cases of 30 and delivered in cases of 24 was held not to answer the description — a condition, though the parties had said nothing about packing at all.

7. Conclusion. Condition and warranty are categories of remedy as much as categories of importance. The name the parties give is evidence of their intention but never the answer; s. 12(4) reserves the answer to the court.

2017Special Contracts (LB-304) Q4(b) · ‘once a condition, always a condition’
Question Discuss the statement ‘once a condition is always a condition’. How does condition differ from warranty under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930?
Model answer

Part I — the statement. The statement is correct as to classification and incorrect as to remedy, and the answer must say both.

The sense in which it is right. s. 13 never ‘converts’ a condition into a warranty. Lord Loreburn L.C. said so in terms in Wallis, Son & Wells v. Pratt & Haynes [1911] AC 394:

“That does not mean that it was really a breach of warranty or that what was a condition in reality had come to be degraded or converted into a warranty. It does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio, but the injured party may treat it as if it had become so, and he becomes entitled to the remedies which attach to a breach of warranty.”

The sense in which it is wrong. The buyer's remedy does change, in three situations under s. 13:

  1. Waiver (s. 13(1)) — the buyer, knowing of the breach, abandons the right to repudiate.
  2. Election (s. 13(1)) — he chooses to treat the breach as a breach of warranty and keep the goods.
  3. Acceptance (s. 13(2)) — in a non-severable contract, once he has accepted the goods or part of them, the breach “can only be treated as a breach of warranty”, unless a term of the contract, express or implied, says otherwise. Acceptance is defined in s. 42. (Note the 1963 amendment: the words about specific goods whose property had passed were deleted by Act 33 of 1963.)

A fourth situation takes the case outside s. 13 altogether: s. 13(3), where fulfilment of the condition is excused by law by reason of impossibility or otherwise (ICA ss. 32, 56).

Part II — the distinction. Give the eight-row table from Set A1 (s. 12(2) versus s. 12(3); repudiation versus damages; s. 57 versus s. 59; examples from ss. 14, 15, 16, 17).

Part III — conclusion. “Once a condition, always a condition” is a sound description of classification and a misleading description of remedy. The accurate formulation is: once a condition, always a condition — but the buyer may lose, or be deprived of, the right to reject.

Exam technique. The question has two labelled halves and the marks are roughly equal. Candidates routinely write four pages on the distinction and one line on the quotation. Do not: the quotation is the intellectual part of the question and it is where the marks are separated.

Set B — Treating a condition as a warranty; Wallis v. Pratt

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q6(a) · the House of Lords in Wallis v. Pratt
Question Discuss the observations of House of Lords in Wallis Son and Well v/s Pratt (1911) A.C. 394 that “when a condition is reduced to warranty, the effect is not that the condition becomes warranty”.
Model answer

1. The facts in three sentences. The buyers contracted for seed described as ‘common English sainfoin’ under a proviso that “sellers give no warranty, express or implied, as to growth, description, or any other matters”. What was delivered was ‘giant sainfoin’, “a thing as distinct in agricultural knowledge from common English sainfoin as in ordinary commerce a silver watch would be distinct from a gold watch”. The buyers, not realising this, accepted the seed, resold it as common English sainfoin, and had to pay damages to their sub-buyers.

2. Why the point arose at all. Once the buyers had accepted the goods, s. 11(1)(c) of the English Act of 1893 — our s. 13(2) — meant the breach “can only be treated as a breach of warranty”. The sellers therefore argued: the only remedy left is the warranty remedy, and the contract has excluded warranties. Nothing remains to sue on.

3. The observation itself. Lord Loreburn L.C.:

“If a man agrees to sell something of a particular description he cannot require the buyer to take something which is of a different description, and a sale of goods by description implies a condition that the goods shall correspond to it. But if a thing of a different description is accepted in the belief that it is according to the contract, then the buyer cannot return it after having accepted it; but he may treat the breach of the condition as if it were a breach of warranty — that is to say, he may have the remedies applicable to a breach of warranty. That does not mean that it was really a breach of warranty or that what was a condition in reality had come to be degraded or converted into a warranty. It does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio, but the injured party may treat it as if it had become so, and he becomes entitled to the remedies which attach to a breach of warranty.”

4. What the observation means, stated as three propositions.

  1. Classification is permanent. The term was a condition when the contract was made, and it remains one. s. 13 is not a transformation provision.
  2. Remedy is what is confined. The buyer loses the right to reject and retains the right to damages. That is all.
  3. Consequences follow for drafting. Because the term is still a condition, an exclusion clause directed only at ‘warranties’ does not catch it.

5. Lord Alverstone C.J.'s supporting reasoning. He walked through the statute — the definition of warranty in s. 62, s. 11(1)(a) and (b), and s. 13 — and concluded that “it is quite impossible to suggest that … when they used the word ‘warranty’ they intended that they were not making themselves liable in regard to any condition as to the goods”. And on the sellers' argument that commercial men used ‘warranty’ in an ‘ambulatory’ sense: “I do not think that these two English commercial men meant ‘warranty’ in a sense of any greater refinement than the breadth of the definition in the Sale of Goods Act.”

6. The decision. The failure to supply common English sainfoin “amounted to a breach of condition, which notwithstanding the terms of the contract entitled the appellants to recover the amount of their loss”. The Court of Appeal's majority was reversed and the trial judgment restored.

7. Contrast and note. Andrews v. Singer [1934] 1 KB 17 — where the draftsman put into the contract a clause excluding implied conditions, warranties and liabilities, the exclusion succeeded. The lesson is purely one of drafting: say what you mean, and mean what you say. And remember s. 62 of the Indian Act permits exclusion by express agreement, course of dealing or a usage binding both parties — but the clause must, on its construction, reach the term it is said to exclude.

Exam technique. Quote the passage. Then do the one thing nearly everyone omits — explain why the sellers wanted the condition to have become a warranty (because their clause excluded warranties only). That explanation is what turns a recited quote into an answer.

Set C — The buyer's options and remedies

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q2(a) · options on breach of condition
Question What are the options to the buyer on breach of condition by the seller?
Model answer

Where the seller breaks a condition, the buyer has a menu. The options are not mutually exclusive in every combination, and part of the marks lies in saying which combinations are available.

  1. Treat the contract as repudiated and reject the goods — s. 12(2). By s. 43 he need not return them; intimation of rejection is enough. This is the primary option and the defining consequence of a condition.
  2. Claim damages for non-delivery — s. 57, which he may combine with rejection.
  3. Waive the condition — s. 13(1) — and keep the goods.
  4. Elect to treat the breach as a breach of warranty — s. 13(1) — and then either set it up in diminution or extinction of the price (s. 59(1)(a)) or sue for damages (s. 59(1)(b)).
  5. Sue for the price already paid — where the consideration has wholly failed, restitution lies under s. 65 of the ICA; the classic case is Rowland v. Divall [1923] 2 KB 500, where a buyer of a stolen car recovered the whole price, not merely its depreciated value, because he had never received title at all.
  6. Sue for specific performance — s. 58, subject to Chapter II of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, and only for specific or ascertained goods.
  7. Interest and special damages — s. 61 preserves the right to recover interest and special damages wherever they are recoverable by law.
  8. Where the contract is severable — s. 38(2): reject the defective instalment(s) and, depending on the ratio of defective to sound deliveries and the likelihood that future instalments will also be bad, repudiate the whole contract or claim compensation only.

The critical limit. If the contract is not severable and the buyer has accepted the goods or part of them, then by s. 13(2) the breach “can only be treated as a breach of warranty” — options (1), (2) and (6) fall away, and the buyer is left with damages.

Where the condition is one of the implied ones — title (s. 14(a)), description (s. 15), fitness (s. 16(1)), merchantability (s. 16(2)), sample (s. 17(2)) — exactly the same menu applies, and the buyer need show no negligence on the seller's part. “no question of negligence is relevant to the liability in contract” (Grant).

Exam technique. Structure the answer as a menu with a limit: eight options, then the s. 13(2) cut-off. Candidates who list options without the cut-off lose the analytical marks.

2011CT · OC Q8 · caveat emptor + the trade-name statement
Question Explain the rule of ‘Caveat Emptor’ and exceptions thereto, and critically examine the validity of the following statement: “The mere fact that an article sold is described in the contract by its trade-name does not necessarily make the sale, a sale under trade name.”
Model answer

Part I — the rule and the exceptions. As in Sets D1–D3, in compressed form: s. 16's opening words, the six statutory exceptions, fraud, and the Jones v. Just limit. About 200 words.

Part II — the statement. It is correct.

The provision. s. 16(1), proviso: “in the case of a contract for the sale of a specified article under its patent or other trade name, there is no implied condition as to its fitness for any particular purpose.”

The authority. Baldry v. Marshall [1925] 1 KB 260 is directly on the point. The buyer asked a motor dealer for a comfortable car suitable for touring; the dealer recommended an eight-cylinder Bugatti; the car was bought under that trade name and proved unsuitable. The Court of Appeal held the dealer liable, and stated the test:

“The test is: did the buyer themselves, in purchasing the goods by name, form the judgment that the goods would be suitable for their own purpose, without reliance on the seller? If so, no term as to fitness is implied. If there was still an indication of purpose and reliance, then such a term may be implied.”

Why the statement is sound — four reasons.

  1. The gravamen of s. 16(1) is reliance, not naming. The operative words are “so as to show that the buyer relies on the seller's skill or judgment”. Naming a brand is evidentially relevant to reliance but never dispositive.
  2. ‘Specified article’ means an article identified as such by the buyer. Where the buyer asks for a type of goods and the seller selects the brand, the proviso has nothing to operate on.
  3. The Board in Grant said the same thing. “The proviso does not apply to a case like the sale of Golden Fleece make such as is here in question” — the garment was sold under a make name, and the implied condition of fitness nevertheless applied.DU p. 221
  4. The proviso reaches fitness only, never merchantability. Even where the sale is under a trade name and s. 16(1) is therefore excluded, s. 16(2) continues to apply. So the buyer has a fall-back.

The contrary argument, and why it is weaker. The seller would say that a buyer who asks for a Bugatti has made up his own mind, and that the statute should respect that. The answer is that the proviso is already generous enough: it applies whenever the buyer's own judgment was in fact decisive, which is a question of fact in every case.

Conclusion. The statement is valid. The trade-name proviso is a shield for the seller who did not advise, not an amnesty for the seller who did.

Exam technique. “Critically examine” requires you to state both sides and then choose. Give the seller's argument one paragraph, reject it, and conclude. Also distinguish the two provisos: the trade-name proviso belongs to s. 16(1); the examination proviso belongs to s. 16(2). Conflating them is the commonest error in this question.

Set E — Implied conditions: merchantability, fitness, title, and the notes

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q2(b) · merchantable but not fit
Question The goods may be of merchantable quality but not fit for the purpose. Comment with the help of decided cases.
Model answer

1. The two conditions are different in scope.

  • s. 16(2) — merchantable quality asks whether the goods are fit for the ordinary purposes for which goods of that description are bought. It requires no disclosure of purpose and no proof of reliance.
  • s. 16(1) — fitness asks whether the goods are reasonably fit for the particular purpose which the buyer disclosed and on which he relied.

It follows that the two conditions are not co-extensive, and that a buyer can succeed on one and fail on the other.

2. Merchantable but NOT fit — the case the question puts.

☕ The clearest illustration

A pair of ordinary walking shoes is perfectly merchantable: they are well made, they fit, they are fit for the ordinary purpose of footwear. But the buyer told the shop that he needed shoes for a twenty-day Himalayan trek in snow and asked what he should buy. If they are not reasonably fit for that purpose, s. 16(1) is broken although s. 16(2) is not.

The decided cases that establish the possibility:

  • Griffiths v. Peter Conway Ltd. [1939] 1 All ER 685 — a fur coat was merchantable, but the buyer's skin was abnormally sensitive and she had not made that known to the seller; there was therefore no reliance, and the fitness claim failed although nothing was wrong with the coat as a coat. (Conversely, in Grant the Board held that the garment was not merchantable precisely because it was unfit to be worn next the skin — “merchantable does not mean that the thing is saleable in the market simply because it looks all right”.)DU p. 222
  • Bristol Tramways v. Fiat Motors [1910] 2 KB 831 — there the goods failed both tests, which is the useful contrast: buses too slightly built for heavy city traffic were “both not fit for purpose and not of merchantable quality”.
  • Ashington Piggeries v. Christopher Hill [1972] AC 441 — mink food was merchantable as animal feed but contaminated with a substance fatal to mink; the particular purpose (feeding mink) had been made known.

3. Fit but NOT merchantable — the converse, which also happens. Niblett [1921] 3 KB 387 is the authority. The condensed milk was perfectly sound and perfectly fit for consumption — but the labels infringed Nestlé's registered trade mark, so it “could not lawfully be sold”. Bankes L.J.: “The state of the packing affected the merchantable quality of the goods.” So goods may be fit and still unmerchantable.

4. Where they overlap. Lord Wright in Grant: “In effect the implied condition of being fit for the particular purpose for which they are required and [the] implied condition of being merchantable produce in cases of this type the same result” — that is, where the goods have only one ordinary use. Underwear, a hot-water bottle, a reaping machine: fit for the one purpose is the same proposition as merchantable.

5. Conclusion. The two conditions are cumulative, not alternative, and neither implies the other. A well-advised buyer pleads both, because the provisos differ: the trade-name proviso can defeat s. 16(1) but not s. 16(2), while the examination proviso can defeat s. 16(2) but not s. 16(1).

Exam technique. The question is a ‘comment’, which means it invites both directions. Deal with merchantable-but-not-fit (the question as asked) and fit-but-not-merchantable (Niblett), then note the single-use overlap. Three cases, two directions, one overlap — about 400 words.

s. 14(a) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 implies, unless the circumstances of the contract show a different intention, a condition on the part of the seller that in the case of a sale he has a right to sell the goods, and in the case of an agreement to sell he will have a right to sell them at the time when the property is to pass. Because (a) is a condition, breach entitles the buyer to reject and recover the whole price, not merely the difference in value — Rowland v. Lord Wright in Grant v. Note: India still uses ‘merchantable quality’; England moved to ‘satisfactory quality’ in 1994, and the modern English cases are therefore not authority on the Indian text.

(e) Fitness for buyer's purpose

s. 16(1) implies a condition of reasonable fitness for a particular purpose where four things concur: (i) the buyer, expressly or by implication, makes known the particular purpose; (ii) so as to show that he relies on the seller's skill or judgment; (iii) the goods are of a description which it is in the course of the seller's business to supply; and (iv) the goods are not reasonably fit for that purpose. Priest v. Last (1903) 2 KB 148 establishes that the purpose may be made known by implication where the goods have only one ordinary use. Baldry v. Marshall [1925] 1 KB 260 establishes that buying under a trade name does not disapply the section where the buyer in fact relied on the seller. Grant v. AKM establishes that reliance “will seldom be express; it will usually arise by implication from the circumstances” — from the very fact that a buyer goes to a shop in the confidence that the tradesman has selected his stock with skill. Set F — Problem questions

2017Special Contracts (LB-304) Q5(b) · the coat and the dermatitis
Question R purchased a coat and caught dermatitis by using it. This disease he caught due to abnormal sensitivity of his skin. Can he claim damages from the seller under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? Refer case law.
Model answer

Issue. Whether a buyer whose own abnormal skin sensitivity caused the injury can recover from the seller under the implied conditions in s. 16.

Rule 1 — s. 16(1), fitness for purpose. Four elements: purpose made known (expressly or by implication); reliance on the seller's skill or judgment shown to have been brought home to him; goods of a description supplied in the course of the seller's business; and a failure of reasonable fitness. A coat has one ordinary purpose — to be worn — so the purpose is made known by implication (Priest v. Last), and reliance is inferred from going to a shop (Grant).

Rule 2 — the causation question, which is where this problem actually turns. In Grant v. Australian Knitting Mills (AIR 1936 PC 34) the respondents ran precisely this defence, contending that “the mischief was attributable to the appellant's own physical defect and not to any defect in the garments”, and that “the respondents… could not be held responsible for anything in the garments which would not be harmful in normal use”. The Board treated whether the appellant's skin was normal as a distinct and decisive issue, and found as a fact that it was normal. That is the significance of the given fact: it is the finding the buyer needs and does not have.

Application. Here it is given that the dermatitis was “due to abnormal sensitivity of his skin”. Therefore:

  1. the coat was merchantable — it was fit for the ordinary purpose for which coats are bought; and
  2. it was reasonably fit for the purpose made known — it harmed this buyer only because of a peculiarity personal to him.

The loss is therefore caused by the buyer, not by a breach. The claim under s. 16 fails.

Authorities to cite.

  • Griffiths v. Peter Conway Ltd. [1939] 1 All ER 685 — a buyer with abnormally sensitive skin bought a fur coat without disclosing her susceptibility. It was held there was no reliance and no breach: she had not made known the relevant fact “so as to show that she relied on the sellers' skill or judgment”. This is the direct authority against the hypersensitive buyer.
  • Grant v. AKM — by contrast: the skin was normal and the sulphites were present; both implied conditions were broken.
  • Bristol Tramways v. Fiat Motors — goods may be reasonably fit “despite minor defects”; the standard is reasonableness, not perfection.

Important qualifications.

  1. If the coat contained an irritant that would have harmed a normal skin, the buyer recovers in full; a defendant takes his victim as he finds him, and the hypersensitivity affects only the extent of the damage.
  2. If the buyer had disclosed his sensitivity and asked for a coat that would not irritate it, reliance would be established and s. 16(1) would apply.
  3. If the seller had concealed a defect or made a false representation about the material, caveat emptor never applied at all — ICA ss. 17–19, and fraud disables the proviso to s. 16(2).
  4. The buyer may still have a remedy under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 if the coat is a ‘defective’ good within s. 2(9) — a lower threshold than unmerchantability.

Conclusion. On the facts given — no. The abnormality of his own skin is the cause, not the coat; neither s. 16(1) nor s. 16(2) is broken; and Griffiths v. Peter Conway denies him the reliance element. He may recover only if he can show the coat would have injured a normal wearer, or that he disclosed the sensitivity, or fraud.

Exam technique. This is Grant with one fact reversed. Say so in the first sentence — it shows you have read the prescribed case and understood which fact carried it. Then answer on causation, not on merchantability: a candidate who spends the whole answer explaining s. 16(2) has missed the point.
2017Special Contracts (LB-304) Q6(b) · the horse on trial (allied)
Question M took a horse on trial for 8 days. The horse died after 2 days due to the fault of M. Who will bear the risk of loss of the horse under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? Refer judicial precedent.
Model answer

Note: this question sits on the boundary of Topics 6 and 7. It has been asked under the Topic 6 list, and it is a good test of the s. 24 / s. 26 machinery, so it is answered here.

Issue. In whom was the property when the horse died, and on whom does the risk fall?

Rule 1 — s. 24, goods sent on approval or ‘on sale or return’. Property in goods delivered on approval or “on sale or return” or other similar terms passes to the buyer (a) when he signifies his approval or acceptance, or does any other act adopting the transaction; or (b) if he does not signify approval but retains the goods without giving notice of rejection, then, if a time has been fixed for the return, on the expiration of that time, and if no time has been fixed, on the expiration of a reasonable time.

Application of s. 24. A time was fixed — eight days — and the horse died on the second. M had not signified approval and had done no act adopting the transaction. Property therefore remained in the seller.

Rule 2 — s. 26, risk prima facie passes with property. “Unless otherwise agreed, the goods remain at the seller's risk until the property therein is transferred to the buyer, but when the property therein is transferred to the buyer, the goods are at the buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not.” Since property had not passed, the prima facie position is that the seller bears the loss.

Rule 3 — but s. 26 has two provisos, and the second is decisive here.

“Provided also that nothing in this section shall affect the duties or liabilities of either seller or buyer as a bailee of the goods of the other party.”

M was in possession of the horse as a bailee (ICA s. 148). By ICA s. 151 a bailee is bound to take as much care of the goods bailed to him as a man of ordinary prudence would, under similar circumstances, take of his own goods of the same bulk, quality and value; and by s. 152, if he does not, he is responsible for the loss. The question stipulates that the horse died “due to the fault of M” — which is precisely a breach of that duty.

Application and conclusion. M bears the loss. Property had not passed (s. 24), so the risk had not passed with it (s. 26); but that does not help M, because s. 26's second proviso preserves his liability as bailee, and the death occurred through his fault. The seller may therefore recover the value of the horse from M in damages for breach of the bailment.

The judicial precedent — and why it is the mirror image. Elphick v. Barnes (1880) 5 C.P.D. 321: a horse was delivered to the buyer on the terms that he should try it for eight days and might return it at the end of that period. It died on the third day without any fault of the buyer. The seller's action for the price failed. Brett L.J. held there was no sale at the moment of death — “the option was his at the moment of the horse's death” — and applied res perit domino: “the maxim of res perit domino applies, I think, in such a case much more reasonably as against the unpaid contingent vendor than as against the possible vendee still having an option to return at the end of a period not yet expired.”

Therefore: without fault → the seller bears the loss (Elphick v. Barnes); with fault → the buyer bears it (s. 26, second proviso, read with ICA ss. 151–152). The single given fact that changes the answer is the word ‘fault’.

Exam technique. Always state the one fact on which the answer turns. Here it is the word ‘fault’, and saying so explicitly is worth more than the conclusion. Also cite the maxim res perit domino — the thing perishes to its owner — which is the Latin behind s. 26.

§5.4 · Ten mistakes DU students make

Swipe the table sideways to see all columns →
#The mistakeWhat to write instead
1Treating the parties' label as decisive. Quote s. 12(4): “a stipulation may be a condition, though called a warranty in the contract”. Classification is for the court.
2Saying s. 13 ‘converts’ a condition into a warranty. It changes the remedy only. Lord Loreburn: “It does not become degraded into a warranty ab initio”.
3Forgetting s. 42 when applying s. 13(2). ‘Acceptance’ has a statutory definition — intimation, an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership, or retention beyond a reasonable time.
4Applying the trade-name proviso automatically. Baldry v. Marshall: it applies only where the buyer formed his own judgment without relying on the seller. And it never excludes s. 16(2).
5Equating ‘merchantable’ with ‘saleable’. Lord Wright: “merchantable does not mean that the thing is saleable in the market simply because it looks all right” — and Niblett shows goods can be unmerchantable because they cannot lawfully be sold.
6Citing modern English ‘satisfactory quality’ cases. India still uses ‘merchantable quality’ in s. 16(2). The 1994 UK amendment has no Indian counterpart.
7Asserting that s. 15 requires the buyer never to have seen the goods. Grant: “there is a sale by description even though the buyer is buying something displayed before him on the counter”.
8Saying the buyer must prove the seller was negligent. Grant: “no question of negligence is relevant to the liability in contract”. Negligence matters only in the tort claim against the manufacturer.
9Ignoring the examination proviso when it helps, and invoking it when it does not. It removes only those defects the examination ought to have revealed. A hidden chemical survives it. Concealment by the seller disables it altogether.
10Answering a ‘caveat emptor’ question with the rule alone. The question is always about the exceptions. Give all six, with a case each, plus fraud and express terms, plus the Jones v. Just limit and caveat venditor.

§5.5 · Three answer templates

Template 1 — ‘Condition or warranty?’
  1. Quote ss. 12(2) and 12(3) verbatim.
  2. State s. 12(4) — construction, not label.
  3. Run the six signposts (root of the contract, adequacy of damages, part of the description, severability, usage, course of dealing).
  4. Ask whether s. 13 has confined the remedy.
  5. State the remedy and its section (s. 57 / s. 59).
  6. One case — Wallis v. Pratt if it is about description.
Template 2 — ‘Implied condition broken?’
  1. Which of ss. 14, 15, 16(1), 16(2), 17 is in play?
  2. Quote the section.
  3. Take its elements one by one and apply each to a fact.
  4. Address the proviso expressly — this is where most marks are lost.
  5. Note whether the term is a condition or a warranty, and the remedy.
  6. Add the second string: if s. 16(1) fails on the trade-name proviso, plead s. 16(2); if s. 16(2) fails on examination, plead s. 16(1).
Template 3 — ‘Caveat emptor’
  1. State the rule and its statutory home: s. 16's opening words.
  2. Give the origin — face-to-face market sales.
  3. Give the eight exceptions, each with one case.
  4. Give the Jones v. Just limit: no opportunity of inspection.
  5. State the two provisos to s. 16(1) and s. 16(2).
  6. Close on caveat venditor and Lord Wright's “conditions of modern commerce”.
