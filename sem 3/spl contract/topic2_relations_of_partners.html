§ How to read these notes

Chapter III of the Partnership Act (ss. 9–17) governs partners as between themselves; Chapter IV (ss. 18–30) governs partners as against the outside world. The hinge between the two is section 18 — "a partner is the agent of the firm" — because agency is what converts an internal relationship into an external liability. Every question in this topic is answered by asking first, what did the partners agree among themselves, and second, did the act fall within the partner's authority to bind the firm to a third party.

The golden rule of this topic: sections 9 to 17 are almost all prefaced by the words "subject to contract between the partners" — meaning they are default rules that the partners can contract out of inter se. Sections 18 to 28 are different: they determine what binds the firm vis-à-vis third parties, and a private restriction is ineffective against an outsider who does not know of it (s. 20). Confusing these two is the single most common mistake in DU answers.

1.2 Section 9 — General duties of partners

s. 9 — General duties of partners

"Partners are bound to carry on the business of the firm to the greatest common advantage, to be just and faithful to each other, and to render true accounts and full information of all things affecting the firm to any partner or his legal representative."

A Anatomy of s. 9 — three duties, one sentence

⚖ Case — Sita Ram v. Radha Rai, AIR 1968 SC 534 s. 9

Facts. A partner sued for rendition of accounts. It was objected that he was a sleeping / non-active partner who had taken no part in the conduct of the business and therefore had no right to call for accounts.

Held. The Supreme Court held that a sleeping partner can maintain a suit for rendition of accounts. Section 9 confers the right on "any partner" without distinguishing between active and sleeping partners, and s. 12(d) gives every partner the right of access to the books. The fact that a partner does not participate in management does not forfeit his right to know how the business is being run or to demand an account.

Principle. The right to accounts is incident to the status of partner, not to the quantum of work he does. Only a minor admitted to the benefits of partnership is restricted — and even he has the right to inspect and copy the accounts (s. 30(2)), though he may not sue for accounts except when severing his connection (s. 30(4)).

Also relevant: the firm's act may be ratified by the partners even where done by one partner beyond authority — the Supreme Court applied the principle that an act done in a representative character can be adopted by those for whom it was done.

◆ Real-life examples of s. 9
  1. Greatest common advantage. Three partners run "Deccan Logistics". A large shipping contract is offered to the firm. Partner A silently routes the contract to a company owned by his wife, and pockets the margin. Result: breach of s. 9 and of s. 16(a) (profit derived from a transaction / business connection of the firm). A must account for the profit and pay it to the firm.
  2. Just and faithful. Two partners run a pharmacy. B renews the firm's drug licence in his own name and tells A the licence was refused, then starts his own shop with it. Result: breach of good faith; A can sue for accounts, damages and dissolution under s. 44(d).
  3. True accounts. C, a sleeping partner in a restaurant, asks for the books three times and is fobbed off with "we'll send them". C is entitled to a decree for accounts — and, per Sita Ram, his non-participation is no defence for the working partners.
+ Related provisions and enactments — s. 9
  • s. 12(d), PA — right to have access to, inspect and copy books (the machinery for s. 9's "full information").
  • s. 13(f), PA — a partner must indemnify the firm for loss caused by his wilful neglect (the damages counterpart of s. 16's account of profits).
  • s. 16, PA — the specific, monetised form of the fiduciary duty.
  • s. 44(d), PA — persistent breach / conduct making it not reasonably practicable to carry on → dissolution by the court.
  • s. 213, ICA 1872 — the agent's duty to render accounts (the agency analogue).
  • s. 88, Indian Trusts Act 1882 — the general law on advantage gained by a person in a fiduciary position, which informs the width of s. 16.
  • Limitation Act 1963, Art. 5 — three years for a suit for an account of a dissolved firm (Art. 22 for a suit for accounts generally).

1.3 Section 10 — Duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud

s. 10 — Duty to indemnify for loss caused by fraud

"Every partner shall indemnify the firm for any loss caused to it by his fraud in the conduct of the business of the firm."

A The four ingredients
  1. There must be fraud — not mere negligence or an error of judgment. "Fraud" here carries the meaning of s. 17, ICA 1872 (suggestion of a false fact, concealment of a material fact, a promise made without intention to perform, etc.).
  2. The fraud must be by a partner (an agent or employee's fraud engages s. 238 ICA, not s. 10).
  3. It must be in the conduct of the business of the firm — a fraud committed in a private venture is outside s. 10.
  4. It must have caused loss to the firm. The indemnity is for the loss actually caused.

◆ Worked example (pattern from DU 1998 Q.2(b) and DU 1995 Q.3)

X, Y and Z are goldsmiths in partnership; X is the managing partner, Y and Z are sleeping partners. X sells 18-carat ornaments to a customer S but charges for 22-carat. S discovers the fraud and sues the firm.

  1. Is the firm liable to S? Yes, on two independent grounds:
    • s. 18 + s. 19(1) — selling ornaments is an act done to carry on, in the usual way, the business of the firm, so X's act binds the firm; and
    • s. 26 — where, by the wrongful act of a partner acting in the ordinary course of the business, loss is caused to a third party, the firm is liable to the same extent as the partner.
  2. Can the firm recover from X? Yes — s. 10: X must indemnify the firm for the loss caused by his fraud in the conduct of the firm's business. Additionally, under s. 13(f) the firm is entitled to be indemnified for loss caused by his wilful neglect, and the secret profit is caught by s. 16.
  3. Are Y and Z personally out of pocket? They must pay S (liability is joint and several under s. 25), but they can recover X's share from X in the settlement of accounts (s. 48) and by a separate claim under s. 10.
+ Related provisions — s. 10
  • s. 17, ICA 1872 — definition of "fraud"; s. 19 ICA — voidability.
  • s. 26, PA — firm's liability to the outsider for the partner's wrongful act (the external counterpart of s. 10, which is the internal recourse).
  • s. 44(c)/(d), PA — conduct prejudicially affecting the business / persistent breach as grounds for court dissolution.
  • Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023, ss. 316 & 318 (formerly IPC ss. 415 & 420) — cheating; a partner's fraud may also be a criminal offence, but criminal liability is personal and does not extend to the innocent partners.
  • s. 238, ICA — principal's liability for the fraud of an agent in the course of the business of the agency.

1.4 Section 11 — Determination of rights and duties by contract between the partners

s. 11 — Determination of rights and duties of partners by contract between the partners

"(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the mutual rights and duties of the partners of a firm may be determined by contract between the partners, and such contract may be expressed or may be implied by a course of dealing.

Such contract may be varied by consent of all the partners, and such consent may be expressed or may be implied by a course of dealing.

(2) Agreements in restraint of trade — Notwithstanding anything contained in section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, such contracts may provide that a partner shall not carry on any business other than that of the firm while he is a partner."

A Why s. 11 is the most important section in Chapter III

Section 11 is the master-key: it means that almost the whole of Chapter III (ss. 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 and 17) is residual. It applies only "in the absence of a contract to the contrary". Therefore:

  1. In any problem question, the first thing to ask is: what does the partnership deed say? Only if the deed is silent do ss. 12–17 supply the answer.
  2. The contract may be oral or implied from a course of dealing — a written deed is not required.
  3. Variation requires the consent of ALL the partners — not a majority. And that consent too may be implied from a course of dealing (e.g. partners who have for years shared profits 40:30:30 contrary to a deed saying "equal" will be taken to have varied it).

But s. 11 is itself "subject to the provisions of this Act" — so the partners cannot contract out of s. 9 (good faith), s. 10 (fraud), or the third-party provisions in Chapter IV.

★ s. 11(2) — the restraint-of-trade carve-out (a DU favourite: asked in 1998 Q.2(a))

The general rule: s. 27, ICA 1872 says "Every agreement by which any one is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void." (Exception: sale of goodwill — a seller may agree not to carry on a similar business within reasonable limits.)

The partnership exception: s. 11(2), PA 1932 permits a partnership agreement to provide that a partner shall not carry on any business other than that of the firm while he is a partner. The rationale is obvious: a partner is an agent of the firm and owes it undivided loyalty; if he were free to run a rival shop, s. 16(b) would be unworkable.

◆ Examples
  • Valid. The deed of "Malhotra & Sons" provides: "No partner shall, during the subsistence of the partnership, engage directly or indirectly in any other trade or business." Partner M opens a textile shop in his own name. The other partners can injunct him and, under s. 16(b), claim the profits — and the clause is not hit by s. 27 ICA because s. 11(2) saves it.
  • Invalid without more. The same clause, extended to "…and for ten years after ceasing to be a partner, anywhere in India." The post-retirement limb is not within s. 11(2); it must satisfy s. 27 ICA, i.e. be reasonable in scope, geography and duration. A blanket all-India ten-year restraint would ordinarily be struck down as unreasonable.
  • Variation by course of dealing. The deed says profits are shared equally. For eight consecutive years the firm credits A 50%, B 30%, C 20%, and all three sign the balance sheets. The variation is binding — s. 11(1): consent "may be implied by a course of dealing".
+ Related provisions — s. 11
  • s. 27, ICA 1872 — restraint of trade void (expressly overridden by s. 11(2)).
  • s. 36, PA — rights of an outgoing partner to carry on a competing business and the restrictions permitted by agreement.
  • s. 92, Indian Evidence Act 1872 — where the partners' contract is reduced to writing, no oral evidence of its terms may be given; but proviso (5) allows proof of a subsequent oral rescission or variation, and proviso (6) allows proof of usage/custom. This is how a "course of dealing" gets in.
  • s. 55, PA — similar restraint can be imposed on a partner on the sale of the goodwill after dissolution.
  • Competition Act 2002, s. 3(3) — an anti-competitive agreement between enterprises is void; a genuine partnership restraint under s. 11(2) is generally treated as ancillary and pro-competitive, but a cartel dressed up as a partnership is not protected.

1.5 Section 12 — The conduct of the business Diagram 2

s. 12 — The conduct of the business

"Subject to contract between the partners —

(a) Every partner has a right to take part in the conduct of the business;

(b) Every partner is bound to attend diligently to his duties in the conduct of the business;

(c) Any difference arising as to ordinary matters connected with the business may be decided by a majority of the partners, and every partner shall have the right to express his opinion before the matter is decided, but no change may be made in the nature of the business without the consent of all the partners; and

(d) Every partner has a right to have access to and to inspect and copy any of the books of the firm."

◆ Examples
  1. Ordinary matter → majority. Three partners run a bakery. Two want to buy a new oven; one does not. After hearing him out, the two may resolve to buy. He is bound — and bound to the supplier too, because the purchase is an act in the ordinary course (s. 19(1)).
  2. Change in nature → unanimity. The same bakery's two partners want to convert the premises into a bar. The third refuses. They cannot: s. 12(c) requires the consent of all. If they do it anyway, the dissenting partner can injunct and sue for dissolution.
  3. Deadlock in a two-partner firm. A and B are equal partners and cannot agree. There is no majority. The remedy is dissolution — by agreement, or by the court under s. 44(d) or s. 44(g) (just and equitable).
  4. Diligence. C, a partner in a chartered-accountancy firm, stops attending and never signs off files, leaving the work to D. C is in breach of s. 12(b) and, if loss results from his wilful neglect, liable under s. 13(f).
+ Related provisions — s. 12
  • s. 11 — the deed overrides s. 12 entirely; most deeds vest management in a managing partner.
  • s. 12A (inserted in some State amendments — Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh) — obligation to maintain proper books of account. Not in the central Act, but useful to mention as a "connected provision".
  • s. 13(a) — a partner gets no remuneration for taking part in the conduct of the business, even if he works full time (unless the deed says so). A managing partner therefore needs an express salary clause.
  • s. 9 — right to "full information"; s. 12(d) is the mechanism.
  • s. 44(d) — persistent breach of the partnership agreement → dissolution by court.

1.6 Section 13 — Mutual rights and liabilities

s. 13 — Mutual rights and liabilities

"Subject to contract between the partners —

(a) a partner is not entitled to receive remuneration for taking part in the conduct of the business;

(b) the partners are entitled to share equally in the profits earned, and shall contribute equally to the losses sustained by the firm;

(c) where a partner is entitled to interest on the capital subscribed by him such interest shall be payable only out of profits;

(d) a partner making, for the purposes of the business, any payment or advance beyond the amount of capital he has agreed to subscribe, is entitled to interest thereon at the rate of six per cent per annum;

(e) the firm shall indemnify a partner in respect of payments made and liabilities incurred by him —
(i) in the ordinary and proper conduct of the business, and
(ii) in doing such act, in an emergency, for the purpose of protecting the firm from loss as would be done by a person of ordinary prudence, in his own case, under similar circumstances; and

(f) a partner shall indemnify the firm for any loss caused to it by his wilful neglect in the conduct of the business of the firm."

⚖ Case — Mandyala Govindu & Co. v. CIT, AIR 1975 SC 2284 s. 13

Facts. A firm claimed a deduction for remuneration/salary paid to partners who were actively working in the business. The partnership deed contained no provision for such remuneration.

Held. The Supreme Court held that, in the absence of a provision in the partnership deed, a partner is not entitled to any remuneration for taking part in the conduct of the business — s. 13(a) is clear, and a payment that is not authorised by the deed is merely an assignment of a portion of the partner's share of profits, not a deductible business expenditure.

Principle. "The well-settled position is that a partner is not entitled to remuneration for services rendered to the firm unless there is a provision for the same in the partnership deed." Because s. 13 opens with "subject to contract between the partners", the deed is decisive. Where the deed authorises salary, it is deductible; where it does not, it is only an application of profit.

Exam use. Cite this for any question on "mutual rights and liabilities of partners", "is a working partner entitled to salary?", or "what are the mutual rights and duties of partners?" (DU 2000 Q.2(a); 1997 Q.2(a); 2017 Q.5(a)).

◆ Worked problem — the s. 13 calculation

Facts. A, B and C are partners. The deed is silent on everything except that A contributes ₹6,00,000, B ₹3,00,000 and C ₹1,00,000 as capital. In the year the firm makes a net profit of ₹90,000 before adjustments. B also advanced ₹2,00,000 to the firm in June when it was short of cash.

  1. Interest on capital? None is payable, because there is no agreement providing for it (s. 13(a)/(c) — no entitlement without a contract).
  2. Interest on B's advance? Yes — 6% p.a. under s. 13(d), i.e. ₹6,000 for a full year on ₹2,00,000 (pro-rated for the period actually outstanding). This is payable regardless of profit.
  3. Division of the balance. ₹90,000 − ₹6,000 = ₹84,000. Under s. 13(b) profits are shared equally — ₹28,000 each — not in the 6:3:1 capital ratio. (If the deed had fixed a ratio, that would govern.)
  4. Salary to A, who works full time? None, unless agreed — s. 13(a) and Mandyala Govindu.

1.7 Section 14 — The property of the firm Diagram 3

s. 14 — The property of the firm

"Subject to contract between the partners, the property of the firm includes all property and rights and interests in property originally brought into the stock of the firm, or acquired, by purchase or otherwise, by or for the firm, or for the purposes and in the course of the business of the firm, and includes also the goodwill of the business.

Unless the contrary intention appears, property and rights and interests in property acquired with money belonging to the firm are deemed to have been acquired for the firm."

A The three categories and the deeming rule
  1. Originally brought into the stock of the firm — what the partners contribute at the outset with the intention that it becomes the firm's.
  2. Acquired by or for the firm, or for the purposes and in the course of the business — everything bought with firm funds, in the firm's name, or for the firm's use as a firm asset.
  3. Goodwill — expressly included by the closing words of the first paragraph. Goodwill is property and is an asset of the firm, to be valued on dissolution or on a partner's retirement.

The deeming rule (paragraph 2): where the firm's money buys an asset, the asset is presumed to be the firm's — but this is a rebuttable presumption ("unless the contrary intention appears"). The leading Indian case is Addanki Narayanappa, below.

Fig. 3 — "Is it firm property?" Reproduce this in the exam for any s. 14 problem; it is the exact reasoning used in Miles v. Clarke and Addanki Narayanappa.
⚖ Case — Addanki Narayanappa v. Bhaskara Krishnappa, AIR 1966 SC 1300 s. 14

Facts. Certain immovable property was purchased with partnership funds but standing in the name of one partner only. On dissolution the other partners claimed it as partnership property; the partner in whose name it stood contended it was his personal property by virtue of the documents of title.

Held. The Supreme Court held that the property was partnership property. The real question is not in whose name the document stands but with whose money and for whose purposes it was acquired. Under s. 14, property acquired with money belonging to the firm is deemed to have been acquired for the firm unless a contrary intention appears, and the document of title is not conclusive.

Principles evolved.

  1. The beneficial interest in firm property is the firm's, not the individual partner's, even where legal title is in one name (cf. the doctrine of resulting trust under s. 82, Indian Trusts Act 1882).
  2. During the subsistence of the firm, no partner can deal with firm property as his own or claim a defined share in it — his share is a right to a share of the surplus after realisation (s. 15; s. 29(1)).
  3. The presumption in s. 14 ¶2 is rebuttable, but the burden is heavy and lies on the partner asserting that the asset is personal.
  4. On dissolution, the asset falls into the pool to be applied under s. 48 and distributed among the partners.

1.8 Section 15 — Application of the property of the firm

s. 15 — Application of the property of the firm

"Subject to contract between the partners, the property of the firm shall be held and used by the partners exclusively for the purposes of the business."

A What s. 15 does
  • It is the user rule: firm assets are for firm purposes. It is the statutory basis for restraining a partner who uses the firm's van, premises or funds for his own venture.
  • It works together with s. 16: s. 15 forbids the misuse, s. 16 confiscates the profit made by it.
  • It is subject to contract, so partners may agree (as they often do) that a partner may use an asset on stated terms.
  • Addanki Narayanappa is the short-note authority: a partner cannot transfer any part of the property of the firm while the partnership continues.
! The three-way test for "can he deal with this asset?"
  1. Is it firm property at all? → s. 14. If not, the partner may deal with it freely.
  2. May he use it for himself? → s. 15 says no, absent agreement.
  3. May he transfer it to a third party? → s. 19(2)(g): a partner has no implied authority to transfer immovable property belonging to the firm. A transferee without notice of the defect takes nothing (contrast s. 19(1) read with s. 20 for ordinary goods).

Example. Two partners own a delivery van as firm property. Partner A uses it on Sundays for his private courier business and earns ₹40,000. Under s. 15 the use is improper; under s. 16(a) the ₹40,000 is profit derived "from the use of the property… of the firm" and must be accounted for and paid to the firm.

  • s. 16(a) — account of profits from the use of firm property / business connection / firm name.

1.9 Section 16 — Personal profits earned by partners Diagram 4

s. 16 — Personal profits earned by partners

"Subject to contract between the partners —

(a) if a partner derives any profit for himself from any transaction of the firm, or from the use of the property or business connection of the firm or the firm name, he shall account for that profit and pay it to the firm;

(b) if a partner carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm, he shall account for and pay to the firm all profits made by him in that business."

Fig. 4 — s. 16. Mnemonic for limb (a): "TPBF — Transaction, Property, Business connection, Firm name." And always add the Trimble limiting principle.
A Seven points that turn a pass answer into a first-class one
  1. s. 16 is strict but not punitive. The partner must hand over the profit; he is not fined, and if there is no profit there is nothing to account for.
  2. "Any profit for himself" includes a secret commission or bribe received from a third party in connection with a firm transaction.
  3. Limb (a) is wider than English law's predecessor: it expressly extends to the use of the business connection and the firm name, not merely firm property.
  4. Limb (b) does not require use of firm property — merely that the competing business is of the same nature. But following Trimble, the connection/rivalry test is read in.
  5. Both limbs are "subject to contract" — partners may authorise a partner to carry on a separate business (common with doctors, lawyers, chartered accountants in multi-discipline firms).
  6. The remedy is an account, usually in the suit for dissolution and accounts; the plaintiff partner sues on behalf of the firm.
  7. Contrast s. 16 with s. 15 and s. 9. s. 15 says don't misuse; s. 9 says be faithful; s. 16 says hand over the profit. They are cumulative, not alternative.
◆ Examples
  1. Limb (a) — secret commission. A, a partner in a firm that imports steel,negotiates a purchase and the supplier secretly pays him 2% "as a personal gift". The 2% is profit derived from a transaction of the firm → A must account under s. 16(a).
  2. Limb (a) — firm name. B, a partner in "Himalaya Tours", runs a parallel trekking operation under a trading style that uses the firm's logo and phone lines, and books the profits to himself. Caught by s. 16(a) ("use of the… business connection of the firm or the firm name") and by s. 15.
  3. Limb (b) — competing business. C is a partner in a firm running a chain of chemist shops. He opens his own chemist shop two streets away. Under s. 16(b) he must account for all the profits of that shop; and under s. 11(2) the firm could in any event have restrained him while he is a partner.
  4. Outside s. 16 — the Trimble situation. D is a partner in a firm that buys and sells commercial real estate. On his own account, and using his own money, D buys shares in a listed pharmaceutical company. That is not a transaction of the firm, uses no firm property or connection, and is not a business of the same nature. s. 16 does not apply — and this is precisely why Goldberg failed in Trimble v. Goldberg.

  • s. 50, PA — after dissolution on death, s. 16(a) continues to apply to transactions by a surviving partner until the affairs are completely wound up.

1.10 Section 17 — Rights and duties of partners after a change, after expiry, or on new ventures

s. 17 — Rights and duties of partners

"Subject to contract between the partners —

(a) after a change in the firm — Where a change occurs in the constitution of a firm, the mutual rights and duties of the partners in the reconstituted firm remain the same as they were immediately before the change, as far as may be;

(b) after the expiry of the term of the firm — Where a firm constituted for a fixed term continues to carry on business after the expiry of that term, the mutual rights and duties of the partners remain the same as they were before the expiry, so far as they may be consistent with the incidents of partnership at will; and

Section 17 supplies the same answer in all three cases: the old rule-book carries over, with one qualification in case (b).

2.1 Section 18 — Partners to be agents of the firm

s. 18 — Partners to be agent of the firm

"Subject to the provisions of this Act, a partner is the agent of the firm for the purposes of the business of the firm."

A Nine words that carry the whole of Chapter IV

This is the pivot of Topic 2. It does three things at once:

  1. It creates the agency. Unlike an ordinary agent (whose authority must be conferred under s. 186–187 ICA), a partner's authority arises by operation of law from the fact of partnership.
  2. It defines the scope. "for the purposes of the business of the firm" — not for the partner's own purposes, and not for a business the firm does not carry on.
  3. It makes the agency mutual. Each partner is agent of the firm; the firm is, in substance, the other partners. So A's act binds B — which is why Cox v. Hickman said "the liability of one partner for the acts of his co-partner is in truth the liability of a principal for the acts of his agent".

Holding. Why it belongs under s. 18. The agency in s. 18 is derivative — it exists only if there is a partnership. No partnership ⇒ no agency ⇒ no liability for the firm's acts. That is the negative proof of s. 18.

  3. Personal purposes. A borrows ₹5 lakh from a moneylender, signing "for A & Co.", and uses the money to pay his daughter's wedding. 2.3 Section 20 — Extension and restriction of partner's implied authority Diagram 7

s. 20 — Extension and restriction of partner's implied authority

"The partners in a firm may, by contract between the partners, extend or restrict the implied authority of any partner.

Notwithstanding any such restriction, any act done by a partner on behalf of the firm which falls within his implied authority binds the firm, unless the person with whom he is dealing knows of the restriction, or does not know or believe that partner to be a partner."

Fig. 7 — s. 20. The formula to memorise: "Private restriction + innocent outsider = firm bound." Two things defeat it: the outsider's knowledge of the restriction, and the outsider's ignorance that the actor was a partner at all.
A Reading s. 20 correctly
  1. First sentence — extension and restriction. Partners may widen a partner's implied authority (e.g. authorise him to buy immovable property, thereby overcoming s. 19(2)(f)) or narrow it (e.g. a clause that no partner may borrow more than ₹1 lakh).
  2. Second sentence — the "notwithstanding" clause. A restriction does not avail against a third party who is unaware of it. This is the statutory form of the common-law rule that a principal is bound by the ostensible authority he has held out.
  3. The two-fold proviso. The firm escapes only if the third party (i) knew of the restriction, or (ii) did not know or believe the actor to be a partner. Note (ii) is a separate, independent escape — it matters where an undisclosed or dormant partner acts.
  4. Burden of proof. The firm asserting that the third party knew of the restriction must prove it. s. 101–102, Indian Evidence Act 1872.

Note the contrast with s. 19(2). s. 19(2) lists acts that are never within implied authority — a third party is fixed with knowledge of the statute and cannot say "I did not know". s. 20 deals with restrictions created by a private contract, of which the third party may well be ignorant. That is why s. 19(2) protects the firm absolutely and s. 20 does not.

◆ DU pattern — the "restriction on borrowing" problem (DU 2003 Q.4(b); 1998 Q.5(a))

Facts. The partnership deed provides that no partner shall borrow money or accept a bill of exchange without the consent of all the others. One partner borrows ₹2 lakh from X and accepts a bill. X had no notice of the restriction.

Answer.

  1. Is borrowing within implied authority? Yes — for a trading firm, borrowing in the ordinary course is an act done "to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm" (s. 19(1)).
  2. Does the deed's restriction help the firm? No. Under s. 20 the restriction is overridden: "notwithstanding any such restriction, any act done by a partner on behalf of the firm which falls within his implied authority binds the firm, unless the person dealing with him knows of the restriction." X did not know.
  3. Therefore the firm is bound and every partner is liable jointly and severally (s. 25).
  4. Internal recourse. The other partners may recover from the acting partner under s. 13(f) (wilful neglect) and, if he misapplied the money, s. 27 makes the firm liable to X but the partner liable to the firm.
  5. Contrast (DU 1998 Q.5(a)): where the deed prohibits the sale of cars and the partner sells a car to which the firm had no title, the buyer's claim against the sleeping partner — the act is outside the firm's business altogether (not merely restricted), so s. 19(1) itself is not satisfied and the firm is not bound. The buyer may still have a personal claim against the acting partner.
+ Related provisions — s. 20

  • s. 18 — the agency whose scope s. 20 adjusts.
  • s. 188, ICA 1872 — "an authority is said to be expressed… or implied"; and the general rule that a principal is bound by the agent's acts within ostensible authority.
  • s. 237, ICA — liability of a principal who induces the belief that unauthorised acts were authorised (estoppel — the conceptual twin of s. 20's second sentence).
  • s. 115, Indian Evidence Act 1872 — estoppel, the doctrine underlying ss. 20, 28 and 237 ICA.
  • s. 45, PA — the post-dissolution counterpart: partners continue liable until public notice of dissolution.
  • s. 32(3) proviso, PA — a retired partner is not liable to a third party who dealt with the firm without knowing he was a partner — the same idea as the second limb of s. 20's proviso.

2.4 Section 21 — Partner's authority in an emergency

s. 21 — Partner's authority in an emergency

"A partner has authority, in an emergency, to do all such acts for the purpose of protecting the firm from loss as would be done by a person of ordinary prudence, in his own case, acting under similar circumstances, and such acts bind the firm."

A The four conditions
  1. An emergency must exist — a sudden or unforeseen situation requiring immediate action.
  2. The act must be for the purpose of protecting the firm from loss — not for profit, and not for the partner's own advantage.
  3. The standard is objective: what "a person of ordinary prudence… in his own case, acting under similar circumstances" would do. This is the same standard as s. 13(e)(ii) and s. 212 ICA.
  4. No time to consult — the emergency must be such that reference to the other partners is impracticable. If there is time to obtain instructions, s. 12(c)/s. 19 governs instead.
! s. 21 can override s. 19(2)

This is the important, often-missed point. If the emergency requires it, a partner may do things that would otherwise be outside his implied authority — even, in principle, an act listed in s. 19(2) — provided the s. 21 conditions are met. Example: a warehouse belonging to the firm catches fire and the only way to save the stock is to buy the adjoining plot on the spot to store it. Ordinarily s. 19(2)(f) forbids a partner from acquiring immovable property; in the emergency s. 21 authorises it and the firm is bound. Equally, s. 13(e)(ii) entitles the partner to be indemnified by the firm for what he spends.

◆ Examples
  1. Emergency — firm bound. A consignment of the firm's perishable fruit is stranded when the transport union strikes. Partner A hires alternative refrigerated transport at double the rate. The firm is bound (s. 21) and A is entitled to indemnity (s. 13(e)(ii)).
  2. Emergency — firm bound. The firm's factory roof collapses during the monsoon. Partner B engages a contractor immediately to tarpaulin and repair it, to prevent damage to machinery. Bound under s. 21.
  3. Not an emergency. Partner C decides, on his own, that the firm should buy a competing business that has come onto the market, and signs an agreement. There is time to consult; s. 19(1) (buying a business is not "carrying on in the usual way") is not satisfied; s. 21 does not apply. The firm is not bound.
  4. ICA parallel. In agency law the equivalent is s. 189, ICA 1872 — an agent has authority, in an emergency, to do all acts for the purpose of protecting his principal from loss.
+ Related provisions — s. 21
  • s. 13(e)(ii) — the firm must indemnify the partner for emergency expenditure.
  • s. 189, ICA 1872 — agency of necessity / authority in an emergency (verbalatim parallel).
  • s. 19(2) — the restrictions that s. 21 can, in an emergency, displace.
  • s. 12(c) — where there is time, ordinary matters go to a majority and a change in the nature of the business requires unanimity.
  • s. 70, ICA — obligation of a person enjoying the benefit of a non-gratuitous act (restitutionary fallback).
  • Disaster Management Act 2005 and Essential Commodities Act 1955 — where the "emergency" is a public one, statutory controls may additionally govern what a firm may do with stock.

2.5 Section 22 — Mode of doing act to bind firm

s. 22 — Mode of doing act to bind firm

"In order to bind a firm, an act or instrument done or executed by a partner or other person on behalf of the firm shall be done or executed in the firm name, or in any other manner expressing or implying an intention to bind the firm."

A The two alternative modes
↔ Swipe table sideways
ModeWhat it coversExample
(i) In the firm nameThe act is done ex facie in the name under which the firm carries on business.A contract signed "For Verma Bros. — A. Verma, partner".
(ii) Expressing or implying an intention to bind the firmAny other formulation — even without the firm name — from which it appears that the actor intends to bind the firm rather than himself.A bill accepted "A. Verma, for and on behalf of the firm"; or a letter on the firm's letterhead ordering goods, signed by A personally.

What s. 22 excludes. Where the partner contracts in his own name and nothing indicates an intention to bind the firm, the firm is not bound — even if the money is in fact used for the firm's business. This is the exact converse of the undisclosed-principal rule in s. 231, ICA for agency, and s. 22 deliberately cuts it down for partnerships.

Who may sign? The section says "by a partner or other person on behalf of the firm" — so a duly authorised manager or attorney can also bind the firm if he uses the firm name.

◆ Examples
  1. Bound. A signs a hire-purchase agreement "Verma Bros., by A. Verma". The firm is bound.
  2. Bound. A signs in his own name but the order is placed on the firm's printed letterhead describing the firm's business, and the goods are invoiced to and delivered at the firm's premises. The intention to bind the firm is implied — the firm is bound. (This is the Tower Cabinet factual pattern, although there the firm was held not bound because the partner had ceased to be a partner and had not "knowingly suffered" the use of his name.)
  3. Not bound. A borrows ₹3 lakh signing a promissory note simply "A. Verma", tells the lender it is "for my business", and uses the money to pay a firm debt. The firm is not bound under s. 22; the lender's remedy is against A personally.
+ Related provisions — s. 22

  • s. 231, ICA 1872 — "performance of contract with an agent supposed to be principal": where a contract is made with a person who is really an agent but appears to be the principal, the real principal may require performance — a rule whose width s. 22 deliberately narrows for firms.
  • s. 233, ICA — the third party may sue the principal or the agent or both where the agent is personally liable.
  • Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, s. 26 — a person signing a negotiable instrument is liable personally unless he indicates that he signs "for or on behalf of" a named principal, or adds words describing himself as an agent.   • s. 69, PA — an unregistered firm cannot sue at all (see Part D, Set I).

◆ DU pattern — "A borrows in the firm's name and spends it on himself"

Facts (DU 1999 Q.3 / 1993 Q.3). A and B are partners in a trading firm. A borrows ₹50,000 from C in the name of the firm, without B's knowledge, and spends the money for his own purposes. Can C hold B liable?

  1. Is borrowing within implied authority? For a trading firm, borrowing in the ordinary course is an act done to carry on the business in the usual way — s. 19(1). (For a non-trading firm — e.g. a firm of solicitors — the position is different, and this is why Rhodes v. Moules had to examine the previous course of dealing.)
  2. Was it done in the firm name? Yes — s. 22 satisfied.
  3. Does A's misuse help B? No. Under s. 27(a), where a partner acting within his apparent authority receives money from a third party and misapplies it, the firm is liable to make good the loss. The third party's ignorance of the misapplication is precisely what s. 27 protects.
  4. So C can recover from B — and from A — jointly and severally under s. 25.
  5. B's recourse. B may recover A's share from A in the settlement of accounts (s. 48), and claim indemnity under s. 13(f) (wilful neglect) or s. 10 if A's conduct amounts to fraud on the firm.
  6. Contrast. If the firm were non-trading and borrowing were not within the usual way of its business, A would have no implied authority, s. 27(a) would not be engaged, and the answer might differ — unless the previous course of dealing clothed him with apparent authority (the Rhodes v. Moules reasoning).

Scope of a partner's liability — the summary to write: "Every partner is liable jointly with all the other partners and also severally for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner (s. 25). His liability is unlimited — it extends to his personal estate (s. 49). It covers contracts made in the firm's name within the partner's implied authority (ss. 19, 22), torts committed in the ordinary course of the business (s. 26), and misapplication of money or property received by the firm or by a partner within his apparent authority (s. 27). He is not liable for acts done before he became a partner (s. 31(2)), and he ceases to be liable for subsequent acts once public notice of his retirement is given (s. 32(3))."

2.7 Section 28 — Holding out The single most-asked provision in Topic 2

s. 28 — Holding out

"(1) Anyone who by words spoken or written or by conduct represents himself, or knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner in a firm, is liable as a partner in the firm to anyone who has on the faith of any such representation given credit to the firm, whether the person representing himself or represented to be a partner does or does not know that the representation has reached the person so giving credit.

(2) Where after a partner's death the business is continued in the old firm name, the continued use of that name or of the deceased partner's name as a part thereof shall not of itself make his legal representative or his estate liable for any act of the firm done after his death."

Fig. 9 — s. 28. Mnemonic: "Represent · Knowingly · Rely · Credit" — R-K-R-C. Quote Lynskey J.'s words: "The words are 'knowingly suffers' — not being negligent or careless in not seeing that all the notepaper had been destroyed when he left."
A The exact words that win marks
  1. "by words spoken or written or by conduct" — three modes; a nod of assent in a shop while someone says "this is my partner" is conduct.
  2. "represents himself, OR knowingly permits himself to be represented" — the second limb is the one that catches the retired partner whose name stays on the letterhead. The adverb is "knowingly" — actual awareness, not constructive.
  3. "on the faith of any such representation" — causal reliance. If the creditor would have given credit anyway, there is no holding out.
  4. "whether the person representing himself … does or does not know that the representation has reached the person so giving credit" — so D, who held himself out before E, is liable to F who heard it from E (cf. DU 1995 Q.5(a) / 1992 Q.2(a)). This is a deliberate widening: the representor need not know the identity of the person who ultimately relied.
  5. s. 28(2) — continued use of a dead partner's name does not by itself make his estate liable. (Contrast a retired partner under s. 32(3), where the name alone may be enough.)

2.8 Section 29 — Rights of transferee of a partner's interest

s. 29 — Rights of transferee of a partner's interest

"(1) A transfer by a partner of his interest in the firm, either absolute or by mortgage, or by the creation by him of a charge on such interest, does not entitle the transferee, during the continuance of the firm, to interfere in the conduct of the business, or to require accounts, or to inspect the books of the firm, but entitles the transferee only to receive the share of profits of the transferring partner, and the transferee shall accept the account of profits agreed to by the partners.

(2) If the firm is dissolved, or if the transferring partner ceases to be a partner, the transferee is entitled as against the remaining partners to receive the share of the assets of the firm to which the transferring partner is entitled and, for the purpose of ascertaining that share, to an account as from the date of the dissolution."

◆ DU pattern — "A transfers his interest without consent" (asked in 2015 OC Q.2(b), 2014 OC Q.2(b), 2012 OC Q.3(b), 2003 Q.2(b), 2000 Q.2(b), 1997 Q.2(b))

Facts. A, B, C and D are partners in firm X. A, without the consent of B, C and D, transfers his interest in the firm to P. What are the rights of P against B, C and D?

Answer.

  1. P does not become a partner. Under s. 31(1) no person may be introduced as a partner without the consent of all existing partners. A transfer by one partner cannot achieve what s. 31(1) forbids.
  2. P's only right while the firm continues is to receive A's share of the profits — s. 29(1). He cannot:
    • interfere in the conduct of the business;
    • require accounts; or
    • inspect the books of the firm.
  3. P is bound by the account of profits agreed between the partners — the closing words of s. 29(1). He cannot say "I think the profits were higher".
  4. On dissolution, or when A ceases to be a partner, P becomes entitled to receive the share of the assets of the firm to which A was entitled, and — to ascertain it — to an account as from the date of the dissolution — s. 29(2).
  5. Does the transfer dissolve the firm? Not by itself. But note s. 44(e): if a partner transfers the whole of his interest to a third party, or allows his share to be charged under O. XXI r. 49, CPC or sold for arrears of revenue, the court may dissolve the firm at the suit of another partner. So B, C or D could apply.
  6. Contrast a mortgage or charge. s. 29(1) covers an absolute transfer and a mortgage and a charge — in all three cases the transferee's rights are identical.

2.9 Section 30 — Minors admitted to the benefits of partnership Diagram 10

s. 30 — Minors admitted to the benefits of partnership

"(1) A person who is a minor according to the law to which he is subject may not be a partner in a firm, but, with the consent of all the partners for the time being, he may be admitted to the benefits of partnership.

(2) Such minor has a right to such share of the property and of the profits of the firm as may be agreed upon, and he may have access to and inspect and copy any of the accounts of the firm.

(3) Such minor's share is liable for the acts of the firm, but the minor is not personally liable for any such act.

(4) Such minor may not sue the partners for an account or payment of his share of the property or profits of the firm, save when severing his connection with the firm, and in such case the amount of his share shall be determined by a valuation made as far as possible in accordance with the rules contained in section 48. [Proviso: the partners may elect to dissolve the firm in such a suit.]

(5) At any time within six months of his attaining majority, or of his obtaining knowledge that he had been admitted to the benefits of partnership, whichever date is later, such person may give public notice that he has elected to become or that he has elected not to become a partner in the firm, and such notice shall determine his position as regards the firm. Provided that if he fails to give such notice he shall become a partner in the firm on the expiry of the said six months.

(6) Where any person has been admitted as a minor to the benefits of partnership in a firm, the burden of proving the fact that such person had no knowledge of such admission until a particular date after the expiry of six months of his attaining majority shall lie on the persons asserting that fact.

(7) Where such person becomes a partner —
(a) his rights and liabilities as a minor continue up to the date on which he becomes a partner, but he also becomes personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits of partnership; and
(b) his share in the property and profits of the firm shall be the share to which he was entitled as a minor.

(8) Where such person elects not to become a partner —
(a) his rights and liabilities shall continue to be those of a minor under this section up to the date on which he gives public notice;
(b) his share shall not be liable for any acts of the firm done after the date of the notice; and
(c) he shall be entitled to sue the partners for his share of the property and profits in accordance with sub-section (4).

(9) Nothing in sub-sections (7) and (8) shall affect the provisions of section 28."

Fig. 10 — s. 30. The three dates to memorise: admission → majority (or knowledge) → + 6 months. And the decisive holding of Shivgouda Ravji Patil: the whole of s. 30 presupposes an existing firm — if the firm is dissolved before he attains majority, s. 30(5) can never operate.
A Nine points on s. 30
  1. Consent of ALL partners is required for admission to the benefits (s. 30(1)) — a majority cannot do it, just as a majority cannot introduce a partner (s. 31(1)).
  2. A minor is not a partner — he is a person admitted to benefits. A firm described as "two majors and one minor" is valid, but the minor is not one of the partners.
  3. His share is liable, he is not (s. 30(3)) — so a creditor of the firm can reach the minor's share in the firm's assets but cannot proceed against his person or his separate property.
  4. He can inspect and copy the accounts (s. 30(2)) — the same right as a full partner under s. 12(d).
  5. He cannot sue for accounts except on severing his connection (s. 30(4)) — a deliberate restriction, because he is not a partner. On severing, the valuation follows the s. 48 rules.
  6. The partners may elect to dissolve in such a suit (s. 30(4) proviso) — the others are not obliged to continue with a claimant.
  7. The six-month clock runs from the LATER of majority or knowledge (s. 30(5)), and the burden of proving want of knowledge is on the person asserting it (s. 30(6)).
  8. Default = partnership (s. 30(5) proviso). Silence is costly: he becomes a partner and is personally liable for all acts of the firm done since his admission (s. 30(7)(a)) — a striking retroactive liability.
  9. s. 30(9) preserves s. 28 — so a minor who held himself out as a partner is liable by estoppel, and (per DU 2014 OC Q.4(b) and 2002 Q.5) the option under s. 30(5) does not wipe out a s. 28 liability already incurred.
⚖ Case — Sanyasi Charan Mandal v. Krishnadhan Banerji, ILR 49 Cal 560 (PC) s. 30

The Privy Council, construing the corresponding provisions of the Contract Act 1872 (ss. 247–248), laid down the classical statement of the minor's position, quoted with approval by the Supreme Court in Shivgouda Ravji Patil:

"A person under the age of majority cannot become a partner by contract … and so according to the definition he cannot be one of that group of persons called a firm. It would seem, therefore, that the share of which Section 247 speaks is no more than a right to participate in the property of the firm after its obligations have been satisfied."

Principle. The minor's "share" is a residuary right — a right to what is left after the firm's debts are paid — not a proprietary interest in the firm's assets during the partnership. This is why s. 30(3) can say his share is liable while he is not, and why the Supreme Court in Shivgouda held that s. 30 "posits the existence of a firm".

  2. (DU 2014 OC Q.4(b) / 2002 Q.5) M, admitted to benefits, tells T he has become a partner; T advances ₹1,00,000; M then, within six months of majority, gives notice of disassociation. Answer: T can still make M liable under s. 28 — s. 30(9) expressly preserves holding out. If M had instead elected to become a partner, s. 30(7)(a) would make him personally liable for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits, which covers the loan.
  3. (DU 2001 Q.2(b)) A minor admitted at 7 wants, at 17, to leave the firm and demand an account. Answer: He may sever his connection at any time during minority and then sue for his share (s. 30(4)); the share is valued as far as possible under s. 48. But he cannot sue for accounts while he remains connected.
  4. (DU 2015 OC Q.5(b)) "Discuss the validity of a partnership consisting of two majors and one minor." Answer: Valid. A minor may, with the consent of all partners, be admitted to the benefits of partnership (s. 30(1)); the partnership between the two majors is unaffected. Only — the minor is not a partner and has no personal liability.

CASE 4 · Miles v. Clarke
[1953] 1 All ER 779 · Chancery Division (Harman J.) · DU reader p. 22
[Partnership Property] — what assets actually go into the common pool? (s. 14)
Facts (from the DU case material)

The defendant ("Clarke"), a gentleman of some means, decided to start a business in commercial and fashion photography. In June 1948 he took a lease, in his own name, of premises at Shepherd's Market which consisted of two squash racquets courts, dressing-rooms and so forth — a seven-year lease from midsummer 1948 at £400 a year, which the judge described as "an advantageous lease", because squash courts, with their good overhead light, are easily adapted as photographic studios. He converted one court into a large studio and divided the other into dark rooms, offices and a smaller studio. He employed others to do the photographic work. At the beginning he made a very considerable loss.

In January 1950 he approached the plaintiff ("Miles"), a well-known freelance photographer with a substantial personal connection, to come in as a partner. Miles was reluctant: during the first month or so of 1950 he came down occasionally and took photographs, and from about the beginning of April 1950 he attended full time and brought with him his own considerable connection. "The upshot of it was a very successful business. The plaintiff's faithful clients followed him, and brought their work to him."

Critically, the parties never settled the terms. Harman J. found:

"These two people, having, as it were, thrown in their lot together in this way, were too busy to think about the terms on which they should carry on business. They agreed that the profits, if there should be any, should be shared equally, and I take it the losses also … There also appears to have been an agreement reached that the plaintiff should draw £125 a month on account of his share of the profits … The only two matters that were agreed were that there should be an equal sharing of the profits and that the plaintiff should have these monthly drawings on account." Harman J., Miles v. Clarke

Solicitors were instructed and a limited company was contemplated; nothing was executed. The defendant's accountant produced accounts which began by debiting the business with the defendant's private bank overdraft of about £1,000, which "neither party had contemplated for one moment as being a liability of the business" — leading the judge to say that "as the so-called accounts start from that monstrous unreality, it seems to me impossible to place any reliance on what should be put on the other side." The parties quarrelled; the business was put into the hands of a receiver; and a partnership action followed.

On 19 February 1953 Harman J. declared that a partnership at will had existed from 1 April 1950 and was dissolved on 29 May 1952, and directed an inquiry as to which of six items formed part of the partnership property: (i) the lease of Shepherd's Market; (ii) the furniture and fittings in the studio; (iii) the equipment of the studio; (iv) the plaintiff's and the defendant's photographic negatives and prints brought in at the outset; (v) the defendant's goodwill or reputation; and (vi) the plaintiff's goodwill or reputation.

Issues
  1. Was there a partnership at all? (Conceded by the defendant at the hearing — "to contest the issue whether in law there was a partnership was to contest the incontestable.")
  2. If there was a partnership, what were the assets of the partnership? — i.e. which of the six items had become partnership property under s. 14, given that the parties had agreed only on equal profit-sharing?
  3. Was either party entitled to be credited in the accounts with a sum on account of any of those items (notably, the value of the plaintiff's "connection")?
Arguments of both sides
For the plaintiff (Miles)
  • The lease, the equipment and the plaintiff's goodwill/connection were "put forward throughout as being brought in as part of the assets of the intended association" — the solicitors' letter of 31 January 1950 said exactly that: "the assets of the company will consist of Mr. Miles's goodwill, your goodwill and a lease and you will each hold shares in accordance with the value of the assets you put into the company."
  • Having come into the business on that footing, it would be inequitable now to deny him a right to share in those assets.
  • The stock-in-trade must in any event be treated as pooled.
  • His connection had a real capital value which ought to be "quantified or valued and treated as an asset of his".
For the defendant (Clarke)
  • "It is not necessary to assume any further agreement between the parties." One need only say that everything belonging to one of them at the beginning still belonged to that one at the end.
  • "The law will not make any imaginary agreement between the parties, it being ascertained as a fact that there was no agreement."
  • The partnership could get on perfectly well if the defendant merely licensed his co-partner to use the leasehold premises and the cameras for the purposes of the business.
  • The plaintiff came in because, with the studio and equipment to hand, he could earn a good profit and "it would be worth his while to take half that profit in return for the benefit of the use of the studio and the equipment" — that was the bargain.
Decision (Harman J.)

The judge adopted a single governing principle and applied it item by item:

"In my judgment, no more agreement between the parties should be supposed than is absolutely necessary to give business efficacy to that which has happened, and that, I think, is the only safe way to proceed." Harman J.

Only "the studio name, or the goodwill, such as it is, which has been attaching to it" was left as a partnership asset. Two further consequential rulings:

  • No depreciation. Because the lease and the plant remained the defendant's separate property and he "did not agree to assign them or to sub-let them", no sum was to be charged against profits by way of depreciation — "the result of no agreement works in the plaintiff's favour, and is that he does not have to contribute to wear and tear on those assets."
  • The plaintiff was not to be credited with any sum for his connection: "Some such scheme was, undoubtedly, envisaged, but it was never agreed on, and it seems to me it would be idle to suggest that, as the parties had not agreed anything of that sort, it ought to be treated as having happened."
Principles evolved
  1. Mere use is not contribution. A partner who allows the firm to use his premises, plant or equipment does not thereby make them partnership property. There must be an intention to bring them into the common stock. This is the classic authority for the proposition DU has asked in four papers (1997 Q.7(b), 1993 Q.7, 2010 OC Q.4, 1995 Q.4(a)).
  2. The "business efficacy" test. Where the partners have not agreed, the court will imply only what is absolutely necessary to make the business work — no more. This is a narrower implication than the ordinary contractual test and is peculiar to partnership.
  3. Consumables are always pooled. Things that are used up in the business (quae ipso usu consumuntur) must be treated as firm property, because the business cannot be carried on otherwise.
  4. Personal skill and connection are not capital assets. A partner's reputation, clientele and know-how are valuable to the firm but are not of the firm unless he agrees to bring them in and have them valued.
  5. The corollary on depreciation. Where an asset is not firm property, the profits are not to be charged with depreciation on it.
  6. An "advantageous lease" taken before the partnership, in one partner's name, stays his. Directly applicable to the DU pattern of a business carried on in premises owned by one partner.

Cite Miles v. Clarke whenever the question is "is X partnership property?" — DU 2010 OC Q.4 (appreciation of a building owned by X), DU 2014 OC Q.2(b) and DU 1998 Q.4(a) / 2011 Q.7(a) (jointly purchased land + cinema hall), DU 1997 Q.7(b) / 1993 Q.7 ("mere use… does not make it partnership property"), DU 1995 Q.4(a). Always quote the "no more agreement than is absolutely necessary to give business efficacy" sentence, and distinguish it from Addanki Narayanappa, where the property was bought with firm money and so the s. 14 ¶2 deeming rule applied.

CASE 5 · Trimble v. Goldberg
(1906) AC 494 (Privy Council) · Lord Macnaghten · DU reader p. 26
[Accountability for profits of competing business — section 16]
Facts (from the DU case material)

Three men entered a joint adventure in the Transvaal in 1902:

  • Goldberg — a land speculator (the plaintiff/respondent);
  • Trimble — an auctioneer, formerly chief detective of the whole of the Transvaal before the war;
  • Bennett — a Durban merchant in a good financial position.

By a partnership agreement dated 10 February 1902 the object of the joint adventure was the purchase and re-sale of certain properties belonging to a Mr Hollard — 5,500 shares in a company called the Sigma (the "Sigma Building Syndicate Limited") and other real estate in Johannesburg and elsewhere in South Africa. The agreement was unremarkable: profits and losses to be shared equally; no partner to sell or dispose of his interest without written consent; and all dealings with the property of the partnership to be transacted by and through Trimble, to whom the other partners were to give powers of attorney.

Hollard, a wealthy man and a director of the Sigma Syndicate, was leaving South Africa and put all his properties on the market through Goldberg for £94,566 (the Sigma shares being put at £30,000). Goldberg's original plan to form a syndicate fell through; he decided to buy on his own account, and Bennett and Trimble joined him — "the one would not come in without the other". £12,500 was telegraphed in advance and Trimble was dispatched to Johannesburg.

Trimble completed the purchase on 14 February 1902, executing the deed on behalf of himself and his partners.

Then the critical episode. After that business was settled, Trimble went one day with Hollard to see the stands belonging to the Sigma Syndicate. At Government Square Trimble asked whether the syndicate would sell the stands en bloc. Hollard said yes, and thought the board would sell for £120,000, and referred him to Davis, the secretary. What Trimble did not know was that the board, holding 23,000 of the 25,000 shares, had already decided to accept £100,000 and was negotiating on that footing. Trimble took an option to buy for £110,000. He at once told Bennett "that he thought from some secret information he had gained, which it seems would not bear the light, that money was to be made out of the deal"; Bennett consented to join and to finance the enterprise. Thus Trimble and Bennett secured the stands at Government Square for themselves at £110,000.

Goldberg was told nothing. He did not hear of it until the end of 1902 or sometime in 1903. Meeting Trimble in the street he said, according to Trimble's uncontradicted evidence, corroborated by an accountant named Winship who was present: "Don't you think you might have let me have a show in?" Later he took a more exalted view of his rights and, in June 1904, brought this action.

Issues
  1. Had the partnership given Trimble a mandate to buy the Government Square stands on joint account? (Both courts below held this not proved.)
  2. If not — was Goldberg nonetheless entitled, "on general principles applicable to all cases of partnership", to share with his partners in the benefit of their purchase? The Supreme Court of the Transvaal said yes; the Witwatersrand High Court (Smith J.) had said no.
  3. Does the secrecy of the transaction change the result?
Arguments of both sides
Goldberg (respondent) — the argument that succeeded below
  • The purchase was "connected with" the partnership adventure indirectly, and was "injurious to the common interest".
  • On general principles the appellants were liable to account to their partner for any profit derived from the transaction.
  • The veil of secrecy was "a damning proof of guilt and aggravation of the wrong" — the Court of Appeal regarded the secrecy as decisive.
  • The Sigma Syndicate's articles said each share gives "a right to a proportionate share in the ownership of the company's assets" — which, it was said, gave the partnership (as a shareholder) an interest in the stands.
  • The conflict-of-duty argument: "the moment Trimble determined to buy these stands, he put himself in a position in which his interest and his duty conflicted. It was his interest to buy as cheaply as he could. It was his duty to sell the Sigma shares as dearly as possible… He ought not to have thought of buying them for less than the utmost price he felt he might have been forced or tempted to give."
Trimble and Bennett (appellants)
  • The purchase was not within the scope of the partnership. "The subject of the purchase was not part of the business of the partnership, or an undertaking in rivalry with the partnership, or indeed connected with it in any proper sense."
  • The information on which Trimble acted "was not acquired by reason of his connection with the Sigma Syndicate"; and in any event "Goldberg is not in a position to complain of that. He at least is not averse to sharing the profit to which it seems to have conducted."
  • Even if the Sigma shares had been divided and registered in the partners' separate names, any one of the three "would have had as good a right to buy any property of the syndicate which the directors might think fit to offer for sale as any other shareholder in the syndicate or any member of the general public."
  • No loss to the partnership — "only a disappointment to the partner left out in the cold". Indeed "the purchase apparently was an advantage to the partnership": it obtained £10,000 more for the syndicate's property than the Government would have paid, so the partnership as a shareholder was proportionately the gainer.
  • There was no legal obligation to disclose — quoting Lord Blackburn in Cassels v. Stewart: "I generally think it is advisable as a matter of prudence, as well as on other grounds, to let everything be above board … But still there was no legal obligation on Trimble or Bennett to tell Goldberg what they were doing unless he had a right to take part in the speculation if he chose to do so."
  • The conflict argument is a fallacy: "it assumes that Trimble had anything to do with selling the stands, or any right to meddle with the conduct of the sale. That was in the hands of the directors. They were dealing at arm's length with him."
Decision (Lord Macnaghten, for the Privy Council)

Appeal allowed; the order of the Supreme Court of the Transvaal reversed; the judgment of Smith J. restored; the appeal from that order dismissed with costs.

"It seems to their Lordships that judgment of the Court of Appeal is not well founded. The purchase was not within the scope of the partnership. The subject of the purchase was not part of the business of the partnership, or an undertaking in rivalry with the partnership, or indeed connected with it in any proper sense." Lord Macnaghten

The Board reasoned:

  1. No mandate — both courts had found the alleged mandate not proved; that finding stood.
  2. No loss to the partnership — "And here there was no loss to the partnership; only a disappointment to the partner left out in the cold."
  3. What if it had been expressly forbidden? Suppose the articles had forbidden such a purchase in express terms: "The other partners… discovering the breach of contract might have claimed immediate dissolution, or even damages, on proof of actual loss to the partnership. But a claim to share in the profits of the forbidden purchase would not have been warranted by principle or precedent." — a devastating hypothetical.
  4. The articles' share clause was "no more than an accurate description of the position of every shareholder in every trading company limited by shares" — nothing special.
  5. The conflict argument rejected — Trimble had no control over the sale; the directors dealt at arm's length with him; and "it seems extravagant to suppose that he would have advanced the interests of the partnership by retiring from the field and declining to enter into a competition which actually had the effect of raising the price of the stands and so improving the value of the Sigma shares."
  6. Cassels v. Stewart applied. In that House of Lords case, Stewart had secretly bought the interest of an outgoing partner, Reid, without telling Cassels, and it was argued for Cassels that "the secret agreement by which the respondent acquired for himself alone a benefit falling within the scope of the partnership business was a breach of the good faith of the partnership" and that "on general principles it was inequitable, having regard to the fiduciary relations due to each other, that such an agreement should be made behind the back of another partner". Without calling on the respondent, the House (Lord Selborne L.C., Lords Penzance, Blackburn and Watson) dismissed the appeal. Lord Macnaghten:
    "There was at least as close a connection between the partnership and the partner's purchase in that case as there is in this. In their Lordships' opinion the order under appeal cannot be supported on authority or on any recognized doctrine of equity." Lord Macnaghten
Principles evolved — and how they map onto s. 16
  1. s. 16 is not unlimited. A partner must account only for profits from a transaction connected with the firm — i.e. one that is (i) a transaction of the firm, or (ii) an undertaking in rivalry with the firm, or (iii) otherwise "connected with it in any proper sense". This is the Trimble limitation, and it is the single most important qualification to write in any s. 16 answer.
  2. Mere secrecy is not enough. However discreditable, secrecy does not by itself create liability. The Court of Appeal "regarded the veil of secrecy as a damning proof of guilt"; the Privy Council declined to follow. There must be a legal right in the other partner to participate before a duty to disclose arises: "there was no legal obligation on Trimble or Bennett to tell Goldberg what they were doing unless he had a right to take part in the speculation if he chose to do so."
  3. Disappointment is not loss. "No loss to the partnership; only a disappointment to the partner left out in the cold." Damages under s. 16 presuppose a profit within the section, not a lost opportunity.
  4. The remedy hierarchy matters. Even for an expressly forbidden act, the remedies are (i) dissolution and (ii) damages on proof of loss — not an account of profits.
  5. Benefit to the firm is a defence. Where the partner's outside dealing in fact enhanced the value of the firm's holding, there is nothing to account for.
  6. A partner may compete in his private capacity where the subject-matter is unconnected with the firm's business — subject always to s. 11(2), which lets the partnership deed forbid it altogether.
  7. Contrast with s. 16(b). Where the partner does carry on "a business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm", s. 16(b) requires him to account for all profits in that business. Trimble did not raise s. 16(b) because land speculation by two partners privately was not "a business" the firm carried on — the firm's business was the single Hollard purchase and re-sale.
★ How to use this case in a DU answer

Trimble v. Goldberg is the s. 16 case in the DU reader. Cite it for DU 2013 OC Q.2: "If a partner carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm, he shall account for and pay to the firm all profits made in competing business.' Discuss the proposition by referring to relevant case." Structure the answer as: (1) state s. 16 limbs (a) and (b); (2) say the text of limb (b) is unqualified; (3) add that Trimble reads in a connection/rivalry requirement; (4) give the facts; (5) quote "no more than a disappointment to the partner left out in the cold"; (6) conclude that a partner is accountable only where the outside business is within the scope of, or in rivalry with, the firm's business. Also useful for DU 2011 OC Q.1(b) "Majority rights" and any inter se duties question.

Cross-reference: the same "connected with the firm" idea appears in s. 50, PA, which extends s. 16(a) to transactions by a surviving partner undertaken after dissolution on death and before the affairs are completely wound up.

CASE 6 · Holme v. Hammond
(1872) L.R. 7 Ex. 218 : 41 L.J. Ex. 157 · Court of Exchequer (Kelly C.B., Martin B.) · DU reader p. 31
[Section 19 — Duty of a partner as an agent of the firm] · printed by DU under the heading "RELATIONS OF PARTNERS TO THIRD PARTIES"
Facts (from the DU case material)

Thomas Fisher, William Henry Fisher and George Henry Smith carried on business in co-partnership as auctioneers, under a deed which provided that in case of the death of Thomas Fisher, the other two partners should carry on the business and should pay to the executors of Thomas Fisher the share of the profits to which he would have been entitled if he had survived.

Thomas Fisher died in August 1869. The two survivors carried on the business until the death of Smith, when W. H. Fisher continued alone.

In May 1870 W. H. Fisher and Smith sold a mill and machinery on account of the plaintiff (Holme) and in the following July received the proceeds. The plaintiff sued the executors of Thomas Fisher (the defendants) to recover that sum as money had and received, contending that:

  • the executors had claimed to be entitled to the share of profits which the testator would have had if he had lived;
  • they had received certain sums in respect of that claim — "together with other moneys due to the estate of Thomas Fisher not specifically as profits, but generally on account";
  • and therefore they had become partners with W. H. Fisher and Smith upon or after the death of Thomas Fisher, and as such were liable for the sum.

At the trial Kelly C.B. had been "inclined to think" that a contract by the executors to succeed their testator and become partners might be inferred, on the basis that part of the testator's capital had been left in the business. On fuller evidence that basis disappeared.

Issues
  1. Were the defendants, the executors of Thomas Fisher, partners of W. H. Fisher and Smith at the time the money was received? Kelly C.B. framed it as "the single question in this case": whether the defendants had "expressly or impliedly entered into a contract of co-partnership… with W. H. Fisher and Smith, who survived him."
  2. Does the mere receipt of a share of profits make the recipient a partner liable to third parties?
Arguments of both sides
The plaintiff (Holme)
  • The executors claimed a share of profits under the deed, acquiesced in that claim being honoured by the survivors, and actually received the proportion of profits said to have been ascertained on an account taken from 30 June 1869 to 30 June 1870.
  • The accounts showed that the defendants, "instead of calling for an account of the state of the partnership at the death of their testator, and withdrawing from the concern whatever money or stock of property belonged to his estate, had left a portion of his capital and his share in the partnership stock and property in the business".
  • Reliance was placed on the older authorities — Grace v. Smith and particularly Waugh v. Carver — for the proposition that participation in profits makes a man a partner as regards third parties.
The defendants (the executors)
  • They did nothing on their own behalf at all: "they merely did that which a court of equity would have compelled them to do as executors under the will."
  • "It would be contrary to reason to hold them liable by that act to a responsibility which must of necessity be borne by them in their own personal capacity, and paid out of their private funds." (Wightman v. Townroe.)
  • The plaintiff's own counsel conceded that "the defendants could not have interfered in or meddled with the sale; so also the money, the proceeds of it, was not their property, and if they had taken possession of it against the will of the surviving partners in order to pay it to the plaintiff they would have been trespassers".
  • On the facts as finally established: there was no capital at all of the testator's or of the other partners in the business; the stock and co-partnership property was merely a small quantity of furniture and fittings worth about £100; and the defendants "neither left in nor drew out any money of the testator's except that they drew out several successive sums of £100 upon the general account".
Decision (Kelly C.B. and Martin B.; Bramwell B. concurring in the result)

Judgment for the defendants. The action was not maintainable.

Kelly C.B. undertook a full review of the authorities and stated the true principle:

"Upon a careful consideration of the authorities bearing on this question, it certainly appears to have been thought in former times, and there are judicial dicta to that effect, that the mere reception of a share in the profits of a commercial co-partnership made the participator a partner… But looking to the decisions themselves … it will be seen that in no one case has the party sought to be charged been held liable except where a contract of co-partnership has been found to have been entered into." Kelly C.B.
  • Grace v. Smith — although the language of De Grey C.J. and Blackstone J. "appears to support the argument for the liability as partners of all who participate in the profits", the decision was that there was no sufficient evidence of a contract of co-partnership.
  • Waugh v. Carver — the defendant was held liable "because the contract proved was decided, and rightly decided, to be a contract establishing a commercial co-partnership".
  • Cox v. Hickman, Bullen v. Sharp and Shaw v. Galt — in each the party was held not liable on the ground that no contract of co-partnership had been entered into.
  • On 28 & 29 Vict. c. 86 (Bovill's Act) — "the effect of the statute is that, with regard to widows, lenders of money, and some other classes of persons taking a share in the profits of a co-partnership, sharing in profits shall be no evidence at all of a contract of partnership, whereas, with regard to others, it is evidence, though insufficient of itself to establish the liability."
  • On the facts: on Thomas Fisher's death "the partnership before subsisting was dissolved by operation of law"; the surviving business was "in contemplation of law, a new partnership"; and "the defendants could not become partners with them but by some agreement, express or implied, to which they were parties." Once it appeared that there was no capital left in and no more than the receipt of profits under the deed, "there is no evidence whatever to establish a contract of co-partnership on the part of the defendants".

Martin B. (who had at one point been inclined to the plaintiff) concurred, and adopted O'Brien J.'s celebrated formulation in Shaw v. Galt:

"The principle to be collected from them appears to be, that a partnership, even as to third parties, is not constituted by the mere fact of two or more persons participating or being interested in the net profits of a business; but that the existence of such partnership implies also the existence of such a relation between those persons as that each of them is a principal and each an agent for the others. If this principle be correct, the defendants are clearly not liable. The surviving partners were not agents of theirs in any sense; all that the defendants did was in an adverse character to them, and was a requirement that they should fulfil their contract with the testator by paying the one-third of the net profits for the benefit of his estate." O'Brien J. in Shaw v. Galt, quoted by Martin B.
Principles evolved
  1. Profit-sharing is evidence, never proof. The receipt of a share of profits is evidence of partnership, insufficient of itself to establish liability. This is the direct ancestor of s. 6, PA 1932 and its Explanation 2.
  2. The decisive test is mutual agency. "Each of them is a principal and each an agent for the others." Without agency there is no partnership — the same proposition that s. 18 codifies positively (a partner is the agent of the firm) and that Cox v. Hickman established negatively.
  3. Doing only what the law compels is not consent. Executors who merely receive what the deed (and a court of equity) entitles the estate to, do not thereby become partners. Receipt of money in an adverse character is the opposite of acting as principal.
  4. Death dissolves a partnership by operation of law. The continuing business is "in contemplation of law, a new partnership" — the forerunner of s. 42(c), PA 1932.
  5. A person cannot be made a partner without his agreement. "The defendants could not become partners … but by some agreement, express or implied, to which they were parties." — the ancestor of s. 31(1).
  6. Statutory protection for widows/lenders removes profit-sharing as evidence altogether for those classes — the ancestor of s. 6, Expl. 2, PA 1932.

(DU 2017 Q.3(a), 2015 Q.3(a), 2014 OC Q.3, 2013 OC Q.3(a), 2012 OC Q.4(a), 2004 Q.5(i), 1997 Q.4(a), 1993 Q.6(a)) — as the negative half of the answer: liability requires partnership, and partnership requires contract + agency. CASE 7 · Rhodes v. Moules
(1895) 1 Ch. 236 (Court of Appeal) · Lord Herschell L.C. · DU reader p. 35
[The securities misappropriated by one partner were habitually held by the firm] (s. 27)
Facts (from the DU case material)

Mr Rew, a solicitor, practised in partnership with Messrs Hughes and Masterman in the City of London. The litigation arose out of Rew's frauds.

Mr Rhodes, the plaintiff, was a client of the firm and the firm had acted for him in previous matters. In August 1891 Rhodes placed certificates for 280 De Beers shares in Rew's hands. Rew fraudulently misappropriated them (he had begun Stock Exchange speculations in January of that year which led to his ruin, and he ultimately disposed of the shares as his own).

Lord Herschell L.C. opened with a sentence that is worth quoting in every answer on this topic:

"This is one of those painful cases in which whatever judgment is pronounced the loss must fall upon some innocent person who has not by act or default contributed to it." Lord Herschell L.C.

The context which proved decisive was the previous course of dealing:

  • On previous occasions the firm had acted for Rhodes in negotiating loans, and in receiving from him these very securities and transmitting them to the lenders, and initially receiving them back from the lenders.
  • "That that was a firm transaction I think it is impossible to dispute, because … it passed through the books of the firm, the firm credited themselves with the charges in respect of it, and an account was sent in the name of the firm, and that account was discharged by Mr Rhodes."
  • Rew had represented to Rhodes that the lenders required collateral security beyond the mortgage of the freehold, that it was to consist of these De Beers shares, and that he would arrange with the lenders that he should hold them as for them collateral security. In truth no such arrangement had been made — Rew had no communication with the Moules on the subject at all; they never required further security. Rew even made himself a mortgagee in the mortgage deed "not only without any authority to do so, but without any legitimate reason for doing so".

Two groups of defendants: the firm (Hughes and Masterman) and the Moules (the alleged lenders, for whom Rew claimed to be holding the shares).

Issues
  1. Were Rew's partners liable to make good the loss to Rhodes, given that taking shares for custody was said to be outside the scope of a solicitor's business and the partners were admittedly ignorant of the transaction?
  2. Were the Moules liable — i.e. did Rew in fact receive and hold the shares for them, and if so with their authority?
Arguments of both sides
Mr Rhodes (plaintiff)
  • The shares were handed to Rew not for safe custody but in connection with a mortgage transaction he was carrying out, "in order that they should pass through him as collateral security to the lenders for whom he was acting".
  • Having regard to the prior dealings — letters in the firm's letter-book, charges made by the firm and paid by him, the previous transaction through the firm's books — "it is impossible for them to say that Mr Rhodes was not perfectly justified in assuming that the partner with whom on this occasion he dealt had authority from the firm to receive from him the shares."
  • The fact that on this occasion the shares were not in fact handed over to the lenders makes no difference — that was a matter between Rew (or the firm) and their other clients.
  • Alternatively, the Moules are liable because Rew held the shares on their behalf and, if so, they cannot call on Rhodes to repay the loan without reconveying and giving up the De Beers shares.
The firm (Hughes & Masterman) and the Moules
  • The firm: it was "beyond the scope of Mr Rew's authority as a solicitor to take the shares for any such purpose, or under such circumstances"; and "inasmuch as his partners were admittedly ignorant of his having so taken them, they cannot be bound by the transaction or incur any liability in respect of it." Reliance was placed mainly on Cleather v. Twisden (28 Ch. D. 340), said to establish that it is not part of a solicitor's business to take bearer bonds for custody.
  • The Moules: Rew "had no communication on the subject with the Moules at all; they never required further security". Mrs Moules "requires to know about everything. He professes to tell her about everything. He asks her approval at every step" — the correspondence conveyed "precisely the opposite impression" of a general authority. "Seeing that neither she nor her son ever learned that these shares had been taken or held for them … it seems to me it would be somewhat extravagant to arrive at the conclusion … that they were held by the firm or Mr Rew for the Moules."
Decision (Lord Herschell L.C.)

As regards the firm — judgment reversed; judgment for the plaintiff with costs. As regards the Moules — appeal dismissed with costs; they are not liable.

(1) The firm is liable.

  • The previous transaction was a firm transaction: "as I have shewn, it passed through the books of the firm, the firm credited themselves with the charges in respect of it, and an account was sent in the name of the firm, and that account was discharged by Mr Rhodes. Therefore, it is impossible to dispute that Mr Rhodes had on the previous occasion actually carried through a transaction with the firm."
  • "Even apart from that, I am not satisfied that it would be outside the scope of a solicitor's business when they were negotiating a loan for one of their clients to receive from him securities, whatever their nature, for the purpose of transmission to any of their clients who were making the loan."
  • Given the prior dealings, "I think it is impossible for them to say that Mr Rhodes was not perfectly justified in assuming that the partner with whom on this occasion he dealt had authority from the firm to receive from him the shares."
  • Cleather v. Twisden distinguished. First, on the facts: "these bonds were not handed to Mr Rew merely for safe custody: they were handed to him in connection with a mortgage transaction which he was carrying out." Secondly, as a matter of authority: in Cleather it was conceded that such a transaction was beyond the ordinary scope of a solicitor's business, so any finding of law "would have been superfluous"; and Bowen L.J. expressly said "This is conceded to be beyond the ordinary scope of the business of solicitors, though, of course, it may be brought within it by special circumstances." Lord Herschell: "it must depend upon the special circumstances; and certainly if it were to appear that it had been part of the practice of solicitors in the City to take securities of this description for safe custody, or if indeed in the case of a particular firm it appeared that such had been the practice, the case would have been one requiring the Court to determine whether in the case of that firm at all events … it was not a matter within the scope of the authority of one of the partners."
  • Applying Bowen L.J.'s test — "whether in letters for which the firm are responsible, language has been used which would justify the plaintiffs in assuming that Parker's custody was the custody of the firm" — the letters, the charges and the payment "would justify the Plaintiff in assuming that when Rew received those shares he received them, not as an individual, but on behalf of the firm, and that his receipt of them was the receipt of the firm."
  • If the shares had been handed to the lenders the firm would have been discharged; but Rew's representation that the arrangement was otherwise "can make no possible difference in the result".

(2) The Moules are not liable. "In order to establish this case I think they must make out two things: first, that Mr Rew did in fact receive and hold these De Beers shares for the Defendants Moules; and secondly, that he did so with the authority of the Moules." Neither was made out: Rew never earmarked or identified the shares as theirs; he "led Mr Rhodes to believe that he did: but that is quite a different question"; and even if he did, there was no authority — the correspondence showed Mrs Moules requiring to be told everything, not "leaving everything to him in that blind way".

Principles evolved
  1. Apparent authority is a question of fact, shaped by the previous course of dealing. Whether an act is "within the scope of the business" depends on the special circumstances of the particular trade and the particular firm — there is no fixed rule that a certain act is always outside scope. This is the Indian s. 19(1) test ("business of the kind carried on by the firm") applied with full factual sensitivity.
  2. A previous firm transaction clothes the partner with authority for a later, similar one. Books, charges and bills in the firm's name are the classic evidence.
  3. Mere custody is weaker than custody incidental to a firm transaction. Taking securities for safe custody alone may well be outside a solicitor's business; taking them in connection with a mortgage transaction the firm is handling is not.
  4. The firm is not discharged by the partner's misappropriation — that is precisely s. 27(a) (a partner acting within his apparent authority receives money or property from a third party and misapplies it → the firm is liable to make good the loss), read with the s. 24 exception (notice to the acting partner is not notice to the firm where the fraud is by or with his consent).
  5. To fix a third party with a receipt, two things must be proved — actual receipt on his behalf and authority. A representation by the partner that he holds for X does not make X liable.
  6. The loss should fall on the innocent party who enabled the fraud. The firm selected Rew and held him out by books, bills and letters; as between two innocents, the loss lies where the firm's own arrangements allowed it to fall.

Rhodes v. Moules is the s. 27 case. Cite it for DU 1999 Q.3 / 1993 Q.3 ("A borrows in the name of the firm without B's knowledge and spends the money for his own purpose — can C hold B liable? What is the scope of liability of a partner for the acts of the firm?") and for DU 2004 Q.4(ii) (partner borrows ₹40,000 in the firm's name and buys a plot in his wife's name). CASE 8 · Hamlyn v. Houston & Co.
(1903) 1 K.B. 81 · Court of Appeal (Collins M.R., Mathew J.) · DU reader p. 39
[Liability of other partners / firm for wrongful acts of a partner — one partner bribed the clerk of a competing firm to obtain information relating to contracts] (s. 26)
Facts (from the DU case material)

The defendant Strong was, in the words of Collins M.R., "a sleeping partner in a firm consisting of himself and the defendant Houston" — or, at any rate, "he delegated the transaction of the whole of the firm's business to Houston".

The firm dealt in grain and dealt with brewers. The jury found that it was in the course of the business of the firm to obtain, by legitimate means, information in regard to contracts made or tendered for with brewers and with buyers of grain by competing firms.

What Houston actually did was to bribe the clerk of the plaintiff, a competitor in business, "to give him access to documents belonging to the plaintiff; indeed, it would appear that he actually had possession of one of the plaintiff's books for a time."

The plaintiff sued the firm for damages. The partner Strong (through the firm) contended that Houston's act was so completely outside the scope of his authority that the firm could not be responsible for it.

Issue

Whether the firm (and hence the innocent sleeping partner) is liable for the fraudulent and possibly criminal act of one partner committed in the course of obtaining information which it was within the scope of his authority to obtain by legitimate means.

Arguments of both sides
The plaintiff (Hamlyn)
  • The jury has found, as a fact, that obtaining information about competitors' contracts and tenders was part of the defendants' business — "the more secret these matters were, the greater was the value of that information to the defendants' firm."
  • Where the end is within the scope of the authority, the fact that the agent chose illegitimate means does not take the act outside the scope of his employment.
  • "It is too well established by the authorities to be now disputed that a principal may be liable for the fraud or other illegal act committed by his agent within the general scope of the authority given to him, and even the fact that the act of the agent is criminal does not necessarily take it out of the scope of his authority."
  • The principal selected the agent; the principal would have had the benefit of his efforts if successful; so the principal should bear the risk.
The defendants (the firm / the sleeping partner)
  • Counsel "endeavoured to frame a definition with regard to what is and what is not within the scope of an agent's authority so as to render his principal liable." Their proposed test:
    "Where the end sought to be obtained by the agent is in itself illegal, and the means employed to accomplish it are illegal, it cannot be said that the action of the agent is within the scope of the general authority given to him to conduct a business, but that it is otherwise where the end and the means employed are legal, or where the end is legal or illegal." as framed by counsel for the defendants
  • Applying that test: obtaining a rival's confidential documents by bribing his clerk was both an illegal end and an illegal means, so the act was wholly outside the scope of Houston's authority and the firm is not responsible.
  • Strong was a sleeping partner who knew nothing of the bribe — he cannot be fixed with liability for a fraud of which he was ignorant.
Decision (Collins M.R., with Mathew J. concurring)

Application dismissed. The firm was liable.

Collins M.R. rejected the defendants' proposed test on its own terms:

  • "Trying this case by the test so suggested, was the end to be obtained here in itself illegal? … it does not appear to me to be so. According to the finding of the jury it was part of the defendants' business to obtain information as to the contracts and tenders of competitors in business … and I do not see that there was anything illegal in so obtaining it" — i.e. obtaining the information legitimately was perfectly lawful.
  • The operative rule as stated by the Master of the Rolls:
    "It is too well established by the authorities to be now disputed that a principal may be liable for the fraud or other illegal act committed by his agent within the general scope of the authority given to him, and even the fact that the act of the agent is criminal does not necessarily take it out of the scope of his authority. If the act done by the agent is within the general scope of the authority given to him, it matters not for the present purpose that it was directly contrary to the instructions of his principal, or even that it may have been an offence against society itself. The test is that which is applied to this case by the learned judge. Was it within the scope of the authority given to Houston to obtain this information by legitimate means? If so, it was within the scope of his authority for the present purpose to obtain it by illegitimate means, and the defendants are liable. That is the law as expressed in the Partnership Act, 1890, and as laid down by decisions previous to that Act." Collins M.R.
  • The doctrine does not rest on holding out. "This doctrine does not appear to rest upon the notion of the principal's holding out the agent as having authority. The grounds upon which it seems to rest, as explained in cases such as Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank, appear to be that the principal is the person who has selected the agent, and must therefore be taken to have had better means of knowing what sort of a person he was than those with whom the agent deals on behalf of his principal; and that, the principal having delegated the performance of a certain class of acts to the agent, it is not unjust that he, being the person who has appointed the agent, and who will have the benefit of his efforts if successful, should bear the risk of his exceeding his authority in matters incidental to the doing of the acts the performance of which has been delegated to him."

Mathew J. added two important clarifications:

"A little confusion has been introduced into this case by the reference made to the criminal law. It is not suggested that Houston's partner would be liable criminally; the question is only one of civil liability. … He did obtain such information by illegitimate means. It being within the scope of his authority to procure the information, it is immaterial for the present purpose whether the acts which he committed in order to procure it were fraudulent or even criminal or not, and his partner is responsible for those acts." Mathew J.
Principles evolved
  1. The "legitimate means" test. If it was within the scope of the partner's authority to obtain a result by legitimate means, then obtaining it by illegitimate means is also within the scope of his authority for the purpose of the firm's civil liability. This is the formulation that maps directly onto s. 26, PA 1932 ("wrongful act or omission of a partner acting in the ordinary course of the business of a firm… the firm is liable therefor to the same extent as the partner").
  2. Civil, not criminal. The firm's liability is civil only. The innocent partner is not criminally liable for the guilty partner's offence — a distinction Mathew J. insisted be kept clear.
  3. The basis is risk-allocation, not estoppel. Following Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank, the principal is liable because he chose the agent, he had the best means of judging him, he delegated the class of acts, and he would have taken the benefit if the venture had succeeded.
  4. Contrary instructions and even criminality are irrelevant once the act is within the general scope: "it matters not … that it was directly contrary to the instructions of his principal, or even that it may have been an offence against society itself."
  5. A sleeping partner is fully liable. Delegation of "the transaction of the whole of the firm's business" to the active partner does not insulate the sleeping partner; it is the very fact that creates the risk.
  6. Internal recourse survives. The firm may look to the guilty partner under s. 10 (fraud), s. 13(f) (wilful neglect) and s. 16 (secret profit).

  1. State s. 26 verbatim.
  2. Ask two questions: (i) was the partner "acting in the ordinary course of the business of the firm"? (ii) did he have the authority of his partners?

CASE 9 · Tower Cabinet Co., Ltd v. Ingram
(1949) 1 KBD 1032 · King's Bench Division (Lynskey J., Lord Goddard C.J.) · DU reader p. 41
[Holding out — section 28] · "The words are 'knowingly suffers' — not being negligent or careless"
Facts (from the DU case material)

The respondent company, Tower Cabinet Co. Ltd., claimed £23 17s. for six suites of furniture sold and delivered to "Merry's", sued as a firm. Judgment was obtained against Merry's. The company then sought to make Mr S. G. Ingram liable for the debts of Merry's, alleging he was liable first under s. 14 (holding out) and secondly under s. 36 (the outgoing-partner / change-of-constitution provision) of the Partnership Act, 1890. The matter went before Master Grundy.

The DU reader inserts the note: "Section 14 of the Act of 1890 was identical with the provisions of section 28 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932."

The master found:

  • In January 1946 Mr A. H. Christmas and Mr Ingram began business in partnership as household furnishers under the name "Merry's" at Silver Street, Edmonton, registered under the Registration of Business Names Act 1916 as carried on by both of them.
  • The partnership was dissolved on 22 April 1947. Ingram gave notice to the firm's bankers that he had ceased to be a partner, and arranged with Christmas to notify those dealing with the firm — but he did not advertise, and did not procure advertisement in the London Gazette.
  • Christmas agreed to pay Ingram some £3,000 for his share; about £1,000 had been paid by May 1948.
  • New notepaper was printed after the dissolution, headed "Merry's" with "A. H. Christmas, Director". While Ingram had been a partner the notepaper bore both names: "A. H. Christmas and S. G. Ingram".
  • In January 1948 Mr Harold Selbey, the company's representative, obtained an order for six suites of furniture. A director, Mr Jack Smead, telephoned Merry's for confirmation. A letter/order dated 5 January 1947 in mistake for 5 January 1948 was written on the old notepaper bearing both names, signed by Christmas as manager.
  • Critically: "Mr Christmas had no authority from Mr Ingram to use it, and in using it he was acting in direct conflict with the arrangement that he had made with Mr Ingram."
  • In May 1948 Ingram grew worried, put some £300 into the business and tried to resuscitate it. Christmas wrote to the company: "as from today I am no longer connected with the above business. Mr S. G. Ingram is now sole proprietor and responsible for all outstanding debts." — written without Ingram's knowledge or authority.
  • "It is clear on the master's finding that in January and February 1948 when the goods were ordered and delivered Mr Ingram was not in fact a partner in this business."
Issues
  1. s. 14 (holding out): had Ingram, by words or conduct, represented himself as a partner — or knowingly suffered himself to be represented as a partner — to someone who gave credit on the faith of that representation?
  2. s. 36(1): what is the meaning of "apparent members" of the old firm — apparent to the whole world (notorious), or apparent to the particular person dealing with the firm?
  3. s. 36(3): where the person dealing with the firm did not know that the retiring partner had been a partner, does that partner escape liability from the date of his retirement?
Arguments of both sides
The company (Tower Cabinet Co. Ltd.)
  • The order was given on notepaper containing Ingram's name — that is a representation by Christmas that Ingram was still a partner, and Ingram ought to have seen that the old notepaper was destroyed.
  • Counsel relied on Farrar v. Deflinne and drew a distinction between "notorious" partners and "profoundly secret" members, contending that s. 36(1) and (2) deal with notorious partnerships and s. 36(3) with profoundly secret ones — and that Merry's was notorious, so Ingram could not rely on s. 36(3) without advertising in the Gazette.
  • The company did know Ingram was a partner, because the order document stated (or appeared to state) that he was one.
Mr Ingram
  • He was not in fact a partner in January/February 1948 — that is the master's unchallenged finding.
  • He gave no representation himself: "There is no evidence of that."
  • The only representation was made by Christmas, without his knowledge and without his authority — and the statute requires that he "knowingly suffers" it.
  • He had in fact arranged with Christmas to notify customers — the use of the old notepaper was "in direct conflict with the arrangement".
  • On s. 36: "apparent members" means apparent to the person dealing with the firm, not to the world at large; and s. 36(3) applies to any partner — apparent or dormant — if the person dealing with the firm did not know he was a partner.
Decision (Lynskey J., Lord Goddard C.J. concurring)

Appeal allowed. Ingram was under no liability.

(1) Holding out (s. 14 ≡ s. 28) fails.

"Before the company can succeed in making Mr Ingram liable under this section, they have to satisfy the court that Mr Ingram, by words spoken or written or by conduct, represented himself as a partner. There is no evidence of that. Alternatively, they must prove that he knowingly suffered himself to be represented as a partner. The only evidence … is that the order was given by Mr Christmas on notepaper which contained Mr Ingram's name. That would amount to a representation of Mr Christmas that Mr Ingram was still a partner in the firm, but on the evidence and the master's finding that representation was made by Mr Christmas without Mr Ingram's knowledge and without his authority. That being the finding of fact, which is not challenged, it is impossible to say that Mr Ingram knowingly suffered himself to be so represented. The words are 'knowingly suffers' — not being negligent or careless in not seeing that all the notepaper had been destroyed when he left." Lynskey J.

(2) The meaning of "apparent members" (s. 36(1)). Lynskey J. rejected the notorious/secret construction: "Apparent to whom? Does it mean apparent to the whole world, or notorious, or does it mean apparent to the particular person with whom the section is dealing? In my reading of that sub-section, 'apparent members' means persons who appear to be members to the person who is dealing with the firm, and they may be apparent either by the fact that the customer has had dealing with them before, or because of the use of their names on the notepaper, or from some sign outside the door, or because the customer has had some indirect information about them." Lord Goddard C.J.: "I need only add that, in my opinion, the words 'all apparent members' in s. 36(1) mean all members apparent to the person dealing with the firm."

(3) s. 36(3) applies to Ingram. "Sub-section (3) again deals with the particular individual. It does not deal with the public at large… Whether he was to other people an apparent partner, or whether he was a dormant partner, the words seem to me to be equally applicable. If the person dealing with the firm did not know that the particular partner was a partner, and if that partner retired, then, as from the date of his retirement, he ceases to be liable for further debts contracted by the firm with that person. The fact that later the person dealing with the firm may discover he was a partner seems to me to be irrelevant, because the date from which the sub-section operates is the date of the dissolution."

(4) The January 1948 order document did not give the company knowledge. "That document … was, no doubt, a representation by Mr Christmas that Mr Ingram was a partner at that particular date. That representation was untrue. … one cannot draw the inference that that gave the company knowledge that, in fact, Mr Ingram had been a partner prior to the date of his dissolution of the partnership in April 1947."

Lord Goddard C.J. on Farrar v. Deflinne: "the true construction to put on this section is that there must be actual knowledge which may be acquired either because of the fact that it is notorious, or because it has been directly communicated, but it is not enough to say that other people knew it. The fact may be so notorious that a jury would be justified in finding that the person did know a certain fact, but it does not follow because other people know it that he knew it."

Principles evolved
  1. "Knowingly suffers" means actual knowledge — negligence is not enough. This is the headline proposition and the one DU examiners are testing. A retiring partner who fails to destroy old letterheads is careless; he is not thereby liable under s. 28 unless he knew the representation was being made.
  2. The representation must be by him, or with his knowledge. A co-partner's unauthorised use of the old notepaper is not attributed to him — "in using it he was acting in direct conflict with the arrangement".
  3. "Apparent members" is a relational concept — apparent to the person dealing with the firm, not to the world. Ways of being apparent: previous dealings, the name on notepaper, a sign on the door, indirect information.
  4. A retiring partner escapes, from the date of dissolution, as against a person who did not know he was a partner — the statutory analogue in India being s. 32(3) proviso and the s. 45 proviso.
  5. Knowledge must be actual — notoriety is merely evidence from which actual knowledge may be inferred; "it does not follow because other people know it that he knew it."
  6. An untrue representation made by someone else does not create knowledge in the person to whom it is made.

CASE 9-b · Snow White Food Products Ltd. v. Sohan Lal
AIR 1964 Cal. 239 · Calcutta High Court (Bijayesh Mukherji J.) · Suit No. 597 of 1961, decided 10 January 1964
[Holding out by words and conduct — s. 28] · named in the DU syllabus; listed in the reader's TOC but NOT reproduced in its text from the reports
Facts

Snow White Food Products Ltd. consigned goods through Shree Hanuman Transport Company (defendant No. 3), a firm carrying on business as common carriers. The goods were tampered with and damaged in transit.

The plaintiff sued for damages — about ₹16,000 in respect of a consignment to Islampur and about ₹931 in respect of a consignment to Raiganj — and impleaded, among others, Sohan Lal Bagla (defendant No. 1) and Madan Lal Poddar, alleging that they were the partners of the transport company. The insurer (New India Assurance Co. Ltd.) was also joined on the question of insurance.

Sohan Lal's defence rested on the Register of Firms: the entries showed Madan Lal Poddar and Babulal Saraogi as the partners of the transport company — not Sohan Lal. On that footing he contended he was a stranger to the firm.

The plaintiff's answer was that, whatever the register said, Sohan Lal had held himself out as a partner: he had stated in the course of verbal negotiations that he was a partner of the firm, and his conduct in carrying on the correspondence over the consignments confirmed that belief.

Issues
  1. (A) s. 68, PA 1932 — what is the evidentiary value of an entry in the Register of Firms recording a notice of change in the constitution of a firm? Is it conclusive against a third party whose name is not in the register?
  2. (B) Procedure — could the court frame a second issue on "holding out" when the first issue was only "Has S been a partner at all material times of defendant No. 3?" (Order XIV, r. 3, CPC.)
  3. (D) s. 28, PA — had Sohan Lal held himself out as a partner by words and conduct?
Arguments of both sides
Snow White Food Products Ltd. (plaintiff)
  • The register is not conclusive against a third party; a person whose name is not in it may still be proved to be a partner.
  • In the course of verbal negotiations Sohan Lal gave out that he was a partner of the firm.
  • His correspondence over the consignments confirmed that belief.
  • The insurance surveyor, on Sohan Lal's instructions, sold the damaged goods to the highest bidder and handed the sale proceeds (₹11,556) to Sohan Lal — conduct wholly consistent with partnership and inconsistent with being a stranger.
  • Having induced the plaintiff to entrust the goods to the firm on the faith of that representation, he is estopped from denying it (s. 28; s. 115, Evidence Act).
Sohan Lal (defendant No. 1)
  • The Register of Firms records Madan Lal Poddar and Babulal Saraogi as partners; his name does not appear.
  • Under s. 68 the entries are conclusive proof of the facts stated in them.
  • He was never a partner; the allegation that he "held out" was denied in the written statement.
  • Procedurally, a new issue on holding out could not be framed — it did not arise on the pleadings.
Decision (Bijayesh Mukherji J.)

Headnote (D): "Partnership Act (9 of 1932), s. 28 — Holding out to be partner by words — Firm carrying on business of transport as common carriers — S giving out in course of verbal negotiations that he is partner of firm — His conduct in carrying on correspondence over the consignments confirming belief that he was partner — Held that S was partner of the firm."

  1. On s. 68 (evidentiary value of the register): "The entry made in the Register of Firm of the notice of change in the constitution of firm is conclusive proof as against person by whom or on whose behalf notice was signed and not against a third party. A third party whose name is not in Register can prove that he is a partner nonetheless. Where however, no such attempt was made by the third party he could not be held to be a partner on the basis of the notice of change in the constitution of the firm of others." — i.e. the register binds those who signed the notice (here, the firm), but a stranger to the notice is neither bound by it nor protected by it.
  2. On the framing of issues: statements made by counsel on both sides are "statements made by pleaders of parties within R. 3(a)" of Order XIV, and the court is entitled to frame an issue on them. Further, where the plaintiff says in the plaint that S was a partner and S denies it in the written statement, "there is a material proposition of fact affirmed by one party and denied by the other", which can form a distinct issue under O. XIV, r. 1(3). And in any event: "the matter of 'holding out' was inherent in the first issue itself and no discussion of the first issue could be regarded as complete without determination of the question whether or not S was a partner by 'holding out'."
  3. On s. 28: on the evidence — the verbal statements, the correspondence over the consignments, and the surveyor's sale on his instructions with the proceeds paid to him — Sohan Lal was a partner of the firm by holding out and was made liable to pay the balance of the claim.
Principles evolved
  1. Holding out may be by words spoken — an oral statement in the course of negotiations is sufficient. s. 28 says "by words spoken or written or by conduct".
  2. Conduct is often the stronger evidence — conducting the correspondence over the consignments, and directing the insurance surveyor's sale and taking the proceeds, are acts that only a person with authority in the firm would do.
  3. The Register of Firms is not a shield for a stranger. s. 68 makes entries conclusive only against the person who signed the notice. A person whose name is absent may still be shown to be a partner — and, a fortiori, may be held liable under s. 28.
  4. "Holding out" is inherent in the issue of partnership. A court may and should decide it without a separately framed issue.
  5. Estoppel, not status. The finding does not make Sohan Lal a partner for all purposes — it fixes him with liability to the creditor who relied on the representation.
★ How to use this case in a DU answer

This is the Indian holding-out case, and the direct authority for the DU question "D, who is not actually a partner in the firm consisting of A, B and C, represents to X that he is also a partner; on the faith of that representation X gives credit to the firm — can D be made liable under the doctrine of holding out?" (2013 OC Q.3(b); 2003 Q.5(b)). Use it to establish the first limb of s. 28 (representation by the person himself), and reserve Tower Cabinet for the second limb ("knowingly suffers"). Also useful for DU 1995 Q.5(a) / 1992 Q.2(a): "A holds himself out as a partner before M; M reports it to F; F advances money to the firm — is A liable?" — answer yes, because s. 28 expressly says it is immaterial whether the representor knew that the representation "has reached the person so giving credit".

CASE 9-c · Scarf v. Jardine
(1882) 7 App. Cas. 345 · House of Lords · named in the DU syllabus; listed in the reader's TOC but NOT reproduced in its text from the reports
[Retiring partner — notice of retirement and the "apparent member" rule] (Indian analogues: ss. 28, 32(3), 45)
Facts

Scarf and Rogers carried on business in partnership. Scarf retired and Beach was introduced as a new partner in his place; Rogers and Beach continued the business. No public notice was given of either event — neither of Scarf's retirement nor of Beach's admission. "The whole change was internal; no one was informed, neither the customer nor the suppliers."

Jardine was an old supplier of the firm. He continued to supply goods without knowing of the change. The new firm did not pay him; it went bankrupt; and Jardine — having discovered the change in the course of his suit — sued Scarf personally for the price of the goods, on the footing that by failing to notify suppliers of his retirement Scarf remained liable.

Issues
  1. Can a retired partner who gave no notice of his retirement be held liable for debts incurred by the firm after his retirement?
  2. What is the nature and manner of the notice required — and does it matter that the creditor is an old customer rather than a new one?
  3. On whom does the burden lie, and what is the effect of the creditor's knowledge?
Arguments of both sides
Jardine (the supplier)
  • He had dealt with the firm throughout and had no notice of any change.
  • A retiring partner must give notice of his retirement in the same manner as notice of his appointment was given — so that those who deal with the firm know his true status.
  • Having failed to do so, Scarf is "held out" as a partner and is estopped from denying the character he allowed to be assumed.
  • Alternatively, under the change-of-constitution rule he is entitled to treat all apparent members of the old firm as still members until he has notice of the change.
Scarf (the retired partner)
  • He had in fact ceased to be a partner before the goods were supplied.
  • Liability after retirement depends on notice, and the question is what notice is sufficient in the circumstances of each creditor.
  • The principle is not a general one of "holding out to the world": what matters is whether this creditor dealt with the firm on the faith of Scarf's continuing membership.
  • The notice required of a retiring partner as against an old customer must be actual, whereas as against persons who dealt with the firm only after the change a general/public notice suffices — and the inquiry is therefore creditor-specific.
Decision and the rule stated

The House of Lords proceeded on the principle, drawn from Cresswell J.'s direction to the jury in Farrar v. Deflinne (a case the Court of Appeal in Tower Cabinet later examined in detail), which distinguishes three situations:

"The law stands thus: If there had been a notorious partnership, but no notice had been given of the dissolution thereof, the defendant would have been liable. If there had been a general notice, that would have been sufficient for all but actual customers; these, however, must have had some kind of actual notice. If the partnership had remained profoundly secret, the defendant could not have been affected by transactions which took place after he had retired; but if the partnership had become known to any person or persons, he would be in the same situation, as to all such persons, as if the existence of the partnership had been notorious. The question for you, therefore, is — was this partnership actually known to the plaintiff, either by general report, or by direct communication? Because, if it were, and he did not know, either from notice of the fact, or from surmise, that the dissolution had taken place, you must infer that he still dealt on the faith of the partnership, and the defendant will therefore be liable." Cresswell J. in Farrar v. Deflinne, quoted in Tower Cabinet Co. v. Ingram

The rule as it is safe to state it in a DU answer:

  1. A retiring partner must give notice of his retirement in the same manner and to the same persons as notice of his appointment was given. The notice must be given by him or by any partner of the reconstituted firm (in India, see s. 32(4)).
  2. As against old customers — those who dealt with the firm while he was a partner — general notice is not enough; actual notice is required.
  3. As against persons who had no dealings with the firm before the change — public/general notice suffices (in India: the Registrar + Official Gazette + a vernacular newspaper under s. 72).
  4. A "profoundly secret" / dormant partner is not affected by post-retirement transactions at all, unless the partnership had become known to the particular person dealing — in which case he is in the same position as a notorious partner as regards that person. (In India this is the s. 32(3) proviso and the s. 45 proviso.)
  5. The question is one of the creditor's actual knowledge, which may be acquired either by general report (notoriety) or by direct communication — notoriety is only evidence from which actual knowledge may be inferred.
Principles evolved
  1. The retiring-partner notice rule. Liability is creditor-specific: what must be proved is that this creditor dealt on the faith of the retiring partner's continuing membership.
  2. Two tiers of notice. Actual notice to existing customers; public notice to the world. This is the structure reproduced in India by s. 32(3)–(4) and s. 72.
  3. Dormant partners are treated differently — a person who never knew he was a partner cannot have relied on his membership, and the retiring partner is protected as against him (s. 32(3) proviso).
  4. The link with holding out. Scarf is usually classified as a holding-out case because the mechanism is estoppel: the retiring partner is stopped from denying the character he allowed to be assumed. But its distinctive contribution is the notice rule, which in India is governed by s. 32(3) and s. 45 rather than s. 28. A first-class answer separates the two.
! A caution for the exam

Secondary commentaries on Scarf v. Jardine differ on the outcome — some state that Scarf was held liable to Jardine, others read Lord Blackburn's speech as turning on whether notice had been given at all. Because the case is not reproduced in the DU reader, you will not be expected to recount its result in detail. State the principle (the two-tier notice rule and the creditor-specific test), cite Farrar v. Deflinne as its source, and then apply the Indian statutory provisions — ss. 28, 32(3)–(4), 45 and 72 — which is where the marks actually lie. If you do state a result, say "on the view that no notice at all was given, Scarf remained liable to a supplier who had dealt with the old firm in ignorance of the change".

CASE 10 · Shivgouda Ravji Patil v. Chandrakant Neelkanth Sadalge
AIR 1965 SC 212 · Supreme Court of India (K. Subba Rao J.) · DU reader p. 46
[Can a minor who was admitted to the benefits of a partnership be adjudicated insolvent on the basis of debts of the firm after the partnership was dissolved, on the ground that he attained majority subsequent to the dissolution but did not exercise his option?] (s. 30)
Facts (from the DU case material)

Mallappa Mahalingappa Sadalge and Appasaheb Mahalingappa Sadalge (respondents 2 and 3) carried on business as commission agents and manufacturers under two firm names, "M. B. Sadalge" and "C. N. Sadalge", under a partnership deed dated 25 October 1946.

At that time Chandrakant Nilakanth Sadalge (respondent No. 1) was a minor, and he was admitted to the benefits of the partnership.

The firm had dealings with the appellants and became indebted to them to the extent of Rs. 1,72,484.

The partnership was dissolved on 18 April 1951.

The first respondent became a major subsequently, and he did not exercise the option not to become a partner of the firm under s. 30(5) of the Indian Partnership Act.

When the appellants demanded their dues, respondents 2 and 3 informed them that they were unable to pay and had suspended payment of their debts. On 2 August 1954 the appellants applied to the Civil Judge, Senior Division, Belgaum, to adjudicate all three respondents as insolvents.

  • Civil Judge: respondents 2 and 3 had committed acts of insolvency; and respondent 1 had become a partner because he did not exercise his option under s. 30(5), and was therefore liable to be adjudicated along with them.
  • District Judge: appeal dismissed.
  • High Court (second appeal): held that the first respondent was not a partner and could not be adjudicated insolvent for the debts of the firm.
  • Supreme Court: the creditors appealed.
Issues
  1. Could the first respondent be adjudicated insolvent on the basis of the acts of insolvency committed by respondents 2 and 3? (This depends on whether he was a debtor who had committed an act of insolvency under ss. 6 and 9 of the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920.)
  2. Had he become a partner of the firm by reason of his failure to exercise the option under s. 30(5), notwithstanding that the firm had already been dissolved before he attained majority?
Arguments of both sides
The appellants (the creditors) — Mr Pathak
  • "The 1st respondent had become a partner of the firm by reason of the fact that he had not elected not to become a partner of the firm under Section 30(5) of the Partnership Act, and therefore he was liable to be adjudicated insolvent along with his other partners."
  • The scheme of s. 30(5) is that a person admitted as a minor becomes a partner by default on the expiry of six months from attaining majority (or from knowledge, whichever is later).
  • Under s. 30(7)(a), once he becomes a partner, "he also becomes personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits of partnership" — a liability which, on its terms, reaches back to before his majority and therefore covers the debt of Rs. 1,72,484.
  • Respondents 2 and 3 were partners, were indebted, and committed an act of insolvency by declaring their inability to pay; the first respondent, being a partner, is in the same position.
The first respondent (Chandrakant)
  • Under s. 30(1) a minor cannot be a partner — he may only be admitted to the benefits of partnership.
  • Under s. 30(2) and (3) he is entitled only to his agreed share of property and profits and has no personal liability for any act of the firm, though his share is liable.
  • s. 30 presupposes the existence of a partnership. The firm was dissolved on 18 April 1951, before he attained majority. "A minor after attaining majority cannot elect to become a partner of a firm which ceased to exist."
  • Every sub-section of s. 30 assumes a subsisting firm: sub-s. (4) speaks of a partner severing his connection "with the firm"; sub-s. (5) speaks of notice determining "his position as regards the firm"; and sub-s. (7) contemplates his being "inducted … as a partner of an existing firm with co-equal rights and liabilities along with other partners".
  • Reliance on the Privy Council in Sanyasi Charan Mandal v. Krishnadhan Banerji (ILR 49 Cal 560, 570): a minor's share "is no more than a right to participate in the property of the firm after its obligations have been satisfied"; and on the reasoning that during minority the partners' acts of insolvency could not found an adjudication against him because he was not a partner.
Decision (K. Subba Rao J., Supreme Court)

Appeal dismissed with costs. The order of the High Court was correct.

The Court accepted the respondent's construction of s. 30 in full:

  • Under s. 30(1) a minor cannot become a partner but may, with the consent of all partners, be admitted to the benefits of partnership. Under sub-ss. (2) and (3) "he will be entitled only to have a right to such share of the properties and of the profits of the firm as may be agreed upon, but he has no personal liability for any acts of the firm, though his share is liable for the same."
  • Quoting the Privy Council: "A person under the age of majority cannot become a partner by contract … and so according to the definition he cannot be one of that group of persons called a firm. It would seem, therefore, that the share of which Section 247 speaks is no more than a right to participate in the property of the firm after its obligations have been satisfied."
  • "It follows that if during minority of the 1st respondent the partners of the firm committed an act of insolvency, the minor could not have been adjudicated insolvent on the basis of the said act of insolvency for the simple reason that he was not a partner of the firm."
  • On s. 30(5): "Under that sub-section the quondam minor at any time within six months of his attaining majority, or of his obtaining knowledge that he had been admitted to the benefits of partnership, whichever date is later, may give public notice that he has elected to become or that he has elected not to become a partner in the firm… If he failed to give such notice, he would become a partner in the said firm after the expiry of the said period of six months." The Court accepted that this is what would happen "if during the continuance of the partnership" — and that s. 30(7) would then make him personally liable "for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits of partnership".
  • But that is not this case. The decisive passage:
    "But in the present case the partnership was dissolved before the first respondent became a major; from the date of the dissolution of the partnership, the firm ceased to exist, though under Section 45 of the Act, the partners continued to be liable as such to third parties for the acts done by any of them which would have been the acts of the firm if done before the dissolution until public notice was given of the dissolution. Section 45 proprio vigore applies only to partners of the firm. When the partnership itself was dissolved before the first respondent became a major, it is legally impossible to hold that he had become a partner of the dissolved firm by reason of his inaction after he became a major within the time prescribed under Section 30(5) of the Partnership Act." K. Subba Rao J.
  • The structural argument:
    "Section 30 of the said Act presupposes the existence of a partnership. Sub-sections (1), (2) and (3) thereof describe the rights and liabilities of a minor admitted to the benefits of partnership in respect of acts committed by the partners; sub-section (4) thereof imposes a disability on the minor to sue the partners for an account or payment of his share of the property or profits of the firm, save when severing his connection with the firm. This sub-section also assumes the existence of a firm from which the minor seeks to sever his connection by filing a suit. It is implicit in the terms of sub-section (5) … that the partnership is in existence. Sub-section (7) which describes the rights and liabilities of a person who exercises his option under sub-section (5) to become a partner also indicates that he is inducted from that date as a partner of an existing firm with co-equal rights and liabilities along with other partners. The entire scheme of Section 30 of the Partnership Act posits the existence of a firm and negatives any theory of its application to a stage when the firm ceased to exist." K. Subba Rao J.
  • The famous closing sentence: "One cannot become or remain a partner of a firm that does not exist."
  • Conclusion: "It is common case that the first respondent became a major only after the firm was dissolved. Section 30 of the Partnership Act, therefore, does not apply to him. He is not a partner of the firm and, therefore, he cannot be adjudicated insolvent for the acts of insolvency committed by Respondents 2 and 3, the partners of the firm."
Principles evolved
  1. Section 30 presupposes a subsisting firm. Every sub-section assumes the existence of the firm. Where the firm is dissolved before the minor attains majority, s. 30 has no application at all — neither sub-s. (5)'s deemed partnership nor sub-s. (7)'s retroactive personal liability can operate.
  2. "One cannot become or remain a partner of a firm that does not exist." The aphorism to quote.
  3. A minor admitted to benefits is not a debtor of the firm's creditors. Only his share is liable (s. 30(3)); he has no personal liability and therefore cannot be adjudicated insolvent on the firm's debts under the Provincial Insolvency Act 1920.
  4. The Privy Council's characterisation of the minor's share — "no more than a right to participate in the property of the firm after its obligations have been satisfied" — is the doctrinal root of s. 30(3).
  5. s. 45 is confined to partners. "Section 45 proprio vigore applies only to partners of the firm." A quondam minor who never became a partner cannot be reached through the post-dissolution continuing-liability provision.
  6. Contrast the position had the firm continued. Where the partnership is in existence when he attains majority and he does not elect within six months, he becomes a partner (s. 30(5) proviso) and is personally liable for all acts of the firm done since his admission (s. 30(7)(a)) — a liability with retrospective reach.
  7. Adjudication requires debtor status. Under ss. 6 and 9 of the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 (now the IBC regime for firms, and the Presidency Towns Insolvency Act 1909 / Provincial Insolvency Act 1920 for individuals), a person can be adjudicated insolvent only if he is a debtor who has committed an act of insolvency.
★ How to use this case in a DU answer

DU has asked the minor question in eleven papers (2017 Q.6(a), 2017 OC Q.1(b), 2015 OC Q.5(b) & Q.8(a), 2014 OC Q.4 & Q.8(a), 2013 OC Q.8(b), 2012 OC Q.8(a), 2011 OC Q.1(e) & Q.4, 2004 Q.5(ii), 2003 Q.8(ii), 2002 Q.5, 2001 Q.2, 2000 Q.7(b), 1998 Q.4(b), 1994 Q.6(a), 1992 Q.6(a)). Shivgouda is the authority for the hardest variant: "the firm was dissolved before he attained majority" (2014 OC Q.4(a); 1992 Q.6(a); 1994 Q.6(a)).

The model answer in four moves:

  1. State s. 30(1)–(3): a minor may not be a partner; he may be admitted to benefits; his share is liable but he is not personally liable.
  2. State s. 30(5) and the proviso: six months from majority or knowledge, whichever is later; default = partner; and s. 30(7)(a): personal liability for all acts since admission.
  3. Then apply Shivgouda: s. 30 presupposes the existence of a firm. The firm was dissolved on 18 April 1951, before majority. Therefore s. 30(5) never operated and he never became a partner.
  4. Quote: "One cannot become or remain a partner of a firm that does not exist." Conclude: he cannot be adjudicated insolvent for the firm's debts; only his share in the firm's assets (as it stood) could be reached.

Always add the contrast: had the firm been subsisting, his failure to elect within six months would have made him a partner with retroactive personal liability under s. 30(7)(a) — and s. 30(9) preserves s. 28, so a minor who held himself out as a partner remains exposed regardless.

SET A — Duties of partners inter se (ss. 9–13) Asked in 7 years

2017LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.5(a)10 marks
Question Discuss in brief the rights and duties of partners as between themselves under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. (Variants: 2014 OC Q.2(a); 2012 OC Q.3(a); 2011 Q.8; 2003 Q.2(a); 2002 Q.2; 2001 Q.7; 2000 Q.2(a); 1997 Q.2(a))
Model Answer

Introduction. Chapter III of the Act (ss. 9–17) governs relations inter se. With one exception — s. 9 — every provision opens with "subject to contract between the partners", i.e. they are default rules which the partnership deed may vary (s. 11(1)).

I. DUTIES.

  1. s. 9 — general duties. Partners are bound to carry on the business to the greatest common advantage, to be just and faithful to each other, and to render true accounts and full information of all things affecting the firm to any partner or his legal representative. Note: s. 9 is not subject to contract — good faith cannot be wholly excluded. Authority: Sita Ram v. Radha Rai, AIR 1968 SC 534 — a sleeping partner can sue for accounts.
  2. s. 10 — indemnity for fraud. Every partner shall indemnify the firm for any loss caused to it by his fraud in the conduct of the business of the firm. Not subject to contract.
  3. s. 12(b) — diligence. Every partner is bound to attend diligently to his duties in the conduct of the business.
  4. s. 13(b) — contribute equally to losses. (Subject to contract.)
  5. s. 13(f) — indemnify for wilful neglect. A partner shall indemnify the firm for any loss caused to it by his wilful neglect in the conduct of the business.
  6. s. 15 — use firm property exclusively for the business.
  7. s. 16 — account for personal profits: (a) profits derived from any transaction of the firm or from the use of the firm's property, business connection or firm name; (b) profits of a business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm. Authority: Trimble v. Goldberg (1906) AC 494.

II. RIGHTS.

  1. s. 12(a) — right to take part in the conduct of the business.
  2. s. 12(c) — right to express his opinion before a matter is decided by majority; and the right that no change may be made in the nature of the business without the consent of all.
  3. s. 12(d) — right of access to, and to inspect and copy, any of the books of the firm.
  4. s. 13(b) — right to share equally in the profits (default rule).
  5. s. 13(c) — interest on capital, where agreed, payable only out of profits.
  6. s. 13(d) — interest at 6% per annum on advances beyond the agreed capital — payable regardless of profits.
  7. s. 13(e) — right to be indemnified by the firm for payments made and liabilities incurred (i) in the ordinary and proper conduct of the business and (ii) in an emergency to protect the firm from loss.
  8. s. 9 — right to true accounts and full information.
  9. s. 46 — on dissolution, the right to have the property of the firm applied in payment of debts and the surplus distributed according to his rights.

III. The limit on all of this. s. 11(1): the mutual rights and duties may be determined by contract, express or implied by a course of dealing, and may be varied by the consent of all (again, express or implied). s. 11(2): notwithstanding s. 27, ICA 1872, the partners may agree that a partner shall not carry on any other business while he is a partner. Authority: Mandyala Govindu & Co. v. CIT, AIR 1975 SC 2284 — no remuneration without a provision in the deed.

IV. Conclusion. The inter se code is a residual code: the deed governs; the Act fills the gaps. Only the duties of good faith (s. 9) and the fraud indemnity (s. 10) cannot be contracted away.

SET C — Competing business and secret profits (s. 16)

2013Business Association–I (OC) · Q.220 marks
Question "If a partner carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm, he shall account for and pay to the firm all profits made by him in that business." Discuss the proposition by referring to relevant case.
Model Answer

The proposition is s. 16(b), PA 1932. It is correct as stated — but it must be read with the limiting principle laid down by the Privy Council in Trimble v. Goldberg (1906) AC 494, which is the case DU's own reader prescribes for s. 16.

1. The section. s. 16 — "Subject to contract between the partners — (a) if a partner derives any profit for himself from any transaction of the firm, or from the use of the property or business connection of the firm or the firm name, he shall account for that profit and pay it to the firm; (b) if a partner carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with that of the firm, he shall account for and pay to the firm all profits made by him in that business."

Note three textual features: the remedy is an account of profits, not damages; the quantum under limb (b) is all profits, not a proportion; and both limbs are "subject to contract" — the partners may authorise a separate business.

2. The facts of Trimble v. Goldberg. Goldberg, Trimble and Bennett entered a partnership on 10 February 1902 to purchase and re-sell the properties of one Hollard (5,500 Sigma shares and other real estate), profits and losses to be shared equally, all dealings to be transacted by Trimble under powers of attorney. Trimble completed the Hollard purchase on 14 February 1902. Afterwards, and purely on his own initiative, he learned from Hollard that the Sigma Syndicate might sell its stands on Government Square; he took an option at £110,000 (the board had privately resolved to accept £100,000); he told Bennett — who financed it — but not Goldberg, who heard of it only at the end of 1902 and said "Don't you think you might have let me have a show in?"

3. The two issues and how they were decided.

  • Was there a mandate to buy on joint account? No — both courts below so found.
  • If not, was Goldberg entitled on general principles to share? The Supreme Court of the Transvaal said yes; the Privy Council said no: "The purchase was not within the scope of the partnership. The subject of the purchase was not part of the business of the partnership, or an undertaking in rivalry with the partnership, or indeed connected with it in any proper sense."

4. The limiting principles to write.

  1. Connection with the firm is required. s. 16 bites only where the profit comes from a firm transaction, firm property, the firm's business connection or firm name, or from a business in rivalry with the firm. A wholly independent speculation is outside s. 16.
  2. Secrecy alone is not enough. The Court of Appeal "regarded the veil of secrecy as a damning proof of guilt"; the Privy Council disagreed. Quoting Lord Blackburn in Cassels v. Stewart: "there was no legal obligation on Trimble or Bennett to tell Goldberg what they were doing unless he had a right to take part in the speculation if he chose to do so."
  3. Disappointment is not loss. "And here there was no loss to the partnership; only a disappointment to the partner left out in the cold."
  4. Even an expressly forbidden act does not yield an account of profits. "The other partners… might have claimed immediate dissolution, or even damages, on proof of actual loss to the partnership. But a claim to share in the profits of the forbidden purchase would not have been warranted by principle or precedent."
  5. Benefit to the firm is an answer. The purchase in fact raised the price the syndicate obtained (£110,000 instead of £100,000), so the partnership as a shareholder was "proportionately the gainer".
  6. The conflict-of-duty argument fails on the facts. Trimble had no control over the sale — "That was in the hands of the directors. They were dealing at arm's length with him."

5. Conclusion. The proposition in the question is correct but incomplete. A partner must account under s. 16(b) where he carries on a business of the same nature as, and competing with, the firm's — but, following Trimble v. Goldberg, the outside venture must be within the scope of the partnership, in rivalry with it, or otherwise connected with it in a proper sense. Where it is not, the other partners' remedies are dissolution and damages on proof of loss — not an account of profits. And in all cases the partners may, by contract under s. 11(1), authorise the separate business; and under s. 11(2) they may forbid any other business altogether while he is a partner, notwithstanding s. 27 of the Contract Act.

Also cite for: DU 1998 Q.2(a) (validity of a restraint-of-trade clause between partners — s. 11(2)); DU 2004 Q.2(ii) (partner buys an adjacent plot in his own name — s. 16(a) + s. 88 Trusts Act).

Question Discuss the implied authority of a partner and how far it is subject to restrictions contained in the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. (Wording used in: 2003 Q.5; 2002 Q.4; 2000 Q.3; 1996 Q.4; 1992 Q.3)
Model Answer

Introduction. A partner is not an agent in the ordinary sense: he is a principal and an agent at once (Shaw v. Galt, per O'Brien J.). This dual character is created by s. 4 ("business carried on by all or any of them acting for all") and given outward effect by s. 18 ("Subject to the provisions of this Act, a partner is the agent of the firm for the purposes of the business of the firm"). The exact scope of that agency is fixed by s. 19.

I. The general rule — s. 19(1). "Subject to the provisions of s. 22, the act of a partner which is done to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm, binds the firm. The authority of a partner to bind the firm conferred by this section is called his 'implied authority'." Two tests, both of which must be satisfied: (i) the act must be done to carry on the business, and (ii) it must be done in the usual way of that kind of business (compare s. 188, ICA 1872: an agent has authority to do every lawful thing necessary to do such an act).

  • s. 19(3) — the emergency proviso. "A partner has authority to do all such acts as are necessary to protect the firm from loss, even though the act is not of the kind carried on by the firm, if the partner acted in an emergency." (Compare s. 189, ICA 1872 — an agent has authority, in an emergency, to do all such acts for the purpose of protecting his principal from loss as would be done by a person of ordinary prudence in his own case.)   1. knew of the restriction, or
  2. did not know or believe that partner to be a partner.

Example. The deed provides that A shall not accept bills for more than ₹50,000. A accepts a bill for ₹1,00,000 from B. If B knew of the clause, the firm is free; if B did not know, and knew A to be a partner, the firm is bound.

V. Extension of the authority — s. 21 (and s. 23). "A partner has authority, in the absence of any usage or custom of trade to the contrary, or of express contract, to make any arrangement or compromise on behalf of the firm with its creditors." (Compare s. 152, ICA 1872 on authority of an agent to do all acts necessary for, and ordinarily incidental to, the purpose for which he is employed.) And s. 23: an agreement to refer a dispute to arbitration may be signed by any partner on behalf of the firm — an important inroad on s. 19(2)(a).

VI. The mode requirement — s. 22. An act or instrument done or executed by a partner in the firm name, or "in any other manner expressing or implying an intention to bind the firm", is binding on the firm — linking back to s. 19(1). Note: this is a mode requirement, not an additional test of authority.

VII. The two cases to cite.

  • Rhodes v. Moules (1895) 1 Ch. 236 — apparent authority proved by the previous course of dealing; the pawn was within the scope of a business that regularly held clients' securities; the firm was liable for the misappropriation.
  • Holme v. Hammond (1872) LR 7 Ex. 218 — the negative: no partnership, no agency, no liability. The executors' receipt of a profit share was evidence only; there was no agreement that they were principals with the surviving partners as their agents.

Conclusion. The implied authority is broad but bounded: s. 19(1) gives it, s. 19(2)(a)–(h) enumerates eight things it does not reach, s. 19(3) supplies an emergency exception, s. 20 makes private restrictions effective only against those who know of them, and ss. 21 and 23 extend it for compositions and arbitration agreements. All of it rests on s. 18's premise that the partner is the firm's agent for the purposes of the business of the firm.

A retired partner was not liable even though the firm continued to use notepaper bearing his name, because he had not knowingly suffered it: "The words are 'knowingly suffers' — not being negligent or careless in not seeing that all the notepaper had been destroyed." And the English s. 36(3) relieved him because the supplier had never known he had been a partner. The mirror image of the present question: here there is consent (so "knowingly permits" is made out) and the firm's name itself contains theirs.
  • Scarf v. Jardine (1882) 7 AC 345 — the rule is creditor-specific: actual notice to existing customers, public notice suffices for those who had not previously dealt.

5. Scope of the liability. They are liable as partners — i.e. jointly and severally with all the partners (s. 25), for acts of the firm done while they were so held out (s. 32(3) analogically), with the unlimited liability of an ordinary partner. And because s. 28(1) dispenses with proof that the representation reached the creditor in the sense of being known to have reached him, it is enough that credit was given on the faith of it.

6. What they should do. They should (i) procure an amendment of the firm's name and of all letterheads, bills, signboards and the entry in the Register of Firms (s. 59); (ii) issue actual notice to existing customers and a public notice in a local newspaper (s. 72); and (iii) stop taking a share of profits. Until actual notice reaches an existing dealer, liability under s. 28 continues.

Conclusion. M and N are liable to third parties as partners by holding out under s. 28, the representation being both by words (the firm's name) and by conduct, and knowingly permitted. Their liability does not depend on their taking any part in the business.

SET G — Minors and partnership (s. 30) Asked in 13 years

2017LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.610 marks
Question "The position of a minor partner is one of privilege and not of obligation." Discuss the statement in the light of the relevant provisions of the Partnership Act, 1932. (Wording used in: 2015 Q.4(a); 2014 Q.4(a); 2014 OC Q.4(b); 2013 Q.4; 2012 Q.4; 2011 Q.4; 2003 Q.4; 2002 Q.5; 2001 Q.6; 2000 Q.5; 1998 Q.3; 1997 Q.4; 1994 Q.6(a); 1993 Q.5(b); 1992 Q.6(a))
Model Answer

The statement is substantially correct. A minor admitted to the benefits of a partnership enjoys every economic benefit of a partner and none of the burdens — until he elects, on attaining majority, to become a partner. The scheme is s. 30, PA 1932, read with s. 11, ICA 1872 (a minor is incompetent to contract) and s. 3, Majority Act 1875.

1. Why the minor cannot be a partner. Partnership is the result of a contract (s. 5). A minor is incompetent to contract (s. 11, ICA — Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose, (1903) 30 IA 114). Hence s. 30(1): "A person who is a minor according to the law to which he is subject may not be a partner in a firm, but, with the consent of all the partners for the time being, he may be admitted to the benefits of partnership." Note: the consent of all partners is required; the partnership deed cannot pre-authorise it.

2. THE PRIVILEGES (what the minor gets).

  • s. 30(2) — a share in the property and profits. "Such minor has a right to such share of the property and of the profits of the firm as may be agreed upon, and he may have access to and inspect and copy any of the accounts of the firm" — the same right as a partner under s. 12(d).
  • s. 30(3) — the share is liable for the acts of the firm, but the minor is not personally liable. "Such minor's share is liable for the acts of the firm, but the minor is not personally liable for any such act." His liability is limited to his share in the firm — the reason the Supreme Court in Shivgouda Ravji Patil held that a minor cannot be adjudicated insolvent, since there is no debt provable under s. 6 read with s. 9, Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 which he is personally liable to pay. Authority: Sanyasi Charan Mandal v. Krishnadhan Banerji, ILR 49 Cal 560 (PC).
  • s. 30(4) — no liability for past acts on electing to become a partner. If he becomes a partner on attaining majority, his liability for acts of the firm since his admission is unlimited, but "his share is not liable for any acts of the firm done before he became a partner" — unless he personally ratifies them.
  • No obligation to contribute to losses beyond his share. Because he is not a partner, s. 13(b) (equal contribution to losses) does not apply to him in person.
  • s. 30(6) — he may sue. "Where such person becomes a partner, or where a minor is severally adjudged insolvent… the court may, on the application of any person interested, order the dissolution of the firm"; and (sub-s. 6 read with s. 30(2)) a minor may sue the partners for an account or for payment of his share, subject to the bar in s. 69(2) if the firm is unregistered. Note s. 30(6)(b): where the minor is severally adjudged insolvent, his share vests in the Official Assignee or Receiver and he cannot sue.

3. THE ONE OBLIGATION (the election on attaining majority).

  • s. 30(5), first limb — "At any time within six months of his attaining majority, or of his obtaining knowledge that he had been admitted to the benefits of partnership, whichever date is later, such person may give public notice that he has elected to become or that he has elected not to become a partner in the firm, and such notice shall determine his position as regards the firm."
  • s. 30(5), proviso — "If he fails to give such notice, he shall become a partner in the firm on the expiry of the said six months."
  • s. 30(6) — but "where a person was under the age of majority when he was admitted… and has elected to become a partner, his rights and liabilities as a minor continue up to the date on which he becomes a partner, except that he also becomes personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits of partnership." — i.e. with retroactive effect.
  • s. 30(7)(b) — if he elects not to become a partner, his rights and liabilities continue to be those of a minor "up to the date of public notice", and his share shall not be liable for any acts of the firm done after the date of the notice.
  • s. 30(8) — where his share is not determined by agreement, his share is determined by the court.

4. The controlling case — Shivgouda Ravji Patil v. Chandrakant Neelkanth Sadalge, AIR 1965 SC 212. R1 was admitted to the benefits of a firm while a minor. Held (K. Subba Rao J.): "The short question is whether s. 30 presupposes the existence of a firm. Answer: Yes — he becomes a partner on the expiry of the six months, and by virtue of s. 30(6) read with s. 30(7)(a) he is personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits of partnership — i.e. for the whole of the pre-majority period as well.

Reasoning.

  1. The proviso to s. 30(5) is automatic. "If he fails to give such notice, he shall become a partner in the firm on the expiry of the said six months." Here a firm exists, so the proviso operates.
  3. The same rule is restated in s. 30(7)(a) for a person who becomes a partner under s. 30(5) generally. This is the one instance in the Act of retroactive personal liability.

  5. And his personal liability is to third parties. What he owes inter se is governed by the settlement of accounts under s. 48.

  • His liability is now joint and several with all the partners (s. 25) and unlimited.
  • He can now be adjudicated insolvent on the firm's debts (s. 6 read with s. 9, Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920) — the very thing Shivgouda said could not happen to a minor.
  • His rights change: he now has all the rights under ss. 12–13, including the right to take part in the conduct of the business, and he now owes the duties under ss. 9, 10, 13(f), 15 and 16.
  • He may, however, still apply for dissolution under s. 30(5)/s. 30(6) or s. 44.

Conclusion. Because the firm was subsisting, the proviso to s. 30(5) made him a partner on the expiry of the six months; and s. 30(6) makes him personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since his admission to benefits — which includes the period before he attained majority. He is liable.

SET H — Firm's liability for a partner's wrongful act (ss. 25–27) Asked in 11 years

2015 (Dec)LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.4(d)12 marks
Question A partner bribed the clerk of a rival trader in order to obtain, for the firm, information as to the business of the rival trader. Is the firm liable for the act of the partner? Discuss with the help of decided cases. (This is Hamlyn v. Houston & Co. — asked in 2015, 2014, 2011, 2003, 2002)
Model Answer

Answer: Yes — the firm is civilly liable, jointly and severally with the bribing partner.

1. The statutory frame.

  • s. 18 — the partner is the agent of the firm for the purposes of its business.
  • s. 25 — "Every partner is liable, jointly with all the other partners and also severally, for all acts of the firm done while he is a partner."
  • s. 26 — "Where, by the wrongful act or omission of a partner acting in the ordinary course of the business of a firm, or with the authority of his partners, loss or injury is caused to any person not being a partner in the firm, or any penalty is incurred, the firm is liable therefor to the same extent as the partner."
  • s. 27 — liability for misapplication of money or property received by the firm or by a partner.

2. The case: Hamlyn v. Houston & Co. [1903] 1 KB 81.

Facts. The plaintiff and one Houston were rival grain merchants. Houston's partner, Campbell, bribed the plaintiff's clerk, Gilby, to disclose the plaintiff's most confidential business letters. Houston did not know. The plaintiff obtained judgment for £500 damages against Houston for slander and £1,000 in another action; Houston became insolvent and the plaintiff sued his partner Hamlyn, who had taken no part in the bribe.

Held (Collins M.R.) — Hamlyn was liable. The decisive passage:

"The test is: Was it within the scope of the partner's implied authority to obtain this information by legitimate means? If so, it was within the scope of his authority for the present purpose to obtain it by illegitimate means. … It was unlawful for the defendants to do what they did, but the fact that the means were unlawful does not prevent the act being within the scope of the defendant's authority." Collins M.R., (1903) 1 KB 81, 86

Mathew J. added the important qualification:

"We are concerned in this action with the civil liability of the defendants and not with any criminal liability. … The object was to benefit the firm; and as the judge has found, that object was attained. … If the contention be right, it would follow that, if the action complained of had been a libel which was in fact true, there would be no cause of action at all." Mathew J., (1903) 1 KB 81, 87

Rationale. The liability rests not on holding out but on the master/servant principle in Barwick v. English Joint Stock Bank (1867) LR 2 Ex 259 — what the master gets the benefit of, he is answerable for. And s. 229, ICA 1872 supplies the parallel: the principal must make good the loss occasioned to a third party by an agent's act done in his employment, even if the act was unlawful, "unless he can show that the act was done without his knowledge or consent in a matter in which the agent was not authorized".

3. Application to the problem.

  1. Obtaining information about a rival trader's business is, for a trading firm, within the ordinary course of the business.
  2. Obtaining it by legitimate means (market reports, published prices, engaging the rival's former employees) would have been within the partner's implied authority under s. 19(1).
  3. Therefore, applying the Hamlyn test, obtaining it by illegitimate means is also within the scope of his authority for the purpose of s. 26.
  4. s. 25 makes every partner — including the innocent ones and sleeping partners — jointly and severally liable. The plaintiff may sue the innocent partner alone.

4. Limits to state for full marks.

  • Civil liability only. The innocent partners are not criminally liable for the bribe (Mathew J.). Under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, s. 318 (cheating) and the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988, the offence is the briber's.
  • The object must be to benefit the firm. A purely private fraud is outside s. 26.
  • Contrast s. 27(b) — where a partner, acting within his apparent authority, receives money or property from a third party and misapplies it, the firm is liable; where a partner other than as agent of the firm receives money belonging to a third party and misapplies it, the firm is liable only if the money came into the custody of the firm.
  • Contrast Rhodes v. Moules — the firm received the bonds in the course of its business; hence s. 27(a) liability, on the narrower ground of receipt plus apparent authority, not on the Hamlyn ground.
  • Internal recourse. The innocent partners may claim an indemnity from the guilty partner under s. 10 (fraud) and s. 13(f) (wilful neglect), and may seek dissolution under s. 44(c)/(d).

Conclusion. The firm is liable — jointly and severally with the bribing partner — for the loss caused by his wrongful act done in the ordinary course of the firm's business, even though the other partners did not know of it. The liability is civil, not criminal.

Model Answer

Answer: The firm is liable to C.

1. The provision — s. 27, PA 1932. "Where a partner acting within his apparent authority receives money or property from a third person and misapplies it, or where a firm in the course of its business receives money or property from a third person, and the money or property is misapplied by any of the partners while it is in the custody of the firm, the firm is liable to make good the loss."

2. The two limbs, and which one applies. The present case falls under limb (a): a partner (A) acting within his apparent authority received money from a third person (C) and misapplied it. ("In the course of the firm's business" makes this plain.) Limb (b) would apply where the firm received the money and any partner misapplied it while it was in the custody of the firm.

3. "Apparent authority" — how it is proved. It is the authority a reasonable person would infer from the previous course of dealing. The controlling authority is Rhodes v. Moules (1895) 1 Ch. 236:

  • Facts. Moules & Co., a firm of solicitors and mortgage brokers, held bonds belonging to the plaintiff for the purposes of a contemplated mortgage. Without her authority the partner W.B. Rhodes pledged them to a firm of stockbrokers to secure his own stock-exchange speculations. She sued the other partners, Alfred and Dayton Rhodes.
  • Held. The firm was liable for the conversion. The prior course of dealing — the firm's books, its own charges, and the fact that the firm had made the earlier advance in its own name — proved that the transaction was one in which the firm was acting.
  • Principles. (i) The test is whether the person dealing with the partner "would have known that the partner had authority" (Poland v. Parr). (ii) The partner need not have actual authority; apparent authority suffices (Lord Watson's speech in Kendal v. Hamilton). (iii) "It is not just that those who deal with the firm should be left without remedy against the firm merely because the partners have chosen to keep the arrangement secret."
  • Distinguished: Cleather v. Twisden — there the deposit was received by the firm merely for safe custody, which is not part of the ordinary business of a firm of estate and loan agents, and the firm was not liable. The contrast is the examiner's favourite: custody incidental to a transaction the firm is handling = within scope; pure safe custody = may not be.

4. Application.

  1. A received the money in the course of the firm's business — the question says so, and that is the s. 22/s. 27(a) mode requirement.
  2. Receiving money in the course of the firm's business is within a partner's implied authority under s. 19(1), and therefore within his apparent authority.
  3. The misapplication is precisely the mischief s. 27 addresses; the section does not require the firm to have benefited.
  4. Under s. 25, B is liable jointly and severally with A, so C may sue the firm (i.e. all the partners together) or B alone.
  5. If A's conduct also amounts to a tort, s. 26 gives an alternative, cumulative ground.

5. The partners' internal position. B may recover from A under s. 10 (fraud) or s. 13(f) (wilful neglect); and the loss is adjusted against A's share on settlement of accounts under s. 48. B may also seek dissolution under s. 44(c)/(d).

6. Contrast: personal borrowing. If A had borrowed the money for himself, in his own name, and the lender knew it, the firm would not be liable — the receipt would not be in the course of the firm's business. The dividing line is whether the lender dealt with A as the firm.

Conclusion. The firm is liable to C under s. 27(a), the receipt being by a partner acting within his apparent authority and in the course of the firm's business; and each partner is liable jointly and severally under s. 25. Rhodes v. Moules supplies the reasoning.

Question Who can be held liable as a partner even when he is not a partner? Discuss. (Wording used in: 2015 Q.2(c); 2014 Q.3(a); 2013 Q.6(a); 2012 Q.2(a); 2003 Q.3(a); 1999 Q.4(b); 1999 Q.6(a); 1998 Q.6(b); 1997 Q.3(a); 1996 Q.5(b); 1993 Q.2(a))
Model Answer

Introduction. The general rule is that only a partner is liable for the firm's debts (s. 25), and partnership itself arises only from contract (s. 5). But the Act creates four categories of person who may be made liable although, as between themselves and the partners, they are not partners at all. The reason is always the same: the outsider relied on an appearance which the person helped to create.

1. A person who holds himself out (s. 28). He represents himself — by words spoken or written, or by conduct — or knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner, and credit is given on the faith of that representation.

  • Representation may be by words (Snow White Food Products Co. Ltd v. Sohan Lal, AIR 1964 Cal 239) or by conduct (allowing his name to stand in the firm's name, on the letterhead, or on the signboard).
  • Knowingly permitting requires actual knowledge: mere negligence in not destroying old notepaper is not enough — Tower Cabinet Co. Ltd v. Ingram [1949] 1 KB 1032 ("The words are 'knowingly suffers'") .
  • Reliance is essential, and the inquiry is creditor-specific: Scarf v. Jardine (1882) 7 AC 345 — actual notice to existing dealers, public notice suffices for those who had not previously dealt.
  • Liability is as a partner — joint and several, unlimited (s. 25).
  • s. 28(2) is an exception: after a partner's death, the continued use of the old firm name or of his name does not of itself make his legal representatives or heirs liable.

2. A retiring partner who has not given notice (ss. 32(2), 32(3), 45, 72).

  • s. 32(2) — liable for acts of the firm until public notice (Official Gazette + one vernacular newspaper in the district — s. 72).
  • s. 32(3) — liable to those who dealt with the firm before retirement until actual notice reaches them.
  • Proviso (b) — a dormant partner need give no notice, because nobody dealt with him as a partner.
  • s. 45 — a partner remains liable for acts done before dissolution until public notice is given, notwithstanding the subsequent dissolution.

3. The legal representative or estate of a deceased partner (ss. 35, 42(c), 37). His estate is liable for the firm's obligations incurred before his death, and — absent a contrary contract — under s. 37, if the survivors continue the business without settling accounts, for a share of the subsequent profits or 6% interest. But he is not liable for post-death acts (s. 35 proviso 1) and the continued use of his name does not make his heirs liable (s. 28(2)). Contrast Holme v. Hammond (1872) LR 7 Ex. 218, where the executors were held not to have become partners: the death dissolved the old firm, and they had made no agreement with the survivors.

4. A person whose name the firm uses with his consent, and who takes a share of profits. See Set E, Q.1 above (DU 2015 Q.3(b)). His consent makes it a case of knowingly permitting; and the Explanation to s. 6 preserves the inference of partnership from profit-sharing where there is no common interest in the property.

5. Contrast — the person who is NOT liable.

  • Holme v. Hammond — executors receiving a share of profits. The true test is mutual agency, per O'Brien J. in Shaw v. Galt (1877) 11 ICLR 231: "Each [partner] is a principal and each is an agent for the others." Without it there is no partnership.
  • Tower Cabinet v. Ingram — the retired partner who did not knowingly suffer the use of his name, where the supplier never knew he had been one.
  • Cleather v. Twisden — a partner acting outside the scope of the firm's business.
  • s. 6, Explanation — a person receiving a share of profits as remuneration or annuity, a lender paid by a share of profits, a widow's annuity, a seller of goodwill paid by a share of profits, and an agent under the proviso to s. 4, are not partners.

Conclusion. Four categories — the person held out (s. 28), the retiring partner without notice (s. 32), the deceased partner's estate (s. 35/s. 37), and the consenting name-lender-cum-profit-sharer (s. 28 + s. 6 Expl.) — may all be made liable although they are not partners; and in every case the reason is reliance created or permitted by that person.

Question Can an unregistered partnership firm file a suit? Discuss the effects of non-registration of a partnership firm. Refer to case-law. (Wording used in: 2017 Q.8; 2014 Q.6; 2013 Q.5; 2011 Q.5; 2003 Q.8; 2000 Q.8; 1998 Q.7; 1997 Q.5; 1996 Q.7; 1994 Q.8; 1992 Q.5; 1989 Q.4(b))
Model Answer

Answer: No — subject to the three exceptions in s. 69(3) and (4), an unregistered firm cannot institute a suit to enforce a right arising from a contract against a third party or against any partner.

1. Registration is optional; the consequence is procedural, not existential. s. 56 says nothing makes registration compulsory; s. 58 permits registration at any time; s. 59 provides the procedure (application to the Registrar of Firms with the prescribed fee, the firm name, place of business, date of commencement and the partners' names and addresses, signed and verified by all partners). Registration is conclusive evidence of the fact of partnership as against the persons who signed the statement (s. 68) — but not against a third party (Snow White Food Products).

2. The three disabilities under s. 69.

s. 69(1)No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract — (a) by the firm or any partner against any third party, or (b) by any person against the firm or any person sued as a partner — in respect of a contract entered into in connection with the firm — shall be instituted by or on behalf of a firm unless the firm is registered and the person suing is or has been shown in the Register as a partner.

  2. any suit or set-off by a firm where the claim does not exceed ₹100 in value and is not of a kind specified in s. 69(2);

And s. 69(4) saves the right of a third party to sue the firm in respect of a tort or other non-contractual wrong.

4. Case law.

  • Snow White Food Products Co. Ltd v. Sohan Lal, AIR 1964 Cal 239 — the Register is conclusive only against those who signed the statement; it is not evidence against a third party, who may still prove that a person is a partner (there, by holding out under s. 28).
  • Sri Ram Laxman Sugar Mills v. Babu Lal Bansal, AIR 1985 All 320; AIR 1984 All 82 — no set-off in a Partnership Act suit founded on an unregistered partnership.
  • Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar, AIR 2000 Del 360 (DB) — an unregistered partnership may maintain a passing-off action.
  • M/s Shreeram Finance Corporation v. Yasin Khan, AIR 1989 SC 1769 — the court has a duty to raise the s. 69(1) bar suo motu. Authoritative on the point.
  • Jagdish Chandra Gupta v. Kajaria Traders (India) Ltd, AIR 1964 SC 1882 — the proper procedure once the court is faced with the bar is to stay the suit until the firm is registered — the suit is not to be dismissed outright.

5. Collateral consequences of non-registration (state these for full marks). No conclusive evidence of partnership (s. 68) and no notice of it (s. 68); s. 42(c) is unaffected; and — importantly — the Act's other rights are unaffected: an unregistered firm can still be sued in tort, can be dissolved, and its partners' criminal liability is untouched.

Conclusion. Non-registration does not make the firm illegal — it disables it from suing on contracts, bars inter se suits, and bars set-offs, subject to the three exceptions in s. 69(3) and the tort exception in s. 69(4).

1995Business Association–I (OC) · Q.6(a)10 marks
Question A, B and C are partners in a firm. The partnership deed provides that all three partners should jointly execute all contracts with third parties. B executes a contract with D alone. Is the contract binding on the firm? Decide with reasons. (Wording used in: 1999 Q.3(b); 2012 Q.2(b) — "C borrowed ₹20,000 from X in the firm's name and misappropriated it")
Model Answer

Answer: Yes, the contract binds the firm — unless D knew of the restriction in the deed (or did not know or believe B to be a partner).

1. Implied authority — s. 19(1). "Subject to the provisions of s. 22, the act of a partner which is done to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm binds the firm." Executing a contract of the kind the firm does business in is squarely within this.

2. Private restrictions — s. 20. The clause requiring joint execution is a restriction among the partners. s. 20 provides that such a restriction does not bind the firm if the third party —

  1. knew of the restriction, or
  2. did not know or believe that the person was a partner.
The negatives matter: if D did not know of the restriction and did know B was a partner, the firm is bound. The burden of proving knowledge of the restriction is on the firm.

3. The mode requirement — s. 22. The contract must be done in the firm name or in a manner "expressing or implying an intention to bind the firm". If B executed it in the firm's name, s. 22 is satisfied; if B executed it in his own name, the firm is not bound by the document — but may still be liable under s. 27(a) for money received by B within his apparent authority.

4. Agency principles converge. s. 188, ICA — an agent has authority to do every lawful thing which is necessary in order to do such an act; s. 195, ICA — a person dealing with an agent who has no authority but acts fraudulently may still recover; and s. 194, ICA — an agent cannot delegate, but here the point is different.

5. Authority. Rhodes v. Moules (1895) 1 Ch. 236 — the firm was liable where the partner acted within the apparent authority established by the prior course of dealing, even though he lacked actual authority. And see the old common-law rule in Galway v. Matthew (specific authority needed for a single transaction, as opposed to the general authority of a trading partner).

6. Internal consequences. If the firm is held liable, B must indemnify it under s. 13(f) (wilful neglect) — or s. 10 if fraudulent — and the loss is adjusted against his share under s. 48. A and C may also seek dissolution under s. 44(c)/(d).

7. The 2012 variant — C borrows ₹20,000 in the firm's name and misappropriates it. s. 27(a) applies directly: C, acting within his apparent authority, received money from a third person and misapplied it → the firm is liable, and A and B are jointly and severally liable under s. 25. Note also s. 19(2)(a)'s converse and the fact that borrowing money is not one of the eight s. 19(2) restrictions — a trading partner may borrow in the firm's name.

Conclusion. The contract binds the firm under s. 19(1) read with s. 22, because s. 20 makes the private restriction effective only against a third party who knew of it. The firm's remedy is against B.

§E.6 — Exam strategy for Topic 2

1 Answer the section, not the story

Every DU question on Topic 2 is a section disguised as a story. In the first two lines, identify and quote the section. Marks are awarded for the section; the story only earns you the application marks.

2 Always deal with both sides

Write: "It is arguable for the firm that… However, on the wording of s. … the better view is…" Two arguments earn more than one elaborate one, and the DU reader's cases are always argued on both sides.

3 Quote one sentence from a case

Never paraphrase when you can quote. One verbatim line — "knowingly suffers", "legitimate means", "one cannot become or remain a partner of a firm that does not exist" — is worth a full page of description.

4 Distinguish, don't just cite

The examiner's favourite move is the pair: Rhodes v. Cleather; Tower Cabinet v. Snow White; Miles v. Addanki Narayanappa. Cite both and say why one applies and the other does not.

5 Add the procedural gate

End every "liability" answer with s. 25 (joint and several), then s. 69 (registered?), then O. 30 CPC (sue in the firm name). This is worth 2 marks that most candidates never claim.

6 Close the loop internally

After holding the firm liable, add: "and the firm may recover from X under s. 10 / s. 13(f) / s. 16." Topic 2 is two-sided by design — external liability and internal recourse.

! Five mistakes that cost the most marks
  1. Saying "profit-sharing makes you a partner." It is prima facie evidence only — Holme v. Hammond. The true test is mutual agency.
  2. Forgetting "subject to contract between the partners." Almost every section in Chapter III begins with it. State it, or you will be treated as saying the rule is absolute.
  3. Confusing s. 26 with s. 27. s. 26 = wrongful act or omission causing loss or injury; s. 27 = misapplication of money or property. Different elements, different cases.
  4. Applying s. 30(5) where the firm is already dissolved. That is Shivgouda — s. 30 presupposes an existing firm.
  5. Answering Chapter III for a Chapter IV question. If the question involves an outsider, the answer is in ss. 18–30; if it is a fight between partners, it is in ss. 9–17. Say which chapter you are in.
