Part A — The Statute: Sections 1 to 10 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930

§1.0 to §1.12 · the vocabulary every DU answer on this topic must use

§1.0 · Five words that decide every case in this Topic

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 is the oldest surviving codification in Indian commercial law. It received the Governor-General's assent on 15 March 1930 (Act No. III of 1930) and came into force on 1 July 1930. It replaced Chapter VII of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (ss. 76–123), which had itself been a hasty transplant of the English Sale of Goods Act 1893. Its structure is deceptively simple: Part I (ss. 1–10) deals with the formation of the contract; Part II (ss. 11–26) with its terms; Part III (ss. 27–44) with performance and transfer of property; Part IV (ss. 45–54) with the unpaid seller; Part V (ss. 55–61) with suits for breach; and Part VI (ss. 62–66) with miscellaneous.

Your syllabus stops at s. 10. That is not an accident. Sections 1 to 10 ask a single question — has a contract of sale come into existence at all? — and everything after s. 10 assumes the answer is yes. Sections 11 to 66 tell you what to do once you have a sale; sections 1 to 10 tell you whether you have one. And in a country where the State compels the supply of cement, pools the entire coffee crop, builds railway coaches on land it does not own and sells flats that do not yet exist, that prior question is ferociously contested. That is why the Faculty has put eleven cases on ten sections.

Section 4(1) — the hinge of the whole Act
“A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price.”

▸ A real-life opening example

The hostel mess. Every morning at 7 a.m. a mess contractor serves 400 students breakfast in a dining hall. Is that a sale of goods?

  • Ask s. 4(1): does the contractor transfer the property in goods to each student? The student does not own the idli on his plate — he cannot pocket it, sell it, or give it to the person next to him. The uneaten food is not his. What he has bought is a right to satisfy his appetite.
  • Ask s. 2(7): are the idlis ‘goods’? Yes — but that is not the question. The question is whether property in them passed.
  • That is Northern India Caterers v. Lt. Governor of Delhi — one of your eleven cases — and it is why the answer, before the Forty-sixth Amendment inserted clause (f) into Art. 366(29-A), was no sale, no sales tax.
  • Now change the facts: the contractor sells a packed thali at a counter for the student to take away. Now property does pass, and it is a sale. The physical idli is identical; the legal analysis is different. That difference is worth a tax, and it is what this Topic is about.

§1.1 · Section 1 — Short title, extent and commencement

Section 1 — verbatim
“(1) This Act may be called the Indian Sale of Goods Act, 1930.
(2) It extends to the whole of India except the State of Jammu and Kashmir.
(3) It shall come into force on the 1st day of July, 1930.”
§ Three points examiners reward
  1. Act No. 3 of 1930; assent 15 March 1930; in force 1 July 1930. The Preamble is “An Act to define and amend the law relating to the sale of goods” — the word define matters, because in Gannon Dunkerley the Supreme Court used it to hold that the expression ‘sale of goods’ is a nomen juris — a term of art with a fixed legal content that the legislature cannot redefine at will.
  2. The word ‘Indian’ was dropped from the title by Act 33 of 1963 (w.e.f. 22-9-1963).
  3. Extent. Originally ‘whole of India except Jammu & Kashmir’; the words ‘except the State of Jammu and Kashmir’ were omitted by Act 34 of 2019, s. 95 and the Fifth Schedule (w.e.f. 31-10-2019), following the re-organisation of that State. So today the Act extends to the whole of India. Before that it had been extended to Berar (Act 4 of 1941), Dadra and Nagar Haveli (Reg. 6 of 1963), Goa, Daman and Diu (Reg. 11 of 1963), Pondicherry (Act 26 of 1968), Lakshadweep (Reg. 8 of 1965, w.e.f. 1-10-1967) and Sikkim (w.e.f. 1-9-1984, Notif. S.O. 645(E) dated 24-8-1984).

▸ Real-life example

Ramesh in Ludhiana sells 200 bales of cotton to a mill in Coimbatore. The bales go by rail. Ramesh takes the railway receipt, endorses it in favour of his bank, and the bank releases it to the mill against payment. The goods never move again — but the document of title moves three times, and each movement can transfer the property. That is why s. 2(4) exists: in long-distance trade, the document is the goods. Contrast a simple lorry receipt or a delivery challan, which are not documents of title — they are evidence of despatch, not of control.

§1.3 · Section 3 — the Indian Contract Act, 1872 continues to apply

Section 3 — verbatim
“The unrepealed provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, save in so far as they are inconsistent with the express provisions of this Act, shall continue to apply to contracts for the sale of goods.”
§ Why s. 3 is far more important than its length

The Sale of Goods Act is not a complete code. It supplies the special law of sale; the Indian Contract Act supplies the general law of contract. Section 3 is the pipe that connects them. Its three limbs are:

  1. Which provisions? Only the unrepealed ones. Chapter VII of the ICA (ss. 76–123), which dealt with sale, was repealed when the SOGA was enacted in 1930 — so those are gone, and the SOGA occupies the field.
  2. How far? Only so far as they are not inconsistent with the express provisions of the SOGA. Where the two collide, the SOGA wins.
  3. To what? To “contracts for the sale of goods” — a phrase wide enough to cover both a sale and an agreement to sell (s. 4(3)).

This is reinforced by s. 2(15), which imports ICA definitions for every expression the SOGA leaves undefined. In Vishnu Agencies (para 40) the Supreme Court used exactly this history to explain why the English ‘mutual assent’ formula had hardened in India: Chapter VII of the 1872 Act had “drawn inspiration from the English law of contract”, whose “growth is marked by features peculiar to the social and economic history of England” and whose emphasis on consensuality came from the action of assumpsit.

★ DU-usage note

A three-mark note on s. 3 should say three things only: (i) the SOGA is not exhaustive; (ii) the general law of contract in the ICA 1872 continues, minus the repealed ss. 76–123 and minus anything inconsistent with the express words of the SOGA; and (iii) s. 2(15) imports ICA definitions. Add one illustration — ‘free consent’ under ss. 13–22 ICA governs a sale just as it governs any other contract — and you have 3/3. Do not drift into ss. 76–123 of the ICA; they are repealed, and saying so shows you have read the statute.

§1.4 · Syllabus theme (a) — the concept of ‘goods’ (s. 2(7))

Section 2(7) — verbatim
“‘goods’ means every kind of moveable property other than actionable claims and money; and includes stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale.”

Read that definition twice. It does three different jobs at once, and the eleven cases in your syllabus are distributed across the three.

Limb 1 — the genus

“every kind of moveable property”

  • The widest possible genus. Not ‘chattels’, not ‘merchandise’, not ‘wares’ — every kind.
  • But moveable. Immovable property as such is outside the Act.
  • Fought over in M.P. Electricity Board (is electricity moveable?).
Limb 2 — the exclusions

“other than actionable claims and money”

  • Actionable claim — ICA s. 3: a claim to a debt or beneficial interest in moveable property not in possession, which courts recognise as affording grounds for relief. A debt, a share in a partnership, a claim for damages.
  • Money — current coin and currency in circulation, not old/rare coins held as curios.
  • Both are directly asked about in the 2017 and 2012 papers.
Limb 3 — the inclusive extension

“and includes stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed”

  • Pulls in things that would otherwise be immovable — but only where there is an agreement to sever.
  • The basis of the doctrine of fructus industriales / emblements.
  • Asked as ‘standing timber’ in 2017.
! The judicial gloss the syllabus actually tests: marketability

The statute says ‘every kind of moveable property’. The Supreme Court has added a fourth, unwritten requirement. In R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma the Court read s. 171 of the ICA together with s. 2(7) of the SOGA and held that the ‘goods’ must be capable of being sold in the market: the test is marketability, derived from Union of India v. Delhi Cloth & General Mills (AIR 1963 SC 791) and CCE v. Eastend Paper Industries ((1989) 4 SCC 244). A thing may be moveable and yet not be ‘goods’ if there is no market for it. A lawyer's case file is moveable — you can pick it up and carry it — but there is no market in which it can be sold, so it is not ‘goods’ and cannot be the subject of a lien. Learn this: moveability + marketability + severability.

▸ Real-life example — the mango orchard

Farmer Bhola owns an orchard. He makes three separate deals.

  • Deal 1: he sells to a fruit contractor “the entire mango crop of the 2026 season” in April, before flowering. → Goods. ‘Growing crops’ and ‘grass’ are expressly included, and in any event this is future goods under s. 2(6), so s. 6(3) makes it an agreement to sell which becomes a sale when the fruit is ascertained.
  • Deal 2: he sells “the ten teak trees in the north-east corner, to be felled by you within a month”. → Goods. Standing timber, agreed to be severed, under the third limb. This is the 2017 DU answer.
  • Deal 3: he sells “the old banyan tree in the courtyard, which you may enjoy for shade”. → Not goods; it is immovable property, and the transfer must comply with the Transfer of Property Act and the Registration Act.

§1.5 · Syllabus theme (b) — ‘Sale’ and ‘Agreement to sell’ (s. 4)

Section 4 — verbatim
“(1) A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price. There may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another.
(2) A contract of sale may be absolute or conditional.
(3) Where under a contract of sale the property in the goods is transferred from the seller to the buyer, the contract is called a sale, but where the transfer of the property in the goods is to take place at a future time or subject to some condition thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is called an agreement to sell.
(4) An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred.”

§1.5.1 · The anatomy of s. 4(1)

1. Two parties

Seller and buyer must be distinct persons — but one part-owner may sell to another part-owner (s. 4(1), second sentence). ICA ss. 11–12 on competency apply through s. 3.

2. Subject-matter: goods

s. 2(7). Existing or future (s. 6). If future, s. 6(3) forces the contract into the agreement to sell box.

3. Transfer of property

Not possession — general property (s. 2(11)). This is the word every works-contract case turns on.

4. Money consideration

‘Price’ = money only (s. 2(10)). Barter or exchange is not a sale under this Act (though it may now be taxed).

§ The three sentences in s. 4(1)–(2) that students skip
  1. “There may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another.” A and B jointly own a car. A sells his half-share to B. That is a sale. This reverses the common-law position and is worth a sentence in any ‘essentials of sale’ answer.
  2. “A contract of sale may be absolute or conditional” (s. 4(2)). A sale on ‘sale or return’ (s. 24), on approval, or subject to a condition subsequent is still a contract of sale — the condition governs when property passes, not whether there is a contract.
  3. s. 4(3) uses the word ‘called’: “the contract is called a sale”. That is the language of nomenclature, and it is why Venkatarama Aiyar J. could say in Gannon Dunkerley that ‘sale of goods’ is a nomen juris.

▸ Real-life example — the wedding lehenga

On 1 May, Meera orders a custom-made lehenga from a boutique for Rs 80,000 and pays Rs 20,000 as advance. Delivery is fixed for 20 May. On 10 May a fire destroys the boutique's stock, including her lehenga, then half-stitched. Who bears the loss? On 1 May there was only an agreement to sell — the goods were future goods (s. 2(6)), so s. 6(3) makes the purported present sale operate as an agreement to sell. Property had not passed on 10 May. Risk therefore remained with the boutique (s. 26). Meera can reclaim her Rs 20,000 and, if she can prove loss, damages. Now change the facts: the lehenga was ready, Meera had approved it, and it was kept aside with her name on it — the goods were then ascertained and property had passed. It is a sale; the loss is Meera's and the boutique can sue her for the balance of the price under s. 55(1).

★ The 2015 DU question, answered in one paragraph

“‘An agreement to sell is a contract pure and simple, whereas a sale is a contract plus conveyance.’ Discuss.” — 2015, Q8(a). This is the classical formulation of Benjamin and it was quoted with approval by Lord Denning M.R. in C.E.B. Draper & Sons Ltd. v. Edward Turner & Sons Ltd. ((1964) 3 All ER 148) — a case the Supreme Court reproduced verbatim in Coffee Board: “I know that often times a contract for sale is spoken of as a sale. But the word ‘sale’ properly connotes the transfer of the absolute or general property in a thing for a price in money.” The ‘conveyance’ is the transfer of general property; the ‘contract’ is the consensus. An agreement to sell has the second but not the first.

§1.5.3 · Hire-purchase (Hire-Purchase Act, 1972, ss. 3–5) — and why it is not a sale

The Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 — ss. 3 to 5
s. 3. Every hire-purchase agreement shall be in writing, and shall be signed by all the parties thereto.
s. 4. Every hire-purchase agreement shall state — (a) the hire-purchase price of the goods to which the agreement relates; (b) the cash price of the goods …; (c) the date on which the agreement is entered into; (d) the number of instalments … (and where any of the instalments is other than an equal instalment, the amount of that instalment); (e) the date, or the mode of determining the date, upon which each instalment is payable; (f) the goods to which the agreement relates … (and, by s. 4(2), a description sufficient to identify them; by s. 4(3), the agreement shall be void if it does not state the hire-purchase price or the cash price);
s. 5. (1) An owner … shall, within the prescribed period, furnish to the hirer a copy of the agreement … (2) Where the owner fails to comply … he shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement … and the hirer shall be entitled to recover the amounts already paid.
Hire-purchase
  • There is a bailment of the goods, coupled with an option to purchase exercisable on payment of the last instalment.
  • Property passes only when the option is exercised — the hirer's payments before that are hire, not price.
  • The hirer is a bailee until then (ICA ss. 148–152). He must take reasonable care; risk of loss is on the owner unless the hirer is at fault.
  • The finance company is the owner, not a seller.
  • Governed by the Hire-Purchase Act 1972 (form, contents, statutory copy) — not by the SOGA.
Sale (including sale by instalments)
  • Property passes at once (s. 4(3)); the buyer becomes owner immediately.
  • Each payment is a payment of price, not hire.
  • Risk passes with property (s. 26), even though the seller retains possession for the instalment period.
  • The seller's remedy on default is a suit for the balance of the price (s. 55(1)); the hire-purchase owner's remedy is repossession plus damages for breach of the bailment.
  • Governed by the SOGA 1930.
§ Sundaram Finance Ltd. v. State of Kerala (AIR 1966 SC 1178 : (1966) 2 SCR 828)

The DU syllabus lists this case against the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972, ss. 3–5. A financier purchased vehicles from dealers at the request of customers, let them out on hire-purchase, and collected the instalments. The Kerala State was the registered owner of the vehicles throughout, and the hire-purchase agreement expressly provided that property would pass only on payment of the last instalment. The Supreme Court held that the hiring is one transaction and the purchase is another; the ‘option to purchase’ is not an agreement to sell, and during the hiring the finance company is the owner and the hirer is only a bailee. The practical consequences the Court identified:

  • The transaction is not a sale for sales-tax purposes until the option is exercised — though modern sales-tax statutes (and Art. 366(29-A)(c), which taxes ‘delivery of goods on hire-purchase or any system of payment by instalments’) now deem it to be one.
  • Because the hirer is a bailee, the financier's location as owner, not the hirer's possession, determines where the goods are.
  • On the hirer's default, the financier repossesses his own goods rather than reselling goods he has sold.
▸ Real-life example — the delivery van

Aryan needs a delivery van costing Rs 9 lakh. He has Rs 1 lakh. Route A (hire-purchase): a finance company buys the van from the dealer for Rs 9 lakh, Aryan pays Rs 1 lakh and then 36 monthly instalments of Rs 26,000. The RC still shows the finance company as owner. Aryan is a bailee. If the van is destroyed in an accident in month 4 through no fault of Aryan, the owner's loss falls on the finance company (subject to the insurance and to the terms of the agreement). If Aryan defaults, the company repossesses. Route B (sale with a loan): Aryan buys the van outright under the SOGA (property passes immediately, he is the registered owner), borrowing Rs 8 lakh from a bank and creating a hypothecation. If the van is destroyed in month 4, the loss is Aryan's — property and risk passed on day one under s. 26 — and he still owes the bank. Identical commercial deal, opposite legal result. That is why s. 4(3) matters.

§1.7 · Section 5 — how the contract of sale is made

Section 5 — verbatim
“(1) A contract of sale is made by an offer to buy or sell goods for a price and the acceptance of such offer. The contract may provide for the immediate delivery of the goods or immediate payment of the price or both, or for the delivery or payment by instalments, or that the delivery or payment or both shall be postponed.
(2) Subject to the provisions of any law for the time being in force, a contract of sale may be made in writing or by word of mouth, or partly in writing and partly by word of mouth or may be implied from the conduct of the parties.”
§ Four propositions buried in s. 5
  1. No formality is required. This is the single most important word in the section: “Subject to the provisions of any law for the time being in force” — meaning that unless some other statute requires writing or registration, a sale of goods of any value can be made orally. Contrast s. 54 TPA, which requires a registered instrument for immoveable property worth more than Rs 100.
  2. Offer and acceptance are governed by the ICA (ss. 2–9) through s. 3 — but note the sequence: s. 5(1) says the offer must be “to buy or sell goods for a price”. An offer that leaves the price entirely at large may still be a contract (see s. 9(2) — reasonable price) where the parties intend to contract.
  3. Time of delivery and payment is irrelevant to formation. Immediate, by instalments, or postponed — all three are contracts of sale. This is what lets an instalment sale be a sale (property passes at once) while a hire-purchase is not.
  4. “Implied from the conduct of the parties” is the hook on which the whole law of statutory transactions hangs. Where a statute compels A to supply and B to take, the consent is found not in a bargain but in conduct — A chose to become a licensed dealer, B chose to apply for a permit. That is how Vishnu Agencies and Coffee Board find a sale inside a command.

Note also the placement of the heading in the Act: s. 5 sits under the marginal note “Formalities of the contract”, and ss. 6–8 under “Subject-matter of contract”.

§1.8 · Syllabus theme (c) — Statutory Transactions

A ‘statutory transaction’ — a phrase the DU syllabus uses but never defines — is a transaction which the parties enter into because a statute obliges them to, on terms the statute dictates, at a price the statute fixes, and usually with a penal sanction for refusal. The cement supplied against an allotment order. The paddy a grower must sell to a licensed procuring agent. The coffee a grower must deliver to the Coffee Board pool.

The question the Supreme Court has had to answer three times is deceptively narrow: can a compelled transaction be a ‘sale’ at all? There are two rival answers, and the syllabus deliberately gives you cases on both sides.

Figure 6 · Statutory transactions — the two rival answers, and the line that separates them
The test is not ‘how much compulsion’ but ‘compulsion of what kind’: a general regulation of a trade leaves a sale; a direction to a particular owner to part with his property produces compulsory acquisition.

! Why the syllabus theme is called ‘Statutory Transactions’ and not ‘Compulsory Sales’

Because ‘compulsory sale’ is, strictly, a contradiction. Chandrachud J. (as he then was), opening the seven-Judge Bench judgment in Vishnu Agencies, called it “what is conveniently, though somewhat loosely, called a ‘compulsory sale’”. The Court's answer was that the compulsion in these cases is antecedent: nobody is compelled to deal in cement, and nobody is compelled to buy it; but if you choose to deal in cement you must deal on the statutory terms. The volition to enter the trade supplies the consent; the Control Order supplies only the terms. “Such volition carries with it the willingness to trade in the commodity strictly on the terms of the Control Orders.”

§1.9 · Section 6 — existing and future goods

Section 6 — verbatim
“(1) The goods which form the subject of a contract of sale may be either existing goods, owned or possessed by the seller, or future goods.
(2) There may be a contract for the sale of goods the acquisition of which by the seller depends upon a contingency which may or may not happen.
(3) Where by a contract of sale the seller purports to effect a present sale of future goods, the contract operates as an agreement to sell the goods.”
Existing goods — s. 6(1)

Owned or possessed by the seller. Possession without ownership is enough (a mercantile agent can sell). These may be specific (s. 2(14)), ascertained or unascertained. Property can pass immediately.

Future goods — s. 6(1) & s. 2(6)

Goods to be manufactured, produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract. They do not yet exist, or are not yet his. Property cannot pass until they come into existence and are ascertained.

Contingent goods — s. 6(2)

A sub-species of future goods: the seller's acquisition depends on a contingency which may or may not happen. (The ‘goods perishing’ sections, ss. 7–8, use specific goods; s. 6(2) uses contingent acquisition.)

★ The one line examiners want on s. 6(3)

s. 6(3) is a rule of conversion, not of invalidity. A purported present sale of future goods is not void; it is re-characterised as an agreement to sell. The policy is obvious: nemo dat quod non habet — you cannot transfer what you do not have, so the law reads the contract as a promise to transfer later. Note the English contrast: at common law a purported present sale of future goods was void; s. 5(3) of the English 1893 Act (and s. 6(3) here) deliberately changed that. Cite s. 6(3) in every answer about crops to be grown, goods to be manufactured, or flats under construction.

▸ Real-life example — the ‘next season's cotton’ contract

In March a ginning mill contracts to buy 500 bales from a trader, delivery in December, from cotton to be grown in the Kharif season. The trader does not own the cotton; indeed it has not been sown. Under s. 2(6) these are future goods; under s. 6(3) the March contract operates as an agreement to sell. Consequences: (i) the mill gets no property in March; (ii) if hail destroys the crop, s. 8 avoids the agreement (if the goods are specific); (iii) if the trader sells the December cotton to someone else, the mill has only a personal claim for damages; and (iv) if the mill becomes insolvent in June, the trader has no lien — the cotton is still his own.

▸ Two exam-grade illustrations

s. 7 illustration. A agrees to sell to B “my grey Maruti 800 standing in my garage”. Unknown to A, the car was destroyed in a garage fire the previous night. The contract is void under s. 7 — specific goods, perished at the time of the contract, without the seller's knowledge. If A had known, s. 7 would not apply: A would be liable in damages for breach (and B could rescind under ss. 17–19 ICA for fraud).

s. 8 illustration. A agrees to sell to B a specific thoroughbred mare, delivery and payment in a fortnight. Three days later the mare dies of colic, with no negligence on anyone's part. The agreement is avoided under s. 8: the goods were specific, they perished without fault, and the risk had not yet passed (property had not passed, so under s. 26 the risk was still A's). B recovers any advance under s. 65 ICA. Contrast: if the contract had said “property shall pass immediately, delivery later”, risk would have passed at once and the loss would fall on B.

§1.11 · Sections 9 and 10 — price

Section 9 — verbatim
“(1) The price in a contract of sale may be fixed by the contract or may be left to be fixed in manner thereby agreed or may be determined by the course of dealing between the parties.
(2) Where the price is not determined in accordance with the foregoing provisions, the buyer shall pay the seller a reasonable price. What is a reasonable price is a question of fact dependent on the circumstances of each particular case.”
Section 10 — verbatim
“(1) Where there is an agreement to sell goods on the terms that the price is to be fixed by the valuation of a third party and such third party cannot or does not make such valuation, the agreement is thereby avoided:
Provided that, if the goods or any part thereof have been delivered to, and appropriated by, the buyer, he shall pay a reasonable price therefor.
(2) Where such third party is prevented from making the valuation by the fault of the seller or buyer, the party not in fault may maintain a suit for damages against the party in fault.”
s. 9 — the three-tier price ladder
  1. Fixed by the contract — the usual case.
  2. Fixed in a manner thereby agreed — e.g. “at the market rate prevailing on the date of delivery”, or “as certified by the Indian Sugar Mills Association”.
  3. Determined by the course of dealing — the parties have never discussed price but have traded for years at a known rate.
  4. If none of the three: reasonable price — and ‘what is reasonable is a question of fact’ (s. 9(2)).
s. 10 — the valuation-of-a-third-party rule
  • The general rule: if the valuer cannot or does not value, the agreement is avoided — not performed at a reasonable price.
  • The proviso: if the goods have been delivered to and appropriated by the buyer, he must pay a reasonable price. Both limbs are required — delivery and appropriation.
  • s. 10(2): if the valuer was prevented by the fault of a party (s. 2(5)), the innocent party gets damages, not avoidance.
  • Contrast s. 9: an agreed manner of fixing price that fails leaves a reasonable-price contract; an agreed valuer who fails avoids the contract.
Figure 8 · Sections 9 and 10 — what happens to the price when the price is not agreed
Note the deliberate asymmetry: failure of a manner (s. 9) keeps the contract alive at a reasonable price; failure of a valuer (s. 10) kills it, unless the goods have already been delivered and appropriated.

▸ Real-life example — the disputed valuation

Priya agrees to sell her grandfather's collection of 2,000 stamps to a dealer “at the price fixed by Mr Iyer, the philatelist”. Mr Iyer is appointed, inspects the collection, and then dies before certifying a figure. Under s. 10(1) the agreement is avoided — Priya keeps her stamps and the dealer gets back any advance under s. 65 ICA. Now suppose instead that Priya had already handed the albums over and the dealer had broken the collection into sets for resale. That is delivery and appropriation; the proviso applies and the dealer must pay a reasonable price. And if it turns out that Priya had quietly asked Mr Iyer not to value the collection because she had found a better buyer, that is fault under s. 10(2) and the dealer can sue her for damages.

! The consequence the Court foresaw — and the Amendment it produced

Venkatarama Aiyar J. knew exactly what he was doing. He ended the judgment by acknowledging that if the Provinces could not tax the materials in a building contract, the revenue would suffer — and observed that “the Legislature alone can, if it so decides, enact a law amending the definition of ‘sale’ in the Act so as to bring within it transactions in which property in goods passes otherwise than by transfer inter vivos”. Parliament did precisely that, thirty years later, by the Constitution (Forty-sixth Amendment) Act, 1982, which inserted clause (29-A) into Article 366. That is why your syllabus calls this theme “Contract for ‘Works’/‘Labour’ (Pre and Post 46th Constitutional Amendment)”.

§2.3 · The Forty-sixth Amendment — Article 366(29-A)

Constitution of India — Article 366(29-A) (inserted by the Constitution (Forty-sixth Amendment) Act, 1982; in force from 2 February 1983)
“(29A) ‘tax on the sale or purchase of goods’ includes —
(a) a tax on the transfer, otherwise than in pursuance of a contract, of property in any goods for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration;
(b) a tax on the transfer of property in goods (whether as goods or in some other form) involved in the execution of a works contract;
(c) a tax on the delivery of goods on hire-purchase or any system of payment by instalments;
(d) a tax on the transfer of the right to use any goods for any purpose (whether or not for a specified period) for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration;
(e) a tax on the supply of goods by any unincorporated association or body of persons to a member thereof for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration;
(f) a tax on the supply, by way of or as part of any service or in any other manner whatsoever, of goods, being food or any other article for human consumption or any drink (whether or not intoxicating), where such supply or service, is for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration;
and such transfer, delivery or supply of any goods shall be deemed to be a sale of those goods by the person making the transfer, delivery or supply and a purchase of those goods by the person to whom such transfer, delivery or supply is made.”
§ Six changes worked by the Amendment — the list to memorise
  1. From indivisible to divisible. The States may now, by legal fiction, bifurcate a single works contract into (i) a contract for the sale of the goods involved and (ii) a contract for labour and services, and tax the first.
  2. From ‘goods’ to ‘goods or in some other form’. The bracketed words in clause (b) are, in the words of Larsen & Toubro, “of utmost significance”: they bring in goods that “have ceased to be chattels or movables or merchandise and become attached or embedded to earth”.
  3. From transfer inter vivos to deemed sale. The closing words deem the transfer to be a sale even where the essential ingredients of ‘sale’ in the SOGA are absent.
  4. From dominant intention to irrelevance. After 1983 the ‘dominant-nature test’ “has no application” in matters of the nature contemplated by clause (29-A) (L&T, para 101(vi)).
  5. From ‘no tax on the service element’ to a hard boundary. The aspects theory permits the State to tax the goods aspect and the Union the service aspect — but the State may not include the cost of the service in the value of the goods (Bharat Sanchar, para 88).
  6. The measure is the value of the goods at the time of incorporation, not the contractor's cost of acquisition (Gannon Dunkerley-II, fifth proposition; reaffirmed in L&T).

§2.4 · After the Amendment — the eleven propositions in Larsen & Toubro

The three-Judge Bench in Larsen & Toubro Ltd. v. State of Karnataka ((2014) 1 SCC 708) was answering a reference: was Raheja Development Corporation correctly decided? In answering it, the Court restated the entire law of works contracts in eleven numbered propositions at paragraph 101. Those propositions are the single most quotable passage in this Topic, and a DU answer that reproduces even five of them will score. They are set out here in full.

§ L&T, para 101 — the eleven propositions, verbatim in substance
  1. Three conditions for the levy. For sustaining the levy of tax on the goods deemed to have been sold in execution of a works contract there must be: (one) a works contract; (two) the goods should have been involved in the execution of a works contract; and (three) the property in those goods must be transferred to a third party either as goods or in some other form.
  2. Valuable consideration completes them. In a building contract or any contract to do construction, if the developer has received or is entitled to receive valuable consideration, the three conditions are fully met — because goods like cement, concrete, steel and bricks are intended to be incorporated in the structure and even though they lost their identity as goods, this factor does not prevent them from being goods.
  3. A contract may comprise both. Where a contract comprises both a works contract and a transfer of immovable property, such contract does not denude it of its character as a works contract. The term ‘works contract’ in Art. 366(29-A)(b) “takes within its fold all genre of works contract and is not restricted to one specie of contract to provide for labour and services alone. Nothing in Article 366(29-A)(b) limits the term ‘works contract’.”
  4. Building contracts are a species of works contract.
  5. In a composite contract the distinction is ‘virtually diminished’. A contract may involve both a contract of work and labour and a contract for sale.
  6. The dominant-nature test has no application. “The traditional decisions which have held that the substance of the contract must be seen have lost their significance where transactions are of the nature contemplated in Article 366(29-A).” Even if the dominant intention is not to transfer property in goods, and even if the ultimate transaction is a transfer of immovable property, the State may still levy sales tax on the materials. The enforceability test is also not determinative.
  7. Deeming works both ways. A transfer of property in goods under clause 29-A(b) is deemed to be a sale by the person making the transfer and a purchase by the person to whom it is made.
  8. Single and indivisible contracts are now on par with divisible ones. By the legal fiction, there is a deemed sale even in a single and indivisible works contract, and “such a deemed sale has all the incidents of the sale of goods involved in the execution of a works contract where the contract is divisible”.
  9. Entry 54 read with clause 29-A includes a tax on the transfer of property in goods “whether as goods or in the form other than goods involved in the execution of works contract”.
  10. Clause 29-A brings in transactions where SOGA ingredients are absent. “Article 366(29-A)(b) serves to bring transactions where essential ingredients of ‘sale’ defined in the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 are absent within the ambit of sale or purchase for the purposes of levy of sales tax.”
  11. Tax after incorporation is permissible, provided the tax is directed to the value of the goods and does not purport to tax the transfer of immovable property. The measure is “the value of the goods at the time of incorporation of the goods in works even though property passes as between the developer and the flat purchaser after incorporation of goods”.
★ The two sentences examiners actually quote

“To say that insertion of clause (29-A) in Article 366 has not undone Gannon Dunkerley-I in any manner, in our view, is not correct. The narrow meaning given to the term ‘works contract’ in Gannon Dunkerley-I now no longer survives.” (L&T, para 71.)

“The dominant nature test has no application and the traditional decisions which have held that the substance of the contract must be seen have lost their significance where transactions are of the nature contemplated in Article 366(29-A). … The enforceability test is also not determinative.” (L&T, para 101(vi).)

! Rainbow Colour Lab — the wrong turn, and how it was corrected

The immediate post-Amendment case law was not settled. In Rainbow Colour Lab v. State of M.P. a two-Judge Bench held that after the Forty-sixth Amendment the works contract could be divided “only if the works contract involved a dominant intention to transfer the property in goods and not in contracts where the transfer in property takes place as an incident of contract of service”, and that “the Amendment … has not empowered the State to indulge in microscopic division of contracts involving the value of materials used incidentally in such contracts.”

Following it, the Karnataka High Court in Keshoram struck down Entry 25 of Schedule VI to the Karnataka Sales Tax Act (which taxed the supply of photographs, photo-prints and photo-negatives) as beyond Article 366, on the ground that processing photographs was a service contract and the photopaper was merely incidental. The SLP against that judgment was dismissed on 20-4-2000.

Within a year, ACC Ltd. doubted Rainbow Colour Lab; BSNL repeated the doubt; and Larsen & Toubro settled it: the dominant intention test “has no application”. Rainbow Colour Lab “is no longer a good law”. That is precisely the issue that reached the Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. Pro Lab (AIR 2015 SC 1098) — your Case 29 — where the Court upheld Entry 25 and set aside the Karnataka High Court, holding that the very basis on which Entry 25 had been struck down “has been found to be erroneous”.

▸ Real-life example — the modular kitchen, worked both ways

Facts. In 2025 you contract with a fabricator to design, manufacture and install a modular kitchen in your flat for a single price of Rs 4 lakh, no component broken out.

Post-1983 answer (the only one that matters today). Q1: post-1983. Q2: it is a works contract — ‘manufacture, fabrication, installation, fitting out’ are all within the ‘all genre’ formula. Q3: the carcass boards, hardware and shutters are goods involved in the execution. Q4: the property in them transfers to you, as goods and then in some other form (fixed to the walls). Result: deemed sale of the goods element; GST is leviable on the goods, and the service element is separately taxable. The dominant intention — you wanted a kitchen, not a heap of chipboard — is irrelevant.

Pre-1983 answer (for comparison). You did not contract to buy boards and hinges as such; the unit came into existence only when fitted to your walls and floor, so the fabricator never owned the kitchen as a unit. On the Sentinel reasoning this is Form 2 — a works contract, no sale, not taxable. The same facts, two opposite answers, forty years apart. That contrast is worth a full page in the exam.

§ Theme (a) — Concept of ‘Goods’

Two cases: one about what is not goods although it is plainly moveable (R.D. Saxena), and one about what is goods although it cannot be touched (M.P. Electricity Board). Read them together: they define the two boundaries of s. 2(7).

19. R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma
(2000) 7 SCC 264 · AIR 2000 SC 2912 · Supreme Court · K.T. Thomas J. (with Sethi J. concurring) · DU Reader p. 108
A lawyer's case file is not ‘goods’ — it has no marketability, so there is no lien.
Facts

The appellant was an advocate practising mainly in the courts at Bhopal, enrolled with the State Bar Council of Madhya Pradesh. In 1990 he was appointed legal adviser to the Madhya Pradesh State Cooperative Bank Ltd. and continued to be retained in that capacity in succeeding years; he was also engaged to conduct cases in which the Bank was a party.

The retainership did not last. On 17 July 1993 the Bank terminated it and asked him to return all the case files relating to the Bank. Instead of returning them, he forwarded a consolidated bill for Rs 97,100 as the balance payable towards his legal remuneration, and informed the Bank that the files would be returned only after his dues were settled.

The Bank complained to the State Bar Council. Thedisciplinary authority found him guilty of professional misconduct. He appealed to the Supreme Court, contending that he was entitled to retain the files in exercise of a retaining lien.

Issues
  1. Has an advocate a lien for his fees on the litigation papers entrusted to him by his client?
  2. Are case files ‘goods bailed’ within s. 171 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872?
  3. Does the English common-law solicitor's retaining lien survive in India after the Advocates Act, 1961 and the Bar Council of India Rules?
  4. Was the punishment imposed on the appellant disproportionate?
Arguments
For the advocate (appellant)
  • s. 171, ICA 1872 entitles “bankers, factors, wharfingers, attorneys of a High Court and policy-brokers” to retain “any goods bailed to them” as security for a general balance of account. An advocate of a High Court is an ‘attorney of a High Court’.
  • At common law in England a solicitor had a right to retain “any deed, paper or chattel which had come into his possession during the course of his employment” — recognised later by the Solicitors Act, 1860 and stated in Halsbury's Laws of England, Vol. 44, para 226.
  • The retaining lien is “founded on the general law of lien which springs from possession” (Cordery on Solicitors, 7th Edn.) and extends to “any deed, paper or personal chattel which has come into the solicitor's possession in the course of his employment … with the client's sanction”.
  • An advocate is “competent to settle the terms of his engagement and his fee by private agreement with his client” — so retention is a legitimate self-help remedy.
For the Bank / the Bar Council (respondent)
  • Files containing copies of records — and perhaps some originals — cannot be equated with ‘goods’ in s. 171. There is neither delivery of goods nor any contract that they shall be returned or otherwise disposed of, as s. 148 requires for a bailment.
  • The word ‘goods’ in s. 171 must be understood in the sense in which it is defined in s. 2(7) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — because Chapter VII of the Contract Act (ss. 76–123) was wholly replaced by that Act.
  • So understood, ‘goods’ must have marketability: the bailee must be in a position to dispose of them for money. “There is no scope for converting the case files into money, nor can they be sold to any third party.”
  • The Advocates Act, 1961 repealed the Indian Bar Council Act and the BCI framed rules; Rules 28 and 29 give the advocate a right to deduct fees only out of moneys of the client remaining in his hands — and they conspicuously provide no lien on the litigation files. Rule 24 forbids an advocate from abusing or taking advantage of the confidence reposed in him by his client.
Decision — per K.T. Thomas J.

No lien. The appeal was dismissed on the point of principle; the punishment was altered to one of reprimand, with a warning that any advocate committing this kind of misconduct in future “would be liable to such quantum of punishment as the Bar Council will determine and the lesser punishment imposed now need not be counted as a precedent.”

“Files containing copies of the records (perhaps some original documents also) cannot be equated with the ‘goods’ referred to in the section. The advocate keeping the files cannot amount to ‘goods bailed’. … In the case of litigation papers in the hands of the advocate there is neither delivery of goods nor any contract that they shall be returned or otherwise disposed of.” R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma, para 8
“Thus understood ‘goods’ to fall within the purview of Section 171 of the Contract Act should have marketability and the person to whom they are bailed should be in a position to dispose of them in consideration of money. In other words the goods referred to in Section 171 of the Contract Act are saleable goods. There is no scope for converting the case files into money, nor can they be sold to any third party.” Ibid., para 9

Six reasons were given in the alternative:

  1. Statutory: s. 171 requires ‘goods bailed’; s. 148 requires delivery of goods; s. 2(7) SOGA supplies the meaning; case files do not answer it.
  2. Lexicographic: Bailey's Large Dictionary (1732) — ‘merchandise’; Johnson — ‘moveables in a house; wares, freight, merchandise’; Webster — ‘wares; merchandise; commodities bought and sold by merchants and traders’.
  3. Authoritative: Union of India v. Delhi Cloth & General Mills Co. Ltd. (AIR 1963 SC 791) — “to become ‘goods’ an article must be something which can ordinarily come to the markets to be bought and sold”; CCE v. Eastend Paper Industries Ltd. ((1989) 4 SCC 244) — goods must be “identifiable articles known in the markets as goods and marketed and marketable in the market as such”.
  4. Statutory scheme: under the BCI Rules, Rules 28 and 29 give a right of deduction only against money in the advocate's hands — “no lien is provided on the litigation files kept with him”. Any such lien “would become susceptible to great abuses and exploitation” in “the conditions prevailing in India with lots of illiterate people among the litigant public”.
  5. Constitutional and procedural: the cause is “far more important for all concerned than the right of the legal practitioner for his remuneration”. Order 3 Rule 4(1) CPC (appointment in force until determined with the leave of the court); Article 22(1) of the Constitution — the right to be defended by a practitioner of his choice, “absolute in itself”, per State of M.P. v. Shobharam (AIR 1966 SC 1910). The words “of his choice” “cannot be whittled down by that advocate by withholding the case bundle”.
  6. Professional standards: In re 'G', a Senior Advocate (AIR 1954 SC 557) and In re M, an Advocate (AIR 1957 SC 149) — the Court deals “not with ordinary legal rights, but with the special and rigid rules of professional conduct expected of and applied to a specially privileged class of persons”.

Sethi J., concurring, added the historical reason: at common law the lawyer's fee was “a gratuity or an honorarium which the client bestowed on him in token of his gratitude”; lawyers were “officers of the court”. Even under English common law, “no lien can be claimed with respect to the case file and such documents which are necessary for the further progress of the lis filed in the court” — and the right of retention had been “much diluted by various exceptions created by decisions, chiefly by the courts of equity”.

Principles evolved
  1. Marketability is an additional requirement of ‘goods’. Moveability alone is not enough. To be ‘goods’ within s. 2(7) SOGA — and therefore within s. 171 ICA — a thing must be capable of ordinarily coming to the market to be bought and sold. This is now the standard fourth test and belongs in every ‘concept of goods’ answer.
  2. s. 171 ICA and s. 2(7) SOGA must be read together. Because Chapter VII of the Contract Act (ss. 76–123) was repealed by the SOGA, the meaning of ‘goods’ in the surviving provisions of the ICA is supplied by s. 2(7).
  3. Bailment requires goods (s. 148). Where there is no bailment there can be no lien under s. 171 — the two stand or fall together.
  4. No professional lien over a client's file in India. The advocate's remedy for unpaid fees is a suit, and the Rules 28–29 right of deduction out of client money — never retention of the brief.
  5. Constitutional overlay. Retention of files would impair Article 22(1) (criminal cases) and the right to change counsel recognised by O. 3 r. 4(1) CPC (civil cases). The litigant's right to the return of the file is “the corresponding counterpart of the professional duty of the advocate”.
  6. A duty higher than contract. Returning the brief on being discharged is “not only a legal duty but a moral imperative”.
★ DU-usage note

This is the case to cite whenever the question is “is X goods?” and X is moveable but unsaleable — human organs, a corpse, personal correspondence, a lawyer's file. Always give the two-step: (i) s. 2(7) requires every kind of moveable property other than actionable claims and money; (ii) plus the marketability gloss from Delhi Cloth Mills, Eastend Paper and R.D. Saxena. Students who stop at step (i) lose two marks. Also note the neat statutory bridge: the Court reached s. 2(7) through s. 3 of the SOGA's logic and the repeal of ICA ss. 76–123 — a good way to show you understand why s. 3 exists.

20. Commissioner of Sales Tax, M.P. v. M.P. Electricity Board, Jabalpur
(1969) 1 SCC 200 · AIR 1970 SC 732 · Supreme Court · A.N. Grover J. · DU Reader p. 115
Electricity is ‘goods’ and the Board is a ‘dealer’ — but steam supplied at actual cost is a works contract, not a sale.
Facts

The Madhya Pradesh Electricity Board generated, distributed and supplied electrical energy throughout the State. It also supplied steam to the Nepa Mills under a working arrangement: the Mills supplied water free of cost to the Board, and in return the Board supplied steam at a rate based solely on the coal consumed in producing it, with the Mills agreeing to reimburse the Board for the loss sustained on account of not taking their “full demand of steam”. The arrangement was reduced to writing but never properly executed as a contract.

The Board was assessed to sales tax. The question referred to the High Court (and from there to the Supreme Court) was framed in three questions, of which two were argued:

“(1) … whether or not the Madhya Pradesh Electricity Board is a dealer within the meaning of Section 2(c) of the C.P. and Berar Sales Tax Act and Section 2(d) of the Madhya Pradesh General Sales Tax Act, 1958, in respect of its activity of generation, distribution, sale and supply of electrical energy?

(3) … whether or not steam is saleable goods and if they are saleable goods is the turnover representing the supply thereof liable to be assessed to sales tax in the hands of the assessee?”

The High Court had held against the Revenue on electricity, going “into a discussion from the point of view of mechanics relating to transmission of electric energy” and concluding that electricity “could not be regarded as an article or matter which could be possessed or moved or delivered”.

Issues
  1. Is electrical energy ‘goods’ — so that the Electricity Board is a ‘dealer’ carrying on the business of buying, selling, supplying or distributing goods?
  2. Is steam supplied to the Nepa Mills sold as goods — or was the arrangement in substance a contract for labour and work?
Arguments
For the Revenue (Mr I.N. Shroff)
  • The statutory definitions are deliberately wide: ‘goods’ means “all kinds of movable property” other than actionable claims, and “includes all material articles and commodities whether or not to be used in the construction, fitting out, improvement or repair of immovable property”. Electricity is not among the excluded items.
  • ‘Movable property’ is defined in s. 2(24) of the M.P. General Clauses Act as “property of every description, except immovable property” — and s. 2(18) defines immovable property as land, benefits arising out of land, and things attached to the earth. Electricity is none of these.
  • Electricity has all the attributes of movable property: it “can be transmitted, transferred, delivered, stored, possessed etc., in the same way as any other movable property”.
  • Parliament itself treats it as property: it is “capable of abstraction, consumption and use which, if done dishonestly, would attract punishment under Section 39 of the Indian Electricity Act, 1910”. You cannot steal what is not property.
  • Precedent: Kumbakonam Electric Supply Corpn. Ltd. (14 STC 600, Madras) — goods “must be property and it must be movable”; the transfer “need not necessarily be in a tangible or a physical sense”. Malerkotla Power Supply Co. (22 STC 325, P&H, Tek Chand J.) — electric energy “has the commonly accepted attributes of movable property. It can be stored and transmitted. It is also capable of theft.” County of Durham Electrical Power & Distribution Co. v. Inland Revenue ((1909) 2 KB 604), cited in Benjamin on Sale, 8th Ed., p. 171 — electric energy “assumed to be ‘goods’”. Erie County Natural Gas & Fuel Co. (1911 AC 105) for gas.
  • The schedules to the two Acts specifically exempt the sale of electrical energy. “If that had not been the case there was no necessity of specifically exempting sale of electric energy from the payment of sales tax” — an exemption presupposes that the item would otherwise be taxable.
  • On steam: the Board carried on the business of selling steam to the Nepa Mills “for a number of years”; “simply because the Electricity Board does not have any profit motive in supplying steam, it cannot escape payment of sales tax because the steam is nevertheless being sold as ‘goods’.”
For the Electricity Board (assessee)
  • Electricity cannot be possessed, moved or delivered in the ordinary sense. It is not “an article or matter” — the High Court accepted this.
  • The constitutional entries point the other way. Entry 53 of List II is “Taxes on the consumption or sale of electricity”; Entry 54 is “Taxes on the sale or purchase of goods other than newspapers”. If electricity were ‘goods’, Entry 53 would be redundant.
  • Rash Behari v. Emperor (AIR 1936 Cal 753) — a Calcutta decision on the scope of s. 39 of the Electricity Act, 1910, relied on to dispute the property characterisation.
  • Benjamin's commentary noted it was “doubtful whether that Act was applicable to such ‘goods’ as gas, water and electricity”.
  • On steam: the water was supplied free by the Mills and became the Board's property; the Board charged only a pro rata conversion charge based on coal consumed, plus reimbursement for short offtake. There was “no contract for the sale of steam as such” — only “for the labour and cost involved in its supply”.
Decision

(1) Electricity is goods; the Board is a dealer. The High Court's answer to question 1 was discharged and the appeals allowed to that extent.

“The term ‘movable property’ when considered with reference to ‘goods’ as defined for the purposes of sales tax cannot be taken in a narrow sense and merely because electric energy is not tangible or cannot be moved or touched like, for instance, a piece of wood or a book it cannot cease to be movable property when it has all the attributes of such property.” M.P. Electricity Board, para 9

The Court disposed of the Entry 53 / Entry 54 argument shortly: “It is difficult to derive much assistance from the aforesaid entries.” What matters is whether electric energy is ‘goods’ within the relevant provisions of the two Acts — and “the definition in terms is very wide”. It followed Kumbakonam and Malerkotla, and distinguished Rash Behari v. Emperor as “altogether different and distinguishable” — a decision about the scope and ambit of s. 39 of the Electricity Act, 1910, not about sales tax.

(2) Steam: not a sale. “On the findings of the tribunal and the High Court, we are of the opinion that the arrangement relating to supply of steam in return for the water supplied by the mills on payment of actual cost was not one of sale but was more in the nature of a works contract.”

Principles evolved
  1. Tangibility is not required. A thing may be ‘moveable property’ — and therefore ‘goods’ — although it cannot be touched. The test is attributes: can it be transmitted, transferred, delivered, stored, possessed, consumed — and stolen?
  2. The ‘capable of theft’ test. Parliament's own provision in s. 39 of the Electricity Act, 1910 (dishonest abstraction of energy) is decisive evidence that electricity is regarded as property. This is the single most quotable argument in the case.
  3. Exemption presupposes taxability. Where a schedule to a taxing statute exempts an item, the inference is that it would otherwise fall within the charge.
  4. Profit motive is not an element of sale. Mr Shroff's argument to that effect was not accepted — indeed the Court decided the steam point against him on a different ground altogether.
  5. Cost-plus supply is a works contract. Where A supplies the raw material free, and B converts it and charges only the actual cost of conversion, the substance is labour and work, not a sale of the finished product. This is a classic ‘materials supplied by the customer’ case, closely analogous to Hindustan Aeronautics.
  6. Statutory definitions prevail over dictionary and constitutional-entries reasoning. The Court refused to let Entries 53 and 54 of List II control the construction of a ‘goods’ definition in a State sales-tax Act.
▸ Real-life illustration

A prepaid electricity meter. You pay Rs 2,000, and 200 units are credited to your meter. Under this decision, each unit is goods, the discom is a dealer, and the credit is a sale the moment the property in the energy passes to you through the meter. When your neighbour taps your line, he commits theft under s. 39 of the Electricity Act, 1910 — and the reason he can ‘steal’ it at all is the reason it is ‘goods’. Contrast the steam arrangement: because Nepa Mills supplied the water free and paid only for the coal, the Board was doing work on someone else's material — the classic works contract.

§ Theme (b) — ‘Sale’ and ‘Agreement to sell’

One case, and it is the most important case in this entire Topic. Gannon Dunkerley is cited in Vishnu Agencies, Coffee Board, Sentinel, Larsen & Toubro and Pro Lab — five of the eleven. Master it and four other cases become easier.

21. State of Madras v. Gannon Dunkerley & Co. (Madras) Ltd.
1959 SCR 379 · AIR 1958 SC 560 · Supreme Court · Venkatarama Aiyar J. · DU Reader p. 119
‘Sale of goods’ is a nomen juris. A building contract that is one and indivisible contains no sale of the materials.
Facts

The respondents were a firm of building contractors. For the assessment year 1949–50 they were assessed to sales tax under the Madras General Sales Tax Act, 1939, as amended by Madras Act 25 of 1947. Two sums were in dispute:

Rs 29,51,528-7-4 the value of the materials used in works contracts, computed under Rule 4(3) Rs 1,98,929-0-3 the price of foodgrains supplied to workmen

The amendment of 1947 had deliberately enlarged the statutory definition. Before it, ‘sale’ meant a transfer of property in goods for a price. After it:

  • s. 2(h) defined ‘sale’ to include “a transfer of property in goods involved in the execution of a works contract”;
  • s. 2(i) carried an Explanation that defined ‘works contract’ for the purposes of the Act; and
  • Rule 4(3) provided the artificial method of computation: from the lump-sum price payable under the works contract, deduct a prescribed percentage on account of labour charges, and treat the balance as the price of the materials.

The contractors challenged the levy. The Madras High Court decided partly in their favour; both sides appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. What is the true meaning of the expression ‘sale of goods’ in Entry 48, List II, Schedule VII to the Government of India Act, 1935?
  2. Can a works contract which is one, entire and indivisible be taxed by splitting it into a sale of materials and a contract for labour — either under the general law or by a statutory definition?
  3. Was the Madras amendment (and Rule 4(3) made under it) within the legislative competence of the Province?
Arguments
For the State of Madras
  • The Constitution Act must be read in its own context. The Government of India Act, 1935 was a new constitutional instrument for a vast sub-continent; the expression ‘sale of goods’ in Entry 48 should not be confined to the narrow English commercial-law sense.
  • Legislative practice. Both in England and in India, the phrase had long been used in taxing statutes in a wider sense, including transactions in which the goods pass otherwise than by a straightforward transfer.
  • The legal meaning is wider. ‘Sale’ in law is not confined to the SOGA definition; it is wide enough to embrace the transfer of property in materials brought about by the execution of works.
  • The Constitution is a flexible instrument and must be construed so as to support, not strait-jacket, a legislature trying to tax a real economic activity.
  • Rule 4(3) is only a machinery provision. The State must be able to measure the goods element somehow; deducting the labour percentage is a reasonable and workable method.
For the assessee (Gannon Dunkerley & Co.)
  • ‘Sale of goods’ is a term of art — a nomen juris. At the time the Government of India Act was enacted, it was “a term of well-recognised legal import in the general law relating to sale of goods” and must receive that meaning.
  • Two ingredients are required, and both are absent: (i) an agreement between the parties for the sale of the very movables for a price, and (ii) the property passing therein pursuant to that agreement.
  • A building contract is one and indivisible. There is no agreement to sell bricks and cement as such; the house-owner wants a house. And the property does not pass as movables — it passes by accretion.
  • The State cannot split what the parties have united. The legislature may tax a sale; it may not create one by disintegrating a contract the parties made as a single whole.
  • Quantum meruit is not price. Where the builder has done part of the work and is prevented from completing, his remedy is quantum meruit (Appleby v. Myres) — restitution under s. 65 of the Indian Contract Act, not an action for the price of materials.
  • The rule of legislative practice points the other way: STO v. Budh Prakash Jai Prakash had already held that the SOGA meaning was the one adopted in Indian sales-tax legislation.
Decision — per Venkatarama Aiyar J.

The levy was held bad. The expression ‘sale of goods’ in Entry 48 List II is a nomen juris — “the name of a particular consensual contract” — and bears the meaning it has in the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. For there to be a sale of goods two ingredients must concur:

“(1) An agreement to sell movables for a price, and (2) property passing therein pursuant to that agreement. … A building contract is one, entire and indivisible … there is no agreement to sell the materials as such, and the property in them does not pass as movables but by accretion.” Gannon Dunkerley, the ratio as restated in every later case

The accretion principle. The maxim quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit — whatever is affixed to the soil becomes part of it — means that as the building rises the materials cease to be movables and become part of the immovable. Nothing is ‘transferred’ to the owner by agreement; it simply accrues to him because it is on his land.

The indivisibility point. Because the contract is one and indivisible, the State cannot dissect it. The 1947 amendment and Rule 4(3) therefore went beyond Entry 48 and were ultra vires the Province.

The four State contentions were each rejected — the GOIA-context argument, the legislative-practice argument, the wider-legal-meaning argument, and the flexible-Constitution argument.

The authorities relied on show the depth of the reasoning: Benjamin on Sale (the four elements of a valid sale, from the 2nd edition of 1873, reproduced in the 8th edition of 1950); Blackstone; Halsbury; Chalmer's Sale of Goods, 12th Ed.; the Roman law of emptio venditio; and STO v. Budh Prakash Jai Prakash on legislative practice.

On Rule 4(3): the Court differed from Pandit Banarsi Das ((1955) 6 STC 93) on the artificial computation, holding that a rule which deems a proportion of a lump sum to be the price of materials cannot manufacture a sale that the general law does not recognise.

The saving the Court expressly preserved:

“It is open to the parties to enter into distinct and separate contracts, one for the transfer of materials for a price and the other for the payment of remuneration for services and for work done. In such a case there are really two agreements, though there may be a single instrument embodying them, and the power of the State to separate the agreement to sell from the agreement to do work and to impose a tax thereon cannot be questioned, and will stand untouched by the present judgment.”

And the Court acknowledged the consequence with complete candour: the Province lacked competence, and “the Legislature alone can, if it so decides, enact a law amending the definition of ‘sale’ in the Act so as to bring within it transactions in which property in goods passes otherwise than by transfer inter vivos” — which is exactly what Parliament did, thirty years later, by inserting Art. 366(29-A).

Principles evolved
  1. Nomen juris. ‘Sale of goods’ in a constitutional entry is a term of art carrying the SOGA meaning. A legislature cannot enlarge it by ordinary legislation.
  2. The two-ingredient test. Agreement to sell the very movables for a price plus property passing pursuant to it. Both must be present.
  3. Accretion, not transfer. Materials incorporated into land pass by quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit, which is not a transfer of movables.
  4. Indivisibility is a shield. The State may not split a single contract in order to tax one of its elements.
  5. Quantum meruit is not price. The builder's restitutionary remedy under s. 65 ICA is conceptually different from an action for the price of goods — a further demonstration that no sale has occurred.
  6. Severability is preserved. Two agreements in one instrument are severable, and the sale element is taxable. This is the seed of the whole post-1983 ‘divisible by legal fiction’ doctrine.
  7. The Amendment was the legislative answer. Gannon Dunkerley is therefore pre-1983 law; it still governs assessment years before 2 February 1983.

§ Theme (c) — Statutory Transactions

Two cases, seven years apart, both answering the same question — does compulsion destroy consent? — and both answering no. Read them as a pair: Vishnu Agencies lays down the principle and overrules New India Sugar Mills; Coffee Board applies the principle to a far more extreme form of compulsion.

22. Vishnu Agencies (P) Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer
(1978) 1 SCC 520 · AIR 1978 SC 449 · Supreme Court · Seven-Judge Bench · Y.V. Chandrachud J. (as he then was) · DU Reader p. 131
A ‘compulsory sale’ is still a sale. New India Sugar Mills overruled.
Facts

The appeals were placed before a seven-Judge Bench “in order to set at rest, to the extent foreseeable, the controversy whether what is conveniently, though somewhat loosely, called a ‘compulsory sale’ is exigible to sales tax.”

The West Bengal batch. M/s Vishnu Agencies (Pvt.) Ltd. carried on business as agent and distributor of cement in West Bengal and was a registered dealer under the Bengal Finance (Sales Tax) Act, 1941. Cement was a controlled commodity: distribution was regulated by the West Bengal Cement Control Act, 1948 (Act 26 of 1948) and the Cement Control Order, 1948 made under s. 3(2). The appellant was a licensed stockist, permitted to stock cement in its godown and to supply it “to persons in whose favour allotment orders are issued, at the price stipulated”. A permit was generally valid for 15 days; as soon as the price of the cement allotted was deposited with the dealer, he was bound to deliver the specified quantity at the specified price.

The relevant provisions of the Control Order were: para 1 (no sale or storage for sale without a licence); para 2 (no disposal except in accordance with a written order of the Director of Consumer Goods); para 3 (no acquisition except under such an order); para 4 (no sale at a price higher than the notified price); para 8 (no refusal to sell to the holder of an order “at a price not exceeding the notified price”, and delivery “within a reasonable time after the payment of price”); and para 8A (weighment in the buyer's presence on request). Contravention was punishable under s. 6 with up to three years' imprisonment or fine or both, with forfeiture of the property.

The appellant supplied cement to allottees, was assessed to sales tax by the Commercial Tax Officer, Sealdah Charge, and paid. Then, on reading New India Sugar Mills Ltd. v. Commr. of Sales Tax (AIR 1963 SC 1207), it discovered that the transactions were said not to be exigible. It filed appeals claiming the payment had been made under a mistake of law, and then a writ petition in the Calcutta High Court — by which time arrears of tax “had already exceeded a sum of rupees eight lacs”.

The Andhra Pradesh batch. Under the A.P. Paddy Procurement (Levy) Orders and the A.P. Procurement (Levy and Restriction on Sale) Order, 1957, paddy-growers could sell only to licensed procuring agents appointed by the State and only at State-fixed prices. The agriculturist could choose his procuring agent, but “he cannot sell paddy to a private purchaser”. Procuring agents had to supply paddy to rice-millers at controlled prices; millers had to declare stocks to the Civil Supplies Department and supply fixed quantities of rice to wholesalers or retailers at prices fixed by the Department; “no miller or other person who gets his paddy milled in any rice-mill can move or otherwise dispose of the rice recovered by milling … except in accordance with the directions of the Collector.” Breach was punishable under s. 7 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955, with forfeiture under s. 6A. Purchase tax was levied on the procuring agents, sales tax on the millers and retailers.

Issues
  1. Must the word ‘sale’ in a Provincial or State Sales Tax Act receive the same meaning as in the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — or else be beyond the legislative competence of the Province/State?
  2. Since under the SOGA there can be no sale without a contract of sale, and since the parties here “had no volition of their own but were compelled by law to supply and receive the goods at prices fixed under the Control Orders”, are the transactions sales at all?
Arguments
For the Revenue (States of W.B. and A.P.)
  • On the face of the definitions, these are sales. s. 2(g) of the Bengal Act defines ‘sale’ as “any transfer of property in goods for cash or deferred payment or other valuable consideration”; s. 2(n) of the A.P. Act defines it as “every transfer of the property in goods by one person to another in the course of trade or commerce, for cash, or for deferred payment or for any other valuable consideration”. “Indisputably, in one case the property in cement and in the other, property in paddy and rice was transferred for cash consideration” — “that is over-simplification”, but it is where the analysis must start.
  • Volition is antecedent, not contemporaneous. “It is not obligatory on a trader to deal in cement nor on any one to acquire it. The primary fact, therefore, is that the decision of the trader to deal in an essential commodity is volitional.”
  • Consent is found in conduct. When the allottee presents his permit, “he signifies his willingness to obtain the commodity from the dealer on the terms stated in the permit. His conduct reflects his consent.” When the dealer acts on the permit, “he impliedly agrees to supply … His conduct too reflects his consent.”
  • Bargaining space is reduced, not eliminated. Para 4 fixes only a maximum; para 8 requires delivery “within a reasonable time”, which the parties can agree; para 8A gives a right of weighment; the buyer can reject for short weight or bad quality. “The circumstance that in these areas, though minimal, the parties to the transactions have the freedom to bargain militates against the view that the transactions are not consensual.”
  • The concept of freedom of contract has changed. Per Hegde J. in Indian Steel and Wire Products: it “has undergone a great deal of change even in those countries where it was considered as one of the basic economic requirements of a democratic life”.
  • Benjamin is out of date. The General Editor of the 1974 edition said the editors had to produce an entirely new work because “the extent of legislative and governmental regulation and intervention” had changed so much since 1868. “The formulations in Benjamin's second edition … evidently require modification in the light of regulatory measures of social control.”
For the assessees
  • Two-fold contention. (i) ‘Sale’ in a Sales Tax Act must receive the SOGA meaning, or the definition is beyond legislative competence; (ii) since under the SOGA there can be no sale without a contract of sale, and since “no volition or bargaining power was left” and “there was no element of mutual consent or agreement” between dealer and allottee, these are not sales properly so called and are not exigible.
  • Reliance on New India Sugar Mills (AIR 1963 SC 1207), which had held that a statutory levy of sugarcane was not a sale.
  • Reliance on Gannon Dunkerley and Benjamin on Sale, 8th Ed. (1950): to constitute a valid sale there must be a concurrence of four elements, one of which is “mutual assent”.
  • Reliance on Kirkness v. John Hudson & Co. (1955 AC 696) — Lord Reid's observation that ‘sale’ is a nomen juris, the name of a particular consensual contract — and on the compulsory-acquisition class of cases (Kirkness patent, Chhitter Mal inferred).
  • Reliance on Cement Distributors Pvt. Ltd.
Decision — per Chandrachud J., for the Court

The appeals were dismissed. The transactions were held to be sales.

“Though both parties are bound to comply with the legal requirements governing the transaction, they agree as between themselves to enter into the transaction on statutory terms, one agreeing to supply the commodity to the other on those terms and the other agreeing to accept it from him on the very terms. It is therefore not correct to say that the transactions between the appellant and the allottees are not consensual. They, with their free consent, agreed to enter into the transactions.” Vishnu Agencies, para 24

The Court's reasoning ran on four planes:

  1. Antecedent volition. Nobody is compelled to trade in cement or to buy it. The choice to enter the trade carries with it “the willingness to trade in the commodity strictly on the terms of the Control Orders”. The same is true of the consumer, who “is under no legal compulsion to acquire or possess cement”.
  2. Consent by conduct. Offer and acceptance “need not be in an elementary form” (Hidayatullah J.'s minority judgment in New India Sugar Mills, at p. 510). The permit-holder's presentation of the permit and the dealer's acting on it are, respectively, offer and acceptance.
  3. Residual bargaining space. The notified price is a ceiling, not a fixed price; delivery time, weighment, rejection for short weight or bad quality all remain open.
  4. History. Chapter VII of the ICA, 1872 (ss. 76–123) was repealed and replaced by the SOGA 1930. But the ICA “drew inspiration from the English law of contract … whose growth is marked by features which are peculiar to the social and economic history of England” and which “is largely founded upon the action on the case for assumpsit, where the essence of the matter was the undertaking”. That history “led the earlier writers on legal theories to lay particular emphasis on the consensual nature of contractual obligations” — an emphasis that needs modification in the age of regulatory control.

What was overruled. The Court was explicit and unapologetic:

“With great deference, the majority decision in New India Sugar Mills is not good law. The true legal position is as is stated in the minority judgment in that case and in Indian Steel and Wire Products, Andhra Sugars, Salar Jung Sugar Mills and Oil and Natural Gas Commission. To the extent to which Cement Distributors Pvt. Ltd. is inconsistent with these judgments, it is also, with respect, not good law.” Vishnu Agencies, para 43

Gannon Dunkerley was confined to its facts, not followed: “Gannon Dunkerley involved an altogether different point and is not an authority for the proposition that there cannot at all be a contract of sale if the parties to a transaction are obliged to comply with the terms of a statute.” And Kirkness was put in its box: Lord Reid's observations “have … to be understood in the context in which they were made, namely, that compulsory acquisition cannot amount to sale”. Note also Ridge Nominees Ltd. (Court of Appeal), where the argument that mutual assent was lacking was rejected: the dissentient shareholder “was overridden by an assent which the statute imposed on him, fictional though it may be”.

Principles evolved
  1. Compulsion of terms is not compulsion to contract. A law that regulates how a trade is carried on does not destroy the consensual core of the individual transaction.
  2. Antecedent volition supplies consent. The choice to enter a regulated trade is itself the exercise of free will; everything after that is adherence to terms voluntarily accepted.
  3. Consent may be implied from conduct — s. 5(2) SOGA, third limb. Presentation of a permit and action on it are offer and acceptance.
  4. Minimal bargaining space is enough. A price ceiling, an open delivery date, a right of weighment and a right to reject are each sufficient to show that the transaction is not a mere execution of an order.
  5. The line preserved: compulsory acquisition. Cases of ‘compulsory acquisition’, “where such acquisition is patent as in Kirkness or is inferred as in Chhitter Mal, fall in a separate and distinct class”. An order directed to a particular party to hand over specified goods for compensation is not a sale; a general law regulating a class does not prevent a sale.
  6. Benjamin's four elements are not exhaustive and require “modification in the light of regulatory measures of social control”.
  7. Statutory definitions may be wider than the SOGA without becoming unconstitutional — the first contention of the assessees was thus answered against them.
★ DU-usage note

This is the direct answer to 2015 Commercial Transactions (OC) Q1(b): “When goods are supplied under a statutory compulsion whether this results in sale or not and consent of the supplier is material or not? Discuss with case law to support your answer.” Answer in five moves: (i) state the question and the two rival views; (ii) New India Sugar Mills (1963) — no sale; (iii) Vishnu Agencies (1978, 7-Judge) — sale, and New India Sugar Mills overruled; (iv) the four reasons (antecedent volition, conduct, residual bargaining, changed conception of freedom of contract); (v) the limit — compulsory acquisition per Kirkness / Chhitter Mal is a separate class. Add Andhra Sugars, Indian Steel & Wire Products, Salar Jung and ONGC as the line approved. Do not say “consent is irrelevant” — the Court said consent was present, not unnecessary.

23. Coffee Board, Karnataka v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes
AIR 1988 SC 1487 · Supreme Court · Sabyasachi Mukharji J. · DU Reader p. 139
Compulsory delivery of coffee to the Board's pool is a sale, not compulsory acquisition — the Board is a dealer, not a trustee, and the pool payment is price, not compensation.
Facts

The Coffee Board is a statutory body constituted under the Coffee Act, 1942 (the Indian Coffee Market Expansion Act, 1942). Its object was to control the development of the coffee industry and to regulate the export and sale of coffee — “pooling legislation” of a kind familiar in the United States, Britain and Australia, “based on the fundamental idea that the collectivist economy is superior to individualistic economy”.

The scheme: s. 25(1) required every registered owner to deliver his coffee to the Board's surplus pool; s. 25(2) empowered the Board to reject coffee offered for delivery; s. 26(2) empowered it to purchase additional coffee; s. 34 governed the pool payments to growers. On delivery, “all their rights therein were extinguished and the coffee vested exclusively in the Board”.

The Board was a registered dealer under the Sales Tax Acts of every State in which it held auctions or ran depots, and under the Central Sales Tax Act. It collected and remitted sales tax on all coffee sold for domestic consumption. But it resisted purchase tax on the growers' delivery to it.

The litigation record: writ petitions filed in the Karnataka High Court in 1982; interim stay granted; on 2 February 1983 the Constitution (Forty-sixth Amendment) Act, 1982 came into force inserting Art. 366(29-A) (prospectively); assessment orders followed for 1975-76 (Rs 3.5 crores), 1976-77 (taxable turnover Rs 92.99 crores; tax Rs 10.18 crores, of which Rs 8.06 crores was purchase tax), 1977-78 (Rs 8.08 crores), 1978-79, 1979-80 and 1980-81 — each met with a fresh writ petition and a stay. From May 1984 the Board began collecting ‘contingency deposits’ from growers to cover purchase-tax liability for the period after 2-2-1983, withholding about Rs 6.8 crores from the pool payment for season 1982-83. The High Court dismissed the writ petitions by a common judgment dated 16 August 1985 (reported at ILR Karnataka Vol. 36, p. 1365). These appeals were brought by certificate.

Issues
  1. Was there a transfer of coffee from the growers to the Board — or an acquisition?
  2. Was there any element of sale involved?
  3. Was the Coffee Board a trustee or agent for the growers in respect of exports?
  4. If it is a sale, is it ‘in the course of export’, immune under Art. 286 of the Constitution?
Arguments
For the Revenue (States of Karnataka)
  • All four essential elements of sale are present: “(1) parties competent to contract, (2) mutual consent — though minimal, by growing coffee under the conditions imposed by the Act, (3) transfer of property in the goods and (4) payment of price though deferred.”
  • Bhavani Tea Produce Co. (AIR 1966 SC 677) had already held that when growers deliver coffee under s. 25 all their rights are extinguished and the coffee vests exclusively in the Board — there was “a ‘sale’ by operation of law”.
  • The Board is a dealer — registered as such in every State where it holds auctions or runs depots. The Madras High Court had so held in Indian Coffee Board, Batlagundu v. State of Madras: after goods enter the pool they “become the absolute property of the Board and the producer … has no right or claim to the goods except to a share in the sale proceeds”.
  • s. 26(2) is supplementary only — a “second avenue of purchase”, the first being s. 25(1). It does not imply that s. 25(1) is not a purchase.
  • The pool payment is price, not compensation. ss. 11 and 12 of the Coffee Act levy duties of customs and excise. “Levy of customs and excise duties on compensation was something unheard of, an incongruity and an anachronism, according to the High Court.”
  • On export: the Board did not purchase or take delivery of any specific coffee of any grower and export it under prior contracts. The purchases and exports were ‘for export’, not ‘in the course of export’.
For the Coffee Board (appellant)
  • Express statutory prohibition. s. 17 read with ss. 25 and 47 enacts “what since 1944 is a total prohibition against the sale of coffee by growers” and a corresponding implied prohibition on purchase by the Board. “Where a statute prohibited a registered owner from selling or contracting to sell coffee from any registered estate, there could be no implication of any purchase on the part of the Coffee Board.”
  • The legislature distinguished the two. It “has made a conscious difference between acquisition of coffee by compulsory delivery by the growers under s. 25(1) and purchase of coffee by the Board under s. 26(2)” — so s. 25(1) cannot be a sale.
  • It is compulsory acquisition — eminent domain. Citing Nichols on Eminent Domain (1950 edn.), para 1.11: “the power of the sovereign to take property for public use without the owner's consent”, whose irreducible terms are (a) power to take, (b) without the owner's consent, (c) for the public use.
  • No volition at all. “The cultivation of coffee can be carried on only in certain types of soil and in high elevations … Coffee is a perennial crop. The growers have no choice in growing coffee one year and then changing to a different crop in the following year. Coffee plants have a life ranging from 30 to 70 years … The coffee grower has thus no choice at all in continuing to be a coffee cultivator.” Not comparable to sugarcane, “the cultivation of which can be discontinued at will”.
  • The growers are agriculturists, not dealers. The demand for purchase tax is “in effect a demand on the growers who were exempt from such levy” — and “the object of the pool marketing system is not to deprive the growers of a fair compensation”.
  • Alternative: the Board is a trustee or agent of the growers.
  • Reliance on Peanut Board v. Rockhampton Harbour Board ((1932-33) 48 CLR 266).
Decision — per Sabyasachi Mukharji J.

All four questions answered against the appellant; the appeals were dismissed. “We are, however, unable to accept the submissions of the learned Additional Solicitor General.”

“All the four essential elements of sale — (1) parties competent to contract, (2) mutual consent — though minimal, by growing coffee under the conditions imposed by the Act, (3) transfer of property in the goods and (4) payment of price though deferred — are present in the transaction in question.” Coffee Board, para 27

On consent. “Coffee growers have a volition or option, though minimal or nominal, to enter into the coffee growing trade. Coffee growing was not compulsory. If anyone decides to grow coffee or continue to grow coffee, he must transact in terms of the regulation imposed for the benefit of the coffee growing industry.” The indicia of consensuality the Court listed:

  • s. 25(2) — the Board has the right to reject coffee not up to standard;
  • the value payable “is the price of the coffee”; “fixation of price is regulation but is a matter of dealing between the parties”;
  • no time is fixed for delivery of coffee either to the Board or to the curer;
  • s. 34(2) ensures periodical payments of price, and the Rules provide for advances to growers.

On ‘no choice at all’. The Court accepted the agronomy but not the conclusion: “Such practical difficulties, however, do not in essence make any difference.”

On the statutory prohibition argument. The scheme is “to provide for a single channel for sale of coffee grown in the registered estates”. The Act “directs the entire coffee produced … to be sold to the Coffee Board through the modality of compulsory delivery and imposes a corresponding obligation on the Coffee Board to compulsorily purchase”, subject to two exceptions: (1) coffee unfit for human consumption, and (2) estates so remote or so small that compulsory delivery would be “an arduous task and an uneconomical provision”.

On compensation vs. price. “The amount paid by the Board to the grower under the Act is the value or price of coffee in conformity with the detailed accounting done thereto under the Coffee Act … The amount paid to the grower was neither compensation nor dividend.” And the decisive confirmation: “The levy of duties of excise and customs under Sections 11 and 12 of the Coffee Act are inconsistent with the concept of compulsory acquisition.”

On trusteeship. “There is no trust created in the scheme of the Act in the Coffee Board: it is a statutory obligation imposed on the Coffee Board and does not make it a trustee in any event.”

On export. Following State of Tamil Nadu v. M.K. Kandaswami (AIR 1975 SC 1871) on s. 7-A of the Tamil Nadu Act (in pari materia with s. 6 of the Karnataka Act), the purchases and exports were “for export” and not “in the course of export”, and so did not earn the Article 286 exemption. The Board “did not purchase or take delivery of any specific coffee or goods of any grower and exported the same under prior contracts of sale”.

On the growers being agriculturists. “Section 6 of the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957 meets the situation created by such circumstances” — the Legislature had already provided for exactly this case.

Principles evolved
  1. Pooling is purchase, not acquisition. A statutory marketing scheme under which the grower must deliver his entire crop to a Board and receives a share of the pool proceeds is a sale — provided the four sale elements are present, even if consent is “minimal”.
  2. Minimal consent is sufficient consent. The volition to enter the trade carries the consent to its statutory terms. Volition “though minimal or nominal” defeats the eminent-domain argument.
  3. Price vs. compensation is the diagnostic test. If the payment were compensation for compulsory acquisition, the levy of customs and excise duties on it would be “unheard of, an incongruity and an anachronism”. That the statute levies them proves the transaction is a sale.
  4. A statutory marketing board is a dealer, not a trustee or agent. Once the goods enter the pool they become the absolute property of the Board; the grower retains only a share in the proceeds.
  5. ‘For export’ ≠ ‘in the course of export’. Article 286 protects only the latter. A sale followed by an export, under a prior or subsequent contract, is a local sale.
  6. A statutory prohibition on selling to others does not negative a sale to the Board — it creates the single channel that constitutes the sale.
  7. Vishnu Agencies applied, at the extreme. What was true of cement under a Control Order is true even of a crop the grower could not realistically stop growing.
▸ Real-life illustration

A Chikmagalur grower delivers his entire Arabica crop to the Coffee Board's pool on 15 December. He receives no payment that day; he receives periodic payments under s. 34(2) as the Board sells the pooled coffee through auctions in Karnataka, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, and through export auctions. The Board may reject his coffee if it is unfit for human consumption. It deducts the excise duty payable by him under s. 13(4) from his pool payment — the duty being a first charge. Each of these features told the Supreme Court the same thing: the grower sold his coffee; he was not expropriated. Contrast a State acquiring a farmer's land for a highway: there is no ‘seller’, no ‘price’, no duty of excise on the compensation — that is compulsory acquisition, the ‘separate and distinct class’ that Vishnu Agencies kept outside the rule.

★ DU-usage note

Use Coffee Board three ways. First, as the application of Vishnu Agencies to the hardest possible facts — quote the Court's own four-element checklist; it is the neatest statement of the test anywhere in the syllabus. Secondly, for the price vs. compensation argument, which is a genuinely creative piece of reasoning examiners reward. Thirdly, as the case that quotes Lord Denning M.R. in C.E.B. Draper & Sons Ltd. v. Edward Turner & Sons Ltd. ((1964) 3 All ER 148) — “the word ‘sale’ properly connotes the transfer of the absolute or general property in a thing for a price in money” — which is the authority behind the 2015 Q8(a) maxim “a sale is a contract plus conveyance”. Note also the constitutional dimension: this litigation is the clearest illustration in the syllabus of Art. 366(29-A) being prospective — the Board's liability, and its ‘contingency deposits’, began only from 2-2-1983.

§ Theme (d) — Contract for ‘Works’/‘Labour’, Part 1: the two pre-Amendment cases in the syllabus

Both decisions were delivered before the Forty-sixth Amendment altered the landscape, and both hold that there is no sale. But they reach that conclusion by different routes, and the difference matters. Hindustan Aeronautics turns on ownership of the materials; Sentinel Rolling Shutters turns on the non-existence of the unit until installation.

24. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes v. Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd.
(1972) 1 SCC 395 · AIR 1972 SC 744 · Supreme Court · S.M. Sikri C.J. · DU Reader p. 153
Where every component already belongs to the customer before it is used, there is nothing left to sell — a pure works contract.
Facts

The respondent, Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd. (formerly Hindustan Aircraft Ltd.), built railway coaches — models 407, 408 and 411 — for the Railway Board. The Ministry of Railways wrote on 3 February 1955 about the coaching programme for 1955-56; after discussions the Board placed orders, the terms of which are found in the Ministry's letter No. 57/142/RE(163) dated 4 May 1957.

The Commercial Tax Officer, by assessment order dated 28 March 1964 for assessment year 1958-59, included the turnover on the supply of these coaches. The assessee's contention that “there was no sale involved in the execution of the works contract” was rejected by the assessing officer, by the Deputy Commissioner in appeal, and by the Commissioner in revision. The assessee then appealed to the High Court of Mysore under s. 24(1) of the Mysore Sales Tax Act read with s. 9(3) of the Central Sales Tax Act.

The High Court called for a report on three questions: (i) whether and to what extent the assessee had drawn advance payment from the Railway Board in respect of material utilised; (ii) whether any material for which no advance had been drawn was utilised; and (iii) whether any material not specifically procured for the contracts was used. The Commercial Tax Officer reported:

“My findings revealed that as and when they purchased materials, they sent to the Railway Board ‘an invoice’ accompanied by a list of the details regarding the materials purchased. 90 per cent. of the value of these materials was then paid to the company after inspection of the materials by the Board's representative. Invoice No. 31009 of October 15, 1956, is obtained as a sample. This invoice shows that materials for the value of Rs 2,60,374-12-0 were purchased by the company for 407 model coaches … asking payment of Rs 2,34,517-4-0 being 90 per cent of the invoice amount. The amount of this invoice is included in the Board's remittance note No. 1290 of October 30, 1956, and a cheque was issued to the company for the total of several such invoices. The amount of the cheque received on October 30, 1956, was Rs 22,90,719-0-0.”

He also found that no material for which no advance had been drawn was used, and that all material was kept in a separately located shed with separate stock registers under code numbers, with no other work undertaken in that section. The High Court allowed the appeal and set aside the inclusion of the turnover.

Issue

Whether the delivery of railway coaches models 407, 408 and 411 to the Railway Board was liable to sales tax under the Central Sales Tax Act — i.e. whether there had been a sale of the coaches.

Arguments
For the Revenue (Commissioner of Commercial Taxes)
  • The order was for the “manufacture and supply” of coaches — the very words indicate a contract of sale.
  • 90 per cent of the value of the materials was paid for in advance on invoices raised by the company, “and when all the material used in the construction of a coach belongs to the Railways” a sale of the coach must be inferred — or so it was contended.
  • A finished coach is an identifiable chattel, delivered for a price; the property in it passed to the Railway on delivery.
  • The material and wage escalation clauses — “material and wage escalator and adjustments” — were said to be consistent with a sale.
For the assessee (Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd.)
  • It was a pure works contract. There was no sale of any ‘coach’ as a chattel.
  • The property in the materials was already in the President of India before they were used. This followed from para 1 of the Indemnity Bond: “the Hindustan Aircraft Ltd. hereby undertake to hold at their works at Bangalore for and on behalf of the President of the Union of India and as his property in trust for him the Stores and articles in respect of which advances are made to them”.
  • 90 per cent advance was paid against inspection of the materials — a financing arrangement, not a price.
  • For coaches 407 and 408, wheelsets and underframes were supplied free of cost by the Railways.
  • Reliance on State of Gujarat v. Kailash Engineering Co. (19 STC 13).
Decision — per Sikri C.J.

Appeal dismissed; no sale. The Court began with the controlling principle:

“The answer to the question whether it is a works contract or it is a contract of sale depends upon the construction of the terms of the contract in the light of the surrounding circumstances.”

Seven “salient features” were then identified:

  1. The Railway booked the capacity of the assessee for the construction of railway coaches.
  2. Advance on account was made to the extent of 90% of the value of the material on production of a certificate by the inspecting authority.
  3. The material used for the construction of coaches, before its use, is the property of the Railway. “This is quite clear from para 1 of the Indemnity Bond … No other meaning can be given to the words in the bond … It seems to us clear that the property in the materials which are used for the construction of the coaches becomes the property of the President before it is used.”
  4. There was “no possibility of any other material being used for the construction” — the Commercial Tax Officer's own report said so.
  5. For models 407 and 408, wheelsets and underframes were supplied free of cost.
  6. The order used the words “manufacture and supply”.
  7. “The material and wage escalator and adjustments which are mentioned in the contract are natural factors.”
“On these facts it seems to us that it is a pure works contract. We are unable to agree that when all the material used in the construction of a coach belongs to the Railways there can be any sale of the coach itself. The difference between the price of a coach and the cost of material can only be the cost of services rendered by the assessee.” Hindustan Aeronautics, para 13

The Court said the case was “more in line with the decision of this Court in State of Gujarat v. Kailash Engineering Co. [19 STC 13] than any other case”. As to model 411, the only difference was that wheelsets and underframes were not supplied free of cost — “This does not make any difference to the result.” Appeal dismissed with costs.

Principles evolved
  1. Whose material is it? The decisive question in a ‘manufacture-and-supply’ contract is whether the property in the materials was already in the customer before they were used. If it was, the contractor has nothing to sell: he supplies skill and labour.
  2. “Manufacture and supply” is not decisive. The words of the order do not control; the ownership of the inputs does.
  3. Advance payment against inspection is not price. A 90 per cent advance released against inspection of purchased material is financing, not consideration for a sale.
  4. Price minus cost of material = cost of services. A neat arithmetic test: if the only difference between the contract price and the cost of the (customer's) material is the value of the contractor's services, there is no sale of goods.
  5. Free supply of components reinforces the conclusion — but is not essential to it, as model 411 shows.
  6. Contrast with Sentinel: here the customer owned the inputs; there the manufacturer owned the inputs but the finished unit never existed as his property at all.
★ DU-usage note

HAL is the syllabus's ‘materials supplied by the customer’ case. It pairs naturally with the steam half of M.P. Electricity Board (Case 20), where the Mills supplied water free and paid only for the coal. Whenever a question turns on who owned the inputs, cite both. Also note that Larsen & Toubro later cited HAL (paras 85–86) for two propositions still good law even after the Amendment: (i) no property in the thing produced as a whole; and (ii) “mere passing of property in an article or commodity during the course of performance of the transaction in question does not render the transaction to be transaction of sale”.

25. Sentinel Rolling Shutters & Engg. Co. (P) Ltd. v. Commissioner of Sales Tax
(1978) 4 SCC 260 · AIR 1978 SC 545 · Supreme Court · P.N. Bhagwati J. · DU Reader p. 156
A rolling shutter does not exist until it is grouted and bolted to the building — so the manufacturer never owned the unit, and could never sell it.
Facts

The assessee was a private limited company carrying on business as “engineers, contractors, manufacturers and fabricators”. On 28 June 1972 it entered into a contract with M/s C.M. Shah & Co. (P) Ltd. for the “fabrication, supply, erection and installation” of Sentinel Pull-and-Push type and Reduction-Gear type rolling shutters in sheds Nos. 3 and 4 of the Sidheswar Sahakari Sakar Karkhana belonging to the company.

Detailed specifications were given, and the price was Rs 7 per sq. ft. for the Pull and Push type and Rs 9 per sq. ft. for the Reduction Gear type — “the price in both cases being inclusive of erection at site”.

The contract was on a printed form with certain special terms written out. The relevant printed terms were:

  • Clause 2: “Once the delivery of the goods is effected, rejection claims cannot be entertained.”
  • Clause 4: “All erection work shall be carried out at customer's own risk and no claim for incidents …”
  • Clause 12: 50 per cent of the amount payable as advance, “the balance against delivery of the goods ex-works”.

The special terms overrode clause 12: 25 per cent by way of advance, 65 per cent against delivery, and the remaining 10 per cent after completion of erection and handing over of the rolling shutters to the satisfaction of the company.

The assessee applied to the Commissioner of Sales Tax for determination. The Deputy Commissioner held it was a contract for sale and that erection was “merely incidental to the sale”, taxing 95 per cent of the amount receivable (5 per cent being attributed to work and labour). The Sales Tax Tribunal agreed it was a sale but held that since 90 per cent was payable at the stage of delivery, that should be the sale price and the balance 10 per cent “the charges for the work”. The reference question was:

“Whether having regard to the facts and circumstances of the case the Tribunal was justified in law in coming to the conclusion that the contract in question essentially consisted of two contracts, one for supply of materials for money consideration and the other for service and labour done?”

The High Court made “a detailed and exhaustive review of the decided cases” and answered the question in favour of the Revenue. The assessee appealed by special leave.

Issue

Was the contract a contract for sale of rolling shutters (with erection incidental) — i.e. a divisible contract consisting of two contracts — or a contract for work and labour?

Arguments
For the Revenue
  • Delivery is ex-works, and clause 2 says that once delivery is effected no rejection claim can be entertained. Therefore the property in the rolling shutters passed to the company as soon as they were delivered at the assessee's works — which is the hallmark of a sale.
  • 90 per cent of the price is payable before erection even begins (25 per cent advance + 65 per cent against delivery) — showing that what is being paid for is the shutters, not the erection.
  • The assessee is described in the contract as a “manufacturer”, and the price is quoted per square foot of shutter.
  • Erection is at the customer's own risk (clause 4) — indicating that the goods have already become the customer's property.
For the assessee
  • The contract is “for fabrication, supply, erection and installation” — four verbs, one price, “inclusive of erection at site”. “The contract does not recognise any dichotomy between fabrication and supply of the rolling shutters and their erection and installation so far as the price is concerned.”
  • A rolling shutter does not exist until it is fixed. It consists of five component parts — two brackets welded with ‘U’ type clamps; a pipe shafting with high-tension springs; a shutter screen; side guides or guide channels welded with iron clamps, with locking arrangements and welded handles; and a top cover. These are fabricated, taken to site and fixed. “It is only when the component parts are fixed on the premises and fitted into one another that they constitute a rolling shutter as a commercial article and till then they are merely component parts.”
  • At no stage does the manufacturer become the owner of the rolling shutter as a unit — so there is no property in it that he could transfer.
  • The Indian Standards Specification confirms it: the curtain and bottom lock plate are interlocked and rolled in one piece, but guide channels, bracket plates, rollers “are despatched separately”, and “all the rolling shutters are erected by the manufacturer or his authorised representative”.
  • Reliance on Vanguard Rolling Shutters of Steel Works v. CST, U.P. ((1977) 2 SCC 250) and on State of Madras v. Richardson & Cruddas Ltd. ((1968) 21 STC 245) (mode of payment irrelevant).
Decision — per Bhagwati J.

Appeal allowed. It is a contract for work and labour.

The Court first laid down the three forms (see Figure 10), and then the primary test from Halsbury's Laws of England, 3rd Ed., Vol. 34, Art. 3, with the Court's own illustrations:

“A clear case of the former category would be a contract for supply of air-conditioner where the contract may provide that the supplier will fix up the air-conditioner in the premises … Such a contract would plainly be a contract for sale because the work of fixing up the air-conditioner would be incidental to the sale. Then take a contract for constructing a building where considerable quantity of materials is required to be used in the execution of the work. This would clearly be a contract for work and labour.”

The installation sequence was decisive. The Court set it out from Ex. C: masonry work by the customer at his own cost → brackets fixed by grouting holes in the masonry walls and inserting bolts → holes filled with cement → pipe shafting with springs inserted into the ‘U’ clamps → the iron curtain hoisted over the springs and tightened by nut bolts → guide channels fixed by grouting → clamps fixed and plastered → the curtain lowered through the guide channels. “The rolling shutter is then ‘born’ and it becomes a permanent fixture to the premises.”

“The erection and installation of the rolling shutter cannot, therefore, be said to be incidental to its manufacture and supply. It is a fundamental and integral part of the contract because without it the rolling shutter does not come into being. The manufacturer would undoubtedly be the owner of the component parts when he fabricates them, but at no stage does he become the owner of the rolling shutter as a unit so as to transfer the property in it to the customer. The rolling shutter comes into existence as a unit when the component parts are fixed in position on the premises and it becomes the property of the customer as soon as it comes into being.” Sentinel Rolling Shutters, para 6

The Revenue's three arguments were each rejected:

  1. Ex-works delivery. “What is obviously meant is that the component parts shall be delivered to the company at the works of the assessee and once they are delivered, they shall not be liable to be rejected.” But “the component parts do not constitute a rolling shutter and it is the obligation of the assessee … to fix the component parts in position on the premises”. The contract “is not completed until the assessee carries out this obligation”.
  2. Clause 12 (50/50 payment) is overridden by the special term (25/65/10), and in any case “the last portion of the special terms … makes it clear that it is only when the component parts are fitted into position in the premises that a rolling shutter would be complete”, and the remaining 10 per cent is payable only then.
  3. Mode of payment is irrelevant. “The true nature of the contract cannot depend on the mode of payment of the amount provided in the contract.” Citing Richardson & Cruddas, where the entire price was payable in advance before the work started and the contract was still held to be a works contract: “The payment of the amount due under the contract may be spread over the entire period of the execution of the contract with a view either to put the manufacturer or contractor in possession of funds for the execution of the contract or to secure him against any risk of non-payment by the customer. That cannot have any bearing on the determination of the question whether the contract is one for sale or for work and labour.”

The conclusion: “We must, in the circumstances, hold, driven by the compulsion of this logic, that the contract was a contract for work and labour and not a contract for sale.” The judgment in Vanguard Rolling Shutters — to which Bhagwati J. had been a party — “completely supported” the view.

Principles evolved
  1. The three forms of contract — divisible composite; work with materials accessory; sale with work incidental. This taxonomy is now standard and was adopted by Larsen & Toubro.
  2. The unit test. Ask: of what thing was the manufacturer owner at some time before delivery? If he owned only components and not the unit, he cannot have sold the unit.
  3. ‘Born on site’ articles. Where a chattel comes into existence only when fixed to the customer's premises and thereupon becomes a fixture, the transaction is work and labour, not sale.
  4. Mode of payment is irrelevant. Advance, stage payments or payment after completion — none of these determines the character of the contract.
  5. ‘Inclusive of erection’ indicates indivisibility. A single un-apportioned price for fabrication + supply + erection + installation points against severability.
  6. Ex-works delivery of components is not delivery of the goods contracted for where the contract is for a completed installation.
  7. No magic formula. “These tests … do not give any magic formula by the application of which we can say in every case whether a contract is a contract for sale or a contract for work and labour.” The question “basically and primarily … depends upon the main object of the parties gathered from the terms of the contract, the circumstances of the transaction and the custom of the trade”. “Thin partitions do their bounds divide.”
▸ Real-life illustration

Compare two purchases for a shop. (1) A split air-conditioner. You buy it at a store for Rs 42,000, and the dealer's technician installs it the next day. The unit existed as a complete, identifiable, saleable chattel on the dealer's shelf. Installation is incidental to the sale — this is Bhagwati J.'s own illustration, and it is a sale. (2) A rolling shutter. The fabricator brings brackets, a spring shaft, a curtain, guide channels and a top cover to your shopfront, grouts them into your masonry and bolts them together. At no moment did a ‘rolling shutter’ sit on his truck. The shutter was born on your wall and became your property the instant it existed. That is work and labour. The distinction is not ‘was work done?’ — it is ‘did the thing ever belong to the person who is said to have sold it?’

★ DU-usage note

Sentinel is the ideal answer to the 2012 CT OC Q3 quotation: “The question whether a particular contract is a contract for sale or for work and labour is always a difficult question. The difficulty lies not in the formulation of tests but in the application of these tests to the facts of the case.” Structure: (i) open with the three forms; (ii) state the primary Halsbury test with the air-conditioner / building contrast; (iii) add the Purshottam Premji subsidiary test; (iv) apply to the facts with the five-component anatomy; (v) rebut the ex-works and payment arguments; (vi) close with the “no magic formula” caveat. Also remember the case is pre-1983 — add a final paragraph on what Art. 366(29-A)(b) and L&T would now do to the same facts.

§ Theme (d) — Part 2: the restaurant cases, and the ‘dominant object’ refinement

Two very short judgments, paired in the syllabus. Northern India Caterers holds that serving a meal is not a sale; Udipikrishna Bhawan holds that this is not a rule of law but a question of fact, and that a bare finding that the assessee “runs a hotel” is not enough. Read together they are the clearest illustration in the syllabus of the dominant-object test — the very test that Larsen & Toubro later said “has no application” to transactions within Art. 366(29-A).

26. Northern India Caterers (India) Ltd. v. Lt. Governor of Delhi
(1978) 4 SCC 36 · judgment on review reported at (1980) 2 SCR 650 · Supreme Court · R.S. Pathak J. · DU Reader p. 163
The diner never becomes the owner of the food — “before consumption title does not pass; after consumption there remains nothing to become the subject of title.”
Facts

The appellant ran a hotel in Delhi in which lodging and meals were provided to residents on “inclusive terms”. Meals were also served to non-residents in the restaurant located in the hotel.

In assessment proceedings for 1957-58 and 1958-59 under the Bengal Finance (Sales Tax) Act, 1941 (as extended to the Union Territory of Delhi), the appellant contended that the service of meals to residents and non-residents “could not be regarded as a sale” and so was not exigible to sales tax. The Sales Tax authorities rejected the contention and “treated a portion of the receipts from the residents and non-residents as representing the price of the foodstuffs served”.

At the appellant's instance the High Court called for a statement of the case on two questions. The first — whether the supply of meals to residents paying a single all-inclusive charge was exigible — was answered in favour of the appellant. The second was the question in these appeals:

“Whether the service of meals to casual visitors in the Restaurant is taxable as a sale — (i) when the charges are lump sum per meal or (ii) when they are calculated per dish?”

The High Court answered in the affirmative. These appeals were brought by special leave.

Issue

Whether the service of meals by the appellant in its restaurant to non-residents constitutes a sale of foodstuffs within s. 2(g) of the Bengal Finance (Sales Tax) Act, 1941 — whether charged as a lump sum per meal or calculated per dish.

Arguments
For the Revenue / Lt. Governor of Delhi
  • Foodstuffs are plainly ‘goods’ within s. 2(7); they are served for money; therefore there is a transfer of property for a price.
  • Where the charge is calculated per dish, the diner is paying for each item — which looks exactly like a sale of each dish.
  • In Associated Hotels of India Ltd. this Court had drawn a distinction between meals supplied to a resident and meals served to a customer in a restaurant — so the exemption for residents does not extend to the restaurant.
  • The Revenue is entitled to split the transaction and treat that portion of the receipt which represents the price of the foodstuffs as taxable.
For the assessee (Northern India Caterers)
  • The origin and historical development of the institution determines its legal character. “This is a case where the origin and historical development of an institution has profoundly influenced the nature and incidents it possesses in law.” The hotelier's position is “assimilable to that of an innkeeper”.
  • An innkeeper does not sell. Crisp v. Pratt ((1939) 79 ER 1072): “innkeepers do not get their living by buying and selling, and … although they buy provisions to be spent in their house, they do not sell them but what they do is to ‘utter’ them. ‘Their gain’, it was added, ‘is not only by uttering of their commodities, but for the attendance of their servants, and for the furniture of their house, rooms, and lodgings, for their guests’.”
  • Newton v. Tries (91 ER 100), Holt C.J.: the innkeeper is an ‘hospitator’, “not paid upon the account of the intrinsic value of his provisions, but for other reasons: the recompence he receives, is for care and pains, and for protection and security … the end of an innkeeper in his buying, is not to sell, but only a part of the accommodation he is bound to prepare for his guests.”
  • The same principle was extended in England to eating-houses and restaurants, and “found acceptance on American soil” — Electa B. Merrill v. James W. Hodson (1915-B LRA 481).
  • If no sale where the guest is a resident, “a similar approach seems to be called for on principle in the case of restaurants. No reason has been shown to us for preferring any other.”
  • The Revenue may not “split up the transaction into two parts, one of service and the other of sale of foodstuffs” — which Associated Hotels had “declined to accept”.
Decision — per R.S. Pathak J.

Not taxable, on either basis.

“In the result, we hold that the service of meals to visitors in the restaurant of the appellant is not taxable under the Bengal Finance (Sales Tax) Act, 1941, as extended to the Union territory of Delhi, and this is so whether a charge is imposed for the meal as a whole or according to the dishes separately ordered.” Northern India Caterers, para 8

The Court's reasoning:

  1. The historical route. The hotelier is assimilated to an innkeeper (Halsbury, 3rd Ed., Vol. 21, p. 442, para 932: one who “received travellers and provided lodging and necessaries for them and their attendants and employed servants for this purpose and for the protection of travellers”). “It was hospitality that he offered, and the many facilities that constituted the components of that hospitality determined the legal character of the transactions flowing from them.”
  2. The American exposition, quoted at length. The passage from Electa B. Merrill v. James W. Hodson is the heart of the judgment:
“The essence of it is not an agreement for the transfer of the general property of the food or drink placed at the command of the customer … The customer does not become the owner of the food set before him, or of that portion which is carved for his use, or of that which finds a place upon his plate, or in side dishes set about it. No designated portion becomes his. He is privileged to eat, and that is all. The uneaten food is not his. He cannot do what he pleases with it. … The true essence of the transaction is service in the satisfaction of a human need or desire, — ministry to a bodily want. A necessary incident of this service or ministry is the consumption of the food required. This consumption involves destruction, and nothing remains of what is consumed to which the right of property can be said to attach. Before consumption title does not pass; after consumption there remains nothing to become the subject of title. What the customer pays for is a right to satisfy his appetite by the process of destruction.” Electa B. Merrill v. James W. Hodson (1915-B LRA 481), reproduced in Northern India Caterers

The Revenue's distinction was rejected. “It was urged for the respondent that in Associated Hotels of India Ltd. this Court drew a distinction between the case of meals supplied to a resident in a hotel and those served to a customer in a restaurant. We are unable to find any proposition of law laid down by the court there which could lead to that inference.”

The mode of charging is irrelevant. Whether the charge is a lump sum per meal or calculated per dish makes no difference.

An important admission in the judgment itself: “The position was radically altered in the United States by the enactment of the Uniform Commercial Code, which provides in effect that the serving for value of food or drink to be consumed either on the premises or elsewhere constitutes a sale.” That is precisely what Parliament later did in India by Art. 366(29-A)(f).

The review (1980) 2 SCR 650. On review the Court softened the absolute rule and substituted the dominant-object test — the passage that Udipikrishna Bhawan applied and that later cases treat as the operative statement:

“Indeed, we have no hesitation in saying that where food is supplied in an eating-house or restaurant, and it is established upon the facts that the substance of the transaction, evidenced by its dominant object, is a sale of food and the rendering of services is merely incidental, the transaction would undoubtedly be exigible to sales tax. In every case it would be for the taxing authority to ascertain the facts when making an assessment under the relevant sales tax law and to determine upon those facts whether a sale of the food supplied is intended.” Northern India Caterers, judgment on review, (1980) 2 SCR 650
Principles evolved
  1. Service of food is prima facie not a sale. The diner acquires a privilege to eat, not property in the food. No designated portion becomes his; he may not remove it or give it away.
  2. The destruction analysis. “Before consumption title does not pass; after consumption there remains nothing to become the subject of title.” This is the most quotable sentence in the case.
  3. Innkeeper's hospitality is the historical root — Crisp v. Pratt (‘utter’, not sell), Newton v. Tries (Holt C.J.'s ‘hospitator’).
  4. Lump sum or per dish — no difference. The mode of charging cannot convert a service into a sale.
  5. No splitting. The Revenue may not dissect a composite transaction into a service element and a sale element.
  6. But (on review) it is ultimately a question of fact: if the dominant object is the sale of food and service is merely incidental, it is taxable.
  7. Statute can reverse it — as the Uniform Commercial Code did in the United States and Art. 366(29-A)(f) did in India.
▸ Real-life illustration

You order a thali at a Delhi restaurant. The food is placed before you. May you ask the waiter to pack up the untouched paneer and take it home? In most restaurants, yes — but that is the restaurant's licence to you, not your property right: the bill will often say so, and some restaurants refuse. Under Northern India Caterers you were never the owner of what was on your plate. Now compare a takeaway: the moment the parcel is handed over and paid for, the property passes — it is a sale, and Art. 366(29-A)(f) now makes even the dine-in meal a deemed sale “by way of or as part of any service” for tax purposes. The law changed; the eating did not.

★ DU-usage note

Always cite the case in both its forms: the 1978 judgment (not a sale) and the 1980 review judgment (dominant object; may be a sale). A student who cites only the first is out of date; one who cites only the second has missed the principle. Then add Art. 366(29-A)(f) — inserted by the Forty-sixth Amendment and covering “the supply, by way of or as part of any service or in any other manner whatsoever, of goods, being food or any other article for human consumption or any drink (whether or not intoxicating)” — which is the constitutional answer to this case, and the reason a restaurant bill carries GST today.

27. State of Karnataka v. Udipikrishna Bhawan
(1981) 3 SCC 76 · Supreme Court · A.P. Sen J. (speaking through Gupta J.) · DU Reader p. 166
“The assessee runs a hotel wherein food and drinks are served to the visitors” is not a sufficient finding — the taxing authority must find the dominant object.
Facts

Two appeals arose out of two revision petitions dismissed by the Karnataka High Court. The petitions had been preferred by the State under s. 8(4) of the Karnataka Appellate Tribunal (Amendment) Act, 1976 read with s. 23 of the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957, against a common order of the Karnataka Appellate Tribunal.

The Tribunal had allowed the assessee's two appeals relating to the assessment years ended 31 March 1976 and 31 March 1977. Following Northern India Caterers, the Tribunal held that the supply of refreshments to the visitors of the two hotels owned by the respondent was “part of a social service and not a sale”. The High Court took the same view and dismissed the revisions.

The only finding recorded by the Tribunal, as summarised by the High Court, was: “The assessee runs a hotel wherein food and drinks are served to the visitors.”

Issue

Is that bare finding sufficient to sustain the conclusion that the supply of refreshments is not a sale — or must the taxing authority, following the judgment on review in Northern India Caterers, determine the substance and dominant object of the transaction?

Arguments
For the State of Karnataka (appellant)
  • The Tribunal and the High Court applied Northern India Caterers as if it laid down an absolute rule that the service of food is never a sale.
  • But the judgment on review ((1980) 2 SCR 650) had restated the law: the question is whether “the substance of the transaction, evidenced by its dominant object, is a sale of food”, and “in every case it would be for the taxing authority to ascertain the facts … and to determine upon those facts whether a sale of the food supplied is intended”.
  • The bare finding that the assessee “runs a hotel” discloses nothing about the dominant object, and so cannot support the conclusion.
For the assessee (respondent)
  • Northern India Caterers is directly applicable: the assessee supplies refreshments to visitors, which is “part of a social service and not a sale”.
  • The Tribunal's finding is a finding of fact and should not be disturbed in revision.
  • The High Court was right to follow the binding authority of this Court.
Decision

Appeal allowed; the matter remanded.

“We do not think that this finding only is sufficient to justify the conclusion reached by the Tribunal and the High Court. … Clearly therefore the only finding recorded in this case that the assessee runs a hotel wherein food and drinks are served to the visitors is not sufficient.”
“We set aside the impugned order and send the case back to the Sales Tax Officer concerned for a fresh assessment according to law following the guidelines appearing in the judgment of this Court disposing of the review petition in the Northern India Caterers case.”
Principles evolved
  1. There is no blanket exemption for the service of food. The ‘no sale’ conclusion in Northern India Caterers is not a rule of law applicable to every eating-house; it is the outcome of a finding of fact.
  2. The dominant-object test is the operative test, and it is a question of fact to be determined by the taxing authority on the material placed before it.
  3. A finding must be adequate to support the conclusion. “The assessee runs a hotel wherein food and drinks are served to the visitors” is a description of the business, not a finding on the dominant object of each transaction.
  4. Appellate discipline. Tribunals and High Courts must apply an authority as restated on review, not as originally reported.
  5. Remand, not reversal. Where the finding is inadequate, the proper course is to send the matter back for a fresh assessment on correct principles, not to decide the question in the Supreme Court.
▸ Real-life illustration

Two establishments on the same street in Bengaluru, both described by their owners as places “where food and drinks are served to visitors”. Establishment A is a darshini: you stand at a counter, pay Rs 40, take a token, collect an idli-vada plate, eat standing, and return the stainless-steel plate. Service is minimal; the customer has very substantially bought food. Establishment B is a business hotel's coffee shop: linen, crockery, air-conditioning, a waiter, background music, and a 10 per cent service charge. On the Udipikrishna Bhawan guidance the assessing officer must find facts about each — and may legitimately reach different conclusions. That is the entire point of the remand.

★ DU-usage note

This is a two-and-a-half page judgment, and it is in the syllabus for one reason: it shows the dominant-object test in operation. Use it as the bridge between Northern India Caterers (1978/1980) and Larsen & Toubro (2014): the test that Udipikrishna Bhawan told the Sales Tax Officer to apply is the very test that L&T later declared “has no application” for transactions within Art. 366(29-A). Quote both and you have demonstrated, in one paragraph, that you understand the constitutional turning point of this entire Topic. Always cite the review judgment ((1980) 2 SCR 650) as the source of the guidelines — the DU syllabus lists both citations side by side on p. 163 for exactly this reason.

§ Theme (d) — Part 3: the post-Amendment trilogy

Larsen & Toubro settles the law; Kone Elevators shows the limits of that settlement (a Constitution Bench decided four months later, on almost identical reasoning, and reaching the opposite result on lifts); Pro Lab clears away the last surviving error — Rainbow Colour Lab. Three cases, one year, the modern law of works contracts.

28. Larsen & Toubro Ltd. v. State of Karnataka
(2014) 1 SCC 708 · Supreme Court · Three-Judge Bench (R.M. Lodha C.J.I., Madan B. Lokur and Kurian Joseph JJ.) · DU Reader p. 167
After Art. 366(29-A)(b), ‘works contract’ takes in all genre; the dominant-nature test “has no application”; and a development agreement followed by a tripartite agreement is a works contract from the moment the flat purchaser signs.
Facts

26 appeals were before the Court — 14 from Karnataka and 12 from Maharashtra. The leading facts were L&T's own.

The ECC division of Larsen & Toubro was engaged in property development along with owners of vacant sites. On 19 October 1995 L&T entered into a development agreement with Dinesh Ranka, owner of land bearing survey numbers 90/1, 91, 92 (Part), 94, 95 and 96/1 (Part), together measuring 34 acres at Kothanur Village, Begur Hobli, Bangalore South Taluk, for construction of a multi-storeyed apartment complex. The owner was to contribute his land; L&T was to construct. After development, 25 per cent of the total space was to belong to the owner and 75 per cent to L&T. A power of attorney was executed by the owner in favour of L&T to enable it to negotiate and book orders from prospective purchasers. L&T accordingly entered into agreements of sale with intended purchasers; the agreements provided that on completion the apartments would be handed over and the purchasers would get an undivided interest in the land also. Sale deeds were executed by L&T and the owner.

The assessment history: business premises inspected on 12 July 2005; details furnished on 24-07-2005 and 26-09-2005; show cause notice on 4 October 2005 relying on Raheja Development; objections on 10-10-2005 and 10-11-2005; premises inspected again on 4 January 2006 with seizure of documents; a further notice on 2 February 2006 proposing to tax the sale of materials used in construction on the ground that L&T was entitled to 75 per cent of the project; and provisional assessment orders under s. 28(6) of the KST Act on 3 July 2006 for the years 2000-01 to 2004-05, raising a total demand of Rs 3,99,28,636.

L&T filed a writ petition in the Supreme Court, withdrew it, and filed under Article 226 in the Karnataka High Court. The Single Judge held that the controversy was covered by Raheja Development and dismissed the writ petition on 10 July 2007.

The reference. On 19 August 2008 a two-Judge Bench of the Supreme Court referred the matter to a larger Bench, saying: “We have prima facie some difficulty in accepting the proposition laid down in Para 20” of Raheja Development; the Show Cause Notice “proceeds only on the basis that the Tripartite Agreement is the works contract”; “if the ratio of Raheja Development case is to be accepted then there would be no difference between works contract and a contract for sale of chattel as a chattel”; and “there is no allegation that the Tripartite Agreement is sham or bogus”.

Issues
  1. Does Raheja Development lay down the correct legal position on the definition of ‘works contract’ in the KST Act?
  2. Is a development agreement (owner contributes land, developer constructs, shares in the built space) a works contract?
  3. If the development agreement is not a works contract, can the Department rely on the subsequent tripartite agreement?
  4. Is ‘works contract’ in Art. 366(29-A)(b) confined to contracts for labour and service, or does it take in all genre?
  5. After the Forty-sixth Amendment, does the dominant-nature test still apply?
Arguments
For the States (Karnataka & Maharashtra) — defending Raheja Development
  • Mr K.N. Bhat (Karnataka): Raheja Development is correct “both on merits as well as on the basis of binding precedents on the principles governing reconsideration of an earlier decision”.
  • Art. 366(29-A) uses the phraseology of Entry 54 List II; for Entry 54, “taxes on the sale or purchase of goods” includes a tax on the transfer of property in goods involved in the execution of a works contract.
  • Once the developer has entered into an agreement of sale with the flat purchaser, the construction is for and on behalf of the purchaser and “so long as there was no termination of the contract” it remains a works contract.
  • Almost all States have amended their laws in line with Raheja Development since May 2005 — “there is no justification for change in the position settled”.
For the assessees — attacking Raheja Development (Mr Rohinton F. Nariman)
  • The well-known tests were not adverted to.
  • “The contract is not read as a whole. Its substance and the main object has not been looked at and one phrase is torn out of context”.
  • The developer's lien is said to be “obviously so that if monies are not recovered from the prospective flat purchasers, the lien can be exercised” — which “show[s] … that the contract is a contract of an agreement to sell immovable property”.
  • The termination and forfeiture clause (10 per cent forfeiture, right to resell) “would have no place in a works contract and can only be consistent with the contract for the sale of immovable property”.
  • “The distinction between a flat being constructed and a flat under construction is a distinction without a difference.”
  • Read as a whole, the main object is to sell and convey a fraction of the land together with a fully constructed flat only when all instalments are paid. “The work undertaken is for the joint development of the project as a whole, i.e., work is undertaken by the developer for himself and for the owner. The construction is not carried out for and on behalf of the purchaser.”
  • The ultimate test: a suit for specific performance by a flat purchaser would be for conveyance of title, not for construction; a suit by the developer would be for the consideration for the flat, not for work done. So it is not a works contract.
  • Alternatively, if the KST definition of ‘works contract’ is wide enough to embrace contracts “not commonly understood as works contract”, it is outside Entry 54 List II, because ‘works contract’ as understood in Gannon Dunkerley-I “has not in any manner been upset by the constitutional amendment”.
Decision — for the three-Judge Bench

Raheja Development approved; the reference answered accordingly. “We are clearly of the view that Raheja Development lays down the correct legal position and we approve the same.”

The reasoning in five steps:

  1. The Amendment undid Gannon Dunkerley-I. “To say that insertion of clause (29-A) in Article 366 has not undone Gannon Dunkerley-I in any manner, in our view, is not correct. The narrow meaning given to the term ‘works contract’ in Gannon Dunkerley-I now no longer survives.” (para 71.)
  2. ‘Works contract’ is amply wide. “The term encompasses a wide range and many varieties of contract … Seen thus, even if in a contract, besides the obligations of supply of goods and materials and performance of labour and services, some additional obligations are imposed, such contract does not cease to be works contract.” (para 76.)
  3. The English and Indian tests, collected. Lee (if the result is a transfer of property in goods, it must be a sale) — not favoured; Robinson (if the substance requires skill and labour, the passing of some materials makes no difference); Chandra Bhan Gosain (depends on its essence); Purshottam Premji (no property in the thing produced as a whole; factors “relevant but not exhaustive”); Associated Hotels (primary object and intention of the parties); Hindustan Aeronautics; and Kone Elevators (para 87).
  4. No absolute tests. “In our view, there are not and there cannot be absolute tests to distinguish a sale and works contract.” (para 81.)
  5. The eleven propositions at para 101 (set out in full in §2.4 above).

On development agreements: “It may, however, be clarified that activity of construction undertaken by the developer would be works contract only from the stage the developer enters into a contract with the flat purchaser. The value addition made to the goods transferred after the agreement is entered into with the flat purchaser can only be made chargeable to tax by the State Government.” (para 115.)

On Gannon Dunkerley-II (para 68) the Court restated its five propositions, of which the most important for students is the fifth: the measure of the levy is “the value of the goods at the time of incorporation of the goods in works and not the cost of acquisition of the goods by the contractor”.

On the 2006 amendment: Explanation (b)(ii) to s. 2(24) of the KST Act was amended with effect from 20 June 2006 to add, after ‘works contract’, “including, an agreement for carrying out for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration, the building, construction, manufacture, processing, fabrication, erection, installation, fitting out, improvement, modification, repair or commissioning of any movable or immovable property”. “There is no doubt in our mind that the amendment … was brought because of the judgment of this Court in Raheja Development.”

Principles evolved
  1. ‘Works contract’ in Art. 366(29-A)(b) is of the widest amplitude — “all genre”, not confined to labour and service.
  2. Additional obligations do not change the character of a contract that satisfies the primary description of a works contract. So a development agreement with a forfeiture clause, a lien and a resale right can still be a works contract.
  3. The dominant-nature test and the enforceability test are both displaced for transactions within Art. 366(29-A).
  4. The taxable event is the deemed sale of the goods involved; the measure is their value at the time of incorporation.
  5. A building contract is a species of works contract, and a contract may simultaneously be a works contract and a transfer of immovable property.
  6. The cut-off is the purchaser's contract. Construction is a works contract only from the stage the developer contracts with the flat purchaser — a critical limitation on the retrospective reach of the levy.
  7. Stare decisis weighed heavily: the Court refused to unsettle a position on which “almost all States have modified their laws”.
28-A. Kone Elevator India Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu
(2014) 7 SCC 1 · Supreme Court · Constitution Bench (R.M. Lodha C.J.I., F.M. Ibrahim Kalifulla, Dipak Misra, Sudhansu Jyoti Mukhopadhaya and A.K. Patnaik JJ.) · decided 6 May 2014 · DU Reader p. 167
A contract for manufacture, supply and installation of a lift is a contract of sale — the L&T ratio “cannot be applied universally” to every contract that contains some element of work.
Facts

Kone Elevator India Pvt. Ltd. manufactured and supplied lifts under contracts with purchasers in Tamil Nadu and other States. The contractual terms were these:

  • 90 per cent of the contract price was payable on the signing of the contract;
  • the balance 10 per cent was payable on commissioning of the lift, or within 30 days of the petitioner's offer to commission it — even where commissioning failed to occur for reasons not attributable to the petitioner or beyond its control;
  • if, within the contract period of 52 weeks, the whole of the components of the lifts were ready for despatch at the petitioner's premises, the purchaser was bound to pay the balance 10 per cent within 90 days of such availability;
  • the lifts were to be handed over to the purchaser on erection.

The assessing authorities and the Tribunal held that there was a sale of the lifts, with installation as a separate element (the Tribunal: “the intention of the parties was that there was a sale qua lifts for money consideration and there was also to be the installation of those lifts by the applicants”). The assessee contended that after the Forty-sixth Amendment and Larsen & Toubro the contract was a works contract and it was entitled to composition. Several States — Tamil Nadu, Orissa, Gujarat, Haryana — appeared; a Haryana notification dated 17 May 2010 had fixed 15 per cent as the labour, service and other like charges for “fabrication and installation of elevators (lifts) and escalators”.

Issues
  1. Is a contract for the manufacture, supply and installation of a lift a ‘works contract’ falling within Art. 366(29-A)(b), or a contract of sale?
  2. Does the ratio of Larsen & Toubro apply to it?
Arguments
For the States (Tamil Nadu, Orissa, Gujarat)
  • Mr Dwivedi (Orissa): the contract is “always one for sale of its branded lifts, which having regard to the nature of the product has to be necessarily erected at the site”. The 90 per cent payment on signing, the obligation to pay the balance on the components merely being ready for despatch, and the handover on erection all point to a sale.
  • Mr Preetesh Kumar (Gujarat): even applying the test in para 72 of Larsen & Toubro, the contract for supply of a lift “could not be brought within the concept of ‘works contract’”.
  • The Haryana notification treating 15 per cent as labour for lifts is, the States submitted, consistent with the lift having been sold and the installation being a separate service.
  • Reliance on M/s Patnaik & Co. (AIR 1965 SC 1655 : (1965) 2 SCR 782), a Constitution Bench decision, and on Vanguard Rolling Shutters.
For the assessee (Kone Elevator)
  • Mr Salve: after the Forty-sixth Amendment and Larsen & Toubro, the contract “was nothing but a ‘contract for works’” and the materials used were liable to be taxed only as a deemed sale under Art. 366(29-A)(b).
  • A lift is assembled and erected on site from components brought in knocked-down condition; like the rolling shutter in Sentinel, it does not exist as a unit until installed, and it becomes a fixture to the building.
  • The Haryana notification specifying 15 per cent labour for “fabrication and installation of elevators (lifts) and escalators” is self-contradictory if lifts are sold: “once it is treated as a composite contract invoking labour and service as a natural corollary, it would be works contract and not a contract for sale”.
Decision — Constitution Bench

It is a contract of SALE, not a works contract.

“Having regard to the essential ingredients of the contractual terms, it is difficult to hold that the supply of LIFT by the Petitioner to its Purchaser can be called as a ‘Works Contract’ and, therefore, since the very first condition is not fulfilled, the other conditions are of no consequence in order to invoke Article 366(29A)(b).”

The Court first restated the tests — the passage later reproduced at para 87 of Larsen & Toubro:

“It can be treated as well settled that there is no standard formula by which one can distinguish a ‘contract for sale’ from a ‘works contract’. The question is largely one of fact depending upon the terms of the contract … If the intention is to transfer for a price a chattel in which the transferee had no previous property, then the contract is a contract for sale. Ultimately, the true effect of an accretion made pursuant to a contract has to be judged not by artificial rules but from the intention of the parties. In a ‘contract of sale’, the main object is the transfer of property and delivery of possession of the property, whereas the main object in a ‘contract for work’ is not the transfer of the property but it is one for work and labour. Another test often to be applied is: when and how the property of the dealer in such a transaction passes to the customer — is it by transfer at the time of delivery of the finished article as a chattel, or by accession during the procession of work on fusion to the movable property of the customer? If it is the former, it is a ‘sale’; if it is the latter, it is a ‘works contract’.” Kone Elevators, para 5

The decisive contractual features. The Court repeatedly returned to the payment terms: “the terms have downright been agreed upon between the parties only to mandate the Purchaser to pay 90% of the contracted amount on mere signing of the contract, and to pay the balance 10% within 30 days of the Petitioner's offer to commission the LIFT and even if the said event of commissioning of the LIFT fails to occur due to any reason not attributable to the Petitioner or beyond its control”; and “if … the whole of the components of the LIFTS are ready for dispatch at its premises, the Purchaser is bound to pay the balance 10% also within 90 days from the date of such availability of materials”.

Larsen & Toubro distinguished, not overruled. The Court held that the L&T ratio arose “in the peculiar facts relating to that case where it related to construction of a building by virtue of the contract between the developer and owner on the one side and the prospective purchaser on the other side”, and that “it is difficult to apply the said ratio rendered in the context of the said contract as applicable universally in all sorts of contracts where some element of work is involved”. “Analyzing the decision from all frontiers it can be concluded that the ratio laid down in Larsen and Toubro Ltd. … would apply in the peculiar facts relating to that case.”

Consequence: since the supply satisfies the definition of ‘sale’ under the Sale of Goods Act itself, “the question of deemed sale does not arise” — Art. 366(29-A)(b) is not even needed.

Principles evolved
  1. There is no standard formula. The question is one of fact and of the true construction of each contract.
  2. Transfer as chattel vs. accession: if the property passes on delivery of the finished article as a chattel, it is a sale; if it passes by accession during the progress of the work, it is a works contract.
  3. Intention of the parties, not artificial rules.
  4. A lift is sold, a building is constructed. The same Court, in the same year, held a flat-construction contract to be a works contract and a lift-supply contract to be a sale — because the payment terms and the commercial reality differed.
  5. Payment terms are powerful evidence. 90 per cent on signing, and the obligation to pay the balance on the components merely being ready for despatch, showed that the purchaser was buying a branded lift, not contracting for work.
  6. Art. 366(29-A)(b) is engaged only when the first condition fails. Where the transaction is already a sale under the SOGA, the deeming provision is unnecessary — a deemed sale presupposes that there is no actual one.
  7. L&T is not a universal solvent. Its ratio must be read in the context of building/development contracts.
★ DU-usage note — the 2017 question, answered

2017, Q3(b): “Whether a contract for manufacture, supply and installation of lifts in a building is a ‘contract for sale of goods’ or a ‘works contract’? Refer case law.” Answer in six moves: (i) state that the question is one of fact and that “there is no standard formula” (Kone, para 5); (ii) give the transfer-as-chattel vs. accession test; (iii) give the Kone holding and its three decisive payment terms (90 per cent on signing; balance on offer to commission even if commissioning fails; balance payable on components being ready for despatch); (iv) state the consequence — an actual sale under the SOGA, so no need to invoke Art. 366(29-A)(b); (v) contrast Larsen & Toubro — building contracts are a species of works contract, and the dominant-nature test is displaced for transactions within Art. 366(29-A); (vi) reconcile: Kone says the L&T ratio applies to its “peculiar facts” and not “universally … where some element of work is involved”. Finish with Sentinel Rolling Shutters as the contrasting pre-1983 illustration, and note that “the very first condition” — the existence of a works contract — must be established before the constitutional deeming fiction can be invoked at all.

29. State of Karnataka and Ors. v. Pro Lab and Ors.
AIR 2015 SC 1098 · Supreme Court · A.K. Sikri J. (with S.A. Bobde J.) · DU Reader p. 190
Processing photographs is a works contract after 1983; Rainbow Colour Lab is no longer good law, and Entry 25 of Schedule VI to the KST Act is constitutionally valid.
Facts

The respondents were photographers and photo-processors. At the relevant time the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957 contained Entry 25 of Schedule VI, which subjected to tax the supply of photographs, photo-prints, photo-negatives and similar articles. Entry 25 had been inserted with effect from 1 July 1989 — i.e. after the Forty-sixth Amendment.

The history that produced this litigation:

  1. The Karnataka High Court, in Keshoram, examined in detail the business of processing and supplying photographs, photo-frames and photo-negatives. It noted the Forty-sixth Amendment. But it held that “the main object of the work undertaken by the petitioner … was not the transfer of a chattel as a chattel and, in fact, it was a contract of work and labour and there was no sale of goods involved”. Applying the dominant intention test, it declared Entry 25 unconstitutional.
  2. The SLP against that judgment was dismissed on 20 April 2000 — four days after Rainbow Colour Lab was delivered.
  3. Within a year, a three-Judge Bench in ACC Ltd. doubted Rainbow Colour Lab; BSNL repeated the doubt; Larsen & Toubro settled it.
  4. The Karnataka Legislature re-enacted Entry 25 by the Karnataka State Laws Act, 2004, which came into force on 29 January 2004, giving Entry 25 retrospective effect from 1 July 1989.
  5. The respondents again challenged Entry 25. The High Court, by the impugned judgment, struck it down again — reasoning that Entry 25 had already been held unconstitutional in Keshoram, against which the SLP had been dismissed, and “in view of that decision, it was not permissible for the legislature to re-enact the said Entry by applying a different legal principle”.

The State appealed. Two questions arose: (i) the correctness of the dominant-intention approach; and (ii) whether retrospective operation from 1-7-1989 was permissible.

Issues
  1. Is the supply of photographs, photo-prints and photo-negatives a deemed sale of goods involved in the execution of a works contract under Art. 366(29-A)(b)?
  2. Is Entry 25 of Schedule VI to the Karnataka Sales Tax Act constitutionally valid?
  3. Was it permissible to give the re-enacted Entry retrospective effect from 1 July 1989?
Arguments
For the State of Karnataka (appellant)
  • The very basis of Keshoram was Rainbow Colour Lab, which “was declared as not a good law in ACC Ltd. (which position is repeated in BSNL as well as M/s Larsen and Toubro cases)”. “Thus, the very basis on which Entry 25 of Schedule VI was declared as unconstitutional, has been found to be erroneous.”
  • Art. 366(29-A)(b), as explained in L&T paras 60–62, includes “a tax on the transfer of property in goods (whether as goods or in some other form) involved in the execution of a works contract” — and “the expression ‘in some other form’ in the bracket is of utmost significance”: it enlarges the ordinary understanding of ‘goods’ to include goods “which have ceased to be chattels or movables or merchandise and become attached or embedded to earth”.
  • The Forty-sixth Amendment “undid” the restricted meaning given to ‘sale of goods’ in Gannon Dunkerley — Builders Association upheld the Amendment precisely to “enlarge the scope of the expression”.
  • On retrospectivity: Hiralal Ratanlal v. State of U.P. — “subject to Constitutional restrictions a power to legislate includes a power to legislate prospectively as well as retrospectively.” And assessments had already been made on the basis of the amendment — “This was admitted by the learned counsel for the respondents at bar”.
For the assessees (respondents)
  • Entry 25 was already declared unconstitutional in Keshoram, and the SLP was dismissed — that is final between the parties and cannot be re-opened by legislation.
  • Rainbow Colour Lab governed: “This division of contract under the amended law can be made only if the works contract involved a dominant intention to transfer the property in goods and not in contracts where the transfer in property takes place as an incident of contract of service.”
  • The photopaper on which positive prints are taken is “simply incidental and ancillary” to what is really a service contract — the skill of the photographer.
  • “The Amendment, referred to above, has not empowered the State to indulge in microscopic division of contracts involving the value of materials used incidentally in such contracts.”
  • In any event, retrospectivity from 1-7-1989 (the Karnataka State Laws Act, 2004 came into force only on 29-1-2004) is impermissible; the Entry should at most be prospective.
Decision — per A.K. Sikri J.

Appeal allowed; the impugned judgment set aside; the writ petitions dismissed; Entry 25 held constitutionally valid. No order as to costs.

On the substantive question. The Court traced the entire line: Gannon Dunkerley → the Forty-sixth Amendment → Builders Association (upholding it) → Rainbow Colour Lab (the wrong turn) → Keshoram → ACC Ltd. → BSNL → Larsen & Toubro. It quoted L&T paras 60–63 verbatim and adopted the “utmost significance” reading of the bracketed words.

“It has been observed in the said case that even if in a contract, besides the obligations of supply of goods and materials and performance of labour and services, some additional obligations are imposed, such contract does not cease to be works contract, for the additional obligations in the contract would not alter the nature of the contract so long as the contract provides for a contract for works and satisfies the primary description of works contract.” Pro Lab, para 23, summarising Larsen & Toubro

On the High Court's approach — a sharp rebuke.

“The reason given by the High Court in invalidating Entry 25 is that this provision was already held unconstitutional by the said High Court in Keshoram's case against which the SLP was also dismissed and in view of that decision, it was not permissible for the legislature to re-enact the said Entry by applying a different legal principle. According to us, this was clearly an erroneous approach … The High Court did not even deal with various facets of the issue in their correct perspective, in the light of subsequent judgments of this Court with specific rulings that Rainbow Colour Lab is no longer a good law.”

On retrospectivity. “We are afraid, even this argument does not cut any ice.” Entry 25 was first inserted w.e.f. 1-7-1989, after the Forty-sixth Amendment. It was struck down only because of Rainbow Colour Lab, “which judgment was declared as not a good law in ACC Ltd.”. “In such circumstances, the legislature will be justified in enacting the law from the date when such a law was passed originally and that date is 01.07.1989 in the instant case.” And: “on the basis of this amendment, there have been assessments made by the assessing authorities. This was admitted by the learned counsel for the respondents at bar.”

Citing Hiralal Ratanlal v. State of U.P.: “the source of the legislative power to levy sales or purchase tax on goods is Entry 54 of the List II of the Constitution. It is well settled that subject to Constitutional restrictions a power to legislate includes a power to legislate prospectively as well as retrospectively.”

Principles evolved
  1. The dominant-intention test is dead for transactions within Art. 366(29-A). Rainbow Colour Lab is “no longer a good law”; Keshoram fell with it.
  2. A service contract that consumes goods is a works contract. Photoprocessing — the photographer's skill plus the photopaper it is impressed upon — is a works contract, and the photopaper is a deemed sale.
  3. “Whether as goods or in some other form” is the operative enlargement. Goods that “have ceased to be chattels or movables or merchandise and become attached or embedded” — or, as here, consumed into the product — remain goods for the purposes of clause (b).
  4. Dismissal of an SLP does not freeze the law where the precedent on which the judgment rested is subsequently overruled. The legislature may re-enact on the corrected principle.
  5. Retrospective re-enactment to the original date is permissible where the original provision was struck down on a basis later held erroneous — the classic ‘validating statute’ situation.
  6. Legislative power to tax includes the power to tax retrospectively (Hiralal Ratanlal), subject only to constitutional restrictions.
  7. Practical confirmation: that assessments had already been made on that basis was treated as relevant.
▸ Real-life illustration

You hand a roll of film (or a memory card) to a photo lab and collect 50 prints an hour later. What did you buy? On Pro Lab, you bought a works contract: the lab supplied the paper and chemicals (goods — ‘goods involved in the execution of a works contract’) and applied its skill and labour (service). The State may tax the value of the paper and chemicals; it may not tax the photographer's skill, and it may not “entrench upon the Union List” by including the cost of the service in the value of the goods. Note the contrast with Northern India Caterers: there, the food was destroyed in consumption and nothing passed to the customer at all; here, the photopaper is handed over as the positive print. That distinction — destruction vs. delivery — is the difference between ‘no sale’ and ‘works contract’, and it is worth a full paragraph in an exam.

★ DU-usage note

Use Pro Lab as the closing case of any works/labour answer, because it is chronologically last (2015) and it ties together every earlier case: Gannon Dunkerley (the rule), the Forty-sixth Amendment (the response), Builders Association (the Amendment upheld), Rainbow Colour Lab (the wrong turn), ACC Ltd. and BSNL (the doubt), Larsen & Toubro (the settlement) and Pro Lab (the application to a service contract). That single sentence of case-history is worth more in the exam than a page of abstract theory. Two quotable sentences: “the very basis on which Entry 25 of Schedule VI was declared as unconstitutional, has been found to be erroneous”; and, from L&T as reproduced here, “goods in some other form would thus mean goods which have ceased to be chattels or movables or merchandise and become attached or embedded to earth”.

Set A — The anatomy of a sale (s. 4)

2011CT · OC Q2(a) · Essentials of sale
Question “A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the Seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price.” Emphasise the essential features of Sale according to the above mentioned definition.
Model answer

Introduction. The quotation is the first sentence of s. 4(1) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. It is the definition on which the entire Act turns, and this question asks you to take it apart word by word. The definition is supplemented by s. 2(1) (buyer), s. 2(13) (seller), s. 2(7) (goods), s. 2(11) (property) and s. 2(10) (price).

Essential feature 1 — there must be a contract. A sale is consensual, not unilateral. The general law of contract applies through s. 3, so the Indian Contract Act, 1872 supplies: offer and acceptance (ss. 2–9), free consent (ss. 13–22), lawful consideration and object (ss. 23–24), competency (s. 11), and certainty (s. 29). At the same time, s. 5(2) provides that no formality is required — a sale may be in writing, oral, partly both, or implied from the conduct of the parties. That last limb is how a statutory transaction is held to be a sale (Vishnu Agencies; Coffee Board).

Essential feature 2 — two parties. There must be a seller (s. 2(13)) and a buyer (s. 2(1)), and they must be distinct persons — except that, by the second sentence of s. 4(1), “there may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another”.

Essential feature 3 — the subject-matter must be ‘goods’ (s. 2(7)): every kind of moveable property other than actionable claims and money, including stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land agreed to be severed. Add the judicial gloss of marketability from Union of India v. Delhi Cloth & General Mills (AIR 1963 SC 791), CCE v. Eastend Paper Industries ((1989) 4 SCC 244) and R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma — the thing must be something that “can ordinarily come to the markets to be bought and sold”.

Essential feature 4 — transfer of ‘property’, that is, GENERAL property (s. 2(11)). Not possession, not custody, not special property. This is the element that fails in every works-contract case: in Gannon Dunkerley the materials passed by accretion; in Sentinel Rolling Shutters the manufacturer never owned the unit; in Hindustan Aeronautics the customer owned the materials already.

Essential feature 5 — ‘transfers OR agrees to transfer’. The disjunctive is what creates the two species in s. 4(3): a sale where property passes at once, an agreement to sell where it is to pass at a future time or on a condition. Under s. 4(4) an agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled. Note also s. 6(3): a purported present sale of future goods operates as an agreement to sell.

Essential feature 6 — ‘for a price’. ‘Price’ is defined in s. 2(10) as the money consideration for a sale of goods. Barter or exchange is therefore not a sale under this Act. The price need not be fixed in advance: s. 9 supplies a three-tier ladder (fixed by the contract; left to be fixed in a manner agreed; determined by the course of dealing), and if none of these applies, the buyer must pay a reasonable price — which is a question of fact (s. 9(2)). s. 10 separately governs valuation by a third party.

Conclusion. Six features, then: contract, two parties, goods, general property, transfer-or-agreement-to-transfer, and money price. Any one missing means there is no contract of sale — and that, in substance, is what every one of the eleven prescribed cases is about.

Exam technique. Write the section out, then take it apart in the order of the words. Students who write a general essay on ‘what is a sale’ without numbering the words of s. 4(1) rarely cross 6/10. Add one case per element — R.D. Saxena for goods, Gannon Dunkerley for property, M.P. Electricity Board for goods once more, Vishnu Agencies for consent — and you have a 10.
2015Commercial Transactions (SOGA) · OC Q1(a) · When an agreement becomes a sale
Question “A contract of sale becomes a sale only when the property in the goods is transferred to the buyer under terms of contract.” Explain.
Model answer

The proposition is sound and is a direct paraphrase of s. 4(3) read with s. 4(4). A ‘contract of sale’ is the genus; ‘sale’ and ‘agreement to sell’ are its two species, and what separates them is the moment at which the general property passes.

Statutory frame. s. 4(3): where under a contract of sale the property in the goods is transferred from seller to buyer, the contract is called a sale; where the transfer is to take place at a future time or subject to some condition thereafter to be fulfilled, it is called an agreement to sell. s. 4(4): “An agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred.”

Why the moment matters — three consequences.

  1. Risk. s. 26: res perit domino — risk prima facie passes with property. Before transfer, the loss falls on the seller; after it, on the buyer, even though the goods are still in the seller's possession.
  2. The seller's remedy. After transfer he sues for the price (s. 55(1)) — the buyer owes a debt. Before transfer he can only sue for damages for non-acceptance (s. 56), measured on a quantum meruit.
  3. Insolvency. After transfer the seller is an unpaid seller (s. 45) with a lien (s. 47) or a right of stoppage in transit (s. 50) and a power of resale (s. 54). Before transfer he simply still owns the goods.

The words ‘under terms of contract’ are important. The property must pass pursuant to the agreement — that is the second of the two ingredients Venkatarama Aiyar J. identified in Gannon Dunkerley. A transfer that occurs by some other route — accretion to land, operation of law on compulsory acquisition, or delivery under a works contract — is not a transfer ‘under the terms of the contract’ and does not produce a sale. This is precisely why the building cases failed.

Illustrations. (i) A agrees to sell his specific Maruti to B, delivery and payment next week. Today it is an agreement to sell. Next week, on delivery and payment, it becomes a sale. (ii) A agrees to sell 100 tonnes of wheat ‘out of my December harvest’. Future goods (s. 2(6)); s. 6(3) makes it an agreement to sell; it becomes a sale only when the wheat is ascertained and appropriated. (iii) A customer is served dinner in a restaurant. On Northern India Caterers the property never passes at all — “before consumption title does not pass; after consumption there remains nothing to become the subject of title” — so it never becomes a sale.

Exam technique. The question is really ‘explain s. 4(3) and s. 4(4)’. Give the subsection text, then the three consequences (risk, remedy, insolvency), then the ‘under the terms of contract’ caveat with Gannon Dunkerley, then two illustrations. That is a complete 10-mark answer in about 450 words.

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q8(a) · ‘contract plus conveyance’
Question “An agreement to sell is a contract pure and simple, whereas a sale is a contract plus conveyance.” Discuss the above statement.
Model answer

The statement is correct and is the classical common-law formulation. It comes from Benjamin on Sale, and it was adopted by Lord Denning M.R. in C.E.B. Draper & Sons Ltd. v. Edward Turner & Sons Ltd. ((1964) 3 All ER 148), whose words the Supreme Court reproduced in Coffee Board, Karnataka v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes (AIR 1988 SC 1487):

“I know that often times a contract for sale is spoken of as a sale. But the word ‘sale’ properly connotes the transfer of the absolute or general property in a thing for a price in money [see Benjamin on Sale, 2nd edn. (1873), p. 1, quoted in Kirkness v. John Hudson & Co., 1955 AC 696]. … In this Act of 1926 I think that ‘sale’ is used in its proper sense to denote the transfer of property in the goods.”

What ‘conveyance’ adds. The ‘contract’ is the consensus — offer, acceptance, consideration. The ‘conveyance’ is the transfer of general property (s. 2(11)) which converts a personal right into a real one. Before conveyance the buyer has a jus in personam against the seller; after it he has a jus in rem — he owns the goods.

Statutory support. s. 4(3) uses the word ‘called’: the contract “is called a sale” where property is transferred, and “is called an agreement to sell” where the transfer is to take place later. s. 4(4) supplies the moment of conversion.

Three practical consequences of the missing conveyance. (i) Risk (s. 26) stays with the seller; (ii) the remedy is damages under s. 56, not an action for the price under s. 55(1); (iii) if the specific goods perish without fault before the risk passes, s. 8 avoids the agreement rather than leaving the buyer to bear the loss.

Where the statement needs care. An agreement to sell is not less of a contract — it is a fully binding executory contract, and the buyer can sue for damages on breach. Nor is every ‘conveyance’ a sale: the property must pass under the terms of the contract, which is why materials that pass into a building by accretion (Gannon Dunkerley) or goods handed over under a works contract (Sentinel Rolling Shutters) do not produce a sale, notwithstanding that the customer ends up owning them. And after Art. 366(29-A)(b) the State may tax such a transfer as a deemed sale — a statutory fiction, not a conveyance.

Exam technique. Always attribute the maxim. Naming Benjamin, Kirkness and Draper in one line — and adding that the Supreme Court quoted it in Coffee Board — is worth two marks on its own. Then close with the accretion caveat, which is what distinguishes a first-class answer from a model-answer reproduction.

Set C — What is and is not ‘goods’

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q5 · define sale + concept of goods
Question (a) Define ‘sale’ under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.
(b) Discuss the concept of Goods under the Act. Whether the following are goods — if yes, give reasons: (i) Electricity  (ii) Old coins  (iii) Timber.
Model answer

(a) ‘Sale’ defined. There are two related definitions and a good answer gives both. s. 4(1) defines a contract of sale of goods: “a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price”, adding that there may be a contract of sale between one part-owner and another. s. 4(3) then tells us when such a contract is called a sale: where “the property in the goods is transferred from the seller to the buyer”. Read with s. 2(13) (seller), s. 2(1) (buyer), s. 2(11) (property = general property) and s. 2(10) (price = money consideration).

(b) The concept of ‘goods’ — s. 2(7), verbatim: “‘goods’ means every kind of moveable property other than actionable claims and money; and includes stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale.”

Three limbs: (1) the genus — every kind of moveable property, the widest possible formulation, but confined to movables; (2) the exclusions — actionable claims (defined in s. 3 of the ICA) and money; (3) the inclusive extension — stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to land agreed to be severed. To these the Supreme Court has added a fourth, unwritten requirement: marketability, from Union of India v. Delhi Cloth & General Mills (AIR 1963 SC 791): “to become ‘goods’ an article must be something which can ordinarily come to the markets to be bought and sold”; CCE v. Eastend Paper Industries ((1989) 4 SCC 244): “identifiable articles known in the markets as goods and marketed and marketable”; and R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma, where a lawyer's case file was held moveable but not marketable and therefore not goods.

(i) Electricity — YES, goods. Authority: Commissioner of Sales Tax, M.P. v. M.P. Electricity Board, Jabalpur ((1969) 1 SCC 200 · AIR 1970 SC 732). Grover J. held that ‘goods’ in the M.P. and C.P. & Berar Sales Tax Acts meant “all kinds of movable property” and that electricity is not among the excluded items. It “can be transmitted, transferred, delivered, stored, possessed etc., in the same way as any other movable property”, and — decisively — it is “capable of abstraction, consumption and use which, if done dishonestly, would attract punishment under Section 39 of the Indian Electricity Act, 1910”. The Court followed Kumbakonam Electric Supply Corpn. (14 STC 600) and Malerkotla Power Supply Co. (22 STC 325), cited County of Durham Electrical Power & Distribution Co. v. Inland Revenue ((1909) 2 KB 604) from Benjamin on Sale, 8th Ed., p. 171, distinguished Rash Behari v. Emperor (AIR 1936 Cal 753), and noted that the Acts specifically exempted the sale of electrical energy — an exemption which presupposes taxability. Result: the Electricity Board is a ‘dealer’.

(ii) Old coins — YES, goods. This is the converse of the ‘money’ exclusion. Currency in circulation is ‘money’ and therefore excluded by s. 2(7). But an old or rare coin collected or sold as a curio or as an article of antiquity is not functioning as money: it is “an identifiable article known in the market as goods”, bought and sold by numismatists. The marketability test is plainly satisfied — there is a recognised market with recognised prices. Contrast: a Rs 100 note handed over in payment is money; the same note sold to a collector at a premium for its rarity or serial number is goods.

(iii) Timber — YES, if standing timber agreed to be severed. This is the third limb of s. 2(7). ‘Timber’ here means standing timber — trees intended to be felled and converted into timber (fructus industriales) — which the statute expressly includes “where … agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale”. The Transfer of Property Act, 1882, s. 3 uses almost the same words in carving “standing timber, growing crops or grass” out of immoveable property, and the two provisions should be read together. But a fruit-bearing or shade tree sold as a tree, to be enjoyed in situ, is fructus naturales and remains immoveable property — not goods, and transferable only by a registered instrument under the TPA.

Exam technique. This question has been asked three times with different lists — 2015 (electricity, old coins, timber), 2017 (electricity, actionable claim, standing timber) and 2012 OC (electricity, currency in circulation, actionable claims, human organs). Learn the method, not the list: (1) quote s. 2(7); (2) run the four tests — moveable? not an actionable claim or money? marketable? severed or agreed to be severed? (3) give the authority. And always add the ‘but’: standing timber yes, shade tree no; old coin yes, currency no.

Set D — Statutory transactions

2015Commercial Transactions (SOGA) · OC Q1(b) · compulsion and consent
Question When goods are supplied under a statutory compulsion whether this results in sale or not, and consent of the supplier is material or not? Discuss with case law to support your answer.
Model answer

The short answer: yes, it can result in a sale; and no, the absence of bargaining freedom does not negative consent — provided the compulsion is a general regulation of a trade and not a direction to a particular owner to part with his property.

1. The statutory setting. s. 5(2) of the SOGA permits a contract of sale to be “implied from the conduct of the parties”. That is the hook. Where a Control Order obliges a licensed dealer to supply an allottee at a controlled price, and the allottee tenders the permit and the price, the law finds offer and acceptance in that conduct.

2. The old law — New India Sugar Mills v. CST (AIR 1963 SC 1207). A majority held that a statutory levy of sugarcane was not a sale, because under Benjamin on Sale, 8th Ed., one of the four elements of a valid sale is ‘mutual assent’, and compulsion left no room for it.

3. The present law — Vishnu Agencies (P) Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer ((1978) 1 SCC 520; AIR 1978 SC 449), a seven-Judge Bench. Chandrachud J. held the transactions were sales and declared: “with great deference, the majority decision in New India Sugar Mills is not good law”. Four reasons:

  1. Antecedent volition. “It is not obligatory on a trader to deal in cement nor on any one to acquire it. The primary fact, therefore, is that the decision of the trader to deal in an essential commodity is volitional.”
  2. Consent by conduct. “When the allottee presents his permit to the dealer, he signifies his willingness … And when, upon the presentation of the permit, the dealer acts upon it, he impliedly agrees to supply … They, with their free consent, agreed to enter into the transactions.”
  3. Residual bargaining space. The notified price is a ceiling (“not exceeding”); delivery “within a reasonable time”; a right of weighment under para 8A; a right to reject for short weight or bad quality. “The circumstance that in these areas, though minimal, the parties … have the freedom to bargain militates against the view that the transactions are not consensual.”
  4. History. The emphasis on consensuality came from the action of assumpsit; “the formulations in Benjamin's second edition … evidently require modification in the light of regulatory measures of social control”.

4. The limit — compulsory acquisition. “Decisions in cases of ‘compulsory acquisition’, where such acquisition is patent as in Kirkness or is inferred as in Chhitter Mal, fall in a separate and distinct class.” The distinction is: a general law regulating a class leaves a contractual core; an order directed to a particular owner to hand over specified goods for compensation does not — it is eminent domain.

5. The extreme application — Coffee Board, Karnataka v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes (AIR 1988 SC 1487). Even the total compulsory delivery of a crop to a statutory marketing pool is a sale. The Court applied four tests: “All the four essential elements of sale — (1) parties competent to contract, (2) mutual consent — though minimal, by growing coffee under the conditions imposed by the Act, (3) transfer of property in the goods and (4) payment of price though deferred — are present.” Two confirmations: (i) the Board had a right to reject coffee under s. 25(2), and no time was fixed for delivery — indicia of consensuality; (ii) “the amount paid to the grower was neither compensation nor dividend”, and “the levy of duties of excise and customs under Sections 11 and 12 of the Coffee Act are inconsistent with the concept of compulsory acquisition” — you do not levy excise on compensation.

Conclusion. Consent is material — the Court did not dispense with it, it found it. What it dispensed with was the requirement that consent take the form of a bargain over terms. The approved line is Indian Steel & Wire Products, Andhra Sugars, Salar Jung Sugar Mills and ONGC; the disapproved line is New India Sugar Mills and Cement Distributors Pvt. Ltd.

Exam technique. The two-part question (‘is it a sale’ / ‘is consent material’) is a trap. Many candidates answer ‘consent is not material’, which is wrong: Vishnu Agencies held consent was present, not unnecessary. Say that explicitly and you gain two marks. Also always state the Kirkness / Chhitter Mal limit — without it the answer looks absolute and incomplete.
2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q4(c) · note on ‘Statutory Transactions’
Question (write notes on any two) Statutory Transactions.
Model answer — a five-paragraph note

Meaning. The phrase is not defined in the Act. A statutory transaction is one into which the parties enter because a statute obliges them to, on terms dictated by the statute, usually at a controlled price, and usually under a penal sanction. Examples: cement supplied against an allotment order under the West Bengal Cement Control Order, 1948; paddy sold to a licensed procuring agent under the A.P. Paddy Procurement (Levy) Orders; coffee delivered to the pool under s. 25(1) of the Coffee Act, 1942; sugarcane supplied to a mill under a State sugar-control enactment.

Why it is difficult. s. 4(1) requires a contract; s. 5(2) allows a contract to be “implied from the conduct of the parties”, but the classical view (Benjamin, 2nd & 8th eds.) required mutual assent. Compulsion and assent appear irreconcilable.

The resolution. Vishnu Agencies (1978, 7-Judge) holds that the compulsion is antecedent: the trader chose to enter a regulated trade; the consumer chose to apply for a permit; the terms are statutory but the transaction is consensual. Consent is found in conduct under s. 5(2). Coffee Board (1988) pushes the principle to the extreme and still finds a sale, resting on the four-element test (competent parties; mutual consent though minimal; transfer of property; price though deferred).

The limit. Kirkness v. John Hudson & Co. and Chhitter Mal — compulsory acquisition is a “separate and distinct class”. The test is whether the compulsion is a general regulation of a class or an order to a particular owner; and whether what is paid is price or compensation (the levy of customs and excise on the payment being inconsistent with compensation).

Statutes to cite. Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (ss. 3, 6A, 7); the Control Orders made under it; the Coffee Act, 1942 (ss. 11–13, 25, 26, 34); the Bengal Finance (Sales Tax) Act, 1941 s. 2(g); the A.P. General Sales Tax Act, 1957 s. 2(n); the Karnataka Sales Tax Act, 1957 s. 2(t).

Exam technique. A ‘note’ question is marked on structure. Five labelled paragraphs — meaning, difficulty, resolution, limit, statutes — is the ideal length, about 300 words. Name at least two cases and two statutes.

Question “The question whether a particular contract is a contract for sale or for work and labour is always a difficult question. The difficulty lies not in the formulation of tests but in the application of these tests to the facts of the case.” Discuss with reference to relevant case law.
Model answer

The quotation is from Sentinel Rolling Shutters & Engg. Co. (P) Ltd. v. CST ((1978) 4 SCC 260; AIR 1978 SC 545), per P.N. Bhagwati J. The full passage is worth learning:

“The difficulty, however, lies not in the formulation of the tests for determining when a contract can be said to be a contract for sale or a contract for work and labour, but in the application of the tests to the facts of the case before the Court. … these tests are not exhaustive and do not lay down any rigid or inflexible rule applicable alike to all transactions. They do not give any magic formula … They merely focus on one or the other aspect of the transaction and afford some guidance … but basically and primarily, whether a particular contract is one for sale of goods or for work and labour depends upon the main object of the parties gathered from the terms of the contract, the circumstances of the transaction and the custom of the trade.”

1. The three forms of contract (Bhagwati J.'s taxonomy — see Fig. 10):

  1. Divisible / composite — work done for remuneration and materials supplied for a price. Two contracts in one instrument. Separable; the sale element is taxable. This is the saving Gannon Dunkerley expressly preserved.
  2. Work, materials accessory — a contract for work in which the use of materials is “accessory or incidental to the execution of the work”. Example given by the Court: constructing a building. Works contract; no sale.
  3. Sale, work incidental — a contract for the supply of goods where some work is required to be done “as incidental to the sale”. Example given by the Court: supplying and fixing an air-conditioner. Sale; taxable in full.

“The dividing line between the two types of contracts is somewhat hazy and ‘thin partitions do their bounds divide’. But even so the distinction is there and it is very much real.”

2. The primary test — Halsbury's Laws of England, 3rd Ed., Vol. 34, Art. 3: is the contract one whose main object is the transfer of property in a chattel as a chattel, though some work may be required as ancillary or incidental to the sale? Or is it the carrying out of work by bestowal of labour and service, materials being used in the execution of such work?

3. The subsidiary test — Commr. of M.P. v. Purshottam Premji ((1970) 2 SCC 287), applied in State of Rajasthan v. Man Industrial Corpn. ((1969) 1 SCC 567):

“The primary difference between a contract for work or service and a contract for sale of goods is that in the former there is in the person performing work or rendering service no property in the thing produced as a whole … In the case of a contract for sale, the thing produced as a whole has individual existence as the sole property of the party who produced it, at some time before delivery, and the property therein passes only under the contract relating thereto to the other party for price.”

4. Application — the four syllabus illustrations.

2017Special Contracts (LB-304) Q3(b) · lifts — sale or works contract?
Question Whether a contract for manufacture, supply and installation of lifts in a building is a ‘contract for sale of goods’ or a ‘works contract’? Refer case law.
Model answer

Answer: a contract of SALE — on the authority of Kone Elevator India Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Tamil Nadu ((2014) 7 SCC 1), a Constitution Bench decision of 6 May 2014.

1. There is no standard formula. The Court began: “It can be treated as well settled that there is no standard formula by which one can distinguish a ‘contract for sale’ from a ‘works contract’. The question is largely one of fact depending upon the terms of the contract including the nature of the obligations to be discharged thereunder and the surrounding circumstances.”

2. Two formulations the Court used.

  • The chattel test: “If the intention is to transfer for a price a chattel in which the transferee had no previous property, then the contract is a contract for sale.”
  • The timing-and-mode test: “when and how the property of the dealer in such a transaction passes to the customer: is it by transfer at the time of delivery of the finished article as a chattel, or by accession during the procession of work on fusion to the movable property of the customer? If it is the former, it is a ‘sale’; if it is the latter, it is a ‘works contract’.”
  • And: “the true effect of an accretion made pursuant to a contract has to be judged not by artificial rules but from the intention of the parties to the contract”; “The predominant object of the contract, the circumstances of the case and the custom of the trade provide a guide.”

3. The decisive contractual terms in Kone.

  1. 90 per cent of the price payable on mere signing of the contract;
  2. the balance 10 per cent payable on commissioning, or within 30 days of the offer to commission — “even if the said event of commissioning of the LIFT fails to occur due to any reason not attributable to the Petitioner or beyond its control”;
  3. if within the 52-week contract period the whole of the components were ready for despatch at the petitioner's premises, the purchaser was bound to pay the balance 10 per cent within 90 days of such availability;
  4. the lifts were to be handed over to the purchaser on erection.

These terms showed that the purchaser was buying a branded lift whose price he essentially had to pay whether or not it was ever installed. Installation was the mode of delivery, not the substance of the bargain.

4. Holding. “Having regard to the essential ingredients of the contractual terms, it is difficult to hold that the supply of LIFT by the Petitioner to its Purchaser can be called as a ‘Works Contract’ and, therefore, since the very first condition is not fulfilled, the other conditions are of no consequence in order to invoke Article 366(29A)(b).” Because the transaction already satisfies ‘sale’ under the Sale of Goods Act, “the question of deemed sale does not arise”.

5. Reconciling Larsen & Toubro. In L&T ((2014) 1 SCC 708) a three-Judge Bench held that a building/development contract is a works contract and that “the term ‘works contract’ in Article 366(29-A)(b) takes within its fold all genre of works contract”. Kone did not dissent; it distinguished: the L&T ratio arose “in the peculiar facts relating to that case where it related to construction of a building by virtue of the contract between the developer and owner on the one side and the prospective purchaser on the other side”, and “it is difficult to apply the said ratio rendered in the context of the said contract as applicable universally in all sorts of contracts where some element of work is involved”. Note also L&T's own clarification at para 115: construction by a developer “would be works contract only from the stage the developer enters into a contract with the flat purchaser”.

6. The contrasting authority. Sentinel Rolling Shutters (AIR 1978 SC 545) is the nearest pre-1983 contrary illustration: there the component parts were delivered ex-works but the shutter did not exist until grouted and bolted on the premises, so there was no sale. The difference is in the payment terms and in whether the purchaser had agreed to buy a finished, branded article.

Exam technique. This is the most ‘current’ question on the paper and the one most often answered badly. Three errors to avoid: (i) citing L&T alone and concluding ‘works contract’ — Kone is a Constitution Bench and is directly on lifts; (ii) forgetting to say that Art. 366(29-A)(b) is not even engaged because the transaction is an actual sale; (iii) omitting the payment terms, which are the entire ratio. Give the tests, then the terms, then the holding, then the reconciliation.

2013CT · OC Q3(b) · sale and hire-purchase
Question Distinguish between the following with relevant cases: (b) Sale and hire-purchase agreement.
Model answer

The statutory frame. A sale is governed by the Sale of Goods Act, 1930; a hire-purchase agreement principally by the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 (ss. 3–5 are in your syllabus). Under s. 3 every hire-purchase agreement must be in writing and signed by all the parties — unlike a sale, which under s. 5(2) SOGA requires no formality at all. s. 4 requires the agreement to state the hire-purchase price, the cash price, the date, the number and amount of instalments, their dates or the mode of determining them, and a description of the goods sufficient to identify them; by s. 4(3), failure to state the hire-purchase price or the cash price renders the agreement void. s. 5 requires the owner to furnish a copy to the hirer, and on failure he “shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement” while the hirer may recover what he has paid.

The case to cite — Sundaram Finance Ltd. v. State of Kerala (AIR 1966 SC 1178 : (1966) 2 SCR 828), which the DU syllabus lists against ss. 3–5 of the Hire-Purchase Act. A financier bought vehicles at the customer's request, let them out on hire-purchase and collected the instalments, remaining the registered owner throughout, with property to pass only on payment of the last instalment. The Court held that the hiring is one transaction and the purchase is another; the ‘option to purchase’ is not an agreement to sell; and during the hiring the financier is the owner and the hirer only a bailee.

Contrast an instalment sale. Under s. 5(1) SOGA a contract may provide for payment by instalments and still be an outright sale. So an instalment sale is a sale with deferred payment; a hire-purchase is a bailment with an option. The commercial cash-flows are identical; the legal consequences are not.

Exam technique. The single sentence that earns the marks: “an instalment sale is a sale with deferred payment; a hire-purchase is a bailment with an option.” Add s. 3 of the Hire-Purchase Act (writing mandatory) as the sharpest formal contrast with s. 5(2) SOGA, and close with Art. 366(29-A)(c).
2015Special Contracts · OC Q4 · notes (any two)
Question Attempt briefly any two of the following: (a) Condition as to title  (b) Hire-Purchase Agreements  (c) Rules relating to delivery.
Model answers — three brief notes
(a) Condition as to title

s. 14(a) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 implies, unless the circumstances show a different intention, a condition on the part of the seller that — in the case of a sale — he has a right to sell the goods, and — in the case of an agreement to sell — he will have a right to sell at the time when the property is to pass. s. 14(b) adds an implied warranty of quiet possession, and s. 14(c) an implied warranty of freedom from encumbrances. The rule is an expression of nemo dat quod non habet (s. 27): no one can transfer a better title than he has. It is a condition, so breach entitles the buyer to repudiate and recover the price (Rowland v. Divall, (1923) 2 KB 500 — the buyer of a stolen car recovered the full price even after months of use, because he never got title). The exceptions to nemo dat are in ss. 27–30: sale by a mercantile agent (s. 27 proviso / s. 2(9)), sale by one of joint owners (s. 28), sale under a voidable contract (s. 29), and sale by a seller in possession after sale (s. 30(1)) or a buyer in possession before sale (s. 30(2)). Note the link to this Topic: the syllabus's ‘condition as to title’ is the s. 14 counterpart of the ‘transfer of general property’ requirement in s. 4(1) and s. 2(11).

(b) Hire-Purchase Agreements

A hire-purchase agreement is a bailment of goods coupled with an option to purchase on payment of the last instalment. Governed by the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972: s. 3 — the agreement must be in writing and signed by all parties, and the hirer may terminate at any time before the final payment; s. 4 — it must state the hire-purchase price, the cash price, the date, the number and amount of instalments, the date or mode of determining each instalment, and a description of the goods sufficient to identify them, and is void if it omits either price; s. 5 — the owner must furnish a copy of the agreement to the hirer within the prescribed period, and on default “shall not be entitled to enforce the agreement” while the hirer may recover the sums already paid. Property passes only when the option is exercised — see Sundaram Finance Ltd. v. State of Kerala (AIR 1966 SC 1178). For tax, Art. 366(29-A)(c) deems the delivery of goods on hire-purchase or any system of payment by instalments to be a sale.

(c) Rules relating to delivery

Delivery is defined in s. 2(2) as voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another. The rules are in ss. 31–37: s. 32 (delivery and payment are concurrent conditions); s. 33 (delivery may be actual, symbolic or constructive — e.g. handing over the keys of a warehouse or a document of title); s. 34 (part delivery may operate as delivery of the whole for the purpose of passing property); s. 35 (the buyer must apply for delivery; the seller must be ready and willing); s. 36(1) (whether the buyer must take delivery in lots depends on the contract); s. 36(2) (unless otherwise agreed, the expenses of putting the goods into a deliverable state are borne by the seller); s. 36(3) (where goods are sent by a route involving sea transit, the buyer must be given notice of insurance, or the risk of sea transit is on the seller); s. 36(4) (delivery to a carrier or wharfinger is prima facie delivery to the buyer, but the seller must make a reasonable contract of carriage); s. 36(5) (deterioration in transit is at the buyer's risk even if he is unaware of it); s. 37(1) (delivery of the wrong quantity or of a mixture — the buyer may reject all or accept the contract quantity); s. 37(2); and ss. 38–39 (buyer's right to examine; acceptance). Note the link to this Topic: ‘deliverable state’ is defined in s. 2(3), and ‘delivery’ in s. 2(2) — both inside your ss. 1–10 syllabus.

Exam technique. A ‘write notes on any two’ question rewards structure over length: definition, statutory hook, three or four numbered rules, one case, and one sentence linking it back to the syllabus. About 150–180 words per note. Do not write 400 words on one note and 60 on the other — the marks are equal and a lopsided answer loses goodwill.

2015Special Contracts (LB-304) Q8(b) · sale or return
Question A, a jeweller, delivers some diamonds to B, on sale or return. B pledged the diamonds with C. B failed to pay the price of diamonds and A commenced an action against C to recover the diamonds. Will he succeed?
Model answer

Issue. Whether C, the pledgee, acquired a good title to the diamonds as against A, the true owner.

Rule 1 — what a ‘sale or return’ contract is. Under s. 24 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, where goods are delivered to the buyer on approval or “on sale or return” or other similar terms, the property in the goods passes to the buyer — (a) when he signifies his approval or acceptance, or does any other act adopting the transaction; (b) if he does not signify approval or acceptance but retains the goods without giving notice of rejection, then, if a time has been fixed for the return, on the expiration of that time, and if no time has been fixed, on the expiration of a reasonable time.

Application — B pledged the diamonds. A pledge is an act inconsistent with A's ownership and can only be done by a person asserting dominion over the goods. It is therefore squarely “any other act adopting the transaction” within s. 24(a). B had no title to pledge, because property had not yet passed to him.

Rule 2 — nemo dat quod non habet. s. 27: “Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force, where goods are sold by a person who is not the owner thereof and who does not sell them under the authority or with the consent of the owner, the buyer acquires no better title to the goods than the seller had.” B had no title, so C took nothing — unless C brings himself within one of the statutory exceptions.

Rule 3 — the exceptions, and why they fail.

  1. Mercantile agent (s. 27, proviso 1; s. 2(9)). Only a mercantile agent in possession with the owner's consent can pass a good title. B is a buyer on sale or return, not a mercantile agent, so this exception is unavailable.
  2. Sale by one of several joint owners (s. 28). Inapplicable; A is the sole owner.
  3. Sale under a voidable contract (s. 29). Inapplicable; there is no suggestion of fraud, coercion or misrepresentation by A.
  4. Seller in possession (s. 30(1)) and buyer in possession (s. 30(2)). s. 30(2) requires delivery or transfer under an agreement to sell coupled with actual possession — and the section protects only a buyer who acts in good faith and without notice. C is a pledgee, and the delivery to C was a pledge, not a sale. Even on the widest reading, s. 30(2) would not save a pledgee who had notice that B held the diamonds merely on sale or return.

Rule 4 — the pledgee's position. A pledge (bailment by way of security) transfers only special property — possession, not general property. That is exactly what s. 2(11) says ‘property’ in this Act does not mean. So C's pledge could never have conveyed more than possession, and possession without title is no answer to a true owner's claim.

Conclusion. A will succeed — subject to the facts being proved. Property in the diamonds had not passed to B at the date of the pledge; the pledge was an act adopting the transaction under s. 24(a), but it occurred after B had already parted with possession, so it could not retroactively confer title; B could convey no better title than he had (s. 27); and none of the exceptions in ss. 27–30 applies. A is therefore entitled to recover the diamonds from C. Note: if B had already signified approval or the reasonable time had already expired before the pledge, property would have passed to B under s. 24, and A would then have only a claim for the price against B — this is the fact on which the answer turns.

Exam technique. Problem questions are marked on method, not conclusion. Use the four-step frame — Issue / Rule / Application / Conclusion — and name every section: s. 24 (when property passed), s. 27 (nemo dat), ss. 28–30 (the exceptions, each rejected with a reason), s. 2(11) (general vs special property). Then state the one fact on which the answer turns — examiners reward that sentence more than the conclusion itself.
2013CT · OC Q1 · the scooter resale (allied, for the s. 4 hinge)
Question A sold to B a scooter for a sum of Rs 7,000. B pays the whole price except Rs 3,000 which he promises to pay within 5 days. A holds the possession over the scooter until the rest of the money is paid. But before the expiry of the stipulated time B becomes insolvent. A resold the scooter to C for Rs 7,500 without giving notice to B. B claims from A Rs 500, i.e. profit on resale. Is A liable to pay Rs 500 to B?
Model answer

Step 1 — was this a sale or an agreement to sell? This is the hinge, and it belongs to Topic 5. The price was settled (Rs 7,000) and the goods were specific and ascertained. Under s. 4(3) the contract is a sale if the property has transferred, and an agreement to sell only if the transfer was to take place at a future time or on a condition. A retaining possession pending payment of Rs 3,000 is not by itself an agreement that property shall pass later — it is the ordinary exercise of a lien. On the usual rule in s. 23 (property passes when intended, having regard to the terms, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances) and on the facts as stated, the property passed to B at the time of the contract. It is therefore a sale, and A is an unpaid seller within s. 45(1)(b) — the whole price was not paid and a bill/amount remains due.

Step 2 — A's rights as unpaid seller. Because A retained possession, he has a lien under s. 47(1): he may retain the goods until payment. The right is possessory and, in the words Evershed M.R. used of the solicitor's lien and the Supreme Court adopted in R.D. Saxena, “merely passive and possessory”.

Step 3 — the resale. s. 54(2) permits an unpaid seller who has exercised his right of lien or stoppage to resell the goods. But s. 54(2) is subject to an important condition for perishable goods (no notice required) and otherwise requires, as a matter of fairness and on the terms of the provision, that where the seller intends to resell he should give the buyer notice of his intention — and where notice is not given, the seller cannot recover damages for any loss on the resale and the buyer may, on tender of the price within a reasonable time, recover the goods.

Step 4 — the profit. s. 54(4): “Where the seller expressly reserves a right of resale in case the buyer should make default, and on the buyer making default, resells the goods, the original contract of sale is thereby rescinded, but without prejudice to any claim the seller may have for damages.” Even apart from an express reservation, on a resale under s. 54 the property revests in the seller and the buyer acquires no title to the resale price. The profit on a resale by an unpaid seller who resells in exercise of his statutory right is the seller's, not the buyer's.

Step 5 — but note the notice point, and the insolvency. B became insolvent within the five days. An insolvent buyer cannot ‘tender the price’ so as to defeat the resale; and where the buyer is insolvent the seller's lien under s. 47 is at its strongest. The absence of notice would matter mainly if B were seeking to complain of a loss on the resale. Here B is claiming the profit, which is a different and weaker claim.

Conclusion. A is not liable to pay Rs 500 to B. The property had passed under s. 4(3), so A was an unpaid seller; he lawfully retained possession under s. 47 and resold under s. 54; on such a resale the buyer acquires no title to the resale proceeds; and B, being insolvent, could not have defeated the resale by tendering the balance. The profit of Rs 500 belongs to A. Contrast: had the parties agreed that property was to pass only on full payment, it would have been an agreement to sell under s. 4(3); A would have remained owner throughout; he would have needed no lien and no s. 54 power; and a fortiori the profit would be his own.

Exam technique. This question is nominally on Topic 8 (unpaid seller), but the first two marks are Topic 5 — deciding under s. 4(3) whether there was a sale or an agreement to sell. Many candidates jump straight to s. 54 and lose those marks. Always begin a sale-of-goods problem by locating the property.

§5.3 · Mnemonics

T — P — G — B — P

The five words of s. 4(1): Transfers · Property · Goods · Buyer · Price.

M — A — M — S

The four tests for ‘goods’ under s. 2(7): Moveable · not an Actionable claim or money · Marketable · Severed (or agreed to be).

F — F — F

The three forms of contract in Sentinel: Form 1 divisible (tax it) · Form 2 work-dominant (no sale) · Form 3 sale-dominant (tax it all).

s. 7 vs s. 8

s. 7 = 7 comes first: perished before, contract VOID, test is the seller's KNOWLEDGE.
s. 8 = 8 comes after: perished after, agreement AVOIDED, test is FAULT, and only before the risk passes.

The 46th Amendment — six clauses

(a) transfer otherwise than by contract · (b) works contract · (c) hire-purchase · (d) right to use · (e) supply to a member · (f) food and drink.
“All Boys Can Drink Every Friday.”

Post-1983 three conditions

L&T, para 101(i): (one) a works contract · (two) goods involved in its execution · (three) property in them transferred ‘as goods or in some other form’.

§5.4 · Ten mistakes DU students make

Swipe the table sideways to see all columns →
#The mistakeWhat to write instead
1Saying ‘goods means moveable property’ and stopping there. Add marketability (Delhi Cloth Mills, Eastend Paper, R.D. Saxena) and quote the third limb on things ‘agreed to be severed’.
2Confusing possession with property. ‘Property’ in s. 2(11) means the general property, “and not merely a special property”. Retention of possession is a lien, not ownership.
3Saying that in Vishnu Agencies the Court held ‘consent is not necessary’. The Court held consent was present — found in antecedent volition and in conduct under s. 5(2). Consent is material; only bargaining was missing.
4Citing Gannon Dunkerley without the date and without amendment. Always say pre-1983 and add Art. 366(29-A)(b) and L&T para 71 (“no longer survives”). The case governs assessment years before 2-2-1983 only.
5Omitting the ‘distinct and separate contracts’ saving. Gannon Dunkerley expressly preserved severable contracts in a single instrument — “will stand untouched by the present judgment”. It is the bridge to the Amendment.
6Applying the dominant-intention test to a post-1983 problem. L&T, para 101(vi): “The dominant nature test has no application” and “the enforceability test is also not determinative”.
7Writing ‘Art. 366(29-A) is retrospective’. It is prospective, in force from 2 February 1983. The Coffee Board litigation shows exactly why this matters — the Board's contingency deposits began only from that date.
8Using ‘void’ and ‘avoided’ interchangeably in ss. 7 and 8. s. 7 → void ab initio. s. 8 → avoided (valid until discharged). Both lead to restitution under s. 65 ICA, but the juristic character differs.
9Applying s. 10’s ‘agreement avoided’ rule and then saying ‘reasonable price’. Reasonable price under s. 10 comes only through the proviso, and requires both delivery and appropriation. Otherwise the agreement is avoided outright.
10Treating hire-purchase as an instalment sale. An instalment sale is a sale with deferred payment (s. 5(1)). Hire-purchase is a bailment with an option. Different property, different risk, different remedies.

§5.5 · Three answer templates

Template 1 — ‘Is X goods?’
  1. Quote s. 2(7) in full.
  2. State the four tests: moveable · not actionable claim/money · marketable · severed.
  3. Apply each to X in one sentence.
  4. Give the authority (M.P. Electricity Board / R.D. Saxena / TPA s. 3).
  5. State the contrast (old coin vs currency; standing timber vs shade tree).
  6. Then, separately, ask whether there was a sale at all.
Template 2 — ‘Sale or works contract?’
  1. Date first. Pre- or post-2-2-1983?
  2. Quote s. 4(1) and the two ingredients from Gannon Dunkerley.
  3. State the three forms from Sentinel.
  4. Apply the Purshottam Premji test: did the thing exist as the maker's sole property before delivery?
  5. If post-1983: run the L&T three conditions; state the deemed sale and the measure.
  6. Add the ceiling: no service cost, no tax on immoveable property.
Template 3 — ‘Was there a sale?’ (problem)
  1. Issue in one sentence: did the property pass?
  2. Rule: s. 4(3), s. 2(11), s. 6(3), ss. 18–25.
  3. Apply to each fact — are the goods specific? ascertained? future? was there an unconditional appropriation?
  4. Consequences: risk (s. 26), remedy (s. 55 vs s. 56), insolvency (ss. 45–50).
  5. Conclusion + the one fact on which it turns.
