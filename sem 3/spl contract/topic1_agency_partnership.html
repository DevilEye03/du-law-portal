Concept of Agency — Indian Contract Act, 1872 (Sections 182–238)

Chapter X · "Agency: Appointment and Authority" (ss. 182–195) · "Sub-Agents" · "Ratification" (196–200) · "Revocation of Authority" (201–210) · "Agent's Duty to Principal" (211–221) · "Principal's Duty to Agent" (222–225) · "Effect of Agency on Contracts with Third Persons" (226–238)

1.1 Why "Agency" opens the Partnership paper

Partnership is itself built on agency. Section 18 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 declares that "a partner is the agent of the firm for the purposes of the business of the firm", and Section 4 defines partnership as a business "carried on by all or any of them acting for all". The Supreme Court in K.D. Kamath & Co. v. CIT (DU case material) said expressly: "There is implicit in the second requirement the principle of agency." Lord Wensleydale in Cox v. Hickman put it even more bluntly — "The law as to partnership is undoubtedly a branch of the principal and agent."

So the DU syllabus deliberately begins Special Contracts with agency: you cannot answer a single partnership question without the vocabulary of agency.

1.2 Statutory Definitions — Sections 182 to 185

Section 182 — "Agent" and "Principal" defined
An "agent" is a person employed to do any act for another, or to represent another in dealings with third persons. The person for whom such act is done, or who is so represented, is called the "principal".
§ Anatomy of Section 182 — five things to write in the exam
  1. Two-fold function: the agent may (a) be employed to do an act for another, OR (b) to represent another in dealings with third persons. Either is enough.
  2. Employment, not contract of service: the word used is "employed" — agency need not be a contract of employment; it may be gratuitous (s. 185: consideration is not necessary).
  3. Consent / authorisation: the essence is that the principal authorises; without authorisation there is no agency (though later ratification can cure it — ss. 196–200).
  4. Third person must exist in contemplation: the agent is the channel through which the principal deals with the outside world (s. 226).
  5. No formalities: agency may be created orally, in writing, or by conduct; no writing or registration is required (contrast: a Power of Attorney for immovable property must be registered under s. 17(1)(g), Registration Act, 1908).
Section 183 — Who may employ an agent
Any person who is of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject, and who is of sound mind, may employ an agent.

Rules under s. 183:

  • Major + sound mind = competent to appoint. A minor cannot appoint an agent (his agreements are void ab initio — Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose, (1903) 30 IA 114).
  • The law to which he is subject governs majority — so in India, the Indian Majority Act, 1875 (age 18; and 21 where a guardian of person/property has been appointed by court).
  • A company, a firm, an LLP, a HUF (through its karta), a trustee, the Government (Art. 299, Constitution) may all appoint agents — but only through their authorised human organs.
  • Alien enemy, foreign sovereign, insolvent — special rules apply; an insolvent cannot appoint an agent to deal with property that has vested in the Official Assignee.
Section 184 — Who may be an agent
As between the principal and third persons, any person may become an agent; but no person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind can become an agent, so as to be responsible to his principal according to the provisions in that behalf herein contained.
★ The classic DU trap — s.184 has TWO limbs. Learn them separately.
↔ Swipe table sideways
LimbRuleConsequence
Limb 1 — vis-à-vis third persons ANY person may become an agent (including a minor, a person of unsound mind, even a non-human? no — but a minor yes). The contract made by a minor-agent between the principal and third party is valid and binding on the principal and the third party. The third party cannot escape by saying "your agent was a minor".
Limb 2 — vis-à-vis his own principal A minor or person of unsound mind cannot be held responsible to his principal under ss. 211–225. The principal cannot sue the minor agent for accounts (s.213), for breach of duty (s.212), for misconduct, etc. The internal remedies disappear; only the external validity remains.

Exam illustration: P, a businessman, authorises M, aged 17, to sell his goods. M sells to T. Later M misappropriates the sale proceeds. Held: The sale to T is valid and binds P (Limb 1); T gets good title. But P cannot sue M under s. 213 for accounts or under s. 212 for want of skill (Limb 2) — M is not "responsible to his principal according to the provisions in that behalf herein contained".

Section 185 — Consideration not necessary
No consideration is necessary to create an agency.

Agency is therefore one of the exceptional agreements valid without consideration (along with those under ss. 25(1) and 25(2) — natural love and affection, and compensation for past voluntary services). This is why a purely gratuitous agent is a perfectly lawful agent — he simply loses the right to remuneration under s. 219, but he still owes the duties under ss. 211–218 and still binds his principal.

◆ Real-world examples
  • Real-estate broker / property dealer: classic agent — employed to represent the owner in dealings with buyers. Remuneration = brokerage (s. 219 becomes due only when the act is completed, i.e., the sale actually goes through).
  • Insurance agent / LIC agent: agent of the insurer; the insurer (principal) is bound by the proposals he solicits, subject to his authority.
  • Chartered Accountant filing your ITR: agent for a specific act; a particular agency.
  • Travel agent booking your ticket: acts for you; if he books within authority, the airline's contract is your contract.
  • Del-credere agent: an agent who, for extra commission, guarantees that the third party will pay (Snow White Industrial Corpn. discusses del-credere agency — see Part C). He is first an agent, and additionally a surety.
  • Power of Attorney holder: an agent appointed by a formal registered instrument (Power of Attorney Act, 1882 + s. 17(1)(g) Registration Act, 1908 for immovables).
  • Karta of an HUF: not an "agent" in the s. 182 sense — he has status, not agency (compare s. 5 of the Partnership Act).

  • Power of Attorney Act, 1882 — formal instrument of agency; donee may act in his own name under s. 2.

  • Specific Relief Act, 1963, s. 14(1)(b) — a contract which is determinable in character or which involves personal skill/volition cannot be specifically enforced. Agency, depending on personal confidence, is generally not specifically enforceable.

1.5 Scope & Limitation of Authority — Sections 186 to 189

Section 186 — Authority may be expressed or implied
The authority of an agent may be expressed or implied.
Section 187 — Definitions of express and implied authority
An authority is said to be express when it is given by words spoken or written. An authority is said to be implied when it is to be inferred from the circumstances of the case; and things spoken or written, or the ordinary course of dealing, may be accounted circumstances of the case.
Section 188 — Extent of agent's authority
An agent having an authority to do an act has authority to do every lawful thing which is necessary in order to do such act.

An agent having an authority to carry on a business has authority to do every lawful thing necessary for the purpose, or usually done in the course of, conducting such business.

Illustration (b) to s. 188: A is employed by B, a shopkeeper, to conduct his shop. A may order goods required in the shop, and may give receipts for them — but A cannot bind B by borrowing money at interest (that is not "necessary" or "usually done").
Section 189 — Agent's authority in an emergency
An agent has authority, in an emergency, to do all such acts for the purpose of protecting his principal from loss as would be done by a person of ordinary prudence, in his own case, under similar circumstances.
§ The three-limb test of s. 188 — write it as a formula

Express authority + implied authority, where implied =

  1. Incidental authority — everything necessary to do the authorised act;
  2. Customary authority — everything usually done in that trade/business (usage of trade, s. 1 of ICA saves usage);
  3. Emergency authority — s. 189, judged by the standard of "a person of ordinary prudence".

Cross-link to Partnership Act: s. 19 (implied authority of a partner), s. 20 (extension/restriction of implied authority by contract — restriction binds only those third parties who know of it) and s. 21 (partner's authority in an emergency) are the partnership version of ss. 187–189. Quote both sets in one answer and you get full marks.

1.6 Appointment of a Sub-Agent — Sections 190 to 195 DU favourite

§ The general rule: delegatus non potest delegare

A delegate cannot further delegate. If I choose you as my agent because of your personal skill, judgment or confidence, you cannot hand my work over to a stranger. This is the Latin maxim that underlies s. 190 — and its exceptions are what the examiner is really testing.

Section 190 — When agent cannot delegate
An agent cannot lawfully employ another to perform acts which he has expressly or impliedly undertaken to perform personally, unless —
  1. by the ordinary custom of trade a sub-agent may be employed; or
  2. from the nature of the agency, a sub-agent must be employed.
Section 191 — "Sub-agent" defined
A "sub-agent" is a person employed by, and acting under the control of, the original agent in the business of the agency.

Section 192 — Representation of principal by sub-agent properly appointed
Where a sub-agent is properly appointed, the principal is, so far as regards third persons, represented by the sub-agent, and is bound by and is responsible for his acts, as if he were an agent originally appointed by the principal.

Agent's responsibility for sub-agent: the agent is responsible to the principal for the acts of the sub-agent.

Sub-agent's responsibility: the sub-agent is responsible for his acts to the agent, but not to the principal, except in cases of fraud and wilful wrong.
Section 193 — Agent's responsibility for sub-agent appointed without authority
Where an agent, without having authority to do so, has appointed a person to act as a sub-agent, the agent stands towards such person in the relation of a principal to an agent, and is responsible for his acts both to the principal and to third parties; the principal is not represented by, or responsible for the acts of, the person so employed; nor is that person responsible to the principal.
Section 194 — Relation between principal and person duly appointed by agent to act in business of agency
Where an agent, holding an express or implied authority to name another person to act for the principal in the business of the agency, has named another person accordingly, such person is not a sub-agent, but an agent of the principal for such part of the business of the agency as is entrusted to him.
Section 195 — Agent's duty in naming such person
In selecting such agent for his principal, the original agent is bound to exercise the same amount of discretion as a man of ordinary prudence would exercise in his own case; and, if he does this, he is not responsible to the principal for the acts or negligence of the agent so selected.
◆ Examples
  • Sub-agent: You authorise a travel agent to book a ticket. The travel agent, without asking, hands the work to a junior in his office. The junior is a sub-agent. If the junior blunders, the travel agent is liable to you (s. 193/192), and you are bound to the airline if the act was in the ordinary course of that employment.
  • Named agent: A solicitor in Delhi is instructed to file a case in the Bombay High Court. He is authorised to engage a Bombay advocate. The Bombay advocate is a named/substituted agent of the client under s. 194 — not the solicitor's sub-agent. If the Bombay advocate is negligent, the client sues him, and the solicitor is liable only if he failed the "ordinary prudence in selection" test under s. 195.
  • Custom of trade / nature of agency (s. 190 exceptions): A clearing and forwarding agent, a shipping agent, a bank's correspondent bank — sub-delegation is inherent in the nature of the business.

1.7 Ratification — Sections 196 to 200 Asked verbatim in DU 2015

Section 196 — Right of person as to acts done for him without his authority. Effect of ratification
Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another, but without his knowledge or authority, he may elect to ratify or to disown such acts. If he ratify them, the same effects will follow as if they had been performed by his authority.
Section 197 — Ratification may be expressed or implied
Ratification may be expressed, or may be implied in the conduct of the person on whose behalf the acts are done. Illustration (a): A, without authority, buys goods for B. Afterwards B sells them to C on his own account; B's conduct implies a ratification of the purchase. Illustration (b): A, without B's authority, lends B's money to C. Afterwards B accepts interest on the money from C. B's conduct implies a ratification of the loan.
Section 198 — Knowledge requisite for valid ratification
No valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is materially defective.
Section 199 — Effect of ratifying unauthorized act forming part of a transaction
A person ratifying an unauthorized act done on his behalf ratifies the whole of the transaction of which such act formed a part.
Section 200 — Ratification of unauthorized act cannot injure third person
An act done by one person on behalf of another, without such other person's authority, cannot, if ratified, have the effect of injuring a third person; unless the third person, at the time of doing the act, knew that there was no authority, and that it was intended that the person doing the act should take the risk of ratification or repudiation.

The Doctrine of Relation Back

Ratification is not a fresh contract — it is an election that relates back to the date of the unauthorised act (omnis ratihabitio retrotrahitur et mandato priori aequiparatur). Once ratified, the position is as if the agent had authority from the very beginning. That is why the principal can sue the third party directly on the ratified contract.

Diagram 4 — Flowchart: Is there a valid ratification?
Six cumulative conditions; failure at ANY step = no ratification = the "agent" is personally liable under s. 235
Fig. 4 — Exam tip: DU has repeatedly asked illustration (b) to s. 197 ("A lends B's money to C; B accepts interest; can B later disown the lending?"). Answer: No — accepting interest is conduct implying ratification, and under s. 199 he ratifies the whole transaction of lending, not just the interest.
§ Six conditions for valid ratification — one line each
  1. Act must be done on behalf of another and in his name / for his benefit (s. 196).
  2. Principal must be in existence and competent at the time of the act — a company cannot ratify a pre-incorporation contract (Kelner v. Baxter, (1866) LR 2 CP 174).
  3. Ratification must be within a reasonable time and before the third party withdraws (s. 200).
  4. Full knowledge of material facts — no valid ratification if knowledge is "materially defective" (s. 198).
  5. Must not injure a third person (s. 200).
  6. Must be of the whole transaction — you cannot ratify the beneficial part and disown the onerous part (s. 199). You take the bitter with the sweet.

1.8 Revocation & Termination of Authority — Sections 201 to 210 Asked verbatim in DU 2015 & 2017

Section 201 — Termination of agency
An agency is terminated by —
  1. the principal revoking his authority; or
  2. the agent renouncing the business of the agency; or
  3. the business of the agency being completed; or
  4. either the principal or agent dying or becoming of unsound mind; or
  5. the principal being adjudicated an insolvent under the provisions of any Act for the time being in force for the relief of insolvent debtors.

SectionRulePractical effect
s. 202 Termination of agency where agent has an interest in subject-matter. Where the agent has himself an interest in the property which forms the subject-matter of the agency, the agency cannot, in the absence of an express contract, be terminated to the prejudice of such interest. Irrevocable agency. E.g. A gives B authority to sell A's land and to pay himself out of the proceeds a debt A owes B — A cannot revoke, and the agency survives A's death/insanity.
s. 203 When principal may revoke agent's authority. The principal may, save as is otherwise provided by s. 202, revoke the authority given to his agent at any time before the authority has been exercised so as to bind the principal. — Partial revocation: the principal may revoke a general authority and retain a particular one, or vice-versa. Once the agent has acted and the third party is bound, revocation is too late — it cannot undo a completed contract.
s. 204 Revocation where authority has been partly exercised. The principal cannot revoke the authority given to his agent after the authority has been partly exercised, so far as regards such acts and obligations as arise from acts already done in the agency. Protects the third party and the agent for things already set in motion.
s. 205 Compensation for revocation by principal, or renunciation by agent. Where there is an express or implied contract that the agency should be continued for any period of time, the principal must make compensation to the agent, or the agent to the principal, as the case may be, for any previous revocation or renunciation of the agency without sufficient cause. This is the damages remedy — the revocation itself is still valid; only compensation follows. (Contrast s. 202, where revocation is ineffective.)
s. 206 Notice of revocation or renunciation. Reasonable notice must be given of such revocation or renunciation; otherwise the damage thereby resulting to the principal or the agent, as the case may be, must be made good to the one by the other. Notice + reasonable time.
s. 207 Revocation and renunciation may be expressed or implied in the conduct of the principal or agent respectively. E.g. principal appoints another agent for the same act = implied revocation of the first.
s. 208 When termination of agent's authority takes effect. As against the agent — when it becomes known to him; as against third persons — when it becomes known to them. Protection of innocent third parties. Compare s. 45, Partnership Act — a partner's authority continues after dissolution until public notice is given.
s. 209 Agent's duty on termination of agency by principal's death or insanity. When an agency is terminated by the principal dying or becoming of unsound mind, the agent is bound to take, on behalf of the representatives of his late principal, all reasonable steps for the protection and preservation of the interests entrusted to him. Agency is dead, but a residual duty survives.
s. 210 Termination of sub-agent's authority. The termination of the authority of an agent causes the termination (subject to the rules herein contained regarding the termination of an agent's authority) of the authority of all sub-agents appointed by him. Derived authority falls with the principal authority.

1.9 Rights, Duties and Liabilities of Principal & Agent — Sections 211 to 225

A. Duties of the AGENT (ss. 211–218)
  • s. 211 — Conduct the principal's business according to the principal's directions; in the absence of directions, according to the custom prevailing in the same business at the place.
  • s. 212 — Exercise the skill and diligence of a person of ordinary prudence; the agent must compensate the principal for direct consequences of his neglect, want of skill or misconduct. (But not for remote/indirect loss.)
  • s. 213 — Render proper accounts to the principal on demand.
  • s. 214 — Communicate with the principal; use all reasonable diligence in communicating; obtain his instructions in cases of difficulty.
  • s. 215 — Not to deal on his own account in the business of the agency without the principal's prior consent and full disclosure of material circumstances. If he does, the principal may repudiate the transaction if the case falls within s. 215's conditions.
  • s. 216 — If the agent so deals on his own account without consent, the principal is entitled to claim from the agent any benefit which may have resulted to him from the transaction.
  • s. 217 — Right of the agent to retain out of sums received on the principal's account all moneys due to himself in respect of advances, expenses and remuneration.
  • s. 218 — Duty to pay over all sums received on the principal's account, after deductions under s. 217.
B. Rights of the AGENT (ss. 217, 219–225)
  • s. 217 — Right of retainer (self-help deduction) out of sums received, for advances, expenses and remuneration.
  • s. 219 — Remuneration becomes due only when the agent has rendered the service / completed the act. (Illustration: an agent employed to sell a house earns commission only on actual sale.)
  • s. 220 — No remuneration for business misconducted. An agent who is guilty of misconduct in the business of the agency is not entitled to any remuneration in respect of that part of the business which he has misconducted.
  • s. 221 — Agent's lien — in the absence of any contract to the contrary, the agent may retain goods, papers and other property (movable or immovable) of the principal received by him, until his dues for remuneration, disbursements and services are paid or accounted for.
  • s. 222 — Indemnity for lawful acts. The employer of an agent is bound to indemnify him against the consequences of all lawful acts done by the agent in exercise of the authority conferred.
  • s. 223 — Indemnity for acts done in good faith. Where the act is done in good faith though it causes injury to a third person, the principal must indemnify the agent.
  • s. 224 — Non-liability of employer for a criminal act. Where the agent does a criminal act (not merely an act "not lawful"), the employer is not liable to the agent, and the agent is not entitled to indemnity — even though the act was directed by the employer.
  • s. 225 — Compensation for injury by the principal's neglect or want of skill.
§ Statutory text worth reproducing
Section 224 — Non-liability of employer of agent to do a criminal act
Where one person employs another to do an act which is criminal, the employer is not liable to the agent, either upon an express or an implied promise, to indemnify him against the consequences of that act.
Illustrations: (a) A employs B to beat C and agrees to indemnify him against all consequences — B is not entitled to be indemnified. (b) A, a engineer, directs B, his assistant, to lower the speed of a train in a manner which is not criminal — A must indemnify B.

If an agent deals on his own account in the business of the agency, without first obtaining the consent of his principal and acquainting him with all material circumstances which have come to his own knowledge on the subject, the principal may repudiate the transaction, if the case shows either —
  1. that any material fact has been dishonestly concealed from him by the agent; or
  2. that the dealings of the agent have been disadvantageous to him.
Section 216 — Principal's right to benefit gained by agent dealing on his own account in business of agency
If an agent, without the knowledge of his principal, deals in the business of the agency on his own account instead of on account of his principal, the principal is entitled to claim from the agent any benefit which may have resulted to him from the transaction.

  1. Severability (s. 227): P authorises A to buy 50 bags of rice at ₹800/bag. A buys 80 bags at ₹800. The contract is severable — P is bound for 50 bags under s. 227, not for 30.

Is a firm a separate legal entity?

No — not in the same sense as a company. But Indian law gives the firm a quasi-legal personality:

Not a full juristic person
  • A firm is only a collective name for the individuals who compose it.
  • It cannot sue/be sued in its own name (must sue through partners; CPC Order 30).
  • It is not a "person" for Art. 19 purposes in the fullest sense.
But a "person" for many purposes
  • Income-tax Act, 1961, s. 2(31)(iv) — a firm is separately assessable as a "person".
  • CPC, Order 30 — suit by or against firm in the firm name.
  • Registration possible under Chapter VII, PA 1932 (ss. 58–59).
Tax personality
  • Dulichand Laxminarayan v. CIT — firm not a "person" for s. 4(2), Partnership Act purposes.
  • Short note in the DU Partnership Act handout: Deoha F. Guzdar (Bombay) v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 74 — in law the firm name is a compendious method of describing the partners.

2.2 Section 5 — Partnership not created by status

Section 5 — Partnership not created by status (verbatim)
The relation of partnership arises from contract and not from status; and, in particular, the members of a Hindu undivided family carrying on a family business as such, or a Burmese Buddhist husband and wife carrying on business as such, are not partners in such business.
★ The three words that win marks: CONTRACT, not STATUS

Partnership is consensual. You cannot become a partner by birth, by marriage, by inheritance, by operation of law, or by being a member of a family that happens to run a business — unless you actually enter into an agreement. This single sentence disposes of most "is he a partner?" problem questions.

2.4 Sections 7 & 8 — Partnership at will and Particular partnership

Section 7 — Partnership at will
Where no provision is made by contract between the partners for (a) the duration of their partnership, or (b) the determination of their partnership, the partnership is "partnership at will".
  • DEFAULT category — if the deed is silent on both duration AND determination, it is at will.
  • Dissolution: s. 43 — any partner may dissolve by giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to dissolve. The firm is dissolved from the date mentioned in the notice, or if no date is mentioned, from the date of communication of the notice.
  • A firm constituted for a fixed term which continues after expiry becomes a partnership at will (s. 17(b)) — and, importantly, the mutual rights/duties remain the same so far as consistent with the incidents of partnership at will.
  • Distinguish: duration (how long) vs determination (how it ends). Silence on both is required. If the deed says "for 7 years", it is not at will, even though it is silent on the mode of determination.
Section 8 — Particular partnership
A person may become a partner with another person in particular adventures or undertakings.
  • Also called "particular partnership" / "joint venture" / partnership for a single adventure.
  • The partnership automatically ends when the adventure is completed — no notice is needed (contrast s. 43 for a partnership at will).
  • Is a single venture "carrying on a business"? Yes — the Supreme Court and the Act both accept that a single adventure can be a business (DU 2003 asked this directly).
  • s. 17(c) — where a firm constituted for one or more adventures carries out other adventures, the mutual rights and duties in respect of those other adventures are the same as those for the original adventures.
  • Contrast s. 42(b) — "the expiry of the term" and "the completion of the adventure" are two separate grounds of compulsory dissolution.

2.5 Concept & Nature of Unlimited Liability Partnerships

§ Meaning

An Unlimited Liability Partnership is the ordinary partnership governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, in which the liability of every partner (other than a minor admitted to benefits) for the debts and obligations of the firm is unlimited, joint and several, and extends to his whole personal estate. There is no ceiling, no corporate veil, and no separate legal person standing between the creditor and the partner's personal assets.

◆ A concrete illustration of "unlimited"

Firm ABC owes ₹2 crore to a supplier. The firm's assets realise only ₹60 lakh. The deficiency of ₹1.4 crore is recovered from the personal estates of A, B and C — their houses, vehicles, bank balances and investments. Under s. 25 the supplier may, if he chooses, recover the entire ₹1.4 crore from A alone; A may then claim contribution from B and C. If a partner is insolvent, the loss falls on the solvent partners. Contrast an LLP, where the creditor's remedy stops at the LLP's property (s. 27(4), LLP Act 2008).

★ Writ of execution and partnership property

Under CPC, Order 21 Rule 49 & 50, a decree against a firm may be executed against (i) the partnership property, (ii) the separate property of any partner against whom the decree has been passed (where he has appeared/been served as a partner), and (iii) with leave of the court, the separate property of a partner who has not been served. This is the procedural engine of unlimited liability.

1. K.D. Kamath & Co. v. Commissioner of Income-tax
(1971) 2 SCC 873 · Supreme Court of India · C.A. Vaidialingam, J. · Bench: J.C. Shah & C.A. Vaidialingam, JJ.
"The entire deed must be considered to decide the existence of partnership" — headnote in the DU case material
DU CASE MATERIAL pp. 1–9
Facts

The appellant was a firm of six partners constituted under a deed of partnership dated 20 March 1959, though the business was recited to have been carried on in partnership from 1 October 1958. The firm was registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 on about 11 August 1959.

For assessment year 1959-60 the firm applied to the Income-tax Officer under s. 26-A of the Income-tax Act, 1922 for registration in the name of M/s K.D. Kamath & Company. The ITO refused, holding that no genuine partnership had been brought into existence and that the business was the sole concern of K.D. Kamath. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner confirmed. The Department did not challenge the genuineness of the document — only its legal effect.

The Appellate Tribunal reversed: it held that the two essential requirements — agreement to share profits, and each party acting as agent of all — were fully satisfied, and directed registration. At the Revenue's instance the Tribunal referred the question of law to the High Court, which answered against the assessee. The assessee appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Whether, on the facts and in the circumstances, M/s K.D. Kamath & Co. could be granted registration under s. 26-A of the Income-tax Act for A.Y. 1959-60?
  2. In substance: whether the partnership deed dated 20-3-1959 creates the relationship of partners inter se between the parties as understood in law — i.e., whether it satisfies s. 4 of the Partnership Act.
  3. Specifically, whether the concentration of management, control, banking and borrowing powers in one partner destroys the element of agency which is essential to partnership.
Arguments
For the assessee–appellant (Mr S.K. Venkataranga Iyengar)
  • The High Court's view that the essential element of agency is absent is erroneous.
  • Read as a whole, the deed leaves no room for doubt that there is an agreement to share profits and losses in the proportion mentioned — so one essential ingredient is satisfied.
  • Though a large amount of control over the conduct of the business is left with the first partner, that circumstance by itself does not militate against the view of one partner acting as agent of the others.
  • Relied on High Court and Supreme Court decisions where, under similar circumstances, greater control in one partner was held not to defeat a partnership arrangement.
For the Revenue (Mr S.K. Iyer)
  • Supported the High Court's reasoning in its entirety.
  • Both questions — agreement to share profits/losses, and each partner being entitled to act as agent of all — must be determined by looking at all the facts as borne out by the deed.
  • On such consideration, the High Court correctly held that the right of one partner to act as agent of all does not exist here.
  • Therefore the opinion that the appellant is not eligible for registration under s. 26-A is correct.
  • Additionally relied on s. 14 of the Partnership Act: since there is no contract to the contrary, the assets brought in by party No. 1 belong to the firm; consequently any partner as agent should be able to pledge the firm's interest or raise a loan — and cl. 9 which restricts that right negatives the theory of agency.
Decision (Supreme Court — C.A. Vaidialingam, J.)

Appeal allowed; the question of law answered in the affirmative, in favour of the assessee. The deed of 20 March 1959 does create a partnership.

  1. Complete picture of the document: "In considering the question whether the partnership deed creates the relationship of partners as between the parties thereto, as understood in law, it is desirable to have a complete picture of the entire document." (¶ 11)
  2. First condition satisfied: cl. 5 (net profits and losses shared in specified proportions), read with cls. 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16, establishes that "the sole proprietary concern of K.D. Kamath has vanished" — the right to share profits and to bear losses is "more than amply satisfied". (¶¶ 17–18)
  3. The correct formulation of the s. 4 test (¶ 23, quoted in full in Part B above).
  4. Nomenclature not decisive (¶ 28): "the mere nomenclature given to a document is by itself not sufficient to hold that the document in question is one of partnership… The fact that the exclusive power and control, by agreement of the parties, is vested in one partner or the further circumstance that only one partner can operate the bank accounts or borrow on behalf of the firm are not destructive of the theory of partnership provided the two essential conditions are satisfied."
  5. Second condition satisfied: even assuming the entire control and management is vested in K.D. Kamath and parties 2 to 6 work under his direction, "from all the other circumstances it is clear that the conduct of business by party No. 1 is done by him acting for all the partners". Clause 16 reinforces this — the affairs are managed by party No. 1 "really for the mutual gain and benefits of all the partners". (¶¶ 29–30)
  6. On s. 18 (¶ 31): a partner is the agent of the firm — but that section is subject to the provisions of the Act. Parties may, under s. 11, agree their mutual rights and duties. Section 18 must be read with s. 4; once s. 4 is satisfied, "there is no escape from the conclusion that in law a partnership has come into existence". Section 18 only emphasises the principle of agency which is already incorporated in the definition of "partnership" under s. 4.
  7. On cl. 9 (¶¶ 32–33): vis-à-vis the outside world, so long as parties 2 to 6 are held out as partners, "their acts would bind the whole partnership". Clause 9 is only an inter se arrangement; it does not negative agency — on the contrary it presupposes it, and merely places a contractual restriction on borrowing. The s. 14 argument was rejected because s. 14 itself is subject to contract between the parties.
Principles evolved
  1. Whole-document rule: the existence of partnership must be decided on a reading of the entire instrument, not on isolated clauses or on its label.
  2. Two conditions under s. 4: (i) agreement to share profits or losses; (ii) business carried on by all or any of them acting for all — agency is implicit in (ii).
  3. Concentration of powers is not fatal: exclusive control, sole operation of bank accounts and sole borrowing power in one partner do not destroy partnership, provided the two conditions are met.
  4. Restrictions in the deed are inter se only: they bind the partners between themselves; they do not defeat agency as against third parties (read with ss. 19–20 and holding out).
  5. s. 18 is clarificatory: it only restates the agency already embedded in s. 4; it cannot be used to disprove a partnership that s. 4 establishes.
  6. Loss-sharing is strong evidence: a clause making partners liable to make good losses (cl. 15) is a powerful indicator of partnership, far stronger than a mere profit-share.
  7. Goodwill in one partner is not decisive (cl. 7) — it is a term of the bargain, subject to contract.

Kamath: dominance ≠ absence of agency. Note also that the context of the case is tax registration under s. 26-A — always say so, because the examiner knows whether you have actually read the case.

2. Cox v. Hickman
(1860) 8 H.L.C. 268 · House of Lords · Lord Campbell L.C., Lord Cranworth, Lord Wensleydale
"Mode of determining the existence of a partnership – sharing of profits – creditor-debtor relationship" — headnote in the DU case material
DU CASE MATERIAL pp. 10–15
Facts

Smith and Smith carried on business as iron-masters under the name B. Smith & Son. They got into difficulties and called a meeting of their creditors. They then executed a deed of arrangement dated 13 November 1849 in favour of their creditors. The parties were:

  • of the first part — S. and S. (the debtors);
  • of the second part — five of the creditors, including Cox and Wheatcroft;
  • of the third part — the general body of creditors.

Under the deed the five creditors of the second part were to carry on the business as trustees for the creditors under the name "The Stanton Iron Company", and to divide the net income, after expenses, among the general creditors, such net income "to be deemed to be of the creditors to be held"; a majority in value of the creditors present at a meeting could make rules for conducting the business or order its discontinuance; when all debts were paid the trustees were to hold the assigned property in trust for S. and S. themselves; and there was a covenant by the executing parties not to sue S. and S. for their debts.

Cox never in fact acted as a trustee; Wheatcroft resigned six weeks after the deed — and before the goods for which the bills were now sued had been supplied. No new trustees were appointed in their place. The remaining three creditors carried on the business under the deed.

Hickman supplied goods to the business and drew three bills of exchange, which were accepted on behalf of the Stanton Iron Company by one of those three creditors. Hickman then sued Cox and Wheatcroft on the bills, alleging they were liable as partners in the Stanton Iron Company, being two of the five creditors who had executed the deed.

Issues
  1. Whether the defendants, by executing the deed of 13 November 1849 as creditors of Messrs. Smith & Co., rendered themselves liable to those who afterwards dealt with the trustees carrying on the concern under the new firm of "The Stanton Iron Company".
  2. Whether there is such a participation in the profits of the new firm by the creditors of the old firm as to make them partners in the new firm.
  3. Whether the creditors, by executing the deed, authorised the trustees as their agents either to purchase goods or to accept bills.
Arguments
Hickman (plaintiff / respondent)
  • Cox and Wheatcroft were two of the five creditors who were the original parties of the second part to the deed and executed it.
  • The deed gave the creditors a share of the net income of the business — which is participation in profits.
  • Therefore, although they "never acted or held themselves out as partners", the creditors of the old firm are partners in the new firm and are liable on the bills.
  • The old rule (Waugh v. Carver): participation in net profits = partnership in law.
Cox & Wheatcroft (defendants / appellants)
  • The creditors never intended to incur such liability; the creditors of the new firm cannot be supposed to have dealt with the firm in the belief that they had a remedy against all or any of the old firm's creditors.
  • They are not partners inter se and could derive no profits beyond payment of the debts due to them from the old firm.
  • The business was to be carried on by the trustees till the debts were paid, then transferred back to the Smiths — i.e., the trade remained the trade of the debtors/their trustees.
  • Executing the deed did not make the trustees their agents.
Decision (House of Lords — judgment reversed)

Held: the defendants were NOT liable as acceptors of the bills; they were not partners. The judgment of the Court of Common Pleas was reversed.

LORD CAMPBELL L.C. — "It is quite clear that the creditors of the old firm, by executing the deed, never intended to incur such a liability… Is there such a participation in the profits of the new firm by the creditors of the old firm, as to make them partners in the new firm? They certainly are not partners inter se … and they could derive no profits from the new business, beyond the payment of the debts due to them from the old firm. There was a formal release of these debts; but we must look at the real nature of the transaction, according to the understanding of all who were parties to it." — "I am of opinion that the creditors of the old firm cannot be considered, by executing the deed, as having authorised the trustees as their agents either to purchase the goods or to accept the bills."

LORD CRANWORTH — the crucial passage:

"The liability of one partner for the acts of his co-partner is in truth the liability of a principal for the acts of his agent. Where two or more persons are engaged as partners in an ordinary trade, each of them has an implied authority from the others to bind them all by contract entered into according to the usual course of business in that trade. Every partner in trade is for the ordinary purposes of the trade, the agent of his co-partners, and all are therefore liable for the ordinary trade contracts of the others. Partners may stipulate among themselves that some one of them only shall enter into particular contracts… but with such private arrangements third persons, dealing with the firm without notice, have no concern. …

Taking this to be the ground of liability as a partner, it seems to me to follow that the mere concurrence of creditors in an arrangement under which they permit their debtor, or trustees for their debtor, to continue his trade, applying the profits in discharge of their demands, does not make them partners with their debtor, or the trustee. The debtor is still the person solely interested in the profits, save only that he has mortgaged them to his creditors. … The trade is not carried on by or on account of the creditors; though their consent is necessary … But the trade still remains the trade of the debtor or his trustees." — Cox v. Hickman, per Lord Cranworth (DU case material pp. 11–13)

On the special powers given to the creditors (to order discontinuance, or to make rules for management): "These powers do not appear to me to alter the case… The effect of these powers is only to qualify their consent. … The trade did not become a trade carried on for them as principals, because they might have insisted on taking possession of the stock…" He added the famous practical observation that one creditor was the Midland Railway Company, a creditor for £39 — "to suppose that the directors could imagine that they were making themselves partners is absurd."

LORD WENSLEYDALE — "The law as to partnership is undoubtedly a branch of the principal and agent; and it would lend to simplify and make more easy of solution the questions which arise on this subject, if this true principle were more constantly kept in view." Quoting Story: "Every partner is an agent of the partnership… a partner virtually embraces the character of both a principal and agent."

"Hence it becomes a test of the liability of one for the contract of another, that he is to receive the whole or a part of the profits arising from the contract by virtue of the agreement made at the time of the employment. I believe this is the true principle of partnership liability. Perhaps the maxim that he who partakes the advantage ought to bear the loss… is only the consequence, not [the] relation of principal, agent, and partner." — per Lord Wensleydale (DU case material p. 14–15)

Conclusion: "It is not true that by this deed the creditors will gain an advantage by the trustees carrying on the trade; for if it is profitable, they may get their debts paid, but this is not that sharing of profits which constitutes the relation of principal, agent and partner." The creditors are not liable, "because the trustees are not their agents."

Principles evolved
  1. Sharing of profits is only prima facie evidence of partnership — it is a presumption of fact, not a rule of law. It can be rebutted.
  2. The real / conclusive test is AGENCY (mutual agency): is the business carried on by persons acting as agents for the alleged partner? If the traders are not his agents, he is not his partner.
  3. Partnership liability is agency liability: the liability of one partner for the acts of his co-partner is in truth the liability of a principal for the acts of his agent.
  4. Receiving profits in discharge of a debt is not profit-sharing: a creditor who permits his debtor (or trustees for the debtor) to continue the trade and apply profits in discharging the debt does not become a partner — the debtor remains solely interested in the profits, having merely "mortgaged them to his creditors".
  5. Powers of control / veto do not by themselves create partnership: a power to order discontinuance or to make rules is only a qualification of the creditor's consent, not a right to carry on the trade as principal.
  6. Look at the real nature of the transaction, "according to the understanding of all who were parties to it" — the precursor of s. 6's "real relation… all relevant facts taken together".
  7. Private arrangements do not bind third parties without notice — later codified in s. 20, PA 1932.

The Snow White line of cases in India repeatedly applies the same "substance over form / agency" reasoning.

3. Mollwo, March & Co. v. The Court of Wards
(1872) L.R. 4 P.C. 419 · Privy Council (on appeal from India) · Sir Montague E. Smith
"Creditor-debtor relationship" — headnote in the DU case material
DU CASE MATERIAL pp. 16–21
Facts

The plaintiffs/appellants, Mollwo, March & Co., merchants of London, sued the late Rajah Pertab Chunder Singh to recover a balance of nearly three lacs of rupees alleged to be due from the firm of W.N. Watson & Co. of Calcutta. The Rajah died pending the suit; the defence was continued by the Court of Wards on behalf of his minor heir.

The plaint alleged that the firm consisted of W.N. Watson, T.O. Watson, and the Rajah — liable as a partner. The two Watsons had commenced business as merchants at Calcutta in 1862. They had little or no capital. The Rajah supported them and in 1862–63 made large advances, partly in cash but chiefly by accepting bills which the Rajah met at maturity.

By mid-1863 the advances were considerable and the Rajah wanted security for his debt, security for future advances, and some control over the business to check what he considered the Watsons' excessive trading. Accordingly, on 27 August 1863 an agreement was entered into between "the Rajah of the one part and Messrs. W.N. Watson & Co. of the other part". Under it, in consideration of money already advanced and to be advanced, the Watsons agreed to carry on business subject to the control of the Rajah in several important particulars:

  • they would not make shipments, order consignments, or sell goods without his consent;
  • no money was to be drawn from the firm without his sanction;
  • he was to be consulted about the office business and could direct reduction or enlargement of the establishment;
  • the shipping documents were to be at his disposal and not to be sold or hypothecated, nor their proceeds applied, without his consent;
  • all the proceeds of the business were to be handed to him for the purpose of extinguishing his debt;
  • they handed over title deeds of certain tea plantations "as security", and agreed that "as further security" all their other property, including stock-in-trade, should be answerable for the debt;
  • the Rajah was to receive a commission of 20 per cent on profits.

The Rajah did not sign the agreement but was "undoubtedly an assenting party to it". He made further advances; the amount due ultimately exceeded three lacs. In 1864–65 the firm fell into difficulties. The Rajah took a formal mortgage of the tea plantations and, by deed dated 3 March 1865, released all right to commission and interest and all other claims.

Critically: the Rajah never in fact received possession of any property or moneys of the firm, nor any proceeds of the business, and did not in fact receive any commission. A sum of Rs. 27,000 was on 30 September 1863 placed to his credit in a separate account, but was never paid and was subsequently written back. And it was conceded that "the Rajah availed himself only in a slight degree of the powers of control conferred upon him… in fact, that he did no more, but much less, than he might have done under it."

Issues
  1. Whether the Rajah became, under the agreement, at least as regards third persons, a partner with the Watsons.
  2. Alternatively, whether, if not a "true partner", the Watsons were the agents of the Rajah in carrying on the business, and the debt to the plaintiffs was contracted within the scope of that agency.
  3. Properly framed: whether participation in profits — here a 20% commission — plus extensive powers of control makes a creditor a partner.

The Court also noted there was no question of "ostensible partnership": "It is admitted that he did not so hold himself out", and a statement by one of the Watsons to the plaintiffs that the Rajah "might be in law a partner" was not authorised by the Rajah.

Arguments
Mollwo, March & Co. (appellants / plaintiffs)
  • First: the Rajah became, by the agreement, at least as regards third persons, a partner with the Watsons.
  • "Whatever may have been the intention, a participation in the net profits of the business was in contemplation of law such cogent evidence of partnership that presumption arose sufficient to establish, as regards third parties, that relation unless rebutted by other circumstances."
  • Relied on the older rule of Waugh v. Carver (1793) and Grace v. Smith (1775) — participation in net profits = partner.
  • Relied on Pott v. Eyton (1844), where Tindal C.J. adopted Lord Eldon's rule and said: "Nor does it appear to make any difference whether the money is received by way of interest on money lent, or wages, or salary as agent, or commission on sales."
  • Secondly: the large powers of control + the right to take possession of consignments and their proceeds + the commission on net profits amounted to an agreement of partnership and the Rajah was in fact constituted the managing partner.
  • Thirdly: even if not a "true partnership", the Watsons were thereby constituted the agents of the Rajah, and the debt was contracted within the scope of that agency.
The Court of Wards (respondent / defendant)
  • The agreement, "on the face of it, is an arrangement between the Rajah, as creditor, and the firm consisting of the two Watsons as debtors, by which the Rajah obtained security for his past advances".
  • "The primary object was to give security to the Rajah as a creditor of the firm."
  • In consideration of forbearance and as an inducement to continue advances, he was to receive a commission of 20% on profits and be invested with powers of supervision and control.
  • The Rajah was not entitled to a share of the profits "as such" — he had no specific property or interest in them qua profits; subject to his security, the Watsons might have appropriated or assigned the whole profits without breach of the agreement. He was entitled only to commission, or a payment equal in proportion to one-fifth of their amount.
  • He had no initiative power — he could not direct what shipments should be made, could not require the Watsons to continue to trade or even to remain in partnership. His powers, "however large, were powers of control only".
  • "The agreement is really founded on the assumption, not of community of benefit, but of opposition of interests."
Decision (Privy Council — appeal dismissed with costs)

Held: the Rajah was NOT a partner and NOT liable for the debts of W.N. Watson & Co. The judgment of the High Court was affirmed.

  1. Applicable law: "It was contended at the Bar that, whatever may have been the intention, a participation in the net profits of the business was in contemplation of law such cogent evidence of partnership that presumption arose… It appears to their Lordships that the rule of construction involved in this contention is too artificial: for it takes one term only of the contract and at once raises a presumption upon it. Whereas the whole scope of the agreement, and all its terms, ought to be looked at before any presumption of intention can properly be made at all."
  2. The old rule rejected: "The rule was evidently an arbitrary one, and subsequent discussion had led to the rejection of the reason for it as unsound" — the reason given in Grace v. Smith, that "by taking part of the profits he takes part of the fund which is the proper security of the creditors, is now admitted to be unsound and insufficient to support it; for of course the same consequences might follow in a far greater degree from the mortgage of the common property of the firm, which certainly would not of itself make the mortgagee a partner."
  3. Cox v. Hickman applied: "the necessity of resorting to these fine distinctions has been greatly lessened since the presumption itself lost the rigid character it was supposed to possess after the full exposition of the law on this subject contained in the judgment of the House of Lords in Cox v. Hickman and the cases which have followed that decision… the judgment in Cox v. Hickman had certainly the effect of dissolving the rule of law which had been supposed to exist, and laid down principles of decision by which the determination of cases of this kind is made to depend, not on arbitrary presumptions. Profits of trade is a strong test of partnership, and there may be cases where, from such participation alone, it may as a presumption not of law but of fact, be inferred; yet that whether that relation does or does not exist must depend on the real intention and contract of the parties."
  4. Distinction between profits "as such" and a commission: "The Rajah was not entitled to a share of the profits 'as such'; he had no specific property or interest in them qua profits… The Rajah was entitled only to commission, or a payment equal in proportion to one-fifth of their amount."
  5. Control ≠ partnership: "The contract undoubtedly confers on the Rajah large power of control… On the other hand, the Rajah had no initiative power; he could not direct what shipments should be made or consignments ordered, or what should be the course of trade. He could not require the Watsons to continue to trade, or even to remain in partnership; his powers, however large, were powers of control only." — "Their Lordships are of opinion that by these agreements the parties did not intend to create a partnership, and that their true relation to each other under the agreement was that of creditor and debtors… the agreement is really founded on the assumption, not of community of benefit, but of opposition of interests."
  6. No implied agency either: "Of course, if there was no partnership, the implied agency which flows from that relation cannot arise, and the relation of principal and agents must on some other ground be shown to exist. It is clear that this relation was not expressly created, and was not intended to be created, by the agreement… But this is again an attempt to create, by operation of law, a relation opposed to the real agreement and intention of the parties… He was not, in any sense, the owner of the business, and had no power to deal with it as owner. None of the ordinary attributes of principal belonged to him."
  7. Warning against sham: "If cases should occur where any persons, under the guise of such an arrangement, are really trading as principals, and putting forward, as ostensible traders, others who are really their agents, they must not hope by such devices to escape liability; for the law, in cases of this kind, will look at the body and substance of the arrangement, and fasten responsibility on the parties according to their true and real character."
Principles evolved
  1. Whole-agreement rule: you cannot take "one term only" of a contract and raise a presumption upon it; the whole scope and all the terms must be looked at first.
  2. Profit-sharing is a presumption of FACT, not of law — rebuttable; the question is the real intention and contract of the parties.
  3. "Profits as such" vs. commission in proportion to profits: a right to a share of profits as such points to partnership; a right to a commission or payment measured by reference to profits points to loan. (This is the "thin but admitted" distinction the Court itself acknowledged.)
  4. Powers of control are not enough: what matters is initiative power — the power to direct what trade shall be done. A creditor with veto/protective powers only is not a partner.
  5. Community of benefit vs. opposition of interests: a partnership presupposes a community of benefit; a loan-and-security arrangement presupposes opposition of interests.
  6. No agency without partnership: the implied agency of s. 18 ICA / s. 18 PA only flows from partnership; if there is no partnership, an agency must be expressly shown.
  7. Sham arrangements fail: the court will "look at the body and substance of the arrangement".
  8. Ostensible partnership is a separate doctrine: a man who holds himself out as a partner, or allows others to do so, "is then properly estopped from denying the character he has assumed" — partner by estoppel (later s. 28, PA 1932).

4. Narandas Morardas Gajiwala v. S.P.A.M. Papammal
AIR 1967 SC 333 · (1966) SCR 38 · Supreme Court · V. Ramaswami & K. Subba Rao, JJ. · decided 25-03-1966
Agency — can an AGENT sue his PRINCIPAL for rendition of accounts?
DU SYLLABUS Reported judgment
Facts

Narandas Morardas Gaziwala and Lakshmi Chand & Co. were two partnership firms carrying on business in lace and silver thread at Surat (Bombay State). They had dealings with another firm at Kumbakonam, Krishna & Co., which acted as their agents for selling goods on a commission basis in the three districts of Tanjore, Tiruchirapalli and Madurai (Madras State) from 1944 to 1951.

Krishna & Co. was dissolved by mutual agreement in 1951. Murugesa Chettiar (the plaintiff) took over all assets and liabilities; the other partner, Gopal Chettiar, retired.

On 1 April 1951, Murugesa Chettiar executed a promissory note for Rs. 7,500 in favour of Narandas Morardas Gaziwala — the amount ascertained as due on settlement of account. On the same day, the Surat firm constituted him as sole agent for selling goods bearing the trade marks "Napoleon", "Vivekananda" etc. for five years, agreeing to pay commission at a flat rate of Rs. 2 per 'mark' for all sales effected in those territories — including sales booked directly.

The plaintiff's case was that the Surat firm circumvented the sole-agency contract by making private/direct sales through others, and that as part of the sole-agency agreement the firm had agreed to adjust its indebtedness under the promissory note against the commission earned by him.

  • The plaintiff filed O.S. No. 87 of 1954 in the District Munsif's Court, Kancheepuram, for rendition of accounts from 1-4-1951 to the date of suit, to ascertain the amount due.
  • The Surat firm filed O.S. No. 21 of 1954 before the Subordinate Judge, Chingleput, to recover Rs. 7,500 on the promissory note.
  • Both suits were, by consent, tried together.

Subordinate Judge: held the plaintiff was the sole agent for five years, that the Surat firm was liable to render accounts, granted a preliminary decree for accounts, and also decreed the promissory note amount but directed that it be adjusted against the commission found due on accounting.

Madras High Court: dismissed both appeals of the Surat firm. The Surat firm appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues
  1. Whether an agent is entitled in law to sue his principal for rendition of accounts (there being no statutory provision for it in the Indian Contract Act, 1872).
  2. Whether the plaintiff is precluded from setting up the parole (oral) agreement by reason of s. 92 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 — i.e., whether the oral agreement that the promissory note would not be enforced for five years and until accounts were taken was a mode of discharge of the obligation (barred) or a condition precedent to the enforceability of the note (provable under Proviso 3).
Arguments
Murugesa Chettiar (plaintiff / agent — respondent)
  • He was constituted sole agent for the three districts for five years at a flat commission of Rs. 2 per 'mark' on all sales in the territory.
  • The Surat firm made direct sales to customers in the territory in contravention of the sole-agency contract.
  • The transactions in respect of which he is entitled to commission are peculiarly within the knowledge of the principal alone — he cannot compute his commission without the principal's books.
  • Therefore he is entitled to sue for accounts.
  • The oral agreement that the promissory note would not be enforced for five years and until accounts were taken is a condition precedent to the attaching of any obligation under the note — provable under Proviso 3 to s. 92.
Surat firm (defendants / principals — appellants)
  • s. 213 of the ICA specifically provides that an agent is bound to render proper accounts to his principal on demand — so the statute recognises only the principal's right to sue an agent for accounts.
  • There is no corresponding provision conferring a right on the agent to sue the principal — therefore no such suit is maintainable.
  • On the second issue: the promissory note is in law an unconditional undertaking to pay an amount the plaintiff was already liable to pay; it is not open to him to plead a contemporaneous oral agreement contrary to its terms.
  • The agreement that the note should be discharged out of commission relates to the mode of discharge of the obligation, and is therefore barred by s. 92 of the Evidence Act.
Decision (Supreme Court — V. Ramaswami, J. — appeals dismissed with costs)

(i) On the agent's right to sue for accounts:

"Section 213 of the Indian Contract Act specifically provides that an agent is bound to render proper accounts to his principal on demand. The principal's right to sue an agent for rendition of accounts is, therefore, recognised by the statute. But the question is whether an agent can sue the principal for accounts. There is no such provision in the Indian Contract Act. In our opinion, the statute is not exhaustive and the right of the agent to sue the principal for accounts is an equitable right arising under special circumstances and is not a statutory right." — AIR 1967 SC 333
  • Such special circumstances arise where: (a) all the accounts are in the possession of the principal and the agent does not possess accounts enabling him to determine his claim for commission; or (b) the agent's remuneration depends on the extent of dealings which are not known to him, or he cannot know the amount due to him unless the principal's accounts are gone into.
  • On the facts: the remuneration was a flat Rs. 2 per 'mark' for all sales effected in the territory — including direct sales by the principal, of which the agent could have no knowledge. There was also prima facie evidence that the Surat firm had made direct sales in contravention of the sole-agency contract. Hence the agent was entitled to sue for accounts.

(ii) On s. 92 of the Evidence Act:

  • The High Court had found a collateral oral agreement that the obligation under the promissory note would not be enforced for five years and unless the amount was due after accounting for the period of the commission agency.
  • The Solicitor-General's contention that this related to the mode of discharge was rejected. The Court held it was a condition precedent to the enforceability of the promissory note, and therefore evidence of the oral agreement could be adduced under the third proviso to s. 92 of the Evidence Act.
  • Following the line of authority where "by the terms of the oral agreement no liability under the note could arise until the happening of an event", the case fell squarely within Proviso 3.
Principles evolved
  1. The ICA is not exhaustive: equity supplements it. Absence of a statutory provision does not extinguish an equitable remedy.
  2. An agent CAN sue his principal for rendition of accounts — but only as an equitable right arising under special circumstances, not as of right. The two recognised special circumstances are: (a) accounts exclusively with the principal; and (b) remuneration dependent on dealings unknown to the agent.
  3. s. 213 is one-way on its face (agent → principal) but equity supplies the converse in an appropriate case.
  4. s. 92, Evidence Act, Proviso 3: existence of a separate oral agreement constituting a condition precedent to the attaching of any obligation under a written contract may be proved. This protects an agent who, as part of an agency arrangement, has given a promissory note.
  5. Contrast with partnership: here the agent's remuneration was commission; had he been a partner sharing profits, s. 9 of the PA 1932 + s. 213-type reasoning would apply even more readily. The DU material's short note on s. 9 records: "Sleeping partner can file a suit for rendition of account — Sita Ram v. Radha Rai, AIR 1968 SC 534."
+ Cross-links

s. 213 ICA (agent's accounts) · s. 9 PA 1932 (partner's duty to render true accounts / sleeping partner's suit) · s. 92 Proviso 3, Evidence Act · Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 34–35 & the old s. 5 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877 on suits for accounts. Contrast K.C. Skaria v. Govt. of Kerala (2006) and Mohan Mewawala v. Damani Dyestuff Ltd. (Del HC, 2018), which follow this case.

5. Kuchwar Lime & Stone Co. v. Dehri Rohtas Light Railway Co. Ltd. & Anr.
AIR 1969 SC 193 · 1969 SCR (1) 359 · Supreme Court · J.C. Shah & Vishishtha Bhargava, JJ. · decided 15-07-1968
Agency — IMPLIED AGENCY inferred from surrounding circumstances; consignee's liability for freight & demurrage
DU SYLLABUS Reported judgment
Facts

At the material time the sale and delivery of coal were controlled under the Colliery Control Order, 1945 (made under the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act, 1946). The supply of coal to the appellant company was sanctioned by the Deputy Coal Commissioner, who also passed an order for priority supply of wagons to carry the coal to Banjari station.

A quantity of steam coal was booked by a Colliery to the appellant — Kuchwar Lime & Stone Co. — for carriage to Banjari station on the respondent Dehri Rohtas Light Railway's line. Under the forwarding notes, the freight was made payable by the Company ("freight to pay").

The Company accepted delivery of part of the consignment but declined to take delivery of the rest which reached Banjari on 12 November 1954, on account of the inferior quality of the coal. After correspondence between the parties and with the Coal Controller, the Railway served notice on 28 April 1955 and sold the coal by public auction on 2 June 1955 for Rs. 1,050.

The Railway then sued the Company and the Colliery, claiming demurrage for 202 days during which six wagons remained detained, and sought a decree for Rs. 17,625/14/- after giving credit for the auction proceeds.

  • Trial court: decree for about Rs. 1,620 with interest.
  • High Court: decreed the Railway's claim in full.
  • Supreme Court: appeal by certificate.
Issues
  1. The Company being only the consignee of goods booked by the Colliery, was there no privity of contract between the Company and the Railway — so that no claim for freight or demurrage lay against the Company?
  2. Even if liable, was the Railway — which did not unload the consignment for about seven months — entitled to demurrage for the full period, or was it obliged to unload and claim demurrage only for a reasonable period?
Arguments
Dehri Rohtas Light Railway (respondent)
  • Freight was made payable by the Company under the forwarding notes.
  • Even where the consignee does not ultimately take delivery, if the wagon is detained for his benefit, the Railway is entitled to hold him liable for demurrage.
  • The wagons were detained for 202 days; full demurrage is payable.
Kuchwar Lime & Stone Co. (appellant)
  • It was only a consignee; the contract of consignment was between the Colliery and the Railway. Hence there is no privity of contract between the Company and the Railway, and no claim for demurrage or freight lies against it.
  • In any event, the Railway ought to be awarded demurrage for only 22 days out of the total period of detention.
Decision (Supreme Court — J.C. Shah, J. — decree modified)

(i) Agency implied — the Company IS liable in principle:

"It is clear that the Colliery supplied coal in pursuance of the 'sanction order' in favour of the Company and arranged to transport it in wagons which were allotted for that purpose by order of the Deputy Coal Commissioner. Under the forwarding notes the freight was made payable by the Company. In these circumstances, it would be reasonable to infer that the Colliery was acting as an agent of the Company in entering into the contract of consignment and the liability for payment of freight and of demurrage charges for failure to take delivery of the goods lay upon the Company." — AIR 1969 SC 193, 361 E–F, 363 D–E

The Court also rejected the narrower argument: "There was no force in the contention that it is only in those cases where delivery of goods is taken by the consignee that the liability to pay demurrage may be imposed upon him. Even where the consignee does not ultimately take delivery, if the wagon is detained for his benefit, normally the Railway would be entitled to hold him liable for demurrage."

(ii) But the Railway cannot claim demurrage for the full 202 days:

  • The wagons reached Banjari on 12-11-1954 and the Company had already declined delivery. The Railway could have exercised its power to sell the coal under s. 56 of the Railways Act.
  • "The Railway was in the position of a bailee qua the Company and was bound to minimise the loss: it could not unreasonably detain the wagons and claim demurrage."
  • Even assuming the Colliery Control Order prevented a sale without the Coal Commissioner's sanction, the Railway could have unloaded the coal and put the wagons to use. After unloading, the consignee would be liable only for wharfage.
  • Therefore the Railway was entitled to demurrage for detention of the wagons for only ONE MONTH. The High Court was in error. Decree modified to Rs. 2,145/14 with proportionate costs.
Principles evolved
  1. Agency may be IMPLIED from the surrounding circumstances, including statutory/regulatory compulsion and the conduct of the parties — no express appointment is necessary (compare s. 187, second limb: "implied when it is to be inferred from the circumstances of the case").
  2. A consignee for whose benefit the consignment is booked is bound by the contract of consignment entered into by the consignor acting as his agent — even though he never signed it and even though he refuses delivery.
  3. Freight "to pay" + statutory allotment + benefit = agency by implication.
  4. Duty to mitigate: a party claiming demurrage/damages must take reasonable steps (here, s. 56 Railways Act sale, or at least unloading) to minimise loss. This is the same principle as s. 73, ICA (compensation for loss caused by breach — claimant must take reasonable steps to mitigate).
  5. Bailee's position: the Railway was a bailee (ss. 148–150, ICA) with the corresponding duty of reasonable care and of acting as a person of ordinary prudence.
  6. Apportionment: demurrage for a reasonable period (one month) is recoverable; the balance caused by the claimant's own inaction is not.
+ Cross-links

s. 187 (implied authority), s. 188 (extent of authority), s. 226 (principal bound by agent's contracts), s. 73 (mitigation), ss. 148–150 (bailment) of the ICA · s. 56, Railways Act, 1890 (power to sell unclaimed goods) · Colliery Control Order, 1945 · Coaching Tariff Rule 108 and Goods Tariff Part III, cl. 20. Contrast the partnership rule in s. 20, PA 1932 — restrictions on authority do not bind third parties without notice.

6. Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal & Son Ltd. v. Government of Hyderabad
AIR 1954 SC 367 · (1955) 1 SCR 393 · [1954] 25 ITR 449 · Supreme Court · S.R. Das, N.H. Bhagwati & B. Jagannadhadas, JJ. · decided 01-04-1954
Agency vs. service — is a managing agent an AGENT carrying on a BUSINESS, or a mere SERVANT earning salary?
DU SYLLABUS Reported judgment
Facts

The appellants, Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal and Son Limited, were the managing agents of Dewan Bahadur Ramgopal Mills Company Ltd. under an agency agreement. The question arose in proceedings under the Hyderabad Excess Profits Tax Regulation, which taxed "business" income but excluded a profession carried on by an individual or by individuals in partnership if the profits depended wholly or mainly on personal qualifications.

The Commissioner of Excess Profits Tax, Hyderabad, referred questions to the High Court of Judicature at Hyderabad; the High Court answered them against the assessee and adjudged liability to excess profits tax on the remuneration received by the appellants from the Mills Company as its agents.

Key terms of the agency agreement (set out in the judgment):

  • cl. 3: "Subject to the control and supervision of the Directors, the said Lachminarayan Ramgopal and Son Limited shall have the general conduct and management of the business and affairs of the company" — including acquiring land, erecting and maintaining factories and warehouses;
  • cl. 4: liberty to deal with the company by way of sale and purchase of commodities;
  • payment of remuneration plus all expenses and charges actually incurred;
  • power to appoint any person or persons as their agents (power of sub-delegation — cf. Article 118 of the Articles);
  • cl. 8: two of the appellants' members to be ex-officio Directors;
  • cl. 9: power to assign the agreement and the rights thereunder, subject to the Board's approval;
  • a right to continue as agents for 30 years, and thereafter until they resigned of their own will.

The appellants contended that the remuneration received was not income, profits or gains from business and was outside the pale of the Regulation — effectively arguing that they were in the position of servants/employees, or that the income depended on personal qualifications and so was excluded.

Issues
  1. Whether the appellants were a partnership firm or a registered company.
  2. Whether, under the terms of the agreement, the appellants were employees (servants) of the Mills Company, or were carrying on a business.
  3. Whether the remuneration received from the Mills was for service, or was remuneration for business — and hence taxable under the Excess Profits Tax Regulation.
  4. Ancillary: whether the exclusion for income depending wholly or mainly on personal qualifications applied.
Arguments
Government of Hyderabad / Revenue (respondent)
  • The appellants were agents, not servants: they had perfect discretion within the limits of their authority as to how the work of general management was to be done, both as to method and manner.
  • They could enter into agreements and contracts, and appoint, employ, discharge, re-employ or replace officers and servants of the company on such terms as they thought fit.
  • They had fixity of tenure for 30 years, assignable rights, and a power of sub-delegation — none of which is consistent with employment as a servant.
  • They could act as agents of other manufacturers and companies too.
  • Therefore their activities constituted a business and the remuneration was business income.
Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal & Son Ltd. (appellant)
  • The Directors exercised control and supervision over them; the company's day-to-day management was within the province of the Directors under Article 116.
  • They were therefore really servants of the company, remunerated by wages or salary.
  • Consequently the receipts were not "income, profits or gains from business" and were not taxable under the Regulation.
  • Alternatively, even if they were agents, the income depended wholly or mainly on personal qualifications and so was excluded — a submission the Court rejected for a registered company/partnership firm, which "as such could not be said to be possessed of any personal qualifications".
Decision (Supreme Court — N.H. Bhagwati, J. — appeals dismissed)

Held: the appellants were AGENTS, not servants; their activities as agents of the Company constituted a BUSINESS; and the remuneration received under the agency agreement was income, profits or gains from business, liable to excess profits tax.

"The control and supervision of the directors was a general control and supervision and within the limits of their authority the appellants as the agents of the Company had perfect discretion as to how that work of general management was to be done both in regard to the method and the manner of such work. … The liberty given to the appellants under cl. 4 of the agency agreement to deal with the Company by way of sale and purchase of commodities therein mentioned also did not spell a relation as between master and servant but empowered the appellants to deal with the Company as Principals in spite of the fact that under cl. 8 two of their members for the time being were to be the ex-officio Directors of the Company. The power to assign the agreement and the rights of the appellants thereunder reserved to them under cl. 9 … was hardly a power which could be vested in a servant. There was further the right to continue in employment as the agents of the Company for a period of 30 years … which also would be hardly consistent with the employment of the appellants as mere servants of the Company. All these circumstances together with the power of sub-delegation reserved under Article 118 … go to establish that the appellants were the agents of the Company and not merely the servants of the Company remunerated by wages or salary." — AIR 1954 SC 367

The Court added an important passage on the definition of partnership/business:

"When a partnership firm comes into existence it can be predicated of it that it carries on a business, because partnership according to Section 4 of the Indian Partnership Act is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all." — AIR 1954 SC 367

And on the objects clause: the words "carry on all kinds of agency business" were capable of including the work done as agents for Governments, authorities, bankers, manufacturers, merchants and shippers. "All these factors taken into consideration along with the fixity of tenure, the nature of remuneration and the assignability of their rights, are sufficient to enable us to come to the conclusion that the activities of the appellants as the agents of the Company constituted a business and the remuneration which the appellants received from the Company under the terms of the Agency Agreement was income, profits or gains from business."

Principles evolved
  1. AGENT vs. SERVANT — the tests: the decisive question is not whether there is some control, but whether the control is general or detailed. A person who retains perfect discretion as to the method and manner of doing the work is an agent/independent contractor, not a servant.
  2. Indicia of agency (not service): (i) discretion as to manner and method; (ii) power to appoint, employ and discharge staff; (iii) power to enter into contracts with third parties; (iv) power to deal with the principal as a principal (buying from and selling to the company); (v) power of sub-delegation; (vi) assignability of the agreement; (vii) fixity of tenure (30 years) and the right to resign only of one's own will; (viii) freedom to act for other principals.
  3. Remuneration by commission/business income ≠ wages or salary. Reinforces s. 6, Expl. 2(b), PA 1932 ("by a servant or agent as remuneration" — the receipt of a share of profits does not of itself make him a partner) and s. 219, ICA.
  4. Carrying on agency can itself be a business — and being an agent does not make one a partner.
  5. s. 4, PA 1932 quoted with approval by the Supreme Court: because partnership is defined as sharing profits of a business carried on by all/any acting for all, the very existence of a partnership firm predicates the carrying on of a business.
  6. Registered company / firm has no "personal qualifications" — so the exclusion for professions depending on personal qualifications cannot apply.

7. Snow White Industrial Corporation, Madras v. Collector of Central Excise, Madras
AIR 1989 SC 1555 · (1989) 3 SCC 351 · 1989 SCR (2) 782 · Supreme Court · Sabyasachi Mukharji & S. Ranganathan, JJ. · decided 28-04-1989
Section 182, ICA — SALE vs. AGENCY: the true relationship is gathered from the substance, not the label
DU SYLLABUS Reported judgment
Facts

The appellants were a partnership firm manufacturing "Supercem Waterproof Cement Paint" and allied products at Madras — "a small manufacturing firm with no branches and/or sales offices in any other State, city or town".

On 1 May 1962 the appellants entered into an agreement — described in its own text as an "agreement of sale" — with Gillanders Arbuthnot & Co. Ltd. of Calcutta, a company with "a very big sales organisation having offices at all important places in the territory of India". In the agreement the appellants were described as "The Manufacturer" and Gillanders as "The Selling Agents".

Salient terms:

  • Gillanders were the sole selling agents throughout India, and were not to handle sales of any other material likely to conflict with the appellants' product;
  • the manufacturer would pay them a discount of 17½% (or 18% for Madras orders) on the transfer prices — described as "discount", "commission" and "over-riding commission" (2½% / 5% on Government sales);
  • selling prices and transfer prices were to be mutually agreed from time to time;
  • any reduction in price during the currency of the agreement was to be duly reflected in the price of the stock lying unsold with the selling agents;
  • stocks left unsold beyond two years could be returned to the appellants, who were bound to replace them free of charge if the container lids were intact and sealed;
  • on termination of the agreement by either party, all unsold stocks were to be returned to the appellants, who would reimburse the net value at the transfer prices then in force;
  • all consignments despatched at railway risk; the manufacturer was to take all action for recovery of damages from the carriers and reimburse the selling agents all losses;
  • the manufacturer would supply publicity material and advertise at its own cost, mentioning Gillanders as the sole selling agents;
  • godown rent, transport, postal/telegram charges and bank commission were to be borne by the selling agents.

For the period July 1977 to March 1979 the appellants were assessed to excise duty under the Central Excises and Salt Act, 1944 on the basis of the price at which the selling agents sold to their customers. The appellants claimed the assessable value should be the price at which they sold to the selling agents, and sought a refund. The Assistant Collector and the Collector rejected the claim; the Tribunal held that the true character of the agreement was sole selling agency and not sale, and that title and ownership of the goods continued with the appellants and did not pass to Gillanders.

Issues
  1. Whether the agreement dated 1 May 1962 is an agreement for SALE or one for SOLE SELLING AGENCY.
  2. Consequently, what is the assessable value under s. 4 of the Central Excises and Salt Act, 1944 — the price at which the appellant sold to Gillanders, or the price at which Gillanders sold to the market?
  3. Whether Gillanders was a "related person" within s. 4(4)(c) of the Act.
Arguments
Collector of Central Excise / Revenue (respondent)
  • Relied on s. 182 of the Indian Contract Act and submitted that the clauses emphasised by the Tribunal clearly established an agreement of agency and not a sale.
  • Title and ownership of the goods continued with the appellants — the most important indicator being the return-of-unsold-stock clause, and the obligation to replace two-year-old stock.
  • The manufacturer bore the advertisement cost, bore the transit risk, and had to reimburse the selling agents for losses.
  • Therefore there was no sale to Gillanders, and the first sale was by Gillanders to the market customers — whose price is the assessable value.
Snow White Industrial Corpn. (appellant)
  • There was an outright purchase of the respondent's goods; the appellant was not an agent. (The Tribunal noted the plea that the return clause was inserted merely to ensure that bad or stale goods did not reach the market and damage the appellants' reputation.)
  • Reliance was placed on the principle that an agent could become a purchaser when he pays the price to the principal on his own responsibility.
  • On s. 4(4)(c): the concept of "having interest directly or indirectly in the business of each other" must be judged independently of the transaction in question, and the distributors must be shown to be related on that independent basis — which was not so here.
Decision (Supreme Court — appeal disposed of; Tribunal upheld on the character of the agreement)

Held: the agreement was one of SOLE SELLING AGENCY and not an agreement of sale. Consequently there was no sale in favour of Gillanders; the first sale was by Gillanders to the customers in the market, and the price of that sale would be the assessable value under s. 4.

  • "The Tribunal also referred to the clause which provided that the stocks left over unsold beyond two years from their receipt with Gillanders could be returned to the appellants who were bound to replace these." — "The most important fact suggesting agency was the clause which enjoined that the stocks left over unsold beyond two years from their receipt could be returned to the appellants who were bound to replace these."
  • The plea that the clause was a quality-control measure "should be considered with the fact that the appellants were to prefer all claims for recovery of damages from the carriers and any reduction in price during the currency of the agreement was to be duly reflected in the price of stock lying unsold with the respondents, and the obligation that on the termination of the contract by either, unsold stocks would be taken back."
  • "In view of the fact that we have come to the conclusion that the Tribunal was right in holding that the transaction with the Gillanders was not a transaction of sale but an agreement for agency, there was, therefore, no sale in favour of Gillanders… Therefore the first sale was by the respondents to the customers of the market and the price of that sale would be the assessable value under s. 4." (¶ 13)
  • However, on the other deductions (transportation cost, duty of excise, sales tax, etc.) the Court, "in the interest of justice", permitted the appellants the benefit of other deductions envisaged in the statutory scheme, even though they had not been claimed before the Tribunal or in the grounds of appeal.

The Court also surveyed the English authorities, noting Lord Justice Scrutton's observation "that in certain trades the word 'agent' is often used without any reference to the law of principal and agent", and the counter-principle that "an agent could become a purchaser when an agent paid the price to the principal on his own responsibility".

Principles evolved
  1. Substance over form / label: the description the parties give the document ("agreement of sale") and the words they use ("selling agents", "discount" vs "commission") are not decisive of the legal relationship.
  2. The true relationship of agent and principal is to be gathered from the NATURE OF THE CONTRACT, its TERMS AND CONDITIONS — the terminology used by the parties is not decisive. (This formulation from Snow White has been repeatedly re-affirmed, including by the NCLAT.)
  3. Decisive indicators of AGENCY (not sale):
    • title/ownership does not pass to the "purchaser";
    • a right/obligation to return unsold stock and have it replaced;
    • the risk of loss/damage in transit is on the manufacturer/principal;
    • price reductions are passed through to stock lying unsold;
    • remuneration is by commission/discount on a transfer price rather than by a profit margin on resale;
    • the principal bears advertisement and publicity costs and controls the brand;
    • exclusivity and a duty not to handle competing goods.
  4. An agent may become a purchaser where he pays the price to the principal on his own responsibility — i.e., where he takes the risk himself.
  5. Del-credere agency distinguished: the judgment notes that "agents are not normally liable for the dues from the creditors and such liability will arise only if the Agent is a Del Credere Agent" — a useful aside for the Topic 1 agency paper.
  6. Practical tax consequence: where the arrangement is agency, the first sale to the market fixes the assessable value.

Dec 2015LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.2(a)10 marks
Question "Post Cox v. Hickman (1860) 8 HLC 268 has added a new dimension to the concept of partnership by agreeing that 'sharing of profit is only a prima facie evidence of existence of partnership while the conclusive test is that of mutual agency.' Comment.
Model Answer

1. The position before Cox v. Hickman — profit-sharing as a rule of law. Under the early English authorities the receipt of a share of the net profits of a business was treated as conclusive proof of partnership. The rule was stated by Eyre C.J. in Grace v. Smith (1775) and re-affirmed in Waugh v. Carver (1793), on the reasoning that "by taking a part of the profits, he takes from the creditors a part of that fund which is the proper security to them for the payment of their debts". A later, equally arbitrary distinction was drawn in Pott v. Eyton (1844) between a right to participate in profits "as such" and a right to a salary or commission "in proportion to a given quantum of the profits".

2. What Cox v. Hickman (1860) 8 H.L.C. 268 changed. Smith and Smith, iron-masters, executed a deed of arrangement in favour of their creditors. Five creditors (including Cox and Wheatcroft) were to carry on the business as trustees under the name "The Stanton Iron Company" and divide the net income among the general creditors until the debts were paid. Hickman supplied goods and drew three bills which were accepted by one of the three acting trustees; he then sued Cox and Wheatcroft as partners. The House of Lords reversed the judgment and held they were not liable.

  • Lord Campbell L.C.: "we must look at the real nature of the transaction" — the creditors "could derive no profits from the new business, beyond the payment of the debts due to them".
  • Lord Cranworth: "The liability of one partner for the acts of his co-partner is in truth the liability of a principal for the acts of his agent." Because "the trade is not carried on by or on account of the creditors", they are not partners.
  • Lord Wensleydale: "The law as to partnership is undoubtedly a branch of the principal and agent"; the true test is "that he is to receive the whole or a part of the profits arising from the contract by virtue of the agreement made at the time of the employment".

Thus the House of Lords dissolved the rule of law that participation in profits creates partnership and replaced it with the test of agency.

3. Reception in India — Mollwo, March & Co. v. The Court of Wards (1872) L.R. 4 P.C. 419. The Privy Council expressly applied Cox v. Hickman in India. A Rajah had advanced large sums to W.N. Watson & Co., obtained wide powers of control and a commission of 20% on profits. Holding him not a partner, Sir Montague E. Smith observed that the contention based on profit-sharing was "too artificial: for it takes one term only of the contract and at once raises a presumption upon it. Whereas the whole scope of the agreement, and all its terms, ought to be looked at", and that after Cox v. Hickman profit-sharing is a "presumption not of law but of fact", so that "whether that relation does or does not exist must depend on the real intention and contract of the parties". The Rajah had no initiative power — "his powers, however large, were powers of control only" — and the agreement was "founded on the assumption, not of community of benefit, but of opposition of interests".

4. Statutory codification — s. 6 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. Section 6 now directs that "regard shall be had to the real relation between the parties, as shown by all relevant facts taken together". Explanation 1 excludes co-owners sharing profits or gross returns from property held in joint or common interest. Explanation 2 excludes the receipt of a share of profits, or of a payment contingent on or varying with profits, by (a) a lender, (b) a servant or agent as remuneration, (c) the widow or child of a deceased partner as annuity, and (d) a previous owner as consideration for the sale of goodwill.

5. Modern Indian restatement — K.D. Kamath & Co. v. CIT (1971) 2 SCC 873 (DU case material): the two legal requirements under s. 4 are "(1) there must be an agreement to share the profits or losses of the business; and (2) the business must be carried on by all the partners or any of them acting for all. There is implicit in the second requirement the principle of agency." The court added that "the mere nomenclature given to a document is by itself not sufficient" and that vesting exclusive power and control in one partner "is not destructive of the theory of partnership".

6. Comment / conclusion. The statement is correct. Profit-sharing creates only a rebuttable presumption; it shifts the evidential burden but does not conclude the issue. The conclusive test is mutual agency — is the business carried on by some person acting for the alleged partner as his agent? Statutorily this appears in the closing words of s. 4 ("carried on by all or any of them acting for all") and is reinforced by s. 18 ("a partner is the agent of the firm"). The rationale is sound: liability in partnership is vicarious, and vicarious liability is intelligible only on the basis of agency. A person who cannot control or bind the business through an agent should not be made to answer for its debts merely because he is paid out of its profits.

2017LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.4(a)10 marks
Question Whether sharing of profits is conclusive evidence of partnership? Discuss in the light of relevant statutory provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 and relevant case laws.
Model Answer

Answer: No — sharing of profits is NOT conclusive evidence of partnership. It is only prima facie evidence, i.e., a rebuttable presumption of fact.

(A) Section 4 — the positive requirement. "Partnership" is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Sharing of profits is therefore a necessary ingredient — but the definition contains a second and independent ingredient, mutual agency. An element that is necessary is not thereby sufficient.

(B) Section 6 — the negative / exclusionary rules. Regard must be had to the real relation of the parties "as shown by all relevant facts taken together". Explanation 2 then lists four cases in which receipt of a share of profits (or of a payment contingent on or varying with profits) "does not of itself make him a partner": (a) a lender of money; (b) a servant or agent as remuneration; (c) the widow or child of a deceased partner as annuity; (d) a previous owner or part owner as consideration for the sale of goodwill or a share thereof. Explanation 1 does the same for co-owners sharing profits or gross returns from property held in joint or common interest.

(C) Case law.

  • Cox v. Hickman (1860) — creditors under a deed of arrangement who were to be paid out of the net income of the business were not partners; the test is agency, not profits.
  • Mollwo, March & Co. v. Court of Wards (1872) — a lender with a 20% commission on profits and extensive powers of control was not a partner; profit-sharing is "a presumption not of law but of fact" and the whole agreement must be looked at.
  • Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal & Son Ltd. v. Govt. of Hyderabad (AIR 1954 SC 367) — a managing agent remunerated by commission was an agent, not a servant and not a partner.
  • K.D. Kamath & Co. v. CIT (1971) 2 SCC 873 — restated the two conditions of s. 4 and held that profit-and-loss sharing plus business carried on by one partner acting for all is sufficient, and that concentration of control in one partner is not destructive of partnership.

(D) The residual role of profit-sharing. Where there is nothing else — an agreement to share the profits of a trade "and no more" — a contract of partnership may be inferred, because "there is nothing to show that any other was contemplated" (Mollwo). But this is an inference of fact, open to rebuttal by other terms of the agreement.

(E) Conclusion. Sharing of profits is a strong test and a prima facie pointer, but the conclusive test is mutual agency under s. 4 read with s. 18, subject always to the "real relation" inquiry mandated by s. 6.

Add-on for extra marks: contrast a share of profits "as such" (points to partnership) with a "commission or payment in proportion to profits" (points to loan) — the distinction drawn in Pott v. Eyton and discussed in Mollwo.
1999Business Association–I (OC) · Q.120 marks
Question A and B, partners of a firm, borrowed a large sum of money from C and agreed to give him a share in the profits and powers in the conduct of the business. A and B could not sell, purchase or consign goods without the consent of C. Is C a partner in the firm? If C is to manage and control the firm and take all the profits himself and is ultimately to return the firm to A and B, will it make any difference?
Model Answer — Part I

Issue: Is C — a lender who receives a share of profits and a veto over sales, purchases and consignments — a partner?

Rule: s. 4 (two ingredients) + s. 6 (real relation; Explanation 2(a) — a lender of money receiving a share of profits is not thereby a partner) + Mollwo, March & Co. v. Court of Wards.

Application:

  • C is a creditor. The advance is a loan; the profit share is in substance interest/consideration for the loan — exactly Explanation 2(a).
  • The powers given to C are negative / protective: A and B "could not sell, purchase or consign goods without the consent of C". This is a veto, not an initiative power. In Mollwo the Privy Council said the Rajah "could not direct what shipments should be made or consignments ordered, or what should be the course of trade… his powers, however large, were powers of control only".
  • C is not held out as a partner, so s. 28 (holding out) is not attracted.
  • The arrangement is founded on opposition of interests (creditor v. debtors), not on a community of benefit.
  • No agency is created: A and B do not carry on the business "acting for" C; C is not their principal and they are not his agents.

Conclusion (Part I): C is NOT a partner. He is a creditor, and his profit share and veto are security for the loan. He will not be liable to third parties for the firm's debts.

Model Answer — Part II

Changed facts: C is to manage and control the firm, take all the profits himself, and ultimately return the firm to A and B.

This DOES make a difference — but C is still not a partner. The facts are now almost identical to Cox v. Hickman itself: there the trustees (standing in the position of C) carried on the business, applied the profits in discharging the debt owed to the creditors (C's position), and were to return the property to the debtors when the trusts were satisfied.

  • Lord Cranworth: "the mere concurrence of creditors in an arrangement under which they permit their debtor, or trustees for their debtor, to continue his trade, applying the profits in discharge of their demands, does not make them partners with their debtor, or the trustee. The debtor is still the person solely interested in the profits, save only that he has mortgaged them to his creditors."
  • The power to order discontinuance or to make rules "does not alter the case" — such powers "only qualify their consent".
  • Applied here: C manages and takes the profits, but only towards satisfaction of his debt, and must restore the firm. The beneficial interest throughout remains with A and B; C holds the business as a mortgagee/security-holder, not as a principal.

Conclusion (Part II): No, C is still not a partner — he is a creditor in possession with a security interest. However, two qualifications must be noted:

  1. If C, while managing, holds himself out as a partner, or allows A and B to do so, he may be liable to third parties under s. 28 (holding out) by estoppel.
  2. If the arrangement is a sham — "under the guise of such an arrangement, [persons] are really trading as principals" — then, as Mollwo warns, "the law… will look at the body and substance of the arrangement, and fasten responsibility on the parties according to their true and real character."
Examiner's note: this is a two-layer question. Part I = Mollwo; Part II = Cox v. Hickman. Candidates who cite both score full marks; those who cite only one lose half.

Dec 2015LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.2(b)10 marks
Question A advanced Rs. 20,000/- to the firm of X & Y. Both X and Y agreed to carry on business subject to control of A on payment of commission of 5% out of profit. Is 'A' a partner in the firm? Explain with case law.
Model Answer

Answer: No. 'A' is not a partner; he is a creditor/lender of the firm.

1. The statutory framework.

  • s. 4, PA 1932 — partnership requires (i) an agreement to share the profits of a business, and (ii) the business being carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Both must co-exist.
  • s. 6, PA 1932 — "regard shall be had to the real relation between the parties, as shown by all relevant facts taken together."
  • s. 6, Explanation 2(a) — "The receipt by a person of a share of the profits of a business, or of a payment contingent upon the earning of profits or varying with the profits earned by a business, does not of itself make him a partner… in particular, the receipt of such share or payment by a lender of money to persons engaged or about to engage in any business."

2. The controlling authority — Mollwo, March & Co. v. The Court of Wards, (1872) L.R. 4 P.C. 419.

Facts (almost identical to this problem): Rajah Pertab Chunder Singh advanced large sums to W.N. Watson & Co. of Calcutta. By an agreement of 27 August 1863 the Watsons agreed to carry on business subject to the Rajah's control — no shipments, consignments or sales without his consent; no money drawn from the firm without his sanction; shipping documents at his disposal; all proceeds handed to him to extinguish his debt; tea-plantation title deeds and all other property hypothecated as security — and the Rajah was to receive a commission of 20% on profits. The plaintiffs sued the Rajah as a partner.

Held — not a partner. The Privy Council's reasoning, directly applicable here:

  • Whole-agreement rule: "it takes one term only of the contract and at once raises a presumption upon it. Whereas the whole scope of the agreement, and all its terms, ought to be looked at before any presumption of intention can properly be made at all."
  • Presumption of fact, not of law: "Profits of trade is a strong test of partnership… yet that whether that relation does or does not exist must depend on the real intention and contract of the parties."
  • Profits "as such" vs. commission: "The Rajah was not entitled to a share of the profits 'as such'… he was entitled only to commission, or a payment equal in proportion to one-fifth of their amount."
  • Control is not initiative: "the Rajah had no initiative power; he could not direct what shipments should be made or consignments ordered, or what should be the course of trade… his powers, however large, were powers of control only."
  • Creditor–debtor relationship: "their true relation to each other under the agreement was that of creditor and debtors… the agreement is really founded on the assumption, not of community of benefit, but of opposition of interests."
  • No implied agency: "if there was no partnership, the implied agency which flows from that relation cannot arise", and here it was "not expressly created, and was not intended to be created". "None of the ordinary attributes of principal belonged to him."

Question — the harder variant A and B are partners in a firm. The firm is facing financial difficulties. Model Answer

Answer: On these facts C may well be a partner — the inference is substantially stronger than in the standard Mollwo problem, because of the liability for losses.

Analysis in two steps.

Step 1 — the interest and the profit share. 15% interest on ₹70,000 plus 10% of profits is, by itself, within s. 6, Explanation 2(a) (a lender of money receiving payment contingent on or varying with profits). Standing alone, it would not make C a partner (see Mollwo).

Step 2 — the liability for 10% of the LOSSES. This is the decisive new fact. The Act does not expressly make loss-sharing an essential of partnership — s. 4 speaks only of sharing "profits" — but:

  • Judicially, an agreement to share losses is the strongest single indicator of partnership, because a creditor/lender never undertakes to bear the debtor's losses.
  • In K.D. Kamath & Co. v. CIT the Supreme Court treated the clause making partners "liable to make good the losses in proportion to their shares" (cl. 15) as materially reinforcing the conclusion that "the sole proprietary concern of K.D. Kamath has vanished".
  • Where there is community of benefit and of burden, the "opposition of interests" on which Mollwo rested disappears.

But the matter is not free from doubt. A candid answer must note both sides:

Arguments that C IS a partner
  • Undertakes to bear 10% of the losses — inconsistent with the position of a creditor.
  • Shares in both profit and loss — "community of benefit", not "opposition of interests".
  • His return is not fixed but varies with the trading result.
  • If he also has any say in the conduct of the business, mutual agency may be inferable.
Arguments that C is NOT a partner
  • The dominant feature is still a loan of ₹70,000 at 15% interest; s. 6 Expl. 2(a).
  • No initiative power in the conduct of the trade is stated — no agency.
  • A "liability for 10% of losses" may be a contractual device to give the lender a stake without partnership — though unusual.
  • Per Mollwo, the whole agreement must be seen; the label and the dominant purpose (rescuing a firm in difficulty by advancing money) point to loan.

Conclusion. Balanced against the identical problem without the loss clause (which is clearly answered "no"), the addition of a 10% loss liability tips the balance in favour of partnership — provided the requirement of mutual agency can also be established. If C has no voice in conducting the business and no authority to bind the others, the safer view is that C is a lender with an unusual risk-sharing term, not a partner; but he would be well advised to have the deed state expressly that no partnership is intended.

  2. The 20% share of profits is security/interest for the loan, not participation in the adventure. In Mollwo the commission was 20%; the Privy Council still held the lender to be a creditor.
  3. "Some control in management" is protective control. Question Ratification & Revocation of an authority in agency.
Model Answer

I. RATIFICATION (ss. 196–200, ICA 1872)

Meaning. Ratification is the adoption by a person of an act done on his behalf but without his knowledge or authority. s. 196: "Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another, but without his knowledge or authority, he may elect to ratify or to disown such acts. If he ratify them, the same effects will follow as if they had been performed by his authority."

Doctrine of relation back. Ratification is not a new contract; it is an election which relates back to the date of the unauthorised act (omnis ratihabitio retrotrahitur et mandato priori aequiparatur). The principal may thereafter sue the third party directly.

Modes — s. 197. Ratification may be express or implied in the conduct of the principal. Illustration (a): A, without authority, buys goods for B; B later sells them to C on his own account — B's conduct implies ratification. Illustration (b): A, without B's authority, lends B's money to C; B afterwards accepts interest on the money from C — ratification of the loan is implied.

Conditions for a valid ratification:

  1. The act must have been done on behalf of the person ratifying (s. 196).
  2. The principal must have been in existence and competent when the act was done (Kelner v. Baxter — a company cannot ratify a pre-incorporation contract).
  3. The act must be ratifiable — not void or illegal.
  4. Ratification must be within a reasonable time and before the third party withdraws (s. 200).
  5. The principal must have full knowledge of material facts — "no valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is materially defective" (s. 198).
  6. Ratification must not injure a third person (s. 200).
  7. Ratification must be of the whole transaction — "A person ratifying an unauthorized act done on his behalf ratifies the whole of the transaction of which such act formed a part" (s. 199).

Consequence of non-ratification. The "agent" is personally liable to the third party under s. 235 (liability of pretended agent / breach of warranty of authority).

II. REVOCATION (ss. 201–210, ICA 1872)

Meaning. Revocation is the withdrawal by the principal of the authority conferred on the agent. Its counterpart by the agent is renunciation. s. 201 lists six modes of termination: revocation by the principal; renunciation by the agent; completion of the business; death or unsoundness of mind of either; and insolvency of the principal.

Question A, without authority of B, lends money to C. Afterwards B accepts interest on the money from C. Can B later disown the lending of money by A to B? Discuss. (Asked in identical terms in 2014 OC Q.6(b))
Model Answer

Answer: No. B cannot disown the lending. By accepting interest from C, B has impliedly ratified the loan, and under s. 199 he ratifies the whole transaction of lending, not merely the receipt of interest.

Legal framework:

  • s. 196 — where acts are done by one person on behalf of another without his knowledge or authority, he may elect to ratify or to disown such acts; if he ratifies, "the same effects will follow as if they had been performed by his authority".
  • s. 197 — "Ratification may be expressed, or may be implied in the conduct of the person on whose behalf the acts are done." Illustration (b) is on all fours with this problem: "A, without B's authority, lends B's money to C. Afterwards B accepts interest on the money from C. B's conduct implies a ratification of the loan."
  • s. 199 — "A person ratifying an unauthorized act done on his behalf ratifies the whole of the transaction of which such act formed a part." B cannot ratify the beneficial part (the interest) and disown the onerous part (the lending). He must take the bitter with the sweet.
  • s. 198 — ratification is valid only if B had full knowledge of the material facts. If B accepted the interest believing it to be, say, a gift or a refund, his knowledge was "materially defective" and there is no valid ratification.

Consequences once ratification is established:

  1. The loan is deemed to have been made with B's authority from the outset (relation back).
  2. B can sue C directly for repayment of the principal and the agreed interest — he is not remitted to a suit against A.
  3. B cannot sue A for having lent his money without authority — the ratification cures the want of authority.
  4. B is bound by the terms of the loan as A made them (rate of interest, period, security).

Practical rider. If, before ratifying, B had expressly repudiated the loan on becoming aware of it, the subsequent acceptance of interest would have to be explained; and if C had already withdrawn on learning that A had no authority, ratification would come too late (s. 200).

One-liner to memorise: "Accepting the fruit is adopting the tree — s. 197 ill. (b) + s. 199."
2011Business Association–I (OC) · Q.6(b)10 marks
Question A holds a lease from B terminable on three months' notice. C, an unauthorised person, gives notice of termination to A. Can the notice be ratified by B so as to be binding on A?
Model Answer

Answer: No. The notice cannot be ratified by B so as to bind A.

Reasoning:

  1. Ratification requires an act done "on behalf of" the ratifier (s. 196). C was not acting on B's behalf, and nothing suggests he purported to. A stranger's gratuitous intermeddling is not an act done "on behalf of another" within s. 196.
  2. The doctrine of relation back cannot manufacture a notice that never emanated from the landlord. A notice determining a tenancy is a unilateral juristic act addressed to the tenant. Its validity depends on it being given by the person entitled to give it. Ratification relates back to validate an authorised act; it cannot convert an act that was a nullity vis-à-vis the tenant into a valid notice.
  3. s. 200 — ratification cannot injure a third person. A, the tenant, is entitled to a notice from B himself (or from a person duly authorised by B). To allow B to adopt C's notice would shorten the notice period to which A is entitled and thereby injure him. Section 200 bars ratification that would have "the effect of injuring a third person".
  4. Contrast the position if C had purported to act as B's agent. Then ss. 196–199 would apply, and B could ratify — provided the other conditions (full knowledge under s. 198, whole transaction under s. 199, no injury to A under s. 200) were satisfied. Even then, A would be entitled to the full contractual notice period running from the date of a valid notice.
  5. Fallback remedy: if C falsely represented himself as B's authorised agent and B does not ratify, C is liable to compensate the person who acted on the representation (s. 235 — liability of pretended agent), and B may be liable under s. 237 if he induced the belief by his words or conduct.

Conclusion: B must serve his own three months' notice on A. The notice given by C is ineffective and cannot be ratified.

Question Who may be an agent? What are his rights, duties and liabilities? (Variants: 2014 OC Q.2(a) & Q.6(a); 2013 OC Q.7; 2012 OC Q.6; 2011 OC Q.8(a); 2010 OC Q.8(ii); 2000 Q.8(a); 1994 Q.8(a))
Model Answer

I. WHO MAY BE AN AGENT — s. 184. "As between the principal and third persons, any person may become an agent; but no person who is not of the age of majority and of sound mind can become an agent, so as to be responsible to his principal according to the provisions in that behalf herein contained." Two limbs:

  • Vis-à-vis third persons: even a minor or a person of unsound mind can validly act as an agent; the contract he makes binds the principal and the third party.
  • Vis-à-vis his own principal: such a person is not liable to the principal for accounts (s. 213), want of skill (s. 212) or misconduct. The internal remedies are unavailable.

Correspondingly, s. 183 provides that only a person who is of the age of majority and of sound mind may employ an agent. A firm, company, LLP or HUF (through its karta) may also appoint agents through its authorised organs. s. 185: no consideration is necessary to create an agency.

II. RIGHTS OF AN AGENT.

  1. s. 217 — Right of retainer: to retain, out of sums received on the principal's account, all moneys due to himself for advances, expenses and remuneration.
  2. s. 219 — Remuneration: becomes due only when the agent has rendered the service / completed the act. (An agent employed to sell a house earns commission only on actual sale.)
  3. s. 221 — Lien: in the absence of a contract to the contrary, to retain goods, papers and other property of the principal received by him until his dues for remuneration, disbursements and services are paid or accounted for.
  4. s. 222 — Indemnity for lawful acts: the principal must indemnify him against the consequences of all lawful acts done in exercise of the authority conferred.
  5. s. 223 — Indemnity for acts done in good faith: even where the act causes injury to a third person.
  6. s. 225 — Compensation: for injury caused by the principal's neglect or want of skill.
  7. s. 205, 206 — Compensation / notice on revocation: if the agency was for a period and is revoked without sufficient cause; and reasonable notice must be given.
  8. s. 230(1)–(3) (reverse side): the agent may personally enforce the contract where he contracted for a merchant resident abroad, or did not disclose his principal, or the principal though disclosed cannot be sued.

III. DUTIES OF AN AGENT.

  1. s. 211: conduct the principal's business according to his directions; absent directions, according to the custom prevailing in the same business at that place.
  2. s. 212: exercise the skill and diligence of a person of ordinary prudence; compensate the principal for the direct consequences of his neglect, want of skill or misconduct (but not for remote or indirect loss).
  3. s. 213: render proper accounts on demand.
  4. s. 214: communicate with the principal; use all reasonable diligence; obtain instructions in cases of difficulty.
  5. s. 215: not to deal on his own account in the business of the agency without the principal's consent and full disclosure — the principal may repudiate the transaction if a material fact was dishonestly concealed or the dealing was disadvantageous to him.
  6. s. 216: if he so deals, the principal may claim the benefit resulting to him.
  7. s. 218: pay over all sums received on the principal's account (after s. 217 deductions).
  8. s. 190: not to delegate — delegatus non potest delegare — except by custom of trade or from the nature of the agency.
  9. s. 209: on termination by the principal's death or insanity, take reasonable steps to protect the estate.
  10. s. 209 read with s. 211: act in good faith and not make a secret profit — the fiduciary duty underlying ss. 215–216.

IV. LIABILITIES OF AN AGENT.

  1. To the principal: for neglect, want of skill or misconduct (s. 212); for the acts of a sub-agent (s. 193 — and whether properly appointed or not, s. 192); for a named/substituted agent only for want of ordinary prudence in selecting him (s. 195); to account and pay over (ss. 213, 218); to repudiate-able transactions under s. 215 and to disgorge secret profits under s. 216.
  2. To third parties: ordinarily none — s. 230 says an agent cannot personally enforce nor is he personally bound by contracts made on behalf of his principal, absent a contract to that effect; excepted cases are in s. 230(1)–(3); and where the agent is personally liable, the third party may hold either him or the principal or both (s. 233).
  3. s. 235 — Pretended agent: a person who untruly represents himself as an authorised agent is liable to compensate the third party if the alleged employer does not ratify.
  4. s. 236: a person who has contracted in the character of agent but was really acting on his own account cannot require performance of the contract.
  5. s. 220: forfeiture of remuneration for business misconducted.
  6. s. 238: liability for misrepresentation or fraud committed by the agent in the course of the business of the agency — it has the same effect as if committed by the principal.
  7. Note — s. 224: where the agent is employed to do a criminal act, he is not entitled to indemnity from the principal.

V. TERMINATION OF AGENCY (s. 201). By revocation by the principal; renunciation by the agent; completion of the business; death or unsoundness of mind of either; insolvency of the principal. Qualified by s. 202 (agency coupled with interest is irrevocable to the prejudice of that interest), ss. 203–204 (no revocation after the authority has been exercised or partly exercised), s. 205 (compensation), s. 206 (notice), s. 207 (may be implied) and s. 208 (takes effect on knowledge — against the agent when he knows; against third persons when they know).

Model Answer

Answer: No — the ratification is not binding on P. P may avoid the transaction.

Two independent grounds:

(1) s. 198 — knowledge materially defective. "No valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is materially defective." When P "accepted the purchase" he knew only that A had bought above the authorised rate. He did not know — and this is plainly material — that A had sold his own goods to him. A principal's consent to be bound is vitiated where a material fact is concealed. On discovering the truth, P is entitled to treat the ratification as invalid.

(2) s. 215 — agent dealing on his own account. An agent "deals on his own account in the business of the agency" when he sells his own goods to his principal. Such dealing requires the principal's prior consent after the agent has acquainted him with all material circumstances within his own knowledge. If the agent deals without that consent and disclosure, the principal may repudiate the transaction where either (a) a material fact has been dishonestly concealed, or (b) the dealing has been disadvantageous to him. Here A dishonestly concealed his ownership — the classic case under clause (a). Additionally, under s. 216, "if an agent, without the knowledge of his principal, deals in the business of the agency on his own account instead of on account of his principal, the principal is entitled to claim from the agent any benefit which may have resulted to him from the transaction" — so P may also claim A's profit on the sale.

  4. No separation of ownership and management is compelled. All partners may manage directly (s. 26 — every partner is the agent of the LLP for the purposes of its business), unlike a company where management vests in the Board.
  5. Lighter compliance. No requirement of a minimum paid-up capital, no compulsory AGM regime of the company type, audit only above the prescribed turnover/contribution thresholds (s. 35(2) and Rule 24).
  6. Flexibility on admission, cessation and profit-sharing. s. 22 (eligibility), s. 24 (cessation on 30 days' notice in the absence of agreement, or per the agreement), s. 24(5) (former partner entitled to capital contribution + accumulated profits less losses).

Part III — The counter-view (state it; examiners reward balance).

  • The LLP has no share capital, so it cannot raise equity from the public — it is unsuitable for large capital-intensive enterprise.
  • It is not a "person" for all purposes in the same way a company is; some regulators and tender authorities still prefer companies.
  • At least two designated partners must be individuals, one resident in India (s. 7) — a compliance burden that companies with wholly-corporate promoters may avoid.
  • Tax treatment, stamp duty and the enforceability of LLP agreements in some States have historically been less settled than company law.

Conclusion. Yes — the LLP is best described as a hybrid: it borrows corporateness and limited liability from company law and internal flexibility and mutual agency from partnership law. That is precisely the gap it was enacted to fill.

2017LB-304 Special Contracts · Q.1(c)10 marks
Question Partnership at will. (Variants: 2017 OC Q.1(d) & Q.1(e); 2014 OC Q.8(a); 2012 OC Q.8(c); 2010 OC Q.1(d))
Model Answer

Statutory text — s. 7, Indian Partnership Act, 1932:

s. 7 — Partnership at will

"Where no provision is made by contract between the partners for the duration of their partnership, or for the determination of their partnership, the partnership is 'partnership at will'."

Essentials. (i) There is a partnership; (ii) the contract makes no provision for its duration; and (iii) the contract makes no provision for its determination. Both limbs are cumulative — a fixed-term partnership whose deed is silent on determination is not a partnership at will.

Incidents.

  1. Dissolution at pleasure — s. 43(1): where the partnership is at will, the firm may be dissolved by any partner giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to dissolve the firm.
  2. Date of dissolution — s. 43(2): the firm is dissolved as from the date mentioned in the notice as the date of dissolution, or if no date is so mentioned, as from the date of the communication of the notice.
  3. No court needed — dissolution is by the act of a partner, not by decree.
  4. Contrast s. 40: a partnership other than at will can be dissolved only by agreement or in accordance with the contract; and a partner cannot unilaterally dissolve it by notice.
  5. Continuing security — s. 43 read with s. 32(3) proviso: a partner's guarantee or continuing security given to a third party stands revoked as to future transactions from the date of dissolution.

Case-law pointers.

  • A firm whose deed fixes no term is at will, even if the business is substantial; the Supreme Court has held that the right to dissolve at will is not arbitrary — the notice must be bona fide.
  • Where the deed provides for determination by mutual consent, or on the happening of a specified event, the firm is not at will, although the court may still dissolve it under s. 44 on grounds such as misconduct, persistent breach, or transfer of the whole interest.
  • Note the interplay with s. 42: even a partnership at will is dissolved by the death or insolvency of a partner (subject to contract to the contrary), whereas dissolution by notice is the distinctive incident of a partnership at will.

Example. A, B and C carry on a textile business under a deed that states the profit-sharing ratio and the capital contributions but says nothing about how long the firm will last or how it may be ended. This is a partnership at will. A may give written notice to B and C on 1 July specifying 1 August as the date of dissolution; the firm stands dissolved on 1 August, and the accounts are then settled under s. 48.

Common mistake: candidates quote s. 7 but forget s. 43. A "partnership at will" answer must include the notice in writing requirement and the date rule of s. 43(2).
2011Business Association–I (OC) · Q.1(c)10 marks
Question Particular partnership.
Model Answer

Statutory text — s. 8, Indian Partnership Act, 1932:

s. 8 — Particular partnership

"A person may become a partner with another person in particular adventures or undertakings."

Meaning. A particular partnership is one formed for a single venture, undertaking or adventure (or for a defined series of such adventures) rather than for a continuing general business. It is the exception to the ordinary assumption that partnership involves a "business" of some continuity.

Features.

  • Scope limited by object. The firm exists to carry out the specified adventure; the authority of each partner (s. 19 implied authority) is correspondingly confined to acts "necessary for, or usually done in, carrying on the business of the kind carried on by the firm" — here, that adventure.
  • Terminates by completion. When the adventure is completed, the firm is dissolved (s. 42(a) — if constituted for a fixed term or a single adventure, the firm is dissolved by the expiry of the term or the completion of the adventure).
  • Everything else is ordinary partnership. ss. 4, 9–17, 18–27, 46–55 apply; the partners are jointly and severally liable for the acts of the firm done in the course of the adventure.
  • Not the same as a partnership at will. A particular partnership has a defined object; it is not at will simply because no fixed period is stated. Conversely, if the deed fixes no duration and no mode of determination, it may be both.
  • Question of fact: whether a single venture amounts to "carrying on business" — the Supreme Court and several High Courts have held that a single isolated transaction may amount to carrying on business if the surrounding circumstances show an intention to trade; whether it does is a question to be decided on the facts (see the DU 2003 question: "Whether single venture may amount to carrying on a business?").

Examples.

  1. A, B and C agree to build and sell one block of flats, sharing the profit. On sale of the last flat the firm is dissolved under s. 42(a).
  2. Three persons jointly underwrite a single public issue and share the commission.
  3. Two firms jointly execute one government contract as "ABC–XYZ (JV)".
  4. A single consignment of goods purchased jointly and sold at a profit.

1995Business Association–I (OC) · Q.2(a)10 marks
Question J, K and L are partners. Their deed provides that if any of them dies, his widow/eldest son will automatically become a partner in his place. J dies and his widow M claims to be admitted as a partner. Decide. (Same pattern as 1997 Q.1(b) — "if any dies his place will be taken by his widow")
Model Answer

Answer: M does not automatically become a partner. She must claim the benefits of the partnership under s. 37 and secure a fresh agreement; in default, the firm is dissolved as regards J's estate and she is entitled only to his share in the assets and profits.

Reasoning.

  1. Partnership arises from contract, not from status (s. 5). As the Supreme Court held in Dulichand Fatehchand Guzdar v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 74, the relation of partnership arises out of contract and not from status. Consequently a person cannot be thrust into partnership without his (or the other partners') agreement. A clause in a deed purporting to make a non-signatory a partner automatically is ineffective as a contract of partnership.
  2. Death dissolves the firm (s. 42(c)) subject to any contract to the contrary. A clause providing for automatic substitution is not, by itself, a "contract to the contrary" that keeps the firm alive with a new partner — it is, at best, an agreement to enter into an agreement.
  3. The widow's statutory rights are under s. 37. Where a partner dies and the surviving partners continue the business without a final settlement of accounts, the legal representative (or the deceased's estate) is entitled, absent a contrary contract, at his option to the share of profits attributable to the deceased partner's share, or to interest at 6% per annum on the amount of his share in the property of the firm. This is a right to benefits, not status.
  4. Minor's position (s. 30) is different and instructive. Under s. 30(1) a person who is a minor may be admitted to the benefits of partnership with the consent of all partners, and on attaining majority may, within six months, by public notice elect to become or not to become a partner. The statute thus contemplates election by the person concerned, never automatic substitution — reinforcing the contractual basis of partnership.
  5. Position of the HUF. If the deceased partner was a member of an HUF, the karta or other representative may be taken into the firm by agreement (see the 1995 Q.2(b) variant), but the other coparceners do not thereby become partners — they are not partners merely by reason of the karta's membership.

Conclusion. M has no automatic right to be admitted as a partner. She may (a) negotiate a fresh partnership with K and L; (b) claim the statutory benefits under s. 37 (share of profits or 6% interest) if the surviving partners continue the business; or (c) demand dissolution and settlement of accounts under ss. 46–48. She remains not liable for the firm's debts unless she holds herself out as a partner (s. 28).

Reasoning.

  2. They did not acquire the house for the purpose of carrying on a business of buying, selling or letting houses; they made one investment and enjoy its income. Realising the investment is not trading.

2015Business Association–I (OC) · Q.120 marks
Question "A partnership firm is not a legal entity." Comment, with reference to statutory provisions and decided cases.
Model Answer

Answer: Correct — a partnership firm has no legal personality distinct from its partners.

1. The statutory basis. s. 4, PA 1932 defines "partnership" as a relation between persons; "firm" and "firm name" (s. 4) mean merely the collective designation of those persons. Unlike s. 3(1) of the LLP Act, 2008 or the Companies Act, the Partnership Act nowhere confers incorporation.

2. Consequences that follow.

  • Suits must be brought by or against partners, impleaded in the firm name under Order XXX, CPC, 1908 — a procedural convenience, not recognition of personality.
  • Property (s. 14) is described as "property of the firm" but is held by the partners jointly for the purposes of the business;
  • Liability is the liability of the partners, joint and several and unlimited (ss. 25, 26);
  • Death or insolvency of a partner dissolves the firm (s. 42(b), (c)) — the firm has no life of its own;
  • A firm cannot be a "citizen" for the purposes of Part III of the Constitution in the same manner as a corporation, and cannot be prosecuted as such without naming partners.

3. The qualifications — how far the fiction is carried.

  • Registration under s. 59 and the effect of s. 69: an unregistered firm's disabilities are procedural (no suit to enforce a right arising from a contract; s. 69(3) permits suits for dissolution, accounts of a dissolved firm and for the value of property/realisation of common property) — not a denial of existence.
  • Tax law: the Income-tax Act treats the firm as a separate assessable unit (s. 2(31)(iv) read with ss. 4, 22 and 184–185) and the IBC, 2016 includes the firm within s. 5(21) "partnership firm" as a corporate debtor.
  • Accounting and commercial practice: the firm keeps its own books, has its own PAN, bank accounts, GST registration and goodwill (s. 14 includes goodwill).

4. Comparative contrast (quote these two provisions verbatim). LLP s. 3(1): "…is a body corporate formed and incorporated under this Act and is a legal entity separate from that of its partners." — and LLP s. 4: "Save as otherwise provided, the provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 shall not apply to a limited liability partnership." A company likewise has full corporate personality from incorporation.

5. Conclusion. The firm is a legal convenience, not a legal person. It has a name, a business, property and goodwill, and is treated as a unit for tax and procedural purposes; but in the ultimate analysis its rights and obligations are the rights and obligations of the partners who compose it.

  3. Quote one sentence from the judgment — e.g. Lord Cranworth's "liability of a principal for the acts of his agent".
  4. State both sides in a problem: "It may be argued… However…".
  5. Conclude in one unambiguous sentence. Never leave a problem hanging.
  6. Cross-link the statutes — ICA 1872 + PA 1932 + s. 3 PA bridge + LLP Act + Evidence Act s. 92 proviso 3 + CPC O.XXX + IT Act s. 2(31)(iv).
  7. Use the statutory lettering — ss. 19(a)–(h), s. 13(a)–(h), s. 6 Expl. 2(a)–(d).
  8. Add a caveat — holding out (s. 28), sham arrangements, third-party notice.
  9. Draw a small diagram — a four-box flow or a triangle takes 20 seconds and often earns a "presentation" mark.
  10. Answer the changed fact in a variant problem. DU reuses the skeleton and alters one fact (adds loss-sharing, adds control, removes notice). Identify it explicitly: "The new element is…".
! The eight traps candidates fall into
  1. Confusing revocation (by principal) with renunciation (by agent).
  2. Forgetting that s. 202 makes an agency coupled with interest irrevocable.
  3. Thinking a minor cannot be an agent — under s. 184 he can, vis-à-vis third parties.
  4. Saying sharing of profits is conclusive — it is only prima facie.
  5. Quoting s. 6 Expl. 2 but forgetting Expl. 1 (co-ownership).
  6. Treating the firm as a legal person.
  7. Saying an LLP partner is an agent of the other partners — LLP s. 26 says he is not.
  8. Forgetting s. 208: termination is effective against third parties only when it becomes known to them.
★ The golden sequence for ANY "Is X a partner / an agent?" problem
  1. Is there an agreement? (s. 4 / s. 5). Status alone — HUF, co-ownership, inheritance, marriage — never creates partnership.
  2. Is there a business? Continuity of transactions; a single venture may suffice (s. 8).
  3. Is there profit-sharing? If yes, is it profits "as such" or commission/remuneration?
  4. Does s. 6 Expl. 2 exempt it? Lender / servant / widow-child annuity / goodwill sale.
  5. Does s. 6 Expl. 1 exempt it? Co-ownership sharing gross returns.
  6. Is there MUTUAL AGENCY? Is the business carried on by some person acting for the alleged partner? Is the control initiative or merely protective?
  7. Liability for losses? Strong indicator, not a statutory essential.
  8. Holding out? Apply s. 28 (partnership) / s. 237 (agency) independently — even a non-partner may be liable.
  9. Sham? Look at "the body and substance of the arrangement" (Mollwo).
  10. Conclude — and add the consequence (liable / not liable; to whom; to what extent).
