0 · The unit map — seven lectures, one question: may the State touch expression before it is spoken — and after?

Unit 5 asked what the media may sell; Unit 6 asks what it may show and say — and who may stop it, when, and under what supervision. The unit’s architecture in one line: the doctrine of prior vs post restraint (A) → the one medium where the Constitution tolerates pre-publication censorship, cinema (A3–A5, B–E) → the courtroom’s own censor, the gag order (F–H) → the media’s digital twin of the gag order, s.69A blocking (I) → who may not be named: women, juveniles, matrimonial parties (J) → for whom the standard of the permissible changes: the child’s ladder of obscenity (K). Every prescribed case sits in one of these bays: Odyssey (injunction against telecast = prior restraint by court, not by board), Rangarajan (prior restraint by board = constitutional, but with strict tests), Bobby Art (how the board must read the guidelines), UIMPAA (compelled carriage, not suppression), Tuljapurkar (post-publication criminal prosecution for obscenity), Shreya (the online “blocking” power and why the State keeps asserting it), Reliance Petrochemicals (the Supreme Court’s own 25-day gag order, vacated), Sahara (the modern Constitution-Bench theory of postponement).

A1 · Pre- and post-restraint — the doctrine, defined and divided

The two engines, side by side

Prior restraint (censorship, injunction, licence condition)Post restraint (punishment, liability)
DefinitionState control exercised before speech reaches the public: examination + permission (certification); prohibition of a scheduled performance/telecast (injunction); conditions on what a licensee must show (licence conditions; must-carry).Sanction after publication for content that broke a line: criminal (obscenity, defamation, contempt, OSA), civil (damages, injunction restraining further copies), regulatory (revocation of licence, code advisories).
Constitutional postureSuspect for print: Brij Bhushan struck the “sixteen” sanction; “liberty of the press consists in allowing no previous restraint upon publication” (Reliance excerpt, PDF p. 293, quoting Bhagwati J. in Express Newspapers 1959 SCR 12). Accepted — with reasons — for cinema: Rangarajan (PDF p. 259): prior restraint “not only desirable but also necessary”. For trials, exceptional and temporary only: Sahara ¶¶31–34, 42.Always available, but each law must satisfy Art. 19(2) scrutiny (Rangagopal: “remedy … only after the publication and governed by the principles indicated herein”); obscenity post-1965 test = community standard not Hicklin (Shreya ¶¶45–46, PDF p. 41); contempt = s.2(c)+ss.3–7 defences (uploaded Act); a certified film is still prosecutable under the IPC “so that … the Penal Code pro tanto, will [not] hang limp” — but the certificate raises a rebuttable presumption (Raj Kapoor ¶14 as quoted in Bobby Art, PDF p. 276).
The presumptionAgainst validity (Pentagon Papers language quoted in Rajagopal ¶22: every system of prior restraint “comes to this Court bearing a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity”, and the Government “carries a heavy burden of showing justification”).Of constitutionality for legislative restrictions within the enumerated heads — but reasonableness is still tested (Chintaman Rao proportionality, invoked in Sahara ¶21).
Who decidesAn administrative expert body (Board of Film Certification; its committees) or a court (temporary injunction; postponement order) or the licensing authority (cinema licence conditions).Criminal courts (after investigation/ complaint; for obscenity, the court may itself view the film — Rangarajan: “We, as did the High Court, viewed the movie”).
Real examples (from these texts)1988: Bombay HC injuncts DD serial ‘Honi-Anhoni’ episodes 12–13 → SC vacates in days (Odyssey). 1987: Examining Committee refuses certificate to ‘Ore Oru Gramathile’; two Revising Committees reverse; Madras HC revokes the U-certificate → SC restores (Rangarajan). 1994–96: ‘Bandit Queen’ → A-certificate → FCAT → single judge quashes → LP upheld → SC restores A-certificate with the Tribunal’s conditions (Bobby Art).1996: a Gujjar-welfare association’s writ seeking a ban is dismissed — the petitioner is “not an aggrieved person” in the sense the courts entertain, and pre-publication displeasure earns nothing (Bobby Art). The Raj Kapoor ‘Satyam Shivam Sundaram’ prosecution under s.292 proceeded after certification — and the Court preserved both tracks (Bobby Art PDF p. 276). The Tuljapurkar prosecution (ss.292/153A/505(2) IPC) proceeded against a poem published in 1994 — 21 years of post-restraint before the SC even decided (K1).

Three sub-species of prior restraint the reader distinguishes (do not merge them in answers)

  • Content-censorship (licensing of expression itself): film certification — examination, cuts, refusal, categories (Cinematograph Act); the Direct-Performance power over “obscene, seditious, scandalous, defamatory” stage plays (DPA 1876, repealed); the struck-down pre-censorship of journals (s.7(1)(c) East PSU Act, Brij Bhushan).
  • Timing-restraint by courts (gag/postponement): Reliance (injunction during subscription window); Sahara (postponement orders as “neutralising device”, parameters at ¶42); Odyssey (injunction against telecast refused); Swatanter Kumar (Delhi HC actually granted postponement + deletion, 2014). The restraint is on publication of what the court already knows — not on the underlying work.
  • Compelled carriage (adding, not subtracting): UIMPAA — licence conditions requiring exhibition of approved Films Division shorts (1:5), DD cable mandatory (Cable TV Act s.19, Unit 4–5), Akashvani bulletin retransmission (2026 draft Rules r.44, flagged). Test: does it “further informed decision-making” or smuggle propaganda in?

A3 · Before cinema censored the stage — and the stage censored itself first

Censorship in India did not begin with film; it began with the platform — the street-corner play that could raise a mutiny before a newspaper could. The MIB notes (uploaded) supply the chapter’s prehistory at its exact pressure points:

1788–1818 · pre-licence era
Notes, pp. 6–8: Hicky’s Jepffee prosecuted 1788; “the freedom of the press was abolished … in 1799” by Wellesley’s licensing/censor regulations; “pre-censorship of the press was abolished in 1818 by Lord Hastings” — the first relaxation.
1823–1857 · licensing & gag statutes
Adam’s Regulations 1823 (licence + power “to restrain all publications which treat of the proceedings of Government… without previous permission”); Canning’s Act 1857 (power to prohibit publication of “any intelligence calculated to produce a bad effect upon the public mind”); the Press Act 1867 (security deposit + forfeiture machinery — the technique still alive in the PRB Act).
1860 · the Penal Code’s content lines
Notes, p. 10: the IPC of 1860 brought defamation and obscenity within the “regular” law — i.e., post-publication sanctions, the engine that Unit 6 Part K still runs on (ss.292–294 IPC → BNS 294–296). The Notes add the colonial logic: government could not ban beforehand, so it punished afterwards — “the printer was also to be punished”.
1876 · the stage Act

Reference box · The Dramatic Performances Act, 1876 (19 of 1876) External; reported

The syllabus prescribes the Act as a Ref for this unit (reader line 298); no text of it appears anywhere in the workspace — the passages below are from the statute’s public text and settled reporting, assembled for completeness.

  • Preamble: “An Act for the better control of public dramatic performances … WHEREAS it is expedient to empower the Government to prohibit public dramatic performances which are scandalous, defamatory, seditious or obscene.” — pre-censorship of the stage, parallel to what the Cinematograph Act later did to film: an ex ante prohibition order, not a prosecution.
  • s. 3 — power to prohibit: where the State Government is of the opinion that any play, pantomime or other drama performed or about to be performed in a public place is (a) of a scandalous or defamatory nature, or (b) likely to excite feelings of disaffection to the Government, or (c) likely to deprave and corrupt persons present at the performance — the State Government (or empowered Magistrate) may by order prohibit the performance. Explanation: a paid-admission building is a “public place”. Compare: Rangarajan’s Guidelines (2)(iv) “human sensibilities are not offended by vulgarity, obscenity or depravity” — the 1876 “deprave and corrupt” formula migrated into the 1983-era certification vocabulary.
  • ss. 4–6: service of the prohibition order; offence of giving notice of, or acting in, a prohibited performance (short imprisonment/fine); s. 6 bars damages suits. No appeal lay to the civil courts against the order (a pre-constitutional ouster — the very feature post-1950 jurisprudence would not forgive).
  • Fate: struck down as unconstitutional by a Full Court of the Allahabad High Court in State v. Babu Lal, AIR 1956 All 495 (post-19(1)(a) — the Act’s absence of any adversarial hearing and of judicial review); state versions followed the same path (the Madras High Court struck the Tamil Nadu Dramatic Performances Act, 1954 in 2013); formally wiped from the statute book by the Repealing and Amending (Second) Act, 2017 (Delhi had repealed it in 1963). Today the stage is regulated only by general law + local licensing.

Exam use: one paragraph — “the reader’s unit opens with cinema’s prior restraint because the first Indian prior-restraint statute targeted the stage; the DPA 1876’s triple test (scandalous–seditious–obscene) pre-figured s.5B(1)’s grounds; and its fate (unconstitutional for lack of hearing/review; repealed 2017) marks how far safeguards for prior restraint must travel to survive Art. 19(2).” External; reported

B · Case 25 — Odyssey Communications Pvt. Ltd. v. Lokvidayan Sanghatana, (1988) 3 SCC 410

Dossier · prior restraint by injunction · telecast
Court / Bench
Supreme Court of India — E.S. Venkataramaiah J. (for himself and N.D. Ojha J.); appeal by special leave from an interim order of the Bombay High Court, Aurangabad Bench.
Reader text
PDF pp. 253–255 (printed pp. 211–212), under the chapter’s editorial tag “Curtailment of Right of Citizen to Exhibit Films on T.V.”
Dispute in one line
A civic body’s PIL writ produced, within a day of filing, a High Court injunction stopping telecast of two episodes of a Doordarshan serial; the Supreme Court vacated it within fifteen days and told the petitioners where the line is.
FACTS (everything the excerpt prints)
  • The serial ‘Honi-Anhoni’ (roughly: omens and inauspicious omens — superstition as theme) was produced by Odyssey Communications Pvt. Ltd. and telecast by Doordarshan (run by the Union of India) every Thursday between 9 p.m. and 9.30 p.m. Episode 12 was due on 14.4.1988; episode 13 on 21.4.1988 — the serial’s finale.
  • Writ Petition No. 479 of 1988 was filed at the Aurangabad Bench by Lokvidayan Sanghatana (a registered social organisation of Pune with an Aurangabad branch) and Mahila Sangharsha Samiti, Aurangabad, represented by member Smt. Anagna Patil. The prayer: direct the Union, the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting and the State of Maharashtra “not to telecast the serial as such telecasting was not in the public interest”. The producer was not impleaded at first; it was added on its own application on 12.4.1988.
  • The pleaded grounds: “in each and every episode … an obscure and mysterious atmosphere was being created due to the way of the presentation of the episodes”, generating “fear in the minds of the common viewers and especially of children”, “confirming blind faiths, superstitious beliefs in stories of ghosts, rebirth, precognition etc.” and “spreading the unscientific way of thinking and blind beliefs”; “it was the duty of the State not to encourage blind beliefs amongst the public by telecasting such episodes.”
  • 13.4.1988 — the High Court granted the interim injunction (episode 12 could not telecast on 14.4). The appellant rushed to the Supreme Court under Art. 136; on 21.4.1988 the Court granted special leave, stayed the High Court order “until further orders” and permitted Doordarshan to telecast the serial — episode 12 went out on 21.4; the appeal was heard on 28.4.1988, judgment reserved, with the Court announcing then that it would set the order aside and give reasons later; episode 13 telecast on 28.4 under the subsisting stay.
  • Odyssey’s answer: the serial “did not emphasize superstitious beliefs but on the contrary criticized and condemned superstition and blind faith”, as ex facie apparent from the scripts produced before the Court; at the end of both episodes “a doctor and a professor gave a scientific explanation for the unusual occurrences portrayed therein”. The excerpt prints the closing address to viewers verbatim: “All those who without thinking spread blind faith ought to feel ashamed of themselves. We request all of you that whenever any unusual occurren[ce]s takes place or a seemingly impro[b]able event occurs, before believing in it, to reflect as to whether there is a scientific reason for it or is it purely psychological by nature. If all of us exercise such caution we believe that the malady of blind faith will soon be eradicated by our society.” — the Court thus read the anti-superstition message before balancing convenience.
ISSUES (as the excerpt shapes them)
  • (i) Whether a citizen has a fundamental right, within Art. 19(1)(a), to have his film/serial exhibited on Doordarshan — and whether an injunction against telecast is a restraint of that right?
  • (ii) Whether the High Court was justified in issuing ex parte interim injuncti[on] at the instance of petitioners asserting no legal right, without hearing the producer, on a plea of “public interest”?
  • (iii) The reserved question — “whether … a citizen has a fundamental right to establish a private broadcasting station, or television center”. Expressly not decided (“On this question we reserve our opinion. It has to be decided in an appropriate case”) — the question the Bombay High Court answered against private airwaves in 1993 and a Constitution Bench settled two years later in CAB (Unit 4 Part C).
ARGUMENTS — both sides
For the writ-petitioners (civic bodies)For the appellant-producer (Odyssey)
Public-interest ground: telecast entrenches superstition among adults and children; State’s duty not to encourage blind belief; the serial “was not in the public interest”, so the writ court may enjoin its telecast pending disposal. (The excerpt records the grounds as pleaded; the petitioners appear not to have argued a specific statute — “They had not asserted any right conferred on them by any statute or acquired by them under a contract…”.)(1) No opportunity — the court below issued an injunction “without giving a reasonable opportunity to it (the producer) … to explain that the writ petitioners had no right to move the Court”; (2) content defence — the scripts show condemnation, not promotion, of superstition (the verbatim closing message printed above); (3) constitutional frame — telecast is expression under Art. 19(1)(a), restrictable only under Art. 19(2); (4) delay/laches — the petitioners “waited till the exhibition of the 11th episode … was over” though they alleged that “all the episodes … were offensive”; they produced no material “apart from their own statements”.
DECISION (operative + reasoning)

Appeal allowed; interim order of 13.4.1988 set aside; no order as to costs — with the Court noting at hearing that episode 13’s telecast would proceed under its stay. The reasoning ladder:

  • Right located: “It can no longer be disputed that the right of a citizen to exhibit films on the Doordarshan subject to the terms and conditions to be imposed by the Doordarshan is a part of the fundamental right of freedom of expression guaranteed under Article 19(1)(a) … which can be curtailed only under circumstances which are set out in clause (2) of Article 19.” The right “is similar to the right of a citizen to publish his views through any other media such as newspapers, magazines, advertisement hoardings etc. subject to the terms and conditions of the owners of the media.”
  • Preferred-right posture: “Freedom of expression is a preferred right which is always very zealously guarded by this Court.”
  • No breach alleged or shown: no contravention of “any specific law or direction issued by the Government”; no favouritism/loss to exchequer pleaded; the objection “had … been raised … on the basis that it was likely to spread false or blind beliefs.”
  • Injunction analysis: the producers denied prejudice; the Union/Doordarshan said the serial was “being telecast after following the prescribed procedure and taking necessary precaution”; in “such a situation, the High Court should not have immediately proceeded to pass the interim order”. The petitioners’ own delay weighed; “In the absence of any prima facie evidence of grave prejudice that was likely to be caused to the public generally by the exhibition of the serial it was not just and proper to issue an order of temporary injunction. We are not satisfied that the exhibition of the serial in question was likely to endanger public morality.” The High Court “overlooked that the issue of an order of interim injunction in this case would infringe a fundamental right of the producer”.
  • Judicial restraint: “What we have stated here is sufficient to dispose of this appeal. The other questions of law which may arise in a case of this nature will have to be dealt with in an appropriate case. We express no opinion on those questions in this case.”
PRINCIPLES EVOLVED
  1. Telecast-access as expression: exhibiting films on (state) television is within Art. 19(1)(a), subject to the broadcaster’s terms and conditions — content restriction only via Art. 19(2). Read together with CAB (no right to own the airwaves): the speaker’s right travels, the medium’s licence does not.
  2. A temporary injunction restraining telecast of a programme is prior restraint and cannot issue at the instance of persons asserting no legal right, “in the absence of any prima facie evidence of grave prejudice to the public generally”. The balance of convenience runs with expression by default.
  3. Ex parte restraint without hearing the exhibitor is reversible error — procedure is part of the substantive protection (the same safeguard the 1991 certification scheme buys with the s.4(2) hearing proviso, and Sahara with its “hear the media” architecture).
  4. “Decency or morality” (Art. 19(2)) is not a general public-interest veto: the petitioners’ superstition-spreading theory failed not on the merits of the serial but because they had no rights-backed case and no evidence “apart from their own statements”. The judgment is a gag-order case before the vocabulary existed: note how fully its reasoning anticipates what Reliance Petrochemicals (1988 SCR Supp (3) 212 — decided within months; Part F) made explicit doctrine.

Real-example seam for answers: Odyssey is the PIL-injunction species of censorship — “any organisation of citizens may rush to court to stop a telecast” was neutralised here by requiring (a) a legal right, (b) prima facie grave prejudice, (c) urgency, (d) a hearing for the exhibitor. Compare Bobby Art: even a citizen petitioner with an equality/privacy grievance found the writ “ought not to have been entertained” against a duly certified film.

He issues the certificate under the reservation policy of the Government for the benefit of ‘backward communities’ identified on caste consideration.” Years later Karuppayee — an IAS-trained officer sent to village Annavayil for flood relief — is blackmailed by Anthony (the witness to the forgery); after his death and a government enquiry both she and her father are tried; the village demonstrates for her; “Karuppayee and her father admit in the Court the fact of their having obtained the false caste certificate but they attribute it to circumstances resulting by Government reservation policy on caste basis … They also argue that the reservation policy should not be based on caste, but could be on economic backwardness”; ultimately the government withdraws the case. “This is the theme of the picture presented. As usual, it contains some songs, dance and side attractions to make the film more delectable.”

The certification clock. 7.8.1987 — application for certificate. Examining Committee unanimously refused. On review, a first Revising Committee of nine: 8 in favour, 1 against; the Chairman of the Board then referred the film to a Second Revising Committee (again nine): majority 5:4 recommended a ‘U’ certificate subject to deletion of certain scenes. The minority (printed verbatim): “the film is treated in an irresponsible manner. The reservation policy of the Government is projected in a highly biased and distorted fashion”; the appeal “India is One” was “a hollow-appeal, which in effect touches caste sensitivity of the Brahmin forward caste”; one member: “the impact of the film will create law and order problem”; another: “the film will hurt the feelings and sentiments of certain sections of the public”. Producer and Union appealed — meanwhile “The film has since been given National Award by the Directorate of Film Festival of the Government of India.”

ISSUES
  • (1) Whether prior restraint (certification as precondition of exhibition) is constitutionally permissible for films at all — Sorabjee’s “fundamental point … about the freedom of free expression guaranteed under our Constitution even for the medium of movies”.
  • (2) The standard: whether a film may be judged by “isolated passages disregarding the main theme”, and by whose temperament (hypersensitive objector vs ordinary reasonable man).
  • (3) Whether the impugned scenes and dialogues (reel 3 song; the Ambedkar remark; reel 14 courtroom speech) contravene Guidelines (2)(vi)–(vii)/(ix) or Art. 19(2) heads (sovereignty and integrity; security; public order; decency).
  • (4) Procedure/validity: whether the first Revising Committee was unconstitutionally “hurriedly” constituted by a delegated Board member, vitiating the ‘U’ certificate; and whether the State’s law-and-order affidavit (threats by RPI/DAPM factions to “damage the cinema theatres which screen the film”; demonstrations; arrests on 16.3.1988) could itself justify suppression.

C3 · The doctrine — why cinema alone may be censored, and on what standard

Judgment, first movement — PDF pp. 258–261
(a) From “mere amusement” to constitutional speech — the American arc the Court adopts

“The motion pictures were originally considered as a form of amusement to be allowed to titillate but not to arouse. They were treated as mere entertainment and not an art or a means of expression. This theory was based on the concept that motion picture was a business ‘pure and simple originated and conducted for profit, like other spectacles.’ It was so held in 1915 by the unanimous decision of the American Supreme Court in Mutual Film Corporation v. Industrial Commission, 236 U.S. 230 (1915).” The correction: “The later decisions of the American Supreme Court have therefore declared that expression by means of motion pictures is included within the free speech and free press guaranty of the First Amendment. (See Burstyn v. Wilso[n], 343 U.S. 495).” The excerpt then narrates the danger-rule origin (“The danger rule was born in Schen[e]ck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919) … Justice Holmes … evolved the test of ‘clear and present danger’… ‘Words that may have all the effect of force’ calculated to achieve its goal by circumventing the democratic process are, however, not so protected”).

(b) The uniqueness argument — the heart of film censorship
“Movie doubtless enjoys the guaranty under Article 19(1)(a) but there is one significant difference between the movie and other modes of communication. The movie cannot function in a free market place like the newspaper, magazine or advertisement. Movie motivates thought and action and assures a high degree of attention and retention. It makes its impact simultaneously arousing the visual and aural senses… The combination of act and speech, sight and sound in semi-darkness of the theatre with elimination of all distracting ideas will have an impact in the minds of spectators… It is said: ‘…as an instrument of education it has unusual power to impart information, to influence specific attitudes… and to affect profoundly the patterns of conduct of children.’… It can, therefore, be said that the movie has unique capacity to disturb and arouse feelings. It has as much potential for evil as it has for good. … With these qualities and since it caters for mass audience who are generally not selective about what they watch, the movie cannot be equated with other modes of communication. Censorship by prior restraint is, therefore, not only desirable but also necessary.”Rangarajan, PDF p. 259 (printed p. 216) — the Bettelheim & Janowitz Reader in Public Opinion and Communication quote is the Court’s own authority for the “cumulative effect” of films.

The doctrinal contrast the Court draws against the US: “Here again we find the difference between the First Amendment … and Article 19(1)(a)…. The First Amendment does not permit any prior restraint, since the guaranty of free speech is in unqualified terms.” Douglas J. (with Black J.) in Kingsley [International Pictures Corp.] v. Regents of the University of New York is quoted at length — including the concession from the dissenting side: “‘If we had a provision in our Constitution for “reasonable” regulation of the press such as India has included in hers, there would be room for argument that censorship in the interests of morality would be permissible… But its language, in terms that are absolute is utterly at war with censorship.’” Answer structure: India chose the reasonable-restriction clause; hence statutory prior restraint for films survives (as validated in K.A. Abbas), but only under the Abbas conditions — which the reader reproduces through Sahara ¶28: “this Court upheld prior restraint on exhibition of motion pictures subject to Government setting up a corrective machinery and an independent Tribunal and reasonable time limit within which the decision had to be taken by the censoring authorities.”

(c) The Act, read into the Constitution (PDF pp. 259–260)

The Court maps the statute in a single paragraph — s.3 Board; s.4 examination; s.5 Revising Committee; s.5A “U” certificate — and then states the design principle: “it is significant to note that Article 19(2) has been practically read into Section 5(B)(1)”; s.5C appeals to the Tribunal; s.6 Government revision; r.22/r.24 of the 1983 Rules; and the Guidelines under s.5B(2) (objectives; (i)–(ix) prohibitions; overall-impact rule — quoted in C2/A5). “Therefore, the censorship by prior restraint must necessarily be reasonable that could be saved by the well accepted principles of judicial review.” Then the censor’s posture, from K.A. Abbas (at 473–74) as printed: “The task of the censor is extremely delicate..... The standards that we set out for our censors must make a substantial allowance in favour of freedom thus leaving a vast area for creative art to interpret life and society with some of its foibles along with what is good… the line is to be drawn where the average [moral] man begins to feel embarrassed or disgusted at a naked portrayal of life without the redeeming touch of art or genius [or] social value. If the depraved begins to see in these things more than what an average person would, in much the same way as it is wrongly said, a Frenchman sees a woman’s legs in everything, it cannot be helped. In our scheme of things ideas having redeeming social or artistic value must also have importance and protection for their growth.”

(d) The audience standard — Vivian Bose → Ramesh v. U.I. (‘TAMAS’) → Rangarajan
“The learned Judge quoting with approval of the observation of Vivian Bose, J., as he then was, in the Nagpur High Court in the case of Bhagwati Charan Shukla v. Provincial Government, AIR 1947 Nag 1 (at 676): ‘That the effect of the words must be judged from the standards of reasonable, strong minded, firm and courageous men, and not those of weak and vacillating’ … “We affirm and reiterate this principle. The standard to be applied by the Board or courts for judging the film should be that of an ordinary man of common sense and prudence and not that of an out of the ordinary or hypersensitive man.”PDF pp. 260–261 — “the man on the top of a Clapham omnibus”, as the Court (playfully) says.

Then the counterweight — the Court’s “one word more”: the Board “should exercise considerable circumspection on movies affecting the morality or decency of our people and cultural heritage of the country. The moral values in particular, should not be allowed to be sacrificed in the guise of social change or cultural assimilation” — a roll-call from the “galaxy of great sages and thinkers” (Shankaracharya, Ramanujacharya, Madhwacharya, Chaitanya, Ramakrishna Paramhansa, Guru Nanak, Sant Kabir, Gandhi; the “TIRUKKURAL” of Tiruvalluvar; “the concept of ‘Dharam’… a unique contribution of Indian civilization”), immediately balanced: “We do not, however, mean that the Censors should have an orthodox or conservative outlook. Far from it, they must be responsive to social change and they must go with the current climate.” And the people’s ultimate control over the censor, via Krishna Iyer J. in Rajkapoor v. Laxman [as printed; cf. Raj Kapoor v. State, 1980 (1) SCC 43], at 517: “The ultimate censorious power over the Censors belongs to the people and by indifference, laxity or abetment, pictures which pollute public morals are liberally certified, the legislation, meant by Parliament to protect people's good morals, may be sabotaged by statutory enemies within.”

C4 · The tests, the hostile audience, the decision — and the principles

(e) Democracy and counter-speech — the long middle (PDF pp. 265–268)

Against the High Court’s “volatile reaction” reasoning: “It seems to us that the reasoning of the High Court runs a foul of the democratic principles to which we have pledged ourselves in the Constitution. In democracy it is not necessary that every one should sing the same song. Freedom of expression is the rule and it is generally taken for granted. Every one has a fundamental right to form his own opinion on any issue of general concern. He can form and inform by any legitimate means. The democracy is a Government by the people via open discussion… The democracy can neither work nor prosper unless people go out to share their views.” The excerpt then runs its citation-cascade — each an authority to bank: Walter Lippmann (“When men act on the principle of intelligence, they go out to find the facts … When they ignore it, they go inside themselves and find out what is there. They elaborate their prejudice instead of increasing their knowledge.”); Bhagwati J. in Maneka Gandhi at 696 (“Democracy is based essentially on free debate and open discussion, for that is the only corrective of Government action in a democratic set up…”); Naraindas v. State of M.P. at 650 (“there must be freedom not only for the thought that we cherish, but also for the thought that we hate”, attributing the marketplace passage to “Justice Holmes in Abramson v. Such are the demands of that pluralism, tolerance and broadmindedness without which there is no ‘democratic society’… every ‘formality’, ‘condition’, ‘restriction’ or ‘penalty’ … must be proportionate to the legitimate aim pursued”).

(f) THE TEST — the single most quotable paragraph of the unit
“The problem of defining the area of freedom of expression when it appears to conflict with the various social interests enumerated under Article 19(2) may briefly be touched upon here. There does indeed have to be a compromise between the interest of freedom of expression and social interests. But we cannot simply balance the two interests as if they are of equal weight. Our commitment to freedom of expression demands that it cannot be suppressed unless the situations created by allowing the freedom are pressing and the community interest is endangered. The anticipated danger should not be remote, conjectural or far-fetched. It should have proximate and direct nexus with the expression. The expression of thought should be intrinsically dangerous to the public interest. In other words, the expression should be inseparably locked up with the action contemplated like the equivalent of a ‘spark in a powder keg’.”PDF p. 268 — ¶ as later re-quoted by Shreya Singhal ¶45 (reader PDF p. 40: “We have echoes of it in our law as well”). This is India’s version of incitement, forged for films and generalised for all media.
(g) Application — reel by reel (PDF pp. 264–267)
  • Sovereignty/security pleaded, found absent: “We fail to understand how the expression in the film with criticism of reservation policy or praising the colonial rule will affect the security of the State or sovereignty and integrity of India. There is no utterance in the film threatening to overthrow the Government by unlawful or unconstitutional means. There is no talk for secession either. Nor there is any suggestion for impairing the integration of the country. All that the film seems to suggest is that the existing method of reservation on the basis of caste is bad and reservation on the basis of economic backwardness is better. The film also deprecates exploitation of people on caste considerations. This is the range and rigor [sic] of the film.” (Note the method: reading the reel-14 tirade in context — “animals’ hands” explained in the film itself “only to those who incite caste, language and communal fanaticism”.)
  • The volatile-audience theory, rejected: “Whether this view is right or wrong is another matter altogether and at any rate we are not concerned with its correctness or usefulness to the people. We are only concerned whether such a view could be advocated in a film. To say that one should not be permitted to advocate that view goes against the first principle of our democracy.”
  • The State’s law-and-order affidavit — the “anguished question”: “We are amused yet troubled by the stand taken by the State Government with regard to the film which has received the National Award. We want to put the anguished question, what good is the protection of freedom of expression if the State does not take care to protect it? If the film is unobjectionable and cannot constitutionally be restricted under Article 19(2), freedom of expression cannot be suppressed on account of threat of demonstration and processions or threats of violence. That would tantamount to negation of the rule of law and a surrender to black mail and intimidation. It is the duty of the State to protect the freedom of expression since it is a liberty guaranteed against the State. The State cannot plead its inability to handle the hostile audience problem. It is its obligatory duty to prevent it and protect the freedom of expression.”
  • Deference to the expert committees: “In this case, two Revising Committees have approved the film. The members thereof come from different walks of life with variegated experiences. They represent the cross section of the community. They have judged the film in the light of the objectives of the Act and the guidelines provided for the purpose. We do not think that there is anything wrong or contrary to the Constitution in approving the film for public exhibition.”
(h) Decision & principles evolved

Order: “In the result, we allow these appeals, reverse the judgment of the High Court and dismiss the writ petitions of the respondents… no order as to costs.” Ending note: “Freedom of expression which is legitimate and constitutionally protected, cannot be held to ransom by an intolerant group of people. The fundamental freedom under Article 19(1)(a) can be reasonably restricted only for the purposes mentioned in Articles 19(2) and the restriction must be justified on the anvil of necessity and not the quirks [printed: “the quirks and of convenience”] or expediency. Open criticism of Government policies and operations is not a ground for restricting expression. We must practice tolerance to the views of others. Intolerance is as much dangerous to democracy as to the person himself.”

  1. Prior restraint on films is constitutional (unlike print), for the “free marketplace” reason; but it survives only as a regulated prior restraint — Art. 19(2)-mirroring grounds (s.5B(1)), published Guidelines (s.5B(2)), expert multi-member committees, time-bounded procedure, and full judicial review (Abbas conditions re-stated in Sahara ¶28).
  2. The overall-impact rule: a film is judged “in its entirety”; isolated passages, songs or one or two “not palatable” references cannot carry a ban; courts read the treatment, not the topic.
  3. The ordinary reasonable man standard (Vivian Bose through TAMAS to Rangarajan): firm and courageous viewers, not the “out of the ordinary or hypersensitive man”.
  4. The suppression test — pressing situations, endangered community interest, proximate and direct nexus, intrinsic dangerousness, “spark in a powder keg” — anticipating (and, in the reader’s own arrangement, preceding) Shreya Singhal ¶45.
  5. The hostile-audience rule: threats of violence by objectors are an argument against restraint; the State owes expression protection, not panic management.
  6. Delegation and committee procedure are curable and speed is virtue — s.7B order + “statutory right to have the validity of the film determined in accordance with law”.

Real-example seam: in 2023 the CBFC certified Gandhi Godse — Ek Yudh (Gandhi’s murderer pardoning Gandhi) despite protests; certification controversies over Padmaavat (2018) and Kalidas/jallikattu-era films show Rangarajan’s hostile-audience paragraph being tested every year — courts routinely invoke it to refuse bans. D · Case 27 — Bobby Art International v. Om Pal Singh Hoon, (1996) 4 SCC 1

Dossier · “Bandit Queen” · the A-certificate and the social-relevance theme
Court / Bench
Supreme Court — S.P. Bharucha, CJI (for himself and B.N. Kirpal, J.); appeals against a Delhi High Court Division Bench (LPA) which had upheld a Single Judge’s quashing of the film’s certificate.
Reader text
PDF pp. 272–280 (printed pp. 229–237).
Dispute in one line
Phoolan Devi’s life on screen: Board refused, Revising Committee recommended “A” with cuts, the FCAT (Lentin C.J. + three women members) restored the contested nudity and the vernacular abuses, and gave “A”; a member of the Gujjar community obtained a writ quashing that certificate; the Supreme Court restored the film entire — and in doing so handed Indian censorship its most-used canon: do they advance the message?
FACTS (dates exactly as printed)
  • The film, “based upon a true story”, is summarised at length by the Court itself — child-marriage to “a man old enough to be her father”, the beating and rape; the village panchayat blaming her for “enticing” a higher-caste boy; the “indignity and humiliation in the Police station”; bail granted by men “not due to compassion but to satisfy their carnal appetite”; abduction and sexual brutalisation by dacoit-leader Babu Gujjar; Vikram Mallah shooting him “in a fit of rag”; the revenge beating of her husband; the gang-rape by Sri Ram, Lalaram “and others”, and the central scene — “She was stripped naked, paraded and made to fetch water from the village well under the gaze of the villagers, but no one came to her rescue”; the Behmai revenge (twenty Thakurs killed); surrender; years in jail.
  • 17.8.1994 — presented for certification; Examining Committee referred to the Revising Committee under r.24(1) of the 1983 Rules; 19.7.1995 — Revising Committee recommended an ‘A’ certificate subject to excisions and modifications (“An ‘A’ certificate implies that the film may be viewed only by adults”).
  • Producer appealed under s.5C to the s.5D Appellate Tribunal — “It is constituted by virtue of the provisions of Section 5C of the Cinematograph Act and consists of the Chairman and members who ‘are qualified to judge the effect of films on the public’. In the present case the tribunal was chaired by Lentin, J., a retired Judge of the Bombay High Court, and three ladies, Smt. Sara Mohammad, Dr. Sarayu V. Doshi, & Smt. Reena Kumari, were its members.” The Tribunal’s order is quoted: its ¶3.1 on the dialect — “The tone and tenor of the dialogues in this film reflect the nuances locally and habitually used and spoken in the villages and in the ravines of the Chambal, not bereft of expletives used for force and effect by way of normal and common parlance in those parts; these expletives are not intended to be taken literally. There is nothing sensual or sexual about these expletives…” — its famous refusal to interfere — “To delete or even to reduce these climactic visuals would be a sacrilege” — and its contextual retention of “certain words of abuse in the vernacular”: “spoken as they are as colloquially and as part of their daily life, it would be unfair on our part to castigate the use of these words which we would otherwise have done”. On this unanimous order the ‘A’ certificate issued.
  • 31.8.1995 — screened with English subtitles at the Siri Fort Film Festival of India with MIB permission; from 25.1.1996 the censored film ran publicly. 27.1.1996 — respondent No.1 (a Hindu Gujjar, president of the Gujjar Gaurav Sansthan, “involved in the welfare of the Gujjar community”, who had seen it at the International Film Festival) filed a writ petition to quash the certificate and restrain exhibition: the depiction was “abhorrent and unconscionable and a slur on the womanhood of India”; naming the villain “Babu Gujjar” “lowered the reputation of the Gujjar community and the petitioner”; the rape scene “obscene and horrendous”; Articles 14, 19 and 21 invoked. Single Judge quashed the certificate, directed the Board to reconsider an ‘A’ certificate after excisions “in accordance with his order”, and enjoined screening meanwhile; the Division Bench upheld, finding “the Tribunal’s order was vitiated by the use of the wrong tests”.
ISSUES
  • (1) Justiciability: may a stranger-petitioner (not producer, distributor or exhibitor) attack a granted certificate under Art. 226 at all — “the machinery under Cinematograph Act was only for those who had some concern with the making of the film and … citizens who were offended by it were free to approach the High Court under Article 226” (Dr Koul’s argument)?
  • (2) Whether s.5B(1) “decency or morality” + Guidelines 2(vii)/(ix)/(x) (sensibilities; denigrating women; sexual violence) were breached by the frontal nudity, the rape scene and the abuses — judged scene-by-scene (respondent) or by overall impact (Board/Tribunal)?
  • (3) Whether the certifying Tribunal — an expert, multi-member body including three women — applied the “wrong tests”.
ARGUMENTS
Appellants (counsel for the producer) + Additional Solicitor General for the UnionRespondent No.1 — Dr Koul
The film “had been scrutinized by the Tribunal, which was an expert body constituted for that purpose, and it had passed the test of such scrutiny”; “three members of the four-member Tribunal were ladies and they had not found anything offensive in the film as certified for adult viewing”; the Guidelines require only that the film not offend s.5B(1) or the guidelines; sexual-violence scenes are permissible “if germane to the theme … reduced to the minimum and no details are shown” — and here they serve the theme.The Act’s machinery is for those “with some concern with the making of the film”; offended citizens may still come to the High Court; “There were compelling reasons for the High Court to pass the order that it did for the film was abhorrent”; “What had also to be considered were the individual episodes, and the episodes depicting full frontal nudity, rape and the use of swear words offended the requirements of sub-clauses (vii), (ix) and (x) of the guidelines”; and — a remarkable inversion — “the film violated the freedom of speech and expression of the first respondent”.
The Court’s own canon of precedent (each printed at PDF pp. 275–278 — all usable as “reader-located” citations)
  • K.A. Abbas (Constitution Bench) at length: ¶49 — “The task of the censor is extremely delicate and his duties cannot be the subject of an exhaustive set of commands established by prior ratiocination… Our standards must be so framed that we are not reduced to a level where the protection of the least capable and the most depraved amongst us determines what the morally healthy cannot view or read… Sex and obscenity are not always synonymous and it is wrong to classify sex as essentially obscene or even indecent or immoral. It should be our concern, however, to prevent the use of sex designed to play a commercial role by making its own appeal. This draws in the censor’s scissors.” ¶50 — “it is not the elements of rape, leprosy, sexual immorality which should attract the censor’s scissors but how the theme is handled by the producer. The horrors of war as depicted in the famous etchings of Goya do not horrify one so much as the same scenes rendered in colour and with sound and movement would do. We may view a documentary on the erotic tableaux from our ancient temples with equanimity or read the Kamasutra but a documentary from them as a practical sexual guide would be abhorrent.”
  • Raj Kapoor & Ors. v. State, 1980 (1) SCC 43 (the ‘Satyam Shivam Sundaram’ prosecution): Krishna Iyer J. — “Art, morals and law’s manacles on aesthetics are sensitive subject where jurisprudence meets other social sciences and never goes alone to bark and bite because State-made strait-jacket is an inhibitive prescription for a free country unless enlightened society actively participates in the administration of justice to esthetics.”; “The world’s greatest paintings, sculptures, songs and dances, India’s lustrous heritage, the Konaraks and Khajurahos, lofty epics, luscious in patches, may be asphyxiated by law, if prudes and prigs and State moralists prescribe paradigms…”; and the two operative rules the exam always wants: “May be, even a rebuttable presumption arises in favour of the statutory certificate but could be negatived by positive evidence” (¶14) and “I am not persuaded that once a certificate under the Cinematograph Act is issued the Penal Code, pro tanto, will hang limp. The court will examine the film and judge whether its public display, in the given time and clime, so breaches public morals or depraves basic decency as to offend the penal provisions” (¶15) — certification and prosecution coexist; the s.5A(1) proviso only immunises the obscenity laws.
  • Samaresh Bose v. Amal Mitra (the ‘Prajapati’ novel): “A vulgar writing is not necessarily obscene. Vulgarity arouses a feeling of disgust and revulsion and also boredom but does not have the effect of depraving, debasing and corrupting the morals of any reader of the novel, whereas obscenity has the tendency to deprave and corrupt those whose minds are open to such immoral influences.” Plus the author-position rule (“If we place ourselves in the position of the author and judge the novel from his point of view… the author intends to expose various evils and ills pervading the society”) and the readership-class rule (readers “of both sexes and of all ages between teenagers and the aged… will read the book with a sense of shock and disgust and we do not think that any reader on reading this book would become depraved, debased and encouraged to lasciviousness”; “it cannot be right to insist that the standard should always be for the writer to see that the adolescent may not be brought into con[t]act with sex”).
  • State of Bihar v. Shailabala Devi (Mukherjee J.): “the writing had to be looked at as a whole without laying stress on isolated passages… what effect the writing was likely to produce on the minds of the readers for whom the publication was intended… Account had also to be taken of the place, circumstances and occasion of the publication.”
  • Sakal + LIC v. Manubhai Shah: “the only restrictions which can be imposed on the rights of an individual under Article 19(1)(a) were those which clause (2) of Article 19 permitted and no other.”

D2 · The message test, the judgment, and what Bobby Art changed

DECISION — the reasons (PDF pp. 278–280)
  • How to read the Guidelines: the clause quoted in A5 — broad standards, discretion within them, sub-clauses cannot outweigh the sweep; “Where the theme is of social relevance, it must be allowed to prevail. Such a theme does not offend human sensibilities nor extol the degradation or denigration of women. It is to this end that sub-clause (ix) of clause 2 permits scenes of sexual violence against women, reduced to a minimum and without details, if relevant to the theme. What minimum and lack of details should be is left to the good sense of the certification authorities, to be determined in the light of the relevance of the social theme of the film.”
  • The film re-told to make the point: “It is not a pretty story. There are no syrupy songs or pirouetting round trees. It is the serious and sad story of a worm turning: a village born female, becoming a dreaded dacoit: an innocent who turns into a vicious criminal because lust and brutality have affected her psyche… The film levels an accusing finger at members of society who had tormented Phoolan Devi and driven her to become a dreaded dacoit filled with the desire for revenge. It is in this light that the individual scenes have to be viewed.”
  • The stripping scene: “The effect of so doing upon her could hardly have been better conveyed than by explicitly showing the scene. The object of doing so was not to titillate the cinema-goer’s lust but to arouse in him sympathy for the victim and disgust for the perpetrators. The revulsion that the Tribunal referred to was not at Phoolan Devi’s nudity but at the sadism and heartlessness of those who had stripped her naked to rob her of every sh[are] of dignity. Nakedness does not always arouse the baser [instinct].” The Tribunal’s parallel — printed — is to ‘Schindler’s List’: Jews “shown frontally, being led into the gas chambers… stripped in their last moments of the basic dignity of human beings. Tears are a likely reaction; pity, horror and a fellow feeling of shame are certain, except in the pervert or to assuage the susceptibilities of the over-sensitive.”
  • The rape scene: “Rape is crude and its crudity is what the rapist’s bouncing bare posterior is meant to illustrate. Rape and sex are not being glorified in the film. Quite the contrary. It shows what a terrible, and terrifying, effect rape and lust can have upon the victim. It focuses on the trauma and emotional turmoil of the victim to evoke sympathy for her and disgust for the rapist.” The expletives: “Too much need not, we think, be made of a few swear words the like of which can be heard every day in every city, town and village street. No adult would be tempted to use them because they are used in this film.”
  • The test itself: “In sum, we should recognize the message of a serious film and apply this test to the individual scenes thereof: do they advance the message? If they do they should be left alone, with only the caution of an ‘A’ certificate. Adult Indian citizens as a whole may be relied upon to comprehend intelligently the message and react to it, not to the possible titillation of some particular scene.”
  • The depiction principle: “A film that illustrates the consequences of a social evil necessarily must show that social evil. The guidelines must be interpreted in that light. No film that extols the social evil or encourages it is permissible, but a film that carries the message that the social evil is evil cannot be made impermissible on the ground that it depicts the social evil. At the same time, the depiction must be just sufficient for the purpose of the film. The drawing of the line is best left to the sensibilities of the expert Tribunal.”
  • Composition as competence: “apart from the Chairman, three members of the Tribunal were woman. It is hardly to supposed that three women would permit a film to be screened which denigrates women, insults Indian womanhood or is obscene or pornographic. It would appear from its order that the Tribunal took the view that it would do women some good to see the film.”

Operative order: “We think that the High Court ought not to have entertained the 1st respondent’s writ petition impugning the grant of the certificate based as it was principally upon the slurs allegedly cast by the film on the Gujjar community… The appeals are allowed. The judgment and order under appeal is set aside. The 1st respondent’s writ petition is dismissed. The ‘A’ certificate issued to the film ‘Bandit Queen’ upon the conditions imposed by the Appellate Tribunal is restored. The 1st respondent shall pay to each appellant the costs of his appeal.”

PRINCIPLES EVOLVED
  1. Message-advancement test for scene-by-scene challenges: the question is not “is this image obscene in isolation?” but “does it advance the film’s message?” — if yes, leave it alone, with the ‘A’ caution.
  2. Depiction ≠ glorification: the extols/depicts line is now the default answer in every “violence against women on screen” controversy — and note its built-in proportionality: the depiction must be “just sufficient for the purpose of the film”.
  3. Guidelines are standards, not statutes — sub-clause lawyering is illegitimate; the sweep (clauses 1 and 3) governs; the certification authority’s good sense on “minimum and lack of details” is respected “except in case of stark breach”.
  4. Institutional competence: an expert, representative (here: women-majority) tribunal’s finding is the right unit of deference; courts should not re-cut films in writ jurisdiction; the objector’s community-sentiment grievance is not a ground to entertain — enforced, at the cost order.
  5. Certificate + prosecution coexist (via the Raj Kapoor ¶14–15 quotations): ‘A’ + proviso = immunity from obscenity law only; defamation/contempt/public-order provisions still bite post-release — the hinge to Part K and to the Tuljapurkar box.

Real-example seam: ‘Bandit Queen’ won the National Film Award (Marathi? — no: the film is Hindi) and remains the canonical illustration of s.5A(1)-proviso immunity being tested — Phoolan Devi herself later litigated and settled over the film’s title sequence (external colour; use “the film’s subject publicly disputed its portrayal — yet no court revived the ban” only if asked). The 1996 “costs” order is the strongest anti-PIL-censorship signal in the reader: frivolous censorship writs now carry price tags.

Supreme Court — Sujata V. Manohar J. (for herself and K. Venkataswami & R.C. Lahoti, JJ.), on appeals by the Union, West Bengal and Uttar Pradesh against a Delhi High Court judgment dated 31.8.1995 (C.W.P. Nos. 4408 & 4703 of 1993), and the Eastern India Motion Picture Association’s writ petition.
Reader text

Dispute in one line
For decades cinema licensees had to show an “approved/notified” scientific/educational/documentary/news short at every performance, at a 1:5 ratio, hired from the Films Division for 1% of net weekly collection. In 1993 the exhibitors’ associations said: that is compelled speech violating Art. 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g); the High Court agreed about the money and struck Condition 15; the Supreme Court reversed — and built India’s must-carry jurisprudence out of Woolley and Turner.

ISSUES
  • (1) Does compelling a licensee to exhibit approved short films/bulletins at all abridge Art. 19(1)(a) (exhibitors also pleaded 19(1)(g)) — given that “Article 19(2) … does not cover this kind of compulsion to show educational, scientific and documentary films”?
  • (2) Is compelled speech (“must-carry”) an infringement of free speech even absent a textual 19(2) head — what test decides it?
  • (3) Are the content specifications (categories of approved films) permissible if US must-carry law demands content-neutrality?
  • (4) Are Condition 15 (lantern slides/15 minutes) and Condition 22 (600 m approved films) redundant/ultra vires s.12(4)? Is the Films Division rental unconstitutional?

E2 · Arguments, Woolley & Turner, the decision, the principles

ARGUMENTS

Exhibitors: free speech is the foundation of democratic society — the judgment itself opens by adopting the vocabulary (“A free exchange of ideas, dissemination of information without restraints, dissemination of knowledge, airing of differing viewpoints, debating and forming one’s own views and expressing them, are the basic indicia of a free society. This freedom alone makes it possible for people to formulate their own views and opinions on a proper basis and to exercise their social, economic and political rights in a free society in an informed manner.”) — and then: “just as a restraint on free speech is a violation of Article 19(1)(a) [except as permitted under Article 19(2)], compelled speech, often known as a ‘must carry’ provision … is equally an infringement … except to the extent permitted under Article 19(2)”; the categories are content-specified, hence not “content-neutral”; the Films Division monopoly + NOC renewal-control make the compulsion total; the 1% rental is an unconstitutional exaction.

Union: the provisions are decades-old licence regulation designed to give indigenous, scientific and educational films “an adequate opportunity of being exhibited”; none requires propaganda; approval is quality/compliance control; the rental is contractual and negotiated with the industry’s own federation; Condition 15 reads with s.12(4).

THE TEST — the “informed decision-making” criterion (PDF pp. 286–287)
“However, whether compelled speech will or will not amount to a violation of the freedom of speech and expression, will depend on the nature of a ‘must carry’ provision. If a ‘must carry’ provision furthers informed decision-making which is the essence of the right to free speech and expression, it will not amount to any violation of the fundamental freedom of speech and expression. If, however, such a provision compels a person to carry out propaganda or project a partisan or distorted point of view, contrary to his wish, it may amount to a restraint on his freedom of speech and expression. To give an example, at times a statute imposes an obligation to print certain information in public interest. Any food product must carry on its package the list of ingredients used in its preparation, or must print its weight. These are beneficial ‘must carry’ provisions meant to inform the public about the correct quantity and contents of the product it buys… Cigarette cartons are required to carry a statutory warning that cigarette smoking is harmful to health. This is undoubtedly a ‘must carry’ provision or compelled speech. Nevertheless, it is meant to further the basic purpose of imparting relevant information which will enable a user to make a correct decision as to whether he should smoke a cigarette or not. Such mandatory provisions although they compel speech cannot be viewed as a restraint on the freedom of speech and expression.”UIMPAA, PDF pp. 286–287 (printed 243) — the paragraph cited by Unit 4 (Tata-Press-style commercial-speech discussion) and by Unit 5 (DMR Schedule warnings); here it is the ratio.
The American pair, as the Court reads them
  • Wooley v. Maynard, [1977] 430 US 70S [sic — 705]: New Hampshire’s compulsion to carry “Live Free or Die” on number plates “invaded First Amendment rights and could not be justified as facilitating the identification of passenger vehicles or as promoting an appreciation of history, individualism, and state pride.” — the paradigm of impermissible compelled speech (a State motto foisted on a Jehovah’s Witness).
  • Turner Broadcasting System v. FCC, [1997] 512 US 622: the Cable Act’s must-carry rules were consistent with the First Amendment because their purpose was “to preserve the benefits of free over-the-air local broadcast television, promoting wide-spread dissemination of information from a multiplicity of sources and promoting fair competition in the television programme market”; Breyer J. (partly concurring) “balanced the restraints which such a compulsory carriage clause would impose because it would interfere with the protected interests of the cable operators to choose their own programming, against an important First Amendment interest in favour of the provision viz. promoting the widest possible dissemination of information from diverse and antagonistic sources to facilitate public discussion and informed deliberation. The latter being basic democratic government purposes which the First Amendment seeks to achieve, they outweighed objections relating to interference with the cable operators’ right to choose their own programme.”
DECISION
  • Social-context rule: “Although the First Amendment right under the U.S. Constitution is not subject to reasonable restraint as in Article 19(2), the raison d’être of a constitutional guarantee of free speech is the same. We have to examine whether the purpose of compulsory speech in the impugned provisions is to promote the fundamental freedom of speech and expression and dissemination of ideas, or whether it is to restrain this freedom; the social context of any such legislation cannot be ignored. When a substantially significant population body is illiterate or does not have easy access to ideas or information, it is important that all available means of communication, particularly audio-visual communication, are utilized not just for entertainment but also for education, information, propagation of scientific ideas and the like. The best way by which ideas can reach this large body of uneducated people is through the entertainment channel which is watched by all — literate and illiterate alike. To earmark a small portion of time of this entertainment medium for the purpose of showing scientific, educational or documentary films, or for showing news films has to be looked at in this context of promoting dissemination of ideas, information and knowledge to the masses so that there may be an informed debate and decision making on public issues. Clearly, the impugned provisions are designed to further free speech and expression and not to curtail it.”
  • On content-neutrality: “None of these statutory provisions require the exhibitor to show a propaganda film or a film conveying views which he objects to… They, however, contend that one of the important requirements for upholding such compulsory speech in the United States is that such speech should be content-neutral. While in the present case, the contents of the compulsory films are specified in the legislation concerned. In the context of Article 19(1) what we have to examine is whether the categories of films so required to be carried promote dissemination of information and education or whether they are meant to be propaganda or false or biased information.”
  • The 19(2) question left unused: “These provisions, therefore, do not violate Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution: they are not in restraint of free speech and expression. Therefore, Article 19(2) is not attracted.” — the structural point examiners love: a beneficial must-carry rule never reaches the limitations clause because it never abridges the right.
  • Films Division “monopoly”: the contracting-in was factual, not statutory — “This is not because of any statutory compulsion but because of the fact that the Films Division is the only organization which produces such short films on sufficient quantities for regular distribution… The reason why they do so is because of a la[c]k of adequate alternative sources.” The renewal NOC was merely compliance-verification. (The reader prints “tack of adequate alternative sources”. This paragraph is also the 1999 Court’s answer to why the Delhi High Court’s “no rental / free delivery” direction could not stand: economics were contractual and industry-negotiated, not constitutional.)
  • Condition 15 & 22: Condition 15 must be read with s.12(4) — the Administrator may require only films/lantern slides of the s.12(4) categories — “When it is so read Condition 15 will have a direct nexus with the object sought to be achieved, and it can be upheld as a reasonable restriction”; the two conditions “do not overlap, but refer to different sizes and types of short films, shorter films or lantern slides”, so the High Court’s redundancy holding fell.
  • Order: “In the premises, the appeals are allowed and the impugned judgment of the High Court in so far as it strikes down the rental and directs the Films Division to deliver the films to the exhibitors is set aside. The writ petition is dismissed. There will, however, no order as to costs.”
PRINCIPLES EVOLVED
  1. Compelled carriage is judged by function, not by label: does it “further informed decision-making” or force propaganda/partisanship? — India’s must-carry test, made a decade before the Cable TV s.19 and Doordarshan relay fights reached the same clause (cross-link: Unit 4 must-carry / CAB; Unit 5 E2 on the 2026 draft Rules r.31(2) must-retransmit and r.44 Akashvani-bulletin rule, external).
  2. Social context is an interpretive source — literacy, access and the entertainment channel’s reach legitimise content-specified carriage duties where a US court would demand neutrality.
  3. Beneficial compulsion is outside 19(2) altogether — no restraint, no exception needed (contrast Odisha? no — contrast the Odyssey injunction, which was restraint). This is why “must-carry ≠ censorship” survives in answers: censorship subtracts content; a good must-carry rule adds it — subject to the propaganda line.
  4. Licence conditions are read into their parent section (cond. 15 + s.12(4)); ultra vires-by-silence (the High Court’s “too wide” reasoning) is cured by reading-in rather than struck down — the proportionality-by-construction approach.
  5. The cinema licence was India’s oldest “platform regulation”: pre-broadcast, pre-internet, the State already tested the proposition that whoever runs a hall opens a forum carrying public duties — the template later used for cable and now for intermediaries (see Part I2; and the Note: Parts III licences remain in force for UTs; states copied — WB/UP Acts quoted above).

Real-example seam: the Films Division newsreel before every feature (the “theatre newsreel” that Indians over 40 remember as the compulsory 7-minute opener) is the physical ancestor of the YouTube ad-skipping debates: the question — may the platform gatekeeper be made to carry what the audience would skip? — has been open since 1957. Also note the 2023 repeal of the statutory Films Division screening requirement (external: 2023’s Cinematograph (Amendment) Bill era debates on mandatory newsreels — flag as external colour if used at all; safer formulation: “the requirement’s practical death came with the end of the theatrical newsreel circuit”).

DECISION — “real and imminent danger”, adopted and applied
“It is necessary to reiterate that the continuance of this injunction would amount to interference with the freedom of the Press in the form of preventive injunction and it must, therefore, be based on reasonable grounds for the sole purpose of keeping the administration of justice unimpaired. In the words of Mr. Justice Brandeis … concurring in Whitney v. California …, there must be reasonable ground to believe that the danger apprehended is real and imminent. This test we accept on the basis of balance of convenience. This Court has not yet found or laid down any formula or test to determine how the balance of convenience in a situation of this type, or how the real and imminent danger should be judged in case of prevention by injunction of publication of an article in a pending matter.”Main opinion, PDF p. 301 (printed 258) — followed by the right-to-know passage and the vacating: “In the peculiar facts of this case now that the subscription to debentures has closed and, indeed, the debentures have been over-subscribed, we are inclined to think that there is no such imminent [danger] … and that the views expressed by Lord Denning, M.R. in Attorney-General v. BBC — though reversed by the House of Lords … and by the American Courts in Bridges … and Pennekamp … should be preferred as more appropriate to present day conditions.”
  • The right-to-know sentence (memorise): “We must remember that the people at large have a right to know in order to be able to take part in a participatory development in the industrial life and democracy. Right to Know is a basic right which citizens of a free country aspire in the broader horizon of the right to live in this age in our land under Article 21 of our Constitution. That right has reached new dimensions and urgency. That right puts greater responsibility upon those who take upon the responsibility to inform.”
  • The “own risk” clause: “At the moment we have to assess whether any article … will so clearly and obviously prejudice or tend to prejudice the course of the proceedings, now pending in this Court, that such publication should be injuncted by, what the respondents describe as, a ‘gagging order’. … It is sufficient … to clarify … that should any newspaper publish any such matter, it will be doing so at its own risk and subject to its liability for being proceeded against by the petitioner or others for defamation, contempt of court or otherwise.”
  • Separate opinion (the second Judge on the Bench) — worth more than a footnote: it rejected the claim that the paper was attacking the Court’s own prima facie findings — “I do not think it would be correct to say that, when we passed the order dated 19.8.1988, we formed any prima facie opinion on the question whether the debenture issue had been validly approved… what predominantly influenced us … was that, even assuming … that there could be some doubt regarding the validity …, the restraint by any court or tribunal on the issue of debentures at a late stage might prove catastrophic, and cause irreparable loss … the balance of convenience required that there should be no order of any court or tribunal staying the debenture issue.” Yet as to the 25.8 order: the article “though not violative of the terms of the injunction granted by this Court, could have the effect of circumventing the order of this Court and rendering it ineffective. It had, prima facie, a tendency to affect the efficacy of, and defeat the object with which this Court had passed the interim order dated 19.8.88. This is the reason why we passed the second order … In my opinion the said order was rightly passed and that the contention … that no such injunction ought to have been granted at all is not acceptable.” But “the position today … has radically changed” — and on the refund-panic argument: “the danger apprehended … is not so real or substantial as to warrant the continuance … There is no possibility that, pending determination of the issues raised, any court will order interim relief to such applicants by way of grant of such refunds. … I therefore agree that there is no justification for the continuance of the interim order dated 25.8.88 any longer.”
PRINCIPLES EVOLVED
  1. Prior restraint of the press is not categorically forbidden nor casually allowed — it requires (a) a sole aim of keeping “the administration of justice unimpaired”, and (b) “reasonable ground to believe that the danger apprehended is real and imminent”, operationalised as a balance of convenience. (Sahara ¶30 later re-states exactly this as the Reliance test: “The Court went by the doctrine propounded by Holmes J., of clear and present danger. This Court treated the said doctrine as the basis of balance of convenience test. Later on, the injunction was lifted after subscription to debentures had closed.”)
  2. Commercial protection is not a gag ground: “the danger” meant danger to the judicial process — investor panic about allotments was not prejudice to the Transfer Petitions; the market effect of honest journalism stays outside the gag calculus.
  3. Protection of the court’s own orders extends to circumvention-by-publication — an article can be restrained while it functionally “render[s] ineffective” a live interim order even without violating its terms (separate opinion; absorbed into Sahara ¶33’s reading of Arts. 129/215 + “administration of justice is not perverted”).
  4. Lifting ≠ licence: publication resumed “at its own risk” — defamation/contempt exposure survives every vacatur; a gag order is about timing, never about innocence of the content.
  5. “Trial by newspaper” is the named enemy — Baig’s phrase, the P.C. Sen standard (“tends to interfere”, “calculated to”), and the refusal to hold any actual contempt on this application all map the space the Contempt Act occupies: s.2(c) punishes after; s.15 gatekeeps before (AG consent absent here!); and nothing in the Act authorises an ex ante publication ban except the courts’ inherent jurisdiction.
  6. Right to know under Art. 21 — the 1988 seed of Sahara’s “rights of equal weight” jurisprudence and of SP Gupta/PT I.C. (which the excerpt does not cite — do not credit Reliance with them).

Real-example seam: the ‘Mega Issue’ was the largest retail subscription India had then seen — the Express’s critical coverage became the textbook illustration of “editorialising while a matter is sub judice”. (The reader records only the controversy over the articles; do not assert outcome details beyond the excerpt.) The vocabulary lesson for students: Denning’s “gagging injunctions”, rejected in 1981 England, was adopted as a term of criticism and as a doctrinal checkpoint in India by 1988 — and by 2012 Sahara gave its polite twin, the “postponement order”.

G · Case 32 — Sahara India Real Estate Corp. Ltd. v. SEBI, (2012) 10 SCC 603

Dossier · the Constitution Bench’s theory of the gag order · G1 — how a leaked settlement letter produced a doctrine
Court / Bench
Supreme Court, five-Judge Bench — S.H. Kapadia, CJI (for himself and D.K. Jain, S.S. Nijjar, Ranjana P. Desai and J.S. Khehar, JJ.).
Reader text
PDF pp. 305–322 (printed 262–279), ¶¶1–45 as printed; the reader ends the excerpt after ¶45 with “******” — subsequent orders are outside the case book (see honesty note at G5).
Dispute in one line
A “without prejudice” security proposal, sent counsel-to-counsel for three weeks of negotiation, was flashed on a news channel the day before the hearing — and the Supreme Court used its own contempt-formulated grievance to declare, for the first time at Bench-of-Five level, when courts may postpone publication, why such orders do not offend Art. 19(1)(a), and what safeguards must accompany them.
FACTS (¶¶1–16, all as printed)
  • The underlying litigation. Civil Appeals Nos. 9813 & 9833 of 2011 challenged the Securities Appellate Tribunal order dated 18.10.2011 directing “the appellants (hereinafter for short Sahara)” to refund the amounts invested in Optionally Fully Convertible Debentures (OFCDs) with interest by a stated date. On 28.11.2011 the Court issued show-cause to SEBI and directed Sahara to put on affidavit “how they intend to secure the liabilities incurred by them to the OFCD holders during the pendency of the Civil Appeals”; Sahara’s affidavit came on 4.1.2012; on 9.1.2012 the appeals were admitted and its interim-relief IA (No. 3) was kept for 20.1.2012.
  • The negotiation and the leak. On 20.1.2012 SEBI’s counsel called the security affidavit insufficient; the Court directed the counsel to “attempt, if possible, to reach a consensus with respect to an acceptable security in the form of an unencumbered asset” and stood the IA over for three weeks. On 7.2.2012, “the learned counsel for Sahara addressed a personal letter to the learned counsel for SEBI at Chennai enclosing the proposal with details of security … as pre-condition for stay of the impugned orders dated 23.06.2011 and 18.10.2011 … together with the Valuation Certificate indicating fair market value of the assets … communicated by e-mail from Delhi to Chennai. Later, on the same day, there was also an official communication enclosing the said proposal by the Advocate-on-Record for Sahara to the Advocate-on-Record for SEBI.” — “A day prior to the hearing of IA No. 3 on 10.02.2012, one of the news channels flashed on TV the details of the said proposal which had been communicated only inter parties and which was obviously not meant for public circulation. The concerned television channel also named the valuer who had done the valuation of the assets proposed to be offered as security.”
  • The Court’s own reaction — quoteable: on 10.2.2012 Shri F.S. Nariman, senior counsel for Sahara, “orally submitted that disclosure to the Media was by SEBI in breach of confidentiality which was denied by the learned counsel for SEBI”; the Court ordered: “We are distressed to note that even without prejudice proposals sent by learned counsel for the appellants to the learned counsel for SEBI has come on one of the TV channels. Such incidents are increasing by the day. Such reporting not only affects the business sentiments but also interferes in the administration of justice. In the above circumstances, we have requested learned counsel on both sides to make written application … so that appropriate orders could be passed by this Court with regard to reporting of matters, which are sub-judice.”
  • The prayers (IA Nos. 4 & 5). That “appropriate guidelines be framed with regard to reporting (in the electronic and print media) of matters which are sub-judice in a court including public disclosure of documents forming part of court proceedings” and “appropriate directions … as to the manner and extent of publicity to be given by the print/electronic media of pleadings/documents filed in a proceeding in court which is pending and not yet adjudicated upon.” Sahara’s case: comments on sub-judice cases are “inappropriate … this principle has been stated in Section 3 of the Contempt of Courts Act”; “disclosure and publication of pleadings and other documents on the record of the case by third parties … can (under the rules of this Court) only take place on an application to the court and pursuant to the directions given by the court (see Order XII, Rules 1, 2 and 3 of Supreme Court Rules, 1966)”; a “thin line has to be drawn” between inter-party matters and matters of public concern — no public-concern question was involved in telecasting a one-sided security proposal; and State of Maharashtra v. Rajendra J. Gandhi, (1997) 8 SCC 386: “A trial by press, electronic media or public agitation is the very antithesis of rule of law.”
  • SEBI’s position (IA No. 10) — denial of the leak (“as a matter of policy SEBI never gives its comments to the media on matters which are under investigation or sub judice”) but agreement that “in view of the incident having happened in court, this Court should give appropriate directions or frame such guidelines as may be deemed appropriate.” The Court’s framing (¶15): two common-law rights — “every citizen has a right to negotiate in confidence inasmuch as he/she has a right to defend himself or herself. The source of these two rights comes from the common law. They are based on presumptions of confidentiality and innocence. Both, the said presumptions are of equal importance.” It clarified it was not acting suo motu (Sahara was free to withdraw the IAs; neither party did; both “sought adjudication”), recorded Sahara’s 28.3.2012 note of criminal cases where the presumption of innocence had suffered “aberration”, and defined the questions: “whether guidelines for the media be laid down? If so, whether they should be self-regulatory? Or whether this Court should restate the law or declare the law under Article 141 on balancing of Article 19(1)(a) rights vis-à-vis Article 21, the scope of Article 19(2) in the context of the law regulating contempt of court and the scope of Article 129/Article 215.” Decision “confined to IA Nos. 4, 5 and 10” — no opinion on the accused-persons’ IAs seeking protection from “excessive media publicity” (¶16).

G3 · “Indian Approach to prior restraint” — the case-law review (¶¶25–34)

The premise (¶25) — memorise, it is the essay’s thesis sentence
“At the outset, it may be stated that the Supreme Court is not only the sentinel of the fundamental rights but also a balancing wheel between the rights, subject to social control. Freedom of expression is one of the most cherished values of a free democratic society… such a consent implies not only that the consent shall be free but also that it shall be grounded on adequate information, discussion and aided by the widest possible dissemination of information and opinions from diverse and antagonistic sources. Freedom of expression which includes freedom of the press has a capacious content and is not restricted to expression of thoughts and ideas which are accepted and acceptable but also to those which offend or shock any section of the population. It also includes the right to receive information and ideas of all kinds from different sources. In essence, the freedom of expression embodies the right to know. However, under our Constitution no right in Part III is absolute… no single value, no matter exalted, can bear the full burden of upholding a democratic system of government… Consequently, free speech, in appropriate cases, has got to correlate with fair trial. It also follows that in appropriate case one right [say freedom of expression] may have to yield to the other right like right to a fair trial.”Sahara ¶25 — PDF p. 311 (printed 268). The Maneka sentence follows: “even Articles 14 and 21 are subject to the test of reasonableness.”
The decisions on prior restraint (¶¶26–30, as printed)
  • Brij Bhushan (AIR 1950 SC 129): “This Court declared the statutory provision as unconstitutional inasmuch as the restrictions imposed by it were outside Article 19(2), as it then stood. However, this Court did not say that pre-censorship per se is unconstitutional.”
  • Virendra v. State of Punjab (AIR 1957 SC 896): “this Court upheld pre-censorship imposed for a limited period and right of representation to the government against such restraint under Punjab Special Powers (Press) Act, 1956. However, in the same judgment, another provision imposing pre-censorship but without providing for any time limit or right to represent against pre-censorship was struck down as unconstitutional.”
  • K.A. Abbas (AIR 1971 SC 481): prior restraint on motion pictures upheld “subject to Government setting up a corrective machinery and an independent Tribunal and reasonable time limit within which the decision had to be taken by the censoring authorities.”

Minocher Rustom Masani, 78 Bom LR 125 and C. Vaidya v. D’Penha, Gujarat High Court, Sp. Later on, the injunction was lifted after subscription to debentures had closed.”

It may be stated that open justice is the cornerstone of our judicial system. It instills faith in the judicial and legal system. However, the right to open justice is not absolute. It can be restricted by the court in its inherent jurisdiction as done in Mirajkar’s case if the necessities of administration of justice so demand [Kehar Singh v. Where the power comes from (¶33) — the constitutional plumbing
  • “Courts of Record under Article 129/Article 215 have inherent powers to prohibit publication of court proceedings or the evidence of the witness… all Courts which have inherent powers, i.e., the Supreme Court, the High Courts and Civil Courts can issue prior restraint orders… in exceptional circumstances temporarily prohibiting publications of Court proceedings to be made in the media and such powers do not violate Article 19(1)(a).”
  • “one of the Heads on which Article 19(1)(a) rights can be restricted is in relation to contempt of court under Article 19(2). Article 19(2) preserves common law of contempt as an ‘existing law’. In fact, the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 embodies the common law of contempt.… The reason being that contempt is an offence sui generis… it is an important adjunct to the criminal process and provides a sanction. Other civil courts have the power under Section 151 of Code of Civil Procedure to pass orders prohibiting publication of court proceedings.”
  • The two-part construction of Arts. 129/215 — first part “shall be a Court of Record and shall have all the powers of such a court”; second “includes the power to punish for contempt” — “These Articles save the pre-existing powers of the Courts as courts of record” [Delhi Judicial Service Assn. v. State of Gujarat, (1991) 4 SCC 406; SCBA v. UOI, (1998) 4 SCC 409]; Parliament’s power over contempt reaches only “investigation or punishment” (Art. 142(2)/Art. 19(2) read-through) — “the said powers cannot be taken away by any law made by the Parliament except to the limited extent mentioned in Article 142(2) [printed; the reference runs through the Contempt Act ss.14–15 as well — see H1].”
  • “To see that the administration of justice is not prejudiced or perverted clearly includes power of the Supreme Court/High Court to prohibit temporarily, statements being made in the media which would prejudice or obstruct or interfere with the administration of justice in a given case pending in the Supreme Court or the High Court or even in the subordinate courts”; by A.K. Gopalan v. Noordeen [(1969) 2 SCC 734] such statements “would include statements in the media which would prejudice the right to a fair trial of a suspect or accused under Article 21 from the time when the criminal proceedings in a subordinate court are imminent or where suspect is arrested.”
  • “The words due course of justice in Section 2(c) or Section 13 of the 1971 Act are wide enough and are not limited to a particular judicial proceeding. That, the meaning of the words contempt of court in Article 129 and Article 215 is wider than the definition of criminal contempt in Section 2(c) of the 1971 Act” [Ram Autar Shukla v. Arvind Shukla, 1995 Supp (2) SCC 130] — and the caveat: “the contempt of court is a special jurisdiction to be exercised sparingly and with caution whenever an act adversely affects the administration of justice [see Nigel Lowe and Brenda Sufrin, Law of Contempt (Third Edition)]. Trial by newspaper comes in the category of acts which interferes with the course of justice or due administration of justice [Lowe & Sufrin, p. 5 of Fourth Edition].”
  • “Presumption of innocence is held to be a human right [See: Ranjitsing Brahmajeetsing Sharma v. State of Maharashtra (2005) 5 SCC 294]. If in a given case the appropriate Court finds infringement of such presumption by excessive prejudicial publicity by the newspapers (in general), then under inherent powers, the Courts of Record suo motu or on being approached or on report being filed before it by subordinate court can… pass orders of postponement of publication for a limited period if the applicant is able to demonstrate substantial risk of prejudice to the pending trial and provided he is able to displace the presumption of open Justice and to that extent the burden will be on the applicant.”
  • ¶34 summation: “in most jurisdictions there is power in the courts to postpone reporting of judicial proceedings in the interest of administration of justice. Under Article 19(2) of the Constitution, law in relation to contempt of court, is a reasonable restriction. It also satisfies the test laid down in the judgment of this Court in R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. [(1994) 6 SCC 632)]… Such an order of postponement has to be passed only when other alternative measures such as change of venue or postponement of trial are not available… courts have to keep in mind the principle of proportionality and the test of necessity… There is no general law for courts to postpone publicity, either prior to adjudication or during adjudication as it would depend on facts of each case. The necessity for any such order would depend on extent of prejudice, the effect on individuals involved in the case, the over-riding necessity to curb the right to report judicial proceedings conferred on the media under Article 19(1)(a) and the right of the media to challenge the order of postponement.”

G4 · The Contempt Act, and what a valid postponement order must be (¶¶35–43)

Statutory reading (¶35) — why ss. 4 and 7 are the unit’s “Ref” pair

“In the context of contempt on account of publications which are not fair and accurate publication of court proceedings, the relevant provisions are contained in Sections 4 and 7 whereas Section 13 is a general provision which deals with defences. It will be noticed that Section 4 deals with report of a judicial proceeding. A person is not to be treated as guilty of contempt if he has published such a report which is fair and accurate. Section 4 is subject to the provisions contained in section 7 which, however, deals with publication of information relating to proceedings in chambers. Here the emphasis is on information whereas in Section 4, emphasis is on report of a judicial proceeding. This distinction between a report of proceedings and information is necessary because Section 7 deals with proceedings in camera where there is no access to the media.… The inaccuracy of reporting of court proceedings will be contempt only if it can be said on the facts of a particular case, to amount to substantial interference with the administration of justice. The reason behind Section 4 is to grant a privilege in favour of the person who makes the publication provided it is fair and accurate. This is based on the presumption of open justice in courts. Open justice permits fair and accurate reports of court proceedings to be published. The media has a right to know what is happening in courts and to disseminate the information to the public which enhances the public confidence in the transparency of court proceedings. As stated above, sometimes, fair and accurate reporting of the trial (say a murder trial) would nonetheless give rise to substantial risk of prejudice not in the pending trial but in the later or connected trials. In such cases, there is no other practical means short of postponement orders… Thus postponement order not only safeguards fairness of the later or connected trials, it prevents possible contempt by the Media.”

¶¶36–42 — the parameters (this list is the answer to “what must a postponement order look like?”)
  • The test: “the publication (actual and not planned publication) must create a real and substantial risk of prejudice to the proper administration of justice or to the fairness of trial.” (¶42) — planned publications are not enjoined “across the board”; the order “operate[s] on actual publication”.
  • Last-resort rule: “Such an order of postponement has to be passed only when other alternative measures such as change of venue or postponement of trial will not prevent the said risk” — i.e., neutralising devices first (the American lesson, ¶¶17, 34).
  • Proportionality: “when the salutary effects of such orders outweigh the deleterious effects to the free expression of those affected by the prior restraint. The order of postponement will only be appropriate in cases where the balancing test otherwise favours non-publication for a limited period.”
  • Content neutrality: “such orders of postponement should be ordered for a limited duration and without disturbing the content of the publication.” No rewriting of the story — only a pause in its telling.
  • Burden on the applicant: “The applicant who seeks order of postponement of publicity must displace the presumption of Open Justice.”
  • Who, how, and challenge: only “the High Court/Supreme Court (being Courts of Record)… under their inherent jurisdictions”; “[t]he very object… is to see that the administration of justice is not perverted, prejudiced, obstructed or interfered with”; and at ¶40 the full architecture — “keeping in mind the timing (the stage at which it should be ordered), its duration and the right of appeal to challenge such orders is just a neutralizing device… a preventive measure to protect the press from getting prosecuted for contempt and also to prevent administration of justice from getting perverted or prejudiced.”
  • No catalogue: “It is not possible for this Court to enumerate categories of publications amounting to contempt… There cannot be any straightjacket formula enumerating such categories… [it would] require the courts in each case to see the content and the context of the offending publication” (¶42); “guidelines on reporting cannot be framed across the Board” (¶45).
  • Media as the public’s witness: “The shadow of the law of contempt hangs over our jurisprudence. The media, in several cases in India, is the only representative of the public to bring to the notice of the court issues of public importance including governance deficit, corruption, drawbacks in the system. Keeping in mind the important role of the media, Courts have evolved several neutralizing techniques including postponement orders subject to the twin tests of necessity and proportionality… Such orders would also put the Media to notice about possible contempt. However, it would be open to Media to challenge such orders in appropriate proceedings.… the postponement order is not a punitive measure, but a preventive measure.”
  • The Art. 21 twin: “Excessive prejudicial publicity leading to usurpation of functions of the Court not only interferes with administration of justice which is sought to be protected under Article 19(2), it also prejudices or interferes with a particular legal proceedings. In such case, Courts are duty bound under inherent jurisdiction… to protect the presumption of innocence which is now recognised by this Court as a human right under Article 21.” And the final limiter: “postponement orders must be integrally connected to the outcome of the proceedings including guilt or innocence of the accused, which would depend on the facts of each case. For aforesaid reasons, we hold that subject to above parameters, postponement orders fall under Article 19(2) and they satisfy the test of reasonableness.”

G5 · Maintainability, judicial law-making — decision, aftermath, principles

¶¶44–45 — “can the Court even do this?” (the separation-of-powers answer)

The three objections (¶44): no lis; law-making is for Parliament so any guidelines must be self-regulatory or advisory; under Art. 142 the Court cannot confer jurisdiction it does not possess. The Court’s reply (¶45): “Article 141 uses the phrase law declared by the Supreme Court. It means law made while interpreting the statutes or the Constitution. Such judicial law-making is part of the judicial process. Further under Article 141, law-making through interpretation and expansion of the meanings of open-textured expressions such as law in relation to contempt of court in Article 19(2), equal protection of law, freedom of speech and expression and administration of justice is a legitimate judicial function.” With Dworkin again: “Arguments of principle are arguments intended to establish an individual right. Principles are propositions that describe rights.” — “In this case, this Court is only declaring under Article 141, the constitutional limitations on free speech under Article 19(1)(a), in the context of Article 21… The exercise undertaken by this Court is an exercise of exposition of constitutional limitations under Article 141 read with Article 129/Article 215… What constitutes an offending publication would depend on the decision of the court on a case-to-case basis. Hence guidelines on reporting cannot be framed across the Board. The shadow of law of contempt hangs over our jurisprudence. This Court is duty bound to clear that shadow under Article 141. The phrase in relation to contempt of court under Article 19(2) does not in the least describe the true nature of the offence which consists in interfering with administration of justice; in impeding and perverting the course of justice. That is all which is done by this judgment.” And on the lis: “the right to negotiate and settle in confidence is a right of a citizen and has been equated to a right of the accused to defend himself in a criminal trial… it cannot be contended that there was no lis.”

  • After the excerpt External; reported: the matter was referred to larger benches; in the reported follow-up order (13.9.2013, Sahara India (II)) the Court — declining to frame media-guidelines — restated that each case must be decided on its own facts with reference to these parameters, and dealt with SEBI’s sealed-cover/RTI questions (the “sealed cover” practice criticism often traced to this case belongs to that phase; not in the reader). PRINCIPLES EVOLVED (the Sahara decalogue — everything here is at ¶¶25–45 as printed)
  1. Competence: the High Courts (Art. 215) and the Supreme Court (Art. 129) — as Courts of Record — possess inherent power, not exhaustible by the Contempt Act, to pass postponement-of-publication orders; civil courts derive the cognate power from s.151 CPC (via Mirajkar); trial courts do not enjoy the “postponement” power of the superior courts (¶42).
  2. Constitutionality: such orders, within the parameters, fall within Art. 19(2) (“in relation to contempt of court” — words of “widest amplitude”) and satisfy reasonableness under Arts. 14/19/21 — they are not “restrictions” requiring separate justification in the US “clash model” sense; the Court expressly rejected importing the clash model (¶42).
  3. The parameters (the examiner will count them): actual publication; real and substantial risk of prejudice to fairness of trial or administration of justice; exhaustion of alternatives (venue/postponement of trial); proportionality (salutary > deleterious); limited duration; no disturbance of content; burden on the applicant to displace open justice; only on Courts of Record; media’s right to challenge; integral connection to the outcome incl. guilt/innocence; no fixed categories.
  4. Two presumptions of equal weight: confidentiality of negotiation ≈ accused’s right to defend (the Harman equalization), and presumption of innocence as a human right (Ranjitsing) — balanced against the presumption of open justice; neither presumption can be erased, only displaced (¶¶15, 33–34).
  5. Preventive, not punitive: the postponement order “protect[s] the media from getting prosecuted for contempt” — the gag order is reconceived as a service to the press, its nearest relative the s.6(2)(c) two-month suspension (time-bounded State touches, always with a clock).
  6. Open justice is the rule; its curtailment is the exception the court must justify — “the right to open justice is not absolute” (¶31) — and fair-and-accurate reporting enjoys s.4’s privilege unless s.7 or a court’s express prohibition bites.
  7. Law-declaring vs law-framing: the Court may restate the law (Art. 141) but will not legislate a media code “across the Board” — the exact boundary that answers the perennial “should the Supreme Court frame guidelines for trial reporting?” essay question (position post-Sahara: parameters yes, catalogue no; the 200th Report’s Bill remains unenacted — Part H2).
  8. Contempt jurisdiction is to be exercised “sparingly and with caution” (¶33) — a self-imposed meta-principle binding all future gag-order litigation.

H2 · The Law Commission, 200th Report (Aug 2006) — the statute the gag order never got

Uploaded primary document · “Trial by Media — Free Speech vs. Fair Trial under Criminal Procedure (Amendments to the Contempt of Court Act, 1971)” · Chairman Justice M. Jagannadha Rao · taken up suo motu

Why it exists (cover letter, verbatim): “The subject was taken up suo motu having regard to the extensive prejudicial coverage of crime and information about suspects and accused, both in the print and electronic media. There is today a feeling that in view of the extensive use of the television and cable services, the whole pattern of publication of news has changed and several such publications are likely to have prejudicial impact on the suspects, accused, witnesses and even Judges and in general, on the administration of justice. According to our law, a suspect/accused is entitled to a fair procedure and is presumed to be innocent till proved guilty… None can be allowed to prejudge or prejudice his case by the time it goes to trial.” And the constitutional gap it set out to close: “Art. 19(2) does not refer to ‘administration of justice’ but interference… is clearly referred to in the definition of ‘criminal contempt’ in sec. 2 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 and in sec. 3… Therefore, publications which interfere or tend to interfere with the administration of justice amount to criminal contempt under that Act and if in order to prevent such interference, postponement orders are required, the power must be statutory.”

The four engineering decisions (Ch. “postponement” + ch. 3–6 + ch. IX–X)
  1. Move the trigger from “pending” to “active” (arrest). “The above amendment as to ‘arrest’ as being starting point is proposed by using the word ‘active’ criminal proceeding in sec. 3, rather than ‘pending criminal proceeding’ and inserting the word ‘arrest’ in the Explanation below sec. 3.” On “imminent”: “‘Imminent’ here means the ‘reasonable likelihood’ of the filing of charge sheet whether immediately or in a reasonable time. If the word ‘imminent’ should mean ‘immediate’, then in all cases where there are delays in investigation such as when investigation is entrusted to the CBI or the ACB… there could be a free licence to issue prejudicial publications. That cannot be the law.” Plus the constitutional hinge: “‘arrest’ is important in another sense because of the 24 hour rule… a person arrested has to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours… ‘imminence’… [means] that he is ‘most likely’ to be charge sheeted.” And the balance clause: “we are not saying that once arrest is made, the media is obliged to make no publications at all. What the law requires is that they should not, while making publications, prejudice the case of the suspect by referring to his character, prior convictions, confessions, photographs (where identity is in question) or describe him as guilty or innocent… Further, sec. 3 grants immunity to publications made without knowledge of the pendency.”
  2. What counts as prejudicial — the list the report endorses (adopting the NSW Law Reform Commission’s catalogue, report pp. 146–147 & 219): “(i) a photograph of the accused where identity is likely to be an issue, as in criminal cases; (ii) suggestions that accused had previous convictions, or has been charged for committing an offence and/or previously acquitted, or has been involved in other criminal activity; (iii) suggestions that the accused has confessed to committing the crime in question; (iv) suggestions that the accused is guilty or involved in the crime for which he or she is charged or that the Jury should convict or acquit the accused; and (v) comments which engender sympathy or antipathy for the accused and/or which disparage the prosecution or which make favourable or unfavourable references to the character or credibility of the accused or a witness.” The report’s own pedagogy line: “This Chapter is, in fact, intended to educate the media and the public that what is going on at present in the media may indeed be highly objectionable. Merely because it is tolerated by the Courts, it may not cease to be contempt.”
  3. Direct access to the High Court. The reference mechanism (subordinate court → HC under s.15(2)) is “cumbersome and time consuming”; “We have, therefore, proposed sec. 10A… the High Court could be approached directly without consent of the Advocate General” for publication-contempt of subordinate courts. Why the High Court and not the trial court: “such powers cannot be vested in the subordinate courts… Under Section 15(2), they can only make a ‘reference’ to the High Court. Further, the balancing of the rights of freedom of speech and the due process right of the suspect/accused as explained in Maneka Gandhi’s case can be done more appropriately by the High Court which is a Constitutional Court.” — and “the High Court for the purpose of passing postponement orders will be a Bench of not less than two Judges.”
  4. Stricter threshold than ordinary contempt — “real risk of serious prejudice”. The report refuses to borrow s.4(2) UK’s “substantial risk of prejudice” because English courts read it as “no substantial risk” or “no remote risk”, and “in view of the interpretation… we are of the view that instead of the words ‘substantial risk of prejudice’ in Section 4(2) of the U.K Act, in the proposed section giving powers… to pass ‘postponement’ orders, we should use the words ‘real risk of serious prejudice’ so that the emphasis is not only on the word ‘risk’ but also on the word ‘prejudice’”. The reason prior restraint deserves a special standard (report p. 229, quotable): “the words used in sec. 2(c) and 3 define what publication may be ‘criminal contempt’ but the question of restricting freedom of speech by passing an order of ‘prior restraint’… requires more stringent conditions… Prior restraint as [p]ointed [out] by the Supreme Court in Reliance Petrochemicals is a serious encroachment on the right of the press for publication under Art. 19(1)(a) and cannot be interfered with merely because the publication interferes or tends to interfere with the course of justice as stated in sec. 2(c) or sec. 3.”
The Draft Bill — ss. 14A and 14B (verbatim, as annexed)

Proposed s.14A(1) — the order

“In any criminal proceedings which are active, the High Court may issue a general direction ex parte, whenever it appears to it to be necessary, that any matter the publication of which may cause real risk of serious prejudice to the cause of such proceeding or to the administration of justice in those proceedings or in any other criminal proceedings or any part of such proceeding, active or imminent, shall not be published by the media or any person for an initial period of seven days from the date of its order or that such matter shall be published subject to conditions and the High Court may direct publication of such an order.”

14A(2) — who may apply: (a) the Advocate General/law officer; (b) “a person who has been arrested or who being an accused… a chargesheet or challan has been filed or summons or warrant has been issued”; (c) “any person who is interested in the avoidance of the risk and prejudice”. 14A(3) — media may seek variation/cancellation within the seven days. 14A(4) — absent such application, the order stands extended beyond seven days (answering the “lapse” danger flagged at p. 230: “danger of the postponement order lapsing at the end of one week”). Explanation — “the High Court shall mean a Bench of the High Court of not less than two Judges”. Also proposed: the order “must be published in the Media, so that the media will have an opportunity to know… and have the order varied/cancelled”.

Proposed s.14B — breach

“Where any person violates any order of postponement of publication or the conditions laid down in orders passed under section 14A, the High Court may initiate proceedings for criminal contempt… and pass such order as to punishment as it may deem fit.”

And the ancillaries: s.3 Explanation amendment (“arrest”); s.10A (direct HC access, no AG consent); the Bill “shall apply to publications… in respect of active criminal proceedings… made after the commencement”. Two further recommendations of the human register: (6)–(7) — applicability note; and “Media persons to be trained in certain aspects of law… It is necessary that the syllabus in Journalism should cover the various aspects of law referred to above. It is also necessary to have Diploma and Degree Course in Journalism and the Law. We recommend accordingly.” — worth a line in any answer; the Commission thought law-media literacy would do what injunctions cannot.

Status — say it correctly

The Contempt of Courts Act was never amended along these lines (no ss. 14A–14B exist today); the “postponement” power therefore rests entirely on the judge-made doctrine — Reliance → Mirajkar (inherent) → Sahara’s parameters, supplemented by statute-specific bars (327(3) CrPC/366 BNSS; matrimonial acts; POCSO/JJ; OSA s.14). The 200th Report is thus “the standard blueprint”: cite it as recommendations, never as law; and note that Sahara (2012) arrived six years later and legislated judicially what the Commission proposed to legislate statutorily — the two documents should be answered side by side.

Who

DECISION — the court’s own statement of the doctrine (¶¶32–60) and the order (¶¶61–66)
  • Start from restraint: “The Courts have time and again emphasized that the media and press should not be unnecessarily restricted… The Courts should thus refrain from making any prior restraints on the publications in order to curtail such freedom.” (¶33) — with Romesh Thapar/Brij Bhushan/Express Newspapers (¶34) and the 1986 Express Newspapers caution that uncontrolled speech “amounts to uncontrolled licence which would lead to disorder and anarchy” (¶35).
  • Then the Reliance rule: “the position of law as to no prior restraint… has been revisited… in Reliance Petrochemicals Ltd v. Proprietors of Indian Express… wherein Sabyasachi Mukherjee, J.… observed that the Court can pass interim orders restraining the publication if the Court finds that there exists a real and imminent danger that the continuance of the publication would result in interference with the administration of justice” (¶37); and the court on facts had vacated (¶38) — “the injunction was vacated for the reason that the said issue was oversubscribed and the date of allotment had arrived”.

I · Gag orders — broadcasting & social media

I1 · Broadcasting: a licensed medium’s gag — content codes, telecast injunctions, and “must-carry” as the mirror device

The doctrinal starting point is Odyssey (Part B): even in a State-run medium, a temporary injunction stopping a scheduled telecast is reviewable as prior restraint — the High Court there was reversed precisely because the petitioners had “no prima facie evidence of grave prejudice”. The regulatory difference for broadcasting is that content lines are statutory before transmission rather than after:

  • Cable (uploaded Act + Notes): the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 s.5 prohibits transmission by a cable operator “except in accordance with the programmes code or the advertising code”; Rule 6 (Programmes Code) bars, inter alia, “criticism of friendly relations with other countries”, obscenity, “violation of the sovereignty and integrity”, “incitement… to violence”, “denigration of any community… by vilification… on grounds of religion, race, language, region, caste or community”, plus the child-protection heads under its sub-head “Protection of identity of Children/juveniles” (Notes pp. 34–36 of the uploaded section; r.6(xiii)(?)). Rule 7 (Advertising Code) makes the DOPT VA/CP rules applicable to Doordarshan and prescribes, for private channels, that no advertisement “shall… project male/female superiority… shall not portray children… in a most undesirable manner…”; the Notes file prints both codes verbatim (md 1436–1520) — the machinery Unit 5 Part B worked through. No pre-certification of TV programmes exists: enforcement is ex post (s.7 of the Act → suspension of cable TV licence by the Authorised Officer under rr. 16/21) — but “the State is not disabled from… [acting] in advance”: the self-regulation (BSI/NBSA codes, cross-ref U4 E5) sits between the two.
  • Satellite & DD: the one broadcasting content case the syllabus routes through this unit’s chapter is the certification-adjacent Director General, DD v. Anand Patwardhan, (2006) 8 SCC 433 — in the reader only as the obscenity-test citation inside Shreya ¶45 (see K1): the Court there refused DD’s reliance on “community standards” for non-telecast of the film, because DD’s own refusal was reviewable for arbitrariness; and in Ajay Goswami (PDF pp. 221–240 — the reader’s next chapter), the norms-of-journalistic-conduct/PCI-notice regime for print is the foil. For the broadcast gag itself, the answer bank is: airwaves scarcity (CAB — “every citizen has a right to use the airwaves… subject to the regulatory conditions”, U4 Part C), the Prasar Bharati autonomy promise (U4 E1) and Odyssey’s short stay on injunctions against telecast.
  • The must-carry mirror: UIMPAA (Part E) is the State adding content to a licensed medium; the DD cable s.19 “mandatory relay” line and the 2026 draft Rules (r.31(2) must-retransmit; r.44(1)(c)–(d) limiting community-radio carriage to Akashvani bulletins — flagged, draft) extend the same question to broadcasting today. The unifying principle from all three: compulsion is scrutinised by its effect on the audience’s access to ideas, not by the medium’s licence history.

The syllabus prints beside Shreya Singhal (case 30) the parenthetical “whether it would be asserted again — need for such power”. Read in this unit’s context, the parenthetical is a question to the student: if the First Amendment’s answer to online prior restraint is “heaviest presumption against”, on what repeated assertion of necessity does India’s only online content-censorship power — blocking under s.69A — rest, and has the State been given it back? The file answers in three moves, all with reader support:

(1) What the reader’s own text decides (Shreya Singhal excerpt; the 69A part — already the dossier of Unit 4 Part G1; re-reading it through censorship)
  • 66A died; 69A was saved with a machine. s.66A was struck “not being saved under Article 19(2)” — the Court’s obscenity discussion shows why even a “decency or morality” charge could not rescue it: “Section 66A cannot possibly be said to create an offence which falls within the expression ‘decency’ or ‘morality’ in that what may be grossly offensive or annoying under the Section need not be obscene at all – in fact the word ‘obscene’ is conspicuous by its absence in Section 66A” (¶47, reader PDF p. 41) — and “Section 66A has nothing to do with ‘incitement to an offence’” (¶44).

  • s.69A upheld as “content-neutrality?” No — as procedurally fenced: the block must record reasons, be necessary/expedient under the eight 19(2)-style heads, go to a Cognizance Officer within the 72-hours/statutory-window scheme of the Blocking Rules 2009 (read down: complainants heard; review committee within specified periods; order communicated; grounds in writing) — a certification-style machine transplanted into the IT Act: the only lawful online “prior” restraint is rule-governed executive blocking, judicially reviewable (Shreya ¶¶84–124 as printed; the U4 file carries the full anatomy).
(2) “Asserted again” — the State’s post-2015 practice (external; flagged)
  • Blocking has expanded, not shrunk: thousands of URLs and whole-app bans (the 2020 TikTok-era orders), the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 — age-gating of “permitted pornographic material”, grievance-officer machinery and a three-layer (self-regulation → MIB-guided body → inter-departmental committee) structure for digital news/current affairs — and the February-2022 amendment inserting emergency-takedown duties (content threatening sovereignty/integrity/security to be disabled by intermediaries within two hours of a government order — reported widely; verify the exact rule number before citing), and the MIB’s June-2026 draft consolidation (uploaded — Unit 4 E6) which keeps the Cable 1994 r.6/r.7 codes alive through the draft Telecom Rules (r.16(2) Explanation). Every one of these re-asserts “need for such power”. External; reported
  • The counter-assertions from the case law: no blanket prohibitions (X v UOI: “Google LLC’s objection to orders of prior restraint or blanket ban of content is wholly unnecessary and misplaced” — because the court’s own order was URL-specific; ¶63, reader PDF p. 216); “the legal framework mandates that the internet be kept free from editorial intervention” and the fear of “privatized censorship” (¶¶47, 61, same pages) — the court’s answer being to route removals through specific-URL orders or the statutory blocking machinery rather than open-ended disablement.
(3) The one-paragraph thesis an examiner wants
Cinema was tolerated as a censored medium because Rangarajan could show a structural disability of the audience (the dark hall, the cumulative impact, the non-selective mass audience); the internet reverses the premise — the audience selects, skips, and can be reached around any block — yet the State keeps re-asserting the certification function through blocking-by-rules (s.69A + Blocking Rules 2009 + 2021 Rules age-gating), because the same two fears animate both: protection of children (Part K) and preservation of the political/social order (public-order heads). Shreya’s answer is not that such power is unnecessary — it is that every assertion of the need must survive the procedural fence: recorded reasons, specific grounds, time limits, review, and open-court challenge. That, precisely, is “whether it would be asserted again” — yes, but only inside the architecture the Court itself re-imagined from the Cinematograph Act’s playbook. J1 · The victim’s name: s.228A IPC → s.72 BNS · s.327(3) CrPC → s.366 BNSS

Section 228A IPC (inserted by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983; offence-list widened by the 2013 and 2018 amendments) — as the bare Act reads: “(1) Whoever prints or publishes the name or any matter which may make known the identity of any person against whom an offence under section 376, 376A, 376AB, 376B, 376C, 376D, 376DA, 376DB or 376E is alleged or found to have been committed (hereafter in this section referred to as the victim) shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years and shall also be liable to fine. (2) Nothing in sub-section (1) extends to any printing or publication of the name or any matter which may make known the identity of the victim if such printing or publication is — (a) by or under the order in writing of the officer-in-charge of the police station or the police officer making the investigation into such offence acting in good faith for the purposes of such investigation; or (b) by, or with the authorisation in writing of, the victim; or (c) where the victim is dead or minor or of unsound mind, by, or with the authorisation in writing of, the next-of-kin of the victim: Provided that no such authorisation shall be given by the next-of-kin to anybody other than the chairman or the secretary… of any recognised welfare institution or organization… (3) Whoever prints or publishes any matter in relation to any proceeding before a court with respect to an offence referred to in sub-section (1) without the previous permission of such court shall be punished [up to two years + fine]; (4) The printing or publication of the judgment of any High Court or the Supreme Court does not amount to an offence.” Twin: s.72 BNS 2023 carries the identical scheme with the BNS sexual-offence range (ss.64–71) substituted — “(1) Whoever prints or publishes the name or any matter which may make known the identity of any person against whom an offence under section 64 or section 65 … or section 71 is alleged or found to have been committed … imprisonment… extend to two years and… fine.” External; reported (verified against bare-Act reprints)

  • Who is bound: “prints or publishes” — the provision reaches the media (and, per the Allahabad High Court in Bablu @ Jitendra v. State of U.P., even judicial drafting that names the victim invites criticism, though the Kerala High Court in XXX v. State of Kerala (2023) refused to prosecute a magistrate for “inadvertent omission”, reading s.228A as aimed at publication and shielded by the Judges (Protection) Act, 1985) External; reported.
  • Lifting: only (i) investigating officer’s written order (for investigation), (ii) the victim’s written authorisation, (iii) next-of-kin of dead/minor/unsound-mind victim — and then only to the chair-secretary of a recognised welfare body; (iv) court permission for matter “in relation to any proceeding”; never for unauthorised reporting of the in-camera trial itself (s.327(3) CrPC / s.366 BNSS + s.7(1)(a) Contempt Act chain).
  • The Supreme Court’s gloss — Nipun Saxena v. Union of India (2018) External; reported: from the filing of the FIR until the conclusion of the trial, the victim of a sexual offence must be referred to in media and judicial documents only by a letter (or by initials of her name), and thereafter her name may be used only with her written consent; the Court traced the rule to s.228A + s.327(2)–(3) and Art. 21 dignity, and directed enforcement (including against courts’ own record-keeping — anonymise before uploading orders). The judgment is the best “real example” to pair with s.228A: even the National Legal Services Authority circulars followed it.
  • Rajagopal’s silent partner: proposition (5) of the six Rajagopal propositions (reader PDF p. 73) preserves the Official Secrets Act and, with it, the principle that identity of protected persons (victims, jurors, juveniles) is one field where a prior bar (publication prohibition) is conceded by the free-press case-law itself.

Why s.327 CrPC is the “other half”

The CrPC section is usually taught as an “in-camera trial” rule; for this unit its point is sub-section (3): once a rape trial is in camera, “it shall not be lawful for any person to print or publish any matter in relation to any such proceedings, save with the previous permission of the Court” — a blanket, automatic publication bar attached to closure (the proviso since 2013 allows lifting “subject to maintaining confidentiality of name and address of the parties”). Under the BNSS, 2023, the same architecture is s.366 (open court · in-camera for rape/sexual offences incl. POCSO · publication only with previous permission · woman judge proviso). Twin-check for answers: s.327 CrPC ⇄ s.366 BNSS; s.228A IPC ⇄ s.72 BNS.

FACTS

A Marathi poem referring to Mahatma Gandhi in an obscene idiom was published in 1994 in an in-house journal (“The Bulletin”) of a nationalised bank; a complaint produced criminal proceedings, and charges under s.292 IPC (sale etc. of obscene objects; the wider complaint cluster included obscenity-adjacent heads — defamation, indecent representation of women, incitement) were framed against the poet and against the publisher and printer of the journal. Quashal was declined by the Bombay High Court; the matter reached the Supreme Court two decades later, on special leave (Criminal Appeal 1149 of 2010).

ISSUES

(1) Whether “despite the doctrines of poetic licence and freedom of expression under Article 19(1)(a)”, the use of the name of “a historically respected personality… in an obscene manner” can attract s.292; (2) whether the charges against the publisher and printer should be quashed; (3) whether the “poetic licence” question required reference to a five-Judge Bench (Art. 145(3)) — declined.

ARGUMENTS

The appellant (through learned counsel) pressed that “the poem does not use obscene words and it does not come within the ambit and sweep of Section 292 IPC and the poet has expressed himself as he has a right to express his own thoughts in words”, invoking “poetic freedom” as “a guaranteed and an enforceable fundamental right”, and European authority that artistic freedom outweighs personal interest. The amicus, Mr. F.S. Nariman, “urged with solemn vehemence” that adjudicating the abstract question without the author’s voice would be inappropriate — and, on the merits, that “the poem does not subserv[e] any artistic purpose and is loathsome and vulgar and hence, it comes within the sweep of Section 292”; crucially: “the use of the name of Mahatma Gandhi enhances the conceptual perception of obscenity… the words… if they are spoken in the voice of an ordinary man… may not come under the ambit and sweep of Section 292 IPC, but the moment there is established identity pertaining to Mahatma Gandhi, the character of the words change and they assume the position of obscenity”; that artistic freedom “cannot… outweigh observance of laws for the prevention of crime or… protection of health or morals”, invoking the European Court in Müller v. Switzerland (App. no. 68354/01, 2009) — limits exceeded “when the image of a person (renowned or otherwise) is substantially deformed by wholly imaginary elements”. The Court framed its own question (printed): “whether in a write-up or a poem, keeping in view the concept and conception of poetic licence and the liberty of perception and expression, use of the name of a historically respected personality by way of allusion or symbol [in an obscene manner] is permissible.”

DECISION

The Bench (Dipak Misra & Prafulla C. Pant, JJ.) declined to interfere with the charge against the author — applying the community-standards/overall-impact analysis (Ranjit Udeshi revisited; the Court reproduced the stanzas “with some self-caution” and held the depiction, judged as a whole, crossed the s.292 line when its target is a figure “verifiably revered”); quashed the charge against the publisher and the printer, weighing that the publisher “immediately after coming to know about the reactions of certain employees… tendered unconditional apology in the next issue”, “even before the inception of the proceedings”, and that “almost more than two decades have passed”, while the printer “had printed as desired by the publisher”. PRINCIPLES / why the syllabus puts it in THIS unit
  1. Prior restraint is dead for text; the s.292 prosecution is alive — the case is the unit’s specimen of pure post-publication content liability (no Board, no injunction, two decades of trial exposure): censorship’s shadow without censorship’s machinery.
  2. Poetic licence is protected expression, not obscenity-immunity — art-status doesn’t answer the obscenity question; the effect on the ordinary reasonable person, judged as a whole, does (Rangarajan’s standard, applied to print).
  3. The target matters — Nariman’s “established identity” move, accepted in substance, is variable-obscenity by audience-relations: the same words about a private person, a public idea, and a “historically respected personality” attract different community reactions — the third axis besides adult/minor.
  4. Role-differentiated liability — author vs publisher vs printer: a graded mens-rea/conduct reading useful for the “intermediary liability” analogy (compare Google in X v UOI — reactive, not publisher: Part I2).

Exam one-liner: “Tuljapurkar completes the circuit: if Rangarajan says the State may stop a film before release, Tuljapurkar says it may also, decades later, put a poet on trial after publication — and the Constitution tolerates both because both are channelled through standards — the Board’s Guidelines there, the s.292 test here — rather than through silence.” (Reader-gap flagged above; keep the flagged phrasing in answers: “the case book lists but does not reproduce it” earns more than pretending.)

K2 · The minor’s standard — the statutory ladder and its limits

(i) The principle: “variable obscenity” — content is judged relative to its audience

The phrase (in American letters, Ginsberg v. New York, 390 U.S. 629 (1968): material “harmful to minors” may be barred for children though lawful for adults — External; reported; and its counter-balances: Butler v. Michigan and, for video games, Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Ass’n, 564 U.S. 786 (2011)) is what Indian statutes implement without naming. India’s implementation is entirely audience-architecture: certificates (U/UA/A/S), age-rules (18/12/3/20), identity-shields for minors in process (POCSO/JJ — J1–J2), and the codes’ children-provisions (Cable r.6; U5). The reader supplies the classic warning against over-protection — Goswami (PDF p. 230) quoting Alfred E. Butler v. State of Michigan: “The state insists that, by thus quarantining the general reading public against books not too rugged for grown men and women in order to shield juvenile innocence, it is exercising its power to promote the general welfare. Surely, this is to burn the house to roast the pig.”

(ii) The Indian ladder — every rung with its statutory bolt
  • 18 = the floor of adulthood (Cinematograph Act s.2(a)); “A” restricts exhibition to adults, s.7(1)(a)(ii) penalises showing an A film to a non-adult, and — the asymmetry worth a footnote — s.7(3) deems it no offence to admit a child below 3 years to an A/S/UA film if accompanying parents or guardians (a lap-age tolerance: enforcement assumes guardian consent).
  • 12 = the caution line — s.4(1)(i) proviso endorsement (“whether any child below the age of twelve years may be allowed to see such a film should be considered by the parents or guardian”); Guidelines 5(ii); and the crucial enforcement carve-out: the last proviso to s.7(1) — “no distributor or exhibitor or owner or employee of a cinema house shall be liable to punishment, for contravention of any condition of endorsement of caution on a film certified as ‘UA’” — the UA caution is addressed to parents, not policed on the door (2023’s UA 7+/UA 13+ sub-categories harden the line only on streaming; flagged external, A5).
  • 20 = the corruption line (print) — s.293 IPC (“obscene object to a person under the age of twenty years”, enhanced punishment; ⇄ BNS s.295, verified in this series) and the Young Persons (Harmful Publications) Act, 1956 (violent/cruel “harmful” pamphlets — the comic-book statute; Part J2). Same age, two logics: s.293 protects morals; the 1956 Act protects behaviour.
  • Films & children in the Guidelines — clause (2)(iii)(a): scenes “showing involvement of children in violence as victims or perpetrators or as forced witnesses to violence, or showing children as being subjected to any form of child abuse” must not be “presented needlessly”; the family-viewing rule (Guidelines 5(i)); and Titles scrutiny (Guidelines 6). The minor here is simultaneously audience and on-screen subject — the only Guidelines clause that regulates both at once.
  • Broadcast & digital — Cable r.6’s “children” heads + r.7 advertising-to-children bars (Unit 5 Part B); Doordarchan-era “children’s time”; the 2021 IT Rules’ classification of “permittable pornographic content” with age-verification (r.4(16)? describe functionally: platforms “shall enable access… only to users who have confirmed their age by self-verification and display a classification for Online Curated Content in accordance with rules — U/A 7+, U/A 13+, U/A 16+, A” — External; reported); POCSO ss.14–15 (using a child for pornographic purposes; transmitting/storing child porn) supply the child-as-content-side, and ss.67/67A/67B IT Act the online transmission side (twin notes at M2).
  • The court-side minors’ shields — JJ s.74 and POCSO s.23 (Part J) complete the picture: variable obscenity ends where a named child begins — publication is barred not because the content is obscene but because the audience is everyone.
How to use the ladder in an answer: (1) open with the constitutional frame — Ranjit D. Udeshi (1965) held obscenity outside the protection of Art. 19(1)(a); the modern gloss is Aveek Sarkar’s contemporary community standards (2014); (2) show the adult test evolving Hicklin→Miller/BUTLER-style balance through Patwardhan; (3) then introduce the child as a third variable — Ginsberg in one line [external]; (4) land on the Indian rungs (A/UA/U/S + 3/12/18/20 + POCSO/JJ) with one case per rung (Rangarajan on the caution; Bobby Art on adult trust; UIMPAA on the illiterate-mass audience as reason to carry rather than ban; Tuljapurkar on post-publication liability); (5) close with the Butler “burn the house” caution and the Sahara-style proportionality reflex — courts ask “what is the least speech-restrictive rung that does the job?”: which is precisely why the UA caution is non-penal at the door while A-admission is penal.

L1.2 · Q6 — model answer (20 marks), Unit-6 lens

Write in five heads: (1) the legal position on trial-reporting; (2) Y’s rights inventory; (3) the remedies — with the postponement order as the crown; (4) what will NOT work; (5) the appeal (bail limb) with a memo skeleton. Every authority below is from this unit’s texts — pin-cites in brackets.

(1) Starting position

Media reporting of judicial proceedings is presumptively lawful: Contempt Act s.4 — a “fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding or any stage thereof” is no contempt — and open justice is “the cornerstone” (Sahara ¶31; Mirajkar, nine Judges). But the presumption is calibrated, not absolute: publication that “interferes or tends to interfere with the due course of justice” in a pending proceeding is criminal contempt (s.2(c)(i)); “the question is not so much of the intention of the contemnor as whether it is calculated to interfere” (P.C. Sen, via Reliance excerpt, PDF p. 294); and “A trial by press, electronic media or public agitation is the very antithesis of rule of law” (Rajendra J. Gandhi, quoted in Sahara ¶12). The one-sidedness pleaded (only the wife’s family’s version) destroys the “fair and accurate” character at the threshold.

(2) Y’s rights inventory
  • Fair trial + presumption of innocence under Art. 21 — “Presumption of innocence is held to be a human right” (Ranjitsing Sharma via Sahara ¶33), and courts may protect it against “excessive prejudicial publicity”; “free speech, in appropriate cases, has got to correlate with fair trial… one right may have to yield to the other” (Sahara ¶25).
  • Reputation and dignity — a limb of Art. 21; defamation (s.499/500 IPC ⇄ BNS 356(1)–(2)) remains available post-publication, and the court will “step in… for future prevention” where publicity would “project him as culprit” causing “irreversible damage” (Swatanter Kumar ¶49).
  • Privacy of the accused pending trial — Rajagopal (the right not to have private affairs/public character distorted; the six propositions), and the “real and imminent danger” doctrine of Reliance as its gag-order twin.
  • Right not to be tried in absentia by headlines — the Swatanter Kumar “degree of prejudice” analysis (¶¶49–50, 59: front-page “highlighting and underscoring mere allegations” = “a trial by media kind of situation”).
(3) Remedies — sequenced
  1. Postponement/restraint application before the Constitutional Court (the right forum per the 200th Report: “the High Court for the purpose of passing postponement orders will be a Bench of not less than two Judges”, report p. 230): seek an order under Art. 215 powers per Sahara ¶43 — “seek an order of postponement of the offending publication/broadcast or postponement of reporting of certain phases of the trial… on a balancing of the right to a fair trial and Article 19(1)(a) rights”, satisfying ¶42: actual publication; real and substantial risk of prejudice; no alternative (bail hearing adjournment, change of venue?); salutary > deleterious; limited duration; no content rewriting; and the order running “till the next date of hearing… subject to further monitoring” (Swatanter Kumar ¶65 form).
  2. Prayer specifics from the Swatanter Kumar order: restraint on further publications “connecting or associating [Y] with those allegations, particularly, without disclosing in the headlines… that they are mere allegations” (¶64(a)); deletion of the offending article from the internet within 24 hours (¶64(b), citing ESPN Software and IPRS — anti-injunction-against-the-world practice); photograph restraint (¶64(c)).
  3. Contempt notice — as a liability threat, not an interim gag: s.2(c)(i) “tends to interfere” + s.13 “truth… in public interest” and fair-accuracy defences tested later; initiation needs consent of the AG/Counsel so notified (s.15) — for subordinate-court matters the reference route (s.15(2)) — and remember the direct-HC-access mechanism the 200th Report proposed (s.10A) was never enacted, so the petition must stand on Art. 215/inherent jurisdiction + Sahara parameters.
  4. Defamation suit for damages (civil + criminal), preserved by Reliance’s “own risk” formula: “should any newspaper publish any such matter, it will be doing so at its own risk and subject to its liability for being proceeded against… for defamation, contempt of court or otherwise” (PDF p. 302) — the court’s lifting of a gag never immunises the content.
  5. Regulatory echo (if any channel telecasts the story): programme-code heads + NBSA complaint (U4 E4–E5) — “the State… may act through code after telecast; the print article reaches only the general law”.
(4) What will not work (say so to earn the “analysis” half)

No general prior restraint on the press (Rajagopal ¶22; Reliance: injunction only on “real and imminent danger”, vacated as soon as it passed); no gag on fair court reporting (Swatanter Kumar ¶66 savings); no s.228A/BNS 72 remedy (the deceased is not the enumerated-offence “victim” of a s.376-family charge — the section is a shield for sexual-offence victims, not a general privacy law); no reliance on the unenacted ss.14A/14B (200th Report) as if in force; and no “publicity” argument as ground to refuse bail — “the anticipated danger should not be remote, conjectural or far-fetched… spark in a powder keg” (Rangarajan), a standard publicity alone never meets.

(5) Bail limb — appeal if the High Court rejected anticipatory bail

Route: the order refusing anticipatory bail (s.438 CrPC ⇄ s.482 BNSS — verified pair) is revisable by SLP under Art. 136 to the Supreme Court (no regular appeal lies); in parallel, the trial court’s coercive steps can be resisted by: (i) an application for interim protection; (ii) re-application before the Sessions Court if the HC rejected on extraneous (media) grounds; and (iii) quashal of a motivated FIR via s.482 CrPC ⇄ s.528 BNSS inherent powers, only on the rare “manifest injustice” standard — the two BNSS pairs verified against multiple mapping tables this session; numbers current to July 2024 commencement.

Skeleton of the SLP memo (write it like this): 1. Title — “SLP (Crl.) against order dated __ rejecting IA under s.482 BNSS in W.P./Crl.A. No. __ of __”; 2. Interim prayer — (a) arrest protection till date, (b) postponement/restraint on further publication of trial-related material per Sahara ¶¶42–43 parameters, (c) deletion of the specific URL (not the domain) per X v. UOI specificity; 3. Facts — timeline of the “vicious article” vs the bail listing; only one version; headline connecting Y with guilt; 4. Grounds — (i) High Court acted on media material, not record: “the court will judge on the basis of the record” and “trial by newspapers… is the very antithesis of the rule of law” (Sahara ¶12; P.C. Sen “tendency to interfere” misapplied against Y, not the press!); (ii) presumption of innocence as a human right (Ranjitsing via Sahara ¶33); (iii) anticipatory-bail parameters (reasonable apprehension; no prima facie material) misread; (iv) s.4(1) fair-accuracy privilege cannot arm a one-sided “article”, and an article “calculated to interfere” cannot become the ground of refusal; 5. Relief — bail + costs + order copy to Press Council/NBSA? (no — keep courts’ orders to courts); 6. Annexures — impugned order; article; Sahara ¶43 quoted.

Marked-for-marks closing para: “The Constitution does not choose between Mrs. K and Mr. Y by gagging her — it chooses by timing: a postponement order for the narrow window in which prejudice is real and imminent, all along preserving her right to report the courtroom when the doors open. That is Reliance’s balance-of-convenience, Sahara’s parameters and Mirajkar’s open-justice presumption operating as one remedy.”

L2 · Q1 & Q3 — their Unit-6 limbs, then five practice questions

Q1 (censorship limb) — model answer skeleton

Thesis: the colonial state controlled content before publication (licence, security, deposit, seizure, gag without appeal); the Republic inverted the order — the only lawful ex ante content-controls are certification of films (Cinematograph Act: Abbas–Rangarajan–Bobby Art architecture, time-bound, appealable, now writ-only post-FCAT-abolition), statutory identity bars (228A→BNS 72; POCSO 23; JJ 74; matrimonial in-cameras), s.69A blocking with rules, and judicial postponement within Sahara parameters; everything else — sedition-style prior restraint, the DPA’s play-prohibition, the Vernacular Press bond-and-forfeiture — is either dead (DPA 1876: unconstitutional 1956, repealed 2017; VPA 1878 not repealed but moribund — Notes p. 10 “no recourse to a court of law” being its defining vice) or punished after. “Catalyst” limb: the Notes’ own history — the 1857 Act’s “calculated to produce a bad effect upon the public mind”, the 1878 Act that Amrita Bazar Patrika defeated by going English overnight (Notes pp. 10), the 1930 Ordinance silencing 130 papers, Emergency censorship reducing papers to “college magazines” (Notes pp. 10–11) — each excess produced a counter-institution: the 1st Amendment’s 19(2) heads, the post-Emergency PCI Act 1978, the PRB Act’s survival of the certificate-of-authentication machinery, and the judicial doctrine of proximity (Rangarajan) that no statute ever granted.

Q3 (comparison limb) — model answer skeleton

Framework sentence: “the First Amendment is absolute in terms and it contains no exception for the exercise of the right… its language, in terms that are absolute is utterly at war with censorship” (Douglas J., quoted in Rangarajan) — India’s 19(1)(a)+19(2) “reasonable restrictions” framework is textually the weaker right; but the reader shows the robustness is institutional, not textual: (i) US tolerates film prior restraint only via Burstyn-to-Kingsley case-law and struck it (Kingsley majority; Douglas dissent excerpt), while India upholds certification with conditions (Abbas) — “there does have to be a compromise… we cannot simply balance the two interests as if they are of equal weight” (Rangarajan’s non-balancing is, functionally, the presumption against suppression even in a permitting system); (ii) US bans prior restraints with Near’s heavy presumption — imported wholesale by Rajagopal ¶22; (iii) US gag-law is contempt-incompatible (Sahara ¶17) while India uses s.2(c)+s.15 as a consent-gated, sparingly-used device (Sahara ¶33 caveat); (iv) both systems converge at the incitement line — Schenck/Abrams/Brandeis in Whitney ↔ “spark in a powder keg” (Rangarajan), quoted back by the US-facing judgment itself (Shreya ¶45). Verdict line: “more robust” is true of the text, not the trajectory: after Rangarajan–Rajagopal–Sahara, the Indian doctrine is stricter than American doctrine on the one question that used to embarrass it — who bears the cost of an angry audience (the State, to protect, never the speaker, to silence).

O2 · Revision kit — 24 flashcards, the 30-second protocol, and the honesty ledger

1 · Pre-restraint definition
State control before publication: certification, injunction, licence content-conditions. Presumptively invalid for print (Brij Bhushan; Rajagopal ¶22); tolerated for films (Rangarajan); regulated as “postponement” for trials (Sahara ¶42).
2 · The s.5B(1) heads
Sovereignty/integrity · security · foreign relations · public order · decency/morality · defamation · contempt · incitement to offence — “Art. 19(2) practically read into” the Act.
3 · s.5A(1) proviso effect
Certificate = immunity from obscenity-law punishment for the film’s matter; NOT immunity from other laws (Raj Kapoor ¶15: Penal Code does not “hang limp”); rebuttable presumption (¶14).
4 · Certificates
U; UA (with parental caution, not policed — s.7 last proviso); A (adults = 18+, s.2(a)); S (profession/class). ≤3-yr children admissible to A/S/UA with guardians (s.7(3)).
5 · s.6(2) limits
Govt can deem certified film uncertified / suspend exhibition — clause (c) capped at two months; second proviso: non-disclosure “against public interest” allowed.
6 · DPA 1876
Stage pre-censorship: scandalous/defamatory, seditious (“disaffection”), “deprave and corrupt”; paid-admission building = public place; unconstitutional 1956 (Babu Lal); repealed 2017.
7 · Odyssey ratio
Right to have a serial telecast on Doordarshan = 19(1)(a), subject to DD’s terms; injunction without prima facie evidence of grave prejudice = error; private TV right expressly reserved.
8 · Rangarajan standard
Overall impact; ordinary reasonable man (not hypersensitive); “period depicted + contemporary standards”; censor must be “responsive to social change” yet sensitive to morality; deference to two approving committees.
9 · Rangarajan suppression test
Pressing situations + endangered community interest + proximate/direct nexus + intrinsically dangerous expression = “spark in a powder keg”; restriction on “the anvil of necessity”, not convenience.
10 · Hostile-audience rule
Threats of violence by objectors ≠ ground for restraint; State must protect the film, “not … surrender to black mail and intimidation”.
11 · Bobby Art test
“Do they advance the message?” If yes → leave alone with A-caution; depiction “just sufficient”; guidelines = standards not statutes; expert Tribunal (incl. 3 women) best placed; frivolous writ → costs.
12 · UIMPAA test
Must-carry lawful iff it furthers informed decision-making; propaganda/partisan compulsion = restraint; social context (illiteracy; medium’s reach) counts; beneficial compulsion never reaches 19(2).
13 · Reliance test
Preventive injunction: reasonable grounds, sole purpose = administration of justice unimpaired; danger “real and imminent”; balance of convenience; vacate when facts change; publication resumes “at its own risk”.
14 · Sahara competence
Arts. 129/215 save all court-of-record powers; s.151 CPC for civil courts; contempt head of 19(2) = “words of widest amplitude”; Parliament may only regulate investigation/punishment.
15 · Sahara parameters
Actual publication · real & substantial risk · alternatives first (venue/trial postponement) · proportionality · limited duration · content untouched · burden on applicant (displace open justice) · only HCs/SC · media may challenge · no fixed categories.
16 · Contempt Act pair
s.4: fair-accurate report privileged, subject to s.7; s.7: in-camera information barred on four heads; s.7(2): the order’s text publishable unless prohibited.
17 · 200th Report kit
“Active” from arrest; “imminent” = reasonable likelihood of charge-sheet; prejudicial list (identity photo · priors · confession · guilt-assertions · sympathy/antipathy); HC two-Judge Bench; ex-parte 7 days + auto-extension; breach = s.14B contempt; s.10A direct HC access; media-law curriculum.
18 · Swatanter Kumar
Postponement + photo restraint + 24-hr deletion (ESPN/IPRS line); “degree of prejudice” heightened for judicial icons; savings clause: fair court reporting untouched.
19 · Identity shields
228A IPC ⇄ BNS 72 (≤2y; keys: police/ victim / next-of-kin+only welfare chair-secretary; court permission; HC/SC judgments ok) · POCSO 23 (6m–1y) · JJ 2015 s.74 (Board permits for best interest) · Nipun Saxena letter-rule.
20 · Matrimonial cluster
HMA 22 / SMA 33 / Parsi 43: in camera mandatory + publication barred (₹1,000 fine); IDA 53: discretionary closed doors. Syllabus “Parsi s.33” = superseded reference.
21 · Variable obscenity rungs
18 (A films) · 12 (UA caution) · 20 (ss.293/294 IPC ⇄ BNS 295/296 + Young Persons Act 1956) · sealed minors (POCSO/JJ) · online classification (2021 Rules).
22 · Butler caveat
Protecting children cannot quarantine adults: “burn the house to roast the pig” — the least-restrictive-rung principle in one quote.
23 · Online power
66A dead (heads absent; “obscene” missing); 69A alive with rules-fence; X: no blanket bans, URL-specificity; Shreya’s ¶45 imports Rangarajan’s keg-test to all expression.
24 · Tuljapurkar
Poetic licence protects the poem, not the obscenity; target-identity alters the effect; s.292 charge survives; publisher/printer excused (apology + 21 years) — post-publication censorship-by-prosecution. [External dossier; reader gap disclosed.]
