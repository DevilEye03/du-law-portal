A1 · The right to know — the constitutional architecture of “public access to information”

Unit 2.3 asks “how far does public access to information go?” The answer has three storeys: (1) the constitutional storey — the right to know as a derivative of Art. 19(1)(a); (2) the statutory storey — the Right to Information Act, 2005, which converts the constitutional claim into an enforceable procedure with time-limits and penalties; and (3) the limits storey — Art. 19(2) restrictions, the s.8 exemptions and the fundamental right to privacy (Art. 21), whose collision is precisely what Case 13 adjudicates.

1. From “freedom of speech” to “freedom to know” — the chain of authority

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that Art. 19(1)(a) is not only a right to express but a right to seek, receive and impart. The case book itself walks you through the chain: Secretary, MoI&B v. Cricket Association of Bengal (Unit 2.1, Case 3 — freedom of speech includes the right to propagate, publish and communicate; “airwaves are public property”); Bennett Coleman (Case 1 — the freedom-of-circulation limb); and — in the Shreya Singhal excerpt (Case 5) — the Court’s insistence that any criminal provision touching information chills the market of ideas:

“It is clear, therefore, that the petitioners are correct in saying that the public’s right to know is directly affected by Section 66A. Information of all kinds is roped in… It is clear that the right of the people to know — the market place of ideas — which the internet provides to persons of all kinds is what attracts Section 66A.” Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) 5 SCC 1 — as printed in the DU case book, ¶13 of the judgment extract

Why the exam asks “how far does public access to information go?”

The question is a balance test, not a definition test. The DU answer: the right is presumptive and universal (any citizen, any record, no reasons required — RTI ss.3, 6(2)); but it ends where (i) the holder is not a “public authority” (s.2(h); s.24 + Second Schedule), (ii) the matter is an enumerated exemption (s.8(1)(a)–(j), s.9), (iii) the public-interest override of s.8(2) / proviso to s.8(1)(j) is not discharged, or (iv) another fundamental right pulls the other way — privacy (Puttaswamy), fair trial (Unit 2.4), or the administration of justice (contempt). Case 13 resolves limb (iv): the “mere fact” that judges filed asset declarations is disclosable; the “contents” are personal information requiring a demonstrated larger public interest.

A2 · How far public access goes — the Right to Information Act, 2005 machinery, in full

Justice A.P. Shah’s Division Bench described the statute in terms that open any good 2.3 answer (printed at Case Book pp. 107–108, ¶29):

“The Act arguably is one of the most important pieces of legislation, in the post independence era, to effectuate democracy. It may be likened to a powerful beacon, which illuminates unlit corners of state activity, and those of public authorities which impact citizens’ daily lives, to which they previously had no access. It mandates disclosure of all manner of information, and abolishes the concept of locus standi, of the information applicant; no justification for applying (for information) is necessary; indeed, Section 6(2) enjoins that reasons for seeking such information cannot be sought…” CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Aggarwal, AIR 2010 Delhi 159, ¶29 — DU Case Book pp. 107–108

Real examples — “how far does access go?” in practice

  • Candidate assets → ADR/PUCL (¶17, pp. 106–107): the “few rays of light” remedy for the “cancerous growth of corruption”; today the ECI affidavit portal is the everyday dataset courts, journalists and the Election Commission itself use.
  • “Fact vs content” (¶¶74–75, p. 116): whether judges declared their assets is “innocuous information” which “does not warrant the protection granted by Section 8(1)(j)”; the contents do. This dichotomy is Unit 2.3’s most quotable holding.
  • CIC & the BCCI (2010): the cricket board is not “constituted by”, “owned or controlled by”, or “substantially financed” by government → outside s.2(h) — the classic outer-edge illustration (a public function without a public purse).
  • PMO vs PM CARES (CIC, April 2021): the Prime Minister’s Office is a public authority (it answers RTIs, including on the Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund), while PM CARES was held not to be government-owned/controlled or substantially financed; in January 2023 the Delhi High Court found PM CARES to be an “instrumentality of the State” and the Supreme Court stayed that finding while appeals were heard — the live edge of s.2(h)(d)–(e).
  • Collegium RTIs: the same applicant’s parallel requests for Collegium correspondence drew a CIC order (November 2009) — stayed by the Supreme Court on 4 December 2009, a stay which (as the Supreme Court Observer’s chronology records) has effectively endured; in 2021 the CIC directed disclosure of Collegium resolutions with reasoning after appointments are made, and that litigation too reached the Supreme Court (2023) — transparency vs judicial independence remains an unfinished line.
  • Election Bonds (2024): a Constitution Bench struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme as violative of the voter’s right to information — the RTI-generation argument (“no anonymity in public money”) winning in its widest application. Full box in F6 below.

A3 · “Public authority” — the gate of access, and how Case 13’s Division Bench built it

Before an applicant reaches exemptions, appeals or balances, one door must open: the information must be “held by or under the control of a public authority” (s.2(j)). The case book made Case 13 the paradigm for that question — because the Supreme Court of India itself had never been “notified” under any transparency regime, and its Registry genuinely did not hold the judges’ asset files. The Division Bench (A.P. Shah C.J., Vikramjit Sen & S. Muralidhar JJ) answered in three moves, reproduced in full because they are the answer:

Move 1 — Textual sweep (CIC ¶¶18–21, quoted at pp. 103–104)

  • “Public authority” means “any authority or body or institution of self-government established or constituted…”. None of the three words is defined, so the Act’s own etymology governs: “authority” = “a Body invested with power to command or give an ultimate decision, or enforce obedience”; “institution” = “an establishment, organization or an association instituted for the promotion of some objects… of public or general utility”.
  • The three terms by implication exclude an individual (relying on Kamaraju Venkata Krishna Rao v. Sub-Collector, Ongole AIR 1969 SC 563: “Its meaning must always depend upon the context in which it is found”).
  • But — the killer syllogism at CIC ¶22 — the Supreme Court “consisting of the Chief Justice of India and such number of judges as the Parliament may by law prescribe, is an institution or authority of which the Hon’ble Chief Justice of India is the Head. The institution and its Head cannot be two distinct public authorities. They are one and the same.” The Registrar, “only a part of the Supreme Court”, cannot be carved out as a separate authority (¶22).

Move 2 — Structural inference (Division Bench ¶¶30–35, pp. 108–109)

  • Art. 124 itself makes the Court “established… by or under the Constitution” — squarely within limb (a) of s.2(h). The CJI is additionally a “competent authority” in relation to the Supreme Court (s.2(e)(ii)) with rule-making power under s.28 — “who cannot disclaim being public authorities” while exercising powers the Act itself confers (¶30).
  • Exemption is only what s.24 + the Second Schedule provides; “There is no clue in these provisions, that the office of the Chief Justice of India, is exempt; on the contrary, internal indications in the enactment point to even the President of India, being covered by the Act” (¶35) — via ss.2(h) & 2(e).
  • The 1997 Resolution names the CJI as the recipient: “To conclude that the CJI does not hold asset declaration information in his capacity as Chief Justice of India, would also be incongruous, since the 1997 resolution explicitly states that the information would be given to him” (¶35).

Move 3 — Functional reality: the CJI wears many hats, one office (¶¶31–34)

“He is, unlike the United States (where the Chief Justice is the Chief Justice of the US Supreme Court) the Chief Justice of India” — appointments of High Court judges and Chief Justices, transfers, tribunals, legal aid, judicial education: “It is quite possible therefore, that the Chief Justice, for convenience maintains a separate office or establishment” — yet all these roles “are directly relatable to his holding the office of CJI, and heading the Supreme Court” (¶35). The petitioners could not point to any separate PIO for that establishment. Result: the CJI holds asset declarations in his official capacity; his office is a “public authority”.

Doctrinal satellites to cite alongside (all standard, exam-safe)

  • “Constitutionally” established — the category that swept in offices never envisaged in 2005: Comptroller & Auditor General, Election Commission, UPSC, Finance Commission, Attorney-General’s office; the case book’s own example is the Supreme Court and (by parity) every High Court and subordinate court (Registrar General’s offices being their PIOs).
  • “Substantially financed” (s.2(h)(e)) — CIC’s working test: money + purpose + control. Recognised examples where RTI was applied: aid-merger NGOs (CIC in Shaneel Chandra v. NCERT? — use generally: school networks, ICWA affiliates); where refused: BCCI (CIC 2010) and PM CARES (CIC 2021) — the governing principle being that a body performing public functions without public funding does not become a public authority.
  • “Owned or controlled” (s.2(h)(d)) — PSU banks (SBI!), LIC, statutory bodies like Bar Councils (State Bar Council of U.P. — CIC/Delhi HC treat advocates’ bodies as public authorities), NITI Aayog (2015 CIC ruling: an advisory body with no statutory or executive powers is not a public authority — a fine contrast for the answer).
  • What matters for DU is the combination of s.2(h)(a) (“by or under the Constitution”) with Case 13’s one-entity rule — not the peripheral case-law.

Pitfall

Students routinely answer Case 13 as “the Supreme Court held the CJI is subject to RTI”. Precisely: the Delhi High Court (Full Bench of three judges) so held in the report at AIR 2010 Delhi 159 printed in your case book; the Supreme Court of India affirmed on 13 November 2019 (five-judges: Gogoi CJI, Ramana, Chandrachud, Gupta & Khanna JJ), adding the s.8(1)(j) balancing gloss and rejecting the fiduciary exemption — with a caution that a stay (4.12.2009) continues over Collegium-correspondence RTIs. Always separate the two decisions by name, court and date; the question papers reward exactly that distinction.

A4 · Access vs privacy — s.8(1)(j), the three-factor test, and how the Division Bench wrote a mini-law of privacy

This is the doctrinal heart of Unit 2.3 — and it is also the hinge that connects the RTI unit to your privacy unit (2.2): the same “public-forum” logic (Rajagopal; Puttaswamy) reappears here as a statutory test. The Division Bench’s own framing (¶62, p. 110):

“The right to access public information, that is, information in the possession of state agencies and governments, in democracies is an accountability measure empowering citizens to be aware of the actions taken by such state ‘actors’. This transparency value, at the same time, has to be reconciled with the legal interests protected by law, such as other fundamental rights, particularly the fundamental right to privacy… no single right must prevail over others, except in clear and express circumstances.” CPIO v. Subhash Chandra Aggarwal ¶62 — Case Book p. 110

1. The lineage the judgment itself invokes (pp. 110–112)

  • Brandeis J’s dissent in Olmstead v. United States, 277 US 438 (1928) — privacy as “the right to be let alone… the most comprehensive of rights and the right most valued by civilised men”, quoted at ¶64, immediately before the Court’s recital of the Indian chain: Kharak Singh (1964) 1 SCR 332; Gobind (1975) 2 SCC 148; R. Rajagopal (1994) 6 SCC 632; Canara Bank (2005) 1 SCC 496 — with the honest observation that “These judgments, however did not explore the latent tension between the two values of information rights and privacy rights; Rajagopal, which is nearest in point, was concerned to an extent with publication of material that was part of court records.”
  • UDHR Art. 12 is quoted side-by-side with Art. 19 (¶62) — the same instrument guarantees both access and privacy; the Act reconciles them through s.8(1)(j) + s.8(2).
  • Public servants do not shed their rights: O.K. Ghosh v. E.X. Joseph AIR 1963 SC 812 and the Constitution Bench in Kameshwar Prasad v. State of Bihar AIR 1962 SC 1166, reproduced verbatim at ¶65 (p. 111): “We find ourselves unable to accept the argument that the Constitution excludes Government servants as a class from the protection of the several rights guaranteed by the several Articles in Part III…” — hence: “an individual or citizen’s fundamental rights, which include the right to privacy — are not subsumed or extinguished if he accepts or holds public office.”
  • …but the protection is calibrated by role (¶67): “in the case of private individuals, the degree of protection afforded is greater; in the case of public servants, the degree of protection can be lower, depending on what is at stake… A private citizen’s privacy right is undoubtedly of the same nature and character as that of a public servant. Therefore, it would be wrong to assume that the substantive rights of the two differ.” — the same “thin-skinned public figure” idea as Rajagopal ¶20–26 (Unit 2.2), now inside a disclosure statute.

2. The three-factor test — the case book’s most exam-usable list (¶66, pp. 112–113)

When “a member of the public requests personal information about a public servant”, the PIO must distinguish “the personal data inherent to the position” from data that “affect[s] only his/her private life”, and the balancing “would take into account of many factors”, which the Court itemises:

  1. Connection to office: “whether the disclosure of the personal information is with the aim of providing knowledge of the proper performance of the duties and tasks assigned to the public servant in any specific case”;
  2. Position-relatedness: “whether the information is deemed to comprise the individual’s private details, unrelated to his position in the organization”; and
  3. Accountability value: “whether the disclosure will furnish any information required to establish accountability or transparency in the use of public resources.”

The judgment then seals the method with Lord Denning (from What Next in Law), quoted in full at ¶66: “Any infringement of it should give a cause of action for damages or an injunction… None of these rights is absolute. Each is subject to exceptions. These exceptions are to be allowed whenever the public interest in openness outweighs the public interest in privacy or confidentiality. In every instance it is a balancing exercise for the Courts.” (This is, in substance, the proportionality template that Puttaswamy would constitutionalise six years later.)

A5 · “Is there a right to gather news or attend meetings?” — open justice and access to proceedings

The syllabus’s second limb asks whether journalism enjoys a privileged right of entry. The case book answers through three of its own texts, read together:

1. Access to courts — “no special privilege; but the oldest of privileges: publicity”

  • Open justice is a right of the litigant-public. In Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra AIR 1967 SC 1 (nine judges), reproduced at ¶20 of the case book’s Naveen Jindal text (pp. 122–123): “the High Court has inherent jurisdiction to hold a trial in camera if the ends of justice clearly and necessarily require… It is hardly necessary to emphasize that this inherent power must be exercised with great caution and it is only if the court is satisfied beyond the doubt that the ends of justice themselves would be defeated if a case is tried in open court…” — i.e., the public attends as of right; exclusion is the reasoned exception.
  • The press attends on the same ticket as citizens. From Sahara India (Constitution Bench, (2012) 10 SCC 603) as re-quoted in the same judgment extract (¶23, p. 124): “there is in the first place a presumption of open justice… Given that the postponement orders curtail the freedom of expression of third parties, such orders have to be passed only in cases in which there is real and substantial risk of prejudice to fairness of the trial or to the proper administration of justice which in the words of Justice Cardozo is ‘the end and purpose of all laws’.”
  • Dockets are public records. R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. ¶26(5) (Unit 2.2, Case 8; your case book pp. 78–84): every citizen may inspect judicial records — and the RTI Act does not displace that: the 200th Report likewise insists “the day after the report of crime is published, media says ‘Police have no cue’” — access is normal; compelled access is not.

2. Attending “meetings” — assemblies vs access to deliberations

3. Gathering by misgathering? — illegally obtained news

Unit 2.2’s holding that the receiver’s right extends to illegally obtained material (Rajagopal ¶26(4)–(5): no liability for publishing public records / court-record-derived information, and the “no prior restraint” principle at ¶26(3)) is qualified in Unit 2.4 by the fair-trial interest: R.K. Anand ¶173 (case book p. 92): “this is, however, not to say that media is free to publish any kind of report concerning a sub-judice matter or to do a sting on some matter concerning a pending trial in any manner they please. The legal parameter within which a report or comment on a sub-judice matter can be made is well defined and any action in breach of the legal bounds would invite consequences.” A journalist who steals a chargesheet has a publishable story (subject to defamation/contempt) and a cognizable offence (s.378 → BNS 303 theft; s.411 → BNS 317 receiving stolen property; s.403 dishonest misappropriation — now under the BNS’s corresponding property-offence provisions) — rights of gathering do not launder rights of taking.

The examiner’s distinction

Right to gather ≠ right to be given. Art. 19(1)(a) protects collection (interviews, observation, documents lawfully obtainable, court records) and 19(2) does not list “secrecy” as such; but affirmative supply duties arise only under the RTI Act, statutory access rights (e.g., Companies Act inspection of registers), or a court’s disclosure order. Case 13 is a supply-duty case — which is why it sits in 2.3, not 2.1.

A6 · “Fairness doctrine and access to the media” — what it meant, whether India has it, and what replaced it

1. The doctrine in its own home (United States)

The Fairness Doctrine was an FCC policy (formalised 1949) with two limbs: (i) broadcasters must devote a reasonable share of airtime to controversial issues of public importance, and (ii) coverage must be fair — licensees had to make their facilities available for the expression of contrasting viewpoints of responsible elements. Two ancillary rules matured it: the personal-attack rule (a person attacked on broadcast must be given prompt free reply time) and the political-editorising rule (a station attacking or supporting a candidate must offer the candidate equal opportunity to respond).

  • Red Lion Broadcasting Co. v. FCC, 395 U.S. 367 (1969) — the doctrine upheld unanimously (White J) against First Amendment attack. The theory: because the broadcast spectrum is scarce, “it is the purpose of the First Amendment to preserve an uninhibited marketplace of ideas in which truth will ultimately prevail, rather than to countenance monopolization of that market”, and “it is the right of the viewers and listeners, not the right of the broadcasters, which is paramount… the licensee… is permitted to use public property in pursuit of private goals” — “there is no sanctuary in the First Amendment for unlimited private censorship operating in a medium not open to all.” In the case itself, journalist Fred Cook, attacked on air by Rev. Billy James Hargis, was held entitled to reply time.
  • CBS, Inc. v. FCC, 453 U.S. 572 (1981) — personal-attack and political-editorising rules sustained (Burger C.J.), restating that the audience’s right prevails.
  • Death of the doctrine: the FCC’s 1985 fairness report found the rules chilled speech; after Congress passed a codification bill, President Reagan vetoed it (1987), and the FCC repealed the doctrine by its Memorandum Opinion & Order of 4 August 1987 (upheld in Radio-Television News Directors Ass’n v. FCC, 819 F.2d 1165 (D.C. Cir. 1987)) on the ground that the scarcity premise of Red Lion had eroded and the doctrine violated the First Amendment. Cable, print and online never carried a fairness obligation.

Model one-paragraph answer to “Fairness doctrine and access to the media”

The Fairness Doctrine was the American rule that broadcasters must cover public controversy and balance viewpoints — constitutional in Red Lion (1969) precisely because “the right of the public to receive… ideas and experiences” outweighed the licensee’s, and repealed by 1987 once scarcity lost its premise. India never adopted it: the constitutional access cases (CAB — “one-way communication is not acceptable”; the Doordarshan reply cases) treat the medium, not the message, as a public trust; “fairness” arrives instead as the Prasar Bharti quality mandate (s.4) with its never-constituted s.12 Council, the statutory Programme Code (r.6 — decency, defamation, contempt bars), and voluntary codes (NBSA; PCI norms) supplemented by judge-made newsroom law — the 12 Uma Khurana guidelines. Exam takeaway: in India fairness is engineered through access-architecture + self-regulation, not equal-time law.

3. What has been proposed — and why it still isn’t law

  • Law Commission of India, 93rd Report — “Disclosure of Sources of Information by Mass Media” (1982): taken up suo motu because the question “pertains to the law of evidence, but… necessitates an examination of many other fields of law”; it surveyed the High Courts, weighed “privilege or discretion”, observed that “the Press Council Act concentrates on ‘newspaper, news agency, editor or journalist’, but presumably that was because that Act was concerned primarily with the press”, while “every medium of communication to the public needs to be covered”; its working clause (cited in the Commission’s discussion): “No Court may require a journalist to disclose the source of information contained in the publication unless it is established to the satisfaction of the Court that disclosure is necessary in the interest of justice or national security or for the prevention of disorder or crime.” The model was the clause that matured in the UK into s.10, Contempt of Court Act 1981. Parliament never enacted it — the honest “reform” paragraph of this sub-unit.
  • Second Press Commission (1982) — urged the same qualified protection; the PCI’s Norms of Journalistic Conduct (2022 ed.) keep the professional obligation (protection of the identity of informants/whistle-blowers; “not to publish… unverified”) — a code-duty, not a privilege.
  • UK: s.10, Contempt of Court Act 1981 — no court may require a source unless “establishing that disclosure is necessary in the interests of justice or for the protection of national security, for the prevention of disorder or crime, or for the protection of the rights or freedoms of others”; Goodwin v. United Kingdom (1996) 22 EHRR 123: source protection is “one of the basic conditions for press freedom… some of the most important public interests… cannot be safeguarded unless journalists can guarantee confidentiality”; restrictions require an “overriding requirement in the public interest”, strictly construed.
  • USA: Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665 (1972) — “the First Amendment does not invalidate every incidental burden on the collect[ion] of news… [a reporter] is not exempt from testifying before a grand jury” (5–4); but the dissent (Powell J) was legislated into life — 40+ states enacted shield laws granting qualified (not absolute) privilege; no federal shield statute exists.
  • Canada: R. v. National Post, 2010 SCC 16 — no class privilege; sources win only case-by-case on the Wigmore criteria (confidentiality essential to the relationship; benefit to society outweighs the litigation harm). A neat common-law “middle path” the answer can recommend.

  • The leak that made the news: R.K. Anand (case book pp. 74–96) began with the media obtaining a copy of the sealed-cover intercepts from the judge’s own court registry — the Court treated the leak with “grave concern” while acquitting the journalist-side publication question of “trial by media” (¶175), appreciating the reporter’s “professional initiative and courage” (¶194). The two propositions can coexist: no privilege to name sources; no liability for publishing what the courts later say was properly obtained — that is the Indian balance in one line.

“Information related to crime” — the RTI mirror-image

The same syllabus sentence has a second half: can citizens demand crime-information? Under the RTI Act, police case-diaries and open investigation files are “information”, but s.8(1)(h) (impeding investigation, apprehension or prosecution) keeps them closed until the process completes; once the chargesheet is on the court record, Rajagopal ¶26(5) (public-record doctrine) lets anyone — including the press — obtain certified copies from the court, not the police. The 200th Report, quoting the Bombay High Court (Deepti Devasthali — reproduced in the case book’s Naveen Jindal text, p. 128), supplies the closing sentence: “Right to information is wrongly interpreted by the police as right to inform. High degree of secrecy is a must when the investigation is in process.” Investigation-publicity helps the guilty escape, contaminates witnesses, and converts the first draft of a story into the last verdict a man gets.

PART B · CASE 13 — CPIO, SUPREME COURT OF INDIA v. SUBHASH CHANDRA AGGARWAL

“Public authority and their privacy: Scope of RTI Act” — the case book’s own subtitle for the unit. · AIR 2010 Delhi 159 · (2008) 153 DLT 342 · ILR (2009) Del 341 · Delhi High Court, Full Bench — A.P. Shah, C.J. (for himself and Vikramjit Sen and S. Muralidhar, JJ.) · affirmed by a five-judge Bench of the Supreme Court on 13.11.2019 · DU Case Book pp. 101–117 (¶ numbers below are the report’s own, exactly as printed).

One-line ratioEvery institution “of self-government”, including the Supreme Court and the office of the CJI, is a public authority under s.2(h) RTI Act; judges’ asset declarations are “information” under s.2(f); the CJI holds them not as a fiduciary (s.8(1)(e) fails); the contents are “personal information” accessible only through s.8(1)(j)’s larger-public-interest procedure with s.11 third-party notice — while whether declarations were filed is disclosable, the (j) procedure being “inapplicable” to it.
Why the case book chose itIt is Unit 2.3’s entire syllabus in one judgment — “how far public access goes” (the Act’s sweep and its two limits: exemption-pleading and privacy), the applicant’s locus, the transfer duty, and DU’s own institution as respondent.
Procedural ladderRTI 10.11.2007 → CPIO order 30.11.2007 → first appeal (limited remand on s.6(3)) → CPIO rejection 10.1.2008 (the “short-circuit” letter) → CIC order 6.1.2009 → writ → this Full Bench → SLP → SC (5 JJ) 13.11.2019.

1 · FACTS (¶¶1–3, Case Book p. 101)

On 10.11.2007 the respondent applicant wrote to the CPIO, Supreme Court of India seeking: (i) a copy of the 7.5.1997 Full Court Resolution requiring every judge to declare all assets to the CJI “in a confidential manner”; and (ii) “information relating to declaration of assets etc., furnished by the respective Chief Justices of States” — i.e., whether any such declarations had ever been filed by Supreme Court judges and High Court judges. By order dated 30.11.2007 the CPIO agreed to furnish the Resolution “on remitting the requisite charges” and denied the rest: the asset information “was not held by or under the control of the Registry of the Supreme Court”. The applicant’s first appeal succeeded in part: the First Appellate Authority found him “justified in contending that if the CPIO was not holding the information, he should have considered the question of Section 6(3)” and remanded on that limited point (¶3, p. 101).

On remand, on 10.1.2008, the CPIO rejected the application in terms the case book prints verbatim — the passage to quote for “procedure as weapon”: “you yourself knew that the information sought by you is related to various High Court in the country and instead of applying to those Public Authorities you have taken a short circuit procedure by approaching the CPIO, Supreme Court of India remitting the fee of Rs.10/- payable to one authority and getting it referred to all the public authorities at the expense of one Central Public Information Officer… and is against the spirit of Section 6 (3)” (p. 102).

The CPIO’s substantive case before the CIC (¶4, p. 102): the 1997 Resolution was “an in-house exercise”; declarations are “only voluntary”; “the resolution itself describes submission of such declarations as ‘Confidential’”; disclosure would breach a “fiduciary relationship”; the declarations went to the CJI “not in his official capacity but in his personal capacity”; disclosure was “contrary to the provisions of section 8(1) of the Act”. The applicant’s answer (¶16, pp. 105–106): the Act’s exemptions are an exhaustive list — “No other ground for excluding information which exists with any public authority can be deduced under the Act, particularly in respect of information merely marked ‘confidential’”; only s.8(1)(j) could apply, and “by no stretch of imagination can the query whether judges have declared their assets, be considered exempt; there is no question of any confidentiality or privacy” — while contents and access rights were “intrinsically linked” and fell to be decided together (¶16). The respondent further armed himself with ADR and PUCL (¶17, below) and with the point that public disclosure — not secrecy — protects judges: publicity “would thwart attempt at blackmailing of individual judges, or ‘corrosion’ of their independence” (¶21, p. 107).

2 · BEFORE THE FULL BENCH: the CIC order (6.1.2009) printed inside the report (¶¶5–32 quoted at pp. 102–105)

  • Rule-making power as proof of status (¶5, p. 103): “Section 2(e)(i) was referred, to say that the Chief Justice of India was a competent authority… empowered to frame Rules under Section 28… It was held that rule making power is conferred by provisions of the Act, upon the Chief Justice and the Supreme Court, who cannot disclaim being public authorities.” CIC ¶16 rounds it off: “the contention… that the provisions of Right to Information Act are not applicable in case of Supreme Court cannot be accepted.”
  • Custody, not capacity (CIC ¶17): “the office of the Chief Justice of India is the custodian of this information. The information is maintained in a confidential manner and like any other official information it is available for perusal and inspection to every succeeding Chief Justice of India. The information, therefore, cannot be categorized as ‘personal information’ available with the Chief Justices in their personal capacity.”
  • Institution ≠ individual (¶¶20–21): “authority” = “a Body invested with power to command… or having a legal right to command and be obeyed”; “institution” = “a… body or organization brought into being for the purpose of achieving some object”; “By very implication, the three terms exclude an ‘individual’” — with Kamaraju Venkata Krishna Rao v. Sub-Collector, Ongole AIR 1969 SC 563 for the point that “its meaning must always depend upon the context in which it is found.”
  • One entity (¶22): “the Supreme Court of India… is an institution or authority of which the Hon’ble Chief Justice of India is the Head. The institution and its Head cannot be two distinct public authorities. They are one and the same.… The Registrar… cannot be categorized as a Public Authority independent and distinct from the Supreme Court itself.” — and ¶23: “It is the duty of the CPIO to obtain the information that is held by or available with the public authority. Each of the sections or department… cannot be treated as a separate or distinct public authority. If any information is available with one section or the department, it shall be deemed to be available with the Public Authority as one single entity.”
  • Exemptions must be pleaded and decided (¶29): “neither the CPIO nor the First Appellate Authority has claimed that the information… is exempt either under Section 8(1)(e)… or… ‘personal information’ attracting exemption under Section 8(1)(j).”
  • What was actually sought (¶30): “Shri S.C. Agrawal is apparently not seeking a copy of the declarations or the contents therein or even the names… he is seeking simple information as to whether any such declaration… has ever been filed… What he is seeking cannot be held to attract exemption under Sections 8(1)(e) or 8(1)(j).”
  • Direction (¶32): the CPIO was ordered to say “whether such declaration of assets etc. has been filed by the Hon’ble Judges of the Supreme Court or not, within ten working days.”

3 · ISSUES (¶27, p. 107) — the five “Points” the FB set down

  1. Point 1 — “Whether the CJI is a public authority”;
  2. Point 2 — “whether the CPIO of the Supreme Court of India is different from the office of the CJI; and if so, whether the Act covers the office of the CJI”;
  3. Point 3 — whether declarations under the 1997 Resolution are “information” under s.2(f);
  4. Point 4 — whether the CJI holds them in a “fiduciary” capacity (s.8(1)(e));
  5. Point 5 — whether the information is exempt under s.8(1)(j) — the privacy point that consumes ¶¶60–75 of the report and the unit’s entire doctrinal mileage.

4 · ARGUMENTS — both sides, as the case book records them

CPIO / petitioners (the judiciary’s side)

  • No “public act”: “any disclosure made by judges, pursuant to the 1997 resolution, is not a public act done in the discharge of duties of their office… the declaration of personal assets, by individual judges, has nothing to do with their duties, as judges… the voluntary nature of such disclosure, and absence of any legal sanction as a result of non-disclosure” (¶11, p. 105).
  • Personal capacity & confidentiality: filed with the CJI “personally”, “voluntary”, “confidential” — outside the Registry’s custody and outside the Act (¶¶2–4, pp. 101–102).
  • Fiduciary: disclosure would breach a “fiduciary relationship” (¶4) — the exemption-head the Act never lets a mere confidence-with-office convert into immunity.
  • Independence: treating judges like public servants subjecting them to public scrutiny of assets would “undermine” the judiciary; US-style disclosure models do not exist for judges (developed at ¶¶45–60 of the report).
  • Exhaustive-list inversion: s.8 “opens with a non-obstante clause… irrespective of the rights of the information seeker… the information providers can justifiably withhold” (¶28, p. 107 — the petitioners read the chapeau as a wall; the Court reads it as a list of doors, each of which must be opened with reasons).

Respondent applicant — with the Delhi HC Bar Association intervening

  • Textual sweep: s.2(h) — any “authority or body or institution of self-government established or constituted (a) by or under the Constitution”: the Supreme Court, Art. 124, ¶30 (p. 108) — “the court is of opinion that the CIC did not commit any error in concluding that the CJI is a public authority.”
  • Labels do not create exemptions: “the only exemption there… is 8(1)(j)… information ‘merely marked “confidential”’ can be deduced under the Act [only through the enumerated lists]” (¶16).
  • ADR/PUCL: the judgment quotes Union of India v. ADR ¶50 in extenso (¶17, p. 106): “In our view this submission is totally misconceived. There is no question of knowing personal affairs… question is not of knowing personal affairs but to have openness in democracy for attempting to cure cancerous growth of corruption by few rays of light.”
  • Public functionaries: judges declare “in their official capacity” — the 1997 Resolution names the office as recipient; immunity “cannot be granted to them” on that basis (¶21).
  • Privacy is calibrated: the “factum of filing” involves no privacy at all; only contents could — and the applicant did not seek contents (¶30 of CIC; ¶16 of FB).

And s.8(1)(j) “confers substantive rights even while engrafting procedural safeguards”, because of five features — recognition of the third party’s privacy; the PIO/CIC’s satisfaction “that the disclosure is… justified by the public interest… on objective material”; the third party’s views taken into account under s.11; “the satisfaction being recorded in writing, through an order, under s.11(3)”; and appealability of an adverse order (¶71). 6 · PRINCIPLE EVOLVED

  • P1 — Institutional identity: “public authority” is judged institutionally: an institution and its constitutional Head are one; internal record-keeping divisions are no answer. “Not with us” is a transfer-duty, not a defence (s.6(3); ¶23).
  • P2 — Information ubiquity: s.2(f) follows the form of material, not the label; “voluntary” + “confidential” ≠ outside the Act (CIC ¶17; ¶16 of the FB’s respondent-submission recital).
  • P3 — Two-tier disclosure: compliance data of any office (did they file?) is disclosable as of right; contents of personal-data records are protected until the applicant discharges the larger-public-interest onus on objective material, through the s.11 notice → representation → reasoned written order → appeal sequence (¶¶67–71, 75).
  • P4 — Exemptions are pleadable, forfeitable, severable: neither (e) nor (j) having been claimed below (CIC ¶29), the CPIO lost even defensible grounds; the appellate hierarchy (s.19) polices reasoning; s.10 keeps records partially open; non-obstante cuts both ways.
  • P5 — Method: the three-factor test + Denning’s “balancing exercise for the Courts” is now the template for every DU privacy-vs-transparency question — and, read across, the same proportionality grammar Puttaswamy (Unit 2.2, Case 6) would later constitutionalise.
  • P6 — Independence ≠ opacity: “no single right must prevail over others, except in clear and express circumstances” (¶62); publicity of compliance strengthens rather than corrodes judicial independence (respondent’s point at ¶21 adopted in substance).

7 · AFTER THE CASE BOOK — the 2019 five-judge chapter (add to every answer)

On 13 November 2019 a five-judge Bench of the Supreme Court (CJI Ranjan Gogoi; N.V. Ramana, D.Y. Chandrachud, Deepak Gupta & Sanjiv Khanna JJ, with separate concurring opinions of Ramana and Chandrachud JJ) — hearing the CJI’s appeals in three connected matters (Union Minister/Aligarh-judge correspondence; Collegium elevation of Justices Dattu, Ganguly and Lodha; and the asset declarations) — upheld the Delhi High Court on the asset-declaration questions: the CJI is a public authority; declarations are “information”; s.8(1)(e) fails; contents are “personal information” subject to the s.8(1)(j) larger-public-interest balance with third-party safeguards; the factum of filing is disclosable. On the Collegium correspondence the Court charted the middle course it had already indicated in 2009 by staying the CIC order (order of 4 December 2009) — candour in appointments and third-party privacy protected, without conceding that the office stands outside the Act. Chandrachud J.’s concurrence is the quotable jurisprudence: transparency and judicial independence are not in “contradiction”, and the “how transparent is transparent enough” question (the first line of his opinion, as recorded in every account of the case) now governs Unit 2.3. In 2021 the CIC, applying the framework, directed that Collegium resolutions be disclosed (with redactions, after the appointment is made) — that order has been carried before the Supreme Court and its fate, as at 2026, is best stated as sub judice; never write “the collegium files are open”.

DEBATE — four lines examiners ask you to acknowledge

  1. “Chilling candidour”: if Collegium notes leak, frankness dies — answer: the s.11 + (j) machinery, redaction and time-lag are the tailored remedy; blanket opacity is not (¶¶67–71; 2019 concurrences).
  2. “Security of judges”: address and asset lists invite targeting — answer: the Court’s own disclosure model is “standard formats… and redaction”, not non-disclosure (¶68).
  3. “Why not extend ADR fully?” — answered at ¶¶72–73: no elective mandate, secured tenure, office not market.
  4. “Was the FB wrong to decide (j) at all when unpleaded?” — the Court itself justifies reaching it anyway (¶¶29–30 of the CIC recital): “the court is of opinion…” — for wider ramifications. Note the 2019 five-judge treatment too: each RTI still requires case-by-case balancing — “how transparent is transparent enough” is a question to be answered record by record, not once for all.

Real example — the dichotomy in operation since 2019

  • Supreme Court PIO now answers RTIs on compliance (whether judges filed returns) — and routes contents requests through s.11 notice to the judges concerned. (This follows directly from ¶¶74–75 & the 2019 affirmance.)
  • Legislators, by contrast, disclose in full: the ECI’s candidate-affidavit regime (upheld in ADR/PUCL — ECI orders under Art. 324 read with RPA 1951/RP Rules, 1961) puts assets on the public net — the asymmetry the FB called out (¶¶72–73) and the 2019 Bench declined to erase. The 2024 Electoral Bonds judgment (Part F below) pushes the same openness logic one layer up — to the parties’ purses.
  • Executives: service-rules asset returns of senior officers (All India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1969) are routinely ordered disclosed by CIC on the Case-13 grid — “position-related” data (property acquisitions, gifts) released; “private-life” data (medical, family) withheld.

PART C · UNIT 2.4 DOCTRINE — “TRIAL BY MEDIA AND FAIR TRIAL”

C1 · The competing value: what a “fair trial” actually contains

The 200th Report — your syllabus’s named reference — opens by defining the terrain better than any commentary (Chapter I, pp. 11–12 of the report):

“The subject of ‘Trial by Media’ is discussed by civil rights activists, constitutional lawyers, judges and academics almost everyday in recent times. With the coming into being of the television and cable-channels, the amount of publicity which any crime or suspect or accused gets in the media has reached alarming proportions. Innocents may be condemned for no reason or those who are guilty may not get a fair trial or may get a higher sentence after trial than they deserved. There appears to be very little restraint in the media in so far as the administration of criminal justice is concerned.” LCI 200th Report, Ch. I (uploaded full text)

and then the sentence that should sit near the start of every 2.4 answer:

“If media exercises an unrestricted… freedom in publishing information about a criminal case and prejudices the mind of the public and those who are to adjudicate on the guilt of the accused and if it projects a suspect or an accused as if he has already been adjudged guilty well before the trial in court, there can be serious prejudice to the accused. In fact, even if ultimately the person is acquitted after the due process in courts, such an acquittal may not help the accused to rebuild his lost image in society.” LCI 200th Report, Ch. I — the same passage ends: publications “calling for proceedings for contempt of court against the media”

1. The ingredients of “fair trial” the Report itemises (Ch. II — verbatim from the uploaded text)

  • Presumption of innocence — “Our criminal law and criminal jurisprudence are based on the premise that the guilt of any person charged in a court of law has to be proved beyond reasonable doubt and that the accused is presumed to be innocent unless the contrary is proved in public, in a court of law, observing all the legal safeguards to an accused.”; UDHR Arts. 10, 11(1), 12; ICCPR Art. 14(2) — “Everyone charged with a criminal offence shall have the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty according to law”; Art. 14(3)(a)–(g) minimum guarantees (prompt informed of the charge; adequate time and facilities; without undue delay; presence and defence; examination of witnesses; interpreter; and “not to be compelled to testify against himself or to confess guilt”).
  • Right to silence — Art. 20(3) Constitution; the Report: “the accused has a basic right to silence… That is also the reason why confessions to the police are inadmissible in a court of law” (cross-referring its 180th Report, 2001, “Art. 20(3) and the Right to Silence”). This single premise does enormous doctrinal work in the publicity rules: publishing an alleged “custodial confession” puts before the public what the law will not allow before the court — the 200th Report’s “publication of confessions” category (Ch. IX(2)).
  • Open (not private) justice — trial “in public” is itself a safeguard (Mirajkar ¶21, reproduced in the case book at Jindal ¶20; s.327 CrPC → s.366 BNSS “Court to be open” — in-camera only for rape etc., public-order or as the Code directs; the 200th Report’s Ch. III adds the psychological dimension: publications affect judges “subconsciously” too — hence the law polices the input, not only the intention).
  • Due process (Art. 21) — the Report’s constitutional hook after Maneka Gandhi: “the procedure protecting liberty must be fair, just and reasonable and not arbitrary” (Ch. X(2)) — which is precisely how the Court justified regulating media publications “after arrest”, even pre-chargesheet.

2. Where the Constitution locates the restriction

There is no “fair administration of justice” head in Art. 19(2). The bridge is the word “contempt of court”: restrictions “in relation to… contempt of court” permit Parliament to legislate for the administration of justice — and the Report’s Ch. X(2) says so expressly: “Though Article 19(2) does not refer to the imposition of reasonable restrictions for the purposes of administration of justice, the reference… that restrictions can be imposed for the purpose of the Contempt of Courts Act clearly indicates that the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (sections 2, 3) take care of the protection of the administration of justice.” Two consequences to state in the answer: (i) the entire pre-trial-publicity law of India runs through the Contempt Act + CrPC/BNSS + special statutes — not through any express constitutional head; (ii) so when a publication falls outside s.2(c)–s.3 (say, before any chargesheet — M.P. Lohia’s bail-hearing situation; Mrs. “ABC”’s complaint stage in Jindal), the protection of the trial comes from judge-made postponement: the Constitution Bench in Sahara India — whose principles your case book reprints verbatim at Jindal ¶23, ¶42–43 — permits postponement “only in cases in which there is real and substantial risk of prejudice to fairness of the trial or to the proper administration of justice which in the words of Justice Cardozo is “the end and purpose of all laws”. However, such orders of postponement should be ordered for a limited duration and without disturbing the content of the publication. They should be passed only when necessary… if reasonable alternative methods or measures such as change of venue or postponement of trial will not prevent the said risk” (Sahara ¶42, reprinted in the case book at Case Book p. 125) — an exercise of the courts’ inherent jurisdiction (s.482 CrPC, now s.528 BNSS) reconciling Art. 21 with Art. 19(1)(a).

3. The definitional core the case book itself supplies

“The expression ‘trial by media’ is defined to mean: ‘the impact of television and newspaper coverage on a person’s reputation by creating a widespread perception of guilt regardless of any verdict in a court of law. During high publicity court cases, the media are often accused of provoking an atmosphere of public hysteria akin to a lynch mob which not only makes a fair trial nearly impossible but means that, regardless of the result of the trial, in public perception the accused is already held guilty and would not be able to live the rest of their life without intense public scrutiny.’” R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court (2009) 8 SCC 106, ¶174 — DU Case Book p. 93 (¶175 applies it: the NDTV sting “cannot be described as a piece of trial by media”)

The case book’s placement of this quotation immediately before Unit 2.3’s materials (pp. 93–96) is deliberate: R.K. Anand supplies the definition, M.P. Lohia the classic violation, Naveen Jindal the remedy, and the 200th Report the catalogue and the draft statute. That four-source grid is the answer’s skeleton.

C2 · Pre-trial publicity — when a publication ceases to be news and becomes contempt

1. The statutory mechanism, stated exactly

s.2(c) Contempt of Courts Act, 1971: “criminal contempt” includes publication (words, signs, visible representations — so TV/online included; and the 200th Report proposed an express Explanation to cover “print, radio broadcast, electronic media, cable television network, world wide web”) which “(ii) prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or (iii) interferes… or obstructs… the administration of justice in any other manner”.

s.3 — the timing rule (the provision that makes “pre-trial” the operative word): sub-ss. (1)–(3) protect an innocent publication about a proceeding that is not then “pending” — and the Explanation fixes when a criminal proceeding becomes “pending”:

  • (B)(i) — for offences: when the charge-sheet or challan is filed, or when the court issues summons or warrant against the accused (whichever earlier, as re-anchored after the 1973 Code is read in); (B)(ii) — otherwise: when the court takes cognizance; and pendency runs “until it is heard and finally decided” (including appeals, or the limitation period for them);
  • execution of a decided case does not keep it “pending” (cl. (b)).

So in law: before the chargesheet/summons, the Contempt Act gives near-complete immunity (s.3(2)), and the real levers against pre-charge publicity are — special statutes (victim-identity bars below), police-discipline rules, defamation, privacy tort and damages (Uma Khurana ¶13: “Damages in accordance with the law can be awarded by Courts in cases of defamation and loss of reputation”), and the court’s inherent/postponement power (Sahara; Jindal). The Law Commission’s entire 2006 proposal was designed to close that gap — see C3 and the Draft Bill box in C5.

2. A.K. Gopalan v. Noordeen — the 1969 seed the Report cultivates

The Report’s Ch. VI asks whether “A.K. Gopalan v. Noordeen (1969) 2 SCC 734 makes ‘imminence’ relevant only in respect of arrest for serious offences. What is the effect of the 24-hour rule?” — its answer drives the amendment: because an arrested person must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours, “a person arrested comes immediately within the protection of a court”; so “publications made even after arrest and before filing of charge sheet could also be prejudicial”, and regulation of such publications “will be reasonable” under Art. 21’s due-process requirement. Hence the recommendation (Ch. X(2)(a)): “the word ‘pending’ in Section 3(1), (2) and Explanation… must be substituted by the word ‘active’… and under the [UK Contempt of Court Act 1981]… a criminal proceeding is defined as being ‘active’ from the date of arrest.” This never became law — the only 2006 amendment actually enacted was the introduction of s.13(b)’s “justification by truth” defence (Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act, 6 of 2006 — your uploaded text records it) — so the gap remains; Sahara and the postponement jurisprudence fill it judicially.

For cameras

  • Open justice as institutional hygiene: “Justice is not a cloistered virtue” — Lord Atkin, Ambard v. AG of Trinidad and Tobago [1936] AC 322, quoted in the Report (Ch. IX(6)): “she must be allowed to suffer the scrutiny and respectful, even though outspoken, comments of ordinary men”.

2. What the Law Commission proposed (200th Report, Ch. X) — the statute India did not get

Draft Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Bill, 2006 (annexed to the 200th Report) — the architecture

  • Explanation to s.2(c) — “publication includes publication in print, radio broadcast, electronic media, cable television network, world wide web” (¶3 of the Bill).
  • “Pending” → “active” in s.3(1), (2) and the Explanation — and s.3(2): the non-contempt rule for “not pending” matter re-worded; criminal proceedings become “active” on arrest, or chargesheet, or summons/warrant, “whichever is earlier” (Bill ¶4) — the A.K. Gopalan/24-hour-rule fix.
  • New s.10A — High Court may act directly (no reference through subordinate courts) on suo motu or petition for publication-contempts of subordinate courts under s.2(c)(ii)–(iii), dispensing with the Advocate-General’s consent — “much damage… can result if the contempt power… through a reference to the High Court” (¶5).
  • New s.14A — postponement orders: in criminal proceedings that are active or imminent, a Division Bench (2 judges) of the High Court may, on application by the AG/law officer, the arrested/charged person, or “any person interested in the avoidance of the risk”, make an order ex parte, for an initial period of seven days, directing that matter whose publication risks “real risk of serious prejudice” shall not be published, or only subject to conditions — extendable if no application to vary is moved within 7 days (media may move to vary/cancel any time) — modelled on s.4(2) UK 1981 Act and NSW’s 2003 Bill.
  • New s.14B — breach of a s.14A order = criminal contempt punishable by the High Court “according to law… under the Contempt of Court Act r. under Art. 215”.
  • Applicability clause: the Act (as amended) to apply to publications concerning proceedings that are “active” under s.3(2) or “active or imminent” under s.14A (¶7); plus the recommendation that media-law training be built into journalism courses: “Diploma and Degree Course in Journalism and the Law” (Ch. X(7)).

Fate: never enacted. The only 2006 change was s.13(b) (truth as defence, on satisfaction of public interest and bona fide request) — which, note, is the Report’s era’s concession that comment on decided cases should widen even as pre-trial restraint is tightened. The courts filled the postponement gap themselves (Sahara, 2012; Swatanter Kumar, 2014; Jindal, 2015).

How to close a 2.4 answer (the “principle” paragraph the examiner waits for)

India has no pre-2006-style “active” contempt and no postponed-publicity statute; what it has is a common law of balance — the presumption of open justice and of innocence meeting at the judge’s desk. Its canon is Sahara’s quartet (necessity, proportionality, short duration, content intact), its enforcement is the Contempt Act (narrow, appellate, one-year-old), its conscience is self-regulation (Code + norms + court-drawn 12 guidelines), and its warning shot is Lohia’s: “the publisher, editor and the journalist… cautioned against indulging in such trial by media when the issue is subjudice” — a caution that, in 2025-26, is still the entire law of the field, which is exactly what the 200th Report meant to change and Parliament never did.

PART D · CASE 14 — M.P. LOHIA v. STATE OF WEST BENGAL (AND ANR.)

(2005) 2 SCC 686 · cited in the 200th Report as AIR 2005 SC 790 · Supreme Court — N. Santosh Hegde, J. (for himself and S.B. Sinha, J.) · Criminal Appeals against the Calcutta High Court’s refusal of anticipatory bail (rejected 13.02.2004) · “TRIAL BY MEDIA” — the case book’s own unit-heading, Case Book pp. 118–119.

One-lineWhile an anticipatory-bail SLP is sub judice, a magazine feature publishing the complainants’ one-sided allegations (“Doomed by Dowry”) “would certainly interfere with the administration of justice” — the Court “deprecates this practice and cautions the publisher, editor and the journalist… against indulging in such trial by media”, treats the matter as closed, and shapes bail conditions that keep the accused out of both police and media pressure.
Why it is THE caseThe Supreme Court’s clearest pre-Sahara pronouncement that one-sided trial by media is not reporting; the “sub judice + one-sidedness + timing” anatomy of the wrong; and the Dec-2024 DU Q6 re-enacts its facts verbatim.
Sections in playIPC 304B → BNS s.80 (dowry death); 406 → s.316 (criminal breach of trust — the stridhan allegation); 498A → ss.85/86 (cruelty), each r/w s.34 → BNS s.3(5) (common intention); statutory presumption for dowry death — former Ev. Act s.113-B, now carried in the BSA’s chapter on presumptions; bail: CrPC s.438(1)–(2) → BNSS s.482; contempt: Contempt of Courts Act ss.2(c)(ii)–(iii), 3, 12, 13, 15, 20.

1 · FACTS (Case Book p. 118)

  • The families. “The appellants live in Ludhiana whereas the complainant and his family are residents of Calcutta.” The complainant’s daughter Chandni (since deceased) married the appellant on 18 February 2002.
  • The death. “Chandni committed suicide on 28th of October, 2003 at her parents’ house in Calcutta.” (Note the book’s internal inconsistency — while discussing the article, a later paragraph records the death as having “taken place on 28th February, 2002”: evidently a scrivener’s slip for 28.10.2003. Flag it and move on; examiners appreciate the catch.)
  • The prosecution story. “They were not satisfied with the dowry given at the time of wedding and were harassing the deceased continuously, consequent to which she developed depression” — despite the parents’ assurances they would “meet their demand of the dowry”; the deceased “was being treated cruelly at her matrimonial home” and “had no love and affection” from her husband; there was an earlier suicide attempt in July 2002 “at the residence of her parents… about a year earlier than the actual date of her death.”
  • The defence story. “The deceased was a schizophrenic psychotic patient with cyclic depression and was under medical treatment” — in Ludhiana and Calcutta; “the defence documents show that the relationship between the husband, wife and the in-laws were cordial and it was only illness of the deceased that was the cause of her premature death.”
  • The Court’s evidentiary truce — the passage to know cold. “One thing is obvious that there has been an attempt on the part of both the sides to create documents… Correctness or genuineness of this document can only be gone into in a full-fledged trial and it will not be safe to place reliance on any one of these documents at this stage. Therefore, we would venture not to comment on the genuineness of these documents at this stage. Suffice it to say that this is a matter to be considered at the trial.”
  • The bail order. “Any expression of opinion on the merits of the case except to the extent of finding out prima facie whether the appellants are entitled for anticipatory bail or not, would likely to affect the trial” — hence, “without expressing any opinion on the same”, bail in the event of arrest: bonds of ₹1,00,000 each with one surety; appellants to “abide by the conditions statutorily imposed under Section 438(2) of the Code of Criminal Procedure” (now s.482(2) BNSS); where the investigating agency requires their presence, “one week’s notice” to be given; appearance and presence “shall not exceed two days at a time”; and crucially — “such interrogation shall not be a custodial interrogation. They shall be entitled to have their counsel present at the time of such interrogation.”

2 · THE “DISTURBING FACTOR” — the trial-by-media holding (p. 119)

“Having gone through the records, we find one disturbing factor which we feel is necessary to comment upon in the interest of justice. … the application for grant of anticipatory bail was disposed of by the High Court of Calcutta on 13.2.2004 and special leave petition was pending before this Court. Even then an article has appeared in a magazine called ‘Saga’ titled “Doomed by Dowry” written by one Kakoli Poddar based on her interview of the family of the deceased. Giving version of the tragedy and extensively quoting the father of the deceased as to his version of the case. The facts narrated therein are all materials that may be used in the forthcoming trial in this case and we have no hesitation that this type of articles appearing in the media would certainly interfere with the administration of justice. We deprecate this practice and caution the publisher, editor and the journalist who was responsible for the said article against indulging in such trial by media when the issue is subjudiced. However, to prevent any further issue being raised in this regard, we treat this matter as closed and hope that the other concerned in journalism would take note of this displeasure expressed by us for interfering with the administration of justice.” M.P. Lohia, closing paragraphs — DU Case Book p. 119 (reproduce verbatim; this is the single most quotable passage in Unit 2.4)

Then one line: “For the reasons stated above, these appeals succeed and the same are allowed.”

3 · ISSUES

  1. Whether, when “documents have been created on both sides” and the merits cannot be canvassed at the bail stage, the courts below were right to refuse anticipatory bail — and if bail is granted, on what conditions can the investigation be made orderly and the trial fair?
  2. Whether the publication, while the SLP was pending, of the complainant family’s version in a print feature — “materials that may be used in the forthcoming trial” — amounts to interference with the administration of justice (a “media trial” of a sub judice issue)?
  3. If so, what judicial response is proportionate: contempt, costs, an order against the press — or deprecation plus caution?

4 · ARGUMENTS — how the contest ran (and where the excerpt is silent)

Appellants (accused)

  • Bail stage demands only a prima facie look; the prosecution’s own record (documents “created”) could not be tested here; custodial interrogation would convert investigation into spectacle given the press climate — hence non-custodial, counsel-present questioning was the balance.
  • The medical history (schizophrenia; July 2002 attempt at the parents’ home) cut against the “harassment → suicide” chain said to justify denial of liberty.
  • (On the article:) nothing was before any court in the print medium’s sense of “proceedings” — but the appellants’ grievance that its publication during the SLP tainted the atmosphere in which bail was sought was recorded by the Court as the “disturbing factor”.

State / complainants

  • Gravity: three charges (304B/406/498A r/w 34) in a dowry-death case where the family alleged years of demands — the High Court had thought “custodial interrogation necessary”; anticipatory protection would allow the accused to shape the record before arrest.
  • The defence documents were afterthoughts; the defence “schizophrenia” narrative was the accused’s own creation; both sides’ papers were to be fought at trial — denial of bail was the safe course.
  • (Media point:) the magazine piece only reported the FIR-family’s account — “reporting a grievance” cannot be contempt; the SLP before the Supreme Court concerned only bail conditions, not guilt.

The excerpt condenses the argument stage — reconstruct these two boxes as above from the Court’s own framing (it answers both), and say so; do not invent counsel’s names beyond what the book prints (none appear).

5 · PRINCIPLE EVOLVED (and its afterlife)

  • P1 — The “sub judice” rule. Where a matter is before a court, publishing “materials that may be used in the forthcoming trial” — one-sidedly, from the complainant’s side — “would certainly interfere with the administration of justice”. Cited by the 200th Report (Ch. IX(7)) for “creating a pressurised atmosphere before the Judge”, and the seed of every postponement order since (Sahara ¶42; Swatanter Kumar ¶59–64; Jindal).
  • P2 — “Trial by media” named and cautioned. The Court’s language — “indulging in such trial by media when the issue is subjudiced” — with the remedy proportioned: deprecate, caution, close; no contempt proceedings, no costs. The forward warning: “the other concerned in journalism would take note of this displeasure”.
  • P3 — Conditional bail as fair-trial engineering. s.438(2) CrPC (s.482(2) BNSS) conditions + non-custodial interrogation with counsel = a template for insulating an investigation from the media circus that “Doomed by Dowry” exemplified; also an answer to “bail = impunity”.
  • P4 — Judicial restraint from merits. “Any expression of opinion on the merits… would likely to affect the trial” — the Court practising on itself the very rule it enforced against the magazine: the bail order is a fair-trial document, written as if published.
  • Afterlife: the caution became doctrine by accretion: Sahara (2012) (postponement power + the six principles), Swatanter Kumar (Del HC 2014) (restraint + 24-hour takedown), Jindal (Del HC 2015) (restraint at the enquiry stage — Part E), the 200th Report’s draft Act (never enacted), and the current practice of courts cautioning anchors and deleting content. For DU, cite the chain in one breath: Lohia → Sahara → Jindal.

Exam pitfalls

  1. Citing “(2005) 3 SCC 90”. The book prints (2005) 2 SCC 686; the 200th Report’s AIR parallel is 2005 SC 790.
  2. Calling it a “contempt conviction” — nothing was initiated; caution was “enough”. Link the s.20 one-year limitation + s.15 AG-consent mechanics only to show why contempt rarely bites the press.
  3. Skipping the bail-conditions half — the PYQ drafting task is built on it (s.438(2) CrPC → s.482(2) BNSS: appearance on notice, no witness-tampering, non-custodial interrogation; model answer, Part G).
  4. Claiming the article was held “defamatory” — it wasn’t adjudicated at all: civil defamation was available (499→BNS 356) but unexercised on this record; the judgment is about interference with justice, not reputation.
  5. Reporting the date confusion as a fact-issue: state the correction (28.10.2003) and keep the analysis on the law.

Real-example extension — the same anatomy since 2005

Wherever a channel runs the complainant’s affidavit, the accused’s “recovery”, and the anchor’s verdict “while the case is in court”, the Lohia triad applies: sub judice + one-sided + trial-material — see the Part F boxes on Swatanter Kumar (ex-Judge sex-allegation headlines; 24-hour takedown ordered) and Jindal (rape-complaint talk-show during preliminary enquiry; injunction granted). Cite those + the Report; do not invent “new Supreme Court rulings on media trials” from memory — the safe contemporary law is: Lohia’s caution, Sahara’s test, Jindal’s stage-extension, and the self-regulatory codes.

PART E · CASE 15 — NAVEEN JINDAL & ANR. v. ZEE MEDIA CORPORATION LTD. & ORS.

CS(OS) 143/2015 & connected matters · Delhi High Court · Jayant Nath, J. · restraint order 30.01.2015 (as printed) · the laboratory for pre-trial publicity, the anchor question, and the injunction-as-restrictive-order — DU Case Book pp. 120–134.

One-lineThe court restrained Zee Media from publishing/telecasting the complainant “ABC”’s allegations against the plaintiffs “until the police complete the preliminary enquiry and file their report”, because 22 hours of bulletins in seven days — including an escalating chain of questions to the DGP/Home Minister/CM — was “a prima facie attempt to prod the police if not pressurize”, while leaving the media free to report the proceedings as they travel and the police’s final conclusion.
DoctrinePre-Sahara tools (injunction, balance of convenience) welded to Sahara’s postponement principles and Swatanter Kumar’s restraint order — a relief that expires by design.
The line to quoteThe “distinction between trial by media and informative media” (Sidhartha Vashisht ¶153(11), as quoted in the excerpt) — invasion of a suspect’s rights “bound to be held impermissible”.

1 · FACTS (pp. 120–124 — chronology is the question)

  • Plaintiffs: Mr Naveen Jindal and his wife (with group entities) vs Zee Media Corporation, its channel(s), the programme, its anchor and the persons behind it.
  • The feud backdrop: (i) FIR No. 240/2012 against Zee persons u/ss 384/511/120-B IPC (extortion of ₹50 crore alleged; now BNS 308/62/61) and Zee’s ₹200-crore defamation-and-recovery litigation against the Jindals; (ii) the coal-gate reply-order of 30.11.2012 and the reply-direction of 01.04.2014 (CS(OS) 881/2014) in which this very court — the same judge — had refused to restrain Zee and instead ordered a reply on-channel (the “right of reply” case, Unit 2.2); (iii) FIR No. 12/2013 u/ss 466/468/469/471 r/w 120-B IPC (now BNS 337/336(3)/336(4)/340 r/w 61) re forged documents used on air; (iv) a criminal complaint u/ss 500/501/506 IPC (now BNS 356(2)/(3)/351) pending at Patiala House.
  • The “ABC” complaint: a married woman, pseudonym ABC, filed W.P.(T) 235/2014 before the Chhattisgarh High Court alleging that in 2001 the first plaintiff raped her and in 2010 assaulted her in connection with land transactions of Jindal Steel & Power Ltd at Raigarh. On 19.12.2014 that court restrained all media from reporting her petition; on 06.01.2015 it disposed of the petition directing the police to act in accordance with Lalita Kumari v. Govt. of U.P. (2014) 2 SCC 1 — i.e., register the FIR if the information disclosed a cognizable offence.
  • The telecasts: between 07.01.2015 and 15.01.2015 the Zee stable ran, on plaintiffs’ count, more than 20 prime-time programmes — an average of eight minutes a day per channel, “in all 22 hours on seven days” — with “ABC” on phone/video (blurred face, distorted voice) rehearsing the allegations; the anchor’s signature device being a public chain of questions addressed to the SHO, SP, IG, DGP, Secretary (Home), Union Home Minister and the Chief Minister — “why has the FIR not been registered…?” — the case book records “it was shown that the police were being directed to register the FIR”; the police did register it during the hearing (u/ss 376 etc. IPC — now BNS 63/64). The channels also aired a “highly edited” extract said to be from the plaintiffs’ counsel.

2 · ISSUES (as framed by the Court)

  1. Is there power at all to restrain publication before anything is “pending” in the contempt sense — is pre-publication restraint res integra in India after Rajagopal, Khushwant and Tata Sons?
  2. If yes, does the power reach the preliminary-enquiry stage (no FIR, no chargesheet), and on what standard?
  3. On the facts — volume, one-sidedness, “prodding” of the police, a pseudonymous complainant — does the balance lie with the plaintiff’s Art. 21 or the defendant’s Art. 19(1)(a)?

3 · ARGUMENTS

Plaintiffs (Jindal)

  • Dignity & privacy (Art. 21): the “most damaging” allegation — rape — aired by a broadcaster with no independent verification, while a High Court had already ordered the matter unreported; a media trial to “browbeat the police” into action; motive: the vendetta chronology (extortion FIR; forgery FIR; reply orders Zee had flouted).
  • Presumption of innocence: Sidhartha Vashisht ¶148–153 (quoted in the excerpt) — parallel media investigations “impermissible”; “distinction between trial by media and informative media”.
  • Investigation secrecy: Deepti Devasthali — “Right to information is wrongly interpreted by the police as right to inform… High degree of secrecy is a must when the investigation is in process”; and “talk shows… pronouncing their verdict” amount to criminal contempt — Kerala HC (M.S. Ravi).
  • Contempt-adjacent authorities: M.P. Lohia’s deprecation of sub-judice trial by media; Swatanter Kumar ¶59–64 (this court’s own restraint of “sex taint” headlines — “trial by media kind of situation… real and tangible risk of the plaintiff in not getting fair trial or open justice… as per Sahara India”).
  • Where the “justification” reflex is satisfied by pleading alone, it becomes a device to delay while the allegation spreads: Fraser v. Evans requires that the plea of justification will probably succeed — and the case-book’s Tata Sons recital of Bonnard counsels only that courts deal “most cautiously and warily” with pre-trial injunctions: wariness is caution, not licence.

Zee Media

  • Art. 19(1)(a) and the public’s right to know: a sexual-violence complaint against a public figure is core speech; anonymisation + “alleged” disclaimers = responsibility; courts are not censors — Rajagopal; Khushwant Singh v. Maneka Gandhi (no injunction against Maneka’s memoir series despite the wife’s privacy); Tata Sons v. Greenpeace (no interim restraint where truth is to be justified — Bonnard v. Perryman / Fraser v. Evans quoted in the excerpt).
  • Nothing sub judice: the Chhattisgarh petition was disposed of; the police were only beginning — the Contempt Act’s s.3(2) makes pre-pendency publication non-contempt; so the suit sought what contempt could not.
  • Public figures get less: plaintiffs are electorally and commercially exposed; their own 2014 “reply” strategy proved courts prefer more speech, not restraint — the 01.04.2014 order refused blanket restraint in Jindal v. Zee and gave a reply; identical facts here.
  • Fair reporting: the complaint is a High Court record; ABC has the benefit of s.228A→BNS s.72 identity protection, which was honoured — “the other side” (plaintiffs) had no such immunity.

4 · DECISION — the five moves (¶¶19–30; ¶¶45–50 as printed)

  1. Power exists (¶19–22): the “no prior restraint” line is the ordinary rule, not an absolute: courts have “been passing orders of restraint in the domain of media” — and where “the ends of justice” require, a pre-publication injunction can issue; the 01.04.2014 reply-order distinguished on facts (¶¶31–38).
  2. The principles imported from criminal-restraint law (¶¶23–26): Sahara ¶42–43 quoted in full — necessity; proportionality; “real and substantial risk of prejudice to fairness of the trial or to the proper administration of justice”; “limited duration”; “without disturbing the content of the publication”; reasoned order. Mirajkar (nine judges): inherent power to order in camera “when the ends of justice… require” — because “a fair trial” can be destroyed by publicity itself. Reliance Petrochemicals: test is “real and imminent danger”, not a remote tendency. Swatanter Kumar ¶64 restraint order (headlines connecting accused with allegations barred; offending internet content “shall be deleted within 24 hours”; the order bound “any other person, entity, in print or electronic media or internet”). Kartongen (Kerala): “ostracized, humiliated and convicted without trial” — “the media must confine itself to the function of reporting and commenting — not of trying”; the court adds, as the Report had, that “the Daler Mehndi… talk-shows pronouncing verdict” instance and “interference with police investigation… amount to interference with administration of justice”.
  3. Stage is no bar (¶30): the jurisdiction “extends to restraining publication even at the stage of a preliminary enquiry” — publicity at the enquiry stage may “prejudice the fairness of the investigation” exactly as publicity at the trial would prejudice the trial: witnesses get coached, the police are “prodded” and the accused is convicted in the court of public opinion “before he is tried.”
  4. Balance on the facts (¶¶45–48): the anchor’s ladder of questions (SHO→DGP→Home Secy→Home Min→CM) and the “highly edited” counsel-soundbite demonstrated — prima facie — “an attempt to prod the police if not pressurize them”; ABC’s allegations were “unverified”; damages inadequate; irreversibility of a rape allegation broadcast at such scale; “truth is a matter for trial — the media cannot be the judge in its own cause”, so Zee’s justification plea did not tilt the balance.
  5. The order (¶¶49–50): interim injunction restraining the defendants from publishing/telecasting the allegations of “ABC”, “or any part of the complaint”, until the completion of the preliminary enquiry/report; media liberty preserved in the order itself: they may report the proceedings “as they proceed in a court of law” and the final conclusion of the police on “true, correct and verified information”, in a “fair” manner — the s.4 Contempt Act safe-harbour restated.

5 · PRINCIPLE EVOLVED

  • P1 — Restraint without pendency: the protection of a fair investigation is a legally cognisable interest independently enforceable by injunction; the Contempt Act’s “pending” trigger is a penal limitation, not the boundary of equity.
  • P2 — The postponement model arrives in a suit: Sahara’s criminal-restraint principles (duration-limited, content-preserving, reasoned, necessary) govern media injunctions; the order self-terminates at the police report — “delay, not suppression”.
  • P3 — Format is evidence: the anchor’s address-to-authorities escalation and volume (22 h/7 d) are themselves indicia of intent to influence state conduct — the first decision to treat news-debate choreography as a factor in the balance.
  • P4 — Access remedies compared: same court, same parties, two different instruments — 2014 reply direction (more speech) vs 2015 restraint (later speech): “access to media” (Unit 2.3 limb) and “restrictive orders” (Unit 2.4 limb) on one page. Excellent comparative table for the examiner.
  • P5 — The closing line: “the distinction between trial by media and informative media” (Sidhartha Vashisht ¶153(11) via Kartongen-era citations printed in the excerpt) — the sentence that ends any 2.4 answer.

DEBATE & status — say it, don’t hide it

  1. “Prior restraint through the back door”? After Rajagopal, courts “cannot restrain publication” even of a private memoir; yet here the court did restrain — the defence: postponement is a timing order with automatic expiry, not a suppression of the story; critics: functionally identical for a news channel — the story dies with the cycle.
  2. Victim-protection flipped? The complainant “ABC” — pseudonymous, blurred, voice-distorted — was the beneficiary of the very law (s.228A→BNS s.72) meant to let sexual-violence complainants be heard safely; restraining her voice to protect the accused’s fairness is a genuine tension. The order answers: she remains free before the police/court; what Zee cannot do is run her case as its own.
  3. Status: interlocutory and time-bound; not elevated to an SCC ratio — cite it (as this syllabus itself does) as the leading illustration of the postponement technique, not as settled doctrine that “courts restrain enquiries”.

F4 · The case book’s other trial-by-media decisions (siblings — the answer gets richer with each)

(i) Swatanter Kumar v. The Indian Express Ltd. & Ors. — Delhi High Court (Jayant Nath, J.), CS(OS) 102/2014 (order 2014); case book pp. 63–73 area

A former Supreme Court judge sued newspapers/channels over front-page “sex taint” headlines with his photograph while a police complaint (the “call-girl” matter) was at the enquiry stage. The court found a “trial by media kind of situation” — “continuous adverse publicity of the persons who are seen from the eyes of public confidence and faith is destructive of their reputation as well as of the public good in the form of the loss of confidence in the institution itself… creating an atmosphere… wherein a person may not be able to put forward his defence properly and his likelihood of getting fair trial would be seriously impaired” — and, applying Sahara India, restrained the headlines/articles (¶64(a)), ordered deletion of offending internet content “within 24 hours” (¶64(b)), barred re-publication of his photograph to suggest involvement (¶64(c)), and bound “any other person, entity, in print or electronic media or internet”. Why it matters for 2.4: the first Indian order with a takedown clause + erga omnes effect, granted before any “pending” proceeding — the bridge between Lohia (caution) and Jindal (stage-extension).

(ii) R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court (2009) 8 SCC 106 — case book pp. 86–100

  • ¶173: stings vs sub-judice coverage: “this is… not to say that media is free to publish any kind of report concerning a sub-judice matter… The legal parameter within which a report or comment on a sub-judice matter can be made is well defined and any action in breach of the legal bounds would invite consequences. Compared to normal reporting, a sting operation is an incalculably more risky and dangerous thing to do… it would attract the legal restrictions with far greater stringency.”
  • ¶174: the definition of “trial by media” (quoted in C1); ¶175: applied — the NDTV sting “cannot be described as a piece of trial by media” because “there was nothing in the programme to suggest that the accused in the BMW case were guilty or innocent.”
  • ¶176–180: s.3 analysis — telecast when the channel “fully knew that the BMW trial was going on”; immunity under s.3(1) unavailable if it interfered; then the merits: “Whichever way we look at the programme we are not able to come to that conclusion… The programme… was thus clearly intended to prevent the attempt to interfere with or obstruct the due course of the BMW trial”; “the sting… served an important public cause” (¶179); excesses noted (¶181: “statements and remarks which are actually incorrect and misleading”; the “Who Wants to Be a Jihadi?”-type anchors criticised elsewhere in the judgment).
  • ¶194–198: appreciation of the “professional initiative and courage” of the reporter; media must be reformed “from inside”; ¶198 “Any attempt to control and regulate the media from outside is likely to cause more harm than good.”

(iii) Court On Its Own Motion v. State (Uma Khurana sting) — Delhi High Court (Mukundakam Sharma & S. Khanna, JJ.), 146 (2008) DLT 429 — case book pp. 108–117

The channel caught the wrong woman: the “student” in Live India’s sting “was neither a school girl nor a prostitute but a budding journalist eager to make a name in the media world” (¶2); the mob, the tearing of the teacher’s clothes, the suspension, then the exoneration — the case book’s proof that trial by media’s first casualty is the truth. The twelve-point code (¶¶18–20 area) now governs Delhi-HC-adjacent practice; its clause 8 reads (verbatim in the book): subject matter of reports “shall not: (a) Deliberately present as true any unverified or inaccurate facts so as to avoid trial by media since ‘a man is innocent till proven guilty by law’; (b) present facts and views… likely to mislead…; (c) mislead the public by mixing facts and fiction…; (d) present a distorted picture of reality by over-emphasizing or under-playing certain aspects that may trivialise or sensationlise the content; (e) make public any activities or material relating to an individual’s personal or private affairs or which invades an individual’s privacy unless there is an identifiable large public interest; (f) create public panic or unnecessary alarm…”, clause 10 (verification “to the fullest extent possible” and “presentation of all facts that are necessary to understand” — the accuracy canon), clauses 11–12 (privacy responsibility; no use of personal/private material without “identifiable larger public interest”), and ¶13’s damages line: “physical hardship gone through by her. Damages in accordance with the law can be awarded by Courts in cases of defamation and loss of reputation…” — the civil remedy leg of C6. Close the case book’s sting consultation-paper (MIB, 2008) with the Report: the government considered legislating stings; both files show reform energy peaked in 2006–2008 and cooled after Shreya’s era arrived.

Q6 (verbatim) — Mrs. K’s “Doomed by Dowry” article: fair-trial protection for Mr. Y + draft appeal against refusal of anticipatory bail
A Media Reporter Mrs. K has written a vicious Article titled “Doomed by Dowry” published in a leading magazine when an application of Anticipatory bail of husband Mr. Y was pending to high court, The only version of deceased wife’s family was published in article, the husband’s right of fair trial need to be protected against the vicious media trial, help Mr. Y to analyse their rights and provide legal aid to draft an appeal if High Court has rejected bail of Mr. Y. 20

Step 0 — Read the question the way the case book does

The hypothetical is M.P. Lohia v. State of West Bengal (Case 14; Case Book pp. 118–119) with the sexes/names genericised: a dowry-death charge-sheet world (wife’s family vs husband), an anticipatory-bail application pending before the High Court, and a magazine article publishing only the deceased’s family’s version. Write the answer as (I) rights analysis of “their rights” (Mr Y’s and the family’s), (II) the article’s legal character, (III) Mr Y’s menu of remedies, (IV) the drafted appeal. Cite the statute in both codes throughout.

I. Mr. Y’s rights at the bail/“trial by media” stage

  • Presumption of innocence & fair trial (Art. 21): guilt “has to be proved beyond reasonable doubt” and the accused “is presumed innocent unless the contrary is proved in public, in a court of law” (200th Report Ch. II; ICCPR Art. 14(2); UDHR Art. 11). The offences charged — IPC 304-B → BNS s.80 (dowry death), 406 → s.316 (criminal breach of trust in stridhan), 498-A → ss.85/86 (cruelty), all r/w s.34 → BNS s.3(5) — carry a statutory presumption (former Ev. Act s.113-B, now BSA’s presumptions chapter) precisely because the trial is where they must be rebutted — not the newsstand.
  • Fair investigation: Sahara’s “presumption of innocence… to be preserved at all stages up to trial, and no person is to be prejudged as guilty” (reproduced at Jindal ¶23 area, Case Book p. 124–125; Sidhartha Vashisht ¶153(11): “the distinction between trial by media and informative media”).
  • Reputation and privacy (Art. 21): “Reputation is an intrinsic part of the right to life” (Subramanian Swamy (2016) 10 SCC 227); even an accused retains rights over his private life — the “right to be let alone” (Brandeis J in Olmstead, quoted by the Delhi High Court at CPIO ¶64).
  • Dignity of the accused family/defence version: the complainant family too has rights — to be heard, to protection as victims — but the exclusive right to determine guilt vests in the court; both sides’ “documents” await trial (Lohia: “it will not be safe to place reliance on any one of these documents at this stage”).

II. The article’s legal character — vicious, one-sided, sub judice

  • Not protected reporting: Contempt Act s.4 immunises only a “fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding”; a feature “extensively quoting the father of the deceased as to his version” while bail was pending is neither fair nor a report of proceedings (Lohia; Deepti Devasthali, via Jindal: one-sided “in-depth” coverage condemned; Kerala talk-show line: verdicts on screen = contempt territory).
  • Criminal contempt made out on the face: publication “tending to interfere with the due course of” the bail proceeding and the trial — s.2(c)(ii)–(iii); the s.3(1) “no reasonable grounds for believing pendency” defence fails: Mrs K knew the bail application was before the High Court. Remedy per Lohia: the Court may merely “deprecate… caution the publisher, editor and the journalist… against indulging in such trial by media when the issue is sub judice” and “treat this matter as closed” — proportionality before punishment.
  • Defamation: imputing a dowry death with the headline “Doomed by Dowry” refers to Y: IPC 499 → BNS s.356(1) (offence), punishment IPC 500 → s.356(2); printing/publishing defamatory matter 501 → within s.356; civil damages independent. Defences: justification (truth) — not yet provable; fair comment/public good — doubtful for a sensationalist one-sided piece (Rajagopal line; Khushwant not on the article’s side because no court record was being reported). Intimidation (506→351) if Y is threatened by the campaign.
  • What the article is NOT: not within s.228A IPC / BNS s.72 (the “victim” identity bar covers sexual-offence victims — a dowry-death complainant does not fit), so Y’s counsel should not over-plead that head; not “prior restraint material” by itself — Lohia shows the Court refusing to proceed against the press; and not a “public record” case — the family’s version is not a public document (Rajagopal ¶26(4) caution).

III. The protective menu Y can seek (with authorities)

  1. Postponement/restraint: application to the High Court (or, with the appeal, to the Supreme Court) under inherent powers — governed by Sahara’s six principles (necessity; proportionality; real and substantial risk; limited duration; content preserved; reasoned) and granted as in Swatanter Kumar ¶64 (a–c: no headline linking him to allegations; 24-hour internet deletion; no photographs) and Jindal (restraint survives even at the pre-FIR stage where publicity would “prod the police if not pressurize”).
  2. Against police-side leaks: seek a direction that the case diary/FIR not be briefed to TV (CPIO/RTI analogy: s.8(1)(h) RTI — impeding investigation; Deepti Devasthali “right to inform” line; Report Ch. IX(5) — no recovery-show).
  3. Conditions in bail itself: non-custodial interrogation, counsel present, no media appearances — the Lohia order’s architecture (s.438(2) CrPC → s.482(2) BNSS).
  4. Post-publication: defamation notice/suit; damages (Uma Khurana ¶13: courts “can award damages… in cases of defamation and loss of reputation”); PCI complaint (Norms: fair report, avoid unverified trial material; Uma Khurana cl. 8(a), 10); NBSA complaint against any TV carriage of the same allegations; right of reply (Y’s version on the same page — the Jindal 01.04.2014 reply-order is the precedent).

IV. Legal aid — the draft appeal (as the question asks)

DRAFT: SPECIAL LEAVE PETITION (CRIMINAL) No. ____ of 20__ — IN THE SUPREME COURT OF INDIA — Mr. Y … Petitioner (Accused) v. State (NCT of Delhi/NCT…) & Ors. … Respondents — against the impugned order of the Hon’ble High Court dated ___ rejecting the petitioner’s application under Section 438 CrPC (now Section 482 BNSS, 2023) for anticipatory bail in FIR No. ___ u/ss 304-B, 406, 498-A r/w 34 IPC (corresponding offences: Sections 80, 316, 85/86 r/w 3(5) BNS, 2023).
SUBMISSIONS: 1. That the petition arises at the threshold of criminal process; no merits are to be adjudicated (as M.P. That the triple test (nature/gravity of accusation; risk of tampering with prosecution witnesses; risk of absconding — Sanjay Chandra v. Q8(C) (verbatim) — “Right to Information Act — an instrument to ensure good governance” — full model note (10 marks)
Write brief notes on any two (10 marks each): … (C) Right to Information Act—an instrument to ensure good governance.

1. Thesis

Good governance rests on transparency + accountability + participation; the RTI Act, 2005 converts all three from constitutional aspiration (Art. 19(1)(a) right to know) into a procedure with teeth. The case book’s own authority frames it best — the Delhi Full Bench’s “powerful beacon, which illuminates unlit corners of state activity… abolishes the concept of locus standi… reasons… cannot be sought” (CPIO v. Subhash Chandra Aggarwal ¶29, Case Book p. 107), and ADR’s “few rays of light… to cure cancerous growth of corruption” (quoted at ¶17).

2. The instruments of good governance (map each to its section)

  • Universal, cheap, reason-free access — ss.3, 6(1)–(2), 6(3) (5-day transfer duty), 7 (30 days; 48 hours for life-liberty; s.11 third-party), ₹10 fee — turns “right to know” into routine practice.
  • Disclosure by design — s.4 suo-motu publication (functions, norms, categories of records, budget, decision-making procedure) + s.4(1)(a) computerisation/networking; “so that the public have minimum resort to the use of this Act” (s.4(2)) — governance becomes legible without asking.
  • Reasoned refusals + reversals — s.8’s closed list of ten exemptions + s.8(2) public-interest override + s.10 severability; “satisfaction recorded in writing” (¶71 of Case 13) → the file itself must argue.
  • Verticals of accountability — first appeal within 30 days (s.19(1)); CIC second appeal with civil-court powers (ss.17–18); burden on the PIO (s.19(5)); ₹250/day penalties up to ₹25,000 + disciplinary recommendation (s.20); silence beyond 30 days is treated as a deemed refusal — appealable under s.19(1) and sanctionable under s.20 — the cheapest compliance-audit India runs daily.
  • Override of the culture of secrecy — s.22 (Act prevails over OSA 1923 and prior rules); s.24+Second Schedule’s carve-out is itself pierced by the 20-day rule for corruption/human-rights allegations — transparency even for the secrets industry.

3. Case-book proof that it works (Case 13 = the exam’s own citation)

CPIO v. S.C. Aggarwal: the highest office is “a public authority” — “even the President of India, being covered by the Act” (¶35); “voluntary”+“confidential” cannot create exemptions (¶16/73); the three-factor test (¶66) disciplines privacy; compliance data is disclosable, contents need larger public interest — accountability and dignity, by procedure. Affirmed by the five-judge Supreme Court on 13.11.2019; the same logic produced disclosure of Collegium-resolutions-era orders (2021 CIC; litigation pending) and the “judges not above, but within, the Act” baseline.

4. Governance outcomes beyond the courthouse (real examples)

Candidates’ affidavits (ADR/PUCL 2002–03) → today’s ECI portal; Electoral Bonds struck down (ADR v. UOI, 6.3.2024) for killing voters’ right to know who pays; PMO answers RTIs, PM CARES fought over s.2(h) (CIC 2021 → Delhi HC 2023 “instrumentality” → SC stay — the definitional frontier keeps offices honest by exposure); RTI-obtained land, PDS and works-register data feeding social-audit practice (MGNREGA’s audit architecture relies on the same open-records principle); CIC penalties in lakhs of rupees imposed on PIOs; s.4 digitisation → e-Office/open-data culture.

5. Limits — what makes the instrument blunt

(i) Non-compliance: s.4’s publication duties are chronically unmet; penalty enforcement thin. (ii) Delay: CIC vacancies/pendency; 30 days becomes 300. (iii) Exemption-maxing: “vague notions of confidentiality” (¶16 rebuke) and the OSA habit persist; 8(1)(a)/(h) reflexive invocations. (iv) Privacy line-drawing remains fact-heavy (the three-factor test itself concedes judges/public servants get calibrated privacy). (v) Structural: 2019 amendment (tenure/salary at Central Government pleasure) raises independence questions; security bodies shielded save for corruption/HR violations; the CIC’s June-2013 declaration of the three national parties as “public authorities” was met by amendments to the RPA that are still litigated in effect — the parties’ status remains contested.

6. Conclusion

RTI does not make governance good — it makes bad governance expensive: every refusal must be reasoned, every exemption pleaded, every file notable by an ordinary citizen for ten rupees. As the Court put it in the case book, the Act “arm[s] citizens with the mechanism to scrutinize government and public processes, and ensure transparency” (¶29) — and, as this unit’s next question (2.4) reminds us, the same openness must be channelled: transparency is a governance instrument only while it is exercised through institutions that can absorb it — courts, PIOs, s.11 notices, appeals. Information without procedure is noise; procedure without information is opacity; the RTI Act is India’s attempt to have both.

1 · Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (as amended up to 2006) — the sections this unit needs

s.2(c) “criminal contempt” means the publication (whether by words, spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representations, or otherwise) of any matter or the doing of any other act whatsoever which — (i) scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; or (ii) prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or (iii) interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner.
s.3(1) no contempt for publication interfering with the course of justice “in connection with any civil or criminal proceeding pending at the time of publication, if at that time he had no reasonable grounds for believing that the proceeding was pending”. s.3(2) publication concerning proceedings “which is not pending” is not contempt (notwithstanding any other law). s.3(3) distributor’s immunity, subject to Press & Registration of Books Act conformity proviso. Explanation: criminal proceeding “pending” (B)(i) when charge-sheet or challan is filed, or summons or warrant issued; (ii) otherwise when the court takes cognizance; pendency continues “until it is heard and finally decided” (incl. appeal/revision period); (b) execution ≠ pending.
s.4 “a person shall not be guilty of contempt… for publishing a fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding or of any stage thereof”. s.5 fair comment on merits of a case “heard and finally decided”. s.6 complaints against subordinate-court presiding officers. s.7 publication of in camera/chambers information — safe only if fair & accurate, except where (a) barred by any enactment, (b) expressly prohibited on public-policy grounds or in exercise of power, (c) the court sat for public order/state security, (d) secret process/discovery/invention; 7(2) orders of chambers/in camera.
s.12(1) punishment: simple imprisonment up to six months, or fine up to ₹2,000, or both; discharge/remission on apology made to the Court’s satisfaction; Explanation: apology not rejected merely for being qualified/conditional if bona fide. s.12(4)–(5) companies & officers. s.13 (as amended 2006): no sentence unless contempt “substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice”; court may permit justification by truth where “in public interest and the request… bona fide”. s.14 in-facie procedure (detain–inform–defend–decide; alternative judge on request). s.15 cognizance: SC/HC suo motu or on motion of Advocate-General/Law Officer or “any other person” with AG consent; subordinate-court contempts by reference. s.17 personal service of notice; s.18 Bench of ≥2 Judges (High Court); s.19 appeal to the Supreme Court from “any order” of a High Court imposing punishment for contempt (the advanced point — whether an appeal also lies against Division-Bench orders — was addressed by a Supreme Court Constitution Bench in 2021 in favour of confining s.19 to Single-Bench contempt orders, leaving SLP/review otherwise; mark it as an optional footnote and verify the case name before viva); s.20 limitation: one year from the date of contempt.
