PART A — UNIT 2.1 · Freedom of Speech & Expression, Reasonable Restrictions, Derivative Rights, Hate Speech

1.1 · Why does the Constitution protect speech? — the four philosophical justifications (with case-book anchors)

The DU case-book begins the unit through Shreya Singhal paras 8–12, which reproduce the philosophy of the First Amendment to argue that our Art. 19(1)(a) must carry at least the same protective spirit. Every long answer on this unit should open with one of these:

① Self-Governance / Democracy

Freedom of speech “lay at the foundation of all democratic organisations” (Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras, cited in Shreya ¶9). Without an informed citizenry “meaningful governance” is impossible (S. Khushboo ¶45, also cited in Shreya). Beg J. in Bennett Coleman: the freedom of speech and of the press is “the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy because public criticism is essential to the working of its institutions” — this exact quotation is preserved in the case-book at Shreya ¶9.

② Marketplace of Ideas

Holmes J.’s dissent in Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616 (1919), reproduced verbatim in the case-book (Shreya ¶11): “the best test of truth is the power of thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market…”. Hence the State may not declare which ideas are true.

③ Autonomy & Self-Fulfilment

Brandeis J. in Whitney v. California (quoted in the case-book at Shreya ¶12): “freedom to think as you will and to speak as you think are means indispensable to the discovery and spread of political truth… the greatest menace to freedom is an inert people… the fitting remedy for evil counsels is good ones.”

④ Tolerance of Unpopular Views

Shreya ¶20 (Justice Jackson, Am. Communications Assn. v. Douds): “Thought control is a copyright of totalitarianism, and we have no claim to it… We could justify any censorship only when the censors are better shielded against error than the censored.”

Du angle

DU examiners love it when you connect the philosophy to the Indian textual choice: unlike the U.S. First Amendment (“Congress shall make no law…” — absolute words, judicially qualified), our framers enumerated the restrictions in Art. 19(2) itself. The SC in Shreya ¶15–18 said this difference, plus India’s diversity and fragility, makes the American “absolute” framing of little direct help — American judgments have “great persuasive value on the content of freedom of speech” but the grounds of restriction are our own.

1.2 · Article 19(1)(a) — text, parties, and the “content” of the right

“All citizens shall have the right — (a) to freedom of speech and expression…”
“Nothing in sub-clause (a)… shall affect the operation of any existing law, or prevent the State from making any law, in so far as such law imposes reasonable restrictions on the exercise of the right… in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or in relation to contempt of court, defamation or incitement to an offence.”— Art. 19(1)(a) & 19(2), Constitution of India (text as it now stands after the 1st & 16th Amendments)
Who holds it?Only citizens (Art. 19). Corporations/companies may claim 19(1)(a) through their members — Bennett Coleman & Shreya both allowed newspaper companies to plead it; non-citizens, foreigners & media “houses” as such cannot (but a news audience that is citizenry is protected: “the right of viewers” — Shreya ¶5, CAB ¶78).
→
What is “speech”?Any expression by any medium: words, writing, print, film, theatre, audio-visual, internet, symbolic acts (picketing), silence (flag case Bijoe Emmanuel), advertising (commercial speech). Shreya ¶24: content “includes the right to propagate and publish one’s views and the right to circulate one’s views”; the medium is legally irrelevant (¶86, citing CAB ¶78 & Reno).
→
Two dimensions(i) Speaker’s right to express & circulate; (ii) Listener’s derivative right — “the right to receive” information/telecast (CAB; Shreya ¶5: “the right of viewers is infringed”).

What the right is not (frequently tested)

  • It confers on the press no special privilege and the press suffers no special disability: “the freedom of the press does not confer on the press any privilege or immunity not available to other citizens” (line of Express Newspapers, Bennett Coleman; SC repeatedly — the media “does not enjoy any right superior to an individual citizen” — R.K. Anand ¶169 recording amicus N. Rao’s accepted position that free & fair trial prevails in conflict).
  • No right to demand information from unwilling persons — Prabha Dutt: the press “has no right to an interview unless the person is willing”; the freedom includes “the right to gather news or information” but subject to regulation.
  • No right to any particular medium — CAB: a citizen may use lawful means but cannot demand spectrum as of right; regulation of access ≠ restriction of speech.
  • Cannot be restricted for “general public interest” — only the eight enumerated grounds (Sakal, quoted in Shreya ¶21). Contrast: business freedom under 19(1)(g) can be curbed for “public interest” (19(6)) — that is precisely what the newspapers argued against the Working Journalists Act in ABP, and failed.

1.3 · Freedom of the Press — content checklist (15 elements)

Write this checklist in any “freedom of press” question; each element has a case behind it. The case-book prints these propositions through Bennett Coleman, ABP and Shreya:

1.5 · Derivative right ②: THE RIGHT TO BROADCAST

Doctrinal core — Secretary, Ministry of I&B v. Cricket Association of Bengal (1995) 2 SCC 161 [dossier]

  • 19(1)(a) “includes the right to telecast; that is to say, the right to broadcast one’s views through any audio-visual/electronic medium… to the viewers”.
  • Correlative right of the viewer: citizens have a right to watch — “the right of the viewers to see… telecasts is the other side of the coin” (the 200th Law Commission Report on Trial by Media, in the project material, summarises CAB thus: “the right of the telecaster… and the right of the viewers” — Report ¶ on pp. 37–38).
  • Airwaves/spectrum are public property: “since the airwaves/frequencies are public property and are also limited, they have to be used in the best interest of the society” (CAB ¶78). The State holds them as trustee; it must establish an independent public authority (the case-book’s words per curiam) to allocate them.
  • No State monopoly as a justification for content control: Doordarshan’s monopoly “cannot be preserved to the exclusion of citizens’ speech rights”; licensing may only be regulated for technical/orderly use — not for silencing content (see Shreya ¶29 quoting CAB ¶37 on regulation of access vs content).

Related statutes & committees — plug these in to show depth

(i) Indian Telegraph Act, 1885, s.4 — exclusive Central Government privilege to lay/establish telegraphs (incl. wireless); licences issued under it were the vehicle for AIR/DD monopoly; CAB read s.4 in light of 19(1)(a).
(ii) Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) Act, 1990 (full text in project material): s.3 — body corporate; s.4(2) objects: “organise and conduct public broadcasting services to inform, educate and entertain the public… ensure a balanced development of broadcasting on radio and television”; s.5 — autonomy in programme matters; s.12 — the Broadcasting Council (President + 10 members of eminence + 4 MPs) to receive public complaints about programme content (ss.13–15) — the statutory public-service answer to “who guards the broadcaster”. Sam Pitroda Expert Committee (2014) (project material) recommended transforming Prasar Bharati into a modern public-service broadcaster with statutory charter of independence, funding from a broadcast development fee, and universal reach — cite in “right to broadcast + public service media” questions.
(iii) Sports Broadcasting Signals (Mandatory Sharing with Prasar Bharati) Act, 2007 + Rules 2009 — the CAB aftermath: every sports event of “national interest” (Olympics, Asian Games, World Cup, India matches, CWG…) must be shared mandatorily with Doordarshan’s terrestrial/DTH network; no exclusivity for private broadcasters; share must be in “clean feed” form. Section 3 & 5 (no blackouts; time-shifting rights). This is the example of “content access right of viewers” legislated.
(iv) Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 (project material): ss. 3 (no transmission without registration), 4 (declaration), 5 (no transmission except through registered cable; DTH conditions) — and the Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994 — Rule 6 (Programme Code), the content code: no programme which “…(xii) contains visuals or words contemptuous of racial, religious or other groups; (xiii) promotes communal, obscurantist, anti-scientific and anti-national attitude; (xiv) calls in question the sovereignty and integrity of India; (xv) jeopardises the security of the State;… (xviii) involves defamation of an individual or a body of individuals, or contempt of court…”; Rule 7 (Advertisement Code); s.16 — offences & punishment; s.21 — power to intercept. The Programme Code is the single most useful statute for both hate-speech and sting-broadcast answers. [Status note: the Cable TV Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 has been replaced by the Broadcasting Act, 2023, with programme/advertisement content codes carried into the 2024 Rules — mention this as the current legislative position.]
(v) Cinematograph Act, 1952 (project material): s.5A (no public exhibition of uncertified films); s.5B(2) Guiding Principles mirroring the 19(2) grounds (decency, defamation, contempt, public order); s.5C (Tribunals); s.6 (revision); ss.7–8 (offences) — the pre-censorship regime examined in Unit 6 of the case-book; note its 19(2)-relationship per K.A. Abbas (1971), Odyssey (2002) & Rangarajan (1992): pre-censorship of films justified by “special features” of cinema but not available for press/internet (see Shreya ¶27, ASG’s 11-point comparison accepted only as differentia under Art. 14, not as a licence to dilute 19(2)).

1.7 · The Supreme Court’s adjudication tests (the “engine” of 19(2))

“The phrase ‘reasonable restriction’ connotes that the limitation imposed on a person in enjoyment of the right should not be arbitrary or of an excessive nature, beyond what is required in the interests of the public. The word ‘reasonable’ implies intelligent care and deliberation…”— Chintaman Rao v. State of M.P. (1950 SCR 759), reproduced at Shreya ¶23 (Case Book p. 30)
“One has to imagine three concentric circles: law and order represents the largest circle within which is the next circle representing public order and the smallest circle represents security of State.”— Dr. Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar (1966) 1 SCR 709, cited in Shreya ¶33
LAW & ORDER — 19(2) does NOT protect “law and order” per se; ordinary public mischief (IPC 505 → BNS 353)
PUBLIC ORDER — Romesh Thappar; Brij Bhushan; Lohia; 153A→BNS 196
SECURITY OF STATE
sedition-era Art.
124A→BNS 152 · UAPA

An act may disturb law and order yet not public order; may disturb public order yet not threaten security of the State. Only the inner circles can justify bans on speech (Shreya ¶¶33–35).

Vagueness & over-breadth — the American tools India adopted in Shreya

  • Void for vagueness: “laws should give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited… may trap the innocent… impermissibly delegate basic policy matters to policemen and judges” — Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab ¶130, quoted at Shreya ¶68. Compare UK DPP v. Collins and Chambers v. DPP (the “blow up the airport” tweet) which the case-book reproduces at length (¶¶79–82) to show even a trained judge cannot stabilise “grossly offensive/menacing”.
  • Over-breadth: a law is bad if it “takes within its sweep protected speech and innocent speech” even if some of its applications would be valid (Shreya ¶90). Consequence in India: severability is rarely a rescue for content restrictions on speech — Romesh Thappar and K.A. Abbas (¶94–95 of Shreya): a provision capable of both constitutional and unconstitutional application “must be held wholly unconstitutional” (unless it is severable in the precise sense shown by Scrutton LJ’s “blue pencil” rule reproduced at pp. 21 ff. of the case-book).
  • Chilling effect: “law should not be used in a manner that has chilling effects on freedom of speech” — S. Khushboo ¶47 (Shreya ¶85); self-censorship argument first invented in N.Y.T. v. Sullivan and imported via Rajagopal ¶19.

Common error to avoid

Never say “Article 19(2) allows restrictions on grounds of defamation etc. absolutely.” Two-stage: (1) the law must fall within a head; (2) it must also be “reasonable”. A restriction outside 19(2) cannot be saved by being reasonable, and a restriction inside 19(2) can fail the reasonableness test. This two-stage structure is exactly how Shreya killed s.66A (¶¶19–21, 90–95).

1.9 · HATE SPEECH — a full sub-unit (3 lectures in the syllabus; asked in DU Dec-2024 Q.2)

(a) What is “hate speech”? — the definitional problem

There is no legal definition of hate speech in Indian law — that is itself a headline point. The most accepted working definition is the Law Commission’s (267th Report, March 2018): “hate speech generally is an expression that… causes or propagates hatred, and oftentimes violence, against a specific group of people… incitement to hatred primarily against a group of persons defined in terms of race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, religious belief and the like”. The Supreme Court in Amish Devgan v. UOI (2021) 5 SCC 542 refused to freeze a single definition, preferring an objective, context-sensitive test (below).

  • Distinction drawn by the Court (Amish Devgan, paras 54–55): “free speech” includes the right to comment, favour or criticise government policy — hate speech “creates or spreads hatred against a targeted community or group”, i.e. “hate which tends to vilify, humiliate and incite hatred or violence against the target group based upon identity of the group”.
  • Constitutional location: hate speech is regulated through the heads of Art. 19(2) — chiefly public order, decency or morality, defamation, incitement to an offence; there is no free-standing “hate-speech clause”. (See Shreya ¶21: the State cannot restrict speech for general “public interest” — it must fasten the restriction to an enumerated head; this is precisely the drafting difficulty the Law Commission flagged in 2018.)

(c) The judicial tests — three competing lines

1. Incitement lineKedar Nath (1962): penal action only where words have the “pernicious tendency or intention of creating public disorder”; Arup Bhuyan v. State of A.P. (2011): mere advocacy/incitement to disorder is not a crime; Brandenburg’s “imminent lawless action” accepted in principle (both restated in Shreya ¶39 and Amish Devgan). Shreya ¶40: s.66A failed because it punished mere discussion without “an imminent causal connection”.
⇄
2. “Tendency” strandRamji Lal Modi (1957): “in the interests of” is wider than “for the purpose of” — a “mere tendency” may suffice where the mischief is grave; V.G. Row (cited in Shreya) — contextual, “probable” harm. India thus sits between U.S. imminence and the old English “bad tendency”, per Shreya ¶¶36–38.
⇄
3. Amish Devgan indicia (2021)Objective, context-sensitive multi-factor test (paras 56–59 as widely digested): (a) content of the message — expresses hatred/hostility; (b) the speech must be capable of causing, and does cause, harm (violence, incitement, “deep psychosocial harm” of stigmatisation); (c) intent of the speaker — deliberate, malicious, or at least knowledge of likely effects (good-faith enquiry protected); (d) context of the speech — time, place, audience, platform (“home speeches” amplified online can themselves be hate speech); (e) at least two groups involved — one community’s feelings alone not enough; (f) standard of the reasonable, strong-minded person (“top of the reasonable member of the public”, not hypersensitive listeners); (g) no redeeming purpose — speech that “carries no meaning other than hatred towards a particular group”. Persons of influence “owe a duty and have to be more responsible”.

(d) “Supreme Court guidelines aimed at curbing hate speech” — the Dec-2024 exam question, answered

There is no single codified set; the Court has produced directions across a line of cases. A top-band answer narrates all five clusters:

  1. Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. UOI, (2014) 6 SCC 473: the Court noted “the menace of hate speech”, declined itself to legislate guidelines, observed that existing penal provisions are sufficient to prosecute hate speech, and referred the whole subject to the Law Commission to consider a new framework — the referral that produced the 267th Report (2018).
  2. Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. UOI, (2018) 2 SCC 501 (mob violence triggered by hate speech/rumour): comprehensive preventive-mechanism directions — designation of a senior police officer in every district for (i) timely preventive measures against hate speeches, (ii) prompt FIRs and speedy investigation; fast-track courts in every district for such offences; videography of trials; victim/witness protection & compensation schemes; social-media monitoring cells; recommendation to Parliament to enact a stand-alone anti-lynching law with hate speech as an aggravating factor.
  3. Amish Devgan v. UOI, (2021) 5 SCC 542 (Sanjiv Khanna & Khanwilkar, JJ.): (i) FIR must be registered on a complaint disclosing cognizable hate-speech offences (s.154 CrPC/BNSS 173) — no “triviality” filter at that stage; (ii) where the same speech spawns multiple FIRs in several States, only the first FIR survives — the rest are clubbed/transferred to the court of the first FIR and treated as statements under s.161 CrPC/BNSS 180, so that one accused faces one consolidated trial (T.T. Antony applied); (iii) quashing petitions must be heard promptly and ordinarily only after investigation, since “the speech has to be judged in context”; (iv) strong speech by politicians/public figures attracts higher responsibility, but political/policy criticism is never hate speech; (v) the test: objective, reasonable-person, content–intent–harm–context (the seven indicia above).
  4. Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. UOI (2021–22) and the “Shaheen Abdullah” PIL: the Court pressed the Union and States to act — States to take suo motu, prompt action against hate speeches irrespective of the community of the speaker, with the police social-media cells monitoring viral hate content; the ECI told the Court that in the absence of a standalone law it deploys IPC + RPA provisions and Model Code of Conduct during elections; the Court declined to frame a parallel code, emphasising even-handed enforcement (“hate speech is complete poison… the role of TV anchors is critical” — per observations reported in these hearings).
  5. General-elections directions (March 2024): hearing a pending PIL during the 2024 Lok Sabha elections, the Supreme Court (CJI-led Bench) directed that hate-speech content in electioneering — including social media — must be acted upon strictly and within time: the ECI and States were pressed to register FIRs suo motu against politicians for Model-Code/IPC violations, with the Model Code of Conduct para 2 prohibition (“no activity aggravating existing differences or creating mutual hatred or tension between castes and communities”) read with BNS 196/197 and RPA 123(3A).

The reform track — Law Commission 267th Report (2018): recommended stand-alone offences — new s.153C (incitement to hatred) targeting threats/abuse inciting fear, hostility or violence on grounds of religion, race, caste, community, gender, sexual orientation etc., and new s.505A (causing fear, alarm or provocation of violence), together with tweaks to CrPC 95/196; it rejected a general “media hate-speech statute”. Conclusion to write: “the Supreme Court’s guidelines operate at the procedural level — FIR discipline, clubbing, speedy trial, preventive policing — while substantive criminalisation remains tied to 153A/295A/505 (now BNS 196/299/353); the Shreya lesson is that hate-speech law survives constitutional scrutiny because of its intent-and-tendency thresholds plus procedural safeguards, which s.66A lacked.”

Real examples to sprinkle

(i) The origin of s.66A’s fall — arrests in 2012 for Facebook posts on the Maharashtra bandh (and the cartoonist who mocked politicians) — the case-book reproduces the chilling-effect statistics narrative at Shreya ¶¶9, 83–92. (ii) Amish Devgan itself: live TV slurs naming a revered Sufi saint → seven FIRs in multiple States → SC: such speech “defames, stigmatises and insults the target group provoking violence or psychosocial hatred” — not protected; but the speech was to be judged “as a whole, not by picking select words”. (iii) Devidas Tuljapurkar v. State of Maharashtra (2015) 8 SCC 80 — ss.153A/295A invoked against a poem on a goddess; the Supreme Court quashed: literary work judged by contemporary community standards with deliberate-malice required — “a poet’s metaphors are not hate speech”. (iv) During the 2024 general elections, the ECI, pressed by the Supreme Court, asked several States to register FIRs against candidates for caste/religion-based appeals — an on-the-ground application of the Tehseen Poonawalla/Amish Devgan architecture.

Case Dossier ① · Unit 2.1 · Syllabus Case 1 · AIR 1973 SC 106

Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India Newspaper (Price & Circulation) Act — “the circulation case”

Citation
AIR 1973 SC 106; (1972) 2 SCC 734 · decided 30.10.1972
Bench
Five-Judge Bench: S.M. Sikri CJI, A.N. Ray, P. Jaganmohan Reddy, K.K. Mathew, M.H. Beg JJ. — majority opinion by Ray J. (with Sikri CJI & Jaganmohan Reddy J.); Beg J. concurring separately; Mathew J. dissenting on the newsprint policy (4:1)
Statutes
Newspaper (Price and Circulation) Act, 1956 (as amended 1962); Newsprint Control Order, 1962; Government’s Newsprint Policy for 1972–73; Import (Control) Order, 1955
Facts

To “break the stranglehold of the big newspapers”, the 1956/62 Act made it unlawful (i) to increase price or number of pages without the Controller’s permission; (ii) for a company publishing two or more newspapers (one a daily) to start a new title/edition; (iii) treated “special newspapers” (≥75% newsprint used for a language other than English or Hindi, or of scientific/technical character) separately; and empowered the government to redistribute the circulation of “too large” newspapers and allot quotas of additional circulation. The executive’s Newsprint Policy 1972–73 then fixed import quotas embodying the same page and circulation caps (10 pages; no adjustment between size and circulation; no new entrants under 75,000 circulation). The Times of India, the Indian Express and other petitioners — whose allotted quotas froze their growth despite spare capacity — challenged the Act’s impugned sections and the Policy under Arts. 14, 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g). (The case-book’s Unit-2.1 index assigns this case to the unit; the DU excerpt set reprints the later Sakal Papers-line jurisprudence inside the ABP and Shreya excerpts, which is where the quotations below are sourced.)

Issues
  1. Whether Art. 19(1)(a) extends to the press — publication, circulation, number of pages, advertising — and whether a corporate newspaper may plead it.
  2. Whether the Act/Policy restricted speech on grounds not enumerated in Art. 19(2) (“public interest”, “equalisation” being grounds under 19(6) for trade only).
  3. Whether indirect restrictions (newsprint, price, page caps) amount to direct curbs of 19(1)(a).
  4. Whether the classification (special vs common; “too large” papers) violated Art. 14, and whether unguided licensing power was excessive delegation/prior restraint.
  5. Whether scarcity of newsprint justified rationing the pages of newspapers.
Arguments — Petitioners

Freedom of the press is 19(1)(a) itself, exercised through a medium; no “existing law” can be saved if its object is equalisation rather than a 19(2) head; a restriction on one freedom (19(1)(g)) cannot be justified by borrowing another clause’s grounds (the Sakal doctrine); advertising pages and circulation are the “oxygen” of speech; unguided permission/quotas = classic prior restraint; “too large” is a penal description of success; the Act discriminates between languages and types of papers without intelligible differentia.

Arguments — Union of India

Newspapers are business corporations; the press has no special immunity; newsprint allocation is economic regulation of a scarce import under the Imports & Exports Control Act, not a speech restriction; concentration of media power itself impairs other citizens’ speech access; the Act enlarges overall diversity and therefore serves, not abridges, freedom; during the Emergency the challenge to executive policy was not maintainable (Art. 358 argument).

Decision
  • Freedom of the press is not expressly guaranteed but is within 19(1)(a): “the right to propagate and publish one’s views and the right to circulate one’s views” (the exact formulation the case-book preserves through Shreya ¶24 and ABP ¶21 quoting Sakal).
  • Restrictions on circulation, pages, price and advertising — even through allocation of newsprint — were held to curtail 19(1)(a) directly and to be unsaved since “public interest” is not a ground under 19(2). The offending provisions of the Act (ss. 3(1), 3(4), 3(5), 10-A, 10-B, 10-D and the Schedule/“too large newspaper” machinery) were struck down.
  • Rationing of a scarce input must be fair and cannot dictate content-size: “if newsprint was in short supply, there could be no objection to ration it equitably — but to further dictate the number of pages… was clearly aimed at controlling the newspapers”; the ostensible object of newsprint control had been “subverted to newspaper control”.
  • Beg J. (concurring): “the freedom of speech and of the press is the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy because public criticism is essential to the working of its institutions” (reproduced in Shreya ¶9 — Case Book p. 25 of PDF p. 26).
  • Mathew J. (dissenting): scarcity is real; equitable rationing is economic regulation; freedom of the press does not include “a right to use an unlimited quantity of newsprint”; the press may be regulated so that all may speak.
Principles evolved
  • 19(1)(a) = right to publish + circulate + read; number of pages, volume of circulation and advertising are “directly affecting” freedom of the press.
  • Only the eight heads of 19(2) can cut 19(1)(a); “general public interest” cannot; “the State cannot make a law which directly restricts one freedom even for securing the better enjoyment of another” (Sakal; BC).
  • Freedom of the press carries neither privilege nor disability — the press is bound by general laws (defamation, contempt, taxation, labour laws — the last confirmed in ABP) but cannot be singled out by “public-interest” shackles.
  • Unguided administrative discretion over publication = prior restraint and void.
Examiner’s note & critique

BC is the high-water mark of the “indirect abridgement” doctrine. Pair it with ABP (2014): same “intention or proximate effect” test, opposite outcome — a labour statute for journalists was upheld because its effect on speech was incidental. This pairing is the cleanest way to display “doctrinal consistency” in the exam — a favourite of DU moderators.

Case Dossier ② · Unit 2.1 · Syllabus Case 2 · 1982 SCR (1) 1184

Prabha Dutt v. Union of India Right to interview prisoners · “right to gather news”

Citation
1982 SCR (1) 1184; AIR 1982 SC 6; (1982) 1 SCC 1 (decided 7.11.1981)
Framework
Prisons Act, 1894 s.59; Manual for the Superintendence and Management of Jails (Delhi) Rules 549(4), 559-A, 552-A
Facts

Billa and Ranga, death-row convicts in the “Ranga–Billa” abduction-cum-murder case, had their mercy petitions rejected by the President. Senior journalist Prabha Dutt sought permission to interview them before execution; the Tihar jail authorities refused. She moved the Supreme Court, pleading her freedom of speech (as the right to gather news / right to know) and the prisoners’ own rights to speak.

Issues
  1. Whether 19(1)(a) confers a legal right on the press to demand an interview with a prisoner (or any person)?
  2. Whether “friends” in Jail Rule 549(4) includes journalists; and whether a refusal without recorded reasons is lawful?
  3. Whether the willing consent of the prisoner is a precondition, and can the State refuse “for his own good”?
Arguments

Petitioner: without the freedom to gather news, the reader’s 19(1)(a) is hollow (BC/Sakal logic); Rule 549(4) is liberal; a condemned man retains dignity & speech rights; no security/discipline reason was ever recorded; journalists are society’s eyes.
Union: no citizen is legally bound to speak to the press (contrast the compulsion of CrPC s.161(2) — and that too runs to police, not media); jail discipline, risk of glorifying crime; journalists are not enumerated in the Rule; the prisoners’ wishes were unverified.

Decision
  • Landmark concession: “the fundamental right to freedom of speech and expression includes within its reach the right to information” — but “the constitutional right… does not confer any right on the press to have an interview with any person; no such right can be claimed by the Press unless the person sought to be interviewed is willing to be interviewed”.
  • “The existence of a free press does not imply or spell out any legal obligation on the citizens to supply information to the Press, such as there is under s.161(2) of the CrPC.”
  • Yet Rule 549(4) is open-textured: journalists “not expressly referred to… does not mean that they can always and without good reasons be denied… newspapermen may broadly be termed friends of the society”; “weighty reasons… will always be recorded in writing” or the refusal is bad. Rule 559-A: “all reasonable indulgence” to condemned prisoners; search under Rule 552-A may apply.
  • Result: interview permission granted subject to jail discipline (the Court having assumed the prisoners’ willingness).
Principles evolved
  • The right to know is a negative liberty — protection from State obstruction of voluntary exchange of information; not an entitlement to compel interviews or access (foundation for the RTI-statute debate: Parliament later supplied the positive right).
  • Access to institutions (prisons, courts) = governed by rule-bound, reasoned discretion, judicially reviewable.
  • Journalists as “friends of the society” — the most quotable line of the case.
Cross-reference

Read with State of Maharashtra v. Rajendra J. Gandhi (1997) 8 SCC 398 (“blanket permission to journalists to interview under-trials is impermissible”) and Sheela Barse (II); in 2.2 the consent-logic reappears in the paparazzi discussion and X v. UOI. For DU: Prabha Dutt is the “right to know” case of 2.1 and the “willingness/consent” root of privacy — use it as the hinge between both units.

Case Dossier ③ · Unit 2.1 · Syllabus Case 3 · (1995) 2 SCC 161

Secretary, Ministry of Information & Broadcasting v. Cricket Association of Bengal Right to telecast · airwaves as public property

Citation
(1995) 2 SCC 161; AIR 1995 SC 1231; decided 9.2.1995 · Three-Judge Bench (S. Mohan, P.B. Sawant, B.P. Jeevan Reddy, JJ.) — majority and minority opinions reported per curiam; the leading reasoning by Jeevan Reddy, J.
Duplicate prescription
Also reprinted in the case-book at Unit 4.2 (pp. 192–195) on broadcasting regulation — and quoted inside Shreya (¶29 → CAB ¶37; ¶86 → CAB ¶78) and inside Sahara (¶¶151, 189) — the DU paper thus keeps citing CAB across units.
Facts

For the 1996 World Cup season, the CAB ( organiser of Eden Gardens matches) sold telecasting rights to ESPN, which planned up-linking through a foreign satellite for live worldwide transmission. Doordarshan claimed “first telecast” rights and the Ministry of I&B withheld up-linking permission, invoking the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 regime and the State’s broadcasting monopoly; the 1994 Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act framework was in force; a Bill was pending. The Calcutta High Court (and later interim orders) favoured CAB; on appeal the Supreme Court settled the constitutional status of broadcasting.

Issues
  1. Whether 19(1)(a) includes the right to telecast (and correspondingly the viewers’ right to receive), and whether an event-organiser may choose its telecasting agency including a foreign agency?
  2. Whether airwaves/frequencies are public property; and the consequences for the State’s role — trustee, owner, or monopolist?
  3. Whether Doordarshan could claim a monopoly to the exclusion of citizens, when no government frequency was even demanded?
  4. Whether a government department that is itself a broadcaster may be the regulator; what checks apply?
Arguments

CAB/ESPN: telecasting is speech; spectrum scarcity justifies regulation of access not of content; DD’s “first-telecast at notified rates” is price-fixing of speech; monopoly distorts both art and market; the Telegraph Act is a licensing statute, not a content-control statute.
Union: unlimited entries would create chaos; national interest in image, public order and morality require State control; DD serves the whole country including the poor; foreign satellites escape 19(2)-compliant oversight.

Decision
  • Right to telecast within 19(1)(a) — “freedom of speech and expression includes the right to telecast, that is to say, the right to broadcast one’s views through any audio-visual/electronic media to the viewers…” (headnote per curiam: “Whether part of freedom of speech — Held, yes”).
  • Viewers’ right: “the right of the viewers to see… is the other side of the coin” (¶78). The 200th Law Commission Report (Trial by Media, in the project material) restates: “the right under 19(1)(a) includes the right to information and the right to disseminate through all types of media… the right of the telecaster and the right of the viewers”.
  • Airwaves are public property (¶78): “since the airwaves/frequencies are public property and are also limited, they have to be used in the best interest of the society and this can be done either by a central authority by establishing its own broadcasting network or regulating the grant of licences to other agencies, including the private agencies”.
  • No monopoly-plus-censorship: where a citizen does not need the government’s frequency, refusal must be justified under 19(2); a broadcaster-cum-regulator cannot be a fair judge of competition; the per curiam direction — control must lie with “an independent public authority”.
  • 19(2) heads are “ultimately referable to societal interest, another name for public interest” (¶189) and are “conceived to maintain conditions in which the right can meaningfully be exercised” (¶151) — the broad-but-anchored reading later adopted in Sahara.
  • Minority view: public broadcasting implicit in the right but no individual right to private broadcasting; up-linking is a licence question. Final disposition: up-linking permitted on conditions; the regulatory architecture left to Parliament (which produced TRAI, DTH Policy 2009, NBW debates, and after 28 years, the Broadcasting Act, 2023).
Principles evolved
  • Medium-neutrality of 19(1)(a) (relied on by Shreya ¶86 for the internet); the “derivative right” label for broadcasting.
  • Public-trust doctrine of spectrum in Indian media law — every auction/policy criticism since (2G, 2010–12) quotes CAB ¶78.
  • Right of the audience (right to receive) as a constitutional value — later used for mandatory sports sharing (Sports Broadcasting Signals Act, 2007), for Doordarshan simulcast directions (DD Free Dish, 2013–14), and for “right to watch” cases.
Critique & current status

Commentators note the Court legislated in the gaps — a method later policed by the 2G natural-resources reference ((2012) 8 SCC 510). The 2023 Broadcasting Act, together with the 2024 Programme-Code Rules, is today’s CAB implementation: self-regulatory bodies for curated content, government oversight for “news & current affairs” including a right-of-reply mechanism for channels (7-hour/72-hour formula in the 2024 Rules) — a convenient bridge to §3.7 (right to reply).

Case Dossier ④ · Unit 2.1 · Syllabus Case 4 · (2014) 3 SCC 327 — Case Book pp. 1–22

ABP Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India Working Journalists · “vocal organs” · press as a class by itself

Citation
(2014) 3 SCC 327; Writ Petition (Civil) 311/2009 etc. · decided 21.3.2014
Composition in case-book
P. Sathasivam, CJI (for himself, Ranjan Gogoi & Shiva Kirti Singh, JJ.) — majority upholding the statutes; a separate opinion of Kurian Joseph, J. (for himself and R.F. Nariman, J.) — the case-book prints only the majority (see pp. 1–22).
Laws challenged
The Working Journalists and Other Newspaper Employees (Conditions of Service) and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1955 (as amended 2001) + the Working Journalists (Fixation of Rates of Wages) Act, 1958; the Press Council of India machinery; the Wage Board notification dated 11.11.2011 (the Majithia Board) fixing minimum/maximum wages, allowances, gratuity etc. for editor, executive editor, news editor etc.
Facts

Newspaper companies (ABP, Dainik Jagran, the Indian Express group and others) attacked the 2001 amendments which (i) made the Act applicable to a wider class of publications, (ii) prescribed the constitution, procedure and recommendations of the Wage Board (including a maximum as well as minimum wage-fixing power, and “variable dearness allowance” linked to cost index), and (iii) empowered the Central Government to notify the Board’s recommendations. The 2011 notification implementing the Majithia Board was the immediate target. The petitioners also complained that the tribunal mechanism and the Board’s composition were biased, and that wage regulation of “editorial cadres” would ruin small newspapers and hence pluralism. The Delhi High Court, as recorded in the excerpt, the bias allegations against the Board member and dismissed the parallel petitions; the matter was referred to a larger bench in the Supreme Court because the ABP Division-Bench (K. Joseph, J.) doubted Express Newspapers and Sakal Papers.

Issues (as framed by the majority, ¶¶27–29)
  1. Whether Parliament is competent to enact, and whether the Act/Wage-Board notification violate Art. 14, insofar as the press is a “class by itself” — is separate legislation for newspapers permissible?
  2. Whether the impugned wage-fixing provisions directly or proximately curtail the freedom of speech and expression of journalists and newspaper owners — Art. 19(1)(a) — and whether they are saved under Art. 19(2)?
  3. Whether the “maximum wage” ceiling and the variable-dearness-allowance mechanism violate Art. 19(1)(g)/Art. 19(6) (business of newspapers) and the right to hold property (Art. 300A)?
  4. Whether the composition of the Board/proceedings suffered from apprehended bias (a member connected with a rival media house; the “real likelihood” test)?
  5. Whether the delegation (fixation by Board, notification by Central Government) was excessive?
Arguments — Petitioners (newspaper companies)

The press must be treated as a class; special/onerous labour laws single it out — violating equality; wage-setting for editors is “regulation of the press” since the journalist is the medium of speech — a direct restriction of 19(1)(a) not relatable to any head of 19(2); maximum-wage caps distort the market and, in Sakal Papers-style, the State cannot restrict one right to improve another; the small/dying newspapers (the very constituency sought to be protected) would be pushed to closure, thus reducing plurality — hence “effect test” is failed; the Board’s recommendations (e.g. 4-installment arrears direction; the “variable dearness allowance” clause) were arbitrary and the member’s prior employment relationship showed real likelihood of bias.

Arguments — Union of India

The 1955 Act is social-welfare legislation for a weaker section — under the settled test (Express Newspapers ¶31), beneficial labour legislation affecting the press is valid unless the intention or proximate effect is to curtail free speech; journalists are not being gagged — better-paid journalists = better journalism; wage equality between “big” and “small” papers levels the playing field of ideas; the press is not above industrial jurisprudence; maximum limits are necessary because the Board heard both unions (who sought ceilings) and managements; the High Court found no actual bias; the delegation is classic policy-subordinate.

Decision (majority)
  • The Acts and the 2011 Wage Board notification were upheld. “The press is a class by itself” for Art. 14 purposes — hence separate legislation is permissible, and intelligible differentia + rational nexus with the object (improving conditions of service of those who run the press) were satisfied (¶¶142–160 range of the judgment; the excerpt condenses it at pp. 14–16). The “hostile discrimination” objection failed because the classification treated the press as an industry with unique features — its own “class legislation”.
  • Test restated & applied: the Court reproduced the Express Newspapers canon — “the journalists are but the vocal organs and the necessary agencies for the exercise of the right of free speech…” — and then held: here neither the intention nor the proximate effect of wage regulation is to abridge speech; the Act “will enable the journalists to improve and enhance the quality of their performance” (¶31–40 of the case-book excerpt). The Sakal doctrine cannot be weaponised to immunise the press from general welfare statutes.
  • Art. 19(1)(g)/19(6): regulation “in the interest of the general public” validly includes wage regulation; no evidence that small newspapers were forced to shut by these rates; arrears were allowed to be paid in four instalments — a safeguard against financial shock (¶¶60–70 of the excerpt).
  • Bias: applying the twin-lines doctrine — “mere apprehension in the mind of a litigant is not sufficient; there must be a reasonable apprehension in the mind of a reasonable man that the concerned person has a hostile attitude or mind against him… there is a difference between ‘real likelihood’ and ‘reasonable likelihood’” — the Court found the alleged association of the Board member with a rival newspaper (through his brother, and a two-year-old resignation from a TV channel, not shown to be “in any supervisory capacity”) insufficient; no veto/decision was attributable to him; hence the notification survived (bias passages at pp. 19–20 of the case-book excerpt, corresponding to ¶¶91–100 of the judgment).
  • The minority (Joseph, J.) would have read down/excised the maximum-wage power as it operated on editors (the excerpt notes the referral history; the minority judgment itself is not reprinted in the case-book — the syllabus focuses on the majority).
Principles evolved
  • “Vocal organs” doctrine: the rights of the press and of the journalist are two sides of one coin — legislation that improves the journalist’s condition is presumptively pro-speech.
  • Bennett Coleman test, re-confirmed benignly: a law affecting the press is valid if (1) it does not intend to curtail 19(1)(a), and (2) its proximate effect does not curtail it; social-welfare laws pass.
  • Press is a class by itself under Art. 14 — separate beneficial legislation constitutional; reverse is also true: no separate burdens relatable only to the “general public interest”.
  • Real-likelihood-of-bias standard restated for administrative/quasi-judicial boards.
Why DU puts it in 2.1 and not in the labour unit

ABP is the modern gloss on Bennett Coleman: the “intention + proximate effect” formula is now the only way to argue that economic regulation = speech restriction. In privacy/defamation answers, cite ¶31 to justify why courts protect the conditions under which journalists work — the same logic (independence of the fourth estate) that the sting guidelines and the Leveson recommendations invoke.

Case Dossier ⑤ · Unit 2.1 · Syllabus Case 5 · (2015) 5 SCC 1 — Case Book pp. 23–43

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India s.66A IT Act struck down · over-breadth & chilling effect enter DU answers

Citation
(2015) 5 SCC 1; AIR 2015 SC 363; decided 24.3.2015 · Bench: R.F. Nariman & Arun Mishra, JJ.
Provisions challenged
IT Act 2000: s.66A (offensive messages etc.), s.69A + Blocking Rules 2009, s.79 + Intermediary Rules 2011 r.3(4)/Rule; Art. 14 & 19(1)(a) & 21 grounds.
Facts (as in ¶¶6–8 of the excerpt)

Petitioners — law students including Shreya Singhal — filed PILs after a series of arrests under s.66A (notably for Facebook posts during the November 2012 Maharashtra bandh — the original two arrests; then the cartoonist, the professor in Puducherry etc.). S.66A punished sending “grossly offensive” or menacing messages, or sending messages “for the purpose of causing annoyance or inconvenience or to deceive or to mislead” — up to 3 years. On 28.3.2012 the Court had stayed arrests; the Union issued advisories (CPs/DIPs to require DCP-rank approval before arrest); Parliament rejected a Private Member’s amendment (2012) and a Union resolution (2013) to delete the section; the PRS background note in the project material records the entire trajectory. The Court examined all three limbs together.

Issues (¶13)
  1. Whether s.66A is facially unconstitutional as violating 19(1)(a) and not saved by any ground of 19(2)?
  2. Whether s.69A and the Blocking Rules, 2009 are unconstitutional?
  3. Whether s.79 (safe harbour) and Rule 3(2) & 3(4) of the Intermediary Rules, 2011 are unconstitutional? (+ Art. 14: classification between internet & other media.)
Arguments

Petitioners: s.66A’s words “grossly offensive… annoying… dangerous to society” do not correspond to any 19(2) head (unlike IPC 294/295/510 which are tethered to obscenity/insult/public mischief — ¶72–77 comparison table of the judgment); the section criminalises “mere discussion or advocacy of any unpopular opinion” (¶83: “if an MP… on social media… says that 100% of finance minister’s decisions are wrong, that would be ‘grossly offensive’” — the classic overbreadth parade of horribles); “annoyance” is impermissibly vague; the chilling effect on the middle-class user; the U.S. authorities (Sullivan; Brandenburg; Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition; Stevens/Reserve Bank digests of obscenity — ¶¶84–86).

Union/ASG (¶¶27, 42, 48): the “content code” of the Draft Broadcasting Services (Regulation) Bill, 2009 (and the IT Rules 2011/2009 Blocking Rules) contain the same or similar expression — the internet must be treated at par with broadcasting (a “narrow-casting” medium); s.66A is not new — it merely transports the content of IPC 505/294 etc. online; and even if drafted loosely, “guidelines” or judicial construction can save it (read down to the 19(2) heads); a relaxed Art. 19(2) standard for “new media” and separate-class classification justified by anonymity, virality, addictiveness.

Decision
  • s.66A struck down as violative of 19(1)(a), not saved by 19(2) — the Court walked through every ground: public order (¶¶31–35: must involve an “element of intention or tendency to create disorder or disturbance of public peace or harm to life and property… that which disturbs the even tempo of life of the community”); “no proximate relationship” — “the effect upon public order is so far fetched and remote”; defamation (¶¶64–68 — vagueness/defamation comparison; ¶42 “Section 66A has nothing to do with defamation”); incitement to offence (¶41: no requirement of “imminent lawless action”); decency/morality (¶45–47: obscenity requires the Aveek Sarkar community-standards test — absent here). The State’s demand to “read into” 66A the 19(2) heads was refused: “we would be doing complete violence to the language” (¶48). Intermedial assimilation (¶¶26–29): even if a different medium justifies classification under Art. 14, “what cannot be restricted directly on any ground of 19(2) cannot be restricted by a supposed power under sub-section (6) of Art. 19 or by any other method” (¶29).
  • Vagueness: “grossly offensive”, “menacing”, “annoyance” — the Court used Collins, DPP v Collins, Chambers v DPP to show that even trained judges cannot pin meaning; a law must “prohibit conduct with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited” (¶¶64–82).
  • Overbreadth + chilling: “if an otherwise valid law prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech, the said law suffers from over-breadth… the chilling effect on the freedom of speech of an ordinary person…” (¶¶83–90); “it is obvious that an Internet law, once passed, is here to stay… Governments may come and governments may go, but such laws as these are still there” (¶92). Severability rejected: where the vice is that the section sweeps in protected speech, striking off “the good part alone would be left” is not possible (¶¶93–95, citing Romesh Thappar, K.A. Abbas).
  • Art. 14 upheld: the internet may be classified separately — the intelligible differentia is “the capacity of the medium to reach the masses”; and “it is perfectly conceivable that Parliament will have to legislate on diverse media…” (¶97–98) — so the differentia is fine, but a relaxed 19(2) standard is not.
  • s.69A & Blocking Rules UPHELD (¶¶109–126): the eight subject-matter categories of Rule 3(2) are “confined within the parameters of 19(2)”, reasons recorded in writing, Grievance Officer, re-examination within 72 hours, quarterly Committee review, and the 2011 Rules — “these guidelines… have within them all the safeguards that can possibly be devised”; hence no prior restraint in the constitutional sense.
  • s.79 + r.3(2)(c) & r.3(4) read down (¶¶110–117): intermediary’s “knowledge” under 79(3)(b) means actual knowledge, and only via “a court order or government notification”; “failure to remove upon complaint alone cannot forfeit immunity” — otherwise intermediaries become State censors (¶116). The Court expressly preserved CrPC 95, 96, 196 and 199 as the speech-sensitive procedural architecture (¶¶99–101).
Principles evolved (¶119 — quote in the exam)
  • “Section 66A of the IT Act does not satisfy the test of reasonableness under Art. 19(2)… is struck down.” “S.69A and the Rules 2009 are valid.” “S.79(2)(c) & Rules 3(2) & 3(4) read down as above.”
  • Constitutional standards imported into Indian doctrine: vanity of vagueness, over-breadth, chilling effect, strict severability for speech laws, medium-neutrality, and “assurance of good administration” as a proviso to — not substitute for — 19(2) (¶92).
  • The advocacy–incitement line restated from Kedar Nath → Arup Bhuyan — “mere discussion of any matter, or advocacy of an unpopular opinion may not be curbed” (¶83).
Aftermath & real examples

Shreya is now the default lens for every internet regulation: IT Rules 2021 (fact-check for MMIs — upheld/provisionally upheld, Delhi HC 2023), the 2013 and 2018 Twitter/WhatsApp “traceability” fights, the 2020 Twitter blocking orders, 2021–22 Koo/Twitter compliance notices, and the first Shreya-compliance: every post-2015 takedown order is tested for “court order/notification”. s.67/67A arrests are routinely struck down on Shreya’s reasoning (several High Courts have since invoked Shreya to quash s.67 complaints over satire and political commentary — e.g. in the JNU-students matter). The PRS note in the project material provides the legislative-history paragraph for a 20-mark answer.

Supplementary Dossier (for 2.1 hate-speech) · (2021) 5 SCC 542

Amish Devgan v. Union of India the SC’s hate-speech framework

Bench
Sanjiv Khanna & A.M. Khanwilkar, JJ. (judgment dated 7.12.2020; reported in (2021) 5 SCC 542)
Statutes
IPC 153A(1)(a)&(b), 295A, 505(2) → BNS 196, 299, 353; IT Act s.66A (dead), s.156/157/162 CrPC → BNSS 175/176/180
Facts

TV anchor Amish Devgan, during a live debate on the ‘Tandav’ row (10.1.2020), used two abusive epithets for a revered Sufi saint. Seven FIRs followed across states (Ajmer the first) under 153A/295A/505(2) etc.; the Allahabad HC refused quashing; he came to the SC under Art. 32 alleging violation of 19(1)(a), absence of cause of action outside one State, “slight harm”, and multiplicity of proceedings.

Issues
  1. Do the words, read in the context of the programme, prima facie attract 153A/295A/505(2)?
  2. Whether the FIRs outside the place of utterance were vitiated (cause-of-action/territorial jurisdiction — s.179 CrPC/BNSS 199; s.156(1))?
  3. Whether multiple FIRs for the one speech are permissible; whether the SC should quash pre-investigation?
Arguments

Petitioner: words were careless, not malicious; the programme must be judged as a whole by a “reasonable and strong-minded person”; the “slight/harmless words” defence (Horilal v. State of U.P.; Arup Bhuyan; Bal Thackeray (2011)); free political debate; multiple FIRs = harassment (Arnab Goswami 2020; T.T. Antony).

Respondents: 153A requires only “promotes or attempts to promote” enmity — intent inferred from the nature of words and context; place of broadcast reaches every PS under s.179; quashing at the pre-investigation stage is exceptional (Babubhai v. State of Gujarat; State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal); the “triviality” principle cannot apply to content that stigmatises a community.

Decision & reasoning
  • Petition dismissed — FIRs not quashed; clubbed with the first FIR at Ajmer; subsequent ones to be treated as statements; the Court recorded that at the pre-investigation stage only the extreme case permits interference.
  • The judgment contains the Court’s most complete essay on hate speech: (i) the definitional difficulty (no global unanimity — ICCPR Art. 20(2); CERD; ECHR; Canadian “hate promotion”; R.A.V. v. St. Paul; the “viewpoint discrimination” debate); (ii) the Indian legal position — 153A/295A/505 are “in the interests of public order” and valid (Ramji Lal Modi); (iii) the indicia for judging a statement: content expressing hostility towards a group; harm (physical or psychosocial); intent judged from “objective” markers plus the speaker’s position and platform; the “reasonable, strong-minded person at the top of the range” standard; and no redeeming purpose; (iv) the reminder that “persons of influence… owe a duty and have to be more responsible”; (v) Kedar Nath’s line — criticism of Government “in howsoever strong words must be protected” — is not the same as attacking a community.
Principles evolved
  • Three-level analysis for every alleged hate speech: content → intent → harm, judged contextually, by the objective reasonable-strong-minded-person standard.
  • “Hate speech… which vilifies, humiliates and incites hatred or violence against the target group upon identity of the group” is outside the protection of 19(1)(a).
  • Procedural: FIR for a cognizable hate-speech offence must be registered; multiplicity cured by clubbing at the first FIR; quashing is exceptional pre-investigation (paras 54–55, 69; the petition was dismissed in the operative part).

PART C — UNIT 2.2 · Privacy, Defamation, Sting Operations & Their Misuse

3.1 · Defining “Privacy” — the case-book’s own starting points

The DU case-book grounds privacy doctrine on Puttaswamy (Case Book pp. 44–51 — excerpts from Chandrachud, Chelameswar, Bobde, Nariman & Kaul JJ. plus the operative order ¶¶652.1–652.4), Rajagopal (pp. 52–64) and the sting cases. The definitional ladder to reproduce:

① “Right to be let alone”Warren & Brandeis, “The Right to Privacy,” 4 Harvard Law Review 193 (1890) — the Court in Rajagopal ¶10 expressly traces the “right to privacy” to this article. Puttaswamy (majority ¶109): “The right to privacy is the constitutional right to be left alone… it recognises the right of each individual to regulate dissemination of information concerning themselves.”
→
② Constitutional & naturalPuttaswamy ¶¶42–46 (case-book p. 46): privacy is “natural, inalienable and intrinsic” — it “does not have its genesis in Article 21”, it “exists despite” it; a “residual right” under Part III; “overrules M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1963)” (operative order ¶652.3).
→
③ Three branchesPuttaswamy: spatial privacy (the home as “sanctuary” — the passage reproduced in Rajagopal from the U.S. jurisprudence); decisional privacy (marriage, procreation, contraception — Griswold/Roe passages reproduced at Rajagopal ¶¶14–15); informational privacy (Bobde, J. ¶521 — the case-book prints his formulation of the “three aspects of privacy”: “personal information autonomy, decisional autonomy and bodily/privacy-of-mind” — p. 50).
“The right to privacy – with reference to an individual – contemplates the freedom to be alone — the right to be let alone… the concepts of seclusion and of limited access are fundamental to the notion of privacy… the expectation of privacy must be viewed from both the perspective of the individual and from the perspective of the society.”— K.S. Puttaswamy v. UOI, per Chandrachud, J., ¶297 (DU Case Book pp. 48–49)

“Reasonable / legitimate expectation of privacy” — the operative test

Translate the American Katz v. U.S. (1967) “reasonable expectation” test into the Indian phrasing printed in the case-book:

  • Dual prong — subjective expectation of privacy + society’s recognition of it as reasonable (Puttaswamy: expectation judged “from the perspective of the individual and of the society”).
  • Place is not destiny: “an individual cannot have an absolute expectation of seclusion or solitude in a public place… partial disclosure does not extinguish privacy… the fact that a person is in a public space does not mean that he forfeits all privacy” (Chandrachud, J. ¶297–298). Conversely, “a person may have a reasonable expectation of privacy in a public place” (the Court cites the U.S./ECtHR line).
  • Contextual factors (the judgment’s catalogue): the role of the person (public official? voluntary public figure? victim?); the nature of the information (intimate/sensitive); the place and manner of acquisition; consent — express, implied, contextual, or withdrawn; the purpose (news? entertainment? commerce?); the severity of intrusion; the expectations of the parties; and the public-interest value of the disclosure.
  • Waiver is limited: appearing in public for one purpose ≠ consent to every use (the “partial disclosure” principle — this is the answer to the paparazzi question).

3.3 · Paparazzi & the Right of Publicity

The legal position on paparazzi in India

  • No special licence, no special immunity: taking photographs is a lawful activity, but when it intrudes upon “seclusion” it violates Art. 21 privacy (Puttaswamy) and can amount to (a) the tort of intrusion upon seclusion (Indian courts recognise it through constitutional tort & general law), (b) breach of confidence, and (c) criminal offences: voyeurism — IPC 354C → BNS 77; following/contacting (stalking) — IPC 354D → BNS 78; insulting the modesty of a woman “or intrudes upon the privacy of such woman” — IPC 509 → BNS 79; IT Act s.66E (capturing/transmitting images of a woman in private act), s.67/67A if the material is obscene/sexually explicit; s.66C/66D if accounts/identity are hijacked.
  • Paparazzi-style intrusion was squarely the mischief in the Uma Khurana sting — the Court: “such incidents should not happen… because of desire to earn more and to have higher TRP ratings” (¶15).
  • At the courthouse gate: fair & accurate report of proceedings is privileged (s.4, Contempt of Courts Act, 1971; BNS 356(1) Exc.4–5), but camera intrusion on victims/children is barred (see above); “no live telecast of a sexual-offence trial.”
  • Right of publicity — the syllabus names it. Puttaswamy, Kaul, J. ¶¶618, 625 (printed at Case Book p. 51): the right to privacy “encompasses within its ambit the right to control dissemination of information about one’s life … “the individual is the sole owner of his own image”” — quoting the American “right of publicity” line (Haelan Laboratories v. Topps Chewing Gum, 202 F.2d 866 (2d Cir. 1953): “the right of every person to the commercial exploitation of his own personality aspects… every person has a legal right to his own picture, and… it is not lawful to use it for advertising purposes without his consent”). The Court held it is an aspect of privacy (economic facet) — not yet a statutory right in India: protection comes through passing off, the Copyright Act, 1957 (s.17(b): for commissioned photographs the commissioner is first owner), contractual releases, and Art. 21.
  • Real example: Anil Kapoor v. Unique Collections & Ors. (Delhi HC, 2023) — a “paparazzi culture” privacy suit where the Court restrained unauthorised commercial and social-media use of the actor’s name, image, voice and personality traits, including morphed/AI-generated content, for 24+ years, expressly resting on the “right of publicity” passage of Puttaswamy and on Rajagopal — the most current illustration for this sub-topic. (For the mirror-image in the U.S.: California Civ. Code s.3344; France: Art. 9 Code civil “droit à l’image”; UK: no general image right — Campbell/Murray remedies through confidence.)

Exam skeleton — “paparazzi & publicity right, 10 marks”

(1) define publicity right (Kaul ¶625 quote); (2) Haelan + Prosser taxonomy; (3) Indian absence of statute → mix of passing off/copyright/Art 21; (4) Anil Kapoor case; (5) limits: news reporting, matters of public record, satire; (6) balance with 19(1)(a): “the press may photograph a public function but not a private grief”.

3.4 · Publishing information obtained illegally

The DU problem: does a dirty source kill a clean story? The case-book answers through three layers:

  1. Rajagopal — the “public-records” and “stolen document” line. R. Rajagopal (Nakkheeran) wanted to serialise Auto Shankar’s prison diary and court-record material; the State sought an injunction. The Court: a newspaper may publish matter from public records without prior restraint, “even if the material is adverse,” but where the document was allegedly stolen from judicial custody, the petitioners were put to terms — they had to “establish that they obtained the material from a lawful source” — publication “can be proceeded against under the law of defamation or privacy if the manner of acquisition was unlawful.” The judgment also records the Bombay High Court’s strictures in Dignity Forum v. Satish Gupte (AIR 1988 Bom 68) — the “police sweeping operation” telecast for TRPs, which the Court found “illegal, unconstitutional and… a contempt of court” — the earliest Indian judicial hostility to media-run covert operations (this passage was quoted by the Law Commission Consultation Paper ¶6 as well).
  2. R.K. Anand — the evidentiary treatment. NDTV’s hidden-camera “sting” tapes: (i) the demand that the channel first seek the trial court’s/Chief Justice’s permission was rejected — “such a course… would tantamount to pre-censorship of reporting… plainly an infraction of 19(1)(a)” (¶173); (ii) but “the media is not free to publish any kind of report concerning a sub-judice matter or to do a sting… in any manner they please… a sting… would attract the legal restrictions with far greater stringency and any infraction would invite more severe punishment” (¶173); (iii) the originals-microchip controversy: the Court held that leaving chips with the channel was a procedural lapse not fatal because the accused effectively admitted the recordings (¶¶132–136); and (iv) a doctored or false sting would have made the channel “equally, if not more, guilty of serious contempt of court and other criminal offences” (¶135). The recording can still be evidence even when the method was deceptive — the illegality of gathering and the legality of publishing are separately assessed.
  3. X v. UOI (Del HC 2021) — the modern removal template. When content itself is unlawful (posted from a hacked private account; s.66C/43 IT Act; s.67), the victim is entitled to a global take-down + search-engine de-indexing; the “source’s” illegality grounds the remedy rather than defeating it; and the intermediaries’ Shreya “actual knowledge” is supplied by the court order — see dossier for the eight directions.

Statutory teeth against unlawful sourcing (both codes)

IT Act 2000: s.43 (penalty for unauthorised access/damage); s.66 r/w 43 (dishonestly/fraudulently); s.66C (identity theft); s.66D (cheating by personation using computer resource); s.66E (privacy — capturing/publishing image of private parts); s.67/67A/67B. IPC → BNS: theft (378 → 303); the dishonest-receiving, criminal-trespass and forgery clusters map onto the corresponding BNS offences in the property chapters (cite the chapter grouping); and, for the sure ones: 120A/120B → BNS 61; 107/109 (abetment) → BNS 107/109; 228A → BNS 72/73; 292–294 → BNS 294–296; 354A–509 → BNS 75–79. Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 s.24 — the citizen/trap-giver who gives the bribe under compulsion and reports within 7 days is not an abettor — the Law Commission consultation question (Q4) was whether this immunity should extend to journalists who pay in a sting. Evidence: tape recordings admissible subject to proof of integrity — Evidence Act 65A–65B → BNSS/BSA 2023: BSA ss.63–65 (electronic records & certificate) — the R.K. Anand ¶¶129–136 discussion is the practical guide.

3.5 · Right to be forgotten (DU prescribes X v. UOI, 2021 SCC OnLine Del 1788)

Concept, foreign law, Indian status

  • What it is: “the right to require deletion of data about you… to no longer be associated with embarrassing or damaging information from the past when continued publication is no longer relevant” — the “rehabilitative” face of informational privacy.
  • Comparative anchors used by the Delhi HC (as printed in the case-book, pp. 196–218): Google Spain SL v. AEPD (C-131/12, 2014) — search-engine delisting of adequate-but-irrelevant data; X v. Twitter Inc. (Supreme Court of NSW, 2013) — “where a third party comes into possession of confidential information and is put on notice… it becomes subject to an equitable obligation of confidence and is liable to be restrained from publishing”; YouTube v. Geeta Shroff (Del HC 2017) — global takedown for content uploaded from India; geo-blocking for foreign uploads; Canada: Google v. Equustek (2017) — global de-indexing permissible; the court also noted “a further balancing act… that of freedom of speech and privatised censorship”.
  • Indian doctrinal basis: no statute; derived from Art. 21 informational privacy (Puttaswamy — Kaul, J. ¶625 “right to control dissemination”; Nariman, J. ¶521–526 cluster: “right to be forgotten… in regard to their personal data”), and implemented through IT Act tools: s.79(3)(b) (actual knowledge — Shreya), s.69A + 2009 Blocking Rules r.10 (“in case of an order from a competent court… the Designated Officer shall… initiate action as directed by the court” — quoted verbatim in the case-book), IT Rules 2021 r.3(2)(b) (intermediary must remove/disable within 24 hours complaints concerning material “exposing the private area… nudity… sexual act… impersonation including artificially morphed images” — verbatim in case-book pp. 211–212), s.85 (offences by companies), and, prospectively, the DPDP Act 2023 (data-principal’s right to erasure of personal data and correction — ss.6 & 12; grievance redressal).

3.6 · Defamation — the full framework

Ingredients (s.499 IPC → BNS 356(1) — text carried over)

  • Any imputation concerning a person (words, spoken or written, or signs, or visible representations — hence TV tickers, memes, reels, hashtags);
  • made or published with intention to cause harm to reputation, or knowing/having reason to believe it will harm reputation;
  • “Harm to reputation” = lowering in the estimation of “right-thinking members of society generally” (Subramanian Swamy; tort law); reputation is “an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty under Art. 21” — the constitutional basis of the 19(2) ground;
  • publication to a third person; imputation must be referable to the plaintiff (innuendo; group defamation: protectable where class is small/identifiable);
  • corporate reputations are protected too (the “person” in s.499/BNS 356 includes companies through the general clauses; trade defamation = imputations lowering estimation of those who deal with the business)

Punishment: IPC 500 (simple imprisonment up to 2 yrs + fine) → BNS 356(2); the printing-for-sale / sale-and-circulation limbs (old 501–502) are consolidated within s.356; BNS adds community-service option for specified low-gravity defamation (the gazette reform — cite as “restorative-justice tweak”).

The ten exceptions (identical provisos now in BNS 356)

  1. Truth for public good — imputation true + publication for public good (question of law for court; burden on maker);
  2. public conduct of a public servant, in good faith;
  3. conduct of any person touching a public question, in good faith;
  4. fair & accurate report of judicial proceedings (civil/criminal) — read with s.4 Contempt Act; BNS 356 Exc.4 covers also executive/quasi-judicial in some states;
  5. merits of case/conduct of parties & witnesses (comment on the case);
  6. merits of any question public — including public performances;
  7. censure in good faith by a person with lawful authority (master/employer/teacher);
  8. accusation preferred in good faith to a person having authority over the accused (complaint to a superior/SPC);
  9. imputation for protection of interests of the maker or another, or for public good;
  10. imputation re character of a person “as it appears to him and no further” in good faith.

Statutory overlays: Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977 (ss.3–4 — substantially true report of Parliamentary proceedings privileged unless published with malice & not for public good; extended to broadcast); absolute privilege for legislators (Arts. 105/194); for the judiciary the protection runs through contempt, not defamation (see §1.8, head 6; Rajagopal ¶26(3) proviso); qualified privilege for reports of Parliamentary debates; Press Council “norms of journalistic conduct” (the uploaded Press Council Act lists the s.13 functions — incl. “ensuring that newspapers maintain high standards of… public taste” — and s.14: the power to censure only; it can neither award damages nor compel a reply).

3.8 · Sting operations — investigative journalism, ethics & the Leveson Report

Definitional & historical frame (Law Commission Consultation Paper, 2010 — project material, read in full)

The paper (¶1–2) records: “A ‘sting operation’… is essentially a deceptive operation, though designed to nab a criminal” (borrowing the Rajat Prasad language); a media sting = “a covert, premeditated journalistic exercise in which the reporter assumes a false identity or conceals recording equipment to expose… wrongdoing”. The paper distinguishes (a) law-enforcement stings — “sting operations by law enforcement agencies… are legal in the US… in a limited manner” (¶2; see Rajat Prasad ¶11–14: Sherman v. U.S., “trap for the unwary innocent and the unwary criminal”, R. v. Mack, R. v. Loosely), (b) media stings — “there is no law governing undercover sting operations by the media” — and (c) fake “reality-TV” stings which “entrap and convict through SMS polls” (the paper opens with the horror of a TV anchor “allowing viewers to vote ‘guilty/not guilty’”). It records the Rajya Sabha Petitions Committee (12.12.2008) “middle path”: neither ban nor licence — “guidelines… with the media itself and an independent watch-dog”, and the Delhi High Court’s draft guidelines (14.12.2007) which became the Uma Khurana 12-point set (below).

  • Journalism pedigree: from Watergate to Tehelka’s 2001 “Operation Black Thunder” (the DU Rajat Prasad prosecution itself grew from a 2003 “Caught on Tape” sting published in the Indian Express — the case-book facts: Union Minister Dalip Singh Judeo caught taking ₹9 lakh for forest-clearance files; A-5 was the son of the then CM of Chhattisgarh, and the sting was designed to embarrass him before elections). Also: NDTV BMW-case sting (R.K. Anand); Live India school-sting (Uma Khurana); the Ram Rahim/“Bhaiyaji” era and the TV-debate “anchor-trial” practice — cite these as the “real examples” the syllabus wants.
  • Self-regulation codes: NBSA Operational Guidelines & Code of Ethics, clause 9 (quoted in the Consultation Paper): resort to covert recording only where “the material is… of such importance to the public” that it “cannot be obtained by fair and honest means”; “the programme must be… based on the unedited recordings”; entrapment only of persons “who are willing to break the law”; identities protected unless “central to the programme”. BBC Editorial Guidelines (secret filming: “justified by an overall public interest in the subject matter… no material misrepresentation or deception beyond the cover itself”; “the proposed deception must be approved in advance by senior management” — the same “approval” logic the Delhi guidelines made statutory-adjacent); Ofcom Broadcasting Code s.8.13 (“any infringement of privacy… must be warranted”).
  • Leveson Report (2011–12): the DU syllabus names it because it is the definitive modern verdict on intrusive practices. Write: “The Leveson Inquiry — An Inquiry into the Culture, Practices and Ethics of the Press (Lord Justice Brian Leveson; Part 1 report 29.10.2012; Part 2 on police/phone-hacking, Nov 2015), triggered by the News of the World phone-hacking scandal. Its relevance to stings/paparazzi: (i) it condemned obtaining confidential information by criminal means (hacking, blagging officials, payments to police for information — the Inquiry recommended: “no payments to public officials for criminal or potentially criminal information”; the Inquiry found systematic unlawful information-gathering and recommended safeguards); (ii) it proposed a new independent self-regulatory body with a Royal Charter (statutory recognition — s.XX “recognition tab”); (iii) an Editors’ Code of Practice — privacy, harassment, covert recording, “public interest” exceptions in Sch. 1; (iv) s.40 costs sanction (never implemented); (v) arbitration scheme & exemplary damages; (vi) it expressly did NOT ban undercover work — “investigative journalism… including secret filming — can be in the public interest”, provided the intrusion is “proportionate to the public interest”. The UK Parliament did not enact Section 40; IPSO operates with Royal Charter recognition (2014 ff.) — the “statutory underpinning” question remains live. Balance: Leveson is the UK’s answer to both the phone-hacking “illegal information” problem and the sting “misuse” problem — ‘rights-based self-regulation underpinned by statute’.”

The Indian regulatory patchwork for broadcasting stings (fill the “related laws” box)

3.9 · Misuse of sting operations — the doctrinal core of 2.2

“Four faces of misuse” — the answer skeleton

  1. Fabrication & entrapment — staging or inducing the act: Uma Khurana (a fake sting built by a suspended employee’s revenge + an intern, aired 30.8.2007; a teacher’s life destroyed; mob attacked the school — “such incidents should not happen” ¶15); R.K. Anand ¶173 (“a sting… is based on deception” → “far greater stringency”); Rajat Prasad ¶10 (the ethics of luring the otherwise-innocent). Consequences: defamation, damages, contempt, criminal prosecution of the operator (abetment/conspiracy; impersonation), plus s.67-family IT charges for fabricated sexual content.
  2. Privacy & dignity harm to uninvolved persons — bystanders, children, families, complainants: guidelines cl. 5, 8(e), 9, 11, 12; BNS 72/73, POCSO s.23, JJ Act s.21; the Swatanter Kumar court’s own protective manners — the complainant intern’s identity concealed, “filed in a sealed envelope… being referred to as the ‘intern’” — even while protecting the plaintiff (¶5 of the excerpt) — a beautiful illustration of reciprocal privacy.
  3. Interference with the administration of justice / trial by media — R.K. Anand ¶174 definition (“regardless of the verdict… the accused is already held guilty… lynch mob”) vs ¶175 (this sting was not a media trial; “informative media” vs “trial by media” — the paper’s Manu Sharma note); the postponement device (Sahara; Swatanter Kumar ¶¶52–62 — “postponement… for short duration… test of necessity and proportionality”); the 200th LC Report: courts should first use “change of venue/postponement of trial”, only then gag publication.
  4. Criminality of the method; blackmail economics — Rajat Prasad ¶15: “the inherent possibilities of abuse of the operation as videographed, namely, retention and use thereof to ensure delivery of the favours assured… has to be excluded”; giving the bribe may itself be s.12 PC Act r/w abetment/conspiracy — the “public interest” defence is not a legal immunity at the charging stage; the Consultation Paper’s Questionnaire Q.4–6 asks whether s.24 PC Act-type immunity should be extended to media trappers, whether a “manipulated sting” should be a new offence, and whether unauthorised sale of material to channels should be penalised. The R.K. Anand corollary: the media that pays to procure official information (phone-hacking/Leveson) or procures court tapes (Rajagopal) is itself “in the dock” — “the place of NDTV was along with the appellants facing the charge” (¶135).

Concluding paragraph for the exam: “In India the sting is neither licensed nor banned. The Supreme Court in R.K. Anand blessed its public-interest use with a caution; Rajat Prasad refused to let ‘exposing crime’ immunise crime; the Delhi High Court in Uma Khurana supplied a quasi-statutory code; and the Law Commission’s 2010 questionnaire shows reform stalled at the trade-off between watchdog journalism and privacy/entrapment. The Leveson lesson completes the picture — a rights-respecting press regulator with teeth plus proportionate, recorded, verifiable covert methods — which is precisely what the 2023 Broadcasting Act-era self-regulation is attempting for television.”

Case Dossier ⑥ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 1 · (2017) 10 SCC 1 — Case Book pp. 44–51

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India Aadhaar & privacy · nine-Judge Bench · the “mother” authority

Case
W.P.(C) 494/2012 · Constitution Bench of 9 Judges: J.S. Khehar CJI, J. Chelameswar, S.A. Bobde, D.Y. Chandrachud, R.F. Nariman, Sanjay Kishan Kaul, S.A. Nazeer, Rohinton Nariman — final composition: Khehar CJI, Chelameswar, Bobde, Chandrachud, Nariman, Kaul, Nazeer, Sapre & A.K. Misra, JJ. (decided 24.8.2017)
What the case-book prints
Majority ¶¶42–46 (natural rights), ¶109 (“constitutional right to be left alone”), ¶¶248–250 (contours), ¶¶297–298 (Chandrachud, J. — privacy in public spaces; legitimate expectation); Chelameswar, J. ¶¶375–380 (standards of review; “History suggests that tyrannies… begin with surveillance”); Bobde, J. ¶521 (three facets); Nariman, J. ¶¶526, 532; Sapre, J. ¶557 (privacy & “freedom to differ”); Kaul, J. ¶625 (right of publicity — “the individual is the sole owner of his image”) & ¶638 (proportionality); operative order ¶¶652.1–652.4.
Facts

A retired Karnataka High Court judge challenged the Aadhaar (Targeted Delivery of Financial and Other Subsidies, Benefits and Services) Act, 2016 and the UIDAI ecosystem, arguing that mandatory biometric enrolment and “seeding” of the 12-digit ID into bank accounts, SIM cards, property records, LPG and school examinations would build “a permanent digital trail” and enable a surveillance state. The three-Judge Bench (2015) recorded the Union’s “no absolute right” stance and the conflicting precedents — M.P. Sharma (1954, 8 JJ, denying any fundamental right to privacy) and Kharak Singh (1963, majority upholding surveillance regulation but with Subba Rao J.’s celebrated dissent; then Gobind (1975), Rajagopal (1994), PUCL (1997), Selvi (2010) and Naz Foundation (Del HC 2009) had all expanded it) — and referred to a nine-Judge Bench the “pure question of law”: whether the Constitution protects a fundamental right to privacy. Hearings: 19–24 July 2017; judgment pronounced 24 August 2017. (The adjudication of Aadhaar’s validity itself was postponed to Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. UOI (2018) 19 SCC 1 — “Puttaswamy II” — where this privacy framework was applied and the Act largely upheld.)

Issues
  1. Is there a fundamental right to privacy, and of what content, in the face of the contrary holdings in M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh?
  2. What are its contours — spatial, decisional and informational privacy; does it extend to the “most intimate” and to public spaces?
  3. Is privacy absolute? What standard of review governs restrictions — “compelling State interest” (US) vs the Art. 19(2)-style / Art. 21 due-process proportionality?
  4. What survives for the legislature — is “legality, legitimate aim and proportionality” the binding test for every invasion of privacy?
Arguments — Petitioners

Privacy is an inalienable natural right, anterior to the Constitution; the Preamble’s “dignity”, Arts. 14, 19(1)(a)&(d), 21 conjunctively “create zones of privacy” (Griswold line); Gobind already held privacy “emanates” from 19(1)(a) & 21 and “has to develop case-by-case”; Kharak Singh’s majority must stand overruled on the “last stopping point” surveillance point; any invasion must meet a “compelling public interest” test with “narrowly drawn” measures; the panopticon objection to Aadhaar — “the State must not know ‘too much’”.

Arguments — Union of India

(KK Venugopal, AG) Privacy “at the highest” is a common-law tort; an absolute right “would be an anti-people right” and defeat welfare delivery (seeding was for leak-proof subsidies); “surveillance” fears are “theoretical”; the Constitution deliberately omitted the framers’ choice of a privacy clause; M.P. Sharma & Kharak Singh, being larger benches, bind; any protection lies in statutory law (IT Rules, Evidence Act). (Intervenors for the State argued that any “reasonable expectation” must be located in statute, not in Part III.)

Decision (unanimous on the reference question)
  • ¶652.1–652.2: “The right to privacy is a fundamental right and it is an inalienable natural right… The right to privacy… is a constitutionally protected right which emerges primarily from the guarantee of life and personal liberty in Article 21… Privacy has varying degrees…” — “the Constitution recognises, as part of life and liberty, the privacy of the individual.”
  • ¶652.3–652.4: to the extent “M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1963) held the contrary, they stand overruled”; the right is not absolute — “its existence at the highest level… and its limitations…”. Restrictions must satisfy legality (law in existence) — legitimate aim — proportionality (the Kaul J. ¶638 formulation, adding procedural guarantees against abuse), which the Court applied in Puttaswamy II (Aadhaar 2018).
  • Contours (per Chandrachud J., ¶¶248–250, 297–304): privacy protects (i) spatial — the home and “sanctuary”; (ii) decisional autonomy — personal choices on marriage, procreation, food, belief; (iii) informational privacy — control over dissemination; plus bodily & mental privacy. “The expectation of privacy must be viewed from both the perspective of the individual and from the perspective of the society” — legitimate expectation of privacy; public place ≠ forfeiture; “partial disclosure” ≠ consent to all use.
  • Standards split, harmonised: Chelameswar J. (¶375–380): “inherent restraint… compelling State interest” for the core; majority: proportionality under 21/19(1)(a) with the enumerated 19(2) heads when speech is also involved; Nariman J. (¶526/532) traced the doctrinal journey; Kaul J. — the right reaches non-State actors too (¶618: “the right to privacy… encompasses within its ambit… the right to be forgotten” in the informational sense, and ¶625 — right of publicity). Sapre J. (¶557) grounded it in “dignity of the individual… freedom to differ”.
Principles evolved
  • Privacy = natural, inalienable, intrinsic Art. 21 right overlapping Arts. 14, 19(1)(a), 21 — “residual rights under Part III” doctrine.
  • Legitimate expectation test — person/place/purpose/consent; “what is a ‘public’ question is judged contextually, not by geography”.
  • Proportionality framework for privacy-limiting laws — legality · legitimate aim · proportionality stricto · procedural safeguards (→ the test in Puttaswamy II & in every data-protection/privacy challenge since).
  • The bridge: informational privacy → right to be forgotten (§3.5); public-figure limits → §3.2; “dignity of the press is not above the dignity of the citizen” → §3.8–3.9 stings.
Critical comment

(i) The Court refused a single rigid standard (scrutiny levels split across 9 opinions) — deliberate “case-by-case development” (Gobind’s words echoed); (ii) it constitutionalised a tort-based doctrine — so defamation/privacy litigation now runs on the same constitutional track; (iii) it expressly left horizontal-application (media/intermediary vs citizen) for future benches — the space filled by Swatanter Kumar (injunctions against media), X v. UOI (orders against intermediaries) and Anil Kapoor (2023); (iv) for the 2.1 side: privacy is the axis on which “decency, defamation and public order” restrictions are weighed, and Puttaswamy’s own ¶297 (Chandrachud J., “privacy and the freedom of the press… coexist”) is your bridge-quote between the two units.

Case Dossier ⑦ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 2 · 1994 SCC (6) 632 — Case Book pp. 52–64

R. Rajagopal (Alias D. Chinna Ramasamy) v. State of Tamil Nadu Auto Shankar · prior restraint · public figures · the six-principle code

Bench
Two-Judge Bench: S. Ahmadi & B.P. Jeevan Reddy, JJ. (decided 26.4.1994)
Reception
quoted inside Shreya (chilling-effect section of the case-book); invoked in Swatanter Kumar & every privacy-media case since; the “reckless disregard… for the truth” phrase of the DU syllabus is lifted verbatim from principle (3) of ¶26.
Facts (¶¶1–7, case-book pp. 52–55)

K. Ramasamy @ “Auto Shankar” — a literate, self-made man, associate of AIADMK leaders and “most wanted” absconder surrendered in the Rajiv assassination era, convicted in nine murder cases and sentenced to death — wrote, in prison, a confessional autobiography exposing how he functioned with the protection of senior police and revenue officers. Parts were serialised / proposed for serialisation by the Madras-based magazine Nakkheeran, whose editor R. Rajagopal was the petitioner (¶1). The ADGP of Tamil Nadu approached the High Court (¶¶2–4), alleging (i) that the diary was built on stolen court records (¶¶6–7: the copy of the Sessions Court record underlying the series was alleged to have been stolen from judicial custody), (ii) publication would interfere with a pending trial (¶6), and (iii) it invaded the privacy of named officers and victims. The Madras High Court restrained publication; the editors moved the Supreme Court under Art. 32: prior restraint on the press, and no Indian law recognising a “privacy right” enforceable by the State against the press.

Issues (¶8)
  1. Does the Constitution (via Art. 21) recognise a right of privacy; and can the State invoke it as a ground of injunctive relief against the press?
  2. Is prior restraint of publication permissible in India?
  3. What is the standard of proof when a public official complains of invasion of privacy/defamation by publication of his official conduct? And what if the material comes from public records — does “stolen” taint matter?
Arguments — Petitioners (the media)

No Indian statute grants “privacy” as an injunction ground; Gobind “right develops case-by-case”; reliance on New York Times v. Sullivan — “debate on public issues… uninhibited, robust and wide-open… erroneous statement is inevitable and… First Amendment requires ‘breathing space’” (Brennan, J., reproduced at ¶¶15–17); Time Inc. v. Hill (¶11 — “the constitutional protections… preclude… redress [of] false reports… in the absence of proof… knowledge of its falsity or [reckless] disregard”); Cox Broadcasting v. Cohn (¶12 — no liability for publishing the rape-victim’s name lawfully obtained from court records); Pentagon Papers — “heavy presumption against the constitutional validity of prior restraint” (¶22, adopted); and the material here is from judicial records — a public document. Also: “the State itself cannot maintain defamation; the officers sue in their own right, and must bear public criticism — they voluntarily entered public life.”

Arguments — Respondents (the State)

Auto Shankar’s “vendetta” against officers who arrested him would be laundered as “journalism”; the record was stolen — a newspaper “cannot take the benefit of its own wrong”; the trial of connected offences was pending (¶6) — risk of prejudicing jurors/judges; named private individuals (victims, family) have full privacy; “the right to know” cannot mean “right to read stolen documents”; and Indian jurisprudence (Gobind; the posited “Indian theory of privacy” as “seclusion”) forbids wholesale import of Sullivan where the press is commercially driven.

Decision — Jeevan Reddy, J.: the six principles (¶26)
  1. Privacy recognised. “The right to privacy… has been… recognised… as ‘the right to be let alone’… It is implicit in Article 21 (life and liberty) and also flows from 19(1)(a)… ‘every man may be “king in his own castle”… a house is “the castle of a man”…’ It is not absolute — “the State can make laws… subject to… 19(2) and Art. 21”. It is both a fundamental right and a tort — “the invasion… is actionable… by suit for damages… [with] discretionary injunctions.”
  2. Public-records rule. “Those who hold public office… cannot be restrained… the publication… of matters of public records… no prior restraint… even if the publication is ‘defamatory’ — a person who has published his own life story “cannot complain of publication by the media of the same…”. The press may publish matter “which it has obtained from the public record… whether or not the person is willing” — the publication cannot be enjoined; only post-publication remedies survive.
  3. Public officials must prove actual malice. “[T]he right to privacy… ceases… when a public official’s ‘official acts’… are… under scrutiny… The official… can sue… if the publication was made… with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not” (adopting Sullivan — with a judiciary exception: “the judiciary… cannot be… exempted… from… the right of the people to know… and… “the judiciary… cannot be exempted… ” — the actual-malice shield does not run in favour of the judiciary; its protection flows from the law of contempt (criticism of judicial conduct is free, imputations to judges are not) — ¶26(3) proviso; Case Book pp. 62–63.
  4. No State libel. “The State has no cause of action… the “right to privacy” cannot be invoked by the State… [to] shield its officers…”. Also — “[t]he State… cannot maintain… an action for defamation” (¶26(4) — “the State… has… no freedom of the press; the freedom is of the citizens”).
  5. Prior restraint — rare. “[T]he law permits… no previous restraint… on publications” (¶22, Pentagon Papers adopted); the trial-court may restrain in aid of fair trial; but here the petitioners were only restrained regarding the private lives of the named individuals.
  6. Private individuals protected; stolen-source caveat. “[A] person… who is not a public official… the publication… of… his private life… is an invasion actionable…” (¶26(6)); the Court refused the State’s injunction as to the public-record portions but held Nakkheeran must prove a lawful source for the rest — “so long as the petitioners… do not establish… lawful source… they should not publish” (¶28–29). And crucially: “Once a matter becomes… a matter of public record, no… prior restraint… [even] the truth or falsity… is for the post-publication process. — the Court did not license theft: the acquisition remains answerable under the general law, which is why the petitioners had to show the source.

Applied: the Court (¶¶27–29) allowed publication from public records but prohibited the magazine “from publishing… the private life of Auto Shankar… the victims… [and] persons… [not] connected with his public acts”, and left the “Auto Shankar willing/not willing” question — he had himself wanted the story out (¶¶5–6 of the excerpt) “so long as… the petitioners get [the material] from court record”. This “case-by-case” balancing was declared the method: “the right to privacy… will necessarily have to go through a process of case-by-case development” (¶¶19, 27).

Principles evolved
  • The six-rule synthesis above (DU’s “public person / reckless disregard / right to reply” cluster answers from ¶26).
  • Presumption against prior restraint in India (with the fair-trial exception) — decades before Sahara’s postponement-order doctrine.
  • No State defamation; judiciary treated distinctly; “right to know the record of public offices.”
  • Lawful-source discipline for media; stolen/public-record bifurcation (foundation of §3.4).
  • “Chilling effect” in Indian constitutional speech law was born here (¶19 — quoted in Shreya).
Critique

(i) The Court imported Sullivan’s substantive rule without American procedural package (no summary judgment rules; burden still on plaintiff); (ii) “case-by-case” invites uncertainty but suits Art. 19(2) architecture; (iii) its “tort-of-privacy” framing was constitutionalised in 2017 (Puttaswamy) — today read both together: Rajagopal = the media’s privacy constitution, Puttaswamy = the citizen’s; (iv) its principle (5) is the doctrinal ancestor of Swatanter Kumar’s “postponement instead of ban” and X v. UOI’s “take-down, not gag”.

Case Dossier ⑧ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 3 · 2021 SCC OnLine Del 1788 — Case Book pp. 196–220

X v. Union of India (W.P.(CRL) 1082/2020, Delhi High Court, Anup Jairam Bhambhani, J.) the take-down template · right to be forgotten in practice

Facts (¶¶1–5, as printed)

The petitioner’s photographs, posted on her private Facebook and Instagram accounts “with the requisite ‘privacy settings’ activated”, were taken without consent by an unknown entity (“Desi Collector”) and uploaded to the pornographic site xHamster with captions, making them “offensive by association”; ~15,000 views within a week; complaints to the National Cyber-Crime Reporting Portal and police went nowhere; FIR 286/2020 (u/ss 354A IPC + 66C IT Act, PS Dwarka South) followed. The Delhi Police Cyber Cell (CyPAD) stated it was “ready and willing” but “by reason of technological limitations… could not… entirely efface” the content, while “errant parties merrily continued to re-post… thereby cocking-a-snook at directions” — giving the judgment its famous opening: “The internet never sleeps; and the internet never forgets.” (¶1). The Court appointed Dr Pavan Duggal, Advocate as amicus (¶7).

Issues (framed vide order 7.8.2020, ¶¶10–11)
  1. What directions make a removal order “implementable and effective”, and against whom (hosts, platforms, search engines, police, MeitY)?
  2. How to prevent “resurfacing” of content and contempt-by-repost despite court orders — i.e. can courts craft a continuing, automated take-down?
Arguments / submissions

Petitioner: publication violates Art. 21 (dignity, informational privacy) and is an IT Act offence (s.67 etc.); intermediaries gain “knowledge” upon court notice; global de-indexing sought. Amicus (Dr Duggal): traced the statutory architecture reproduced in the case-book (ss.2, 66, 67/67A/67B/67C, 69A, 75, 79, 81, 85; Blocking Rules 2009 r.10 — court-order route; Intermediary Rules 2011 — the 36-hour take-down duty (r.3(2)(b)); 2021 Rules — r.3(1)(b)(i)–(x) content prohibitions (“belongs to another person… defamatory… invasive of another’s privacy… including bodily privacy… patently false… impersonat[ing]…”), r.3(2)(a) grievance officer “acknowledge within 24 hours… dispose within 15 days”, r.3(2)(b) — 24-hour take-down of material “exposing the private area… nudity… sexual act… impersonation… artificially morphed images”, r.4 SSMI duties (Chief Compliance Officer, 24×7 nodal officer, monthly compliance reports, traceability r.4(2) proviso — noting the SC had stayed 2021 Rules portions; “no view on vires” — ¶29) — and the comparative law: Google Spain; X v. Twitter (NSW — “equitable obligation of confidence… on notice… liable to be restrained”); YouTube v. Geeta Shroff (upload-from-India → global takedown; foreign upload → geo-block). MeitY: direct the “appropriate government”/agency route; “the internet be kept free from editorial intervention”; role of search engines “reactive”. Google LLC: “a further balancing act… freedom of speech and privatised censorship”; its template — host-site order → organic search removal; delisting only where a legal right is demonstrated. Delhi Police: use s.79(3)(a)&(b) + r.3(2) of the 2011 Rules with “unique identifiers… URL, Account ID, Handle name, IP Address, hash value, pixel matching, structural similarity index”.

Decision — the directions (¶92)
  1. Petitioner to furnish to the IO, within 24 hrs, all Image URL & Web URL particulars of the offending content;
  2. Delhi Police/CyPAD to remove/disable access from all websites and platforms “forthwith and in any event within 24 hours” (xHamster had already removed — noted);
  3. Google Search, Yahoo, Bing, DuckDuckGo directed to globally de-index and de-reference the content, including all pages/sub-pages/sub-directories hosting it;
  4. search engines to “endeavour to use automated tools… proactively identify and globally disable” exactly identical re-postings;
  5. IO to communicate URLs + the judgment to every directed entity (24 hrs) and police to obtain “URL, account ID, handle name, IP Address, hash value” etc. from platforms for the investigation (72 hrs);
  6. continuing liberty for the petitioner to notify the IO of any new re-posting, and a “corresponding direction to the IO to notify such website/online platform or search engine(s) to comply… immediately and in any event within 72 hours” — no fresh application to court;
  7. where removal is impossible, the intermediary must report to the court within the stipulated time “with cogent reasons”;
  8. non-compliance → forfeiture of the s.79(1) safe harbour (via s.79(3)(a)&(b) and r.7 of the 2021 Rules) and exposure to action under s.85 IT Act (offences by companies); courts remain free to refer the matter for blocking under s.69A + 2009 Rules r.10 (¶91(ix)).
Principles evolved
  • Operational Indian right to be forgotten: de-indexing + de-referencing + automated duplicate-suppression, ordered by courts and executed through the IT Act, not through a general censorship of the internet.
  • “Actual knowledge” (Shreya) = court order/government notification — after which the intermediary can claim no immunity (bridging Unit 2.1 ↔ 2.2).
  • Victim-first balance: the Court refused to record “certain other details… for reasons of privacy and confidentiality” (¶2) — i.e., judgments themselves anonymise; and it declined to opine on the 2021 Rules’ vires — judicial restraint mirroring the Rajagopal “case-by-case” method.
Why DU reads it under 2.2 (privacy) & 4.3 (obscenity) simultaneously

Same order, two lessons: (i) it is the “non-consensual pornography/removal” template (also PYQ Dec-2024 Q.7 — see §4); (ii) its “unique-identifier + always-on-injunction” mechanism is the machinery any obscenity/content-regulation answer must now describe. Critically: the order’s reliance on “hash values/pixel matching” precedes statutory “proactive measurement” duties, and DPDP Act 2023 (s.6/8 erasure-cum-correction duties; s.12 rights; s.17(2) journalism exemption) is the statutory sequel the exam will start expecting. Real-life echoes: the 2023–24 “celebrity-morphing” matters — including an order of the Delhi High Court invoking precisely this template to direct Google and social-media platforms to disable AI-morphed images of film stars — show the mechanism in live use; Anil Kapoor (2023) is the publicity-rights twin. These are also, almost verbatim, the facts of DU’s Dec-2024 Q.7.

Case Dossier ⑨ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 4 · 207 (2014) DLT 221 — Case Book pp. 65–94

Swatanter Kumar v. The Indian Express Ltd. & Ors. (Delhi High Court, Manmohan, J., 2014) “reckless disregard” applied to a retired SC Judge; postponement orders; media trials

Plaint
Suit for permanent injunction + damages (≥ ₹5 crore) against (1) The Indian Express Ltd.; (2) its reporter Maneesh Chibber; (3) Bennett, Coleman (Times Now); (4) GBN/CNN-IBN (Turner); (5) the “Intern” (identity protected); (6) Union of India (MIB) as necessary party.
Context
decided during Jan–Feb 2014; the Court relied on Sahara India Real Estate v. SEBI (2012) 7 SCC 425 (reprinted in the case-book at pp. 294–317) and Rajagopal; the interlocutory nature matters — the Court stressed “prima facie”.
Facts (¶¶1–17 of the order, as printed at pp. 74–80)

The plaintiff is Swatanter Kumar, a former Judge of the Supreme Court of India (elevated 18.12.2009; resigned 19.12.2012 to chair the National Green Tribunal) — an “eminent lawyer for 23 years”, ex-Judge of the Punjab & Haryana and Delhi High Courts, “illustrious… impeccable reputation” (¶¶3, 55). On 30.11.2013 a person who claimed to have “interned” under him at the Supreme Court sent an affidavit alleging sexual harassment to the Chief Justice of India. On 10.1.2014 the Indian Express (reporter Maneesh Chibber) published “Another intern alleges sexual harassment by another SC Judge” without the affidavit in its possession and without verification (¶¶8–9); Times Now ran a prime-time debate with tickers — “If a sitting Supreme Court Judge has sexually harassed his intern, should his name be made public?” “If Justice AK Ganguly’s name was made public, should the Judge’s name be made public…?” (¶10) — plus a public poll on Facebook on whether to disclose the name (¶13); CNN-IBN (Rajdeep Sardesai’s show) “allegedly with a view to steal a march over… TRPs… proceeded to name the plaintiff” (¶13); Times Now’s anchor repeatedly displayed his photograph (¶14). The next day, the Express front page published the complainant’s graphic words — “Justice S Kumar… put his right arm around me, kissed me on my left shoulder… I was shocked” — with his photograph, on the website already at 11:20 p.m. on 10.1.2014 (¶12). The plaintiff’s version: the complaint is “baseless, fraudulent and motivated”; the copy circulated to the media had the names blacked out (¶15); no F.I.R. or inquiry had ever tested the allegation (¶53).

Issues
  1. Whether a restrained (postponement) order against further publication of unverified allegations is permissible at all — or is it forbidden prior restraint (Rajagopal ¶22 vs Sahara ¶¶50–60)?
  2. Whether the publications were “fair reporting” or fell into “reckless and irresponsible… media trial” territory — and what standard governs a public person’s(retired judge’s) complaint: defamation? privacy? both?
  3. Whether photographs and headlines that “connect” a named person with untested allegations may be ordered deleted and withheld, and whether the MIB/Union can be directed to regulate?
  4. Whether the complainant’s identity/privacy also commands protection while her complaint pends?
Arguments

Plaintiff (Mr Rohatgi, Sr. Adv.): excessive adverse publicity beyond fair reporting injures reputation and the fair administration of justice; the Court has “inherent power… to interdict and pass interim orders including postponement of the publications as per the settled law” (¶30–31 range of excerpt); no verification at all; TRP-chasing; the “institution of justice” degraded; “the media itself cannot form its own opinion and pre-judge the matter… otherwise it amounts to… Media Trial” (plaintiff’s submission as recorded); even assuming fairness, “the Court can proceed to prevent… on the basis of excessive prejudice” (¶62).
Defendants: merely reproduced an affidavit already before the CJI; right to report and public debate on a public-interest issue; “no danger of the plaintiff’s not getting a fair trial”; identity already out (¶¶38–45 range of the order); courts “cannot be made the complaint departments of the media”; no statutory identity-shield was engaged (s.228A family inapplicable); delay answered by the speed of republication — all as recorded in the excerpt.

Decision (¶¶59–66)
  • Interim restraint granted — “Prima facie, I find that the publications… connect the plaintiff… in a manner which creates a trial-by-media kind of situation by creating a sensation amongst the public by highlighting… mere allegations on the front pages” (¶59); “freedom of press cannot be extended beyond reporting of facts… freedom of speech… is a right… of the reader also?” — the Court recorded (¶55) the classic line “the freedom of press cannot be extended beyond reporting of facts”.
  • Operative directions (¶64): (a) restrained from “further publishing the write ups… or… telecast which highlights the allegations… particularly, without disclosing in the headlines… that they are mere allegations”; (b) same for print/electronic — and deletion of the offending content from the internet “within 24 hours”; (c) restrained from publishing the plaintiff’s photographs suggesting connection with the allegations + removal from internet (¶¶61, 64(c)).
  • Doctrinal scaffolding: “postponement orders… are a neutralising device… evolved by the Courts to balance interests of equal weightage — freedom of expression vis-à-vis freedom of trial… in the context of the law of contempt” (¶51 quoting Sahara); “guidelines on reporting cannot be framed across the board… Courts must examine the content and the context on a case-to-case basis” (¶52 quoting Sahara ¶32–33 — reproduced at case-book pp. 294–317); orders “for short duration… test of necessity and proportionality” (¶60 quoting Sahara ¶60); “no general law for courts to postpone publicity… depend[s] on the extent of prejudice… effect on individuals… over-riding necessity to curb the right to report…” (¶60 — quoting Sahara).
  • Complainant also protected: the Court itself anonymised the “intern”, keeping her identity confidential “with a view to safeguarding her dignity and maintaining her privacy” — and sealed the document (¶5); “assuming the complaint is found false… who would… return the repute and sufferings” (¶58); suggested a limitation period for belated sexual-harassment complaints after the POSH Act, 2013 (¶57) — a striking, and often-quoted, observation.
  • But: “the observations are prima facie… will not preclude the defendants from reporting the Court cases and happenings as facts which are covered [by the] ambit of fair reporting on the basis of true, correct and verified information” (¶66) — the door to responsible journalism left open.
Principles evolved
  • For media: “headlines + photograph + unverified complaint” = actionable even when the underlying complaint is genuine; the constitutional “actual malice” standard (Rajagopal ¶26(3)) bites — publishing without any attempt at verification against a public person is precisely “reckless disregard”.
  • Postponement (not prohibition) is the Indian remedy-of-choice when Art. 21 fair-trial/reputation values collide with 19(1)(a) — “a neutralising device… necessity + proportionality + short duration”.
  • “Fair reporting” is the safe harbour, and its boundary is verification: the Court twice recorded that defendants “are entitled to inform the public… Court orders and events… [but] media cannot form its own opinion and pre-judge”.
  • Reciprocity: even a defamation plaintiff must respect the complainant’s privacy (the sealed-cover practice) — privacy runs both ways.
Critique & what commentators say

Press bodies (PCI, editors) criticised the order as “equivalent to prior restraint” and “a chilling precedent for investigative reporting on the judiciary”; defenders point to (i) its interim, reversible character, (ii) the explicit fair-reporting carve-out, and (iii) that the alternative — letting a front-page “trial” run — forecloses the plaintiff’s Art. 21. It is the best Indian laboratory for the Rajagopal “case-by-case” method: pair it with Sahara (Case Book pp. 294–317) in the answer. The case-book prints this interim order; thereafter the suit proceeded to trial, with the restraints monitored by the Court from time to time.

Case Dossier ⑩ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 5 · (2009) 8 SCC 106 — Case Book pp. 95–113

R.K. Anand v. Registrar, Delhi High Court the BMW-case sting · contempt · “far greater stringency”

Bench (as printed)
Aftab Alam, J. for a Three-Judge Bench (himself, B.N. Agrawal & G.S. Singhvi, JJ.) — appeals under s.19(1), Contempt of Courts Act, 1971; decided 13.8.2009 (reported (2009) 8 SCC 106; the case-book prints the consolidated judgment).
Facts (¶¶1, 13–27, case-book pp. 95–101)

“The present is a fallout of a criminal trial arising from a hit-and-run accident on a cold winter morning in Delhi in which a car travelling at reckless speed crashed through a police check post and crushed to death six people, including three policemen” — the BMW trial (Sanjeev Nanda, 1999). The trial “meandered endlessly… even after eight years”, witnesses “flip-flopped”. In May 2007 NDTV aired a sting: its young reporter Poonam Agarwal and an ex-prosecution witness, Sunil Kulkarni, secretly recorded (with a microchip camera wired to his shirt) Kulkarni negotiating his “sell-out” with the defence — meetings with R.K. Anand, Sr. Advocate (counsel for Nanda), the Special Public Prosecutor I.U. Khan, and junior Bhagwan Sharma (¶¶1, 16–18): money, witness-management, even “how to get me removed from the case”. “Shocked by the programme the Delhi High Court suo motu” initiated contempt (W.P.(Crl) 796/2007), called for “the entire unedited original record of the sting operation… and the names of the employees/reporters of NDTV who were part of the said sting operation” (¶1, 21–27 range), and convicted Anand & Khan under s.2(c)(ii)&(iii) CoCA — 4 months’ prohibition from appearing, ₹2,000 fine, and a recommendation to the Full Court to divest them of Senior designation (¶1). A co-accused, “Lovely”, died before notice; Bhagwan Sharma was acquitted by the High Court. Both contemnors appealed (s.19(1), CoCA).

Issues (as framed by the Court)
  1. Whether the conduct of R.K. Anand (defence counsel) & I.U. Khan (prosecutor) fell within criminal contempt — “interference with / obstruction of the due course of judicial proceedings” — and what standard of proof (beyond reasonable doubt) applies;
  2. Whether the procedure adopted by the High Court (suo motu; inquiry under s.14(2) CoCA by a retired judge; leaving original microchips with NDTV) was lawful;
  3. Whether truth/“larger public interest” is available in contempt (s.13 as it stood pre-2006 — the truth defence came only with the 2006 amendment; s.3(1) innocent publication; s.5 fair criticism);
  4. Whether media sting operations and their telecast in sub-judice matters are lawful — i.e., “was this a media trial of the case?” and (v) “the role of NDTV in carrying out sting operations and telecasting the programme” (issue 5, case-book p. 100);
  5. Whether the privileges/immunities of counsel/prosecution (Advocates Act; statutory functions) sheltered their conduct.
Arguments

Contemnors (Gopal Subramaniam, P.P. Rao &c.): no interference with the trial — mere “professional misconduct”, not contempt; conversation snippets taken out of context; NDTV’s method illegal — the channel “set up the trap”, the Court used its product; the microchip originals not seized = tampering risk (chain of custody “seriously defective”); the s.14(2) inquiry was improper; the “beyond reasonable doubt” standard was not satisfied; and: a prosecutor talking to a witness was doing his job. Amici & Union (as recorded in the judgment): stings telecast before trial = “trial by media… no place for it… freedom of speech… the press or the electronic media do[es] not enjoy any right(s) superior to an individual citizen. Further, the right of free and fair trial was of far greater importance and in case of any conflict between free speech and fair trial, the latter must always get precedence” (¶169); s.3(3) proviso (unregistered newspaper) defeated the channel’s immunity; “larger public good” no legal defence; Saibal Kumar; In re P.C. Sen; Reliance Petrochemicals; M.P. Lohia cited. NDTV (Mr Salve): exposing “something foul concerning proceedings… is not contempt if… substantially accurate, bona fide and in public interest”; the two competing public interests must be balanced, the Court the “final arbiter”; and after 17.3.2006, s.13(b) truth defence squarely applies (¶170).

Decision
  • R.K. Anand’s conviction upheld; I.U. Khan’s set aside (¶206: “appeal of IU Khan is allowed… his conviction… set aside… the Full Court… may still consider whether or not to continue the honour of Senior Advocate… [for] R.K. Anand — appeal dismissed” with notice of enhancement of punishment; and direction that High Courts without rules under s.34 Advocates Act frame them “without further delay”).
  • On stings — the canonical passage (¶173): “the media… is not… free… to… obtain… the… sting… only… after… prior consent… of the court would ‘tantamount to pre-censorship of reporting of court proceedings… an infraction of 19(1)(a). This is, however, not to say that media is free to publish any kind of report concerning a sub-judice matter or to do a sting… in any manner they please. The legal parameter within which a report or comment on a sub-judice matter can be made is well defined… Compared to normal reporting, a sting operation is an incalculably more risky and dangerous thing to do. A sting is based on deception and, therefore, it would attract the legal restrictions with far greater stringency and any infraction would invite more severe punishment.”
  • Media-trial charge rejected: quoting the dictionary definition — “trial by media… creating a widespread perception of guilt regardless of any verdict… a lynch mob” (¶174) — the Court: “in light of the above it can hardly be said that the sting programme… was a media trial… [it] was not about the accused… [it] showed some people trying to subvert the BMW trial” (¶175).
  • Contempt analysis: s.3(1) immunity unavailable when publication occurs with knowledge of pendency — but “the programme… did not interfere with or obstruct the due course of the BMW trial… [it] was clearly intended to prevent the attempt to interfere” (¶178); s.13(b) truth (2006) and “public interest” — the Court held the telecast “was indeed in larger public interest and it served an important public cause” (¶179); excesses (¶181: “regrettable overreach… many instances… actually incorrect and misleading”) did not convert it into contempt.
  • Evidence & procedure: leaving originals with NDTV “strange” but “not fatal” once copies seized — and had the accused denied the recordings, “the place of NDTV was along with the appellants facing the charge of contempt” (¶¶132–136); the s.14(2) inquiry by a retired High Court Judge was proper (¶¶95–100 of the judgment).
  • Self-regulation & the media’s own duty (¶¶194–198): “for all its faults the stings… rendered valuable service… We appreciate the professional initiative and courage shown by the young reporter Poonam Agarwal… the privately-run TV channels… are very young… The better news channels… are second to none in the world… but the media trips mostly on TRPs — when commercial considerations assume dominance over higher standards of professionalism… the norms to regulate the media and to raise its professional standards must come from inside — any attempt to control and regulate the media from outside is likely to cause more harm than good.”
Principles evolved
  • Stings are not illegal per se; their legality turns on truth, context, sub-judice status and purpose (prevent obstruction vs create prejudice).
  • Sub-judice reporting is bounded by the Contempt Act (ss.2, 3, 5, 13); stings attract “far greater stringency”; “no prior permission for reporting, but consequences follow breach”.
  • Contempt conviction of counsel for attempting to suborn a witness — a rare, stern affirmation that “officers of the court” owe duties even beyond ordinary citizens.
  • The Court is arbiter, not client: “it would be a sad day for the court to employ the media for setting its own house in order; and media too would certainly not relish the role of being the snoopers for the court” (¶173 — one of the most-quotable sentences in the unit).
Read with

M.P. Lohia v. State of W.B. (Case Book pp. 136–137: gag orders “should be last resort”; “trial by media… interference with fair administration of justice”); Reliance Petrochemicals (pp. 278–293); the Sahara postponement code (pp. 294–317); and, for the sting-misuse side, Rajat Prasad & Uma Khurana (dossiers below).

Case Dossier ⑪ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 6 · (2014) 6 SCC 495 — Case Book pp. 114–124

Rajat Prasad v. CBI the bribe-giving sting, criminalised at the margin · entrapment jurisprudence

Bench
Ranjan Gogoi, J. for himself, P. Sathasivam (CJI) & N.V. Ramana, JJ. (decided 3.4.2014)
Charges
s.120-B IPC r/w s.12 Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (→ today BNS 61 r/w s.12 PC Act; s.12 PC Act unchanged) — against the “sting operators”, not the Minister.
Facts (¶¶1–6, case-book pp. 114–116)

16.11.2003, the Indian Express (Delhi edition) ran “Caught on Tape: Union Minister Taking Cash — saying ‘money is no less than God’”: hidden-camera footage of Dalip Singh Judeo, Union MoS for Environment & Forests, receiving ₹9 lakh in Hotel Taj Mahal Room 822 from “Rahul @ Bhupinder Singh Patel” (A-3), with the Minister’s APS (A-2) present; the room’s hidden camera had been installed by A-4 through an electronics dealer (Manoj Hora), suite bookings in fictitious names by A-6 (Rajat Prasad — a PR consultant), the operation conceived by A-5, Amit Jogi, son of the then Chief Minister of Chhattisgarh, to “discredit” the Minister on the eve of State elections (¶¶4–5). The tape was “subsequently released to the media”; FSL Hyderabad authenticated it. A 5.12.2005 chargesheet under s.12 PC Act r/w s.120B IPC; charges framed (2007); Delhi HC declined to quash (S.482) — hence the appeals by the accused “sting financiers”.

Issues
  1. Whether a sting operation “prompted by overwhelming public interest” can attract criminal conspiracy/abetment liability at all — i.e., whether the commission of one crime to expose another is obliterated by “public interest”.
  2. What is the position of entrapment in Indian law (comparative: U.S./Canada/UK), and is the sting operator’s mens rea for s.107/120B IPC imputed?
  3. Whether the charge is sustainable when the alleged conspiracy was against A-1 (to expose him) rather than with him; and whether, at the s.482 stage, the trial must run to determine intent (motive vs intent).
Arguments

Appellants (Uday Lalit & P.S. Narsimha, Sr. Counsels): journalistic motive recorded in the chargesheet itself; ingredients of criminal conspiracy (common object; plan; agreement) absent; “conspiracy to defame ≠ conspiracy to bribe”; the bribe was a sham payment — no criminal intent; A-4’s only role was arranging recording equipment; A-6’s role exhausted in a hotel booking for a witness who “developed cold feet” (¶8) — quash. ASG P.P. Malhotra: giving the bribe to a Minister in exchange for favours is itself abetment within s.107; “pious desire to expose corruption” does not “obliterate the criminal act”; whether the money was real or “pretence” is a matter of evidence after trial (¶9).

Decision — Ranjan Gogoi, J.
  • Appeals dismissed; charges stand (trial to record the evidence on intent).
  • Sting definition & ethics (¶10): “The expression ‘sting operation’ seems to have emerged from the title of a popular movie called ‘The Sting’ (1973)… Being essentially a deceptive operation, though designed to nab a criminal, a sting operation raises certain moral and ethical questions. The victim, who is otherwise innocent, is lured into committing a crime on the assurance of absolute secrecy… raising the potential question as to how such a victim can be held responsible for the crime which he would not have committed but for the enticement. Another issue… is the fact that the means deployed to establish the commission of the crime itself involves a culpable act.”
  • Comparative law (¶¶11–14): U.S. — “unlike the U.S… the same is not the position in India”: entrapment defence; Sherman v. U.S. — “the trap for the unwary innocent and the trap for the unwary criminal”; “the crime must not be the product of the creative activity of law-enforcement officials” (Sorrells); Canada — R. v. Mack’s ten-factor inducement test (reproduced in full at ¶12); U.K. — R. v. Sang: no substantive defence; mitigation or stay for abuse of process (R. v. Loosely, paras 16–17 quoted: “Entrapment goes to the propriety of there being a prosecution at all… having regard to the state’s involvement”).
  • The core doctrine (¶15): “A crime does not stand obliterated or extinguished merely because its commission is claimed to be in public interest. Any such principle would be abhorrent to our criminal jurisprudence. At the same time the criminal intent… will have to be established before the liability… [s.107/s.120A IPC (now BNS) require] a culpable mental state…”; the Court also flagged the suspicious 12-day delay between the sting and its release, and “the inherent possibilities of abuse of the operation as videographed — namely, retention and use thereof to ensure delivery of the favours assured by the receiver of the bribe — [which] has to be excluded before liability can be attributed or excluded”.
  • The journalism shield (¶16): “A journalist or any other citizen who has no connection, even remotely, with the favour that is allegedly sought in exchange for the bribe offered, cannot be imputed with the necessary intent to commit the offence of abetment… or conspiracy… Non-applicability of the aforesaid provisions… may be ex-facie apparent… The cause of journalism and its role and responsibility in spreading information and awareness will stand subserved. It is only in cases where the question reasonably arises whether the sting operator had a stake in the favours… that the issue will require determination… in the course of a full-fledged trial.”
Principles evolved
  • Sting ≠ immunity: exposing a crime does not legalise committing one; abetment/conspiracy liability of the operator is judged on mens rea.
  • Two classes of media stings crystallise: (a) “recording-only” stings — shielded (¶16); (b) “stake-holding” stings — inducement, political motive, or benefit → full trial (¶¶15–16).
  • Indian entrapment posture: no codified defence; courts take account of police/media-created crime through mens rea and (post-Rajat Prasad) through abuse-of-process arguments; law-enforcement stings remain “yet to be experimented” (¶14).
Aftermath & examples

The Minister’s own trial (s.7 PC Act) continued on other material; the case remains the standard citation for the proposition that “the end does not sanctify the means” — and the source for the Law Commission’s 2010 question whether s.24 PC Act-type immunity (unwilling bribe-giver who reports promptly is “not deemed to have abetted”) should be extended to journalists. Real-world: Tehelka editors were in fact prosecuted after Operation X (criminal conspiracy + cheating charges for the arms-deal sting, later discharged) — the exact Rajat-Prasad pattern. Pair with the Uma Khurana dossier (misuse → civil damages) to complete the misuse map: R.K. Anand (media wins for truth) — Rajat Prasad (media’s methods may be criminal) — Uma Khurana (media pays damages for falsity).

Case Dossier ⑫ · 2.2 Syllabus Case 7 · 146 (2008) DLT 429 — Case Book pp. 124–129

Court on Its Own Motion v. State (the “Uma Khurana” fake-sting case) the Delhi High Court’s 12-point sting guidelines

Bench
Division Bench: Mukundakam Sharma, J. (as she then was) & Khanna, J. — Case Book caption: (2008) 146 DLT 429, decided in August 2008
Facts (¶¶1–4, case-book pp. 124–125)

On 30.8.2007 the Noida-based news channel Live India telecast a “sting operation” allegedly showing teachers of the Directorate of Education (NCT of Delhi), including Ms. Uma Khurana (PGT Economics), demanding money from parents for marks. A mob attacked the school; the teachers were manhandled; Ms. Khurana was suspended, then dismissed from service (¶2). Subsequent police investigation (and the channel’s own confession in parts) revealed that the “sting” was manufactured: persons with an axe to grind against the teacher — the narrative recorded by the Court — arranged it, with an aspiring journalist casting acquaintances (one posing as a parent) to induce the teachers on camera; the tapes were edited; the “bribe” scene was created by inducement, not recorded. A revenge-fabrication became prime-time news; the Hindustan daily of 7.9.2007 carried it as fact (¶3). A Hindi daily (Hindustan, 7.9.2007) reproduced it as fact (¶3). A Delhi HC judge learned of it and the Court took suo motu note in pending proceedings (¶¶1, 22: “we discharge the notices suo motu issued by us to the State”).

Issues
  1. Whether the State/Channel are answerable when a “sting” is fabricated; is there any law governing media stings?
  2. Whether entrapment by journalists is permissible at all — the line between investigative journalism and creating a crime;
  3. Whether a self-regulatory code &/or statutory guidelines should govern telecast of stings; role of the MIB/NBA/PCI;
  4. Remedies for the victim (service, reputation, damages).
Arguments

The amici and counsel appearing (the judgment records a line-up of senior counsel assisting the Court and of counsel for the channels and the News Broadcasting Standards Authority) — argued for statutory guidelines; counsel for the channels resisted “prior permission” regimes as prior restraint, invoking public-interest journalism (R.K. Anand was on record); the State pleaded helplessness — no law; the victim’s counsel sought service reinstatement + damages.

Decision (¶¶15–22)
  • Remedial: damages “left open” to Ms. Khurana — “she could file an appropriate case for causing damage to her reputation and for the mental agony and physical hardship… Damages in accordance with the law can be awarded by Courts in cases of defamation and loss of reputation” (¶13); Directorate of Education directed to reconsider her dismissal (¶14).
  • The ethics rule (¶¶15–19): “Such incidents should not happen and false and fabricated sting operations directly infringing upon a person’s right to privacy should not recur because of desire to earn more and to have higher TRP ratings. Right to freedom of press is a valuable right but the right carries with it responsibility and duty to be truthful and to protect rights of others.” (¶15). Quoting Barkha Dutt’s own column (“something unseemly and mildly sleazy about reporters playing Pied Pipers… much easier to justify the hidden camera when it is capturing the event that would take place whether or not the camera was there… Entrapment… can’t shake off the suggestion of fabrication” — ¶16); adopting Jacobson v. United States, 503 US 540 (¶17): law-protectors must not “originate a criminal design, implant in an innocent person’s mind a disposition to commit a criminal act, and then induce commission of the crime so that the Government may prosecute”; “we are also of the view… the media… is not to test individuals by… the ‘inducement test’… the individual may as well claim that the person offering inducement is equally guilty” (¶19); “a hidden camera cannot be allowed to depict something which is not true… and is not happening but has happened because of inducement” (¶18).
  • Self-regulation call (¶19): “all TV channels/Media shall take steps and prohibit its reporters from producing or airing any programme which is based on entrapment… responsible and senior TV journalists/reporters and editors… should take steps for drawing up a self-regulatory code of conduct. The Press Council of India should also examine and can take initiative in this regard.”
  • The 12-point guidelines (¶20 — reproduce in the exam; the case-book prints all of them): (1) a certificate of genuineness from the person who recorded/produced the sting; (2) concurrent record in writing of the various stages; (3) permission to telecast from an MIB-appointed committee — headed by a retired High Court Judge appointed in consultation with the HC, plus a member not below Additional Secretary and the Additional Commissioner of Police — “permission… after satisfying itself that it is in public interest to telecast… This safeguard is necessary since those who mount a sting operation themselves commit the offences of impersonation, criminal trespass under false pretence and making a person commit an offence”; (4) unedited films/tapes to be preserved & produced (transcript may be edited); (5) scheduling with audience-awareness (children, “avoid shocking or offending”); (6) compliance with the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 & the Programme Code Rules; (7) Chief Editor responsible for self-regulation; (8) subject-matter rules — no unverified facts as true (“a man is innocent till proven guilty by law” — trial-by-media ban), no misleading mixing of facts and fiction, no distortion by over/under-emphasis, “no making public any… material relating to an individual’s personal or private affairs or which invades an individual’s privacy unless there is an identifiable large public interest”, nothing “likely to create public panic or unnecessary alarm which… lead[s] to disorder or [is] offensive to public or religious feeling”; (9) “general community standards of decency and civility… particular care to protect the interest and sensitivities of children”; (10) “News should be reported with due accuracy… verification (to the fullest extent possible)…”; (11) “Infringement of privacy in a news-based/related programme is a sensitive issue… greater degree of responsibility”; (12) “Channels must not use material relating to persons’ personal or private affairs… unless there is an identifiable larger public interest”.
  • Disposition (¶21–22): “The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting is already examining whether a statute and/or a code of conduct should be enacted. The above proposed guidelines should be considered by the concerned Ministry and if they find favour, they may be incorporated in the enactment/guidelines…”, notices discharged — the Court declined to make the guidelines binding law; Parliament never legislated. (The Law Commission then issued its 2010 Consultation Paper — the questionnaire reproduced in the project material — and the reforms stalled; the field today is occupied by the BBCI/NBWO self-regulation + Broadcasting Act, 2023 Codes.)
Principles evolved
  • Fabricated “stings” are actionable invasions of privacy — not protected speech; inducement-based stings are condemned for both ethics and criminality.
  • The “capture-what-was-going-to-happen-anyway” criterion (via Jacobson) is the working test for legitimate investigative journalism (adopted later in the Consultation Paper & discussed in R.K. Anand ¶173).
  • Interim “quasi-statutory” guidelines as a technique where the legislature sleeps — the only Indian judicial code for stings; cited by the Law Commission (2010) and the Delhi HC’s own 2007 order (14.12.2007).
Links

Q1 · Colonial vs contemporary media laws — model answer

Thesis. Colonial media law was a law of control — licence, deposit, seizure, sedition — aimed at suppressing a subject press; independent India’s framework is textually a charter of liberty with enumerated limits, yet the colonial architecture survives in the statute-book (the sedition lineage now BNS 152; the OSA 1923; the “enmity” offences now BNS 196/197) while every new medium has drawn a new regulatory statute. A high-scoring answer shows both rupture and continuity.

(i) The colonial stack. Wellesley’s Censorship of Press Regulations, 1799 (no printing without licence; printer/publisher names on every sheet; foreign papers banned) — the “declaration & name-plate” idea still lives in the Press and Registration of Books Act, 1867 (whose s.5 registration rules the Contempt Act itself borrows via s.3(3) proviso, quoted in R.K. Anand); Adam’s Regulation, 1823 (licence of Government, summary seizure); Metcalfe’s Act, 1835 (the liberalisation — “liberty of the press” by abolition of pre-licence; its premise: a free press is the best antidote to sedition); Licensing Act, 1857 (post-1857 — Magistrate’s licence, heavy penalty, ban powers); Vernacular Press Act, 1878 (Lytton’s “Gagging Act” — targeted “seditious writing” in Indian-language papers; forfeiture of press; Amrita Bazar Patrika famously turned overnight into an English daily to escape it; repealed 1882); IPC 1860 ss. 124A, 153A, 295A, 505; Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908 (Bombay) & Part II of the Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1908 (special courts, security, forfeiture, ban on “seditious” publications); Official Secrets Act, 1923; Dramatic Performances Act, 1876; the Rowlatt era. Tilak’s two sedition trials (1897 — Queen-Empress v. Bal Gangadhar Tilak 22 Bom 1128; 1908; and his Privy Council appeal (1909) narrowing “disaffection”) are the human centre of this chapter — the “incitement”/“exciting feelings of enmity” distinction the Privy Council drew is precisely the one the Supreme Court kept in Kedar Nath (1962) and the one Shreya ¶¶25, 38–41 re-argued.

(ii) Independent India’s framework. Art. 19(1)(a) (no separate press clause — freedom of the press “implicit”, Romesh Thappar: “freedom of the press lies at the foundation of all democratic organisations… free political discussion… without which public education… cannot be carried on in a manner which would assist the propagandist activities of the parties in return for the freedom”); the 1st Amendment, 1951 (adding public order, decency/morality); Romesh Thappar (1950) & Brij Bhushan (1950) striking colonial-style bans and pre-censorship; Sakal Papers (1962) & Bennett Coleman (1973) killing economic strangulation; the Emergency Press Censorship (1975–77) and the post-Emergency corrective — Press Council Act, 1978 (statutory self-regulation: s.13 functions, s.14 censure, no punitive powers); Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (defining and limiting contempt, s.3–7 safe harbours, truth defence added 2006); Cinematograph Act, 1952 (certification, not colonial licence — K.A. Abbas); Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 + Programme Code (regulating carriage + content standards by rules); IT Act 2000 — and Shreya Singhal (2015) reasserting the post-colonial rule of law over executive-cyber paternalism; PRB Act, 1867 penal provisions repealed (2018/19) — the last colonial-era press-punishment ghost removed; RPA 1951 s.123(3A)? (1966) converting hate speech into electoral liability; Young Persons (Harmful Publications) Act, 1956 (a 1950s heir to the “corruption of youth” anxiety). The trajectory: from “who may print?” to “what may be proved after printing?”.

(iii) The comparison (answer core). Structure of restriction: colonial — executive discretion + prior restraint, reviewable only for mala fides; contemporary — restrictions only through an Art. 19(2) head + reasonableness (Chintaman Rao) + proximate nexus (Lohia; Shreya), with the court as final arbiter (“assurance of good administration” insufficient — Shreya ¶92). Object: colonial law protected the State from the people (disaffection = sedition); contemporary law protects the people from each other (defamation, privacy, hate, contempt) and permits only that much State-protection that Part III allows (CAB ¶189’s “societal interest” reading). Instrument: forfeiture & licence (1857/1878) vs post-publication sanction: prosecution (BNS 196/299/356…), damages, take-down/blocking orders (X v. UOI), regulatory codes (Programme Code; IT Rules 2021). Medium: print-only anxiety vs medium-neutral speech doctrine (Shreya ¶29/86 via CAB ¶37) — yet each new medium draws its own statute (TV: 1995; digital: 2000/2011/2021/2023; films: 1952) — the “regulatory whack-a-mole” critique. Penology: transportation of editors vs mens-rea-gated criminalisation with procedural safeguards (sanction CrPC 196→BNSS 218; seizure CrPC 95/96→BNSS 110/111; 1-year limitation; appeal s.19).

(iv) “Catalyst for mass awakening.” The repressive Acts unintentionally created the Indian public sphere: the Vernacular Press Act unified print-opinion across presidencies; the 1908 Act made editors national figures (Tilak; Gandhi’s Young India — sedition sentence 1922, “I am engaged… in the service of the nation”; B.G. Horniman’s 1919 deportation from Bombay turned a journalist into a martyr). The Freedoms of the press movement became the training ground for the constitutional settlement of 1949–51: the framers deliberately placed the press inside 19(1)(a) — as Beg J. later said, “the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy” (Bennett Coleman) — and gave the courts, not the Secretary, the last word.

Conclusion. “Independent India inherited the master’s tools but, through Part III adjudication, dismantled the master’s house — imperfectly. OSA 1923 survives; sedition found a narrower heir (BNS 152); UAPA (2019) supplies the modern cousin of the 1908 Act. That is precisely why, post-Shreya and post-Puttaswamy, freedom-of-press questions remain the most-litigated part of our constitutional law: the colonial question — how far may the State trust its people with words? — is still being answered, one statute at a time.”

Q2 · HATE SPEECH — trend, characteristics, Supreme Court guidelines — full model answer

Introduction. Art. 19(1)(a) protects even “unpopular views” (S. Khushboo; Shreya ¶83 — “mere advocacy… of an unpopular opinion may not be curbed”), but no right exists to destroy the equality on which the right rests. Indian law has no definition of hate speech; the Law Commission (267th Report, 2018) described it as “incitement to hatred primarily against a group of persons defined in terms of race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, religious belief and the like”. Constitutionally, regulation runs through Art. 19(2) heads — public order, decency or morality, defamation and incitement to an offence; hence every hate-speech law must show (i) the head, (ii) the proximate nexus (Ram Manohar Lohia, quoted in Shreya ¶22), and (iii) a mens rea high enough not to swallow innocent speech.

(a) The increasing trend (3 phases). (1) Print era: communal pamphlets prosecuted under the 1908/153A lineage — the Ramji Lal Modi jurisprudence was forged there. (2) Live-TV era (2003–): prime-time debate formats converted enmity into entertainment — Amish Devgan itself arose from a TV show; the Delhi HC’s 2008 “trial by media” observations (Uma Khurana) and the Rajya Sabha Committee notes (12.12.2008) are documents of this era. (3) Platform era: algorithmic amplification — WhatsApp forwards, YouTube rants, coordinated handle-campaigns; the Supreme Court noted in Amish Devgan that “home speeches” acquire statewide harm “as the said speeches are now uploaded and circulated in the virtual world”. Institutional markers: ECI/MCC violation tallies run into hundreds per general election; Hate Speech Watch data show electoral clustering; repeat speakers with impunity; and the 2018 Supreme Court (Tehseen Poonawalla) linked hate speech to organised mob violence. Structural features: electorally productive; organisationally sponsored; platform-mediated; repeat-offence-prone; and harmful through “silent” stigmatisation even absent violence (Amish Devgan).

(b) Key characteristics (doctrinal). (1) Group-directedness: hate “which tends to vilify, humiliate and incite hatred or violence against the target group based upon identity of the group” (Amish Devgan) — speech aimed at identity, not at policy; (2) Identity-immutable harm → dignity (Arts. 14, 21) injury, not merely offence; (3) Layered offences: s.153A (→ BNS 196) “promotes or attempts to promote… feelings of enmity…” — “likely to promote” suffices, but intent/knowledge required by the section itself; s.295A (→ BNS 299) “deliberate and malicious” intent mandatory — “insults to religion offered unwittingly or carelessly… [do not attract] criminality” (Amish Devgan restating Ramji Lal Modi); s.505(2) (→ BNS 353); s.298 (→ BNS 302); RPA s.123(3A) — promoting enmity during elections = corrupt practice; (4) advocacy vs incitement: Kedar Nath’s “pernicious tendency or intention of creating public disorder”; Arup Bhuyan (2011) — incitement to imminent lawless action; Shreya ¶40 — “no imminent causal connection” = no restriction; (5) context-as-evidence: the Amish Devgan indicia — content expresses hatred; intent judged objectively; harm (physical or psychosocial); audience/platform; two communities must be involved; “reasonable, strong-minded person” standard (not the hypersensitive listener); “no redeeming purpose”; and “persons of influence… owe a duty and have to be more responsible”; (6) procedural architecture that keeps the law speech-protective: sanction (CrPC 196 → BNSS 218), seizure only under CrPC 95/96 (→ BNSS 110/111, with HC representation and a three-judge bench; and the sanction requirement itself (CrPC 196 → BNSS 218), together with the Shreya ¶101 observation that these safeguards “reveal a certain degree of sensitivity to the fundamental right to free speech”.

(c) The Supreme Court’s guidelines against hate speech — five clusters. (1) Pravasi Bhalai Sangathan v. UOI (2014) 6 SCC 473 — the Court declined to frame guidelines, said “existing penal law provides sufficient remedy”, and referred the subject to the Law Commission (→ 267th Report, 2018, proposing new offences of incitement to hatred (153C) and causing fear/alarm/violence (505A) — not enacted). (2) Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. UOI (2018) 2 SCC 501 — a 3-Judge Bench treated hate speech as the “root” of mob violence: (i) every district to designate a senior police officer in each district to take timely preventive measures; (ii) prompt FIRs, speedy investigation & trial; (iii) designated fast-track courts in every district; (iv) videography of trials to protect evidence; (v) victim/witness protection and compensation schemes; (vi) monitoring of “grievous social media” content; and it urged Parliament to enact a stand-alone law. (3) Amish Devgan v. UOI (2021) 5 SCC 542 — the operative “guidelines” of today: (i) registration of FIR is mandatory for a cognizable hate-speech complaint (s.154 CrPC → BNSS 173) — the court will not shut its eyes because the speech is “only words”; (ii) quashing is the exception at the pre-investigation stage (the petitioner must show the extreme Babubhai case); (iii) multiplicity cure — for one speech with FIRs in several States, “the subsequent FIRs are transferred to the jurisdiction of the first FIR and treated as statements under s.161 CrPC/BNSS 180” so the accused faces one consolidated trial; (iv) quashing/§482 pleas must be heard promptly; (v) the “triviality” and “slight harm” defences were rejected for group-attack speech; (vi) the objective “reasonable strong-minded person” standard + contextual factors; (vii) heightened responsibility of public figures & anchors — “their words… may ignite passions of millions”. (4) Ashwini Kumar Upadhyay v. UOI / “Shaheen Abdullah” PIL (2021–22): the Court asked the Union and the States for even-handed enforcement — suo motu action against hate speeches irrespective of the community of the speaker and of the victim, prompt FIRs, and monitoring by cyber cells; the ECI’s affidavit (recorded in the public reports) confirmed deployment of IPC + RPA + MCC; and the Court, in later hearings, refused to create a new code, directing citizens to “approach the authorities… otherwise go to the High Court” — i.e., the enforcement duty lies with the executive, with judicial backstops. (5) Election-time directions (March 2024): during the general elections, the Supreme Court pressed the ECI and States to act against hate speech on the campaign trail; the ECI circulated directions to Chief Electoral Officers to register cases suo motu against politicians delivering hate speeches under the Model Code of Conduct (para 2: no activity “aggravating existing differences or creating mutual hatred or cause of tension between different castes and communities, religious or otherwise”) read with BNS 196/197 and RPA 123(3A); candidate affidavits and expenditure circulars were also pointed to — a “prevention + consequence” bundle.

(d) Critique & conclusion. The judicial response is procedural — FIR discipline, clubbing, speed, even-handedness — while substantive criminalisation stays in the intent-tethered sections, because that is what 19(2) tolerates: the case-book’s teaching (Shreya) is that a provision punishing “annoyance” or “gross offensiveness” without a nexus dies. The Tehseen Poonawalla template (preventive machinery) + the Amish Devgan test (contextual, objective) + the LC 267 threshold (incitement + fear of violence) together outline the constitutional Indian approach: “curb hate, not speech”. Close with the two lines the examiner wants: “Freedom to express and speak is the most important condition for political democracy” (Amish Devgan) and “the remedy for false speech is more speech — unless it becomes an instrument of enmity, when the Constitution permits the State to speak for the victim through law” (Shreya; Ramji Lal Modi; the Lohia circle).

Q3 · US v India press freedom — full model answer

Framework sentence. The comparison in the question is about architecture, not outcomes: “Congress shall make no law… abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press” (absolute in text, exception-laden in practice) versus “Art. 19(1)(a)… subject to… reasonable restrictions… in the interests of [eight heads]” (qualified in text, jealously guarded in practice). Shreya ¶¶15–18 holds the American absolutism “cannot be transplanted” wholesale, while granting it “great persuasive value on the content of freedom of speech and expression”. Use five case-pairs:

  1. Press clause: US — express, yet “no special privilege” (Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665 (1972): press subpoenaed like any citizen). India — no press clause; “implicit” (Sakal; Bennett Coleman: Beg J., “the freedom of speech and of the press is the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy”; ABP ¶31: “journalists are but the vocal organs” — all three in the DU case-book). Both converge: the press has the rights of citizens, no more and no less (Prabha Dutt; R.K. Anand amicus passage accepted by the Court).
  2. Prior restraint: Near v. Minnesota (1931 — anti-abatement injunction = prior restraint, unconstitutional) and NYT v. U.S. (Pentagon Papers, 1971 — “heavy presumption against”) → Rajagopal ¶22 imports the presumption into Art. 19(1)(a) (“even if the publication… is of questionable taste… no prior restraint”); but India permits, unlike the US, case-specific postponement orders (Sahara; applied in Swatanter Kumar) as “neutralising devices”.
  3. Defamation of public men: NYT v. Sullivan — “actual malice” (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; Brennan J.’s “breathing space”); extended by Curtis Publishing v. Butts; AP v. Walker; private figures: Gertz. India: Rajagopal ¶26(3) adopts the same standard for public officials, refuses “State defamation” (¶26(4)), carves out the judiciary, and — uniquely — keeps criminal defamation, upheld in Subramanian Swamy (2016: “reputation is an intrinsic part of Art. 21… the exceptions are the balance”). The US has no criminal libel equivalent: this is the single biggest “robustness” gap the question asks you to concede — with the counter-argument that 356’s ten exceptions + sanction make abuse hard.
  4. Hate speech: US protects hate absent incitement (Brandenburg; R.A.V. v. St. Paul 1992; Skokie) — viewpoint-neutrality is near-absolute. India criminalises group-enmity speech (153A/295A/505 → BNS 196/299/353 + RPA 123(3A)) and the SC defends the distinction (Shreya ¶¶15–16 “the sedition… enmity… our social conditions”); Amish Devgan supplies the objective contextual test. Write: “America trusts the marketplace to drown haters; India trusts the sanction to deter them.”
  5. Obscenity & medium: US: Miller test (prurient interest + patently offensive by contemporary community standards + lacks serious literary/artistic/political/scientific value); Roth; Ashcroft v. ACLU (strict scrutiny for internet speech). India: Hicklin (1868) → Ranjit D. Udeshi (1965) → Aveek Sarkar (2014) — the SC “moved away from the Hicklin test and applied the contemporary community standards test” (Shreya ¶46, quoting Aveek Sarkar’s citation of Miller/Doordarshan v. Anand Patwardhan); broadcasting: Red Lion (scarcity → fairness duties) vs CAB (scarcity → trust + independent authority, not content control) — and the internet: Shreya treats online speech at par with print (¶¶29, 86 citing CAB ¶37/78), rejecting Doordarshan-style paternalism — “the content of the right is the same irrespective of the medium”.

Analysis (structure for the essay). The US engine: categorical exclusions + strict scrutiny of content-based laws + prior-restraint bar + “chilling effect” as a first-order value. The Indian engine: enumerated heads + “reasonable restriction” (intelligent care and deliberation — Chintaman Rao) + proximate-relationship requirement (Lohia; Shreya ¶¶22, 32–35) + (since 2015) the imported vagueness/overbreadth/chilling-effect jurisprudence. Where India lags: criminal defamation; OSA/UAPA-style secrecy offences; vague content codes (Programme Code (xii)/(xviii)) vs US “void-for-vagueness” discipline. Where India leads: right to receive (CAB); privacy as constitutional right (Puttaswamy — a 9-judge unanimity no US court offers the press as a shield); “stings in public interest” (R.K. Anand); media-plaintiff neutrality in RTI; and the SC’s refusal to treat “public interest” as a residual 19(2) ground (Sakal/BC/ABP) — a limitation the absolutist First Amendment never had to articulate.

Conclusion. “The American guarantee is robust in form because its text is absolute; the Indian guarantee is resilient in function because its courts read the enumerated restrictions as themselves constitutional commitments — to dignity (Rajagopal), to equality (Amish Devgan) and to the open market of ideas (Bennett Coleman, CAB, Shreya). A comparative answer that stops at ‘US is freer’ misses the point Shreya makes: India has internalised the American grammar without borrowing its text — and that, not the First Amendment, is the modern Indian law of the press.”

Q4 · Prasar Bharati — composition, powers, functions — model answer

Genesis. The Working Group on Autonomy of Akashvani and Doordarshan (Verghese Committee, 1978) recommended an autonomous public-service broadcasting corporation modelled on the BBC → a decade of drift → the Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) Act, 1990: the statutory instrument meant to insulate broadcasting from day-to-day political control, a promise Cricket Association of Bengal (1995) reasserted (“control of broadcasting must vest in an independent public authority”). s.3 — Prasar Bharati is “a body corporate… having perpetual succession and a common seal”, may acquire, hold and dispose of property and sue/be sued; s.4 — its “objects and duties” are “to organise and conduct public broadcasting services to inform, educate and entertain the public and to ensure a balanced development of broadcasting on radio and television”; s.5 — while discharging its functions the Corporation is to “ensure that the programmes and presentation… reflect… the ideals of the Constitution… and maintain the highest professional standards” (independence from government direction). (The uploaded full text carries these provisions — quote s.4 verbatim.)

Composition of the Board (uploaded text of the Act): “The Board shall consist of— (a) a Chairman; (b) one Executive Member; (c) one Member (Finance); (d) one Member (Personnel); (e) six Part-time Members; (f) Director-General (Akashvani), ex officio; (g) Director-General (Doordarshan), ex officio” — appointed by the President of India; for senior staff appointments (above the rank of Joint Secretary) the Act prescribes a Recruitment Board — “the Chairman, other Members, the ex officio Members, the Nominated Member and the elected Members” (as printed in the uploaded text); the Corporation appoints its own whole-time and other employees under the Act; financial provisions — grants and advances by the Central Government, funds of the Corporation, C&AG as auditors, and annual reports/accounts to be laid before Parliament (Chapters/sections on “Fund”, “Accounts and Audit”, “Annual Reports”); the Board frames bye-laws with the Central Government’s prior approval on specified matters (the uploaded arrangement of sections lists these).

The accountability organ the statute forgot: s.12 — Broadcasting Council: “(2) The Broadcasting Council shall consist of (i) a President and ten other members to be appointed by the President of India from amongst persons of eminence in public life; (ii) four members of Parliament — two nominated by the Speaker and two by the Chairman”. Its duty: to “examine and consider… complaints… relating to programme content” and advise (s.12 read with the following sections providing for its reports and their laying before Parliament). The Council was never constituted — the single most-failed promise of the Act, cited by the Pitroda Committee: with no Council, audience grievances against DD/AIR reach only the Ministry, leaving the broadcaster accountable to the government rather than to the public.

Powers & functions in practice: operating Doordarshan (terrestrial + DD Free Dish DTH) and Akashvani (national, regional and external services; community radio policy, 2006); educational and developmental services; statutory sharing rights in its favour under the Sports Broadcasting Signals (Mandatory Sharing with Prasar Bharati) Act, 2007 — every sports event of “national interest” (Olympics, Asian Games, World Cups, India matches etc.) must be shared with DD’s terrestrial & DTH networks on demand, live, in clean-feed form — the viewers’ right legislated, per CAB; and carriage/freedom-of-channel disputes in the digital age (DD Free Dish must-carry, 2012–18 series of orders — decided under the 1995 Act & must-carry rules). The Broadcasting Act, 2023 (replacing the 1995 Act) now regulates content codes of all broadcasters, private and public alike.

The Pitroda Committee (Expert Committee on Prasar Bharati, January 2014 — uploaded): (i) a statutory charter of public service broadcasting — editorial autonomy, universality, diversity, distinctiveness; (ii) secure funding — it proposed a Broadcast Development Fee on pay-TV subscribers to end grant/ads dependency (the proposal lapsed); (iii) governance — a smaller, professional Board with transparent selection; (iv) an autonomous News & Current Affairs division for DD, with an editor’s board and an ombudsman for audience redress; (v) AIR re-structured as a federal, locally-responsive public-radio network; and, conspicuously, constituting the s.12 Broadcasting Council at last. (Vol. 1 of the report is in the uploads — cite its “public service media” framing.)

Critical evaluation & conclusion. Autonomy on paper, dependence in fact: executive control of appointments, grant-based funding, the never-operational Broadcasting Council and DD News’ editorial dependence on government (the recurring criticism in Pitroda and in the parliamentary debates) mean Prasar Bharati remains “a public broadcaster without a public’s veto”. The 2023 Act shifts content regulation from MIB-licensing to self-regulation-plus-oversight for all TV, reducing (but not removing) the government’s direct handle. Conclusion line: “Prasar Bharati is CAB’s unfinished sentence — the statute created the corporation, and never the citizen-facing accountability machinery that makes public-service broadcasting public.”

Q5 · Contempt of court — provisions + leading judgments — model answer

Frame. “In relation to contempt of court” is the seventh head of Art. 19(2); the “last hope” premise of the question is the Court’s own theory — the law protects the administration of justice and public confidence in it, not the feelings of judges (Sahara ¶38 quoting E.M.S. Namboodripad: “The law of contempt stems from the right of the courts to punish by imprisonment or fines persons guilty of words or acts which either obstruct or tend to obstruct the administration of justice… Formerly, it was regarded as inherent in the powers of a court of record and now, by the Constitution of India, it is a part of the powers of the Supreme Court and the High Courts” — Case Book p. 274). The Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 “define[s] and limit[s]” that power.

Provisions (the spine — per the arrangement of sections in the uploaded Act): s.2 — civil contempt (wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or process of a court or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court); criminal contempt — publication (words spoken or written, signs or visible representations) or doing of any act which (i) scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; (ii) prejudices, or interferes with the due course of, any judicial proceeding; (iii) interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice. s.3 — innocent publication & distribution not contempt: (1) no reasonable grounds for believing the proceeding “pending”; (2) publication after the proceeding is finally decided; (3) distributor’s immunity; the Explanation fixes “pending” (criminal: from the chargesheet/challan or summons/warrant) — reproduced verbatim in R.K. Anand ¶176, with its provisos tied to registration under the Press and Registration of Books Act, 1867. s.4 — fair and accurate report of judicial proceedings in open court not contempt (open-justice privilege; Sahara ¶35 — “the media has a right to know what is happening in courts… which enhances the public confidence in the transparency of court proceedings”). s.5 — fair criticism of judicial acts in a case “heard and finally decided”. s.6 — complaints in good faith against presiding officers of subordinate courts to the appropriate authority; s.7 — publication of information relating to in-chambers/in-camera proceedings, not protected save the section’s exceptions; s.8 — other defences preserved; s.9 — Act does not enlarge contempt; s.10–11 — High Court’s powers over subordinate courts (incl. offenders outside jurisdiction); s.12 — punishment: simple imprisonment up to 6 months or fine up to ₹2,000 or both, with remission where a “correct and timely apology” is made to the court’s satisfaction (the proviso); s.13 — no punishment unless the contempt substantially interferes with the due course of justice, and (Act 6 of 2006) the proviso permitting justification by truth “if… it is in public interest and the request for invoking the said defence is bona fide” (the NCRCW 12th Report ¶3.8.2 proposed a constitutional proviso on these lines; the Standing Committee’s report on the 2004 Bill — in the uploads — records the debate); s.14 — face-of-the-court procedure (the s.14(2) inquiry by a retired Judge, as in R.K. Anand, upheld); s.15 — cognizance on the motion of, or with the consent of, the Advocate-General (and the accepted suo motu power); s.16 — contempt by a judicial officer; s.17 — procedure after cognizance; s.18 — bench of two judges; s.19 — appeal to the Supreme Court as of right from a High Court order under s.19(1) (the route of the R.K. Anand appeals); s.20 — limitation: one year; s.22 — Act in addition to other laws; s.23 — rule-making.

Leading judgments (ten): (1) E.M.S. Namboodripad v. T.N. Nambiar (1970) — “the law of contempt is… not for the personal indemnity of judges”; fair criticism is welcome, scandal is not. (2) In re. P.C. Sen (1970) & Saibal Kumar v. B.K. Sen (1961) — the sub-judice doctrine: “no distinction… between a jury trial and a trial by Judges — [judges] may be subconsciously influenced” (both cited by the amicus in R.K. Anand ¶169; the 200th LC Report rests on them). (3) Re: Mulgaokar (1978) (Case Book pp. 153–172) — the Court’s classic act of self-restraint: criticism of the judiciary strengthens it; the contempt motion against the editor in the “Judges’ Bill” episode was declined — “the judiciary… is not here for the sake of the judges.” (4) The Rajendra Sail and Sanjoy Narayan contempt matters (Case Book pp. 173–187) — scandalising language about judges; apologies tested under the s.12 proviso (timeliness + contrition); “the majesty of law is not the vanity of judges.” (5) Reliance Petrochemicals v. Express Newspapers (1988) (pp. 278–293) — comment on judgments vs interference; “due course” requires real prejudice. (6) M.P. Lohia v. State of W.B. (2005) (pp. 136–137) — gag orders “a last resort”; “trial by media… is itself interference with… the administration of justice.” (7) R.K. Anand (2009) — sting telecast during the BMW trial: s.3(1) immunity unavailable (knowledge of pendency), yet the programme “did not interfere with or obstruct the due course of the BMW trial… [it] was clearly intended to prevent the attempt to interfere” (¶178); and s.13(b) truth-in-public-interest is the modern shield (¶¶170–179). (8) Sahara India v. SEBI (2012) (pp. 294–317) — the postponement-order code: “neutralising device… real and substantial risk of prejudice… short duration… necessity & proportionality… not an unreasonable restriction” (¶60, reprinted in Swatanter Kumar); and In re: Rajeev Dhawan (2020) (pp. 187–191) — a senior advocate’s robust speech at a judicial conference: even the bar’s criticism has the “no imputation” line. (9) Prashant Bhushan v. Registry (2021) 8 SCC 606 (real example) — suo motu contempt for two tweets (2020) alleging “selective assignment” of constitutionally sensitive matters and intimidation of judges; ₹1 nominal fine; judges “must be receptive to public criticism… but… imputations… destroying the institution… cannot be protected.” (10) Swatanter Kumar (2014) — postponement, not prohibition, of publications “highlighting… mere allegations,” to protect the fair-trial limb of Art. 21.

Analysis/conclusion. Two principles decide every contempt problem: the Court is protected for the litigant, not the judge; and substantial, not technical, interference is required (s.13). The Act’s defences (ss.3–7, 12, 13(b)) are Art. 19(2) “reasonableness” compressed into statute; the 2006 truth defence completed the Sullivan-isation of the law; and the postponement order (Sahara) is the proportionate alternative to both gag orders (Lohia) and unregulated media trials (R.K. Anand ¶174; Uma Khurana cl. 8(a)). Conclude with Sahara’s caution as reprinted in the case-book: “the phrase ‘in relation to contempt of court’… does not… describe the true nature of the offence — which consists in interfering with the administration of justice.”

Q6 · “Doomed by Dowry” — the media-trial problem: rights analysis + draft appeal — full model answer

Stage 1 — Mr. Y’s rights and the article’s legal character

  • Presumption of innocence (Art. 21). The case-book’s own code — Court on Its Own Motion (Uma Khurana), guideline cl. 8(a): reports “shall not… present as true any unverified or inaccurate facts so as to avoid trial by media since ‘a man is innocent till proven guilty by law’.” R.K. Anand ¶174 defines the mischief: media trial “creat[es] a widespread perception of guilt regardless of any verdict in a court of law… an atmosphere of public hysteria akin to a lynch-mob… the accused is already held guilty.”
  • Fair trial. Sahara ¶60: “anyone… who genuinely apprehends… an infringement of his/her rights under Article 21 to a fair trial… is entitled to… seek an order of postponement of the offending publication/broadcast”; pre-trial publicity creates “an atmosphere… wherein a person may not be able to put forward his defence properly and his likelihood of getting a fair trial would be seriously impaired” (Swatanter Kumar ¶59).
  • Reputation & privacy. “Reputation is an intrinsic part of the right to life and personal liberty” (Subramanian Swamy (2016) 10 SCC 227); even an accused retains “the right to be let alone” over the non-public parts of his life (Rajagopal ¶26(1), (6)); publishing only the complainant family’s version exceeds fair reporting of proceedings.
  • Privileges/immunities lost. s.4 CoCA protects only a “fair and accurate” report — a “vicious”, one-sided feature is neither (R.K. Anand ¶181: “statements and remarks which are actually incorrect and misleading”); and with the bail application pending, s.3(1) is unavailable (the publisher had reasonable grounds to know the proceeding was live — s.3 Explanation).
  • Criminal exposure of the publication. (i) Defamation — imputing a dowry-death with the headline “Doomed”, referable to Y: IPC 499 → BNS 356(1); punishment IPC 500 → BNS 356(2); Exc.1 (truth/public good) fails for want of proof; Exc.4 (fair report) fails for want of fairness; (ii) criminal contempt — s.2(c)(ii)&(iii) — prejudice to the due course of the bail proceedings; (iii) damages for the privacy tort (Uma Khurana ¶13: “Damages in accordance with the law can be awarded by Courts in cases of defamation and loss of reputation”).
  • The underlying charges are for the trial, not the newsroom: dowry-death — IPC 304B → BNS 84; cruelty — 498A → BNS 85/86; abetment of suicide — 306 → BNS 108; with the Evidence Act’s presumption for dowry death (s.113-B) now under the BSA 2023 chapter on presumptions.
  • What Y cannot get: a general gag — prior restraint is disfavoured (Rajagopal ¶22; Swatanter Kumar: courts “should refrain from making any prior restraints”); he gets a postponement order + post-publication remedies.

Stage 2 — Draft appeal (SLP under Art. 136 against the High Court’s rejection of anticipatory bail)

Heading: “SPECIAL LEAVE PETITION (CRIMINAL) No. ____ of 20__ · In the Supreme Court of India · Mr. Y … Petitioner (Accused) vs State (NCT of Delhi) & Anr. …Respondents — against the order dated ___ of the Hon’ble High Court rejecting the anticipatory-bail application under s.438 CrPC (now s.482 BNSS 2023) — praying: (a) reversal; (b) bail in the event of arrest in FIR No. ___ u/ss 498-A, 304-B, 306 IPC (corresponding BNS: 85/86, 84, 108); (c) no arrest meanwhile; (d) directions against further publication.” (Alternatives to acknowledge in a line: review before the same Bench; a fresh bail petition only on change of circumstances; quashing under s.482 CrPC/BNSS 528 where the FIR itself is untenable.)

Grounds: 1. Media prejudice imported into “gravity”. Publicity is not a bail factor (Sanjay Chandra v. CBI (2012) 1 SCC 40 — the triple test: prima facie case; gravity; tampering/flight); the “atmosphere of hysteria” is itself a constitutional harm to the trial that the Court must neutralise, not amplify (R.K. Anand ¶174; Uma Khurana cl. 8(a)). 2. The only “material” was the article itself. The complainant family’s version, published without testing, was “neither examined or tested in any Court of law nor… proved” (Swatanter Kumar ¶53); cl. 10 of the Uma Khurana code demands “verification… and presentation of all facts that are necessary to understand” the event — a one-sided piece fails even the media’s own standard. 3. Personal circumstances. First offender; willing to join the investigation; no flight risk; the statutory conditions (bond; availability; no contact with witnesses) fully meet any concern (BNSS 482(3)). 4. Interim protection against the media. A postponement order in the Sahara terms — necessity, proportionality, short duration — restraining “further publication… which highlights the allegations… without disclosing… that they are mere allegations”, with deletion of online copies within 24 hours, and preservation of “fair reporting… [on] true, correct and verified information” (Swatanter Kumar ¶¶64, 66). 5. Remedies against the publisher reserved. Contempt (s.2(c)(ii)&(iii)) and a private complaint for defamation (s.200 CrPC/BNSS 223 — no sanction needed as Y is not a public servant) + damages/injunction suit (Puttaswamy; Rajagopal ¶¶27–29); PCI/NBSA complaint for the one-sided publication (right-of-reply norms). 6. Art. 21. “The trial court is the sole arena for adjudication; its verdict must be by evidence, not echo.”

Prayer: as above, plus directions to the magazine and intermediaries for removal of the offending content (the X v. UOI ¶92 mechanism). Conclusion: “Bail and media restraint are two faces of the same Article 21 coin: the accused’s presumption of innocence and the press’s Rajagopal freedom coexist; the law asks only that the press not become the trial, and that the court not become the echo.”

Q7 · X’s private photos on porn sites — remedies under Social/Digital Media regulations — full model answer

Is re-posting an offence? Yes — a cluster on two tracks (penal + platform-regulatory) plus a constitutional-remedy track.

(i) Penal track (IPC/BNS twin citations). IT Act — s.67 (publishing/transmitting obscene material in electronic form — up to 5 yrs + ₹10 lakh; second offence 7 yrs) and s.67A (sexually explicit material — up to 5 yrs). The case-book preserves the petitioner’s framing: “even though her photographs and images are otherwise unobjectionable, by placing the same on a pornographic website… [the respondents] have ex-facie committed the offence of publishing and transmitting material that appeals to the prurient interest[s], and which has the effect of tending to deprave and corrupt persons… likely to see” — the s.67 test met by “offence by association”. Account compromise/impersonation — s.43 (penalty), ss.66, 66C, 66D; images of a woman’s private act — s.66E. IPC → BNS: voyeurism 354C → 77; cyber-stalking 354D → 78; insult to modesty/“intrudes upon the privacy of a woman” 509 → 79; criminal intimidation (the “pay or it spreads” sextortion pattern) 503/506 → 351; defamation 499/500 → 356; public mischief 505 → 353. Child victim: IT s.67B + POCSO ss.14–15 + JJ Act identity bar. Print re-use: Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986. Procedure: FIR under s.154 CrPC/BNSS 173 (as in the real case — “FIR No. 286/2020… u/s 354A IPC and 66C IT Act, PS Dwarka South”); NCRP portal complaint; evidence preserved with BSA 2023 ss.63–65 (← Evidence Act 65A–65B); platform records (“URL, account ID, handle name, IP Address, hash value”) collected within 72 hours (X v. UOI direction (vi)).

(ii) Platform-regulation track (IT Rules 2021 — reproduced in the case-book, pp. 209–213). r.3(1)(b)(i)–(ii) — platforms must forbid users from hosting content that “belongs to another person and to which the user does not have any right” or is “defamatory, obscene, pornographic… invasive of another’s privacy, including bodily privacy, insulting or harassing on the basis of gender, libelous, racially or ethnically objectionable…”; r.3(2)(a) — Grievance Officer: “acknowledge the complaint within twenty-four hours and dispose of… within 15 days”; r.3(2)(b) — the star clause: on a complaint concerning material “which exposes the private area… full or partial nudity… any sexual act or conduct, or… impersonation in an electronic form, including artificially morphed images”, the intermediary must “take all reasonable and practicable measures to remove or disable access… within twenty-four hours from the receipt of such complaint” — no court order needed for this gendered category; appeal to the Grievance Appellate Committee (r.3A, 2023); non-compliance forfeits the s.79 safe harbour (s.79(3); r.7); SSMI duties (r.4 — Chief Compliance Officer; 24×7 nodal officer; monthly compliance reports) reinforce it.

(iii) Site-level blocking. Obscene/porn sites → s.69A + 2009 Blocking Rules; where “an order from a competent court” exists, r.10 requires the Designated Officer to block “as directed by the court” (case-book verbatim); in practice such domains are blocked under the Rules, while mirror sites remain the enforcement gap that the High Court called the “cat-and-mouse game”.

(iv) The court track — X v. UOI (2021 SCC OnLine Del 1788) is the pleading template. Art. 226 writ seeking the ¶92 directions: list of Image-/Web-URLs; police/CyPAD removal “from all websites and online platforms… within 24 hours”; global de-indexing and de-referencing by Google/Yahoo/Bing/DuckDuckGo (incl. sub-pages); “automated tools… to proactively identify and globally disable… exactly identical” re-posts; IO circulation of the order (24/72 hrs); standing liberty to notify the IO for future re-postings (72-hour forwarding duty — no fresh litigation); non-compliance → s.79 forfeiture + s.85 (offences by companies); upload-from-India → global takedown, foreign uploads → geo-block (YouTube v. Geeta Shroff; X v. Twitter — “an intermediary put on notice… owes an equitable obligation of confidence”); and the proceedings themselves anonymise the victim (¶2).

(v) Civil & soft remedies. Suit for injunction + damages (constitutional privacy — Puttaswamy ¶¶297–304; misuse of private information & defamation — Rajagopal; right of publicity if monetised — Kaul J. ¶625; Anil Kapoor (Del HC 2023)); PCI/NBSA complaints against any media re-use; NCW/NCPCR; and under the DPDP Act 2023 — correction/erasure rights (s.12), data-fiduciary duties (ss.6, 8), and penalties imposed by the Adjudicating Officer (up to ₹200 crore in the gravest categories), once the Board is fully operational.

(vi) Critical closing. “X’s remedies reveal India’s three-layer architecture post-Shreya: the intermediary acts on complaint for the most gendered categories (24-hour rule), on court order for everything else (‘actual knowledge’), and the court acts on the Constitution — global de-indexing, automated re-blocking, s.79 forfeiture as the whip. Gaps to flag: no statutory mirror-site duty; no public-interest/journalism carve-out in take-downs; intermediary over-compliance (the ‘privatised censorship’ Google warned of); and a right-to-be-forgotten statute still awaited — X v. UOI is the common-law prototype of what the DPDP era must perfect.”

Q8 · Short notes — (A) Paid News · (B) Fake News · (C) RTI & good governance · (D) Commercial speech

(A) Paid news — “a threat to independent and fair media”. “News which is not news”: PR money presented as journalism — column-inch-priced “editorials”, sponsored “ground reports”, paid anchor seats, “news bulletins” with price tags. Why a threat: it (1) corrupts the marketplace of ideas (Holmes J.’s “competition of the market”, quoted in Shreya ¶11, presupposes honest signals; paid news is a rigged auction); (2) hollows the 19(1)(a) right to receive (Tata Press consumer-interest logic inverted); (3) subverts elections — the Press Council study “News that is not News” (2009–10) documented it across the 2007 Gujarat/2008 Delhi elections and recommended: statutory disclosure of paid items as advertisements; treatment as candidate election expenditure (RPA 1951 s.77; ECI circulars 2012–13, reinforced 2019/2024); and making paid news a corrupt practice (the 2013 RPA amendment Bill was never enacted); (4) is not protected speech — paid deception sits outside commercial-speech protection (Hamdard; Tata Press limits). Digital sequelae: IT Rules 2021 r.4(3) — platforms must label content “advertised, marketed, sponsored, owned, or exclusively controlled” (case-book text); ASCI; CPA 2019 “misleading advertisement”. Conclusion: “disclose, don’t gag — transparency converts paid news back into an advertisement, preserving the listener’s right to know without prior restraint.”

(B) Perils of Fake News & legal mechanisms for its control. Perils: mob violence (2018 WhatsApp-lynching cluster — the Tehseen Poonawalla matrix), electoral manipulation, health panic (quack cures), reputational annihilation of individuals (X v. UOI is itself a fake-caption case), and the “whack-a-mole” removal economy. Legal mechanisms: BNS 353 (← IPC 505) — statements conducing to fear/alarm/offence; BNS 196 (←153A) — communal rumours; BNS 356 — targeted lies; Disaster Management Act, 2005 s.51 (misinformation causing panic during disasters); Drugs & Magic Remedies Act, 1954 s.3 (miracle-cure claims); RPA s.123(4) — publication of false statements re a candidate’s personal character (corrupt practice) + the Model Code of Conduct; Contempt (s.2(c)) for pending cases; IT Act — s.66A is dead (Shreya: any “fake news” law must fasten on a 19(2) head + intent); platform duties — 2021 Rules r.3(1)(b) (prohibitions incl. “patently false… reasonably perceived as fact”), 24-hour takedown (r.3(2)(b)), 15-day redressal; the 2023 amendment’s “voluntary verification/fact-check” architecture is under constitutional challenge (Delhi HC 2023; the Supreme Court has agreed to examine it — as of 2024–25); and court-driven removal per X v. UOI. Victim’s remedies: FIR (BNS 353/196/356), defamation & malicious-falsity suits, take-down notices, s.69A complaints. Conclusion: “the s.66A autopsy teaches the answer — truth ministries chill; narrowly drafted incitement/panic offences with mens rea, plus transparency duties, curb.”

(C) RTI — an instrument to ensure good governance. The RTI Act, 2005 converts the 19(1)(a) “right to know” (Prabha Dutt’s negative liberty; CAB; PUCL) into a positive entitlement: s.3 — inspection of records/works, certified copies, material in electronic form; s.4 — suo motu “maximum disclosure… to minimise the need to invoke the Act”; ss.6–7 — requests (30 days; 48 hours where life & liberty are concerned — the Art. 21 proviso); s.8 — exemptions (sovereignty; fiduciary 8(1)(e); personal privacy 8(1)(j)) read narrowly, with the larger-public-interest override (provisos to ss.8–9); s.11 — third-party notice; ss.12–13 — CIC/SICs; s.18 — inquiry & recommendations; s.19 — first/second appeals; s.20 — ₹250/day penalty (cap ₹25,000) + disciplinary recommendations; s.22 — Act overrides inconsistent laws; s.24 — security-organisation exceptions; s.25 — protection for acts done in good faith. Governance payoffs: MGNREGA social-audit culture; the CWG-2010 paper-trail; candidate disclosure (Union v. ADR, 2002; PUCL, 1997); and transparency inside institutions — CPIO, Supreme Court of India v. Subhash Chandra Aggarwal (Case Book pp. 119 ff.): the Act “exists to subserve transparency and accountability”, balanced against judicial privacy through s.8(1)(j). For the media it is the lawful answer to Prabha Dutt (“no obligation on citizens to supply information to the press”). Strains: pendency; the 2019 tenure-by-notification amendment (independence criticism); DPDP interplay. Conclusion: “RTI + PCA + Lokpal = accountability ex ante and ex post — governance no longer a courtesy of the ruler but a contract with the citizen.”

(D) Commercial speech under Art. 19(1)(a). The journey: Hamdard Dawakhana (1960) — advertising held outside speech (Drugs & Magic Remedies Act upheld) → Bennett Coleman (1973) — advertising pages inseparable from circulation, hence from speech → Tata Press v. MTNL (1995) — commercial advertising (telephone directories) is protected speech; the consumer’s right to know is its other face; only false or misleading commercial speech falls outside the guarantee → restated in ABP (2014: the “vocal organs” passage presupposes the advertising economy of the press) and in the case-book’s Unit 5 (Ajay Goswami (2007) — restrictions on product advertising in school examination papers upheld for child-protection, while commercial speech’s place under 19(1)(a) was affirmed). Regulatory grid: Drugs & Magic Remedies Act 1954 (s.3), COTPA 2003 (tobacco ads), Drugs & Cosmetics rules, ASCI Code, Consumer Protection Act 2019 (misleading advertisement; CCPA; product liability), Union of India v. M.C. Mehta (2016) (surrogate liquor-advertising restrictions sustained on public-health grounds), IT Rules 2021 r.4(3) (sponsor labelling), and the 2023–24 online-gaming self-regulation code. Doctrine to quote: “the listener’s right to know is the soul of commercial-speech protection; the State may forbid deception, but not the marketplace of truthful choices.”
