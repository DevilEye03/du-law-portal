  • Trap 1 — “paid news is an offence” is false as written: it is not yet a corrupt practice under s. 123. The Court’s own frame sits in ¶55 of the reader’s reprint: “money power virtually controls the whole field of election and … people are taken for a ride by such unscrupulous elements” (PDF p. 332 / printed p. 289) — and the remedy it enforced was an expenses remedy (s. 10A), not a media offence. Turning paid news itself into a corrupt practice was recommended (PCI 2010; MoL&J Committee 2010; 47th Standing Committee 2013; Law Commission 255th Report 2015) and, on the record this file could verify, not enacted as at 2026 [external; reported]. A 20-mark answer states the gap as the finding, then the reform packages (Parts B1–B2).
  • Trap 2 — s. 77 is not a media law, and yet media is its enforcement arm: paid news was, at the material time, the “other material” device for hiding election expenses (s. 77) and for exceeding the ceiling (s. 77(3)–(4), s. 94(1) by reference in the reader’s own footnotes). The Chavan Court’s whole architecture argument is that s. 10A exists to close exactly this laundering. Explain it that way and you have analysis, not recitation.
  • Trap 3 — Chavan overruled the Tribunal, not the disqualification: the Supreme Court restored the candidate’s election on a finding of fact — the documents did not, without more, “establish the authorship of the candidates” — while endorsing the constitutional validity of the power used against her. Umlesh Yadav’s disqualification as a disqualification survives the appeal untouched; what died was the inference on that record. (Read ¶117 before repeating “the Supreme Court cleared her of paid news”.)
  • Trap 4 — TRAI’s 2009 recommendations are half binding, half “merely suggestions”: the Authority’s own text (Chapter 3) records MIB’s position that print is outside TRAI’s ambit, so its print-facing caps were recommendations in the courtesy sense. The uploaded 2026 draft rules are where caps first get statutory teeth — and only vertical ones. The matrix in D3 is the honest version.
  • Trap 5 — the exit-poll ban is a time law, not a content law: s. 126A does not forbid exit polls; it forbids them, and their publication, inside the Commission-notified window (first poll opens → half an hour after the last poll closes, nationally). Pre-poll polls in print remain lawful — self-regulated only — while s. 126’s publication/display bar (electronic media and display only — print, news portals and social media sit outside it, which is why the pre-poll question remains largely self-regulated) shuts broadcast matter across the 48 hours before the last poll closes, reinforced by s. 135C’s ban on public meetings in the silence period [external; reported — RPA text verified against neutral reprints and the Election Commission’s own reforms paper]. A1 · 8.1 — What “paid news” means in the debates, and what the law actually catches

Start from the reader, because this unit is the reader’s own last word. The case book closes — after censor, contempt, elections — with a chapter headed simply “Paid News” (reader lines 14500 → 15039; PDF pp. 324–335 / printed pp. 313–321), and its first printed line is not an essay but the citation that anchors the whole unit: Ashok Shankarrao Chavan v. Madhavrao Kinhalkar, (2014) 7 SCC 99. Everything in Part A is built from that reprint; the surrounding debate (definitions, reports) is marked as external where it is.

The operative description — verbatim from the reader’s reprint (¶51; PDF p. 329 / printed p. 286)

“…it was alleged that the Appellant Ashok Shankarrao Chavan got several advertisements published in various newspapers, in particular, Lokmat, Pudhari, Maharashtra Times and Deshonnati during the election campaigning period, which appeared in those newspapers in the garb of news eulogizing him and his achievements as Chief Minister of Maharashtra. It was further alleged that the huge expenditure … was incurred or authorized by the Appellant for getting those advertisements published as news and is now a well-known phenomenon, as ‘paid news’.”

Read ¶51 slowly and three elements pop out, and they are the exam-ready anatomy of the practice: (i) content that looks like news — “in the garb of news”; (ii) consideration that does not look like payment — the money never enters the candidate’s accounts (the same ¶ records that Chavan showed Rs. 5,379 of newspaper advertising while “the expenditure on the above mentioned ‘paid news’ ran into several crores”); and (iii) the transaction that never appears as a transaction — the paper denies payment (¶52: the papers said they published “gratuitously as they had links with or leanings towards the Congress Party and the Appellant”). Paid news is therefore best defined structurally: news merchandise priced and invoiced outside the ledger that the law requires to be kept.

The settled definitions at point of use

SourceWhat it says
Press Council of India, Report on Paid News (Report No. 1 of 2010) External; reported“Paid news” means “any news or article appearing in any media (print or electronic) brought about in consideration of money or kind” — this definition was reproduced by the Law Commission in its 255th Report and thereby entered the official reform debate; the PCI’s inquiry (set up suo motu after the 2009 general election) documented the phenomenon as concentrated in the Hindi belt and running through “media planners”/intermediaries rather than direct cash-for-copy contracts.
47th Report, Parliamentary Standing Committee on Information Technology (2013) External; reportedExamined the PCI report, the Ministry of Law & Justice committee report (2010) and the Income Tax/ECI material; concluded paid news “defeats the very purpose of free and fair elections”, recommended: statutory definition, making it a corrupt practice in the RPA, ECI power to act, and disclosure of paid content in candidates’ accounts. Its diagnosis of the cause — “corporatisation” of the media, with reporters treated as output units — is quoted verbatim below because it sits in an official document this file read whole.
TRAI, Recommendations on Issues Relating to Media Ownership (12.8.2014) Verified — official PDFChapter 1 reproduces the debate with direct quotations, including the 47th Report’s causation passage and President Mukherjee’s 27 February 2014 rebuke that paid news is “a sad commentary on the state of the media”. TRAI’s own frame: paid news is an ownership-and-business-model problem (part of the same reform package as cross-media caps — see Part D).
Election Commission of India (circulars of 8.6.2010 and 23.9.2010, and the Commission’s own electoral-reforms paper) External; reportedAsked Chief Electoral Officers to gather paid-news complaints, urged media houses to frame internal self-regulatory policies, and treated favourable “coverage” as an election expense to be accounted under s. 77 RPA whether paid by candidate, party or friend — the same notional-expenditure logic the Chavan judgment enforces. The Commission’s reforms paper records that its own 1998 guidelines regulating poll publication had to be withdrawn after the Supreme Court observed the Commission could not enforce them against the media — the structural reason it prefers exhortation to orders.

Not in the statute. The RPA 1951 contains no definition of “paid news”; s. 123(2)(b) still requires the court’s satisfaction that a publication was “paid for”. This is the hole every report tried to plug (Part B).

Five things paid news is NOT (each is a classic MCQ trap)

  • Not an ordinary advertisement. Ads are disclosed, rate-card priced and counted in the s. 77 account (with party ads presumed to the candidate — Common Cause, as applied in the Allahabad Division Bench extract at ¶113–114). The “ADVT” tag at the bottom of a block is the tell the Umlesh Yadav record produced — and it cut both ways: the tag made the item an advertisement in the papers’ defence (¶54), and thereby a countable expense the candidate had not shown (¶114–115).
  • Not “free press support”. “Our papers published it gratis because of leanings towards the Congress” (¶52) is the editorial-independence defence — politically respectable, but the Court’s scheme does not need the paper’s motive: it needs only the candidate’s non-accounting (ss. 77–78 + 10A).
  • Not yet a corrupt practice. Election-petition remedies under s. 123 (bribery via publication) remain hard to reach because the petitioner must trace money to the candidate’s authority; the reader’s own reprint is all about the ECI route that avoids s. 123 — s. 10A (Parts A2–A3).
  • Not the same as media capture by ownership (8.3, Part D) — though every report links them: the same concentration that lets a proprietor “gift” an election also lets a conglomerate negotiate “private treaties” (business-for-coverage deals reported since 2003–09) [external; reported].
  • Not poll manipulation (8.2, Part C) — a rigged sample mispredicts; paid news manufactures. Both aim at the voter’s last-minute choice; only one has a statutory time-bar.

Real example — the numbers do the arguing

Three ledgers from the reader itself: Maharashtra 2009 — declared newspaper-ad spend ₹5,379 against allegations “running into several crores” (¶51). Jharkhand 2009 — declared ₹18,92,353 against the Income Tax Department’s prima-facie finding of ₹9,32,56,259 in a constituency whose legal ceiling was ₹25,00,000 (¶53). UP 2007 — the candidate produced a ₹840 receipt (7×6 cm box), while the papers’ own bills read ₹8,000 (Amar Ujala) and ₹21,250 (Dainik Jagran), both raised on D.P. Yadav, paid in cash (¶115). Every paid-news answer should carry at least one such triplet: practice, ceiling, invoice.

The reader’s own frame — corporatisation and “yellow journalism”

The syllabus line at reader PDF p. 6 lists 8.1 with its case and three reports; the reader’s framing chapters make the doctrinal link the examiners reward: paid news is not a rogue reporter story but a business-model story. The 47th Report’s causation passage, preserved verbatim inside TRAI’s own 2014 document (read whole for this file), says the rot runs through the organisation chart:

TRAI 2014 Recommendations ¶1.16, quoting the 47th Report [verified — official document]

The Committee attributed paid news to the “corporatisation of media houses”, the “de-segregation of roles” (journalism and sales in the same hands), the “contract system” in newsrooms and depressed wages; it dismissed the industry’s self-regulation bodies (NBSA, BCCC) as “eye wash”, and found the PCI’s and EMM’s punitive powers inadequate.

That passage is the bridge to the rest of Unit 8: if the cause is structure, the law must reach structure — ownership caps (8.3), competition scrutiny (8.4), licence conditions on the licensee (8.5), and the property rights that decide who profits from content (8.6). Say “yellow journalism” as the older cousin — sensationalism sold on circulation rather than on invoices — and locate the difference: yellow journalism deceives by style; paid news deceives by source. The law has long tolerated the first; it is only lately inventing tools for the second.

Appeal 3 · UP 2007 (Umlesh Yadav) — the full factual arc, ¶54 verbatim (PDF pp. 330–331)

“A day before the date of polling, a publication was made in a newspaper ‘Amar Ujala’ dated 17.04.2007 mentioning that there is a wave in favour of the Appellant … A similar publication was also made in the newspaper ‘Dainik Jagaran’ … On 27.04.2007 a complaint was submitted by the Respondent No.2 to the Press Council of India that the newspapers … have published one sided news item in the form of advertisement … by taking huge sums of money … On 12.05.2007 the Appellant submitted the accounts of his election expenses … The Press Council thereby, decided the complaint vide order dated 31.03.2010, wherein it held that the publication though camouflaged as news items, in reality it was only an advertisement. It further held that the newspapers … were guilty of ethical violation … the Election Commission … disqualified her for a period of three years [order 20.10.2011] … the Allahabad High Court … dismissed the said writ petition … dated 03.05.2013.”

Note the chronology the answeriners compress wrongly: PCI first (2010 finding on the papers), ECI second (disqualification of the candidate, 2011), Allahabad DB third (2013), Supreme Court last (2014) — one complaint, three forums, all inside the ¶54 text.

Issues (as the record frames them)

  1. Jurisdiction v. the exclusive tribunal: can the ECI hold a s. 10A enquiry into the truth of a return of expenses when the election has already been tried (unsuccessfully) by the Election Tribunal — Article 329(b) + s. 80 RPA bar alleged?
  2. Form or substance: does s. 10A reach only late/non-filing (default disqualification), or also a false return — and is Sucheta Kripalani (return is “in form and not in substance”) still good law after the 1956 and 1966 amendments?
  3. Who may set it in motion; must the enquiry ride only on the DEO’s report (Rule 89), and may anyone but a voter complain — against s. 123(6)’s narrow channel?
  4. Merits in Umlesh: are party-funded advertisements “expenditure incurred or authorised by the candidate” under s. 77 (and outside Explanation 1), so that not showing them is a breach?

Arguments — Gopal Subramanium for the appellants (¶10 (a)–(j), distilled with the reprint’s own turns of phrase)

MoveThe printing

(b) Sucheta Kripalani rulesWhatever s. 7(c) said before 1956/1966 (and s. 10A after), Sucheta Kripalani still holds: submission is “only in form and not in substance”; L.R. Shivaramagowda “was clearly distinguishable and required reconsideration”.
(c) No-procedure objectionA s. 10A enquiry would be “conducting a trial … ultra vires of Article 329(b)” with no statutory procedure — CPC powers? Evidence Act? limitation? parties? — “anomalous consequences” follow.
(d) “Default, not stigma”s. 10A is “only a default disqualification and not a stigmatized one”, founded on the DEO’s report alone; reasons are needed only when removing a disqualification under s. 11 — so setting an election aside through s. 10A is “very severe” and impermissible.

Arguments — Jayant Bhushan for the complainant (¶13 (a)–(g)) and Ashok Desai for the ECI (¶19)

Bhushan: (a) s. 10A read with ss. 77(1)–(2), 78 and Rules 86–89 shows only the Commission can disqualify for a false account; (b) s. 10A is “independent of Article 329(b)” — no ultra vires question; (c) by Rule 89’s “as it thinks fit”, satisfaction may rest on the DEO report, or after hearing the parties, or on the Commission’s own enquiry; (d) the paid-news allegations “having not been disclosed by the Appellant in the return, have now come to light through the Press Council and other sources” — so the DEO could not have been the only trigger; (e) tribunal jurisdiction (to invalidate) and Commission power (to disqualify) are “different” — the petition’s dismissal is no res judicata; (f) want of particulars “cannot be a ground to thwart the exercise of the power”; (g) the case is squarely covered by Shivaramagowda, and Sucheta Kripalani is “no longer good law” — its “substratum … erased by the subsequent amendments … 1956 and again in 1966 … the whole scheme of the Act had undergone a drastic change”.

Two evidentiary gears to name in your answer: (1) the rebuttable presumption that party spending is candidate spending (Common Cause, applied via ¶112–113), and (2) the self-defeating denial: the papers’ own replies — “it was an advertisement, billed to D.P. Principles evolved

  1. s. 10A is substantive, not merely procedural. The Commission may enquire whether a lodged return is false in a material particular — “in the manner required” (s. 78 r/w s. 77) carries truthfulness, not just timeliness.
  2. Two parallel tracks, no collision with Art. 329(b). The election petition invalidates an election (Tribunal, s. 80/100, 45-day limitation); the s. 10A order disqualifies a person (Commission, gazette publication, three years, any citizen’s complaint within reasonable time). One failing does not bar the other — the Desai comparator table, adopted.
  3. Liberal construction of the Commission’s power. Where Art. 324 supervision meets statutory silence on procedure, the enabling provision is read “with a very liberal approach” (¶55) — the Commission may frame its own day-to-day process under Rule 89’s “as it thinks fit”.
  4. Attribution through presumption. Expenditure “incurred or authorized” includes party-published campaign material unless within Explanation 1; the burden of severing the candidate from the spend is the candidate’s (Common Cause + ¶114).
  5. Speed as a remedy. A 45-day, day-to-day direction to conclude pending s. 10A proceedings (¶111) — the Court knew disqualification after a term ends is no disqualification.

Reading the sequence — the one paragraph that earns analysis marks

The three reports disagree about the villain and therefore about the remedy, and saying so is the difference between a list and an answer. The PCI treated paid news as a professional-ethics failure (its instinct: codes and censure, because the Council is “a statutory body with no punitive teeth”). The MoL&J committee treated it as an electoral-corruption failure (its instinct: a new entry in s. 123, because corrupt practice is how the RPA punishes conduct that taints the mandate). The 47th Report treated it as a business-structure failure (its instinct: ownership and labour conditions — hence the corporatisation passage TRAI preserved). This is why Unit 8’s later items (ownership, competition, licensing) are not appendages of 8.1 but its sequels: if the diagnosis is structural, the prescription must be structural too. The Chavan judgment, decided between the last two reports, chose the fourth framing none of them had: an accounting offence — reachable, as ¶¶114–117 prove, with ordinary evidence tools.

Related instruments at point of use (the “holistic” layer)

Reforms that were actually attempted: private members’ and government bills touching RPA definitions circulated in 2010–13 (the “electoral reforms” wave after the 2009 report) — none carried paid news into the statute; the MCA/Companies Act 2013 disclosure architecture (related-party transactions) remains the indirect net for outlet-side payments; the IT Act 66/67 and BNS cheating provisions have been invoked in consumer-facing “paid content” (influencer) contexts decades later — a live frontier the 2010 reports never saw (2026 influencer-ads guidelines context; External; reported). B2 · The response architecture II — Law Commission 255, the gap map, and why nothing became law

The 255th Report (Law Commission, chairmanship Justice Aftab Alam, 2015) — the best drafting India has had for paid news External; reported — paraphrase of drafts, not verbatim

“Electoral Reforms” (255th Report) took the whole 2009–14 debate in one stride. On paid news it recommended amending s. 123 so that “paid news” is defined and made a corrupt practice, with a statutory definition drawn from the PCI formulation (“in consideration of money or kind”), and a companion duty of disclosure on candidates and media; on polls and the media it proposed a new s. 127B directed at exit-poll publication and dissemination during notified periods, with penal consequences — the Commission’s reasoning tracking the ECI’s long-standing admission that it cannot bind the media without a statute. The report also weighed the constitutional question head-on: such a restriction travels through Art. 19(2) (“sovereignty and integrity… public order”) as readily as through the anti-corruption rationale, because the harm is to the electorate’s information environment, not merely to an opponent’s ledger. Honesty note: this build could not retrieve the report’s clause-by-clause draft text (the draft amendment clauses sit past the parse window of the official PDF this build fetched); the file therefore paraphrases and flags — students quoting sub-clause lettering should check the library copy before citing it.

Why the amendment never came — three structural reasons worth a conclusion

  1. Who legislates is who is regulated. Every MP is a candidate in a paid-news story; the Standing Committee’s recommendation was met with “under consideration” responses and the RPA amendment files between 2010 and 2015 never reached a vote. (TRAI’s parallel line — that the government should not regulate the media, 2014 Recommendations, ch. 1 — was the same fear seen from the other side: give a ministry the power to define paid news and it will use it on everyone.)
  2. The definition cuts against the advocacy reflex. “In consideration of money or kind” requires the State to open the newsroom’s books — what the press called licensing the editor. The compromise the law has in fact built — reach the money (s. 77/10A), never the copy (PCI ethics only) — is doctrinally elegant and politically survivable: that is why it is the part that exists.
  3. Enforcement was already half-built. Once Chavan confirmed the s. 10A engine, the ECI began using it (notices across the 2010s cycles; the 2017 Uttarakhand s. 126A arrest showing criminal tools exist for the adjacent abuse), and legislative urgency faded into “review” — the same cold-storage dynamic that swallowed the ownership caps (D4).

Status ledger, 2026 (as far as this build could verify) External; reported — paid news: still no RPA entry; the ECI continues by circular and pre-poll advisories (including advisories reported in the 2026 assembly cycles alongside the exit-poll reminders); the PCI’s norms live in the Norms of Journalistic Conduct (2022 edition) and are restated each election; the Law Commission’s drafts (255th) remain unimplemented; and the June 2026 broadcasting consultation (Part F2) is the first structural reform in the pipeline — media-business caps, not media-content offences.

Constitutional position — how to argue both sides in four lines

For a paid-news offence: it targets commercial deception of the electorate, not political speech — the same logic that lets the State police false advertising inside Art. 19(1)(a)’s commercial-speech protection (Hamdard Dawakhana; Tata Press — Units 1/5) — hence a straightforward Art. 19(2) restriction serving free-and-fair elections, itself basic structure (Desai’s ¶19 preface, adopted). Against: “consideration of money or kind” is inseparable from editorial judgment (patronage, advocacy, unpaid alignment — the ¶52 defence); a definition vague enough to catch crores of influence chases is vague enough to chill support for unpopular candidates; the RPA already owns the correct lever (accounting), and criminalising the outlet hands the State the “media licensing” genie TRAI itself refused to open. C2 · The statute — s. 126, s. 126A and the silence period: a timed ban, not a content ban

Section 126 — the poll-eve bar (paraphrase from verified reprints External; reported)

Substance: no person shall publish or communicate, by means of the electronic media or by display, any matter referable to an election in a constituency during the period beginning two days before the day of polling in that constituency and ending with its conclusion (the provision has travelled through amendments refining “display”, cinema-van and television coverage of election matter). Print is outside s. 126 — which is why a “wave in favour” story can lawfully sit in a newspaper the night before polling, while its television reenactment cannot. Breach attracts imprisonment up to two years, or fine, or both (s. 126(3)). Companion: s. 135C — the 48-hour silence period bans convening public meetings (with the same sanction range) in furtherance of free and fair polls; a candidate’s poll-eve rally is caught there, not in s. 126.

The 2013 overlay: by Supreme Court direction (2013), no political advertisement — print or electronic — may appear on poll day or the day before without pre-certification by the MCMC, which checks the s. 77 ceiling compliance and the content; a living bridge from this unit to 8.1: the “advertisement disguised as news” dies at the MCMC desk because it must first pass as an advertisement External; reported.

Four drafting facts worth more marks than any essay paragraph: ① the section bans the conduct of the poll as well as its publication — an unpublished exit poll inside the window is itself an offence; ② it reaches print (unlike s. 126) — a newspaper tabulating exit data on the final poll-day morning violates it; ③ “disseminate in any other manner, whatsoever” + the internet/mobile inclusion were an ex-ante platform-neutral design (2009 — before social-media-only leaks were the norm, and now doing that work); ④ the operative period is not in the statute — it is ECI-notified per election, i.e. Parliament delegated the timing to the body that runs the poll.

Enforcement anatomy — who prosecutes a newsroom

  • FIR, not the Commission. The ECI has no penal jurisdiction under s. 126A: it reports/asks police to act; the Uttarakhand 2017 arrest went through the ordinary criminal process (police case → magistrate) External; reported. Contrast s. 10A, where the Commission itself decides and gazettes.
  • Pending trials ≠ election petitions. s. 126A creates a general criminal offence triable as such; election-petition allegations of “publication of a false statement” (s. 123(2)(a)? the malice-falsity limb) continue separately via the tribunal.
  • PCI complaints run in parallel. The Council cannot try an offence, but it records the ethical dimension and (per the 2026 advisory) expressly ties compliance with s. 126A to “the sanctity of the democratic process” External; reported — one act, two tracks.
  • The 45-day/“reasonable time” contrast from Chavan ¶19 applies here too: criminal s. 126A liability is open-ended in time; corrupt-practice complaints are 45 days from the election (s. 81). Forum-shopping in an exam answer shows the student has read the sections, not the op-eds.

Mini-answer drill (10-mark “poll surveys” note, in 90 seconds)

“Poll surveys occupy the space between speech and statistics: pre-poll surveys predict intention and are governed only by the Press Council’s 1996 Guidelines (disclosure of agency, sponsor, sample, methodology, margin of error — norms restated in the Council’s 2026 election advisories); exit polls capture behaviour and are governed by s. 126A RPA (inserted 2009, applied from 2010), which bans their conduct and any publication of results — print, electronic, internet or mobile — inside the Election Commission’s notified window, punishable to two years. Broadcast/display and public meetings are separately sealed for 48 hours by ss. 126 and 135C; political advertisements need MCMC certification in the same window (SC, 2013). The design is deliberately asymmetric — the law times speech, it does not license it — a compromise forced on India when its 1998 poll orders were withdrawn after the Supreme Court held the Commission could not enforce them on the media (ECI reforms paper). Then close with Chavan: the 1996 disclosure logic and the s. 126A timing logic meet the same enemy — undisclosed influence; the RPA reached the money (s. 10A) but still refuses to reach the press.”

Why the “market for ideas” argument — TRAI 2014 Recommendations, ch. 1 (verified — official document)

TRAI conceded that market-power arithmetic does not translate directly to news: it recorded the study showing the top three dailies in Delhi controlling about 70% of the market (IRS study, 2012) with the caution that “numbers alone don’t matter”, and adopted the framers’ language that the “market for ideas is very different from the market for shoes or biscuits” — concentration in a consumer good costs consumers; concentration in news costs citizens, which is why it wanted ex-ante structural safeguards (TRAI caps) rather than only ex-post conduct policing (CCI).

The doctrinal chain the reader has already taught you: the same “market of ideas” logic that built Sakal Papers (1962 — newsprint rationing = restriction on circulation = invalid; “the right of the citizens to receive news of their own choice”) and Bennett Coleman (1973 — no fundamental right of a newspaper to grow unlimitedly) now runs in reverse: those cases forbade the State to thin big media by fiat; CMO caps ask the State to cap big media by rule. The bridge is Art. 19(6): TRAI’s 2009 chapter on constitutionality (official text) cites Sakal (AIR 1962 SC 305), Express Newspapers (AIR 1958 SC 578), Bennett Coleman (AIR 1973 SC 106) and the CAB airwaves case (1995 AIR SC 1236 — spectrum as public trust, para 78) and rests ownership regulation on 19(1)(a) as read with the “general interests of the people” limb of 19(6): a law regulating a commercial activity involving public resources/industry — permitted; a law abridging editorial content — not.

The jurisdiction puzzle — how a telecom regulator got the file

Nothing in the TRAI Act mentions newspapers. The sequence (all in TRAI’s own 2009 preface — official): (1) broadcasting was declared a “telecommunication service” by government notification in November 2004 — dragging DTH/cable/HITS into the TRAI Act’s net; (2) the Ministry then referred media-ownership questions to TRAI under s. 11(1)(a)(ii) & (iv) TRAI Act by reference dated 22.5.2008; (3) MIB’s standing objection, recorded in the same chapter: print is not within TRAI’s ambit, so TRAI’s print-facing items were, in the Authority’s own candid phrase, “merely suggestions” — a two-medium regulator drafting a three-medium policy; (4) TRAI had itself planted the seed years earlier: its 2005 digitalisation recommendations wanted the Cable Act amended to carry cross-media-ownership provisions (official text) — the idea of legislating caps through the carriage statute, exactly what the 2026 draft (Part F2) finally does, twenty-one years later.

Exam frame in a sentence: “India’s CMO policy is the odd product of a jurisdictional accident — broadcasting became ‘telecom’ in 2004, and a telecom regulator ended up writing the country’s pluralism economics for print, TV and radio, with only the telecommunication half of its advice legally reachable.”

Constitutionality — how TRAI wrote its own 19(6) note (official ch. 3)

  • Not a censorship law: caps restrict ownership of commercial entities, protected under 19(1)(g) as business; the editorial voice of each outlet is untouched — one newspaper’s readers lose nothing, they gain (in the framers’ view) a market with more independent owners.
  • Sakal/Express/Bennett Coleman chain invoked exactly as in Unit 2.1: citizens’ right to receive plural news is the beneficiary of caps; the airwave half rests on CAB 1995 ¶78 — spectrum is a public trust, its allocation reviewable for fairness: DTH/HITS caps are trusteeship rules on a public resource, hence “in the general interests of the people”, within 19(6).
  • Print asymmetry acknowledged: applying caps to newspapers “through the telecom regulator” was itself constitutional-difficulty avoidance — TRAI suggested, MIB would have to legislate; it never did (B2’s pattern again).

What the 2014 Recommendations actually proposed (official text + the same-day press record)

  1. Regulated universe: TV news + print dailies (business dailies included); radio excluded (private radio could not then broadcast news — the very rule F2 carries into 2026), internet excluded “to be reviewed when private radio news and online media become significant” — the sentence that has aged best.
  2. Markets: the relevant market = language medium × state — the document lists the combinations (Hindi, English, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Bengali, Oriya, Assamese, Malayalam, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada etc. across roughly twenty state groupings; Hindi/English on a national scale), with TV measured by GRP × reach and print by circulation.
  3. Concentration test: markets with HHI above 1,800 are “highly concentrated”; in such a market, an entity whose holdings contribute more than 1,000 HHI points in TV and more than 1,000 in print must disengage — either exit one medium or dilute to 20%, with a two-consecutive-years window before the duty bites (the 2014 press record read out a 32% share trigger on the print leg) Official structure; 32% External; reported.
  4. Vertical leg: continuation of the 2009 20% equity cap between content and platforms, plus non-discriminatory carriage duties — the tariff-and-interconnect world the Delhi High Court ad-cap case (E2) later policed.
  5. Eligibility walls: political parties, political associations/organisations, government, and religious bodies — and their “proxies” — barred from media ownership; “under no circumstances should government regulate the media” is the document’s own caveat (bar government from owning and from regulating — the double refusal of 2014). Transparency: disclosure of major shareholding, top-10 advertisers, loans and guarantees with related concerns; large-house equity and loan caps were stated to be “seriously considered” but not finalised.
  6. The paid-news limb (same document, ch. 3): penalties for indulgence in paid news and “private treaties” — the business-model clause promised in B1, drafted here; TRAI deliberately routed it through election/companies law rather than any content law.
  7. M&A and compliance: mergers crossing thresholds barred during transition; annual compliance filings; review cycle of three years (the clause the 2022 paper later invoked to reopen everything).
  8. The regulator question: one independent, non-ministerial mechanism for media — the earlier recommendations had floated a Media Commission class body chaired by a retired Supreme Court judge with eminent non-media members; 2014 repeated the need for independence from the I&B ministry — the ghost that walks through every “single regulator” question in Units 4 and 8 alike.

HHI in thirty seconds (the formula earns easy marks)

HHI = sum of squares of market shares (in percentage points). A monopolised market = 10,000; four equal firms = 2,500. TRAI borrowed the merger-guideline vocabulary — below 1,000 unconcentrated; 1,000–1,800 moderately; above 1,800 highly concentrated — and built the cross-media trigger on top: contributions over 1,000 points in both TV and print force disengagement. One worked line for the script: two 40% dailies already give 3,200 — “highly concentrated” — so combining them adds 3,200 points, tripping every limb at once. That single arithmetic sentence converts a name-drop into an analysis.

FDI note for the same answer: news channels 26% / advertising & news agency 26% / entertainment 74% were the May-2013 reshuffle after the Mayaram Committee (100% allowed only for DBN-type carve-outs for NRIs) External; reported — the FDI column interacts with caps: 20% domestic control ceilings and 26% foreign ceilings together decide who can actually assemble a national news chain.

D4 · The fate of the caps — cold storage, the 2022 reopening, and the 2026 back door

Eighteen years, in five beats External; reported — but each beat tied to a fetched document

  1. 2009–14: recommendations, no rules. MIB accepted the vertical logic (20% DTH/HITS caps got codified in the guidelines; horizontal caps did not); India in 2014 remained, in the monitoring project’s phrase, a market with “no horizontal CMO thresholds” — the NewsLaundry explainer of July 2014 answered its own title flatly: “No, not yet” — no cross-media restrictions in print, TV or radio, city caps for FM being the lone policy island.
  2. 2014–21: the formal limbo. The 2014 Recommendations sat “under consideration by the Inter-Ministerial Committee” (as the comparative-monitor record had it in January 2019); MIB’s own file No. 8/17/2014-BP&L dated 19.2.2021 (quoted inside TRAI’s 2022 paper) then “sought reconsideration”, observing that “considerable time has elapsed” and the “M&E landscape has changed drastically, particularly with the advent of new digital technologies”.
  3. 12.4.2022: TRAI reopens — and starts doubting its own metric. Consultation Paper No. 04/2022 asked whether safeguards for horizontal/vertical integration across broadcasting and distribution were still needed; it records the shift of scholarly opinion (“some scholars are shifting away from HHI”, the index being criticised as considering “only market power… not… pluralism” — TRAI’s 2022 words), and floated a novel first limb: restrictions triggered by mere presence in more than one media segment, before any share arithmetic.
  4. The industry answer (2022): the four trade bodies — IBDF (broadcasters), NBDA (news publishers), INS, AROI (radio) — collectively said: plurality already exists; monitoring mechanisms already exist sector-wise; caps would interfere with normal business and violate Art. 19(1)(a) for houses and the citizens’ right to receive; and telecom operators are now the biggest news distributors, so regulation must have parity before it imposes parity on media. A strong submission — and a confession in the same breath: the “plurality” case for ownership was argued without a word about the paid-news business model that TRAI’s own 2014 chapter had linked to concentration.
  5. 12.6.2026: the back door opens. The uploaded consultation on the Telecommunications (Television, Radio and Associated Services) Rules, 2026 — framed under the Telecommunications Act, 2023 — does not enact “CMO rules”. It does something quieter and stronger: it writes the surviving caps into authorisation eligibility and licence conditions (Part F2): 20% vertical ceilings in both directions (r.6(5)–(10)), radio city cap 40%/minimum three operators (r.9(4)), the 51%-largest-Indian-shareholder rule and the anti-trust/anti-political control clauses (r.9(3)), control-change prior permission (r.22) and transfer gates (r.47) — ownership regulation as terms of a licence, enforceable by revocation instead of penalty. The 2005 TRAI instinct — “amend the carriage statute” — has finally been executed.

Still open (state these as the answer’s closing tension): no horizontal cap for TV channels; nothing for print; nothing for the platforms that now carry the news (telcos, OTT, social feeds — the 2014 “review when internet matters” note that never came back); no disclosure of top-10 advertisers regime; no Media Commission. The caps died as competition rules and were born as licensing rules — which is why 8.3, 8.5 and 8.2’s MCMC all teach the same lesson: in India, media structure is regulated through the carriage pipe, never through the content pipe.

The carriage litigation the caps needed anyway — must-carry and the tariff orders

Why TRAI could regulate channel-line-ups at all is a mini-version of the constitutional argument in D1, and it was litigated to the Supreme Court twice [external; reported — decisions described from law-firm case records verified this build]: (i) Creative Media & Ors. v. UOI (Supreme Court, 2016) upheld the must-carry architecture — the 2004 amendments to the TRAI Act and the Notification requiring DTH operators to carry channels on request and the NTO — rejecting the claim that the “service provider” delimitation unconstitutionally fettered broadcasters; (ii) the 2017 NTO batch (Star India’s challenge, Madras High Court, affirmed by the Supreme Court in 2018) — the argument that pricing/bundling rules are content regulation was rejected: TRAI regulates carriage and the commercial terms of channels, and even where burdens touch content exploitation, “the TRAI Act’s objectives of fair competition and consumer interest” justify them; no Copyright Act right was encroached; the regulator “does not guarantee profitability”. The loop: what broadcasters sued over (exclusive carriage deals), the 2026 rules simply prohibit — r.34(b): “An authorised entity shall not… enter into any agreement for the exclusive distribution of television channels” (verbatim, uploaded). The next two decades of carriage fights will be licence-condition fights, not tariff fights.

The one-paragraph essay answer on “cross-ownership” if it comes (and it will)

“Cross-media ownership caps have been India’s longest draft: referred 2008, recommended 2009, refined with an HHI machine in 2014, reconsidered on MIB’s 2021 reference, and — in the 2026 broadcast rules consultation — finally disguised as licensing. Print remains untouched because it was never within TRAI’s remit (the Authority’s own 2009 concession: ‘merely suggestions’); the vertical 20% ceilings survived because spectrum and carriages are public-trust resources regulable under Art. 19(6); and the horizontal architecture failed because the news market defies shoe-market arithmetic (‘the market for ideas is very different… numbers alone don’t matter’ — TRAI 2014). The honest conclusion: India regulates who may carry and who may hold how much of the pipe, not how many voices one hand may own — leaving plurality to the same instrument that polices paid news: the candidate’s and the owner’s ledger.”

E2 · The ad-cap trial — the Broadcasting Sector’s longest case, decided on 29 May 2026 (live case study)

Delhi High Court · W.P.(C) 7982/2013 & connected petitions · decided 29.5.2026 · Kshetarpal & Mahajan, JJ.

Why a 2013 writ is Unit 8’s freshest case

Because it decides, on a full record, the question Parts D and E kept re-opening: can a regulator cap how much advertising a channel may carry, or is advertising volume part of “content” and therefore the licence’s domain? And because the answer it gave — yes, and by TRAI — lands squarely on the economics that the paid-news reports made famous: an outlet whose advertisement inventory is capped at twelve minutes an hour cannot fund a “free” supplement on the back of an advertiser either. The judgment was not in the workspace; the dossier below is built from five contemporaneous law-press and news accounts (all External; reported) — pleadings and quotes are reproduced as those reports record them.

FACTS. In 2012–13 TRAI exercised its QoS-powers to regulate advertising load in TV channels: a cap of 12 minutes per clock hour (10 minutes of advertisements + 2 minutes of channel self-promotion), with a further ≤10-minute per-programme limb and penalties for breach — carried into the interconnect/tariff architecture and the 2017 NTO-era rules. The cable/satellite rules’ corresponding provision (r. 7(11)-class of the 1994 Rules, as amended) gave it statutory expression. Broadcasters’ associations — claiming the industry lost thousands of crores of revenue — filed seventeen petitions in the Delhi High Court from 2013 (the lead being W.P.(C) 7982/2013), attacking both the regulator’s competence and the caps’ constitutionality; the batch heard and dismissed on 29 May 2026.

ISSUES. ① Is TRAI competent to prescribe advertising volume under the TRAI Act, ss. 11(1) and 36, after the 2004 notification declaring broadcasting a telecom service — or is ad-content the exclusive province of the Ministry under the 1995 Act? ② Do the caps regulate “content” impermissibly, colliding with Art. 19(1)(a) (right to circulate) and 19(1)(g) (right to carry on the business of broadcasting)? ③ Can broadcasters complain of revenue loss as an infringement of a fundamental right?

ARGUMENTS — broadcasters. Advertising is part of a channel’s editorial-commercial mix; volume caps are content regulation; TRAI’s Act reaches “carriage”, not the programme platter; spectrum/airwave arguments cut the other way since the channels up-link and DTH-carry without any scarce-resource bargain; revenue starvation cannot be justified by consumer “protection” when viewers themselves choose to watch; the 2-minute self-promo limb micromanages programming hours; and the caps were never preceded by the cost-benefit discipline any other telecom regulation demands.

ARGUMENTS — UOI / TRAI. Advertising volume is a quality-of-service parameter — a service issue for subscribers who pay for a bouquet and receive an ad-break marquee; the caps are time-based and content-neutral (no rule addresses what may be said, only how much may interrupt per hour); broadcasting was notified as a telecom service in Nov 2004, squarely engaging ss. 11–13; and the airwaves-public-trust doctrine (CAB 1995, ¶78 — the same paragraph TRAI quoted in D1) means a broadcaster exploits a public resource on terms, not of right; on revenue: regulation of the market does not warrant profitability.

DECISION. Petitions dismissed; the cap and the 2012/13 framework upheld. The Court held (as the reports distil the ruling): TRAI was competent — the notified-service architecture plus ss. 11 and 36 cover the regulation; the caps are a legitimate time-based, content-neutral QoS measure; the regulatory scheme “does not guarantee profitability” of a broadcaster’s business, there is “no unfettered right to exploit” public spectrum/airwave resources, and revenue grievance sounds, if anywhere, in Art. 19(1)(g) — where a reasonable-restriction analysis on a licensed public-resource business readily answers it — and not in Art. 19(1)(a), since no speech is suppressed.

PRINCIPLE EVOLVED. ① “Content” ≠ “container”: volume, timing and interruption of advertising are carriage-regulation even when embedded in programmes — the sharpest modern gloss on the old MIB-content / TRAI-carriage split. ② Public-trust reasoning now does commercial work: CAB’s airwaves doctrine (Unit 4’s case) has migrated into a pure market-structure holding — no unfettered exploitation, so no vested claim to the pre-cap ad inventory. ③ Profitability is not a right: “does not guarantee profitability” is the sentence future petitions will plead around for a decade. ④ For this unit: the ad-cap regime is the economic half of an anti-paid-news architecture — cap the paid inventory, and “editorial generosity” becomes a cheaper tell; the Court’s indifference to the revenue protest is exactly the balance the 47th Report sought when it asked for money-trail rules the legislature never wrote.

F1 · 8.5 “Licensing and responsibility of the licensee” — the Cable Act, 1995, as the duty-template

First, the word the unit is really about: “licensee” without a licence

The 1995 Act is famous for what it abolished: the permit-raj over cable (the 1994 Ordinance’s regime, and before it the Telegraph Act’s permission culture). Its s. 3 is a registration bar, verbatim from the uploaded reprint: “No person shall operate a cable television network unless he is registered as a cable operator under this Act.” Yet the Act is saturated with licensee-duty: eligibility conditions in delegated rules, terms of registration policed like licence conditions, suspension and revocation, a prohibition power, and criminal punishment. That is the syllabus phrase decoded: India’s broadcasting “licensee” is a registrant with licence-grade duties and licence-grade sanctions. The 2026 draft rules (F2) simply complete the arc by renaming registration back into “authorisation” — with 27 pages of conditions.

Real examples — the Act biting

(a) Digitisation (2012–14): the phased s. 4A notifications (analog switched off in four phases to 31.12.2014 — the dates the Notes compendium records) worked purely through registration terms: no DAS undertaking, no new registration; millions of operators re-equipped because a proviso said so. (b) Prohibition orders: s. 19 is the section behind episodic channel black-outs during communal flashpoints — the “public interest” standard that the 2026 rules inherit verbatim in r.62 directions and the r.48/60 architecture. (c) The missing example — be honest: prosecutions under s. 16 are vanishingly rare; enforcement reality is the suspension/registrar track — the same asymmetry (civil heavy, criminal shy) that makes the s. 10A route Chavan’s chosen path in 8.1.

(a) A newspaper syndicates its investigations to a foreign daily → the proprietor may do this because s. 17 proviso (a) gave her exactly the publication right — but turning the series into a documentary needs the reporters’ fresh licence: the proviso “so far as” limb in action. ESPN Star Sports v. Global Broadcast News Ltd. (Del HC, 2008) — “the broadcast right is separate and distinct”

Facts. ESPN Star Sports, exclusive licensee for the telecast of major cricket tournaments, sued Global Broadcast News (a then-live 24×7 sports/news channel) over use of short clips and highlights in its sports bulletins and over mobile/ancillary exploitation; the exclusive licensee had not joined the tournament boards (the copyright owners of the underlying event footage) as parties. Issues. ① Is the broadcast reproduction right a “species of copyright”, dragging Chapter XII’s machinery — including s. 61 (exclusive licensee must implead the owner) and ss. 18–19 (assignment formalities) — into a s. 37 case? ② Can the licensee sue alone for infringement of BRR in the telecast of live events? Arguments. Defendant: Chapter VIII is a “complete Code” expressly invoking only chosen provisions — s. 61 out. Plaintiff: harmonious reading keeps ss. 18/19/61 in; deliberate omissions in s. 39A “rule out the application of all other provisions” only where the statute says so. Decision. Single Judge (S. Ravindra Bhat, J., 18.2.2008) granted and shaped relief; Division Bench (Mukul Mudgal, C.J. & V.K. Shali, J., 26.9.2008) in terms now standard: “the Broadcast Reproduction Right, which is different from Copyright, is with the Broadcasting Organization which is causing the Broadcast to be communicated to the public under their Logo by any means of wireless diffusion or by wire”; “the Satellite television broadcasting right is an independent right for which the plaintiff cannot claim any copyright” — the suit was founded on broadcasting right, not licensee-right in underlying copyright; “Section 61 is not applicable to broadcast reproduction right”, Chapter VIII being “a separate and distinct” code. Principle. ① BRR is an autonomous neighbouring right — the broadcaster owns its signal regardless of who owns the match’s underlying copyright; ② a BRR licensee needs no co-plaintiff rights-holder to police clip piracy; ③ the flip side for TV news: the BRR cuts against the third-party clipper, but s. 39’s fair-use limb (reporting current events) is where “news highlights” defence lives — every sports-vs-news-channel interim order since 2008 walks that line. For the exam, this is Unit 8’s case for 8.6: facts, issues, both sides’ clauses, double judgment, principle.

Q8(A) · Dec-2024 verbatim — “Paid News—A threat to independent and fair media—Elucidate.” · 10 marks · model answer (~700 words, exam prose)

PAID NEWS: THE THREAT, THE CASE LAW, THE MISSING LAW

1. Meaning. “Paid news” is not a statutory term; its settled working definition comes from the Press Council’s Report on Paid News (No. 1 of 2010): news or an article appearing in any media (print or electronic) “brought about in consideration of money or kind”. The Supreme Court, in the case the DU reader reprints, described the election form of it: material published “in the garb of news eulogizing” a candidate — here the Chief Minister of Maharashtra — an expenditure “now a well-known phenomenon, as ‘paid news’” (Ashok Shankarrao Chavan v. Madhavrao Kinhalkar, (2014) 7 SCC 99, ¶51). The defining feature is structural: news merchandise priced outside every ledger the law requires to be kept — no invoice in the candidate’s accounts, no disclosure to the reader, sometimes no payment to the editor’s desk at all (the outlet claiming it published “gratuitously… [from] links with or leanings towards” the candidate’s party, ¶52).

2. Why it threatens independent and fair media. (i) Independence: journalism funded by a concealed paymaster is advertising with a newsroom’s credibility borrowed — the reader cannot discount what the reader cannot see; the “fourth estate” becomes the candidate’s billboard. (ii) Fair elections: paid coverage is unaccounted money power purchasing votes through the press — the same “money power virtually controls the whole field of election” the Supreme Court condemned in ¶55. (iii) Business-model capture: the 47th Parliamentary Standing Committee located the cause in corporatisation, de-segregation of editorial and commercial roles, and contract-system newsrooms (quoted in TRAI’s 2014 Recommendations, ¶1.16) — outlets dependent on advertiser-goodwill become structural, not episodic, suspects; “private treaties” (business-for-coverage arrangements) make the same trade on the commercial side. (iv) Chilling of the honest press: once favourable copy is purchasable, investigative independence becomes a competitive disadvantage.

3. The legal response actually available. The RPA punishes paid news as hidden expenditure: s. 77 (account of all expenditure “incurred or authorized”, incl. party-published matter under the Common Cause presumption) → s. 78 (lodging) → s. 10A (ECI satisfaction of an account “in the manner required”, no good justification → gazette disqualification for three years). Chavan holds (¶111) that “Section 10A clothes the Election Commission with the requisite power and authority to enquire into the allegations” of falsity, even after the election petition failed, the Article 329(b) objection notwithstanding, and orders day-to-day conclusion in 45 days. On the merits of the parallel appeal, the Court upheld the first-ever paid-news disqualification — Smt. Umlesh Yadav, disqualified 20.10.2011 after the PCI found her “wave” publications “camouflaged as news items… only an advertisement” (¶54) and her declared ₹840 receipt stood against the papers’ own ₹8,000 + ₹21,250 cash bills in her husband’s name (¶115) — “on merits as well… no ground to interfere” (¶117). Collateral law: s. 123(6) corrupt practice (expending beyond the ceiling; tribunal route, 45-day limitation); the election-expenditure provisions have IPC-era twins (Ch. IXA — e.g. bribery, s. 171B → BNS s. 170), though paid news itself fits the accounting, not the bribery, frame; PCI censure (31.3.2010 order against the papers) — ethics without teeth; MCMC certification of poll-window political advertisements (SC, 2013) now catches “disguised ad” items before publication.

4. The gap, honestly stated. Paid news is not yet a corrupt practice: the PCI (2010), the Law Ministry’s Committee on Electoral Reforms (2010), the 47th Standing Committee Report (2013) and the Law Commission’s 255th Report (2015) each recommended a statutory definition and a s. 123 entry; none was enacted — the reader’s reprint’s practical lesson is that courts can only enforce the law of accounts that exists. No media-side offence exists, no outlet may be penalised by the Commission, and “editorial leanings” remain a complete answer to a PCI complaint (¶52).

5. Conclusion. The threat to “independent and fair media” is thus twofold and asymmetrically answered: to the media, paid news converts influence into inventory — policed today by ethics and competition law alone; to elections, it converts money into mandate — policed by s. 77/10A with real force since Chavan. The remedy India has chosen reaches the money, not the masthead; the reform agenda (definition, corrupt-practice entry, outlet-side disclosure, and the 2026 rules’ prophylactic bar on advertising-agency control of broadcasters, r. 9(3)(c)) concedes exactly that the ledger route is necessary but insufficient. A free press paid for by invisible hands is unfree at source — and until the RPA defines the payment, its harshest lawful consequence remains an accounting one: disqualification of the buyer, not the seller.

₹5,379 declared vs “several crores” paid-news (¶51); Jharkhand ₹18,92,353 shown vs ₹9,32,56,259 I-T finding vs ₹25,00,000 ceiling (¶53); Umlesh ₹840 receipt vs ₹8,000 + ₹21,250 cash bills in D.P. Yadav’s name (¶115).
4 · s. 10A elements.
(a) failure to lodge “within the time and in the manner required”; (b) “no good reason or justification” — satisfaction by ECI → gazette → 3 years; s. 11: ECI may remove/reduce, with recorded reasons.
5 · Two-track rule.
Election petition (tribunal; s. 123 corrupt practices; 45-day s. 81 limitation; invalidates election) vs s. 10A (Commission; any citizen; reasonable time; disqualifies person). Chavan: parallel, no Art. 329(b) clash.
6 · The presumption.
Party spend presumed candidate-authorized (Common Cause); s. 77 Explanation 1’s exception (party leaders’ travel) is the only shelter — “clearly untrue” result at ¶114.
7 · Exit-poll statute.
s. 126A (2009): no conduct + no publication/dissemination, print included, in the ECI-notified window (first poll open → last close + 30 min); 2 years / fine / both; “disseminate in any other manner whatsoever”.
8 · s. 126 ≠ s. 126A.
126 = poll-eve bar on publication/communication by electronic media or display (48 h before each constituency’s poll; print outside). 126A = exit polls, all media, window = whole multiphase election.
9 · PCI 1996 five + one.
Disclose: agency, sponsor, sample, methodology, margin of error; embargo: no exit-poll publication till last phase ends — restated by the Council 30.3.2026.
10 · Why no opinion-poll law?
ECI’s 1998 guidelines withdrawn after the Supreme Court’s view (could not be enforced against media); 2004 paper sought statute; Parliament did exit polls only (2009).
11 · MCMC.
SC, 2013: no political advertisement on poll day + one day before without Media Certification & Monitoring Committee pre-certification — the disguised-ad filter.
12 · 2009 caps (what is safe).
HITS verbatim: no >20% equity in a HITS licensee across other broadcasters/DTH “and vice-versa”, financial-investor carve-out, no HITS–cable-MSO bar; vertical-integration 20%; print items “merely suggestions”; telecom-media: none + 2-year review.
13 · 2014 machinery.
Control = 20% equity OR >50% votes OR majority board OR management say; markets = language×state (Hindi/English national); HHI>1,800 + >1,000 pts both TV and print → dilute/exit; 3-year review; political/religious/govt barred; “market for ideas ≠ shoes or biscuits”.
14 · The fate.
Never notified; MIB reference 19.2.2021 (file 8/17/2014-BP&L); CP No. 4/2022 (12.4.2022); IBDF/NBDA/INS/AROI pushback; 2026 draft imports vertical caps + radio caps as licence conditions; no horizontal/aggregate caps to date.
15 · Ad caps.
12 min/hour = 10 ads + 2 self-promo (TRAI 2012/13 + rules); Delhi HC, 29.5.2026: competent time-based content-neutral QoS; “does not guarantee profitability”; airwaves public trust; 19(1)(g) not (a) for revenue claims.
16 · Cable Act gate.
s. 3 registration-only; s. 4(6) terms in 19(2) idiom; s. 4(7) suspension/revocation after hearing; s. 16 ≤2yr/₹1k first, ≤5yr and ₹5k later; s. 16(2) s. 4A cognizable; s. 18 authorised-officer complaint only; s. 19 prohibition order.
17 · 2026 authorisations.
Validity: TV channel/teleport/CR 10y; news agency 10y; distribution 20y; private radio 15y (r. 13); renewal ≥120 days + “≤5 code breaches” condition (r. 14(2)); 51% largest-Indian + no trust/religious/political + no ad-agency affiliation + majority resident KMP for news (r. 9(3)/19(3)).
18 · 2026 control gates.
Shareholding intimation 30/15 days; change in control = prior written permission (r. 22); transfers post lock-in only via court scheme/business transfer/group (r. 47); revocation for false documents (r. 60); ₹5,000 citizen complaint (r. 51(3)).
19 · Copyright newsroom rule.
s. 17 proviso (a): proprietor is first owner only for publication in that newspaper/magazine class, absent agreement; reporter keeps the rest. Term: life + 60; films/records/photos/govt works 60 from publication; BRR 25; performers 50.
20 · BRR independence.
ESPN Star Sports v. Global Broadcast News (Del HC DB 26.9.2008): BRR “different from Copyright”, “independent right”; s. 61 not applicable — broadcaster licensee sues alone; s. 39 fair uses (news) are the clip-channel’s defence.

RPA 1951 · s. 126A — complete text (verified against a neutral statutory reprint)

Section 126A — Restriction on publication and dissemination of result of exit polls, etc.

(1) No person shall conduct any exit poll and publish or publicise by means of the print or electronic media or disseminate in any other manner, whatsoever, the result of any exit poll during such period, as may be notified by the Election Commission in this regard.
(2) For the purposes of sub-section (1), the Election Commission shall, by a general order, notify the date and time having due regard to the following, namely:— (a) in case of a general election, the period may commence from the beginning of the hours fixed for poll on the first day of poll and continue till half an hour after closing of the poll in all the States and Union territories; (b) in case of a bye-election or a number of bye elections held together, the period may commence from the beginning of the hours fixed for poll on and from the first day of poll and continue till half an hour after closing of the poll: Provided that in case of a number of bye-elections held together on different days, the period may commence from the beginning of the hours fixed for poll on the first day of poll and continue till half an hour after closing of the last poll.
(3) Any person who contravenes the provisions of this section shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years or with fine or with both.
Explanation.— For the purposes of this section, — (a) “exit poll” means an opinion survey respecting how electors have voted at an election or respecting how all the electors have performed with regard to the identification of a political party or candidate in an election; (b) “electronic media” includes internet, radio and television including Internet Protocol Television, satellite, terrestrial of cable channels, mobile and such other media either owned by the Government or private person or by both; (c) “print media” includes any newspaper, magazine or periodical, poster, placard, handbill or any other document; (d) “dissemination” includes publication in any “print media” or broadcast or display on any electronic media.

Closing map — the unit in four moves you can say aloud

Move 1 (8.1–8.2): influence and information. Money enters the news (garb-of-garbed copy, ¶51) and prediction tries to enter the vote; the RPA answers with ledgers (s. 77/10A — Chavan) and clocks (s. 126/126A) but refuses to name the practice — paid news has never been made a corrupt practice, whatever the three prescribed reports said.

Move 2 (8.3–8.4): structure. TRAI wrote the ownership architecture (2009), refined it into arithmetic (2014), and the Union shelved both — until the carriage statutes quietly adopted its surviving pieces: 20% vertical, 51% nationality, agency-separation, control-permission, all enforceable by licence revocation (2026 draft); the Competition Act catches what the caps cannot (Star–Sony, cartels, the 2026 ad-cap judgment policing the economic space advertising occupies).

Move 3 (8.5): the duty-holder. From 1995 registration-with-code-duties to 2026 authorisation-with-security-clearance-and-90-day-recordings — “responsibility of the licensee” is the only continuous strand of Indian broadcast regulation; the State that won’t define paid news will happily revoke a licence.

Move 4 (8.6): the property. And when it all becomes an asset, copyright allocates — proprietor-for-the-news (s. 17), broadcaster-for-the-signal (ss. 37–39), performer-for-the-voice (s. 38A), societies-for-the-money (s. 33), statutory licences for the friction (s. 31D) — the reader’s last case-pair (Badal Dhar Chowdhury, ESPN) proving that media law and property law are one syllabus read twice.
