0 · The unit map — three media, one question

Unit 4 is the structural counterpart of Units 1–3. Unit 1 showed how Parliament legislated the press from 1780 to 2026; Units 2.1–2.2 fixed the right (Art. 19(1)(a) expanded; privacy, defamation, stings as the counter-rights); Unit 2.3–2.4 gave the public’s access and the fair-trial limits; Unit 3 policed contempt. Unit 4 now assembles the machinery: who regulates which medium, under what power, with what teeth, and how far the courts let them go. The unit’s organising sentence — the syllabus’s own — is that “regulation can occur at the Central, State, local or self-regulatory level”; and the unit’s governing principle, lifted from the case book, is the Supreme Court’s endorsement of the F.S. Nariman Committee at PDF p. 205: “Regulation of the media is not an end in itself; and allocative regulation is necessary because the ‘air waves’ are public property … However, allocative regulation is different from regulation per se.” (In re Destruction of Public & Private Properties, ¶33(iii)).

4.1 Print
No licence. No prior restraint. Registration only (PRB Act 1867) + one soft-touch statutory council (Press Council Act 1978) + the 2012 proposal to convert it into a Media Council covering all media.
4.2 Broadcasting
The medium runs on public property (airwaves), so it has always been licensed: Telegraph Act 1885 → Doordarshan monopoly → CAB 1995 breaks the monopoly → Cable TV Networks Act 1995 + Programme/Advertisement Codes → Prasar Bharati for the public broadcaster → self-regulation (BCCC/NBSA) for private content, with pre-certification reserved for cinema.
4.3 Social media
No licences at all; a delegated “notice-and-takedown” State: intermediary immunity (s.79) conditioned on compliance; blocking power (s.69A); a criminal fringe (66A — struck down; 66E/67/67A/67B — alive); courts commanding global de-indexing (X v. UOI, 2021).

A1 · Why regulate at all — the theories the unit silently assumes

Every question in this unit is a clash between two propositions the case book puts side by side. The first is Rajagopal/Goswami: the press “is well within its rightful domain” and any blanket ban “would fetter the independence of free press which is one of the hallmarks of our democratic setup” (Ajay Goswami, PDF p. 240). The second is Court on its Own Motion (the Uma Khurana sting): “Right to freedom of press is a valuable right but the right carries with it responsibility and duty to be truthful and to protect rights of others” (PDF p. 125, ¶15). Regulation answers the second; legitimacy of any particular answer is judged by the first.

JustificationCore argumentWhere the project material deploys it
Scarcity of spectrumElectromagnetic frequencies are finite; allowing “first come first served” would make communication impossible. Allocation must therefore be administered — and administration is not itself censorship.Case Book Destruction ¶33(iii): “allocative regulation is necessary because the ‘air waves’ are public property and cannot technically be free for all but have to be distributed in a fair manner. However, allocative regulation is different from regulation per se.” (PDF p. 205)
Public trust / public propertyAirwaves belong to the public; the State is trustee, not owner; the medium “should be under the control of the public as distinct from government”.External; reported CAB per Jeevan Reddy, J. (¶¶89–91 of the reported judgment) and Sawant, J.: “the airwaves or frequencies are a public property … to be controlled and regulated by a public authority in the interests of the public”. Case Book echoes it inside Shreya ¶29 and the UIMPAA excerpt.
Captive audience / harm to the un-consentingBroadcasting intrudes into the home; children cannot unsubscribe. Hence time-slot, warning and watershed devices.Case Book Destruction ¶31(c): media “must classify its work through warning systems as in cinema so that children and those who are challenged adhere to time, place and manner restraints”; Uma Khurana proposed guideline 5 (schedule “with an awareness of the likely audience in mind”) (PDF p. 127).
Diversity / anti-propagandaConcentration or government capture of a scarce medium starves the market of ideas; independent allocation and plural ownership protect the listener’s (not only the speaker’s) right.Case Book UIMPAA excerpt (PDF pp. 285–286, quoting CAB at p. 213): “the right to communicate … includes right to communicate through any media that is available whether print or electronic or audio-visual”; Goswami petitioner: “growing tendency among youngsters …” for receiving-side control; PCI s.13(2)(e) (keep under review developments “restricting the supply and dissemination of news”).
Administration of justice & fair trialIrresponsible live reporting can prejudice trials, provoke violence, defeat police work — regulation is measured by “real and substantial risk”.Case Book Naveen Jindal excerpt quoting Sahara ¶42 and CAB: postponement orders pass the 19(2) test because “the several grounds mentioned in Article 19(2) … are ultimately referable to societal interest” (PDF pp. 153–154, 285); Uma Khurana: unverified trial-by-media caused a woman’s beating, dismissal and “stinking experience” (PDF pp. 122–125).
Privacy, dignity, gender security onlineThe same liberal premises justify a duty on platforms: a woman’s images uploaded without consent are “offensive by association”; free speech does not license the distributor of an intimate-image harm.Case Book X v. UOI ¶¶2, 92 and directions (PDF pp. 207, 218); balance quoted at ¶109: “what is to be borne in mind is also the rights of privacy, the right of reputation of a citizen … The balance is always hard to seek, however, Courts can only endeavour to strike the balance.”
Self-regulation as the preferred floorIf the media cannot expose lapses under government regulation, the profession must “evolve institutional checks and safeguards” — peer monitoring, not State desks.Case Book Destruction ¶32 (NBA preamble, verbatim): “censorship and free speech are sworn enemies … ‘self-governance’, and a monitoring by a ‘jury of peers’”; ¶33(viii): endorses self-regulation “but … need not to drift from self regulation to some statutory structure which may prove to be oppressive and full of litigative potential”.

One-line memory

Broadcast is regulable because its channel is scarce and public; print is not regulable because its channel is free and infinite; the internet was to be the infinite channel — until courts and codes made the intermediary the choke point. (Everything else in the unit is detail.)

Exam caution

Never write “media can be regulated because it is powerful”. The case book’s answer (Destruction ¶33(iii)) is technical and constitutional: allocation is legitimate for spectrum; content control needs Art. 19(2) justification, “proportionality built into the concept of reasonableness”, “the least invasive approach”. Power alone justifies nothing.

B1 · 4.1 Print — the registration spine before the Press Council (PRB Act 1867 and company)

Before asking what the Press Council does, know what the State actually controls about a newspaper — very little, and all of it clerical. The MIB compendium uploaded to the workspace (“Media Laws in India”, Notes) states the position exactly:

“The earliest surviving enactment specifically directed towards the press was passed in 1867, the Press and Registration of Books Act (PRB Act) (XXV of 1867). The objective was however not to establish governmental control over the freedom of the Press. It was a regulatory law which enabled Government to regulate printing presses and newspapers by a system of registration and to preserve copies of books and other matter printed in India … major amendments were carried out in 1955 following the recommendations of the First Press Commission in 1953, consequent upon which the Office of the Registrar of Newspapers of India (RNI) was created and started functioning in 1956.” — “Media Laws in India” (MIB compilation, uploaded), PDF p. 22
ProvisionContent (per the uploaded compilation)Regulation or not?
PRB Act s.3Every book or paper printed in India must carry the printer’s name and place of printing, and the publisher’s name and place of publishing, printed legibly.Identity rule — the anti-anonymity ancestor of today’s traceability debates (compare IT Rules r.4(2), G1).
PRB Act s.4The keeper of a press makes and subscribes a declaration before the District/Presidency/Sub-divisional Magistrate (Form I, Registration of Newspapers (Central) Rules 1956); fresh declaration if press shifts jurisdiction; no new declaration if the change is ≤ 60 days and within the same magistrate’s local jurisdiction; Title Verification Letter from the Press Registrar before declaring.Clerical; the Magistrate is a deponent-collector, not a licensing authority. Compare the colonial s.3 of the Vernacular Press Act 1876 (Unit 1) — same offices, opposite purpose.
PRB Act s.5For newspapers: masthead must name publisher and editor with date; printer and publisher appear before the Magistrate to declare title, language and periodicity; declaration must specify the particulars of the newspaper.Registration, not permission — but the RNI title verification is the practical gate (real example: the 2019–21 fight over the title “Telegraph”-style names; or any paper’s masthead).

Why the exam cares: the Press Council sits on top of this spine without touching it: s.27 of the 1978 Act was the (repealed) “Amendment of Act 25 of 1867” — the only direct link between the two regimes. Registration supplies the addressee (who can be summoned by the PCI, censured by a court, prosecuted by the State); the PCI supplies the standard of conduct. Everything else — licence, permit, pre-clearance — is absent by design; the MIB-notes say it plainly of the post-1977 era: “Press Council advised the Government not to put curbs on the press even in disturbed areas like Jammu and Kashmir. Debate box — “A Media Council with penal powers should replace the Press Council.”

For:

  • Jurisdictional whack-a-mole: print alone is policed by nobody; convergence made the line absurd (PCI counsel’s own “no jurisdiction over the electronic media”, PDF p. 239).
  • Censure without consequence is education, not regulation — the 1999–2003 unanswered letters prove it.
  • Peer bodies with discipline exist for every other profession (Bar, Medicine) — the “preferred freedom” argument cuts both ways: professionals protect the public first.
  • The Courts already do it piecemeal (contempt, defamation, Goswami directions to “seriously look into” amendments) — a statutory council would regularise and cabin executive power.

Against:

  • Executive “de-recognition” powers over media were the colonial playbook (Unit 1: the 1908/1910 Acts’ licensing + gag machinery) — a fine paid by the paper is censorship the reader pays for.
  • State appointment of even a “professional” council remains State appointment — the case book’s repeated prescription is autonomy and self-governance “by a jury of peers” (¶32–33), not a bigger regulator.
  • Determination of “public taste” by a tribunal replicates the vague-standard defect condemned in Shreya Singhal.
  • Scarcity logic justifies allocation, not content — importing it to print is category error (¶33(iii) itself separates the two).

Verdict line for a 10-mark note: “accept the diagnosis (jurisdiction + teeth), reject the licence-logic; give the council post-publication adjudication and statutory right-of-reply, keep prior restraint constitutionally unavailable, and fund broadcast allocation through an independent public trust as CAB asked for 1995 and Pitroda asked for 2014.”

Facts (reported decision — flagged; the reader assumes them)
  • The Cricket Association of Bengal (and the BCCI in parallel) organised international cricket and had agreed that a private foreign broadcaster would produce the live telecast, with the right sold to it for consideration; the arrangement contemplated outside-broadcast equipment being imported and the live feed uplinked for carriage by the chosen agency.
  • Doordarshan/Ministry of I&B objected: under the then regime, telecasting required the Government’s permission — the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 gave the Central Government the exclusive privilege of telegraphy (s.4(1), incl. wireless), and broadcasting policy sat in MIB; the Secretary (Telecom) issued orders withholding facilities; DD asserted its own right to cover the matches.
  • Before the Calcutta High Court (in the run-up to the 1995–96 series/World-Cup-window), CAB succeeded: the Single Judge and then the Division Bench held that a private organiser could telecast through an agency of its choice — the “no monopoly of the State” decision that the Union appealed to the Supreme Court (the matches were in fact telecast pursuant to the interim arrangement; the Court records that fact to show the live dispute had passed).
  • Two legal frames collided: (a) entry into broadcasting (a licence under the Telegraph Act, s.4(1) proviso) — CAB had never applied for that licence, relying instead on permissions/clearances from several departments; (b) freedom — whether Art. 19(1)(a) itself carries a right to broadcast, and whether the State monopoly of airwaves is constitutional.
Issues (the Court’s own terrain; the reader preserves two)
  1. Does Art. 19(1)(a) include the right to telecast/broadcast — and does the citizen’s right to know include being able to receive information through any media? (Answered yes in the broad sense; but see 3.)
  2. Are airwaves public property, and what follows for State monopoly versus private control?
  3. Is a Government monopoly of electronic broadcasting permissible under the Constitution — may it restrict who may broadcast, on what terms, without a statutory basis?
  4. Was the restriction regime (executive permissions under the Telegraph Act; ad-hoc clearances) lawful, and did CAB, having not applied for a licence under the s.4(1) proviso, have any right to telecast through an agency of its choice?
  5. What is the remedy for the structural defect — to whom should airwaves be entrusted? (The reader’s ¶29-of-Shreya reference and ¶37 of CAB: the necessity question — whether such a power “would be asserted again”: see G3.)
Arguments — both sides (as preserved in the reader)

CAB / event-organisers (as the excerpts record the claim being made):

  • Freedom of speech is medium-neutral: “the right to communicate … includes right to communicate through any media that is available whether print or electronic or audio-visual” — the passage the UIMPAA bench quotes from CAB at p. 213 and adopts.
  • Freedom of expression includes the right to acquire information and to disseminate it; the State cannot reserve the channels of dissemination to itself as proprietor; monopoly kills the diversity that Art. 19(1)(a) exists to protect (the Court’s own framing, borrowed from Art. 10 ECHR, is quoted verbatim in UIMPAA).
  • A “must-carry”/compelled-speech analogy cuts the other way: compelled carriage is acceptable only where it “furthers informed decision-making”, not where it is propaganda (UIMPAA’s development of the point, PDF pp. 286–287, citing Wooley v. Maynard and Turner Broadcasting v. FCC).
  • The revenue argument: an event-owner sells rights it lawfully holds; DD’s financial interest is not a fundamental-rights interest.

Union of India / Ministry (as the excerpts record):

  • Airwaves are scarce and public; 19(1)(a) protects speech, not the use of public property: “no one can claim the fundamental right to … use the airwaves … Only where the statute permits him to use the public property … can he use it” (Jeevan Reddy, J. recording the accepted position, ¶90).
  • Technical necessity: frequencies must be allocated by a single authority or transmissions collide — regulation is a condition of any broadcasting at all (the same premise the reader’s Destruction ¶33(iii) adopts).
  • Analogy with the press is “wholly inapt” for broadcasting (Jeevan Reddy, J., ¶90): visual media reach a captive domestic audience, can be falsified, and can be captured by “powerful economic, commercial and political interest”.
  • Procedure: without the s.4(1)-proviso licence, the courts cannot substitute themselves for the licensing State; the permissions CAB obtained were departmental courtesies, not legal entitlements.
  • Even the grounds of Art. 19(2), though not using the words “national interest”, are “conceived in the interest of ensuring and maintaining conditions in which the … right can meaningfully be exercised” (¶151) and “ultimately referable to societal interest” (¶189) — as paraphrased by the Constitution Bench in Sahara, preserved in the reader at PDF pp. 153–154.

C2 · CAB — the decision, the airwaves doctrine, and the seven propositions

Decision

External; reported The appeals were disposed of (the telecasts had already occurred, making the interim dispute academic), on these lines of holding — Sawant, J. (for himself and Mohan, J.), holding as follows (the operative enumeration of the leading judgment):

  1. Airwaves/frequencies are public property. “Their use has to be controlled and regulated by a public authority in the interests of the public and to prevent the invasion of their rights.”
  2. Freedom to broadcast is not freedom to use spectrum. 19(1)(a) covers use of any medium available to the citizen — but access to the scarce public medium is regulated by law: “19(1)(a) does not enable a citizen to impart his information, views and opinions by using the airwaves. He can do so without using the airwaves” (Jeevan Reddy, J., ¶90). “While broadcasting cannot be effected without using airwaves, receiving the broadcast does not involve any such use” — the State may open or close carriage, but may not close the right to speak.
  3. State monopoly is not constitutionally compelled and not acceptable as permanence. “Monopoly of this medium, whether by Government or by an individual, body or organisation is unacceptable … The broadcasting media should be under the control of the public as distinct from Government.”
  4. Structural remedy ordered to the Legislature/Executive: the Central Government was directed to take “immediate steps to establish an independent autonomous public authority, representative of all sections and interests in society, to control and regulate the use of the airwaves”. (Compare the unit’s own echo: Pitroda 2014 recommendation 1.1.4 “Set up a Regulatory Body …”.)
  5. On the facts — CAB, having never applied for a licence under the first proviso to s.4 of the Telegraph Act, had no legal right to have its matches telecast by an agency of its choice; the charge of mala fides against Doordarshan and MIB was rejected; organisers were to approach the nodal ministry (Committee of Secretaries decision dated 12.11.1993) which would consider requests on merits, in public interest. No costs.

Why this is “Case 21” of a MEDIA unit at all

Because the violence was media-produced: cameras and live broadcast turned agitations into copycat destruction (the videography recommendation cuts both ways — evidence and publicity), and the Court used its Art. 142 platform to choose, on the full advice of a judge-led and an editor-led committee, between State regulation and self-regulation of the electronic media. The answer given at ¶34 — “at this juncture we are not inclined to give any positive directions” — is the unit’s single most quotable judicial act of restraint, and the Supreme Court has returned to it whenever “regulate the news channels” is litigated (cf. the file’s sister treatment in Units 2.4 and 3).

D2 · The two Committees’ media part — the reader’s text, worked through

K.T. Thomas Committee (¶4) — four recommendations + the videography guideline
  1. The Prevention of Damage of Public Property Act, 1984 (“PDPP Act”) must be amended to incorporate a rebuttable presumption of the accused’s guilt once the prosecution establishes the two facets.
  2. The Act must make leaders of the organisation that calls for direct action guilty of abetment.
  3. The Act must contain a provision for rebuttable presumption [repeated in the reader’s numbering — note it].
  4. (iv) Evidentiary modernisation — the media hook: “Enable the police officers to arrange videography of the activities damaging public property. In respect of (iv) … we felt that one of the areas to be tapped is evidence through videography in addition to contemporaneous material that may be available through the media, such as electronic media. With the amendments brought in the Evidence Act, through Act 21 of 2000 permitting evidence collected through electronic devices as admissible in evidence.” (BSA 2023 successor note: the old s.65B EA → BSA s.63; the electronic-evidence principle is the reason “media footage = evidence” is routine in riot trials.)

The Committee then added guidelines “to effectuate the modalities for preventive action and adding teeth to enquiry/investigation”, of which the reader keeps the one that matters here — (VI): “In the event that demonstrations turn violent, the officer-in-charge shall ensure that the events are videographed through private operators and also request such further information from the media and others on the incidents in question.” (PDF p. 204.) Read it twice: the State may ask the media for its material — but the Committee, and the Court accepting it, never empowered the State to compel the media’s output. That is source protection (PCI Act s.15(2)) holding the line against disclosure of origins, while permitting production of already-taken images.

The adopted recommendations (¶¶32–33) — the reader’s own list, all nine

  1. “India has a strong, competitive print and electronic media.”
  2.   3. “Regulation of the media is not an end in itself; and allocative regulation is necessary because the ‘air waves’ are public property and cannot technically be free for all … However, a fair interpretation of the constitutional dispensation is to recognize that the principle of proportionality is built into the concept of reasonableness whereby any restrictions on the media follow the least invasive approach.”
  4. Print “under the supervision of the Press Council” — “need for choosing effective measures of supervision — supervision not control.”

It is a fundamental paradigm of freedom of speech that media must be free from governmental control in the matter of ‘content’ and that censorship and free speech are sworn enemies. It, therefore, falls upon the journalistic profession to evolve institutional checks and safeguards, specific to the electronic media … There are models of governance evolved in other countries which have seen evolution of the electronic media, including the news media, much before it developed in India. The remarkable feature of all these models is ‘self-governance’, and a monitoring by a ‘jury of peers’.”

D3 · The NBA model, the Court’s refusal — and what happened next

What the “NBA model” was, as the reader preserves it. The News Broadcasters Association — the news channels’ industry body — had offered a two-tier self-regulation: channel-level compliance officers bound by the NBA Charter + Code of Ethics, and an industry-level complaints authority, with standards (accuracy, right of reply, privacy, minors, live-violence handling) and a published sanction ladder. The K.T. Thomas/Nariman committees found this “a welcome move and should be explored further” (¶32); the Court’s own response was ¶34’s restraint. The syllabus’s references to the “NBA Code of Ethics” and “NBSA Regulations” are the later, institutionalised versions of precisely this model.

E3 · 4.2 Private broadcasting — the entry regime (Telegraph/Wireless) and the content regime (Cable Act 1995 + Codes)

(i) Entry: why a channel is “licensed”, not “free” — the Telegraph Act architecture

Uploaded — MIB compilation “Broadcasting in India is governed by the Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 and by the Indian Wireless Telegraphy Act, 1933. The Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 gives the Government of India (GOI) exclusive rights for the establishment and working of telegraphy using electro-magnetic waves. Governance of broadcasting has its basis in the powers bestowed on the GOI by this Act.” The definition of “telegraph” is capacious — “any appliance … for transmission or reception of signs, signals, writing, images and sounds … by wire, visual or other electro-magnetic emissions, Radio waves or Hertzian waves”, radio waves being “electro-magnetic waves of frequencies lower than 3,000 giga-cycles per second propagated in space without artificial guide”, and “judicial decisions have also held that the term ‘telegraph’ includes the term telephone, television, radio, wireless, mobile and video equipment”.

One-line synthesis

For private TV, India runs two laws of very different age: an 1885 statute that says who may transmit (licence, privilege, seizure) and a 1994/95 set that says what may be transmitted (codes, prohibition orders) — with a 2001 TRAI layer for at what price. CAB is the constitutional glue: all three are legitimate only as public-property management, not as government authorship.

Must-carry continuesRule 31(2): the Central Government “may, from time to time, notify the details of the television channels transmitted by entities specified in Schedule 1, to be mandatorily re-transmitted” by distribution platforms — the s.8 (2011) lineage from CAB’s principle #7. A modernised enforcement ladderCh. 7: the Competent Authority/Adjudicating Officer — notice for breach (s.32) or suo motu/complaint-based inquiry (s.33); complaint must carry a ₹5,000 fee + government-ID proof + evidence or “shall not be considered”; hearing; inquiry to close within six months (reasons for delay recorded); orders with reasons, ex parte permitted; “voluntary undertakings” route (s.34(4)) with civil penalties under the Second/Third Schedules for non-compliance; appeal to a Designated Appeals Committee within 30 days with ₹10,000 fee; the Central Government “may specify the gradation of penalties”; performance bank guarantees encashed for rollout failures; Schedules amendable by notification.

Conclude with the Destruction verdict as the constitutional ruler both must satisfy: “allocative regulation is different from regulation per se” and codes must follow “the least invasive approach” (¶33(iii)).

E7 · Case-study — broadcasting regulation in action: Court on Its Own Motion v. State, 146 (2008) DLT 429 (the Uma Khurana sting)

Reader PDF pp. 122–128 · printed within Unit 2.2 (stings) — but this is the unit’s best live example of ss.5, 6, 19, 20 and the self-regulation idea
Facts (as printed — Mukundakam Sharma, J. for himself and S. Khanna, J.)

“In today’s age and world, the impact of media is far reaching. Electronic media as compared to print media has an added advantage because visuals have greater ramification and impact as it directly and immediately influences the mind of the viewer. With the growth of the number of News Channels and increasing popularity of ‘breaking news’, electronic media has come to play a major role in stirring public opinion and consciousness. It is this potency to reach the public that entails that all the channels understand and realize the heavy responsibility that is thrust on them and that there is no case for possible misuse.” Live India (TV news channel) telecast on 30.8.2007 a “sting operation” showing Ms. Uma Khurana, a Delhi government school teacher, “purportedly forcing a girl student into prostitution”; a crowd gathered, “some persons physically attacked Ms. Uma Khurana and even tore her clothes”; she was suspended, dismissed, and later arrested; a Hindustan Times report (7.9.2007) then showed the sting was “stage-managed”: the “student” was Rashmi Singh, “a budding journalist eager to make a name in the media world”, roped in by Mr. Prakash Singh in a plot with Mr. Virender Kumar over a “monetary dispute”. The Court took suo motu notice, issued notices to NCT of Delhi and the Delhi Police and appointed Mr. A.S. Chandiok and Mr. S.S. Gandhi as amicus.

The regulatory trail the Court records (¶¶9–12 — print these citations)
  • MIB show-cause, 11.9.2007: “it was alleged that the telecast of the sting operation by the Janmat TV-Live India is defamatory, deliberate, containing false and suggestive innuendos and half truths. It is also indicated therein that as per Section 5 of the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 read with provisions of Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994, no programme can be transmitted/re-transmitted on any cable service which contains anything obscene, defamatory, deliberate, false and suggestive innuendos and half truths. The said TV channel was further informed that it was bound to follow the Programme Code and Advertising Code … The said Channel was directed to show cause as to why action as per the provisions of Section 20 of the Cable Act should not be taken against them.”
  • Prohibition order, 19.9.2007 “by the Director (Broadcast Content) in exercise of the powers conferred by sub-section (2) of Section 20” — “transmission/re-transmission of JANMAT TV-Live India channel was prohibited with effect from 20th September, 2007 and utpo 20th October, 2007 through cable television networks and any other platform throughout the country”. (One month — 20 days from the show-cause notice: a speed no trial ever matched.)
  • The statutory restatement (¶¶10–11): the 1994 Rules were made under the 1994 Ordinance; s.5 conditions transmission on code-conformity; “the programme code is fairly exhaustive and stipulates that no programme which encourages and incites violence, maligns and slanders any individual and person, contain any false and half truths etc, should be carried and broadcast in a cable service”; s.20 (as the Court reads it) lets government “prohibit operation of any cable television network … by a notification in the official gazette”, “regulate or prohibit transmission or re-transmission of any programme which is not in conformity with the programme code”, and act on integrity/security/public order grounds.
  • The larger policy frame (¶12): “Regulation of electronic media has always invoked sharp and divergent views with emotive and logical pleas and counter arguments. We are informed that the Ministry has invited suggestions from the general public including the media on a proposed Broadcasting Bill and Code of Conduct. A decision in this regard has to be taken by the government. But it cannot be denied that electronic media should and must protect innocent people …” — the court-room birth-cert of the 2013-style consultation-paper cycle (E6).
Arguments / materials the Court weighed

The damage, stated by the amici and the record: reputation destroyed — “the sting operation has become a stinking experience for Ms. Uma Khurana as she has not only lost her reputation but also her job” (¶8); entrapment — the police found “no concrete evidence … to support the allegations” and the “victims” were charged with impersonation, conspiracy and fabricating evidence (¶7). The defence of the medium is recorded through Barkha Dutt’s column (¶16): “There is something unseemly and mildly sleazy about reporters playing Pied Pipers who lay out the cheese to seduce conmen into their rat traps … Entrapment somehow can’t manage to shake off the suggestion of fabrication.” And the counter — journalism’s duty: “No doubt the media is well within its rightful domain when it seeks to use tools of investigative journalism to bring us face to face with the ugly underbelly of the society” (¶19).

Decision and the twelve guidelines (¶¶15–22, verbatim-condensed)

The Court held truth may be shown “whether in the nature of sting operation or otherwise but … entrapment of any person should not be resorted to and should not be permitted”, borrowing Keith Jacobson v. United States, 503 US 540 (law-enforcement-style inducement condemned; predisposition must be proved), applying its ratio “to the instant context also i.e. to the media” (¶17); “a hidden camera cannot be allowed to depict something which is not true, correct and is not happening but has happened because of inducement by entrapping a person” (¶18); channels must prohibit entrapment-based programmes; “responsible and senior TV journalists/reporters and editors … should take steps for drawing up a self-regulatory code of conduct. The Press Council of India should also examine and can take initiative in this regard” (¶19). The twelve proposed guidelines (¶20) read as a finished code — memorise the architecture:

  1. Channel proposing a sting must obtain a certificate from the person who recorded/produced it that the operation is genuine to his knowledge.
  2. Concurrent written record of the various stages of the operation.
  3. Prior permission of an MIB-appointed committee — “headed by a retired High Court Judge to be appointed by the Government in consultation with the High Court & two members, one of which should be a person not below the rank of Additional Secretary and the second one being the Additional Commissioner of Police” — satisfying itself that telecast is in public interest; justified because those who mount stings “themselves commit the offences of impersonation, criminal trespass under false pretence and making a person commit an offence”.
  4. Transcript may be edited; the films/tapes themselves must not be; both edited and unedited versions before the committee.
  5. Scheduling “with an awareness of the likely audience/reader in mind”; “great care and sensitivity … to avoid shocking or offending the audience”.
  6. Every channel must ensure compliance with “the Certification Rules prescribed under the Cable Television Network (Regulation) Act 1995 and the Rules made there under”.
  7. The Chief Editor of the channel is responsible for self-regulation and for legal compliance of broadcast content.
  8. Reports on current events shall not: (a) present unverified/inaccurate facts as true — “a man is innocent till proven guilty by law” (the Unit 3 A6 duty in a different dress); (b) mislead about factual inaccuracy; (c) mix facts and fiction; (d) distort by over/under-emphasis that “trivialise[s] or sensationali[s]e[s] the content”; (e) publicise private affairs absent “an identifiable large public interest”; (f) create public panic or unnecessary alarm “likely to encourage or incite the public to crime”.
  9. “Broadcasters/Media shall observe general community standards of decency and civility in news content, taking particular care to protect the interest and sensitivities of children and general family viewing.”
  10. “News should be reported with due accuracy. Accuracy requires the verification (to the fullest extent possible) and presentation of all facts that are necessary to understand a particular event or issue.”
  11. Privacy: “greater degree of responsibility should be exercised by the channels while telecasting any such programmes, as may be breaching privacy of individuals”.
  12. Channels “must not use material relating to persons’ personal or private affairs or which invades an individual’s privacy unless there is identifiable larger public interest reason for the material to be broadcast or published.”

Disposition: the Court left Ms. Khurana’s civil remedy open (¶13 — “Damages in accordance with the law can be awarded by Courts in cases of defamation and loss of reputation”), noted the Directorate of Education’s reconsideration (¶14), and — ¶21, which completes the unit’s theme — “The Ministry of Information and Broadcasting is already examining whether a statute and/or a code of conduct should be enacted. The above proposed guidelines should be considered by the concerned Ministry and if they find favor, they may be incorporated in the enactment/guidelines, with modifications as deemed fit and proper.” Notices discharged; petition disposed of (¶22).

Principles (for this unit)

(1) The Programme Code is immediately enforceable against live news — one month off-air, 20 days from show-cause, while no prosecution or even charge-sheet stage had been reached; (2) self-regulation is directed at the profession and the PCI in the same breath — the “mixed model” the unit describes; (3) guideline 3 (prior permission) was proposed but never enacted — exactly the “prior content control [that] goes to the root of censorship” the Nariman Committee rejected a year later (¶31(c)); cite the pair — a court drafting a prior-restraint device in 2007, the Supreme Court refusing it in 2009 — as the unit’s dialectic in one line; (4) the sting-specific rules (4, 6, 8) are the template the NBSA code later generalised (clause 9 — see the Units 2.1/2.2 file); (5) the MIB act on a Code complaint because s.5 makes the code statutory — the difference between “advisory” and “Act” that every broadcasting answer must keep straight.

F1 · Case 22 — X v. Union of India, 2021 SCC OnLine Del 1788 (W.P.(CRL) 1082/2020) · the dossier

Case 22 · Delhi High Court · Anup Jairam Bhambhani, J. · Reader PDF pp. 207–219 (→ syllabus p. 196)
Facts (¶¶1–5, as printed)
“The internet never sleeps; and the internet never forgets” — “The true enormity of this fact has dawned over the course of hearings … when it transpired that despite orders of this court, even the respondents who were willing to comply with directions issued to remove offending content from the world-wide-web, e-Expressed their inability to fully and effectively remove it in compliance with court directions; while errant parties merrily continued to re-post and re-direct such content from one website to another and from one online platform to another, thereby cocking-a-snook at directions issued against them in pending legal proceedings.” — ¶1 (Case Book PDF p. 207)
  • The petitioner — styled only “X” — complained that photographs and images she had posted on her private social-media accounts on Facebook and Instagram, with “the requisite ‘privacy settings’ activated”, had been taken “without her knowledge or consent” and unlawfully posted on a pornographic website, www.Xhamster.com, by an unknown entity called ‘Desi Collector’, “whereby the petitioner’s photographs and images have become offensive by association”. The court deliberately declined to record identifying details “for reasons of privacy and confidentiality” (¶2).
  • Captions were attached to her photographs; the contention was that the actors committed “publishing and transmitting material that appeals to the prurient interest[s] and … tending to deprave and corrupt” — s.67 IT Act — “and other penal provisions of the IT Act and the Indian Penal Code 1860” (¶2). Twin-cite now: the IPC face (292 → BNS 294; 509 → BNS 79 for words/gestures intruding privacy; morphing cases also invoked 66C IT Act (identity theft) in the FIR). Nothing in the IT Act was amended by the BNS — the obscene-electronic-material offences (ss.67, 67A, 67B) stand as they are (Shreya-era law; see G1).
  • Before the writ she had complained on the National Cyber-Crime Reporting Portal and to the jurisdictional police “but to no avail”; in a week the images had received “some 15000 views” (¶3). Respondents 5–6 (the website; the unknown uploader) could not be served — no addresses available — and none was needed “considering the nature of the matter” (¶4).
  • Delhi Police’s CyPAD (Cyber Prevention Awareness and Detection Unit) told the court it was “ready and willing to comply … but by reason of technological limitations and impediments, it could not assure the court that it would be able to entirely efface the offending content from the world-wide-web”; meanwhile “errant respondents and other mischief-makers had re-directed, re-posted and re-published the offending content onto other websites and online platforms, thereby rendering the orders of the court ineffective” (¶5).
The judicial response — questions framed (¶¶6–11)

The court “perceived that the issue of making effective and implementable orders in relation to a grievance arising from offending content placed on the world-wide-web, needed to be closely examined; and a solution to the problem needed to be crafted” (¶6); appointed Dr Pavan Duggal, Advocate, cyber-law specialist, as amicus curiae (¶7); pressed the point that interim-stage effectiveness is everything — “if the court is not in a position to pass effective and implementable orders … subsequent adjudication … could well be rendered infructuous” (¶8) — and declared it “cannot permit itself to resign to the cat-and-mouse game of errant parties evading court orders … in an act of defiance and contumacy” (¶9). By order dated 07.08.2020 two queries were framed (¶¶10–11):

  1. Remedial design: “where a party seeks relief … that certain offending or illegal content be removed from the world-wide-web, what directions are required to be passed by a court to make its order implementable and effective; and to which parties are such directions required to be issued”;
  2. Enforcement: “what steps are required to be taken by law enforcement agencies to implement such directions … to ensure that despite court orders/directions offending content does not ‘resurface’ or remain available … and such parties do not succeed in brazenly evading compliance … with impunity.”
Arguments — five positions, each in the book’s own order (¶¶12–89)

Amicus (Dr Pavan Duggal) — ¶¶12–13: map the field from statute up: IT Act ss.1(2) (extraterritorial application to offences committed outside India by any person), 2(1)(o) data, 2(1)(v) information, 2(1)(w) intermediary (“any person who on behalf of another person receives, stores or transmits that record … includes telecom service providers, network service providers, internet service providers, web-hosting service providers, search engines, online payment sites, online-auction sites, online-market places and cyber cafes”), 67/67A/67B (obscene / sexually explicit / child), 67C (preservation by intermediaries), s.75, s.79 (exemption), s.81 (overriding effect), s.85 (offences by companies); the 2009 Blocking Rules r.10 (“In case of an order from a competent court in India for blocking of any information … the Designated Officer shall, immediately on receipt of certified copy of the court order, submit it to the Secretary, Department of Information Technology and initiate action as directed by the court”); 2011 Rules r.3 (due diligence; 36-hour removal; 90-day preservation; termination rights; assistance to lawful requests); then the 2021 Rules superseding the 2011 Rules (definitions of grievance/SSI/social media intermediary/user; r.3 due diligence; r.4 additional SSI duties) — with the court’s caveat that “proceedings have since been stayed by the Hon’ble Supreme Court” on the 2021 Rules’ challenge and “no view is being expressed … as to the constitutional vires” (a passage to quote when asked about the Rules’ status at the time).

Delhi Police — ¶42: to defeat resurfacing, issue directions to intermediaries under s.79(3)(b) for removal of content “as identified through unique identifiers such as the URL, Account ID, Handle name, Internet Protocol Address, hash value, pixel matching, structural similarity index etc.”; and further directions under s.79(3)(a) r/w s.107 CrPC and r.3(2) of the 2011 Rules “to prevent further posting … of ‘identified unlawful content’”; notices u/s 91 CrPC (→ BNSS s.94) are already “commonplace”. (Read: the police wanted a standing filtered-list mechanism.)

Google LLC — Mr Sajan Poovayya, Sr. Adv. — ¶¶46–55, 62: no opposition to removing the specific content; but — search engines are not publishers: they “merely ‘index’ existing information … owing to the automated manner of functioning”; “the legal framework mandates that the internet be kept free from editorial intervention”; the role is “reactive”, limited to disabling specific URLs when reported or ordered, not “proactive”; search engines “cannot be tasked with adjudicating the legitimacy of content”; prior restraint, blanket ban or censorship of content cannot be ordered — contrary to Art. 19(1)(a) and with “a chilling effect on fundamental rights”; the publisher/search distinction (webmaster controls content; Google hosts none); the crawler→index→“library catalogue” analogy (¶49); image-search is harder — algorithms keyed on file names etc. (¶50–51); de-indexing tools belong to the webmaster (developers.google.com “control what you share”), not to Google (¶53); Google’s Grievance Officer acts on complaints “on priority basis” (¶55); “The remedy here is not to target intermediaries but to ensure that infringing Material is removed in an orderly and reasonable manner. A further balancing act is required which is that of freedom of speech and privatized censorship” (¶62); Google’s own proposal: order the host website first; search removals “organically” follow; court/agency shares specific URLs for de-listing/cache-removal (¶62 second limb).

MeitY — ¶¶65–67 (PowerPoint): directions may go (a) to the intermediary platforms, or (b) to the “appropriate government” or its agency, or (c) granting the petitioner liberty “to directly approach or send requests through a specific email” to platforms/government/police; complaint routes: Grievance Officer or the cybercrime.gov.in portal “now widened … to include all cyber crimes”; relevant penal law s.66E (violation of privacy) and Rules 3(2)(b), 3(2)(e); law-enforcement primacy — courts directing takedown “is not what the Legislature intended”.

Facebook/Instagram — Mr Parag Tripathi, Sr. Adv. — ¶¶72–73: robust privacy policy; “privacy settings … Profile Picture Guard” and “Audience selector”; the petitioner pleaded no relief against Facebook; the platform “actively collaborates with other stakeholders for removal of unlawful online content; and complies with the mandate as contained inter alia in Shreya Singhal”; ready to cooperate with any court order.

Foreign material the court collected (¶¶30–39): X. v. Twitter Inc. (Supreme Court of New South Wales) — where an intermediary “comes into possession of confidential information and is put on notice of the character of the information and the circumstances in which it was unlawfully obtained, it becomes subject to an equitable obligation of confidence. It is liable to be restrained from publishing the information”; worldwide restraint upheld, “a requirement for the ‘application of some degree of filtering, or checking’”; “The plaintiff should not be left without a remedy”. YouTube LLC v. Geeta Shroff (Del HC) — content uploaded from India must be removed worldwide “following the path by which it was uploaded”; ABC v. DEF (Del HC) — platforms “directed to remove any other material which the plaintiff may report as objectionable qua her”. And the Canadian apex court in Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions Inc. — global de-indexing “does not require the search engine to ‘take any steps around the world, it requires it to take steps only where its Search engine is controlled’” (¶89 quoting it) — with the court’s own restatement at ¶108–109 (geo-blocking alone “would … result in partial disabling and partial removal”; Indian courts’ orders “are meant to be implemented fully and effectively”; upload-from-India ⇒ world take-down, upload-from-abroad ⇒ India geo-block).

F2 · X v. Union of India — reasoning, the nine directions, and the principles

Reasoning (¶¶63, 87–94 — the reader’s selected paragraphs)
  • Prior-restraint objection answered: “Google LLC’s objection to orders of prior restraint or blanket ban of content is wholly unnecessary and misplaced, inasmuch as…” — the order operates on specific identified unlawful content already found by a court (¶63); compare the Nariman Committee line (¶31(c)) — post-adjudication enforcement is not prior content control.
  • Intermediaries cannot plead impossibility once notified: “an intermediary cannot be heard to say that it is unable to remove or disable access to offending content despite such actual knowledge as contemplated in law” (¶87) — Shreya Singhal’s s.79(3)(b) reading applied to facts; but the court was candid about the practical difficulty: content “appearing in various disguises and corrupted avatars”, and the “too onerous and impractical” burden of perpetual lookout (¶88). No intermediary refused compliance — “none … has at all taken a stand … that they are not ready or willing to remove” (¶88).
  • Global effect for local orders: “for an order directing the removal or access disablement of offending content to be effective even within India, a search engine must block the search results throughout the world since no purpose would be served … if it has no realistic prospect of preventing irreparable harm” (¶89, invoking Equustek).
  • The sanction that makes it real: the court “must refer to the provisions of section 79(3)(a) and (b) read with section 85 of the IT Act and Rule 7 of the 2021 Rules, whereby an intermediary would forfeit the exemption … if it were to fail to observe its obligations” (¶89(ix)); non-compliance “would make the non-compliant party liable to forfeit the exemption, if any, available to it generally under section 79(1) … and shall make such entity and its officers liable for action as mandated by section 85” (¶93) — s.85 = company + officers in charge, with the standard due-diligence defence.
  • The finding on the merits: the taking from private accounts + posting + re-posting “amounts prima facie to an offence under section 67 of the IT Act in addition to other offences under the IPC” (¶92).
The directions — the “template” (¶92(i)–(viii)) — worth reproducing in an answer as the judgment’s product
  1. Petitioner to furnish to the Investigating Officer, in writing, all available information about the offending content — Image URL and Web URL — within 24 hours.
  2. Delhi Police/CyPAD to remove/disable access to the content “from all websites and online platforms, forthwith and in any event within 24 hours” of the petitioner’s information. (Already done at Xhamster, per the record.)
  3. Search engines — Google Search, Yahoo Search, Microsoft Bing and DuckDuckGo — to globally de-index and de-reference the offending content by Web URL and Image URL “including de-indexing and de-referencing all concerned web-pages, sub-pages or sub-directories”, within 24 hours.
  4. Same search engines “to endeavour to use automated tools, to proactively identify and globally disable access to any content which is exactly identical to the offending content, that may appear on any other websites/online platforms”.
  5. IO to pass the URLs + judgment to all directed entities within 24 hours; Delhi Police to obtain from the website and search engines “all information and associated records … such as the URL, account ID, handle name, Internet Protocol Address, hash value” for investigation of FIR No. 286/2020 dated 18.07.2020 (u/s 354A IPC → BNS 75; s.66C IT Act; P.S. Dwarka South) within 72 hours.
  6. Continuing liberty to the petitioner to write to the IO for removal of “the same or similar offending content appearing on any other website/online platform or search engine(s), whether in the same or in different context”, with a direction to the IO to notify those entities to comply “immediately and in any event within 72 hours”.
  7. Even after disposal, any website, platform, search engine or law-enforcement agency in doubt “shall be at liberty to approach this court to seek clarification”.
  8. (General guidance preceding the directions, ¶89: courts may direct complainants to use the NCRP portal; and must invoke the forfeiture consequences — the “template of directions”.)
Principles evolved (Case 22)
  1. Courts may craft “implementable and effective” removal orders under Art. 226 read with the IT Act/2021 Rules — including global de-indexing of identified unlawful content, and proactive automated-tool disablement of “exactly identical” resurfacing content — without that constituting prior restraint or a blanket ban (¶¶63, 89, 92).
  2. Intermediary “actual knowledge” = court order or notified government agency (Shreya ¶119(c)) — once received, “cannot be heard to say” technical impossibility; immunity is the consideration for compliance (s.79; ¶87; ¶93).
  3. The jurisdiction map for online content is upload-site + dissemination-site: uploaded in India ⇒ worldwide removal; uploaded abroad ⇒ geo-blocking for India (¶109, after Equustek/Shroff).
  4. Privacy settings do not excuse platform diligence — but the primary wrong lies with uploader and host; the intermediary route is enforcement, not adjudication (“the remedy here is not to target intermediaries”, ¶62; balancing “freedom of speech and privatized censorship” — same, ¶62).
  5. By-standards for victim-protection drafting: anonymisation of the complainant in the judgment itself (¶2); identification through unique identifiers (URL, handle, IP, hash value, pixel-matching); NCRP portal first-instance complaint.
  6. Status of the 2021 Rules was open (SC stay of challenges noted, no merits view) — the directions therefore rested on statutory sections, the 2009/2011 Rules and the court’s own powers, with the 2021 Rules cited as context (the extract of the 2021 Rules is printed at Case Book PDF pp. 210–213 immediately after the 2009/2011 Rules).

G2 · Case 5/21(4.3) — Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, (2015) 5 SCC 1 · the dossier (Unit-4 lens)

Reader PDF pp. 30–50 · R.F. Nariman, J. (for himself and J. Chelameswar, J.) · complete case dossier also in the Units 2.1/2.2 file — this restatement focuses on the 66A→69A→79 architecture the regulation unit needs
Facts / genesis (¶¶1–5, as printed)

“This batch of writ petitions filed under Article 32 … raises very important and far-reaching questions … The immediate cause for concern … is Section 66A of the Information Technology Act of 2000. This Section was not in the Act as originally enacted, but came into force by virtue of an Amendment Act of 2009 with effect from 27.10.2009.” A related challenge to s.69A, introduced by the same amendment (¶2). The Statement of Objects and Reasons justified the amendment by “a rapid increase in the use of computer and internet [which] has given rise to new forms of crimes like publishing sexually explicit materials in electronic form, video voyeurism and breach of confidentiality … e-commerce frauds like personation commonly known as Phishing, identity theft and offensive messages through communication services” (¶3) — and the petitioners fired back the amendment’s own logic against it: the claim that it addresses new crime “is incorrect, and … Sections 66B to 67C and various Sections of the Indian Penal Code … are good enough to deal with all these crimes” (¶4). The 2012 trigger (the 11-page shutdown after Bal Thackeray’s death; the Arundhati Bhattacharya arrest; the Maharashtra arrests over Facebook posts) is External; reported — the reader skips straight to arguments; the PRS 66A background (uploaded) records the same misuse wave: “incidents related to comments, sharing of information, or thoughts expressed by an individual to a wider audience on the internet have attracted criminal penalties … In November 2012, there were various reports of alleged misuse of the law, and the penalties imposed were said to be disproportionate to the offence.”

The section struck down (s.66A) — the reader prints “(refer to the Section)”; the three limbs from the PRS background note: punishable with imprisonment up to three years and fine whoever, by means of a computer or communication device, sends information that is (i) grossly offensive or having the character of menacing; (ii) false and sent “for the purpose of causing annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill will”; (iii) sent to deceive or mislead the recipient about the origin of such messages. Elements for your skeleton — no requirement of publication to the world, no defences, no intelligible standard, cognizable (per the PRS note: the court’s issues “relate to the powers of the police to decide what is abusive … instead of the examination of the offence by the judiciary”).

Issues
  1. Does s.66A infringe Art. 19(1)(a), and is it saved by any of the eight grounds of Art. 19(2)?
  2. Is s.66A void for vagueness (and is “overbreadth” a distinct Indian ground)?
  3. Is the chilling effect recognised — including the right of the audience (the “viewers’ right”) to shades of grey?
  4. Can the Court save the section by reading in 19(2) grounds or by severance?
  5. Are s.69A and the 2009 Blocking Rules valid (safeguards; “grounds same as 19(2)”)
  6. What exactly is the scope of s.79 immunity and the 2011 Rules r.3(4) — what is “actual knowledge”?
  7. Was the classification of internet-speech offences (vs. identical offline offences) valid under Art. 14?
Arguments — both sides (with the reader’s own citations)
Petitioners (bar-council led; several counsel)Union (learned Additional Solicitor General)
· 66A’s operative words — “annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred or ill-will” — are “all outside the purview of Article 19(2)” (¶5, verbatim list).
· Vagueness: unlike s.66, “none of the aforesaid terms are even attempted to be defined and cannot be defined”, so “innocent persons are roped in” and authorities may be “as arbitrary and whimsical as they like” (¶5).

Decision (¶119, as printed at PDF p. 50)
(a) Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 is struck down in its entirety being violative of Article 19(1)(a) and not saved under Article 19(2). (b) Section 69A and the Information Technology (Procedure & Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information by Public) Rules 2009 are constitutionally valid. (c) Section 79 is valid subject to Section 79(3)(b) being read down to mean that an intermediary upon receiving actual knowledge from a court order or on being notified by the appropriate government or its agency that unlawful acts relatable to Article 19(2) are going to be committed then fails to expeditiously remove or disable access to such material. Similarly, the Information Technology “Intermediary Guidelines” Rules, 2011 are valid subject to Rule 3 sub-rule (4) being read down in the same manner as indicated in the judgment. — Summary of holdings, printed in full in the Case Book; the excerpt closes with these three (PDF p. 50). The judgment also disposed of the Art. 14 plea (¶29/¶31: the 19(1)(a) test is enough — “an Article 14 challenge has been repelled”), and treated “proximate causal connection” for public order (¶75–77 area): “the anticipated danger should not be remote, conjectural or far-fetched … [the expression must be] intrinsically dangerous … the equivalent of a ‘spark in a powder keg’” (Rangarajan, quoted in the reader’s Goswami survey too).
Principles evolved
  1. A criminal provision is judged “on its own merits without any reference to how well it may be administered” — assurances, SOPs and advisories cannot save a facially over-broad speech offence (¶92). (The DoIT 9.1.2013 advisory — prior DCP/IG approval before registration under 66A, and the May-2013 Supreme Court order, uploaded in the PRS note — is the exhibit of how executive self-restraint fails to cure the statute.)
  2. “Annoyance” has no place in 19(2). Offence-creating language untethered to the enumerated grounds, covering advocacy and mere discussion alike, is over-broad; reading grounds into a silent statute is impermissible where the legislature used them elsewhere (69A contrast) (¶¶5, 48–49, 83–90).
  3. Blocking is the constitutional answer, and it is State-side, not intermediary-side: 69A survives because (i) it incorporates 19(2) grounds, (ii) it is pre-recorded-reasons, review-committee-checked executive action — and the intermediary’s duty crystallises only at “actual knowledge” = court order/government notification (¶119(b)–(c)).
  4. Voluntary takedown is safe: intermediaries may moderate without losing immunity (the 2011/2021 provisos; 2021 Rules r.3(1)(d) final proviso as printed in X digest: removal “under clause (b) on a voluntary basis … shall not amount to a violation of the conditions of clauses (a) or (b) of sub-section (2) of section 79”).
  5. Procedural parity matters: the offline safeguards (CrPC 95/96/195 → BNSS 114/115/217) frame the standard against which online criminal law is measured (¶¶99–101).
  6. Medium-neutrality with teeth: “the content of the right under Article 19(1)(a) remains the same whatever the means of communication including internet communication” (¶86) — so the only valid online-specific criminal law must be “narrow[ly] dra[wn] … such as Section 69A for instance” (¶29/¶48 logic).

H1 · PYQ Q4 (Dec-2024, 20 marks) — “Composition, Powers and Functions of Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) under the Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) Act, 1990” · full model answer

Question 4 [20] — model answer (write in four movements: why → who → what → whose leash) · a briefer version also lives in the Units 2.1/2.2 file — this is the full statute-based answer

Introduction — 1990 as the statute that ended the Emergency’s lesson. Doordarshan and All India Radio were, till 1990, departments of the Ministry of Information & Broadcasting; their use in 1975–77 produced the B.G. Verghese Committee (1977–78), whose blueprint for “a free and independent public corporation” became the Prasar Bharati Bill of May 1979, lapsed with the Janata government, and was revived by the National Front to become the Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) Act, 1990 (41 of 1990; assent 12 September 1990) — though only “brought to life” on the appointed day, 15 September 1997 (S.O. 509(E), 22.7.1997 — the seven-year gap is a fact worth stating and blaming in one clause). The statute performs exactly the separation Unit 4 theorises: the State keeps entry-power (the Act’s Explanation to s.12 preserves the Telegraph Act’s exclusive privilege), but hands content-stewardship to a body corporate. Prasar Bharati is thus India’s answer to the CAB directive that broadcasting be “under the control of the public as distinct from Government”.

I. Composition. (1) The Corporation is established by notification (s.3(1)); body corporate with perpetual succession, common seal, capacity to acquire property and contract, suing and being sued (s.3(2)); headquarters at New Delhi, kendras/stations anywhere, offices abroad only with Central Government approval (s.3(3)). (2) The Prasar Bharati Board holds “the general superintendence, direction and management of the affairs of the Corporation” (s.3(4)) and consists of fifteen members (s.3(5)): the Chairman; Executive Member (the Chief Executive — s.5); Member (Finance); Member (Personnel); six part-time members; the Director-General (Akashvani) and Director-General (Doordarshan) ex officio; one member nominated by the Union Ministry of I&B; and two representatives of the employees elected by the engineering staff and by the other employees respectively — the statute’s own answer to bureaucratic capture. Committees are Board-majority, outsiders without vote (s.3(6)–(7)); vacancies do not invalidate acts (s.3(8)). (3) Appointment of the Chairman and members (other than ex officio/nominated/elected) is by the President of India “on the recommendation of a committee consisting of” the Chairman of the Council of States, the Chairman of the Press Council of India, and one nominee of the President, and “the recommendations … shall be binding for the purposes of appointment” (s.4(1), (4)) — an insulation device (the PCI chairman inside the selection committee) that deserves comment in a critical paragraph. (4) Qualifications: Chairman and part-time members — “persons of eminence in public life”; Executive Member — special knowledge or practical experience of administration, management, broadcasting, education, literature, culture, arts, music, dramatics or journalism; Finance/Personnel accordingly (s.4(3)). (5) Tenure and removal: three years or age 70 (the Chairman, after the 2008 amendment, is a part-time office-holder); removal only for proved misbehaviour or incapacity “in an inquiry held in accordance with such procedure as the Supreme Court may by rules provide”, the President acting on that report (s.7(1)) — constitutional-office security; deemed-public-servant status (s.27) and good-faith immunity (s.28) complete the service architecture; staff, Recruitment Boards and terms of service under ss.9–11 (as amended in 2012 to regulate IIS/CS deputation posts).

II. Powers and functions. The Act states a duty, a set of objectives, an enabling list, and rider clauses — every one of them is “component” material: (1) Duty — “the primary duty of the Corporation to organise and conduct public broadcasting services to inform, educate and entertain the public and to ensure a balanced development of broadcasting on radio and television” (s.12(1)); Explanation: additional to, not in derogation of, the Telegraph Act 1885. (2) Sixteen statutory objectives guiding the discharge of functions (s.12(2)(a)–(p)), quoted in compressed form: unity/integrity/constitutional values; the citizen’s right to be informed “freely, truthfully and objectively … a fair and balanced flow of information including contrasting views without advocating any opinion or ideology of its own”; education, literacy, agriculture, rural development, environment, health, science; the cultures and languages of the regions; sports; youth; women’s status, problems and upliftment; social justice and the fight against untouchability; the working classes; rural, border, backward and remote areas; minorities and tribal communities; children, the blind, the aged, the handicapped and other vulnerable sections; national integration through the languages of India and the distribution of regional services; coverage, spectrum-utilisation and reception quality; R&D; and expansion of transmission channels. (3) Enabling powers (s.12(3)): broadcasting as a public service; establishing a system for the gathering of news for radio and television; negotiating and allocating rights for sports, films, serials and events of public interest; maintaining programme libraries; audience research; other services by regulations. (4) Riders: managing external services for the Central Government on reimbursement (s.12(4)); the Central Government’s power “to determine the maximum limit of broadcast time in respect of the advertisement” to protect public-service content (s.12(5)); no civil liability merely for failure to meet s.12(2)–(3) objectives (s.12(6)) — objectives are justiciable-policy standards, not private-law duties; fee/charge-setting within government-fixed limits (s.12(7)). Executive powers run through the delegated Chief-Executive office (s.5), with the Board controlling (s.3(4)).

III. Accountability — three levers, in a deliberate order. (1) To the audience: the Broadcasting Council — a President (whole-time) + ten members of eminence appointed by the President of India + four MPs, with Regional Councils (s.14); it receives complaints of programmes “not in accordance with the objectives” and of any person (outside the Corporation’s staff) “treated unjustly or unfairly … including unwarranted invasion of privacy, misrepresentation, distortion or lack of objectivity” (s.15(1)); it may “advise the Executive Member”, escalation to the Board, reasons to be recorded if declined — and, uniquely, s.15(7): where it deems it appropriate, it “may, for reasons to be recorded in writing, require the Corporation to broadcast its recommendations … in such manner as the Council may deem fit” — an on-air right of reply, India’s 1990 statutory prototype of the BCCC/NBSA orders the private sector adopted two decades later. (2) To Parliament: the s.13 Parliamentary Committee of 22 MPs (15 Lok Sabha + 7 Rajya Sabha, proportional single transferable vote) overseeing compliance “in particular [with] the objectives set out in section 12”, reporting to Parliament; the C&AG audits the accounts, the audit report and annual reports (of the Corporation and of the Broadcasting Council’s recommendations and action taken) being laid before each House (ss.21, 31). (3) To the Government — only at the margins: directions “in the interests of the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India or the security of the State or preservation of public order” requiring the Corporation not to broadcast, to broadcast on a matter of public importance, with the option to announce the fact of the direction, and copies laid before Parliament (s.23); information-powers (s.24); and, on persistent default, a report to Parliament and, “on the recommendation of the Parliament”, a President’s notification superseding the Board for up to six months after a show-cause, powers exercised meanwhile by persons the President directs (s.25). Note the design: the government’s only lawful content-instruments are a transparent direction and a double-locked dissolution.

IV. Assessment — the Act of 1990 between CAB (1995) and Pitroda (2013/14). The Act supplies charter + insulated appointments + audience council + parliamentary oversight, but not the two conditions of genuine autonomy: money (s.17 pays by grant after appropriation; the non-lapsable fund transferred under s.16 kept commercial revenue tied to government approval; s.20(2) budget “as may be agreed to” by the Central Government) and management (the 2008 part-time Chairman amendment; MIB’s nominated Board member; s.12(5)/(7) advertising caps). The Pitroda Expert Committee (28.1.2013 → 24.1.2014) diagnosed exactly this and prescribed: constitute the dormant s.13 Committee (1.1.1); professionally reconstitute the Board (1.1.2); complete transfer of assets and HR (1.1.3); an internal Regulatory Body for content accountability (1.1.4); a separate State channel for government messaging (1.2); “amend the Prasar Bharati Act 1990 where necessary so as to impart genuine and effective autonomy” (1.3); a funding-mechanism study (2.1); power to frame its own service rules (3.1); 50% of expenditure to content (4.1); Prasar Bharati Connect as a third arm (7.2); a world-class “new-media first” platform (8.1). None was legislated as of the reader’s horizon; the 2023 Broadcasting Services (Regulation) Bill cycle proposes an independent BAI for the private sector while leaving Prasar Bharati’s 1990 charter intact External; reported. Conclude: Prasar Bharati is CAB’s unfinished sentence — a public broadcaster with statutory objectives and audience complaints machinery, still funded and staffed like a ministry; the composition answers to the public, but the purse has not yet.

H2 · PYQ Q7 (Dec-2024, 20 marks) — the reposted-photographs problem · full model answer (digital-regulation lens)

Question 7 [20] — model answer (the question is X v. Union of India restated as a problem — answer with the source case + the statute ladder) · the 2.1/2.2 file’s companion answer runs the privacy/doctrine layer; this one is the regulation-and-remedy map

Frame: X posted photographs on her private Facebook/Instagram accounts with privacy settings activated; unknown persons lifted them, captioned them sexually, and posted them on pornographic websites; copies resurface on other platforms despite takedown orders. This is precisely the fact pattern adjudicated in X v. Union of India, 2021 SCC OnLine Del 1788 (Delhi HC; Bhambhani, J.) — the reader prints it at PDF pp. 207–219. The answer proceeds in five layers: crime → platform → State → court → civil, and ends with the court’s nine-direction template as the practical remedy-map.

1. Criminal characterisation. (a) The unauthorised lifting/captioning and posting on a porn site: s.67 IT Act (publishing/transmitting obscene material in electronic form — the “offensive by association” theory the court accepted at ¶¶2, 92), s.67A (sexually explicit), s.66E (violation of privacy — capturing/publishing images of a woman’s private area; MeitY’s own pointer, ¶66), s.66C/66D (identity theft/impersonation — the FIR in X carried 66C; and 354A IPC → BNS s.75 where the captions amount to sexual harassment/demand); (b) defamation for the captioned falsehoods (499/500 IPC → BNS 356) and insult to modesty (509 → BNS 79); (c) if the complainant were a minor, POCSO 2012 (child-pornography offences) and IT s.67B. The IT Act is untouched by the BNS transition — the twin citations exist only for the parallel IPC-style counts. Procedure: FIR under the general clauses (zero-FIR; Cyber-Cell/CyPAD), the NCRP portal www.cybercrime.gov.in (MeitY ¶67: “any aggrieved person can report any unlawful content through this portal”), preservation requests to intermediaries (r.3(1)(j)/(g): 72-hour assistance, 180-day preservation), and record requisitions u/s 91 CrPC → BNSS s.94 (“or other device”).

2. Platform-level remedies (self + delegated regulation). Complaint to the Grievance Officer — acknowledgement within 24 hours, disposal within 15 days (2021 Rules r.3(2)(a)); but for intimate-image content the rule is surgical: for complaints “prima facie in the nature of material which exposes the private area … full or partial nudity … sexual act or conduct … or impersonation in an electronic form, including artificially morphed images”, the intermediary must “take all reasonable and practicable measures to remove or disable access” within twenty-four hours of the complaint — r.3(2)(b) (printed in the X digest, PDF p. 212); users’ toolset too (Facebook/Instagram record: privacy settings, Profile Picture Guard, Audience Selector — ¶72; the platform’s “complaints on this web form are acted upon on priority basis” — Google, ¶55). Non-compliance forfeits the s.79 shield; officers of companies become liable u/s 85 (¶¶89(ix), 93).

3. State-side levers. A blocking complaint under s.69A + the 2009 Rules (ground: decency/morality — the section’s own tether to 19(2) is what saved it in Shreya ¶119(b)); escalation through the Cyber Cell to the MeitY nodal contact/Resident Grievance Officer (r.4(1) — Chief Compliance Officer, 24×7 nodal person: the SSMI duty set that makes platforms answerable in hours); police use of unique identifiers (URL, account ID, handle, IP, hash value — the Delhi Police submission, ¶42; the court’s direction (vi) to collect exactly these from Xhamster and the search engines).

4. Constitutional-litigation layer — where the case becomes the remedy. A writ under Art. 226 seeking implementable and effective directions; the court will (X ¶92 template): direct the complainant to furnish Web/Image URLs to the IO within 24 h; direct the police to remove/disable access on all websites and platforms within 24 h; direct search engines (Google, Yahoo, Bing, DuckDuckGo) to globally de-index and de-reference the content and every page/sub-page carrying it within 24 h; direct engines to use automated tools to proactively disable “exactly identical” resurfacing content; direct IO to notify all entities of URLs + the judgment; direct police to obtain account-ID/handle/IP/hash records within 72 h; grant the complainant a standing liberty to write to the IO for removal of the “same or similar offending content … whether in the same or in different context”, the IO to notify platforms within 72 h; retain a clarification route; and warn that non-compliance forfeits s.79(1) immunity with s.85 exposure (¶93). Doctrine to cite for global reach: X v. Twitter Inc. (equitable obligation of confidence; worldwide orders; “The plaintiff should not be left without a remedy”), YouTube v. Geeta Shroff (uploaded from India → removal everywhere), ABC v. DEF (platforms to remove any further reported material), Google v. Equustek (global de-indexing = “steps only where its Search engine is controlled”); the court’s own jurisdiction formula at ¶109 (upload abroad → geo-block India); the “cat-and-mouse” rejection at ¶9. Answer the implicit question — courts do not thereby license private censorship: the order operates only on content adjudicated unlawful, prior restraint objections are “wholly unnecessary and misplaced” (¶63), and the balance named by Google (“freedom of speech and privatized censorship”, ¶62) is respected by keeping removals URL-specific and reasons-recorded.

5. Civil and constitutional-privacy layer. Suit for damages/injunction against uploader, host and re-publishers (defamation; breach of confidence — the X v. Twitter basis; breach of the platform’s user-agreement by the uploader; and vicarious exposure of a site that ignores court-notice); Puttaswamy privacy as the ground of the whole package, with the right to be forgotten as an evolving facet (Units 2.1/2.2 file); after the DPDP Act 2023 became operative (rules notified 2025 — External; reported), a data-principal grievance for erasure before the Data Protection Board against the processing platforms, without prejudice to these remedies; reporting to the NCW for gender-violence documentation; and — if a media house re-publishes — NBSA/PCI complaints for the “by association” harm.

Sequence to advise (practical, mark-fetching): evidence first (hash/screen-record URLs, NCRP receipt), then r.3(2)(b) platform complaint within hours, FIR, s.69A/MeitY escalation, writ seeking the X-template directions, and the damages action preserved — “the internet never sleeps”, so every clock runs in hours.

Everything above is sourced: X excerpt (PDF pp. 207–219) for facts, arguments, directions; 2021/2011 Rules printed in it; Shreya for 79/69A; foreign cases printed in the excerpt; BNS mappings per the correspondence tables; DPDP-status line flagged. H3 · PYQ Q8(B) (Dec-2024, 10 marks) — fake news · model answer · and Q8(A)/(D) routing

Question 8(B) [10] — “Dangers of fake news and the legal mechanisms to deal with it” · model answer (Unit-4 variant; the Unit-3 file answers the contempt half of the same question)

Definition and the constitutional starting point. “Fake news” — information patently false as fact, disseminated to deceive or harass — is not, in Indian law, an offence by that name, and that is deliberate: Shreya Singhal killed s.66A IT Act precisely because it criminalised falsity/annoyance untethered from Art. 19(2) (¶119(a)); any “fake news offence” must therefore live inside the enumerated heads or die as over-broad. The audience-side harm is real — the Act is measured by reach: “15000 views within a week” (X ¶3) is the evidentiary signature of why speed-based machinery (not new crimes) is the answer.

Dangers (4–5 lines, mapped to the unit’s cases): (i) mob violence — the Uma Khurana telecast → crowd, assault, dismissal (E7); (ii) panic/riot incitement — Destruction’s backdrop (bandh copycat violence) and the Code’s item against “encourag[ing] or incit[ing] violence”; (iii) elections — paid/faked content, the “marketplace of ideas” distortion CAB’s diversity rationale guards (principle #2, C2); (iv) gendered harm — morphed intimate images, the r.3(2)(b) category; (v) reputational destruction with the persistence problem — “the internet never sleeps; and the internet never forgets” (X ¶1).

The existing legal mechanisms — a complete inventory: (1) Criminal (only when speech falls in a 19(2) class): promoting enmity (IPC 153A/153B → BNS 196/197); false reports/intimidating statements (IPC 505 → BNS 196(1)/353); obscenity (292–294 → 294–296); defamation (499/500 → BNS 356) — with the CrPC 195 → BNSS 217 sanction and BNSS 114/115 seizure mirrors Shreya said the online law must equal (¶¶99–101). (2) IT Act machinery: s.69A + 2009 Rules — executive blocking with recorded reasons, validity confirmed (¶119(b)); intermediary duties — r.3(1)(b)(vi) (“deceives or misleads … about the origin … patently false or misleading in nature but may reasonably be perceived as a fact”) and (x) (“patently false and untrue … intent to mislead or harass … for financial gain”) — the closest thing to a fake-news rule, operating as a take-down duty not an offence; 24-hour removal for intimate-image impersonation variants (r.3(2)(b)); 36-hour order-compliance (r.3(1)(d)). (3) Codes of the media itself: Cable Programme Code r.6 (“false and suggestive innuendos and half truths”); PCI Norms (accuracy; verification; the Uma Khurana guidelines 8(a)–(d) & 10 as the print mirror); NBA/NBSA and BCCC standards (external; E5) — the destruction unit’s answer: “self-regulation, but never drift into a statutory structure that is oppressive” (¶33(viii)). (4) Election-specific: RPA 1951 (Model Code; s.126A exit-poll publication bar; sanction machinery) and ECI fact-check/declarations; (5) Courts: takedown/anti-resurfacing orders on the X template — including global de-indexing (F2); for broadcast, postponement orders as “neutralizing devices” within 19(2) (Sahara via Naveen Jindal, PDF pp. 153–154). What deliberately does NOT exist: no pre-publication licensing of news; no general “falsehood” offence — and the reason is the unit’s history (Unit 1: the Vernacular Press Act’s “disloyal” seditious standard; s.66A’s autopsy).

Recommendations — graded, defensible: (i) keep criminal law only at the public-order/defamation edges (Rangarajan “spark in a powder keg”); (ii) fast-track 69A blocking + court-supervised take-downs for demonstrable harm (X); (iii) strengthen intermediary transparency so falsehood’s spread is auditable (monthly SSMI reports r.4(1)(d); compliance reports; traceability as a judicial-order-only tool, r.4(2) proviso); (iv) statutory recognition — not replacement — of self-regulation (the PCI→Media-Council design, B4; the BNN/NBSA model, E5); (v) media literacy and platform UI (labels; the r.4(3) paid-content labelling — the fake-news twin is paid propaganda); (vi) reject the s.66A redux and the “wholesale substitution” judicial rewrite (¶49).
