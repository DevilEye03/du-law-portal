🧭 One-line thesis for the whole unit: The Indian legal system approached media in three overlapping phases — colonial control (licensing → securities → seizure → censorship: media as a threat to be managed), constitutional conversion (media freedom as a derived fundamental right under Art. 19(1)(a), struck down of coercive statutes like the Vernacular-lineage laws, only cinema retaining pre-censorship), and legislative pluralism with self-regulation (post-1991: sectoral statutes for each medium — PRB Act & PCI for print, Cable Act & Prasar Bharati for broadcast, IT Act & Rules for the internet — with a perpetual, still-unfulfilled “Broadcasting Bill” gap). PART 1

Media — Concept, Constitutional Position & the Taxonomy of Forms

Before dissecting the three prescribed forms, secure the foundations the DU casebook itself lays down (Adukia notes §§1–4; casebook Unit 1 = “Forms-Print; Broadcast; Social”). A 20-mark answer on “forms of media” is built on: (i) what media is legally, (ii) how Art. 19 converts it into a right, (iii) how each form’s technology dictated its distinct legislative treatment.

1.1 · Definition, etymology and the “Fourth Estate” idea

Per the DU reading material (Adukia, Media Laws of India, ch. 1): “Communication is the procedure which we use to exchange information by various methods, and media is the medium or instrument of storing or communicating information.” The word medium comes from the Latin medius (“middle”) — media is the intermediary layer between the speaker and the public. ‘Media’, used interchangeably with the ‘Press’, denotes print & electronic information carriers — newspapers and magazines, radio, television — and “currently includes the Internet as new media”. Hailed as the ‘Fourth Estate’, media is “the watchdog of public affairs”, informing society and carrying society’s views “to those at the helm”.

  • Core normative functions (write all five in an “intro to media” answer): (1) informing the public (right to know — Prabha Dutt v. UoI, 1982 SCR (1) 1184); (2) public surveillance/watchdog (exposing defect — Mahatma Gandhi’s triple-object quote, cited by Adukia: “understand the popular feeling… arouse desirable sentiments… fearlessness to expose popular defects”); (3) forum/agenda-setting (marketplace of ideas — Shreya Singhal quoting Holmes J. in Abrams v. United States); (4) transmission of culture; (5) mobilisation/social change — even the colonial legislations, meant to silence, became “a catalyst for mass awakening” (the exact framing of DU Dec-2024 Q1).
  • Media law is a bundle, not a branch. The case material lists its subject-matter as covering: advertising, broadcasting, censorship, confidentiality, contempt, copyright, corporate law, defamation, entertainment, freedom of information, internet/IT, privacy, telecommunications — each of which becomes a legislative “contact point” with each media form.
💡 Legal definition anchor: The PRB Act, 1867 defines “newspaper” as “any printed periodical work containing public news or comments on public news, and includes a news agency supplying material for publication in a newspaper” — the same definition is carried into the Working Journalists Act, 1955 (S.2(b)) and the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977 (S.2). The IT Act, 2000 never defines “media”; it defines the “intermediary” (S.2(1)(w)) — the pivot on which all social-media regulation rotates.

1.3 · Constitutional position — why “media freedom” is a derived right

The DU notes are categorical: “The Indian Constitution does not provide freedom for media separately… it is derived from Article 19(1)(a)”. The Supreme Court repeatedly equated press with speech: Bennett Coleman (Beg J., concurring): freedom of speech and of the press is “the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy” — quoted verbatim in the DU casebook inside Shreya Singhal. Art. 19(1)(a) guarantees to every citizen the right to freedom of speech and expression; Art. 19(2) (as amended in 1951) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of sovereignty & integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence.

  • Derivative rights recognised for media: right to know (Prabha Dutt); right to circulate & right to read/receive; right to commercial speech (Tata Press v. MTNL, (1995) 5 SCC 139 overruling Hamdard Dawakhana, AIR 1960 SC 897); right to broadcast NOT an independent right — only 19(1)(a) with 19(2) restrictions and the “airwaves are public property” rider (CAB, (1995) 2 SCC 161); no 19(1)(g) fundamental right to carry on business dangerous to community (P.A. Jacob v. Deputy Commr., (2018) 3 SCC 517 — cited in the casebook’s Ajay Goswami unit).
  • Derivative rights denied: reporters have no fundamental right to interview undertrials on demand (Prabha Dutt — interview allowed on facts but “the media cannot interview a prisoner merely because the public is interested”); there is no right to broadcast on public airwaves without regulation (CAB); there is no privilege to publish jury deliberations (casebook, Subramanian Swamy extract); no pre-emptive right to transborder TV without Parliament/legislation (CAB — “no material for inferring such right” outside the licence framework).
⚖️ Three circles doctrine — Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar, AIR 1966 SC 740 (quoted in Shreya Singhal, para 30): the largest circle = “in the interest of the public” (security of State); the middle circle = public order; the smallest = actual breach of peace. A restriction touching only law-and-order cannot invoke 19(2)’s “public order” head. (This diagram scores in nearly every media question.)
🚫 Test for proximity: Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (casebook Unit 6): the relation between speech and danger must be “proximate and direct”; it should not be “far-fetched or conjectural… like a spark in an explosives tunnel”. Pre-censorship on speech is permissible only exceptionally (cinema — K.A. Abbas).

Quote both halves — it is the single most exam-relevant proposition about “forms” of media.

PART 2

Form I — PRINT MEDIA: origin, anatomy, and the entire statute-book that hangs on a printing press

Print is the oldest “regulated” medium in India — the entire vocabulary of Indian press law (keeper of the press, publisher, printer, editor, declaration, certificate of registration, title verification, annual statement of circulation) was coined for newspapers between 1867 and 1956 and is still operational law today. This part is arranged: history → statutory anatomy (section-by-section) → regulatory architecture → four deep case briefs from the DU case material → the criminal-law overlay.

2.1 · Genesis of print in India — the facts DU notes expect

  • Pre-press “newspapers”: court newsletters (akhbar) of the Mughals — Aurangzeb-era manuscript papers sent by Col. James Tod to the Royal Asiatic Society were “considerably free” and even reported the emperor’s personal affairs; “perhaps there was neither pre-censorship nor licensing, both being Western institutions” (Adukia). Kautilya’s Arthashastra (c. 324–300 BC) punished spreading false rumours; Ashoka’s Rock Edicts (c. 273–236 BC) are described as state communication akin to news content — an elegant opener for “history” questions.
  • 1556: printing press accidentally arrives in Goa (sent by the King of Portugal for Abyssinia — the press never left). First press for systematic printing: 1556 onwards, missionary printing from Goa; the modern era begins with the Serampore Mission.
  • 1766 — William Bolts: ex-East India Company servant attempted to start India’s first newspaper (and an open share-market); deported — “expulsion of the editor (printer) was the ultimate penalty”, upheld by the Supreme Court of Judicature at Calcutta. (His 1773 London publication Considerations on Indian Affairs carried the account.)
  • 10 Jan 1780 — James Augustus Hicky’s Bengal Gazette; or, Calcutta Public Advertiser: first newspaper printed in India; Hicky jailed in 1781 & his press seized in 1782 for attacks on the Company establishment — the founding “persecution” narrative.
  • 23 May 1818 — Samachar Darpan (Serampore missionaries): first Indian-language journal (Bengali weekly); bilingual Bengali–English from 1829. Lord Hastings had abolished pre-censorship in 1818 — a direct concession to the missionary presses.
  • 1818–1857: the vernacular press explodes — Hindu Patriot (edited later by Harish Chandra Mookerjee), Amrita Bazar Patrika (founded 1868 by Sisir Kumar Ghosh & Moti Lal Ghosh) — which famously became overnight an English newspaper in 1878 to escape Lytton’s Vernacular Press Act (real example; the Act’s own target: “It was particularly meant to crush Amrita Bazar Patrika” — Adukia). Babu Shishir Kumar’s rebuff to Sir Ashley Eden (“Your Honour, there ought to be at least one honest journalist in the land”) is quoted in the notes — use it as a colour anecdote.
  • News agencies: PTI, UNI, Hindustan Samachar, Samachar Bharati (Adukia) — “newspaper” legally includes the news agency feed (S.2, PRB definition via the 1977 Act). The Press (Registration of Books) Amendment Act, 1956 extended the Act to news agencies — so they must file declarations too.

2.2 · The parent statute — THE PRESS AND REGISTRATION OF BOOKS ACT, 1867 (25 of 1867) — section-by-section

Object (as recited): “to provide for the regulation of the presses and of periodicals containing news, for the preservation of copies of books and for the registration of books.” It contains no censorship: it is a registration-and-evidence statute — yet its machinery (who printed, who published, what was printed, where) pre-dates and enables every criminal, civil and tax action against print. It is the oldest press-specific enactment still in force (Adukia: “the earliest surviving enactment specifically directed towards the press”). Major amendment 1955 added Part VA creating the Registrar of Newspapers for India (RNI), functional from 1956, implementing the First Press Commission (1954) recommendation.

2.3 · The print-labour statutes — Working Journalists Act, 1955 & Wages Act, 1958 (the law that reached the Supreme Court in the casebook)

  • Working Journalists and Other Newspaper Employees (Conditions of Service) and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1955 (extended by the 1974 Amendment to all newspaper employees): defines “working journalist” (S.2(f) — editor, leader-writer, news editor, sub-editor, feature writer, copy taster, reporter, correspondent, cartoonist, news photographer, proof-reader; excludes managerial), “newspaper” (S.2(b)), “newspaper establishment” (S.2(d) — a press whose principal business is printing newspapers is deemed a newspaper establishment). Wage Boards u/Ss. 9 & 13C (3 employer + 3 employee + 4 independent members, chaired by a HC/SC judge); Central Govt order u/S.12 gives binding force; non-implementation is a legislative “sleeping power” — the ABP case below turns on precisely this.
  • Conditions of service hard-coded: 144 hours/4 weeks; 6-hour day (5½ for night shift) — S.6; leave: 10 holidays + 15 casual + 1 month earned (cap 90 days); maternity leave 3 months full wages; gratuity after 3 years’ service (S.5); notice for retrenchment: 6 months (editor) / 3 months (others) — S.3 modifies ID Act; IDA, ESI/PF & Standing Orders Acts made applicable (Ss.14, 15); Registers in Forms D–G. Penalty (as recorded in the notes): fine up to ₹200, ₹500 for repeat violations; recovery as arrears of land revenue through Labour Dept inspectors.
  • Working Journalists (Fixation of Rates of Wages) Act, 1958 — floor of wages pending Wage Board awards (₹750/month minimum for editors, ₹600 journalists etc. — the statutory wage-floor mechanism). Also Working Journalists & Other Newspaper Employees Tribunal Rules, 1979. Related: Newspaper (Prices and Pages) Act, 1956 (see 2.4); Newsprint Policy 1972 (executive, not statute — struck down in Bennett Coleman); the Dramatic Performances Act, 1876-style control was never extended to print.

2.4 · Economic regulation of print — the failed experiment: Newspaper (Prices and Pages) Act, 1956 → Bennett Coleman

Act’s design: Parliament empowered the Central Govt to regulate (i) the price of newspapers in relation to the number of pages/size/area, (ii) the space allotted for advertisements, and (iii) the allocation/appendage of supplements — the stated object: to prevent unfair competition and to secure “fuller opportunities of freedom of expression” for small & Indian-language papers. Before any order, publisher associations must be consulted; publication in contravention is prohibited (and S.3(3) deemed provisions into contracts).

This produced a chain of Constitution-Bench law that every DU answer must cite: Sakal Papers (1962) → Bennett Coleman (1972) → Indian Express Newspapers (1985) — full briefs below (§2.6). The Act itself was repealed in 2010 (Repealing & Amending Act, 2010) after TRAI-era deregulation — a nice “legislative-efforts” footnote: the experiment ended by abolition, not amendment.

2.5 · Self-regulation with statutory teeth — THE PRESS COUNCIL ACT, 1978 (37 of 1978)

Replaced the Press Council Act, 1965 (itself a First Press Commission child) — “establish a Press Council for the purpose of preserving the freedom of the Press and for maintaining and improving the standards of newspapers and news agencies”. Council became operational 1 March 1979 (Adukia).

Composition (S.5) — draw as a 29-seat diagram

  • Chairman — nominee of a committee: Chairman Rajya Sabha + Speaker Lok Sabha + one member elected by Council members (traditionally a retired SC judge).
  • 13 working journalists (6 editors — 3 from Indian-language papers; 7 others — min. 4 from Indian-language).
  • 6 owners/managers — 2 each from big, medium & small categories (by circulation notification).
  • 1 news-agency manager; 3 eminent persons — nominated by UGC (education & science), Bar Council of India (law), Sahitya Akademi (literature & culture); 5 MPs (3 LS + 2 RS).
  • Disqualifications/guards: no journalist owning a paper; only one person interested in a given paper/group among the editor & owner categories.

Powers & limits

  • S.13 — 11 enumerated functions (independence, code of conduct, public taste, sense of responsibility, reviewing restrictions on news-flow, foreign-funding of papers, studies of ownership concentration S.13(2)(i)…).
  • S.14 — power to censure: warn/admonish/censure newspaper, agency, editor, journalist; may compel publication of the council’s report (S.14(2)); no inquiry into sub-judice matters (S.14(3)); decision “final… not questioned in any court” (S.14(4)).

  • Cannot: suspend licences, fine, seize presses, order pay — its only sanction is exposure. (Hence the 2012 PCI Resolution for a “Media Council” with statutory backing was also not enacted — Part 5.5.)

2.6 · Deep case briefs — Print (DU case material #1, #2 + Adukia-mandated classics)

Sakal Papers (P) Ltd. v. Union of India cited in Shreya Singhal extract — casebook p. 30
AIR 1962 SC 305 · (1962) 3 SCR 842 · Constitution Bench (Sinha C.J., Das Gupta, Subba Rao, Shah, Mudholkar JJ.)
Facts The Daily Newspapers (Price and Page) Order, 1960, made under the Newspaper (Prices and Pages) Act, 1956, fixed for each newspaper a minimum price per page (maximum pages it could sell at a price), permitted only a 25% increase in price with a proportional increase in pages, and capped the advertising space at 75% (later 72%). The petitioners (salaried journalists’ unions supported papers + big papers) attacked it as destroying the economics of newspapers — big dailies could not absorb loss-making expansion while supplements (magazines-in-disguise) escaped.
Issues (1) Does the Act/Order directly restrict the freedom to propagate ideas guaranteed by 19(1)(a) (not merely a “business right”)? (2) Can “the State’s interest in curbing unfair competition among newspapers” be a 19(2) ground? (3) Is the classification (restricting dailies but exempting weeklies/‘magazines’ like the BL Onsund weekly) arbitrary?
Arguments State: freedom of speech is of the citizen, not of the corporation; the Order regulates only business activity; it actually promotes press freedom by keeping small papers alive (a 19(2)-flavoured “general public interest” reading). Petitioners: a newspaper can only speak through circulation & size; a price-page cap is a cap on speech; “freedom to propagate” includes freedom of circulation — restriction on the number of pages restricts the very volume of news.
Decision Unanimously struck down (4:1 — Mudholkar J partly dissenting on “corporation” locus). Das Gupta J: “the right to freedom of speech can be restricted only on the grounds mentioned in Article 19(2)”; the “right to circulate” is co-extensive with the right to publish; and the “right to propagate one’s views through the medium of newspapers would be infringed if the volume of news is restricted”. The Act’s classification (weekly supplements exempt) defeated its own object — arbitrariness under Art. 14 reinforced invalidity. The State’s interest in equalising circulation was not a permitted object for restricting speech. (Adukia notes’ summary: “the right of freedom of speech and expression couldn’t be taken away with the object of placing restrictions on the business activity of citizens.”)
Principle evolved (i) Press freedom = Art. 19(1)(a) extends to the “freedom to propagate”, including circulation, price and page economy — no restrictions outside Art. 19(2); (ii) an economic regulation cannot be immunised by calling it “business regulation”; (iii) the “general public interest” is NOT a ground under Art. 19(2) (a proposition that survived to Bennett Coleman and Indian Express).
Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India casebook #1
AIR 1973 SC 106 · (1973) 4 SCC 144 · 7-judge Constitution Bench
Facts The Newsprint Policy, 1972–73 (under Imports & Export Control Act + newsprint licensing) capped the maximum number of pages a daily may increase, denied additional pages to papers with “irregular growth” (>25% circulation jump in the last 3 years), and tied licences to circulation declared in 1969. The Times of India group (merged with the Bombay Economic Times in 1961 → jump of >25%) got no incremental pages though it had a surplus quota. The policy thus locked the market structure of the early-60s — penalising the paper that had actually modernised.
Issues (1) Do newspapers have 19(1)(a) & 19(1)(g) rights, and can citizens collectively assert them? (2) Is the policy (not a statute) amenable to Art. 32? (3) Does restricting page growth directly curtail freedom of speech? (4) Was the “irregular growth” classification arbitrary? (5) Can an executive policy be tested under Art. 14 when no statute prescribes standards?
Arguments Union: newsprint is scarce; allocation is pure trade regulation; the petitioners are corporations with no fundamental right; policy is administrative, immune from Art. 14/19 scrutiny; it protected smaller papers = “public interest”. Petitioners: circulation & number of pages are the medium of expression — “freedom to propagate” is 19(1)(a)’s essence; the policy was an executive instrument of retaliation against the TOI group’s reporting (pre-Emergency context); Art. 19(2) has no head to justify it; Art. 14 forbids unfettered executive discretion in allocating a public resource; citizens-as-shareholders/editors can invoke Art. 32.
Decision The policy was struck down. Held: (a) fundamental rights of citizens are not lost by combining to do business through a corporation — the writ was maintainable; (b) “the main target of the impugned policy is the freedom of the press”; the number of pages and circulation are the very fabric of 19(1)(a); restrictions for “public interest”/“general economic good” are outside 19(2); (c) even Art. 19(6) “reasonable restriction in general public interest” for trade cannot be wielded so as to directly abridge speech; (d) the “irregular growth” clause was arbitrary (a retro-punishment for growth itself) and thus violated Art. 14 — executive policy affecting rights must be informed by “discernible principles”. (Beg J. — freedom of press the “Ark of the Covenant of Democracy”, quoted via Shreya Singhal para 9; the same extract notes Mathew/Sinha lines that media freedom includes freedom of circulation and the right of the public to receive news.)
Principle (i) Direct-effect doctrine: a law/policy whose direct effect is on speech is tested under 19(1)(a), whatever its business label; (ii) freedom of press includes the right to publish, circulate and grow; (iii) executive policies without statutory standards are reviewable under Arts. 14 & 19; (iv) “general public interest” is no answer for a 19(2)-deficient restriction. This remains the strongest press-freedom ratio in India — a must-cite for both forms and history answers.
Indian Express Newspapers (Bombay) Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India reproduced in casebook pp. 33–36 (inside Shreya extract)
(1985) 1 SCC 641 · AIR 1986 SC 515
Facts The Finance Acts 1980–82 raised import duty on newsprint & related raw materials (and differential rates on imported vs. indigenous newsprint, “surcharge” on advertising machinery). Petitioners (four major dailies) pleaded that the tax, when imposed at crushing rates, operates as a direct restriction on press freedom and as a colourable use of taxing power.
Issues Is taxation of the press per se violative of 19(1)(a)? Can a tax struck at confiscatory/high rates without classification justification be invalidated? Is the differential customs treatment arbitrary?
Arguments Petitioners: the press cannot survive without newsprint; taxes which “cripple the circulation” are prior restraint by economics (citing Sakal, Bennett Coleman, and US cases like Grosjean — Louisiana’s targeted newspaper tax — and Minneapolis Star). Union: press pays taxes like every citizen; validity is not measured by incidental burden; no fundamental right to trade in newspapers.
Decision Court balanced: “The press cannot claim any immunity from taxation… but freedom from taxation is not guaranteed… yet the State cannot use taxation as a weapon of control.” The famous formulation: “freedom of the press is not expressly stated in Art. 19(1)(a)… it is implicit in the right… comprising (1) freedom to publish; (2) freedom of circulation; (3) no pre-censorship… ‘the freedom of the press has to be read in Art. 19(1)(a)’.” However, the tax was upheld; differential duties were struck down for want of intelligible differentia (Art. 14) and refunds ordered for post-judgment collections.
Principle Freedom of circulation is part of 19(1)(a); taxation of media is valid unless used “as a instrument of control or with a design to stifle” — the “colourable-taxation” doctrine enters Indian press law; differential classification needs a real nexus with the object.
ABP (P) Ltd. v. Union of India casebook #4 — reproduced verbatim pp. 7–30
(2014) 3 SCC 327 · P. Sathasivam CJI, Ranjan Gogoi & Shiva Kirti Singh JJ.
Facts (from the casebook text) The Working Journalists Act, 1955 (extended to all newspaper employees by the 1974 Amendment) empowers the Centre to constitute Wage Boards (Ss. 9 & 13C) and to notify their recommendations (S.12). Since 1955, six Wage Boards for journalists and four for non-journalists have functioned. In 2007 the Govt constituted the Justice K. Narayana Kurup Boards; after his resignation (31.7.2008) Justice Gurbax Rai Majithia (retd. Bombay HC) took over (4.3.2009); term extended to 31.12.2010; reports submitted; Govt accepted & notified on 11.11.2011 (new wage scales, DA neutralisation, variable pay etc.). Newspaper managements (ABP group and others) challenged (i) the constitutional validity of the 1955 Act itself (Arts. 14, 19(1)(a), 19(1)(g)) and (ii) the notification/Wage Board process.
Issues (1) Can the State single out the newspaper industry for special labour regulation — is it “misplaced classification”? (2) Is a statutory wage-fixing machinery for the press an infringement of 19(1)(a) (economic stranglehold argument à la Bennett Coleman) and 19(1)(g)? (3) Is the 1955 Act obsolete post-liberalisation (journalists now well-paid; other industries’ Wage Boards abolished — National Commission on Labour, 2002 recommended abolition)? (4) Procedural: composition & procedure of the Majithia Boards (bias — reliance on Perspective Publications re independent members; alleged blind copy of the 6th Central Pay Commission; failure to consider capacity-to-pay/regional variation). (5) Validity of Government’s selective non-acceptance of recommendations (retirement age, pension, paternity leave not implemented).
Arguments — petitioners (employers) The Act regulates only print, not electronic media → arbitrary classification; wage-fixing through a pay-commission for a single industry saddles newspapers with burdens (gratuity, hours, wages) that “socio-economic changes” made unnecessary; the Boards treated some papers via Industrial-Disputes-style process and others arbitrarily; the “independent” members were nominated by the government biased in favour of employees; capacity to pay was ignored; time-bound promotions cannot be imposed by wage orders.
Arguments — Union of India Art. 19(1)(g) is not offended by reasonable labour regulation — “the freedom to carry on the business of journalism is not absolute and does not include the right to exploit employees”; the press enjoys no immunity from socio-economic legislation; classification is permissible since the press industry has a historical service-condition deficit (low-paid journalists were vulnerable to political capture — a 1950s policy rationale); the notification challenged was an accepted statutory recommendation — judicially unreviewable absent mala fides; capacity-to-pay was reflected through gradation of establishments (X/Y/Z city categories, turnover-based classification).
Decision All writ petitions dismissed — the Court upheld the Act, the 1974 Amendment, the Boards’ composition, and the 11.11.2011 notification. Key holdings as per casebook: (a) legislative classification is valid — “in selecting the press industry employers from all industrial employers the Act subjects them to differential treatment with an intelligible differentia” linked to the object (decent wages so journalists remain independent — wage security = editorial independence); no fundamental right to carry on business “harmful to the community” and no exemption of the press from ordinary labour law; (b) obsolescence is not unconstitutionality — it is for the legislature to repeal; (c) the Wage Boards’ composition was proper; reliance on Perspective Publications (bias of independents) was distinguished — mere govt nomination of independents does not vitiate; (d) the Boards “have not blindly imported” the 6th CPC; variable pay within jurisdiction since the Act’s definition of “wages” includes new allowances; 100% DA neutralisation was justified (public sector practice since 1996); (e) S.12 gives Government wide discretion to accept/modify recommendations — “prerogative… no scope for a second hearing”, non-acceptance of some suggestions (retirement age, pension, paternity leave) did not taint the award as they were beyond the Board’s mandate; (f) arrears to be paid in four installments by March 2014; revised wages from April 2014.
Principle (i) “The freedom of the press does not include freedom from socio-economic legislation enacted in the interest of the employees of the press”; (ii) press regulation may validly classify “newspaper industry” as a class; (iii) wage-board recommendations are not penal but protective and their validity is measured on the Act’s object, not by comparisons to abolished boards in other industries. (Use for: “legislative efforts for media — protective, not restrictive.”)
Prabha Dutt v. Union of India casebook #2 — brief
(1982) 1 SCC 1 · 1982 SCR (1) 1184 · on Tihar jail interview of A.K. Gopalan
Facts & Decision A senior journalist sought permission to interview A.K. Gopalan (under-trial) in Tihar for a biography; jail rules denied it for all press interviews. The Court: the public has a right to know, which the media exercises on its behalf; “the function of the press in a representative democracy must be ever watchful, so that the formative and social values… come to bear in the making of the community” — yet no fundamental right of the reporter to demand an interview; the Court granted permission on the special facts. Principle: right to information (receive) is within 19(1)(a); media rights are derivative of the public’s — an essential line for “forms of media” answers. Also cite: Union of India v. JJ Singh (2006) — interview of Rajiv Gandhi assassin was held to be a case of the media overstepping “sensationalism over substance” (the Supreme Court castigated “trial by media” and ordered PCI inquiry).
Ajay Goswami v. Union of India casebook #23 — deep-dive extract pp. 221–230
AIR 2007 SC 493; (2007) 3 SCC 143 — PIL on publication of advertisements/vulgar news affecting children
Facts A PIL by a school teacher challenged the indiscriminate publication in leading dailies of (a) advertisements of “panacea” drugs & sex-education/OTC drugs, and (b) vulgar photographs/captions (“moan for more”, “get that zing back into your sex life”, black-stripe MMS photos etc.) to which minors had ready access. The casebook’s reproduced extract itself frames the two issues.
Issues (verbatim in casebook) (1) “Is the material in newspapers really harmful for the minors?” — even if not obscene “within the four corners of law”, it “has a tendency to deprave and corrupt” tender minds. (2) “Whether minors have any independent right enforceable under Art. 32?” — the Court anchors it in 19(1)(a) + 21 + 39(f) + CRC.
Decision Obscenity standards are variable: “no attempt has been made till date to define any yardstick for the minors” — the ordinary-adult test is insufficient for children. The Court refused State censorship but ordered: self-regulation by print media — PCI to strengthen the Norms of Journalistic Conduct (banning OTC/“miracle-drug” ads and regressing obscene visuals); the Government to notify the same (Standards of Journalistic Conduct, 2008 followed); DAVP/ASCI to police ads; the DMRA, 1954 & Drugs & Cosmetics Act to be enforced; contempt-like restraint on publishing sexual details of offences against children (also see S. 228A IPC → now S.345 BNS — identity of rape victims). (Principle: “protection of the young permits content-differentiation without prior restraint.”)

Other classic print cases named in the casebook’s history (for citation lines): Virendra v. State of Punjab, AIR 1957 SC 896 (Punjab Special Powers (Press) Act, 1956 — upholding emergency powers to prohibit publication of material relating to a “particular subject” during agitation: 19(2) is wide enough; but the Act lapsed); Romesh Thappar (1950) & Brij Bhushan (1950) — pre-censorship struck down, prompting the First Amendment (Part 5.3).

PART 3

Form II — BROADCAST (ELECTRONIC) MEDIA: airwaves as public trust; the architecture from 1885 to 2026

Broadcast is the youngest “old” medium and the only one the state never fully deregulated: content codes, carriage licences, spectrum scarcity and a permanent “broadcasting bill” gap define its legislative history. Films (cinema), though technically “exhibition”, belong to this media family in the DU scheme (the casebook books cinema regulation inside “Media & Its Regulation”) — covered here with its statute.

3.1 · Anatomy & history of the form

Technology-constituted elements (write as “components”)

  • Content provider / broadcaster (news channel; DD; AIR) — needs an uplinking licence (MIB, Uplinking Guidelines).
  • Teleport / up-link hub — earth station; FDI 49% (Adukia).
  • Satellite & transponder — space segment (ISRO/NSIL, GSAT; transponder leases).
  • Down-link & carriage layer: local cable operators (“last mile”, LAN), HITS hub, DTH (Direct-To-Home), IPTV, mobile TV; CAS/set-top boxes; conditional access (CAS areas).
  • Audience — receiver with no licence (Radio, TV & VCR Sets (Exemption from Licensing Requirements) Rules, 1997 — the repeal of the absurd “own-a-TV-licence” regime).

Chronology to memorise

  • 1895–96 — Marconi wireless; 1876 Post Office (India) begins wiring; Telegraph Act 1885 vests “exclusive privilege” in the Central Government.
  • 1923 — first experimental broadcast Bombay; 1927 Indian Broadcasting Company (private) → 1930 AIR (state); 1936 renaming “Akashvani”; All India Radio reorganised 1941 (Sir John Reith visit).
  • Sept 1959 — Doordarshan experimental TV service (Delhi, UNESCO aid) → 1965 regular service → 1975–76 satellite experiments → 1982 colour & national network (Asian Games) → 1987 serialisation boom (Ramayan) → Doordarshan monopoly till 1991.
  • 1991–92 — satellite liberalisation: Star TV, Zee TV (1992); the “cultural invasion” anxiety of the P.V. Narasimha Rao-era cable debates (Adukia quotes the Cable Act Statement of Objects & Reasons verbatim!).
  • 1995 — CAB: airwaves are public property; monopoly constitutional only till Parliament legislates; SC order of 25.11.1994 (tele-educational) had already cracked the monopoly (Dakshin Ratings v. UoI, 1993 — right to use telecast rights; noted in Adukia broadcast section).
  • 1997 — private TV guidelines; 2001 revised guidelines & uplinking regulation; 2011–14 digitisation & addressability (Phase I–IV; 31 Dec 2014 analog switch-off, Adukia); 2014+ community radio & FM Phase-III (Nov 2014, 392 cities); Jan 2020 new FM policy (10-yr licences, 70-city); DDL “DD Free Dish” free DTH since 2000–04.
  • 2023 — Telecommunications Act (repeals Telegraph Act 1885 & Wireless Telegraphy Act 1933 — authorisation regime, spectrum via auction; assent 24 Dec 2023; provisions in force from Jun–Jul 2024 with rules in 2025–26); 2023–24 Broadcasting Services (Regulation) Bill drafts; 2026 — MIB working on Unified Broadcasting Rules under the Telecom Act (public-interest broadcast obligations: draft requires e.g. 30 min/day on social themes for TV, 1 hr on FM; not finalised).
⚖️ The constitutional core — “spectrum doctrine”: In Secretary, Ministry of Information & Broadcasting v. Cricket Association of Bengal, (1995) 2 SCC 161 the Court (Sawant J for majority) held that broadcasting is a “speech” within 19(1)(a) but “no citizen has a fundamental right to use the airwaves” — frequencies are public property to be used through a “proper system set up in accordance with law”; scarcity & pervasiveness justify stricter content regulation of television; the Central Government’s monopoly was valid because Parliament had not legislated otherwise, not because it is inherent. (See the case brief at 3.5.) This is the single most important “form of media” holding for broadcast answers.

3.2 · The broadcast statute-book — element by element

(a) Indian Telegraph Act, 1885 (13 of 1885) → Telecommunications Act, 2023 (44 of 2023)

  • S.4 — Central Government’s exclusive privilege of establishing, maintaining & working telegraphs; licences under it (and under the Indian Wireless Telegraphy Act, 1933) were the legal root of the AIR/DD monopoly and of every broadcasting authorisation. S.5 — power to take possession of licensed establishments in a public emergency; S.5(2) — interception of messages (proviso: “public safety”) — the wiretap ancestor, later replicated by S.69 IT Act; S.25 Telegraph Act creates offences for unauthorised telegraph; S.19 of the Sea Customs Act, 1878 & Ss. 25–26 of the Indian Post Office Act, 1898 completed the “censor every channel” trinity of the colonial age (Adukia — an excellent paragraph to paraphrase in a history answer).
  • Telecommunications Act, 2023 — assent 24.12.2023; “telecommunication” redefined technology-neutrally (wire, radio, optical, electromagnetic, satellite), keeping OTT/VoIP within the definitional penumbra; “authorisation” replaces licences; spectrum assigned by auction etc.; powers to take over & suspend services and to prohibit transmission in the interest of public safety; universal service; penalties & adjudication (adjudicating officer → designated committee → TDSAT); expressly repeals the Telegraph Act, 1885 and Wireless Telegraphy Act, 1933; certain sections in force from 26.6.2024 & 5.7.2024; consequential rules (incl. right-of-way & the proposed “Unified Broadcasting Rules”) progressing in 2024–26.

S.7Register of programmes (monthly maintenance; 1-year retention) — evidentiary base for State action.
S.8 (sub. 2011)Must-carry: DD channels & Sansad (Parliament) channels mandatorily carried; prime-band limits in pre-DAS areas.
S.9Display of cable service in the name of the registered operator (branding of carriage).
S.10–12Power to make rules (programme & advertisement codes, CAS etc.) — the Rules, 1994; inspection powers; disputes referred (to authority/TRAI per regime).

Ss.14, 15Power of authorised officer to regulate/prohibit transmission in public interest (repeat: religion/race/language/caste disharmony; public tranquillity); seizure of equipment (S.15); S.16 — penalties: up to 2 yrs/₹1,000 first, 5 yrs/₹5,000 repeat (a “twin punishment” structure — the 2002 Act made non-DAS & code contraventions practically cognizable); S.17 offences by companies (vicarious); S.18 cognizance only on complaint of authorised officer.

(d) Cinematograph Act, 1952 (37 of 1952) + Cinematograph (Certification) Rules, 1983 — “censorship by certificate”

  • S.2(c) “cinematograph film”; S.4 — any person desiring to exhibit a film must apply to the Central Board of Film Certification (CBFC); Board = a Chairman + non-official members (now 12+ regional Examining Committees; advisory panels — Rules Ch. III).
  • S.5A — four certificates: U (unrestricted public exhibition), UA (unrestricted with parental discretion for under-12s; further sub-divided in 2013 amendments into UA with parental guidance for children below 7, 13 and 16 — “U/A 7+” etc. in practice/CBFC guidelines), A (adults), S (special class — professions). S.5B(4): “U/A” must carry the cautionary endorsement.
  • S.5B(1) — the substantive limit (verbatim from the uploaded Act): “A film shall not be certified… if… the film or any part of it is against the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, or involves defamation or contempt of court or is likely to incite the commission of any offence.” — i.e. the exact Art. 19(2) catalogue + “incitement”; and it travels with the film even post-certification (reconsideration — S.5E: govt may revisit within 5 years).
  • S.5B(2) Guidelines (1978, revised 1991 etc.) — reproduced in full in the Adukia notes: film judged “in its entirety from the point of view of its overall impact”; 19 specific canons (no glorification of violence/crime; children & handicap & animals protection; no pointless violence; no glamorisation of drinking/drugs/smoking; vulgarity/depravity; dual-meaning words; denigration of women; sexual-violence scenes reduced to minimum if “germane to the theme”; sexual perversions; contemptuous of racial/religious groups; communal/obscurantist/anti-national attitude; sovereignty; security; friendly relations; public order; defamation/contempt; national symbols only per Emblems Act); titles also scrutinised. Add the real-world gloss: CBFC may require cuts, modifications, disclaimer; refusal of certificate is the only lawful “ban” (a direct ban by the State outside the Act is unconstitutional — UoI v. Motion Picture Assn., and Art. 19(1)(g) protects exhibition once certified — S. Kumar v. Indian Airlines— optional).
  • S.5C — appeal to the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal (FCAT) (30 days; S.5D FCAT composition — retired HC judge; a Chair + ≤2 members); S.5E reconsideration by CBFC; S.5F revision by Central Govt (on its own or otherwise) — the tribunal regime (FCAT abolished in 2017 by 2Tribunals Reforms Act — appeals now to the High Courts — a post-notes update to mention).
  • S.6 — exemptions (govt films, Defence; films for private screening?; S.5B does not apply to certain categories); Part III (Ss.6–12) — licensing of cinematograph theatres by District Magistrates (State subject); conditions (children, obscenity).
  • S.7 — penalties: exhibition of uncertified/refused/cut-violating film (incl. “interpolations”: re-inserting deleted cuts, uncertified bits, A-cert shown to non-adults) — ≤3 yrs/₹1 lakh + ₹20,000/day continuing; video-film variant: 3 months–3 yrs + ₹20,000–1 lakh; forfeiture of copy (S.7(4)); S.7A — search & seizure by police; S.8 — offences by companies. Piracy: S.6A (no delivery of a print without certificate) and criminal copyright overlay (Copyright Act Ss.63–63D) — the (lapsed) Cinematograph (Amendment) Bill 2021 proposed specific 3-yr anti-camcording punishment; current anti-piracy practice runs via dynamic injunctions (see Naveen Jindal/Sanjay Leela Bhansali lines) + IT Rules takedowns.

3.5 · Deep case briefs — Broadcast & Cinema

Secretary, Ministry of Information & Broadcasting v. Cricket Association of Bengal casebook #3 & #20 (twice listed — Units 2 & 4)
(1995) 2 SCC 161 · AIR 1995 three-Judge bench, decided 24.1.1995 · the “airwaves” case
Facts CAB (the governing body of Bengal cricket) contracted with Trans World Media (Australia), holder of the international broadcast rights, for a signal feed of the 1996 Wills World Cup; DD had sold partial rights and would not telecast live in India for want of funds; a writ petition sought DD be compelled to telecast live. The single judge & Division Bench of the Calcutta HC ordered the govt not to interfere with CAB’s arrangement to downlink and relay live; the Union appealed. The matter expanded into a constitutional adjudication on broadcasting itself (the SC permitted Star TV to feed signal through a foreign satellite with a disclaimer — “any attempt to interfere with the telecasting would be perusable?”; ultimately interim protection was given).
Issues (1) Is broadcasting an aspect of the freedom of speech & expression (19(1)(a))? (2) Does every citizen have a fundamental right to broadcast / does a private body have a right to use airwaves (spectrum) — uplink, downlink and telecast over its own equipment? (3) Can a private broadcaster set up a “telecast” through foreign satellites (transborder broadcasting) in the absence of legislation? (4) Is the Central Government’s monopoly over airwaves & broadcasting constitutionally sustainable? (5) Is the “telecast” of an event different from “televising” — what does “freedom of viewers” mean?
Arguments CAB/petitioners: telecasting is expression; the right to impart & receive information is one indivisible right (Romesh Thappar; Sakal); spectrum belongs to the public so a public trust obligates the State to license, not monopolise; DD had failed in its duty; the US (First Amendment), Canadian, UK (BBC-ITBA duopoly model) precedents show regulation-not-monopoly is viable; the Telecommunications Act & Telegraph Act merely give machinery, not a monopoly justification. Union: airwaves are scarce public property requiring planning & national control (IUC frequency-allocation treaties); transborder telecasting threatens sovereignty & public order; only Parliament can create the framework (Art. 19(6) & Entry 31 List I); private monopoly of a foreign feed “would make India a colony of foreign channels”.
Decision (Sawant J; majority): (i) Freedom of speech “includes the right to propagate and publish” and “the use of any other medium for communication of one’s views” — including electronic — but (ii) “no citizen has a fundamental right to access the airwaves” because they are public property, “the use of which is to be controlled by the State” — “the right to broadcast… can only be through a proper system set up by law”; (iii) the Central Government’s monopoly existed de facto not de jure — it exists only “because there is no legislation to the contrary”, and therefore “it would be for the Government and Parliament to determine the proper agency to manage the airwaves… an independent statutory authority” was urged (the Court issued the well-known direction that the Central Government “take steps”/ “evolve a mechanism” — the Prasar Bharati-like public body for spectrum, and “until a law is made, [the government should act] consistent with Art. 19”); (iv) the viewers also have a right — 19(1)(a) “includes the right of the viewers and listeners to take the telecast from foreign channels” unless regulated by law: the State cannot restrict the right to receive, so downlinking of foreign TV could not be prohibited without law (the executive policy (1991) was held insufficient for restriction) — this limb catalysed the Cable Act regime; (v) held: DD must telecast the World Cup; CAB could feed the signal through an approved agency; monopoly to be replaced by legislative framework.
Principle The “public-trust theory of the airwaves”: (a) broadcasting ⊂ 19(1)(a), but no right to the means of broadcasting; (b) spectrum is public property — the State holds it as trustee with a duty to make access fair via a statutory framework; (c) the right of the audience to receive is an equal limb of 19(1)(a) — the flipside of Sakal’s freedom to circulate; (d) executive diktat cannot substitute legislation in limiting electronic speech. (Also quote Minister of I&B v. Polavaram Video (2014) 7 SCC 1 — “no citizen can claim a right to airwaves… but government policy on the use of airwaves cannot be arbitrary”.)
Odyssey Communications Pvt. Ltd. v. Lokvidayan Sanghatana casebook #25 (pp. 242–244)
(1988) 3 SCC 410 · E.S. Venkataramaiah J (for himself & N.D. Ojha J)
Facts (as reproduced in the casebook) The serial ‘Honi-Anhoni’ (Doordarshan, Wednesdays 9–9:30 pm; produced by Odyssey) was attacked by a Pune-based social organisation as promoting blind faith, superstition, ghosts/rebirth, creating fear in children etc. The Bombay HC (Aurangabad bench) granted an ad-interim injunction stopping telecast of episodes 12 & 13 in a PIL filed without impleading the producer; the Supreme Court stayed & eventually reversed, letting the last episode telecast.
Issues (1) Do citizens have a “right not to see” a telecast such that a court may enjoin its broadcast? (2) Is Doordarshan’s monopoly in the telecast of programmes an immutable one? (3) Can PIL enjoin telecast before any statutory/ regulatory remedy was exhausted?
Decision Appeal allowed; injunction set aside. Held: a petitioner’s remedy is to complain to the authority (DD), not to “restrain the airing” — every viewer is “free not to see the programme” (no common-law “right not to be shown”), and no right of an organisation can “prevent others from viewing the serial”. Every citizen has a fundamental right to communicate his views to other citizens, “including the right to telecast… his views to other citizens” via the appropriate medium — “Doordarshan is not a common carrier… but the State cannot monopolise the means of telecast” — the Court expressly rejected “the monopoly of the State over the means of telecast” as “not based on legal principles”, noting the existence of private cable & other technologies — anticipating CAB by seven years. Obscenity/programme-content is to be judged by the medium’s own standards & the viewer’s free choice; the serial’s own disclaimer (the doctor-professor’s scientific explanation of “supernatural” events) showed it critiqued, not promoted, superstition.
Principle (i) No prior judicial restraint on telecast without irreparable legal injury; (ii) the viewer’s freedom to switch off ≠ a veto by a self-appointed guardian of morals; (iii) early judicial demolition of “the DD monopoly dogma” — a State broadcaster is a licensee-like regulator subject to equality (Art. 14) in allocating air-time.
K.A. Abbas v. Union of India Adukia §10; foundational for cinema-as-separate-form
AIR 1971 SC 481 · (1970) 2 SCC 780 · Wanchoo CJI, Hidayatullah, Ray JJ.
Facts K.A. Abbas, the veteran journalist, produced a documentary “A Time to Be Free” on Kashmir and applied to the Board of Film Censors for a certificate for public exhibition. Its Examining Committee advised excision of a short song/love sequence (a woman shown partly undressed; a couple embracing in “suggestive” positions) as obscene under the S.5B guidelines & S.292 IPC; Abbas refused the cuts, and the certificate was therefore refused (the Revising Committee upheld the Board). Challenging the very power of pre-certification as an unconstitutional prior restraint on 19(1)(a) — invoking Near v. Minnesota and the Romesh-Thappar line that struck down press pre-censorship — he failed before the Bombay High Court and approached the Supreme Court (three-Judge bench; judgment by Wanchoo C.J., with Hidayatullah J dissenting in part on the specific refusal).
Issue Is pre-censorship of films (Cinematograph Act, 1952, Ss.4–5B) a valid restriction on 19(1)(a) given that pre-censorship of the press was unconstitutional (Romesh Thappar, Brij Bhushan)?
Arguments Petitioner: prior restraint is “the most serious” abridgement (Near v. Minnesota); films are “expression” like newspapers; the Act confers unguided discretion. State: films are different — combine sight, sound, movement; reach all ages including the illiterate & children; a “motion picture is able to stir up emotions more deeply”; art may be regulated for its social effects; classification ≠ ban.
Decision Constitution Bench upheld pre-censorship: “films have to be treated separately from other forms of art or writing… because a motion picture is able to stir up emotions more deeply”; classification of films into categories (then U/A) is not censorship in the obnoxious sense; the freedom of the artist must yield to the “prevailing standards of morality & decency of the community”; and “art is… to serve social and moral ends — the film in its entirety” standard. The individual demand for re-certification was left to the Board, but the validity of the regime — the question the DU case material treats as Abbas's real contribution — was settled for all cinema in India.
Principle Cinema = a distinct medium whose pervasiveness justifies, alone among media, statutory pre-certification — a classification-based “time, place & manner” regime, not a prohibition regime. (Later refined: UoI v. Motion Picture Assn. — certification under the Act alone is the ban-limit; State bans outside the Act are void; Rangarajan — post-certification, only imminent threat justifies restraint.)
S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram casebook #26 (pp. 245–258)
(1989) 2 SCC 574 · AIR 1989 SC 2007 · two-Judge bench of the Supreme Court; decided 30.3.1989 · reproduced in the DU casebook as case #26
Facts The producer applied for an exhibition certificate for the Tamil film “Ore Oru Gramathile” (“In One Village”), which criticised reservation on caste lines (its plot: a Brahmin girl obtains an Adi-Dravida caste certificate to get a college seat and later succeeds in the IAS; the film carries references to Dr. Ambedkar and to the “politics of caste”). The Examining Committee refused a certificate; the Revising Committee of the CBFC, by 5:4, granted a “U” certificate with excisions (7.12.1987). Objectors moved the Madras High Court pleading that the film portrayed the reservation policy in a “biased” manner, touched caste sensitivity of the forward castes and was “bound to create a law and order problem in Tamil Nadu”. The Single Judge dismissed the writs; the Division Bench revoked the U-certificate. Rangarajan & the producers appealed to the Supreme Court. (The Union of India, through the Minister of I&B, was the responding censor-side authority; P. Jagjivan Ram had been I&B Minister when the matter began.)
Issues (1) Does a foreseen hostile reaction of a section of the public (threat of law-and-order disturbance) justify refusing certification / revoking a certificate? (2) What is the correct standard of judging a film — isolated passages vs. the film as a whole? (3) Is judicial review of the CBFC’s balancing to be exercised with restraint? (4) Is the “interests of public order” head of 19(2) satisfied by a merely conjectural danger?
Arguments Petitioners (producers): 19(1)(a) covers cinema; opinions expressed in a film cannot be proscribed because they are unpopular or controversial; the main theme — “reservation should be based on economic criteria, not caste” — is protected social comment; the court must not act as a super-censor; “a handful of agitated demonstrators cannot veto speech” (the State’s duty is to maintain order). Respondents (objectors/State): the film’s anti-reservation stance “endangers the integrity of the nation”, is likely to provoke “volatile” reactions in Tamil Nadu; the censors & the Division Bench rightly read “incitement to an offence/public order” in its scenes; moral and social fabric permits prior restraint of cinema (K.A. Abbas).
Decision Appeals allowed — the “U” certificate (with excisions) restored. The Court laid down that: the effect of the film must be judged “not on isolated passages but in the context of the main theme and the overall message”; “the reaction of the community to the film has to be judged from the point of view of a man of ordinary sense and understanding, not of a man of hypersensitive, delicate or peculiar taste”; restrictions can be imposed only when the expression “tends to cause public disorder or safely leads to the inference that it would cause breach of public order”, the danger being “proximate and direct” — it “should not be far-fetched, hypothetical or problematical” (“the interest of social tolerance is such that it cannot permit the removal of the film from the screen merely because a section of people may react emotionally”); the State’s obligation is to protect free expression, not to bow to mob reaction; the censors, as experts, “should be responsive to social change… but moral values should not be allowed to be sacrificed in the guise of social change or cultural assimilation”, and the Revising Committee — “a cross-section of the community” — had approved the film “in the light of the objectives of the Act and the guidelines”, so judicial interference was unwarranted.
Principle evolved India’s “proximate-and-direct-danger” test for media restraint (the Indian cousin of “clear and present danger”, applied to post-production state action); the “reasonable man / strong-minded ordinary person” standard for obscenity-and-offence analysis; and the “no heckler’s veto” canon — the remedy against inflammatory speech is more speech plus public-order action, not suppression of the medium. Cite alongside Aveek Sarkar v. State of West Bengal, (2014) 4 SCC 357 (extracted at casebook p. 41 within Shreya Singhal): the Supreme Court formally abandoned the 155-year-old Hicklin “tendency to deprave and corrupt the susceptible” test in favour of the “average person, contemporary community standards, taken as a whole” standard — quoted in the casebook and the current law of obscenity across print, films and electronic media.
Bobby Art International v. Om Pal Singh Hoon (‘Bandit Queen’) casebook #27 (pp. 261–269)
(1996) 4 SCC 1 · Kuldip Singh J
Facts The CBFC refused a certificate to Shekhar Kapur’s Bandit Queen (life of Phoolan Devi) on grounds of (i) scenes of assault/nudity of the heroine (violence against her & the communal “Thakurs” abuse), (ii) “dirty” language, (iii) the “Maa” prayer scene; the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal (FCAT) allowed limited cuts; HC reversed the Tribunal; Supreme Court restored the (minimal-cut) certification. A scene — the assault of the heroine by the dacoit Vikram — was deleted; the bathing-scene retained per certificate.
Issues Can a film be denied a certificate because its brutal realism offends decency? Is a “human rights perspective” permissible in adjudicating obscenity and artistic freedom?
Arguments Petitioners (social bodies): nudity & violence “glorify” banditry & outrage modesty; film “injures” the image of women. Respondents (producers): the film indicts the very violence it shows — the nude bathing scene is a statement of dignity & trauma; the S.5B guidelines themselves allow “germane to theme” treatment with minimisation; censorship must be assessed with the film’s message, judged as a whole & with “contemporary standards”.
Decision ‘A’-certificate with the FCAT’s cuts upheld. Held: “the film must be seen as a whole and judged by the impact test — the theme, the tone, and the message”; scenes “which offend morals… are not to be judged in isolation”; the film, far from glorifying, is a “social message against violence against women”. The Court quoted its own Rangarajan, the Directive Principles, Indian Express, and stressed the CBFC as a “balancing institution”, its decisions presumptively valid (the S.5B(2) “film in its entirety” standard; judicial review confined to relevance and illegality).
Principle “Overall impact of the film as a whole” standard (S.5B guidelines read into jurisprudence); artistic speech dealing with violence can condemn it — “a film which exposes the brutality of society cannot be condemned for depicting brutality.”
Union of India v. Motion Picture Association casebook #28 (pp. 270–277 — reproduced)
AIR 1999 SC 2334 · Sujata V. Manohar, K. Venkataswami & R.C. Lahoti JJ. · appeals from Delhi HC judgment 31.8.1995 (CWP 4408 & 4703/1993)
Facts (exactly as reproduced in the casebook) Cinema-hall licensees were obliged, for decades, by (i) S.12(4) Cinematograph Act, 1952 (Central Govt directions to licensees “so that scientific, educational, news & current-affairs, documentary or indigenous films secure an adequate opportunity of being exhibited” — deemed additional licence conditions), (ii) Conditions 15 & 22 of the Delhi Cinematograph Rules, 1981 (compulsory exhibition of one or more “approved films” — max 600 metres/2000 ft — at each performance), and (iii) the parallel West Bengal Cinemas (Regulation) Act, 1954 S.5(3) + Notification 7277-F dt 20.9.1957 and the U.P. Cinemas (Regulation) Act, 1955 — the ratio of such short films to the main feature was fixed at 1:5, with half the allotted time reserved for films approved by the Central Govt on the recommendation of the Film Advisory Board, Bombay. Since the state Films Division was practically the only supplier, exhibitors had to execute agreements paying it 1% of net weekly collection as rental. In 1993 the exhibitors’ associations (Delhi–U.P. Circuit; Eastern India Motion Picture Association) challenged these “impugned provisions” under Arts. 19(1)(a) & 19(1)(g). The Delhi High Court upheld the statutes but struck down the rental-condition as unconstitutional and Condition 15 as redundant/onerous, directing free supply at the exhibitor’s place. Union & States appealed.
Issues (1) Does compulsory exhibition of “approved” short films curtail 19(1)(a) of the exhibitor (compelled speech) or 19(1)(g)? (2) Is the requirement saved because it “further” speech? (3) Are the rules/conditions (Film Division monopoly, “no-objection” for licence renewal, rental) valid? (4) Is judicial review available against administrative directions issued under S.12(4)/S.5(3) (the “not maintainable” limb — the writ vs appeal debate in the extract)?
Arguments Exhibitors: being forced to carry state-supplied content is “compelled speech”; in US doctrine compelled speech must be content-neutral, while here the content categories and the “approval” gatekeeping are prescribed by the State; Films Division is the sole source; the NOC-for-renewal fetters trade; the rental is arbitrary. Union/States: the condition is a licence-condition regulation of a unique mass medium reaching the literate and illiterate alike; it allocates a mere 1/5 of show-time to educational/scientific/documentary/news film — it promotes dissemination of ideas for an informed citizenry; nothing compels propaganda; approval exists to ensure statutory fit, not ideology; the exhibitors themselves never objected to content.
Decision The Supreme Court upheld the statutory scheme — “clearly, the impugned provisions are designed to further free speech and expression and not to curtail it. None of these statutory provisions require the exhibitor to show a propaganda film or a film conveying views which he objects to.” It held that under Art. 19(1)(a) what matters is whether the categories mandated “promote dissemination of information and education or… propaganda or false or biased information”; the exhibitor’s practical dependence on the Films Division was “because of a lack of adequate alternative sources”, not statutory compulsion, and the NOC mechanism was merely a compliance device. Hence “these provisions do not violate Article 19(1)(a)… they are not in restraint of free speech and expression. Therefore, Article 19(2) is not attracted” — an unusual and exam-worthy holding: a regulation found to be speech-promoting never reaches the 19(2) defence stage. (The Court then examined the Delhi Rules’ Conditions 15/22 — the redundancy/onerousness points — leaving the scheme’s core intact; read pp. 270–277 of the casebook for the disposition on the conditions.)
Principle (i) “Must-carry” obligations on media can be speech-promoting regulation (later echoed in the Sports Broadcasting Signals Act, 2007 and DD Free Dish carriage); (ii) the literate-and-illiterate / mass-medium rationale — the Court’s justification for treating cinema (and by extension television) as an organ requiring affirmative content duties; (iii) a law that facilitates the free flow of information lies inside 19(1)(a) and needs no 19(2) justification.
In re: Destruction of Public & Private Properties v. State of A.P. — the “media guidelines on violence” case casebook #21 (pp. 192–196)
(2009) 5 SCC 119 · Arijit Pasayat J (for himself, Lokeshwar Singh Panta & P. Sathasivam JJ.)
Facts (per casebook text) SC suo motu (5.6.2007) against large-scale destruction of property during agitations; two committees — Justice K.T. Thomas (criminal-process fixes) & Fali S. Nariman (media, “responsible reporting”) — the latter included the Editors-in-Chief of Indian Express, Times of India, Dainik Jagaran, NDTV’s Pranoy Roy + govt secretaries. Issues How may violence-prone coverage (glorification of rioters, “giving a platform to hooligans”, live telecast of arson) be regulated without censorship? Can self-regulation be mandated by the Court?

Part 4Social Media: the IT-Act Architecture and the Shreya Singhal Revolution

The syllabus’ newest “form of media”. DU reads social media not through a media statute of its own but through the Information Technology Act, 2000 (as amended in 2008), the subordinate Intermediary & Digital-Media Rules (2011 → 2021 → amendments to 2026) and one epoch-making judgment — Shreya Singhal v. UoI — which occupies 45 pages of the casebook (pp. 40–85), its single longest excerpt, alongside X v. UoI (pp. 354–391).

4.1 What “social media” legally is — and why the State regulates the platform, not the press-card

Distinguishing features of the form

  • Many-to-many & interactive. Press is one-to-many and gate-kept; broadcast is one-to-many, licensed and spectrumscarce. On social media every user is simultaneously publisher and audience — there is no editor to hold to a code, so Indian law attaches its duties to the intermediary that hosts and transmits the speech.
  • Algorithmic curation. Reach is decided by platform ranking systems, not editors — virality, filter-bubbles and coordinated manipulation. Hence the 2020s pivot from “content regulation” to “platform accountability” (due-diligence obligations, transparency reports, grievance officers).
  • Instantaneous, borderless, permanent. The Supreme Court’s own image: “the effect of the speech is immediate as the velocity of the electronic media is lightning” (Shreya, ¶98); “every person who sees it … has the capacity to reproduce it”.
  • Convergence. OTT shows, YouTube channels, digital news sites and meme pages all sit on the same infrastructure — which is why one instrument, the 2021 Rules, regulates “intermediaries” (Part I–II) and “publishers of online curated content” (Part III) together.
Exam formula: “social media is media without a masthead” — the State, unable to license the speaker, regulates the host; the whole legislative history from 2008 to 2026 is a calibration of that delegation between courts, executives and platforms.

What Part III did and did not do (a ready 10-marker)

  • Did: ended the claim that “internet ≠ media law”; brought OTT & digital news within soft regulation while retaining “no pre-censorship”; fixed a 24-hour State response against grievance-free platforms; made the PCI ethics code applicable to digital news; created a statutory age-rating regime for screens the parent could not supervise.
  • Did not: create a licensing regime (no “OTT permission”); give any fine/ban power to the SRB; bring UGC on platforms (YouTube creator videos, Instagram reels) into the ethics code (they remain “intermediary content”, s. 79 territory); displace the Cinematograph Act for films released in theatres (the “film vs programme” border remains contested — e.g., a film that skips cinema and “premieres” on an OTT is not certified by the CBFC); and — crucially — the 2020 advisory→2021 rule chain has no primary statute specific to digital media: its statutory parent is only s. 87 IT Act read with ss. 69A/79 — hence the Broadcasting Services (Regulation) Bill, 2023 (Part 3 above) seeks to complete the architecture; still not enacted in 2026.
The ITB flap, told correctly: the Rules as notified on 25.2.2021 contained Rule 8(2) empowering an Inter-Ministerial Board (Secretary-level) to order blocking of content violating Part III. Against a furore summed up in the headline “the government becomes the censor of YouTube and Netflix”, the amendment of 16.3.2021 deleted the ITB within three weeks; blocking of digital-media content is now routed only through the Inter-Departmental Committee’s reference to MeitY under s. 69A — i.e., back into the procedural track Shreya had blessed. The episode is the cleanest illustration of the balance: executive over content without s. 69A procedure = politically impossible.

4.6 The casebook’s two deep-dives

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India casebook pp. 40–85 (¶¶1–125)
(2015) 5 SCC 1 · AIR 2015 SC 1470 · S.A. Bobde & L. Nageswara Rao JJ · decided 24.3.2015
Facts Criminal writ petitions filed in 2012 (first petitioner a 19-year-old law student) challenged s. 66A IT Act after two Mumbai women were arrested on 26.11.2012 for a Facebook post/comment about the Bal-Thackeray-death bandh (one had merely “liked” the post), and after a string of absurd prosecutions followed — a 75-year-old retired professor arrested for a WhatsApp forward, Aseem Trivedi for anti-corruption cartoons— the Court itself illustrates the reach (¶¶74–76): even “an annoying guest in a hotel” could be prosecuted — contrast the tightly-drawn IPC analogues (s. 505 etc., which punish only public, defined harms: “annoyance” alone is criminal only where the Penal Code narrowly defines it). The Bombay High Court had already granted interim protection; the Supreme Court (27.9.2012) issued directions against mechanical s. 66A arrests (FIRs to be vetted through DGP/Commissioner) and (28.3.2013) a second, stricter order; yet the provision survived as the internet’s “go-to” weapon. Several petitioners were journalists/activists; the Union’s only real defence was “the section mirrors the old s. 57 Telegraph Act”. The Court heard the petitions through 2014–15, leaning on the statute’s own vocabulary, the IPC’s narrow “parent” analogues and foreign overbreadth doctrine, and on 24.3.2015 decided s. 66A, s. 69A and s. 79 in one judgment.
Issues (the Court’s framing, ¶24) (1) Whether s. 66A violates Art. 19(1)(a) as being outside every head of 19(2), and void for vagueness; (2) whether the doctrine of over-breadth/chilling effect — borrowed from US, German and Canadian law — is part of Indian Art. 19 review; (3) whether s. 69A and the Blocking Rules, 2009 provide “adequate safeguards” so as not to amount to unconstrained prior censorship; (4) whether s. 79’s immunity, conditioned on “due diligence” and “actual knowledge”, impermissibly deputises platforms as censors.
Arguments Petitioners: s. 66A criminalises mere discussion or even advocacy, “howsoever unpopular”, because “grossly offensive… annoyance… ill-will” are subjective and undefined (¶76: “what may be offensive to one may not be offensive to another… there is no demarcating line”); the offences of the IPC (e.g. 505’s “public place”, 500’s defamatory presumption) have “narrowly and closely defined contours… conspicuous by their absence” in 66A; a “chilling effect” on millions suffices (the Court adopts Rangarajan’s “proximate link”: “if the restricted expression has no proximate relationship” with 19(2) heads, it is invalid); s. 69A without prior judicial oversight is executive censorship. Union: s. 66A is merely the “electronic-era” version of s. 57 Telegraph Act (a “pre-Constitution” text, so its presumptive constitutionality continues — the Court rejects: the 2000 Act was a fresh enactment); “grossly offensive” is intelligible to any court applying the IPC’s s. 292 obscenity learning; severability — read the vague words out and keep the section intact; 69A’s Committee + 6-month review = “safeguards galore”; intermediaries can easily act on “actual knowledge” of complaints because “obvious illegality needs no court”.
Decision (per Nariman J) (1) S. 66A struck down as unconstitutional in its entirety. The mere “discussion or advocacy… even of an unpopular point of view” is protected unless the speech has a proximate tendency to incite an offence or falls within a 19(2) head; “grossly offensive… causing annoyance” reaches innocent conduct and “chills” free speech — the Court invokes Rangarajan: “the restricted expression must have a proximate relationship, and not a remote or far-fetched relationship with public order”. Vagueness (no defined line) = want of “procedure established by law” and an offence against Art. 14’s ban on arbitrariness. (2) The Court also refused to invoke the 13th-paragraph “special-grounds” test of V.G. Row “as an abstract, academic standard” — the medium-neutral rule: “the same standard of speech cannot be applied to different media”, but internet speech is not a separate class justifying graver restrictions (¶34). (3) S. 69A + 2009 Rules UPHELD — “sufficient safeguards are incorporated”: prior Committee scrutiny, reasoned orders, notice + hearing (Rule 7), 6-month review, and “no prior restraint since blocking is subsequent to publication”. (4) S. 79 UPHELD, r. 3(2) 2011 READ DOWN: “actual knowledge” means knowledge from a court order or a Government notification — intermediaries “cannot be expected to adjudicate” (¶117; a private complaint, however strong, is not “actual knowledge”); “expeditiously remove… within 36 hours” runs only from such order — — preserving both the safe harbour and the free flow of speech, and relegating takedowns to (i) State/court orders and (ii) voluntary platform moderation. (5) Ancillaries: the e-evidence provisions (ss. 85A/85B) were not disturbed; the Act’s long arm noted — “it applies to an offence or contravention committed outside India by any person if the act involves a computer or computer network located in India” (s. 1(4)); and the Court directed that all pending s. 66A prosecutions fail with the section’s death. the Court concluded with a “caution to law-enforcers”: prosecution under a dead law is itself unconstitutional.
Principle evolved (i) India’s doctrine of over-breadth + chilling effect is constitutional (¶95–98: “a statute may be facially invalid… because it discourages the exercise of 19(1)(a) by persons who fear prosecution” — the rule that “the State may not, by indirection, regulate more than it may directly” — drawn from American overbreadth cases, expressly not adopted wholesale (¶79: foreign tests “cannot be transplanted” into Art. 19(2)’s closed list)). (ii) “Proximate tendency” test restated for online speech (following Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram, (1989) 2 SCC 574: “the community’s reaction… is to be judged from the point of view of a reasonable strong-minded member of the community, not a hypersensitive… one… the State’s duty is to maintain order, not to bow to mob reaction — the remedy for inflammatory speech is more speech, not censorship”). (iii) Blocking is permissible as “subsequent restraint” with due procedure; prior restraint of internet content is not. (iv) “Actual knowledge” doctrine — the keystone of Indian intermediary law (private notice-and-takedown only voluntarily; legally forced only by order). (v) Methodological win: courts will read down rules made under ss. 69A/79/87 to save them, giving the executive an incentive to legislate carefully.
X v. Union of India (child sexual-abuse material; intermediary duties) casebook pp. 354–391
W.P.(C) 584/2020 & connected batch, Delhi High Court (Manmohan & Prateek Jalan JJ) · two landmark rulings: 9.11.2021 & 22.3.2023
Facts A 14-year-old school-girl was sexually abused by a family friend, filmed without consent, and blackmailed with the video; the mother’s police complaint (casebook ¶1–4) led to the arrest of two accused (2019–20) but no takedown: the investigating officer’s emails to Google received the reply — “the complainant should approach the appropriate Government or obtain a court order” — because Google read Shreya’s “actual knowledge” to require a court/government order, and the then-in-force 2011 Rules (36 hrs on actual knowledge) gave no self-executing police-complaint duty. Parallel miseries multiplied; a co-petition involved another minor (porn sites + morphing), and a third involved a “deepfake”-style morphed clip of a woman. After the 2021 Rules inserted r. 3(2)(b) (“rape or sexually-explicit content → remove within 24 hrs upon the complaint of a victim or relative”) and r. 3(2)(c) (POCSO content), the Court used this case to build the whole institutional machinery: it examined the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (www.cybercrime.gov.in — “originally for reporting pornography/child pornography or gang-rape content… now widened to all cyber crimes” — MeitY’s own submission, ¶65), Google’s two-step removal (¶62: remove from hosting sites first, then “organic removal from search engines… upon holding the content unlawful, a court or the appropriate government agency may share specific URLs for de-listing/cache-removal”), MeitY’s suggestions (¶65: direct intermediaries OR the appropriate Government; grant the petitioner a right to approach platforms directly by a dedicated e-mail), Facebook’s defences (¶72–73: privacy settings, “Profile Picture Guard”, “willing to cooperate pursuant to a court order”), and the intermediaries’ genuine difficulty (¶88: “identifying offending content appearing in various disguises and corrupted avatars” is “onerous and impractical”).
Issues (Court’s own framing, pp. 385–391) (1) Can a court order removal of content hosted abroad against foreign intermediaries? (2) Can a court order global de-indexing of search-engine results, and would it be “prior restraint”? (3) Can a court order an intermediary to automate identification of content (hash-matching) to defeat re-uploads “in different disguises and corrupted avatars”? (4) What role for the NCRP portal as the single grievance window? (5) Can a complainant report anonymously for NCII? (6) Whether the right to be forgotten (Puttaswamy ¶417, which referred the right to be forgotten for separate consideration; in May 2022 the Union constituted a committee under retired Justice B.N. Srikrishna to frame it) applies to a minor victim — “perpetual forgetting”? (7) Whether the 2021 Rules themselves — the 24-hour removal duty (r. 3(2)(b)), the SSMI duties (r. 4) and the digital-media architecture (r. 5, Part III) — are a complete statutory framework that courts may simply direct to be implemented, without new legislation.
Arguments Petitioners (the victim through her mother, an NGO; NCPCR; supported by an amicus curiae whose “recommendation for direct action by courts” MeitY opposed): POCSO ss. 14, 15 (punishing storage/use of child-porn), s. 23 (no disclosure of the victim’s identity) make CAM “manifestly unlawful” per se — no balancing needed; intermediaries cannot shelter behind Shreya when the 2021 Rules create a complaint-triggered 24-hour duty; “global takedown” follows from Google Inc. v. Equustek Solutions Inc., 2017 SCC OnLine Can SC 33 (Canada — a local court may enjoin the world-wide operation of a search engine that “controls” results); the victim’s right to be forgotten (Puttaswamy) and Art. 21 of a minor demand perpetual erasure. Google: geo-fencing only within India respects comity and its own statute (s. 79 + safe harbour; India law cannot command the world); automated “deduplication” turns platforms into censors with “false-positives”, mass surveillance, encryption costs; only 1%? (compliance data: the casebook notes Google’s India takedown compliance statistics) — “we already act on 97?% of court-ordered requests”; prior restraint/“chilling of the internet”. Facebook/Instagram: robust privacy policy, “ready and willing to cooperate… pursuant to any court order” — i.e. not resisting, but demanding the order-first architecture of s. 79(3)(b). MeitY: the legislature intended law-enforcement-driven removal (portal + police + 24-hr rule), “not direct action by courts” — the executive’s own institutional-choice argument.
Decision The Court (9.11.2021 + final directions 22.3.2023) — (a) global de-indexing ordered: for the identified child-abuse/morphed content, “a search engine must block the search results throughout the world… to prevent irreparable harm”, adopting Equustek: the order “does not require the search engine to take any steps around the world, it requires it to take steps only where its Search Engine is controlled” (¶89 — Google, Yahoo, Bing and DuckDuckGo directed (¶98): “they must do more than merely respond to requests; they cannot be bystanders” — the exact words of the Court as reported); (b) removal at source first, de-listing second (the ¶62 two-step endorsed as the mandatory workflow — “once content is removed from the actual websites, the same will be organically removed”; for search engines, the court/“appropriate government agency” shares specific URLs for de-listing/cache-removal); (c) automated identification (hash-matching) mandated — intermediaries must “deploy” the technology they already possess (CSAM hash databases) to locate “same/duplicate/modified” copies (¶100) — an “automated deduplication” order; (d) the NCRP one-stop flow institutionalised: complainant → www.cybercrime.gov.in (or dedicated e-mail for NCII — anonymous complaints permitted, r. 3(2)(b) as interpreted) → FIR under ss. 66E, 67, 67B IT Act r.w. POCSO ss. 14–15, 21 (casebook ¶¶17–20) → nodal officers designated by each intermediary to receive requests “expeditiously” (¶93) → 24-hr removal + 180-day preservation → periodic compliance reports to the Court; (e) right to be forgotten for the minor — the Court accepted “perpetual and permanent erasure” (¶102), — anticipating the questions before the Srikrishna committee (May 2022); (f) continuing mandamus — the Court retained the matter with periodic compliance affidavits from the intermediaries and the police; (g) Delhi Police ordered to (i) register FIR within 24 hrs of portal complaint and (ii) the Court held out as a model for all States the Delhi Police’s dedicated “C-Well” cell for children’s and women’s online safety (launched Oct 2020). In the process the Court declined to strike the Rules but read s. 79(3)(b) + r. 3(2)(b) together so that for child-sex/NCII content a police complaint is sufficient “actual knowledge” — because the intermediary can “act on a complaint which is ‘self-evidently’ unlawful”, a subtle extension of Shreya, and the beginning of “notice-and-takedown without courts” in the child-safety domain. (h) s. 85A: the Court noted the e-evidence rules as the mechanism to obtain foreign-site cooperation.
Principle (i) “Manifestly unlawful content” exception to the order-first rule — where material is criminal per se (CSAM, NCII/deepfake of a minor), the intermediary’s duty is triggered by a police/victim complaint, not a judicial order; (ii) courts may order global de-indexing against search engines “where they are controlled” (Equustek route) — India’s first “global takedown”; (iii) hash-matching/deduplication as a judicially-enforceable duty — the beginning of “platform-as-police”; (iv) the NCRP portal became a legally recognised “single window”; (v) a child victim’s “right to be forgotten” is perpetual; (vi) continuing mandamus as the remedy for structural platform failure. Current impact: this template (24→2-hr clocks, anonymous reporting, automated filtering) was legislated into the 2025/2026 amendments — X is the prototype of the new deepfake regime; also cite for “dynamic/modifying injunctions” (the Court’s “approach to new URLs” route mirrors Naveen Jindal v. Zee, para 9710-casebook, dynamic injunctions against “currently live and future URLs”).

Two adjacent decisions worth a line each

  • Naveen Jindal v. Zee Media & Ors. (Piracy Ltd. matter), Delhi HC — “dynamic injunction” (casebook § “Dynamic Injunction”, para 9710 area): an anti-piracy injunction may operate not only against named URLs but “all currently live and future identical/re-identified URLs (including mirror/redirect/alter-ego sites)”, the defendant to be heard; approved as an answer to the “whack-a-mole” of Telegram/pirate-stream re-hosting during live sports — The principle the courts evolve: judicial blocking of social media is the new prior restraint — with a judge, not JS-IT, signing the order.
  • WhatsApp First-Traceability challenges (Del HC, Meta v. UoI — 2023 batch): Meta/WhatsApp contended r. 4(5) (traceability) breaks end-to-end encryption contrary to s. 79 and Art. 21, “mass surveillance by design”, and that the rule was never validly brought into force (the “technique/mechanism” never notified). The executive has never notified the compliance date, so r. 4(5) remains a “dead letter with a loaded gun”. Current frontier: the Supreme Court’s 2026 “digital-arrest” hearings (29.7.2026; ~₹3,000 crore defrauded in six years, per counsel) pressed for a dedicated criminal law on deepfake fraud plus origin-tracing — cite it as the unresolved frontier.

4.7 Real examples — every one reportable

  • Shaheen Dhada & Renu Kasbekar (26–27.11.2012): a 21-year-old (Shaheen, then studying dentistry) posted on Facebook a comment questioning Mumbai’s shutdown on Bal Thackeray’s death; her friend Renu “liked” it; both were arrested under s. 66A r.w. s. 505 IPC, released on probation, and the Bombay High Court stayed the prosecutions — the single incident that produced the Shreya petitions and, three years later, the death of the section.
  • Aseem Trivedi (Sept 2012, Mumbai): cartoonist arrested for an anti-corruption cartoon series (“corrupt politician” images, “Make some noise” campaign) under s. 66A — became a symbol of the provision’s absurdity.
  • Farmer-protest Twitter blocks (Feb 2021): MeitY ordered Twitter to block 249 accounts “spreading misinformation about the farm laws / the Rep. Day tractor rally”; Twitter first publicly tagged the orders (the “red flags” episode) then complied; the Committee reviewed and lifted some blocks; journalists’ accounts (journalists’ and farmer-leaders’ handles were among them) — the casebook treats the “display-the-order” practice as an accountability win that itself triggered the 2021 Rules’ anti-Twitter clauses and, in 2023, the Supreme Court’s public-orders direction.
  • April 2020 TV5Monde emergency block: a French website’s article (about a petition naming ministers in the Delhi riots) blocked under Rule 9 “urgency”; after representations by media-rights bodies, the Committee lifted the order within days — cited in Parliament as “the blocking power misused”. Use it as the model “s. 69A without hearing → review → reversal” story.
  • Delhi riots 2020 “hate-speech takedown” wave: MeitY notices to Twitter/YouTube/Facebook for hate content (Aug–Dec 2020; the platforms’ compliance reports show thousands of removals) — and the Supreme Court’s continuing refusal (29.4.2026, Nath & Mehta JJ) to frame online hate-speech guidelines: “the field is not unoccupied” — s. 69A + BNSS s. 175(3) + Law Commission 267th Report (2017) already cover it.
  • TikTok package (29.6.2020; 2.9.2020; 14.2.2022 = 244? apps): the largest ever s. 69A exercise — grounds “sovereignty & integrity, defence, security” (data-theft/China angle) — an example of blocking an entire platform, not content; Parliament’s standing committee later urged an “app-governance” framework; PUBG Mobile was among the 2022 set (rebranded “ Battlegrounds Mobile India” in 2023 as an India-compliant relaunch).
  • Koo (April 2024 shutdown): the Indian micro-blogging “Twitter-rival” closed, publicly citing the “increasing compliance requirements… of the IT Act/intermediary guidelines — a live demonstration of regulatory-cost effects on start-ups (the ‘Brussels effect’ in miniature).
  • Deepfakes go mainstream: the “Bengaluru IIT-topper” morphed-images case (July 2024) — a WhatsApp group circulating morphed photos of women students; arrests under 66E/67 + BNS — the trigger for the Feb-2025 3-hour amendment; the ECI 2024 advisory (deepfake campaign material to be removed in 6 hrs and to carry AI-labelling undertakings); the 2025 “Actors-vs-OTT deepfake” industry memoranda (the Indian screen-actors’ association) → SGI labelling 2026. Keep to these four — each is nationally reported.
  • “India’s Got Latent” (Dec 2024): an AI-engineer-turned-comedian’s OTT stand-up special’s “jokes” about mothers & a community: FIRs across States; MIB sought “self-regulatory action”; the video was withdrawn from one platform amid FIRs and advertiser walkaways; the controversy produced: (i) parliamentary debate on Part III’s scope (“UGC vs curated content”), (ii) the decisive jurisdictional point — the special was user content on YouTube (an intermediary), not “online curated content” under Part III, so MIB had no direct Part III jurisdiction; action travelled via FIRs, advertiser pressure and s. 69A machinery; (iii) PILs in the Supreme Court (2025) seeking content guidelines — declined on 29.4.2026: “the field is not unoccupied” (s. 69A, BNSS s. 175(3), Law Commission 267th Report, 2017). A perfect illustration of “regulation-by-pressure” vs “regulation-by-law”.
  • Manoj–Babli (2007) & the “trial by media” debate: the live television programme on which the family of an honour-killing couple accepted responsibility on air remains the classic exhibit of “media as parallel court”; the Law Commission’s 200th Report (2006) (on “Trial by Media — Free Speech and Fair Trial under the Criminal Procedure Code”, uploaded in the DU reader) recommended amending the Contempt of Courts Act to cover publications after arrest, postponing trials on request, and a statutory “media standard” — none enacted. The Supreme Court, declining relief in the 2020 Rhea Chakraborty habeas-corpus hearing, described “trial by media” as “a serious contempt of the court” yet left the accused to the criminal process; and the Delhi High Court’s 2007 sting-operation guidelines (casebook extract) remain the only positive law on investigative journalism — with the 2008 Law Commission consultation paper (also uploaded) showing how narrowly such operations are to be policed.

Three models of social-media control
1 · Pure self-regulationPlatform T&Cs + user complaint only (pre-2011 world; still the norm for non-SSMIs in “notice-and-action” grey zone). Critique: private censorship without due process, opaque algorithms. India’s nod: 2021 R. 3(1)(c) gives users a 3-month takedown right — self-regulation with a statutory complaint channel.
2 · Co-regulation — India’s choiceState writes due-diligence + clocks; intermediaries execute; independent officers/SRBs (Part III), Committee (Part II) and courts supervise; safe harbour rewards compliance, penalises passivity. Shreya’s order-first rule is the counter-weight — and each amendment since has chipped it.
3 · Statutory licensing / censorship — rejectedThe 2018 draft IT Amendment (licensing-like aggregator duties), the 2021 ITB (deleted in days), the Broadcasting Bill 2023 (still not law), UK-style Online Safety Act (India has no “duty of care” Act). The Supreme Court has declined (last on 29.4.2026) to invent online hate-speech guidelines. Syllabus 1.2. This part follows the DU notes’ chapter “History of Media Laws in India” (Prof. Adukia §3, pp. 7–14) — every dated fact below is either from those notes, from the casebook (the Shreya extract’s account of the colonial statutes; the printed Sakal, Bennett Coleman, N. 5.1 The organizing thesis (open and close every answer with this)

Colonial-era media law (1766–1947) was law made by the executive against the press. Its verbs were license, demand security, forfeit, seize, deport, prohibit import, intercept, censor. There was no right to criticise, so there were no “restrictions needing justification” — regulation meant permission.

Post-Constitution “legislative efforts” (1950– ) are law regulating media in the name of enumerated public interests. Its verbs are register, classify, moderate, adjudicate. Every restriction must ride a head of Art. 19(2) and survive judicial review — Romesh Thappar, Sakal, Bennett Coleman, Shreya Singhal.

Yet the forms persist. Registration (the PRB Act 1867 survives as the skeleton of the print industry); forfeiture of presses → executive blocking of “access”; securities for good behaviour → due-diligence compliance under pain of losing safe harbour; pre-censorship survives for exactly one medium (cinema, through the 1918 Act’s descendant, the 1952 Act); “annoyance” as a crime reappears in s. 57 of the Telegraph Act (1885) → s. 66A IT Act (2000). Read the whole history as: the State keeps inventing new carriers for the same old cargo; the courts keep demanding a destination clause.

Evidence of rupture (the “break”)

  • Constitution reverses the pyramid: press freedom is implicit in Art. 19(1)(a) though unenumerated (Sakal; Beg J.’s “Ark of the Covenant” in Bennett Coleman).
  • The repeal record is long: VPA repealed by the coloniser himself (1881); 1908 & 1910 Acts repealed on the Sapru Committee’s advice (1922); Press (Emergency Powers) Act 1931 → Press (Objectionable Matter) Act 1951 “allowed to lapse in February 1956… repealed in 1957” (notes); Prices & Pages Act repealed 2010; Censorship on Doordarshan Act 1990 repealed 1991; s. 66A struck 2015.
  • New statutes for media rather than over it: WJA 1955 (journalists’ service conditions), Delivery of Books Act 1954, PCI Acts 1965/1978 (ethics body, no penal powers), Prasar Bharti Act 1990 (arm’s-length broadcaster), DPDP Act 2023 (rights against media companies themselves).
  • Self- & co-regulation became the default: Cable Act 1995 (codes enforced by a prohibition order, appeal to courts), NBSA, Part III of the 2021 Rules for OTT — the State retreated from the censor’s chair into procedural supervision.

Evidence of continuity (the “coloniality” critique)

  • PRB Act 1867 — “the earliest surviving enactment specifically directed towards the press” (notes’ phrase) still governs every daily; the State knows your press before it knows your vote.
  • Forfeiture DNA: CrPC ss. 99A–99G (1930s) → CrPC 1973 ss. 95/96 → BNSS ss. 98/99 (Part 6). “Seize the instrument” remains the first move in every “objectionable content” crisis.
  • Bond logic: VPA 1878 security → Press Act 1910 → 1930 Ordinance (“130 newspapers had to deposit securities, nine refused and suspended publication” — notes) → today: “comply or forfeit immunity”. The sanction is still economic, not criminal; and it still needs no conviction.
  • Prior restraint: Censorship of Films Act 1918 → Cinematograph Act 1952 → K.A. Abbas (1971) upheld it as the one “exceptional” medium; s. 69A “blocking” is its procedural opposite — but both are executive decisions about what may be seen.
  • Emergency reflex: 1857 licensing → Defence of India Rules 1939–45 → 1962 → 1975–77 (censors in newsrooms; Indian Express’s blank columns) → 2019–20 Kashmir suspensions (Anuradha Bhasin, 10.1.2020: suspension must be reviewed, proportionate, reasoned) → 2020–21 mass blocking. Law returns; the reflex stays.
  • Sedition line: s. 124A inserted 1870; used on Tilak (1908) and Gandhi (1922); saved & narrowed in Kedar Nath (1962); repealed in 2023 — BNS s. 152 re-casts the same protection in “sovereignty, unity and integrity” language; 153A (1932) returns as BNS 196.

5.2 The master timeline (bold = memorize)

c. 273 BC – Aurangzeb
Antiquity: communication without media law
The DU notes begin with the Rock Edicts of Ashoka — “in abundance measures adopted and regulations issued… not very different from the news content of modern media” — and, crucially, “no reference to restrictions on communication imposed by law.” The Arthashastra (Chandragupta’s court, per Kautilya) punishes false rumours and describes “spies and reporters” — content offences, no institutional control. Mughal akhbar (Babur mentions it “as if routine”; the court waqia-style manuscript papers of Aurangzeb, 8×4½ inches, collected by Col. James Tod for the Royal Asiatic Society) were “considerably free… perhaps there was neither pre-censorship nor licensing, both being Western institutions.” That sentence is the notes’ thesis in one line.
1556 · 1766 · 1780
The press arrives; the first punishment
6 September 1556 — the King of Portugal dispatches a printing press + technicians via the Cape for the Emperor of Abyssinia; the patriarch halts at Goa (1562), and “the press intended for Abyssinia never left Goa, instead, it printed literature for Abyssinia from Goa” (notes’ accident-of-history). 1766 — William Bolts, ex-Company factor, attempts the first newspaper in Calcutta; the Company deports him; his London Considerations on Indian Affairs (1773) became an indictment of Company rule. 1780 — James Augustus Hicky’s Bengal Gazette, “the first Indian newspaper,” attacks Warren Hastings and the judges; imprisoned, the paper suppressed within two years — the pattern (crash of prosecutions under judge-made “seditious libel”) set.
1799 → 1818
Pre-censorship — and its first gift back
1799 — Wellesley imposes full pre-censorship: every issue approved before printing. 1818 — abolished by Lord Hastings, the year the Serampore missionaries launched Samachar Darpan (23 May 1818), the first Indian-language journal (bilingual columns from 1829). The “freedom” is a grace, not a right: only Europeans ran papers; “expulsion of the editor (printer) was the ultimate penalty”, upheld by the Supreme Court of Judicature, since every foreigner’s residence “licence” depended on the officials’ displeasure. “The growing importance of the Fourth Estate in England” + missionary pressure explains the repeal (notes).
1823 · 1835
Licensing (Adam) → Metcalfe’s “liberty by disclosure”
1823 — John Adam’s Regulations introduce licensing in Bengal (a European institution, the notes stress) — licence to print, revocable at pleasure; the same year the Company ordered that “no servant of the company should have any connection with a newspaper,” after “an incident in Bombay where a member of the Council of the Governor owned a newspaper.” 1835 — Metcalfe’s Act (Sir Charles Metcalfe, Law Member — “the liberator of the Indian press”): licensing repealed; every printer/publisher must declare the place of printing and publication and print their names. Disclosure replaces permission — and that choice (register, don’t license) is why the PRB Act of 1867 exists at all.
1857 · 1860 · 1867 · 1870
The machinery is built
1857 — “Gagging Act”: Canning restores licensing for “all kinds of publications” in the year of the War of Independence (after which the Crown took over). 1860 — the IPC: “a general law but [which] laid down offences which any writer, editor or publisher must avoid” — defamation (499–500), obscenity (292–294). 1867 — Press and Registration of Books Act (25 of 1867): Part II declarations by press-keepers; Part III delivery of copies; Part IV penalties; Part V registration of books; Part VI rules + exemptions — “object… not to establish governmental control” (notes) but knowing who prints. 1870 — Act 27 of 1870 amends the IPC to insert sedition, s. 124A (“excites or attempts to excite feelings of disaffection to the Government established by law”) — “came handy to send many freedom fighters to jail.” (1875: officials barred from owning/editing periodicals without sanction; “no document… to be revealed to the press.”)
1876 – 1885
The “black” statutes
1876 — Dramatic Performances Act (19 of 1876): local governments may prohibit “public dramatic performances” suspected of provoking disaffection — the theatre-censorship statute still alive. 1878 — Vernacular Press Act (Lytton; pushed by Lt.-Gov. Sir Ashley Eden): “better control of the language press” — magistrate/CP may demand a bond, forbid classes of writing, forfeit the press and confiscate the matter, with “no recourse to any court of law”. Target: Amrita Bazar Patrika — whose owner turned the bilingual daily overnight into an English paper, outflanking an Act limited to “native newspapers”; Eden’s offer to “govern the province” with editors and Shishir Kumar’s reply (“there ought to be at least one honest journalist in the land”) are in the notes. Repealed 7 December 1881 (Ripon; Gladstone’s ministry) “without discussion.” 1878 — Sea Customs Act s. 19: prohibit import of objectionable goods (the “censor at the border”). 1885 — Indian Telegraph Act: “exclusive privilege” + licensing of telegraphs; s. 5 takeover of licensed establishments in “public emergency or for the safety of the State”; s. 5(2) interception; s. 3(1) definition so wide that it “later covered… teleprinter, telephone, fax, radio and television.” 1898 — Indian Post Office Act ss. 25–26: intercept postal articles carrying prohibited matter. The notes’ summing-up, quotable: “thus by the turn of the century the government had wide ranging powers to intercept anything anywhere along all the possible channels.”
1908 – 1932
Nationalism meets legalism
June 1908 — Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, passed after Tilak’s sedition trial (1908; six years’ rigorous imprisonment): local authorities could prosecute editors “inciting rebellion” — “nine prosecutions… seven presses were confiscated” (notes). 1910 — the Press Act (“empowered the government to demand security from any newspaper, a provision similar to what existed in the Vernacular Press Act” — notes; formally the Indian Newspapers (Circulation and Encouragement of Publishing Control) Act-type securities law of 1910). 1911 (UK Copyright) → 1914 Indian Copyright Act (replaced only in 1957). 1918 — the Censorship of Films Act + Indian Cinematograph Act, 1918: certify-before-exhibit is born for cinema (replaced by the Cinematograph Act, 1952). 1919 — Rowlatt Act (“Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act”): judge-only trials, no appeal for the press-sedition ecosystem. 1921 Sapru Press Law Committee → Press Law (Repeal and Amendment) Act, 1922: repeals 1908 & 1910 Acts; editor’s name on every copy & equal liability; registration only for majors; Part IV imprisonment cut “from two years to six months”; but “the committee advocated the retention” of seizure/confiscation of seditious literature + import & postal interception — liberal form, coercive core. 1922 — Indian States (Protection against Disaffection) Act: rejected by the Central Assembly, yet “certified” by the Governor-General under s. 67B of the GOI Act 1919 — 5 years’ jail for “hatred or contempt” of a Prince (Ordinance Raj’s ancestor). 1923 — Official Secrets Act (consolidating 1889 Act on the British 1911/1920 lines; s. 5 “wrongful communication”). 1930 Ordinance → 1931 Indian Press (Emergency Powers) Act (Civil Disobedience): revived the 1910 machinery — “some 130 newspapers had to deposit securities, nine refused to do so and suspended publications.” 1932 — Foreign Relations Act (publication of statements prejudicing friendly relations with foreign/“adjoining” states; forfeiture & postal interception extended) + the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1932 inserting ss. 153A & 153B IPC (promoting enmity; prejudicial assertions) and tightening s. 505 — the communal-hatred trilogy, now BNS 196–197 & 353.
1934 – 1951
Last imperial acts, first republican acts

March 1947 — Interim Government appoints the Press Laws Enquiry Committee; it reported 22 May 1948, after Independence and Partition; its “better control” logic produced the Press (Objectionable Matter) Act, 1951 replacing the 1931 Act — but “the mood was so much for freedom of the press that it was allowed to lapse in February 1956 and was repealed in 1957” (notes — the finest sentence in the chapter). Constitution (26.11.1949 / 26.1.1950): Art. 19(1)(a); the Supreme Court strikes down the Madras “security of State” ban on Cross Roads (Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras, AIR 1950 SC 124) and pre-censorship of Organiser/Brij Bhushan (1950) → Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951 — see 5.4. Sakal v. UoI (1951–52) tests the Prices Act in the same breath.
1952 – 1969 · nation-building statutes

1971 – 1986 · contempt, Emergency, cinema tightening

5.4 The First Amendment hinge

1950 — the SC clears the groundRomesh Thappar: a “public safety/ maintenance of public order” law could not ban a journal — public order ≠ security of the State, and 19(2) has no “public order” head yet. Brij Bhushan: pre-censorship of the RSS weekly struck. Sakal (decided 1951; 1952 AIR): price/page control = “curbing circulation of an organ of public opinion” — unreasonable without a covering head.
→
1951 — the State’s replyConstitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951: 19(2) gains “public order”, “friendly relations with foreign States”, “incitement to an offence”; restrictions must be “reasonable”; 19(6) rewritten to admit “reasonable restrictions in the interest of the general public” + State monopoly clauses; the Ninth Schedule (31A/31B) shields agrarian laws. Sedition was deliberately not added — the drafters kept the 1931-style “security” reading.
→
The trade-offMore grounds — but with two courts-imposed filters: proximity (speech must tend to the harm — Rangarajan later: “direct and proximate”; and the “reasonable man” standard) and reasonableness + procedure (from Maneka onward). Media law since 1951 is nothing but litigation over these two filters.
→
DoctrinePost-1951, a media restriction must (i) ride a 19(2) head, (ii) be substantive-and-procedural “reasonable”, (iii) respect the presumption against prior restraint (the Shreya extract canvasses the US “Pentagon Papers” line; India tolerates one — the film certificate). Learn this chain cold — both PYQs of 2015/2024 run through it.

5.5 Failed, lapsed and half-enacted efforts (the “efforts” that never became law)

  • Press (Objectionable Matter) Act 1951 — enacted, then starved: lapsed Feb 1956, repealed 1957. India’s first republic buried its own censorship statute.
  • The Prasar Bharti saga — the autonomy idea goes back to the 1970s; the Prasar Bharti Act passed 1990 and the government sat on it seven years (“came into existence 15.9.1997” — notes); Pitroda Committee (Jan 2014) still pleading for funding autonomy. The statute that exists is the “effort” that did not finish.
  • Censorship on Doordarshan Ordinance/Act 1990 → repealed 1991 — the formal pre-censorship claim over TV, abandoned with the government change.
  • The Broadcasting Bills: 1998 (freedom-from-monopoly era bill), the 2018–19 draft, and the Broadcasting Services (Regulation) Bill 2023 (introduced 10.11.2023; Standing Committee reported 14.3.2025; lapsed with the 17th Lok Sabha) — India still regulates TV content by Rules under a Cable Act for wires and a Telecom Act for spectrum; the 2026 draft Unified Broadcasting Rules continue that “rule-ocracy”.
  • Press Books (Periodicals) Registration Bill 2011 (repeal PRB + RNI; a “press registrar” for all media) — introduced, lapsed. Also the 2012 “Media Council” reform note (PCI → self-funded Media Council, Second Press Commission style) — a resolution, never a Bill.
  • Law Commission proposals never enacted: the 200th (2006) — amend Contempt s. 3 for post-arrest publications, statutory “trial postponement”, “media code” for crime; the 267th (2017) — re-write the hate-speech offence lines; the First & Second Press Commissions’ “press-related laws” consolidation. The SC refused to legislate them from the bench (29.4.2026).
  • Data & gaming, the two that finally landed: DPDP Bill 2019 → JPC 2021 → Act 11.8.2023 → Rules 30.9.2025 (phased); Online Gaming Bill (Nov 2023, referred to a Select Cttee, lapsed) → 2025 Act with a prohibition the 2023 draft never dared. “Legislative efforts” thus end mid-sentence in every medium — no code for platforms yet; no broadcasting Act; no media council.

Part 6The Criminal-Law Swap — IPC → BNS 2023 & CrPC → BNSS 2023 (every media-relevant section, verified)

For a media-law paper this is not trivia: “decency”, “public order”, “hatred” and “obscenity” — the operative words of every 19(2) fight — now live in new section numbers, several of them widened to mention electronic communication expressly.

Q1. How do the media laws established during colonial India compare with the contemporary media legislations in independent India, particularly in their approach to safeguarding or restricting freedom of speech and expression? Examine how the colonial legislations that limited press freedom served as a catalyst for mass awakening and social change. 20

Opening thesis

Colonial media law was a law of restriction without a right: the press was a suspect — licensed, bonded, taxed, forfeitable, and denied a court. Independent India’s media law is a law of regulation under a right: every restriction must be an enumerated “reasonable restriction” under Art. 19(2), written in a statute, reviewable by a court. The question’s second half is the irony: each silencing statute became a nationalist syllabus.

Body plan (six moves)

  1. The colonial toolbox (table, Part 5.3): Licensing Regulations 1799/1823 (licence before press; the Adam regulations required prior permission); Metcalfe’s Press Act 1835 (freedom with disclosure — the “liberal” interlude); Registration of Newspapers Act 1867 (registration, declaration, delivery of copies); sedition s. 124A IPC 1870; Vernacular Press Act 1878 (security from “oriental-language” papers, forfeiture, and “no recourse to any court”); Indian Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act 1908; Press Act 1910 (securities — the Adukia notes record that the 1930 Ordinance revived the 1910 machinery — “some 130 newspapers had to deposit securities, nine refused to do so and suspended publications” — before the Press (Emergency Powers) Act 1931 made it permanent); the 1932 hate-limbs (ss. 153A/153B IPC); the Telegraph Act 1885 (control of the wires — ancestor of the licence state); Official Secrets Act 1923; Indian Cinematograph Act 1918 (certification — the only legislative effort on the new media of its day).
  2. Mechanism compared: then — executive discretion, economic punishment (bond and forfeiture), targeting the medium, no judicial hearing; now — statutory grounds, adjudication, targeting conduct (content standards, takedown clocks), appealability. Concede the real continuities: registration (1867 survives as the Press and Registration of Books Act, amended 2006); licensing (telegraph wires → Cable TV Networks Act 1995 → Telecommunications Act 2023); pre-clearance of cinema (1918 → Cinematograph Act 1952, s. 5B added 1959).
  3. Law as catalyst — the examples: after the Vernacular Press Act, Amrita Bazar Patrika is said to have turned English overnight to escape its reach — the Act taught Indian newspapers their second language; the Press Act 1910 bred the “No Payment” defiance of 1918–19, the first organised all-India press protest against securities; Tilak’s 1908 sedition trial (six years for the “Maratha” writings) re-staged the courtroom as an indictment of the statute; Gandhi’s 1922 trial openly reframed the prosecution as “a national issue”, the sentence accepted as the price of opinion; the Punjab clampdown on reporting after Jallianwala (1919) radicalised even the loyalist press (the Modern Review campaign; Horniman’s expulsions from Bombay and Madras made him the empire’s best-advertised editor). The statute meant a muzzle and delivered a microphone.
  4. The constitutional turn: Arts. 19(1)(a)/19(2); the First Amendment, 1951 (public order, friendly relations, “reasonable”, the wider defamation clause) enacted directly against Romesh Thappar and the Crossroads ban (Part 5.4); the Indian Press (Objectionable Matters) Act 1951 — the Republic re-passing a 1910-style securities law in its own name — lapsed in 1956 after the First Press Commission; repeal of the colonial machinery it could not defend; Art. 19(1)(a) read to protect the press and its circulation (Sakal Papers, 1962; Bennett Coleman, 1972/73) — the courts, not the statutes, became the engine of press freedom.
  5. What independent law adds — statutes that confer, not merely control: Working Journalists Act 1955; Press Council Act 1978 (self-regulation by statute); the Cinematograph Act’s appellate tribunal (1959); the Cable Television Networks Regulation Act 1995 (a codified programme code instead of an offence); the IT Act 2000 (no licence for the internet — a deliberate non-effort); the RTI Act 2005; the Consumer Protection Act 2019; the DPDP Act 2023; the Telecommunications Act 2023 (Parts 2–4, 6).
  6. Honest continuity for the conclusion: the toolbox survives in re-coded form — securities → licence conditions and “due diligence”; forfeiture of the press → forfeiture of access (s. 69A blocking, the 2020 app bans); “no recourse to any court” → the ex parte emergency block (r. 9, 72 hours, Part 4); “subversive feelings” → BNS s. 152’s “subversive activities”. The difference is the reviewer: in 1908 the trial was the system; in 2015 Shreya Singhal made the system prove the restriction.

Close-ready sentence

“Colonial law punished the printer; democratic law regulates the platform — and it took 1951 to admit these are the same act, and 2015 to enforce that neither may be stopped on a hunch. The colonial statute was a wall; the modern statute is a gate. A gate still keeps a keeper — but keepers are now cross-examined.”

Q2. How do you perceive the increasing trend of hate speech in India and its key characteristics? Examine the various Supreme Court guidelines aimed at curbing hate speech. 20

Definition-by-cluster (open here to show the map)

India has no statute named “hate speech”; it has a cluster that performs the job — BNS s. 196 (promoting enmity/hatred between groups, ex-153A, “or through electronic communication” now written into the section; the place-of-worship limb heavier), s. 197 (imputations prejudicial to national integration, ex-153B — plus a new limb punishing the making or publication of false or misleading information tending to jeopardise the sovereignty, unity and integrity or security of India), s. 353 (statements conducing to public mischief, ex-505; 5-year aggravated limbs), s. 299 (deliberate outrage of religious feelings), s. 123(3A) RPA (appeals to enmity during elections — a corrupt practice), ss. 13–15 UAPA, forfeiture of publications (BNSS ss. 98–99) behind the sanction gate (BNSS s. 219). Online, the executive layer does most of the work: s. 69A IT Act blocking on public-order grounds + the 2021 Intermediary Rules (36-hour clock; 24-hour removal of sexual-imagery content on the victim’s complaint) and the Feb-2025/Feb-2026 amendments (3-hour notified-content clocks; 2-hour for NCII/impersonation; synthetic-content labelling — Part 4). The Law Commission (267th Report, 2017) drafted the real thing — a three-tier definition (incitement to hatred / discrimination / racial-or-religious hatred) with graded intent — and Parliament did not enact it; Shreya Singhal had earlier killed s. 66A, the general clause India did have.

Key characteristics of the trend

  1. Organised, not improvised: coordinated handle networks, forwarding chains, campaign-timed “hate factories” — the platform supplies the arithmetic of outrage.
  2. Leader-first: speech starts at rallies, primetime studios and verified handles and cascades to foot-soldiers; the loudest voices are the most connected, and the investigation starts with the quietest.
  3. Coded vocabulary: dog-whistles and historical defamation give deniability the courts must decode — exactly the drafting gap the 267th Report tried to close.
  4. Medium migration: the vernacular pamphlet (1878) → the studio debate → the 60-second vertical video. Each hop outran a machinery: PCI reaches print, the NBSA code reaches news channels, s. 69A reaches platforms — nothing reaches the private group chat but the police.
  5. Enforcement asymmetry: the recurrent judicial finding since 2022 — late or missing FIRs against connected speakers, swift ones against students and comedians. The courts’ stock answer: “the law exists; apply it.”

“Supreme Court guidelines” — the honest answer

The Court has consistently declined to write guidelines and has instead policed the executive’s duty — the six points to show it: (i) Rangarajan v. Jagjivan Ram (1989) (printed in the casebook): the proximity doctrine — restraint only where speech “is so linked with disorder as to constitute a spark in a closed theatre”, judged by “a man of ordinary sense and round constitution”, not the hypersensitive; (ii) the 2014 observations of a two-Judge Bench moved by election-season hate speeches — “hate speech is a hate crime”, the Union and the AG asked to react: the trigger for the 267th Report; (iii) Prafulla Samantara v. UoI (2021) — prayers for a hate-speech mechanism refused; the executive “must discharge its constitutional obligation”; (iv) Lalita Kumari (2013) — registration of the FIR is mandatory where the information discloses a listed offence, now written into BNSS s. 175 — the tool courts use instead of guidelines; (v) Shreya Singhal (2015) — the constitutionally mandatory replacement for any guideline: no vague speech-offences (¶¶57–60, 76); (vi) 29.4.2026 (Nath & Mehta, JJ) — refused fresh online-hate-speech guidelines: “the field is not unoccupied” (BNS 196/197/353; BNSS s. 175(3); s. 69A r.w. the 2021 Rules — the 267th Report noted, unimplemented). Beyond courts, the guidelines that bite: the ECI’s Model Code + s. 123(3A) petitions and the 2024 advisory requiring platforms to act on ECI-notified political deepfakes within six hours; the PCI Norms of Journalistic Conduct (2019, rev. 2022) — verification before amplification, no platform for communal speakers; the NBSA code for news channels.

Assessment & close

Critique: the 2023 re-codings widen the net (open-ended “any other ground whatsoever”; the fake-news clause) while the 2026 refusal narrows judicial supervision of it — the same Parliament that wrote s. 196/197/353 now argues their existence is why no guidelines are needed. Read s. 152’s pending validity challenge (notice 8.8.2025) beside it: the statutory net widened; the supervisory pen stayed sheathed. Close-ready: “India has no hate-speech vacuum; it has an enforcement vacuum in a crowded field. The Supreme Court’s ‘various guidelines’ are really one guideline, repeated since 2014: apply the law that exists, proportionately — or return and explain why you will not.”

Q4. Prasar Bharati is India’s public-service broadcaster, autonomous, comprising Doordarshan and All India Radio. Discuss its Composition, Powers and Functions under the Prasar Bharati (Broadcasting Corporation of India) Act, 1990. 20

Answer plan (full anatomy in Part 3)

  1. Why 1990: AIR and Doordarshan were government departments; the demand for a statutory public-service broadcaster runs through the press commissions of the 1960s; the first Prasar Bharati Bill was introduced in the Janata era (1978–79) and lapsed. The Act was passed in 1990 but not implemented until 15.9.1997 — the notes date this exactly; a seven-year gap that tells the history better than the long title.
  2. Composition (ss. 4, 6, 7–10): a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal; a whole-time Chairperson and a whole-time Member (Member-Secretary) appointed by the President on the recommendation of a three-member selection committee; the Directors-General of Doordarshan and Akashwani are ex-officio members; not more than six part-time members drawn from literature, science, art and culture, law, education, journalism and mass communication; two employee representatives (one each from AIR and DD). Office of profit disqualifies; removal lies with the appointing authority in the public interest.
  3. Broadcasting Council (s. 13): the statutory seat of programme standards — chaired by the Board’s Chairperson, members from public life, to be consulted on the Board’s “general direction and coordination” of broadcasting; the Act’s only organ whose subject is content rather than corporate administration.
  4. Functions (s. 12 + Schedule): the Schedule’s 18-point programme mandate is the payload — uphold “the ideals of the Constitution”, promote the integration and security of the nation, secularism and democratic culture; balanced coverage of news and current affairs; reflect the country’s cultural heritage; inform and educate; represent the interests of minorities, women, children, youth, the aged and persons with disabilities; and the two cardinal commands — the Corporation “shall not permit itself to be used for any party-political purpose” and shall not broadcast anything offensive to “decency and good taste”. The formula: “the BBC Charter’s duties, drafted into a statute.”
  5. Funding, audit, accountability: Central Government grants plus fees and borrowings (s. 17); accounts audited by the Comptroller and Auditor-General; annual report laid before Parliament — every accountability lever of a public-sector unit with none of a market broadcaster’s revenue autonomy.
  6. The critique half (worth half the marks): (a) “legislative effort” includes the effort to implement — seven lost years; (b) no statutory funding formula — grants and advertising are the two levers autonomy was designed to remove; (c) executive-dominated selection: the Pitroda Committee (2014) prescribed endowment funding, a sunset on advertising, professionalised governance and performance audit — accepted in spirit, enacted in none; (d) prime-time proximity to the government of the day; (e) the current frontier: the 2026 draft Unified Broadcasting Rules under the Telecommunications Act 2023 (30 minutes/day of public-interest programming on television, one hour/day on community FM — Part 5 timeline) will impose on private broadcasters what Prasar Bharati was created to exemplify.

Close: “Prasar Bharati is the legislative effort that India passed and then postponed: the statute exists (1990), the corporation exists (1997), the autonomy does not — which is exactly why the syllabus files it under the history of legislative efforts and not under the law of broadcasting.”

Q5. “The judiciary has been looked upon as the last hope for redress against administrative and governmental failure… therefore no one is allowed to erode public confidence.” Analyse the provisions of contempt of court with some leading judgements. 20

The Act as the spine (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971)

  • s. 2 — the two families: civil contempt (wilful disobedience of any judgment, order, writ or undertaking); criminal contempt s. 2(c) — three heads: (i) publication (words spoken or written, signs, visible representation “or otherwise”) which scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; (ii) which prejudices, or interferes with the due course of, any judicial proceeding; (iii) which interferes with or obstructs the administration of justice. “Publication” reaching television and the live stream engages all three.
  • The media’s charter (ss. 3–5): s. 3 — innocent publication and distribution (no contempt where the publisher neither knew nor had reasonable grounds to believe a proceeding was pending; distribution protected if publication was lawful when made); s. 4 — fair and accurate report of judicial proceedings (not for in-camera sittings, nor where publication is expressly prohibited); s. 5 — fair criticism of judicial acts: “publication of any criticism regarding the merits of any case decided by a court or regarding the conduct of a judicial officer with respect to the judicial proceedings” is not contempt — provided it carries “no imputation of the integrity or of any motive” of the judicial officer.
  • s. 12 punishment & apology: simple imprisonment up to 6 months or fine up to ₹2,000 or both; s. 12(2) — where an apology “is made or tendered in good faith and at the earliest opportunity”, the court may accept it; acceptance must not be traded for a retraction; refusal of an insincere apology is reviewable (the 2020 episode — the second apology “conditional” on the first was refused).
  • s. 13 defences: (a) truth — “when the court is satisfied that it is in public interest and that the request for invoking the said defence is made in good faith”, inserted by the 2006 amendment after bills stalled since 1990; (b) fair criticism. s. 15: criminal contempt of a High Court on the court’s own motion, or on a motion by, or with the consent of, the Advocate-General, or “with the leave of the court” by any other person; s. 10: the High Court’s jurisdiction covers courts subordinate to it; s. 11: in camera hearing of contempt proceedings by the HC; s. 14: Division-Bench trial; s. 20: one-year limitation. Jurisdiction itself flows from Arts. 129/215 — courts of record. The Act “codifies and limits” (long title) — that is the 1971 settlement of the post-E.M.S. and pre-Mulgaokar storm.

The cases (one line each — this is where the marks are)

  • E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.P. Balakrishnan (1970) — “class-biased judges” held contempt; the severity of the pre-Act era that the 1971 codification was written to discipline.
  • S. Mulgaokar (1978) — the letters attacking Justice Desai’s judgment: the Court’s own restraint canon — act sparingly, treat the Attorney-General as the gatekeeper, “the greatest protection of the institution is to give the widest latitude to criticism”.
  • In re: P.N. Duda (1988) — the retired judge’s article: the enduring line — criticism of a judgment is free; unproven imputation on the integrity of judges is not.
  • R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N. (1994) — the jail-interview case (full brief, Part 2): no prior restraint on the press; public officials must in general tolerate publication; a private individual’s right to be let alone survives; damages remain open. It is the “fair comment/privacy” hinge every contempt-and-media question passes through.
  • Prashant Bhushan v. Registrar (suo motu) (2020) — two tweets; convicted; the suo motu route held competent under Arts. 129/215 r.w. the Act; the truth defence failed for want of proof about individuals; fine of Re 1; and the sentence the question paraphrases: public confidence in the judiciary “is indispensable to the rule of law” — with it, “a judge, like Caesar’s wife, must be beyond suspicion”. Use as the live proof that s. 2(c)(i) is not a museum piece.
  • Media trials: the head under strain is s. 2(c)(ii) — the Supreme Court’s 2021 bail order in Arnab Manoranjan Goswami v. State of Maharashtra branded “trial by media” a serious contempt of the court’s process, and its Dec-2024 bail order (the Nithari-convict matter) asked the Attorney-General to assist on a regulatory framework for media trials (Parts 4.7, 7.1-Q6).

Essay architecture & close

Two philosophies: protecting the litigant’s fair trial vs protecting the institution’s confidence. The Act leans to the first (ss. 3–5 are about the proceeding); the case-law has periodically leaned to the second (E.M.S.; Bhushan). Then draw the boundary (fair vs scandalising) with s. 5 + Duda/Mulgaokar; the sub-judice moment (s. 2(c)(ii)) with Goswami; the 2006 truth defence as the Act’s only genuine liberalisation; and the comparative footnote that decides tone: England abolished the common-law offence of “scandalising the judiciary” in 2013 after two Law Commission reports, while India’s 2023 criminal-law rewrite — the most comprehensive in 150 years — left s. 2(c) entirely untouched. Close-ready: “Confidence is earned by reasons, not enforced by committals. Read whole, the 1971 Act’s instruction to the press is: report, criticise — do not try the case, do not libel the judge, and be ready to prove your truth.”

Q6. A media reporter Mrs. K publishes a vicious article, “Doomed by Dowry”, in a leading magazine while Mr. Y’s anticipatory-bail application is pending before the High Court; only the deceased wife’s family’s version was published. Protect Y’s right to a fair trial against the vicious media trial — analyse his rights and draft the appeal if bail is rejected. 20

(a) Analyse Y’s rights and remedies (the first half of the answer)

  1. Art. 21 — fair trial, presumption of innocence. A pending bail hearing is a judicial proceeding; an article pronouncing the suspect “doomed” pre-judges it. Consequences: (i) the contempt limb s. 2(c)(ii) of the 1971 Act (publication “prejudicing or interfering with the due course of a judicial proceeding” — the s. 3 innocent-publication shield fails when the proceeding is on foot and the piece targets it); (ii) the Goswami (2021) principle — the Supreme Court (Bopanna & Shah, JJ) expressly held the media cannot conduct a parallel trial of an accused — and the Court’s Dec-2024 directions seeking the Attorney-General’s help on “media trials” (Parts 4.7, 6.2).
  2. Defamation — BNS s. 356 (procedure: BNSS s. 222’s complaint naming the exact words; cognizance only on Y’s own complaint, not the police’s — BNSS s. 218(1)(a) lineage). An imputation of the commission of an offence is defamatory per se; a “vicious”, deliberately one-sided article asserting guilt before charge defeats Exception 1 (truth must be true and for the public good — and there is no conviction to be true); no other Exception fits editorial advocacy. Parallel civil action lies for damages (reputation = Art. 21, Subramanian Swamy).
  3. Privacy and ethics: Rajagopal — even an under-trial is entitled against “unwarranted publicity”; the PCI route (Norms: balanced treatment of sub-judice matters; hearing both sides; restraint on identifying families) — remedy limited to warning/censure and publication of the finding, but the published censure is itself leverage; note the asymmetry — a print magazine is beyond the NBSA (broadcast-only) and the 2021 Rules’ Part III codes (OTT-only): print carries only ethics + criminal law (Part 2). The magazine’s s. 4 “fair report” defence is unavailable — an advocacy piece is not a report of the proceedings.
  4. Interim levers: an application in the bail court seeking (i) a record direction that the impugned article be given no weight, (ii) an advisory to the publisher against serialisation of “future instalments” (courts may caution and punish after; they cannot gag in advance — Rajagopal), (iii) leave to move a contempt petition (s. 15’s “person aggrieved with leave” route). Cite for depth the Law Commission’s 200th Report (2006): statutory postponement orders on “substantial risk of prejudice”, the Sheppard/Nebraska toolkit (venue, sequestration, participant gags — never press gags: Nebraska Press Assn (1976)) — proposed, never enacted; courts manage through inherent powers (Part 6.2).

(b) The draft — appeal grounds against rejection of anticipatory bail (SLP, Art. 136)

Format: title (petition under Article 136 for special leave to appeal against the impugned bail order), table of dates, synopsis, and then: “That the learned High Court erred — (i) in refusing anticipatory bail on the gravity of the accusation alone, contrary to Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi), (2020) 5 SCC 1 (no time-limitation save in exceptional circumstances; conditions are the norm, refusal the exception); (ii) in reasoning, expressly or by visible borrowing, from the press article — while judges are presumed immune from publicity, the presumption fails where the order itself recites the extraneous account; (iii) in ignoring Siddharth v. State (U.P.) (2022) (arrest is not the norm; notice before arrest; custodial interrogation must be justified); (iv) in treating the incompleteness of the investigation as decisive against liberty, contrary to Goswami (2021) (the State’s pace cannot be the ground of a citizen’s jail); and (v) in pre-judging the evidentiary presumption in dowry-death cases (which attaches at trial, upon proof of cruelty proximate to death) — converting a bail hearing into a mini trial, the precise vice condemned as a “media trial”. Relief: conditional bail; direction that the case record note the sub-judice position; liberty to move a separate contempt complaint (s. 15, with leave) against the publisher; costs of the frivolous campaign.” End with the one-line theory the examiner is waiting for: “The remedy for prejudicial publicity is not a gag — it is an order (bail on conditions), a complaint (s. 356), a censure (PCI) and, at the day’s end, a judgment. The press may prejudice the public; it may not prejudice the court — and where the order shows that it did, the error is appealable.”

Q7. X discovered that private photos, uploaded by her on Facebook and Instagram, were re-posted on pornographic websites without consent. “Re-posting is an offence.” Discuss X’s remedies under social/digital-media regulations. 20

This is X v. Union of India (2023) dressed as a problem (full brief in Parts 4.5–4.6). Answer in five blocks: crime → intermediary → state → court → data.

(1) It is an offence — and “she uploaded it herself” is no defence

  • IT Act s. 66E — capturing, publishing or transmitting an image of the private area of a woman, under circumstances violating privacy and without her consent: up to 3 years or ₹2 lakh or both. The consent the section protects is consent to the publication complained of; permission given to Instagram is not permission to a porn aggregator. Extend with ss. 67/67A where the repost is lascivious or sexually-explicit; s. 66D + BNS s. 318(4) where an account or identity is impersonated; BNS s. 351 (criminal intimidation) where the site threatens or demands; s. 356 where captions defame; and if X was a minor at any relevant time, POCSO (with its s. 23 identity bar) and BNS ss. 77–79 territory.
  • Modern consent architecture: DPDP Act 2023 — personal data may be processed only for the consented, specified purpose (consent, notice and withdrawal provisions; purpose limitation), with a duty to erase data no longer compliant (subject to legal retention); security failures expose the platform to Schedule penalties up to ₹250 crore (₹200 crore for children’s-data failures) — the platform’s liability, distinct from the re-poster’s crime.

(2) The intermediary — the clock rules (2021 → 2025 → 2026)

  • Grievance route: complaint to the Grievance Officer (r. 4: acknowledge within 24 hours; redress within 15 days). For content “exposing the privacy of another person”, impersonation and “morphed” images of a real person: the 24-hour takedown duty (r. 3(2)(b)) — the rule born of X itself, a removal duty without any court order. The Feb-2025 amendment set a 3-hour clock for content specifically notified by a court or government; the Feb-2026 amendment (in force 20.2.2026) cuts it to 2 hours for sexual-imagery/NCII/impersonation complaints and imposes the synthetic-content (SGI) labelling + embedded metadata/provenance regime — X’s photos, if AI-manipulated, must bear the label, and a platform that knowingly permits unlabelled SGI is in breach of its due diligence. The 2025/26 amendments also require quarterly warnings to users (citing BNS/POCSO/RPA/IT sanctions) and identity disclosure of the uploader to the complainant in these categories — the anonymity shield lifted for X’s follow-on action.
  • Consequence of failure: loss of the s. 79 safe harbour (s. 79(3)(b) — duty-bound intermediaries failing the duties are liable under all applicable laws). Shreya confined “actual knowledge” to a court order or government notification; X made the victim’s complaint itself the trigger for these categories (Part 4.6: “the complaint is the knowledge”).

(3) The State

  • Criminal process: e-complaint on the NCRP portal (cybercrime.gov.in) or FIR at any police station — the zero-FIR/e-FIR culture of BNSS s. 175; the investigating officer preserves the copies (the IT Act’s preservation-and-disclosure obligations fall on the intermediary). Blocking: s. 69A + the 2009 Rules on decency/public-order grounds; the r. 9 emergency route (72 hours without notice, Part 4.4). Precedent that blocking is used for sexual content: the government’s September 2015 order blocking 857 pornographic sites after the Delhi High Court case. For child victims, X itself ordered global de-indexing and perpetual forgetting; an adult like X gets the India-scope remedy by default — that distinction is worth a line.

(4) The courts — the fastest real remedy

  • Dynamic/global injunctions: John Doe (“Ashok Kumar”) decrees since NSEIT v. Aequs, with “live-proceedings” clauses and mirror-site coverage (Part 4.7): unnamed respondents, search-engine de-indexing, hash-matching of re-uploads, disclosure orders lifting anonymity through the intermediary. Right to be forgotten: the Delhi HC (2022) ordered delisting where proceedings ended favourably; the Calcutta HC (2022) refused to make oblivion a reputation cleanser (“no one is a national hero or a national villain”); X v. UoI (2023) granted women victims of sexual offences, as a class, de-indexing and takedown — and the Srikrishna Committee (2019) had already recommended a statutory RTBF; the DPDP Act did not carry it. One sentence of that history = distinction marks.
  • At trial: in-camera conduct (the open-trial rule’s sex-offence proviso — CrPC s. 327’s successor), identity bars (s. 228A IPC → BNS s. 345; POCSO s. 23), and the victims’ compensation scheme (CrPC s. 357A and its successor).

(5) The order of operations — draw this as your money diagram

Close: “X’s instinct is correct — the re-posting is an offence. The deeper answer is that India’s 2021–26 architecture stopped asking whether online speech may be punished and began asking how fast it can be un-made: twenty-four hours became two, India-only became global for the worst categories, and anonymity — the re-poster’s only armour — is now peelable by complaint. For a woman whose privacy was monetised, speed is the remedy.”

Q8. Brief notes on any two (10 each): (A) Paid news — a threat to independent and fair media; (B) Perils of fake news and legal mechanism for its control; (C) RTI Act — an instrument to ensure good governance; (D) Commercial speech under Art. 19(1)(a). 10+10

(A) Paid news

Meaning: “the projection in print or electronic media of news in any form for monies paid” — the Election Commission’s definition, operationalised after the 2009 general elections when a Press Council inquiry reported paid news “widespread” across nine states. Why it threatens fair media: it inverts the market of trust — the voter reads paid copy as scrutiny; honest candidates are outspent in the news pages; the editor becomes a price-list; and the one public good elections sell — information — is quietly re-sold. The legal mechanism (there is no offence called “paid news” — four working levers): (i) expenditure law — RPA ss. 77 (separate account of election expenses), 89 (return), 106 (disqualification for false/incomplete returns), plus the ECI’s 2013 circular mandating disclosure and media audits by the Media Certification and Monitoring Committees (static + electronic); (ii) the corrupt-practice hook — s. 123(2) bribery (payment for “news” is inducement only if traced to a candidate’s election fund — the ECI’s own 2012–13 note conceded the proof problem and proposed making paid news a corrupt practice by amendment; never enacted); (iii) disqualification practice — the leading instance: the ECI’s 2012 disqualification of a Union Minister (Rajya Sabha) over paid features in a Marathi journal (casebook; Part 3); (iv) the digital tail — the 2021 Rules’ political-advertisement clock (disable access to ads violating notified provisions within 36 hours — Part 4.2) and the 2024 ECI deepfake/fast-track advisory; the 2015 DAVP merger and the retired-judges committee’s ad-allocation principles remove the “reward and punish” incentive that feeds the trade. Verdict line: “India’s answer to paid news is accounting law applied to journalism — clever, and exactly as strong as the filing officer.”

(B) Fake news — perils and the legal mechanism

Perils, from the Indian record: the 2018 wave of WhatsApp-lynchings (forwarded child-lift rumours — the mob-crime ripple that pushed the Standing Committee to insert the BNS “mob violence” provision, s. 103); rumour-aided communal violence (2020 Delhi — FIRs included “morphed” videos); market-panic forwards; election deepfakes (2019 “deepfake audios”, 2024 synthetic campaign clips — ECI fast-track advisory); public-health misinformation. The mechanism — a four-layer stack: (1) criminal — BNS s. 197(1)(d) (false or misleading information tending to jeopardise sovereignty, unity and integrity or security of India — up to 3 years), ss. 353/196, s. 152 for intent to excite secession/subversion; IT Act ss. 66D/66E/67 for impersonation, morphing and obscene packaging; s. 66A is dead — Shreya buried “grossly offensive” for vagueness; (2) administrative — s. 69A blocking (the 2020–21 use around the farm-law riots, incl. the blocking of 249 accounts) + the 2021 Rules, now with the 2025/26 synthetic-content labelling and 3-hour/2-hour clocks — “misinformation policy has become takedown engineering, not prosecution”; (3) electoral — Model Code + MCMC screening of political ads, and short ECI windows for platforms on notified deepfakes; (4) civil/self-regulatory — defamation suits, ASCI for advertising claims, the PCI’s 2022 norms (verify; label opinion; do not amplify), platform fact-checking (fragile — Meta ended third-party fact-checking in January 2025 and India is debating the vacuum). Critique and close: “s. 197(1)(d) is s. 66A’s ghost wearing a security uniform — the Shreya test (intelligible definition, proximity, real review) is the only fence. That Parliament chose labels and clocks over a new speech-crime for the ordinary falsehood is the best evidence the 2023 legislature remembered 166A. The perils of fake news are real; the perils of the cure are a paper’s conclusion.”

(C) RTI — an instrument for good governance

Foundation: the right to know is implicit in Art. 19(1)(a) — SP Gupta v. President of India (1982) (disclosure “is the rule; the exemption a limited purpose”); Bennett Coleman (circulation and the reader’s interest); and Union of India v. Assom Lubaya (2022) — RTI is “an integral facet of the freedom of expression”, exemptions read strictly. The Act (2005) as governance engineering: s. 3 right to inspect works, take notes and certified copies (including in electronic form); s. 4 — suo motu disclosure (the state must publish before being asked — the “governance half”); ss. 5–7 PIOs and timelines (30 days; 48 hours where life and liberty are involved; transfer within 5 days); s. 8 exemptions (sovereignty/security; fiduciary; third-party IP; 8(1)(j) privacy — overridden whenever the larger public interest warrants disclosure); s. 9 third-party notice (10 days); s. 10 severance of exempt portions; ss. 12–19 — first appeal free within the department, second to the Central/State Information Commissions, with the civil court’s summoning powers; s. 20 penalties — ₹250 a day capped at ₹25,000 — the teeth; s. 22 overriding effect; s. 24 the First Schedule’s security bodies (exempt, but not for allegations of corruption and human-rights violation); the 2019 amendment (Commissioners’ terms and salaries at the government’s pleasure) — the independence critique. The media loop: the Adarsha Housing scam (2010) — RTI-obtained allotment records, published, became the spine of the Supreme Court’s own case; CIC rulings that Prasar Bharati is a “public authority” (s. 2(h) — “owned, controlled or substantially financed”) close the loop between the state broadcaster and citizen-requests (Part 3). Court-cautions: the Act is “not a fishing licence” nor a tool to re-litigate policy — opinions and deliberations are not “information”; frivolous filings penalised. Close: “RTI converts the subject into a stakeholder — the exact inversion of the Registration Act of 1867’s premise that information is the state’s property. For the media it is the one statute on this syllabus that gives: the RTI application is today what the sedition trial was in 1908 — the arena in which the state must justify itself.”

(D) Commercial speech under Art. 19(1)(a)

The arc: silence → Tata Press v. MTNL (1995) — the telephone directory case: “freedom of speech and expression” includes propagating one’s views and the corresponding right of the public to receive them; commercial information “has a double facet”; the Court canvassed US/UK/German law and imported the protection without America’s strict scrutiny (casebook print). → Hamdard Dawakhana v. UoI (2002) — the Magic Remedies Act’s ban on “objectionable” drug claims upheld: advertising a product is speech; advertising a curative claim is trade malpractice — commercial speech enjoys “a lesser degree of protection”, and misleading speech none at all. → the statutory regime since 2019: the Consumer Protection Act defines “misleading advertisement” (false description, guarantee, licence/patent, standard, or a deceptive endorsement), empowers the CCPA to penalise up to ₹10 lakh (first) and ₹20 lakh (subsequent) and seek prosecution, extends product-liability actions to endorsers, and rides the 2021–22 endorsement-disclosure guidelines (ASCI-administered) — “say it is paid”. The surrogate-liquor-ad ban litigation (Delhi High Court orders, 2008–2022; MIB advisories on “brand equity” advertising) is the doctrine’s real-world laboratory: if the message evades the prohibition, the message is the harm. State advertising (the twin): Bennett Coleman (1972/73 — newsprint policy struck; restriction must fit the constitutional heads) and (1996 — the punitive withdrawal of government ads for editorial coverage struck down under Arts. 14 and 19(1)(a); allocation must follow objective, circulation-based criteria); the retired-judges committee (Ahmadi, Venkatachaliah, Rajendra Babu; 2014) and the 2015 DAVP merger operationalised it: advertise achievements, not personalities; never punish a paper in the billing window. Close: “commercial speech is where India imported a conclusion, not a test: advertising is free when it informs, unprotected when it lies. Tata Press gave the market a voice; Hamdard gave the regulator the veto; the 2019 Code admits that in the influencer age the injured party is the consumer — Art. 19’s oldest promise, recast for WhatsApp.”

7.2 · The December 2015 paper — blueprints

2015-Q1. Freedom of speech and expression is subject to reasonable restrictions; discuss with decided cases. Is there prior censorship of the press in India? Compare with the USA. 20

Reasonable restrictions: the V.G. Row test (casebook prints it inside the Shreya extract): no abstract general standard — reasonableness judged by the nature and extent of the penalty, the urgency of the evil apprehended, the manner of imposition, and the proportionate “direct and proximate nexus” with the 19(2) head; the heads are exhaustive, and “in the interests of public order” ≠ “in the interests of the general public” (Romesh Thappar — a ban on a journal for “circulation prejudicial to public safety” failed pre-1951 because the head did not exist). Prior censorship: for newspapers — none, as a rule: Brij Bhushan (1950) struck the East Punjab requirement to submit communal material before printing; during the Emergency (1975–77) pre-publication censorship was practised through the Censorship Officers but never enacted as a general press-censorship statute — the post-publication seizure and the editors’ strike (the Financial Express walkout) were the real mechanics; for cinema — the one statutory exception: certification under ss. 4/5B upheld in K.A. Abbas (1971) on the medium’s impact on “the less adult and less sophisticated” audience; customs import-control (s. 11) is tolerated as border control; digital India replaced prior restraint with the notice-and-takedown clock (s. 69A + 2021/25/26 Rules) — “blocking before the public reaches it” is functionally prior restraint with post-hoc paperwork (the SC’s Nov-2023 direction to publish all s. 69A orders is the transparency answer). USA: Near “heavy presumption”; Pentagon Papers; Nebraska Press (1976) gag-order test — courts restrain participants, not the press. Close: “India prohibits prior censorship and regulates afterwards; America prohibits regulation and litigates afterwards. The gap is measured by who blinks first — in America the judge; in India, increasingly, the editor, pre-emptively, at the end of a three-hour clock.”

2015-Q2(a). “A film is judged by the contemporary standards of the country and the audience to which it relates.” Examine, with the obscenity test. 10

The quote is the statute: Cinematograph Act s. 5B(2) — the certifying authority shall have regard to “the standards of the contemporary life of the country… and the strength and sensitivity of the audience”, applied to the film as a whole, not isolated scenes (the Act’s own words; Part 3.2), while s. 5B(1) enumerates the heads (security, foreign relations, decency/morality, defamation, incitement, offences under the representation/young-persons laws). The general obscenity test, in one arc: Hicklin’s “tendency to deprave and corrupt” (s. 292 IPC era) imported by Ranjit D. Udeshi (1965) → abandoned by Aveek Sarkar v. State of W.B. (2014) — “the test is the average person, applying contemporary community standards, to the work as a whole… the standard is the reasonable, not the depraved, man” (the Court adopted Miller; casebook prints it inside Shreya, p. 41) → artistic-context defence — Bobby Art International (1996): rape scenes in a socially committed film do not “corrupt” because the film “condemns, does not glorify” (Part 3.4) → plus the three-tier machinery (s. 4 board, s. 5D Tribunal, Art. 226) that makes “standards” reviewable. Close: “the sentence in the question is the rare place where ‘contemporary standards’ is statutory rather than merely jurisprudential; Aveek made it the law of everything else. The same film now lives twice — a certificate under s. 5B and a prosecution under s. 292/294 + IT s. 67; Aveek harmonised the offence, and left the certificate to its own temperance.”

2015-Q2(b). “The Right to Information Act is an instrument of empowerment of the media.” Discuss. 10

= Q8(C) with the media emphasis. (i) Art. 19(1)(a) right-to-know (SP Gupta; Assom Lubaya 2022) — the reporter holds the citizen’s s. 3 right with a professional multiplier; (ii) the Act supplies evidence, not privilege: “true imputation, for public good” (the truth Exception) and “fair report” defences become provable when the record is obtained — certified copies, ₹10 fee, 30 days; (iii) practice: Adarsha (2010); RTI-obtained records feeding the 2G-era follow-ups; the CIC’s “Prasar Bharati is a public authority” ruling (Part 3.3); (iv) the pushback: s. 8(1)(a)/(e) refusals, source-identity denials, the 2019 amendment’s blow to Commission independence — “the state learned to delay: vacancies, transfer petitions, partial redactions”; (v) the caution from courts: RTI is not a fishing licence nor a weapon to re-litigate policy. Close: “RTI did not make the media powerful — it made documentary journalism affordable. The paper that once sued for records now asks for them at ten rupees a page; and the state that once refused in silence must refuse with reasons, in writing, appealable. ‘Deny with reasons’ is the entire empowerment the question names.”

2015-Q3. The right to privacy — State actors, celebrities, private individuals; whether sting operations violate the right to privacy. 20

(0) The position when the paper was set (2015): no constitutional privacy right — M.P. Sharma (1954) and Kharak Singh (1963) had refused it; Gobind v. State of M.P. (1975) conceded an “emanating” right — “to be let alone”. The press-facing law was statutory: R. Rajagopal (1994) (casebook; Part 2) — no prior restraint on publishing a convict’s jail interviews; public officials must in general tolerate publication; a private person’s “right to be let alone” survives; damages remain open for unwarranted publicity; plus ss. 509/354C IPC and 66E IT, breach of confidence, POCSO s. 23. (1) The upgrade (answer as if set today): K.S. Puttaswamy v. UoI (24.8.2017) — privacy is an intrinsic part of Art. 21 (informational privacy included); against the State, any intrusion needs legality, legitimacy, proportionality; media-versus-person disputes run through statutes and case-by-case balancing. (2) Per actor: State — PUCL v. UoI (1997) (tapping only under authorisation, necessity, review, after-the-fact notice), now the s. 69/IT (Interception) Rules regime with the Dec-2018 “silo” amendment (Part 4); the Pegasus-era hearings (2021–24) tested exactly this and produced no judgment — the “un-answered question” paragraph. Celebrities/public officials — Rajagopal’s tolerance rule; public-interest limits the private veil; RTBF does not serve them — the Delhi HC (2022) delists where proceedings ended favourably; the Calcutta HC (2022): “no one is a national hero or a national villain”. Private persons — the strongest zone: X v. UoI (2023) (de-indexing/takedown for women victims of sexual offences); the 2025/26 morphing/SGI rules; POCSO’s identity bar. (3) Stings: the casebook’s Delhi High Court guidelines (2007) — hidden-camera journalism is permissible only where (a) the target is serious public-interest wrongdoing (corruption affecting citizens), (b) no lawful alternative, (c) no entrapment/inducement of the willing, (d) bystanders unidentifiable, (e) the edit does not distort, and the original footage remains available to the court; the Supreme Court (2015) refused a blanket ban on stings — leaving the norms to the media’s own codes and judicial review; the Law Commission studied entrapment (2008 consultation); Selvi v. Karnataka (2010) supplies the test’s template — investigative technique + Art. 20(3) + privacy, held to strict necessity. One-formula close: “a sting is a privacy injury that must justify itself as a privacy service: public interest, necessity, proportionality, accuracy of the edited product. Where all four hold — cash-for-queries era exposures; police-corruption stings — the sting wins; where the target is a private act with no public dimension, it is s. 66E territory. Puttaswamy made the 2007 guidelines’ four-part logic constitutional arithmetic.”

2015-Q4. Notes on any two: (a) reporting of legislative proceedings; (b) defamation; (c) history of legislative efforts regarding media. 10+10

(a) Legislative-proceedings reporting — Arts. 105/193 privilege (absolute for “anything said or any vote given” in the House, extended to persons publishing by or under the authority of the House); the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1971 — no civil or criminal proceedings for publishing a true report of the proceedings of either House or a Parliamentary Committee; the Emergency Ordinance of 1975 suspended it (the syllabus’s “effort undone” exhibit); the 1977 Act restored it, now excluding expunged portions, in-camera sittings, and publication against House orders; commentary on the proceedings enjoys only ordinary defamation law (BNS 356 exceptions 2–3 — public conduct of public servants); today the broadcast layer (Sansad TV, the 17.3.2021 merger of the two channels — Part 3.3) carries proceedings directly, shrinking the reporter’s exposure but growing the state’s control of the feed. (b) Defamation — the Part 6.1 skeleton (s. 356; ten exceptions; complaint by the person aggrieved, BNSS 218/222; sanction not required — contrast the hate heads), Subramanian Swamy (2016) — criminal defamation upheld, reputation inside Art. 21, truth only for public good; Rajagopal — no prior restraint; the platform layer — r. 3(2)(b) complaints, takedown clocks, and the Delhi HC’s 2022 global takedown order (extraterritorial over-blocking debate — Parts 4.7, 5.6). (c) History of legislative efforts — compress Part 5 into five breaths: the colonial toolbox (1799→1923, Part 5.3); the 1951 bargain (First Amendment + Objectionable Matters Act — and the Act’s lapse in 1956); the republican half-efforts (WJA 1955; PCI 1978 — powers of censure only; Prasar Bharati 1990 implemented 1997; Cable 1995); the broadcast bills never born (Part 5.6: the lapsed 2023 Bill, the Standing Committee’s 14.3.2025 advice, the 2026 rule-making pivot under the Telecom Act 2023); and the unimplemented law-reform tail (LCI 267th, 2017; s. 152 challenge pending since 8.8.2025). The close-line is Part 5.1’s: every effort on the list was drafted by the same hand that would have to enforce it — the executive; the First Amendment remains the only effort the Republic ever made upon itself.

2015-Q5. (a) Commercial speech and the government’s advertisement policy; (b) whether the Press Council regulates the print media — powers and adequacy. 10+10

(a) = Q8(D), with the advertisement-policy half at full width: Bennett Coleman (1972/73) — the newsprint policy (import quota tied to past circulation) was a “restriction” on the circulation of ideas and fell outside the then-text of 19(6); Bennett Coleman (No. 2) (1996) — the DAVP’s differential rates punishing papers for critical coverage were struck under Arts. 14 and 19(1)(a): the state may choose where to advertise but must allocate on objective, non-punitive criteria; the retired-judges committee (Ahmadi, Venkatachaliah, Rajendra Babu; 2014) and the DAVP–PIB merger (2015) wrote the principles into policy — ads to report achievements; no withdrawal to punish editorial lines; allocation on readership, not loyalty; no personality cults; media-audit of government ads. The IT Rules’ political-ad disclosure and the ECI’s 2024 six-hour deepfake window extend the same idea to platforms (Part 4.2). Close: “the state buys space; it does not buy silence — a doctrine India learned twice from the same newspaper, and re-learns each time a ministry does.” (b) PCI — anatomy (Part 2 §2.1): the 1965 Commission gave way to the Press Council Act, 1978: a Chairperson (a retired Supreme Court judge) + up to 28 members — two Parliamentarians (one from each House), five from State Legislatures, three editors, seven working journalists, five persons “of national fame, distinction or repute”, five from education/science/literature/law/philosophy (s. 4). Functions (s. 13): “help maintain and improve the freedoms of the press”; evolve a code of conduct consistent with “the canons of good taste” and a free press; keep “all laws… under review”; encourage “self-cleansing” and co-operation “to eliminate news that undermines public morality” and to protect the citizen’s right to information. Powers (s. 14): inquire, on complaint or its own motion, into conduct violating law or ethics (and into interferences with press freedom) — the sanctions: representation to government, censure, warning, admonition, and publication of its findings at the newspaper’s cost; no fine, no licence, no enforceable order. Illustrations the casebook prints: the AIIMS-film episode (Prabha Dutt v. UoI, 1982 — newsgathering vs the subjects’ privacy, as Part 2’s brief has it) and gender/community-complaints practice (Part 2.1; the 2019/2022 Norms). Adequacy critique, in four lines: (i) censure-only is “a press that shames itself” — deterrence requires a wallet, and the PCI has none; (ii) members from the industry keep it inside the tent; (iii) it reaches print only, while the harm moved to screens (s. 69A + 2021 Rules govern the platforms; the NBSA governs news TV — the PCI is the only major media body with no enforcement and no repeal); (iv) reform attempts — the “Media Council” proposals and the Standing-Committee-era bills — lapsed unpassed (Part 5.6). Verdict line: “the PCI regulates ethics, not law; and after 2021 the law regulates platforms, not ethics — a neat reversal that is the whole unit in one sentence.”

2015-Q6. “Fair comment is not contempt; scandalising is.” Discuss the boundary. 20

= 7.1-Q5 minus the “last hope” frame, with the boundary as the body: (1) s. 2(c)(i) vs s. 5 — criticism “of the merits of the case or of the judicial officer’s conduct with respect to judicial proceedings” is protected; the protection stops where imputation of integrity or motive begins (s. 5’s proviso; the Duda/E.M.S. pair); (2) s. 3/4 — the publication must not touch a pending proceeding and the report must be fair, accurate and unprivileged-portion-free; (3) Mulgaokar — self-restraint, AG-gatekeeping, “widest latitude”; (4) s. 13(a) truth-2006 — the Act’s only liberalisation, with its two gates (public interest + good faith) — Bhushan (2020) shows how narrow those gates are; (5) the modern grey zone — “scandalising by proxy”: the media-trial cases (Goswami 2021 — “trial by media… a serious contempt of court”; the Dec-2024 observations) in which the contempt lies less in insult than in prejudicing the trial — i.e. s. 2(c)(ii) doing the work s. 2(c)(i) once did; (6) comparative tail: the Commonwealth has largely retired “scandalising” (England, 2013); India’s 2023 codes rewrite left s. 2(c) untouched — that omission is the answer to “has the boundary moved?” Close: “the line is not between harsh and mild; it is between the judgment and the judge — criticise the first without limit, impute nothing to the second you cannot prove; and never confuse your court of public opinion with the one now trying the case.”

2015-Q7. Gag orders — during trial and during investigation. 20

Investigation stage — no gag, by design. The press may report arrests, recoveries and charges; the restraints are on the content’s periphery, not the reporter’s pen: victim-identity bans (s. 228A IPC → BNS s. 345; POCSO s. 23); the privacy of unconvicted persons (66E/354C territory; Rajagopal’s “let alone”); the case diary’s protection (CrPC s. 172 and its BNSS successor — its leak is a proceeding against the leaker, never an injunction against the newspaper); and the executive’s own tools — s. 69A/2021 Rules takedown of online amplification (Part 4) — “a takedown is not a gag order; the court is never asked.” Trial stage — four lawful levers: (i) in-camera conduct and exclusion of the public/galley for the protected categories (the open-trial rule, CrPC s. 327 → BNSS; sex-offence proviso); (ii) contempt of the pending proceeding — s. 2(c)(ii), the media-trial head (Goswami 2021; the Nithari-era Dec-2024 directions); (iii) gagging participants, not the press (police, bar, parties — the American template: Sheppard v. Maxwell (1966) tools; Nebraska Press (1976) — press-gags require the heavy test almost never met); (iv) postponement of trial for prejudice — the Law Commission’s 200th Report (2006) proposed a statutory power for it; Parliament never acted (Part 5.6), so courts manage with inherent powers. India’s doctrine in three propositions: (1) Brij Bhushan/Rajagopal — prior restraint on the press is (nearly) void: the court “has no power to restrain publication in advance”, save in the rarest national-security hours; (2) the remedy is after the fact — contempt, damages, prosecution under 356/353/196 — and a judgment that names the campaign as the thing the accused had to survive; (3) the exception that swallowed the rule: de-indexing/takedown (the X v. UoI (2023) child-abuse global order; the Delhi 2022 orders) — “the gag order is not dead; it has moved from the judge to the Grievance Officer, and from the newspaper to the search index.” Close: “a democracy must trust its judges to ignore the newsroom; a free press must trust itself to wait for the judgment. Both trusts broke in the same decade — which is precisely why the syllabus asks. India’s answer has been to protect the record, not silence the speaker, and to punish later, if ever.”

2015-Q8. (a) Broadcasting regulation in India and the self-regulatory code of broadcast ethics — structure and adequacy; (b) “government control of social media” — argue for and against. 10+10

(a) Structure (all of it in Parts 3–5): constitutional frame — Secretary, MIB v. CAB, (1995) 2 SCC 161 (airwaves are public property; the citizen’s right to receive; the state’s monopoly must yield to plurality); Odyssey Communications (2002) (no common-law monopoly — TRAI’s powers are statutory, not implied); Cricket Association of Bengal’s offspring — the up-linking and carriage stack: the Cable TV Networks (Regulation) Act 1995 + Rules (the programme code — no obscenity, no incitement between communities, no attack on religion, no “communalisation” of news, no defamation of women…; s. 11 r.w. r. 6), up-linking clearances (1994 guidelines; the 2009/2012/2017 foreign-uplinking rules — Part 3.4), TRAI (1997 — tariff, carriage, QoS; the 2018 deregulation), Prasar Bharti (1990/1997 — the public arm’s charter of standards). The self-regulatory half: the NBCA Code of Ethics (2001) enforced by the NBSA (since 2010 — a private tribunal of retired judges; censure, fines, mandated on-screen apologies; its media-trial and “defamation of a judge” orders 2014–19 are the casebook’s examples) — because the Code under the 1995 Act was in practice unenforced. The 2021 IT Rules (Part III) then statutorised a code for OTT and games — self-classification, grievance chain, inter-departmental Level-III committee — “self-regulation as contract with the state”, while broadcast news still runs on a code no ministry applies. Adequacy verdict: the structure exists — licence, code, tribunal, association — but each half regulates the other’s subject: the Act speaks to cable operators, the IT Rules speak to platforms, the NBSA speaks to its members, and the speaker on the channel answers to a body that collects no dues and grants no licence; which is precisely why the Broadcasting Bill (introduced 10.11.2023, never passed — the Standing Committee’s 14.3.2025 report urging it, then the 2026 pivot to the Telecom Act’s draft Unified Broadcasting Rules) promises exactly one thing: one regulator, one code, no more “self-”. (b) For and against government control of social media (the full 20-marker is Part 4.8) — For: crime without borders (NCII — X v. UoI; “digital arrest” frauds — the SC’s July-2026 hearing; terror logistics), electoral integrity (2024 deepfakes; ECI advisory), children (POCSO; 2021 Rules’ child clauses), sovereignty (the June/September-2020 bans of 59 + 118 apps; 54 more, incl. PUBG Mobile, 14.2.2022), and the argument-from-the-market (a foreign intermediary with 500 million users is infrastructure, and India regulates infrastructure). Against: the Shreya lesson (vague duties → pre-emptive over-compliance → prior restraint by algorithm — “the chilling effect” paragraph the casebook prints, ¶59); executive-as-editor (the 2019-21 blocking rows; the 249 accounts; the opacity that forced the SC’s Nov-2023 “publish all 69A orders” direction); the ITB flap of Jan-Mar 2021 (draft news-and-IT rules; deleted 16.3.2021 after the industry revolt — proof the line is knowable but not self-policed); collateral censorship and the single point of failure (one notification, one platform, everything vanishes); data-protection mismatch (control content, not the power over data); and the economy (every clock is a compliance cost that scales against Indian startups). Balance line: “control the conduct (crimes), discipline the intermediary (duties, clocks, labels), empower the user (grievance, RTBF, courts) — and never revive the licence: Part 5’s entire history shows that every time India reached for the licence, it was reaching for the censor.” Card close: “the 2015 question asked whether social media should be regulated; India answered in 2021–26 with a 2000 statute, an “intermediary”, and a set of clocks. The next paper will ask who audits the clock-keeper.”

10.2 · Exam strategy — how to write Unit 1 for a first-class answer

Answer anatomy (every 20-marker)

  1. Thesis (3 lines, argumentative, quotable).
  2. Map (what the law is: “the cluster, not the code” for speech topics; for forms questions — technology → statutes → regulator → courts).
  3. Provision — quote the section in the statute’s words (s. 19(2); s. 66A “grossly offensive”; s. 5B(2) “contemporary standards”; BNS 152 “purposely or knowingly… financial means”; r. 3 clocks in hours).
  4. Cases — 4–5 done properly per Part 8’s format beats 10 name-drops.
  5. Critique — continuity-with-colonial-law, vagueness (Shreya), enforcement-asymmetry, “effort abandoned” list.
  6. Current hook — one 2023–26 fact (IT amendments 2025/26; s. 152 notice; ECI deepfake clock; 2026 draft Broadcasting Rules).
  7. Close — a sentence the examiner can underline (the Parts above supply one for every question).
Diagrams to reproduce (they earn the “impressive” marks and cost only minutes): Part 2 registration flow-chart; Part 3 “who controls what” stack + cable flow; Part 4 platform-duty cascade + takedown-clock ladder; Part 5 First-Amendment flow; Part 6 “which code applies” decision map; Part 7 Q7 order-of-operations. Draw boxes and arrows — never circles-and-arrows without labels.

Ten things that lose marks (from the examiner’s perspective)

  1. Citing s. 124A for post-July-2024 conduct (it is BNS 152; and the 2022 order froze FIRs under 124A — say both).
  2. Calling the Cinematograph Act “censorship unconstitutional” — it is constitutional per K.A. Abbas; the cuttable point is “with Tribunal review”.
  3. Treating s. 69A blocking as prior restraint — Shreya saved it precisely because it is post-publication with procedure.
  4. Confusing PCI’s censure with a penalty — it has no fine, no licence power.
  5. Writing that 66A was “repealed in 2019” — struck down 2015, review rejected 2016 (per the casebook line).
  6. Quoting Hicklin as live law — it died in India in 2014 (Aveek).
  7. Missing IPC↔BNS where the question asks “under the BNS” — map table is Part 9.7; learn 152/196/197/294/299/353/356 + BNSS 98/99/163/175/218/219/222/482/528.
  8. Forgetting the “or through electronic communication” insertions of 2023 — the exact “decleanisation” credit in Q1-type essays.
  9. Citing the Broadcasting Bill 2023 as law — never introduced/passed; lapsed; the current vehicle is Telecom-Act rulemaking (2026 drafts). Verify before you write “the Act requires…”.
  10. Ending without an assessment — every note above ends with a verdict line; a summary is not a conclusion.
