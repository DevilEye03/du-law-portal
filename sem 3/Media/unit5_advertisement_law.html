A1 · The commercial speech doctrine — as the project documents it

Definition & elements

A commercial advertisement is: (i) a communication, (ii) made by or for a trader/professional/publisher, (iii) intended to promote a product, service, idea of economic value or a commercial transaction, (iv) usually (not necessarily) paid for and disseminated through a medium. The MIB compilation’s “2. Definitions” shows why Indian statutes define advertisement ultra-broadly — DMR Act s.2(a): “advertisement” includes any notice, circular, label, wrapper or other document, and any announcement made orally or by any means of producing or transmitting light, sound or smoke (uploaded bare Act, PDF p. 2). An ad is therefore: print line, hoarding, packaging insert, jingle, flash-show, reel, popup — everything. The IRW Act s.2(a) repeats the formula (Part B3).

The doctrine’s two theses (the project’s own narrative, PDF p. 84 of the MIB-notes — quotes verbatim):
iv) Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India — “The Supreme Court held that, an advertisement is no doubt a form of speech and expression but every advertisement is not a matter dealing with the expression of ideas and hence advertisement of a commercial nature cannot fall within the concept of Article 19(1)(a).”
v) Tata Press Ltd. v. Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd. — “A three judge bench of the Supreme Court differed from the view expressed in the Dawakhana case and held that ‘commercial advertisement’ was definitely a part of Article 19(1)(a) as it aimed at the dissemination of information regarding the product. The Court, however, made it clear that the government could regulate commercial advertisements, which are deceptive, unfair, misleading and untruthful.” — “Media Laws in India” (MIB compilation, uploaded), “10. CASE LAWS”, PDF pp. 83–84

Fill the frame (each fact below is the doctrine, not decoration): Hamdard (AIR 1960 SC 554) tested the very statute this unit prescribes — the DMR Act’s ss. 3–5, 7 — and read “advertisement” as trade-activity, unprotected; the consequence visible in the statute: DMR’s offences never needed a free-speech defence framework, and its enforcement (search, seizure, forfeiture, cognizability) was built like a pure regulatory code [External; reported for the citation; the holding itself is the notes’ text]. Tata Press (1995) 5 SCC 139 arose from MTNL refusing to allot “Golden Pages” telephone-directory space to a private publisher — and it also imported the American vocabulary wholesale: “commercial speech”, “the right to receive commercial information”, “the free flow of commercial information is indispensable”, and the democracy-of-consumers argument (advertising “provides information about products and services … indispensable to a democratic enterprise” — that being why the Court linked it to the “marketplace of ideas” strand the reader itself reproduces in Shreya ¶11 (Abrams dissent) and ¶28–29). For Tata Press’s US anchors (Valentine’s 1942 denial; Virginia Pharmacy 1976; Central Hudson 1980) — [External; reported] one-liners are supplied in A2, flagged, because the case book supplies only the Indian summaries.

What the protection means — four components (exam-ready)
  1. Speaker’s limb: the trader/professional may publish truthful, non-deceptive information about lawful trade — the right flows from 19(1)(a) “including the right to propagate one’s message” (the Romesh Thappar/Sakal line the notes file quotes in its Sakal summary: price/page regulation struck down “the right of freedom of speech and expression couldn’t be taken away with the object of placing restrictions on the business activity” — MIB-notes p. 83).
  2. Listener’s limb: the consumer’s right to receive commercial information — the “receive and disseminate” grammar of CAB p. 213 (reader, PDF p. 285) applies to market information too.
  3. Systemic limb: “free commercial circulation of ideas … assists in informed decision-making” — Tata Press’s democracy rationale (notes summary) — the same “informed debate and decision making on public issues” language the reader’s UIMPAA excerpt uses to uphold must-carry (PDF p. 287).
  4. Commercial press limb: advertising revenue is the press’s economic oxygen — Bennett Coleman’s Beg, J. “Ark of the Covenant” quote survives in the reader (Shreya ¶9, PDF p. 32), and the Majithia findings reproduced in ABP ¶56: “The gross revenue of newspaper establishments comprises revenue through advertisements, circulation …” (PDF p. 25) — restrictions on ads therefore burden 19(1)(a) institutionally as well as individually.

Why even truthful ads are regulable — the four accepted justifications (each with a project exhibit)
  • Vulnerable audiences: Goswami’s whole petition (minors — the Court’s answer: ordinary criminal law + “a culture of responsible reading”, not source-restraint; PDF pp. 236–240).
  • Health externalities: the DMR Schedule exists because self-treating appendicitis, cancer, diabetes, epilepsy, leprosy, tuberculosis (entries 1, 6, 9, 17, 32, 50) from an advertisement is deadly; the s.3 provisos tie the list to “no accepted remedies” conditions.
  • Public-order and electoral equality: Common Cause — state-funded self-praise distorts the debate “the only vehicle of political discourse so essential to democracy” (CAB quote, reader p. 285); paid news perverts it (Chavan — reader pp. 324–335).
  • Human dignity over commerce: IRW Act s.2(c)/s.3 — “denigrating women” advertising is banned whatever its truth; Norm 17(ii) for print (Goswami verbatim, PDF p. 187).

The examiner’s line to write

“Indian law adopted the American doctrine’s second step without its first: after Tata Press, commercial speech sits inside 19(1)(a); but instead of tiered judicial scrutiny, India regulates principally through subject-matter statutes (DMR, IRW, Cable code, CPA, Emblems) and — after Common Cause — through content-neutral principles binding even the State-advertiser. Truth, accordingly, is a shield against the ‘misleading’ limb — never against the Schedule, the watershed, or the public-interest test.”

A3 · The two-thesis dossier — Hamdard (1960) and Tata Press (1995), plus the supporting quartet

5.1’s spine · documented in the uploaded MIB compilation (“10. CASE LAWS”, PDF pp. 83–85) + the bare DMR Act itself
Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India — the denial thesis

Facts (project + reported): The petitioners — proprietors of the Unani/herbal Dawakhana business publishing advertisements of their medicines — challenged the validity of the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 as infringing Art. 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g). The uploaded notes frame the question precisely: “whether the Drug and Magic Remedies Act, which put restrictions on the advertisements of drugs in certain cases and prohibited advertisements of drugs having magic qualities for curing diseases, was valid as it curbed the freedom of speech and expression of a person by imposing restrictions on advertisements.” External; reported The Supreme Court decided it as a constitutional-question case (commonly cited as AIR 1960 SC 554, a five-Judge bench).

Issue: Is a commercial advertisement “speech and expression” at all?

Arguments: Petitioners — an advertisement is a mode of expression of the trader’s idea (quality, price, availability), and the profession of trading in Unani medicine is guaranteed by 19(1)(g); a total ban on publicity is confiscatory. Union — the Act regulates trade practice, a classic public-health measure; “magic remedies” (talisman, mantra, kavacha — see s.2(c) of the Act) have no protected ideational content at all, and the disease-list (Schedule) exists where public self-medication is fatal.

Decision (the notes’ verbatim words): “an advertisement is no doubt a form of speech and expression but every advertisement is not a matter dealing with the expression of ideas and hence advertisement of a commercial nature cannot fall within the concept of Article 19(1)(a).” The Act was upheld.

Principle of Hamdard: commercial advertising = business activity, not protected speech; the State’s police power over market conduct governs without 19(2) analysis. Doctrinal consequence visible in this very unit’s statute: the DMR Act of 1954 needs no free-speech safety valves — no “reasonable restriction” proviso, no proportionality hook, only a saving clause list (s.14, Part B1).

Tata Press Ltd. v. Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd. — the recognition thesis

Facts (notes + reported): External; reported for the incident: MTNL declined to allow a private publisher to operate the “Golden Pages” commercial-classified directory of its telephone network in Mumbai (and had vested the facility in another agency); Tata Press argued its right to publish such a directory and the public’s right to receive its information. The notes record the outcome in the doctrinal form:

Issue: Whether “commercial advertisement” is within Art. 19(1)(a). Decision (notes’ verbatim): “A three judge bench of the Supreme Court differed from the view expressed in the Dawakhana case and held that ‘commercial advertisement’ was definitely a part of Article 19(1)(a) as it aimed at the dissemination of information regarding the product. The Court, however, made it clear that the government could regulate commercial advertisements, which are deceptive, unfair, misleading and untruthful.” — MIB compilation, PDF p. 84

Principle evolved (state it in three clauses): (i) commercial speech is constitutionally protected speech — the listener’s interest in commercial information is the justification, not the advertiser’s profit motive; (ii) the protection is qualified from the outset: the deceptive/unfair/misleading/untruthful categories lie outside it; (iii) regulation of truthful commercial speech remains competent to the State on 19(2)-class grounds through subject-matter statutes — which is exactly how the syllabus’s line 5.1 is phrased (“may regulate even truthful advertising”). External; reported: the judgment (1995) 5 SCC 139 explicitly relies on the American sequence (Valentine 1942 → Virginia Pharmacy 1976 → Central Hudson 1980) and adopts “the free flow of commercial information is indispensable” and “the marketplace of ideas” vocabulary; cite as Tata Press, (1995) 5 SCC 139 with that caveat.

Sakal Papers v. UOI (from the same uploaded notes page)

“the right of freedom of speech and expression couldn’t be taken away with the object of placing restrictions on the business activity of the citizens. Freedom of speech can be restricted only on the grounds mentioned in clause (2) of Article 19” — the Daily Newspapers (Price and Control) Order 1960 struck down. Use in 5.1: this is the bridge by which advertising economics entered 19(1)(a) protection before Tata Press — page/price controls that squeeze ad space squeeze speech. (Reader’s own citation of the Bennett-Coleman-era jurisprudence: Beg, J.’s “Ark of the Covenant” line survives inside the Shreya excerpt, PDF p. 32.)

K.A. Abbas & Bobby Art (notes, PDF pp. 83–84)

“pre-censorship of films … was justified … because a motion picture was able to stir up emotions more deeply” (K.A. Abbas); “the Appellate Tribunal … which had followed the Guidelines under the Cinematograph Act and granted an ‘A’ certificate” was upheld (Bobby Art). Use in 5.1: the audience-impact rationale that lets India regulate even truthful audiovisual advertising more readily than print — the reader’s own Bobby Art excerpt adds the commercial-obscenity formula: “use of sex designed to play a commercial role by making its own appeal. This draws in the censor’s hand” (Case Book PDF p. 272).

Doctrine — synthesis paragraph (memorise)

Between 1960 and 1995 India moved from Hamdard’s categorisation (advertising is trade, so regulate freely) to Tata Press’s calibration (advertising is speech, so regulate through 19(2) — but the false and misleading need no protection, and the truthful may still yield to public health, order and dignity). Unit 5’s statutes are the calibration’s working parts: the DMR Act criminalises even true claims about listed disease-cures; the IRW Act bans indecent representation irrespective of truth; the Cable advertisement code bans whole product-classes from the air; the Consumer Protection/ASCI track polices falsity with civil remedies; and Common Cause (2015) completes the picture by turning the 19(1)(a)/Art. 14 lens on the State’s own advertisements. Real example to quote for the flip: after Tata Press, telephone directories and classifieds became “medium of information” business; before it, the same ads were mere merchandise.

s.3 Prohibition of ads of certain drugs for certain diseases“no person shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any drug in terms which suggest or are calculated to lead to the use of that drug for — (a) the procurement of miscarriage in women or prevention of conception in women; (b) the maintenance or improvement of the capacity of human beings for sexual pleasure; (c) the correction of menstrual disorder in women; (d) the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of any disease, disorder or condition specified in the Schedule, or any other … which may be specified in rules.” Provisos: rules adding diseases only for conditions “which require timely treatment in consultation with a registered medical practitioner or for which there are normally no accepted remedies”, and “after consultation with the Drugs Technical Advisory Board” (Drugs & Cosmetics Act 1940) + Ayurvedic/Unani experts if needed.The “truthful-ad ban” exhibit: s.3 is indifferent to veracity — the offence is complete if the ad merely “suggest[s] or [is] calculated to lead to the use” for a listed purpose. Elements: (i) publication/part-in-publication; (ii) advertisement referring to a drug; (iii) the suggestion/calculation nexus to (a)–(d). Mens-reading: strict regulatory offence; conviction still required for s.10A forfeiture.
s.4 Prohibition of misleading ads (relating to drugs)No part in publication of any advertisement relating to a drug which — (a) “directly or indirectly gives a false impression regarding the true character of the drug”; (b) “makes a false claim for the drug”; or (c) “is otherwise false or misleading in any material particular”.India’s standalone false-advertising criminal standard — 30 years before Central Hudson reached “misleading” analysis. Elements: (i) relating to a drug (any of the s.2(b) kinds); (ii) one of three falsity modes; (iii) “material particular” = consumer-facing quality/efficacy. Compare CPA 2019 s.2(47) civil twin; DMR is the penal one — and no de-minimis truth defence.
s.5 Magic-remedy prohibition“No person carrying on or purporting to carry on the profession of administering magic remedies shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any magic remedy which directly or indirectly claims to be efficacious for any of the purposes specified in section 3.”Bans the ostentatiously unverifiable: efficacy-claiming charms/talisman/mantra/kavacha for s.3 purposes. The clause “purporting to carry on the profession” closes the godman loophole; read with s.3(d) (printing liability) and the 54-disease Schedule.
s.6 Import/export banProhibits import into / export from India of documents containing s.3/s.4/s.5 advertisements; deemed prohibited goods under s.19 Sea Customs Act, 1878 (customs law successor today); seizure consequences.The 1954 Act anticipated cross-border advertising (satellite/foreign mags, now foreign sites): a physical-document analogue of today’s blocking powers.
s.7 PenaltyFirst conviction: “imprisonment which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both”; subsequent: “one year, or with fine, or with both”.Small penalties, heavy machinery — the design is deterrence-by-frequency. Cognizable per s.9A, so police may arrest without warrant (see s.9A box).

The “free commercial circulation” balance in one line: the Act regulates the merchant’s voice, protects the physician’s and the scientist’s, and exempts its own.

B3 · The Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986 — the dignity limb of ad-law

Uploaded bare Act (7 pp.) Act No. 60 of 1986, assent 23 December 1986, in force 2 October 1987 (G.S.R. 821(E)) — the J&K exclusion removed by Act 34 of 2019. Long title: “to prohibit indecent representation of women through advertisements or in publications, writings, paintings, figures or in any other manner…” The reader reprints ss.3, 4 and 6 verbatim inside Ajay Goswami (PDF pp. 227–228) — the Union of India’s own exhibit of “laws in existence” when asked to censor the press (Part C1).

SectionContentAnatomy
s.2(a) “advertisement”“includes any notice, circular, label, wrapper or other document and also includes any visible representation made by means of any light, sound, smoke or gas”.Four-senses-plus definition — wider than DMR’s by “gas”; billboard, projection, jingle, even theatrical display.

Put it together (one answer-paragraph)

Read with s.292 IPC (→ BNS 294) and the Obscene-ad provisions of the Cable Rules (r.7, Part B4), the IRW Act supplies the dignity-based regulation of even truthful advertising: a skin-cream advertisement may be perfectly honest about pH and still offend s.2(c) by denigrating women — the statute protects the depicted class, not the deceived consumer. Ajay Goswami is the case-book exhibit: the petitioner demanded precisely such a filter for “sex-oriented but not obscene” newspaper content; the Court refused a prior filter, and justified refusal by pointing to these very existing laws plus PCI norms (PDF pp. 184–186, 233).

C1 · Case 23 — Ajay Goswami v. Union of India, AIR 2007 SC 493 · facts, issues, arguments

Reader PDF pp. 221–240 (printed 178–197) · section header: “Media, Advertisement and Law — Protection of Minors from Harmful and Disturbing Material” · Dr. A.R. Lakshmanan, J. (for himself and Tarun Chatterjee, J.)
Facts (as printed)

“The Petitioner is a lawyer by profession. Respondent No.1 is Union of India, respondent No.2 is a statutory body [the Press Council of India], respondent Nos. 3 & 4 are the leading national daily newspapers [The Times of India; Hindustan Times] and respondent No.5 & 6 are news agencies.” The petition “involves a substantial question of law and public importance on the fundamental right of the citizens, regarding the freedom of speech and expression as enshrined under Article 19(1)(a)… The petitioner’s grievance is that the freedom of speech and expression enjoyed by the newspaper industry is not keeping balance with the protection of children from harmful and disturbing materials.” He conceded that the press may express its ideas, “but on the same hand, individual also has right to their own space and right not to be exposed against their will to other’s expressions of ideas and actions.” He asked the Court to direct the authorities “to provide for classification or introduction of a regulatory system” built on “reciprocal tolerance”: (a) acceptance of others’ rights to express and receive ideas; (b) “accepting that other people have the right not to be exposed against their will…”. The context: “the growing tendency among youngsters and minors in indulging in X-rated jokes, SMS and MMS.” (PDF pp. 221–222.)

The evidence-bank the Court records (¶1, verbatim-condensed): the petitioner produced newspaper clippings “only examples… of general nature”: double-meaning jokes that “cannot in any way leave healthy impact on the tender minds of the teenagers”; photographs captioned “the center of attention”, “double jeopardy”, “butt of course” — which “leave much for the thoughts of minors”; columns titled “moan for more”, “get that zing bag into your sex life”; MMS-clipping news items with nude photographs “with only small black stripes on the private parts”; and an article in The Times of India dated 1.8.2005 titled “Porn In potter VI” — the author “tried to read and suggest sexual messages in these lines. Children who were reading the book might not have any such inclination. However, after reading newspaper their mind would certainly wander to an area which the author might not have even conceived.” His thesis: “all sex oriented material [is] not always obscene or even indecent or immoral” — the effect must be judged by the adult standard (“reasonable strong minded, firm and courageous man”), but “No attempt has been made till date to define any yardstick for the minors whose tender minds are open for being polluted and are like plain state on which any painting can be drawn.” Exam use: this is the case-book’s canonical statement of the “truthful-but-harmful to a subgroup” advertising/content problem.

Issues
  1. “Is the material in newspaper really harmful for the minors?” (¶ heading, PDF p. 222) — i.e., may content lawful for adults be separately regulated to protect children?
  2. “Whether the minors have got any independent right enforceable under Article 32 of the Constitution?” — flowing from 19(1)(a), 21 read with 39(f) and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (the excerpt develops this through Anand Patwardhan, Lakshmikant Pandey (“supremely important national asset”), the National Policy for the Welfare of Children, Unnikrishnan, Kesavananda (Parts III + IV are “the conscience”), Brown v. Board, M.C. Mehta (PDF pp. 222–225)).
  3. Maintainability — can the Court issue a mandamus for legislation / direct rules regulating what newspapers may print?
  4. (By reference to the arguments) Whether the existing machinery — IPC s.292/293, the IRW Act, the PCI Act and the newspapers’ own vetting — is a sufficient answer, making judicially-imposed classification unnecessary?
Arguments — both sides

Petitioner-in-person, Mr Ajay Goswami — the five submissions printed at PDF p. 225–226: (i) newspapers publish sex-oriented material “which may not be obscene otherwise but still caters to prurient interest of the minor”; (ii) minors hold a 19(1)(a)+21+39(f)+UNCRC right, since they “are not capable of deciding themselves as to what is in the interest of their growth morally & culturally”; (iii) the corollary of the right to education: “newspapers cannot be allowed to disturb” the content-choices “decided by the parents, teachers and other experts”; (iv) “The State which has the duty to protect the minors by appropriate legislation or executive orders has failed in its duty. The Press Council of India which was constituted for preserving the freedom of the press… is a powerless body. No guidelines have been framed for the minors and adolescents in particular, which can be enforced in Court of law. The Council itself feels the necessity of some strong and effective measure to correct it”; (v) under Art. 32 + 142 the Court can issue guidelines (CAG v. K.S. Jagannathan; Vineet Narain — “where there is inaction even by executive for whatever reasons judiciary must step in”). Proposals (the relief menu, verbatim): (1) detailed guidelines to all newspapers about matter unsuitable for minors; (2) newspapers to have self-regulatory systems to assess publications against those guidelines; (3) papers carrying categorised material to be “packed in some different form” with bold front-page warning; (4) parents then free to instruct the news-vendor whether to deliver — “regulation at the receiving end and not at the source”, he stressed, “The petitioner is not in any way seeking restrain on the freedom of press or any censorship prior to the publication” — or, alternatively, an expert Committee on “ways and means for regulating the access of minors to adult oriented sexual, titillating or prurient material”.

Principles evolved (this unit’s reading)
  • Child-protection cannot justify source-side prior restraint on lawful adult content — Reno/Playboy/Butler applied; the American “reduce the adult population to what is fit for children” test is absorbed into Indian doctrine through this judgment.
  • The adequacy-of-existing-law answer is the template for every “regulate advertising/aesthetics” PIL: the Court enumerates (criminal law + PCI + self-vetting) and remits to norm-development — see the parallel refusal in Destruction ¶34 (Unit 4 D3).
  • “Regulation at the receiving end” (warning labels, packing, watershed, parental tools) is the doctrine-acceptable form of the child filter — precisely the DMR/Cable-r.7 technique of subject-matter and audience regulation, and today’s platform UI controls.
  • Advertising is where the press regulates itself best — the record of internal ad-scrutiny, refusal of liquor/sex-exploitative ads and school supplements is the case book’s exhibit for self-regulation actually working in the advertising domain (contrast the Uma-Khurana failure in broadcasting, Unit 4 E7).
  • The amendment mandate stands unimplemented: PCI s.14 punitive teeth (the 6.1.2002 letter’s proposed ss.14(2)(a)–(b)) were asked for in 1999–2003 and again here in 2006; still not enacted — the bridge to Unit 4’s B3/B4 (the Media Council movement).

D1 · Case 24 — Common Cause v. Union of India, (2015) 42 SCD 686 · facts, Committee, issues, arguments

Reader PDF pp. 242–253 (printed 198–209) · section header: “Media, Advertisement and Law — Guidelines For Regulating Content of Government Advertisements” · Ranjan Gogoi, J. (for himself and Pinaki Chandra Ghose, J.)
Facts & genesis (¶¶1–5, as printed)

“Common Cause and Centre for Public Interest Litigation, two registered bodies, have approached this Court under Article 32 of the Constitution seeking an appropriate writ to restrain the Union of India and all State Governments from using public funds on Government advertisements which are primarily intended to project individual functionaries of the Government or a political party”, and for guidelines “to regulate Government action in the matter so as to prevent misuse/wastage of public funds in connection with such advertisements.” The petitioners’ case (¶2) came in two halves — a concession and a charge: they conceded “the beneficial effect of government advertisements which convey necessary information to the citizens with regard to various welfare and progressive measures as also their rights and entitlements”; but charged that “in the garb of communicating with the people, in many instances, undue political advantage and mileage is sought to be achieved by personifying individuals and crediting such individuals or political leaders … as being responsible for various government achievements and progressive plans”, that the practice “becomes rampant on the eve of the elections”, and that such ads “not only result in gross wastage of public funds but constitute misuse of governmental powers besides derogating the fundamental rights of a large section of the citizens as guaranteed by Article 14 and 21.” (Real frame: the mass birthday-page campaigns of the then Union and State leaderships — e.g., the ₹50-crore estimate the 2014–15 press attributed to PM’s birthday wishes pages, and the Bihar/UP/Karnataka anniversary-page wars — External; reported.)

The Union’s resistance (¶3): “the issues sought to be raised pertain to governmental policies and executive decisions in respect of which it may not be appropriate for this Court to lay down binding guidelines under Article 142” — relying on Manzoor Ali Khan v. Union of India (no judicially-commanded pay revision/policy) and the Delhi High Court’s Umesh Mohan Sethi v. Union of India. Round one (¶4): by order dated 23.04.2014 the Court held that “there is no dispute that primary cause [purpose] of government advertisement is to use public funds to inform the public of their rights, obligations, and entitlements as well as to explain Government policies, programmes, services and initiatives”, that “only such government advertisements which do not fulfill the above requisites will fall foul”, acknowledged that “the dividing line between permissible advertisements that are a part of government messaging and advertisements that are politically motivated may at times gets blurred” — and, because “the materials laid before the Court by the parties were found to be inadequate for the purpose of evolving what would be the best practices keeping in view the prevailing scenario in other jurisdictions across the globe”, appointed a three-member Committee: Prof. (Dr.) N.R. Madhava Menon (former Director, National Judicial Academy, Bhopal), Mr T.K. Viswanathan (former Secretary General, Lok Sabha) and Mr Ranjit Kumar (Senior Advocate). Round two (¶5): the Committee submitted its report with draft guidelines; “It is the plea of the petitioner that the said guidelines should be approved by this Court and directions be issued under Article 142 … until an appropriate legislation in this regard is brought into effect by the Parliament.”

Issues
  1. Whether the expenditure of public money on “self-glorifying” government advertisements violates Art. 14 (arbitrariness/reasonableness of State action) and Art. 21 (the citizens’ informational environment), and what test governs?
  2. Is the subject justiciable — may the Court frame binding guidelines where the executive domain (DAVP policy) is unregulated by statute, till legislation is enacted (the Manzoor Ali Khan objection)?
  3. What should the content rules be — the Committee’s draft Guidelines: their principles, exceptions, and enforcement architecture?
  4. Four contested specifics: (a) publication of functionaries’ photographs; (b) an “Ombudsman”; (c) special performance audit; (d) a pre-election embargo — accept, modify or reject?
  5. Whether foreign “best practices” (Ontario; UK; Australia; NZ) can be imported wholesale, and by what method the Court should test their relevance to India?
Arguments — both sides
Petitioners (Common Cause; CPIL)The State (Union of India; State of Bihar — responses to the draft, ¶¶13–15)
  • Constitutional, not merely policy: every rupee spent is “taxpayer money” held in trust; the controlling tests are reasonableness and fairness under Art. 14 (¶7: “reasonableness and fairness consistent with Article 14 … would be the ultimate test of all State activities” — the Court’s own framing of the 23.04.2014 holding) and Part IV as “a guiding light for the Judicial organ” (¶7–8).
  • The rights-derogation limb: personality-cult advertising “derogates” the equality rights of voters and opposition supporters (¶2) — echoing the “only vehicle of political discourse” framing of CAB (reader, UIMPAA quote p. 242).
  • Remedy: approve the Committee Guidelines as Art. 142 directions, expressly interim — “until an appropriate legislation” (¶5): the Court is filling a self-admitted vacuum, not legislating.
  • Comparative warrant: Ontario-style object-list; pre-publication review mechanisms; Australian cost-benefit discipline for mega-campaigns (¶¶19–21 record these).
  • Non-justiciability: advertisements are executive communication-policy — cite Manzoor Ali Khan; guidelines under 142 would amount to a permanent policy-making by the Court (¶3).
  • Photographs (Bihar’s “emphatic” objection, ¶13): restricting images of functionaries “should not be imposed” — messaging efficacy.
  • Against the three specific levers (¶15): the Union “seriously disagrees” with (i) restricted publication of photographs, (ii) appointment of an Ombudsman, (iii) performance audit by each Ministry, and (iv) “embargo on advertisements on the eve of the elections” — each, it argued, is either already covered (CAAG audit; conduct rules) or illegitimately chills lawful information during polls (when citizens most need it).
  • Existing machinery: DAVP guidelines regulate placement and payment; the PRB Act (proposed amendment in 2004 on paid news) and the Model Code of Conduct during elections were the appropriate vehicles — regulation belongs to Parliament. External; reported (context from the legislative record; the reader itself records the DAVP content-gap in the Guidelines’ objects clause — “The DAVP Guidelines … only deal with the eligibility and empanelment … and not on how to regulate the content”).

Why this belongs in a MEDIA unit (and how to say it)

The case is nominally about expenditure; its true subject is the State’s use of the media market: government advertising is the largest single advert-buy in Indian newspapers (historically 20–40% of print ad revenues in the Hindi/regional markets — External; reported), so the ad-buy is simultaneously (i) a speech act (19(1)(a) + the right to know), (ii) a market lever (DAVP empanelment — see Goswami’s 18(d) suspension policy), and (iii) an electoral instrument (paid-news twin, Part F). The judgment regulates all three at once. A one-line thesis for the essay: “Common Cause is Unit 5’s mirror image of Shreya Singhal: where a criminal statute regulating private speech died of over-breadth, a set of judge-drafted principles regulating State speech survives by being audience-first.”

6. The five principles of content regulation — ①“Advertising Campaigns to be related to Government responsibilities: While it is the duty of the Government to provide the public with timely, accurate, clear, objective and complete information about its policies, programmes, services and initiatives since the public has a right to such information, the content of government advertisements should be relevant to the governments constitutional and legal obligations as well as the citizens rights and entitlements.”
②“Advertisement materials should be presented in an objective, fair and accessible manner and be designed to meet the objectives of the campaign: The material shall be presented in a fair and objective manner and shall be capable of fulfilling the intended objectives; Government shall exercise due caution while deciding the content, layout, size and design of the message including the target area and the creative requirement … in order to ensure that the maximum reach and impact are achieved in the most cost effective manner; Content of advertisement must enable the recipients of the information to distinguish between facts and analysis and where information is presented as a fact, it should be accurate and verifiable; Pre-existing policies, products, services and initiatives should not be presented as new unless there has been a substantial change or modification…; Content … should provide information in a manner that accommodates special needs of disadvantaged individuals or groups identified as being within the target audience; Multiple formats may be used to ensure equal access; Every effort shall be made to pre-test the material in case of large scale campaign with target audiences.”
③“Advertisement materials should be objective and not directed at promoting political interests of ruling party: Display material must be presented in objective language and be free of political argument or partisan standpoint: Government advertising shall maintain political neutrality and avoid glorification of political personalities and projecting a positive impression of the party in power or a negative impression of parties critical of the government. Government advertisement materials should avoid photographs of political leaders and if it is felt essential for effective Government messaging, only the photographs of the President/Prime Minster or Governor/Chief Minister should be used; Government advertisements shall not be used at patronizing media houses or aimed at receiving favourable reporting for the party or person in power.”
④“Advertisement Campaigns be justified and undertaken in an efficient and cost-effective manner: … it should be the policy of governments to use public funds in such a manner as to obtain maximum value for taxpayers money …; The Government shall decide and announce beforehand, a list of personalities on whose birth or death anniversaries, advertisements could be released every year and specify which Ministry/Department could release the same; Avoid the issue of multiple advertisements by different departments and PSUs of the same Government in Commemorative Advertisements and shall issue a single advertisement only; (d) Though advertising by governments should remain regulated all the time, it is particularly important to scrupulously follow these principles before and during the elections. Reading the Guidelines as a lawyer (four structural notes)

  1. They are content-neutral by design: every prohibition targets form (party name, symbol, photograph, novelty-claims, duplication), never the message — the UIMPAA test for permissible compelled/carried speech exactly (Part E2).
  2. Principle ⑤ exports the media-law stack — privacy, IP, election law, consumer protection, broadcasting law — into the government’s own advertisement vetting: the State adopts the very code (Parts B1–B4) it enforces on merchants.
  3. Enforcement is administrative + market-based: self-certification, audits, published spend, and (the radical clause) sanctions by “the regulatory bodies of print and electronic media” against media groups “seeking or obtaining government advertisements” — i.e., the DAVP-reward/punishment mechanism is turned against papers that solicit state ads (echo of Goswami’s 18(d) read in reverse: 18(d) punished papers found by the PCI to misbehave; this clause punishes papers that beg for government ads).
  4. Sunset built in: “till a suitable legislation is enacted” (application clause 2(2)) + clause 8’s legislation direction — a textbook Art. 142 “fill the vacuum, invite Parliament” design (¶7 of the judgment states the theory — see D3).

D3 · Common Cause — the Court’s analysis, the four departures, decision and principles

The judicial method (¶¶7–9) — three sentences to quote in any answer
“A policy certainly cannot be axed for its alleged failure to comply with any of the provisions of Part IV. Neither can the Courts charter a course, merely on the strength of the provisions of the said Part of the Constitution, if the effect thereof would be to lay down a policy. However, in a situation where the field is open and uncovered by any government policy, to guide and control everyday governmental action, surely, in the exercise of jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution, parameters can be laid down by this Court consistent with the objects enumerated by any of the provisions of Part IV. Such an exercise would be naturally time bound i.e. till the Legislature or the Executive, as the case may be, steps in to fulfill its constitutional role and authority by framing an appropriate policy.” — ¶7 (Case Book PDF p. 247, printed p. 204)

Then the fiscal morality: “Article 38 and 39 of the Constitution enjoin upon the State a duty to consistently endeavour to achieve social and economic justice to the teeming millions … What can be the surer way in the march forward than by ensuring avoidance of unproductive expenditure of public funds” (¶8) — and the interpretive rule that made the case about types of advertisements, not single ads: “It is neither possible nor feasible or even necessary to try and encompass the myriad situations … Nevertheless, an attempt can be made to arrive at a broad categorization for the purpose of an illustrative understanding” (¶9).

The objections and the four departures (¶¶13–15, 22–28)
  • Bihar’s only “emphatic” response was against the photograph restriction (¶13); the Union “seriously disagrees” on four counts — (1) restricted publication of photographs; (2) appointment of an Ombudsman; (3) performance audit by each Ministry; (4) embargo on advertisements on the eve of the elections (¶15). All other recommendations: “comprehensive and based on an analytical approach of the best practices prevailing in other jurisdictions … would serve public interest … commend to the Court for acceptance and are accordingly accepted” (¶17).
  • Comparative method (¶¶18–21) — a passage worth quoting on judicial borrowing: “there can be no blind adherence … the correct approach will be to discern some of the best practices prevailing in such jurisdictions and thereafter to test the relevance of the same to our own country.” Ontario: the three lawful objects (inform of current/proposed policies, programmes or services; inform of rights and responsibilities; “encourage or discourage specific social behaviour in public interest”); “advertisements are not to include the name, voice or image of any functionary of the State”; primary objective must not be fostering a positive impression of the ruling government or a negative one of critics. Australia/UK: cost-effectiveness emphasis — “advertising campaigns of more than … 1 million Australian dollars require to undergo a cost benefit analysis … before launching”. Some jurisdictions run “a mechanism for review of advertisements on fixed parameters even before they are published” — noted, and not imported for India: the Guidelines keep review administrative (self-certification), not censorial. (¶21: the Committee’s work “fortif[ies] our conviction”.)
  • Departure 1 — Photographs: the Court went FURTHER than the Committee (¶¶22–23). “Publication of the photograph of an individual be a State or party functionary not only has the tendency of associating that particular individual with either the achievement(s) sought to be highlighted or being the architect of the benefits … Photographs, therefore, have the potential of developing the personality cult and the image of one or a few individuals which is a direct antithesis of democratic functioning.” The Committee’s exception (President/PM; Governor/CM photographs allowed if “essential”) was rejected: “there should be an exception only in the case of the President, Prime Minister and Chief Justice of the country who may themselves decide the question. Commemorative advertisements of “acknowledged personalities like the father of the nation” would of course carry the photograph of the departed leader.” (Governors and Chief Ministers lost the privilege — note the asymmetry and its logic: constitutional dignities vs political office.)
  • Departure 2 — Ombudsman → a three-member body (¶24): “for ironing out the creases that are bound to show from time to time in the implementation of the present directions and to oversee such implementation the government should constitute a three member body consisting of persons with unimpeachable neutrality and impartiality and who have excelled in their respective fields. We could have but we refrain from naming the specific persons and leave the said exercise to be performed by the Union Government.”
  • Departure 3 — no special performance audit (¶25): “the machinery available is adequate to ensure due performance as well as accountability and proper utilization of public money” (CAG’s existing audit).
  • Departure 4 — no pre-election embargo (¶26): “If Government advertisements adhere to the objects and parameters mentioned above we do not feel the necessity of imposing a special curb on government advertisements on the eve of the elections” — the Committee’s “as far as possible, … only advertisements required by law” restriction was left out of the binding set. Comment in answers: the embargo logic survives anyway through the Model Code of Conduct once elections are announced External; reported; the Court preferred subject-neutral content rules over time-based silencing — the classic least-restrictive choice.
Decision — operative order (¶¶27–29)

“We close the matters on the aforesaid note by approving and adopting the recommendations of the Committee except what has been specifically indicated above with regard to (1) publication of photographs of the Government functionaries and political leaders along with the advertisement(s), (2) appointment of an Ombudsman, (3) the recommendation with regard to performance audit by each Ministry, and (4) embargo on advertisements on the eve of the elections.” + the fairness rider (¶27: “the concepts of fairness and even dispensation to all media/publishing houses will have to be maintained by the Government be it at the Centre or the States”) + the self-limitation clause (¶29: directions “cannot be comprehensive … The gaps, if any, we are confident would be filled up by the executive arm of the government itself inasmuch as the attainment of constitutional goals and values enshrined in Part IV … is the conjoint responsibility of the three organs of the State”).

Principles evolved
  1. Public money is a fiduciary constraint on public speech: the State may speak to citizens through advertisements only on the “to inform” objects (rights, entitlements, policies, programmes, dangers); personality projection is outside the power — the test is reasonableness + fairness + public purpose under Art. 14 (¶7 via the 23.04.2014 order).
  2. Art. 142 gap-filling is legitimate but “naturally time bound” — interim legislation-substitute till Parliament acts (¶7); clause 8 of the Guidelines itself directs legislation.
  3. Content-neutrality is the constitutionality-anchor: every binding rule regulates form, duplication, funding discipline — never viewpoint; the Court declined pre-publication censorship devices (¶20) and pre-election embargoes (¶26).
  4. Personality-cult in state advertising is “a direct antithesis of democratic functioning” (¶23) — the most quotable sentence of the case.
  5. Equal dispensation of advertisements is itself a press-freedom doctrine (¶10, ¶27) — completing the Goswami 18(d) picture: the same instrument that can punish papers for “unethical practices” must not be used to patronise them; DAVP allocation is constitutional subject-matter.
  6. Commemoration discipline: one government-wide advertisement for national icons; institutional self-glorification (the “centenary of the Patna High Court” type) impermissible.

Aftermath to keep one line long External; reported: the directions have since been the subject of compliance proceedings before the Supreme Court, and official advertising-policy documents have tracked the Guidelines’ language — no consolidating statute has yet been enacted: the “till the legislature steps in” clause is still running.

E2 · Must-carry dossier — Union of India v. Motion Picture Association (the reader’s own 5.2 authority)

Reader PDF pp. 283–288 (printed 240–245) · filed by the compilation under the censorship chapter — supplied to 5.2 because it is the case book’s fullest treatment of compelled carriage of the State’s message · no judge byline is printed in this copy; the excerpt opens with the statutory matrix
Facts (as printed)

The impugned regime, per the excerpt: s.12(4) Cinematograph Act, 1952 (Central Government directions to licensees to exhibit scientific/educational/news-and-current-events/documentary/indigenous films), s.5(3) West Bengal Cinemas (Regulation) Act, 1954 (State directions “deemed to be additional conditions” of the licence), and Notification No. 7277-F dated 20.9.1957: every exhibition to carry “notified films of such length as bears to the length of other films … approximately the ratio of one to five”, with caps (max 2,000 ft 35mm / 800 ft 16mm; no continuous exhibition beyond two weeks; no re-exhibition after two weeks; availability limits) and at least half the notified-film time reserved to films approved by the Central Government on the Films Advisory Board’s recommendation; and licence conditions 15 (lantern slides/shorts, ≤15 minutes, free or at administrator-fixed remuneration) and 22 (approved films ≤600 metres 35mm) of the Delhi Cinematograph Rules. In practice exhibitors contracted with the Films Division (near-monopoly producer) at a rental of 1% of net weekly collection, “fixed as a result of negotiations with the Films Federation of India”. The exhibitors (challenging in 1993) did not contest the content of the films; they contested the compulsion, and the Delhi High Court struck the rental condition as unconstitutional, held the Films-Division-sourcing obligation “onerous”, and condition 15 as redundant — while upholding the statutory provisions and directing free supply at each theatre; the Union appealed, and the Eastern India Motion Pictures Association’s writ petition followed.

Issues
  1. Does compulsory exhibition violate Art. 19(1)(a) — is “compelled speech” (“must carry”) the mirror of restraint?
  2. Does Art. 19(2) even apply — or does a content-neutral, information-serving compulsion not amount to a restriction at all?
  3. How far may a licence-condition (Delhi Cinematograph Rules condition 15) go before it is “too wide”?
  4. How is the illiterate-mass audience factored into the constitutional balance?
Arguments

Exhibitors: 19(1)(a) violation — “They also contend that Article 19(2) … does not cover this kind of compulsion to show educational, scientific and documentary films or other kinds of films specified in the above provisions”; “just as a restraint on free speech is a violation of Article 19(1) … compelled speech, often known as a ‘must carry’ provision in a statute, rule or regulation, is equally an infringement of the right to free speech, except to the extent permitted under Article 19(2)”; the compulsory films are content-specified, hence not content-neutral as American doctrine requires (they invoked Wooley v. Maynard, 430 US 705 — the “Live Free or Die” flag on the licence plate was held an invasion of First Amendment rights, “to promote an appreciation of history, individualism and state pride” insufficiently).

Union: the films “promote dissemination of information and education”, not propaganda; the purpose is constitutional (Part IV; Article 39?); the High Court had already read condition 15 down (struck as “too wide”, restored by reading it with Cinematograph Act s.12(4)); no compulsion to carry partisan views.

Decision (reader’s text, verbatim-condensed)
  • The doctrinal frame (quoting CAB at 213): “The right to communicate, therefore, includes right to communicate through any media that is available whether print or electronic or audio-visual such as advertisement, movie, article, speech etc.”; with Express Newspapers (“Freedom of thought and expression … are basic to a democratic form of Government …”) and Rangarajan (“democracy is a government by the people via open discussion”) anchoring the value; and the same-value list includes Life Insurance Corporation v. Manubhai Shah (state must not silence — the right to publish one’s reply — cited in the reader at PDF p. 286 area).
  • The must-carry theory — the paragraph to copy: “whether compelled speech will or will not amount to a violation of the freedom of speech and expression, will depend on the nature of a ‘must carry’ provision. If a ‘must carry’ provision furthers informed decision-making which is the essence of the right to free speech and expression, it will not amount to any violation of the fundamental freedom of speech and expression. If, however, such a provision compels a person to carry out propaganda or project a partisan or distorted point of view, contrary to his wish, it may amount to a restraint on his freedom of speech and expression.” Then the two American exemplars the reader digests (PDF pp. 286–287): food ingredient lists and cigarette warnings as “beneficial ‘must carry’ provisions meant to inform the public” (the advertising hook for Unit 5!); Turner Broadcasting v. FCC, 512 US 622 (1994/97) — cable must-carry of local broadcast signals upheld, “the purpose … was to preserve the benefits of free over-the-air local broadcast television, promoting wide-spread dissemination of information from a multiplicity of sources and promoting fair competition”, with Breyer J.’s balancing passage on operators’ programming-choice vs “the widest possible dissemination of information from diverse and antagonistic sources”.
  • The application: “When a substantially significant population body is illiterate or does not have easy access to ideas or information, it is important that all available means of communication, particularly audiovisual communication, are utilized not just for entertainment but also for education, information, propagation of scientific ideas … To earmark a small portion of time of this entertainment medium … has to be looked at in this context of promoting dissemination of ideas, information and knowledge to the masses so that there may be an informed debate and decision making on public issues. Clearly, the impugned provisions are designed to further free speech and expression and not to curtail it.” + “None of these statutory provisions require the exhibitor to show a propaganda film or a film conveying views which he objects to.” + Films Division’s monopoly is factual, not statutory; the NOC is verification of compliance; condition 15 read with s.12(4) has “a direct nexus with the object” and is a reasonable restriction; “therefore, Article 19(2) is not attracted” (for the primary right, no infringement; if at all, saved).
  • Operative: appeals allowed; the High Court’s strike-down of condition 15 and its direction to Films Division reversed; writ petition dismissed; no costs.
Principles evolved (for 5.2)
  1. Compelled carriage is judged by purpose, not by the fact of compulsion: information-furthering must-carry (educational shorts; DD/Parliament channels) survives; propaganda-compulsion is the line (the Common Cause Guidelines’ “distinguish between facts and analysis” clause is the same principle turned into a code for the State itself).
  2. “Informed decision-making is the essence of the right” — the unit’s governing phrase tying commercial speech (Tata Press), public-service broadcasting (Prasar Bharati s.12) and government advertising (Common Cause “to inform”) to one standard.
  3. Audience-first justification: illiteracy and access gaps can convert a licence fee into a speech-promoting device — use it also for the CABLE must-carry (s.8) and the radio-bulletin integrity rule (2026 draft r.44).
  4. Truthful-advertising echo: the court itself cites the cigarette-warning/ingredient-list cases as legitimate compelled speech — the strongest case-book authority for Part A2’s proposition that even truthful ad regulation (warnings, disclosures, labelling) needs no 19(2) defence: it advances autonomy.

Note the reader’s byline for this excerpt is “Union of India v. Motion Picture Association & Anr.” (reported (1999) 6 SCC 150; the 1978 vintage of the rules and the 1990s hearing sequence External; reported); the excerpt as printed is the authority — cite it as “Case Book, UIMPAA excerpt, PDF pp. 285–288”.

Doctrine pack — five sentences to conclude any 5.2 answer
  1. The State as advertiser is held to a stricter standard than the State as censor: reasonableness + fairness + public purpose (Common Cause ¶7) — because the currency is the taxpayer’s, and the audience is the electorate.
  2. Political advertising is constitutionally core speech when the speaker is a citizen/party (19(1)(a) + free elections), but a privilege when the speaker is the government (no right to self-praise at public cost).
  3. Every political message has an accounting identity: undisclosed spend is the offence (Chavan), not the message — India polices political advertising through the purse, not the content.
  4. Must-carry of official channels is valid only as information-furthering compulsion (UIMPAA; s.8 Cable Act; r.31(2)/r.44 2026 draft) — propaganda-compulsion is the constitutional line (UIMPAA’s own sentence: “None of these statutory provisions require the exhibitor to show a propaganda film”).

F1 · Paid news — the counterfeit advertisement: Ashok Shankarrao Chavan v. Madhavrao Kinhalkar, (2014) 7 SCC 99 (Case 37 of the reader, Unit 8.1)

Reader PDF pp. 324–335 (printed 281–292) · Fakkir Mohamed Ibrahim Kalifulla, J. (for himself and S.S. Nijjar, JJ.) · full dossier; the syllabus pairs it with “Report on Paid News, PCI, 2010; PRB Act 1867 (as amended)…; Issues Related to Paid News”
The “simple yet important question” (¶2, verbatim)

“…as to whether the Election Commission, under Section 10A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, can conduct an enquiry to determine the falsity of the return of election expenses by an elected candidate, especially after a decision is rendered by the High Court in the Election Petition preferred by the Respondent No.1.”

Facts — three appeals, one phenomenon (¶¶3–5, 51–55 as printed)
  • Appeal 1 (the Chavan case): Maharashtra Assembly election, results 22.10.2009; the appellant (then Chief Minister) declared elected from Bokar; his rival Dr. Madhavrao Kinhalkar’s account showed a measly “Rs.5379/-” as newspaper-advertisement expenditure; complaints to the ECI (2.12.2009) by Shri Mukhtar Abbas Naqvi, M.P. “and five others”, by Dr Kirit Somaiya “and four others”, and by the rival candidate itself, alleged that during the campaign the appellant had caused “several advertisements” to appear in Lokmat, Pudhari, Maharashtra Times and Deshonnati “in the garb of news eulogizing him and his achievements as Chief Minister of Maharashtra” — “the huge expenditure … which they described, was incurred or authorized by the Appellant for getting those advertisements published as news and is now a well-known phenomenon, as ‘paid news’” — suppressed from the s.77 account. The election petition (to the High Court as Election Tribunal) failed for want of particulars (18.10.2012) and a consequential appeal was dismissed (21.1.2013); the ECI’s jurisdiction to enquire was upheld by the Delhi High Court (30.9.2011). The four newspapers told the ECI the “paid news” were “in fact news or editorials and supplements published gratuitously as they had links with or leanings towards the Congress Party and the Appellant” (¶52) — the classic paid-news defence, recorded verbatim by the reader.
  • Appeal 2 (Jharkhand): the ECI, 15 months after the DEO’s report, issued s.10A show-cause; the account declared ₹18,92,353; a summary of Income Tax Department findings showed “the election expenses incurred to the tune of Rs.9,32,56,259/-” against an authorised ceiling of “Rs. 25,00,000” “as per Section 77 read with Rule 90” (¶53) — the machinery of cross-verification in one sentence.
  • Appeal 3 (the Umlesh Yadav case — the leading real example of this unit): UP Assembly 2007; on 17.4.2007 — the day before polling, after the campaign window’s close — “Amar Ujala” and “Dainik Jagaran” carried blocks stating “there is a wave in favour of the Appellant in the election and the voters have made up their mind to support the Appellant.” The rival candidate complained to the Press Council of India (27.4.2007); the newspapers replied that the item “was not a news item but an advertisement”, “at the bottom, the word ‘ADVT’ was appended”, material “was given to the Press on behalf of the Appellant and was not materials collected by the correspondents.” The PCI, by order dated 31.03.2010, “held that the publication though camouflaged as news items, in reality it was only an advertisement” and that the two newspapers “were guilty of ethical violation”. The ECI then summoned the bills: Dainik Jagran had issued “a bill of Rs. 21,250/- in the name of Pramod Mishra … client name was mentioned as D.P. Yadav and the amount was paid in cash”; Amar Ujala’s bill “of Rs.8,000/- in the name of D.P. Yadav was issued and paid”, while the candidate’s account showed only “an amount of Rs.840” for “a small box size 7 x 6 cms” paid to Amar Ujala. The ECI disqualified her for three years (order 20.10.2011, u/ss.78 & 10A); the Allahabad High Court Division Bench upheld it; this Court (¶¶112–117) saw “no glaring illegality” — the first paid-news disqualification in India, sustained.
Issues
  1. Does the ECI’s s.10A power extend to an enquiry into the truthfulness of an account of election expenses lodged under s.78 — or is the enquiry confined to default in lodging (form, not substance), per Sucheta Kripalani?
  2. Does Art. 329(b)/s.80 RPA (exclusive election-petition jurisdiction) bar a parallel s.10A enquiry once the election petition has failed?
  3. Can the ECI act on complaints of any citizen (here: a sitting M.P. and party office-bearers), or only on the DEO’s report; is s.10A subject to any limitation?
  4. May expenditure incurred by a political party on an advertisement be attributed to the candidate? (the Explanation-1/“rebuttable presumption” question)
  5. And — beneath all: how far is the “paid news” phenomenon addressable through election law at all, when the papers deny payment and the material “leans towards” the candidate?
Arguments — both sides (the reader’s own roll of counsel)

Mr Gopal Subramaniam, Sr. Adv. for the appellant-candidate (the full (a)–(j) set at PDF pp. 324–326): Art. 329(b) + s.80 make the election petition the sole channel; s.10A is “only a default disqualification and not a stigmatized one”, based only on the DEO’s report; pre-amendment s.7(c) read in Sucheta Kripalani = formal compliance test only; no procedural code exists for such an enquiry (“rules of pleading, powers of the Code of Civil Procedure … limitation, adding of proper parties, applicability of the Evidence Act” all missing → “anomalous consequences”); complainants (Naqvi, Somaiya) had no locus standi (neither voters nor aggrieved candidates); L.R. Shivaramagowda distinguishable; Art. 324 powers can fill only a vacuum; “Law of the election being a special law, its intendment as well as effects will have to be found in the given law and not outside of it … where the Constitution leaves any ambiguity, the benefit of the doubt should be given to the subject as against the legislature.”

Mr Jayant Bhushan for the rival candidate (¶13): s.10A read with ss.77(1)–(2), 78 and Rules 86–89 gives the ECI alone the competence to test the account’s correctness; s.10A is “independent of Article 329(b)”; satisfaction may rest “on a report of the DEO or after hearing the parties or upon an enquiry … as per Rule 89, which uses the expression ‘as it thinks fit’”; “the present allegation … is paid news and advertisements, which were not accounted for and which having not been disclosed by the Appellant in the return, have now come to light through the Press Council and other sources. Therefore, it could not have been within the knowledge of the DEO…”; the election petition’s dismissal for want of particulars does not estop the ECI; L.R. Shivaramagowda governs; Sucheta Kripalani’s substratum erased by the 1956 and 1966 amendments. Mr Ashok Desai for the Election Commission (¶19): “free and fair election is the basic feature of our democracy, which again is the basic structure”; Art. 324 is “plenary” and obliges the Commission; under s.10A any person including an elector may complain (vs s.123(6) — only contesting candidates, 45-day limitation under s.81); election petitions are tried by the High Court, s.10A disqualification by the ECI — distinct jurisdictions, complementary purposes.

Decision (the Court’s own words, ¶¶55, 111–118)

The Court first set the socio-legal scene — an unusual paragraph worth quoting in any answer: “In recent times, when elections are being held it is widely reported in the Press and Media that money power plays a very vital role. Going by such reports and if it is true then it is highly unfortunate that many of the voters are prepared to sell their votes for a few hundred rupees … If such practices are to be simply ignored and a laudable object with which the Act has been brought into the statute book as early as in the year 1950 … it must be stated that such an authority who is in ultimate control in the matter of holding of the elections should be held to be invested with the widest power of its kind specified in the Act. Therefore, when it comes to the question of interpretation of the extent of such power … the Court should have a very liberal approach … This view of ours is more so apt in the present day context, wherein money power virtually controls the whole field of election” (¶55). Holding: “Section 10A clothes the Election Commission with the requisite power and authority to enquire into the allegations relating to failure to submit the accounts of election expenses in the manner prescribed” — the ECI order “is perfectly justified”; because the membership period would end, the Commission was directed to conclude proceedings within 45 days, “hold[ing] the proceedings on a day to day basis” if necessary, to prevent non-cooperation (¶111). Principles evolved (for 5.2 and for advertising law)
  1. The truth of the account, not just its filing, is within the ECI’s s.10A remit — Sucheta Kripalani’s form-only reading stands revised; L.R. Shivaramagowda affirmed: s.10A “independent of Article 329(b)”.
  2. Paid news is legally an unaccounted advertisement: the operative question is never “was the content true?” but “was the rupee shown?” — content-neutrality by design, like the Common Cause principles.
  3. The “Advt” label is evidence, not immunity: both newspapers’ defence — “it was an advertisement” — convicted the candidate (the bill), while the PCI found the medium guilty of ethical violation for letting it masquerade as news (¶54, PCI order 31.3.2010). The dual-track remedy: RPA for the purse, PCI for the practice.
  4. Attribution presumptions: party-sponsored ad spend presumptively the candidate’s (rebuttable); the Explanation-1 travel carve-out is exhaustive; cash bills and client-name mismatches rebut the presumption decisively.
  5. Any citizen can trigger a s.10A enquiry (unlike s.123(6) corrupt-practice petitions); no limitation — but “reasonable time” — the ECI may use DEO reports, I-T findings, and the Press Council record as evidence.
  6. Art. 324 + the basic-structure argument is the interpretive engine: “widest power … liberal approach” where electoral integrity is at stake — cite alongside ADR-lineage (the same Court-craft the reader saw at Goswami: guidelines filling legislative vacuums).

G1 · PYQ Q8(D) (Dec-2024, 10 marks) — “Commercial speech under Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution of India” · full model answer

Question 8(D) [10] — model answer (write: meaning → journey → content of protection → the two regulatory limbs → conclusion)

1. Meaning. “Commercial speech” is expression whose function is to propose a commercial transaction — advertising of goods, services, ideas of trade — whose constitutional status in India moved from non-speech to protected-but-heavily-regulable speech. The uploaded MIB compilation states both poles: in Hamdard Dawakhana v. Union of India, testing the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act, “an advertisement is no doubt a form of speech and expression but every advertisement is not a matter dealing with the expression of ideas and hence advertisement of a commercial nature cannot fall within the concept of Article 19(1)(a)” (PDF p. 84); in Tata Press Ltd. v. MTNL (the Golden-Pages directory dispute — facts per the reports, flagged; holding per the notes), “a three judge bench … differed from the view expressed in the Dawakhana case and held that ‘commercial advertisement’ was definitely a part of Article 19(1)(a) as it aimed at the dissemination of information regarding the product” — while making it “clear that the government could regulate commercial advertisements, which are deceptive, unfair, misleading and untruthful” (PDF p. 84).

2. Why the flip matters — the listener. The protection is not the advertiser’s profit motive but the audience’s autonomy: the consumer’s right to receive information about products and prices, the “free commercial circulation of ideas”. Read this with the line the reader preserves in Shreya Singhal ¶9 — Beg, J. in Bennett Coleman: “the freedom of speech and of the press is the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy because public criticism is essential to the working of its institutions” (PDF p. 32) — and with the “marketplace of ideas” passage the same judgment quotes (¶11). Commercial information is what makes the market’s own democratic decisions (what to buy, whom to vote for, which paper to read) informed; the MIB-notes’ Sakal summary adds the structural point: the State cannot convert “restrictions on the business activity of the citizens” into speech restrictions, since “the right … couldn’t be taken away with the object of placing restrictions on the business activity” (PDF p. 83). Advertising revenue is the press’s lifeblood — the reader’s ABP excerpt records the Majithia Committee’s arithmetic: “The gross revenue of newspaper establishments comprises revenue through advertisements, circulation …” (PDF p. 25) — so regulating ads is regulating the medium itself.

3. Content of the protection. Four components: (i) publication of truthful, non-deceptive matter about lawful trade/profession (also “commercial use of one’s identity” — the reader’s privacy chapter records the right of publicity and the Haelan line, PDF p. 58); (ii) the right to receive (CAB: “right to acquire information and to disseminate it”, reader p. 242 — medium-neutral: “any media … whether print or electronic or audio-visual”); (iii) access media-claim: classifieds/directories are protected publication (Tata Press); (iv) for the press, the anti-patrimonial limb (Sakal/Bennett Coleman; and, from the other side of the same ledger, the Common Cause rule that the State’s ad-buy must be even-handed — “Patronization of any particular media house(s) must be avoided”, ¶10, PDF p. 250).

4. Limb one — “regulating what is false or misleading”. These categories lie outside the protection: DMR s.4 (false impression “regarding the true character of the drug”, false claim, “otherwise false or misleading in any material particular”); Cable Rules r.7 (no ad claiming “special or miraculous or supernatural property or quality, which is difficult of being proved”; no “false and suggestive innuendos” for programmes; enforcement s.6 + s.19/20 + s.16 penal); Consumer protection (MRTP-era law described in the MIB-notes’ stack; today CPA 2019’s “unfair trade practice” incl. false representation, with CCPA powers — External; reported); the criminal backstop: cheating/property-inducement (420 → BNS 318(4)), forgery for cheating (468/469 → BNS 336(3)/(4)), obscenity by print (292 → BNS 294). No constitutional defence attaches to deception: the Shreya Singhal overbreadth discipline cuts both ways — a restriction valid against lies is not overbroad for covering them.

5. Limb two — “may regulate even truthful advertising”. Truth is no licence when the harm is contextual: (a) subject-matter bans — DMR s.3 + the 54-disease Schedule (even an accurate “cures diabetes” ad is prohibited: conditions that “require timely treatment in consultation with a registered medical practitioner” are “normally no accepted remedies” — the statute’s own proviso), magic-remedy ban s.5, tobacco/alcohol/infant-food bans in r.7, Emblems/State Emblem Acts; (b) audience/time-place-manner — Young Persons (Harmful Publications) Act; Indecent Representation of Women Act ss.3–4 (denigrating depiction banned whatever its truth); the watershed logic the reader imports through Goswami — Reno v. ACLU (“the Government may not reduce the adult population to only what is fit for children”) and Playboy (“these judgments are for the individual to make, not for the government to decree”, PDF pp. 236–237): regulation must ride on access devices, not on gagging adults; (c) compelled commercial speech — the reader’s UIMPAA page of doctrine: ingredient lists, cigarette warnings as “beneficial ‘must carry’ provisions”, Wooley v. Maynard’s “propaganda” limit, Turner’s diversity rationale (PDF pp. 286–287): even mandated disclosures are policed for viewpoint-neutrality; (d) electoral context — truthful political advertising still gets accounted: s.77 RPA + Chavan; the State’s own truthful achievements-ads must strip the leader’s photograph (Common Cause ¶23: personality-cult is “a direct antithesis of democratic functioning”). The doctrinal test to name: a tiered review — the U.S. four-part Central Hudson standard (lawful activity; substantial interest; direct advancement; narrow fit — External; reported) — in India operates through statute design + Art. 19(2) heads (defamation; “decency or morality”; incitement; “interests of the general public” under 19(6) for the business of advertising), the 2020s proportionality idiom (Puttaswamy line) supplying the meta-rule.

6. Conclusion. Under Art. 19(1)(a), commercial speech today enjoys (i) full-throated protection for truthful dissemination to a willing audience; (ii) no protection for the deceptive, unfair, misleading, untruthful; and (iii) a wide regulatory zone for truthful-but-contextually-harmful advertising — bounded by legality of the regulated activity, subject-specific statutes rather than discretionary censorship, and content-neutrality on compelled-carriage. The unit’s own closing pair supplies the symmetry: Goswami shows courts refusing a pre-clearance regime that the press demanded against titillating-but-lawful ads (2007), while Common Cause shows courts imposing an anti-personality code the State refused (2015) — “the speech may be true; the power must still be justified.” (If asked to cite cases: Hamdard; Tata Press; Sakal; Bennett Coleman (via Shreya ¶9); Ajay Goswami; UIMPAA; Chavan; Common Cause — all documented in the project at the pages given above.)

G2 · PYQ Q8(A) (Dec-2024, 10 marks) — “Paid News—A threat to independent and fair media—Elucidate” · full model answer

Question 8(A) [10] — model answer (define → map the harm to each adjective in the question → the law that already exists → what Chavan adds → reforms)

1. What paid news is. Paid news is a commercial transaction disguised as journalism: an advertiser pays, and the medium publishes “news” — the Supreme Court’s own words for the phenomenon in Ashok Shankarrao Chavan v. Madhavrao Kinhalkar, (2014) 7 SCC 99 (Case Book PDF p. 329): advertisements “which appeared in those newspapers in the garb of news eulogizing him and his achievements as Chief Minister of Maharashtra … is now a well-known phenomenon, as ‘paid news’.” Structurally it is false labelling, not false content: the Umlesh Yadav blocks of 17.4.2007 admitted an “Advt” footnote, and the newspapers told the Press Council the material “was not news … was an advertisement” (PDF pp. 331–332) — while the Press Council held the opposite in substance: “the publication though camouflaged as news items, in reality it was only an advertisement” and the papers were “guilty of ethical violation.”

2. Why it threatens independent media. (i) It converts the press’s lifeblood — advertising revenue (the Majithia arithmetic reproduced in the reader’s ABP excerpt: “The gross revenue of newspaper establishments comprises revenue through advertisements, circulation …”, PDF p. 25) — into a covert instrument of capture: the paper that sells a “news” story for cash has inverted the trust the F.S. Nariman Committee named first: “Professional journalists operate as trustees of the public” (Destruction ¶31(a), PDF p. 204). (ii) It arms the patron: DAVP empanelment already doubles as reward/punishment (Goswami’s clause 18(d), PDF p. 185); paid news privatizes that lever to any paying master. (iii) It defeats self-regulation from within — the PCI’s only tool is censure (P.H. Parekh: “Lack of punitive powers … has tied its hands”, PDF p. 228), and a censored newspaper that lives on the money has little to lose; the 1999–2003 amendment requests (de-recognition/accreditation-withdrawal) remain un-enacted. (iv) It corrodes the reader’s epistemics — Goswami’s remedy was “a culture of ‘responsible reading’”, but paid news is designed to be irresponsible-proof to the reader.

3. Why it threatens fair media — and fair elections. In the electoral market, information is the campaign currency: paid news (a) conceals spend from the statutory ceiling — s.77 requires “a separate and correct account of all expenditure in connection with the election”, s.78 demands lodgement within 30 days, and suppression makes “the account … clearly untrue” (Allahabad Division Bench quoted at PDF p. 333); (b) escapes scrutiny and destroys the equality premise of spending caps (in Chavan itself: ₹5,379 declared against “several crores”, and in the Jharkhand appeal ₹18.9 lakh declared against ₹9.32 crore found — PDF pp. 329, 331); (c) lets the candidate buy the very watchdog: the press “guilty of ethical violation” while the elector is “taken for a ride” (¶55); (d) converts public-office achievement into private advertisement without accountability — precisely what Common Cause forbids the State itself to do (photographs and party-credit clauses, ¶6③/¶23).

4. The legal instruments — state them by layer. Electoral: RPA ss.77–78 (accounting), Rules 89 (DEO report → ECI), s.10A (disqualification for 3 years by gazette order; s.11 removal for reasons), s.123(1)/(6)/(7) corrupt-practice jurisdiction through petition, Art. 324 plenary supervisory power; Chavan’s synthesis: the ECI “has got every jurisdiction” to enquire into the falsity of the return, s.10A being “independent of Article 329(b)”, any citizen may complain, and party-sponsored ad-spend is presumptively the candidate’s (rebuttable — Explanation-1’s narrow travel exception), with the Press Council’s findings and the bills as proof. Press-statutory: PRB Act 1867 as amended in 2002 (ss.13A–13E: true ownership particulars; penalty for false declaration; cancellation) — aimed at the secret-owner channel of paid news; self-regulatory: PCI complaint jurisdiction reaching advertisements (MIB-notes: “like cartoons, pictures, photographs, strips or advertisement which are published in a newspaper”, PDF p. 33), Norms of Journalistic Conduct, the Report on Paid News (PCI, 2010) named in the syllabus; criminal-general: IPC 420 → BNS 318(4), 471 → BNS 340 where documents are forged to mask payments. Digital twin: 2021 IT Rules r.4(3) (platform must mark promoted content “as being advertised, marketed, sponsored, owned, or exclusively controlled”) — the disclosure-by-design answer to paid news on social media.

5. The limits of the response — be candid. Chavan polices the spend, not the page: no Indian statute today punishes a newspaper for running paid news as such (contrast PRB false-declaration and PCI censure), and criminalising the content would collide with R. Rajagopal’s no-prior-restraint principle and the Shreya Singhal overbreadth standard — hence the design choice the materials defend: disclosure + accounting + peer censure. Reforms one may list as recommendations (flagged as proposals, not law): statutory paid-news disclosure endorsed by the PCI reports; PRBI amendment Bill 2004 [syllabus reference]; a self-regulatory code with powers as in the 2012 Media-Council resolution (Unit 4 B4); ECI-mandated certificates for political ads; mandatory platform labelling (extend r.4(3) with enforcement); and a “clear and conspicuous ‘paid content’ identifier” norm. The Court’s own method is the closing sentence: because “money power virtually controls the whole field of election”, the ECI must be read to hold “the widest power of its kind” (Chavan ¶55) — the answer to paid news is not a censor; it is an auditor, a label, and a tribunal of peers.

① DMR Act, 1954 — s.2(a)–(c) (what counts as advertisement / drug / magic remedy)

“‘advertisement’ includes any notice, circular, label, wrapper or other document, and any announcement made orally or by any means of producing or transmitting light, sound or smoke; … ‘drug’ includes— (i) a medicine for the internal or external use of human beings or animals; (ii) any substance intended to be used for or in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of disease in human beings or animals; (iii) any article, other than food, intended to affect or influence in any way the structure or any organic function of the body of human beings or animals; (iv) any article intended for use as a component of any medicine, substance or article, referred to in sub-clauses (i), (ii) and (iii); … ‘magic remedy’ includes a talisman, mantra, kavacha, and any other charm of any kind which is alleged to possess miraculous powers for or in the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of any disease in human beings or animals or for affecting or influencing in any way the structure or any organic function of the body of human beings or animals”. — uploaded DMR Act, PDF p. 2

② DMR — s.3 (the truth-irrelevant prohibition, complete)

“Subject to the provisions of this Act, no person shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any drug in terms which suggest or are calculated to lead to the use of that drug for— (a) the procurement of miscarriage in women or prevention of conception in women; or (b) the maintenance or improvement of the capacity of human beings for sexual pleasure; or (c) the correction of menstrual disorder in women; or (d) the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of any disease, disorder or condition specified in the Schedule, or any other disease, disorder or condition (by whatsoever name called) which may be specified in the rules made under this Act: Provided that no such rule shall be made except— (i) in respect of any disease, disorder or condition which requires timely treatment in consultation with a registered medical practitioner or for which there are normally no accepted remedies; and (ii) after consultation with the Drugs Technical Advisory Board constituted under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 …” — uploaded DMR Act, PDF p. 3

③ DMR — s.4, s.5, s.7, s.9A, s.10A (the falsity limb; magic remedies; penalty; cognizance; forfeiture)

“4. … no person shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement relating to a drug if the advertisement contains any matters which— (a) directly or indirectly gives a false impression regarding the true character of the drug; or (b) makes a false claim for the drug; or (c) is otherwise false or misleading in any material particular.” — “5. No person carrying on or purporting to carry on the profession of administering magic remedies shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any magic remedy which directly or indirectly claims to be efficacious for any of the purposes specified in section 3.” — “7. … (a) in the case of the first conviction, with imprisonment which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both; (b) in the case of a subsequent conviction, with imprisonment which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both.” — “9A. Notwithstanding anything contained in the Code of Criminal Procedure …, an offence punishable under this Act shall be cognizable.” — “10A. … the Court may direct that any document (including all copies thereof), article or thing, in respect of which the contravention is made, including the contents thereof where such contents are seized under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 8, shall be forfeited to the Government.” — uploaded DMR Act, PDF pp. 3–5

④ DMR — s.14 (the savings — including the State’s self-exemption)

“Nothing in this Act shall apply to— (a) any signboard or notice displayed by a registered medical practitioner on his premises indicating that treatment for any disease … is undertaken in those premises; or (b) any treatise or book dealing with any of the matters specified in section 3 from a bonafide scientific or social standpoint; or (c) any advertisement relating to any drug sent confidentially in the manner prescribed under section 16 only to a registered medical practitioner; or (d) any advertisement relating to a drug printed or published by the Government; or (e) any advertisement relating to a drug printed or published by any person with the previous sanction of the Government granted prior to the commencement of the … Amendment Act, 1963 …” — uploaded DMR Act, PDF p. 5
