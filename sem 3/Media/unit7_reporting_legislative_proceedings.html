Reporting of Legislative Proceedings

The two-lecture unit the Constitution never finished writing. A1 · The question, the gap, and the three exposures of a reporting press

The question in one sentence

When a newspaper prints what was said on the floor of Parliament or a State Assembly, what makes the report safe — the members’ privilege, a statute, or the Constitution itself — and who is allowed to take that safety away? The entire unit, and all four cases, are the Indian answer to that sentence, delivered in four instalments between 1951 and 1998.

Two constitutional facts frame everything. First, the public does not attend the House; it receives the House through a relay — “the Press provides the principal vehicle of expression of their views to citizens” (Iyer J., In re S. Mulgaokar, quoted in your Unit 3 file at reader PDF p. 164). If the relay is unprotected, representative government degrades into reported-on demand. Second, the relay was, at the commencement of the Constitution, almost entirely at the discretion of the relayed: the common law of defamation offered no exception for legislative reports (Exception 4 to s. 499 IPC protected reports of courts of justice only), and the Houses inherited the House of Commons’ power to forbid publication altogether. The story of this unit is the movement from that default to a guaranteed — but qualified — shield.

Real example — run one week of news through the map

On any budget day: (a) an MP calling a Minister a “thief” on the floor → Zone ①, untouchable (Art 105(2)); the same words in a post-debate press conference outside the precincts → Zone ② territory, the member’s own risk. (b) your newspaper printing the expunged passage in full → Zone ③, prima facie breach of privilege whatever its truth — “as if that portion had not been spoken” (Searchlight). (c) printing the same day’s debate substantially and honestly, minus expunged matter → Zone ④, protected unless the complainant proves malice or that the passage was not for the public good — and today the trial court is expected to see that at the process stage (Darda). This one worked example, adapted, answers most short notes.

The reader’s own words — privileges as an “exception” to press freedom of information (Case Book, PDF p. 72)

“It needs no reiteration that judiciary, which is protected by the power to punish for contempt of court and Parliament and legislatures protected as their privileges are by Articles 105 and 104 respectively of the Constitution of India, represent exceptions to this rule.” R. Rajagopal v. State of T.N., ¶26(3) [sic — Art 194] — as printed in the reader: the privacy rule that public figures cannot sue for reports on their official conduct does NOT run in favour of… the judiciary (contempt) or the legislatures (privilege). This single sentence is the reader’s bridge from Unit 2.1 to Unit 7: the public’s right to know stops where the Houses’ self-protection begins.

And the same judgment’s ¶26(2) is the quiet hinge of this unit’s privacy dimension: once a matter is in the public records “including court records”, privacy recedes — a report of proceedings is by definition from a public record; what the House has sealed (expunged, in-camera) is what never entered the public record at all. Read that way, Searchlight’s doctrine and Rajagopal’s privacy rule are the same rule: the shield follows the publicity; the embargo kills it.

Section 2 — the definition that decides who may claim the shield

“In this Act, ‘newspaper’ means any printed periodical work containing public news or comments on public news, and includes a news agency supplying material for publication in a newspaper.” s.2, verbatim (uploaded reprint)
ElementConsequence — and what it excludes
“printed periodical work”Born in 1977 vocabulary: a print-first definition. It does capture “comments on public news” — so the privilege is not confined to bare verbatim reports: an editorial based on the report remains within the definition (though the shield itself attaches to the “report” published — analysis is judged by the ordinary defamation exceptions, Ex 1–9 to s.499 / BNS s.356 schedule). Flagged tension [external; reported]: pure digital editions and TV-only outlets are, textually, outside “newspaper” — which is exactly why Article 361A(2)/s.4 put broadcast expressly inside, and why today’s answers should note that Parliament and the Assemblies now stream themselves (Sansad TV/DD, Rajya Sabha TV since 2017 — rebranded from RS TV; note dates [external]) making the question largely practical, not doctrinal.
“includes a news agency”The PTI feed defence: the agency that supplies the report to papers is itself covered — “includes” language mirrored, constitutionally, by Art 361A’s Explanation. Chain-of-publication answers (who is liable at which link — the Unit 2.1/S. Rajendra Sail attribution ladder) now carry a protected link at the top.

Section 3 — the shield, verbatim, then element-by-element

“(1) Save as otherwise provided in sub-section (2), no person shall be liable to any proceedings, civil or criminal, in any court in respect of the publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of any proceedings of either House of Parliament, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice.
(2) Nothing in sub-section (1) shall be construed as protecting the publication of any matter, the publication of which is not for the public good.” s.3(1)–(2), verbatim (uploaded reprint) — lift this block into every answer that cites the Act
ElementReadingHard cases it decides
“no person”Not just editors: any person sued — printer, publisher, author, distributor — for this publication. Wider than the “person” in the member-immunities (105/194).Cures, at one stroke, the liability-chain misery of S. Rajendra Sail-type facts where the matter is a legislative report.
“any proceedings, civil or criminal”Defamation suits and prosecutions (old ss. 499/500/501 → BNS 356(1)–(3)), and by its terms any other court proceeding grounded on the publication. It does not bind the House itself: breach-of-privilege jurisdiction is not a proceeding “in any court” — Searchlight survives s.3 intact.The unit’s deepest point: the statute tamed the courts, not the Houses. Only Art 361A raised the shield’s rank, and even 361A cannot touch a House’s own determination that its proceedings were abused — hence the risk ledger (G1) still keeps “privilege” as an unshielded column.
“substantially true report”Not literal verbatimness: substance governs; garbling, selective invention or distortion forfeits the cover, while condensation does not. Compare Exception 4 to s.499 IPC (courts): “report of the proceedings…” — read “fair and accurate”; the legislature shield is worded more forgivingly than the judicial one.Answers the perennial question: “editorial shortening = loss of privilege?” No, if substantial truth survives. A selectively edited report manufactured to skew, yes forfeits (and per Searchlight, an over-inclusive report of expunged matter is “in law … perverted and unfaithful” — the converse trap).
“report of any proceedings”“Proceedings” = what the House treated as its business — hence Jatish’s question (disallowed by the Speaker) was not “proceedings”, and a press conference outside is never “proceedings”. The Act never defined the word [flag: the reprint has no definition clause beyond s.2] — the 1951 ratio supplies the content.Also explains why committee material is the hardest edge (F1/F2): committee sittings are “proceedings” once reported to the House — but the press bar operates before that.
“either House of Parliament”Parliament only. On the face of the uploaded text, State Assemblies/Councils are not covered — a point the answer must make, because candidates reflexively write “and Legislatures”. The States’ protection came six months later, constitutionally, via Art 361A (see A4; and note that Darda, a Maharashtra Assembly report (1998), was decided — as reported — on defamation-exception grounds, the route the States had pre-1978 and the safe route after: both 361A and ss.499-Ex1/Ex2-BNS schedule now stand behind such a report).Timeline logic in one line: 1977 = Parliament; 1978 = every legislature.
Defeasor ① — “unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice”Qualified, not absolute privilege: burden of proof expressly on the complainant (“is proved”), and “malice” in the defamation sense — improper motive dominating the publication (Unit 2.1’s actual-malice/Sullivan adjacency: compare Rajagopal ¶26(3)’s “reckless disregard” standard for public officials — the Act is friendlier than that American-style test because ordinary negligence does not defeat it).Licence vs libel: quoting the debate is privileged; spiking it with the paper’s own insinuations is comment — outside s.3’s report, judged under ordinary defamation law.
Defeasor ② — “not for the public good” (s.3(2))A second, independent gate — absence of public good strips protection even from a true, honest report. Note the structure: unlike Ex 1 to s.499 (truth + public good both required of the defendant), here public good is cast as a carve-out “nothing … shall be construed as protecting” — the reprint text keeps the onus question open; practice treats the privilege as defeated by showing the matter had no public relevance [external; reported — flag as debated].What could fail it while remaining “proceedings”? Gratuitous publication of a member’s purely private slurs embedded in a speech — the report may be true and honest yet “not for the public good”. This is the clause that keeps Searchlight’s ethics alive inside the statute.

Section 4 — the broadcast extension (the 1977 answer to Sansad TV)

“This Act shall apply in relation to reports or matters broadcast by means of wireless telegraphy as part of any programme or service provided by means of a broadcasting station situate within the territories to which this Act extends as it applies in relation to reports or matters published in a newspaper.” s.4, verbatim (uploaded reprint)

“Wireless telegraphy” is the Telegraph Act vocabulary — read as Akashvani/DD live sessions in 1977, and as Doordarshan’s Sansad TV, the Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha web streams and the official RSS feeds today [external practice; reported]. Three consequences worth a short note: (i) the broadcaster must be a “broadcasting station” — a private news channel’s report of the debate is not “by” DD; its protection instead travels (like print’s) through s.3/Art 361A as a “report… of proceedings” — for print outlets s.3 never confined the privilege to authorised media, only its own definition of “newspaper” operates; (ii) s.4 “applies the Act” — so all three s.3 gates (substantial truth, malice, public good) follow the broadcast; (iii) Art 361A(2) copies s.4’s design almost word-for-word — one more proof (if any were needed) that the Article was drafted from the Act (compare the verbatim boxes A3/A4 — [external] for the drafting history, the textual match is in front of you in the workspace file).

Cross-check box — the uploaded reprint vs the “textbook” six-condition version

Many guides recite the “conditions” of the 1977 privilege as: true report; not of a secret/in-camera sitting; not of a House prohibiting publication; no legislative privilege for defamatory matter in committee proceedings before they are laid; no editorial malice; not for the public good, etc. The uploaded reprint contains none of the prohibition/in-camera/committee clauses — its full equipment is s.3(1)–(2) + s.4. A4 · Article 361A — the shield constitutionalised; and the Feroze Gandhi lineage behind it

The Article, verbatim

“361A. Protection of publication of proceedings of Parliament and State Legislature.—(1) No person shall be liable to any proceedings, civil or criminal, in any court in respect of the publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of any proceedings of either House of Parliament or the Legislative Assembly, or, as the case may be, either House of the Legislature, of a State, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice:
Provided that nothing in this clause shall apply to the publication of any report of the proceedings of a secret sitting of either House of Parliament or the Legislative Assembly, or, as the case may be, either House of the Legislature, of a State.
(2) Clause (1) shall apply in relation to reports or matters broadcast by means of wireless telegraphy as part of any programme or service provided by means of a broadcasting station as it applies in relation to reports or matters published in a newspaper.
Explanation.—In this article, ‘newspaper’ includes a news agency report containing material for publication in a newspaper.” Article 361A, Constitution of India (inserted by the Constitution (Forty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1978) — text transcribed from the standard constitutional printing [external; reported]; place it beside the uploaded s.3(1)–(2)/s.4/s.2 (Part L1) and watch the verbal identity: the Article is the Act, promoted.
What 361A changedDetail (and the exam use)
RankA privilege in the Constitution cannot be repealed by the same Parliament that granted it — the Emergency had just demonstrated exactly how fragile a statutory privilege is (the 1956 Act repealed by ordinance on 8.12.1975, and by Act on 11.2.1976, as one move of a three-law night: together with the Prevention of Publication of Objectionable Matter Act, 1976 and the Press Council (Repeal) Act, 1976 [external; reported]). Any answer on “why was the Act constitutionalised” is incomplete without this sequence.
Reach“…or the Legislative Assembly, or, as the case may be, either House of the Legislature, of a State” — the States added. Feroze Gandhi’s 1956 bill had aimed at both; it was “narrowed in the House to apply only to Parliamentary procedure” because the Union “was not empowered to legislate” for State Legislatures [external; reported]. The legislative-competence point is the unit’s best constitutional-law footnote: Entry 39, List II (powers/privileges of State Legislatures) being a State subject, only a constitutional amendment could finish the job — which is also why Art 194(3) still lets a State “define by law” its privileges.
One exclusion only — but a loud oneThe proviso: no protection for reports of “the proceedings of a secret sitting”. Note the economy of the drafting: unlike the commonly recited “six conditions”, the constitutional shield excludes only secret sittings — everything else in the working list (expunged matter; committee embargo; prohibition orders) survives by privilege law, not by this proviso, because none of it is court proceedings at all (Searchlight logic, Part C). This is the single best sentence to bring into any question on the interplay of 361A with House practice.
What 361A did NOT change(i) the malice defeasor survives (identical words); (ii) the public-good limb of s.3(2) is not carried across — the Article drops it; on the face of the text the constitutional shield has one defeasor, the statute had two [flag: some accounts read public good into 361A through Art 19(2)/defamation exceptions; the textual omission is the safer point]; (iii) the Act covered “either House of Parliament”; 361A adds States; (iv) “secret sitting” is not further defined — the Houses’ in-camera powers under their rules remain the practical test (F1).

“Article 361A, inserted by the 44th Amendment, 1978, constitutionalises the privilege first conferred by the Feroze Gandhi-inspired Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1956, restored on 25 March 1977 by Act 15 of 1977 after its Emergency repeal. B1 · Case 33 — Dr. Jatish Chandra Ghosh v. Harisadhan Mukherjee, AIR 1951 SC 613 The case that named the gap

Facts — the anatomy of a disallowed question

Dr. Jatish Chandra Ghosh, a member of the West Bengal Legislative Assembly, proposed a question for the Minister. The Speaker refused to allow it — its formulation was objectionable (the report the Assembly received was thus never to exist). The question — and the answer it sought to force — found its way into the public print anyway: they were published in the fortnightly “Janamat”, of which the petitioner was said to be the moving spirit, the publication being, in the High Court’s words, made “at the appellant’s request”. Harisadhan Mukherjee, the person at whom the question’s imputation was aimed, went to the Magistrate with a criminal complaint; the Editor and Printer/Publisher of the journal were proceeded against under s. 501 I.P.C. (printing/publishing defamatory matter with knowledge or reason to believe it defamatory — the member’s liability running through the abettor/prosecutor routes available in 1948–51 procedure), and the member himself faced the consequential prosecution.

Two procedural notes the exam rarely sees but the reasoning turns on them: (i) the case straddled the commencement of the Constitution — the plea that had to be met was framed under Art. 194, which is why the reported judgment pauses over “clauses (1) and (2) of Article 194” even on conduct that began under the Government of India Act, 1935; (ii) the relief sought was not a defence of truth but immunity: “it is settled law that the prosecution against the appellant cannot be dropped unless he can show that he has an absolute privilege with respect to the publication that is the subject-matter of the charge in this instance.” The petitioner had also tendered pleas of absence of malice and public good — defences for trial, not for the threshold.

Issues
  1. Were the disallowed question and answer part of “proceedings in the Legislature” such that Art. 194(2) (or its predecessor) immunised their publication “by or under the authority of the House”?
  2. Does membership of a Legislature carry, outside the Chamber, any higher privilege of speech or publication than the citizen’s Art. 19(1)(a) freedom?
  3. Has the common law of defamation been displaced in India by any legislative-reporting privilege analogous to Wason v. Walter (1868) (UK, absolute privilege for either House’s reports), or is the criminal prosecution governed by the ordinary Penal Code — including Exception 4 to s. 499 (substantially true report of court proceedings, courts only)?
  4. Was quashing (s. 249A/250 CrPC route of the day) the proper remedy at all where the plea rested on contested privilege constructions?
Arguments — both sides, as reported
For Dr. Ghosh (petitioner/appellant)For the complainant (and the prosecution’s frame)
  • “Proceedings” is broad: a question sought to be asked by a member forms part of the proceedings of the House even though disallowed by the Speaker — the Speaker’s ruling regulates the agenda, not the member’s speech-privilege.
  • Art. 194(2), second limb: publication “by order or under the authority of the House” of “any report of proceedings” cannot be made actionable; the member’s communication to his own constituency is the same authority, democratically — otherwise the electors hear what their member said only if the Speaker permits it.
  • The analogy of faithful court reports: Exception 4 shows the policy — a report of proceedings “faithful” deserves protection; extend it, as Wason v. Walter did in England, to the Legislature.
  • Free speech as the background: the ordinary criminal law should not be read to catch a member discharging his legislative function; the trial court’s burden is the malice/public-good pleas which, pleaded here, show the absence of any case fit for trial at all.
  • The Speaker’s ruling is definitional: what was never raised could not become “proceedings”; the disallowed question never existed for the House and its “publication” could not, “under no circumstances”, have been “authorized by the House”.
  • Absolute privilege is a closed list: Art. 194(1)/(2) protect speech in the Chamber, votes, and publications by or under the House’s authority — the petitioner stood outside all three; and clauses (1)–(2) address liability for what was said or voted, not liability of the printer for new currency given to the words.
  • No common law transplant: Exception 4 covers courts only — its deliberate silence about legislatures (recalled again a decade later: “the fourth exception says that it is not defamation to publish a substantially true report of the proceedings of a court of justice, but does not make any such concession in respect of proceedings of a House of Legislature or Parliament”) defeats any reliance on Wason v. Walter: “cannot be extended to this country … the ordinary criminal law of the land must apply so long as reports of proceedings of the Legislative Assembly were not treated as privileged under the Indian law.”
  • Quashing is the wrong court: privilege/defences of good faith and public good are triable issues; the threshold relief demands showing an absolute privilege on the face of the record — which the appellant could not.
Decision

Appeal failed; the prosecution proceeded. The reported reasoning in four moves:

  1. Not “proceedings”: “It must also be observed that… the question was never raised in the House and could not be considered to have been part of its proceedings”; therefore its publication was “not under the authority of the House”, and Art. 194(2)’s second limb was unengaged.
  2. Members are not privileged citizens: “As a member of the Assembly the petitioner cannot have any further or higher constitutional rights of speech and publication than those guaranteed under clauses (1) and (2) of Article 194”; and the publication “being a communication between a member of the Legislature and his constituency enjoys no more privilege than is enjoyed by an ordinary citizen.”
  3. Only official publication is absolutely privileged: the corollary passage — “Immunity from liability to proceedings in court can only be claimed on proof of the fact that what was said was a part of the proceedings of the legislature or that the offending words were part of the proceedings published under authority” — plus the official publications point: publications of the Legislature itself are privileged precisely because “the right of free speech would be liable to be defeated if an official publication exposed to liability those responsible for the discussion.”
  4. Everything else is left to “legislative measures”: “Rights, privileges and immunities other than these are left to be secured by legislative measures and pending their adoption they will be those of the House of Commons at the commencement of the Constitution.” — the sentence that reads like an instruction to Parliament, which it in effect was: the Feroze-Gandhi bill (1956) is the response; the 1977 Act is the echo; Art 361A is the seal. External; reported for the verbatim lines.
Principle evolved

Ratio, in one sentence each

  1. A disallowed question is not a proceeding. The Speaker’s control over the agenda is constitutive: what the House never entertained, its privileges never covered.
  2. Privilege stops at the Chamber door. A member republishing outside — in his own journal or a news conference — speaks as a citizen, liable under ordinary criminal law (then ss. 499/500/501 IPC; now BNS s. 356(1)–(3) — see J1).
  3. In India of 1951, reports of legislative proceedings had NO privilege at all — unlike reports of court proceedings (Exception 4 to s. 499); any extension must be (and was) statutory.
  4. The absolute/quilibrium of immunity turns on “by or under the authority of the House” — the phrase that decides both this case and, twenty-seven years later, which reports are inside Art. 105(2) and which only inside the 1977 Act/361A (qualified privilege, malice-defeasible).
Why the syllabus kept it first in the unit — and how to use it 75 years later

Every protection the reporting press now enjoys is an answer to this judgment. Three working uses in an exam: (a) as the premise — “in Jatish the Court found no privilege precisely because Parliament had legislated none; the 1956/1977 Acts and 361A are that legislation” — the arc argument; (b) as the trap — in any problem about a member’s press conference, WhatsApp statement or Facebook post of “what I said in the House”, Jatish is the citation that the privilege does not follow the words out of the doors; the member’s risk is real and current [the principle has since been re-stated in Searchlight itself and remains unshaken]; (c) as the comparative hinge — Wason v. Walter was declined in 1951 and then legislated in, partially and conditionally, in 1977/1978; the English “absolute privilege” (Defamation Act lineage) and the Indian “qualified privilege” are cousins, not twins — say exactly which defeasors India kept (malice; public good under the Act; secret sittings under the proviso).

Real-example bridge (1951 → today, one fact pattern)

Re-run Jatish’s facts in 2026: the member’s disallowed question still is not “proceedings” (Issue 1, untouched by any later law) — publication still fails the 105(2)/194(2) gate; the 1977 Act/361A give the newspaper nothing, because s.3(1)/Art 361A(1) cover a “report of any proceedings”, and what the House never entertained is not proceedings. But the story now has two further chapters: the Editor’s best shield is Exception 1/2 analysis at the process stage (Darda — Part E), and the House may choose to treat the leak of its embargoed document as a breach (Searchlight machinery — Part C) if the question was obtained by unauthorised means. That three-law overlay, in one incident, is this unit.

C1 · Case 34 — Pandit M.S.M. Sharma v. Shri Sri Krishna Sinha, AIR 1959 SC 395 “The Searchlight case” — I

Why two “Searchlight cases” exist, and which is in the syllabus

Pandit M.S.M. That is AIR 1959 SC 395 — the syllabus case, “Searchlight I”. Five years later a larger bench of the Supreme Court answered the President’s reference arising from the Uttar Pradesh Keshav Singh clash — a second Searchlight moment — In re, Art. 143 of the Constitution, AIR 1965 SC 745 (“Searchlight II”; reported also under that Keshav Singh label and dated 30.9.1964 in the Indian-Kanoon printing). Facts — 31 May 1957, and the Chair’s ruling that made it a breach

In a debate in the Bihar Legislative Assembly (29 May 1957 session business — the reported date of the publication is 31 May 1957), a member, addressing the State Khadi Board and its Chairman, used passages about a third person (“Mahesh Babu”). The Speaker ruled in the House, and the reported text of the ruling is worth reading for its surgical double move:

“I have already ruled with reference to whatever has been said about Mahesh Babu that such words would be expunged from the proceedings. But, whatever may be said with reference to the Chairmanship of the State Khadi Board will remain in the proceedings and the Hon’ble Member has the right to speak on the matter.” The Chair’s expunction ruling, as printed in the report of the case [external; reported] — the exact boundary the publication crossed.

Searchlight published the speech in its entirety — the expunged words included — and thereby, on the House’s view, printed something the House had ordered “not to have been said”. A privilege motion (reported as moved by another member) was referred to the Committee of Privileges, which issued a notice to the editor to attend and explain — Sharma invoked Art. 32 before the Supreme Court, saying the privilege proceeding abridged his Art. 19(1)(a) and Art. 21 rights. Pt. Krishna Sinha, the Chief Minister, and the Assembly/its Committee stood respondent.

Issues
  1. Is the privilege “until defined by law” under Art. 194(3) (and pari passu 105(3)) subordinate to Art. 19(1)(a) — or is it a constitutional provision of equal rank which, on a conflict, prevails as the special provision?
  2. Is that privilege “law” within Art. 13(2) so that, being inconsistent with Part III, it (or its exercise) is void?
  3. Did the House of Commons on 26.1.1950 actually possess the power to prohibit publication of even faithful reports of publicly conducted proceedings, so as to be “the privilege” the Indian House inherited?
  4. Assuming the power exists, was the publication of an expunged portion within its reach — and does an Art. 32 court examine the procedural proprieties of the Committee (delay, dates, service) at all (Arts. 212, 208)?
  5. Was the notice itself an actionable “deprivation” under Art. 21, or is the Assembly’s procedure — rules made under Art. 208 — “procedure established by law”?
Arguments — both sides
For Sharma (petitioner — the press side)For the House / respondents
  • The press right is the citizen right: freedom of the press is implicit in Art. 19(1)(a) and includes propagation/circulation of a true report of what every citizen could hear from the gallery — the proceeding punishes the publication of the public’s own knowledge.
  • Art. 194(3) is transitional: the “until so defined” limb is a placeholder; a law defining privileges would be void under Art. 13(2) if it cut into Part III; the placeholder cannot enjoy what the statute could not do — “the second limb of Art. 194(3) is transitory and cannot override fundamental rights” (the dissent’s later articulation).
  • The 1950 time-capsule had shrunk by practice: by 1950 the House of Commons claimed but no longer enforced a prohibition on faithful reports — the privilege recognised by its courts extended at most to mala fide, garbled or expunged-with-malice reporting; importing a privilege “never in fact exercised” misreads “those of that House at the commencement”.
  • Comparative comfort: the American Constitution tolerates publication of even expunged congressional debates — “the people were more forthright”, and it was for the Indian court to read 194(3) in harmony with Part III (the reported exchange of judicial asides).
  • Nothing on the face alleged mala fides: neither notice nor enclosing documents disclosed that the editor knew of the expunction or acted with malice — so even on the House’s own doctrine no prima facie breach was made out for Art. 32 interference?
  • Textual hierarchy: Art. 194 subjects clause (1) expressly to the other provisions of the Constitution; clauses (2)–(4) carry no such rider; “the provisions of Art. 194(3) are not subordinate to Art. 19(1)(a)” — the special prevails over the general by harmonious construction.
  • Art. 13 is the wrong key: the privileges are conferred by the Constitution itself — Art. 13 voids laws made by the legislature; a constitutional clause is not a law it can void (“Article 13 has no application to the provisions of the Constitution itself” — repeated in the 1964 Reference).
  • The Commons’ 1950 law was exactly as claimed: at commencement the Commons possessed and its courts recognised the power to punish publication of its debates (including of expunged matter) as contempt — a fortiori it could prohibit an inaccurate or garbled version; the Bihar House stepped into that heritage since no State law had defined privileges under Entry 39, List II.
  • Expunged ≠ published: once expunged, the words are, for the House, no part of its record; printing the “whole speech” is thus a false account of what the House said — the “perverted and unfaithful report” reasoning (below, verbatim).
  • Non-cognisability: under Arts. 212/208 the court cannot inspect the Committee’s scheduling and service; the petitioner was not being “deprived” without procedure — the Assembly acts under its constitutional rules; Art. 32 premature while the Committee was “yet to conclude its proceedings”.

C2 · Searchlight — the decision, the dissent, and the road to 1977

Decision — majority (five-Judge Constitution Bench; 4:1)
  1. Clause-rank holding: Art. 194(3) is not subordinate to Art. 19(1)(a). The constitutional privileges of the House stand on the same plane as the fundamental rights; where they meet, the special constitutional provision governs the general guarantee — “it is a well-settled rule of interpretation that a special provision controls a general one”; harmonious construction “must be applied to Art. 194(3) so as not to subject it to Art. 19(1)(a)”.
  2. The Art. 13 answer: the privileges “until defined by law” are part of the Constitution — not “law” within Art. 13(2) and therefore not void for inconsistency with Part III; the first limb (law to be made) is future-bound and will then, of course, answer to Art. 13.
  3. The Commons’ 1950 inventory: “at the commencement of the Constitution the House of Commons possessed the power to prohibit publication of its debates… and to punish its breach”; the Bihar Assembly, no State law having been made under Entry 39 List II, “enjoys the powers, privileges and immunities of the Commons at commencement”; “a fortiori the House had at the relevant time the power or privilege of prohibiting the publication of an inaccurate or garbled version of such debates or proceedings.”
  4. The expunction rule — the sentence that has governed Indian legislative reporting for sixty-seven years, and which your next two units will keep quoting:
    “The effect in law of the order of the Speaker to expunge a portion of the speech of a member might be as if that portion had not been spoken and a report of the whole speech, despite the Speaker’s order, might be regarded as a perverted and unfaithful report and prima facie constitute a breach of the privilege of the Assembly. Whether there had in fact been a breach of the privilege of the Assembly was, however, a matter for the Assembly alone to judge.” Searchlight, as reproduced by the Supreme Court’s own subsequent citations and by the Rajya Sabha Committee on Privileges (2022) [external; reported].
  5. Art. 21 and the courts’ hands: any deprivation arising from a privilege proceeding follows “procedure established by law” — the Assembly’s rules made under Art. 208 being law for the purpose; and under Arts. 212/105(3) the court cannot scrutinise “merely irregular” procedure “in the internal management of the House’s business”; with the Committee “yet to conclude”, the petition was premature in part and dismissed overall.
  6. The press-equality clause: “the liberty of the Press, though implicit in Art. 19(1)(a), stands on no higher footing than the freedom of speech guaranteed to any citizen. There is no special privilege attached to the Press” [as reported for the majority’s reasoning] — the sentence that keeps Unit 4’s “no press-specific rights” doctrine and this unit’s “no press-specific immunity” doctrine on one hinge.
The dissent of Subba Rao, J. — the majority that became the statute
“The wide powers and privileges enjoyed by the Legislature and its members should… be so exercised as not to impair the fundamental rights of the citizen, particularly of one who was not a member of the Legislature. In case of a conflict, Art. 19(1)(a) must prevail over Art. 194(3) and not vice versa, and the privilege must yield to the extent it affected the fundamental right… Art. 194(3) does not import into the Indian Constitution the powers, privileges and immunities in their entirety — the right to prohibit publication altogether could not be imported… in England there was no written constitution; the House of Commons had claimed the right to prohibit publication but in fact and in actual practice never exercised that right.” Subba Rao, J., dissenting (as reported in the digests of the case [external; reported]) — the judgment that Parliament reversed, first by legislation (1977) and then by constitution-making (1978).

Facts — an Assembly revelation, a minister’s words, an MLA’s wound

What happened inside the Maharashtra Legislative Assembly was reported outside, more or less exactly: “He had published a news item disclosing what happened during an Assembly Debate. Arguments — both sides (as the later reports compress them)
Newspaper (Darda and others)Complainant (Kapsikar)

Decision

The practical checklist (what a desk must do with a debate)

  1. Confirm the sitting was public — not in camera, no “secret sitting” element — and that you are working from floor, gallery, live stream or the official record, public in every sense.
  2. Check the printed record for “Expunged as ordered by the Chair” and the committee status of anything not yet laid on the Table.
  3. Report substantially, not selectively-skewed: the malice defeasor and the “wilful misrepresentation/suppression” breach-standard both turn on pattern, not one word.
  4. Attribute on the face — “the Minister said in the House”; keep your own adjectives out of the quoted passage (Darda’s safe lane; the Dapthary ditch).
  5. If the House complains: apology early, unconditional, in its terms (Melaram route); if a court issues process: Darda threshold application (quash on the face of the complaint) with the 361A/Act pleadings plus Ex. 1/2 (BNS schedule) in the alternative.

H1 · Privilege today — the un-coded power, the judicial re-entry, and what the reporter inherits

The codification vacuum — the unit’s open ending

Article 105(3) and 194(3) were written as a bridge “until defined by law”. No such law has ever been passed — not for Parliament, not (so far as the workspace shows) for any State — so the time-capsule of 26 January 1950 still carries every unlegislated privilege, including the prohibition-of-publication power Searchlight enforced. The 1977 Act/361A answered the press-facing half of the vacuum (immunity in courts); the House-facing half (the Houses’ own process against publishers) was left where the Constitution left it — a matter of imported, contested, judge-reviewed practice. State that structure first in any “privileges” essay and the case list then reads as illustrations, not as the answer.

What the 2024 re-centring hands the legislative-reporting press — three consequences

  1. The Houses’ publication-controls survive — the two-fold “function” test is aimed at member immunity, and expunging/in-camera/committee practice is plainly “tethered to the collective functioning of the House”; no reporter should over-read Sita Soren as the end of privilege machinery. (Exam sentence: “Sita Soren contracts privilege around the member; it does not contract 361A away from the publisher.”)
  2. But “exclusive cognisance” now has a visible ceiling: any privilege asserted against a non-member publisher must pass necessity to collective functioning and the claimant carries the burden — the doctrinal air that a 1959-style prohibition found scarce today (and why modern episodes end in apologies — F2).
  3. The evidentiary habit this unit teaches: where a House punishes publication, its record is the text (the expunction order, the prohibition); where a person sues over publication, the newspaper’s record is the source-chain. Sita Soren’s “burden of satisfying… lies with the person or body claiming the privilege” is the same discipline the Darda threshold demands of the complainant and the malice clause of the Article demands of the plaintiff: whoever seeks to defeat a report must point to the paper’s record — and the paper should have kept a better one.

I2 · Five exam-modelled questions — plans, and two in full answer-form

P-1 (20 marks · essay) — “Discuss the law relating to the publication of reports of the proceedings of Parliament and the State Legislatures in India. Has it achieved a balance between freedom of the press and the privileges of the Legislature? Refer to Article 361A, the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977, and the case law.”

Full model answer — first movement (write this prose; ~350 words):

“The Indian law on reporting the legislature is a three-layer structure, and its history is the argument for why layers were needed at all. Layer one is constitutional immunity of the members: Articles 105(1)–(2) and 194(1)–(2) make ‘anything said or any vote given’ in the House or a committee unimpeachable in any court, and extend that absolute immunity only to publications made ‘by or under the authority of’ the House — the official report, not the newspaper. Layer two is the Houses’ control over publicity: under clauses (3) of the same Articles, powers and privileges remain, until defined by law, those the House of Commons possessed on 26 January 1950 — including, as the Supreme Court held in Pandit M.S.M. Sharma v. Krishna Sinha (AIR 1959 SC 395), the power to prohibit publication of even a faithful report, and to treat the republication of an expunged passage as ‘a perverted and unfaithful report and prima facie a breach of the privilege of the Assembly’, since the Speaker’s order works ‘as if that portion had not been spoken’. Layer three is the qualified privilege of the publisher, built in response: the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977 — a restoration, in the words of its own Statement of Objects, of the privilege the Emergency had abolished by repealing the 1956 Feroze-Gandhi Act — whose s.3(1) provides that ‘no person shall be liable to any proceedings, civil or criminal, in any court in respect of the publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of any proceedings of either House of Parliament, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice’, s.3(2) refusing cover to matter ‘not for the public good’, and s.4 extending the Act to wireless-telegraphy broadcasts; and, since 1978, Article 361A, which constitutionalises the same immunity, extends it to State Legislatures, and removes it only for reports of the proceedings of a ‘secret sitting’.”

Second movement — the case law as proof of stress (bullets to expand): Jatish Chandra Ghosh v. Harisadhan Mukherjee (1951): the gap — a disallowed question is no “proceeding”, a member’s publication to his constituency carries “no more privilege than is enjoyed by an ordinary citizen”, and Wason v. Walter was expressly declined; Searchlight (1959): the prohibition power upheld, 4:1, with Subba Rao J.’s dissent (“in case of a conflict Art. 19(1)(a) must prevail”) as the future’s draft; Dapthary (1971): the statute’s words are its limits — a non-periodical pamphlet of one’s own attack on a Judge is neither a “newspaper” nor a “report of proceedings”, and the legislature channel (governed by Arts. 121/211) cannot launder scandalising matter — two months’ simple imprisonment; Darda (1998): the shield and the Penal Code exceptions working together — bona-fide, public-good reportage of a Minister’s Assembly statement about public funds quashed at the threshold.

Third movement — the balance sheet (the answer’s verdict paragraph): “Balance has been achieved in doctrine and only half-achieved in machinery. In doctrine: the press may publish everything the House permits the record to hold, absolutely if the House itself published it (layer one’s second limb), qualifiedly if the press did (layer three), and not at all what the House sealed (layer two) — a tripartite allocation no common-law system ever stated more clearly, since even the English rule is statutory and (since 1996) narrower on the publisher’s side. In machinery: the Article’s immunity binds only ‘courts’ — the House process (Committee of Privileges, admonition, the historical committal) runs outside it; the Act’s public-good clause and the Article’s silence on the same limb leave the newspaper’s best defence inside the ordinary law of defamation exceptions; and the un-enforced expunction norm is, on a realistic view, enforced not by punishment but by the apology-economy. Yet the direction of travel is unmistakable: from 1951’s bare floor to Darda’s threshold-quashing, and to Sita Soren (2024)’s insistence that no privilege may exceed what the ‘collective functioning of the House’ requires — the modern rule, stated once, that keeps both halves of the balance answerable to the Constitution.”

P-5 (20 marks · problem question) — the model answer in the series’ house style

The problem: “The Citizen publishes (i) a verbatim account of a State Assembly speech in which the Finance Minister alleged that the Leader of the Opposition had diverted relief funds; (ii) a passage of a subsequent speech which the Speaker ordered expunged; (iii) minutes of the Public Accounts Committee not yet laid on the Table; and (iv) a column alleging that the Judge hearing the diversion case ‘wants to please the Chief Minister’. Model answer (item-by-item, zone-tagged — 14 lines that exhaust the paper’s marking scheme):

  1. (i) Publication of a minister’s on-floor allegation = “report of… proceedings”: 361A(1)/s.3(1) immunity engaged — “substantially true” satisfied; complainant (the LoP) must prove malice; the paper’s own adjectives would move it out of the shield into comment (Ex 1–3 analysis, Darda). Verdict: strong shield; at worst, trial of a failed suit.
  2. (ii) Expunged passage: outside the Article/Act — “proceedings” no longer contain it (Searchlight: “as if that portion had not been spoken”; “perverted and unfaithful” prima facie breach). Court exposure still nil on the defamation axis (361A does not say “no breach” — it says no liability in court for substantially-true reports; and a truthful-in-fact report of an expunged line may satisfy the courts even as the House punishes it — the split the examiner loves; flag it, do not hide it). Verdict: no court liability of consequence; real House exposure — apologise early (F2).
  3. (iii) PAC minutes before laying: standing embargo (2022 restatement, F1); the committee report, once laid, converts to “proceedings” and the full shield re-attaches. Until then — stop. Defamation-wise the paper could still argue Ex-1 (truth + public good); privilege-wise it is indefensible; injunction-wise nothing (prior restraint: Unit 6 — Reliance/Sahara standards make an injunction on (iii) as good as unobtainable: “real and imminent danger” absent).
  4. (iv) The column: not a report of proceedings at all; it is the paper’s imputation of improper motive to a sitting Judge — Dapthary: “scurrilous attack… inevitable effect of undermining the confidence of the public in the Judiciary”; Mulgaokar’s countervailing restraint (no punishment where the criticism, even if erroneous, is fair) is the sentence to plead, and s.13(2) “just and fair criticism” the provision to plead; expect notice, apology-if-reckless defence, and — because the Judge is seised of the case — the additional sub-clause 2(c) limb (prejudicing due course): the paper’s best friend here is the relator rule of Unit 3 (attributing, not asserting) — but a column asserts. Verdict: advise the editor to kill or re-plead (ii) and (iv); print (i) as is; hold (iii) for the Table.
  5. Cross-cutting: Arts. 211/121 (the (iv) subject is not debatable in that Assembly — (iv) is therefore also unreportable as “proceedings”); no vicarious free pass for the proprietor — the Rajendra Sail ladder (Unit 3) — but Darda’s threshold may end all four actions at process stage for (i) alone.

P-6 (10+10 marks · the two short notes in FULL, to be copied as models)

(a) Expunging of proceedings and the press — full note.

“A member’s words which the Chair strikes out never, in law, existed for the record. Rule 380 of the Lok Sabha Rules allows the Speaker, in discretion, to order expunction of words ‘defamatory or indecent or unparliamentary or undignified’; the printed proceedings then carry the formula ‘Expunged as ordered by the Chair’, and the Rajya Sabha’s rules are to the same effect. The press consequence was settled by a five-Judge Constitution Bench in Pandit M.S.M. Sharma v. Krishna Sinha (AIR 1959 SC 395): ‘the effect in law of the order of the Speaker to expunge a portion of the speech of a member might be as if that portion had not been spoken and a report of the whole speech, despite the Speaker’s order, might be regarded as a perverted and unfaithful report and prima facie constitute a breach of the privilege of the Assembly’ — with the finding of an actual breach reserved to ‘the Assembly alone to judge’. Two regimes therefore coexist. In the courts, the Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1977, s.3(1) and Article 361A(1) immunise a ‘substantially true report of any proceedings… unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice’; expunged matter is, by definition, outside the House’s proceedings after the Chair’s order, so a report that keeps it is at best a report of a fact about the House (the expunction itself) and, more usually, outside the shield. Before the House, publication is a breach whatever its truth, the Committee on Privileges pathway (notice → report → motion) supplies the only effective sanction, and modern practice ends in apology: the 2022 official restatement is blunt — ‘it is a breach of privilege and contempt of the House to publish expunged proceedings of the House’. No court can enjoin such publication in advance (prior-restraint doctrine: Reliance Petrochemicals, 1988; Sahara, 2012, postponement only on ‘real and imminent danger’), and no damages claim can reach the reporter while the Article’s immunity holds — the residue is procedure, cost and the Bar of the House. One-line verdict: accuracy is measured against the expunged record, not the acoustics of the Chamber.”

(b) Articles 121/211 and Dapthary’s no-laundering rule — full note.

“Articles 121 and 211 forbid discussion in Parliament and the State Legislatures ‘with respect to the conduct of any Judge… in the discharge of his duties’, except upon a motion for presenting an address to the President/Governor for the Judge’s removal. The two clauses invert the protective geometry of reporting: the member’s absolute immunity (105(1)/194(1)) attaches to what the House may lawfully discuss, and judicial conduct is fenced out of that zone until the removal motion engages it. C.K. Daphtary v. O.P. Gupta (1971) 1 SCC 626 is the decision that closes the circle: for a booklet ascribing bias and dishonesty to a sitting Judge — published while the removal channel was being invoked and pleaded, wrongly, under the (then-existing) Parliamentary Proceedings (Protection of Publication) Act, 1956 — the editor was held guilty and sentenced to simple imprisonment for two months. The reported reasoning is doubly instructive here. First, statutory textualism: a non-periodical pamphlet is not a ‘newspaper’, and the author’s own allegation is no ‘report of… proceedings’ — the very definitions the 1977 Act carried over verbatim (‘any printed periodical work containing public news…’; a ‘substantially true report of any proceedings’). Second, the public-wrong test: ‘a scurrilous attack on a Judge in respect of a judgment or past conduct has adverse effect on the due administration of justice… If confidence in the Judiciary goes, the due administration of justice definitely suffers’ — as the case book itself records (Reader PDF p. 187), with the Court’s disapproval of even ‘condign and quick punishment’ practice reserved to fair comment (13(2), Contempt of Courts Act; In re Mulgaokar, ¶44, Reader p. 178). Combined principle for legislative reporting: the legislature is neither a forum for attacking judges nor a shield for having attacked them elsewhere; 361A’s privilege guards the relay of proceedings, and never the reporter’s own insinuation.”

K1 · Twenty-four flashcards — the unit compressed

1 · The unit’s one question

Who owns the public’s right to hear what its representatives say? Answer layers: member-privilege (105/194) → House control (expunge/in-camera/prohibit) → publisher’s shield (1977 Act s.3 / Art 361A).

2 · 1977 Act in one line

“No person shall be liable to any proceedings, civil or criminal, in any court in respect of the publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of any proceedings of either House of Parliament, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice.” (s.3(1))

3 · The two defeasors

Malice (proved by the complainant — s.3(1)) and “not for the public good” (s.3(2)). The Article drops the second; the Act has both.

4 · s.4

Broadcast by “wireless telegraphy” = newspaper for the Act’s purposes — 1977’s Akashvani, today’s Sansad TV; Art. 361A(2) copies it.

5 · The lineage

Feroze Gandhi’s 1956 Act → Ordinance 8.12.1975 + Repeal Act 11.2.1976 (Emergency) → restoration 18.4.1977, deemed force 25.3.1977 → constitutionalisation by Art. 361A, 44th Amendment 1978.

6 · Why 361A was needed

Parliament could not cover State Legislatures (Entry 39, List II is the States’ subject — the 1956 bill’s narrowing proves it); only the Constitution could extend the same shield to Bihar and Maharashtra.

7 · The proviso

361A’s only express exclusion: reports of the proceedings of a secret sitting. Everything else in the “conditions” lists comes from privilege-law, not the Article.

8 · 105/194 — the press-facing half

The second limb of clause (2): absolute immunity for publications “by or under the authority of” the House. Official report = absolute; press report = qualified (or nothing, pre-1977). Jatish is the cautionary tale of the gap.

9 · clause (3) asymmetry

105(3) imports the Commons’ powers at commencement; 194(3) freezes the Indian provincial House’s privileges at commencement. Sita Soren (2024) re-centres both on function.

10 · Jatish (1951) — holding

Disallowed question ≠ “proceedings”; member-to-constituency publication = no higher privilege than any citizen; Wason v. Walter declined; Exception 4 covers courts only; quash refused. The gap, named.

11 · Searchlight (1959) — holdings

194(3) not subordinate to 19(1)(a); not “law” under 13(2); Commons 1950 had the prohibition power; expunged = “as if not spoken”; breach determination is the Assembly’s alone; press has no higher footing. 4:1; Subba Rao J. dissent = the future.

12 · The Searchlight sentence (memorise verbatim)

“The effect in law of the order of the Speaker to expunge a portion of the speech of a member might be as if that portion had not been spoken and a report of the whole speech… might be regarded as a perverted and unfaithful report and prima facie constitute a breach of the privilege of the Assembly.”

13 · Keshav Singh / Reference (1964–65)

Allahabad FC vs UP Assembly; In re Art. 143: privileges are constitutional (Art. 13 out), only “claimed and recognised” Commons powers travel, prohibition-power affirmed; the content of a privilege is finally judicial.

14 · Dapthary (1971) — facts

Booklet imputing bias/dishonesty to a sitting Judge; shelter pleaded = the 1956 Act; Court: a pamphlet is not a “newspaper” and the allegations are no “report of proceedings”; editor O.P. Gupta — 2 months’ simple imprisonment; printer’s unqualified apology accepted; third respondent untraced.

15 · Dapthary — the unit-principle

The legislature is no laundering channel: Arts 121/211 fence judicial conduct out of the House; what cannot be debated there cannot become “proceedings” by being printed beside it.

16 · Darda (1998) — facts

Lokmat (Nagpur) reported a Minister’s Assembly revelation on misappropriation; MLA Kapsikar’s s.500 complaint; JM Nasik issued process → Sessions Court quashed → HC refused → SC restored quashing.

17 · Darda — the passage

“If the accused bona fide believing the version of the Minister to be true published the report in good faith, it cannot be said that they intended to harm the reputation of the complainant… The news item was published for public good.” (as reproduced by following High Courts)

18 · Darda’s procedural gift

The benefit of the exceptions (and now 361A) can be granted at the threshold — quashing on the face of the complaint — not only after a full trial; and no chain-vicarious liability for editors absent personal attribution.

19 · Rule 380 & the formula

“…the Speaker may, while exercising discretion, order that such words be expunged…”; the record then reads “Expunged as ordered by the Chair”; accuracy is measured against the expunged record, not the floor.

20 · The committee embargo

“…the press is forbidden to publish any part of the proceedings or evidence given before, or any document presented to, Parliamentary Committees before such proceedings or evidence or document has been reported to the House.” — the laying-on-Table is the trigger.

21 · Malice is the plaintiff’s

Both texts say “proved”: the complainant must prove malice; good faith need not be proven first — it is the default of a substantially true report (Darda at threshold; contrast Rajagopal’s “reckless disregard” in the public-official lane, Unit 2.1).

22 · The apology economy

Melaram (1971), Hindustan Times’ editor (2000s), the Sail apologies (2005): the House’s usual currency; “unconditional and unqualified”, in its vocabulary, early. The committal era is over practically, and Sita Soren made it doctrinally expensive.

23 · Sita Soren (2024)

7-Judge bench; Narasimha Rao (1998) overruled; two-fold test (tethered to collective functioning + functional necessity); burden on the privilege-claimant; “not islands… enclaves”; lawmakers subject to the ordinary law. Reporter-use: privilege around the House shrinks; the publisher’s shield is untouched — and the House process now walks under judicial review of its necessity.

24 · The three-forum reflex (problem questions)

Report published → court (defamation? 361A/Act → done) · the House (breach? expunged/embargoed/mala fide → the only exposure left) · contempt court (judge attacked? no shield — Dapthary). Find the forum first; the law follows the forum.

② Article 361A — verbatim [external; reported transcription] — and the mirror-articles’ operative clauses

361A.(1) No person shall be liable to any proceedings, civil or criminal, in any court in respect of the publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of any proceedings of either House of Parliament or the Legislative Assembly, or, as the case may be, either House of the Legislature, of a State, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice:
Provided that nothing in this clause shall apply to the publication of any report of the proceedings of a secret sitting of either House of Parliament or the Legislative Assembly, or, as the case may be, either House of the Legislature, of a State.
(2) Clause (1) shall apply in relation to reports or matters broadcast by means of wireless telegraphy as part of any programme or service provided by means of a broadcasting station as it applies in relation to reports or matters published in a newspaper.
Explanation.—In this article, “newspaper” includes a news agency report containing material for publication in a newspaper.

105.(1) Subject to the provisions of this article and to the rules and standing orders regulating the procedure of Parliament, there shall be freedom of speech in Parliament.
(2) No member of Parliament shall be liable to any proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given by him in Parliament or any committee thereof, and no person shall be so liable in respect of the publication by or under the authority of either House of Parliament of any report, paper, votes or proceedings.
(3) The powers, privileges and immunities of either House of Parliament and of its committees and members shall be such as may from time to time be defined by Parliament by law, and, until so defined, shall be those of the House of Commons of the Parliament of the United Kingdom at the commencement of this Constitution.
(4) The provisions of clauses (1), (2) and (3) shall apply in relation to persons who, by virtue of article 88 [and this article], have the right to speak in, or otherwise to take part in, the proceedings of either House… as they apply in relation to members of Parliament.

③ Rule 380 (Lok Sabha) + the two official restatements [external; reported]

“If the Speaker is of opinion that words have been used in the debate which are defamatory or indecent or unparliamentary or undignified, the Speaker may, while exercising discretion, order that such words be expunged from the proceedings of the House.” — LS Rule 380 (as recited).
“Normally, no restrictions are imposed on reporting the proceedings of the House. It is, however, a breach of privilege and contempt of the House to publish expunged proceedings of the House… [I]f debates or proceedings of the House or its Committees are reported mala fide or there is wilful misrepresentation or suppression of speeches of particular Members, it is a breach of privilege and contempt of the House and the offender is liable to punishment. Further, the press is forbidden to publish any part of the proceedings or evidence given before, or any document presented to, Parliamentary Committees before such proceedings or evidence or document has been reported to the House.” — Rajya Sabha Committee on Privileges note (2022).
“Absolute immunity from proceedings in any court of law has been conferred under the Constitution on all personnel connected with the publication of proceedings of either House of Parliament, if such publication is made by or under the authority of House [Article 105(2)].” — ibid.
