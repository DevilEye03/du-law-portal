A1 · The “why” and the “where-from”

A1 · Contempt of Court — its rationale, its history, and the four justifications the case book itself gives

1. What the power actually is

Contempt jurisdiction is not a law of “politeness towards judges”. It is a protective jurisdiction exercised for the public, not for the court. The case book makes you read the point three times over, in three different registers:

“When the court exercises this power, it does not do so to vindicate the dignity and honour of the individual Judge who is personally attacked or scandalised, but to uphold the majesty of the law and of the administration of justice.”Rajendra Sail (Case Book p. 157), summarising settled principle
“the key word is ‘justice’, not ‘judge’; the key — not thought — is unobstructed public justice, not the self-defence of a judge; the cornerstone of the contempt law is the accommodation of two constitutional values — the right of free speech and the right to independent justice.”V.R. Krishna Iyer, J., In re S. Mulgaokar ¶46 (Case Book p. 150)
“…it is entrusted with the power to commit for Contempt of Court, not in order to protect the dignity of the Court against insult or injury as the expression ‘Contempt of Court’ may seem to suggest, but, to protect and to vindicate the right of the public…”Parliamentary Standing Committee on the Administrative Tribunals (Amendment) Bill, 2006 — the reference document placed in your folder, quoting the debate on why tribunals need contempt power

2. Four justifications — and the counter-argument to each (write these as a two-column table)

JustificationThe claimThe answer the critics (and Krishna Iyer, J.) give
Confidence in adjudication“The foundation of the judiciary is the trust and the confidence of the people in its ability to deliver fearless and impartial justice. When the foundation itself is shaken by acts which tend to create disaffection and disrespect for the authority of the court…, the edifice of the judicial system gets eroded.” (Rajendra Sail, Case Book p. 156)Confidence is earned, not enforced: “the best way to sustain the dignity and status of their office is to deserve respect from the public at large by the quality of their judgments… If judges decay the contempt power will not save them and so the other side of the coin is that Judges, like Caesar’s wife, must be above suspicion.” (Mulgaokar ¶46, quoting Gajendragadkar, C.J.)
Non-responsiveness of judgesA judge cannot reply in the media; the law is the only answer available to the institution. “Judges do not defend their decisions in public and if citizens disrespect the persons laying down the law, they cannot be expected to respect the law laid down by them.” (Rajendra Sail, Case Book p. 163) — and, from England, Lord Denning: “from the nature of our office, we cannot reply to their criticisms” (Mulgaokar ¶40, Case Book p. 147).That asymmetry is the reason for restraint, not for eagerness: “In contempt jurisdiction, silence is a sign of strength since our power is wide and we are prosecutor and judge.” (Mulgaokar ¶24)
Aid to fair trialPublications can prejudice minds — including judicial minds: judges “may be influenced subconsciously” (200th Report, quoting AG v BBC and In re P.C. Sen) — so the Act is part of due-process protection for the accused as well as the institution.The Act protects the pendency window only (s. 3 & its Explanation); the pre-FIR, post-arrest gap is a hole that the Law Commission tried to plug by the 2006 Draft Bill (Part F1).
Rule of law / enforcement of ordersCivil contempt (s. 2(b)) is what makes writs, injunctions and undertakings worth anything; without it the court is, in the Standing Committee’s words, “a toothless tiger”.Almost nobody attacks this limb; the reform debate is about the criminal limb and, above all, about “scandalising”.

3. The history your answer must be able to narrate in eight lines

What to write beside this diagram: (i) scandalising the court is a common-law import — “the law which punishes those who scandalize judges is as old as the Common Law itself” (Mulgaokar ¶25); (ii) the Indian Acts of 1926 and 1952 did not define civil or criminal contempt (200th Report, Ch. IV — Case Book cross-reference); (iii) the 1971 Act is the Sanyal Committee’s report legislated — the Statement of Objects and Reasons says exactly that; (iv) 1976 and 2006 are the only two substantive parliamentary amendments in force (plus the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 which merely edited the extent clause).

Verbatim — the statute’s own self-description (Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, preamble & Objects and Reasons, as printed in the bare-Act file in the project)

Long title: “An Act to define and limit the powers of certain courts in punishing contempts of courts and to regulate their procedure in relation thereto.”

Statement of Objects and Reasons (Y.B. Chavan, 14 February 1968): “It is generally felt that the existing law relating to contempt of courts is somewhat uncertain, undefined and unsatisfactory. The jurisdiction to punish for contempt touches upon two important fundamental rights of the citizen, namely, the right to personal liberty and the right to freedom of expression. It was, therefore, considered advisable to have the entire law on the subject scrutinised by a special committee. In pursuance of this, a Committee was set up in 1961 under the Chairmanship of the late Shri H. N. Sanyal, the then Additional Solicitor-General… The recommendations of the Committee have been generally accepted by Government after considering the views… of the State Governments, Union territory Administrations, the Supreme Court, the High Courts and the Judicial Commissioners.”

Note the two verbs in the long title — “define and limit”. That is the answer to the exam question “is the Act a charter of the courts’ power or a charter of citizens’ protection?”: it is both, and ss. 3–9 are the “limit” half. Section 9 says so expressly: “Nothing contained in this Act shall be construed as implying that any disobedience, breach, publication or other act is punishable as contempt of court which would not be so punishable apart from this Act.”

A2 · The constitutional shelf

A2 · Articles 129, 142(2), 215 and Entry 77, List I — the syllabus’s own “Ref:” line, decoded

Your syllabus prints the reference as: “Ref: Articles 129, 142 (2), 215; entry 77, List I, Seventh Schedule, Constitution of India”. Read them as a single argument in four steps — status → power → source of competence → who else may exercise it.

ProvisionWhat it saysWhy it is in this unit (with the case-book link)
Art. 129“The Supreme Court shall be a court of record and shall have all the powers of such a court including the power to punish for contempt of itself.”The source of the Supreme Court’s contempt jurisdiction. Case 19 was invoked precisely under this article: Dr. Rajeev Dhawan filed “Contempt Petition (Crl.) before this Court … by invoking the jurisdiction of this Court under Article 129 of the Constitution of India” (Case Book p. 169). Because the power is constitutional, no statute can abolish it — only regulate it. That single sentence is the answer to “should scandalising be scrapped?” and the Law Commission said exactly this in 2018.
Art. 142(2)“The Supreme Court shall have all and ancillary powers which may be necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it” — (with the clause enabling investigation and punishment for its contempt, as the text read after 1951 in the formulation the syllabus relies on).Gives the Court the ancillary reach: to investigate, to secure attendance, to punish obstruction of its own proceedings. In Case 19 the Court used this breadth in a striking way — it appointed the Solicitor General as prosecutor in its own contempt matter (¶7, Case Book p. 171), a structure impossible in ordinary criminal law.
Art. 215“Every High Court shall be a court of record and shall have all the powers of such a court including the power to punish for contempt of itself.”The High Courts’ own power. Cases 17 and 18 are pure Art. 215/s. 15 stories: the Madhya Pradesh High Court punished contemners for scandalising itself; the Allahabad High Court did the same, and the Supreme Court heard the appeal under s. 19(1)(b). Note the wording — “of itself”: a High Court’s power over subordinate courts comes only from s. 10 of the Act, and is therefore statutory and limited.
Entry 77, List I“Constitution, powers, jurisdiction and authority of the Supreme Court; constitution, powers, jurisdiction and authority of courts of justice”.The legislative competence for the 1971 Act: Parliament could define and limit contempt because contempt of the Supreme Court and of “courts of justice” is a Union subject. This is why the Act is uniform all over India and why State legislatures are incompetent to legislate on it.

The companion provisions you should cite in the same paragraph (they do most of the argumentative work)

  • Articles 121 and 211 — no discussion of a judge’s conduct even in Parliament or a State Legislature except on a motion for his removal. The case book has the CJI’s own use of these articles: “Articles 121 and 211 of our Constitution, prohibiting discussion of the conduct of a Supreme Court or a High Court Judge in the discharge of his duties even by Parliament or a State Legislature, except upon a motion for his removal by the constitutionally prescribed procedure of addresses presented by each House of Parliament after proved misconduct or incapacity of a Judge and resolutions by 2/3 majorities of each House present and voting, are there in our Constitution to ensure this. Can ordinary citizens do elsewhere, with impunity, what members of Parliament cannot do in Parliament and legislators cannot do in a State Legislature, and, if so, to what extent? Such questions will have to be answered by Courts with reference to the facts of particular cases if and when brought to their notice.” (Mulgaokar ¶6, Case Book p. 137)
  • Articles 105 / 194 — legislative privilege: publications under the authority of the House are immune (contrast: a journalist’s report of a parliamentary debate on a pending case enjoys no such blanket immunity).
  • Article 19(2) — the words “in relation to contempt of court” are the constitutional permission-slip for the whole statute. Krishna Iyer, J. ties the two together: “All laws relating to contempt of court had, according to the provisions of Article 19(2), to be ‘reasonable restrictions’ on the exercise of the right of free speech. The courts were given the power — and, indeed, the responsibility — to harmonize conflicting aims, interests and values.” (¶28, Case Book p. 143)
  • Article 19(1)(a) & Article 21 — the counterweights: freedom of expression; and, for the contemner, “the right to personal liberty” which the Act’s own Objects and Reasons concedes is touched by this jurisdiction.
  • Article 361A — no publication shall be made of proceedings in respect of the President or Governor “except with the permission of the court specified in article 361” — the nearest textual cousin of s. 7 of the Act (in camera reporting). [Constitution text; not reproduced in the case book — flag it as an “additional related provision” in your answer, which is exactly what the question wants.]

Pitfall · “inherent” does not mean “unfettered”

Because Arts. 129/215 are constitutional, two careless propositions get marks deducted. (1) “The Act curtails the Supreme Court’s contempt power.” — Not quite: the Act regulates the exercise (defences, standard, punishment, limitation) but cannot abolish the constitutional jurisdiction; the Law Commission made precisely this point when asked in 2018 to confine the Act to civil contempt. (2) “Any court can punish contempt of itself.” — No: only the Supreme Court and High Courts; s. 10 borrows the power for subordinate courts into the High Court’s hands; and a commission of inquiry is not a “court”, however senior its presiding judge — Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2014, five judges): “When a sitting Supreme Court judge is appointed as a Commissioner by the Central government, he does not carry with him all the powers and jurisdiction of the Supreme Court… The Commission in our view is not a court.”

Real example · the National Commission would push the “inherent” logic into the Constitution itself

Recommendation (127) of the NCRC (the report your syllabus names) proposes a constitutional proviso to Article 129 to settle who may punish: “A proviso should be inserted in article 129 so as to provide that the power of court to punish for contempt of itself [is] inherent only in the Supreme Court and the High Courts and is available as part of the privilege of Parliament and State Legislatures, and no other court, tribunal or authority should have or be conferred with a power to punish for contempt of itself.” [Para 7.4.7] — Pair this with the same Commission’s recommendation that tribunals lose the parallel contempt machinery (your folder’s Standing Committee report argues the opposite for the CAT: that tribunals retain civil contempt power). This live disagreement is a first-class 2-mark “critical comment”.

A3 · The statutory anatomy

A3 · “Contempt of court” = civil or criminal — and a word-by-word reading of s. 2(c)

1. The definitions (verbatim — s. 2, Contempt of Courts Act, 1971)

Section 2 — “In this Act, unless the context otherwise requires”

(a) “contempt of court” means civil contempt or criminal contempt;

(b) “civil contempt” means wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court;

(c) “criminal contempt” means the publication (whether by words, spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representations, or otherwise) of any matter or the doing of any other act whatsoever which —
  (i) scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; or
  (ii) prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or
  (iii) interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner;

(d) “High Court” means the High Court for a State or a Union territory, and includes the court of the Judicial Commissioner in any Union territory.

2. The six-element checklist for a charge of criminal contempt (learn this as the “actus reus grid”)

#ElementWhat must be shown — and where your four cases supply it
1A “publication” or “act”Words (spoken/written), signs, visible representations or otherwise; or “the doing of any other act whatsoever”. The 200th Report wanted an Explanation naming “world wide web” (Draft Bill, cl. 3: “publication includes publication in print, radio broadcast, electronic media, cable television network, world wide web”) — not enacted, but courts already treat online posts as publications. In Case 19 the alleged publication was a press-conference statement plus its reportage; in Case 17, a speech and a news report; in Case 16, a news item and an article.
2As to a court / the judiciaryThe target must be the court as an institution or a judge as judge. Mulgaokar ¶29–30: personal attack = libel; “the former is not contempt, the latter is, although overlapping spaces abound”.
3Scandalising / lowering authority or prejudice to a pending proceeding or obstruction “in any other manner”Three alternative limbs. Limb (i) is “scandalising” — the head your syllabus names first. Limb (ii) is the trial-by-media head (Unit 2.4 in the companion file). Limb (iii) is the residual — “tendency” is enough (Dr D.C. Saxena, quoted in Rajendra Sail, Case Book p. 160: “a tendency to scandalise the court or tendency to lower the authority of the court… would be a criminal contempt”).
4Tendency, not resultActual interference need not be proved: “it is well established that it is not necessary to prove affirmatively that there has been an actual interference with the administration of justice… it is enough if it is likely, or tends in any way, to interfere with the proper administration of law” (Brahma Prakash Sharma, as quoted in Rajendra Sail, Case Book p. 157).
5No statutory defence (ss. 3–8)Innocent publication; fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding; fair comment on the merits of a decided case; representation in good faith about a subordinate court; in camera rules; truth under s. 13(b) as a permitted defence. Mapped in A4.
6“Substantial” interference gate + mens-rea shadowPost-2006, s. 13(a) is a mandatory filter: no sentence unless the court “is satisfied that the contempt is of such a nature that it substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice”. And though mens rea is not an element, “the courts are loath to punish a contemnor, if the act or omission complained of, was not willful” (S. Abdul Karim, quoted at Case Book p. 162).

Pitfall · s. 3(2) is not a licence to write about judges

Students routinely write: “a case was not pending, so there is no contempt”. Wrong for limb (i). Section 3 answers only the “interference with a pending proceeding” limb (2(c)(ii)). The scandalising limb (2(c)(i)) has no pendency requirement — which is precisely why Mulgaokar (a controversy about a draft code of ethics, no lis) and Rajendra Sail (criticism of an already delivered appellate judgment) were both litigated as criminal contempt. Then the s. 13(a) “substantially interferes” test does the filtering work instead.

A5 · First syllabus head

A5 · “Scandalising, vilification of the institution of Court” — the offence in full detail

1. What “scandalising the court” legally means (the case book’s own definition)

Verbatim — the two formulations the case book puts in front of you

(a) From England, adopted in India — Lord Esher M.R., Queen v. Gray (1900) QBD 36, quoted in Mulgaokar ¶37 (Case Book p. 145): “Any act done or writing published calculated to bring a Court or a judge of the Court into contempt, or to lower his authority, is a contempt of Court. That is one class of contempt. Further, any act done or writing published calculated to obstruct or interfere with the due course of justice or the lawful process of the Courts is a contempt of Court. The former class belongs to the category which Lord Hardwicke L.C. characterised as ‘scandalising a Court or a Judge’.” And the guideline that follows it: “Judges and Courts are alike open to criticism, and if reasonable argument or expostulation is offered against any judicial act as contrary to law or the public good, no Court could or would treat that as contempt of Court. The law ought not to be astute in such cases…”

(b) The Indian statutory gloss — Dr D.C. Saxena v. Hon’ble the Chief Justice of India (1996) 5 SCC 216, extracted at length in Rajendra Sail (Case Book pp. 159–160): “Scandalising the court… would mean hostile criticism of Judges as Judges or judiciary. Any personal attack upon a Judge in connection with the office he holds is dealt with under law of libel or slander. Yet defamatory publication concerning the Judge as a Judge brings the court or Judges into contempt, a serious impediment to justice and an inroad on the majesty of justice… It would, therefore, be scandalising the Judge as a Judge, in other words, imputing partiality, corruption, bias, improper motives to a Judge is scandalisation of the court and would be contempt of the court. Even imputation of lack of impartiality or fairness to a Judge in the discharge of his official duties amounts to contempt. The gravamen of the offence is that of lowering his dignity or authority or an affront to the majesty of justice… the tendency if it may lead to or tends to lower the authority of the court is a criminal contempt.” Also, from the same passage: “Any caricature of a Judge calculated to lower the dignity of the court would destroy, undermine or tend to undermine public confidence in the administration of justice.”

4. Krishna Iyer, J.’s six “functional canons” (Mulgaokar ¶¶27–33) — the theoretical heart of Unit 3

He expressly calls them “broad guidelines — not a complete inventory, but precedentially validated judicial norms” (¶26). Learn the numbering; it is what a good answer is built on:

  1. Wise economy of use. “The first rule in this branch of contempt power is a wise economy of use by the Court of this branch of its jurisdiction. The Court will act with seriousness and severity where justice is jeopardized by a gross and/or unfounded attack on the judges, where the attack is calculated to obstruct or destroy the judicial process. The court is willing to ignore, by a majestic liberalism, trifling and venial offenses — the dogs may bark, the caravan will pass. The court will not be prompted to act as a result of an easy irritability.” (¶27)
  2. Harmonisation of two constitutional values. “The second principle must be to harmonise the constitutional values of free criticism, the fourth estate included, and the need for a fearless curial process and its presiding functionary, the judge. A happy balance has to be struck, the benefit of the doubt being given generously against the judge, slurring over marginal deviations but severely proving the supremacy of the law over pugnacious, vicious, unrepentant and malignant contemners, be they the powerful press, gang-up of vested interests, veteran columnists or Olympian establishmentarians… to criticise the judge fairly, albeit fiercely, is no crime but a necessary right, twice blessed in a democracy.” (¶28)
  3. No confusion between protecting a libelled judge and protecting public justice. “The third principle is to avoid confusion between personal protection of a libeled judge and prevention of obstruction of public justice and the community’s confidence in that great process. The former is not contempt, the latter is, although overlapping spaces abound.” (¶29)
  4. Free play for the Fourth Estate within responsible limits. “The fourth functional canon… is that the Fourth Estate which is an indispensable intermediary between the State and the people and necessary instrumentality in strengthening the forces of democracy, should be given free play within responsible limits even when the focus of its critical attention is the court, including the highest Court.” (¶31)
  5. Not to be hypersensitive. “The fifth normative guideline for the judges to observe in this jurisdiction is not to be hypersensitive even where distortions and criticisms overstep the limits, but to deflate vulgar denunciation by dignified bearing, condescending indifference and repudiation by judicial rectitude.” (¶32)
  6. Strike, when it must be struck. “The sixth consideration is that, after evaluating the totality of factors, if the court considers the attack on the judge or judges scurrilous, offensive, intimidatory or malicious beyond condonable limits, the strong arm of the law must, in the name of public interest and public justice, strike, a blow on him who challenges the supremacy of the rule of law by fouling its source and stream.” (¶33)

How to use the diagram in the exam: draw the funnel left-to-right, then write one line for each canon with the key phrase in quotation marks. It answers “scandalising” questions (because it shows the restraint principle), and it also answers “critically examine the law of contempt” questions (because it shows the Court regulating itself).

Debate box · is “scandalising” a “vestigial organ”?

For retention (the case book’s own balance plus the Law Commission): the offence protects litigants’ confidence, not judges’ feelings; s. 13(a) now filters out trivia; truth is a permitted defence (s. 13(b)); the Law Commission of India, asked in March 2018 whether the Act should be confined to civil contempt, answered no — “amending the existing definition will lead to ambiguity… it could potentially lessen the respect for or fear of the courts”, and, since Arts. 129/215 are constitutional, deleting limb (i) “would not eliminate the power… it would only create ambiguity”. For abolition (the criticism you must be able to state fairly): the United Kingdom abolished the common-law offence by s. 33 of the Crime and Courts Act, 2013, having not used it since 1931; the offence is “incapable of precise definition”; it chills accountability journalism at the exact moment when “the quality of their judgments” should be the judiciary’s only armour; and the “coloured populations” rationale (McLeod v. St. Aubyn) is a colonial argument Krishna Iyer, J. himself refused to accept. The middle position Mulgaokar actually endorses: retain, but with “benign neglect not judicial genuflexion” as the ordinary prescription, and — crucially — the suggestion that “the Bar and the Press evolve a dignified consensus on the canons of ethics in this area… so that the Bench may give it a close look and draw the objective line of action” (¶56, Case Book p. 153).

A6 · Second syllabus head

A6 · “Unverified reporting” — the media-law head that the case book treats as a distinct species of contempt

Note the drafting of the syllabus: “Scandalising, vilification of institution of Court; Unverified reporting; Fair comment and criticism”. The middle head is not a sub-species of scandalising: it is the process failure — publishing about courts and judges without verification. Two of your four cases (17 and 18) are essentially unverified-reporting cases, and they supply a rule-set that no other Indian media-law topic does.

1. The duty, in the Court’s own words

“With this immense power, comes the burden of responsibility. With the huge amount of information that they process, it is the responsibility of the media to ensure that they are not providing the public with information that is factually wrong, biased or simply unverified information. The right to freedom of speech is enshrined in Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution. However, this right is restricted by Article 19(2) in the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, decency and morality and also Contempt of Courts Act and defamation.”Sanjoy Narayan ¶7 (Case Book p. 167)
“The role of the media is to provide to the readers and the public in general with information and views tested and found as true and correct. This power must be carefully regulated and must reconcile with a person’s fundamental right to privacy. Any wrong or biased information that is put forth can potentially damage the otherwise clean and good reputation of the person or institution against whom something adverse is reported. Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions must be avoided.”Sanjoy Narayan ¶8 (Case Book p. 167)
“His image was sought to be tarnished by a newspaper report which was apparently based on surmises and conjectures and not based on facts and figures. The dignity of the courts and the people’s faith in administration must not be tarnished because of biased and unverified reporting. In order to avoid such biased reporting, one must be careful to verify the facts and do some research on the subject being reported before a publication is brought out.”Sanjoy Narayan ¶9 (Case Book p. 168)

2. The seven ingredients of “unverified reporting” as an offence (or as an aggravating factor)

#IngredientAuthority and what it means for a newsroom

3Attribution is contested and the report cannot be stood up.The classic evidential problem: the speaker denies the interview; the reporter says he was there. If this is done in a reasonable manner, which pre-supposes accuracy of information about a matter on which any criticism is offered, and arguments are directed fairly against any reasoning adopted, I would, speaking for myself, be the last person to consider it objectionable even if some criticism offered is erroneous.”

3. The “contempt clock” — when unverified reporting becomes dangerous, and when it becomes punishable

Text to write beside it (from the Explanation below s. 3(3), verbatim): a criminal proceeding is “pending” “when the charge-sheet or challan is filed, or when the court issues summons or warrant, as the case may be, against the accused”, and in any other case “when the court takes cognizance”; it “shall be deemed to continue to be pending until it is heard and finally decided” or, where no appeal is preferred, until the appeal/revision limitation expires; and a decided case “shall not be deemed to be pending merely by reason of the fact that proceedings for the execution” are pending. The 200th Report’s remedy: replace “pending” with “active”, begin it at arrest (“whichever is earlier”), and insert the words “world wide web” into s. 2(c) (Part F1).

Two case-book consequences you must state: (a) scandalising (limb (i)) operates outside the clock — Case 17 concerned criticism of a judgment already delivered and was still a contempt; (b) inside the clock, s. 5 does not help at all, because fair comment is protected only “on the merits of any case which has been heard and finally decided” — that is the trap in most media-problem questions.

5. The related codes you must bolt on (this is where “holistic” marks are earned)

Cable TV — Programme Code, Cable Television Networks Rules, 1994, r. 6(15)(xviii)

Press Council of India Act, 1978 — the self-regulation leg

s. 13(2)(b): the Council’s functions include “to build up a code of conduct for newspapers, news agencies and journalists in accordance with high professional standards”. s. 14: power to “warn, admonish or censure” after inquiry — but “nothing… shall be deemed to empower the Council to hold an inquiry into any matter in respect of which any proceeding is pending in a court of law” (s. 14(3)) and its decision “shall be final and shall not be questioned in any court of law” (s. 14(4)). s. 15(3): “Every inquiry held by the Council shall be deemed to be a judicial proceeding within the meaning of sections 193 and 228 of the Indian Penal Code” → read today: BNS s. 229 (false evidence) and BNS s. 267 (intentional insult/interruption of a public servant sitting in a judicial proceeding).

Pitfall · the Press Council “does not work” argument is in your own case

In Case 17 the Supreme Court imported Robertson & Nicol on Media Law to record that “media’s self regulation has failed in United Kingdom”, that the Press Complaints Commission “has no legal powers”, its adjudications being published “usually in small print and without prominence”, and that the PCC “has been regarded as [a] public relations operation” — then it said, of India: “we too have Press Council. The only aspect, we wish to emphasise is that the present matter reinforces the need to ensure that the right of freedom of media is exercised responsibly. It is for media itself and other concerned to consider as how to achieve it.” That is a deliberately unfinished sentence: quote it, then supply your own concrete suggestions (see the checklist below).

6. A verification protocol you can actually write as “suggestions” (derived from the three cases + Mulgaokar)

  1. Two-source rule for any judicial fact; where the source is a participant’s memory, obtain the record — the case file, the order copy, the transcript (the Allahabad/Bhopal courts’ own instinct was to summon “the audio and video recording of the speech… as well as the transcript” and to juxtapose them with the news report).
  2. Right of reply to the person criticised, in writing, with a deadline; and the copy of the intended extract, as the High Court did in Case 17 by “direct[ing] the supply of the copies of the transcript to the contemnors and g[iving] opportunity to file objections”.
  3. Quotation hygiene: mark paraphrases as paraphrases; never put in quotation marks what was not recorded; keep the recording (a trainee’s notebook lost a six-month sentence to nobody).
  4. Legal-eyes-on-the-page review for anything that (a) imputes a motive to a judge, (b) uses words like “rubbish/dustbin/for sale/no guts”, (c) concerns a matter still pending, or (d) relies on a leak/in-chamera material (s. 7).
  5. Pre-judgment ban internally: no verdict language, no “guilty”, “killer”, “the accused confessed”, no polls on guilt (see Practice P3 in Part G).
  6. Correction protocol: if wrong, correct promptly, prominently and unconditionally — Case 17 shows an apology published on the front page before the contempt notice arrived was enough to save the newspaper staff; Case 18 shows that an apology which the High Court thought “was not unqualified” had to be re-tendered in unconditional form before the Supreme Court accepted it.
  7. Self-regulation with teeth: an internal judicial-desk manual + reference to the Press Council for standards, recognising the Council cannot inquire once contempt proceedings are pending (s. 14(3)) and cannot fine anyone.

A7 · Third syllabus head

A7 · “Fair comment and criticism” — the complete code of what may be said about courts

Three sources combine into one rule: s. 5 of the Act (the statutory immunity), Ambard’s proviso-conditions (the common-law soul, re-adopted by the Privy Council and by the Supreme Court), and the case book’s four decisions which draw the line in each direction.

1. The statute — read s. 5 and s. 4 together, then notice what is not there

Verbatim

s. 4: “Subject to the provisions contained in section 7, a person shall not be guilty of contempt of court for publishing a fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding or any stage thereof.”

s. 5: “A person shall not be guilty of contempt of court for publishing any fair comment on the merits of any case which has been heard and finally decided.”

What s. 5 does not say (and this is the whole exam point): it does not protect comment on a judge’s conduct or on the functioning of the institution; it does not protect comment on a case that is still pending; it says nothing about tone; and it contains no “public interest” or “justification” limb — those live only in s. 13(b).

Gap-filler: s. 8 preserves “any other defence which would have been a valid defence in any proceedings for contempt of court”, and s. 6 protects “any statement made by him in good faith concerning the presiding officer of any subordinate court” made to another subordinate court or to the controlling High Court.

2. The charter passage — and the five conditions inside it

“…no wrong is committed by any member of the public who exercises the ordinary right of criticizing in good faith in private or public the public act done in the seat of justice. The path of criticism is a public way: the wrong-headed are permitted to err therein: provided that members of the public (1) abstain from imputing improper motives to those taking part in the administration of justice, and (2) are genuinely exercising a right of criticism and (3) not acting in malice or (4) attempting to impair the administration of justice, they are immune. Justice is not a cloistered virtue: she must be allowed to suffer the scrutiny and respectful, even though outspoken comments of ordinary men.”Lord Atkin, Ambard v. Attorney-General for Trinidad and Tobago (1936) A.C. 322 — quoted by the case book in Mulgaokar ¶38, again in Brahma Prakash Sharma at ¶43, and at length in Rajendra Sail (Case Book pp. 146, 148, 157)

The case book preserves a variant of the same passage (from Aswini Kumar Ghose v. Arabinda Bose, AIR 1953 SC 75, quoted at Case Book p. 157) with a fifth condition: “…(5) not acting… in an attempt to impair the administration of justice”, plus the important gloss that “the Supreme Court is never over-sensitive to public criticism; but when there is danger of grave mischief being done in the matter of administration of justice, the animadversion cannot be ignored and viewed with placid equanimity.”

3. What “fair” requires — the ten positive rules, each with its authority

#RuleAuthority (all in the case book)
1Any criticism of a judgment is open, however vigorous — a judgment is a public document and a public act.Rajendra Sail: “No criticism of a judgment, however vigorous, can amount to contempt of Court, provided it is kept within the limits of reasonable courtesy and good faith” (p. 164); R.C. Cooper: “the Court like any other institution does not enjoy immunity from fair criticism… This Court does not claim to be always right” (pp. 148, 158); P.N. Duda: “in the free market place of ideas criticism about the judicial system or Judges should be welcome so long as such criticism does not impair or hamper the administration of justice” (p. 159).
2You may say the judgment is wrong — on law or on facts — and you may say the court erred.Rajendra Sail: “Such a criticism may fairly assert that the judgment is incorrect or an error has been committed both with regard to law or established facts. It is one thing to say that a judgment on facts as disclosed is not in consonance with evidence or the law has not been correctly applied” (p. 165). And R.C. Cooper: they “do not think themselves in possession of all truth or hold that wherever others differ from them, it is so far error.”
3Mistaken criticism is still protected if honestly made.Mulgaokar, opening of ¶1 (Begee, CJI): “I would… be the last person to consider it objectionable even if some criticism offered is erroneous”; R. v. Brett (an article that “made ridiculous mistakes of fact and… its logic was greatly at fault”) was held not to be contempt (¶35); and the Mill/Milton passages quoted in ¶1 (the “dialectical process”; “Let her and Falsehood grapple”).
4“Outspoken” and “strong” are permitted words; the test is not politeness but absence of imputed motive and abuse.Lord Denning M.R. in ex p. Cooper: “while fair and temperate criticism of this Court or any other Court even if strong, may not be actionable…”. In Re Harijai Singh the Court accepted the apology “with a warning that they should be careful in future”.
5Accuracy and reasoning matter: the court weighs the language, the fairness, the factual accuracy, the logical soundness, and the care taken in analysing the material.Mulgaokar ¶17 (Begee, CJI) — the five factors. Add ¶1: “reasonable manner… pre-supposes accuracy of information about a matter on which any criticism is offered, and arguments are directed fairly against any reasoning adopted”.
6Use the lawful channels for institutional change: appeal or revision; a complaint to the proper authority; a representation against a subordinate court’s presiding officer; an address for removal; and (per the Privy Council in Ogilvie) “approach in the proper form to Parliament”. “Motives to the Judges need not be attributed” (P.N. Duda, p. 159).R. v. Ogilvie (¶36); Brahma Prakash Sharma (¶43); s. 6 of the Act; Mulgaokar ¶9 (a “Committee of Chief Justices… could itself forward its findings for appropriate action under Article 124… and the Judges’ Inquiries Act, 1968”).
7Reform advocacy is safe and desirable — even when it criticises judges as a class — provided it is constructive.Mulgaokar ¶47 (Krishna Iyer, J.): “if Judges have frailties — after all they are human — they need to be corrected by independent criticism… the contempt power should not be an interdict… Even bodies like the Law Commission or the Law Institute and researchers, legal and sociological, may run ‘contempt’ risks because their professional work sometimes involves unpleasant criticism of judges, judicial processes and the system itself… Creative legal journalism and activist statesmanship for judicial reform cannot be jeopardised by an undefined apprehension of ‘contempt’ action.”
8But the critic’s standing and knowledge are weighed — an insider’s reckless allegation is worse than a layperson’s heat.Arundhati Roy (2002) 3 SCC 343, quoted at p. 161: “To ascertain the good faith and the public interest, the courts have to see all the surrounding circumstances including the person responsible for comments, his knowledge in the field regarding which the comments are made and the intended purpose sought to be achieved… All citizens cannot be permitted to comment upon the conduct of the courts in the name of fair criticism which, if not checked, would destroy the institution itself.” Applied in Case 17 to hold a law graduate and seasoned PIL petitioner to a higher standard of caution.
9The litigant’s venting is the classic abuse of “fair criticism”.Roshan Lal Ahuja: “No litigant can be permitted to overstep the limits of fair, bona fide and reasonable criticism of a judgment and bring the courts generally in disrepute or attribute motives to the Judges rendering the judgment”; “Liberty of free expression is not to be confused with a licence to make unfounded, unwarranted and irresponsible aspersions against the Judges or the courts in relation to judicial matters. No system of justice can tolerate such an unbridled licence.”
10Threats of proceedings against a judge, or demands for enquiry into a judge, are outside “criticism” altogether.Ajay Kumar Pandey (1996) 6 SCC 510: “any threat of filing a complaint against the Judge in respect of the judicial proceedings conducted by him in his own Court is a positive attempt to interfere with the due course of administration of justice… If, however, litigants and their counsel start threatening the Judge or launch prosecution against him for what he has honestly and bona fide done in his Court, the judicial independence would vanish eroding the very edifice on which the institution of justice stands” (p. 159–160). Compare the fatal sentence in Case 17: “he would also get an enquiry held as regard to the conduct of one of the judges who delivered the judgment, as that particular judge is to retire within a month”.

Debate box · “criticism must be constructive” — a hidden content test?

Read Arundhati Roy (as quoted in Case 17) with P.N. Duda and you find a tension the examiners love: Duda welcomes criticism “in the free market place of ideas”, while Roy requires the court to look at “the person responsible for comments, his knowledge in the field… and the intended purpose” and says “all citizens cannot be permitted to comment upon the conduct of the courts in the name of fair criticism”. In plain terms: the law of fair criticism is partly speaker-sensitive. A senior advocate’s technical critique and a layperson’s emotive tweet are not judged on the same scale. Mulgaokar cuts the other way, insisting the benefit of the doubt goes “generously against the judge” (¶28). Your best answer names the tension and resolves it functionally: the speaker test is a device for assessing capacity to cause real damage to public confidence, not a licence to protect the bench from experts.

A8 · Punishment · the law of apology · procedure, bench, proof, limitation and appeal

1. The sentencing provisions (verbatim, s. 12 & s. 13)

Section 12 — Punishment for contempt of court

“(1) Save as otherwise expressly provided in this Act or in any other law, a contempt of court may be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine which may extend to two thousand rupees, or with both: Provided that the accused may be discharged or the punishment awarded may be remitted on apology being made to the satisfaction of the Court. Explanation.—An apology shall not be rejected merely on the ground that it is qualified or conditional if the accused makes it bona fide.

(2) … no court shall impose a sentence in excess of that specified in sub-section (1) for any contempt either in respect of itself or of a court subordinate to it.

(3) … where a person is found guilty of a civil contempt, the court, if it considers that a fine will not meet the ends of justice and that a sentence of imprisonment is necessary shall, instead of sentencing him to simple imprisonment, direct that he be detained in a civil prison for such period not exceeding six months…

(4) Where the person found guilty… in respect of any undertaking given to a court is a company, every person who, at the time the contempt was committed, was in charge of, and was responsible to, the company for the conduct of business… shall be deemed to be guilty… with the leave of the court, by the detention in civil prison of each such person: Provided … if he proves that the contempt was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent its commission.

(5) … where the contempt… has been committed with the consent or connivance of, or is attributable to any neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary or other officer… such person shall also be deemed to be guilty… Explanation: “company” means any body corporate and includes a firm or other association of individuals; and “director”, in relation to a firm, means a partner.”

Section 13 (substituted by Act 6 of 2006, w.e.f. 17.3.2006): “Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force, — (a) no court shall impose a sentence under this Act for a contempt of court unless it is satisfied that the contempt is of such a nature that it substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice; (b) the court may permit, in any proceeding for contempt of court, justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that it is in public interest and the request for invoking the said defence is bona fide.”

Read s. 12(4)–(5) into media cases: for a newspaper company, the persons “in charge of… the conduct of business” are deemed guilty, with a due-diligence defence — that is the statutory skeleton behind the case book’s editorial-liability chain (Case 17). And s. 12(2) answers a question that is often asked: a High Court cannot exceed six months/₹2,000 even for contempt of a subordinate court.

The distilled test (and the modern restatement)

Elements of an acceptable apology: (1) unconditional/unqualified in form — though after the 2006 insertion of the Explanation to s. 12(1) a qualified apology made bona fide cannot be rejected merely because it is qualified; (2) timeliness — before or immediately after notice counts heavily in the contemner’s favour (Case 17); (3) admission of fault, not of strategy — “conveying remorse” (Case 18 ¶12), not an argument that the contempt “may have” occurred; (4) repetition of the correct position to the same audience — the publication-of-apology direction (Case 18) is the media-law-specific ingredient; and (5) the gravity check — “acceptance of apology and sympathy in a case like this would be uncalled for”, i.e. where deliberate, motivated and calculated damage is discernible (Roshan Lal Ahuja: “If a person committing such gross contempt of court were to get the impression that he will get off lightly it would be a most unfortunate state of affairs. Sympathy in such a case would be totally misplaced…”).

Modern restatement: in December 2025 a Bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta set aside a Bombay High Court sentence of one week’s imprisonment (for a residential-society circular about the judiciary concerning stray dogs) and held: “The power to punish necessarily carries within it the concomitant power to forgive… this power is not a personal armour for judges, nor a sword to silence criticism… Mercy, therefore, must remain an integral part of the judicial conscience, to be extended where the contemnor sincerely acknowledges his lapse and seeks to atone for it”, reading the Explanation to s. 12 into “the scheme” of the section. [Reported 11.12.2025 — a near-perfect, quotable fusion of Mulgaokar and the 2006 amendment.]

4. Procedure and safeguards — the eleven protections of an alleged contemner

  1. Specification of the charge — “Every motion or reference made under this section shall specify the contempt of which the person charged is alleged to be guilty” (s. 15(3)). In Case 19 the notices had to be “accompanied by the charges”; their non-service after 1994 is what killed the case in 2014.
  2. Personal service of notice, unless the court records reasons otherwise, with the motion and its affidavits (or the reference) annexed (s. 17(1)–(2)).
  3. Two-judge Bench for hearing criminal contempt (s. 18(1)).
  4. Defence on affidavit, and evidence only as the court thinks necessary (s. 17(5)).
  5. Standard of proof — beyond reasonable doubt, with the Bramblevale gloss, “where there are two equally consistent possibilities… it is not right to hold that the offence is proved” (Case 17, pp. 161–162).
  6. The “accuser and judge in one” caution — “A question whether there is contempt of court or not is a serious one. The court is both the accuser as well as the judge of the accusation. The court has to act with [the] greatest circumspection. It is only when a clear case of contemptuous conduct not explainable otherwise, arises that the contemnor must be punished” (Arundhati Roy, quoted at p. 161).
  7. Right to be tried by another judge in in-face contempt, on application under s. 14(2), the matter being placed before the Chief Justice for directions; and the special evidence rule in s. 14(3).
  8. Bail / bonds pending determination (s. 14(4)); attachment only if the court is satisfied the contemner is likely to abscond to avoid service, with mandatory release on appearance (s. 17(3)–(4)).
  9. The 13(a) substantive gate and 13(b) truth defence (see above).
  10. Apology — s. 12(1) proviso + Explanation; discharge or remission.
  11. Appeal as of right — s. 19(1), 30/60 days; s. 19(2) suspension, bail and “hearing notwithstanding unpurged contempt”; s. 19(3) allows the High Court itself to grant those reliefs if satisfied that an appeal is intended.

Three lessons to write: (i) s. 20 bars initiation, not delayed adjudication — so a case validly initiated in 1994 could still be “alive” in 2014; (ii) initiation = at least the decision to issue notice / take cognizance, which is why the Court recorded in Case 19 that on 13.4.1994 notice to show cause was issued “but no cognizance was taken on that date”; (iii) after the limitation period passes, the court’s equity matters: “at this distance of time, when the subject matter remained dormant for almost two decades and now contemner No. 3 is 96 years and he is not able to respond to the charges due to old age and illness, we do not think that this is a fit case where we should deal with the matter further”. A 20-year-old contempt trial of a 96-year-old would itself have been an affront to the majesty of justice.

Pitfall · “the court cannot take cognizance of contempt which is also an IPC offence” — read the proviso carefully

s. 10’s proviso disables only the High Court’s power to punish contempts of subordinate courts where the same contempt “is an offence punishable under the Indian Penal Code, 1860” (now the BNS, 2023 — a coincidence worth naming: the IPC was Act 45 of 1860; the BNS is Act 45 of 2023). It does not bar (a) the Supreme Court, (b) a High Court acting for contempt of itself under Art. 215, or (c) punishment of a contempt that merely also happens to be defamatory. Practical examples: imputing bribery to a subordinate-court judge is both BNS s. 161-type bribery-adjacent/ s. 217 (false information to injure, ex-IPC 182) territory and contempt; in-face abuse of the presiding judge is squarely IPC s. 228 → BNS s. 267, and the proviso is then engaged for contempt of that subordinate court.

I. FACTS — reconstructed from the case book, paragraph by paragraph

The case book presents Mulgaokar not as a trial but as a notice-stage proceeding, and the facts come to us through the judges’ own narrative. Chronology (all from ¶¶1–13):

  1. A suggestion for judicial self-regulation. The Chief Justice of India had written a letter to the Chief Justices of various High Courts “suggesting, inter-alia, that Chief Justices could meet and draft a code of ethics themselves or through a Committee of Chief Justices so as to prevent possible lapses from the path of rectitude and propriety on the part of Judges” (¶3). The letter was confidential: “Judges of this Court were not even aware of the contents of the letter before it was sent … as Chief Justice of India” (¶3).
  2. The news item — 13 December 1977, The Indian Express. “Some people perhaps believe that attempts to hold trials of everything and everybody by publications in newspapers must include those directed against the highest Court of Justice in this country and its pronouncements” (¶1). The offending sentence, quoted verbatim in ¶2, said: “So adverse has been the criticism that the Supreme Court Judges, some of whom had prepared the draft code, have disowned it.” — describing “the supposed code of judicial ethics assumed wrongly to have been drafted by some Judges of the Supreme Court”.
  3. Correction sought; correction refused — and the confidential letter revealed. The error was pointed out to the Editor “in a letter sent by the Registrar of this Court”. “Instead of publishing any correction of the mis-statement about the conduct of Judges of this Court, the Editor offered to publish the whole material in his possession, as though there was an issue to be tried between the Editor of a newspaper and this Court and the readers were there to try it and decide it” (¶3). The Registrar’s reply made clear that the letter to the Chief Justices “were known to the Editor”.
  4. The article — 21 December 1977, “Behaving like a Judge”. It characterised the suggestion that judges should themselves frame a code of judicial ethics as “so utterly inimical to the independence of the judiciary, violative of the Constitutional safeguards in that respect, and offensive to the self-respect of the Judges as to make one wonder how it was conceived in the first place” (¶7). It “proceeded on the assumption that there is already a formulated code of ethics sent to the Chief Justices” (¶9), and it “ends by attempting to make a distinction between the wonderful performance of High Court Judges and the ‘disappointing’ record of the Supreme Court”, attributing that to the fact that the Supreme Court is “packed by the former Prime Minister, Mrs. Gandhi, ‘with pliant and submissive judges except for a few’” (¶10).
  5. The borrowed authority. “The supposed writer of the article was evidently so shaky about his ability to substantiate his suggestions … that he took shelter behind views alleged to have been expressed by Mr. Jayaprakash Narayan on some occasion” (¶11) — which the Court held it could not adjudicate “without giving notice to other parties, and without taking evidence about the circumstances and the context”.
  6. Earlier, parallel proceeding. The Chief Justice’s opinion refers to his “separate statement of detailed reasons which place on record my difference of opinion with the order ultimately passed by a majority in this Court upon a case resulting from a news item published in the Times of India recently” (¶2) — i.e. a previous contempt episode against the same newspaper had been resolved by the majority in favour of not proceeding, over his dissent.
  7. The appearance before the Court. “Mr. Jethmalani appearing for A. G. Noorani, to whom we had issued no notice” argued that the writing was innocent (¶12); Mr. Tarkunde appeared for the Editor (see ¶18: “we have accepted the submissions of Mr. Tarkunde and Mr. Jethmalani”). The contemned was S. Mulgaokar, then of the Indian Express; the proceedings were in re — initiated by the Court.

Notice what is not in these facts: there is no pending case which the publications could prejudice, no witness interfered with, no order disobeyed. Everything in Mulgaokar turns on s. 2(c)(i) — scandalising/lowering the authority of the court — which is precisely why the syllabus places it first in a unit headed “Scandalising, vilification of institution of Court … Fair comment and criticism”.

III. ARGUMENTS — both sides

The case against the publication (as the Court put it, because the petitioner was the Court itself)

  • Gross distortion for collateral ends. “I find, however, that gross distortions of what was actually held by this Court in what is known as the Habeas Corpus case (Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. S. Shukla) being made presumably to serve ulterior objects” (¶2) — i.e. the writing was part of a campaign, not isolated error.
  • Imputation of untruthfulness and cowardice. “Comments about Judges of the Supreme Court suggesting that they lack moral courage to the extent of having ‘disowned’ what they had done, or, in other words, to the extent of uttering what was untrue, at least verge on contempt” — because such suggestions “would … make Judges of this Court look ridiculous or even unworthy, in the estimation of the public, of the very high office they hold” (¶4).
  • Trial by newspaper is not the lawful forum. “Editors of at least responsible newspapers should be aware that it is Courts of law and not newspaper readers who have to try certain issues which courts alone are empowered to determine. Courts adopt a procedure designed to prevent, as far as possible, unfair prejudices, irrelevances, and untruths creeping in. The character and the legal consequences of any publication about conduct of judges are certainly matters for Courts to determine” (¶5).
  • Constitutional architecture. Arts. 129 (Court of record) + 121/211 (no legislative discussion of judges’ conduct) embody “the principle of Supremacy of the Constitution”, which requires “an executive which respects the judiciary and its verdicts” and “the absence of any legislative interference with judicial functions in a manner characterised by Dean Roscoe Pound as ‘legislative lynching’… It would be a sad day for the supremacy of the Constitution and for the Rule of Law… if malicious or ill informed persons, filled with the irrationality involved in the spirit of what Dean Pound called ‘lynching’… were allowed to take upon themselves the task of passing judgments on actions of others particularly of Judges performing judicial functions. That would certainly sound the death knell of what Dean Roscoe Pound calls ‘judicial justice’ and the Rule of Law” (¶6).
  • Confidentiality. The Editor possessed a confidential communication and, instead of correcting, proposed to try the issue in his columns.
  • Institutional harm. “when the question is of injury to an institution, such as the highest Court of justice in the land, one cannot overlook its effects upon national honour and prestige in the comity of nations” (¶16).

The case for the newspaper / against proceeding

  • No intent to injure; a public-purpose motive. “Mr. Jethmalani … tried to convince us that there was no intention on the part of the writer of the article or the editor to injure the dignity or position of this Court but the intention was only to direct public attention to matters of extreme importance to the nation. If this were so it would be a desirable object.” (¶12)
  • The reply (and the Court’s answer). “But, as we should all know, there are proper and permissible ways of carrying out such an object and others which are not permitted by law, or, at least by elementary rules of fairness” (¶12).
  • Nothing unlawful in judicial self-regulation — the premise of the article was examined and rejected by the Court itself: “there is no constitutional safeguard or provision relating to the independence of the judiciary which could possibly prevent Judges themselves meeting to formulate a code of judicial ethics… Indeed, in America, the American Bar Association has formulated a code of this kind. None has been formulated so far in this country.” (¶8). A code “if scrupulously observed by all the Judges, could only enhance their independence and prestige and not injure these in any way whatsoever” (¶8).
  • Context of the Emergency. “we have been passing through a period of exceptional strain and stress and excitement in this country in which unusual remarks made have not been confined to what appears in newspapers” (¶13) — a plea of historical circumstance, which the Chief Justice expressly accepted as a reason for dropping the proceeding.
  • Judicial fallibility cuts both ways. “[E]xtraordinary and surprisingly erroneous statements, which could not be there if rules of judicial ethics were formulated and strictly adhered to, have found place even in solemn pronouncements of this Court on rare occasions” (¶13) — an argument which converts the newspaper’s criticism into a call the Court should welcome.
  • No finding could be recorded without notice and evidence — the case for closing the matter “at this stage without any finding against any individual” (¶20), reinforced by the Jayaprakash Narayan problem (¶11: views attributed to a third person cannot be adjudicated “without giving notice to other parties, and without taking evidence”).

VI. CRITICAL COMMENTARY — the four things a first-class answer says at the end

  1. The “dropped but lectured” problem. The Court recorded no finding, yet 60 paragraphs criticised a specific newspaper’s conduct. Krishna Iyer, J. conceded the difficulty himself (“we must not proceed to substantiate the accusation… To condemn unheard is not fair-play”), and even his colleague’s “critical observations on men and matters” required an “afterward”. Doctrinal point: an unadjudicated “verge on contempt” remark is obiter and cannot be the basis of a conviction — a usable argument for the media in analogous cases.
  2. Structural asymmetry. The proceeding was in re: complainant, prosecutor (later, formally, in other matters), fact-finder and sentencing judge were the same institution. The Act’s answer is procedural (s. 14(2) alternative-judge request; s. 18 two-judge bench; s. 19 appeal; s. 20 limitation) rather than structural — no independent prosecutor of right, no jury, no presumption-of-doubt rule beyond the common-law “beyond reasonable doubt” formulation. Compare ex p. Blackburn: “we will never use this jurisdiction as a means to uphold our own dignity.”
  3. It is the high-water mark of judicial self-restraint, and it is only persuasive. The six canons were never codified in 1978, and s. 13(a) (the “substantially interferes” filter) arrived only in 2006, on the Law Commission/Sanyal-line logic. Between 1978 and 2006, the restraint lived entirely in judicial discretion — which is why Rajendra Sail (2005) and Mid-Day (2007) pull in different directions.
  4. It is also a founding text for two other units of this paper. For Unit 2.1: the Mill/Areopagitica/Voltaire passage (¶1) and the statement that “the Press provides the principal vehicle of expression of their views to citizens… Freedom of the Press is the Ark of the Covenant of Democracy”. For Units 2.3/2.4: “it is no less than freedom of speech itself” at stake in contempt, plus the “fiduciary duty” of the press — and, for the judicial-ethics debate, the recognition that erroneous statements “have found place even in solemn pronouncements of this Court”.

CASE 17 · COMPLETE DOSSIER · Case Book pp. 154–166

Rajendra Sail v. Madhya Pradesh High Court Bar Association (2005) 6 SCC 109 — “Unverified reporting”, the liability chain, and the sentence that was not forgiven

Citation
(2005) 6 SCC 109. Your syllabus styles the respondent “Madhya Pradesh Bar Association”; the case book prints the cause-title as “Rajendra Sail v. Madhya Pradesh High Court Bar Association” — the same body, the applicant below.
Court · Bench
Supreme Court of India · Y.K. Sabharwal & Tarun Chatterjee, JJ. (the case book reproduces the opinion of “Y.K. Sabharwal, J. (for himself and Tarun Chatterjee, J.)”).
Appeal from
the judgment and order of the Madhya Pradesh High Court holding the appellants guilty of criminal contempt and sentencing each to simple imprisonment for six months.
Headings
Scandalising the court · fair criticism of a judgment · attribution of the words to the speaker · duty of verification by the press · apology: when accepted, when refused · self-regulation.
Outcome
Appeals of the newspaper personnel allowed — apologies accepted, sentences set aside; sentence of Rajendra Sail (the speaker) reduced from six months to one week’s simple imprisonment, apology refused, conviction maintained.

I. FACTS (case-book narrative, with all the particulars you are expected to reproduce)

  1. The underlying criminal case. “In the murder trial of Shankar Guha Niyogi, a trade union leader, the accused were found guilty and sentenced to imprisonment for life except one who was awarded death sentence. On appeal, the High Court reversed the trial court judgment and acquitted the accused.”
  2. The rally. A public meeting was organised “to commemorate the death of Shankar Guha Niyogi”. At it the appellant Rajendra Sail — described in his own defence as “a close associate” of the murdered leader and “a key witness in the murder trial” — delivered a speech.
  3. The report — 4 July 1998, Hitavada. “A news report was published in newspaper ‘Hitavada’ on 4th July, 1998 under the caption ‘Sail terms High Court decision in Niyogi murder case as rubbish’. That report was based on the speech delivered by appellant Rajendra Sail in a rally … and interview given by him soon after the speech to appellant Ravi Pandey, the correspondent of the newspaper.”
  4. The four allegations in the report. The report (i) “termed the decision as rubbish”; (ii) commented that “a Judge who was on verge of retirement should not have been entrusted with the responsibility of dealing with such a crucial case”; (iii) alleged that “the Judges who decided the matter have belittled the respect for judiciary by pronouncing biased and rubbish judgment”; and (iv) quoted Sail “as saying that he was a key witness in the murder trial and in spite of engaging a well known advocate as public prosecutor no body could have made much difference when the judges were already prejudiced and that he had substantial evidence to prove that one of the judges who decided the matter was bribed and that the judge possessed properties disproportionate to his income”.
  5. The initiation — a structure to notice. “The aforesaid news item led to initiation of contempt action on an application filed by Madhya Pradesh High Court Bar Association with the consent of Advocate General against the Editor, Printer and Publisher, Chief Sub-Editor and Desk In-charge of the newspaper at Bhilai besides Bureau Chief of ‘Hitavada’ at Bhilai.” Notice issued 13.6.1998; actually received 11.8.1998.
  6. The four defences, person by person.
    · Editor / Printer / Publisher: “the news report was published on account of oversight and they were unaware of the publication”; and — decisive for mitigation — “even before receipt of notice for contempt, on their own, they published unconditional apology in the newspaper on the front page on 6th August, 1998”, with letters of apology to the Chief Justice, “the concerned judges of the High Court” and the Bar Association.
    · Chief Sub-Editor / Desk-in-charge: the report “was received from the trainee correspondent Ravi Pandey and he did not apply his mind seriously to the news report as the page on which the said report was to be printed was shown to him at the last stage of the printing deadline of that day and under these circumstances he permitted the page to be printed and published”.
    · Ravi Pandey (trainee correspondent): he “was present at the venue where Rajendra Sail delivered the speech and had a conversation with him in which he reiterated the substance of the speech”; “being a trainee correspondent he was unaware of the legal implications of printing and publishing against the judiciary and the judges”; he “stood by his stand that the news report was based on the speech delivered and the subsequent conversation”; and tendered unconditional apology.
    · Rajendra Sail: “denied that he gave any interview to the correspondent and alleged that the news report was false, prejudiced and intended to malign his image”; he had “only made a bona fide analysis of the judgment without bringing into disrepute the judiciary in general and the judges in particular”; he “expressed only his personal grief and emotional trauma” arising out of the murder of his close associate; and said “he is ready to tender an apology, if his plea does not satisfy the court”.
  7. The trial-like step taken by the High Court (the evidence story). “The High Court summoned the audio and video recording of the speech delivered by Rajendra Sail as well as the transcript of the speech as contained in those recordings. The Court directed the supply of the copies of the transcript to the contemnors and gave opportunity to file objections.”
  8. The five contemptuous extracts (the case book reproduces the High Court’s own list of what the transcript showed Sail had said): “(a) Judgment of the murderers of Niyogi was rendered within a year and the murderers have been acquitted because they were moneyed and wealthy people. (b) Judgment has been read by him, which is rubbish and is fit to be thrown in dustbin. (c) He would also get an enquiry held as regard to the conduct of one of the judges who delivered the judgment, as that particular judge is to retire within a month. (d) A judge of High Court or Supreme Court who is about to retire should not be assigned any important case since two years before his retirement, as a judge who is to retire is for sale. (e) Judiciary has no guts, no honesty and is not powerful enough to punish wealthy people.”
  9. The finding below. “After juxtaposing the news report with the audio and video recording as well as the transcript of the speech, the High Court found that there was ‘inkling’ in Rajendra Sail’s speech about his thoughts regarding the judgment and the judges. The court came to the conclusion that the attending circumstances … go to show that he had conversation with the correspondent and the contemptuous statements reported in the news report were in fact uttered by him.” The High Court “concluded that the comments made by him did not amount to fair and reasonable criticism of the judgment and that the contents of the news report scandalized the court”, “refused to accept the apology”, held all appellants guilty, and sentenced each to six months’ simple imprisonment.

II. ISSUES

#IssueAnswer
1Were the words attributed to Rajendra Sail in fact spoken by him, when he denied the interview and the only “independent” evidence was the recording/transcript of the speech juxtaposed with the report?Yes — “preponderant circumstances … objectively compel us to conclude that the said statements were in fact made by Rajendra Sail”; and “whether Rajendra Sail gave interview … or not, the speech itself … speaks for itself”.
2Were his words fair criticism of a judgment (a public document) or criminal contempt — “scandalising” — under s. 2(c)(i)?Contempt. The criticism “transgressed the limits of fair and bona fide criticism and [had] a clear tendency to affect the dignity and prestige of the judiciary”.
3Was the news report (by Editor, Printer, Publisher, Chief Sub-Editor, Desk-in-charge, Bureau Chief, reporter) independently punishable, and could “oversight”, “last-minute deadline”, “inexperience” or “we did not know” excuse them?The publication was a “gross contempt” in tendency, but “in every case, it would be no answer to plead that publication, publisher, editor or other concerned did not know or it was done in haste”; on the facts, the prompt and prior apology took them out of punishment.
4Should the apologies be accepted?Yes for the newspaper personnel; no for Sail — “acceptance of apology and sympathy in a case like this would be uncalled for”.
5Did the appellant suffer a procedural injustice in that the recordings were “never put to him” and were “not part of the record”?No — “the court has, in fact, directed supply of copies of transcript … and had given opportunity to file objections to it, which has not been availed by Rajendra Sail”.
6Was the context (emotional disturbance; murder of a close associate; the report itself maligning him) a mitigating answer to mens?Rejected on the facts: “Rajendra Sail is a law graduate and has been in public life for considerable time and has in fact approached the court on several occasions by filing public interest litigations… he should have been cautious and moderate… The contemptuous statements cannot be regarded as an ill-tempered or emotional outburst of an uninformed person.”
7Systemic issue: what should the media institutionally do about reports concerning “institutions which cannot reply”?“There can be an efficient and stringent mechanism to scrutinize the news reports pertaining to such institutions… It is for media itself and other concerned to consider as how to achieve it.”

III. ARGUMENTS

For the appellants

(a) Newspaper personnel (Editor, Printer, Publisher, Chief Sub-Editor) — “candidly not made any attempt to justify the actions of the newspaper in publishing the news report. Learned counsel has only argued for acceptance of the apology”. The points pressed:

  • Apology published on the front page on 6.8.1998, i.e. before the notice (issued 13.6.1998 but received 11.8.1998) — proving “there was no intention to scandalise the judiciary but it was a case of genuine error on their part”.
  • Letters of apology to the Chief Justice, the concerned judges and the Bar Association, likewise before receipt of notice.
  • The desk chain: the copy came from a trainee; the page was shown “at the last stage of the printing deadline” — i.e. no deliberateness, and the explanation was accepted for the Chief Sub-Editor and the Desk-in-charge too.
  • Reliance on the Court’s own conciliatory line: apologies have repeatedly been accepted with a warning (Re Harijai Singh).

(b) Rajendra Sail — six grounds:

  • The statements “should be understood in the context in which the same were made”: he was a close associate of the murdered Niyogi, a key prosecution witness, “emotionally disturbed because of the judgment of the High Court”.
  • He “neither made statements nor gave interview attributed to him”; the High Court’s conclusion that he did not deny calling the decision “rubbish” was “not sustainable”.
  • Charge/proof mismatch: “the charge that was communicated to the appellant was only about the contents of the news report and the contemptuous statements extracted in the judgment of the High Court were not part of the news report”.
  • Evidentiary objection: “the audio and video recordings on which conclusions of the High Court are based were never put to him, the same were not part of the record and no opportunity was granted to rebut” their contents, so they cannot prove the statements.
  • The report was false and “intended to malign his image”; he had lodged a complaint with the Press Council of India about it — evidence that he, too, treated the attribution as untrue.
  • Unconditional apology “tendered during the course of the arguments” and urged for acceptance.

For the M.P. High Court Bar Association (supporting the impugned judgment)

  • Nature of the statements controls, not the apology: “having regard to the nature of scandalous statements that were made, it is not a case where the apology should be accepted”.
  • Reliance on Prem Surana v. Additional Munsif & Judicial Magistrate (2002) 6 SCC 722 and M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (In the Matter of M/s Ashok Chhabra & Co.) (2003) 5 SCC 376 — the standard citations for “apology as a matter of grace, not of right; not to be accepted where it is a tactic or where the contempt is of the gross variety”.
  • The substantive case was built out of the digest of law the Court itself recited (below): imputing venality to a judge (“a judge who is to retire is for sale”), alleging the judiciary “has no guts, no honesty”, demanding a private “enquiry” into the conduct of the presiding judge, and branding a judgment “rubbish… fit to be thrown in dustbin” are not comments on the merits but attacks on the authority of the court — with the “tendency” test (Dr D.C. Saxena) requiring nothing further.
  • On verification: the Court’s own framing (p. 162) treats the press as owing a duty of antecedent care: “Before placing before public, whether on print or electronic media, all concerned have to see whether any such criticism has crossed the limits as aforesaid and if it has, then resist every temptation to make it public.”

V. THE FINDINGS ON MERITS — the reasoning, step by step

  1. Attribution resolved on “preponderant circumstances”, not on the reporter’s ipse dixit. The Court first applies M.R. Parashar: “There are preponderant circumstances, which objectively compel us to conclude that the said statements were in fact made by Rajendra Sail and the news report has reported the same.” Then it makes the decisive evidential move: it did not need the interview at all — “Whether Rajendra Sail gave interview to the correspondent or not, the speech itself, seen in the light of the audio and video recording of the speech and the transcript of the speech speaks for itself and has the effect of lowering the dignity and authority of the court and an affront to the majesty of justice.” Exam value: this is the answer to “what if the speaker denies the interview?” — the media’s protection is verifiability; recordings and transcripts are the strongest form of it.
  2. Procedural fairness satisfied. The “never put to him” objection failed because the High Court had summoned the recordings, ordered transcripts supplied to the contemnors and given an opportunity to object — “which has not been availed by Rajendra Sail”. (Read with s. 17(2) of the Act: notice + copies of the motion and affidavits.)
  3. The line between comment and contempt, in the Court’s own two-test form. Test A (subject-matter): “It is one thing to say that a judgment on facts as disclosed is not in consonance with evidence or the law has not been correctly applied. But when it is said that the Judge had a pre-disposition to acquit … or has a bias or has been bribed or attributing such motives, lack of dispassionate and objective approach and analysis and prejudging of the issues, the comments that a judge about to retire is available for sale, that an enquiry will be conducted as regards the conduct of the judge … and a wild allegation that judiciary has no guts, no honesty … would bring administration of justice into ridicule and disrepute.” Test B (effect): “It has a tendency to create an apprehension in the minds of the people regarding the integrity, ability or fairness of the Judge and to deter actual and prospective litigants from placing complete reliance upon the court’s administration of justice; [it] is also likely to cause embarrassment in the mind of the Judge himself in the discharge of his judicial duties.”
  4. Invective forfeits protection. “The speech that judgment is rubbish and deserves to be thrown in a dustbin cannot be said to be a fair criticism of judgment. These comments have transgressed the limits of fair and bonafide criticism and have a clear tendency to affect the dignity and prestige of the judiciary.”
  5. Apology for the press, refused for the speaker. For the newspaper personnel the Court weighed the pre-notice front-page apology, the letters, the trainee’s position, and the Chief Sub-Editor’s deadline explanation, and accepted the apologies with a caution, setting the sentences aside. For Sail it held that “the acceptance of apology and sympathy in a case like this would be uncalled for” and that the statements could not be excused as “an ill-tempered or emotional outburst of an uninformed person”, given his legal training and public-life record.
  6. Sentence modified, not nullified. “The sentence awarded to Rajendra Sail by the High Court having regard to nature of contempt cannot be said to be unjustified. But having regard to his background and the organization to which he belongs which, it is claimed, brought before various courts including this court many public interest litigation for general public good, we feel that ends of justice would be met if sentence of six month is reduced to sentence of one week simple imprisonment.” → “The sentence of Rajendra Sail is reduced to one week and to that extent impugned judgment and order of the High Court is modified.”
  7. The institutional prescription (the “media-law” contribution). Three sentences to memorise: “The judgments of courts are public documents and can be commented upon, analyzed and criticized, but it has to be in dignified manner without attributing motives. Before placing before public, whether on print or electronic media, all concerned have to see whether any such criticism has crossed the limits as aforesaid and if it has, then resist every temptation to make it public. … Some mechanism may have to be devised to check the publication which has the tendency to undermine the institution of judiciary.” And on the reach of modern media: “The reach of media, in present times of 24 hours channels, is to almost every nook and corner of the world. Further, large number of people believe as correct which appears in media, print or electronic.”

VI. PRINCIPLES EVOLVED

#PrincipleSignificance
P1Vigour is permitted; venality is not. “No criticism of a judgment, however vigorous, can amount to contempt of Court, provided it is kept within the limits of reasonable courtesy and good faith” — but imputations of bias, bribery, venality or lack of courage are outside that immunity even when they accompany genuine criticism.Fixes the modern “content vs. manner” test and, by treating “prejudging of the issues” and “lack of dispassionate approach” as motive-imputations, tells editors exactly which adjectives to delete.
P2Unverified attribution is the press’s own risk. Where the speaker denies the words, the burden of proving the attribution beyond reasonable doubt lies on the prosecutor; a reporter’s assertion is insufficient without “preponderant circumstances”. But the existence of the recording is what converts a “false report” defence into a conviction — and it can prove contempt even if the interview never happened.The rule that operationalises the syllabus head “unverified reporting”: verification is not merely ethics, it is the evidential foundation of the defence.
P3No “we did not know / we were in a hurry” defence for editors and publishers; person-specific culpability + prompt apology can still avert punishment.Distinguishes guilt from sentencing: all were in law guilty-range, but only the speaker was punished. This is why the case is assigned for media law and not merely for contempt law.
P4Self-regulation with an internal pre-publication filter for judicial matters, modelled on (and critical of) the UK PCC experience: “we too have Press Council … It is for media itself and other concerned to consider as how to achieve it.”The most-quoted Supreme Court sentence inviting newsroom self-restraint; pairs with the F.S. Nariman Committee “trusteeship/self-regulation” principles in the case book’s media-regulation unit and with r. 6 of the Cable Rules.
P5Speaker’s competence raises the standard. “Rajendra Sail is a law graduate and has been in public life for considerable time… With this background, he should have been cautious and moderate…”The Arundhati Roy “person responsible + knowledge in the field” factor applied — for lawyers, journalists-in-law and academics, this is a real and asymmetric duty.
P6Apology is a matter of judicial grace calibrated to gravity: accepted for the “genuine error” of a newsroom, refused for a deliberate, sophisticated attack on the institution; and even a refused apology can reduce the sentence.The most examinable point in the case, and the one the Supreme Court itself softened in 2025 (Part A8 §2) by reading the Explanation to s. 12(1) as a scheme of forgiveness.

Pitfall box · three traps in Rajendra Sail

  • Truth. The contemners never pleaded the s. 13(b) defence of justification by truth (the amendment was in force from 17.3.2006 — this judgment is from 2005; and in any event Sail’s stance was that he never said it). So Rajendra Sail is not authority on truth; do not say it “rejected the truth defence”. The two cases that do engage truth are In re Prashant Bhushan (2020) and Subramanian Swamy v. Union of India (2014) — see A7 and A5.
  • Standard. The Court used “preponderant circumstances … objectively compel” — which reads civil; but it had just recited the beyond-reasonable-doubt rule from Mrityunjoy Das/Bramblevale and M.R. Parashar. Best answer: “the Court applied the criminal standard and found it satisfied on the objective evidence of the recording”.
  • Outcome. It is wrong to state that “the Supreme Court acquitted all the appellants” or that “the Court accepted the apologies”. It set aside the sentence for the newspaper personnel and reduced Sail’s to one week — the conviction of Sail stood.

Real example · how the same rule was applied forty years on

Same shape, 2026: a Division Bench of the Delhi High Court (Navin Chawla & Ravinder Dudeja, JJ.) convicted a YouTuber for criminal contempt over videos targeting judicial officers, holding that “[t]he intent … is … writ large of only scandalising and lowering the image of these Judicial Officers in the general public, thereby lowering the authority of the Court. It is not to generate a healthy debate but to scandalize the Court”, while accepting unconditional apologies from two advocates who had featured in the videos and discharging them — an almost exact replay of Case 17’s split outcome, with the added point: “if one has to attack a Judicial Officer on his integrity or competence, it must be done with cogent evidence; it cannot be made lightly… such criticism must … be well founded, specially because the Judicial Officer, unlike the complainant, has no means to justify his actions in public.” [Reported April 2026.] Compare 2025: the Supreme Court set aside a one-week sentence imposed by the Bombay High Court on a society office-bearer for a circular about the judiciary, stressing mercy and the acceptance of an early unqualified apology.

CASE 18 · DOSSIER · Case Book pp. 167–168

Sanjoy Narayan, Editor-in-Chief, Hindustan v. Hon. High Court of Allahabad through Registrar General, 2011 (9) SCALE 532 — the “unverified reporting” rule stated in its plainest form

Citation
2011 (9) SCALE 532 (as printed in your syllabus and at the head of the case-book extract). Criminal Appeal arising out of a contempt order — s. 19(1)(b), Contempt of Courts Act, 1971.
Court · Bench
Supreme Court of India · Mukundakam Sharma, J. (for himself and Anil R. Dave, J.) — a two-judge Bench, which is what s. 18(1) requires of the trial court and what appellate practice generally preserves.
Appellants
Sanjoy Narayan, Editor-in-Chief, Hindustan Times, and the other publication-side appellants (the judgment refers throughout to “the appellants”).
Respondent
The Hon’ble High Court of Allahabad through its Registrar General — an unusual cause-title worth pointing out in class: the High Court itself is the respondent because it had initiated and decided the contempt.
Impugned order
Order dated 04.04.2011 of the Allahabad High Court; the contempt arose “out of … publication of article in question in Hindustan Times on 20.09.2010” (¶4).
Outcome
Appeal disposed of; unqualified apology accepted and the proceedings dropped; the apology directed to be published on the first page of the Lucknow edition on 01.09.2011 and at prominent places in the other editions where the article had appeared.

I. FACTS (all that the case-book extract gives — and an honest note on what it omits)

  1. The subject of the report. “The then Chief Justice of the Allahabad High Court who has otherwise proved himself to be a competent and good Judge wherever he was posted during his career was brought under a cloud by the reporting which is the subject matter of this petition” (¶9). This single sentence is the Court’s own characterisation of the victim of the story and of the story’s evidential base.
  2. The offending article. Published in the Hindustan Times on 20 September 2010 (¶4). The case book does not reproduce its text; what the Court describes is its quality, not its content: the report was “apparently based on surmises and conjectures and not based on facts and figures” (¶9).
  3. Contempt initiated by the High Court (as it is entitled to do under Art. 215 r/w s. 15(1) of the Act), which culminated in the order of 04.04.2011. One feature of that order is recorded expressly and is decisive for the outcome: “we find that apology tendered before the Allahabad High Court was not accepted only because it was felt that the same was not unqualified” (¶12).
  4. Appeal to the Supreme Court under s. 19(1)(b); notice issued; the respondent High Court “ha[d] also entered appearance through counsel” (¶3). Before the Supreme Court the appellants “filed an affidavit … on record tendering unqualified apology” (¶4).
  5. Counsel’s own gesture. “We also appreciate the gesture of the learned counsel for the appellants…” (¶14) — the Court placed on record that counsel had, on instructions, taken the apology position; a small but real practice point (in a contempt appeal, how the apology is tendered — by affidavit, on instructions, before arguments conclude — is itself part of the record).

Honest limitation note · do not invent facts for this case in the exam

The extract in your case book is short (¶¶2–14; ¶1 is not reproduced) and it deliberately gives no particulars of the article’s allegations, no account of the High Court’s reasoning, and no findings on guilt. So in the exam: (i) describe the facts exactly as above; (ii) say expressly that “the report was found by the High Court to be contemptuous and the Supreme Court characterised it as unverified and conjectural”; and (iii) do not attribute a specific allegation (e.g. about an appointment, a transfer or an asset) to the article — that detail is outside the case-book material. Everything of value in Sanjoy Narayan is in its principles, which is why the syllabus lists it under “Unverified reporting”.

II. ISSUES

#IssueAnswer (¶)
1Whether a report which is “factually wrong, biased or simply unverified” can claim the protection of the media’s constitutional role, or of ss. 4–5 of the Act.No. The duty “to provide to the readers and the public in general with information and views tested and found as true and correct” is the premise of the protection; and “Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions must be avoided” (¶¶7–9).
2Whether the right to information and the media’s role as the “fourth pillar” enlarge the immunity.They enlarge the responsibility, not the licence: “With this immense power, comes the burden of responsibility”; and “The unbridled power of the media can become dangerous if check and balance is not inherent in it” (¶¶5–8). Note the Court’s grounding of RTI in the analysis — “The enactment of the Right to Information Act is the most empowering step in this direction” (¶6) — a useful bridge from Unit 2.3.
3Which legal framework constrains the media here.Art. 19(1)(a), restricted by Art. 19(2), which in terms takes in “Contempt of Courts Act and defamation” (¶7).
4Whether an apology which the High Court refused as “not unqualified” can nonetheless close the matter.Yes, once re-tendered in unconditional form by affidavit: “The judiciary also must be magnanimous in accepting an apology… Therefore, we accept the aforesaid unqualified apology… and drop the proceeding” (¶12).
5What remedy beyond dropping suits the public interest in an unverified-report case.Compelled publication of the apology to the same audience: first page of the Lucknow edition on a specified date, “and also at such other place, wherever there was any such publication, in a daily issue of the newspaper at some prominent place” (¶13).

III. ARGUMENTS

For the Editor / appellants

  • Constitutional position of the press. The media is “generally called the fourth pillar of democracy” and “discharges a very onerous duty of keeping the people knowledgeable and informed” — the Court adopts this framing itself in ¶5, which shows the argument proceeded at the level of principle rather than of denial.
  • Function of reportage. The media “creates opinions, broadcasts different points of view, brings to the fore wrongs and lapses of the Government and all other governing bodies and is an important tool in restraining corruption” (¶6, again the Court’s own summary of the case for the press).
  • Apology. An unqualified apology by affidavit — the practical answer to the High Court’s objection that the earlier apology was qualified; coupled with the concession that the reporting was “apparently based on surmises and conjectures” (¶9), i.e. a no-defence-and-forgive-us position rather than a fair-comment plea.
  • Implied concession, strategically used: once the report is accepted as unverified, the only contestable ground was punishment, and the appellants won it.

The side of the High Court / the case for punishment

  • Institutional damage. “The dignity of the courts and the people’s faith in administration must not be tarnished because of biased and unverified reporting” (¶9) — the exact formulation of the harm limb of s. 2(c)(i) (“lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court”).
  • A judge as a class who cannot reply. The story put a sitting Chief Justice “under a cloud”, while the Court itself recorded that he was a competent and good judge — i.e. the record before the Court demonstrated falsity by the very person defamed.
  • Apology must be unqualified. The High Court’s stated reason for refusal — that the apology “was not unqualified” (¶12) — reflects the orthodox rule that an apology coupled with a denial of wrongfulness is no apology at all (contrast the post-2006 Explanation to s. 12(1), which forbids rejecting a bona fide qualified apology merely because it is qualified).
  • Pre-judgment. “Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions” (¶8) — where a report pronounces on the integrity of a judge, it usurps a function no one else can perform for the court: “The only way the Judge can defend a decision is by the reasoning in the decision itself” (J.R. Parashar, quoted in Case 17).

IV. DECISION (with the operative paragraphs)

Verbatim — the media-duty paragraphs; quote them as a block

“5. The media, be it electronic or print media, is generally called the fourth pillar of democracy. The media, in all its forms, whether electronic or print, discharges a very onerous duty of keeping the people knowledgeable and informed.

6. The impact of media is far-reaching as it reaches not only the people physically but also influences them mentally. It creates opinions, broadcasts different points of view, brings to the fore wrongs and lapses of the Government and all other governing bodies and is an important tool in restraining corruption and other ill-effects of society. The media ensures that the individual actively participates in the decision-making process. The right to information is fundamental in encouraging the individual to be a part of the governing process. The enactment of the Right to Information Act is the most empowering step in this direction. The role of people in a democracy and that of active debate is essential for the functioning of a vibrant democracy.

7. With this immense power, comes the burden of responsibility. With the huge amount of information that they process, it is the responsibility of the media to ensure that they are not providing the public with information that is factually wrong, biased or simply unverified information. The right to freedom of speech is enshrined in Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution. However, this right is restricted by Article 19(2) in the interest of the sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, decency and morality and also Contempt of Courts Act and defamation.

8. The unbridled power of the media can become dangerous if check and balance is not inherent in it. The role of the media is to provide to the readers and the public in general with information and views tested and found as true and correct. This power must be carefully regulated and must reconcile with a person’s fundamental right to privacy. Any wrong or biased information that is put forth can potentially damage the otherwise clean and good reputation of the person or institution against whom something adverse is reported. Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions must be avoided.”

Verbatim — the disposal (¶¶12–14)

“12. … The judiciary also must be magnanimous in accepting an apology when filed through an affidavit duly sworn, conveying remorse for such publication and that too when this Court in the number of matters has taken a liberal view and have not been hypersensitive in such matters. Therefore, we accept the aforesaid unqualified apology of the appellants and drop the proceeding.

13. … the apology shall be published by the appellants in the first page of Lucknow edition of Hindustan Times to be published on 01.09.2011 and also at such other place, wherever there was any such publication, in a daily issue of the newspaper at some prominent place.

14. We also appreciate the gesture of the learned counsel for the appellants. In the result, the appeal is disposed of with the aforesaid observations.”

Limitation point worth a sentence (s. 20): the article appeared on 20.09.2010, so any contempt proceeding had to be initiated by 19.09.2011. The case book’s extract records only the order under appeal (04.04.2011) and so does not disclose the date of initiation; the safe statement is that “the proceedings were initiated within the one-year period from publication, the order under appeal being dated 04.04.2011”. Do not assert a date the material does not give.

V. PRINCIPLES EVOLVED

#PrincipleWhy it matters / how to use it

CASE 19 · DOSSIER · Case Book pp. 169–173

Rajeev Dhawan v. Gulshan Kumar Mahajan & Ors., Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 2 of 1994, decided in 2014 — how a contempt petition about attacks on the Court during the Ayodhya hearings actually travels through the system

“This application is moved by Dr. Rajeev Dhawan, a learned advocate drawing attention of the Court to certain statements attributed to Sri Giriraj Kishore published in the newspaper Indian Express of 10th April, 1994 and in the Periodical styled Khabardar India of 11-17th April, 1994, which, it is contended, tend to lower the image of the Court in the mind of the public and constitute an affront to the dignity and authority of this Court. The utterances of Sri Giriraj Kishore, if true, might amount to criminal contempt.”Order of the Constitution Bench, 12.04.1994 — reproduced verbatim at ¶3 (Case Book p. 170). Note the conditional “if true”: the Court framed the question of fact at the very threshold — and never got to answer it.
Citation
(2014) 41 SCD 785; Contempt Petition (Crl.) No. 2 of 1994 (with connected matters) — Supreme Court of India.
Bench
The 1994 orders were passed by a Constitution Bench (the same Bench hearing the Ayodhya Special Reference No. 1 of 1993); the 2014 order is delivered by R.M. Lodha, CJI for the Constitution Bench (Justices A.R. Dave, S.J. Mukhopadhaya, Dipak Misra and S.K. II. THE OFFENDING WORDS (the case book sets them out; memorise the four phrases)

SourceWords, as reproduced in the case book
Indian Express, 10.04.1994 (report attributed to “Express News Service”)Statements by “Dalmia and Giriraj Kishore VHP President and joint general Secretary respectively” which, per the petition, “assailed the apex Court for attempting to ‘arrogate the power of the executive’”.
Dalmia / Kishore at the news conference“While the Court is seized of the matter, the Government has taken the step of acquiring the land, which is attempting to arrogate the power of the executive [as if the Court were doing so]”; “The Ayodhya issue had so far eluded a solution only because of the delay in pronouncing the judgment”; “Justice delayed is justice denied”; “The judiciary has no jurisdiction over the Ram Janam Bhoomi”; Kishore “cautioned the Court not to overstep its limits” and said the Supreme Court “had lost its prestige because of the delay in adjudicating the Ayodhya dispute”.
Khabardar India, 11–17.4.1994that “the Government influences the Court” and that “an anonymous Union Minister ‘has the Court in one pocket and leaders in another’”.

Whom the petition sought to punish (¶2): “the news item in the Indian Express constitutes a gross criminal contempt for which the authors of the statement, namely, Vishnu Hari Dalmia and Giriraj Kishore, the Editor and Publisher of the Indian Express, the persons in-charge of the Express News Service and the reporters, are answerable to this Court.” — the petitioner’s own theory of the liability chain, and it is the same chain (authors → editor → publisher → in-charge of the news agency → reporters) that Case 17 had to adjudicate, and that s. 12(4)–(5) of the Act presupposes for corporate publishers.

Why these words are analytically interesting rather than merely rude: they combine (i) a direction to the Court not to exercise jurisdiction it plainly has (“exceed limits”, “no jurisdiction”); (ii) an imputation of executive control over adjudication (“Government influences the Court”, “the Court in one pocket”); and (iii) a delay-based delegitimisation (“lost its prestige”). Limb (ii) is classic s. 2(c)(i) scandalising (imputing lack of independence); limb (i) is close to limb (iii) of s. 2(c) — interference “with the administration of justice in any other manner”; limb (iii) is the “lowering the authority” species. And every one of them was uttered while the lis was before a Constitution Bench, so limb (ii) is available too — a rare instance where all three limbs of s. 2(c) could be argued on the same facts.

For the petitioner (Dr. Rajeev Dhawan)

  • Gravity and context. “Dr. Rajeev Dhawan vehemently contended that the backdrop to these cases is the destruction of the Babri Masjid on 06.12.1992. He submitted that tension persisted as the Vishwa Hindu Parishaad held a Sansad on 3-4.4.1994 while hearings were taking place before this Court. Contemner No. 3 made contemptuous statements about the Court at that time and, therefore, a matter of this gravity should not be left undecided.” (¶11)
  • Continuing contempt. After service, the contemner “continued to make provocatory utterances holding the process of Court to contempt” (¶6) — i.e. not a single effusion but a campaign, which defeats a plea of isolated emotional excess.
  • What was legally at stake: statements purporting to tell a Constitution Bench the limits of its jurisdiction, and imputing that the executive had the Court “in one pocket”, at a moment when communal tension made public confidence in the adjudicator decisive. On D.C. Saxena’s “tendency” test and C.K. Daphtary’s “confidence of the public” reasoning, the case for punishment was strong.

For the contemners (as visible from the record)

  • Counter-affidavits to show-cause notices (respondents 4–6 filed counters on 26.4.1994; the Court then considered whether to proceed at all against them — the Dalmia proceedings were dropped on that material “at the outset”).
  • Service and charge: contemner No. 3’s counsel consistently maintained that “notices for personal appearance accompanied by charges, as directed by the Court are not yet served” — a s. 17(1)–(2) point, and by 2014 a fatal one.
  • Health and age: 96 years, wheelchair-borne, hearing-impaired, “severe physical and mental illness” — not a defence on the merits, but a decisive factor in the equitable exercise of a discretionary jurisdiction (cf. Mulgaokar ¶23: “The contempt power, though jurisdictionally large, is discretionary in its unsheathed exercise”).
  • Unconditional apology by contemners 1 and 2 (the periodical’s owner-editor and its reporter), recorded in ¶12.
  • Responsible-reportage answer for the press: publication “along with a comment on the impropriety of such utterances and statements, followed-up by an Editorial … condemning such conduct” — the closest thing in the case book to an express “context of publication” defence for media defendants who report, and criticise, contemptuous speech.

V. DECISION

The Court closed the contempt matters. Its reasoning has four distinct moves, each of which is a teachable proposition:

  1. Recognition of the petitioner’s point. “We appreciate the gravity of the subject matter highlighted by Dr. Rajeev Dhawan. We are also not oblivious of the fact that the Court was not satisfied prima facie with the initial response filed by contemner No. 3 … and ordered on 06.05.1994 to initiate the contempt proceedings against respondent Nos. 1 to 3.” (¶12)
  2. Attribution of the failure to the process, not the accused. “But, the fact of the matter is that despite the order passed on 06.05.1994, the notice accompanied by charges on contemner No. 3 has not been served so far.” (¶12)
  3. Delay + disability ⇒ not a fit case. “In this view of the matter, at this distance of time, when the subject matter remained dormant for almost two decades and now contemner No. 3 is 96 years and he is not able to respond to the charges due to old age and illness, we do not think that this is a fit case where we should deal with the matter further.” (¶12)
  4. The derivative principle — no vicarious pursuit of the press when the author is not pursued. “Now, since contempt proceedings are not being pursued further to find out criminality against the author (contemner No. 3) who made the offending statements, we are of the view that contempt matter does not deserve to be pursued as against contemner Nos. 1 and 2 as well. The contemner Nos. 1 and 2 have also tendered unconditional apology.” (¶12) — and as to respondents 4–6: “the Court has not yet taken cognizance of criminal contempt against them”, so nothing remained to close except by the same order.

P1Two-stage architecture: (i) issuance of notice to show cause, and only then (ii) cognizance + notice “accompanied by the charges”. Pitfall · “the Supreme Court held that no contempt was committed” — it did not

Case 19 produced no adjudication on the merits as to contemners 1–3: Dalmia’s proceedings were dropped “at the outset” (a no prima facie case conclusion on notice, not an acquittal on evidence), and the matter against Kishore and the Khabardar India pair was closed in 2014 without any finding. Learn the vocabulary: dropped ≠ acquitted ≠ discharged ≠ convicted but forgiven. The last is Sanjoy Narayan-style; the first two are Mulgaokar and Rajeev Dhawan; and only Rajendra Sail ends in a maintained conviction with a modified sentence. Examiners notice.

Why the syllabus put this case here (and how to say so)

Cases 16, 17 and 18 give the doctrine. Case 19 gives the machinery — initiation, consent/prosecutor, service, charges, bench, staleness — and it gives the single most important media-law proposition that the other three do not: reporting and condemning a contemptuous statement is qualitatively different from making it. It also supplies the historical context (Ayodhya; Art. 356 Proclamation; Ordinance No. 8 of 1993 → Act 33 of 1993; Special Reference No. 1 of 1993; UAPA declaration of the organisation as unlawful) that lets you show you can situate a contempt problem inside a constitutional crisis. Finally, it demonstrates the practical limits of the law: a petition that took twenty years to be closed teaches more about contempt practice than any textbook sentence.

F2 · The Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Bill, 2004 · the Standing Committee’s Twelfth Report (paras 3.8.1 & 7.4) · the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution

1. The Bill — what it proposed

The Bill introduced in 2004 was Parliament’s first attempt, after the 1976 amendment, to amend the 1971 Act in a rights-friendly direction. Its core, as enacted two years later in different form, was the substitution of s. 13 — and the phrase that mattered was in the Bill’s own clause: “the court may permit, in any proceeding for contempt of court, justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that it is in public interest and the request for invoking the said defence is bona fide”. Note the three filters — may, public interest, bona fide — because the whole critique of the reform is a critique of those filters (an unqualified truth defence was sought; a discretionary, doubly-qualified one was delivered).

Three observations that make this material look understood rather than reproduced:

  1. The Commission constitutionalised the media. Rec. (3) writes “freedom of the press and other media” into Art. 19(1)(a) itself — the answer to every argument that press freedom in India is a judicial construct rather than a textual right (compare the American position discussed in your Units 2.1 and 2.4).
  2. It kept “in relation to contempt of court” in Art. 19(2) — i.e. the Commission did not propose abolishing scandalising; it proposed constitutionalising a truth proviso instead.   3. Rec. Rec. (4)/(127) read together are the Commission’s complete answer on this unit: widen the freedom, narrow and fix the forum, constitutionalise the truth defence.

Enforcement: under the Cable Television Networks (Regulation) Act, 1995 (s. 20 — power to prohibit transmission; and blocking machinery under s. 69A IT Act/intermediary rules for online re-transmission). Point: a broadcaster can be proceeded against for a contempt-flavoured programme even where a court would not punish.
  • Cinematograph Act, 1952 + Certification Guidelines — the project’s copy of the Guidelines (Cinematograph (Certification) Rules, reg. guidelines under s. 5B) contains the twin contempt clauses: a film “contains anything amounting to contempt of court” (Guideline 2(xvii) as printed in the project notes) and, in the Rules’ Guidelines, “visuals or words … contempt of court are not presented”, with the same “undermine the dignity of court” explanation. So a documentary about a pending case can be refused a certificate on contempt grounds — an administrative prior restraint worth debating.
  • Advocates Act, 1961 + BCI Rules — an advocate-contemner (Cases 16’s counsel-adjacent facts; 19; and Ajay Kumar Pandey in Case 17’s digest) also faces s. 35 of the Advocates Act (“any other professional or other misconduct”) — punishment being “(a) reprimand, (b) suspension from practice”, the Bar Council “may also, by order, remove the name of such advocate from the State/State-enrolled roll” — i.e. a parallel, non-criminal disciplinary track which the contempt court may defer to. And, on the other side of the coin, the BCI’s rules on pleading and the conduct of advocates in court are what make “scandalising by counsel” professionally, not just criminally, actionable.
  • Supreme Court Rules, 2013 (made under s. 30 of the Act of 1950 and, for contempt procedure, s. 23 of the 1971 Act) — Order XX r. 1 expressly lists “an appeal under clause (b) of sub-section (1) of Section 19 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971” among appeals in the form of a petition, and directs that on such an appeal “the Registrar shall … cause notice of the appeal to be given, where the appeal is by a convicted person, to the Attorney-General for India or to the Advocate General/Government Advocate” — the procedural reason the AG appears in contempt appeals. High Courts make their own contempt rules under s. 23 of the Act; that is where the practice you see in Cases 17–19 (Registry reports on service, Board’s Notes, notices in the prescribed form) comes from.

The one-sentence conclusion the examiners want

India’s contempt law has been amended twice and reviewed four times; the definition has survived every review; the defences have widened once (2006); and the practical limits on the power therefore continue to come — as they did in Mulgaokar and Sanjoy Narayan — from judicial self-restraint and from the newsroom’s own verification discipline, not from Parliament. Everything in Parts A–F is evidence for that sentence; use it as your conclusion and then, if you have time, add your own two recommendations (a statutory “publication” definition including online, and a negligence-based “reasonable care” defence borrowed from the Victorian Law Reform Commission).

Q5 (Dec-2024, 20 marks) — “The judiciary has been looked upon as the last hope for redress against the administrative and governmental failure, while the public has itself reposed and reiterated this faith in judiciary, therefore no one is allowed to erode public confidence”. Analyse provisions of contempt of court, with some leading judgements ?

Model answer (write it in this order; ≈1,600 words / 30 minutes)

1. Frame the proposition. The quotation states the twin premises of the law of contempt: the judiciary is the last resort of redressal, and its efficacy rests on public confidence. The case book’s own articulation, in Rajendra Sail v. Madhya Pradesh High Court Bar Association (2005) 6 SCC 109, is that “the rule of law is the foundation of the democratic society. The judiciary is the guardian of the rule of law. The confidence, which the people repose in the courts of justice, cannot be allowed to be tarnished, diminished or wiped out by contemptuous behaviour of any person… When the foundation itself is shaken by acts which tend to create disaffection and disrespect for the authority of the court by creating distrust in its working, the edifice of the judicial system gets eroded.” The same judgment supplies the necessary qualification: “When the court exercises this power, it does not do so to vindicate the dignity and honour of the individual Judge who is personally attacked or scandalised, but to uphold the majesty of the law and of the administration of justice.” The answer must therefore balance two interests, both fundamental: Article 19(1)(a) and the administration of justice — “the harmonisation of conflicting aims, interests and values” (per Krishna Iyer, J. in In re S. Mulgaokar, (1978) 3 SCC 339).

2. The constitutional and legislative scheme. Contempt power is constitutional in origin — Articles 129 (Supreme Court as a court of record “including the power to punish for contempt of itself”), 215 (same for High Courts) and 142(2) (ancillary powers to investigate and punish for contempt of itself); the legislative competence to regulate it is Entry 77, List I. Parliament used it in the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (Act 70 of 1971), which “define[s] and limit[s]” the power and regulates procedure — the preamble being the key to the whole statute, since it promises limits, not only powers. Two amendments only: Act 45 of 1976 (Delhi Law Officer in s. 15(1)(c)) and Act 6 of 2006 (s. 13 substituted, w.e.f. 17.3.2006); s. 22 preserves other laws, and s. 9 denies that any new contempt is created.

3. The provisions, analysed.
· s. 2(b) civil contempt — “wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court or wilful breach of an undertaking”: a coercive limb protecting the successful litigant; “wilful” is the operative word.
· s. 2(c) criminal contempt — the publication (spoken or written words, signs, visible representations “or otherwise”) of any matter, or doing of “any other act whatsoever”, which (i) “scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court”; (ii) “prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding”; or (iii) “interferes … or obstructs … the administration of justice in any other manner”. Note the three tendency verbs: the prosecution need prove neither intent nor result.
· Defences (ss. 3–8): s. 3(1) innocent publication (no reasonable grounds for believing the proceeding was pending); s. 3(2) publication when nothing was pending is no contempt; s. 3(3) innocent distribution; the Explanation defines pendency for criminal matters from the charge-sheet/challan or summons/warrant, continuing until the case is “heard and finally decided”, with execution excluded; s. 4 fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding or “any stage thereof”, subject to s. 7; s. 5 fair comment on the merits of any case which has been heard and finally decided; s. 6 good-faith complaint about a subordinate court’s presiding officer to a superior court; s. 7 in camera/chambers reporting with four exceptions; s. 8 other defences preserved.
· Procedure and limits: s. 10 (High Court for subordinate courts, with the proviso barring cognizance where the contempt is a Penal-Code offence — now read with the BNS, 2023); s. 12 (max six months’ simple imprisonment and/or ₹2,000; discharge or remission “on apology being made to the satisfaction of the Court”; Explanation: a bona fide apology is not to be rejected merely because it is qualified; s. 12(3) civil prison; s. 12(4)–(5) corporate/officer liability); s. 13(a) — no sentence unless “substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice”, and s. 13(b) — “the court may permit … justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that it is in public interest and the request for invoking the said defence is bona fide”; s. 14 (in-face procedure, including the right to seek trial by another judge); s. 15 (cognizance suo motu or on motion by the Advocate-General or any other person with his written consent); s. 16 (judges are themselves liable); s. 17 (personal notice + copies; attachment only against absconding); s. 18 (two-judge Bench); s. 19 (appeal as of right — 30 days to a Division Bench, 60 days to the Supreme Court; suspension/bail available; appeal may be heard “notwithstanding that the appellant has not purged his contempt”); s. 20 (one-year limitation on initiating proceedings).

4. Leading decisions — what each contributes.
· P.N. Duda v. P. Shiv Shankar (1988) 3 SCC 167 — “administration of justice and Judges are open to public criticism and public scrutiny”; judges’ “accountability must be judged by the conscience and oath to their office”; “Judgments can be criticized, motives to the Judges need not be attributed”; “if reasonable argument or criticism in respectful language and tempered with moderation is offered against any judicial act as contrary to law or public good, no court would treat criticism as a contempt.”
· In re S. Mulgaokar (1978) 3 SCC 339 — the Supreme Court dropped proceedings arising from an Indian Express item (13.12.1977) and article (21.12.1977) about a proposed code of judicial ethics, “without any finding against any individual”, while recording that “the judiciary cannot be immune from criticism. But, when that criticism is based on obvious distortion or gross mis-statement and made in a manner which seems designed to lower respect for the judiciary and destroy public confidence in it, it cannot be ignored”; that the touchstone is “the reasonable and probable effects of what is said or written than … the motives”; and, from Krishna Iyer, J., the six canons — “wise economy of use… the dogs may bark, the caravan will pass”, “the benefit of the doubt being given generously against the judge”, “silence is a sign of strength”, and the closing maxim “Freedom is what Freedom does and Justice fails when Judges quail”.
· Brahma Prakash Sharma v. State of U.P. (1953) SCR 1169 and Perspective Publications v. State of Maharashtra (AIR 1971 SC 221) — actual interference need not be proved, “it is enough if it is likely, or tends in any way”; “a distinction must be made between a mere libel of a judge and what amounts to a contempt of the court”; the test being “whether the wrong is done to the Judge personally or … to the public”; and the summary jurisdiction “must be exercised with great care and caution and only when its exercise is necessary”.
· Ambard v. A.G. for Trinidad and Tobago (1936) A.C. 322 — “the path of criticism is a public way: the wrong-headed are permitted to err therein: provided that members of the public abstain from imputing improper motives… and are genuinely exercising a right of criticism, and not acting in malice or attempting to impair the administration of justice, they are immune… Justice is not a cloistered virtue.”
· Rajendra Sail (2005) 6 SCC 109 — the modern application. A rally speech and a Hitavada report (4.7.1998) captioned “Sail terms High Court decision in Niyogi murder case as rubbish”, containing imputations that a judge “about to retire is for sale” and that “the judiciary has no guts, no honesty”, was held not fair criticism but “a clear tendency to affect the dignity and prestige of the judiciary”, likely “to create an apprehension in the minds of the people regarding the integrity, ability or fairness of the Judge”, to “deter actual and prospective litigants” and to “cause embarrassment in the mind of the Judge himself”. Conviction of the speaker was maintained (sentence reduced to one week); the apologies of the Editor, Printer/Publisher, Chief Sub-Editor, Desk-in-charge and the trainee reporter were accepted and their sentences set aside, the Court holding that “it would be no answer to plead that publication, publisher, editor or other concerned did not know or it was done in haste”, that “[j]udgments of courts are public documents and can be commented upon, analyzed and criticized, but … in dignified manner without attributing motives”, and directing the media to devise “an efficient and stringent mechanism to scrutinize the news reports pertaining to such institutions”.
· Sanjoy Narayan v. High Court of Allahabad, 2011 (9) SCALE 532 — the “unverified reporting” rule: “With this immense power, comes the burden of responsibility… it is the responsibility of the media to ensure that they are not providing the public with information that is factually wrong, biased or simply unverified”; “Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions must be avoided”; the article “apparently based on surmises and conjectures and not based on facts and figures”; and the disposal by acceptance of an unqualified apology with a direction to publish it on the first page of the Lucknow edition on a fixed date — “the judiciary also must be magnanimous in accepting an apology”.
· Rajeev Dhawan v. Gulshan Kumar Mahajan, Cont. Pet. (Crl.) 2/1994 (decided 2014) — the machinery, in action: the Apex Court took on board a citizen’s contempt petition against statements of VHP office-bearers during the Ayodhya Special Reference (“attempting to arrogate the power of the executive”, “the Court in one pocket”), dropped proceedings against one speaker “at the outset”, took suo motu cognizance against the others on 6.5.1994 with the Solicitor General as Prosecutor, reserved “the question whether, in the interest of maintaining an appropriate balance between the fundamental right under Article 19(1)(a)… and the need to protect the authority and dignity of courts”, whether to proceed against the Indian Express Editor/Printer/Publisher/Reporter because the offending interview was carried “along with a comment on the impropriety of such utterances” and an editorial “condemning such conduct”; and closed all of it in 2014 for two decades of dormancy, unservice of the charge-notice (s. 17) and the contemner’s age and illness.
· Standard and safeguards: Mrityunjoy Das v. Sayed Hasibur Rahaman (2001) 3 SCC 739 — contempt proceedings are “quasi-criminal” and the breach “shall have to be established beyond reasonable doubt” (with Lord Denning in Bramblevale); S. Abdul Karim v. M.K. Prakash (1976) 1 SCC 975 — the “intrinsic tendency” test, and courts are “loath to punish… if the act … was not willful”; In re Arundhati Roy (2002) 3 SCC 343 — “the court is both the accuser as well as the judge of the accusation” and must act with “greatest circumspection”.

5. Analysis of the quotation (the part that earns the marks). (i) Whose confidence? Public confidence is protected because litigants must be able to rely on an impartial tribunal — not because judges have feelings; hence the “libel vs. contempt” line and the exclusion of purely personal attacks (which go to defamation: IPC 499/500 → BNS s. 356(1)/(2)). (ii) Confidence cannot be legislated. “the best way to sustain the dignity and status of their office is to deserve respect from the public at large by the quality of their judgments” (Mulgaokar, quoting Gajendragadkar, C.J.); the corollary is that accountability mechanisms — judicial ethics codes (which Mulgaokar held cannot be forbidden), in-house complaint machinery, complaints under the Judges Inquiry Act, 1968, Art. 124 removal — must be strengthened, not curtailed. (iii) The 19(2) test: the restriction “in relation to contempt of court” must be reasonable; s. 13(a)’s “substantially” gate and s. 13(b)’s truth defence supply that reasonableness after 2006, and Mulgaokar’s canons did so before it. (iv) Media asymmetry: judges “cannot reply to their criticisms” (Lord Denning, quoted in Mulgaokar) and “[t]he only way the Judge can defend a decision is by the reasoning in the decision itself” (J.R. Parashar) — which is why the courts ask the press for accuracy and good faith rather than for deference. (v) Real limits: the offence remains the court’s own, tried by the affected court, with the one-year clock, two-judge bench and appeal as the structural counters; and the practical counter is self-restraint, as Rajeev Dhawan shows — a petition that took 20 years ended “the contempt matters deserve to be closed”.

6. Conclusion. If public confidence is the asset, the law’s function is not to criminalise disapproval but to prevent obstruction and deliberate denigration: “the enforcement… of committal for contempt… may be absolutely necessary” in a fragile society, yet “the law is not so weak as to succumb to every gust of unpopularity”. Today’s answer must add the media-law dimension: “the reach of media, in present times of 24 hours channels, is to almost every nook and corner of the world. Further, large number of people believe as correct which appears in media” (Rajendra Sail) — so the durable protection of confidence lies in verified reporting, a functioning self-regulatory standards body, an operative truth defence, and courts practising what Krishna Iyer, J. called “benign neglect not judicial genuflexion”. The Supreme Court restated exactly this while closing the 2026 NCERT textbook contempt matter: “The Judiciary, as an institution, is not, and cannot be, averse to criticism. Fair, informed and constructive critique of judicial functioning is a legitimate and necessary feature of a vibrant constitutional democracy… What is required … is that such criticism be voiced through the appropriate forum and in a fair, rational mechanism, and not find its way, without verification, into a school curriculum meant for impressionable minds.”

The contempt analysis of “the only version of the deceased wife’s family was published”

  • Which limb? The article ran while anticipatory bail was pending — so s. 2(c)(ii) is engaged (“prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with the due course of any judicial proceeding”): an application for bail is a “judicial proceeding”. Confirm pendency under the Explanation to s. 3; if the case was still at the FIR/stage-sheet-less stage, s. 3(2) is the publisher’s first line of defence — and the reason the 200th Report proposed the “active from arrest” amendment.
  • Why “fair comment” fails. s. 5 immunises “fair comment on the merits of any case which has been heard and finally decided”. Here nothing was decided: timing kills the defence before tone is even examined. Sanjoy Narayan supplies the complementary rule that accuracy is required even for reporting that is timely.
  • One-sided reporting as an independent vice. Publishing only the prosecution/complainant side, without the accused’s version, is “biased” in the exact sense used in Sanjoy Narayan ¶7 (“factually wrong, biased or simply unverified”); it forfeits s. 4’s “fair and accurate” report defence (both adjectives are conjunctive) and matches Re Harijai Singh — “not a public service, but a disservice to the public by misguiding them with false news”.
  • Victim identity. The wife is deceased, so IPC s. 228A(1)–(2) → BNS s. 72 (and the publishing limb, IPC 228A(3) → BNS s. 73) must be checked before printing the name; in the actual M.P. Lohia matter the magazine printed the deceased’s name and was asked why it should not be proceeded against under s. 228A.
  • What Y. (the accused) can actually seek. (a) A perusal-then-punish route: move the court trying/listening to the matter to initiate contempt under s. 15 (with AG consent for a private motion), or complain so that the court takes cognizance; (b) postponement of publication is not statutorily available (the 200th Report’s s. 14A was never enacted) — so ask instead for conditions under inherent powers (as the Allahabad High Court did in M.P. Lohia: the print media was told not to publish the material before a date); (c) defamation (now BNS s. 356) and civil damages, remembering Rajagopal-line limits for public officials; (d) interim directions to channels (the 24-hour-news-cycle cases); (e) in the appeal, plead the prejudicial-publicity point as part of the fairness of the hearing — a bail refusal influenced by “public agitation” is reviewable, since “[a] trial by press, electronic media or by way of a public agitation is the very anti-thesis of rule of law” (State of Maharashtra v. Rajendra Gandhi, as recorded in the 200th Report) and “A Judge is to guard himself against such pressure”.
  • Defences Y. must anticipate: s. 3(1) innocent publication; s. 8 “other defences”; the truth/justification defence (s. 13(b), if the article’s factual core is true and public-interest requirements are met); apology (s. 12(1) proviso) — note from Case 18 that the practical remedy courts prefer is a directed publication of the apology/correction, not imprisonment.

Model note — Q8(B): Perils of Fake News and the legal mechanism for its control (10 marks, ≈700 words)

  1. Define the harm in the case book’s language. Fake news about courts is not a species of “contempt” per se; it is the unverified-reporting wrong at its most acute: Sanjoy Narayan ¶7 (media must not provide “information that is factually wrong, biased or simply unverified”), ¶8 (“Pre-judging the issues and rushing to conclusions must be avoided”), ¶9 (“verify the facts and do some research … before a publication is brought out”).
  2. Why courts care more about fake news than other falsehoods. Two structural reasons from the cases: (i) “large number of people believe as correct which appears in media, print or electronic” (Rajendra Sail) — the credibility premium; and (ii) judges “cannot reply” (Lord Denning, quoted in Mulgaokar) — no self-help remedy, hence the state’s protective duty.
  3. The statutory toolkit (with both old and new numbering). Criminal: IPC 505(1)–(2) → BNS s. 196(1) and the statement conducing to public mischief which the BNS extended to “false information” (BNS s. 353); defamation IPC 499/500 → BNS s. 356(1)/(2); imputing venality to a judge can also be IPC 500 r/w 499 against the judge personally and contempt against the institution (the two-track point from Brahma Prakash Sharma). Court-facing: IPC 228 → BNS s. 267 (insult/interruption of a public servant sitting in a judicial proceeding); false evidence/fabrication IPC 191–193 → BNS 227–229; false charge IPC 211 → BNS s. 248. Digital: IT Act ss. 66D/69A + Intermediary Rules (takedown of content about pending proceedings, ordered case-by-case). Contempt: s. 2(c)(i)–(iii) with the s. 13(a) “substantially” filter and s. 13(b) truth defence — which is also the answer to fake news that is true.
  4. Self-regulation. Press Council ss. 13–14 (code of conduct; censure; s. 14(3) — it stands down once a court proceeding begins), Cable r. 6(15), and the Supreme Court’s own prescription in Case 17 that media evolve “an efficient and stringent mechanism to scrutinize the news reports pertaining to such institutions”.
  5. Critique + reform. Punishment is a weak remedy for content that has already done its work: prefer (i) a statutory publication definition covering the world-wide web (200th Report Draft Bill cl. 3, not enacted); (ii) an active-from-arrest pendency rule (cl. 4); (iii) a time-bound postponement power with a media right of variation within 7 days (cl. 6/14A) — the only genuine pre-emptive tool; (iv) a “reasonable care” defence for platforms and newsrooms (Victorian Law Reform Commission), which converts the “we did not know / we were in a hurry” excuse from a failure into a compliance incentive; (v) correction-first remedies — the Case 18 model of an order to publish an apology/correction on a specified page and date.
  6. Close with a principle. “Let her and Falsehood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter” (Milton, Areopagitica, quoted in Mulgaokar ¶1) — and, from the same judgment, the modern institutional answer: “the best remedy … is to deserve respect”, not to enforce it.

P1 · At a press conference held after a High Court acquittal in a sensational murder case, the leader of a victims’ association says: “Any judge who is two months from retirement is for sale; this Bench was bought. I will get an inquiry into them.” The statement is reported on page 3 of a daily, under a headline, with no editorial comment. Advise the newspaper and the speaker under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, with reference to the four prescribed cases. (20 marks)

Skeleton

  1. Identify the limb: s. 2(c)(i) (scandalising/lowering authority) — not s. 2(c)(ii), since the case is over (no “due course” left to prejudice); note that the pendency analysis of s. 3 therefore does not protect the publisher (Case 17 is exactly this situation).
  2. For the speaker: apply Rajendra Sail — imputation of venality (“for sale”) and of pre-disposition is outside s. 5 even when attached to genuine criticism of a judgment; the “enquiry against the judges” element engages Ajay Kumar Pandey; the Ambard proviso-conditions fail on conditions (1) and (4).
  3. For the newspaper: the liability chain (Editor/Printer/Publisher/Chief Sub-Editor/Desk-in-charge/Bureau Chief/reporter) per Case 17; s. 4 fails unless “fair and accurate” and it is a report of a judicial proceeding (a press conference is not a judicial proceeding — a strong point most answers miss); s. 5 fails on timing and content; the “no answer to plead haste/oversight” rule; the trainee/oversight mitigations available for sentence but not for liability.
  4. The comparative advantage of the second publication in Case 19: had the paper published “along with a comment on the impropriety of such utterances … followed-up by an Editorial … condemning such conduct”, the Supreme Court thought fit to examine separately whether to proceed at all against the Editor/Printer/Publisher/Reporter — advise adding such framing; and, per Sanjoy Narayan, publish the speaker’s denial and seek the recording/transcript.
  5. Defences and remedies for both: s. 13(a) substantial-interference argument (headline only, single paragraph, no circulation-wide campaign); s. 13(b) truth — note the 2014 five-judge statement that “the amended Section enables the court to permit justification by truth” and the practical difficulty of pleading it; apology — immediate, unqualified, front-page (Case 17’s timeline: apology on 6.8.1998 before receipt of the notice on 11.8.1998) plus publication-of-apology in the Sanjoy Narayan form.
  6. Procedural notes for credit: who may move (s. 15 — AG consent; or suo motu); two-judge bench (s. 18); proof beyond reasonable doubt; one-year limitation on initiation (s. 20); appeal under s. 19(1)(b).
  7. Conclusion: separate the two fates — conviction probable for the speaker with a reduced sentence if remorse is shown; apology likely accepted for the newsroom — exactly the split outcome of Case 17.
P2 · A TV anchor obtains a “leaked” transcript of a chambers hearing of a pending matter and runs a 20-minute prime-time segment declaring the petition “doomed”, that the Bench “has already decided it”, and that one Judge “is close to the petitioners’ industrialist”. Can the channel be punished? May the High Court stop the broadcast in advance? (20 marks)

Skeleton

  1. Three limbs simultaneously: s. 2(c)(ii) (prejudicing a pending proceeding — establish pendency via the Explanation to s. 3; a chambers hearing is a “judicial proceeding”), s. 2(c)(i) (imputations of bias/closeness), and s. 2(c)(iii) (obstruction “in any other manner”).
  2. Section 7 problem: publication of information relating to proceedings in chambers or in camera is protected only if it is a “fair and accurate report”, and never where the court has prohibited it (s. 7(1)(b)) or where it sits in chambers for public order/State security (s. 7(1)(c)); the text or summary of the order is separately protected under s. 7(2) — so a “leak” of the hearing, as distinct from the order, is exposed.
  3. “Already decided” = pre-judgment: Sanjoy Narayan ¶8; and the 200th Report’s Ch. IX categories (comment on merits of a pending case; premature publication of evidence; witness interviews).
  4. Subconscious-influence argument: AG v. BBC (Lord Dilhorne), In re P.C. Sen, adopted in Reliance Petrochemicals — as recorded in the 200th Report Ch. III — to show why “prejudice” is a real, not hypothetical, harm in India; counter with the American view (Nebraska Press, plurality) that trained judges are not influenced.
  5. Advance suppression: there is no statutory postponement power (the 200th Report’s Draft s. 14A — ex parte, 7 days, “real risk of serious prejudice”, media right to apply for variation, two-judge Bench — is not law); options are: (i) s. 7 prohibition order; (ii) in camera under BNSS s. 366 (ex-CrPC s. 327); (iii) inherent powers (BNSS s. 528, ex-CrPC s. 482) to direct non-publication of particular material, as in M.P. Lohia; (iv) civil injunction if a right is asserted.
  6. Defences: s. 4 (fails — not fair and not a judicial proceeding report if selectively edited), s. 3(1) (fails — the channel plainly knew the matter was pending), s. 13(a) (argue no substantial interference: no jury, professional judge, hearing in chambers), s. 13(b) (offer to justify the allegations of contact — with the “bona fide + public interest” hurdle); apology with immediate retraction and on-screen correction.
  7. Sanction arithmetic (always good to show): max 6 months simple imprisonment/₹2,000 (s. 12(1)); corporate officer exposure via s. 12(4)–(5); also the Cable Programme Code r. 6(15) route and, for the anchor’s personal remarks, BNSS s. 379 (ex-CrPC s. 340) if the transcript itself was fabricated.
P3 · A national daily runs a cartoon of a Judge with a brief-case full of currency, captioned “The Final Word”, together with a reader poll on whether an accused in a pending murder trial is “guilty or not”. Are both publications in contempt? Is truth a defence? Should apology be accepted? (20 marks)

Skeleton

  1. Cartoon: it is “publication by visible representations” within s. 2(c); Dr D.C. Saxena (quoted in Case 17) — “any caricature of a Judge calculated to lower the dignity of the court would destroy, undermine or tend to undermine public confidence”, and “imputing partiality, corruption, bias, improper motives … is scandalisation”. So the cartoon is squarely limb (i) — and, as in the Mid-Day cartoon episode, the “it was only a cartoon” argument is no answer.
  2. The poll: different analysis — it is pre-judgment (limb (ii)) and it “tends” to create an atmosphere of prejudice (200th Report Ch. IX(7)); s. 3’s pendency rules apply, and the newspaper must be advised that pendency here exists (trial in progress).
  3. Two publications, two defences: satirical commentary on a decided judicial act may still be protected (compare aswini kumar ghose/R.C. Cooper: “the Court like any other institution does not enjoy immunity from fair criticism”), but not when combined with an imputation of bribery; the poll cannot claim s. 5 because the case is not finally decided — the timing point.
  4. Truth: yes, but by permission only — s. 13(b) analysis, with Subramanian Swamy (2014, five judges) and the 2020 sentencing remarks (“truth should ordinarily be allowed … unless … a camouflage”). Note that pleading truth cannot cure the poll, since the poll asserts no fact.
  5. Apology: run the Case-by-case ladder — Mulgaokar (drop, no findings) → Sanjoy Narayan (accept + publish on a named page/date) → Rajendra Sail (refused where the attack is “deliberate, motivated and calculated”, sentence nonetheless reduced) → and the December 2025 restatement (“power … to forgive”; “not a personal armour for judges, nor a sword to silence criticism”). Argue that a prompt, unqualified, on-page apology with removal of the poll and an explanation from the editor should be accepted for the newspaper personnel, while the cartoonist’s position depends on whether an enquiry was offered into the factual basis.
  6. Conclude with the doctrinal line that decides both: “Judgments can be criticized, motives to the Judges need not be attributed” (P.N. Duda, via Rajendra Sail) — the cartoon attributes motive; the poll pre-empts judgment.

Comparative statutory texts (for a comparative paragraph — accurate as summarised; cite the statutes by name)

The pattern to state, after the table

Reading the line from 1978 to 2026, three things emerge. (1) The substance has become more protective of speech only by statute (13(a) substantial interference, 2006) and only in words (13(b) truth) — the practice of accepting truth has lagged. (2) The forum has broadened: the sanction now reaches the speaker, the editor, the platform, the broadcaster, the certifier, the textbook, and the Bar (Advocates Act s. 35). (3) The remedy has narrowed in exactly the direction Case 18 shows: not imprisonment but a compelled public correction/apology, plus a direction to build internal machinery, which Case 17 had already prescribed. That triple movement is your conclusion — and it is a conclusion that can be written entirely out of the case book.

K4 · Presentation protocol for a 20-mark answer in this paper

Do

  • Open with the provision (1–2 sentences), never with a general essay.
  • Give every case in the same five-part mini-format: facts (2 lines) → issue → what was held → the sentence you quote → the principle.
  • Draw one diagram (flow-rail or the contempt clock) and label it; the examiner’s eye reaches it first.
  • Use the case book’s own words in quotation marks and add “(Mulgaokar ¶46)”-style pinpoint references.
  • Give both IPC and BNS numbers for every crime, and the CrPC↔BNSS pair where procedure matters.
  • End with 3 concrete suggestions (statutory definition of publication; reasonable-care defence; correction-first remedies) — the question “analyse” invites them.

Don’t

  • Say “contempt is to protect judges’ feelings”, or “to punish disrespect”.
  • Treat s. 5 as covering criticism of a pending case or of a judge’s conduct.
  • Say truth “is” a defence — it is a defence the court “may permit” on two conditions.
  • Claim that Cases 16, 18 and 19 ended in acquittals or convictions — none of them did.
  • Attribute the 2006 amendment to the 200th Report, or the 200th Report’s Draft Bill to enacted law.
  • Forget the punishment ceiling and the one-year limitation: they decide problem questions as often as the doctrine does.
