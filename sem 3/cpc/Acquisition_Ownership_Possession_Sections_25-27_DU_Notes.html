Possession as a Source of Title — The Concept and the Framework

Sections 25–27 of the Limitation Act, 1963: where time itself transfers ownership
Core Idea
Ordinarily, limitation bars the remedy and leaves the right untouched. But for suits for possession of property, the Act goes further: when the owner sleeps beyond the prescribed period, s. 27 extinguishes his very title — and the person in possession, by satisfying the classic tests of adverse possession, acquires that title. Sections 25 and 26 do the same work for easements: twenty years' open, peaceful enjoyment as of right makes the easement absolute and indefeasible. Together they make possession, over time, a source of ownership — the doctrine the 2019 Constitution-strength decision in Ravinder Kaur Grewal emphatically reaffirmed.

1.1  The General Maxim and its Famous Exception

  • The maxim: limitatio tempus adimat actionem, non jus — limitation bars the remedy, not the right. A time-barred debt can still be paid voluntarily; payment of a barred debt is not unjust enrichment recoverable back (First National Bank v. Seth Santlal, AIR 1954 Punj 328: "after a debt becomes barred a person is still deemed to owe and it does not cease to be due").
  • The exception: s. 27 — in a suit for possession of property, at the determination of the period limited for instituting the suit, "his right to such property shall be extinguished." Not merely the remedy — the right and title itself die, and (as the case law holds) they die in favour of the possessor.
  • The policy (Halsbury, quoted in Rajender Singh): (1) long dormant claims have more of cruelty than justice in them; (2) a defendant may have lost the evidence to disprove a stale claim; (3) persons with good causes of action should pursue them with reasonable diligence. Statutes of limitation are statutes of "repose," "peace" and "justice" — interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt.
  • The utilitarian side (Ravinder Kaur Grewal, para 2): the possessor who maintains, tills and improves the land "must hold the best title to the land"; the concept cures clouds on title, develops "wild" lands with weak or indeterminate title, and honours the attachment built by caring for property — from the Code of Hammurabi (Law 30: three years' use defeats the returning owner, save the soldier captured in battle) through the Statute of Westminster 1275 and the 1639 twenty-year limitation, to our own Art. 65.
Why an exam loves this sections. 27 is the point where the law of limitation stops being procedural and becomes substantive: it does not merely close the courtroom door, it re-distributes property. That is why every DU paper from 2017 to 2020 has carried a question on this cluster — and why the 2019 decision in Ravinder Kaur Grewal (Case 36) matters so much.

Sections 25 & 26 — Acquisition of Easements by Prescription

Direct acquisition of incorporeal rights by long enjoyment: light, air, way, water

2.1  Section 25 — The Statute

Section 25, Limitation Act, 1963 — Acquisition of easements by prescription

25(1). Where the access and use of light or air to and for any building have been peaceably enjoyed therewith as an easement, and as of right, without interruption, and for twenty years, and where any way or watercourse or the use of any water or any other easement (whether affirmative or negative) has been peaceably and openly enjoyed by any person claiming title thereto as an easement and as of right without interruption and for twenty years, the right to such access and use of light or air, way, watercourse, use of water, or other easement shall be absolute and indefeasible.

25(2). Each of the said periods of twenty years shall be taken to be a period ending within two years next before the institution of the suit wherein the claim to which such period relates is contested.

25(3). Where the property over which a right is claimed under sub-section (1) belongs to the Government, that sub-section shall be read as if for the words "twenty years" the words "thirty years" were substituted.

Explanation. Nothing is an interruption within the meaning of this section, unless where there is an actual discontinuance of the possession or enjoyment by reason of an obstruction by the act of some person other than the claimant, and unless such obstruction is submitted to or acquiesced in for one year after the claimant has notice thereof and of the person making or authorising the same to be made.

2.2  Ingredients of s. 25(1)

  • Peaceably (nec vi) — no force; the enjoyment must not be gained or maintained by violence.
  • Openly (nec clam) — not clandestinely; visible to the servient owner who, with ordinary observation, would know of it.
  • As of right (nec precario) — without permission, acknowledgement of a superior title, or resort to force or stealth; "as of right" means the user asserts a right, not a favour.
  • Without interruption — see the Explanation: a mere obstruction is not an "interruption" unless enjoyment actually stops, the obstruction is by a third person, and the claimant acquiesces for one year after notice of it. Occasional interruptions that are resisted do not break the period.
  • For twenty years (thirty for Government property) — and by s. 25(2), the twenty years must be the period ending within two years before the suit: prescription must have been complete (or nearly so) when the claimant came to court, so that freshly-minted enjoyment cannot be sued upon.
  • Claiming title thereto as an easement — the user must be referable to the claimant's own land (a dominant heritage), not a mere personal tolerance; s. 25 concerns only the acquisition of easements, not the extent of the right acquired or the remedy for disturbing it (for those, one looks to the general law of easements).
What "absolute and indefeasible" meansOnce the period runs out, the easement is no longer a revocable enjoyment — it is a vested incorporeal right, effective against the servient owner and his successors, transferable with the dominant heritage, and protected by injunction. The servient owner cannot extinguish it by unilateral obstruction; his remedy (blocking and resisting for over a year) had to be exercised before the period completed.

2.3  Section 26 — Exclusion in Favour of the Reversioner of the Servient Tenement

Section 26, Limitation Act, 1963 — Exclusion in favour of reversioner of servient tenement

Where any land or water upon, over or from which any easement has been enjoyed or derived has been held under or by virtue of any interest for life or in terms of years exceeding three years from the granting thereof, the time of the enjoyment of such easement during the continuance of such interest or term shall be excluded in the computation of the period twenty years in case the claim is, within three years next after the determination of such interest or term, resisted by the person entitled on such determination to the said land or water.

  • The mischief: a tenant for life (or a lessee for more than three years) of the servient tenement tolerates a neighbour's pathway for twenty years; prescription completes during his term. Without s. 26, the reversioner — who owned the fee all along — would find an easement crusted onto his land by another's acquiescence.
  • The cure: the enjoyment during the limited interest is simply excluded from the computation; the twenty years can only be built from enjoyment adverse to the reversioner himself — i.e., from the determination of the interest or term.
  • The condition: the reversioner must resist the claim within three years after the interest determines — a window of vigilance; a reversioner who sleeps past it cannot complain.
  • Essentials (as the DU compendium frames them): (i) an exception to s. 25; (ii) benefit claimed in a suit where the enjoyment was under an interest for life or a term; (iii) exclusion of the enjoyment-period from computation, with the suit to be brought within 3 years after determination. It does not apply to a donee or transferee from a Hindu widow — the transferee steps into her shoes as full owner for the remainder of her life interest, and prescription during her lifetime cannot run against him.
  • Contrast with s. 12: mechanically s. 26 is another "exclusion of time" provision — but the excluded time is the servient owner's incapacity period, not the claimant's procedural delay.

Section 27 & the Law of Adverse Possession

Extinguishment of right to property — and the doctrine that gives the extinguished title to the possessor

3.1  Section 27 — The Statute

Section 27, Limitation Act, 1963 — Extinguishment of right to property

27. At the determination of the period hereby limited to any person for instituting a suit for possession of any property, his right to such property shall be extinguished.

  • Applies only to suits for possession — not applications for possession; and not to suits on other footings: a mortgagee's suit for sale of the mortgaged property may become barred, but his right (the debt and the security) is not extinguished; where no period of limitation is prescribed at all, s. 27 has no application.
  • Substantive, not procedural — it operates on rights, not remedies; "possession" here embraces actual possession and possession in law (constructive possession). An owner in symbolic possession is not "dispossessed" merely because a stranger occupies a corner.
  • Applies only to a person out of possession who could have sued — an owner still in possession cannot have his title extinguished; and the right extinguished is the right of that particular person: if a Hindu widow's right is extinguished, the reversioner's right survives; if the mortgagor-in-possession's right to sue possession is barred, the simple mortgagee's right to bring the property to sale is untouched.
  • Government: thirty years (Art. 65/112 with s. 27) — the State's right to possession of land is extinguishable, but on a longer fuse; for private owners, twelve years.
  • The 1908 Act's ancestor: s. 28 of the Limitation Act, 1908, in identical terms — the provision at work in Gunga Govind Mundul (11 M.I.A. 212), Sarangadeva Periya Matam, Rajender Singh, and every other classic in this topic.

3.2  Adverse Possession — The Classic Requirements

The DU compendium, the Privy Council and the Supreme Court converge on one formula — the possessor must show possession that is adequate in continuity, in publicity and in extent, and hostile in intention to the true owner, for the full statutory period.

The Three Latin Tests
nec vi — not by force: adequate in continuity;
nec clam — not clandestine: adequate in publicity;
nec precario — not by the owner's leave: adverse to the competitor, in denial of his title and to his knowledge (S.M. Karim v. Bibi Sakina, AIR 1964 SC 1254; Karnataka Wakf Board; Balkrishan v. Satyaprakash, (2001) 2 SCC 498). The animus possidendi must exist from the commencement of possession (Annakili v. Vedanayagam, (2007) 14 SCC 308) — and all three requirements must co-exist at the same time (Ravinder Kaur, para 57).
The Karnataka Wakf Board Checklist
A claimant must show: (a) on what date he came into possession; (b) the nature of the possession; (c) whether the factum of possession was known to the other party; (d) how long it continued; (e) that it was open and undisturbed. A person pleading adverse possession has no equities in his favour — he must clearly plead and establish every fact. Knowledge of the adverse claim may be presumed from open and notorious acts.

3.3  The Seven Working Rules (from the DU compendium & the cases)

  1. Actual possession required — mere entries in revenue records in the defendant's name are not enough; but physical possession of every square foot is not required — the test is whether the acts of possession show the dominion normally exercised over such property (a wrongful do gains title only to the portion actually held).
  2. Adequate in continuity, publicity and extent — open, notorious, actual, exclusive and adverse; "some acts of ownership" are not enough.
  3. Intention to hold — the possessor must intend to hold as owner; if he believes he holds only a life estate, his possession is not adverse beyond that belief.
  4. No adverse possession under a lawful title — a manager's, guardian's or tenant's possession is referable to the owner's title and becomes adverse only on a distinct repudiation of that title, brought home to the owner (Des Raj v. Bhagat Ram: hostile declaration in a written statement of 1968 could start the clock; P. Lakshmi Reddy: adverse possession cannot commence "until he obtains actual possession with the required animus").
  5. Knowledge or means of knowledge — possession does not become adverse until the owner knows, or has circumstances that would give him knowledge; but knowledge is presumed from open and notorious acts — the owner who does not care to know notorious facts is charged with knowledge (Ravinder Kaur, para 57).
  6. Immediate entitlement — possession is not adverse until the plaintiff was entitled to immediate possession (e.g., against a reversioner it turns adverse only when the estate falls into possession — Art. 65, Explanation (a)).
  7. Long possession ≠ adverse possession — "a mere suggestion in the relief clause that there was uninterrupted possession for 'several 12 years'... was not enough to raise such a plea. Long possession is not necessarily adverse possession and the prayer clause is not a substitute for a plea" (S.M. Karim); a trespasser's casual user is construed as possession on behalf of the owner (Ravinder Kaur, para 57).
The two-pronged enquiry (P.T. Munichikkanna Reddy v. Revamma, (2007) 6 SCC 59)(1) Apply the limitation provision: the owner's "wilful neglect" distances the title from the paper-owner. (2) Find the possessor's specific positive intention to dispossess: that intention "effectively shifts the title already distanced from the paper-owner to the adverse possessor." Simple efflux of time, without the intention to dispossess, is not enough.

3.4  Articles 64 and 65 — The Two Doors to the Twelve Years

  • Art. 64 (suit based on previous possession, not title): 12 years from the date of dispossession — the possessory action for one who was in possession and was turned out. Until the title completes, this — and s. 6, Specific Relief Act — is the dispossessed possessor's protection.
  • Art. 65 (suit for possession based on title): 12 years from when the possession of the defendant becomes adverse to the plaintiff. Explanations: (a) against a remainderman/reversioner/devisee, possession becomes adverse only when the estate falls into possession; (b) against a Hindu/Muslim heir of a female holder, only when the female dies; (c) a purchaser at an execution sale where the judgment-debtor was out of possession steps into the judgment-debtor's shoes.
  • The crucial reading (Case 36): "title" in Art. 65's opening part includes title acquired by adverse possession — so the possessor whose twelve years have completed sues on the very title the owner lost; Art. 65's column 3 poses no bar to a plaintiff pleading adverse possession.
  • Heritability and tacking (Ravinder Kaur, para 58): adverse possession is heritable and the right is transmissible; successive possessors in privity (purchaser, legatee, assignee claiming through the same right over the same property) may tack their possessions to complete the period — but "two distinct trespassers cannot tack their possession."

CASE 35 — DU CASE MATERIAL · ACQUISITION OF OWNERSHIP BY POSSESSION

Rajender Singh v. Santa Singh

AIR 1973 SC 2537  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Beg, J.  |  Plaintiffs' appeal by special leave from the Full Bench of the Punjab High Court

Facts in Detail

  • The land: 331 kanals 11 marlas (khasra numbers given in the plaint), originally owned by Sham Singh (deceased).
  • The devolution: Smt. Malan, Sham Singh's widow, gifted the plots in 1935, half and half, to the plaintiffs (sons of Smt. Premi, Sham Singh's daughter) and to Smt. Khemi (Premi's younger sister). Smt. Khemi, issueless, made a further gift to the plaintiffs before her death in 1944. The plaintiffs claim they obtained possession of the whole land through the Tehsildar about 13-12-1946.
  • The cloud: because the rights of daughters and a widow's powers of donation under Punjab customary law were then uncertain, the defendants — the 8th degree collaterals of Sham Singh — filed a suit on 3-7-1940 for declaration and possession. It was stayed from 1941 to 29-5-1946 under the Indian Soldiers (Litigation) Act, 1925 (the plaintiffs being soldiers' beneficiaries); a parallel mutation fight in the revenue courts ended for the donees before the Financial Commissioner on 13-12-1946; the 1940 suit, renumbered in 1949, ended in the donees' favour in the Punjab High Court on 21-11-1958.
  • The dispossessions alleged: the plaintiffs' Suit No. 179 of 1959 (filed 20/16-4-1959) alleged the defendants took illegal and forcible possession after 21-11-1958 and sought possession back. The defendants' case: they had taken possession after Smt. Khemi's death in 1944 and held "in open, continuous, exclusive possession as owners, adversely to the rest of the world" — the suit was barred by limitation and they had become owners by adverse possession.
  • Courts below: the trial court framed three issues — (1) whether plaintiffs got possession through the Tehsildar c. 13-12-1946; (2) whether defendants took possession after 21-11-1958; (3) whether defendants became owners by adverse possession — placing issues 1–2 on the plaintiffs and issue 3 on the defendants. On evidence ("oral evidence coupled with the entries in the revenue records"), the courts below rejected the plaintiffs' possession story and accepted the defendants' possession from 1944 onwards, holding the suit barred under Art. 142 of the 1908 Act. The first appellate court, however, relying on Sukhubai v. Eknath Bellappa (AIR 1948 Nag 97), held that lis pendens prevented the defendants' rights from maturing during the 1940 suit — possession became adverse only on 21-11-1958, when the High Court dismissed the defendants' own appeal.
  • The Full Bench (Punjab HC): majority (A.N. Grover, J.) — adverse possession commenced during the pendency of the earlier suit, and "once having begun to run, could not stop running merely because of the pendency" of that suit; minority (I.D. Dua, J.) — lis pendens would exclude the time of the 1940 suit pending. The plaintiffs appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  1. Does the doctrine of lis pendens in s. 52, Transfer of Property Act, 1882, arrest the running of limitation during the pendency of the defendants' 1940 suit — so that the defendants' adverse possession matured only after 21-11-1958?
  2. Was Art. 142 (or Art. 144) of the Limitation Act, 1908, correctly applied — including s. 3's duty to apply the bar even when not pleaded?
  3. Can banjar (uncultivated) land be adversely possessed at all?
  4. Could the plaintiffs invoke s. 14 of the 1908 Act (exclusion of time of proceedings bona fide in a court without jurisdiction) or the Custodian of Evacuee Property's claim under s. 8(4) of the Administration of Evacuee Property Act, 1950?

Arguments for Rajender Singh (plaintiffs-appellants)

  • Lis pendens excluded time: while their opponents' 1940 suit over the very land was pending (1940–1958), the property could not be "dealt with" so as to affect the parties' rights — the defendants' possession could not ripen into title during the litigation (following Sukhubai, AIR 1948 Nag 97, as the first appellate court had).
  • Extinction of title during the pendency of a suit must itself be hit by s. 52 — otherwise a party could gain by the litigation what he could not gain by a transfer.
  • Banjar land: portions entered as banjar are vacant and incapable of cultivation — possession follows title, so they must be deemed in the plaintiffs' constructive possession; adverse possession of banjar land is impossible.
  • Art. 142 was wrongly applied: its applicability was not put in issue by pleadings; and (via the Custodian) rights vesting under s. 8(4) of the Evacuee Property Act deserved adjudication.

Arguments for Santa Singh (defendants-respondents)

  • Concurrent findings of fact: possession from 1944 was theirs — open, continuous, exclusive, as owners, adverse to the world; over 12 years completed before the 1959 suit.
  • s. 52 TPA strikes at transfers and dealings — bilateral acts like sales — not at the continuance of wrongful possession, which is "one sided wrongful act"; the Limitation Act alone governs maturation of title by time.
  • The plaintiffs' own plaint asserted possession and dispossession — squarely Art. 142; under Bindhyachal Chand (AIR 1934 All 993, Sulaiman C.J.) "the question of limitation in one sense becomes the question of title" — without subsisting title the suit must fail.
  • s. 14 of the 1908 Act could not help: the cause of action in the 1940 suit was entirely different (indeed it was the defendants who sued there).

The Decision — Beg, J.

  • Art. 142 squarely attracted: the plaintiffs having alleged possession and dispossession and sought delivery back, "the case fell squarely within the ambit of Article 142." Issues 1 and 2 bore directly on it; an issue need not recite the provision of law. And it is "the duty of the Court, in view of Section 3, to apply the bar of limitation where, on patent facts, it is applicable even though not specifically pleaded." Per Bindhyachal Chand: where the only question is discontinuance of the plaintiff's possession, limitation becomes the question of title, "because by virtue of Section 28, Limitation Act, if the claim is barred by time, the title must be deemed to be extinguished."
  • Art. 142 or Art. 144 — immaterial for s. 28: "It is immaterial... whether Article 142 or Article 144... is applicable" — on the accepted findings, all elements of "open, adverse, hostile, continuous, and exclusive possession of the defendants for over 12 years were present," so extinguishment followed either way.
  • Banjar land can be adversely possessed: "It may be that Banjar land cannot be cultivated, but... it could possibly [not] be urged that it is per se incapable of being actually physically possessed by use for other purposes, such as building or storing of wood or crops, apart from cultivation." Besides, the plaintiffs' own pleadings admitted the defendants' actual adverse possession of all the land — the point was a belated afterthought (CMP No. 2487 of 1967 seeking to introduce it was dismissed).
  • Lis pendens — the core ruling: extinction of title by the Limitation Act "will not be hit by the doctrine of lis pendens simply because it is an extinction during the pendency of a suit... a case in which the extinction of title takes place by an application of the specific and mandatory provisions of the Limitation Act falls outside the scope of Section 52 of the Transfer of Property Act" — it is governed by the Limitation Act "exclusively."
  • Why s. 52 cannot serve as a time-exclusion device: the doctrine strikes at attempts "to circumvent the jurisdiction of a court... by private dealings" — transfers and comparable bilateral transactions. "Taking of illegal possession or its continuance neither resemble[s] nor [is] comparable to a transfer. They are one sided wrongful acts and not bilateral transactions of a kind which ordinarily constitute 'deals' or dealings with property... They cannot confer immediate rights on the possessor. Continued illegal possession ripens into a legally enforceable right only after the prescribed period of time has elapsed." If the policy was to exclude litigation time, the provision would have been in the Limitation Act — indeed Part III of that Act deals with exclusion of time (s. 14 being its only litigation-time provision, inapplicable here).
  • s. 3 forecloses judicial extension: "The effect of Section 3 Limitation Act is that it expressly precludes exclusion of time on a ground outside this Act even if it parades under the guise of a doctrine which has no application whatsoever here. Courts of Justice cannot legislate or reconstruct law contained in a statute or introduce exceptions when statutory law debars them from doing so. Even hard circumstances of a case do not justify the adoption of such a course." The plaintiffs could not "complain of hardship when their own negligence or failure to act in time enabled defendants to acquire rights."
  • Support: Subbaiya Pandaram v. Mahammad Mustapha Marcayar (AIR 1923 PC 175) and Narayan Jivangouda Patil v. Puttabai (AIR 1945 PC 5) — limitation runs from the taking of possession notwithstanding the pendency of litigation; "We are in complete agreement with the majority view" (Grover, J.) of the Full Bench. The Custodian's s. 8(4) claim was not decided (dispute between co-defendants; no res judicata; the plaintiffs could not raise it "in a desperate attempt to clutch at a straw").
  • Result: Full Bench affirmed; appeal dismissed; parties to bear their own costs throughout.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. Lis pendens does not suspend limitation: adverse possession once begun runs through pending litigation; a possessor's title can mature by the Limitation Act even while a suit over the property is pending — the two doctrines belong to different statutes and different policies.
  2. Wrongful possession is not a "dealing": s. 52 TPA hits bilateral transactions (transfers, sales, comparable acts with immediate effect); continued illegal possession is a unilateral wrong that ripens only by efflux of time under the Limitation Act.
  3. The Limitation Act is exclusive on its own ground: s. 3 "precludes exclusion of time on a ground outside this Act even if it parades under the guise of a doctrine" — courts cannot create equitable exceptions; exclusion of time belongs to Part III (s. 14), whose conditions are strict.
  4. Limitation can be the question of title: in Art. 142 cases, barring the suit by time itself extinguishes title (s. 28 of the 1908 Act) — so the plea need not be raised by the parties; the court must apply it suo motu.
  5. Banjar land is not immune: uncultivable land can still be physically possessed for building, storing wood or crops — no per se exclusion from adverse possession.

The Reference and the Question of Law

This is a batch decision settling, across a group of appeals (the lead matter being Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjeet Kaur), a pure question of law on which smaller benches and High Courts had divided. The Court heard senior counsel for the parties and two Amici Curiae — Shri P.S. Patwalia and Shri Huzefa Ahmadi — and directed the matters to be placed for merits before the appropriate Bench after settling the law (para 62). The question (para 1):

  • Whether a person claiming title by adverse possession can maintain a suit under Art. 65 for declaration of title and permanent injunction protecting his possession, or for restoration of possession if dispossessed — by the very owner whose title stood extinguished, or by a third party.
  • In other words: is adverse possession only a shield for a defendant, or can it be wielded as a sword by a plaintiff? Is the perfected possessor remediless?
  • And if the owner sells the property after his title is extinguished, what is the possessor's remedy against the sale and the resulting interference or dispossession?
Why this matteredA 2014 two-judge decision, Gurudwara Sahib v. Gram Panchayat Village Sirthala, (2014) 1 SCC 669, had observed (para 8) that a plaintiff in adverse possession "cannot seek a declaration that such adverse possession has matured into ownership" — the plea was "a shield and not a sword." Followed without argument in State of Uttarakhand v. Mandir Shri Lakshman Sidh (2017) and Dharampal v. Punjab Wakf Board (2018), that line threatened to leave every perfected possessor without a cause of action. The three-judge bench in Ravinder Kaur took up the task of testing it against the entire line of precedent.

The Historical Canvas (para 2)

  • Code of Hammurabi (c. 2000 BC), Law 30: if a man leaves his house, garden and field and another takes and uses it for three years, the first owner's later claim "shall not be given to him" — with an exception for the soldier captured or killed in battle and for the owner's juvenile son.
  • Roman law: a kind of spirit attached to the land, nurtured by the possessor; the user of land could have a greater "ownership" than the titled owner.
  • English roots (inherited as a British colony): William 1066 consolidated ownership under the Crown; Statute of Westminster 1275 — when land records were scarce and literacy rare, "the best evidence of ownership was possession"; 1639 — a statute fixed 20 years for recovery of possession; Statute of Wills 1540; Statute of Tenures 1660 ended feudal tenure and created the concept of title.
  • The utilitarian justification: the person who possesses and produces "something of ultimate benefit to the society" holds the best title; revenue laws confer title on the actual tiller; adverse possession permits rapid development of "wild" lands with weak or indeterminate title; it cures clouds on title as memories grow dim and evidence disappears; the possessor who cares for the land builds an attachment "which cannot be easily parted with" — while the owner who neglects it loses it.

The Conflict that Triggered the Reference

  • The 2014 decision: in Gurudwara Sahib, Sirthala, the trial court had found the gurdwara in adverse possession since 13-4-1952 (proved by "a plethora of documentary evidence") — yet held, following the P&H High Court's Gurudwara Sahib Sannauli (2009) 154 PLR 756, that no declaration could be sought: adverse possession "can be used as a shield and not as a sword." The Supreme Court agreed there was "no quarrel" with that proposition — without the contrary precedents being cited or the point argued (paras 4–5, 39, 47: "a decision based upon concession cannot be treated as precedent").
  • The buried authority: a three-judge bench in Sarangadeva Periya Matam v. Ramaswami Goundar, AIR 1966 SC 1603 (itself resting on the Privy Council's Musumut Chundrabullee Debia v. Luchea Debia, 1865 SCC OnLine PC 7), had held the opposite — and "was not placed for consideration" of the two-judge bench.
  • The High Court's inference: the P&H line (Bhim Singh v. Zile Singh, AIR 2006 P&H 195; Prem Nath Wadhawan, Delhi) reasoned from Art. 65's third column — "when the possession of the defendant becomes adverse to the plaintiff" — that the plea is available only to a defendant. The Ravinder Kaur bench calls this "inferential process... not at all permissible" (para 46).

The Decisive Precedent — Sarangadeva Periya Matam (3 judges, 1966)

  • Facts: in 1883 the mathadhipathi of Sri Sarangadevar Periya Matam (Kumbakonam) granted a perpetual lease of the melwaram and kudiwaram interests in inam lands (Kannibada Zamin, Dindigul, Madurai) to Chinna Gopiya Goundar at ₹70 a year. The mathadhipathi died in 1915 without nominating a successor; rent ceased to be paid; between 1915 and 1939 there was no mathadhipathi (Basavan Chetti managed for 20 years); a new mathadhipathi was elected in 1939. In 1928 the Collector resumed the inam and issued a joint ryotwari patta. The plaintiff (Goundar's descendant) held until January 1950, when the math obtained possession; on 18-2-1954 he sued for recovery of possession, pleading title by adverse possession and the patta.
  • Held: the math is a juristic person which "may acquire property by prescription and may likewise lose property by adverse possession"; limitation was not suspended by the absence of a mathadhipathi (it runs against a de facto mathadhipathi — Vithalbowa v. Narayan Daji — and even where there is none). Under Art. 144 read with s. 28 of the 1908 Act, the math's title was extinguished in 1927 — the lessee's descendants "acquired title to the lands by prescription"; the delivery of possession in January 1950 "did not transfer any title to the math" — the suit of 1954 was "well within time" and was decreed.
  • The point for Case 36: a plaintiff successfully sued to recover possession on a title acquired by adverse possession — three judges, 1966, squarely contrary to the shield-only rule.

The Decision — Arun Mishra, J. (paras 45–62)

  • Art. 65's language holds no bar: "Column No. 3 of Schedule of the Act nowhere suggests that suit cannot be filed by the plaintiff for possession of immovable property or any interest therein based on title acquired by way of adverse possession. There is absolutely no bar for the perfection of title by way of adverse possession whether a person is suing as the plaintiff or being sued as the defendant. The inferential process of interpretation employed by the High Court is not at all permissible" (para 46).
  • Decisions on concession are not precedent: Gurudwara Sahib, Sirthala rested on an uncontested proposition — contrary larger-bench authority was never cited; "only an observation was made as to proposition of law, which is palpably incorrect" (para 47).
  • The nature of the title (paras 50, 53, 55): "Law of adverse possession does not qualify only a defendant for the acquisition of title... it may be perfected by a person who is filing a suit." On extinguishment, "the concept of adverse possession as evolved goes beyond it — on completion of period and extinguishment of right [it] confers the same right on the possessor which has been extinguished, and not more than that." The word "title" in Art. 65 "would include the title acquired by the plaintiff by way of adverse possession."
  • What is acquired depends on what is prescribed against (paras 31, 33, 56): possession is the root of title; ownership may be sole, contingent, corporeal, legal or equitable — a person can prescribe only against the limited right actually held by the person dispossessed (tenant's right extinguished → sub-tenant gains that right, not the freehold). The kind of possession enjoyed (de facto, de jure, constructive, concurrent, symbolic) and even personal law shape the enquiry; a co-owner's possession is not presumed adverse.
  • The three classics must co-exist (para 57): nec vi (continuity), nec clam (publicity), nec precario (adverseness in denial of title and knowledge) — "visible, notorious and peaceful"; if the owner does not care to know notorious facts, knowledge is attributed to him. Animus possidendi under a hostile colour of title is required; a trespasser's long possession is not adverse — the casual user does not constitute adverse possession; the owner can retake from a trespasser at any time.
  • Heritable and transmissible, with tacking (para 58): the right is heritable; successive possessors in privity (purchaser, legatee, assignee claiming through, same property, same right) may tack to complete the period — "two distinct trespassers cannot tack their possession." Once perfected, the right "cannot be defeated on re-entry except as provided in Article 65 itself."
  • The operative holding (para 59): "a person in possession cannot be ousted by another person except by due procedure of law and once 12 years' period of adverse possession is over, even owner's right to eject him is lost and the possessory owner acquires right, title and interest possessed by the outgoing person/owner... once the right, title or interest is acquired it can be used as a sword by the plaintiff as well as a shield by the defendant within ken of Article 65 of the Act and any person who has perfected title by way of adverse possession can file a suit for restoration of possession in case of dispossession." Before ripening, a dispossessed possessor has the possessory suit under Art. 64; after ripening he can sue under Art. 65 — against the dispossessing owner or a third party (who must himself have prescribed against the possessor to resist).
  • Other infringements: if the owner sells after his title is extinguished, the perfected possessor can sue to question the alienation and the attempted dispossession; and under other Articles, for infringement of other rights so acquired.
  • The public-property caution (para 60): for land dedicated to public use, courts have been loath to allow prescription; encroachers raising the plea on public/utility land should find no rights accruing — "it would be advisable that... it is made clear in the statute of limitation that no rights can accrue by adverse possession" as to such property.
  • Formal result (para 61): Gurudwara Sahib, Sirthala, Mandir Shri Lakshman Sidh and Dharampal v. Punjab Wakf Board "cannot be said to be laying down the law correctly, thus they are hereby overruled." The plea of acquisition of title by adverse possession can be taken by a plaintiff under Art. 65, and there is no bar in the Limitation Act, 1963 to suing on that basis.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. Sword as well as shield: title perfected by adverse possession is a subsisting title on which the possessor may sue as plaintiff under Art. 65 — for declaration, injunction, and restoration of possession against a dispossessing owner or third party; the erstwhile owner, whose title s. 27 extinguished, is a stranger to the land.
  2. Art. 65's "title" includes prescriptive title: the third column ("when the possession of the defendant becomes adverse to the plaintiff") merely fixes the starting point of limitation; it cannot be read inferentially to abolish a plaintiff's cause of action.
  3. What is gained equals what is lost — no more: adverse possession transfers the extinguished right itself; against a limited owner (tenant, life-tenant), the possessor gains only that limited right; reversioners and remaindermen outside the extinguished right are untouched.
  4. Before ripening, protection is possessory: the unperfected possessor relies on s. 6, Specific Relief Act and Art. 64 — not on title; the casual user of a trespasser is never adverse; co-owners' possession is not presumed adverse.
  5. Pleading discipline: the plaintiff pleading prescription must plead and prove the date, nature, knowledge, duration and open, undisturbed character of possession — the whole case, without equities (S.M. Karim; Krishnamurthy Setlur; Karnataka Wakf Board).
  6. Decisions on concession are no precedent; and public/utility land should, as a matter of policy, lie beyond the reach of adverse possession — a legislative invitation flagged by the Court.

PYQ — 2019
Q. 8(a)/2019 — Write a detailed note on: Acquisition of ownership by possession.
MODEL ANSWER — ACQUISITION OF OWNERSHIP BY POSSESSION (SECTIONS 25, 26, 27)

The architecture. Ownership by possession is acquired through three sections of the Limitation Act, 1963: s. 25 (easements by prescription), s. 26 (exclusion in favour of the reversioner of the servient tenement), and s. 27 (extinguishment of right to property). ss. 25–26 confer easements directly; s. 27 works indirectly — it extinguishes the owner's right, and the possessor steps into the vacuum.

s. 25 — prescription of easements. Where access and use of light or air for a building, or a way, watercourse, use of water or other easement (affirmative or negative), has been (i) peaceably enjoyed, (ii) as an easement, (iii) as of right, (iv) without interruption, and (v) for 20 years (30 for Government land, s. 25(3)) — the right becomes absolute and indefeasible. By s. 25(2) the period must be one "ending within two years next before the institution of the suit." The section concerns only the acquisition of prescriptive rights, not the extent of the right or the remedy for disturbance (recourse for those lies in the general law of easements); a person may acquire title under it though he had none at the start.

s. 25's ingredients: peaceable (nec vi); open (nec clam); as of right, i.e., without permission (nec precario); without interruption — the Explanation makes an interruption real only if enjoyment actually discontinues by another's obstruction and the claimant acquiesces for one year after notice; and the full period.

s. 26 — the reversioner's shield. Where the servient land was held under an interest for life or a term exceeding three years, enjoyment during that interest is excluded from the 20-year computation, provided the person entitled on determination resists within three years. The section is an exception to s. 25, entirely for the benefit of reversioners — to prevent easements being acquired against the inheritance by a life-tenant's or long lessee's acquiescence. It does not apply to a donee/transferee from a Hindu widow: he succeeds to her capacity as full owner for her life, so prescription during her lifetime counts against him.

s. 27 — extinguishment. "At the determination of the period hereby limited to any person for instituting a suit for possession of any property, his right to such property shall be extinguished." The general rule is that limitation bars only the remedy; s. 27 is the exception for possession suits — after the period, "not simply the remedy is barred but the title is extinct in favour of the possessor" (Gunga Govind Mundul; Rajender Singh; Ravinder Kaur Grewal). Confines: it applies only to suits for possession (not applications, not a mortgagee's suit for sale); it is substantive, not procedural; possession includes possession in law; it applies only to one out of possession who could have sued; and it extinguishes only that person's right — a widow's barred right does not take the reversioner's with it; a mortgagor's barred possession-suit leaves the simple mortgagee's sale-rights intact. Government: 30 years; private owners: 12 years.

Adverse possession — the operative doctrine. Hostile possession, "expressly or impliedly in denial of the title of the true owner," commenced in wrong and maintained against right; the claimant must show "by clear and unequivocal evidence" hostility in denial of the owner's title — the animus of the possessor is the crucial factor. Essentials (the seven rules): (1) actual possession — revenue entries are not enough, but dominion normally exercised over the kind of property suffices; a wrongful doer gains only what he actually holds; (2) adequate in continuity, publicity and extent — open, notorious, actual, exclusive, adverse; (3) intention to hold as owner (a possessor believing he holds a life estate is adverse only to that extent); (4) possession under a lawful title (manager, guardian, tenant) is not adverse until distinctly repudiated and the repudiation brought home; (5) knowledge or means of knowledge on the owner's side (presumable from open, notorious acts); (6) adversity only when the owner is entitled to immediate possession; (7) possession of part is not possession of the whole.

Conclusion. Possession + time + hostility = ownership: directly for easements (s. 25, with s. 26 protecting reversioners), and by extinguishment for corporeal property (s. 27 with Arts. 64–65) — culminating in Ravinder Kaur Grewal (2019): the perfected possessor "can use [the title] as a sword by the plaintiff as well as a shield by the defendant within ken of Article 65."

Q. 8(b)/2018 — What do you understand by Adverse Possession? Explain in detail its relevant requirements and recent developments in terms of case law. Do you think it is an exception to section 3 of the Limitation Act that it also destroys the right of the owner?
MODEL ANSWER — ADVERSE POSSESSION: MEANING, REQUIREMENTS, RECENT DEVELOPMENTS

Meaning. Adverse possession is possession of property held hostile to the true owner — expressly or impliedly in denial of his title — commenced in wrong and maintained against right, for the full statutory period (12 years private; 30 Government). The possessor does not acknowledge the owner's right; he denies it. When the period completes, s. 27 extinguishes the owner's right and the possessor acquires it.

Requirements (the classic triad + the modern restatements): nec vi (peaceable, continuous), nec clam (open, public), nec precario (as of right, without leave) — "adequate in continuity, in publicity and in extent" (S.M. Karim; Balkrishan); animus possidendi from commencement (Annakili); actual, visible, exclusive, hostile and continued possession over the whole period (Karnataka Wakf Board: date, nature, knowledge, duration, open and undisturbed — all pleaded and proved, for the claimant "has no equities in his favour"); the two-pronged enquiry — owner's wilful neglect + possessor's positive intention to dispossess (Munichikkanna Reddy); mere long possession is not adverse, and a trespasser's possession is construed on behalf of the owner.

Recent developments. (1) P.T. Munichikkanna Reddy v. Revamma (2007): the modern two-pronged test; statutes protect the possessor who cared for the land. (2) Hemaji Waghaji Jat v. Bhikhabhai Harijan (2009): the law is "harsh" — Legislature may consider changing it; courts must insist on strict proof, especially for public property (Harphool Singh: destruction of State title must be scrutinised "more seriously and effectively"). (3) Ravinder Kaur Grewal v. Manjeet Kaur, [2019] 11 SCR 74 — the watershed: a plaintiff CAN claim title by adverse possession under Art. 65 (declaration, injunction, restoration of possession); "it can be used as a sword by the plaintiff as well as a shield by the defendant"; Gurudwara Sahib, Sirthala (2014) and its followers overruled; adverse possession is heritable and tackable in privity; the Court advised legislation to bar prescription against public/utility lands. (4) Rajender Singh v. Santa Singh (1973) remains the anchor on the mechanics: adverse possession runs even during pending litigation — lis pendens (s. 52 TPA) does not arrest it.

Is it an exception to s. 3 that destroys the owner's right? — Yes. s. 3 is the procedural bar: every suit filed after the prescribed period "shall be dismissed," even if limitation is not pleaded. The general principle (personal actions): limitation bars the remedy, not the right — a barred debt still "does not cease to be due" (First National Bank v. Seth Santlal). s. 27 is the deliberate exception: in suits for possession of property, at the determination of the period, the right itself "shall be extinguished." So the maxim yields: limitation destroys the right only in this class. Two clarifications: (i) s. 27 does not "confer" title on the possessor by transfer — its operation is negative, "it extinguishes the right and title of the dispossessed owner and leaves the occupant with a title gained by the fact of possession... a good title, both at law and in equity" (Halsbury ¶785, approved in Ravinder Kaur); (ii) it is not an "exception to s. 3" technically — it is a consequence written into the Act by the Legislature, applying only where the suit barred is a suit for possession. Rajender Singh (quoting Bindhyachal Chand): "by virtue of Section 28 [1908 Act], if the claim is barred by time, the title must be deemed to be extinguished" — limitation becomes the question of title.

PYQ — 2020 (OBE)
Q. 6/2020 — Mohan has taken possession of immovable property of Sohan in the year 2005. Till 2019 Mohan was enjoying possession of immovable property peacefully and uninterrupted with clear knowledge of denial of title to Sohan. In 2020 Sohan filed a suit for recovery of immovable property. Decide in the light of the maxim “limitation bars the remedy, but not the right”. Discuss and also mention the development in law in this area, if any, has taken place. (25 marks)
MODEL ANSWER — PROBLEM: EXTINGUISHMENT UNDER s. 27

Relevant provisions: s. 27, Limitation Act, 1963; Art. 65, Schedule (12 years, from when possession becomes adverse); the maxim "limitation bars the remedy, but not the right."

2005
Mohan takes possession of Sohan's immovable property — with clear knowledge of denial of title, i.e., hostile animus from the start.
2005 → 2017
Peaceful, uninterrupted, open, hostile possession — all 12 years (Art. 65) complete by 2017: nec vi, nec clam, nec precario fully satisfied.
2017
On the determination of the period for Sohan's suit for possession, s. 27 extinguishes Sohan's right to the property.
2020
Sohan sues for recovery — three years too late; his suit is barred (s. 3) and his title already gone.

Decision: Sohan's suit fails. Mohan took possession in 2005 and enjoyed it peacefully, uninterrupted, and with open denial of Sohan's title till 2019 — within Sohan's knowledge. Limitation for a suit for possession based on title (Art. 65) is 12 years from the date possession becomes adverse; the window closed in 2017. Therefore, by application of s. 27, Sohan's right to the property stands extinguished. s. 27 is an exception to the maxim "limitation bars the remedy, not the right": here, limitation has destroyed not merely the remedy but the right itself — Mohan holds the title Sohan lost (and since Ravinder Kaur Grewal, could even sue on it affirmatively).

Development in law: the maxim holds generally (barred debts still due), but s. 27's extinguishment for possession suits has been progressively deepened — from Gunga Govind Mundul (extinction "in favour of the party in possession") through Rajender Singh (extinction runs even during pending litigation) to Ravinder Kaur Grewal (2019) (the extinguished title enures to the possessor as an enforceable, heritable title — "sword as well as shield" — while the Court has invited legislation to exclude public lands from prescription).

PYQ — 2018
Q. 7(a)/2018 — “Section 3, Limitation Act only bars the remedy and does not destroy the right to which the remedy relates.” Do you agree with the statement? Explain with case laws.
MODEL ANSWER — THE MAXIM AND ITS EXCEPTION (s. 3 vs s. 27)

Partly. The statement is the general rule — with one famous statutory exception. The law of limitation bars the remedy in a court of law once the period expires, but does not extinguish the right that cannot be enforced: if a time-barred claim is satisfied voluntarily outside court, that payment is not recoverable back; "after a debt becomes barred a person is still deemed to owe and it does not cease to be due" (First National Bank Ltd. v. Seth Santlal, AIR 1954 Punj 328). The Act's object is not to destroy rights but to compel diligence: to quiet long possession, extinguish stale demands, and end litigation — interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt (Halsbury's three reasons: dormant claims are cruel; evidence disappears; diligent claimants must sue in time).

The exception — s. 27: "At the determination of the period... for instituting a suit for possession of any property, his right to such property shall be extinguished." For possession suits, limitation destroys the right along with the remedy: "it declares not simply that the remedy is barred, but that the title is extinct in favour of the possessor" (Gunga Govind Mundul v. Collector of 24 Pergunnahs, 11 M.I.A. 212); extinction cannot be revested by re-entry or acknowledgment (Halsbury ¶783, applied in Ravinder Kaur). In Rajender Singh v. Santa Singh, AIR 1973 SC 2537, the principle carried the day: once the defendants' adverse possession ran 12 years through the pendency of the plaintiffs' own related litigation, "by virtue of Section 28 [of the 1908 Act], if the claim is barred by time, the title must be deemed to be extinguished" — the donees' title died even though a suit over the land had been pending throughout.

Why the exception is narrow: s. 27 applies only to suits for possession; it spares the mortgagee's right to sue for sale, the reversioner untouched by a widow's barred right, and all non-possession rights (debts, damages, accounts). And its operation is negative — it destroys the owner's title and leaves the possessor with a title gained by possession, "a good title, both at law and in equity" (Halsbury ¶785; Perry v. Clissold; reaffirmed for plaintiffs in Ravinder Kaur Grewal, [2019] 11 SCR 74).

Conclusion: Agree with the statement as the general rule; qualify it with s. 27 — the one place where the Limitation Act, by express words, destroys the right itself, and the possessor's gain is the law's design.

Quick Revision Sheet — One Last Look Before the Hall

Sections 25–27 and the two cases, compressed
The Three Sections in One Breath
s.25 — 20 years' peaceable, open, as-of-right enjoyment of light/air, way, water or other easement (30 for Govt.) → absolute and indefeasible; period must end within 2 years before suit (s.25(2)); "interruption" = actual obstruction + 1 year's acquiescence after notice. s.26 — enjoyment during a life interest or term >3 years is excluded; the reversioner resists within 3 years of determination. s.27 — at the determination of the period for a suit for possession, the right to the property is extinguished (12 years private; 30 Government).
Adverse Possession — The Checklist
nec vi (continuity) · nec clam (publicity) · nec precario (adverse, in denial of title, to the owner's knowledge — presumable from open, notorious acts); animus possidendi from commencement; actual possession = dominion normally exercised (part ≠ whole); permissive/co-owner/tenant possession not adverse until repudiation brought home; adverse only when owner entitled to immediate possession (Art. 65 Expl.); long possession ≠ adverse; prayer clause ≠ plea (S.M. Karim); the five-point Wakf Board plea: date, nature, knowledge, duration, open & undisturbed.
Case 35 — Rajender Singh (1973, Beg J.)
Gift 1935 by widow; collaterals' suit 3-7-1940 (stayed under Soldiers' Litigation Act to 29-5-1946; decided for donees 21-11-1958); defendants in adverse possession from 1944; donees' possession suit 20-4-1959 under Art. 142. Held: Art. 142 squarely attracted (limitation = question of title, Bindhyachal Chand); lis pendens does not arrest limitation — wrongful possession is a one-sided act, not a "dealing"; extinguishment by the Limitation Act falls outside s.52 TPA, "governed by the Limitation Act, exclusively"; s.3 forbids extra-statutory exceptions; banjar land CAN be adversely possessed. Appeal dismissed.
Case 36 — Ravinder Kaur Grewal (2019, 3 JJ.)
Question: can a plaintiff claim title by adverse possession under Art. 65 (declaration, injunction, restoration)? Held (para 59): once 12 years complete, the owner's right to eject is lost and the possessor acquires the owner's right — "it can be used as a sword by the plaintiff as well as a shield by the defendant within ken of Article 65"; title in Art. 65 includes prescriptive title; what is gained = exactly what was extinguished (limited right → limited gain); heritable + tackable in privity (not between strangers); Gurudwara Sahib Sirthala (2014), Mandir Laxman Sidh (2017), Dharampal (2018) overruled (concessions are not precedent); public/utility lands should be excluded by legislation.
The Maxims and Latin
Limitatio tempus adimat actionem, non jus — limitation takes the action, not the right (general rule; Santlal: barred debt still due). Exception: s. 27 possession suits — right extinguished. Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium — the State needs an end to litigation. Vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt — the law aids the vigilant, not the sleeping. Nec vi, nec clam, nec precario — without force, without stealth, without leave. Animus possidendi — the intention to hold as owner.
The English Line, Tamed
Taylor v. Twinberrow (1930), Fairweather v. St Marylebone (1962), Tichborne v. Weir (1892): limitation merely destroys rights, confers none — but those were limited-interest cases (tenant v. sub-tenant). The rule they yield: prescribe only against the right actually extinguished. Against an owner, extinguishment of ownership leaves the possessor with ownership — "a good title, both at law and in equity" (Halsbury ¶785). Perry v. Clissold (1907) remains the fountainhead: possessory owner acquires absolute title on the owner's default.

Memory Hooks

  • 25-26-27 ladder: 25 gives the right (easement), 26 protects the reversioner, 27 takes the owner's right away — possession climbs the ladder into ownership.
  • "20 for an easement, 30 for the Crown, 12 for the field" — the three periods in one line (and s. 25's 20 must end within 2 years before suit; s. 26's reversioner has 3 years to resist).
  • 3 Latin tests = 3 qualities: no force → continuity; no stealth → publicity; no leave → hostility. All must co-exist for the whole 12 years.
  • Rajender Singh's two clocks: the suit clock (lis pendens) freezes dealings; the limitation clock never pauses for anyone — "governed by the Limitation Act, exclusively."
  • Banjar is not barren of possession: build on it, stack wood, store crops — that is possession too.
  • Sirthala's one-liner died in 2019: "shield, not sword" was said on concession; Ravinder Kaur overruled it — the possessor now walks into court with both.
  • What you gain is what he lost: prescribe against a tenant → get the tenancy; against the owner → get the ownership; the reversioner behind a widow is always beyond your reach.
  • Case-number = theme: 35 keeps the clock running (lis pendens fails to stop it); 36 hands over the sword (plaintiff's suit succeeds).
