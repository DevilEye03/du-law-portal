The Law of Limitation — Concept, Object & Policy

Why the law compels litigants to be diligent — and what exactly limitation destroys
Core Definition
The law of limitation prescribes the time-limits within which suits, appeals and applications must be brought before a court; a proceeding launched after the prescribed period is liable to be dismissed as time-barred (s. 3, Limitation Act, 1963). It creates no rights and defines no causes of action — it only bars the judicial remedy for enforcing existing rights after the lapse of the fixed period: "vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt" — the law aids the diligent, not those who sleep over their rights.

1.1  Statutes of Repose, Peace and Justice

Limitation laws are called statutes of repose: they quiet long possession, extinguish stale demands and bring litigation to an end — "disputes would be immortal, when men are mortal". The principle on which the statute is founded is interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium — the interest of the State requires that litigation come to an end.

  • Policy pillars: (i) keeping litigation alive for unlimited periods disturbs the peace of society; (ii) a constant threat of involvement in judicial process generates insecurity, retarding national growth; (iii) the law compels litigants to approach courts within the prescribed time, and quiets long possession.
  • Two broad considerations underlying the doctrine: (1) a right not exercised for a long time is presumed not to exist; (2) rights in property and rights in general must not remain in constant uncertainty, doubt and suspense.
  • Settled Common Law Principles — three objects of limitation statutes: (i) long dormant claims have often more of cruelty than justice in them; (ii) the defendant may have lost the evidence to rebut a stale claim; (iii) persons with good causes of action should pursue them with reasonable diligence.
  • Best, C.J. in A'Court v. Cross (1825) 3 Bing. 329 (quoted in Butler): "It is, as I have heard it often called by great judges, an act of peace. Long dormant claims have often more cruelty than justice in them."

1.2  The Master Maxim — Limitation bars the Remedy, not the Right

Punjab National Bank v. Surendra Prasad Sinha, AIR 1992 SC 1815 (K. Ramaswamy, J.)"The rules of limitation are not meant to destroy the rights of the parties. Section 3 of the Limitation Act... only bars the remedy, but does not destroy the right which the remedy relates to. The right to the debt continues to exist notwithstanding the remedy is barred by limitation. Only exception in which the remedy also becomes barred by limitation is that the right itself is destroyed — for example under Section 27 of the Act a suit for possession of any property becoming barred by limitation, the right to property itself is destroyed... The time barred debt does not cease to exist by reason of Section 3. That right can be exercised in any other manner than by means of a suit."
  • Consequences of the maxim: a time-barred debt is still due — "after a debt becomes barred a person is still deemed to owe and it does not cease to be due" (First National Bank Ltd. v. Seth Santlal, AIR 1954 Punj 328); a claim satisfied outside court after expiry of limitation is not illegal, and the payment cannot be recovered back.
  • Enforcement without suit: a creditor may adjust a time-barred debt out of money paid by the debtor, and may appropriate it against a security in his possession (the PNB holding itself — Case 27 below).
  • The s. 27 exception: in a suit for possession of immovable property based on title, once limitation (Art. 65 — 12 years) expires, not only the remedy but the owner's very title is extinguished, and the adverse possessor's title matures — the one great inroad on the maxim (see the PYQ on Mohan & Sohan, Section 13).
  • Criminal law overlay: a creditor acting within his contractual rights to adjust a security is not guilty of criminal breach of trust (ss. 405/409 IPC) — PNB: "Action in terms of the contract... is a negation of criminal breach of trust."

The Statutory Framework & Key Definitions

Act 36 of 1963, the Schedule of Articles, s. 2 definitions, s. 29(2) special laws, and the CPC's position

2.1  The Limitation Act, 1963

  • Act 36 of 1963, enacted 5 October 1963, brought into force 1 January 1964; it consolidates and amends the law of limitation of suits and other proceedings (replacing the Indian Limitation Act, 1908).
  • Structure: Part I (ss. 1–2, preliminary) · Part II (ss. 3–11, limitation of suits/appeals/applications) · Part III (ss. 12–24, computation) · Part IV (ss. 25–27, acquisition of ownership by possession) · Part V (ss. 28–32, miscellaneous) · the Schedule of 137+ Articles prescribing periods and starting points.
  • Key definitions (s. 2): "period of limitation" = the period prescribed for any suit, appeal or application by the Schedule; "prescribed period" = the period computed in accordance with the provisions of this Act (i.e., after applying ss. 4–24 — extension, exclusion, disability). "Suit" does not include an appeal or an application. "Application" includes a petition; "applicant" includes a petitioner and persons through whom he derives his right to apply.
The distinction that matters in every problem question"Period of limitation" is the raw Schedule period; "prescribed period" is that period as adjusted by ss. 4–24 (court-closed extension, exclusions under s. 12–15, disability, acknowledgment). Section 3's bar operates on the prescribed period; Section 5 can extend it for appeals and applications. Examiners test exactly this vocabulary.

2.2  Special & Local Laws — s. 29(2)

Section 29(2), Limitation Act, 1963
Where any special or local law prescribes for any suit, appeal or application a period of limitation different from the period prescribed by the Schedule, the provisions of section 3 shall apply as if such period were the period prescribed by the Schedule, and for the purpose of determining any period of limitation prescribed for any suit, appeal or application by any special or local law, the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24 shall apply only in so far as, and to the extent to which, they are not expressly excluded by such special or local law.
  • A special law (e.g., the U.P. Sales Tax Act in Madan Lal Das, the Indian Railways Act in West Coast) may prescribe its own periods — but s. 3's mandatory bar still applies to them, and ss. 4–24 (including s. 5 condonation and s. 12 exclusion) apply unless expressly excluded.
  • The "prescribed period" for s. 3 and s. 5 therefore includes periods prescribed by any law for the time being in force, not merely the Schedule (PYQ book).

2.3  The Code's Position

  • The CPC, 1908 originally contained its own s. 3 (Limitation of suits); upon the Limitation Act, 1963 coming into force, the field is occupied by the Act — the CPC's present s. 3 is "Subordination of Courts", and the Code now repeatedly defers to the Limitation Act (e.g., O.7 r.10B: re-present of returned plaint "subject to the provisions of the Limitation Act, 1963").
  • Interaction with the Code: plaints (s. 26 CPC), appeals (s. 96; O.41), second appeals (s. 100), review (O.47 r.1), execution (O.21; Art. 136 — 12 years) and set-off/counter-claims (treated as separate suits under s. 3(2)(b) of the Act) all take their limitation from the Act.
  • Criminal side: the Limitation Act applies to court proceedings; for prosecutions, s. 468 CrPC governs cognizance-periods — but s. 5's philosophy governs "leave to appeal" applications by the State under s. 378(3) CrPC (Lipok AO, Case 29) and condonation practice in criminal appeals.

Section 3 — Bar of Limitation & its Effect SUB-TOPIC (a) OF THE SYLLABUS

The mandatory dismissal rule, the deemed dates of institution, and the effect of expiry on remedy and right
Section 3, Limitation Act, 1963 — Bar of limitation
(1) Subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24 (inclusive), every suit instituted, appeal preferred, and application made after the prescribed period shall be dismissed, although limitation has not been set up as a defence.
(2) For the purposes of this Act —
(a) a suit is instituted — (i) in an ordinary case, when the plaint is presented to the proper officer; (ii) in the case of a pauper, when his application for leave to sue as a pauper is made; (iii) in the case of a claim against a company being wound up by the court, when the claimant first sends in his claim to the official liquidator;
(b) any claim by way of set-off or counter-claim shall be treated as a separate suit and deemed instituted — (i) in the case of set-off, on the same date as the suit in which it is pleaded; (ii) in the case of a counter-claim, on the date it is made in court;
(c) an application by notice of motion in a High Court is made when the application is presented to the proper officer of that court.

3.1  The Mandate — a Duty, not a Option

  • "Shall be dismissed" — the word is imperative. The bar is applied by the court ex debito justitiae; the plea of limitation need not be taken by the defendant.
  • Limitation is a question of law for the court: it must be considered at any stage, even suo motu, in appeal and second appeal as well (Mohinder Singh Jagdev: "the court is bound to take note thereof... and dismiss the suit, if it is barred by limitation").
  • But the court cannot refuse relief to a plaintiff on the mere possibility that limitation may be pleaded — where the date of institution and the accrual are clear and the suit is within time, dismissal is impermissible (PYQ book).
  • Subject "to the provisions of ss. 4 to 24": the bar applies to the prescribed period, i.e., after the Act's own extensions and exclusions are worked out.

3.2  When does the clock start?

  • The Schedule fixes both the period and the starting point ("time from which period begins to run") for each Article — usually when the right to sue accrues (Art. 113: "when the right to sue accrues") or "first accrues" (Art. 58, declarations).
  • Cause of action accrues when every ingredient exists — a person capable of suing and a person who can be sued (Butler, Case 25); ignorance of the wrongdoer's identity does not stop the clock.
  • "Once limitation starts running, it runs its full course" until interdicted by an order of a competent court (P.K. Kutty — though overruled on merger in West Coast, this proposition still holds where no appeal is pending).
  • Continuing breaches: for continuing wrongs (e.g., dispossession — Art. 64/65), fresh limitation runs from each act; s. 22 and Arts. 26–27 supply special rules.

Section 4 — Expiry of Prescribed Period when Court is Closed S.4

The gentlest section of the Act — and a favourite short-note
Section 4, Limitation Act, 1963
Where the prescribed period for any suit, appeal or application expires on a day when the court is closed, the suit, appeal or application may be instituted, preferred or made on the day when the court re-opens.
Explanation. A court shall be deemed to be closed on any day if during any part of its normal working hours it remains closed on that day.

4.1  Points to Note

  • Operation: s. 4 does not extend limitation generally — it only permits filing on the re-opening day when the last day of the period falls on a closed day (holiday, strike-closure, vacation where the court is closed).
  • "Closed" is generous: even partial closure during normal working hours makes the day a "closed" day (Explanation) — so a court that functions only in the morning, or is closed by a strike for part of the day, counts as closed.
  • Contrast with the strike problem: in the 2019 PYQ (Section 13), the limitation expired on 12 October 1980 during a widespread strike, and the State appealed on 15 October — s. 4 was not enough (the closure did not cover all days, and the appeal was 3 days late); the rescue had to come from s. 5 condonation on "sufficient cause".
  • Prospective operation: s. 4 applies when the period expires on a closed day — not when the cause of action arose on a closed day.
  • Section 4 applies to suits too (unlike s. 5) — and via s. 29(2) to special laws unless excluded.

Section 5 — Extension of the Prescribed Period: Condonation of Delay SUB-TOPIC (b) OF THE SYLLABUS

"Sufficient cause" — the most litigated phrase in the Limitation Act
Section 5, Limitation Act, 1963 — Extension of prescribed period in certain cases
Any appeal or any application, other than an application under any of the provisions of Order XXI of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, may be admitted after the prescribed period if the appellant or the applicant satisfies the court that he had sufficient cause for not preferring the appeal or making the application within such period.
Explanation. The fact that the appellant or the applicant was misled by any order, practice or judgment of the High Court in ascertaining or computing the prescribed period may be sufficient cause within the meaning of this section.

5.1  Scope — the Three Exclusions

  • No suits: s. 5 applies only to appeals and applications — a suit filed late cannot be condoned; it stands dismissed under s. 3.
  • No execution: applications under O.XXI CPC (execution proceedings) are expressly excluded.
  • Courts only: s. 5 empowers only a "court" — it has no application to quasi-judicial tribunals, labour courts, bodies acting as persona designata, or executive authorities (PYQ book; Settled Procedural Law).
  • Trigger: the "prescribed period" must be over; "prescribed period" means any period prescribed by any law in force (including special laws via s. 29(2)).
  • Explanation — misled by the High Court: an order, practice or judgment of the HC that misleads a litigant in computing time is itself sufficient cause.
  • Not a decree: an order allowing or rejecting condonation is not a decree; dismissal of an appeal as time-barred follows the rejection (ULS: Ratan Singh v. Vijaya Singh, AIR 2001 SC 279).

5.2  "Sufficient Cause" — the Working Definition

  • Not defined in the Act. It must mean a cause beyond the control of the party invoking the section; a cause which by due care and attention the party could have avoided is not sufficient cause — "nothing is bona fide unless it is done with due care and attention".
  • The extension rests on judicial discretion — "discretionary, not arbitrary"; no hard-and-fast rule; each case on its own facts; reasons must be recorded.
  • Proof of sufficient cause is a condition precedent to the exercise of discretion; even after cause is shown, condonation is not a matter of right (Ram Lal v. Rewa Coalfields; Lipok AO).
  • Liberal construction to advance substantial justice: N. Balakrishnan v. M. Krishnamurthy (AIR 1998 SC 3222) — the court must place itself in the position of the applicant and ask whether the delay resulted from the cause adduced.
  • The discretion "should not be defined or crystallised so as to convert a discretionary matter into a rigid rule of law" (New India Assurance v. Shanti Misra, (1975) 2 SCC 840).
  • The true guide is whether the applicant acted with reasonable diligence in prosecuting the appeal (Brij Indar Singh v. Kanshi Ram, AIR 1917 PC 156).

5.3  Ram Lal v. Rewa Coalfields Ltd., AIR 1962 SC 361 — the Three Canons

  1. It would be unreasonable to require a party to act on the first day; a party may file on any day within the period — so it is prima facie not required to explain conduct during the whole limitation period.
  2. "Within such period" means within the period ending with the last day — the party must show cause why it did not file on the last day, and thereafter explain the delay day by day from that last day to the actual filing.
  3. Even after sufficient cause is shown, condonation is not a matter of right — it remains a discretionary jurisdiction vested by s. 5.

The Evolution — Government as a Litigant: from Katiji's Liberality to Bherulal's Stick THE EXAM ARC

The single most testable storyline in Topic 1 — 1987 → 2020 in one narrative

6.1  Three Ages of Section 5

The doctrinal pendulum
1917–1987 · FOUNDATIONS
Brij Indar Singh (PC, 1917): reasonable diligence is the guide. Ram Lal (1962): day-by-day explanation. Katiji (1987): the liberal charter — substantial justice over technicality; State entitled to the same even-handed treatment; "step-motherly treatment" deprecated; impersonal machinery and file-pushing understood.
1988–2005 · CONSOLIDATED LIBERALITY
G. Ramegowda (1988): "a little play at the joints" for Government. Chandra Mani (1996): pragmatic, justice-oriented approach; State ≠ individual in decision-speed. Lipok AO (2005): 57 days condoned; missing file + departmental follow-up = sufficient cause; delay by length irrelevant — sufficiency is everything.
2012–2020 · THE STRICT TURN
Living Media (2012): modern technology has destroyed the bureaucratic excuse; condonation is an exception, not an anticipated benefit; law of limitation binds everybody including Government. Bherulal (2020): 663 days! — SLP dismissed as time-barred with ₹25,000 costs recoverable from the responsible officers; "certificate cases" deprecated; Katiji cannot be invoked mechanically.
How to reconcile them in an answerKatiji/Lipok and Living Media/Bherulal are not opposites. The liberal line governs short, non-deliberate delays where the State acted bona fide and with some diligence; the strict line governs inordinate, unexplained delays supported only by generic "red tape" assertions. Bherulal itself accepts that "some leeway is given for the Government inefficiencies" — but Katiji-era liberality cannot be claimed as of right in the age of email, e-filing and tracked files. State the length of delay, the specificity of the explanation, and the era of the authority relied upon.

R.B. Policies at Lloyd's v. Butler

(1949) 2 All ER 226  |  King's Bench Division (England)  |  Streatfeild, J.  |  Limitation Act, 1939 (UK), ss. 2(1), 3(1), 26
accrual of cause of actionunknown defendantstale demandsinnocent purchaseract of peaceconversion / detinue

Facts in Detail

  • R.B. Policies at Lloyd's (the plaintiffs, underwriters) owned a motor car, registration JD 6412. It was stolen from them by some person or persons unknown on 27 June 1940.
  • In January 1947 the car — now bearing registration ALN 765 — was found in the possession of Mr. Alfred Butler, having reached him through a line of intermediate purchasers during the previous seven years.
  • The plaintiffs issued a writ on 16 July 1947 claiming return of the car as wrongfully detained by the defendant.
  • Defence: the cause of action was barred by the Limitation Act, 1939 (UK), s. 2(1) — "no action shall be brought after the expiration of six years from the date on which the cause of action accrued"; the theft having occurred in June 1940, six years expired in June 1946, a year before the writ.
  • The plaintiffs were owners; Butler was an innocent purchaser for good consideration and in good faith, years after the theft. The preliminary issue: on what date did the cause of action accrue?

Issues

  1. On what date did the plaintiffs' cause of action accrue — the date of the theft (1940), or the date the car (and a suable defendant) was discovered (1947)?
  2. Does the fact that the thief was unknown and untraceable prevent the accrual of a cause of action against him?
  3. Could s. 26 of the 1939 Act (fraudulent concealment) or the principle of discovering the wrong postpone the running of time?
  4. What is the policy of a limitation statute in a contest between an owner and an innocent purchaser?

Arguments for the Plaintiffs (R.B. Policies at Lloyd's)

  • A cause of action requires a person capable of suing and a person who can be sued — the thief being unknown, no action could be brought in 1940, so the cause of action cannot have accrued then.
  • Only in January 1947 was there an identifiable defendant (Butler) in possession — limitation should run from discovery of the wrong and the wrongdoer.
  • It would be unjust to deprive the true owner of property merely because the thief concealed his identity.

Arguments for the Defendant (Butler)

  • The theft itself was a complete conversion the moment it occurred — every ingredient of the cause of action existed in June 1940.
  • S. 3(1) of the 1939 Act: subsequent conversions by the chain of purchasers are comprehended in the original accrual — no action lies after six years from the original cause of action.
  • S. 26 (fraudulent concealment) is a special, limited exception — and it postpones the running of time only until fraud is or could be discovered; it does not postpone accrual for a merely unknown thief.
  • The Act protects innocent purchasers against stale demands — that is its policy.

The Decision

Preliminary point decided in favour of the defendant: the claim was barred by limitation. The cause of action accrued in June 1940, when the car was stolen.

  • Accrual: "A cause of action cannot accrue unless there be a person in existence capable of suing and another person in existence who can be sued" — the thief was in existence, though not traceable: "It was, no doubt, a misfortune to the plaintiffs that they could not find a defendant whose name they could insert in a writ, but the fact remains that every other ingredient of the cause of action was present."
  • S. 26 is the sole postponement: it says time "shall not begin to run" until discovery of fraud — it does not say the cause of action accrues afresh on discovery; absent pleaded fraud, time runs from the theft notwithstanding ignorance of the thief's identity.
  • The watch illustration: "It would lead to appalling results if someone, having lost a watch and discovered it fifty or sixty years later in the possession of a wholly innocent person who had bought it many years previously, was able to bring an action for its recovery merely because he did not know who the thief was."
  • Policy — the twin principles: (i) "those who go to sleep on their claims should not be assisted by the Courts in recovering their property"; (ii) equally, "there shall be an end of these matters... protection against stale demands" — quoting Best, C.J. in A'Court v. Cross: the Act is "an act of peace"; "long dormant claims have often more cruelty than justice in them".
  • A claim made seven-eight years after the loss, against a perfectly innocent holder who gave value without knowing of the theft, "does not seem just" — the Act exists to prevent precisely such injustices.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. A cause of action accrues when all its ingredients exist — the existence (not the identity or traceability) of a wrongdoer suffices; ignorance of the defendant's name does not postpone accrual.
  2. Limitation statutes are acts of peace designed to bury stale demands, protect defendants from lost evidence, and shield innocent purchasers — dormancy is cruelty, not justice.
  3. Postponement of limitation is exceptional (fraudulent concealment-type provisions) and must be strictly construed — it delays the running of time, it does not re-create the cause of action.
  4. Owners must be diligent: he who sleeps on his claim forfeits the court's assistance — the creditor's remedy dies while the duty to be alert lives.

CASE 26 — DU CASE MATERIAL · SUB-TOPIC (a): SECTION 3

Union of India v. West Coast Paper Mills Ltd.

AIR 2004 SC 1596  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Three-Judge Bench: R.C. Lahoti, C.J.I., S.B. Sinha & S.H. Kapadia, JJ.  |  P.K. Kutty overruled
starting point of limitationdoctrine of mergerArt. 58 vs Art. 113special leave & finalityss. 14–15 exclusionliberal construction of s.3

Facts in Detail

  • The freight dispute: West Coast Paper Mills (respondent) transported goods by rail over the branch line Alnavar → Dandeli, paying a common freight rate based on weight. The Railways revised the rate w.e.f. 1-2-1964 — levying standard telescopic class rates on three times the inflated distance.
  • 18-4-1966: The Railway Rates Tribunal declared the levy unreasonable (complaint held: unjust, unreasonable, discriminatory).
  • Special leave: The Union of India appealed to the Supreme Court. Special leave was granted with a limited interim order: "The Railway may charge the usual rates without inflation of the distance, and the Respondent will give a Bank guarantee... for Rupees Two Lakhs to be renewed each year until the disposal of the appeal." The SLP was ultimately dismissed on 14-10-1970.
  • 5-1-1972: The mill filed a writ petition (W.P. No. 210/1972); the High Court declined jurisdiction on 29-10-1973, leaving it to an ordinary civil suit — parties free to raise all contentions.
  • 12-12-1973 & 18-4-1974: The mill filed two civil suits (later O.S. 38/1982 & 39/1982) for refund of excess freight for the periods 24-6-1963 to 1-2-1964 and 1-2-1964 to 18-4-1966, with interest.
  • The limitation objection: UOI contended the cause of action arose with the Tribunal's declaration (18-4-1966) — under Art. 58 (declaration suits: 3 years from when the right to sue first accrues), the suits (filed 1973-74) were hopelessly late; and since no stay of the Tribunal's judgment had been granted, the pendency before the SC did not stop the clock.
  • Trial Court & High Court: suits were within time — the mill was entitled to the benefit of ss. 14 and 15 (exclusion of time spent bona fide prosecuting the writ remedy). UOI appealed; a Division Bench doubted P.K. Kutty and referred the matter to a three-Judge Bench.

Issues

  1. From what date did limitation run for a suit for refund of excess freight — the Tribunal's declaration (18-4-1966) or the Supreme Court's final order (14-10-1970)?
  2. Did the absence of a stay of the Tribunal's judgment make it operative from 1966, so that the SLP's pendency did not matter?
  3. Which article governed — Art. 58 (declaration suits, "right to sue first accrues") or Art. 113 (residuary, "right to sue accrues")?
  4. Did the doctrine of merger apply to the Tribunal's decision once special leave was granted — and was P.K. Kutty, which ignored merger, good law?
  5. Were the periods of the writ petition excludable under ss. 14–15?

Arguments for the Union of India (appellant)

  • The cause of action arose on the Tribunal's declaration (18-4-1966); under Art. 58 the three-year clock then began; suits of 1973–74 were 4–5 years late.
  • No stay of the Tribunal's judgment was ever granted by the SC — the interim order was only partial — so the judgment was operative and enforceable, and pendency of the SLP could not exclude time.
  • Under s. 46A, Indian Railways Act, the Tribunal's judgment was final; the refund claim stood on it and could not be deferred.
  • Reliance: Juscurn Boid (PC), Maqbul Ahmad (PC), P.K. Kutty (SC), Mohinder Singh Jagdev (SC) — "once limitation starts running, it runs its full course".

Arguments for West Coast Paper Mills (respondent)

  • Art. 113 (residuary), not Art. 58, governed — this was a suit for refund of money, not for a declaration; the declaration had already been obtained.
  • Once special leave was granted and an interim order passed, the Tribunal's judgment was "in jeopardy" — its finality was gone; correctness stood "wide open".
  • Doctrine of merger (Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala, (2000) 6 SCC 359): the Tribunal's order merged in the SC's order; limitation for consequential relief runs from the appellate order.
  • In any event, the writ interlude was excludable under ss. 14–15 — a bona fide prosecution of a remedy before the appropriate forum.

The Decision — Three-Judge Bench (S.B. Sinha, J. for the Bench)

  • Tribunal's powers were declaratory only: under the Railways Act "the Tribunal was only entitled to make a declaration... It did not have any jurisdiction to execute its own order" — a suit was the execution path.
  • s. 46A could not exclude Art. 136: finality before the Tribunal did not touch the Supreme Court's special appellate power; once special leave was granted, "the correctness or otherwise of the judgment of the Tribunal becomes wide open... In such an event the correctness of the judgment is in jeopardy."
  • Merger applies to tribunals as to courts — following the Constitution Bench line (Mohammad Nooh — Vivian Bose, J.: justice "in a common sense liberal way... broadbased on human values rather than... hair-splitting technicalities"; S.S. Rathore — the court/tribunal distinction "is one without any legal justification"): "an appeal is a continuation of the suit", the decree becomes executable only when finally disposed of by the last court.
  • Starting point = the SC's order (14-10-1970); the writ period was additionally excludable under ss. 14–15 — the suits were within limitation. Trial Court and High Court affirmed; nothing to differ.
  • Art. 58 vs Art. 113 distinction: Art. 58 counts three years from when the right to sue first accrues; Art. 113 counts from when the right to sue accrues — "the right to sue may accrue to a suitor... at different points of time", the last determination fixing the date. A refund suit needs a finally determined right; the mill did not sue for a declaration at all.
  • Overruled P.K. Kutty — it "does not lay down the law correctly": merger and the Constitution Bench precedents were never argued or noticed there. Mohinder Singh Jagdev distinguished on facts; Maqbul Ahmad and Juscurn Boid turned on peculiar facts.
  • The liberal gloss on s. 3 itself: "Despite the rigours of Section 3 of the Limitation Act, 1963, the provisions thereof are required to be construed in a broad based and liberal manner."
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. Where an order is subjected to appeal (including special leave under Art. 136), its finality is in jeopardy; by the doctrine of merger the appellate order becomes the sole operative order — limitation for consequential relief (refund suit) runs from the date of the last order, not the first.
  2. Stay is not the touchstone of jeopardy: the absence of a formal stay does not restart finality once the superior court has entertained the challenge and applied its mind to merits.
  3. Art. 58 ≠ Art. 113: "first accrues" (first accrual of right to sue) vs "accrues" (accrual, including the final determination) — classification of the article decides many limitation problems.
  4. Time spent bona fide prosecuting a remedy in the appropriate forum (including a writ petition wrongly but genuinely chosen) is excluded under ss. 14–15.
  5. Section 3's rigour is real but its provisions are construed broadly and liberally in aid of justice.

Punjab National Bank v. Surendra Prasad Sinha

AIR 1992 SC 1815  |  Supreme Court of India  |  K. Ramaswamy, J.  |  Quashing of private complaint u/ss 409, 109/114 IPC
s.3 bars remedy not righttime-barred debt still dueadjustment against securityguarantor's liabilitycriminal breach of trustabuse of process

Facts in Detail

  • 5-5-1984: PNB's Katni branch lent ₹15,000 to one Sriman Narain Dubey. The respondent — an Advocate and Standing Counsel for the Bank itself — and his wife Annapoorna stood guarantors, executed a security bond and handed over a Fixed Deposit Receipt of ₹24,000 (F.D. A/c No. 77/83, maturing 1-11-1988; value at maturity ₹41,292).
  • The principal debtor defaulted. At maturity, the Branch Manager adjusted ₹27,037.60 (the debt due as on December 1988) from the FDR and credited the balance ₹14,254.40 to the respondent's Savings Bank Account (No. 3763), intimated to him on 14-1-1989.
  • The complaint: the respondent filed a private complaint (C.C. No. 933 of 1991) before the Additional Chief Judicial Magistrate, Katni, against the Bank, its Chairman, Managing Director and four officers, alleging offences under s. 409 IPC (criminal breach of trust by banker/merchant) and ss. 109/114 IPC (abetment) — the Bank had "criminally embezzled" the FDR amount.
  • His theory: the principal debt became barred by limitation on 5-5-1987 (three years from the loan); the guarantor's liability being co-extensive with the debtor's, it too stood "extinguished" on that date; the Bank, with a "dishonest interest to save itself from the financial obligation", neglected to recover from the debtor in time and then converted the entrusted FDR money to its own use — in violation of his specific direction to credit the entire ₹41,292.
  • The Magistrate mechanically issued process against all the appellants; the High Court declined to quash, finding the Bank had acted "prima facie high-handedly". The Bank appealed to the Supreme Court. The respondent (served 29-7-1991) neither appeared nor was represented.

The Security Bond — the contract that decided the case

  • "We confirm having handed over to you by way of security... F.D. Account No. 77/83 dated November 1, 1983 for Rs 24,000..." — "the F.D.R. will continue to remain with the bank as security here."
  • "The amount due and other charges, if any, be adjusted and appropriated by you from the proceeds of the said F.D.R. at any time before, or on its maturity at your discretion, unless the loan is otherwise fully adjusted from the dues on demand in writing made by you..."
  • "We give the bank right to credit the balance to our savings bank account... and adjust the amount due from the borrowers out of the same."
  • "We authorise you... the F.D.R. pledged as security... shall also be security including the surplus proceeds thereof for any other liability... and the bank shall be entitled to retain/realise/utilise/appropriate the same without reference to us."

Issues

  1. Did the bar of limitation under s. 3 extinguish the debt (and the co-extensive guarantor's liability), so that the Bank's appropriation from the FDR became criminal misappropriation?
  2. Is a creditor entitled to adjust a time-barred debt out of a security in his possession, under the contract?
  3. Did the facts make out even a prima facie case of criminal breach of trust (ss. 405/409 IPC) — "entrustment" and "dishonest conversion"?
  4. Was the Magistrate right to issue process against the Bank's senior-most officers, and the High Court right to refuse to quash?

Arguments for PNB (appellants)

  • s. 3 bars only the remedy, not the right: the debt subsisted despite limitation; the Bank was not obliged to sue at all.
  • The security bond expressly authorised appropriation from the FDR at maturity "at your discretion" and "without reference to us" — the Bank acted precisely as the contract permitted.
  • The FDR was entrusted as guarantor's security, not in the course of banking business — s. 409 IPC has no application; and action under contract negatives "dishonesty" (s. 405).
  • The complaint was a patent abuse of process to harass the Bank and its officers — the respondent was himself the Bank's Standing Counsel pursuing a personal vendetta.

Case for the Respondent (Sinha)

  • The loan became time-barred on 5-5-1987; the guarantor's co-extensive liability stood extinguished; the Bank's neglect to sue the debtor was with a dishonest interest to escape its own obligation.
  • Having allowed the claim to become barred, the Bank could not help itself to the entrusted FDR proceeds — that was embezzlement (s. 409) by the Bank and abetment (ss. 109/114) by its officers.
  • His direction that the entire ₹41,292 be credited was disobeyed; only ₹14,254.40 was credited despite repeated demands.
  • The High Court had found the Bank's conduct "prima facie high-handed"; the Magistrate's process could not be interfered with at that stage.

The Decision

Appeal allowed; complaint quashed. K. Ramaswamy, J. held the complaint "eloquently manifest on its face a clear abuse of the process of the court to harass the appellants".

  • The maxim applied: "The rules of limitation are not meant to destroy the rights of the parties. Section 3... only bars the remedy, but does not destroy the right which the remedy relates to. The right to the debt continues to exist notwithstanding the remedy is barred by limitation." The only exception: where the right itself is destroyed — s. 27 (possession suits).
  • Time-barred debt does not cease to exist: "It is not obligatory to file a suit to recover the debt." A creditor may adjust a time-barred debt from payments, and — "when he is in possession of an adequate security" — may adjust the debt from that security. The Bank's appropriation was per the contract; the balance was duly credited.
  • No criminal breach of trust: "Action in terms of the contract expressly or implied is a negation of criminal breach of trust defined in Section 405 and punishable under Section 409 IPC. It is neither dishonest, nor misappropriation." Moreover the FDR was entrusted in the capacity of guarantor, not in the course of the Bank's business as banker.
  • No prima facie case at all: "The complaint does not make out any case much less prima facie case, a condition precedent to set criminal law in motion." The Magistrate issued process "obviously, in a mechanical manner"; the High Court committed "grave error" in refusing to quash.
  • Process as oppression: "Judicial process should not be an instrument of oppression or needless harassment." Impleading the Chairman and MD by name and a host of officers = "wreak[ing] personal vengeance"; criminal justice exists for "vindication of majesty of justice", not private vendetta.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. S. 3 bars the remedy, not the right (s. 27 the sole statutory exception) — a time-barred debt subsists, is still "due", and may be satisfied, set off, or adjusted against a security in the creditor's possession in terms of the contract.
  2. Non-suiting a debtor within limitation is a business misfortune, not a crime: mere failure to sue before the bar cannot convert lawful contractual appropriation into "dishonest" misappropriation under ss. 405/409 IPC.
  3. Entrustment under s. 409 must be in the course of business as banker/merchant — a FDR pledged by a guarantor is outside it.
  4. Criminal process must not become an instrument of oppression: no process without a prima facie case; impleading senior officers without basis is harassment; quashing is the proper cure for complaint-vendettas.

Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Mst. Katiji

AIR 1987 SC 1353  |  (1987) 2 SCC 107  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Thommen & Thakkar, JJ. (Thakkar, J.)
sufficient causeliberal constructionState as litigantequality before lawsubstantial justice4 days' delay

Facts in Detail

  • The State of Jammu & Kashmir filed an appeal against a decision enhancing compensation for land acquired for a public purpose — an upward revision of nearly 800% (from ₹1,000 per kanal to ₹8,000 per kanal), involving roughly ₹14 lakhs and raising important questions on principles of valuation.
  • The appeal was four days beyond limitation; the accompanying application for condonation of delay was rejected, and the appeal was dismissed as time-barred by the High Court.
  • The State appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.
The opening of the judgment (Thakkar, J.) — a classic"To condone or not to condone, is not the only question. Whether or not to apply the same standard in applying the 'sufficient cause' test to all the litigants regardless of their personality in the said context is another."

Issues

  1. Should the delay of four days in filing the State's appeal be condoned under s. 5 — was there "sufficient cause"?
  2. Should the same standard of the "sufficient cause" test apply to all litigants regardless of personality — does it matter that the applicant is the State?

Case for the State (appellant)

  • The delay of four days was neither deliberate nor mala fide — caused in the ordinary course of governmental file movement; the merits of a ₹14-lakh, 800%-enhancement valuation challenge deserved a hearing.
  • "Sufficient cause" must receive a justice-oriented, liberal construction; refusing condonation throws out a meritorious cause at the threshold.
  • Equality before law (Art. 14) demands the same treatment for the State as for any private litigant — the HC's stricter approach to a State applicant was impermissible.

Case for Katiji (respondent)

  • "Every day's delay must be explained" — the State had shown no cause for each of the four days, only a general plea of official procedure.
  • The decree-holder's rights had crystallised; the law of limitation binds the Government equally, and condonation would reward governmental lethargy at the cost of a poor landowner.
  • A liberal approach for the State would create a separate, privileged limitation regime for the biggest litigant of all.

The Decision & the Six Principles

Appeal allowed; the HC's order set aside; matter remitted to the High Court for disposal on merits. The Court held that the legislature conferred the s. 5 power "to enable the Courts to do substantial justice to parties by disposing of matters on merits", and that "the expression 'sufficient cause' employed by the legislature is adequately elastic to enable the Courts to apply the law in a meaningful manner which subserves the ends of justice — that being the life-purpose for the existence of the institution of Courts."

The liberal approach rests on six numbered principles:

  1. Ordinarily a litigant does not stand to benefit by lodging an appeal late.
  2. Refusing to condone delay can result in a meritorious matter being thrown out at the very threshold and the cause of justice being defeated.
  3. As against this, when delay is condoned the highest that can happen is that a cause would be decided on merits after hearing the parties.
  4. "Every day's delay must be explained" does not mean a pedantic approach — "Why not every hour's delay, every second's delay? The doctrine must be applied in a rational, common-sense, pragmatic manner."
  5. When substantial justice and technical considerations are pitted against each other, substantial justice deserves to be preferred — "the other side cannot claim to have vested right in injustice being done because of a non-deliberate delay."
  6. There is no presumption that delay is occasioned deliberately, or on account of culpable negligence, or on account of mala fides. "A litigant does not stand to benefit by resorting to delay. In fact he runs a serious risk."
  • On the State: "The fact that it was the 'State' which was seeking condonation and not a private party was altogether irrelevant. The doctrine of equality before law demands that all litigants, including the State as litigant, are accorded the same treatment and the law is administered in an even-handed manner. There is no warrant for according step-motherly treatment when the 'State' is the applicant."
  • The bureaucratic insight: "experience shows that on account of an impersonal machinery (no one in charge of matter is directly hit or hurt by the judgment sought to be subjected to appeal) and the inherited bureaucratic methodology imbued with the note-making, file-pushing and passing-on-the-buck ethos, delay on its part is less difficult to understand though more difficult to approve... the State which represents the collective cause of the community does not deserve a litigant non grata status."
  • The credo: "It must be grasped that judiciary is respected not on account of its power to legalize injustice on technical grounds but because it is capable of removing injustice and is expected to do so." — Sufficient cause existed; the HC order dismissing the appeal as time-barred was set aside and the appeal remitted for decision on merits.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. "Sufficient cause" under s. 5 is adequately elastic and must receive a justice-oriented, liberal construction — the section exists to bring matters to merits, not to bury them on calendars.
  2. The "every day's delay must be explained" doctrine demands a rational, common-sense, pragmatic application, not pedantry.
  3. Substantial justice prevails over technicality; no party acquires a vested right in another's non-deliberate delay.
  4. Equality of all litigants — the State included: the same sufficient-cause standard, no step-motherly treatment; but the State's impersonal, file-driven machinery makes its delays "less difficult to understand though more difficult to approve".
  5. Short delays (here: 4 days) supported by an honest explanation should ordinarily be condoned and the appeal heard on merits.
Caveat for the examBherulal (2020) holds that Katiji's liberality belongs to "a period of time when technology had not advanced"; it is not a licence for inordinate delay. Pair the two cases in every answer on governmental delay (see Section 6).

State of Nagaland v. Lipok AO

(2005) 3 SCC 752  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Arijit Pasayat, J. & S.H. Kapadia, J.  |  Appeal from Gauhati High Court, Kohima Bench
57 days' delaymissing files.378(3) CrPC leavesufficiency > lengthchain of authoritieslegal cells

Facts in Detail

  • The prosecution story: on 29-5-1999, five accused police officials formed the escort party of a State Cabinet Minister at Dimapur, Nagaland. Their version: a Maruti Zen crossed the Minister's cavalcade and shouted at it; the Minister's PSO saw an occupant with a firearm; later the escort vehicle spotted the car, whose occupant opened fire; the police party fired back. The car was later found with one occupant dead; of the two injured survivors, one died in hospital, the other lost an arm. An FIR under ss. 302/307/326/34 IPC followed.
  • Acquittal (18-12-2002): the trial court (Additional Deputy Commissioner (Judicial), Dimapur) found the ballistic report established the bullets came from the accused's guns and that they had exceeded their powers ("misfeasance") — but the absence of the post-mortem report "vitally affected the prosecution case", self-defence (AK-47/M-22 fire) was made out, and the accused received the benefit of doubt.
  • The State's leave application: the State desired to appeal the acquittal — requiring special leave of the High Court under s. 378(3) CrPC. The chronology of officialdom: copy of the judgment received 15-1-2003; papers put up the same day before the DIG (Headquarters); forwarded next day to DIG (M&P); the entire file with the note-sheet went missing and could not be traced despite best efforts; traced on 15-3-2003 (on directions to reconstruct the file, it "suddenly appeared" in the DGP's office); put up to the ADG (Headquarters), who opined on 26-3-2003 that an appeal should be filed; a Special Public Prosecutor was appointed; when no appeal was filed, the Secretary (Law & Justice) chased the Additional Advocate General; the appeal was finally filed 14-5-2003 — 57 days beyond limitation.
  • High Court: refused condonation — "it is the duty of the litigant to file an appeal before the expiry of the limitation period"; the AAG's inaction despite instructions was not sufficient cause; missing records were not a valid ground; the department should have pursued the matter and made enquiries. The leave application fell with it. The State appealed.
  • Notably, police officials were themselves the accused, and the Court did not miss the possibility "that the mischief [was] played by some persons interested to help the accused colleagues" — the file's disappearance was not necessarily innocent.

Issues

  1. Whether the delay of 57 days in filing the leave application (with the appeal) was protected by "sufficient cause" under s. 5?
  2. What is the correct judicial approach to "sufficient cause" — length of delay versus sufficiency of explanation?
  3. Do Government departments deserve any latitude for their impersonal machinery — and how much?

Arguments for the State of Nagaland (appellant)

  • Every fact in the condonation application stood unrebutted — no denial of the missing file, the reconstruction efforts, the appointments and reminders; bona fide departmental action at every step.
  • The approach of the HC was contrary to the settled line of authority — Katiji, Chandra Mani, Ramegowda: sufficiency, not length, is the test; a pragmatic, justice-oriented view must govern.
  • The file's disappearance was plausibly the mischief of persons helping accused colleagues — the department should not suffer for sabotage.
  • The acquittal of police officials in a double-death shooting deserved appellate scrutiny in the public interest.

Case for Lipok AO (respondent)

  • It is the litigant's duty to file within limitation; instructions to the AAG were no excuse — the department should have pursued and enquired whether the appeal had actually been filed (the HC's reasoning).
  • Missing records are the State's own internal failure; acceptance would make every file-misplacement a ground for extension.
  • The acquittal rested on benefit of doubt and self-defence; nothing in the "sufficient cause" showing warranted disturbing it.

The respondent did not appear before the Supreme Court despite service.

The Decision — and the gallery of authorities marshalled

Appeal allowed; the 57 days' delay condoned; the High Court directed to grant leave and dispose of the appeal on merits (the SC itself directed grant of leave given the long passage of time). Pasayat, J. first laid the foundations, then applied them:

  • Condition precedent: "The proof of sufficient cause is a condition precedent for exercise of the extraordinary discretion vested in the court. What counts is not the length of the delay but the sufficiency of the cause, and shortness of the delay is one of the circumstances to be taken into account in using the discretion."
  • Liberal construction — N. Balakrishnan v. M. Krishnamurthy (AIR 1998 SC 3222): the court "has to go in the position of the person concerned" and ask whether the delay resulted from the cause adduced.
  • No hard-and-fast rules — New India Assurance v. Shanti Misra (1975): discretion under s. 5 must not be "crystallised into a rigid rule of law".
  • Reasonable diligence is the guide — Brij Indar Singh v. Kanshi Ram (PC 1917).
  • Bona fides must be presumed absent proof — Shakuntala Devi Jain v. Kuntal Kumari (3-Judge Bench): "unless want of bona fides... or negligence as would deprive a party of the protection of Section 5 is proved, the application must not be thrown out".
  • Counsel's mistake — Concord of India (negligence of counsel misleading a litigant — condoned); Lala Mata Din v. A. Narayanan: no general proposition that counsel's mistake is always sufficient — the question is whether it was bona fide or a device for an ulterior purpose.
  • Question of fact — State of Kerala v. E.K. Kuriyipe; reassessment under Art. 136 possible — Milavi Devi.
  • Grave miscarriage of justice as a ground — O.P. Kathpalia (3-Judge Bench).
  • Katiji recited in full — substantial justice, even-handed treatment, no step-motherly approach to the State, the "less difficult to understand though more difficult to approve" bureaucracy passage; Prabha v. Ram Parkash Kalra: no "injustice-oriented approach".
  • The State's special texture — G. Ramegowda (1988): Government decisions are "proverbially slow", encumbered by procedural red tape; "a certain amount of latitude is not impermissible"; those who bear the responsibility of Government must have "a little play at the joints"; if Government appeals are lost for such defaults "no person is individually affected, but what in the ultimate analysis suffers is public interest"; yet no special standards — only a realistic view of how Government works. Chandra Mani (1996) and Special Tehsildar v. K.V. Ayisumma (1996) endorsed; strict standards can produce "public mischief by skilful management of delay".
  • Institutional prescription: Government should constitute legal cells to sift appeals from settlements; the officer responsible for filing must be personally responsible for lapses; the State "cannot be put on the same footing as an individual" in decision-speed — the individual is "a person legally injured", the State "an impersonal machinery working through its officers or servants".
  • Result: on these facts the delay of 57 days "deserved condonation"; leave to appeal directed to be granted, the appeal to be heard on merits (no opinion expressed on its merits).
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. Sufficiency, not length: the inquiry under s. 5 is into the quality of the explanation; the number of days is only one circumstance weighing on discretion.
  2. S. 5 is construed liberally to advance substantial justice; delays are generally condonable where no gross negligence, deliberate inaction or lack of bona fides is imputable (Ramegowda formulation, adopted).
  3. Government cases: the same law, realistically applied — "a little play at the joints" for the slow wheels of the State; public interest, not individuals, suffers when State appeals default; legal cells and officer accountability are the systemic cure.
  4. Unrebutted, specific, official explanations (the missing file chronology) constitute sufficient cause; appellate courts may reassess condonation refusals under Art. 136.

State of Madhya Pradesh v. Bherulal

(2020) 10 SCC 654  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Sanjay Kishan Kaul, J. & Dinesh Maheshwari, J.  |  663 days' delay — SLP dismissed with costs
663 daysLiving Media strictnesscertificate casescosts on officerscondonation = exceptionKatiji contextualised

Facts in Detail

  • The State of Madhya Pradesh filed a Special Leave Petition in the Supreme Court with a delay of 663 days — almost two years beyond limitation.
  • The entire explanation in the condonation application: the delay occurred "due to unavailability of the documents and the process of arranging the documents", and (in the next paragraph) — "bureaucratic process works, it is inadvertent that delay occurs". No dates, no movement-chart, no officer-wise responsibility — only a generic apology.
  • The Bench (Sanjay Kishan Kaul, J.) found itself "constrained to pen down a detailed order" because "all our counseling to Government and Government authorities [has] fallen on deaf ears" — the Supreme Court "cannot be a place for the Governments to walk in when they choose, ignoring the period of limitation prescribed".
  • The Court had earlier suggested that if the Government's machinery was "so inefficient and incapable", the remedy might be legislative — a longer limitation period for Government authorities; but "that is not so": "Till the Statute subsists, the appeals/petitions have to be filed as per the Statutes prescribed."

Issues

  1. Whether a delay of 663 days, explained only by "unavailability of documents" and generic references to bureaucratic process, constitutes "sufficient cause" under s. 5?
  2. Whether the liberal Katiji-era approach to governmental delay still holds in the age of modern technology?
  3. What consequences can the Court visit on State machinery that treats limitation casually and uses the Supreme Court for "certificates"?

Case for the State of MP (petitioner)

  • The delay was inadvertent — born of the bureaucratic process of collecting and arranging documents, not of deliberate inaction or mala fides.
  • The matter had some merit — a meritorious case ought not to be shut out for delay alone.
  • The line of authority from Katiji through Lipok AO requires a liberal, justice-oriented approach to the State's delays.

The Court's answer (no appearance needed to state it)

  • Some leeway exists for Government inefficiencies — but the authorities "keep on relying on judicial pronouncements for a period of time when technology had not advanced and a greater leeway was given" (Katiji contextualised).
  • Merit is no answer: "If a case is good on merits, it will succeed in any case. It is really a bar of limitation which can even shut out good cases."
  • The application disclosed "the casual manner" of drafting — no plausible, acceptable, officer-wise explanation; Living Media (2012) had already warned: red-tape explanations shall not be accepted; condonation is an exception, not an anticipated benefit.

The Decision — the Living Media charter applied, with teeth

Office of the Chief Post Master General v. Living Media India Ltd., (2012) 3 SCC 563 (quoted at length)"...the claim on account of impersonal machinery and inherited bureaucratic methodology of making several notes cannot be accepted in view of the modern technologies being used and available. The law of limitation undoubtedly binds everybody including the Government. ... it is the right time to inform all the government bodies, their agencies and instrumentalities that unless they have reasonable and acceptable explanation for the delay and there was bona fide effort, there is no need to accept the usual explanation that the file was kept pending for several months/years due to considerable degree of procedural red-tape... The government departments are under a special obligation to ensure that they perform their duties with diligence and commitment. Condonation of delay is an exception and should not be used as an anticipated benefit for government departments. The law shelters everyone under the same light and should not be swirled for the benefit of a few." — and Bherulal adds: "Eight years hence the judgment is still unheeded!"
  • The "preposterous proposition" rejected: that merit alone earns condonation — limitation "can even shut out good cases"; the court's power to condone remains, but must be exercised on cause, not on merits.
  • "Certificate cases" exposed: governments file hopelessly time-barred SLPs "to obtain a certificate of dismissal from the Supreme Court to put a quietus to the issue" — to complete the formality and "save the skin of officers who may be at default"; the Court "strongly deprecated" the practice; "in none of the cases any action is taken against the officers who sit on the files and do nothing"; counsel even addressed merits without touching limitation until the Bench pointed it out.
  • The signal: State authorities "must pay for wastage of judicial time which has its own value" — such costs "can be recovered from the officers responsible".
  • The order: looking to the period of delay (663 days) and the casual drafting — costs of ₹25,000 on the petitioner State, payable within four weeks to the Mediation and Conciliation Project Committee, recovered from the officers responsible for the delay with a certificate of recovery filed; SLP dismissed as time-barred; non-compliance would invite contempt proceedings against the Chief Secretary; a copy of the order placed before the Chief Secretary, MP.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. The law of limitation binds everybody, including the Government — condonation under s. 5 is an exception, never an anticipated benefit for government departments.
  2. Generic pleas — "unavailability of documents", "bureaucratic process", "procedural red-tape" — are not reasonable and acceptable explanations in the era of modern technology (Living Media applied).
  3. Merit does not purchase time: limitation can shut out even good cases; the court may condone only on sufficient cause, not on the strength of the case.
  4. "Certificate cases" — time-barred SLPs filed to obtain a dismissal certificate and shield defaulting officers — are an abuse of the Supreme Court's process; the officers, not the public exchequer silently, must bear the consequences: costs recoverable from the officers responsible, with compliance policed through the Chief Secretary.
  5. Katiji's liberality survives only for short, honestly explained, non-deliberate delays; Bherulal is the modern counterweight every answer on State delay must cite.

PYQ — 2019
Q. 6/2019 — "Admittedly there was widespread strike by non-gazetted officers of the State between 5th September to 13th October, 1980. Various offices including those of the Deputy Commissioner as well as majority of Courts were not functioning properly, because of strike. Though the strike was called off on 13th October, 1980, it took some time before the employees came back to service and normally returned. State filed an appeal on 15th October, 1980. Period of limitation had expired on 12th October, 1980. An application was filed for condonation of delay on the ground that there was a general strike and the work in the offices was disrupted. How would you decide?"
MODEL ANSWER — CONDONATION OF DELAY (SECTION 5)

The provision. Section 5: any appeal or application (other than an application under O.XXI CPC) may be admitted after the prescribed period if the appellant/applicant satisfies the court that he had sufficient cause for not preferring it within time. Here the proceeding is an appeal by the State — squarely within s. 5 (a late suit could not have been saved).

The delay is of 3 days (limitation expired 12-10-1980; appeal filed 15-10-1980). Meaning of sufficient cause: not defined in the Act; it means a cause beyond the party's control; a cause avoidable by due care and attention is not sufficient; the discretion is judicial, not arbitrary, exercised case-by-case with reasons; proof of sufficient cause is a condition precedent. Even when shown, condonation is not a matter of right (Ram Lal v. Rewa Coalfields, AIR 1962 SC 361).

The strike as sufficient cause. A general strike paralysing the offices of the Deputy Commissioner and the majority of courts is an event outside the State's control — akin to the court itself being closed. It prevented the preparation, processing and filing of the appeal through no deliberate inaction or negligence of the State. The explanation is bona fide, specific and co-extensive with the delay. Under the settled approach:

  • Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Katiji (AIR 1987 SC 1353): "sufficient cause" is adequately elastic; ordinarily a litigant gains nothing by late filing; refusal may throw out a meritorious cause at the threshold; every day's delay need not be explained pedantically — a rational, common-sense, pragmatic manner; substantial justice preferred over technicality; no presumption of deliberateness or mala fides.
  • State of Haryana v. Chandra Mani (AIR 1996 SC 1623): for Government appeals, a pragmatism in justice-oriented approach rather than "technical detection of sufficient cause for explaining every day's delay"; the State's impersonal machinery cannot decide as fast as an injured individual.
  • State of Nagaland v. Lipok AO (2005) 3 SCC 752: "what counts is not the length of the delay but the sufficiency of the cause" — a 57-day delay with an unrebutted official explanation was condoned; short delays supported by honest explanations deserve condonation.

Distinction from s. 4: s. 4 (court closed) could not help directly — the period expired on 12-10-1980, which was not itself shown to be a day the court was closed for the whole of its working hours; the rescue must come from s. 5, and it can.

Decision. The application deserves to be allowed: there exists sufficient cause for the delay of 3 days born of a general strike that stalled the State's offices and the courts themselves. The delay is condoned and the appeal is to be heard on merits. (The same framework decides the 90-day State criminal appeal problem — Q.7/2017(a) below.)

PYQ — 2018
Q. 7/2018(a) — "Section 3 [Limitation Act] only bars the remedy and does not destroy the right to which the remedy relates. Do you agree with the statement? Explain with case laws."
MODEL ANSWER

Yes — the statement is the settled law, with one statutory exception. The law of limitation bars the remedy in a court of law once the prescribed period expires, but does not extinguish the right itself; a claim satisfied outside court after expiry of limitation is not illegal and cannot be recovered back.

The classic authority — Punjab National Bank v. Surendra Prasad Sinha (AIR 1992 SC 1815): "Section 3... only bars the remedy, but does not destroy the right which the remedy relates to. The right to the debt continues to exist notwithstanding the remedy is barred... The time-barred debt does not cease to exist by reason of Section 3. That right can be exercised in any other manner than by means of a suit." The Bank there adjusted the time-barred loan from the guarantor's FDR as per the security bond — lawful appropriation, not criminal breach of trust; the complaint was quashed as an abuse of process.

Supporting propositions: a time-barred debt is still deemed due (First National Bank v. Seth Santlal, AIR 1954 Punj 328); a creditor may adjust a time-barred debt from payments and from a security in his possession; it is not obligatory to sue.

The exception — s. 27: in a suit for possession of property, on determination of the limitation period the right itself is extinguished — the owner's title dies and the adverse possessor's matures (12 years, Art. 65). Hence, outside s. 27, remedy goes, right stays.

Policy frame for the answer: limitation statutes are statutes of repose — interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt; Settled limitation principles (dormant claims cruel; evidence lost; diligence expected); the Act does not create rights, only fixes enforcement windows.

Conclusion: the statement is correct, as explained in PNB — s. 3 kills the remedy and, except for possession suits under s. 27, leaves the right intact and enforceable by every lawful non-judicial means.

PYQ — 2018
Q. 7/2018(b) — "The expression 'sufficient cause' employed by the legislature is adequately elastic to enable the courts to apply the law in a meaningful manner which subserves the ends of justice. Explain the relevant section with case laws."
MODEL ANSWER

The section: s. 5 (appeals and applications, excluding O.XXI; court's satisfaction of sufficient cause; Explanation — misleading HC order/practice/judgment). The quoted words are from Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Katiji (AIR 1987 SC 1353), the fountainhead of the liberal construction.

Content of the elasticity: (i) proof of cause is a condition precedent, but no pedantry — "every day's delay must be explained" is applied rationally, common-sensibly, pragmatically ("Why not every hour's delay, every second's delay?"); (ii) substantial justice outweighs technicality — no vested right in another's non-deliberate delay; (iii) no presumption of deliberate delay, culpable negligence or mala fides; (iv) refusal may kill a meritorious cause at the threshold, while condonation's worst case is a decision on merits; (v) same standard for the State — no step-motherly treatment, its impersonal, file-pushing machinery being "less difficult to understand though more difficult to approve".

The gallery: N. Balakrishnan (AIR 1998 SC 3222 — liberal construction to advance substantial justice); Shanti Misra (1975 — discretion not to be crystallised); Brij Indar Singh (PC 1917 — reasonable diligence); Shakuntala Devi Jain (3J — bona fides presumed); Lala Mata Din / Concord of India (counsel's bona fide mistake); Ramegowda (1988 — "a little play at the joints" for Government); Chandra Mani (1996 — pragmatic approach); Lipok AO (2005 — 57 days condoned; sufficiency over length).

The modern boundary: Living Media (2012) and Bherulal (2020) 10 SCC 654 — the elasticity is not endless: inordinate delays explained by generic "red tape" fail in the technology era; condonation is an exception, not an anticipated benefit; 663 days' delay was refused with ₹25,000 costs recoverable from the officers responsible.

Conclusion: "sufficient cause" is a judicially elastic concept — generous to honest, short, explained delays; stern with inordinate, unexplained ones — always harnessed to the ends of substantial justice.

PYQ — 2017
Q. 7/2017(a) — "A State Government files a criminal appeal in the High Court against the acquittal of the accused person along with the application for condonation of delay of 90 days in filing the appeal. Decide whether the said delay can be condoned under section 5 of the Limitation Act on the basis of the decided cases."
MODEL ANSWER — THE LIPOK PATTERN

Framework. The State's appeal against acquittal requires leave/grant procedures (s. 378(3) CrPC) and falls to be governed, for its delay, by the s. 5 discipline as interpreted for State litigants. The question is not the bare length (90 days) but the sufficiency of the explanation.

Step 1 — the controlling cases. Katiji (AIR 1987 SC 1353): even-handed treatment of the State; elasticity of "sufficient cause"; substantial justice over technicality. Chandra Mani (AIR 1996 SC 1623): 109 days' delay of the Government condoned with the "pragmatism in justice-oriented approach" — the State's impersonal machinery and collective decision-making are relevant considerations. Lipok AO (2005) 3 SCC 752: a State's leave application 57 days late (missing police file, reconstructed records, SPP appointed, Secretary's follow-up) condoned — every fact unrebutted, bona fides undisputed.

Step 2 — the modern counterweight. Living Media (2012) 3 SCC 563 and Bherulal (2020) 10 SCC 654: the plea must be specific, plausible and acceptable; "the file was pending due to red tape" without more fails; condonation is an exception, not an anticipated benefit; costs may follow, recoverable from officers. Also Ram Charan (AIR 1964 SC 215): no special privilege for Government — Art. 14 parity.

Step 3 — application to the 90 days. The answer turns on the affidavit's content: (a) If the delay is explained officer-wise, date-wise — movement of the file, consultation with the Law Department, appointment of counsel, and the period genuinely consumed by these steps — the delay is condonable: 90 days sits between Chandra Mani's 109 and Ramegowda's 365+, both condoned on proper explanations. (b) If the application offers only "the appeal could not be filed in time due to administrative delays" — it must be refused post-Living Media/Bherulal, for red-tape is not a reasonable explanation in the technological era.

Decision. On the decided cases: the 90-day delay can be condoned under s. 5 provided the State makes out a specific, bona fide, unrebutted explanation of the causes beyond its control (the Katiji–Chandra Mani–Lipok line); a bare plea of departmental slow motion, however, invites refusal (the Living Media–Bherulal line) — with the possibility of costs on the State.

Quick Revision Sheet — One Last Look Before the Hall

Sections 3–5 and the six cases, compressed
The Sections in One Breath
s.3 — dismissal after the prescribed period, even if limitation is not pleaded; subject to ss.4–24; institution = plaint presented (pauper: application; winding-up: claim to liquidator); set-off/counter-claim = separate suits. s.4 — last day closed → file on re-opening day ("closed" = closed during any part of normal working hours). s.5 — appeals/applications only (never suits; never O.XXI; only "courts"); "sufficient cause"; Explanation — misled by HC order/practice/judgment.
The Vocabulary Trap
"Period of limitation" = raw Schedule period. "Prescribed period" = period as computed under the Act (after ss.4–24). s.3's bar + s.5's extension both operate on the prescribed period. Special laws: s.29(2) — s.3 applies; ss.4–24 apply unless expressly excluded. "Suit" excludes appeal and application. Art. 58: "right to sue first accrues" ≠ Art. 113: "right to sue accrues" (West Coast).
Case 25 — Butler (1949, KBD)
Car stolen 27-6-1940 by unknown thief; found with innocent purchaser Jan 1947; writ 16-7-1947. Held: cause of action accrued at the theft — every ingredient existed; unknown identity of thief ≠ no accrual; s.26 (fraud) only postpones the running, never re-creates accrual; "appalling results" otherwise (the watch after 60 years). Policy: act of peace; stale claims = cruelty; protect innocent purchasers; sleepers get no help.
Case 26 — West Coast (AIR 2004 SC 1596, 3J)
Freight declared unreasonable by Railway Rates Tribunal (18-4-1966) → SC special leave (interim: no inflated distance; ₹2L bank guarantee) → SLP dismissed 14-10-1970 → writ refused (29-10-1973) → refund suits 1973/74. Held: merger (Kunhayammed; Rathore — tribunals equal courts; Mohammad Nooh) → refund suit's limitation ran from SC's order, not the Tribunal's; stay irrelevant once correctness "in jeopardy"; Art. 113, not 58; writ period excluded (ss.14–15); suits in time; P.K. Kutty overruled; s.3 to be construed "broad based and liberal[ly]".
Case 27 — PNB (AIR 1992 SC 1815)
Loan ₹15,000 (5-5-1984); guarantor's FDR ₹24,000 → ₹41,292; debtor defaults; debt time-barred 5-5-1987; bank adjusts ₹27,037.60 at maturity, credits balance. Complaint u/ss 409/109/114 IPC. Held: s.3 bars remedy, not right (s.27 sole exception); time-barred debt still due; adjustment from security in creditor's possession = lawful contract performance = negation of s.405/409; FDR entrusted as guarantor, not in banking course; no prima facie case; complaint quashed — process is not for oppression/vendetta.
Case 28 — Katiji (AIR 1987 SC 1353)
₹14 lakh / 800% enhancement (₹1,000→₹8,000/kanal); State's appeal 4 days late; HC refused condonation. Held (Thakkar, J.): "To condone or not to condone is not the only question"; sufficient cause is "adequately elastic"; six principles (no benefit in delay; refusal kills merits; condonation's worst case = merits; no pedantry — why not every hour/second?; substantial justice > technicality; no presumption of deliberateness); State ≠ litigant non grata — equality, no step-motherly treatment; impersonal machinery, note-making/file-pushing/passing-the-buck — "less difficult to understand though more difficult to approve"; judiciary respected for removing injustice. Delay condoned; remitted to HC.
Case 29 — Lipok AO ((2005) 3 SCC 752)
Acquittal (18-12-2002) of police escort in shooting case (2 dead; post-mortem report missing → benefit of doubt); State's s.378(3) CrPC leave application 57 days late: copy 15-1-2003 → file missing → traced 15-3-2003 → ADG 26-3-2003 → SPP → Secretary chases Addl. AG → filed 14-5-2003. HC refused. Held (Pasayat, J.): proof of cause = condition precedent; sufficiency, not length; liberal construction (N. Balakrishnan); no rigid rules (Shanti Misra); reasonable diligence (Brij Indar Singh); State gets "a little play at the joints" (Ramegowda); public interest suffers when State appeals default; legal cells + officer accountability; 57 days condoned; leave directed.
Case 30 — Bherulal ((2020) 10 SCC 654)
SLP 663 days late; explanation: "unavailability of documents" + "bureaucratic process... inadvertent". Held (Kaul, J.): SC is not a place for governments "to walk in when they choose"; Katiji belonged to the pre-technology era; Living Media: red-tape explanations rejected — "condonation of delay is an exception and should not be used as an anticipated benefit"; "law shelters everyone under the same light"; merit is no answer — "a bar of limitation can even shut out good cases"; "certificate cases" to save officers' skin deprecated; ₹25,000 costs recoverable from the responsible officers, certificate of recovery to court, copy to Chief Secretary, contempt on default; SLP dismissed as time-barred.

Memory Hooks

  • "Dismiss–Deem–Don't-plead" — s.3: dismiss as time-barred even without the plea; deemed dates of institution in s.3(2).
  • "Last-day grace" — s.4 cares only about the day the period expires; s.5 cares about the days after it.
  • "A-A-A" — s.5 covers Appeals and Applications (not suits) made to a Acourt... minus O.XXI.
  • Case-number = delay-scale: Katiji 4 days → Lipok 57 → Chandra Mani 109 → Bherulal 663! The bigger the number, the harsher the Court.
  • Butler's watch: if the owner wins, a 60-year-old claim on an innocent buyer succeeds — "appalling results"; hence accrual at theft.
  • PNB's ledger: the right survived in the FDR — limitation killed the suit, not the security.
