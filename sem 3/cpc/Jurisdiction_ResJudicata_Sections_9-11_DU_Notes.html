Jurisdiction of Courts — Section 9 CPC

The general plenary jurisdiction of civil courts; suits of a civil nature; express and implied bars; who decides and how
Core Principle — Section 9
The civil courts of India possess a general (plenary) jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature, unless their cognizance is expressly or impliedly barred. The section is enacted in affirmative, mandatory terms (“the Courts shall … have jurisdiction”) and is presumed in favour of the civil court: exclusion of jurisdiction is never readily inferred (Secretary of State v. Mask & Co., AIR 1940 PC 105).

1.1  What “jurisdiction” means

The word “jurisdiction” has not been defined in the Code. It is derived from the Latin juris + dicto — “I speak by the law.” Stated simply, it is the power or authority of a court of law to hear and determine a cause or matter — the authority to entertain, deal with and decide a suit, action, petition or other proceeding presented in a formal way for its decision.

In Official Trustee v. Sachindra Nath, AIR 1969 SC 823, the Supreme Court held that before a court can be said to have jurisdiction it must (i) have jurisdiction to try the suit brought, and (ii) have the authority to pass the orders sought. It is not enough that it has some jurisdiction over the subject-matter; its jurisdiction must include the power to hear and decide the particular controversy between the parties. The limits of this authority are fixed by the statute, charter or commission under which the court is constituted.

Two things jurisdiction is NOT
1. Not conferred by consent. Consent can neither confer nor oust jurisdiction. A decree passed by a court without inherent jurisdiction is nullity (coram non judice), assailable even in execution or collateral proceedings, at any stage, even suo motu (Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan, AIR 1954 SC 340; A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak, (1988) 2 SCC 602). Only the legislature can create or enlarge jurisdiction.

2. Not lost by deciding wrongly. “A court has jurisdiction to decide wrong as well as right” (Lord Hobhouse, in Malkarjun v. Narhari, (1900) 27 IA 216 (PC)). An erroneous exercise of existing jurisdiction is curable in appeal/revision — not a nullity (Ittyavira Mathai Mathai v. Varkey Varkey, AIR 1964 SC 907 — even decreeing a time-barred suit is an error, not a want of jurisdiction).

Where two or more courts do have concurrent jurisdiction, parties may by agreement choose one exclusive forum among the competent ones (Hakam Singh v. Gammon (India) Ltd., AIR 1971 SC 740) — that is selection, not creation, of jurisdiction.

1.2  The section itself, with its two Explanations

Section 9 — Courts to try all civil suits unless barred
The Courts shall (subject to the provisions herein contained) have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature excepting suits of which their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred.

Explanation I. A suit in which the right to property or to an office is contested is a suit of a civil nature, notwithstanding that such right may depend entirely on the decision of questions as to religious rites or ceremonies.

Explanation II. It is immaterial whether or not any fees are attached to the office referred to in Explanation I, or whether or not such office is attached to a particular place.

Anatomy of s. 9 — four building blocks

  • “The Courts shall” — mandatory, not enabling. No court can refuse to entertain a suit merely because the claim is small or doubtful; jurisdiction is a matter of law, not of discretion.
  • “all suits of a civil nature” — the word “civil” is contrasted with “criminal”; “nature” stretches the section further — even suits not strictly “civil proceedings” but bearing the characteristics of affecting civil rights are covered (Most Rev. P.M.A. Metropolitan v. Moran Mar Marthoma, AIR 1995 SC 2001).
  • “excepting suits … expressly or impliedly barred” — the only limits: a statutory bar in terms, or one necessarily implied. “A suit for its maintainability requires no authority of law; it is enough that no statute bars it” (Abdul Waheed Khan v. Bhawani, AIR 1966 SC 1718; Ganga Bai v. Vijay Kumar, AIR 1974 SC 1126).
  • Explanations I–II — the test is the right to property or to an office, not the incidental question. A suit about a mahant’s right of worship is civil even if it turns on whether particular religious rites were correctly performed.

Suits of civil nature — the two lists (Takwani)

YES — suits of a civil nature
Rights to property · rights of worship · taking out religious processions · shares in offerings · damages for civil wrongs · specific performance / damages for breach of contract · specific reliefs · restitution of conjugal rights · dissolution of marriage · rent · accounts · franchise · hereditary offices · yajmanvritti · salaries and wrongful dismissal from service.
NO — not of civil nature

Explanation I in action: in Sinha Ramanuja v. Ranga Ramanuja, AIR 1961 SC 1720, disputes about the right to manage temple property and ceremonies were held cognizable by civil courts because a right to property and office was in substance contested.

1.3  Express and implied bars — when the civil court’s door is shut

Expressly barred

A suit is expressly barred when barred by an enactment for the time being in force (Mohd. Mahmood v. Tikam Das, AIR 1966 SC 2010). The Code itself creates bars: s. 11 (res judicata), s. 10 (stay of trial of later suit), s. 47 (questions relating to execution), s. 95, O. II r. 2, O. IX r. 9, O. XXII r. 11. External statutes may likewise bar — e.g. s. 170, Representation of the People Act, 1951: “No civil court shall have jurisdiction to question the legality of any action taken … in connection with an election.” Matters assigned to exclusive fora — Revenue Courts, Industrial, Election, Rent, Co-operative, Income-tax Tribunals, MACT — and domestic tribunals (Bar Council, Medical Council, University, Club) are excluded on the same footing.

Impliedly barred

Implied bar arises when a statute creates a special obligation and a special remedy: where an Act creates an obligation and enforces its performance in a specified manner, “that performance cannot be enforced in any other manner.” Two recognised grounds: (i) general principles of law — e.g. acts of State, actions against judges for acts done in their judicial capacity; (ii) public policy — a court will not countenance matters “injurious to and against the public weal” (Babu Ganesh Dutt v. Mungoo Ram) — suits to recover costs of a criminal prosecution, or upon a contract hit by s. 23 Contract Act, or to try political questions. E.g. State of Gujarat v. Babuji Mulaji, AIR 2007 Guj 27 — the Essential Commodities Act’s complete appeal/revision scheme bars a suit for injunction restraining interference with a kerosene licence.

The governing presumptions — quote these
(1) Presumption in favour of jurisdiction. “Every presumption should be made in favour of the jurisdiction of a civil court … exclusion should not be readily inferred unless the statute contains an express provision to that effect or leads to a necessary and inevitable implication of that nature” (Dhulabhai v. State of M.P., AIR 1969 SC 78).

(2) Strict construction of ouster. Ouster clauses are strictly construed; if there is doubt, the court leans to the interpretation that maintains jurisdiction (Mask & Co., AIR 1940 PC 105; M.P. Electricity Board v. Vijay Timber Co., (1997) 1 SCC 68).

(3) Substance over form. A plaintiff cannot, by clever drafting, invest jurisdiction the court does not possess — “one must have regard to the substance of the matter and not the form” (Bank of Baroda v. Mod Bhai, AIR 1985 SC 545).

Who decides the question of jurisdiction? The court itself, at the commencement of the inquiry: every court is “not only entitled but bound” to determine whether the matter before it falls within its jurisdiction (Bhatia Co-op. Housing Society v. D.C. Patel, AIR 1953 SC 16). The plaintiff chooses his forum at his peril: if the facts alleged do not make the suit cognizable by that class of court, the suit is dismissed; if only territorial or pecuniary limits are missed, the plaint is returned for presentation to the proper court (Ananti v. Chhannu, AIR 1930 All 193 (FB), approved in Topandas v. Gorakhram, AIR 1964 SC 1348).

Dhulabhai’s seven principles (AIR 1969 SC 78) — when is the civil court barred?

#Principle (Hidayatullah, C.J.)

Res Sub Judice — Stay of Suit — Section 10 CPC

One litigation at a time: staying the trial of a later suit that mirrors a pending earlier suit
Core Principle — Section 10
No court shall proceed with the trial of a suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties (or parties under whom they claim, litigating under the same title), where the earlier suit is pending in the same or another competent Indian court, a court beyond India established or continued by the Central Government, or the Supreme Court. The bar is to trial — never to institution.

2.1  The section and its scheme

Section 10 — Stay of suit (with the Explanation)
No Court shall proceed with the trial of any suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title where such suit is pending in the same or any other Court in India having jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed, or in any Court beyond the limits of India established or continued by the Central Government and having like jurisdiction, or before the Supreme Court.

Explanation. The pendency of a suit in a foreign Court does not preclude the Courts in India from trying a suit founded on the same cause of action.

Etymology and nature

Res = a thing or matter; sub judice = under judicial consideration. Res sub judice = a matter pending before a court. Section 10 is a rule of procedure, pure and simple — it does not affect the jurisdiction of the court to entertain the later suit, does not bar its institution, and creates no substantive right. The later suit must be stayed (not dismissed), and the stay order can be made at any stage.

Object

To prevent courts of concurrent jurisdiction from simultaneously trying two parallel suits and producing inconsistent findings on the same matter; to confine the plaintiff “to one litigation,” protecting defendants from multiplicity and vexation. The policy of the section gives advance effect to the rule of res judicata — the test of s. 10 is whether the decision in the previously instituted suit would operate as res judicata in the subsequent suit (S.P.A. Annamalay Chetty v. B.A. Thornhill, AIR 1931 PC 263).

The provisions of s. 10 are mandatory — no discretion is left with the court once the conditions are satisfied (Manohar Lal v. Seth Hiralal, AIR 1962 SC 527). But the section does not stop the court from deciding the later suit on a pure point of law without entering the triable issues (Pukhraj D. Jain v. Gopalakrishna, (2004) 7 SCC 251).

Six conditions (Takwani’s checklist)

  1. There must be two suits — one previously instituted and the other subsequently instituted.
  2. The matter in issue in the subsequent suit must be directly and substantially in issue in the previous suit (matters collaterally or incidentally in issue do not count).
  3. Both suits must be between the same parties or their representatives.
  4. The previously instituted suit must be pending — in the same court, or in any other court in India, or a court beyond India established/continued by the Central Government, or before the Supreme Court.
  5. The court in which the previous suit is pending must have jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed in the subsequent suit.
  6. The parties must be litigating under the same title in both suits.

Note the Explanation: a suit pending in a foreign court does not stay an Indian suit on the same cause of action — Indian courts retain their jurisdiction; foreign proceedings enjoy no parity under s. 10.

The Supreme Court held: the word “trial” in s. 10 must be read in the context of the summary procedure; in a summary suit the trial begins only after leave to defend is granted, so the court may lawfully proceed up to and including judgment on the summons for judgment. Res Judicata — Section 11 CPC

The rule of conclusiveness of judgments: a matter once finally decided between the parties is never tried again — actually, constructively, in suits, writs, arbitrations and executions
Core Principle — Section 11
No court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties (or parties under whom they claim, litigating under the same title), in a court competent to try the subsequent suit, and has been heard and finally decided by that court. The doctrine rests on public policy: interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium — there must be an end to litigation.

3.1  The section and its eight Explanations

Section 11 — Res judicata (main provision)
No Court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim, litigating under the same title, in a Court competent to try such subsequent suit or the suit in which such issue has been subsequently raised, and has been heard and finally decided by such Court.
ExplanationDeeming rule
Expl. I“Former suit” = a suit decided prior to the suit in question, whether or not instituted prior thereto — a later-instituted but earlier-decided suit can be the “former suit.”
Expl. IICompetence of the former court is determined irrespective of any right of appeal from its decision.
Expl. IIIThe matter must in the former suit have been alleged by one party and either denied or admitted, expressly or impliedly, by the other — the “actually in issue” rule.
Expl. IVAny matter which might and ought to have been made a ground of defence or attack in the former suit is deemed to have been a matter directly and substantially in issue in such suit — constructive res judicata.
Expl. VAny relief claimed in the plaint and not expressly granted by the decree is deemed to have been refused.
Expl. VIWhere persons litigate bona fide in respect of a public right or a private right claimed in common for themselves and others, all persons interested are deemed to claim under those litigants.
Expl. VIISection 11 applies to proceedings for execution of a decree; “suit / issue / former suit” read as “execution proceeding / question arising therein / former execution proceeding” (inserted by the 1976 Amendment).
Expl. VIIIAn issue heard and finally decided by a court of limited jurisdiction, competent to decide that issue, operates as res judicata in a later suit even though that court could not try the later suit (also inserted 1976).

The doctrine behind the section

Three maxims (write them in every answer)
1. Nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa — no man should be vexed twice for the same cause (private justice).

2. Interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium — it is in the interest of the State that there be an end to litigation (public policy).

3. Res judicata pro veritate accipitur — a judicial decision must be accepted as correct.

History: known to Hindu law as Purva Nyaya (the rule of the former judgment), recognised in Roman law (“one suit and one decision is enough for any single dispute” — exceptio rei judicatae), and received into English law — classically stated by Sir William de Grey in the Duchess of Kingston’s case: a judgment of a court of concurrent jurisdiction, directly upon the point, is a bar, or as evidence conclusive, between the same parties upon the same matter directly in question in another court. The doctrine is founded on justice, equity and good conscience, and is a rule of universal law pervading every well-regulated system of jurisprudence.

The classic exposition — Das Gupta, J., in Satyadhyan Ghosal v. Deorajin Debi, AIR 1960 SC 941:

Quote bank
“The principle of res judicata is based on the need of giving a finality to judicial decisions. What it says is that once a res is judicata, it shall not be adjudged again. Primarily it applies as between past litigation and future litigation. … neither party will be allowed in a future suit or proceeding between the same parties to canvass the matter again.”

Public policy — Chandrachud, J., in Lal Chand v. Radha Krishan, AIR 1977 SC 789: the principle “is conceived in larger public interest which requires that all litigation must, sooner than later, come to an end”; a party who has once succeeded on an issue should not be harassed by multiplicity of proceedings involving the determination of the same issue.

Rule of law — Daryao v. State of U.P., AIR 1961 SC 1457 (Constitution Bench, Gajendragadkar, J.): “the binding character of judgments pronounced by courts of competent jurisdiction is itself an essential part of the rule of law.” A writ petition under Art. 226 dismissed on merits bars a fresh petition under Art. 32 for the same relief.

3.2  Conditions of application (the DU checklist)

  1. Two suits — a former suit and a subsequent suit or issue (Expl. I: former = earlier decided, not earlier instituted).
  2. The court deciding the former suit must be competent to try the subsequent suit or the suit in which the issue is raised (Expl. II & VIII — Expl. VIII relaxes this for issues decided by courts of limited jurisdiction competent to decide that issue).
  3. The matter directly and substantially in issue in both suits must be the same — actually (Expl. III) or constructively (Expl. IV); the bar extends to execution proceedings (Expl. VII).
  4. The matter must have been heard and finally decided in the former suit (Expl. V: relief claimed but not granted is deemed refused).
  5. Same parties, or parties under whom they or any of them claim (Expl. VI: representative litigation binds all interested).
  6. The parties must have litigated under the same title in the former suit.

A matter collaterally or incidentally in issue in the former suit does not bar: e.g. an ex parte eviction decree’s finding of ownership does not bar a later title suit by the sub-tenant, because title was not directly and substantially in issue there. Findings on all points on which the decision rests — even alternative ones — are each res judicata (Vithal Yeshwant v. Shikandarkhan, AIR 1963 SC 385).

Matter in issue — the classification that decides everything

“Matter in issue” = the rights litigated between the parties — the facts on which the right is claimed and the law applicable to its determination (Mathura Prasad v. Dossibai Jeejeebhoy, AIR 1971 SC 2355). It may be an issue of fact, law, or mixed law and fact. “Directly” means without intervention — in contradistinction to “collaterally or incidentally”; a fact is directly in issue if the judgment cannot stand without deciding it. “Substantially” = in effect though not in express terms — of real importance for the decision, determined from the pleadings, issues and judgment of the former suit.

Constructive res judicata (Expl. IV) — Somervell, L.J., in Greenhalgh v. Mallard: res judicata “covers issues or facts which are so clearly part of the subject-matter of the litigation and so clearly could have been raised that it would be an abuse of the process of the court to allow a new proceeding to be started in respect of them.” The Supreme Court in Workmen v. Board of Trustees, Cochin Port Trust, AIR 1978 SC 1283: a matter which might and ought to have been made a ground of defence or attack but was not, is deemed constructively in issue and taken as decided. And in Forward Construction Co. v. Prabhat Mandal, AIR 1986 SC 391: “might” conveys the possibility, “ought” the propriety, of joining all grounds — an alternative basis omitted in the first suit cannot sustain the second.

Constructive res judicata applies to writ petitions — the Daryao line

From reluctance to full application (the line of authority decided in Nawab Hussain)
Daryao (1961)
AIR 1961 SC 1457 — writ under Art. 226 dismissed on merits bars a fresh Art. 32 petition: general res judicata applies to writs (rule-of-law foundation).
Amalgamated Coalfields (1964)
AIR 1964 SC 1013 — earlier attack on coal-tax notices failed on some grounds, additional ground disallowed; later petition for later years on entirely distinct grounds: held, constructive res judicata is a “special and artificial form” and should not generally be applied to Art. 32/226 petitions.
Devilal Modi (1965)
AIR 1965 SC 1150 — Gajendragadkar, J., settles the law: constructive res judicata does apply to writ proceedings, for otherwise “a party can file as many writ petitions as he likes and take one or two points every time … opposed to considerations of public policy” and destructive of finality.
Gulabchand (1965)
AIR 1965 SC 1153 — Raghubar Dayal, J. (majority): s. 11 is not exhaustive; a decision of the High Court on merits after contest in a writ petition operates as res judicata in a later regular suit between the same parties; the nature of the former proceeding is immaterial.
Nawab Hussain (1977)
AIR 1977 SC 1680 (Case 4) — Shinghal, J., fuses the strands: the plea not taken in the writ petition but available there is barred by constructive res judicata in the later suit; the “left open” observation in Gulabchand was misunderstood by the High Court, since Devilal Modi had already settled the point.

Section 11 is NOT exhaustive

Section 11 in terms applies to suits; but the general doctrine travels beyond — to execution proceedings (now directly by Expl. VII; earlier by the general principle — Mohanlal Goenka v. Benoy Krishna, AIR 1953 SC 65), writ petitions (Daryao; Gulabchand; Nawab Hussain), arbitration, taxation, industrial adjudication, administrative orders, interim orders and even criminal proceedings. “Section 11 … is not exhaustive and the principle which motivates that section can be extended to cases which do not fall strictly within the letter of the law” (Lal Chand, AIR 1977 SC 789). Conversely, where the legislature has excluded the doctrine (e.g. Explanation VII’s pre-1976 regime, or special statutory exceptions), the section alone speaks: in L. Janakirama Iyer v. P.M. Nilakanta Iyer, AIR 1962 SC 135, it was conceded that in terms s. 11 could not apply to a representative suit, and the general doctrine could not be invoked either — s. 11 being the only channel of the plea in suits.

Res judicata between co-defendants / co-plaintiffs

Section 11 speaks of parties “under whom they claim” — the doctrine also runs between co-defendants and between co-plaintiffs, where three conditions are satisfied (Union of India v. S. Narasimhulu Naidu (2021 SC); classically Iftikhar Ahmed, AIR 1974 SC 749 (Case 3)):

  1. There was a conflict of interest between the co-defendants / co-plaintiffs inter se;
  2. It was necessary to decide that conflict in order to give the relief the plaintiff claimed; and
  3. The court actually decided the question.

The bar operates even where the earlier plaintiff’s suit was dismissed (Munni Bibi v. Tirloki Nath, AIR 1931 PC 114), and even against a party who did not contest, provided it is clearly proved that he had, or must be deemed to have had, notice that the relevant question was in issue and would be decided (Lord Simonds, Chandu Lal v. Khalilur Rahman, AIR 1950 PC 17). It does not apply to proforma defendants. The Sheoparsan Singh caution governs the whole field: the rule should be applied by reference to matter of substance within the limits allowed by law, not technical considerations of form (AIR 1916 PC 78).

Mixed question of law and fact. The question of res judicata is a mixed question of law and fact; the bar applies not only to the decision itself but to all facts and circumstances that were the necessary ground-work of that decision. Being mandatory (a plea touching the court’s jurisdiction to try), even a concession by counsel cannot bind a party once its conditions exist (Talluri Venkata v. Thadikonda Kotiswara, AIR 1937 PC 1) — yet, because it is procedural, the plea may be waived (Sheodan Singh v. Daryao Kunwar, AIR 1966 SC 1332; Medapati Surayya v. Tondapu Bala Gangadhara, AIR 1948 PC 3).

Order II, Rules 1 & 2 — The Suit Must Carry the Whole Claim

Frame of suit; the prohibition of splitting causes of action, claims and reliefs; O. II r. 2 vs s. 11; and the rule’s extension to writ petitions
Core Principle — Order II, Rule 2
Every suit must include the whole of the claim which the plaintiff is entitled to make in respect of the cause of action; a part may be relinquished only to bring the suit within a court’s jurisdiction; but whatever is omitted or intentionally relinquished is lost forever — and of several reliefs available on the same cause of action, any relief omitted (except with the leave of the court) can never be sued for afterwards.

4.1  The two rules in the DU syllabus

Order II, Rule 1 — Frame of suit
Every suit shall as far as practicable be framed so as to afford ground for final decision upon the subjects in dispute and to prevent further litigation concerning them.
Order II, Rule 2 — Suit to include the whole claim
(1) Every suit shall include the whole of the claim which the plaintiff is entitled to make in respect of the cause of action; but a plaintiff may relinquish any portion of his claim in order to bring the suit within the jurisdiction of any Court.
(2) Relinquishment of part of claim. Where a plaintiff omits to sue in respect of, or intentionally relinquishes, any portion of his claim, he shall not afterwards sue in respect of the portion so omitted or relinquished.
(3) Omission to sue for one of several reliefs. A person entitled to more than one relief in respect of the same cause of action may sue for all or any of such reliefs; but if he omits, except with the leave of the Court, to sue for all such reliefs, he shall not afterwards sue for any relief so omitted.

Object — the Privy Council’s words

In Naba Kumar v. Radhashyam, AIR 1931 PC 229, the Privy Council described the rule as aimed at “the vice of splitting a cause of action … the object of this salutary rule is doubtless to prevent multiplicity of suits.” The Supreme Court echoed the “cardinal principle that the defendant should not be vexed twice for the same cause” (Deva Ram v. Ishwar Chand, (1995) 6 SCC 733). The provision counteracts two evils: splitting up of claims and splitting up of remedies (Mohd. Khalil v. Mahbub Ali, AIR 1949 PC 78; Kewal Singh v. Lajwanti, (1980) 1 SCC 290).

Rule 1 is the policy statement — “as far as practicable” makes it directory, a guide to framing suits so that one suit ends one dispute (Takwani, citing Shankarlal v. Gangabisen, AIR 1972 Bom 326). Rule 2 is the sanction — penal and divestive in effect.

Three conditions before r. 2 bars a second suit (Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal, AIR 1964 SC 1810)

  1. The second suit must be in respect of the same cause of action as the previous suit;
  2. In respect of that cause of action the plaintiff was entitled to more than one relief (or to a larger claim); and
  3. Being so entitled, the plaintiff omitted — without the leave of the court — to sue for the relief/portion now claimed.

The three interrogatives (Kewal Singh; State of Maharashtra v. National Construction Co., (1996) 1 SCC 735)

  • Is the cause of action in the two suits identical?
  • Could the relief now claimed have been granted in the earlier suit on the pleadings made there?
  • Did the plaintiff omit to claim it in that suit?

Same cause of action — the governing tests. The cause of action must not be merely similar; it must be the same. “The rough test, although not a conclusive one, … is to see whether the same evidence will sustain both the suits” (Mohd. Khalil, PC). Mulla: the rule “does not require that when several causes of action arise from one transaction, the plaintiff should sue for all of them in one suit. What the rule lays down is that where there is one entire cause of action, the plaintiff cannot split the cause of action into parts so as to bring separate suits in respect of those parts.” A suit for specific performance bars a later suit for damages for the same breach; but its dismissal does not bar a suit for return of the consideration; an ejectment suit on the lease is no bar to a later suit on title.

Strict construction, early objection. The bar is “penal in nature and divestive in effect” and must be construed strictly — not lightly upheld (Gurbux Singh). The plea should be raised at the earliest opportunity and in any event at or before the settlement of issues (Rikabdas v. Deepak Jewellers, (1999) 6 SCC 40), and it must be established by the defendant by placing the earlier plaint on record — courts will not take it up suo motu.

4.2  Exceptions — when the later suit survives

Classic illustrations (adapted from the DU material & Takwani): (1) A lets a house to B at ₹1,200 a year; rent of 1905–1907 is due; A sues in 1908 for 1906 alone — he cannot afterwards sue for 1905 or 1907. (2) A lends ₹2,200, sues for ₹2,000 to catch court X’s jurisdiction — the balance ₹200 is gone. (3) A sues B for ₹200 and takes set-off of ₹200 out of ₹1,200 due to him, omitting to counter-claim the balance — the ₹1,000 is lost. (4) A suit for possession on tenancy dismissed because B is mortgagee-in-possession — a later suit for redemption is not barred (different relief on a different footing).

Introduction to the Commercial Courts Act, 2015

A specialist, time-bound channel carved out of the general civil jurisdiction — commercial disputes of specified value tried by dedicated courts and divisions
Core Principle — the Act of 2015
The Commercial Courts, Commercial Division and Commercial Appellate Division of High Courts Act, 2015 (Act 4 of 2016, deemed commenced 23 October 2015) constitutes specialist fora — Commercial Courts at district level, Commercial Divisions in High Courts with ordinary original civil jurisdiction, and Commercial Appellate Divisions — to try “commercial disputes” of a “specified value” (₹1 crore as enacted; ₹3 lakh since the 2018 Amendment) under a time-bound, amended CPC procedure, with mandatory pre-institution mediation where no urgent interim relief is sought.

5.1  Genesis and objects

Why the Act came. Commercial disputes in India were trapped in the ordinary civil track — decades of pendency, repeated adjournments, no cost consequences. The Ordinance of 23 October 2015 (promulgated to improve India’s ease-of-doing-business rankings and support the “Make in India” initiative) created the new regime; Parliament replaced it with the Act (assented 31 December 2015; Act 4 of 2016; s. 1(3) deems commencement from 23 October 2015). The Act extends to the whole of India (as enacted, except Jammu & Kashmir).

Objects: (i) speed — fixed timelines at every stage, argument-closure and judgment deadlines; (ii) specialisation — judges experienced in commercial disputes; (iii) case management — court-controlled calendars instead of party-driven adjournments; (iv) costs discipline — a rewritten s. 35 CPC making the unsuccessful party pay; (v) decongestion of the ordinary civil courts; (vi) promoting settlement culture via pre-institution mediation.

The 2018 Amendment (Commercial Courts (Amendment) Act, 2018) — (a) slashed the specified value from ₹1 crore to ₹3 lakh (s. 2(1)(i)); (b) permitted Commercial Courts at District Judge level even in territories where the High Court has ordinary original civil jurisdiction, with a notified pecuniary ceiling (proviso to s. 3(1); new s. 3(1A)); (c) introduced Chapter IIIA — s. 12A pre-institution mediation; (d) inserted summary judgment (Order XIIIA CPC) and other tightening amendments.

5.2  Specified value, mediation, appeals — the operative machinery

“Specified value” — s. 2(1)(i) + s. 12

The value of the subject-matter determined under s. 12, not less than ₹3 lakh (2018 Amendment; originally ₹1 crore) or such higher notified value. s. 12 fixes the computation: money claims — principal plus interest up to filing; movable/immovable property — market value at filing; intangible rights — the plaintiff’s estimated market value; counterclaims — the counterclaim’s value at its date. A plaint below the floor falls to the ordinary civil court. No appeal or revision lies from an order finding jurisdiction (s. 12(3)).

Pre-institution mediation — s. 12A (2018)

A plaintiff who does not seek urgent interim relief must first invoke pre-institution mediation before the authorities constituted under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (procedure under the Commercial Courts (PIMS) Rules, 2018). The process runs three months, extendable by two. A settlement has the status and effect of an arbitral award on agreed terms under s. 30(4) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — i.e. it is executable as a decree.

Appeals — ss. 13–14

Appeal from a Commercial Court/Division decree lies to the Commercial Appellate Division within 60 days (s. 13(1A)); only orders enumerated in Order XLIII CPC and appeals under s. 37 Arbitration Act are appealable interlocutorily; the Letters Patent Appeal is excluded; no civil revision lies against interlocutory orders, including on jurisdiction (s. 8) — such objections travel only with the appeal against the decree. The Appellate Division must endeavour to dispose of appeals within six months (s. 14). Pending cases stand transferred (s. 15).

5.3  Assessment — for the “critical evaluation” marks

Achievements

  • Specialisation and dedicated infrastructure; monthly public statistics of pendency (s. 17) — transparency.
  • Real procedural discipline: the 120-day WS cliff, disclosure-on-filing, CMH-driven calendars, judgment in 90 days — the litigation culture shifts from party-driven delay to court-driven management.
  • Costs with teeth (s. 35) and summary judgment (O. XIIIA) — early disposal of hopeless cases.
  • Mediation mainstreamed at the doorstep of commercial litigation (s. 12A).
  • Arbitration interface (s. 10): one specialist roof for s. 8/9/11/34/37 applications.

Critiques

  • The ₹3-lakh floor (2018) flooded the Commercial Courts with small commercial suits, straining infrastructure and judges (infrastructure and training depend on State governments — ss. 19–20).
  • Recruitment and PIM bottlenecks: shortage of trained mediators; criticism that mandatory PIM delays genuine urgent matters and adds a layer.
  • Timelines depend on judicial vacancies; without the 90-day/6-month culture becoming real, the Act risks remaining “CPC with new labels”.
  • Exclusion of revisional supervision over interlocutory orders (s. 8) can leave errors uncorrected till final appeal.
  • Definitional litigation at the gate — every defendant now fights on “commercial dispute” and “specified value” before the merits (see Ambalal Sarabhai, Borse Brothers).

CASE 1  ·  Jurisdiction of civil courts — DU case material, pp. 1–12
1
Gundaji Satwaji Shinde v. Ram Chandra Bhikaji Joshi
AIR 1979 SC 653 · (1979) 2 SCC 459 · (1979) 2 SCR 586 · Supreme Court of India · D.A. Desai, J. · Appeal by certificate from the Bombay High Court · DU Case Material (LB-302), pp. 1–12

FFacts

  • By a sale agreement dated 15 December 1965 (supplemented on 26 April 1966), the respondent Ram Chandra Bhikaji Joshi agreed to sell to the appellant Gundaji Satwaji Shinde land admeasuring 45 acres 5 gunthas of Survey No. 2, Mouje Dongaon, Taluka North Sholapur, for ₹42,000 — ₹5,000 earnest money and a further ₹5,000 paid on extension of time.
  • The appellant sued for specific performance (Special Civil Suit No. 39 of 1966). Defence: the land was agricultural land within the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948, and s. 63 of that Act barred its purchase by a non-agriculturist.
  • The plaintiff relied on Ext. 78 — a certificate of the Mamlatdar certifying that he was an agricultural labourer — and contended that whether a person is an “agriculturist” for the Tenancy Act is for the Mamlatdar to decide exclusively, the civil court having no jurisdiction over the question.
  • Trial court and Bombay High Court (First Appeal 117 of 1968): Ext. 78 invalid; the issue whether the plaintiff was an agriculturist was merely an incidental issue arising in a properly constituted suit for specific performance which the civil court itself could decide; suit dismissed. The plaintiff obtained a certificate of fitness and appealed to the Supreme Court.

IIssues

  1. Whether, in a suit for specific performance of a contract for sale of agricultural land — a suit otherwise within the civil court’s jurisdiction — the question whether the plaintiff is an agriculturist within the Tenancy Act could be decided by the civil court as an “incidental issue”.
  2. Whether the civil court was under an obligation to refer that issue to the Mamlatdar under ss. 70, 85 and 85-A of the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948.

AThe statutory framework the case turns on

Bombay Tenancy & Agricultural Lands Act, 1948 — the relevant provisions (as extracted in the judgment)
s. 2(2) “agriculturist” — a person personally cultivating land; s. 2(8) “person cultivating” — with exclusion of servants merely paid in wages and mortgagees in possession.
s. 63 — bar on transfer of agricultural land to a person who is not an agriculturist (the disqualification operates at the threshold).
s. 70(a) — the Mamlatdar shall decide “whether a person is an agriculturist” for the purposes of the Act.
s. 85 — the jurisdiction of the civil court is barred in respect of questions which the Mamlatdar / Tribunal / Manager / Collector / Maharashtra Revenue Tribunal / State Government is empowered to decide under the Act; the authority’s decision is final and not questionable in any civil or criminal court (s. 85(2)).
s. 85-A (inserted by Bombay Act XIII of 1956, i.e. after Dhondi Tukaram Mali, AIR 1954 Bom 100) — where in a suit before a civil court an issue arises which is required to be settled, decided or dealt with by the competent authority under the Act, the civil court shall stay the suit and refer the issue to that authority, and dispose of the suit in accordance with the decision on reference.

Arguments for Gundaji Shinde (appellant-plaintiff)

  • The question whether he was an agriculturist was, by force of ss. 70(a), 85 and 85-A, within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Mamlatdar; the civil court had no jurisdiction to decide it even as an incidental issue.
  • Ext. 78 showed he was an agricultural labourer; the disqualification of s. 63 was therefore crossed.
  • The courts below should have stayed the suit and referred the issue to the Mamlatdar instead of deciding it themselves.

Arguments for Ram Chandra Joshi (respondent-defendant)

  • A suit for specific performance of a contract for sale of land is cognizable by the civil court; every issue arising in it — principal or incidental — falls for decision by that court.
  • ss. 85/85-A should be read as operating only in proceedings under the Tenancy Act, not in suits under the general law (Contract Act, Transfer of Property Act): “the prohibition of the Tenancy Act alone cannot corrode the civil court’s jurisdiction in a suit on contract.”
  • Ext. 78 was invalid, and the plaintiff was in any event not an agriculturist — the suit was rightly dismissed.

DDecision — the Supreme Court’s reasoning

  • Starting principle (para 8): exclusion of civil jurisdiction is not readily inferred — it must be express or clearly implied (Secretary of State v. Mask & Co., AIR 1940 PC 105). But here the ouster is express: s. 85 in terms withdraws from civil courts the questions assigned by the Tenancy Act to its authorities, and s. 70(a) assigns to the Mamlatdar the question “whether a person is an agriculturist.”
  • Section 85-A’s design (paras 9–10): the section “comprehends civil suits which civil courts are competent to decide but takes note of the situation where upon a contest an issue may arise therein which would be required to be settled, decided or dealt with by the competent authority under the Tenancy Act” — the civil court is obliged “not to arrogate jurisdiction to itself … treating it as a subsidiary or incidental issue” but to refer it. Calling an issue “principal or subsidiary, substantial or incidental” is a camouflage: “by such camouflage … Civil Court cannot arrogate to itself jurisdiction which is statutorily ousted.”
  • The illustration (para 10): a suit on title for possession against an alleged trespasser is properly before the civil court; but if the defendant contends he is a tenant of agricultural land, s. 70(a)(ii) with ss. 85/85-A precludes the civil court from deciding that issue — each issue must still be determined to mould the final relief, so nomenclature is unhelpful.
  • The precedential line (paras 11–17-A): Trimbak Sopana Girme, AIR 1953 Bom 241 (Chagla C.J.) — status of protected tenant is for the revenue forum; Dhondi Tukaram Mali, AIR 1954 Bom 100 — approved, and the legislature implemented it by enacting s. 85-A; Bhimji Shanker Kulkarni v. Dundappa Vithappa, AIR 1966 SC 166 — “S. 85-A proceeds upon the assumption that though the Civil Court has otherwise jurisdiction to try a suit, it will have no jurisdiction to try an issue arising in the suit, if the issue is required to be settled, decided or dealt with by the Mamlatdar”; Ishverlal Thakorelal v. Motibhai Nagjibhai, AIR 1966 SC 459 — District Judge erred in dismissing the suit instead of referring; Jambu Rao Satappa v. Neminath Appayya, AIR 1968 SC 1358 — distinguished (there the invalidity under s. 84-C arises only after acquisition pursuant to a decree; s. 63’s disqualification is “at the threshold”, so the reference is inevitable); Musamiya Imam Haider Bax Razvi, AIR 1969 SC 439 — distinguished (past tenancy was not within s. 70(b); the Tribunal’s s. 68 jurisdiction was not argued); Noor Mohd. Khan v. Fakirappa, AIR 1978 SC 1217 — in pari materia provisions of the Mysore Land Reforms Act: reference “inevitable”, “no discretion left in the Civil Court”.
  • Held (paras 18–19): “Both on principle and on authority there is no escape from the conclusion that where in a suit properly constituted and cognizable by the Civil Court upon a contest an issue arises which is required to be settled, decided or dealt with by a competent authority under the Tenancy Act, the jurisdiction of the Civil Court … is not only ousted but the Civil Court is under a statutory obligation to refer the issue to the competent authority … and upon the reference being answered back, to dispose of the suit in accordance with the decision.” Whether the plaintiff is an agriculturist is within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Mamlatdar; the trial court and the High Court “were in error in clutching at a jurisdiction which did not vest in them” — appeal allowed on this ground.

PPrinciple

What Gundaji settles — five sentences
(1) The civil court’s plenary jurisdiction (s. 9 CPC) survives undiminished unless expressly or by necessary implication ousted — but an ouster may attach to a single issue even while the suit as a whole remains cognizable.
(2) Where a special statute creates a competent exclusive authority for a category of questions, the civil court cannot decide such a question even as an “incidental” issue — labels (principal/subsidiary) are a camouflage.
(3) A statutory duty to stay and refer (s. 85-A) displaces the ordinary rule that the court trying the suit tries all issues in it.
(4) The authority’s decision is binding (s. 85(2)); the suit is then disposed of in the light of that decision.
(5) A threshold statutory disqualification (s. 63) must be resolved before specific performance can be decreed — unlike post-acquisition invalidity (s. 84-C), which the authority may pursue after.

CASE 2  ·  Res sub judice meets summary procedure — DU case material, pp. 13–15
2
Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-operative Marketing Federation Ltd.
AIR 1998 SC 1952 · (1998) 5 SCC 69 · Supreme Court of India · G.T. Nanavati, J. · Appeals by special leave from a Division Bench of the Bombay High Court · DU Case Material (LB-302), pp. 13–15

FFacts

  • On 5 June 1989 the respondent Federation applied to the appellant Indian Bank to open an irrevocable letter of credit for ₹3,78,90,000 in favour of M/s Shankar Rice Mills; the Bank opened the LC on 6 June 1989. Documents tendered under it were to be forwarded to the Federation for acceptance, the Bank then paying the Mills on the Federation’s behalf.
  • On 6 February 1992 the Bank filed Summary Suit No. 500 of 1992 in the Bombay High Court under Order XXXVII CPC for recovery of ₹4,96,58,160 said to have become due under the LC, and took out summons for judgment (No. 278 of 1992).
  • The Federation appeared and moved for stay of the summary suit under s. 10, contending it had earlier instituted Suit No. 400 of 1992 against the Bank for recovery of ₹3,70,52,217.88 on the same subject-matter — a previously instituted suit, making the summary suit the “subsequent suit” to be stayed.
  • Single Judge: the concept of “trial” in s. 10 applies only to a regular/ordinary suit, not to a summary suit; proceedings not stayed; leave to defend granted conditionally on deposit of ₹4 crores; the Federation’s defence was held to have no merit.
  • Division Bench (Appeals 953 & 954 of 1994): the word “trial” in s. 10 is not used in a narrow sense — it means the entire proceedings after the defendant enters appearance; s. 10 therefore applies to summary suits; the Bank’s suit being the subsequently instituted suit, both appeals allowed, the summary suit stayed till disposal of the prior suit. The Bank appealed to the Supreme Court.

IIssue

Whether the bar against proceeding with the trial of a subsequently instituted suit contained in s. 10 CPC applies to a summary suit filed under Order XXXVII CPC.

Arguments for Indian Bank (appellant)

  • The Single Judge was right: if s. 10 is applied to summary suits, the very object of Order XXXVII — quick relief on liquidated demands — would be frustrated.
  • The expression “trial of any suit” should be construed with reference to the special procedure of Order XXXVII, in which the “trial” stage arises only after leave to defend is granted.

Arguments for the Federation (respondent)

  • “Trial” is of the widest import — in its widest sense it embraces all proceedings from institution to final determination by judgment and decree; the summary suit was instituted later and must await the earlier suit.
  • Preventing courts of concurrent jurisdiction from simultaneously trying two parallel litigations requires staying the entire summary proceeding.

DDecision — Nanavati, J.

  • “Trial” — its colour depends on context (para 7): in legal parlance “trial” means a judicial examination and determination of issues by a competent tribunal (Webster; Stroud) — in its widest sense, all proceedings from institution to final judgment and decree. But “whether the widest meaning should be given … must necessarily depend upon the nature and object of the provision and the context in which it is used.”
  • Object of s. 10 (para 8): to prevent courts of concurrent jurisdiction from simultaneously trying two parallel suits and to avoid inconsistent findings. The provision is a rule of procedure; it does not affect jurisdiction over the later suit, does not bar its institution, and does not prevent interlocutory orders — consolidation, receiver, injunction, attachment before judgment. So “trial” in s. 10 is not used in its widest sense.
  • Harmonious construction (para 9): s. 10 is a general provision; Order XXXVII applies to a certain class of suits and provides quick relief; both must be read so that neither’s object is frustrated. Under the scheme of Order XXXVII, the defendant must enter appearance within 10 days and apply for leave; if he does not, or leave is refused, or he breaks its conditions, the plaintiff is entitled to judgment forthwith — “the stage of determination of the matter in issue will arise in a summary suit only after the defendant obtains leave. The trial would really begin only after leave is granted.”
  • Held (paras 10–12): the wider interpretation of “trial” is not called for. In a summary suit, “trial” in s. 10 begins after leave to defend is granted; the court may therefore proceed up to the stage of hearing the summons for judgment and passing judgment for the plaintiff where (a) the defendant has not applied for leave, or his application is refused, or (b) he fails to comply with the conditions of leave. The Division Bench was in error; its reliance on Harish Chandra v. Triloki Singh, AIR 1957 SC 444, was misplaced — that was a Representation of the People Act case, an interpretation not transferable to s. 10-in-Order-XXXVII context. Appeals allowed; Single Judge’s order restored.

PPrinciple

What Indian Bank settles
(1) s. 10 is a mandatory rule of procedure: it bars trial, never institution, and never interlocutory orders.
(2) The word “trial” takes its meaning from the nature, object and context of the provision — a contextual, not a dictionary-wide, construction.
(3) In a summary suit under Order XXXVII, the “trial” for s. 10 purposes begins only upon grant of leave to defend — so the earlier-suit pendency does not stop the summons for judgment or judgment itself where leave is refused/unclaimed/conditional-broken.
(4) General provisions (s. 10) and special provisions (Order XXXVII) must be harmoniously construed so neither is rendered infructuous.

CASE 3  ·  Res judicata between co-defendants — DU case material, pp. 16–19
3
Iftikhar Ahmed v. Syed Meharban Ali
AIR 1974 SC 749 · Supreme Court of India · K.K. Mathew, J. · Appeal by special leave from the Allahabad High Court · DU Case Material (LB-302), pp. 16–19

FFacts — three generations of one property dispute

  • Properties originally belonging to Buniyad Ali. In consolidation proceedings under the U.P. Consolidation of Holdings Act, 1953, a title dispute arose between Ishtiaq Ahmed (father of the appellants) on one side and Meharban Ali and Kaniz Fatima on the other: they claimed to be co-bhumidars with Ishtiaq Ahmed, who contended he was exclusive owner — Buniyad Ali’s assets devolved on his son Aftab Ali, and after Aftab Ali’s death (1910) and his widow’s (1925), the other heirs relinquished their rights; he also claimed title by adverse possession.
  • As the dispute concerned title, the Consolidation Officer referred it through the Civil Judge, Meerut to an arbitrator appointed under the Act (Mr. K.C. Govil).
  • First award: the arbitrator held Meharban Ali and Kaniz Fatima had no title — relying principally on an Allahabad High Court judgment (in Suit No. 600 of 1934, second appeal) which, he thought, operated as res judicata on title between the parties.
  • II Civil Judge, Meerut: that judgment did not operate as res judicata; the award was vitiated by an error of law apparent on its face; set aside and remitted for fresh decision (s. 16, Arbitration Act).
  • Second award (Mr. B.P. Gupta): on oral and documentary evidence the parties were co-bhumidars except as to 9 bighas 3 biswas; shares determined; the High Court judgment again treated as a piece of evidence, not res judicata.
  • II Civil Judge confirmed the second award; on appeal the District Judge, Meerut reversed — the award suffered from an error of law apparent on the face of the record because the arbitrator ignored the High Court judgment which did operate as res judicata on title; remitted again.
  • The Allahabad High Court (revision) restored the decree confirming the second award, holding the 1934-line judgment was not res judicata. Ishtiaq Ahmed’s legal representatives (he died pending appeal) came to the Supreme Court by special leave.

IIssues

  1. Whether the Allahabad High Court judgment in Suit No. 600 of 1934 operated as res judicata between Ishtiaq Ahmed on the one hand and Meharban Ali / Kaniz Fatima on the other, in respect of title — though they had stood as co-plaintiffs in that suit, the conflict having been fought through the defendant, Ishari Prasad.
  2. Whether the second award, proceeding on the contrary view, was vitiated by an error of law apparent on its face (s. 30, Arbitration Act, 1940).

AThe judgment the question turned on

Suit No. 600 of 1934: Meharban Ali, Kaniz Fatima and Ishtiaq Ahmed sued Ishari Prasad (mortgagee under a mortgage deed of 5 November 1925 by Matlub-un-nissa) for a declaration that the mortgage and the decree Ishari Prasad had obtained in O.S. 128 of 1929 did not affect their shares. Ishari Prasad’s defence was in substance that Matlub-un-nissa alone owned the properties — the mother of Ishtiaq Ahmed — because Kaniz Fatima’s and Meharban Ali’s mother had relinquished her share. The suit was dismissed on that footing; appeals failed; the High Court confirmed in second appeal. The effect (as the Supreme Court read it): Kaniz Fatima and Meharban Ali failed to establish title; the properties belonged exclusively to the mortgagor, mother of Ishtiaq Ahmed.

DDecision — Mathew, J.

  • Res judicata between co-defendants / co-plaintiffs — three conditions (settled by a long line of decisions): “(1) there was a conflict of interest between co-defendants; (2) that it was necessary to decide the conflict in order to give the relief which the plaintiff claimed in the suit and (3) that the Court actually decided the question.” There is “no reason why a previous decision should not operate as res judicata between co-plaintiffs if all these conditions are mutatis mutandis satisfied.”
  • Applied to the 1934 suit: by the written statement Ishari Prasad denied the title of Kaniz Fatima and Meharban Ali and set up exclusive title in Matlub-un-nissa — “There was, therefore, an actual conflict of interest between Ishtiaq Ahmed on the one hand and Kaniz Fatima and Meharban Ali on the other, and it was necessary to decide the conflict in order to give relief to the defendant … and the Court decided that the properties belonged exclusively to the mortgagor.” The effect: they “failed to establish their contention that they had title” — they could not be allowed to agitate the same question again.
  • The Privy Council’s qualification — Chandu Lal v. Khalilur Rahman, AIR 1950 PC 17 (Lord Simonds): the doctrine may apply even against a party who did not contest the earlier suit, but “if such a party is to be bound … it must be proved clearly that he had or must be deemed to have had notice that the relevant question was in issue and would have to be decided.” And Sheoparsan Singh v. Ramnandan Prasad, AIR 1916 PC 78: the rule’s application “should be influenced by no technical considerations of form, but by matter of substance within the limits allowed by law.” The raison d’être: finality for decisions reached between interested parties after genuine contest — one who “deliberately chose a position” cannot “blow hot now when they were blowing cold before” (Ram Bhaj v. Ahmed Said Akhtar Khan, AIR 1938 Lah 571).
  • Error apparent on the face of the award: “If the judgment of the High Court operated in law as res judicata, it would be an error of law apparent on the face of the award if it were to say that the judgment would not operate as res judicata.” An award setting forth an erroneous proposition of law is liable to be set aside under s. 30 of the Arbitration Act; and the Arbitration Act applies to arbitrators under the Consolidation Act (Charan Singh v. Babulal, AIR 1967 SC 57).
  • Remittal no bar (para 44): the first remittance (s. 16) could not be challenged by Ishtiaq Ahmed (no appeal lay under s. 39), so his legal representatives were not precluded from challenging that order’s basis in this appeal.
  • Held: High Court set aside; “We restore the award dated March 30, 1959, passed by Mr. K.C. Govil, the first arbitrator” — it would be “an empty formality” to remit again.

PPrinciple

What Iftikhar Ahmed settles
(1) Res judicata operates inter se between co-defendants and co-plaintiffs where there was a real conflict of interest between them, the conflict had to be — and was — decided to mould relief in the suit.
(2) A party bound need not have personally contested, if clearly proved that it had, or must be deemed to have had, notice that the question was in issue (Chandu Lal).
(3) Substance over form (Sheoparsan Singh) — the doctrine is applied to the matter in substance, not the labels of the pleadings.
(4) An award proceeding on an erroneous view of res judicata is vitiated by an error of law apparent on its face and falls under s. 30, Arbitration Act, 1940.

CASE 4  ·  Constructive res judicata in writs and suits — DU case material, pp. 20–25
4
State of U.P. v. Nawab Hussain
AIR 1977 SC 1680 · (1977) 2 SCC 806 · (1977) 3 SCR 428 · Supreme Court of India · Shinghal, J. · Appeal by special leave against the Allahabad High Court · DU Case Material (LB-302), pp. 20–25

FFacts

  • Respondent Nawab Hussain, a confirmed Sub-Inspector of Police in Uttar Pradesh, faced an anonymous complaint investigated by Inspector Suraj Singh (report to the SP, 25 February 1954); two cases under the Prevention of Corruption Act and the Penal Code were registered; he was dismissed from service by the Deputy Inspector-General of Police on 20 December 1954; his departmental appeal was rejected on 17 April 1956.
  • Writ petition (Art. 226, Allahabad High Court): dismissal should be quashed because he was not afforded a reasonable opportunity to meet the allegations and the action was mala fide. Dismissed on 30 October 1959.
  • Suit (7 January 1960, Civil Judge, Etah): he challenged the dismissal afresh — on the altogether different ground that he had been appointed by the Inspector-General of Police, so the DIG was not competent to dismiss him under Art. 311(1) of the Constitution.
  • The State pleaded res judicata — “all the matters in issue … had been raised or ought to have been raised both in the writ petition and special appeal.”
  • Trial court (21 July 1960): suit dismissed (DIG deemed the appointing authority) — but held it was not barred by res judicata. District Judge (15 February 1963): affirmed. High Court (second appeal, 27 March 1968): allowed — suit decreed; not barred by constructive res judicata, and the DIG was incompetent to dismiss. The State appealed by special leave.

IIssues

  1. Whether the plea of incompetence of the dismissing authority (Art. 311(1)) — available but not raised in the earlier writ petition — was barred in the subsequent suit by constructive res judicata.
  2. Whether, and how far, the principles of (constructive) res judicata apply to writ petitions and from writ proceedings to regular suits.

DDecision — the doctrinal march (paras 3–8)

  • The doctrine (para 3): estoppel per rem judicatam is a rule of evidence — “the broader rule of evidence which prohibits the reassertion of a cause of action” (Marginson v. Blackburn Borough Council). Its two tap-roots: (i) public policy — finality and conclusiveness of judicial decisions in the general interest of the community; (ii) private interest — protection from multiplication of litigation. A cause of action that has merged in a judgment “must lose its identity and vitality”; it cannot survive or spawn a second action on the same facts.
  • Constructive res judicata (para 4): where the same facts give two or more causes of action, a litigant may not dole them out over successive suits — Greenhalgh v. Mallard (Somervell, L.J.): res judicata “covers issues or facts which are so clearly part of the subject-matter of the litigation and so clearly could have been raised that it would be an abuse of the process of the court to allow a new proceeding to be started in respect of them.” An “aspect or amplification of the general principle,” well designed for “subduing a cantankerous litigant.”
  • Section 11 and writs (para 5): s. 11 “relates to suits and former suits, and has, in terms, no direct application to a petition for the issue of a high prerogative writ” — but the general principles have long been applied to renewed writ applications (ex parte Thompson, Lord Denman, C.J. — no “same application repeated from time to time”), and to certiorari, quo warranto and prohibition in England, and in India.
  • The line of authority (paras 6–7): L. Janakirama Iyer (AIR 1962 SC 135) was a different case (s. 11 in terms inapplicable to a representative suit; general doctrine could not fill the gap in suits). In Amalgamated Coalfields (AIR 1964 SC 1013) this Court had hesitated to apply the “special and artificial” constructive form to Arts. 32/226 petitions; but in Devilal Modi (AIR 1965 SC 1150) Gajendragadkar, J. settled the law: “if the doctrine of constructive res judicata is not applied to writ proceedings, it would be open to the party to take one proceeding after another and urge new grounds every time; and that plainly is inconsistent with considerations of public policy.” In Gulabchand Chhotalal Parikh (AIR 1965 SC 1153) the majority (Raghubar Dayal, J.) held s. 11 not exhaustive: “any previous decision on a matter in controversy, decided after full contest or after affording fair opportunity … by a Court competent to decide it, will operate as res judicata in a subsequent regular suit. … The nature of the former proceeding is immaterial.” The clarificatory observation in Gulabchand — that constructive res judicata was “not considered” there — was misunderstood by the High Court as leaving the question open; after Devilal Modi nothing was left open.
  • Applied (para 8): it was not in controversy that the Art. 311(1) plea was not raised in the writ petition, that it was an important plea within the respondent’s knowledge, and that it “could well have been taken” there. He contented himself with the other pleas (no reasonable opportunity; mala fides). “It was therefore not permissible for him to challenge his dismissal, in the subsequent suit, on the other ground … That was clearly barred by the principle of constructive res judicata, and the High Court erred in taking a contrary view.”
  • Held (para 9): appeal allowed; High Court’s judgment dated 27 March 1968 set aside; the sub-inspector’s suit dismissed; parties to bear their own costs.

PPrinciple

What Nawab Hussain settles
(1) A plea which might and ought to have been raised in an earlier proceeding is as dead as one actually decided — constructive res judicata is an “aspect or amplification” of the general principle, not a technicality.
(2) The doctrine applies to writ proceedings (Devilal Modi) and from writ proceedings to regular suits (Gulabchand: the nature of the former proceeding is immaterial; s. 11 is not exhaustive).
(3) Public policy and finality of judgments are “important constituents of the rule of law” — they yield to no plea that fundamental rights deserve a second bite where the point was available and forgone.
(4) The same logic bars pleas “taken but not pressed” at the earlier hearing.

CASE 5  ·  Order II Rule 2 extends to writ petitions — DU case material, pp. 26–29
5
C.A. Balakrishnan v. Commissioner, Corporation of Madras
AIR 2003 Mad 170 · High Court of Madras · A. Kulasekaran, J. · Writ petition for mandamus · DU Case Material (LB-302), pp. 26–29

FFacts

  • The petitioner ran the “Udipi Canteen” (1,839 sq. ft., R.S. 1269 PT) in the Ripon Building compound, Madras, catering to Corporation employees for about 16 years — claiming succession to a lease from his father, who had succeeded the original lessee Seetharama Uduppa. Rent, revised by Corporation Special Officer’s Resolution 4945/93 (16 December 1993) from ₹200 to ₹766.25 a month, was paid (arrears of ₹36,780 paid in two instalments); receipts issued in his name; a District Revenue Officer’s No Objection Certificate (for the police licence) was obtained; the Labour Officer permitted up to 20 workers.
  • 25 May 1995, 12:30 p.m. (peak lunch hours): the Corporation’s Junior Engineer, allegedly without notice or warning, ordered workers and customers out, and locked and sealed the canteen — eatables and materials worth over ₹26,000 inside. Lawyer’s notice followed on 27 May 1995.
  • The High Court being on vacation, the petitioner filed O.S. No. 3743 of 1995 before the City Civil Court for mandatory injunction and restoration of possession, with three interlocutory applications: I.A. 8055/95 (remove lock; restore possession; restore electricity), I.A. 8056/95 (Advocate Commissioner for inventory), I.A. 8054/95 (injunction against interference). All were dismissed on 10 July 1995 — only the movables were ordered to be delivered. No appeal was filed against those orders. The suit was later decreed ex parte in his favour.
  • 1 August 1995: the petitioner filed the present writ petition under Art. 226 for mandamus to restore possession of the premises and for exemplary costs/damages at ₹500 per day from 25 May 1995 — without withdrawing the suit. The Corporation raised a preliminary objection to maintainability.

Arguments for C.A. Balakrishnan (petitioner) — A. Sadanand, counsel

  • Resorting to a suit during the court vacation cannot disentitle him from the writ jurisdiction — he sought enforcement of a guaranteed right and protection from arbitrary State action.
  • He was a statutory tenant under the Tamil Nadu Lease and Rent Control Act; the sealing was a “commando action” violating fundamental rights.
  • Having granted the NOC, the Corporation was estopped from dispossessing without notice; s. 374 of the Madras City Municipal Corporation Act prescribes four modes of service, one after another, none followed; no inspection preceded the action; s. 107(B) of the TN Public Health Act, 1939 requires notice before cancelling a licence.
  • Two distinct rights were taken away — lessee’s right and licensee’s right — both statutorily guaranteed.

Arguments for the Corporation — P. Bagyalakshmi, counsel

  • Having invoked the jurisdiction of the competent civil court and obtained (adverse) orders on interim applications, the petitioner cannot seek the same relief by writ — the petition is not maintainable.
  • He was an unauthorised occupant, neither licensee nor lessee: the licence stood in Seetharama Uduppa’s name (granted till 1996 under s. 357); the canteen was inspected on 15 May 1995 by the Assistant Health Officer and Zonal Officer and found defective — no white-wash, no residual chlorine in drinking water, defective drainage/overflow of sewage, no food-handlers’ certificates, no sterilisation, unhygienic condition.
  • A s. 379(A) notice was issued to the licensee Uduppa; the petitioner refused it, so it was served by affixture; the premises were sealed on 26 May 1995 and the 1995-96 licence revoked; Uduppa never challenged the revocation.
  • Rent demands by subordinate officials were unauthorised; the Commissioner issued no NOC — the DRO was incompetent to issue one and it did not bind the Corporation.

IIssue

Whether, the petitioner having already instituted a civil suit (O.S. 3743/1995) seeking restoration of possession of the same premises, a subsequent writ petition claiming the same relief on the same cause is maintainable — i.e., whether Order II Rule 2 CPC applies to writ petitions. (If the objection succeeded, the other issues need not be decided.)

DDecision — Kulasekaran, J.

  • Admitted facts: the suit for mandatory injunction for restoration of possession “is also the subject matter of the writ petition”; the three interlocutory applications were dismissed (10 July 1995); no appeal was filed; the writ petition followed on 1 August 1995; the suit was not withdrawn; and the suit was later decreed ex parte.
  • The principle: “The principle underlying Order II, Rule 2 being based upon public policy. A person who files a suit seeking certain relief in respect of a cause of action … is precluded from instituting another suit for seeking other reliefs in respect of same cause of action under Order II, Rule 2, CPC.” The rule requires the whole claim and all reliefs to travel in one suit: relinquishment of part is impermissible and omission of one of several reliefs prohibited.
  • Extension to writs — following Devilal Modi v. Sales Tax Officer, Ratlam, AIR 1965 SC 1150 (at 1153): “Consideration of public policy and the principle of the finality of judgments are important constituents of the rule of law … if constructive res judicata is not applied to such proceedings a party can file as many writ petitions as he likes and take one or two points every time … harassment and hardship to the opponent”; and the Andhra Pradesh High Court, Division Bench, in K. Madhadeva Sastry v. Director, Post Graduate Centre, Anantapur, AIR 1982 AP 176 (paras 11 and 13): “A person who files a suit seeking certain relief in respect of a cause of action and who is precluded from instituting another suit for seeking other reliefs with respect to the same cause of action, cannot be allowed to invoke the writ jurisdiction of this Court for obtaining the very same reliefs. Indeed, if a suit is barred, a writ petition would equally be barred, public policy underlying Order II, Rule 2, CPC is attracted with equal vigour in this situation as well.”
  • Held: “The present writ petition is hit by Order II, Rule 2, CPC. For the reasons mentioned supra, the above writ petition is dismissed.” (The merits — licence, notice, statutory tenancy — were left untouched.)

PPrinciple

What C.A. Balakrishnan settles
(1) Order II Rule 2 embodies a rule of public policy: one cause of action must be exhausted in one proceeding — claim all, lose the rest.
(2) If a second suit would be barred by O. II r. 2, a subsequent writ petition for the very same relief is equally barred — the public policy of the rule operates “with equal vigour” in writ jurisdiction.
(3) Election of forum is not an addition of fora: once the civil suit is chosen (and its interlocutory orders allowed to stand), the same cause cannot be re-run under Art. 226.
(4) (Doctrinal coda) the writ court will not permit what the CPC forbids — finality, not forum-shopping.

Q. 1 / 2019CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
What is meant by ‘Res Judicata’? What is the public policy behind this principle? Whether the provisions contained in the Code of Civil Procedure are exhaustive? Whether the principle of Res Judicata applies to execution proceedings? When A does … [does res judicata apply] between co-defendants? Answer under separate sub-headings with the help of case laws.
Model Answer

1. Meaning of Res Judicata

Res judicata (“a matter judged”) is the rule of conclusiveness of judgments: once a matter — of fact, of law, or of mixed law and fact — has been finally decided between the same parties by a competent court, neither party may canvass it again in any future suit or proceeding. Section 11 CPC codifies the doctrine: “No Court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue in a former suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim, litigating under the same title, in a Court competent to try such subsequent suit … and has been heard and finally decided by such Court.” The doctrine is one of the oldest in legal history — “as old as the law itself”; the maxim is res judicata pro veritate accipitur — a judicial decision must be accepted as correct. Das Gupta, J., in Satyadhyan Ghosal v. Deorajin Debi, AIR 1960 SC 941: “once a res is judicata, it shall not be adjudged again.” Its three founding maxims: (i) nemo debet bis vexari pro una et eadem causa; (ii) interest reipublicae ut sit finis litium; (iii) res judicata pro veritate accipitur.

Conditions of application: (i) two suits — a former suit (decided prior, whether or not instituted prior: Expl. I) and a subsequent suit; (ii) the former court competent to try the subsequent suit or the suit in which the issue is raised (Expls. II & VIII); (iii) the same matter directly and substantially in issue — actually (Expl. III) or constructively (Expl. IV); (barring execution proceedings, Expl. VII); (iv) heard and finally decided (Expl. V); (v) same parties or parties claiming under (Expl. VI); (vi) same title. The doctrine is a rule of procedure, mandatory — yet waivable — and the question of its application is a mixed question of law and fact.

2. Public policy behind the principle

The doctrine rests on the twin pillars of public policy (there must be an end to litigation — certainty and finality of judicial decisions, avoidance of conflicting judgments of equal authority, protection of the administration of justice from disrepute) and private justice (an individual should not be vexed twice for the same cause). It is founded on justice, equity and good conscience. In Lal Chand v. Radha Krishan, AIR 1977 SC 789, Chandrachud, J. observed: “The principle of res judicata is conceived in larger public interest which requires that all litigations must, sooner than later, come to an end … a party which has once succeeded on an issue should not be permitted to be harassed by a multiplicity of proceedings involving the determination of the same issue.” In Daryao v. State of U.P., AIR 1961 SC 1457, the Constitution Bench placed the doctrine on the broader foundation of the rule of law: “the binding character of judgments pronounced by courts of competent jurisdiction is itself an essential part of the rule of law.” Because of this public policy, the doctrine applies to civil suits, execution, arbitration, taxation, industrial adjudication, writ petitions, administrative orders, interim orders and criminal proceedings.

3. Are the provisions of s. 11 exhaustive?

No. Section 11 applies in terms to suits; but the general doctrine extends far beyond the letter of the section. In Lal Chand (supra): “Section 11, it is long settled, is not exhaustive and the principle which motivates that section can be extended to cases which do not fall strictly within the letter of the law.” In Gulabchand Chhotalal Parikh v. State of Bombay, AIR 1965 SC 1153: “the provisions of Section 11 CPC are not exhaustive with respect to an earlier decision operating as res judicata between the same parties … any previous decision on a matter in controversy, decided after full contest or after affording fair opportunity to the parties … by a Court competent to decide it, will operate as res judicata in a subsequent regular suit. … The nature of the former proceeding is immaterial.” Hence a writ decision bars a later suit, and a dismissed writ bars a fresh writ under Art. 32 (Daryao; Devilal Modi v. STO, Ratlam, AIR 1965 SC 1150 — constructive res judicata too applies to writs: otherwise “a party can file as many writ petitions as he likes and take one or two points every time”). Contrast L. Janakirama Iyer, AIR 1962 SC 135 — where s. 11 could not apply on its terms (representative suit, different parties), the general doctrine could not be invoked in a suit, s. 11 being the only statutory channel there.

4. Does res judicata apply to execution proceedings?

Yes — now directly, by Explanation VII (inserted by the Amendment Act 104 of 1976, w.e.f. 1-2-1977): “The provisions of this section shall apply to a proceeding for the execution of a decree,” with “suit / issue / former suit” read as “execution proceeding / question arising therein / former execution proceeding.” Before 1977, the section did not in terms apply, but the general principles were applied: Mohanlal Goenka v. Benoy Krishna Mukherjee, AIR 1953 SC 65 — all questions between the parties arising in execution relating to the rights of the decree-holder as such must be determined on the earlier occasion and cannot be reagitated.

5. Res judicata between co-defendants

A matter may be res judicata not only between plaintiff and defendant but also between co-defendants or co-plaintiffs. Three conditions must exist (Union of India v. S. Narasimhulu Naidu (2021 SC); classically Iftikhar Ahmed v. Syed Meharban Ali, AIR 1974 SC 749): (1) a conflict of interest between the co-defendants/co-plaintiffs; (2) it must have been necessary to adjudicate that conflict in order to give the relief claimed by the plaintiff; and (3) the question between them must have been finally decided.

Illustration: A sues B and C; a matter directly and substantially in issue arises between B and C inter se, and unless adjudicated, A’s suit cannot be determined — the adjudication operates as res judicata in a later suit between B and C, in which one is plaintiff and the other defendant. It operates even where the earlier plaintiff’s suit was dismissed (Munni Bibi v. Tirloki Nath, AIR 1931 PC 114), and even against a co-defendant who did not contest — provided it is clearly proved that he had, or must be deemed to have had, notice that the relevant question was in issue and would have to be decided (Lord Simonds, Chandu Lal v. Khalilur Rahman, AIR 1950 PC 17). It does not apply to proforma defendants.

Applied — Iftikhar Ahmed (prescribed case): in the 1934 suit, the defendant mortgagee Ishari Prasad denied the title of co-plaintiffs Kaniz Fatima and Meharban Ali and set up exclusive title in the mortgagor (Ishtiaq Ahmed’s predecessor); there was an actual conflict of interest between the co-plaintiffs, its decision was necessary for the defendant’s relief, and the court decided it — the properties belonged exclusively to the mortgagor. The later consolidation-proceedings dispute over the same title was therefore barred as res judicata; the arbitrator’s second award proceeding on the contrary view was set aside and the first award restored (error of law apparent on the face of the award, s. 30 Arbitration Act).

Q. 5(e) / 2019Short note (4 of 6 attempted)
Write a short note: Res sub judice (Section 10, Code of Civil Procedure, 1908).
Model Answer

Meaning. The expression combines res (a thing or matter) and sub judice (under judicial consideration) — a matter pending decision before a court. Section 10 provides that no court shall proceed with the trial of any suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties (or parties under whom they claim litigating under the same title), where the earlier suit is pending in the same or any other court in India having jurisdiction to grant the relief claimed, or in a court beyond India established/continued by the Central Government having like jurisdiction, or before the Supreme Court.

Object. To prevent courts of concurrent jurisdiction from simultaneously trying two parallel suits, to avoid conflicting decisions on the same matter, to protect parties from multiplicity of proceedings and vexation, and to give advance effect to the rule of res judicata.

Conditions: (i) two suits — previously and subsequently instituted; (ii) the matter in issue in the subsequent suit directly and substantially in issue in the previous one; (iii) same parties or their representatives; (iv) the previous suit pending in the same court, another Indian court, a Central-Government-established foreign court, or the Supreme Court; (v) the earlier court competent to grant the relief claimed in the later suit; (vi) same title in both.

Nature and scope. The rule applies to the trial of a suit, not its institution — the subsequent suit must be stayed, not dismissed; interim orders (receiver, injunction, attachment) remain permissible; it applies to appeals and revisions. The conditions are mandatory (Manohar Lal v. Seth Hiralal, AIR 1962 SC 527); the order may be made at any stage. Test: whether the decision in the previously instituted suit would operate as res judicata in the subsequent suit — if yes, stay (S.P.A. Annamalay Chetty v. B.A. Thornhill, AIR 1931 PC 263). Suit pending in a foreign court does not bar Indian courts (Explanation). Where s. 10 does not strictly apply, the court retains inherent power under s. 151 to stay or consolidate in the interest of justice.

Effect of contravention. Section 10 enacts a rule of procedure which may be waived by consent; a decree passed in contravention is not a nullity and cannot be disregarded in execution (Pukhraj D. Jain v. Gopalakrishna, (2004) 7 SCC 251). No appeal lies against a stay order, but revision does (Ram Chandra Pillai v. Nirmala, AIR 1923 Mad 88).

Leading case — Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-op. Marketing Federation Ltd., AIR 1998 SC 1952: the bar of s. 10 applies to summary suits under Order XXXVII, but with a crucial gloss — the word “trial” must be read in the context of the summary procedure. The object of s. 10 is to avoid parallel trials and conflicting findings; the section is procedural, does not bar institution or interlocutory orders, and therefore “trial” is not used in its widest sense. In a summary suit the trial really begins only after leave to defend is granted; hence the court may proceed up to the hearing of the summons for judgment and pass judgment for the plaintiff where leave is unclaimed, refused, or its conditions are broken. The Division Bench of the Bombay High Court (which had read “trial” as the entire proceedings, following Harish Chandra v. Triloki Singh, AIR 1957 SC 444 — a Representation of the People Act case, wrongly transposed) was reversed.

Q. 7(b) / 2018CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
“No Court shall proceed with the trial …” — Which provision of the Civil Procedure Code begins with these words? What conditions does it lay down, and under which important doctrine of universal application does it fall? Explain with case laws.
Model Answer

1. The provision identified

The words open Section 10 CPC — Stay of suit — the statutory home of the doctrine of res sub judice, an expression of the universal doctrine that there must be finality in litigation (no man vexed twice; one dispute, one decision). Section 10: “No Court shall proceed with the trial of any suit in which the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties, or between parties under whom they or any of them claim litigating under the same title …”. Its conditions, test (Annamalay Chetty, AIR 1931 PC 263) and the summary-suit gloss (Indian Bank, AIR 1998 SC 1952) are set out in the short note on Q.5(e)/2019 above — the doctrine is procedural, mandatory once its six conditions exist, stays (never dismisses) the later trial, and leaves interlocutory orders untouched.

2. The wider frame — the jurisdiction of civil courts (s. 9), as developed in the DU answer

Ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy: a litigant with a grievance of a civil nature has the right to institute a civil suit in a competent civil court unless its cognizance is expressly or impliedly barred by statute (Abdul Waheed Khan v. Bhawani, AIR 1966 SC 1718). Jurisdiction — from juris + dicto, “I speak by the law” — is the power or authority of a court to hear and determine a cause; before a court has jurisdiction it must have both the power to try the suit and the authority to pass the orders sought (Official Trustee v. Sachindra Nath, AIR 1969 SC 823).

Suits expressly barred — barred by an enactment for the time being in force (Mohd. Mahmood v. Tikam Das, AIR 1966 SC 2010): by the Code itself (ss. 10, 11, 47, 95; O. II r. 2; O. IX r. 9; O. XXII r. 11) or by other laws (e.g. s. 170, Representation of the People Act, 1951); matters within the exclusive jurisdiction of revenue courts or special tribunals (Industrial, Election, Rent, Co-operative, Income-tax, MACT) and domestic tribunals. Suits impliedly barred — where a statute provides a special remedy (e.g. the Essential Commodities Act scheme — State of Gujarat v. Babuji, AIR 2007 Guj 27), or on general principles of law (acts of State — Bhavani Shankar v. Somasundara, AIR 1965 SC 316) or public policy (Babu Ganesh Dutt v. Mungoo Ram, (1873) 41 BLR 321).

Presumption as to jurisdiction — every presumption is made in favour of the civil court’s jurisdiction; exclusion is not readily inferred unless express or by necessary and inevitable implication (Dhulabhai v. State of M.P., AIR 1969 SC 78 — the seven principles; Secretary of State v. Mark & Co., AIR 1940 PC 105; M.P. Electricity Board v. Vijay Timber Co., (1997) 1 SCC 68).

3. Relevant case law — the prescribed authority

Gundaji Satwaji Shinde v. Ramchandra Bhikaji Joshi, AIR 1979 SC 653: the plaintiff sued for specific performance of a contract to sell agricultural land; the defendant pleaded s. 63 of the Bombay Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act, 1948 (bar on purchase by a non-agriculturist); the plaintiff relied on the Mamlatdar’s certificate. Issues: (i) whether the plaintiff was an agriculturist under the Tenancy Act; (ii) whether that issue required reference to the Mamlatdar; (iii) whether the civil court’s jurisdiction was barred. Held: where in a suit properly constituted and cognizable by the civil court an issue arises which is required to be settled, decided or dealt with by the competent authority under the Tenancy Act, the civil court’s jurisdiction over that issue “is not only ousted but the [court] is under a statutory obligation to refer the issue to the competent authority … and upon the reference being answered back, to dispose of the suit in accordance with the decision of the competent authority.” Whether the plaintiff is an agriculturist is within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Mamlatdar (ss. 70(a), 85, 85-A); the courts below erred “in clutching at a jurisdiction which did not vest in them.”

Conclusion: the quoted words open s. 10 (res sub judice — conditions, object and the Indian Bank gloss as above), a facet of the universal doctrine of finality of litigation; and the trial-stage analysis of any suit necessarily begins with s. 9’s twin tests — suits of a civil nature, and cognizance not expressly or impliedly barred — with every presumption favouring the civil court, and a statutory duty to refer where a special statute (as in Gundaji) carves out even a single issue.

Sections, tests, numbers and the case matrix to scan in the last hour before the exam

Section 9 — jurisdiction in six lines

s. 9 CPC
“The Courts shall have jurisdiction to try all suits of a civil nature excepting suits of which their cognizance is either expressly or impliedly barred.” Civil nature = right to property or office contested — even through religious rites (Expl. I). Presumption for jurisdiction; ouster strictly construed (Mask, AIR 1940 PC 105; Dhulabhai’s 7 principles, AIR 1969 SC 78). Consent cannot create or oust jurisdiction; decree without jurisdiction = nullity (Kiran Singh); wrong decision within jurisdiction = appealable error only (Ittyavira Mathai).
Gundaji formula (AIR 1979 SC 653)
Issue assigned to a special authority by a statute + issue arising in a civil suit → civil court’s jurisdiction over that issue ousted + statutory duty to stay and refer (s. 85-A pattern) → decide the suit in accordance with the authority’s answer. “Incidental issue” is no camouflage.

Section 10 — res sub judice

The six conditions
1. two suits (earlier + later) · 2. same matter directly & substantially in issue · 3. same parties/representatives · 4. earlier suit pending (same / other Indian court / CG-established foreign court / Supreme Court) · 5. earlier court can grant the later relief · 6. same title.
Effects
Trial barred, institution not; stay (not dismissal); interim orders allowed; mandatory but waivable; contravention ≠ nullity; no appeal but revision; foreign-court pendency no bar (Explanation); residual power s. 151. Test: would the earlier decision be res judicata in the later suit? (Annamalay Chetty, PC). Summary suit: “trial” begins only after leave to defend (Indian Bank, AIR 1998 SC 1952).

Section 11 — res judicata

Six conditions + Explanations map
former suit earlier decided (I) · competent court (II, VIII) · same matter direct & substantial — actually (III) or constructively (IV) · execution included (VII) · heard & finally decided (V) · same parties/under (VI) · same title. Relief not granted = refused (V); representative litigation binds all (VI).
Beyond the section
Not exhaustive (Lal Chand; Gulabchand — “nature of the former proceeding is immaterial”) → writs (Daryao → Devilal → Nawab Hussain), execution (Mohanlal Goenka; Expl. VII), arbitration (Iftikhar), co-defendants (3 conditions: conflict + necessity + decision). Constructive: “might & ought” — Greenhalgh; Forward Construction. Substance over form — Sheoparsan Singh (PC).

Order II rr. 1–2 — whole claim

Gurbux Singh’s three conditions
1. same cause of action · 2. entitlement to more than one relief · 3. omission without leave. Same-evidence test (Mohd. Khalil, PC). Exceptions: different cause; relief not then available; leave (express/implied). Penal & divestive — strict construction; plea at the earliest (Rikabdas). Writ parity: “if a suit is barred, a writ would equally be barred” (C.A. Balakrishnan, AIR 2003 Mad 170).

r. 2: at institution · plaintiff only · reliefs on one cause. s. 11: after decision · both parties · matters in issue. Write the four-row table in long answers.
