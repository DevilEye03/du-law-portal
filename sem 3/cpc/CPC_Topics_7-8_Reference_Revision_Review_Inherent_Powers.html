1. REFERENCE — Section 113 + Order XLVI S.113 (SUBSTANTIVE) + O.46 Rr.1–7 (PROCEDURE) + PROVISO (VIRUS CHALLENGE) + ART.228 CONTRAST

BARE ACT Reference is the consultative jurisdiction — a subordinate court states a case and refers for opinion of High Court instead of deciding a difficult question of law itself. It is not appeal, not revision, not review — the suit remains pending below; only the point of law travels up.

★ S.113 CPC — REFERENCE TO HIGH COURT (VERBATIM) “Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, any Court may state a case and refer the same for the opinion of the High Court, and the High Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit:

Provided that where the Court is satisfied that a case pending before it involves a question as to the validity of any Act, Ordinance or Regulation or of any provision contained therein, the determination of which is necessary for the disposal of the case, and is of opinion that such Act/Ordinance/Regulation/provision is invalid or inoperative, but has not been so declared by the High Court to which that Court is subordinate or by the Supreme Court, the Court SHALL state a case setting out its opinion and reasons therefor, and refer the same for the opinion of the High Court.”
Explanation: “Regulation” = Bengal/Bombay/Madras Code Regulations or as defined in General Clauses Act.Added proviso by Act 24 of 1951. Note MAY (discretionary) in main part v SHALL (mandatory) in proviso — favourite exam trap.
★ ORDER XLVI — REFERENCE (PROCEDURE) R.1 – Reference of question to High Court: Where, before or on hearing of a suit or appeal in which decree is NOT subject to appeal, or in execution of any such decree, any question of law or usage having force of law arises, on which the Court entertains reasonable doubt, the Court may, either of its own motion or on application of any party, draw up statement of facts + point of doubt + its own opinion and refer for decision of High Court.
R.2 – Contingent decree: Referring court may either stay proceedings OR proceed and pass decree/order contingent upon High Court’s decision — but no execution until receipt of HC judgment copy.
R.3 – HC judgment to be transmitted: HC after hearing parties (if they desire) decides point referred, transmits copy under Registrar’s signature; lower court disposes case in conformity.
R.4 – Costs: Costs of reference = costs in the cause (general rule).
R.4A + R.5 – S.113 proviso references + Power to alter: Rr.2–4 apply to proviso references; HC may return case for amendment, alter/cancel/set aside decree/order and make such order as it thinks fit; may refuse to answer or quash unwarranted reference.
R.6 – Small Cause jurisdiction doubt: Court doubting whether suit is cognizable by Small Causes may submit record + reasons to HC; HC orders to proceed or return plaint.
R.7 – District Court reference on Small Cause mistake: District Court, if subordinate court erroneously held suit cognizable/not cognizable by Small Causes (failure/excess of jurisdiction), may, and if required by party shall, submit record to HC; HC makes such order as it thinks fit.

1.1 Object & Rationale (Takwani + Universal)

  • Ensure Acts of legislature are interpreted by highest court in State — subordinate courts should not finally pronounce on validity/meaning of doubtful law.
  • Avoid conflict & multiplicity: one authoritative HC opinion binds all subordinates; prevents contradictory decrees on same legal point.
  • Prevent ultra vires adjudication by munsifs: proviso ensures a civil judge who prima facie thinks a statute is unconstitutional does not strike it down himself — he must refer.
  • Consultative, not appellate: HC does not rehear facts; it advises on law; merits remain with referring court (except R.5 power).

1.3 Essential Conditions / Elements (6-point checklist for exam)

  1. Pending lis: suit, appeal or execution pending before referring court. No reference on hypothetical/future/moots — question must have actually arisen between parties whom court is called upon to adjudicate (Banarsi Yadav v. Krishna Chandra, AIR 1972 Pat 49 FB; Ranganath v. Hanumantha).
  2. “Court” only: Court of civil judicature. Tribunal / persona designata / arbitrator cannot refer (Phul Kumari; Ramakant Bindal; Nanak Chand).
  3. Question of law/usage or vires: fact questions never referable. Must be bona fide live issue.
  4. Reasonable doubt (R.1) / prima facie invalid (proviso): No reasonable doubt if point already concluded by HC to which judge is subordinate — unless that ruling is doubted by later HC/SC/PC decision (Ralia Ram v. Sadh Ram; Fatima-ul-Hasna; Puttu Lal v. Parbati).
  5. No-appeal condition for R.1: decree not subject to appeal. If appeal lies, aggrieved party has remedy — reference unnecessary.
  6. Statement + opinion: Referring court draws up facts + question + own opinion (O.46 Rr.1, 4A). Defective statement → HC may return for amendment or quash (Garling v. Secy. of State; Krishnarao v. Lakshman).
⚠️ WHAT CANNOT BE REFERRED

Questions of fact; academic/hypothetical questions; questions already settled by binding HC decision (no reasonable doubt); reference by tribunal; reference after disposal (no pending case); reference to Supreme Court (only to HC); reference on constitutional interpretation simpliciter (that is Art.228, not S.113 — see below).

1.4 Who May Apply? Power & Duty of Referring Court v High Court

  • Who may move: Court suo motu OR any party’s application (S.113 + O.46 R.1). Party cannot demand — court decides if conditions satisfied. Refusal to refer = “case decided” → revisable under S.115 (Cantonment Board v. Phulchand).
  • Duty of referring court: formulate precise question, state facts, give opinion, may stay or pass contingent decree; cannot execute till HC answers (R.2).
  • Power of High Court (consultative): not confined to questions framed — can consider new aspect (S.K. Roy v. Board of Revenue; CIT v. Scindia Steam); may answer, refuse to answer, return for amendment, quash, or alter/cancel/set aside decree (R.5); cannot decide merits or give suggestions beyond point (Municipal Corpn. v. Shivshanker, (1998) 9 SCC 197).
  • Costs: ordinarily costs in cause (R.4); if reference altogether unwarranted, HC may order referring judge to pay personally (L.S. Sherlekar v. D.L. Agarwal, AIR 1968 Bom 439) — quote for extra marks.

✍️ MODEL MINI-ANSWER (2-MARKER): “WHEN IS REFERENCE MANDATORY?”

Under proviso to S.113 reference is mandatory when (i) case pending, (ii) involves question of validity of Act/Ordinance/Regulation, (iii) determination necessary for disposal, (iv) court is of opinion it is invalid/inoperative, (v) not already so declared by HC/SC. Then court shall state case with opinion + reasons and refer. Under O.46 R.1 general reference is discretionary (may). Refusal is revisable.

2. REVIEW — Section 114 + Order XLVII (Rr.1–9) SUBSTANTIVE RIGHT (S.114) + PROCEDURE & GROUNDS (O.47) • HARIDAS DAS IS THE LEAD CASE • FUNCTUS OFFICIO EXCEPTION

BARE ACT Review = judicial re-examination by the same court & same judge which passed decree/order. S.114 gives the substantive right (“may make such order as it thinks fit”); O.47 prescribes when + on what grounds + how. It is an exception to functus officio (O.20 R.3 — once judgment signed & pronounced, court ceases to have control). Hence strictly confined, reluctantly resorted, not an appeal in disguise.

★ S.114 CPC — REVIEW (VERBATIM) “Subject as aforesaid, any person considering himself aggrieved—
(a) by a decree or order from which an appeal is allowed by this Code, but from which no appeal has been preferred,
(b) by a decree or order from which no appeal is allowed by this Code, or
(c) by a decision on a reference from a Court of Small Causes,
may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order, and the Court may make such order thereon as it thinks fit.”
★ ORDER XLVII R.1 — APPLICATION FOR REVIEW (GROUNDS — HEART OF TOPIC, MEMORISE VERBATIM) “(1) Any person considering himself aggrieved— (a)/(b)/(c) as in S.114 — and who, from the discovery of new and important matter or evidence which, after the exercise of due diligence was not within his knowledge or could not be produced by him at the time when the decree was passed or order made, OR on account of some mistake or error apparent on the face of the record OR for any other sufficient reason, desires to obtain a review of the decree passed or order made against him, may apply for a review of judgment to the Court which passed the decree or made the order.

(2) A party who is not appealing may apply for review notwithstanding pendency of appeal by some other party, EXCEPT where ground of such appeal is common to applicant & appellant, or when, being respondent, he can present to Appellate Court the case on which he applied for review.

Explanation (inserted 1976): The fact that the decision on a question of law on which the judgment is based has been reversed or modified by subsequent decision of superior Court in any other case, shall NOT be a ground for review.”DU LB-302 quotes this Explanation in Haridas Das (para 10) to reject rehearing because later law changed.
★ ORDER XLVII Rr.3–9 — PROCEDURE (SHORT) R.3: Form as of appeals (mutatis mutandis). R.4(1): If no sufficient ground → reject. R.4(2): If prima facie grant → grant only after notice to opposite party; on discovery ground → strict proof of due-diligence allegation required. R.5: Heard by same Judge(s) if still attached & not precluded for 6 months; no other Judge shall hear. R.6: If Bench equally divided → rejected; majority prevails. R.7(1) (1976): Rejection not appealable; grant appealable (at once or in appeal from final decree). R.7(2)-(3): Rejected for default → may seek restoration on sufficient cause + notice. R.8: On grant → register note + re-hear or order re-hearing. R.9: Bar of second review — no application to review an order on review or decree/order on review shall be entertained.

2.1 Meaning, Nature & Scope (Takwani Ch.8 + DU Haridas Das paras 10–12)

  • Dictionary: “to look again, reconsider, re-examine” (Chambers/Random House/Black’s). Legal: same court, same judge reconsiders earlier order in circumscribed situations.
  • Serious step, reluctant resort: only where glaring omission, patent mistake, grave error by judicial fallibility (Sajjan Singh; Sow Chandra Kante; Northern India Caterers; Aribam; Meera Bhanja; Kewal Chand).
  • Not appellate power: appellate court can correct all errors; review court can correct only errors within O.47 R.1. Finality cannot be reopened unless earlier view manifestly wrong. Filing review to “fight over again the same battle fought and lost” wastes public time — deprecated.
  • Haridas Das formulation (DU p.51, para 10 — quote in exam): “Neither [limb] postulates a rehearing because a party had not highlighted all aspects or could have argued more forcefully / cited binding precedents and thereby enjoyed a favourable verdict.” Former part = situation attributable to applicant (discovery); latter = jural action manifestly incorrect / no two conclusions possible.

2.2 Who May Apply? Who May Decide? (Rr.1, 5)

  • Applicant: “Any person considering himself aggrieved” — not necessarily party; includes third party prejudicially affected (e.g., purchaser pendente lite) if decree affects him. Must fall in (a)/(b)/(c).
  • Decided by: same court which passed decree/made order; under R.5 same Judge(s) if available (attached + not absent 6 months). No forum-shopping to another Judge.
  • No inherent power of review: review must be conferred by statute; court cannot review suo motu unless statute permits. “There is no provision either in S.114 or O.47 providing for suo motu review.” Exception: courts of plenary jurisdiction under Art.226/32 have inherent review to prevent miscarriage (Shivdeo Singh; Aribam), and Supreme Court under Art.137.

⛔ WHEN REVIEW DOES NOT LIE
  • After appeal preferred by same party on same ground (election; R.1(2) bar).
  • After appeal disposed of on merits (doctrine of merger — only superior court can review).
  • Second review (O.47 R.9) — order on review cannot be re-reviewed.
  • On ground that later SC/HC decision changed law (Explanation to R.1).
  • To correct erroneous view on merits requiring long reasoning — that is appeal, not review.

2.4 Grounds — The Trinity in O.47 R.1(1) (MOST IMPORTANT — 6 marks alone)

Ground (i): Discovery of new and important matter or evidence

  • Ingredients: (a) new + important + bearing on decision; (b) after exercise of due diligence was not within knowledge OR could not be produced when decree passed. Burden = strict proof (R.4(2)(b)).
  • Not available if evidence was known/available but negligently not produced, or if party wants to fill lacuna / produce additional evidence that could have been produced.
  • Subsequent events: may be considered in review if decisive (BCCI v. Netaji Cricket Club, (2005) 4 SCC 741 — DU Universal cites: subsequent event taken into account).

Ground (ii): Mistake or error apparent on the face of the record

  • Thungabhadra test (DU Haridas Das para 10, AIR 1964 SC 1372 — QUOTE): “There is a distinction which is real, though it might not always be capable of exposition, between a mere erroneous decision and a decision vitiated by ‘error apparent’. A review is by no means an appeal in disguise whereby an erroneous decision is reheard and corrected, but lies only for patent error. Where without any elaborate argument one could point to the error and say here is a substantial point of law which stares one in the face and there could reasonably be no two opinions, a clear case of error apparent is made out.”
  • Parsion Devi v. Sumiri Devi (DU Haridas Das para 12): error not self-evident, detectable only by process of reasoning = not error apparent. Cannot rehear erroneous decision.
  • Examples of error apparent: overlooking specific statutory provision bearing on controversy (not mere wrong view of law) — Surinder Singh Arora v. Major Sohan Singh, AIR 1986 Del 293 (DU Universal: S.21(2) CPC not noticed; failure to advert = error analogous to apparent); arithmetical slip going to root; applying repealed Act; ignoring binding precedent cited and on point (but failure to cite precedent by counsel is not ground — Haridas Das).
  • Not error apparent: wrong appreciation of evidence; two plausible views; subsequent reversal of law (Explanation); desire to argue better.

Ground (iii): “Any other sufficient reason” — ejusdem generis

  • Must be read analogous to (i) & (ii) — i.e., reason of same genus: discovery-like or patent-error-like (State of WB v. Kamal Sengupta, AIR 2009 SC (Supp) 476; Chhajju Ram).
  • Illustrations accepted: fraud played on court; judgment obtained by suppression; court’s inadvertent omission to decide material issue; subsequent retrospective legislation changing law (DU Universal: amendment retrospective = good ground; prospective = not).
  • Not sufficient: subsequent prospective overruling; mere hardship; change of counsel’s opinion.

2.5 Limitation, Appeal, Revision, LPA on Review Orders

  • Limitation: Art.124, Limitation Act — 30 days from decree/order (for review to same court). Delay condonable under S.5 if sufficient cause.
  • Appeal: O.43 R.1(w) — appeal lies from order granting review; no appeal from rejection (O.47 R.7(1)). But rejection may be challenged in revision/writ if jurisdictional error, or in appeal from main decree.
  • Revision: rejection of review = case decided → revisable if conditions of S.115 satisfied; grant also revisable where no appeal lies (rare).
  • LPA: whether LPA lies from single-judge review order depends on HC Letters Patent + whether “judgment” — generally order granting review affecting merits = judgment → LPA may lie; pure procedural rejection may not.
  • Review in writs & SC: HC has review under Art.226 (plenary) within O.47 analogues (Aribam); SC has express review under Art.137 + S.114 analogy, plus curative (Rupa Ashok Hurra).
✍️ MODEL 3-LINER: “REVIEW IS NOT APPEAL IN DISGUISE”

Review (S.114/O.47) lies only on (i) discovery despite due diligence, (ii) error apparent staring in face with no two opinions (Thungabhadra), (iii) analogous sufficient reason — strictly confined (Meera Bhanja), not to correct erroneous merits (Aribam), not on subsequent overruling (Explanation). Erroneous decision reheard/corrected = province of appeal (Haridas Das).

3. REVISION — Section 115 REVISIONAL JURISDICTION OF HIGH COURT • SUPERVISORY, DISCRETIONARY, JURISDICTION-ERROR ONLY • 1976 + 1999 PROVISOS ARE EXAM CORE

BARE ACT Revision = High Court calls for record of subordinate court to satisfy itself about jurisdiction-legality. It is not rehearing, not appeal — HC checks how jurisdiction was exercised, not whether decision was right on facts. Word “may” = discretionary. Lies only where no appeal lies to HC.

★ S.115 CPC — REVISION (VERBATIM WITH AMENDMENTS) “(1) The High Court may call for the record of any case which has been decided by any Court subordinate to such High Court and in which no appeal lies thereto, and if such subordinate Court appears—
(a) to have exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it by law, OR
(b) to have failed to exercise a jurisdiction so vested, OR
(c) to have acted in the exercise of its jurisdiction illegally or with material irregularity,
the High Court may make such order in the case as it thinks fit:

Provided that the High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding, EXCEPT where the order, if it had been made in favour of the party applying for revision would have finally disposed of the suit or other proceedings.” [Substituted by Amendment Act 1999, w.e.f. 1-7-2002]

“(2) The High Court shall not, under this section, vary or reverse any decree or order against which an appeal lies either to the High Court or to any Court subordinate thereto.”
“(3) A revision shall not operate as a stay of suit or other proceeding before the Court except where such suit or proceeding is stayed by the High Court.”
Explanation: “any case which has been decided” includes any order made, or any order deciding an issue, in the course of a suit or other proceeding.” [Inserted 1976]1976: renumbered as (1), added (2)(3)+Explanation. 1999: narrowed proviso — interlocutory orders largely out. Memorise proviso + (2)+(3)+Explanation verbatim.

3.1 Meaning, Nature, Object (Takwani + Universal)

  • Dictionary: “to look again carefully with a view to correcting/improving.” Legal: HC’s supervisory power akin to certiorari (but narrower — only on S.115 grounds, not all jurisdictional errors as in writ).
  • Object: keep subordinates within bounds of jurisdiction; prevent arbitrary/capricious/illegal exercise; subserve, not defeat, ends of justice. Where substantial justice done, HC will not interfere on technicality; where failure of justice/hardship, HC will interfere.
  • Discretionary + equitable: petitioner must come with clean hands, disclose entire case (Dominion of India v. Gobordhan Das; J.N. Basu v. Tarakchandra); conduct, delay, alternative remedy, acquiescence relevant. HC may refuse even if clause (a)/(b)/(c) technically satisfied.
  • Revision vs certiorari vs superintendence: S.115 narrower than Art.227 (superintendence) and Art.226 (certiorari). If revision barred (e.g., proviso), writ/227 may still lie in exceptional jurisdictional failure — but cannot be used to circumvent S.115 bars routinely.

3.3 Jurisdictional Error — Clauses (a)/(b)/(c) in Depth (most tested)

General principle (Privy Council + SC — quote)

“S.115 is not directed against conclusions of law or fact in which question of jurisdiction is not involved.” — Amir Hassan Khan v. Sheo Baksh Singh (PC); Manindra Land v. Bhutnath Banerjee; M.L. Sethi v. R.P. Kapur. “While exercising jurisdiction under S.115, it is not competent to HC to correct errors of fact, however gross, or errors of law, however erroneous, unless jurisdictional.” HC cannot re-appreciate evidence.
  • Error of jurisdiction v error of law/fact: If court has jurisdiction and decides wrongly (even grossly), no revision. If court assumes jurisdiction it lacks, or refuses jurisdiction it has, or exercises in illegal/irregular way going to jurisdiction, revision lies. Illustration: Munsif tries suit valued beyond pecuniary limit → cl.(a). Munsif refuses to try properly valued suit saying no jurisdiction → cl.(b). Munsif tries properly but decides without evidence / violates natural justice / ignores mandatory procedure → cl.(c).

Clause (a): Exercise of jurisdiction not vested by law (excess/assumption)

  • Trying suit barred by law (S.11 res judicata, S.10 stay ignored where mandatory, pecuniary/territorial excess); entertaining time-barred appeal without condonation; deciding without territorial jurisdiction; holding Act ultra vires in violation of S.113; accepting plaint without court fee and proceeding; granting relief beyond pleadings/jurisdiction.

Clause (b): Failure to exercise jurisdiction vested (refusal/omission)

  • Declining to entertain maintainable suit/appeal/application; refusing to decide preliminary issue it must decide; dismissing for non-existent bar; refusing to condone delay where sufficient cause proved (jurisdictional failure); refusing to implead necessary party / grant amendment where mandatory (rare — usually discretionary, so HC reluctant).

Clause (c): Illegal or materially irregular exercise (manner of exercise)

  • “Illegally” = breach of mandatory provision / fundamental procedure (e.g., deciding without framing issues, without evidence, violating O.41 R.31, S.35 costs illegally). “Material irregularity” = serious procedural lapse affecting rights / causing failure of justice (e.g., refusing cross-examination, deciding ex parte without service, ignoring res judicata plea going to jurisdiction).
  • Not cl.(c): mere wrong inference, erroneous interpretation where two views possible, discretionary orders (adjournment, interrogatories) unless perverse.
  • Takwani list (xxii–xxiv): acting in violation of S.113 by holding Parliament Act ultra vires; accepting plaint without court fees — classic cl.(a)/(c) examples.

3.4 The Two Great Bars — Proviso to (1) + Sub-section (2) (1999 amendment)

  • Proviso to S.115(1) (1999, w.e.f. 2002) — “Finally-disposed-of test”: HC shall not vary/reverse any order made or issue-decision in course of suit/proceeding EXCEPT where order, if made in favour of revision-petitioner, would have finally disposed of suit/proceeding.
    Effect: Interlocutory orders largely non-revisable. E.g., rejecting O.7 R.11 plaint-rejection plea → revisable? If plaint rejected, suit ends → YES, revisable (had it been in favour, suit disposed). Dismissing amendment / refusing adjournment / admitting document → NO (suit would continue anyway). DU favourite: “Order rejecting preliminary objection on limitation — revisable?” Yes, because if upheld, suit dismissed.
  • S.115(2): where appeal lies (to HC or subordinate), no revision. Even if appeal lies to subordinate court (e.g., appealable order under O.43), HC cannot revise.
  • S.115(3): revision does not operate as stay unless HC stays. Filing revision ≠ automatic stay — must seek stay.
  • Mechalec paradox (DU PYQ Q.4(a)/2019): HC in revision interfered with discretionary unconditional leave (O.37) — SC set aside, holding HC exceeded S.115 (no jurisdictional error). Quote to show discretionary orders rarely revised.

3.5 Who May File? Suo Motu, Form, Limitation, Procedure

  • Who: aggrieved party (including State); HC may also act suo motu (call for record on its own — “may call for record”; must have some ground, not mere conjecture; once invoked, interference only on S.115 grounds). Third party aggrieved may also invoke.
  • Form: no prescribed form; revision petition + certified copy + grounds of jurisdictional error; court fee as per State; affidavit. Caption: “Civil Revision No. __ of __ under S.115 CPC.”
  • Limitation: 90 days (Art.131, Limitation Act — revision to HC). Delay condonable under S.5. No abatement on death — HC may bring LRs anytime (unlike appeal).
  • Procedure: admission → notice → record called → hearing → HC may confirm/vary/reverse, remand, pass consequential orders (“such order as it thinks fit” — but subject to proviso + (2)). Must record reasons; dismissal in limine with brief reasons permissible. No LPA from revisional order (single judge exercising revision = not “judgment” in most HCs; SC: Shah Babulal Khimji test).
  • Conversion: appeal filed as revision or vice versa → court may treat/convert invoking S.151 to do justice (no formal bar). Revision → appeal conversion allowed if appeal lies; appeal → revision if no appeal but jurisdictional error.
  • Death/merger: revision does not abate; doctrine of merger applies if HC decides on merits (subordinate order merges).

5. INHERENT POWERS — Section 151 (+ Ss.148–153) “NOT CONFERRED, BUT SAVED” • EX DEBITO JUSTITIAE • ENDS OF JUSTICE + ABUSE OF PROCESS • MANOHAR LAL + MAHANT RAM DASS

BARE ACT CPC is procedural law — legislature cannot contemplate all future situations. Ss.148–153 therefore save complementary powers to fill gaps. S.151 is the salutary saving clause: it does not confer power; it acknowledges power inherent by virtue of duty to do justice (Manohar Lal Chopra v. Hiralal, AIR 1962 SC 527, Raghubar Dayal J. — DU p.126).

★ S.151 CPC — SAVING OF INHERENT POWERS (VERBATIM — MEMORISE) “Nothing in this Code shall be deemed to limit or otherwise affect the inherent power of the Court to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the Court.”Two triggers only: (1) ends of justice, (2) prevent abuse of process. Source = necessity; breadth = coextensive with necessity (Newabganj Sugar Mills v. UOI).
★ ALLIED INHERENT-POWER SECTIONS (SCHEME Ss.148–153B) S.148 – Enlargement of time: Where period fixed/granted by Court for doing act prescribed/allowed by Code, Court may enlarge (≤30 days total now) even if original period expired. Discretionary (“may”); conduct relevant. Two conditions: (i) period fixed by Court, (ii) for act prescribed/allowed by Code. Mahant Ram Dass is the lead case.
S.149 – Deficiency of court-fees: Court may at any stage allow payment of deficit fee; on payment document has same force as if paid in first instance (retrospective). Discretionary; only where failure due to beyond control / bona fide; “any stage” = while matter pending (Banta Singh); notice to affected party (Buta Singh).
S.150 – Transfer of business: Transferee court has same powers/duties (properly belongs with Ss.22–25).
S.152 – Clerical/arithmetical slips: Correct accidental slip/omission anytime suo motu/on application. Only accidental, not intentional/merits omissions (e.g., “Rs.1000 as prayed” for Rs.10,000 suit → correctable; omitting interest claim → appeal/review, not S.152). Based on actus curiae neminem gravabit + duty to keep true records.
S.153 – General power to amend: Amend defect/error in any proceeding to determine real question (Jai Jai Ram Manohar Lal; Purushottam Umedbhai).
S.153A – Amendment where appeal summarily dismissed (O.41 R.11): First court may still amend under S.152 despite confirming effect.
S.153B – Open court: Trial place deemed open court.
S.148A – Caveat: Right to lodge caveat (belongs with institution, placed oddly).Takwani: scheme “not based on intelligible pattern” — S.150/S.148A misplaced; S.151 is the reservoir.

5.1 Meaning & Juristic Basis (Universal + Takwani)

  • “Inherent” = natural, inseparable, intrinsic, essential attribute of office (Oxford/Chambers/Webster). Inherent powers = powers to do full and complete justice between parties before court.
  • Ex debito justitiae: exercised as of debt of justice in absence of express provision. Cannot be exercised in contravention/conflict/ignoring express provision (Durgesh Sharma v. Jayshree, AIR 2009 SC 285; Mahendra Manilal v. Sushila, AIR 1965 SC 364).
  • Roots in necessity: Code not exhaustive; inherent powers rescue unforeseen circumstances (Manohar Lal).
  • Maxims to quote: actus curiae neminem gravabit (act of court shall prejudice no one); ubi jus ibi remedium; quando lex aliquid alicui concedit, concedere videtur et id sine quo res ipsa esse non potest (grant includes essentials).

5.2 Two Heads of S.151 — Ends of Justice + Abuse of Process

Head 1: “Ends of justice” (affirmative)

  • Two rules (Universal): (a) injury should be remedied; needless expense/inconvenience avoided; (b) not in ends of justice if it interferes with third-party rights or causes mischief/injustice.
  • Illustrations courts have allowed: recall own orders & correct mistakes; set aside ex parte order in appropriate case; issue temporary injunction beyond O.39 (Manohar Lal); add/delete/transpose parties; revive execution; allow amendment; consolidate suits; stay suit beyond S.10 to avoid multiplicity; extend time (S.148); restore suit/proceeding despite peremptory order (Mahant Ram Dass; Lachmi Narain v. Balmakund, AIR 1924 PC 198); remand in exceptional cases (narrow).

Head 2: “Prevent abuse of process” (negative — by court or party)

  • Abuse by court: employing procedure it never intended → miscarriage → must remedy on actus curiae basis (Kanai Lal Shaw; Forasol v. ONGC).
  • Abuse by party: fraud on court/party (Dadu Dayal; UP Junior Doctors); circumventing statute (Manilal Mohanlal; Jibon Krishna); multiplicity/vexatious/dilatory tactics (Nair Service Society; Jethabhai; Mula v. Babu Ram); scandalous matter (Shankerlal); securing undue advantage (Yasin Ali; Rajappa Hanamantha).
  • Ram Chand synthesis (DU Universal, AIR 1966 SC 1899): “Whatever limitations imposed by construction on S.151, they do not control undoubted power to make suitable order to prevent abuse of process.” Striking defence for director’s default under O.29 R.3 via S.151 upheld — O.29 does not expressly/impliedly preclude S.151.

5.3 Manohar Lal Principles — S.151 v Express Provisions (DU p.124–126, MUST QUOTE)

Padam Sen v. State of UP, AIR 1961 SC 218 (quoted in Manohar Lal, DU p.125): “Inherent powers are in addition to powers specifically conferred. They are complementary. Court is free to exercise them for S.151 purposes when exercise is not in conflict with what is expressly provided or against Legislature’s intention.”
Raghubar Dayal J. explanation (DU p.125): Restriction is not because controlled by Code (S.151 says “nothing shall limit”) but because procedure specifically provided is presumed dictated by interests of justice.
On S.94 + O.39 (DU p.124–125): S.94 is supplemental, not exhaustive; O.39 Rr.1–2 only say when court MAY grant injunction, not that it SHALL NOT in other cases. Hence injunction beyond O.39 can issue under S.151. Maqbul Ahmad v. Onkar Pratap (PC) distinguished — that was about dispensing with Limitation Act, not S.151 which expressly saves power.
  • Substantive v procedural (DU p.125–126): S.151 covers procedure to be followed in deciding cause, not substantive rights. Padam Sen commissioner to seize plaintiff’s books struck down — affected substantive rights; specific power needed. Inherent powers = regulate practice/procedure.
  • Exceptional + cautious (DU p.126–127): exercised in very exceptional circumstances where Code lays down no procedure; order restraining party from proceeding in another regularly-constituted court needs great care, absolutely essential for ends of justice (Cohen v. Rothfield; Hyman v. Helm — burden on applicant to show multiplicity vexatious, mere inconvenience ≠ vexation).

5.4 Limitations — 7 Tests (Takwani + Universal + DU)

  1. No power to do what is prohibited expressly/impliedly; cannot defeat statutory provisions or assume jurisdiction over excluded matters (Raja Soap Factory v. Shantharaj).
  2. Where Code exhaustively covers topic, follow that manner only — cannot devise own procedure (Ram Chand; Arjun Singh v. Mohindra Kumar; Nain Singh v. Koonwarjee). E.g., cannot circumvent S.10 stay, O.21 R.84 set-off, O.41 Rr.23/25 remand, O.9 Rr.9/13 ex parte, O.37 decree (Ramkarandas), S.75/O.26 commissioner (Padam Sen limits), review without statute.
  3. No remedy to indolent / unclean hands: equity aids vigilant; suppression = refusal (Durgesh Sharma).
  4. Procedural only, not substantive: cannot create/abolish rights, direct fresh award, restrain execution against surety, implead LRs after abatement.
  5. Cannot override res judicata / decided issues: cannot reopen finally decided questions.
  6. Cannot dispense with limitation: no general discretion outside Act (Maqbul Ahmad); S.151 ≠ S.5 Limitation.
  7. Very exceptional, with reasons, after judicial mind: not arbitrary; must look to facts; restraining another court’s proceedings = rarest (Manohar Lal).

• Sympathy cannot override legal principle (Latham).

📖 Decision (Hidayatullah J., DU pp.56–57, paras 5–6)

  • Orders of 13.7.54 + subsequent orders set aside; dismissal of appeal/suit set aside; 2 months fresh time from SC judgment to pay deficit; no costs (ex parte); warning: “after lesson, appellant will not ask vainly for more indulgence.” No remand — SC itself satisfied sufficient cause made out (avoid delay).
  • Application filed in time, not considered (para 5): HC went by letter of original order, ignored that petition was before expiry; vacation + no Bench should have been considered; order even if passed 13.7.54 would have operated from 8.7.54. If HC had considered and rejected on merits, other considerations might arise — but it did not.
  • S.148/S.149 liberal (para 5): S.148 “in terms allows extension even if original period expired”; S.149 equally liberal; a fortiori invocable when time had not actually expired.
  • Peremptory orders doctrine (para 5 — QUOTE): “How undesirable to fix time peremptorily for future happening leaving Court powerless… Such procedural orders, though peremptory (conditional decrees apart) are in essence in terrorem, so dilatory litigants put themselves in order and avoid delay. They do not completely estop Court from taking note of events within time fixed… Such orders are not like law of Medes and Persians.” Robbery illustration + Lachmi Narain Marwari v. Balmakund (AIR 1924 PC 198, Lord Philimore) — courts mould practice to restore despite final order; prompt obedience still important.
  • Inherent powers available on both occasions (para 5): HC could have exercised powers 13.7.54 (timely petition) and again on S.151 petitions; Ss.148/149 would have “clothed them with ample power to do justice”; HC erroneously felt unable to come to aid despite sympathy. “Time should have been extended 13.7.54 if sufficient cause, and again on inherent petitions.”

💡 Principle Evolved / Ratio

“Conditional/peremptory procedural orders (‘in default appeal stands dismissed’) are in terrorem, not jurisdictional self-denial; S.148 permits extension even after expiry, S.149 retrospectively validates fee, and S.151 preserves power to advance justice on sufficient cause — especially where extension sought before expiry but heard after. Court must consider merits (illness, market, diligence, part-payment, vacation), not dismiss on letter.” — Mahant Ram Dass. Relied in Johri Singh v. Sukh Pal (1989); Chinnamarkathian; Ganesh Prasad on discretionary extension.
✍️ HOW TO USE IN EXAM (S.151 OR S.148 ANSWER)

Mahant Ram Dass (1961): Patna HC’s peremptory 3-month fee order; in-time extension + S.151/review rejected as “already dismissed/finally disposed”; SC (Hidayatullah J.) held Ss.148/149 + S.151 gave ample power, peremptory orders in terrorem, not Medes-Persians, time relates back, sufficient cause (illness/slump) → dismissal set aside, 2 months granted. Use for: S.148 conditions, peremptory orders, S.151 gap-filling, sufficient cause, retrospective fee validation.

6C. (DU Allied — Essential for S.151) Manohar Lal Chopra v. Rai Bahadur Rao Raja Seth Hiralal, AIR 1962 SC 527 — Raghubar Dayal J.

DU LB-302 pp.121–127 • Temporary injunctions beyond O.39 via S.151 • Two suits (Asansol + Indore) • Indore forum clause • PYQ Q.4(b)/2019

HYPOTHETICAL VISUAL — MANOHAR LAL: TWO COURTS, ONE PARTNERSHIP, ONE INJUNCTION

📜 Facts (DU pp.121–124, paras 1–14 — condensed but complete)

  • Partnership: Manohar Lal (appellant) + Hiralal (respondent), Indore firm Diamond Industries (coal/cement, head office Indore); dissolved 22.8.45; Manohar to render true accounts, entitled to ¼ of Rs.4 lakh only after audit. Second proviso: “all disputes… decided amicably or in court at Indore and at nowhere else.”
  • Notices (1945): Hiralal alleges falsified accounts; Manohar denies, invites meeting at Asansol/Kajora.
  • Asansol Suit 18.8.48 (M.S.39/48): Manohar v. Hiralal for Rs.1,18,000; attaches dissolution deed. Hiralal: S.34 Arbitration stay (rejected 20.8.49); written statement 28.11.49 pleads no jurisdiction (Indore clause) + vexatious; issues 4.2.50 (jurisdiction + accounts). Hiralal’s Ss.10/151 stay (Dec.51) rejected 9.8.52 (“embarrassment ≠ abuse; S.10 inapplicable — Asansol earlier”); Calcutta HC affirms 7.5.53 + directs expeditious jurisdiction hearing.
  • Indore Suit 3.1.49 (COS 71/49): Hiralal v. Manohar for Rs.1,90,519 + render accounts. Manohar’s Ss.10/151 stay (28.4.50) rejected 5.7.51 (Indore clause; Asansol not “internal court” under Madhya Bharat CPC; different subject-matter); MB HC affirms 20.8.53.
  • Injunction (14.9.53): After both stays failed, Hiralal applies under S.151 in Indore to restrain Manohar from Asansol proceedings (alleges trouble/expense/time; violation of Indore clause). Suppresses Calcutta direction. ADJ Indore grants under O.39. MB HC (10.5.55) agrees O.39 R.1 inapplicable but sustains under S.151. Manohar appeals (SLP).

❓ Issues (DU para 15)

  1. Whether court can exercise inherent powers when specific provisions (S.94 + O.39) exist for injunctions?
  2. Whether inherent discretion was properly exercised on these facts?
  3. (Arising at hearing) Legal effect of Indore forum clause on Asansol maintainability?

⚖️ Arguments

Appellant Manohar (injunction-opponent)Respondent Hiralal (injunction-holder) + MB HC view
• S.94(c) + O.39 exhaustive; S.94 + O.39 cover injunctions → S.151 barred; allowing S.151 = override Legislature.
• Even if power exists, misused: restraining earlier competent suit; defendant restraining plaintiff (inverse); no vexation proved; suppresses Calcutta order; forum clause ≠ ouster of jurisdiction.
• Maqbul Ahmad (PC): no general discretion outside Act.		• S.94 supplemental; O.39 permissive (“may”), not prohibitory; S.151 saves power for uncovered cases (Padam Sen).
• Indore clause makes Asansol vexatious/oppressive; Asansol continuance violates agreement; Indore court as agreed forum may protect its proceedings via S.151.
• MB HC: O.39 inapplicable but S.151 available.

📖 Decision (Raghubar Dayal J., DU pp.124–127)

  • Q1 — Principle for Hiralal (power exists): Civil courts HAVE inherent jurisdiction to issue temporary injunctions in circumstances not covered by O.39. S.94 supplemental; O.39 Rr.1–2 do not say “not to be issued in other cases”; S.151 “nothing shall limit” — not controlled by Code (Padam Sen quoted); restriction only presumption that specific procedure dictated by justice; Maqbul Ahmad distinguished (Limitation Act has no saving clause like S.151). First contention repelled (para 24).
  • Q2 — Relief for Manohar (exercise improper): On facts, courts below erred (para 25). Inherent powers in very exceptional, no-procedure cases (para 26); restraining another regularly-constituted court needs great care, absolutely essential (para 27). Cohen: jurisdiction rarely exercised, great caution, applicant must show no advantage to defendant in other action; here defendant of earlier suit restraining plaintiff — inverse, no principle. Hyman: burden to show multiplicity vexatious; mere witnesses/contract/inconvenience ≠ vexation. Injunction set aside.
  • Forum clause: parties cannot confer/oust jurisdiction by agreement where court inherently lacks/has it; at best relevant to stay discretion, not to injunction as of right (para 15 third point; Takwani notes).

💡 Principles Evolved (twin ratio — quote both)

“(1) S.151 saves power to issue injunction beyond O.39 where not in conflict with express provision or legislative intent — O.39 is permissive, S.94 supplemental, S.151 complementary (Padam Sen). (2) But such power is exceptional, cautious, never to restrain bona fide proceedings in competent court unless proved vexatious and absolutely essential (Cohen; Hyman). S.151 governs procedure, not substantive rights.”

PYQ 1 — Q.2(b)/2019 (also Q.4(a)/2017 [10 marks] & Q.6(b)/2018): “Distinguish between Revision, Review and Reference. Can there be a second review?”

Weightage: 10 marks (2017) / 5+5 (2019). This is the most repeated question — memorise table + second-review paragraph + Haridas Das garnish.

★ BEST MODEL ANSWER (10-mark structure — introduction + table + second review + case law + conclusion)

Introduction (1 mark): Ss.113–115 + O.46–47 provide three distinct non-appeal remedies: Reference (consultation during pendency), Review (reconsideration by same court), Revision (supervision by HC). Appeal (Ss.96–112) rehears on facts+law; these three are narrow, conditional, purpose-specific.

Statutory framework (1 mark): Reference = S.113 + O.46 Rr.1–7 (reasonable doubt/vires; HC opinion); Review = S.114 + O.47 Rr.1–9 (discovery/error/sufficient reason; same court); Revision = S.115 (jurisdictional error; HC only; no appeal lies; proviso + (2)+(3)).

Can there be second review? (2–3 marks): No. O.47 R.9 bars review of order on review / decree on review. Decision on review = issue decided → res judicata for successive petition. Successive applications on same ground = abuse. Remedy = appeal from grant (O.43 R.1(w)) / appeal from fresh decree / revision / writ. Rationale: review is exception to functus officio (O.20 R.3); endless reviews defeat finis litium.

Case law (2 marks): Haridas Das v. Usha Rani (2006) 3 SCALE 287 — SC set aside Gauhati HC review on imported O.2 R.2 plea; S.114 gives no ambit, O.47 does; error must be patent with no two opinions (Thungabhadra), strictly confined (Meera Bhanja), not erroneous merits (Aribam), not reasoning-detected (Parsion Devi); subsequent law change no ground (Explanation).

Conclusion (1 mark): Reference consults, review reconsiders, revision supervises — each with distinct forum, stage, grounds. Second review is expressly barred.

PYQ 2 — Q.4(b)/2019 (also Q.5(b)/2017, Q.6(a)/2018): “Identify tests for granting temporary injunction to preserve status quo. Discuss with case laws.” [S.94 + O.39 + S.151]

Why here: DU model answer invokes S.151 inherent powers (Manohar Lal) alongside O.39. Write O.39 tests + S.151 beyond O.39.

★ BEST MODEL ANSWER (condensed 10-mark — injunction + inherent)

Meaning/Object: Injunction = judicial process requiring party to do/refrain. Temporary (S.37 SRA + S.94(c) + O.39 Rr.1–2) preserves status quo till disposal; co-terminus with suit; either party may apply.

O.39 Rr.1–2 grounds: (i) property in dispute wasted/damaged/alienated; (ii) defendant threatens to dispossess / commit breach; (iii) other injury. Court may grant interim + notice + final injunction.

Three tests (Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh, AIR 1993 SC 276 + American Cyanamid): (1) Prima facie case (bona fide substantial question, not prima facie title; burden on plaintiff via affidavit/evidence); (2) Balance of convenience (comparative hardship); (3) Irreparable injury (not compensable in money). Add: clean hands, delay/laches, suppression relevant; ex parte ad-interim (R.3 proviso) only on urgency + recorded reasons + notice.

S.151 beyond O.39 — Manohar Lal (1962) (2 marks): S.94 supplemental; O.39 permissive (“may”), not prohibitory; HC/SC hold courts can issue injunction beyond O.39 under S.151 if not conflicting (Padam Sen complementary). But exceptional, cautious, procedural-only; restraining another competent court needs vexation proof + absolute necessity (Cohen; Hyman) — on facts injunction set aside. Also Ram Chand (abuse survives), Padam Sen (no substantive rights), Ramkarandas (no O.37 via S.151).

Consequences of disobedience: O.39 R.2A — attachment + detention (≤3 months) + compensatory; inherent to recall fraud orders.

PYQ 3 — Q.4(a)/2019: Mechalec Engineers v. Basic Equipment (AIR 1977 SC 577) — O.37 leave + S.115 interference [Revision use-case]

★ MODEL GIST (use when revision/discretion asked)

Facts: Dishonoured cheque; O.37 suit; trial court granted unconditional leave; HC in revision (S.115) held defence dishonest → conditional leave. Issue: Could HC interfere under S.115 with discretionary unconditional leave on reasonable grounds? Held: No — SC applied Kiranmoyee Dassi 5 classes (a–e): unconditional if good/fair defence or triable issue; conditional only if plausible-but-doubtful (time/mode, not money) or moonshine (money). Case not in class (e) → deposit condition unjustified; HC exceeded S.115 (no jurisdictional error in discretion reasonably exercised); trial order restored. Principle: discretionary orders reasonably exercised ≠ revisable; S.115 ≠ appeal.

Conclude: S.151 = residuary, complementary, justice-oriented, not jurisdiction-creating.

EXPECTED Q: “Order rejecting O.7 R.11 application — revisable? Discuss proviso to S.115(1) after 1999.” [5 marks]

Yes, revisable (if no appeal + jurisdictional error). Apply finally-disposed-of test: had O.7 R.11 been allowed (in favour of revision-petitioner defendant), plaint rejected → suit finally disposed. Hence proviso satisfied. Contrast: refusal of adjournment/amendment → suit continues → not revisable; remedy = S.105 objection in appeal from final decree or Art.227 exceptionally. Cite S.115(1) proviso + (2)+(3)+Explanation verbatim.

EXPECTED Q: “Subsequent Supreme Court decision overrules law on which decree based — review?” [3 marks]

No. Explanation to O.47 R.1 (1976): subsequent reversal/modification by superior court in another case = not ground. Review needs discovery/patent error/analogous reason existing at decree time. Remedy = appeal/review to superior court, or retrospective legislation (which IS analogous ground). Cite Haridas Das para 10.
