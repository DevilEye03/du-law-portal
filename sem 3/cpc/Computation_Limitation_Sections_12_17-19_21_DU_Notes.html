Computation of Limitation — The Concept and the Framework

Part III of the Limitation Act, 1963: the machinery that decides whether a proceeding is "late"
Core Idea
A limitation problem is never solved by the period alone. The court must compute the prescribed period: begin with the Schedule period for the right Article, fix its starting point (usually when the right to sue accrues), then apply the Act's adjustment machinery — exclusions of specified periods (ss. 12–15), postponement of the start (s. 17), fresh periods on acknowledgment or payment (ss. 18–19), and the deemed institution dates for added parties (s. 21). Only the resulting date can be tested against the bar of s. 3.

1.1  The Vocabulary (s. 2) — revision from Topic 1

  • "Period of limitation" = the period prescribed for any suit, appeal or application by the Schedule; "prescribed period" = the period computed in accordance with the provisions of this Act — i.e., after the s. 12–24 machinery has done its work.
  • Section 3 dismisses proceedings filed after the prescribed period; ss. 4–24 exist precisely to enlarge or postpone that period in the situations they govern (the bar of s. 3 is "subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24").
  • Where each section sits: s. 12 (copies & the first day) · ss. 13–15 (pauper leave, wrong-forum proceedings, injunctions/notices/stays — the exclusion family) · ss. 16–17 (death, fraud & mistake — starting-point rules) · ss. 18–20 (acknowledgment & payment — fresh-period rules) · s. 21 (added parties — deemed institution) · ss. 22–24 (continuing breaches, special damage, calendars).
  • Special laws: by s. 29(2) the ss. 4–24 machinery applies to periods prescribed by special or local laws unless expressly excluded — the hinge on which both Madan Lal Das (U.P. Sales Tax Act) and West Coast (Railways Act) turned.
The two moves a court can makeEverything in Part III does one of two things: (1) EXCLUDE a slice of time already elapsed (s. 12 — the day of judgment and the time for the copy; ss. 13–15 — time spent elsewhere in good faith), or (2) RESTART / POSTPONE the clock (s. 17 — from discovery of fraud or mistake; ss. 18–19 — a fresh period from acknowledgment or payment; s. 21 — a later deemed date for new parties, or an earlier one under the good-faith proviso). Keep this dichotomy in mind and every section finds its place.

Section 12 — Exclusion of Time in Legal Proceedings SUB-TOPIC (a) OF THE SYLLABUS

The first day, the judgment day, and the time requisite for copies — the appellate litigant's arithmetic
Section 12, Limitation Act, 1963 — Exclusion of time in legal proceedings
(1) In computing the period of limitation for any suit, appeal or application, the day from which such period is to be reckoned shall be excluded.
(2) In computing the period of limitation for an appeal or an application for leave to appeal or for revision or for review of judgment, the day on which the judgment complained of was pronounced and the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the decree, sentence or order appealed from or sought to be revised or reviewed shall be excluded.
(3) Where a decree or order is appealed from or sought to be revised or reviewed, or where an application is made for leave to appeal from a decree or order, the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the judgment shall also be excluded. [Words "on which the decree or order is founded" omitted by Act 46 of 1999, w.e.f. 1-7-2002.]
(4) In computing the period of limitation for an application to set aside an award, the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the award shall be excluded.
Explanation. In computing under this section the time requisite for obtaining a copy of a decree or an order, any time taken by the court to prepare the decree or order before an application for a copy thereof is made shall not be excluded.

2.1  What Each Sub-section Does

  • s.12(1) — the dies a quo rule: the starting day itself is never counted (the period runs from the next day). Applies to every suit, appeal and application.
  • s.12(2) — the appellate double exclusion: for appeals, leave-to-appeal, revision and review — (i) the day the judgment was pronounced, and (ii) the time requisite for obtaining a copy of the decree/sentence/order, are excluded. The 1963 Act adds revision to the 1908 list (Madan Lal Das).
  • s.12(3) — copies of the judgment: even where the decree/order copy is what is formally appealed from, the time for the judgment copy is also excluded.
  • s.12(4) — arbitration: for applications to set aside an award (now under ss. 33–34, Arbitration Act, 1996 as applied), time for the award copy is excluded.
  • The Explanation — the applicant's trap: the court's own delay in preparing the decree/order before a copy application is made is not excluded — apply for the copy promptly; only the time after the application is protected.

2.2  Scope & Working Rules (from the PYQ compendium)

  • True effect: "the periods referred to in the various sub-sections have to be added to the period of limitation for ascertaining the last date for filing the appeal" — exclusion works by extension backwards.
  • Plain reading of s.12: only for computing limitation for (1) an appeal; (2) an application for leave to appeal; (3) revision; (4) review — the day of judgment and the time for the decree copy are excluded (PYQ book).
  • Conduct matters: in determining "time requisite", the conduct of the appellant must be considered; no period is requisite which need not have elapsed had the appellant taken reasonable steps for the copy — but see Maharaj Narain: this does not import an obligation of promptitude.
  • Holidays & vacations: where judgment is pronounced just before holidays during which the appellant cannot apply for a copy, such holidays should be excluded; where judgment is delivered on the last working day before a vacation in which limitation would expire, the application for copies should be made on the re-opening day and the vacation counts as time requisite and is excluded; time during which the decree is not drawn up is excluded even though the copy application was made before drawing up (PYQ book, on Commissioner of Sales Tax v. Madan Lal Das).
  • Special laws: s.12(2) applies to revision under the U.P. Sales Tax Act via s.29(2), nothing therein expressly excluding it — Madan Lal Das.

2.3  "Time Requisite" — the Two Competing Readings (the exam's favourite duel)

The strict view (Lahore: Mathela v. Sher Mohammad, AIR 1935 Lah 682; accepted by the Allahabad HC in Maharaj Narain)

  • "Requisite" is a strong word — "properly required" (J.N. Surty, PC): it throws on the appellant the burden of showing that no part of the delay beyond the prescribed period is due to his fault.
  • Time requisite = the shortest time in which a copy could have been obtained by an appellant acting with reasonable promptitude and diligence — not the time he actually took.
  • Delay caused by the appellant's laches is not deductible (Pramatha Nath Roy v. Lee, 49 IA 307).

The liberal view (FB Madras: Panjam v. Trimala Reddy, ILR 57 Mad 560 — adopted by the Supreme Court)

  • Time requisite = the time beyond the party's control actually occupied in obtaining the copy which is filed with the memorandum — "not an ideal lesser period which might have been occupied if the application for the copy had been filed on some other date".
  • S.12(2) imposes no obligation to be prompt: the appellant may apply for the copy on the last day of the period and still deduct the time it takes (Hedge, J. in Maharaj Narain).
  • A minimum-time enquiry in every appeal would be "unworkable" — courts cannot go hunting for other copies the litigant may have obtained for other purposes.
The settled lawMaharaj Narain (AIR 1968 SC 960) adopts the liberal reading for appeals; Madan Lal Das (AIR 1977 SC 523) extends s.12(2) to revisions under special laws and holds the exclusion available even where the copy is not required to be filed with the application (following J.N. Surty, PC), and even where an earlier copy was lost and a fresh one had to be obtained. Together they make s.12(2) exclusion near-automatic once a copy was genuinely applied for and obtained for the proceeding.

Sections 17 to 21 — Fraud & Mistake, Acknowledgment, Payment, Added Parties SUB-TOPIC (b) OF THE SYLLABUS

Four engines that move the clock: postpone, restart, restart, re-date
Section 17 — Effect of fraud or mistake
17(1) Where in any suit/application for which a period of limitation is prescribed — (a) it is based upon the fraud of the defendant/respondent or his agent; or (b) the knowledge of the right or title is concealed by such fraud; or (c) it is for relief from the consequences of a mistake; or (d) a document necessary to establish the right has been fraudulently concealed — the period does not begin to run until the plaintiff/applicant has discovered the fraud or mistake, or could with reasonable diligence have discovered it; for concealed documents, until he first had the means of producing it or compelling its production.
Proviso: bonafide purchasers for value without notice of the fraud/mistake/concealment are protected.
17(2) Where the judgment-debtor by fraud or force prevents execution of a decree within limitation, the court may, on the creditor's application made within one year of discovery of the fraud or cessation of force, extend the period for execution.

3.1  Section 17 — Elements (PYQ compendium)

  • Object: to keep the right to sue suspended so long as the person is, without his own fault, ignorant of the fraud — "the right of a party defrauded cannot be affected by lapse of time... so long as he remains, without any fault of his own, in ignorance of the fraud".
  • Applies to suits and applications — not appeals; it is an enabling, postponing section (fixes the starting point, does not exclude elapsed time).
  • Three essentials to invoke: (i) fraud/mistake/concealment exists; (ii) it kept the plaintiff from knowledge of his right; (iii) the extension operates only against the person guilty of (or accessory to) the fraud — not against a bona fide purchaser for value.
  • Fraud (s.17 read with s.17, Contract Act): promise without intention to perform; act declared fraudulent by law; suggestion of falsehood; act fitted to deceive; active concealment.
  • Mistake of law: in refund-of-tax suits, the mistake "becomes known" when a competent court declares the levy void — Kanhaiya Lal; Mahabir Kishore — "it is seldom that a person can, even with reasonable diligence, discover a mistake of law before a judgment adjudging the validity of the law."

3.2  Section 18 — Acknowledgment in Writing

18(1) Where, before the expiration of the prescribed period for a suit/application in respect of any property or right, an acknowledgment of liability in respect of such property or right has been made in writing signed by the party against whom the right is claimed, or by a person through whom he derives title or liability, a fresh period of limitation is computed from the time the acknowledgment was signed.
18(2) Undated writing: oral evidence of the time of signing is allowed; but oral evidence of contents is not (subject to the Evidence Act).
Expl. (a) sufficient though it omits the exact nature of the property/right, avers time has not come, is coupled with refusal to pay or with a claim to set-off, or is addressed to a stranger; (b) "signed" = personally or by duly authorised agent; (c) execution applications are not applications "in respect of any property or right".
  • Six conditions (PYQ book): (1) made before expiry of the prescribed period — a barred debt cannot be revived (Sampuran Singh); (2) by the party liable or one through whom he derives title/liability; (3) in writing; (4) signed; (5) an admission of liability — no promise needed, refusal to pay is no obstacle, but a denial of liability is fatal; (6) may be made to anyone, not only the creditor.
  • Express or implied (Bhagwan v. Madhav, 46 Bom 1000); must distinctly relate to the liability claimed (Gopal Rao v. Harilal, 9 Bom LR 715).
  • Conscious liability: the person acknowledging must be conscious of his liability; if the facts constituting liability are admitted, acknowledgment may be inferred (State of Kerala v. T.M. Chacko, (2000) 9 SCC 722).

3.3  Section 19 — Payment on Account of Debt / Interest on Legacy

Where payment on account of a debt or of interest on a legacy is made before the expiration of the prescribed period by the person liable or his duly authorised agent, a fresh period of limitation is computed from the time the payment was made: Provided that an acknowledgment of the payment appears in the handwriting of, or in a writing signed by, the person making the payment.
Expl. (a) receipt of rent or produce of mortgaged land in mortgagee's possession is deemed payment; (b) "debt" does not include money payable under a decree or order of court.
  • Two ingredients: (i) the payment, and (ii) a writing recording it in the payer's hand or signed by him.
  • s.18 vs s.19 (PYQ book): not mutually exclusive — a defective s.19 payment can still save limitation as an s.18 acknowledgment if its conditions are met; s.18 covers any property or right, s.19 only debts and legacies; s.18 binds only the acknowledgor, s.19 payment operates against all persons liable for the debt; s.18 writing need not be addressed to the creditor, s.19 payment must be made to the person entitled; s.18 needs only an admission of liability, s.19 needs payment + record (Parasuraman v. Purushottaman & Co., AIR 1977 Ker 132).

3.4  Sections 20 & 21 — Agency for 18/19; Added Parties

Section 20 — acknowledgment or payment by another person
(1) For persons under disability, "agent duly authorised" includes guardian/committee/manager or their agent. (2) One of several joint contractors/partners/executors/mortgagees is not chargeable by reason only of another's acknowledgment/payment. (3) A Hindu limited owner's acknowledgment binds the reversioner; the manager's acknowledgment binds the whole Hindu undivided family.
Section 21 — substituting or adding new plaintiff or defendant
21(1) Where after institution of a suit a new plaintiff or defendant is substituted or added, the suit shall, as regards him, be deemed to have been instituted when he was so made a party: Provided the court, satisfied that the omission was due to a mistake made in good faith, may direct that the suit as regards him be deemed instituted on an earlier date.
21(2) Does not apply where a party is added/substituted owing to assignment or devolution of interest during the suit, or where a plaintiff is made a defendant (or vice versa).
  • Object of s.21: to let courts get over technical/procedural bottlenecks and implead necessary parties even after limitation, in the cause of real justice (PYQ book).
  • Good-faith proviso: the non-impleading must be a bona fide mistake, proved to the court's satisfaction — the court's satisfaction "breathes life in the suit" (Karuppaswamy v. C. Ramamurthy, AIR 1993 SC 2324; Ram Prasad Dagaduram v. Vijay Kumar, AIR 1967 SC 1178). The proviso is of a piece with s.14 (bona fide wrong forum) and s.17 (fraud/mistake).
  • s.21(2) logic: an assignee/legal representative steps into the shoes of the original party — no new limitation question arises; purchaser pendente lite comes on record within the decree's limitation, not from the purchase (K.S. Lokaiah v. M.B. Shivaraya, AIR 2007 NOC 430 (Kant)).
  • s.21 governs addition of parties only, not transposition; and not execution proceedings.

The Commissioner of Sales Tax, U.P. v. M/s. Madan Lal Das & Sons, Bareilly

AIR 1977 SC 523  |  Supreme Court of India  |  H.R. Khanna, J. (for the Bench)  |  Appeal by special leave from the Allahabad High Court
s.12(2) exclusions.29(2) special lawsrevision applicationlost copyJ.N. Surty (PC)U.P. Sales Tax Act

Facts in Detail

  • Assessment year 1960-61. The dealer-respondent (Madan Lal Das & Sons, Bareilly) appealed against a Sales Tax Officer's order; the Additional Commissioner (Judicial) Sales Tax disposed of the appeal.
  • 22-8-1965: the copy of the appellate order was served on the dealer under the Act. He then lost that copy.
  • 15-6-1966: he applied for another copy of the order. The copy was ready 17-8-1967 and delivered 18-8-1967.
  • 9-9-1967: he filed a revision under s.10 of the U.P. Sales Tax Act, 1940 before the Judge (Revision), Sales Tax.
  • The limitation rule: s.10(3-B) prescribed one year from service of the order, with power in the revising authority to entertain within a further six months on proof of sufficient cause.
  • The objection: the revenue contended the revision was out of time; the dealer claimed exclusion under s.12(2), Limitation Act, 1963 of the time spent obtaining the fresh copy.
  • The Judge (Revision) and the Allahabad High Court (on a referred question under s.11(3) of the Act) accepted the dealer's contention; the Commissioner appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  1. Does s.12(2) of the Limitation Act, 1963 apply to computing limitation for a revision application under a special law (the U.P. Sales Tax Act) — or does the special law form a "complete code" excluding it?
  2. Is the time for obtaining a copy excluded where the copy was not required to be filed along with the revision application?
  3. Where a copy had been served and lost, is the time spent obtaining a fresh copy nonetheless "requisite"?

Arguments for the Commissioner (appellant)

  • The U.P. Sales Tax Act is a complete code prescribing its own limitation for revisions; the Limitation Act's s.12(2) has no room.
  • The rules did not require a copy of the order to be filed with the revision petition — so no time for obtaining it could be "requisite".
  • A copy had already been served on the dealer under the Act; the loss was his own — the fresh copy was a luxury, not a requisite.

Arguments for Madan Lal Das (respondent)

  • s.29(2), Limitation Act: ss.4–24 apply to special-law periods "in so far as... not expressly excluded" — nothing in the Sales Tax Act expressly excluded s.12(2).
  • Without the copy, the dealer and his adviser could not decide whether to revise and on what grounds — the time was genuinely requisite.
  • The 1963 Act's s.12(2) covers revision expressly (the 1908 Act's did not) — the legislative design supports exclusion.

The Decision — H.R. Khanna, J.

  • Special law, but s.12(2) applies: the U.P. Sales Tax Act is a special law; by s.29(2) the provisions of s.12(2) apply to its limitation "in so far as and to the extent to which they are not expressly excluded" — and there was no express exclusion. Hence "the provisions of sub-section (2) of Section 12 can be invoked for computing the period of limitation prescribed for filing a revision petition under Section 10".
  • Copy need not be filed — still excluded: nothing in s.12(2) limits the exclusion to cases where the copy must accompany the application; it would be "impermissible to read in" such a proviso the legislature never enacted. "It is also plain that without procuring copy of the order... the respondent and his legal adviser would not have been in a position to decide as to whether [the] revision petition should be filed... and if so, what grounds should be taken."
  • The authority — J.N. Surty v. T.S. Chetiyar (AIR 1928 PC 103): the Privy Council held s.12(2) (of the 1908 Act) applies even where the rules did not require the copy to be filed with the memorandum of appeal: the section "makes no reference to the Code of Civil Procedure or to any other Act. It does not say why the time is to be excluded, but simply enacts it as a positive direction." The decree "may be complicated... the practitioner may well want to see its form before attacking it"; the Legislature "may not wish him to hurry to make a decision till he has well considered it."
  • The precedent-line: FB Lahore (Punjab Co-operative Bank v. Official Liquidators, AIR 1941 Lah 257 — Companies Act appeals); FB Patna (Mt. Lalitkumari v. Mahaprasad N. Singh, AIR 1947 Pat 329 — Letters Patent appeals); SC (Additional Collector of Customs v. Best & Co., AIR 1966 SC 1713).
  • On the lost copy — Maharaj Narain applied a fortiori: there, three copies obtained on different dates, and the SC excluded the time for the copy filed with the memorandum (which took the maximum time), refusing a minimum-time enquiry. "If that be the position of law in a case where there was no allegation of the loss of any copy, a fortiori... where the copy served upon a party is lost and there is no alternative except to apply for a fresh copy... the time spent in obtaining that copy would necessarily have to be excluded under Section 12(2)."
  • Result: the High Court correctly answered the referred question for the dealer — time for the fresh copy excluded; revision within time.

PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. s.29(2) gateway: a special law's limitation period is computed with the full ss.4–24 machinery (including s.12(2)) unless expressly excluded — "complete code" arguments fail in the absence of express exclusion.
  2. Exclusion does not depend on filing requirements: the time for obtaining a copy is excluded even if the copy need not accompany the application — s.12(2) is "a positive direction" (J.N. Surty).
  3. Lost copies: where the served copy is lost, the time for obtaining a fresh copy is necessarily requisite and excluded (a fortiori from Maharaj Narain).
  4. The 1963 Act extends s.12(2) to revisions expressly — a deliberate widening of the 1908 position.

CASE 32 — DU CASE MATERIAL · SUB-TOPIC (a): SECTION 12

State of Uttar Pradesh v. Maharaj Narain

AIR 1968 SC 960  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Hedge, J.  |  Appeal by certificate from the Allahabad High Court  |  s.12(2), Indian Limitation Act, 1908 (Item 157)
"time requisite"three copiesno duty of promptitudePanjam FBMathela disapprovedcriminal appeal from acquittal

Facts in Detail

  • The respondents were tried for various offences before the Assistant Sessions Judge, Farrukhabad, who acquitted them on 10-11-1962.
  • The State appealed to the Allahabad High Court. Item 157, First Schedule (Act 1908): an appeal under CrPC 1898 from an order of acquittal lies within three months from the date of the order; s.12(2) excluded the day of judgment and the time requisite for a copy.
  • The memorandum of appeal was filed on 29-3-1963. The copy of the order filed with the memorandum showed: applied for 15-11-1962, ready 3-1-1963 — on that footing the appeal was within time.
  • But the State had obtained two more copies: one applied for on 3-12-1962, ready 20-12-1962; yet another applied for on 21-12-1962, ready the same day. Computing the three months from the earliest available copy (20/21-12-1962), the appeal (29-3-1963) was out of time.
  • Allahabad High Court: "requisite" means "properly required" — limitation must be computed on the basis of the copies made available in December 1962; appeal dismissed as time-barred. The State appealed by certificate — purely to settle the question of law.

Issue

The sole question (Hedge, J.)"The only question that arises for decision is as to the true scope of the expression 'time requisite for obtaining a copy of the decree, sentence or order appealed from'" in s.12(2) — do other, earlier copies obtained by the appellant for other purposes curtail the exclusion?"

Arguments for the State (appellant)

  • The copy filed with the memorandum governs: its timeline showed the appeal within time.
  • S.12(2) imposes no obligation of promptitude — the appellant could have waited till the period was nearly exhausted and still deducted the copy time (a position not disputed by the other side).
  • Copies obtained for other purposes are irrelevant — the court has "nothing to do" with them.

Arguments for the respondents

  • "Requisite" = "properly required" (J.N. Surty, PC) — once copies were available in December 1962, no further time was properly required; the delay after 20-12-1962 was the State's own laches.
  • The Lahore rule (Mathela): time requisite is the shortest time in which a diligent appellant could have got a copy — not the time actually taken for the copy he chose to file.
  • Limitation should not be enlarged by the litigant's choice of the slower copy.

The Decision — Hedge, J.

  • No promptitude obligation: it was "not disputed that it was not necessary for the appellant to apply for a copy... immediately after the order was pronounced. The appellant could have, if it chose to take the risk, waited till the ninety days period allowed to it by the statute was almost exhausted" — and still deducted the copy time. Hence "time requisite" cannot mean the time absolutely necessary, nor the minimum time in which a copy could have been obtained.
  • The plain rule: s.12(2) "permits the appellant to deduct from the time taken for filing the appeal, the time required for obtaining the copy of the order appealed from and not any lesser period which might have been occupied if the application for the copy had been filed at some other date... There is no justification for restricting the scope of that provision."
  • The workability test: if appellate courts had to find the minimum time in every case, they would have to enquire into "other copies which he might have got and used for other purposes with which the court has nothing to do" — "a great deal of confusion" would follow; the rule would be "unworkable".
  • Mathela v. Sher Mohammad disapproved: the Lahore reading — reasonable promptitude + shortest time — "does not lay down the law correctly"; no such requirements can be "spelt out" from s.12(2)'s language.
  • The PC cases confined to their facts: Pramatha Nath Roy v. Lee (49 IA 307) — the appellant there was guilty of laches; time lost by laches is not deductible — the case "does not bear on the question". J.N. Surty (55 IA 161) — "requisite is a strong word... 'properly required'... throws upon the pleader... the necessity of showing that no part of the delay... is due to his fault" — means only that delay due to the pleader's default cannot be deducted; "there can be no question of any default if the steps taken by the appellant are in accordance with law".
  • The anchor — FB Madras, Panjam v. Trimala Reddy (ILR 57 Mad. 560): "the words 'time requisite for obtaining a copy of the decree' mean the time beyond the party's control occupied in obtaining the copy which is filed with the memorandum of appeal and not an ideal lesser period which might have been occupied if the application for the copy had been filed on some other date" — followed in Travancore-Cochin (Kunju Kesavan, AIR 1953 TC 552), Allahabad (B. Govind Raj Sewak Singh, AIR 1950 All 486) and MP (K.U. Singh, AIR 1960 MP 140).
  • Result: the High Court's decision "does not lay down the law correctly" — but the State did not press the appeal on merits (it had come only to settle the law); appeal dismissed, the rule of law vindicated.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. "Time requisite" = the time actually taken, beyond the party's control, to obtain the copy filed with the memorandum of appeal — not the minimum or ideal time (Panjam FB, affirmed).
  2. No obligation of promptitude in applying for a copy: a litigant may apply on the last day of limitation and still exclude the copy time.
  3. Copies obtained for other purposes cannot curtail the exclusion; a minimum-time enquiry in every appeal would be unworkable.
  4. What the strict line still polices: laches and the pleader's own default — delay genuinely attributable to the appellant's fault is not deductible.
  5. Read with Madan Lal Das: the exclusion extends to revisions under special laws, and to fresh copies after loss — s.12(2) is a beneficent, generously construed provision.

Mahabir Kishore v. State of Madhya Pradesh

AIR 1990 SC 313  |  Supreme Court of India  |  K.N. Saikia, J.  |  Plaintiffs' appeal by special leave from the M.P. High Court
s.17(1)(c) mistakemistake of laws.72 Contract Actunjust enrichmentArt. 113seven days late?

Facts in Detail

  • The appellant firm (Rai Saheb Nandkishore Rai Saheb Jugalkishore) was allotted contracts for manufacture and sale of liquor — calendar year 1959 (for ₹2,56,200) and 1-1-1960 to 31-3-1961 (for ₹4,71,900) — by the Government of M.P., which also charged 7½% over the auction money as "mahua and fuel cess".
  • Writ petitions challenging the charge were pending; the government announced it would continue collecting pending its decision, which would bind the contractors. The firm paid a total extra ₹54,606.
  • 24-4-1959 (reported 1960 MPLJ 39): M.P. High Court in Surajdin v. State of M.P. declared the 7½% collection illegal — yet the government continued to charge it.
  • 31-8-1961 (reported 1962 MPLJ 130): the High Court again held the charge illegal (N.K. Doongaji v. Collector, Surguja). The appellants say they learnt of this only in or about September 1962.
  • 17-10-1961: the government internally decided not to charge the extra 7½% any more — letter Ex. D-23, never communicated to the appellants or any contractor.
  • 17-10-1964: s.80 CPC notice for refund of ₹54,606; 24-12-1964: Civil Suit No. 1-B of 1964 filed before the Additional District Judge, Jabalpur.
  • Trial court: Arts. 62 & 96 of the 1908 Act applied — limitation ran from the dates of payment — suit barred. High Court: Art. 113 read with s.17 of the 1963 Act (not Art. 24) — limitation ran from 17-10-1961 (the uncommunicated letter); with the two months of s.80 CPC, the suit expired 17-12-1964 — seven days late; "unfortunate" but barred. Leave to appeal refused; the firm came to the SC by special leave.

Issues

  1. What is the correct starting point of limitation for a suit for refund of money paid under a mistake of law — the date of payment, the date of the government's internal decision, or the plaintiff's knowledge of the mistake?
  2. Does s.17(1)(c) require actual discovery, or does "could with reasonable diligence have discovered" start the clock from an uncommunicated internal order?
  3. Which Article governs (Art. 113 / Art. 24 / Arts. 62–96 of the old Act), and how does s.72, Contract Act interact with the Limitation Act?

Arguments for Mahabir Kishore (appellants)

  • The letter of 17-10-1961 was purely internal, never communicated — the appellants had no opportunity to know of it; limitation cannot start from a fact locked in a government drawer.
  • Under s.17(1)(c), in mistake-of-law cases knowledge ordinarily comes only with a judgment declaring the law void; at the least, "a week" must be allowed for knowledge to percolate — making the suit timely.
  • Their statement that they knew only in September 1962 (after Doongaji) was never disproved; on that footing too, the suit (December 1964) was within three years.
  • The payments were made under a mistake common to both parties — the State was unjustly enriched; restitution under s.72, Contract Act should not be defeated by a technical starting point.

Arguments for the State of M.P. (respondent)

  • 17-10-1961 was the date the government finally decided to stop the extra charge — the right to refund then crystallised; the HC rightly ran limitation from that date.
  • Even if not communicated, the contractors with reasonable diligence could have known of the decision on that very date.
  • On either Art. 24 or Art. 113, the suit of 24-12-1964 was barred (by seven days on the HC's reckoning).
  • The payments were made voluntarily, pending a declared government policy — no "mistake" should reset the clock.

The Decision — K.N. Saikia, J.

  • Characterising the suit: the levy was charged "believing that it had power, but the High Court in two cases held that the power was not there. The money realised was under a mistake and without authority of law. The appellants also while paying suffered from the same mistake... the suit was for refund of money paid under mistake of law." Restitution tracks the three elements of unjust enrichment — enrichment, at the plaintiff's expense, unjust retention.
  • s.72 Contract Act includes mistake of law — STO v. Kanhaiya Lal (AIR 1959 SC 135): money paid under mistake of law is recoverable, subject to estoppel, waiver, limitation; and on limitation, s.17(1)(c) applies — three years from knowledge of the mistake ("within 3 years of the knowledge of the mistake").
  • The settled rule (para 28): "in a suit for refund of money paid under mistake of law, Section 72 of the Contract Act is applicable and the period of limitation is three years under Article 113... and the provisions of Section 17(1)(c)... will be applicable so that the period will begin to run from the date of knowledge of the particular law... being declared void; and this could be the date of the judgment of competent court declaring that law void."
  • The authorities run one way: D. Cawasji & Co. (AIR 1975 SC 813 — start from the judgment declaring the law void); Shri Vallabh Glass Works (AIR 1984 SC 971 — s.72 + s.17(1)(c) + Art. 113); CST v. Auraiya Chamber ((1986) 3 SCC 50 — mistake of law within s.72; no estoppel where the mistake is common to both); Salonah Tea ((1988) 1 SCC 401 — "no State has the right to receive or to retain taxes... without the authority of law"; limitation from knowledge, i.e., the Loong Soong judgment). And Madras Port Trust v. Hymanshu International ((1979) 4 SCC 176, Bhagwati J.): public authorities should not fling technical limitation pleas at just claims.
  • Why the HC's start-date failed: the 17-10-1961 letter "was purely an internal communication... never communicated to the appellants or other liquor contractors. There could, therefore, be no question of the limitation starting from that date. Even with reasonable diligence, as envisaged in Section 17(1)(c), the appellants would have taken at least a week to know about it" — and State counsel conceded there was nothing to show they knew of it on that date or within a reasonable time thereafter.
  • Two saving computations: (i) knowledge a week after 17-10-1961, or (ii) knowledge in September 1962 (after Doongaji — the appellants' undisputed statement) — on either, the suit was within three years under Art. 113.
  • Result: High Court set aside; appeal allowed; suit remanded for decision on merits, expeditiously; costs to the appellants.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. Mistake of law postpones limitation under s.17(1)(c): the clock for a refund suit runs from the plaintiff's knowledge of the mistake — usually the date of the judgment declaring the levy void — not from the payment, nor from an uncommunicated internal order.
  2. "Could with reasonable diligence have discovered" gives a small, realistic allowance (here, at least a week) — not an assumption of omniscience.
  3. Unjust enrichment + s.72, Contract Act is the restitution engine for taxes collected without authority of law; the Limitation Act accommodates it through Art. 113 + s.17(1)(c).
  4. An undisputed assertion of knowledge (September 1962) supplies the start-date; the burden lay on the State to disprove it.
  5. Courts frown on public authorities raising limitation technicalities against just refund claims (Hymanshu; Salonah Tea).

Sampuran Singh v. Smt. Niranjan Kaur

AIR 1999 SC 1047  |  Supreme Court of India  |  A.P. Misra, J.  |  Appeal from the Punjab & Haryana High Court  |  Oral mortgage of 1893 — suit of 1980
s.18 acknowledgmentrevival only in timeoral mortgage validityArt. 61(a) — 60/30 yearsright to redeem accrues at once₹53 mortgage

Facts in Detail

  • 21-3-1893: Rekha and others mortgaged 37 kanals 15 marlas of land in Village Sambhli, Tehsil & District Karnal (Khewat 260, Khatauni 448) orally, for ₹53, to Bakhatwara, Raju and Matu — possession delivered; mutation sanctioned. (A mortgage of under ₹100 was then valid in Punjab either by registered instrument or by delivery of possession — the Transfer of Property Act reached Punjab only on 1-11-1956, and Haryana on 5-8-1967.)
  • 1959: the appellants (purchasers from the original mortgagors) bought the equity in the land by three registered sale deeds; the mortgagors remained in possession through tenants till 1960-61.
  • 11-1-1960: the mortgagee Matu and Smt. Dasondha sold their mortgagee rights by registered deed to the respondents, who thereby acknowledged the existence of the mortgage in the deed.
  • 1980: the appellants sued for possession by redemption on payment of ₹53. The respondents pleaded the suit was hopelessly time-barred — they were owners, the mortgagee rights having been transferred to them outright; only limitation was pressed at every stage.
  • Trial court: decreed redemption — reliance on Inder Singh v. Kishno ((1966) 68 Punj LR 408): for an unregistered/oral mortgage, limitation begins only after 12 years from the mortgage (when the mortgagee's possession matures into a valid mortgage); 1893 + 12 = 1905; + 60 years = 1965; the acknowledgment of 11-1-1960 was within that period → fresh limitation from 1960 → the 1980 suit in time.
  • First appellate court: reversed — oral mortgages were valid in Punjab at the relevant time; principal < ₹100; mortgage with possession complete on the day of execution (March 1893) → 60 years expired in 1953 → suit barred.
  • Punjab & Haryana High Court (second appeal): relied on its Full Bench decision Shri Chand v. Nathi ((1983) 85 Punj LR 288), which had overruled Inder Singh — appeal dismissed. The mortgagors appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  1. When did the limitation for a redemption suit on the 1893 oral mortgage begin — at execution, or 12 years later (when possession might have "perfected" an invalid mortgage)?
  2. Could the acknowledgment of the mortgage contained in the 1960 registered sale deed give a fresh period of limitation under s.18 — even though the original 60-year period had already expired in 1953?
  3. Does the "right to redeem" accrue only on tender of the mortgage money (or the mortgagee's communication of satisfaction by usufruct), as Art. 61(a)'s "when the right to redeem accrues" might suggest?

Arguments for Sampuran Singh (mortgagor-appellants)

  • s.18 revival: the respondents, in the registered deed of 11-1-1960, acknowledged the mortgage; a fresh period should run from that date, making the 1980 suit timely.
  • Inder Singh line: for an unregistered mortgage, possession perfects a valid mortgage only after 12 years — limitation (60 years) could not start before 1905; the acknowledgment of 1960 fell within the live period.
  • Approbate-reprobate (C. Beepathuma, AIR 1965 SC 241): the respondents took the benefit of the mortgage under the deed — they cannot deny the mortgagors' corresponding right to redeem.
  • The Redemption of Mortgages (Punjab) Act, 1913 first gave a redemption procedure — limitation for redemption could not run before 1913.
  • Under Art. 61(a), the right to redeem accrues only when the mortgagor tenders the amount or the mortgagee communicates satisfaction — neither had happened, so time never began.

Arguments for Niranjan Kaur (respondents)

  • The oral mortgage of 1893 was valid when made (Punjab law; < ₹100; possession delivered) — the right to redeem accrued from day one, and 60 years expired in 1953.
  • s.18's opening words require the acknowledgment to be made "before the expiration of the prescribed period" — an acknowledgment of 1960 cannot revive a period dead since 1953.
  • The Full Bench in Shri Chand v. Nathi had already overruled Inder Singh, which had misapplied Purusottam Das v. S.M. Desouza (AIR 1950 Ori 213 — a case of an invalid unregistered mortgage where the TPA applied).
  • The respondents purchased the mortgagee rights outright — they claim as owners, not as mortgagees bound to account.

The Decision — A.P. Misra, J.

  • Validity of the oral mortgage — Shri Chand FB affirmed: "there was no bar to give effect to an oral mortgage in a case where a mortgagor gave possession of the land to a mortgagee"; TPA s.59 had not reached Punjab/Haryana then; registration was "wholly irrelevant with regard to the issue of its validity". Inder Singh was rightly overruled: it misapplied Purusottam Das (where the TPA did apply and the mortgage was invalid). "We find no error committed in coming to the said decision by the Full Bench."
  • The s.18 kill-shot: "Section 18 sub-section (1) itself starts with the words: 'Where, before the expiration of the prescribed period for a suit or application in respect of any property or right, an acknowledgment of liability... has been made...'. Thus, the acknowledgment, if any, has to be prior to the expiration of the prescribed period... if the limitation has already expired, it would not revive under this section." With 60 years from March 1893 expiring in 1953, the 1960 acknowledgment came too late.
  • Approbate-reprobate rejected: C. Beepathuma concerns a person accepting benefit under an instrument while renouncing its obligations — here "there is neither any deed nor document of mortgage"; the only obligation of an oral mortgagee is to return possession when the mortgage money is paid, and the mortgagor never tendered it. The doctrine of election is irrelevant.
  • The 1913 Act argument rejected: the Redemption of Mortgages (Punjab) Act, 1913 was enacted to give the Deputy Commissioner powers of restoration (mirroring s.7(5) of the Punjab Alienation of Land Act) and a summary procedure — it "has no correlation with the period of limitation"; and even from 1913, sixty years expired in 1973 — the 1980 suit was still late.
  • Art. 61(a) argument rejected — right to redeem accrues at once: "Right accruing means, right either existing or coming into play thereafter. Where no period in the mortgage is specified, there exists a right to a mortgagor to redeem the mortgage by paying the amount that very day... or any day thereafter." The tender/satisfaction theory would mean limitation "never start[s] running... for an infinite period" — rejected. (At the relevant time the period was 60 years; the present Art. 61(a) prescribes 30.)
  • Result: limitation ran from March 1893 and expired in 1953; no revival; the first appellate court and the High Court were right — appeal dismissed.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. s.18 revives only a living period: an acknowledgment of liability (or a part payment under s.19) made after the prescribed period has expired confers no fresh limitation — the opening words of s.18(1) are a condition precedent, not decoration.
  2. Validity first, limitation second: the court asks only whether the mortgage is valid; if valid (as an oral mortgage with possession then was in Punjab), the right to redeem accrues on the day of its execution — no 12-year incubation, no waiting for tender or satisfaction.
  3. Art. 61(a)'s "when the right to redeem... accrues" means the day the mortgagor could lawfully have paid and demanded possession — the very first day, absent a contractual restriction; otherwise limitation would run "for an infinite period".
  4. Local-law context matters: TPA s.59's registration requirement applied to Punjab only from 1-11-1956 (Haryana 5-8-1967) — earlier oral mortgages with possession stand valid (Shri Chand FB).
  5. Equitable doctrines (election; approbate-reprobate) cannot stretch a statutory limitation period; nor can procedural statutes like the Punjab Redemption Act, 1913 postpone its start.

PYQ — 2017
Q. 7(b)/2017 — “The expression ‘time requisite’ under section 12(2) of the Limitation Act cannot be understood as the time necessary for obtaining the copy of the order.” Discuss the statement in the light of the decided cases.
MODEL ANSWER — EXCLUSION OF TIME (SECTION 12(2))

The provision. Section 12 provides for exclusion of time in computing limitation. Its true effect: the periods referred to in the sub-sections have to be added to the period of limitation for ascertaining the last date of filing. The section excludes (a) the day from which the period is reckoned, and (b) the time requisite for obtaining copies of the judgment/order/decree, for an appeal, an application for leave to appeal, revision or review.

What ‘time requisite’ means. It means “properly required”, and it throws on the appellant the necessity of showing that no part of the delay beyond the prescribed period was due to his default. It is not the time absolutely necessary or the minimum time in which a copy could have been obtained — what is deductible is the time actually taken for obtaining the copy which is filed with the memorandum of appeal.

The conduct rule. In determining ‘time requisite’, the conduct of the appellant must be considered: no period is requisite which need not have elapsed had he taken reasonable and proper steps to requisition the copy. It is a question of fact determined on the facts of each case.

Working rules from the case law: (i) where judgment is pronounced just before holidays during which a copy cannot be applied for, the holidays are excluded; (ii) where judgment is delivered on the last working day before a vacation on which limitation would expire, an application on the reopening day suffices and the vacation counts as time requisite; (iii) time during which the decree is not drawn up is excluded even if the copy application was not made before the decree was drawn.

Case 1 — Commissioner of Sales Tax, U.P. v. M/s. Madan Lal Das & Sons, AIR 1977 SC 523 (3 judges, Khanna J.). Copy of the sales-tax appellate order served 22-8-1965; the assessee lost it; applied 15-6-1966 for a fresh copy, ready 17-8-1967, delivered 18-8-1967; revision filed 9-9-1967 (limitation: 1 year from service + 6 months condonable under s.10 U.P. Sales Tax Act). Held: s.12(2) of the 1963 Act is attracted via s.29(2) (the special law does not exclude it); and even though a copy was not required to be filed with the revision, the time for obtaining the fresh copy was excluded — a practitioner may reasonably want to study the decree before attacking it (J.N. Surty, AIR 1928 PC 103 — exclusion where there is a “positive direction” to file, and a fortiori where the served copy is lost). A fortiori from Maharaj Narain: if time for a copy one need not obtain is excluded, time to replace a lost served copy is necessarily excluded.

Case 2 — State of U.P. v. Maharaj Narain, AIR 1968 SC 960. Acquittal 10-11-1962; appeal in the High Court 29-3-1963 (limitation: 3 months). Copy applied 15-11-1962, ready 3-1-1963 (timely if this copy governs); two other copies obtained earlier in December. The High Court read ‘requisite’ as ‘properly required’ and computed limitation by the December copies — appeal barred. Supreme Court (Hegde J.) reversed: the State was under no duty of promptitude — it could wait till the 90 days were nearly exhausted before applying; the time requisite is “the time beyond the party’s control occupied in obtaining the copy which is filed with the memorandum of appeal” (Panjam FB, ILR 57 Mad 560), not an ideal lesser period. A minimum-time enquiry would compel appellate courts to chase copies “used for other purposes with which the court has nothing to do” — unworkable. The whole period from application (15-11-1962) to receipt of the filed copy (3-1-1963) was excluded — appeal within time. (Mathela, AIR 1935 Lah 682, which demanded minimum time, “does not lay down the law correctly”; Pramatha Nath Roy, 49 IA 307, is a laches case and “does not bear on the question”.)

Conclusion. ‘Time requisite’ is a practical, fact-centric concept — the time actually occupied in getting the copy actually used — disciplined only by the appellant’s own conduct; it is emphatically not a notional minimum, and Maharaj Narain is the charter of that reading.

PYQ — 2019
Q. 7(a)/2019 — What is the effect of addition of plaintiff or defendant in a pending suit upon the limitation for filing such suit?
MODEL ANSWER — EFFECT OF ADDITION/SUBSTITUTION OF PARTIES (SECTION 21)

The provision. Section 21 deals with the effect of substituting or adding a new plaintiff or defendant — it applies to all persons brought on record after the expiry of limitation. It covers the suit stage up to decree by the trial court and the appellate stage; it does not govern execution proceedings, nor transposition of parties (a defendant made plaintiff or vice versa is not ‘addition’).

Object. The emerging concept of justice empowers courts to get over technical and procedural bottlenecks; s.21 lets courts implead absolutely necessary parties even after limitation has expired, so that the matter is decided on merits.

s.21(1) — the general rule. Where a new plaintiff or defendant is added or substituted, the suit shall, as regards him, be deemed to have been instituted when he was made a party — i.e., the fresh party’s presence dates from his addition, and if limitation had already run, the suit qua him is late.

Proviso — the good-faith escape. Where the court is satisfied that the omission to include the new plaintiff/defendant was due to a mistake made in good faith, it may direct that the suit as regards him be deemed instituted on the earlier date. Ram Prasad Dagaduram v. Vijay Kumar, AIR 1967 SC 1178: to earn the proviso the plaintiff must prove the non-impleadment was a bona fide mistake; the discretion is then exercised to relate the addition back and save the suit. (s.21(1) is confined to plaintiffs added in their own right; a plaintiff added through assignment from the original plaintiff falls under s.21(2).)

s.21(2) — the exceptions. s.21(1) has no application where a party is added or substituted owing to (a) assignment or (b) devolution of interest during the pendency of the suit — the newcomer steps into the shoes of the assignor/deceased, and no new limitation question arises.

Leading cases. (i) Karuppaswamy v. C. Ramamurthy, AIR 1993 SC 2324 — suit filed against a defendant who had in fact died six weeks earlier; the plaintiff knew of the death only from the process server’s endorsement, and immediately applied under O.XXI CPC r.4 to bring in the legal representatives. Held: the plaintiff acted promptly and in good faith; the proviso to s.21(1) was attracted and the substitution related back to the original institution. The Court read the proviso as kindred to s.14 (bona fide proceedings without jurisdiction) and s.17 (fraud/mistake discovered later): all three soften limitation’s edge for the diligent but mistaken suitor; an averment of good faith must be made and proved, for “only the court’s satisfaction breathes life into the suit”. (ii) K.S. Lokaiah v. M.B. Shivaraya, AIR 2007 NOC 430 (Kant) — a purchaser pendente lite impleaded in execution steps into the decree-holder’s shoes and may come on record within 12 years of the decree (not of his purchase); s.21(2), not s.21(1), applies.

Conclusion. Addition generally restarts the suit qua the new party (s.21(1)); good faith plus diligence buys relation-back through the proviso; assignment/devolution cases ride on the original institution (s.21(2)).

PYQ — 2019
Q. 7(b)/2019 — Describe what is an “acknowledgement” under the Limitation Act. What is the effect of “acknowledgement” on limitation? Discuss with illustrations.
MODEL ANSWER — ACKNOWLEDGMENT (SECTION 18)

Meaning. An acknowledgment is a definite, clear admission of existing liability in respect of any property or right — not necessarily a promise to pay; the simple admission of a debt suffices. It creates no new right of action; it only enlarges time: a fresh period runs from the date of the acknowledgment. An acknowledgment of an already barred debt gives the creditor nothing.

Six conditions of validity.

  1. Before expiry — it must be made before the expiration of the prescribed period; once the period has expired it “would not revive” (Sampuran Singh v. Niranjan Kaur, AIR 1999 SC 1047 — deed of 11-1-1960 could not revive a 60-year mortgage period that died in 1953).
  2. By the right person — by the party against whom the right is claimed, or a person through whom he derives title or liability; an acknowledgment by a person not then liable is a nullity.
  3. In writing — if undated, oral evidence of the time of signing is admissible (s.18(2)); but oral evidence of its contents is barred.
  4. Signed — by the party or his duly authorised agent (Explanation (b): ‘signed’ = personally or by agent authorised in that behalf).
  5. An admission of liability — not necessarily a promise; may be express or implied (Bhagwan v. Madhav, 46 Bom 1000); it must distinctly relate to the liability claimed (Gopal Rao v. Harilal, 9 Bom LR 715). It may even be coupled with a refusal to pay, a set-off claim, or a statement that time has not yet come — yet survive (Explanation (a)) — but a statement implying no liability is not an acknowledgment.
  6. Any addressee — it need not be made to the creditor; even a stranger will do.

Agents and incapables. A guardian/manager of a person under disability, a duly authorised agent, the manager of a joint Hindu family (within his borrowing authority), and a legal practitioner acting for a client can each sign a valid acknowledgment. But the Official Assignee is not the agent of the insolvent, and the Official Receiver is likewise not technically an agent — their acknowledgments do not save limitation.

Illustrations. (1) A debtor’s letter: “I owe you ₹40,000” — valid acknowledgment, fresh 3 years from its date. (2) A balance-sheet signed by a company’s authorised officer showing the creditor’s dues — valid (State of Kerala v. T.M. Chacko, (2000) 9 SCC 722: a fresh period is computed from when the acknowledgment was signed). (3) A mortgagor’s heir writing in 1960 acknowledging an 1893 mortgage whose 60 years expired in 1953 — no revival (Sampuran Singh). (4) An unsigned diary entry or oral admission — not an acknowledgment; s.18 demands writing and signature.

Effect. On a valid acknowledgment, limitation is reset: a fresh period (the same as the original) runs from the acknowledgment’s date — against the maker and those claiming under him, not against co-debtors who did not sign.

PYQ — 2017
Q. 8(a)/2017 — Discuss the effect of fraud and mistake on the period of limitation.
MODEL ANSWER — EFFECT OF FRAUD OR MISTAKE (SECTION 17)

The provision. Under s.17, limitation is computed from the time the fraud became known to the person defrauded; where a document necessary to establish the right has been fraudulently concealed, from when he first had the means of producing or compelling its production; and where the suit is for relief from the consequences of a mistake, from when the mistake was discovered (or could have been discovered with reasonable diligence).

Object and principle. The object is to keep the right to sue suspended so long as the person is unaware of the fraud. The principle: the right of a party defrauded cannot be affected by lapse of time while he remains, without fault of his own, ignorant of the fraud; once the circumstances constituting the fraud become known, subsequent lapse operates as a bar.

Application. The section applies to suits and applications, not appeals; it is enabling — it postpones the starting point. It covers: (a) suits based on the fraud of the defendant/respondent or his agent; (b) concealment of knowledge of right or title; (c) suits for relief from mistake; (d) fraudulent concealment of documents. In the first three, time runs from discovery (actual or with reasonable diligence); in the fourth, from when production was first possible. Bona fide purchasers for value without notice remain protected.

Three essentials to invoke the section: (i) there has been fraud; (ii) by means of it the plaintiff was kept from knowledge of his right to sue or of the title on which it is founded; (iii) extension operates only against the person guilty of fraud or an accessory — not a transferee in good faith and for value.

Ingredients of fraud (to plead it): (i) a promise made without intention of performing; (ii) an act or omission the law specifically declares fraudulent; (iii) suggesting as a fact that which is not true, by one who does not believe it true; (iv) any other act fitted to deceive; (v) active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of it. Absent fraud by the defendant, the section gives the plaintiff nothing.

s.17(2). Where execution of a decree/order in time was prevented by the fraud or force of the judgment-debtor, the court may, on the creditor’s application made within one year of discovering the fraud (or cessation of force), extend the period for execution.

Leading case — Mahabir Kishore v. State of M.P., AIR 1990 SC 313. Liquor contractors paid ₹54,606 as an illegal 7½% cess; the government’s internal letter of 17-10-1961 stopping the cess was never communicated; the contractors knew only by September 1962 (after Doongaji); refund suit filed 24-12-1964. Trial court (payments → Arts. 62/96, old Act) and High Court (17-10-1961 + s.17 → 7 days late) both held the suit barred. Supreme Court (Saikia J.) allowed the appeal: the suit was one for refund of money paid under mistake of law; s.72 Contract Act (which includes mistake of law) plus Art. 113 plus s.17(1)(c) govern — three years from knowledge, typically the judgment declaring the law void; limitation cannot start from an uncommunicated internal letter, and even on ‘reasonable diligence’ the appellants would take at least a week — on either computation (a week after 17-10-1961, or September 1962) the suit was in time. Principle: the clock of limitation runs against knowledge, not against the calendar of the wrongdoer.

PYQ — 2017
Q. 8(b)/2017 — Distinguish between section 18 and section 19 of the Limitation Act, 1963.
MODEL ANSWER — ACKNOWLEDGMENT (s.18) vs PAYMENT (s.19)

The two provisions. s.18: an acknowledgment in writing signed of liability in respect of any property or right, made before expiry, gives a fresh period from its date. s.19: where payment on account of a debt (or of interest) or a legacy is made before expiry — evidenced in writing signed by the payer or his agent — a fresh period runs from the payment.

The Kerala formulation — Parasuraman v. Purushottaman & Co., AIR 1977 Ker 132: “As in the case of an acknowledgment under section 18, a payment under section 19 is also required to be recorded in writing. But unlike payment under section 18, the writing must contain within itself an admission of existing liability, while under section 19, it is sufficient if the writing merely records the fact of payment. An endorsement of payment need not imply an acknowledgment of liability… whereas an acknowledgment for the purpose of section 18 must be by the person against whom the property or right in question is claimed or by some person through whom he derives the title or liability, a payment for the purpose of section 19 need only be by the person liable to pay the debt… an acknowledgment under section 18 only operates against the person who makes it… but subject to the provision of sub-section (2) of section 20, a payment under section 19 saves limitation against all the persons who are liable for the debt.”

PYQ — 2020 (OBE)
Q. 5/2020 — A took a loan of ₹50,000 from B on 01.04.2016. On 01.04.2017, A paid ₹20,000 to B in cash against due receipt. On 24.03.2020, A wrote a letter through courier wherein he stated that he owed the sum of ₹40,000. On the very next day the Government of India imposed a complete lockdown due to Covid-19. B received the letter on 15.06.2020 and filed a suit on 20.06.2020. A contended that the suit is barred by limitation. Decide with relevant provision and case law. (25 marks)
MODEL ANSWER — PROBLEM: PART-PAYMENT (s.19) + ACKNOWLEDGMENT (s.18)

Relevant provisions: s.19 (effect of payment on account of debt or interest), s.18 (effect of acknowledgment in writing), Art. 19, Schedule (money lent — 3 years), with State of Kerala v. T.M. Chacko, (2000) 9 SCC 722 and Sampuran Singh v. Niranjan Kaur, AIR 1999 SC 1047 as the governing case law.

01.04.2016
Loan of ₹50,000 — original limitation (Art. 19) would run to 01.04.2019.
01.04.2017
s.19 part-payment of ₹20,000 against due receipt (payment + writing) — made before expiry → fresh period of 3 years from the payment, i.e., to 01.04.2020.
24.03.2020
s.18 acknowledgment — A’s signed letter (“I owe ₹40,000”) made within the subsisting period (8 days before 01.04.2020) and despatched by courier — the admission of existing liability is complete and out of A’s hands → fresh 3-year period from the acknowledgment.
24.03.2020 → 24.03.2023
New limitation period extinguishes the earlier period; the lockdown of 25.03.2020 (court closures) could only have extended time further (s.4 / Supreme Court’s Covid limitation extensions) — it cannot shorten it.
20.06.2020
Recovery suit for ₹40,000 filed — well within limitation (due till 24.03.2023). B’s delay in receiving the letter (15.06.2020) is immaterial: the acknowledgment operates from its date and despatch.

Decision: The suit is not barred. The part-payment of 01.04.2017 (s.19) gave B a fresh period ending 01.04.2020; A’s signed acknowledgment of 24.03.2020 — made before that expiry, in writing, by the person liable (T.M. Chacko: fresh period computed from the signing; Sampuran Singh: the only fatal defect would have been expiry — not the case here) — restarted limitation afresh till 24.03.2023. The Covid lockdown and the courier delay are red herrings: they could not prejudice B, and the suit of 20.06.2020 is comfortably within time.

Quick Revision Sheet — One Last Look Before the Hall

Sections 12, 17–19, 21 and the four cases, compressed
The Five Sections in One Breath
s.12 — exclude the first day; exclude time requisite for copies (appeal/leave/revision/review); s.12(5) no exclusion where a copy is not required at all (but Madan Lal Das reads it down). s.17 — fraud/mistake: clock from knowledge (reasonable diligence = at least a week's realism); s.17(2) execution barred by fraud/force → 1 year to apply. s.18 — signed writing admitting liability before expiry → fresh period from its date; nothing after expiry. s.19 — part-payment/interest on debt or legacy, evidenced in writing → fresh period, binds all co-debtors. s.21 — added party's suit dates from addition, unless good-faith mistake (proviso; Dagaduram, Karuppaswamy); assignment/devolution ride the original suit (s.21(2)).
Case 31 — Madan Lal Das (1977)
Copy served 22-8-1965 → lost → fresh copy 15-6-1966 to 18-8-1967 → revision 9-9-1967. Held: s.12(2) applies via s.29(2); time for the replacement copy excluded even though no copy needed filing — a practitioner may want to study the decree first (Surty: "positive direction"); a fortiori from Maharaj Narain. Khanna J., 3 judges.
Case 32 — Maharaj Narain (1968)
Acquittal 10-11-1962; three copies (filed one: applied 15-11-1962, ready 3-1-1963); appeal 29-3-1963. Held: no duty of promptitude — wait out the 90 days if you wish; deduct time for the copy filed; "time beyond the party's control" (Panjam); minimum-time enquiries "unworkable"; Mathela wrong, Pramatha Nath Roy irrelevant. Appeal held within time.
Case 33 — Mahabir Kishore (1990)
Illegal 7½% cess ₹54,606; internal uncommunicated letter 17-10-1961; knowledge September 1962 (Doongaji); notice 17-10-1964; suit 24-12-1964 — "7 days late" in the HC. Held (Saikia J.): refund for mistake of law → s.72 Contract Act + Art. 113 (3 yrs) + s.17(1)(c); clock from knowledge — usually the judgment declaring the levy void; uncommunicated letter starts nothing; "at least a week" for diligence; Salonah Tea: no State may keep money without authority of law. Appeal allowed, remand.
Case 34 — Sampuran Singh (1999)
1893 oral mortgage ₹53 (valid in Punjab: <₹100 + possession; TPA came only 1956); equity bought 1959; mortgagee rights sold 11-1-1960 with acknowledgment; redemption suit 1980. Held (A.P. Misra J.): right to redeem accrued the very day of the mortgage → 60 years expired 1953 → the 1960 acknowledgment is after expiry — revives nothing ("Where, before the expiration..."); Shri Chand FB affirmed, Inder Singh overruled; Art.61(a) tender theory would make limitation infinite; 1913 Act procedural only. Appeal dismissed.
The Three Formulas to Memorise
(1) Copy rule: last date = judgment date + limitation + time to get the copy filed (conduct disciplined). (2) Refund rule: money under mistake of law → Art. 113 (3 yrs) + s.17(1)(c) from knowledge = the voiding judgment (Kanhaiya Lal → Cawasji → Vallabh Glass → Auraiya → Salonah Tea → Mahabir Kishore). (3) Revival gate: acknowledgment (s.18) / part-payment (s.19) only while the period lives — after expiry, nothing revives (Sampuran Singh).

Memory Hooks

  • 12 = Copies: "twelve" rhymes with "send anew" — the day, the copy, the fresh copy.
  • 17 = Secrets: fraud and mistake hide the clock; knowledge drags it into daylight (and s.17(2) gives execution one year).
  • 18 = Ink: acknowledgment must be in writing, signed, in time — "18 words: before expiry, in writing, signed, by the liable hand."
  • 19 = Cash + chit: payment on account of debt/legacy, plus a writing to record it — and it binds every co-debtor.
  • 21 = Late guests: a party added after limitation re-dates the suit — unless the invitation was a bona fide mistake (then it relates back).
  • Case-number = fix: 31 fixes lost copies; 32 fixes multiple copies; 33 fixes the seven-day shortfall; 34 fixes nothing — the mortgage was already dead in 1953.
  • Maharaj Narain's three copies: only the one filed matters — the other two are "used for other purposes with which the court has nothing to do."
  • Sampuran's gate: s.18's first words — "Where, before the expiration" — are a locked gate; 1953 had already turned the key.
