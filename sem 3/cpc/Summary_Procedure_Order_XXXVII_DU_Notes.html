The Concept of Summary Procedure

What a summary suit is, where it comes from, why it exists, and its legal character
Core Definition
A summary suit under Order XXXVII CPC is a suit upon a negotiable instrument or for a debt / liquidated demand in money in which the defendant is not entitled, as of right, to defend the suit as in an ordinary suit. The defendant must apply to the court for leave (permission) to defend within the prescribed time; if leave is refused or not sought, the plaintiff becomes entitled to judgment forthwith and the decree may be executed immediately.

1.1  Essence of the Summary Suit

  • Right of audience is conditional: In an ordinary suit, appearing and defending is the defendant's right; in a summary suit it is a privilege granted by the court's leave — "the defendant shall not defend the suit unless he enters an appearance" (Rule 2(3)).
  • Leave to defend: Granted only if the defendant's affidavit discloses facts which would make it incumbent on the plaintiff to prove consideration, or such other facts as the court deems sufficient; leave may be unconditional or on terms (deposit, security, time/mode of trial).
  • Deemed admission: On default of appearance, "the allegations in the plaint shall be deemed to be admitted" and the plaintiff is entitled to a decree forthwith (Rule 2(3)).
  • Speed in commercial causes: The Order targets classes of money claims where speedy decisions are desirable in the interests of trade and commerce.
  • Rules of procedure only: The provisions of Order XXXVII are merely rules of procedure — they do not alter the nature of the suit or the jurisdiction of the court (Prayag Deb v. Rama Roy, AIR 1977 Cal 1 (FB), as cited in Takwani). Filing under O.37 R.2(1) has no bearing on the jurisdiction of the court or on the nature of the suit (Mulla).

1.2  Historical Background

  • The device descends from the English Chancery / RSC Order XIV procedure for signing judgment where there is no defence — the House of Lords decision in Jacobs v. Booth's Distillery Co., (1901) 85 LT 262 laid down that whenever the defence raises a "triable issue", leave must be given, and later cases added that such leave must be unconditional, otherwise it is illusory.
  • In India the procedure was consolidated in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 as Order XXXVII, originally confined largely to suits on negotiable instruments (the Order's heading then read "Summary Procedure on Negotiable Instruments").
  • The CPC (Amendment) Act, 1976 (Act 104 of 1976), w.e.f. 1-2-1977 — (i) omitted the words "On Negotiable Instruments" from the Order's heading; (ii) substituted Rules 1 and 2 wholesale; and (iii) inserted a new Rule 3 — expanding summary procedure from bills/hundies/notes to debts on written contracts, enactments and guarantees, and introducing the twin provisos (substantial defence / deposit of admitted amount).
  • The 1976 text consciously absorbed the Bombay High Court's amendment to Rule 2/3 which the Supreme Court had examined in Milkhiram (India) (P) Ltd. v. Chamanlal Bros. (4-judge bench) — permitting conditions even where an apparently triable issue is raised.
Exam hookThe 1976 amendment is the single most important legislative event for this topic: it is the reason the Mechalec propositions were later superseded in IDBI Trusteeship v. Hubtown (2016). Always mention it when discussing leave to defend.

1.3  Object & Rationale

  • Primary object — "to prevent unreasonable obstruction by the defendant who has no defence and to assist expeditious disposal of cases" (Takwani; Universal Law Series).
  • Commercial rationale — "Trading and commercial operations are liable to be seriously impeded if, in particular, money disputes between the parties are not adjudicated upon expeditiously" (Milkhiram, adopted in Santosh Kumar through Kesavan v. South India Bank Ltd. and Sundaram Chettiar v. Valli Ammal).
  • Balance struck — Santosh Kumar (Vivian Bose, J.): "the object is to see that the defendant does not unnecessarily prolong the litigation and prevent the plaintiff from obtaining an early decree by raising untenable and frivolous defences in a class of cases where speedy decisions are desirable in the interests of trade and commerce."
Natural-justice foundationQuoting Sangram Singh v. Election Tribunal, Kotah (AIR 1955 SC 425), Bose J. reminded courts that a code of procedure is "something designed to facilitate justice and further its ends; not a penal enactment for punishment and penalties; not a thing designed to trip people up" — hence the summary jurisdiction must be exercised along judicial lines.

1.4  Legal Character of the Procedure

QuestionPosition
Is a summary suit a different kind of "suit"?No. It is an ordinary suit tried by a special procedure. Rule 7 keeps the ordinary procedure alive "save as provided by this Order".
Is the decree special?No. It is a decree under s. 2(2); a first appeal lies under s. 96. Where leave to defend has been refused, the decree follows "almost automatically" (Mulla).
Does O.37 affect jurisdiction?No. "A suit by summary jurisdiction provided by O.37 r.2(1) does not mean that court is not competent to try that suit if it is otherwise within its jurisdiction" (Mulla, citing Prayag Deb).
Is the defendant arrestable?A defendant in a summary suit is privileged from arrest even though he has not obtained leave to defend (Mulla on exemption from arrest of parties).
Plaintiff's optionSummary procedure is optional — "if the plaintiff desires to proceed hereunder" (R.2(1)). A plaintiff entitled to it may always file an ordinary suit instead.

Rule 1 — Courts and Classes of Suits R.1 — APPLICABILITY

The territorial and subject-matter reach of the summary jurisdiction
Order XXXVII, Rule 1 — Courts and classes of suits to which the Order is to apply
(1) This Order shall apply to the following Courts, namely:—
(a) High Courts, City Civil Courts and Courts of Small Causes; and
(b) other Courts:
Provided that in respect of the Courts referred to in clause (b), the High Court may, by notification in the Official Gazette, restrict the operation of this Order only to such categories of suits as it deems proper, and may also, from time to time, as the circumstances of the case may require, by subsequent notification in the Official Gazette, further restrict, enlarge or vary, the categories of suits to be brought under the operation of this Order as it deems proper.
(2) Subject to the provisions of sub-rule (1), the Order applies to the following classes of suits, namely:—
(a) suits upon bills of exchange, hundies and promissory notes;
(b) suits in which the plaintiff seeks only to recover a debt or liquidated demand in money payable by the defendant, with or without interest, arising,—
  (i) on a written contract, or
  (ii) on an enactment, where the sum sought to be recovered is a fixed sum of money or in the nature of a debt other than a penalty; or
  (iii) on a guarantee, where the claim against the principal is in respect of a debt or liquidated demand only.

2.1  Element I — The Courts (Rule 1(1))

  • Automatic application: High Courts, City Civil Courts and Courts of Small Causes — the superior commercial courts of Presidency towns.
  • Other courts: The Order reaches them only if (and to the extent that) the High Court notifies categories of suits in the Official Gazette; the High Court retains a continuing power to restrict, enlarge or vary those categories.
  • This is why, historically, the vigorous development of O.37 practice occurred in Bombay, Calcutta and Delhi — and why the leading cases in the DU material (Santosh Kumar — Delhi; Mechalec — Delhi; ONGC & IDBI — Bombay HC original side) all arise from those courts.

2.2  Element II — The Suits (Rule 1(2))

(a) Suits upon bills of exchange, hundies and promissory notes

The classical core of the Order — suits on negotiable instruments where the document itself is the debt. Santosh Kumar (dishonoured cheque of ₹60,000) and Mechalec (dishonoured cheque of ₹21,265.28) both belong to this class.

(b) Suits to recover a debt or liquidated demand in money

  • "Only to recover" — the relief must be purely monetary; a suit seeking additional non-monetary relief (declaration, injunction, possession) falls outside the Order.
  • "Debt or liquidated demand" — a sum certain, or capable of ascertainment by computation from the document; unliquidated damages (e.g., damages for breach requiring proof of loss) are outside.
  • Three sources: (i) written contract; (ii) enactment — but only where the sum is a fixed sum or in the nature of a debt other than a penalty; (iii) guarantee — where the claim against the principal is itself for a debt or liquidated demand.
Guarantee ≠ IndemnityState Bank of Saurashtra v. Ashit Shipping Services (P) Ltd. (2002) 4 SCC 736 (Universal Law Series chapter): where the document was in substance an indemnity bond, the SC held a summary suit was not maintainable — "in case of claims on indemnity bonds the loss would first have to be proved. Thus a summary procedure cannot be adopted in such cases." Order XXXVII R.1(2)(iii) speaks only of guarantees. Leave to defend was granted.
Post-1976 expansionBefore 1977, class (b) as such did not exist in the central Code (it was the Bombay amendment's innovation); suits were confined to negotiable instruments. The 1976 amendment nationalised the wider classes — which is why ONGC v. SBI (bank guarantee) and IDBI Trusteeship v. Hubtown (corporate guarantee) could be brought as summary suits.

Rule 2 — Institution of Summary Suits R.2 — INSTITUTION

How the summary suit is launched, and the consequence of the defendant's default
Order XXXVII, Rule 2 — Institution of summary suits
(1) A suit, to which this Order applies, may if the plaintiff desires to proceed hereunder, be instituted by presenting a plaint which shall contain,—
(a) a specific averment to the effect that the suit is filed under this Order;
(b) that no relief, which does not fall within the ambit of this rule, has been claimed in the plaint; and
(c) the following inscription, immediately below the number of the suit in the title of the suit, namely:—
"(Under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908)."
(2) The summons of the suit shall be in Form No. 4 in Appendix B or in such other Form as may, from time to time, be prescribed.
(3) The defendant shall not defend the suit referred to in sub-rule (1) unless he enters an appearance and in default of his entering an appearance the allegations in the plaint shall be deemed to be admitted and the plaintiff shall be entitled to a decree for any sum, not exceeding the sum mentioned in the summons, together with interest at the rate specified, if any, up to the date of the decree and such sum for costs as may be determined by the High Court from time to time by rules made in that behalf and such decree may be executed forthwith.

3.1  The Three Plaint Formalities — R.2(1)(a)–(c)

  1. Specific averment: the plaint must say in terms that the suit is filed under Order XXXVII — the defendant must know at a glance that his right to defend is curtailed.
  2. Confinement of relief: an averment that no relief outside R.1's ambit is claimed — this ties the plaint to the "only to recover a debt or liquidated demand in money" limitation.
  3. The inscription: "(Under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908)" printed immediately below the suit number in the cause title — the visible stamp of the summary procedure.

Mulla's note: instituting under O.37 R.2(1) has no bearing on the jurisdiction of the court or on the nature of the suit — if a court of the lowest grade lacks summary jurisdiction, that does not make it incompetent to try the suit if otherwise within its jurisdiction.

3.2  The Summons — R.2(2)

The summons is in Form No. 4, Appendix B (or a prescribed alternative). Appendix B Form 4 warns the defendant, in substance, that he must appear within ten days of service, that in default the allegations in the plaint will be deemed to be admitted and a decree may be executed forthwith — the summons thus carries the entire coercive logic of the Order (cf. bare Act, Appendix B).

3.3  Default of Appearance — R.2(3)

  • The defendant "shall not defend the suit unless he enters an appearance" — defence is conditional on appearance.
  • On default: deemed admission of the plaint's allegations → the plaintiff is entitled to a decree (capped at the sum in the summons, plus specified interest and High-Court-rule costs) → decree executable forthwith — no trial, no evidence, no O.8 written statement.
  • The defendant's protection against such a decree is later found in Rule 4 (setting aside under special circumstances) — see Section 6.
RememberThe Supreme Court in Rajni Kumar v. Suresh Kumar Malhotra spelt out the sequence: "In default of his entering an appearance, the plaintiff becomes entitled to a decree for any sum not exceeding the sum mentioned in the summons together with interest at the rate specified, if any, up to the date of the decree together with costs. The plaintiff will also be entitled to judgment in terms of sub-rule (6) of Rule 3."

Rule 3 — Procedure for the Appearance of the Defendant R.3 — THE ENGINE ROOM

The step-by-step chronology from service to judgment — the most examined provision of the Order
Order XXXVII, Rule 3 — Procedure for the appearance of defendant
(1) In a suit to which this Order applies, the plaintiff shall, together with the summons under rule 2, serve on the defendant a copy of the plaint and annexures thereto and the defendant may, at any time within ten days of such service, enter an appearance either in person or by pleader and, in either case, he shall file in Court an address for service of notices on him.
(2) Unless otherwise ordered, all summonses, notices and other judicial processes, required to be served on the defendant, shall be deemed to have been duly served on him if they are left at the address given by him for such service.
(3) On the day of entering the appearance, notice of such appearance shall be given by the defendant to the plaintiff's pleader, or, if the plaintiff sues in person, to the plaintiff himself, either by notice delivered at or sent by a pre-paid letter directed to the address of the plaintiff's pleader or of the plaintiff, as the case may be.
(4) If the defendant enters an appearance, the plaintiff shall thereafter serve on the defendant a summons for judgment in Form 4-A in Appendix B or such other Form as may be prescribed from time to time, returnable not less than ten days from the date of service supported by an affidavit verifying the cause of action and the amount claimed and stating that in his belief there is no defence to the suit.
(5) The defendant may, at any time within ten days from the service of such summons for judgment, by affidavit or otherwise disclosing such facts as may be deemed sufficient to entitle him to defend, apply on such summons for leave to defend such suit, and leave to defend may be granted to him unconditionally or upon such terms as may appear to the Court or Judge to be just:
Provided that leave to defend shall not be refused unless the Court is satisfied that the facts disclosed by the defendant do not indicate that he has a substantial defence to raise or that the defence intended to be put up by the defendant is frivolous or vexatious:
Provided further that, where a part of the amount claimed by the plaintiff is admitted by the defendant to be due from him, leave to defend the suit shall not be granted unless the amount so admitted to be due is deposited by the defendant in Court.
(6) At the hearing of such summons for judgment,—
  (a) if the defendant has not applied for leave to defend, or if such application has been made and is refused, the plaintiff shall be entitled to judgment forthwith; or
  (b) if the defendant is permitted to defend as to the whole or any part of the claim, the Court or Judge may direct him to give such security and within such time as may be fixed by the Court or Judge and that, on failure to give such security within the time specified by the Court or Judge or to carry out such other directions as may have been given by the Court or Judge, the plaintiff shall be entitled to judgment forthwith.
(7) The Court or Judge may, for sufficient cause shown by the defendant, excuse the delay of the defendant in entering an appearance or in applying for leave to defend the suit.

Leave to Defend — The Jurisprudence R.3(5)–(6) — DISCRETION

From Kiranmoyee Dassi (1945) to IDBI Trusteeship (2016): the standards that govern the grant, refusal and conditioning of leave

5.1  The Judicial Frame of Mind

  • Discretion, but judicial: "the Court is given a discretion [which] must be exercised along judicial lines... in consonance with the principles of natural justice that form the foundations of our laws" (Santosh Kumar).
  • No hard-and-fast rules: "It is always undesirable, and indeed impossible, to lay down hard and fast rules in matters that affect discretion" (Santosh Kumar); "it would be undesirable and inexpedient to lay down any rule of general application" (Milkhiram).
  • Answers from pleadings & affidavits only: "Whether the defence raises a triable issue or not has to be ascertained by the court from the pleadings before it and the affidavits of parties and it is not open to it to call for evidence at that stage" (Milkhiram).
  • Proof comes later: "the stage of proof can only come after the defendant has been allowed to enter an appearance and defend the suit" (Santosh Kumar).
  • Revisional restraint: the High Court under s.115 should not interfere with the trial court's discretionary grant of leave on a "pure question of fact — whether the defences could be honest and bona fide" — deciding that before evidence "is generally hazardous" (Mechalec).

5.2  The Foundational Tests

Jacobs v. Booth's Distillery Co. (1901) 85 LT 262 (HL) — via Santosh Kumar & MilkhiramWhenever the defence raises a "triable issue", leave must be given; and later cases say that when that is the case it must be given unconditionally; otherwise the leave may be illusory.
Santosh Kumar v. Bhai Mool Singh (1958) — Bose J."In general, therefore, the test is to see whether the defence raises a real issue and not a sham one, in the sense that, if the facts alleged by the defendant are established, there would be a good, or even a plausible defence on those facts."
Raj Duggal v. Ramesh Kumar Bansal, AIR 1990 SC 2218 (via DU PYQ book)"the test whether leave to defend should be granted or not is to see whether the defence raises a real, honest and bona fide dispute and raises a triable issue or not. If the Court is satisfied that the defence has raised a triable issue or a fair dispute has arisen, leave to defend should not be refused." Summary judgment should not be given where there is serious conflict as to matter of fact, or any difficulty on issues as to law arises; the court should not reject the defence merely because of its inherent implausibility or its inconsistency.

When is a "triable issue" raised? (illustrations from the DU PYQ material)

  • a fair dispute to be tried as to the meaning of the document on which the claim is based; or
  • uncertainty as to the amount actually due; or
  • facts of such a nature as to entitle the defendant to interrogate the plaintiff; or
  • to cross-examine his witnesses.

But where the triable point is purely a question of law not dependent on facts to be investigated, no leave is needed — the court may decide the point at once (Universal Law Series).

5.3  The Five Kiranmoyee Dassi Propositions — approved in Mechalec

Smt. Kiranmoyee Dassi v. Dr. J. Chatterjee, (1945) 49 CWN 246 = AIR 1949 Cal 479 (Das, J.) — as extracted in the DU case material within Mechalec:

ClassNature of defence disclosedConsequence
(a)Good defence to the claim on its meritsPlaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; defendant entitled to unconditional leave.
(b)Trible issue raised — fair / bona fide / reasonable defence though not positively goodPlaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; unconditional leave.
(c)Facts disclosed lead to the inference that a defence may possibly be established at trialLeave granted, but conditions may be imposed as to time or mode of trial only — not as to payment into court or furnishing security.
(d)No defence, or illusory / sham / "practically moonshine" defenceOrdinarily, plaintiff entitled to sign judgment; no leave.
(e)No defence or sham/moonshine — but court shows mercyCourt may protect the plaintiff by conditional leave — defence allowed to proceed only if the amount claimed is paid into court or otherwise secured.
Mechalec's use of the tableIn Mechalec the Court held that a deposit condition is justifiable only in class (e) — "It is only in that class of cases that an imposition of the condition to deposit an amount in Court before proceeding further is justifiable." Since the defendant there had denied privity of contract itself, the case fell outside (e), and the Delhi HC's conditional order could not stand.

5.4  Milkhiram — the Four-Judge Bridge to the Amended Law

Milkhiram (India) (P) Ltd. v. Chamanlal Bros., AIR 1965 SC 1698 (bench of four) construed the Bombay amendment of O.37 R.2 — textually the ancestor of today's R.3(4)–(6) — and held:

  • A triable issue is ascertained from pleadings and affidavits, not by calling for evidence.
  • If the defence is sham, fantastic or highly improbable, the court may put the defendant on terms before granting leave.
  • Even where a defence is plausible but improbable, the court may conclude the issue is not triable and impose terms.
  • Devlin LJ in Fieldrank Ltd. v. Stein and Chagla CJ in Rawalpindi Theatres v. Film Group Bombay approved as guidance for appellate courts: a "real doubt about the defendant's good faith" justifies protecting the plaintiff by conditions.
  • Care directives: (i) the object of speedy disposal of commercial causes must not be defeated; (ii) "real and genuine triable issues are not shut out by unduly severe orders as to deposit".

In IDBI Trusteeship, Nariman J. identified Milkhiram as "a direct authority on the amended O.XXXVII provision" — since the central amendment of 1976 reproduced the Bombay text — though the two provisos to sub-rule (5) were "new".

5.5  Reading the 1976 Amendment — Defiance Knitting and Southern Sales

  • Defiance Knitting Industries (P) Ltd. v. Jay Arts, (2006) 8 SCC 25 (¶13): (a) triable issue → leave ordinarily unconditional; (b) no substantial defence / frivolous-vexatious → refuse altogether; (c) genuine doubt whether defence is genuine or sham → conditions may be imposed.
  • Southern Sales & Services v. Sauermilch Design & Handels GMBH, (2008) 14 SCC 457 (¶15): the second proviso makes deposit of the admitted amount a condition precedent to leave; "the earlier concept of granting unconditional leave when a triable issue is raised has been supplemented by a mandate" to deposit the admitted sum.

5.6  IDBI Trusteeship (2016) — the Six Governing Principles Today

Nariman, J. held that "the principles stated in paragraph 8 of Mechelec's case will now stand superseded, given the amendment of O.XXXVII R.3, and the binding decision of four judges in Milkhiram's case", and restated the law as:

  1. Substantial defence (a defence that is likely to succeed) → plaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; defendant entitled to unconditional leave.
  2. Trible issues indicating a fair or reasonable defence (though not positively good) → plaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; defendant ordinarily entitled to unconditional leave.
  3. Trible issues but doubt about the defendant's good faith or the genuineness of the issues → judge may impose conditions both as to time/mode of trial and as to payment into court or furnishing security; care that the object of expeditious disposal is not defeated and that triable issues are not shut out by unduly severe orders.
  4. Plausible but improbable defence → conditions as to time/mode of trial and as to payment into court or furnishing security; since such a defence does not raise triable issues, conditions can extend to the entire principal sum plus such interest as the court thinks just.
  5. No substantial defence / no genuine triable issues; frivolous or vexatious → leave refused; plaintiff entitled to judgment forthwith.
  6. Admitted part of the claim → leave (even if triable issues or substantial defence raised) shall not be granted unless the admitted amount is deposited in court.
IDBI Trusteeship — the "multi-coloured coat""The discretion that a Judge exercises under Order XXXVII to refuse leave to defend or to grant conditional or unconditional leave to defend is a discretion akin to Joseph's multi-coloured coat — a large number of baffling alternatives present themselves. The life of the law not being logic but the experience of the trial Judge, is what comes to the rescue in these cases... Justice, like Equality... and arbitrariness... are sworn enemies."

Rule 4 — Power to Set Aside Decree R.4 — POST-DECREE RELIEF

The defendant's rescue route after a summary decree — "special circumstances" and the contrast with Order IX Rule 13
Order XXXVII, Rule 4 — Power to set aside decree
After decree the Court may, under special circumstances, set aside the decree, and if necessary stay or set aside execution, and may give leave to the defendant to appear to the summons and to defend the suit, if it seems reasonable to the Court so to do, and on such terms as the Court thinks fit.

6.1  Anatomy of Rule 4 (as read by the SC in Rajni Kumar v. Suresh Kumar Malhotra)

  • Trigger: "special circumstances" — something exceptional in character, extraordinary, significant, uncommon; the antonym of common, ordinary and general. It is neither practicable nor advisable to enumerate such circumstances exhaustively.
  • Non-service of summons will undoubtedly be a special circumstance; the court asks, on the facts of each case, whether the circumstances pleaded are "so unusual or extraordinary as to justify putting the clock back by setting aside the decree".
  • Bundle of reliefs: (i) set aside the decree; (ii) stay or set aside execution (post-decree); (iii) leave to the defendant (a) to appear to the summons and (b) to defend the suit (pre-decree matters revived) — granted "if it seems reasonable" and "on such terms as the Court thinks fit".
  • All reliefs in ONE application: since more than one of the specified reliefs may be granted, "all such reliefs may be claimed in one application. It is not permissible to claim such reliefs in successive petitions as it would be contrary to the letter and spirit of the provision."
  • Twin burden: it is not enough to show special circumstances for the non-appearance/non-application — the defendant must also show facts which would entitle him to leave to defend (by affidavit or otherwise). "In this respect, Rule 4 of Order 37 is different from Rule 13 of Order 9."
Bar of inherent powerRamkarandas v. Bhagwandas, AIR 1965 SC 1144 (cited in Takwani & the DU PYQ book): the court's power under special circumstances under R.4 excludes recourse to s.151 inherent power to set aside a summary decree. (Supplementary: Ethirajulu v. Panikkar, AIR 1957 Mad 572 — the R.4 power is wide enough to cover not only non-service but sufficient cause for non-appearance.)
Mulla's practical glossWhere an application is styled as one under O.9 R.13 but contains all the ingredients required by R.4 (special circumstances + disclosable defence), it can be treated as an application under R.4 — substance over form.

Rules 5 to 7 — Auxiliary Provisions R.5–R.7

Custody of the instrument, noting charges, and the residuary procedure rule

Rule 5 — Deposit of bill, etc.

In any proceeding under the Order, the court may order the bill, hundi or note on which the suit is founded to be forthwith deposited with an officer of the court, and may further order that all proceedings be stayed until the plaintiff gives security for costs.

Rationale: the instrument is both the debt and the evidence; its custody prevents manipulation and protects the defendant's right to inspect the original.

Rule 6 — Noting charges

The holder of every dishonoured bill of exchange or promissory note has the same remedies for recovering the expenses of noting the instrument for non-acceptance or non-payment (or other expenses by reason of dishonour) as he has under the Order for recovering the amount of the bill or note itself.

Rule 7 — Procedure in suits

"Save as provided by this Order, the procedure in suits hereunder shall be the same as the procedure in suits instituted in the ordinary manner."

  • The Order is an overlay, not a parallel code: once leave to defend is granted (and conditions, if any, fulfilled), the suit re-enters the ordinary stream — written statement, issues, trial, judgment, decree.
  • "Inasmuch as Order 37 does not speak of the procedure when leave to defend the suit is granted, the procedure applicable to suits instituted in the ordinary manner will apply" (Rajni Kumar).
  • It is also the interpretive key to R.4: because R.4 specifically provides for setting aside the decree, O.9 R.13 (the ordinary route) does not apply.

8.2  Remedial Map — Appeals, Revision, Article 227

  • Decree (leave refused / no application / conditions broken): first appeal lies under s.96. The decree follows "almost automatically" upon refusal of leave, but the consequence cannot be avoided by seeking a writ — "When an appeal would lie, an application under Article 227 of the Constitution would not be entertained" (Mulla, quoting Sinha J.). The defendant may also move the trial court under R.4 to reopen the decree.
  • Order granting / refusing leave to defend: it is not appealable (not a decree, and not in the O.43 list — Mulla), but it is a "case decided" under s.115 and is therefore revisable; the High Court, however, generally will not interfere with a discretion exercised on sound judicial lines (Mechalec; Takwani). Wada Arun Asbestos (P) Ltd. v. Gujarat Water Supply & Sewerage Board (2009): imposing a condition on leave raises a jurisdictional question, hence revisable.
  • Recording reasons: while granting conditional leave the court should ordinarily record reasons; absence of reasons does not, however, invalidate the order (Takwani, citing V. Kamalanathan, Milkhiram, Ashok Umraomal Sancheti).

8.3  Section 10 (Res sub judice) and the Summary Suit — Indian Bank

Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-operative Marketing Federation Ltd., AIR 1998 SC 1952 (in the DU case material under Section 10): the Federation had earlier filed an ordinary suit for recovery of ₹3.70 crore; the Bank later filed a summary suit for ₹4.96 crore on an irrevocable letter of credit. Could s.10 stay the summary suit as a "subsequently instituted suit"?

  • The word "trial" in s.10 is not used in its widest sense; it must be construed in the context of O.37.
  • "In a summary suit the 'trial' really begins after the Court or the Judge grants leave to the defendant to contest the suit."
  • Hence the court seised of the summary suit may proceed up to the hearing of the summons for judgment and pass judgment if leave is not sought/refused or conditions are broken — s.10 does not bar those stages. The Division Bench of Bombay HC (which had stayed the summary suit) was in error; the Single Judge's order stood restored.
Also rememberS.10 is a rule of procedure, not a bar on jurisdiction or institution; it does not prevent interlocutory orders (consolidation, receiver, injunction, attachment before judgment) — the same harmonious-construction approach drives the O.37 holding.

Santosh Kumar v. Bhai Mool Singh
AIR 1958 SC 321 · (1958) SCR 1211 · Supreme Court of India · Vivian Bose, J. · Appeal by special leave (Art. 136) from Delhi Circuit Bench of the Punjab High Court (Art. 227) · DU Case Material, pp. 95–98

FFacts

  • The plaintiff, Bhai Mool Singh, filed suit on a cheque for ₹60,000 drawn by the defendants — Santosh Kumar and the Northern General Agencies — in his favour, which was dishonoured on presentation to the bank.
  • The suit was filed in the Court of the Commercial Subordinate Judge, Delhi, under Order XXXVII; the defendants applied for leave to defend under Rule 3.
  • Their defence: they admitted executing the cheque but pleaded it was given only as collateral security for the price of goods supplied by the plaintiff; those goods, they said, had since been paid for by cash payments made from time to time and by other cheques — they set out the exact dates of payments and the numbers of the cheques; the cheque in suit had "served its end" and should be returned.
  • The trial judge held the defence "raises a triable issue" — yet went on to hold that the defendants had "not placed anything on the file to show that the defence was a bona fide one" and that, without the supporting documents, the defence was "vague consisting of indefinite assertions". He granted leave conditionally: security to the extent of the suit amount plus costs.
  • Review before the trial judge failed; Art. 227 petition to the Delhi Circuit Bench of the Punjab High Court failed; the defendants obtained special leave under Art. 136.
Suit & leave application
Cheque of ₹60,000 dishonoured → summary suit under O.37 before Commercial Subordinate Judge, Delhi; defence: collateral security + payments detailed by date and cheque number.
Trial court
"Defence raises a triable issue" — but "not bona fide" absent documents; conditional leave on security of suit amount + costs.
Review → Art. 227 (Punjab HC, Delhi Circuit Bench)
Both failed.
Supreme Court (Art. 136)
Appeal allowed; orders of HC and trial judge set aside; case remanded for trial of the issues raised by the defendants; costs to the appellants.

IIssues

  1. Where a defence raises a triable issue, can the court nonetheless refuse unconditional leave and impose conditions (security for the entire suit amount) on the ground that the defence is "not bona fide" because the defendant produced no documentary evidence along with his affidavit?
  2. Is the discretion under (old) R.3(2) — "unconditionally or subject to such terms as to payment into Court, giving security, framing and recording issues or otherwise as the Court thinks fit" — unfettered, and if not, along what lines must it be exercised?
  3. Does an appeal lie against such an exercise of discretion, and when will the Supreme Court interfere?
⚖ Plaintiff–respondent's arguments
  • The discretion conferred by R.3(2) is unfettered; it has been exercised by the trial judge, so no appeal lies unless there is a "grave miscarriage of justice or flagrant violation of law".
  • Reliance: D.N. Banerji v. P.R. Mukherjee (AIR 1953 SC 58) and Waryam Singh v. Amarnath (AIR 1954 SC 215).
  • Supported by Gopala Rao v. Subba Rao (AIR 1936 Mad 246), Manohar Lal v. Nanhe Mal (AIR 1938 Lah 548) and Shib Karan Das v. Mohammed Sadiq (AIR 1936 Lah 584): where the court opines the defence is not bona fide, it may impose conditions rather than refuse leave.
⚖ Defendants–appellants' arguments
  • Laws of procedure are grounded in natural justice — no man should be condemned unheard (Sangram Singh v. Election Tribunal, AIR 1955 SC 425): procedure "designed to facilitate justice... not a thing designed to trip people up".
  • Under Jacobs v. Booth's Distillery (1901, HL), a defence raising a triable issue must get leave — and unconditionally, else the leave is illusory.
  • Their affidavit was clear and precise (dates, cheque numbers); demanding documentary proof before leave reverses the order of stages — proof comes only after leave is granted.

DDecision

  • The SC accepted the existence of a "third course" (conditions where the defence is not bona fide — agreeing with Varadachariar J. in the Madras case) but stressed the conclusion that a defence is not bona fide "cannot be reached arbitrarily" — it is hemmed in by judicial rules and procedure.
  • Object of the Order (via Kesavan v. South India Bank and Sundaram Chettiar): to prevent the defendant prolonging litigation by untenable, frivolous defences where speedy decisions serve trade and commerce.
  • Test: whether the defence raises a real issue, not a sham one — if the facts alleged are established, there would be a good, or even plausible, defence.
  • The defence (collateral security + itemised payments) "at once raised an issue of fact, the truth and good faith of which could only be tested by going into the evidence"; the trial judge himself found a triable issue.
  • A defence clear on its face does not become "vague" merely because the evidence to prove it is not filed with the affidavit — "the stage of proof can only come after the defendant has been allowed to enter an appearance and defend the suit".
  • Conditions exist only to ensure a speedy trial (where the defendant is trying to prolong litigation) — not to punish non-production of evidence at the affidavit stage; and once the court is satisfied the facts alleged would make a good/plausible defence, "leave cannot be withheld and no question about imposing conditions can arise".
  • Interference with discretionary orders is exceptional — but warranted where the discretion is exercised arbitrarily or on a misunderstanding of the governing principles resulting in failure of justice: "there is an obvious failure of justice if judgment is entered against a man who, if he is allowed to prove his case, cannot but succeed."
  • Appeal allowed; orders of the HC and trial judge set aside; case remanded for trial of the issues raised by the defendants; costs to the appellants.
⚖ Principles Evolved
  • The triable-issue test: leave to defend turns on whether the defence raises a real issue (not sham); if the facts alleged by the defendant are established there would be a good or even plausible defence → leave must follow.
  • Unconditionality: once a genuine triable issue is found, leave cannot be withheld nor conditions imposed — conditions are for securing speedy trial, not for suspected weakness of proof.
  • Stage discipline: affidavit-disclosure is judged on its face; evidence and proof belong to the post-leave trial.
  • Judicial discretion: O.37 discretion must be exercised along judicial lines consistent with natural justice (Sangram Singh).
  • Appellate correction: arbitrary or principle-less exercises of the discretion will be interfered with where justice has failed.
Key extract — Bose, J."Taken by and large, the object is to see that the defendant does not unnecessarily prolong the litigation and prevent the plaintiff from obtaining an early decree by raising untenable and frivolous defences in a class of cases where speedy decisions are desirable in the interests of trade and commerce. In general, therefore, the test is to see whether the defence raises a real issue and not a sham one, in the sense that, if the facts alleged by the defendant are established, there would be a good, or even a plausible defence on those facts."
Later career of this caseSantosh Kumar was heavily relied on in Milkhiram (1965) — which however qualified it for "plausible but improbable" defences under the Bombay-amended rule — cited in Mechalec (1977), and described as "a case where a cheque, the execution of which was admitted by the defendant, had been dishonoured" whose test governs unconditional leave. In IDBI Trusteeship it remains authority for principle 1–2 of the six-principle restatement (substantial defence / triable issues → ordinarily unconditional leave).

M/s Mechalec Engineers & Manufacturers v. M/s Basic Equipment Corporation
AIR 1977 SC 577 · (1976) 4 SCC 687 · Supreme Court of India · M.H. Beg, J. · Appeal by special leave from Delhi High Court (s.115 revision) · DU Case Material, pp. 99–101

FFacts

  • The plaintiff-respondent, a registered partnership firm, filed suit on 25 April 1974 through Smt. Pushpa Mittal (shown as one of its partners) to recover ₹21,265.28 as principal and ₹7,655 as interest at 12% p.a. (according to law and mercantile usage) on a cheque dated 12 May 1971 drawn by the defendant on the State Bank of India — dishonoured on presentation.
  • The plaintiff alleged the cheque was given as the price of goods supplied.
  • The defendant-appellant firm admitted issue of the cheque by its managing partner but denied any privity of contract with the plaintiff firm, and had "its own version as to the reasons and purposes for which the cheque was drawn".
  • Suit instituted under Order XXXVII; the defendant applied for leave to defend under Rule 2 (as the Order then stood).
  • The Additional District Judge, Delhi granted leave unconditionally after perusing "the cases of the two sides".
  • On revision under s.115 CPC, a learned Judge of the Delhi High Court, after setting out not less than seven questions on which the parties were at issue and holding that triable issues arose, nevertheless concluded the defences were not bona fide and granted leave only conditionally — deposit of ₹21,265.28 with 6% interest from the date of suit till payment + court-fee costs, within two months.

IIssue

The only question before the Supreme Court: "Could the High Court interfere in exercise of its powers under Section 115, Civil Procedure Code, with the discretion of the Additional District Judge, in granting unconditional leave to defend to the defendant-appellant upon grounds which even a perusal of the order of the High Court shows to be reasonable?"

⚖ Plaintiff–respondent's stance (supporting the HC)
  • The defences, though raising triable issues, were not bona fide; the court could therefore impose terms (the "third course" recognised in the older cases — Gopala Rao, Manohar Lal, Shib Karan Das).
  • A conditional order protects the plaintiff-creditor pending trial; the revisional jurisdiction was validly exercised to correct the trial court's indulgence.
⚖ Defendant–appellant's stance (attacking the HC)
  • The trial court's order granting unconditional leave was based on a reasonable perusal of both sides' cases; the High Court's s.115 jurisdiction — meant for jurisdictional error — was not attracted.
  • Deciding bona fides of a defence as a "pure question of fact" before evidence is hazardous and unfair (Santosh Kumar); a mere different view cannot justify revisional interference.

DDecision

  • The SC surveyed Santosh Kumar (including its approval of Jacobs v. Booth's Distillery — triable issue → leave, and unconditionally where the defence cannot be shown dishonest in limine) and held the well-settled principles governing s.115 jurisdiction were ignored by the High Court.
  • The HC had differed on a pure question of fact — whether the defences could be honest and bona fide; "any decision on such a question, even before evidence has been led by the two sides, is generally hazardous... not fair to pronounce a categorical opinion... before the evidence of the parties is taken".
  • Threshold for questioning unconditional leave: "It is only in cases where the defence is patently dishonest or so unreasonable that it could not reasonably be expected to succeed that the exercise of discretion by the trial Court to grant leave unconditionally may be questioned."
  • On the facts: the defendant had denied liability to pay anything for the alleged supply of goods (no privity) — no ground of interference under s.115 appeared in the HC's judgment.
  • The Court then adopted the five propositions of Das, J. in Smt. Kiranmoyee Dassi v. Dr. J. Chatterjee, (1945) 49 CWN 246 (classes (a)–(e), extracted in full — see Section 5.3 above).
  • Held: the case did not fall within class (e); it is only in class (e) that a deposit condition is justifiable. The High Court's judgment and order were set aside and the Additional District Judge's order restored.
⚖ Principles Evolved
  • Revisional restraint: an order granting or refusing leave is a "case decided" revisable under s.115, but the High Court will not substitute its own view of a fact-question (bona fides of defence) decided before evidence.
  • Patent dishonesty threshold: only a patently dishonest or unreasonably hopeless defence justifies disturbing unconditional leave.
  • Deposit conditions belong to class (e): under the unamended rule, ordering deposit/security before trial is justifiable only where the defence is illusory, sham or "practically moonshine" — the court's "mercy" regime.
  • Institutionalised Kiranmoyee Dassi (a)–(e) as the governing framework — the single most quoted paragraph in DU exams on this topic.
Critical update (must write in exams)In IDBI Trusteeship v. Hubtown (2016), the SC held that paragraph 8 of Mechalec (the (a)–(e) propositions) "will now stand superseded" given the 1976 amendment of R.3 and the binding four-judge decision in Milkhiram. Write Mechalec for the pre-amendment framework and revisional discipline — then show the examiner you know IDBI.

Oil & Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. v. State Bank of India, Overseas Branch, Bombay
AIR 2000 SC 2548 · Supreme Court of India · S. Rajendra Babu, J. · Appeal by special leave from Bombay High Court (order dated 27-4-1998 granting unconditional leave) · DU Case Material, pp. 102–106

FFacts

  • The contract: ONGC entered a contract with a consortium of M/s Saipem SPA / Snamprogetti of Italy for construction of a system of undersea pipelines — the Gas Lift Pipelines: pre-engineering survey, design & engineering, procurement, wrap & coat, fabrication, transportation, laying, installation, testing and pre-commissioning of forty sub-marine pipeline segments of approximately 181.8 kms.
  • Price & dates: contract price US $63,875,000 plus ₹8,06,00,000; whole works to be completed by 30 April 1991; liquidated damages (LD) at 3% of contract price per month of delay, capped at 10%.
  • The guarantee: contractor to furnish a bank guarantee covering LD for 10% of the price; the State Bank of India, Overseas Branch, Bombay furnished a guarantee for US $6,387,500 plus ₹8,060,000, undertaking "irrevocably and unconditionally" to pay ONGC "on demand in writing without demur or protest and irrespective of any contest or dispute" between ONGC and the contractor, without reference to the contractor; ONGC's notice of demand and amount claimed would be "final and binding" as to the factum of LD and the amount payable; governed by Indian law; validity to be extended if completion slipped.
  • The delay & invocation: after assessing total delay of 306 days, ONGC assessed LD at US $4,320,432 + ₹55,15,959 (letter of 17-3-1993 asking the contractor to extend the guarantee; the Bank furnished an enhanced guarantee valid to 4-10-1993); by letter of 13-9-1993 ONGC advised the contractor to extend validity; on 23-9-1993 the contractor issued notice referring the dispute to arbitration and appointed its arbitrator; on 27-9-1993 ONGC told the Bank that if validity were not extended by 1-10-1993, the letter be treated as invoking the guarantee.
  • The Bank's refusal (3-12-1993): (a) its guarantee was issued against the counter-guarantee of Credito Italiano, Milan, and the contractor had obtained an injunction from an Italian court restraining Credito Italiano from paying SBI; (b) it was "considering" the validity of ONGC's demand; (c) under exchange control regulations, rupee payment would be made only on receipt of reimbursement from the foreign bank; (d) the matter was sub judice — ONGC should await the outcome.
  • Both sides appeared before the Italian court; on 2-3-1994 that court ordered Credito Italiano and SBI "to abstain from payment of any sum in execution of the agreement of guarantee/counter-guarantee".
  • The suit: ONGC filed a summary suit under Order XXXVII before the Bombay High Court for US $4,320,432 + ₹55,15,959 with 18% interest; the Bank applied for unconditional leave to defend, which the Single Judge allowed (27-4-1998) on four grounds: (i) the invocation letter of 27-9-1993 did not state the LD amount; (ii) the guarantee required a clear notice of demand towards LD; (iii) that letter was not a legal notice communicating LD; (iv) arbitration was pending and the Italian court was seised. ONGC appealed by special leave.

IIssues

  1. Did any of the four grounds relied on by the Bombay High Court furnish a sufficient basis for granting unconditional leave to defend a summary suit on an unconditional bank guarantee?
  2. Can the beneficiary's failure to quantify LD in the invocation notice, pending arbitration between ONGC and the contractor, or a foreign court's injunction on the counter-guarantee, constitute a credible defence for the guarantor bank?
  3. What is the interplay between the autonomy of bank guarantees (fraud / irretrievable injustice exceptions) and the leave-to-defend jurisdiction under Order XXXVII?
⚖ ONGC (appellant) — Shri Ashok H. Desai, Sr. Adv.
  • None of the HC's grounds could justify unconditional leave; the defence was totally untenable.
  • Svenska Handelsbanken v. Indian Charge Chrome: a confirmed bank guarantee / irrevocable letter of credit cannot be interfered with unless there is established fraud or irretrievable injustice — there must be a prima facie case of established fraud and special equities; mere irretrievable injustice without fraud is of no consequence.
  • Hindustan Steelworks Construction Ltd. v. Tarapore & Co.: encashment of an unconditional guarantee does not depend on adjudication of disputes; no distinction between performance guarantees and security-deposit guarantees; where the beneficiary is sole judge of breach, the bank must pay on demand without demur; absent a plea of fraud, the guarantee must be given effect to.
  • Excess recovery, if any, could be corrected in the pending arbitration by way of damages.
⚖ SBI (respondent) — the defence accepted by the HC
  • The invocation letter of 27-9-1993 did not state the amount of liquidated damages; the guarantee required a clear notice of demand towards LD; the letter was "not a legal notice to communicate the LD".
  • Arbitration between ONGC and the contractor was pending and the Italian court was seised of the matter (injunction of 2-3-1994).
  • Under exchange-control position, payment under the rupee guarantee depended on reimbursement from Credito Italiano — which the Italian injunction had frozen; the Bank feared it would be left uncompensated.

DDecision

  • The SC extended the Svenska / Tarapore principle — though those were injunction cases — "to understand the nature of defence raised by the respondent Bank": in the absence of a plea of fraud, much less a finding, such defences are totally untenable.
  • On quantification: in Svenska itself the contentions about liquidated damages and delayed invocation were rejected — separate communications to the bank before expiry served as notice of invocation; wrong recovery could be corrected in arbitration.
  • The Italian order was irrelevant to ONGC's rights: "the said order arose out of the counter guarantee with which the appellant had nothing to do."
  • The Foreign Exchange Manual, 1999 (and its predecessor) made clear that authorised dealers may make rupee payments to the resident beneficiary immediately when the guarantee is invoked and simultaneously arrange reimbursement from the overseas bank; if the bank wishes to make payment conditional on reimbursement, that condition must be made clearly known to the beneficiary in the guarantee itself — which was not done. None of the Bank's claims was an impediment to payment.
  • Held: "the High Court plainly erred in having granted leave to defend unconditionally. We vacate that order and dismiss the application filed by respondent Bank for leave to defend by allowing this appeal." No costs. (The guarantee's autonomy prevailed; the summary jurisdiction did its work.)
⚖ Principles Evolved
  • Autonomy of the bank guarantee in summary suits: an unconditional, irrevocable guarantee is an independent contract — the guarantor bank cannot set up disputes under the main contract, pending arbitration, or defects in the beneficiary's invocation (absent fraud / irretrievable injustice) as a defence under O.37.
  • Fraud / irretrievable injustice as the sole exceptions (Svenska), and the beneficiary-as-sole-judge / pay-without-demur rule (Tarapore).
  • Counter-guarantee insulation: the beneficiary of the guarantee takes it free of the bank's back-to-back arrangements; the bank's reimbursement risk cannot be shifted to the beneficiary unless expressly stipulated in the guarantee.
  • Leave to defend is for credible defences: an "untenable" defence — however elaborate — deserves no leave, unconditional or otherwise.

IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. v. Hubtown Ltd.
[2016] 11 S.C.R. 660 · Supreme Court of India · R.F. Nariman, J. · Appeal from Summons for Judgment No. 39 of 2013 in a Summary Suit on the original side of the Bombay High Court (impugned judgment of the Single Judge dated 8-5-2015) · DU Case Material, pp. 107–120

FFacts

(i) The investment structure

  • In 2009–2010, FMO (Nederlandse Financierings-Maatschappij voor Ontwikkelingslanden N.V. — a Dutch development-finance institution) invested in equity shares and compulsorily convertible debentures (CCDs) of Vinca Developer Private Limited: 10% Class A equity + 3 CCDs (which on conversion would give FMO 99% of Vinca's equity and voting/economic rights).
  • Hubtown Ltd. (formerly Ackruti City Ltd.) owned 49% of Vinca's Class A equity; individual promoters Hemant Shah and Vyomesh Shah held 41% and all Class B shares.
  • Vinca used FMO's money (₹418 crores in all) to subscribe to optionally partially convertible debentures (OPCDs) — secured, non-marketable, transferable — of Rubix Trading Private Limited (₹128.5 crore tranche 1 + ₹139.5 crore tranche 2) and Amazia Developers Private Limited (₹150 crore), carrying a variable running coupon and back-ended coupon to ensure an IRR of 14.75% p.a. Proceeds were to go towards FDI-compliant township/housing/build-up projects.
  • IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. — "India's largest trusteeship company" — was appointed Debenture Trustee under the Debenture Subscription and Debenture Trust Deeds (DTDs) dated 1-12-2009 (as amended 8-9-2010).

(ii) The guarantee and the default

  • To secure the OPCDs, Hubtown executed a Deed of Corporate Guarantee cum Mortgage dated 9-12-2009 — "unconditional, absolute and irrevocable" — in favour of IDBI Trusteeship for the benefit of Vinca: Hubtown guaranteed due and punctual payment of the Amazia/Rubix secured obligations as primary obligor, payable forthwith on demand (Demand Certificate) within 5 business days; the trustee's independent opinion of default and shortfall stated in the Demand Certificate would be final and binding; default interest at 14.75% p.a.; a continuing guarantee until full discharge.
  • Defaults alleged from mid-2011: non-payment of interest on OPCDs (since 15/16-6-2011); cross-default under the CCDs; failure to furnish financial statements; breach of financial ratios (Net Debt-to-EBITDA, Debt Service Cover, Interest Coverage) for the period 1-4-2011 to 30-9-2011; breaches of positive covenants (application of proceeds) and negative covenants.
  • Notices of 2-5-2012 to Amazia and Rubix went unanswered; redemption notices of 27-6-2012 required full redemption on 3-7-2012 of ₹4,843,299,862.97; a Demand Certificate dated 3-8-2012 was served on Hubtown — no reply.
  • The summary suit (through Summons for Judgment No. 39 of 2013) claimed ₹532,11,29,364.05 as on 6-5-2013 (revised principal ₹477,51,90,932.97 + default interest ₹42,26,78,815.12 and ₹12,32,59,615.96 + future interest at 14.75%).

(iii) The defence and the High Court's order

  • Hubtown's main defence: the entire structure was a colourable device to defeat FEMA / FDI policy — FDI in townships/construction/housing was permitted only via equity (including CCDs) and no assured return was permissible; FMO, wanting a fixed 14.5–14.75% return on ₹418 crores, had Vinca interposed as the nominal recipient: FDI came in as equity + CCDs (apparent compliance) but was immediately routed to Amazia/Rubix as OPCDs bearing fixed return; on conversion FMO would own 99% and reap the fixed return; the guarantee was part of the illegal structure, hence unenforceable — the court should not lend its aid to an illegality (Imam Appa Rao v. G. Ramalingamurthi).
  • The Single Judge (8-5-2015): prima facie the routing of FMO's FDI through Vinca was "a colourable device"; the guarantee, "though ostensibly in favour of Vinca", was part of the illegal scheme and prima facie illegal and unenforceable; rejected some defences (discharge of guarantee, downstream-investment, ECB points); found triable issues; granted unconditional leave to defend; transferred the suit to the commercial causes list for expeditious trial.

IIssues

  1. The core question of law: whether the judgment in Mechalec (para 8 — the Kiranmoyee Dassi propositions) continues to govern leave to defend after the 1976 amendment of O.XXXVII R.3.
  2. Did Hubtown's FEMA/FDI "colourable device" defence disclose triable issues entitling it to unconditional leave — or was the defence "plausible but improbable", justifying terms?
  3. Can a court refuse to enforce a guarantee on public policy where the underlying structure is alleged to circumvent FEMA/FDI regulations (Imam Appa Rao; doctrine of pari delicto)?
⚖ IDBI Trusteeship (appellant) — Dr. Abhishek Manu Singhvi, Sr. Counsel
  • No FEMA violation: under Regs. 4 & 5, FEMA (Non-Debt Instruments) Regulations, the suit is between an Indian trustee and an Indian company (Hubtown), for the benefit of another Indian company (Vinca) — no funds leave India; the FDI came in as equity + CCDs, fully convertible — permissible instruments.
  • ₹418 crores stood "swallowed" by the development project; defaults began only in 2011; the guarantee is unconditional and its invocation is not alleged to be wrong.
  • The defence is "entirely frivolous and vexatious" — leave ought to have been refused; alternatively the plaintiff should at least be fully secured.
  • Mechalec is no longer good law post-1976 amendment: even with triable issues, the court may secure the plaintiff monetarily as a condition of leave.
⚖ Hubtown (respondent) — Shri Aspi Chinoy, Sr. Counsel
  • Clear breach of FEMA/FDI policy: fixed/assured return to FMO via the OPCD structure was prohibited; Vinca was interposed only as a facade of compliance; the guarantee secured the illegal object.
  • Per Imam Appa Rao v. G. Ramalingamurthi, where the plaintiff seeks the court's active assistance to effectuate what the law prohibits, the court will refuse aid — even if the defendant is pari delicto; public interest prevails over estoppel.
  • The 1976 amendment made only one change — deposit of admitted amounts (second proviso); beyond that the law is unchanged, so triable issues → unconditional leave (clause (e) not attracted), and the Single Judge was right; nothing perverse to warrant Art. 136 interference.

DDecision

  • On the law: Mechalec was decided on the unamended R.3; Milkhiram (4 judges) is "a direct authority on the amended O.XXXVII provision" (the central text mirroring the Bombay amendment), though the two provisos are new. Following Defiance Knitting and Southern Sales, "O.XXXVII has suffered a change in 1976, and that change has made a difference in the law laid down."
  • The trial judge's discretion "has to result in justice being done on the facts of each case" — but discretion and arbitrariness are "sworn enemies"; hence guideline principles (the six restated propositions — Section 5.6 above) to obviate arbitrary exercise.
  • Para 8 of Mechalec stands superseded by the six principles.
  • On the facts: the equity/CCD investment itself was not alleged to be violative; the suit was on an unconditional guarantee, not challenged as wrongly invoked; payment ran to an Indian trustee for an Indian company; FMO's 99% conversion and any repatriation would require RBI permission — "If RBI permission is not granted, then again there would be no infraction of FEMA Regulations".
  • Imam Appa Rao "would be attracted only if the illegal purpose is fully carried out, and not otherwise" — the illegality remained incomplete.
  • Therefore Hubtown had no substantial defence; "arguably at the highest... a triable issue may be said to arise" on the FEMA angle, but the Court was "left with a real doubt about the Defendant's good faith and the genuineness of such a triable issue" — the defence was "plausible but improbable"; ₹418 crores had gone into the project and payments stopped only in 2011.
  • Order: leave to defend granted only if Hubtown deposits in the Bombay High Court the principal sum of ₹418 crores or gives security for it to the satisfaction of the Prothonotary and Senior Master, within three months; Single Judge's judgment set aside; suit to be tried expeditiously, preferably within one year, uninfluenced by the Court's observations.
⚖ Principles Evolved — the definitive post-amendment statement
  • Supersession of Mechalec ¶8 and restatement of the six governing principles (substantial defence → unconditional leave; triable issues → ordinarily unconditional; triable issues + doubt → measured conditions; plausible-but-improbable → conditions up to the entire principal + interest; frivolous/vexatious → refusal; admitted amount → deposit first).
  • "Multi-coloured coat" discretion disciplined by anti-arbitrariness (justice as a constitutional value) — guided judicial discretion on the facts of each case.
  • Illegality defence contained: courts will not refuse enforcement of an unconditional guarantee for a structure whose illegality is incomplete and where regulatory clearance stages remain (Imam Appa Rao distinguished).
  • Protective conditioning: a "plausible but improbable" defence can lawfully be met with a deposit/security condition covering the entire principal — here ₹418 crores.
Key extract — Nariman, J. (¶17)"The discretion that a Judge exercises under Order XXXVII to refuse leave to defend or to grant conditional or unconditional leave to defend is a discretion akin to Joseph's multi-coloured coat – a large number of baffling alternatives present themselves... At one end of the spectrum is unconditional leave to defend, granted in all cases which present a substantial defence. At the other end of the spectrum are frivolous or vexatious defences, leading to refusal of leave to defend. In between these two extremes are various kinds of defences raised which yield conditional leave to defend in most cases."

Whether the High Court committed jurisdictional error in declining to set aside the ex parte decree under Rule 4 on the ground that the appellant failed to disclose facts sufficient to entitle her to defend.

  • No proof on record that the registered notice was ever issued — the respondent took the notice from the court but filed no proof of issuing it: a special reason for non-appearance.

⚖ Respondent (landlord, in person)

  • Rule 4 empowers the court, under special circumstances, to set aside the decree and grant (i) stay/setting aside of execution and (ii) leave to appear to the summons and defend — if reasonable, on such terms as it thinks fit.

  • Because R.4 specifically provides for setting aside decrees, O.9 R.13 will not apply to an O.37 suit (contrast with ordinary suits, where the ex parte defendant need not show a defence).
  • All available reliefs must be claimed in one application; the defendant must show special circumstances and facts entitling him to defend — "In this respect, Rule 4 of Order 37 is different from Rule 13 of Order 9."
  • Held: the appellant showed sufficient cause for her absence but failed to disclose any defence (nothing about amounts in deposit, nor about the suit's maintainability) — no jurisdictional error; dismissal upheld. IIssue

Whether the bar against proceeding with the trial of a subsequently instituted suit in Section 10 applies to a summary suit filed under Order XXXVII.

⚖ Bank (appellant)
  • The Single Judge was right: applying s.10 to summary suits would frustrate the very object of the special procedure — speedy decrees for commercial claims.
⚖ Federation (respondent)
  • "Trial" in s.10 is of wide import (judicial dictionaries; Harish Chandra v. Triloki Singh, AIR 1957 SC 444) — the entire proceedings; the later summary suit must await the earlier ordinary suit to avoid parallel proceedings and conflicting findings.

DDecision

  • S.10's object: prevent courts of concurrent jurisdiction from simultaneously trying parallel suits and avoid inconsistent findings — a rule of procedure, not a bar on jurisdiction or institution; interlocutory orders still permissible.

In a summary suit the "trial" (for s.10 purposes) begins only when leave to defend is granted — s.10 does not stay the preliminary stages of a summary suit. General and special procedural provisions are construed harmoniously.

Q. 4(a) / 2019CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term
What are the principles laid down by the Supreme Court in M/s. Mechalec Engineers & Manufacturers v. M/s. Basic Equipment Corporation, AIR 1977 SC 577, while dealing with the application for leave to defend in a suit filed by the plaintiff under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908?
Model Answer

1. Introduction — the summary procedure

Order XXXVII provides for summary procedure. A summary suit is one in which the defendant is not entitled, as a matter of right, to defend the suit, as in ordinary suits. He must apply for the court's leave to defend within the prescribed time (ten days from service of the summons for judgment — R.3(5); Art. 118, Limitation Act), and such leave is not granted if the defendant does not disclose facts indicating a substantial defence, or if his defence is frivolous or vexatious. Leave may be granted unconditionally or on such terms as the court deems just; if no leave is granted, the plaintiff is entitled to a decree.

2. Scope and object

The object of summary procedure is to prevent unreasonable obstruction by a defendant who has no valid defence and to secure the expeditious disposal of suits. Speedy decision is desirable "in the interests of trade and commerce" (Santosh Kumar, Bose J.).

3. Procedure under Rules 2 and 3

  • Rule 2: plaint with the special averments and inscription; Form 4 summons; if the defendant does not enter appearance, the plaint's allegations are deemed admitted and the plaintiff obtains a decree executable forthwith.
  • Rule 3: service of plaint + appearance within ten days; summons for judgment (Form 4A) with the plaintiff's affidavit (cause of action, amount, and belief that there is no defence); application for leave within ten days disclosing sufficient facts; hearing and outcomes under R.3(6); delay excusable under R.3(7).

4. When leave should be granted — the triable-issue principle

Leave should be granted where there is a triable issue, i.e. — (a) a fair dispute to be tried as to the meaning of the document on which the claim is based; (b) uncertainty as to the amount actually due; (c) facts entitling the defendant to interrogate the plaintiff; or (d) to cross-examine his witnesses. Summary judgment should not be granted where there is a serious conflict as to matters of fact or difficulty on issues of law; the defence should not be rejected merely for inherent implausibility or inconsistency (Raj Duggal v. Ramesh Kumar Bansal, AIR 1990 SC 2218).

5. Facts, issue and decision of Mechalec

  • Facts: a registered partnership firm sued on a dishonoured cheque for ₹21,265.28 + ₹7,655 interest, alleging it was given as the price of goods supplied; the defendant admitted issuing the cheque (by its managing partner) but denied privity of contract. Under O.37, the trial court (Additional District Judge, Delhi) granted unconditional leave; the Delhi High Court in revision (s.115) held the defences not bona fide and granted leave only on deposit of the amount with interest and costs.
  • Issue: could the High Court, under s.115, interfere with the trial court's discretion granting unconditional leave on grounds which the HC's own order showed to be reasonable?
  • Decision: the SC held that deciding bona fides as a pure question of fact before evidence is hazardous; only where the defence is patently dishonest or so unreasonable that it could not reasonably be expected to succeed may unconditional leave be questioned. The HC had ignored settled principles of s.115 jurisdiction; its order was set aside and the trial court's restored.

6. The principles stated in Mechalec (adopting Das J. in Kiranmoyee Dassi)

  1. Good defence on merits → plaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; defendant entitled to unconditional leave.
  2. Trible issue — fair/bona fide/reasonable defence though not positively good → unconditional leave.
  3. Facts sufficient to entitle him to defend (defence may possibly be established at trial) → leave, with conditions possible only as to time or mode of trial, not payment into court or security.
  4. No defence / illusory / sham / "practically moonshine" → ordinarily, plaintiff entitled to sign judgment; no leave.
  5. Even where there is no defence, the court may protect the plaintiff by allowing the defence to proceed only if the amount claimed is paid into court or secured — mercy to the defendant.

In Mechalec the case did not fall within class (e); only in that class is a deposit condition justifiable.

7. Conclusion (with the modern position)

Mechalec remains the leading exposition of the leave-to-defend framework and of revisional restraint. However, after the 1976 amendment (w.e.f. 1-2-1977) substituted Rule 3, the Supreme Court in IDBI Trusteeship Services Ltd. v. Hubtown Ltd. (2016) held that the propositions in paragraph 8 of Mechalec stand superseded: today, even where triable issues are raised, conditions (including deposit/security) may be imposed if the judge doubts the defendant's good faith; a "plausible but improbable" defence may attract conditions up to the entire principal with interest; and the admitted amount must in any case be deposited (second proviso; Southern Sales). A complete answer states Mechalec and then notes IDBI Trusteeship.

Q. 5(a) / 2018(20 marks) — problem question
Mr. A filed a suit against Mr. B, as the cheque drawn by Mr. B in favour of Mr. A was dishonoured. It is pertinent to mention that the suit was filed in the year 1995 and the suit was a summary suit. Mr. B claims that he should be given an unconditional leave to defend as there is a "triable issue". Will Mr. B get an unconditional leave to defend? Explain the summary procedure in detail.
Model Answer

Step 1 — Frame the issue (IRAC)

Issue: whether a defendant to a 1995 summary suit on a dishonoured cheque, who claims that his defence raises a "triable issue", is entitled as of right to unconditional leave to defend.

Step 2 — Rule

  • The suit (1995) is governed by the post-1976 (amended) Order XXXVII, Rules 2 and 3.
  • Santosh Kumar v. Bhai Mool Singh (AIR 1958 SC 321): the test is whether the defence raises a real issue, not a sham one — if the facts alleged are established there would be a good or even plausible defence; once the court is so satisfied, "leave cannot be withheld and no question about imposing conditions can arise". Conditions exist only to ensure a speedy trial.
  • Mechalec (AIR 1977 SC 577) — classes (a)–(e): classes (a)/(b) (good defence / triable issue) yield unconditional leave; a deposit condition is justifiable only in class (e) (sham/moonshine defence where the court shows mercy).
  • Milkhiram (AIR 1965 SC 1698, 4JJ): under the amended rule, even where an apparently triable issue is raised, the judge may impose conditions if he doubts the genuineness — a plausible but improbable defence may be put on terms.
  • IDBI Trusteeship v. Hubtown ([2016] 11 SCR 660): the six current principles — substantial defence → unconditional; triable issues → ordinarily unconditional; triable issues + doubt about good faith → conditions (time/mode of trial and/or deposit/security); plausible-but-improbable → conditions up to the whole principal + interest; frivolous/vexatious → refusal; admitted part → deposit first.

Step 3 — Application

  • Mr. B's bare assertion of a "triable issue" is not enough — the triable issue must be disclosed by his affidavit filed within ten days of the summons for judgment (R.3(5)).
  • If the affidavit genuinely discloses a fair/bona fide dispute (e.g., the cheque was only collateral security and the debt was otherwise discharged — the very facts of Santosh Kumar; or denial of privity of contract — Mechalec), Mr. B is ordinarily entitled to unconditional leave: as Bose J. held, the stage of proof comes only after leave, and the court cannot demand documents or evidence with the affidavit.
  • But if, on the affidavits, the court entertains a real doubt about Mr. B's good faith (post-amendment law — Milkhiram/Defiance Knitting/IDBI principle 3), or finds the defence plausible but improbable (principle 4), it may grant leave only on terms, including deposit or security; and if any part of the claim is admitted, that part must be deposited as a condition precedent (second proviso; Southern Sales).
  • If the defence is illusory/sham/"moonshine", leave will be refused and Mr. A is entitled to judgment forthwith (R.3(6)(a)).

Step 4 — Conclusion

Yes — but only conditionally on the quality of his defence: Mr. B will get unconditional leave if (and only if) his affidavit discloses a substantial defence or genuine triable issues and no part of the claim is admitted; a mere label of "triable issue" without disclosing supporting facts will not do. Where doubt exists as to good faith, the court may impose conditions; where the defence is sham, leave is refused altogether.

Step 5 — The summary procedure in detail (the "explain" limb)

  1. Applicability (R.1): High Courts, City Civil Courts, Courts of Small Causes (and notified other courts); suits on bills of exchange, hundies, promissory notes; and suits to recover a debt/liquidated demand in money on a written contract, an enactment (fixed sum/debt, not penalty) or a guarantee. A dishonoured cheque suit squarely falls under R.1(2)(a).
  2. Institution (R.2): plaint with specific averment, no outside relief, and the inscription "(Under Order XXXVII of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908)"; Form 4 summons. If Mr. B does not appear, the plaint's allegations are deemed admitted and decree follows, executable forthwith.
  3. Appearance (R.3(1)–(3)): within ten days of service, entering appearance (person/pleader) and filing address for service; intimation to Mr. A's pleader; deemed service at the address given.
  4. Summons for judgment (R.3(4)): Mr. A serves Form 4A summons, returnable not less than ten days, with an affidavit verifying the cause of action and amount and stating that in his belief there is no defence.
  5. Leave to defend (R.3(5)): within ten days, Mr. B applies by affidavit disclosing sufficient facts; leave unconditional or on just terms; refusal only if no substantial defence or the defence is frivolous/vexatious; admitted amounts must be deposited.
  6. Hearing (R.3(6)): no application/refusal → judgment forthwith; leave granted → security/directions, non-compliance with which → judgment forthwith.
  7. Condonation (R.3(7)): delay excusable for sufficient cause. (Art. 118, Limitation Act: ten days from service of summons.)
  8. After decree (R.4): under special circumstances (e.g., non-service), the decree may be set aside, execution stayed, and leave to appear and defend granted — all in one application, and only if the defendant also discloses a defence (Rajni Kumar).
  9. Rules 5–7: deposit of the instrument with the court; noting charges recoverable; otherwise the ordinary procedure applies.
Q. 5(a) / 2017(10 marks)
Discuss the concept of 'triable issues' in relation to summary procedure, on which leave to defend is granted.
Model Answer

1. Meaning

In a summary suit the defendant has no right to defend; he applies for leave (R.3(5)). A triable issue is a question of fact or law raised by the defendant's affidavit which, if resolved in his favour at a trial, would constitute a defence — i.e., the issue is fit for judicial investigation in the ordinary course rather than fit for summary elimination.

2. The tests

  • Jacobs v. Booth's Distillery (HL, via Santosh Kumar): whenever the defence raises a triable issue, leave must be given — unconditionally, else it is illusory.
  • Santosh Kumar: "whether the defence raises a real issue and not a sham one, in the sense that, if the facts alleged by the defendant are established, there would be a good, or even a plausible defence on those facts".
  • Raj Duggal v. Ramesh Kumar (AIR 1990 SC 2218): whether the defence raises a real, honest and bona fide dispute raising a triable issue; if so, leave should not be refused.
  • Milkhiram: triable-ness is judged from pleadings and affidavits only; the court cannot call for evidence; sham, fantastic or highly improbable defences, and defences "plausible but improbable", may be put on terms.

3. Illustrations of triable issues

  • fair dispute as to the meaning of the document on which the claim is founded;
  • uncertainty as to the amount actually due;
  • facts entitling the defendant to interrogate the plaintiff or cross-examine his witnesses;
  • serious conflict on facts, or real difficulty on a question of law.

Conversely: a pure question of law not dependent on facts may be decided at once without leave (ULS); a defence never taken before the suit raises no triable issue (Punjab & Sindh Bank, Del); an indemnity claim requiring proof of loss is outside O.37 altogether (SB Saurashtra v. Ashit Shipping).

4. Consequences of a triable issue being raised

LawResult of a triable issue
Pre-amendment (Santosh Kumar, Mechalec classes (a)–(b))Unconditional leave as of course; conditions only in class (e) (sham defence + mercy).
Post-amendment (Milkhiram, Defiance Knitting, Southern Sales, IDBI Trusteeship principles 2–3)Ordinarily unconditional; but with genuine doubt about good faith, conditions as to time/mode of trial, deposit or security are permissible; admitted amounts must be deposited first.

5. Conclusion

The triable-issue concept is the hinge of Order XXXVII: it separates the defendant who deserves a trial from the one who deserves a decree against him, while the conditioning power (post-1976) lets the court calibrate protection to the quality of the defence — "care must be taken to see that real and genuine triable issues are not shut out by unduly severe orders as to deposit" (Milkhiram; IDBI).

Q. 5(b) / 2019short note (cross-topic)
"Ex parte decree and its remedies." (Attempt any four — short note)
Model Answer — with the Order XXXVII angle

Remedies against an ex parte decree (as listed in the DU PYQ book): (1) application under O.9 R.13 within 30 days of the decree (or of knowledge where summons were not duly served); (2) appeal under s.96(2) — a statutory right not lost by dismissal of the O.9 R.13 application (Bhanu Kumar Jain v. Archana Kumar, AIR 2005 SC 626); (3) review under s.114 / O.47; (4) a regular suit on fraud; and, for decrees on non-service, recourse in appropriate cases.

The summary-suit overlay: where the decree has been passed under Order XXXVII (on default of appearance, non-application for leave, refusal of leave, or breach of conditions), the proper remedy is Rule 4 of Order XXXVII, not O.9 R.13 — because R.4 specifically provides for setting aside such decrees (R.7). In Rajni Kumar v. Suresh Kumar Malhotra (2003), the SC held: relief is available only under special circumstances (non-service of summons being a clear example); all reliefs — setting aside the decree, staying/setting aside execution, and leave to appear and defend — must be claimed in one application; and the applicant must also disclose facts showing a defence, failing which the decree stands (as happened to the tenant-appellant there, whose interest was however reduced from 18% to 6% under s.34 since the liability was not commercial). The High Court's refusal to set aside was upheld for want of any disclosed defence.

Q. 5(e) / 2019short note (cross-topic)
"Res sub judice." (Attempt any four — short note)
Model Answer — with the Order XXXVII angle

Section 10 bars the court from proceeding with the trial of a subsequently instituted suit where the matter in issue is also directly and substantially in issue in a previously instituted suit between the same parties, in a court competent to try both. The test of applicability: whether the decision in the earlier suit would operate as res judicata in the later one (SPA Annamalay Chetty v. B.A. Thernhill). It is a rule of procedure — not a bar on jurisdiction, institution, or interlocutory orders.

The summary-suit exception (Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-op. Marketing Federation, AIR 1998 SC 1952 — the DU case material's illustration): the Bank's summary suit on an irrevocable letter of credit was later in time than the Federation's ordinary recovery suit. Holding that s.10 did not require the summary suit to be stayed, the SC reasoned that "in a summary suit the 'trial' really begins after the Court or the Judge grants leave to the defendant to contest the suit" — the court may therefore proceed up to the hearing of the summons for judgment and pass judgment where leave is not sought/refused or conditions are broken. The Division Bench's contrary view (relying on Harish Chandra — an election-law case) was set aside. Write this case whenever s.10 and O.37 intersect.

The tests in one breath

  • Applicability: qualifying court + qualifying class (bill/hundi/note; debt on written contract / enactment (non-penalty) / guarantee).
  • Leave (old law): real issue, not sham → unconditional (Santosh Kumar); deposit only for moonshine-mercy class (e) (Mechalec).
  • Leave (current law): six principles of IDBI — quality of defence calibrates the terms.
  • R.4 decree-relief: special circumstances + disclosed defence, in one application (Rajni Kumar).
  • S.10 overlay: "trial" in a summary suit begins only after leave (Indian Bank).
  • Bank guarantee: autonomous; only established fraud or irretrievable injustice breaks it (ONGC, applying Svenska & Tarapore).
One-line definitions
Summary suit: a suit where the defendant must obtain the court's leave to defend, failing which judgment follows forthwith.
Trible issue: an issue whose facts, if established at trial, would yield a good or plausible defence.
Special circumstances: exceptional, extraordinary, uncommon circumstances justifying "putting the clock back" on a summary decree.
