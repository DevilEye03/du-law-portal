The Concept of Injunctions & Interlocutory Orders

Meaning, kinds, object and nature of the interim relief that Order XXXIX regulates
Core Definition
An injunction is a judicial process whereby a party is required to do, or to refrain from doing, any particular act — a remedy in the form of an order of the court addressed to a particular person that either prohibits him from doing or continuing a particular act (prohibitory injunction) or orders him to carry out a certain act (mandatory injunction). A temporary (interim / interlocutory) injunction restrains a party only temporarily — until disposal of the suit or further orders — and is regulated by Order XXXIX CPC (defined in s. 37, Specific Relief Act, 1963).

1.1  Interim Relief is Ancillary

It is well-settled that interim relief can always be granted in the aid of, and as ancillary to, the main relief available to a party on final determination of his rights in a suit or other proceeding (Takwani; ULS). The temporary injunction does not decide rights — it preserves the subject-matter so that any final decree remains meaningful.

  • Status quo is the key: "the Court in exercise of the power of granting ad interim injunction is to preserve the subject matter of the suit in the status quo for the time being" (Dalpat Kaur); it "can only be granted to restore status quo and not to establish a new state of thing" (DU PYQ book).
  • Preventive, not punitive: it is "in the nature of preventive relief to a litigant to prevent future possible injury" (Dalpat Kaur).
  • Equitable and discretionary: "the grant of injunction is a discretionary relief" exercised on sound judicial principles; a party is not entitled to it as of right (Dalpat Kaur; Shiv Kumar Chadha v. MCD).
  • Co-terminus with the suit: it may be date-wise, event-wise, result-wise or till further orders, "but always co-terminus with the final decision in the suit" (DU PYQ book).

1.2  Who may apply — and against whom

  • Not the plaintiff alone — a defendant may also apply for an injunction against the plaintiff (Takwani: Rattu v. Mala; Gujarat Bottling).
  • An injunction may be issued only against a party to the suit/proceeding — not against a stranger or third party, not against a court or judicial officer; normally only against persons within the jurisdiction of the court (Takwani).
  • Applications are made by affidavit or otherwise — "proved by affidavit or otherwise" (R.1).
What is NOT an injunctionFood Corporation of India v. Sukha Deo Prasad (AIR 2009 SC 2330, via ULS): a direction to a tenant to deposit arrears of rent in court (with interest on default) is not an injunction — "a direction to pay money either by way of final or interim order is not considered to be an injunction"; it fell outside rr. 1 and 2 of Order XXXIX.

1.3  Object — the balance metaphor

The object of interim relief is "to evolve a workable formula... striking a delicate balance between two conflicting interests, i.e., injury and prejudice likely to be caused to the plaintiff if the relief is refused; and injury and prejudice likely to be caused to the defendant if the relief is granted" (Takwani).

"The plaintiff's need for such protection must be weighed against the corresponding need of the defendant to be protected against injury resulting from his having been prevented from exercising his own legal rights... The court must weigh one need against another and determine where 'the balance of convenience' lies." — American Cyanamid Co. v. Ethicon Ltd. (1975, HL, per Lord Diplock — adopted in Takwani).

Interim injunctions maintain status quo at institution and prevent change in it "until the final determination of the suit" — protective relief against future possible injury (Manohar Lal; ITO v. M.K. Mohd. Kunhi).

Statutory Framework & Rule 1 — When a Temporary Injunction May be Granted R.1 — GROUNDS

Sections 94 & 95 CPC and the three statutory grounds (plus the inherent-power residual head)

2.1  The Enabling Provisions — ss. 94 & 95 CPC

Section 94 (supplementary proceedings) — in order to prevent the ends of justice from being defeated, the Court may, if it is so prescribed:
(a) order arrest & imprisonment; (b) order attachment of property; (c) grant a temporary injunction and in case of disobedience commit the person guilty thereof to civil prison and order that his property be attached; (d) appoint a receiver; (e) make such other interlocutory orders as may appear just and convenient.
Section 95 (compensation for obtaining injunction on insufficient grounds)
Where a temporary injunction has been obtained by the plaintiff on insufficient grounds, or where the suit fails and it appears there was no reasonable ground for instituting it, the court may, on the defendant's application, order the plaintiff to pay up to ₹50,000 as reasonable compensation for expense or injury (including injury to reputation) caused by the injunction.
Mulla's key analysis of s. 94(c)"Section 94(c) shows that a court may grant a temporary injunction thereunder, only if it is so prescribed" — 'prescribed' (s. 2(16)) means prescribed by rules, and the rules are O.39 rr. 1 & 2. Therefore a temporary injunction may be granted under s. 94(c) only if the requirements of O.39 rr. 1 & 2 are made out — it is one integrated source of power, not two alternative ones. The court may also grant a temporary injunction in exercise of inherent power under s. 151 — but there it does not act under the Code at all (the Manohar Lal territory).
Order XXXIX, Rule 1 — Cases in which temporary injunction may be granted
Where in any suit it is proved by affidavit or otherwise—
(a) that any property in dispute in a suit is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by any party to the suit, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree; or
(b) that the defendant threatens, or intends, to remove or dispose of his property with a view to defrauding his creditors; or
(c) that the defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury to the plaintiff in relation to any property in dispute in the suit,
the Court may by order grant a temporary injunction to restrain such act, or make such other order for the purpose of staying and preventing the wasting, damaging, alienation, sale, removal or disposition of the property or dispossession of the plaintiff, or otherwise causing injury to the plaintiff in relation to any property in dispute in the suit as the Court thinks fit, until the disposal of the suit or until further orders.

2.2  Elements of the Three Grounds

ClauseDanger / threat to be proved (by affidavit or otherwise)Illustrative protection
(a)Property in dispute in danger of being wasted, damaged, alienated by any party, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decreeRestrain alienation / waste / damage / sale — preserve the res for the decree
(b)Defendant threatens or intends to remove or dispose of his property to defraud creditorsRestrain removal/disposal — an ancillary conservatory shield (compare attachment before judgment, O.38)
(c)Defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury in relation to property in disputeRestrain dispossession/injury until disposal — the anti-eviction clause
History of clause (c) — as narrated in Dalpat KaurClause (c) was inserted pursuant to the recommendation of the Law Commission by the CPC (Amendment) Act 104 of 1976, w.e.f. 1-2-1977 (the DU text prints the Act year as "1966" — a misprint; the bare Act's footnotes confirm Act 104 of 1976). Earlier, there was no express power to grant ad interim injunction against dispossession — courts acted only under the inherent power of s. 151 (Dalpat Kaur, K. Ramaswamy J.). Clause (c) also carried into Rule 1 the "dispossession of the plaintiff... or otherwise causing injury" language in the operative limb.

The residual head — "interest of justice"

Beyond rr. 1(a)–(c), injunction may issue: (d) where a defendant is about to commit a breach of contract or other injury of any kind — that is Rule 2's territory; and (e) where the court is of the opinion that the interest of justice so requires — the residual head sourced from ss. 94(c) & 151, whose classic authority is Manohar Lal v. Seth Hiralal (Takwani; ULS). This is exactly the majority/minority battleground in Manohar Lal — see Case 23.

Note also: r. 1 grounds are illustrative of when an injunction may issue — the rule "nowhere provides that no temporary injunction can be granted unless the case falls within the said provision" (Takwani, §13) — the Manohar Lal (majority) position.

Rule 2 — Injunction against Breach; Rule 2-A — Consequences of Disobedience R.2 & R.2-A

The contractual-injury injunction and the penal machinery that enforces every Order XXXIX order
Order XXXIX, Rule 2 — Injunction to restrain repetition or continuance of breach
(1) In any suit for restraining the defendant from committing a breach of contract or other injury of any kind, whether compensation is claimed in the suit or not, the plaintiff may, at any time after the commencement of the suit, and either before or after judgment, apply for a temporary injunction to restrain the defendant from committing the breach of contract or injury complained of, or any breach of contract or injury of a like kind arising out of the same contract or relating to the same property or right.
(2) The Court may grant such injunction on such terms as to the duration of the injunction, keeping an account, giving security or otherwise, as the Court thinks fit. (Sub-rules (3)–(4) of the old rule were omitted by the 1976 Amendment.)

3.1  Working Elements of Rule 2

  • Subject-matter: breach of contract or "other injury of any kind" — the injury may be to property, right or person; compensation need not be claimed.
  • Timing: any time after commencement of suit — even after judgment (e.g., pending appeal).
  • Reach: the injunction may cover not only the breach complained of but any like-kind breach from the same contract or the same property/right.
  • Terms: duration, keeping accounts, security — the court's equitable conditioning power.
Order XXXIX, Rule 2-A — Consequence of disobedience or breach of injunction (inserted 1976)
(1) In the case of disobedience of any injunction granted or other order made under rule 1 or rule 2, or breach of any of the terms on which the injunction was granted, the Court granting the injunction (or any court to which the suit is transferred) may order the property of the person guilty of such disobedience or breach to be attached, and may also order such person to be detained in civil prison for a term not exceeding three months, unless in the meantime the Court directs his release.
(2) No attachment shall remain in force for more than one year; at the end of that time, if the disobedience or breach continues, the property attached may be sold, and out of the proceeds the Court may award such compensation as it thinks fit to the injured party and shall pay the balance, if any, to the party entitled thereto.

3.2  Anatomy of the Enforcement Machinery

  • Three-step ladder: (i) attachment of property; (ii) detention in civil prison up to 3 months; (iii) on continued breach beyond a year of attachment — sale of the attached property and compensation to the injured party from the proceeds.
  • Who is liable: not only the party — "a person is liable to proceedings under Order XXXIX Rule 2A even if he was not personally a party to the suit, provided he is shown to have been agent or servant of the defendant and to have violated the order of injunction in spite of knowledge that there was such an order" (Ram Prasad Singh v. Subodh Prasad Singh, AIR 1983 Pat 278; Brijendra Prasad Narain Singh, AIR 1963 Pat 449 — via ULS).
  • The expression "person" in r. 2-A is used compendiously — to cover everyone against whom the injunction runs (defendant, his agents, servants, workmen); otherwise "the order of injunction would be frustrated and the power rendered ineffective and unmeaning".
  • Special provision over general law: refusal to respect a restraining order is destructive of the rule of law; r. 2-A is a special provision which prevails over the general law under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (DU PYQ book).
  • Contempt overlay: wilful and deliberate disobedience of interim orders "can never be said to be bona fide, honest and in good faith" — contemners have been held guilty under s. 12 Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 read with s. 94(c), O.39 r. 2-A and Art. 129 (Mulla, quoting C.K. Thakker J.); a tactical apology ("tendered when the contemners are in a tight corner") has been rejected.

Rules 3, 3-A, 4 & 5 — Procedure: Notice, Ex Parte Orders, Discharge & Corporations R.3–R.5

The procedural safeguards that discipline the discretionary power
Order XXXIX, Rule 3 — Before granting injunction, Court to direct notice to opposite party
The Court shall in all cases, except where it appears that the object of granting the injunction would be defeated by the delay, before granting an injunction, direct notice of the application to be given to the opposite party:
Provided that, where it is proposed to grant an injunction without giving notice, the Court shall record the reasons for its opinion that the object of granting the injunction would be defeated by delay, and require the applicant—
(a) to deliver to the opposite party, or send him by registered post, immediately after the order, a copy of the application for injunction together with (i) a copy of the affidavit in support; (ii) a copy of the plaint; and (iii) copies of documents relied on; and
(b) to file an affidavit (on the day of the order or the next day) stating that the copies have been so delivered or sent.
Order XXXIX, Rule 3-A — Court to dispose of application for injunction within thirty days (inserted 1976)
Where an injunction has been granted without notice, the Court shall endeavour to finally dispose of the application within thirty days from the date of grant; where it is unable to do so, it shall record its reasons for the inability.
Order XXXIX, Rule 4 — Order for injunction may be discharged, varied or set aside
Any order for an injunction may be discharged, varied or set aside by the Court on application by any party dissatisfied with it:
Provided that if in the application or supporting affidavit a party has knowingly made a false or misleading statement on a material particular and the injunction was granted without notice, the Court shall vacate the injunction unless, for recorded reasons, it considers it unnecessary in the interests of justice:
Provided further that where an order was passed after hearing a party, it shall not be discharged, varied or set aside on that party's application except for changed circumstances, or if the Court is satisfied the order has caused undue hardship to that party.
Order XXXIX, Rule 5 — Injunction to corporation binding on its officers
An injunction directed to a corporation is binding not only on the corporation itself, but also on all members and officers of the corporation whose action is sought to be restrained.

4.1  The Ex Parte Injunction — a Jurisdiction of Exception

  • Rule: notice to the opposite party before injunction. Exception: where the object would be defeated by delay — but then reasons must be recorded, copies served immediately by the applicant, and an affidavit of service filed (R.3 proviso).
  • Shiv Kumar Chadha v. MCD (1993) 3 SCC 161: the requirement to record reasons is mandatory, not a formality — "a party... who is being restrained from exercising a right which such party claims to exercise either under a statute or under the common law, must be informed why, instead of following the requirement of Rule 3, the procedure prescribed under the proviso has been followed". If compliance were optional, "the introduction of the proviso by Parliament shall be a futile exercise" — the proviso attracts the principle that a thing required by statute to be done in a particular manner must be done in that manner or not at all. Ex parte injunctions are "for cases of real urgency, where there has been a true impossibility of giving notice".
  • Morgan Stanley Mutual Fund v. Kartick Das (1994) 4 SCC 225 — the seven factors for ex parte injunctions: (i) will irreparable/serious mischief ensue to the plaintiff? (ii) would refusal involve greater injustice than grant? (iii) the time at which the plaintiff first had notice of the act complained of (preventing improper orders behind a party's back); (iv) acquiescence — no ex parte order for one who sat by; (v) utmost good faith expected of the applicant; (vi) even if granted, only for a limited period; (vii) the general trident (prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable loss) still applies.
  • Purpose of Rule 3: to let the opposite party show cause, and if an order has already issued, to file objections — "to prevent the petitioner [from] retain[ing] undue advantage of the situation and abuse of the process of law" (Morgan Stanley, via ULS).
  • Rule 4's discipline: knowingly false statements → injunction shall be vacated; orders made after hearing → revisable only for changed circumstances or undue hardship. Mulla adds: an ex parte temporary injunction is set aside under R.4, not under O.9 r.13 (an injunction order is not a decree).

Interlocutory Orders — Rules 6 to 10 R.6–R.10

The Order's second half: protective orders passed during the pendency of the suit

5.1  What is an interlocutory order?

Orders passed by a court during the pendency of a suit, before final adjudication — relating to matters of procedure, passed to assist parties in prosecuting their case, to protect the subject-matter, or to ensure determination on merits. They do not determine substantive rights (ULS). Like the temporary injunction, they exist to prevent the ends of justice from being frustrated.

The Trident — Tests for Grant of Temporary Injunction DALPAT KAUR FORMULATION

Prima facie case × irreparable injury × balance of convenience — the doctrine every DU examiner tests
Dalpat Kaur v. Prahlad Singh (1993) — the governing formulation (K. Ramaswamy, J.)Grant of injunction is a discretionary relief. The exercise thereof is subject to the court satisfying that (1) there is a serious disputed question to be tried in the suit and that, on the facts before the court, there is probability of the applicant being entitled to the relief asked for; (2) the court's interference is necessary to protect the party from the species of injury — irreparable injury or damage would ensue before the legal right would be established at trial; and (3) the comparative hardship or mischief or inconvenience which is likely to occur from withholding the injunction will be greater than that likely to arise from granting it.

6.1  Test I — Prima facie case

  • "Prima facie case is not to be confused with prima facie title which has to be established on evidence at the trial. Prima facie case is a substantial question raised, bona fide, which needs investigation and a decision on merits" (Dalpat Kaur).
  • Martin Burn Ltd. v. R.N. Banerjee (AIR 1958 SC 79): "A prima facie case does not mean a case proved to the hilt but a case which can be said to be established if the evidence which is led in support of the same were believed... the relevant consideration is whether on the evidence led it was possible to arrive at the conclusion in question and not whether that was the only conclusion."
  • Not a fool-proof case — only a fair question to agitate about the existence of the right, with the property preserved till the question is decided (DU PYQ book; Paidsetti Bhanknarayna, AIR 1999 Ori 92 — title need not be established, only a fair question about the right).
  • At this stage the court does not try title or resolve conflicts of evidence — that is for the trial (Lord Diplock, American Cyanamid).

6.2  Test II — Irreparable injury

  • Non-interference would result in irreparable injury, and no other remedy is available except injunction (Dalpat Kaur).
  • "Irreparable injury does not mean that there must be no physical possibility of repairing the injury, but means only that the injury must be a material one, namely one that cannot be adequately compensated by way of damages" (Dalpat Kaur; Cotton Corpn. of India).
  • American Cyanamid (HL): if damages would adequately compensate the plaintiff, no interlocutory injunction should normally be granted, however strong the claim — and conversely, if the defendant can be compensated under the plaintiff's undertaking as to damages, there is no ground to refuse.
  • An injury is irreparable where no certain pecuniary standard exists for measuring damages (Takwani).

6.3  Test III — Balance of convenience (better: "balance of inconvenience")

  • The court "should exercise sound judicial discretion to find the amount of substantial mischief or injury which is likely to be caused to the parties if the injunction is refused and compare it with that which is likely to be caused to the other side if the injunction is granted. If on weighing competing possibilities or probabilities of likelihood of injury the court considers that, pending the suit, the subject-matter should be maintained in status quo, an injunction would be issued" (Dalpat Kaur).
  • Takwani's gloss: the proper expression is "balance of inconvenience" — analogous to "greater hardship" under Rent Laws.

6.4  The Overlay — Conduct, Clean Hands & the Sequence of Tests

  • Sequence is strict: "when a party fails to prove prima facie case to go for trial, question of considering the balance of convenience or irreparable loss and injury... would not be material at all" (Kashinath Sansthan v. Srimad Sudhindra Thirtha Swamy, AIR 2010 SC 296 — via ULS). The trident is applied prong by prong; failure of prong one ends the matter.
  • Conduct: grant of injunction is an equitable relief — the applicant must come with clean hands; delay, laches, acquiescence, suppression of material facts, or monetary adequacy of relief are independent grounds of refusal (Takwani; Mandati Ranganna v. T. Ramachandra, AIR 2008 SC 2291: "a person who had kept quiet for a long time and allowed another to deal with the properties exclusively, ordinarily would not be entitled to an order of injunction").
  • Gujarat Bottling Co. v. Coca Cola Co. (AIR 1995 SC 2372): the equitable-conduct discipline applies not only to the applicant for an injunction, but equally to a party seeking to vacate one already granted — "His conduct should be fair and honest."
  • The warning of Dalpat Kaur: the three phrases "are not rhetoric phrases for incantation, but words of width and elasticity, to meet myriad situations presented by man's ingenuity in given facts and circumstances, but always is hedged with sound exercise of judicial discretion to meet the ends of justice."
  • Public interest caution: interim orders can injure the public interest as violators "perpetuate the contraventions made by them by delaying the final disposal" — the court must not protect a wrongdoer (Shiv Kumar Chadha v. MCD).

Appeals, Revision & Allied Remedies; the Anti-Suit Injunction

The remedial map around interlocutory orders — and the cross-border cousin of Manohar Lal

7.1  Remedial Map

OrderRemedy
Order granting / refusing / modifying temporary injunction (O.39 rr. 1 & 2)Appeal under s. 104 read with O.43 r. 1(r). Such an order is not a "decree" (excluded by s. 2(2)(a) because an appeal lies as an order) — hence O.9 r.13 cannot be used to set aside an ex parte temporary injunction; the route is O.39 r.4 (Mulla). No second appeal against the appellate order (s.104(2)).

RevisionAn order granting/refusing injunction is a "case decided" under s.115 and revisable (Takwani: Firm Ishardass Devi Chand; Saharanpur Cane Union; Hindustan Lever). LPA maintainable where a single judge of an HC passes an O.39/O.40 order (Mulla).
Appellate restraintSkyline Education Institute v. S.L. Vaswani (AIR 2010 SC 3221): where the trial court's discretion rests on objective consideration of material and cogent reasons, the appellate court "will be loath to interfere simply because on a de novo consideration... it is possible... to form a different opinion" — interference only for error apparent, perversity or manifest injustice (see also Wander Ltd. v. Antox India).
Injunction obtained on insufficient groundsCompensation up to ₹50,000 — s.95 CPC (Takwani: "attachment on insufficient grounds").

7.2  The Anti-Suit Injunction — Modi Entertainment Network v. WSG Cricket Pte Ltd. (2003) 4 SCC 341

The modern charter for injunctions restraining proceedings in another (including foreign) court — the refined descendant of the Manohar Lal problem. The SC laid down seven principles:

  1. Discretion requires: (a) the defendant is amenable to the personal jurisdiction of the court; (b) refusal would defeat the ends of justice; (c) comity — respect for the other court — must be borne in mind.
  2. Where more forums than one are available, the court examines which is the appropriate forum (forum conveniens), and may restrain proceedings that are oppressive or vexatious or in a forum non conveniens.
  3. Under a jurisdiction clause, recitals of exclusive/non-exclusive jurisdiction are relevant but not determinative — true interpretation of the contract governs.
  4. A court of natural jurisdiction will not normally restrain a defendant where parties agreed to the exclusive jurisdiction of a chosen court (including a foreign court), save exceptionally for good and sufficient reasons (impossibility, vis major etc.).
  5. Under a non-exclusive clause for a neutral foreign forum, ordinarily no anti-suit injunction — the parties are presumed to have weighed their convenience.
  6. A party to a jurisdiction clause cannot normally be prevented from approaching the court of choice — that would aid breach of contract; proceedings in the chosen court are not per se vexatious.
  7. The burden of proving forum non conveniens or oppressiveness is on the party so contending.
Link it backManohar Lal (1962) posed the same question domestically: could the Indore court restrain the plaintiff from proceeding at Asansol? The majority acknowledged the power (via s.151) but struck down the order — an anti-suit-type injunction demands great care, ample evidence of vexatiousness, and is "very rarely exercised" (Cohen v. Rothfield). Modi Entertainment systematises that caution for the international arena.

CASE 23 — DU CASE MATERIAL

Manohar Lal v. Seth Hira Lal

AIR 1962 SC 527  |  Supreme Court of India  |  Majority: Raghubar Dayal J. (for himself and on behalf of the Bench)  |  Dissent: J.C. Shah, J.  |  Appeal from the Madhya Pradesh High Court
O.39 temporary injunctions.151 inherent poweranti-suit restraints.10 stay of suits.22 interpleaderCohen v. RothfieldHyman v. Helm

Facts in Detail

  • The partnership: Manohar Lal (the appellant) and Seth Hira Lal (respondent No.1) were partners in Diamond Industries, a registered firm at Indore — a partnership at will. Dissolution followed. By a dissolution deed dated 22 August 1945 all assets and business were taken over by Manohar Lal, who undertook to pay the liabilities and dues.
  • The arbitration clause: the dissolution deed contained a second proviso that any dispute about the deed's terms would be referred to arbitration, and — crucially — that "the court at Indore and nowhere else" shall have jurisdiction over such disputes.
  • Round 1 — Asansol suit (18 August 1948): Hira Lal sued Manohar Lal in the Court of the Sub-Judge, Asansol (West Bengal), for ₹1,00,000 as his share on dissolution of the partnership plus ₹18,000 for all other claims — plain money claims.
  • Arbitration move (27 October 1948): Manohar Lal, relying on the deed, petitioned the Indore court under s.34, Arbitration Act, 1940 to stay the Asansol suit and refer the disputes to arbitration.
  • Round 2 — Indore suit (3 January 1949): Manohar Lal himself filed a suit at Indore for a declaration and accounts — that the respondent was not entitled to anything further and must account for and pay ₹1,90,519-0-6 due from him.
  • The injunction attempt: in that Indore suit, Manohar Lal applied for a temporary injunction to restrain Hira Lal from proceeding with the Asansol suit. It was refused at first.
  • Calcutta High Court (7 May 1953): on appeal by Hira Lal, the Asansol suit was restored (it had been struck off for default) — so the two parallel proceedings were now live again.
  • The s.151 application (14 September 1953): Manohar Lal moved the Indore court afresh — this time expressly under s.151 CPC (inherent power) — for an order restraining Hira Lal from continuing the Asansol suit.
  • ADJ Indore: Additional District Judge granted the injunction (2 December 1953) — restrained Hira Lal from proceeding at Asansol, modified to continue until the s.34 arbitration application was decided.
  • MP High Court (10 May 1955): Hira Lal's revision succeeded — the ADJ's order was set aside. Manohar Lal appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues

  1. Whether the stay of the Asansol suit was governed exclusively by s.10 CPC (stay of suit) — and if so, whether its conditions were satisfied.
  2. Whether the Calcutta HC's restoration order foreclosed further consideration at Asansol, making the Indore injunction an attempt to overreach a decision of a competent superior court.
  3. Whether the two suits were in the same court for the purposes of s.22(2) (power to stay proceedings where common questions of fact/law arise in separate suits), read with the definitions in s.2(3)–(5) CPC.
  4. The core issue: whether the Indore court had power — under O.39 rr. 1 & 2 or s.151 CPC — to grant a temporary injunction restraining a party from proceeding in another court of concurrent jurisdiction.
  5. If such power exists, whether it was properly exercised here.

Arguments for Manohar Lal (appellant)

  • The dissolution deed's second proviso confined jurisdiction to Indore and provided for arbitration; the Asansol suit violated both — proceeding there was oppressive.
  • The Asansol suit was a device to avoid the agreed forum; courts should restrain litigants from pursuing proceedings instituted in breach of contract.
  • Independent of O.39, every court possesses inherent power (s.151) to prevent abuse of its process and to prevent the ends of justice being defeated — the very power the ADJ invoked.
  • Parallel litigation over the same partnership accounts in two provinces invites conflicting decrees; the Indore court could avert that by holding Hira Lal to the forum he contracted for.
  • On the merits the trident was satisfied: a serious question (the deed's effect), irreparable injury (multiplicity of proceedings), and balance of convenience favouring one forum.

Arguments for Seth Hira Lal (respondent)

  • s.10 is exhaustive of the power to stay a suit in certain cases; its conditions — matters "pending in the same court", issues "directly and substantially in issue" in both — were absent, so no stay could be granted.
  • The Asansol suit had been restored by the Calcutta High Court — a competent superior court; an Indore injunction would nullify that order and produce conflict between courts of co-ordinate jurisdiction.
  • Neither s.94 nor O.39 rr. 1 & 2 authorises restraining a suitor from proceeding in another court; O.39 protects property in dispute in the suit — not forum-selection.
  • s.151 cannot be used to create a power the Code withholds or to side-step s.10's conditions.
  • The Indore suit and the Asansol suit rested on different causes (accounting vs. money claims) — not the "same matter" in the same court.

The Decision & the Two Judgments

MAJORITY — Raghubar Dayal J. (appeal dismissed; MP HC upheld)
  • Power exists: "courts have inherent power to issue injunctions in cases not covered by the provisions of sub-section (c) of section 94 or rule 1 of order XXXIX" — the power "to make orders for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of the process of the Court" remains, though "such power is not to be exercised when its exercise will conflict with the policy of the Civil Procedure Code".
  • O.39 not exhaustive: Rule 1 (post-1976, including cl.(c) inserted in 1976 — after this case) "does not and cannot provide for all cases in which injunctive relief may be needed"; r.1's opening ("may... grant") means it merely regulates instances — "it cannot be said that the court has no power to issue an injunction in cases other than those specified" (Takwani).
  • But s.10 governs stays: the stay of a suit is a matter for s.10, whose conditions (same court; same matters directly and substantially in issue) were not satisfied — the Asansol and Indore suits were in different courts and rested on different matters. The power could not be used to circumvent s.10.
  • Calcutta HC order: the restoration of the Asansol suit was a decision by the court of competent jurisdiction; it was not open to the Indore court to disregard it while granting an injunction. (Shah, J. read this as effectively the ratio: an inferior court cannot stultify a superior court's order.)
  • Exercise was flawed: the ADJ's order would run even during the pendency of the s.34 arbitration application — the very forum where the agreed forum question fell for decision. Interference refused.
MINORITY — J.C. Shah, J. (would dismiss; no power at all)
  • Courts cannot bind other courts: an injunction by one court cannot operate upon the proceedings of another court; s.94 is the only source of power to grant temporary injunctions in a suit, and it permits injunctions only as prescribed by the rules — rr. 1 & 2 — which "deal with entirely different matters" (property in dispute, breach of contract/injury), not restraint of suits.
  • s.151 gives no new substantive power: s.151 merely saves the court's inherent power "to make such orders as may be necessary for the ends of justice or to prevent abuse of process" — it does not confer power to issue injunctions restraining a party from proceeding in another court; if rr. 1 & 2 do not permit it, s.94(c) does not permit it.
  • Conflict of jurisdiction: an injunction operating to obstruct proceedings in a court of co-ordinate (or superior) jurisdiction would create intolerable conflict between courts.
  • Notably, Shah, J. — consistent with his premise — would allow the appeal on the ground of absence of power, but the operative result for the appellant was the same: no injunction.
The English authorities weighed in the judgment Cohen v. Rothfield (1919, CA): where two courts have concurrent jurisdiction, one may restrain a plaintiff from proceeding in the other — but (per Scrutton, L.J.) the power is discretionary, will be exercised with the greatest care, and is one that "should be very rarely exercised". Even if the power exists, this is not a fit case for it.
Hyman v. Helm (1881, CA): a court cannot by injunction restrain proceedings in a court of co-ordinate or superior jurisdiction; injunctions are for parties, not courts. But where a suit is pending in a court not of co-ordinate jurisdiction — e.g., a Scottish court vis-à-vis an English one, an anti-suit injunction may issue against the party (the anti-suit line running through to Modi Entertainment).
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED
  1. A civil court retains inherent power (s.151) to grant interim injunctions even in situations falling outside s.94(c) and O.39 rr. 1 & 2 — O.39 is not exhaustive of injunctive power. (This proposition — the majority's — is the settled law: cited in Dalpat Kaur and throughout Takwani/ULS.)
  2. But that inherent power cannot be exercised so as to conflict with the Code's policy — the stay of suits is governed by s.10 and s.10's conditions (same court; same matters directly and substantially in issue) must be satisfied before one suit's progress can be arrested on account of another.
  3. A court will not, by injunction, overreach or nullify orders of a court of concurrent/superior jurisdiction; injunctions run against parties, not against courts (Hyman v. Helm), and the anti-suit type of restraint is to be exercised with the greatest care and "very rarely" (Cohen v. Rothfield).
  4. Temporary injunctions aim to preserve the subject-matter of the suit and prevent irreparable injury pending final adjudication — they are ancillary protective relief, not jurisdictional weapons.

CASE 24 — DU CASE MATERIAL

Dalpat Kaur (Dulip Kaur) v. Prahlad Singh

AIR 1993 SC 276  |  Supreme Court of India  |  K. Ramaswamy, J.  |  Appeal from the Rajasthan High Court (Jaipur Bench)
O.39 R.1(c)trident testsdispossession threatlis pendens u/s 52 TPAappellate correctionstatus quo

Facts in Detail — Four Rounds of Litigation

  • Round 0 — the family settlement (14 June 1979): Prahlad Singh (respondent) agreed to purchase a house at Jaipur for ₹51,000 from one Sardar Singh; possession was to be handed over to him. The vend/possession arrangement embroiled the vendor's family — his wife Dalpat Kaur (appellant, a housewife with no independent means) and her sons — in years of litigation.
  • Round 1 — money suit & ex parte decree: Prahlad Singh sued Sardar Singh for refund of the price. A decree was passed ex parte against Sardar Singh. The house was attached in execution — and on 10 August 1983 it was sold; a sale deed was executed in favour of Prahlad Singh.
  • Round 2 — the wife's suit (No. 83 of 1984): Dalpat Kaur sued for a declaration that the sale deed was not binding on her and for possession, and obtained an ex parte ad interim injunction restraining alienation. But she did not apply for a final injunction after notice issued; the suit was later dismissed for default (protracted proceedings followed).
  • Round 3 — execution objections & the sons' suit (No. 278 of 1988): five objections were raised in execution of Prahlad Singh's decree by Dalpat Kaur and her sons, all dismissed. The sons then filed their own suit (278/1988), which was also dismissed for default and never restored.
  • Round 4 — the fraud suit (7 December 1988): Dalpat Kaur filed a fresh suit — for declaration that the sale deed dated 10-8-1983 was void and obtained by fraud — and applied for a temporary injunction to restrain Prahlad Singh from alienating the house pending the suit. Notice issued; the trial court directed that the application be decided expeditiously.
  • Trial court (3 November 1990): refused the temporary injunction.
  • Rajasthan High Court (26 February 1991): allowed the appeal and granted the temporary injunction restraining alienation or transfer pendente lite.
  • Prahlad Singh appealed to the Supreme Court; Dalpat Kuar and her sons contested — seeking affirmance, and in the alternative pressing that any injunction be conditioned so as to prevent Prahlad Singh from defeating the decree.

Issues

  1. Whether the circumstances disclosed a case for a temporary injunction under O.39 r.1(c) — a threat of dispossession / injury in relation to property in dispute.
  2. What is the correct content of the three tests — prima facie case, irreparable injury, balance of convenience — and how must a court exercise its discretion under them?
  3. Whether the High Court was right in reversing the trial court's refusal of the injunction.
  4. Whether the decree-holder Prahlad Singh's rights (acquired through court sale) could be protected while the fraud suit was pending — the correct terms of any interim order.

Arguments for Prahlad Singh (appellant before SC)

  • He was a purchaser in court auction under a valid, binding decree — sale deed executed 10-8-1983; the house was his.
  • Dalpat Kaur's earlier suit (83/1984) had been dismissed for default and she had never pursued a final injunction; the sons' suit too had suffered the same fate — the fresh "fraud" suit was an abuse of process, a third bite at the cherry.
  • No prima facie case: fraud was not substantiated at the interlocutory stage; her conduct (defaults, delays) disentitled her to equitable relief.
  • Since he held under a court sale, there was no danger of waste or alienation by him — no interim order was needed; restraint would harm a decree-holder's rights.

Arguments for Dalpat Kaur (respondent before SC)

  • The sale deed was void, obtained by fraud — a bona fide, serious question requiring trial; injunction was needed to keep the res intact.
  • Under r.1(c) (inserted 1976 precisely to cover dispossession threats), a plaintiff threatened with dispossession from property in dispute is entitled to protection of status quo.
  • Irreparable injury: she was a housewife with no independent means — eviction from the family home could not be compensated in money; the balance of convenience favoured preserving possession.
  • Transfer/alienation pendente lite would be hit by lis pendens (s.52 TPA) but would still cloud and complicate; prevention was better.

The Decision

Appeal dismissed; High Court's injunction restored (with modifications). K. Ramaswamy, J. found the High Court had reached the right result but by a "manifest error" in reasoning path — the SC corrected the approach and re-affirmed the injunction, directing expeditious disposal of the suit and of the pending injunction applications.

  • Trident satisfied: (i) a serious disputed question (fraud voiding the sale) existed, with probability of the plaintiff being entitled to relief; (ii) interim protection was needed to prevent irreparable injury before trial; (iii) the mischief of refusing (possible eviction/defeat of the suit) outweighed the mischief of granting (delay to the decree-holder's enjoyment).
  • Rule 1(c)'s purpose: before 1976 there was no express power to restrain threatened dispossession — courts acted under s.151; clause (c) now expressly covers a defendant threatening to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise injure her in relation to property in dispute.
  • On auction purchasers & lis pendens: transfer of the property pendente lite would not destroy the plaintiff's rights (s.52 TPA) — but alienation attempts during litigation must be restrained to avoid multiplicity; meanwhile, if the decree-holder was obstructed, he could be compensated for use and occupation, for which an appropriate allowance could be made in moulding the interim order.
  • Directions: the trial court was to dispose of the suit and all injunction applications expeditiously; the injunction would continue on appropriate terms until then.
PRINCIPLE EVOLVED — the authoritative statement of the trident
  1. Prima facie case ≠ prima facie title: "a substantial question raised bona fide, which needs investigation and a decision on merits." (Echoing Martin Burn: not "proved to the hilt".)
  2. Irreparable injury = "a material one, i.e. one that cannot be adequately compensated by damages"; and where no other remedy than injunction exists.
  3. Balance of convenience = weighing the substantial mischief of refusal against that of grant; if the mischief of refusal is greater, status quo is maintained by injunction until final decision.
  4. The three tests are "not rhetoric phrases for incantation" — words of "width and elasticity" hedged by sound judicial discretion to meet the ends of justice.
  5. Interim injunction is preventive relief, designed to preserve the subject-matter/status quo co-terminus with the suit — and appellate courts will intervene where the court below's exercise of discretion is vitiated by manifest error.
  6. O.39 r.1(c) is the express statutory home for injunctions against threatened dispossession (post-1976); before it, such relief flowed from inherent power — the bridge back to Manohar Lal.

PYQ — 2019
Q. 4(b) — "What are the tests for grant of temporary injunction? Discuss with the help of decided cases."
MODEL ANSWER

Introduction. A temporary injunction is interim relief granted at any stage of a suit, pending its final decision, to restrain a party from doing some act. It is regulated by O.39 rr. 1 & 2 CPC read with s. 94(c) and s. 37, Specific Relief Act, 1963, and is co-terminus with the suit. Its object is to preserve the status quo and prevent the ends of justice from being defeated, by evolving a workable formula balancing the plaintiff's need for protection against the defendant's need to be unhampered in exercising legal rights (Takwani).

The three tests. Although rr. 1 & 2 state the situations in which an injunction may issue, the courts have evolved three interlocking conditions which must ordinarily co-exist (Dalpat Kaur v. Prahlad Singh, AIR 1993 SC 276):

1 · Prima facie case
A substantial question raised bona fide which needs investigation at trial — not prima facie title. "A prima facie case does not mean a case proved to the hilt, but one which can be said to be established if the evidence led in support of it were believed": Martin Burn Ltd. v. R.N. Banerjee, AIR 1958 SC 79. The interim court does not conduct a mini-trial of title.
2 · Irreparable injury
Injury before the legal right is established at trial which cannot be adequately compensated in damages. It does not mean physical impossibility of repair, but "the injury must be a material one, i.e., one that cannot be adequately compensated by way of damages": Dalpat Kaur; American Cyanamid v. Ethicon (1975, HL) — if damages are an adequate remedy, no interlocutory injunction should normally issue, however strong the claim.
3 · Balance of convenience
Weighing the substantial mischief or inconvenience likely if the injunction is withheld against that likely if it is granted. The court maintains the subject-matter in status quo where the balance of hardship tilts towards the applicant: Dalpat Kaur.

Sequence and overlays. The tests are applied in sequence — where a party fails to make out a prima facie case, "the question of considering the balance of convenience or irreparable loss... would not be material at all" (Kashinath Sansthan v. Srimad Sudhindra Thirtha Swamy, AIR 2010 SC 296). Being an equitable relief, the applicant must come with clean hands; delay, laches, acquiescence or suppression of material facts defeat the application (Mandati Ranganna, AIR 2008 SC 2291); conduct is equally relevant to a party seeking to vacate an injunction (Gujarat Bottling v. Coca Cola, (1995) 5 SCC 545). The phrases are, warned Ramaswamy, J., "not rhetoric phrases for incantation" — they are hedged with sound judicial discretion.

Case applications. In Dalpat Kaur, a housewife of no means, whose house had been sold in court auction under a decree allegedly obtained by fraud, was held entitled to an injunction preserving the house: a serious question (fraud) needed trial, eviction could not be compensated in money, and the balance favoured preserving the res. In Manohar Lal v. Seth Hira Lal, AIR 1962 SC 527, the SC held that the injunctive power is not confined to O.39 (inherent power under s.151 exists) but cannot be used to defeat the scheme of s.10 (stay of suits) — showing that the trident operates within the Code's structure, not over it.

Conclusion. The three tests together ensure that the discretionary, equitable remedy protects the suitor's substantive rights pendente lite without prejudging the suit: the court preserves, but does not decide.

PYQ — 2020 (Open Book / ORE) · 25 MARKS
Q. 4 — "A filed a suit for permanent injunction against B, his neighbour, in respect of a boundary wall. Pending the suit, the court granted a temporary injunction restraining B from demolishing the wall. Despite the injunction, B's husband C demolished the wall and raised a construction of a room thereupon, claiming that the injunction operated only against B and not against him. A applied to the court for action against B and C under Order XXXIX, Rule 2-A CPC. Decide."
MODEL ANSWER

Issues raised. (1) Does a temporary injunction under O.39 bind only the party against whom it is issued, or also persons acting in concert with her — here B's husband C, not a party to the suit? (2) What action can the court take on A's application under O.39 r. 2-A, and against whom?

Relevant law — Rule 2-A and its reach. Rule 2-A(1) provides that on disobedience of any injunction granted under rr. 1 or 2, or breach of its terms, the court may order the property of the person guilty of the disobedience to be attached, and may order such person to be detained in civil prison for a term not exceeding three months; if the disobedience continues, the attached property may after one year be sold, with compensation awarded to the injured party (r.2-A(2)). The question is whether C — a non-party — is "a person guilty" within the rule.

The expression "person" in r. 2-A is used compendiously: it covers not only the party but every person against whom the order can operate, otherwise "the order of injunction would be frustrated and the power rendered ineffective and unmeaning". It is well-settled that a person is liable to proceedings under r. 2-A even if he was not personally a party to the suit, provided he is shown to be the agent or servant of the defendant and to have violated the order in spite of knowledge that such an order existed: Ram Prasad Singh v. Subodh Prasad Singh (AIR 1983 Pat 278, following Brijendra Prasad Narain Singh, AIR 1963 Pat 449). An injunction also binds all persons who act under the authority of, or in concert with, the party restrained — a doctrine long familiar to injunction law.

Application to the problem. C is B's husband, residing with her; the demolition of the very wall protected by the injunction, followed by construction of a room, was done in concert with and for the benefit of B, to defeat the court's order — C cannot be heard to say the injunction did not name him. If the expression were confined to parties, a defendant could flout any injunction through her spouse or agents, making r. 2-A "ineffective and unmeaning". Both B and C are therefore amenable to r. 2-A: B for breach of the injunction (and for connivance), C for disobedience with knowledge as a person acting under B's authority.

Relief the court can grant. On A's application the court may: (i) order attachment of the property of B and/or C; (ii) order their detention in civil prison up to three months unless earlier released (r.2-A(1)); (iii) if disobedience persists for a year, order sale of the attached property, award compensation to A from the proceeds and pay the balance to the party entitled (r.2-A(2)). Additionally, wilful and deliberate defiance of a court order is never bona fide and may be dealt with as contempt under s. 12 of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 read with s. 94(c) and Art. 129/215 — though r. 2-A, being a special provision, prevails for the party-enforcement route. The court may further require restoration of the wall / demolition of the unauthorised room while moulding interim protection (the exact course being within its discretion under the rule to "award such compensation... as it thinks fit to the injured party").

Conclusion. C's plea fails: the temporary injunction operates against B and all who act under her authority or in concert with her with knowledge, and both B and C are liable under O.39 r. 2-A — to attachment, civil prison, and (on continued disobedience) sale with compensation; their conduct may also attract contempt jurisdiction.

[Note: the PYQ book prints the rule as "Order XXXIX Rule 12A" — an OCR corruption; the correct provision, confirmed by the bare Act and ULS, is Rule 2-A, inserted by the CPC (Amendment) Act, 1976.]

PYQ — 2017
Q. 5(b) — "On what grounds can a temporary injunction be granted? Explain."
MODEL ANSWER (in brief — the grounds answer mirrors the 2019 answer's framework)

Statutory grounds (O.39 r.1, proved by affidavit or otherwise, in relation to property in dispute in a suit): (a) danger of the property being wasted, damaged or alienated by any party, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree; (b) defendant threatens or intends to remove or dispose of property to defraud creditors; (c) defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury in relation to property in dispute (inserted 1976). Rule 2: restraining breach of contract or other injury of any kind, repetition or continuance thereof, before or after judgment, on terms as to duration, accounts or security. Residual head: where the interest of justice so requires — the inherent power of ss. 94(c) and 151 recognised in Manohar Lal. Judicially evolved conditions: prima facie case, irreparable injury, balance of convenience (Dalpat Kaur; Martin Burn; American Cyanamid), with equitable overlays of delay, acquiescence and clean hands. For the full model answer, see the 2019 Q.4(b) answer above and Sections 2, 3 and 6 of these notes.
