The Topic in One Look

Six statutory pillars, three prescribed cases, one golden thread — the appeal is a creature of statute, and the second appeal is a luxury the law grants only for the law

Reading map — how this topic is examined

The Appeal — Meaning, Essentials and the Right of Appeal

No definition in the Code — the Privy Council supplies it; the right is statutory, vested, and governed by the law at the date the suit was instituted

What is an appeal?

Nagendra Nath Datta v. Suresh Chandra, AIR 1932 PC 165
“There is no definition of appeal in the Code of Civil Procedure, but their Lordships have no doubt that any application by a party to an appellate court, asking to set aside or reverse a decision of a subordinate court, is an appeal within the ordinary acceptation of the term.” An appeal is the removal of a cause from an inferior court to a superior court for the purpose of testing the soundness of the decision of the inferior court — “a complaint made to the higher court that the decree passed by the lower court is unsound and wrong.”
Attorney-General v. Sillem (1864) 10 HLC 704 (per Lord Westbury, LC)
Appeal is “a right of entering a superior court and invoking its aid and interposition to redress an error of the court below” — quoted and applied by the Supreme Court in Dayawati v. Inderjit, AIR 1966 SC 1423.
Three essentials of every appeal (Louis Blom)
(1) a decision (of a court or authority); (2) a person aggrieved — often, though not necessarily, a party to the original proceeding; (3) a reviewing body ready and willing to entertain the appeal.

The right of appeal — five settled propositions

  • Creature of statute: appeal is not a natural or inherent right. There is no right of appeal unless it is given clearly and in express terms by a statute — Ganga Bai v. Vijay Kumar, (1974) 2 SCC 393; Rami Manprasad v. Gopichand, (1973) 4 SCC 89. A statute that omits to provide an appeal cannot be declared ultra vires merely on that ground (Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab, (1994) 3 SCC 569).
  • Substantive, not procedural: the right of appeal is a vested right which accrues to the litigant on the date the lis commences and is governed by the law prevailing at the date of institution of the suit, not the law at the date of judgment or of filing the appeal — Garikapati Veeraya v. N. Subbiah Chaudhry, AIR 1957 SC 540; Bhau Ram v. Baij Nath, AIR 1961 SC 1327. It can be taken away only by a later enactment, expressly or by necessary implication.
  • Conditions attach: because the right is statutory, the legislature may impose conditions for its exercise, so long as they are not onerous (Anant Mills v. State of Gujarat, (1975) 2 SCC 175, per Khanna J.). Sections 100(3)–(4) — state the question, formulate it — are exactly such conditions.
  • A continuation of the suit: an appeal is treated as a continuation of the suit, not a new proceeding — hence the rules of the First Schedule (e.g., withdrawal of appeals) mirror the trial rules.
  • Who may appeal? Any person aggrieved by the decree — including a proforma defendant, a guardian ad litem, a trustee, and even a non-party whose interests are directly and prejudicially affected. A stranger with no interest cannot appeal. A person aggrieved by a finding in the judgment may file cross-objections under O.XLI r. 22 even where the decree was passed in his favour.

Exam pointer: when a problem asks “can X appeal?”, walk down this ladder: Is there a decree or an appealable order? → Which statute grants the appeal? → Is X a person aggrieved? → Is the appeal within time (Limitation Act ss. 5–6, O.XLI r.3)?

Appeals from Decrees — Section 96 and its Companions (ss. 97–99)

Every decree appealable unless the Code itself withdraws the appeal — the four carve-outs in s. 96, the preliminary-decree trap in s. 97, and the rule of tolerance in s. 99
Section 96 — Appeal from original decree (as amended by Act 104 of 1976, w.e.f. 1-2-1977; bare Act text)
(1) Save where otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any other law for the time being in force, an appeal shall lie from every decree passed by any Court exercising original jurisdiction to the Court authorized to hear appeals from the decisions of such Court.
(2) An appeal may lie from an original decree passed ex parte.
(3) No appeal shall lie from a decree passed by the Court with the consent of parties.
(4) No appeal shall lie, except on a question of law, from a decree in any suit of the nature cognisable by Courts of Small Causes, when the amount or value of the subject-matter of the original suit does not exceed ten thousand rupees (raised from ₹3,000 by Act 46 of 1999, w.e.f. 1-7-2002).

ss. 97–99 — the companions of s. 96

s. 97 — appeal from final decree where none from preliminary
A party aggrieved by a preliminary decree who does not appeal from it is precluded from disputing its correctness in an appeal from the final decree. Policy: preliminary decrees (e.g., partition, partnership, mortgage) are drafted to be challenged then — not after rights have crystallised in the final decree.
s. 98 — benches of two or more judges
Appeal decided by the opinion of the majority; where there is no majority for varying or reversing, the decree is confirmed. On a difference of opinion on a point of law, an even-numbered bench may state the point for hearing by other judges of the same court. (Sub-s. (3) preserves Letters Patent provisions.)
s. 99 — error not affecting the merits
No decree shall be reversed or substantially varied, nor a case remanded, for misjoinder or non-joinder of parties or causes of action or any error, defect or irregularity not affecting the merits of the case or the jurisdiction of the Court — Provided: does not apply to non-joinder of a necessary party. s. 99A (1976) extends the same tolerance to orders under s. 47 unless the decision is prejudicially affected. Policy: appeals correct outcomes, not every procedural slip.

Machinery of the first appeal — Order XLI in one scan

  • r. 1 — memorandum: must be in prescribed form, signed by the appellant or his recognised agent, accompanied by a certified copy of the decree; grounds must be substantial questions of law as well as questions of fact.
  • rr. 3–5 — limitation and registration: appeal beyond time or against a party not entitled — as in the trial court (s. 5 of the Limitation Act operates; O.XLI r.3(2) mandate to reject time-barred appeals).
  • rr. 17–21 — dismissal / readmission / rehearing: dismissal for non-prosecution; application to readmit within 30 days; refusal to re-admit or re-hear is appealable (O.XLIII r.1(t)).
  • r. 22 — cross-objections: the respondent may attack any part of the decree within one month of service of notice of hearing — “nothing but an appeal, a cross-appeal at that”; it survives even if the main appeal is withdrawn or dismissed for default; no court-fee discrimination, and limitation is counted like an appeal.
  • r. 23 / 23A / 25 — remand and reference (treated with s. 107(1)(b)–(c) in §5).
  • rr. 27–29 — additional evidence (treated in full in §6).
  • rr. 30–31 — judgment: pronounced in open court, signed and dated; must state the points for determination, the decision thereon, the reason and the operative decree.
  • r. 33 — power of the court of appeal: may pass any decree and make any order which ought to have been made, or remand for a new trial — the residual power that makes s. 107 work.
  • r. 35 — decree in appeal bears the date of the judgment; a dissenting judge need not sign it (r. 34); copies furnished to parties (r. 36) and certified copy sent down to the trial court (r. 37).
Doctrine of merger — the other side of the appeal: when the appellate court decides, there cannot be, at one and the same time, more than one operative decree governing the same subject-matter — the decree of the trial court merges in the decree of the appellate court. But the doctrine “is not one of rigid and universal application” and depends on the nature of the appellate or revisional order and the scope of the statutory jurisdiction (State of Madras v. Madhuri Mills, AIR 1967 SC 681; CIT v. Amritlal Bhogilal, AIR 1958 SC 868) — see Shankar Ramchandra v. Krishnaji Dattatreya, (1969) 2 SCC 74. This is a favourite DU short-note (Q.6(a)/2017).

Appeals from Orders — Sections 104–106 and Order XLIII

Every decree carries an appeal; only a closed list of orders does — and errors in the rest travel as grounds of objection inside the decree-appeal
Section 104 — Orders from which appeal lies (bare Act text; cls. (a)–(f) omitted by Act 10 of 1940)
(1) An appeal shall lie from the following orders, and save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any law for the time being in force, from no other orders: — (ff) an order under s. 35A (compensatory costs); (ffa) an order under s. 91 or s. 92 refusing leave to institute a representative suit; (g) an order under s. 95 (compensation for arrest/attachment without sufficient cause); (h) an order under any provision of this Code imposing a fine or directing arrest or detention in civil prison (except in execution of a decree); (i) any order made under rules from which an appeal is expressly allowed by rules — Provided that no appeal shall lie against an order under cl. (ff) save on the ground that no order, or an order for payment of a less amount, ought to have been made.
(2) No appeal shall lie from any order passed in appeal under this section (no second appeal from an order-appeal).
O.XLIII r. 1 exhaustively lists the rule-orders appealable under s. 104(i) — the live clauses include: (a) O.VII r.10 return of plaint; (c) O.IX r.9 refusal to set aside dismissal; (d) O.IX r.13 refusal to set aside ex parte decree; (f) O.XI r.21 consequences of failure to discover; (i) O.XXI r.34; (j) O.XXI rr.72/92 setting aside / refusing to set aside a sale; (ja) O.XXI r.106(1); (k) O.XXII r.9; (l) O.XXII r.10; (n) O.XV r.5; (na) O.XXXIII rr.5/7 indigent person; (p) O.XXXV interpleader; (q) O.XXVIII payment into court; (r) O.XXXIX rr.1, 2, 2A, 4, 10 interim orders; (s) O.XL rr.1, 4; (t) O.XLI rr.19/21 refusal to re-admit or re-hear an appeal; (u) O.XLI rr.23/23A remand; (w) O.XLVII r.4 order granting review.

Decree vs order — the appealability divide

The DU solved papers state the rule crisply (Q.5/2019): “Generally the decrees are appealable in the first appeal unless expressly prohibited, but every order is not appealable — only those orders specifically enumerated in Section 104 and Order XLIII, Rule 1, are appealable.”

  • Why the difference? A decree conclusively determines the rights of the parties; an order is every other formal expression of the court’s decision (s. 2(2), (14)). If every interlocutory order were appealable, litigation would never end.
  • Test for an appealable order: is it named in s. 104 or O.XLIII r. 1 (or a special statute)? If not, no appeal lies — and the court cannot enlarge the list by analogy. The list is exhaustive, not illustrative.
  • s. 105(1) — the safety valve: where a decree is appealed from, any error, defect or irregularity in any order affecting the decision of the case may be set forth as a ground of objection in the memorandum of appeal. The non-appealable order thus gets reviewed as part of the decree-appeal, not separately.
  • s. 105(2): a party who could appeal from an order of remand but does not, is precluded from disputing its correctness later.
  • s. 106 — forum: an appeal from an order lies to the court to which an appeal would lie from the decree in that suit; where the order was made by a court (not a High Court) in appellate jurisdiction, the appeal lies to the High Court.
  • s. 104(2) — no second appeal from an order decided in appeal under s. 104 — the mirror of s. 100A for orders.

Special regimes that displace ss. 96–104

“Save as otherwise provided”
Remember the opening words of s. 96(1) and s. 104(1): special statutes channel their own appeals. Examples to cite in a problem answer: Rent Control legislation (see Gill & Co., c3 — s. 39(2) of the Delhi Rent Control Act confines the second appeal to a substantial question of law exactly like s. 100 CPC); Commercial Courts Act, 2015 ss. 13(1), 13(1A) — appeal to the appellate commercial court / High Court within 60 days, and no second appeal; Decree under O.XXXVII — the defendant’s fight is at the leave-to-defend stage. Always check the special Act before answering under the general Code.
Revision is not appeal — exam trap
Where no appeal lies, the High Court may still act under s. 115 (revision) in cases of jurisdictional error, illegal material, or illegality — but that is Topic 7, exercised sparingly and not as a disguised appeal. Never write “the party can appeal under s. 115” — it is an instant marker of confusion.

The Second Appeal — Section 100 and the Substantial Question of Law

From three loose grounds to one strict gateway: the 1976 amendment, the Law Commission’s rationale, Chunilal’s test — and the discipline Koppi Setty enforces
Section 100 — Second appeal (substituted by Act 104 of 1976, s. 37, w.e.f. 1-2-1977 — bare Act text)
(1) Save as otherwise expressly provided in the body of this Code or by any other law for the time being in force, an appeal shall lie to the High Court from every decree passed in appeal by any Court subordinate to the High Court, if the High Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law.
(2) An appeal may lie under this section from an appellate decree passed ex parte.
(3) In an appeal under this section, the memorandum of appeal shall precisely state the substantial question of law involved in the appeal.
(4) Where the High Court is satisfied that a substantial question of law is involved in any case, it shall formulate that question.
(5) The appeal shall be heard on the question so formulated and the respondent shall, at the hearing of the appeal, be allowed to argue that the case does not involve such question: Provided that nothing in this sub-section shall be deemed to take away or abridge the power of the Court to hear, for reasons to be recorded, the appeal on any other substantial question of law, not formulated by it, if it is satisfied that the case involves such question.
s. 100A (subs. by Act 22 of 2002): where an appeal from an original or appellate decree or order is heard and decided by a Single Judge of a High Court, no further appeal (no Letters Patent Appeal) shall lie from that judgment.
s. 101: no second appeal except on the ground mentioned in s. 100.
s. 102: no second appeal where the subject-matter of the suit is recovery of money not exceeding ₹25,000.
s. 103: in any second appeal, the High Court may, if the evidence on record is sufficient, determine any issue of fact (a) not determined by the lower appellate court or both courts below, or (b) wrongly determined by reason of a decision on a question of law as referred to in s. 100.

Before and after 1976 — why the section was rewritten

The old s. 100 (pre-1976)
A second appeal lay on any of three grounds: (a) the decision being contrary to law or usage having the force of law; (b) failure to determine some material issue of law or usage; (c) a substantial error or defect in procedure which may possibly have produced error in the decision on the merits. High Courts interpreted these liberally — “mixed questions of fact and law”, “legal inference from facts proved”, “the case has not been properly approached” — producing, in the Law Commission’s words, “a practical goodbye to the basic principle that on questions of fact decisions of courts of first instance would be final subject to one appeal.”
The Fifty-fourth Report & the Shah Committee
The Law Commission (54th Report) recommended confining the second appeal to cases where the question of law involved was substantial — but not limited to questions of “general importance”: if the law is clearly laid down by the High Court and a subordinate court’s decision violates it, the High Court’s power to correct it must remain intact. The Shah Committee on High Court arrears pressed for “stricter and better scrutiny of second appeals”. The result: the substituted s. 100 — “drastically changed and considerably curtailed”.
The six consequences of the amendment (Takwani)
(i) HC must be satisfied the case involves a substantial question of law; (ii) the memorandum must precisely state it; (iii) the HC must formulate it at admission; (iv) the appeal is heard only on that question; (v) at the hearing, the respondent may argue the case does not involve it; (vi) the HC may still hear another SQL for reasons recorded (proviso to s. 100(5); O.XLII r. 2).

What the amended section forbids — the phrases examiners love

  • The second appeal must not become a “third trial on facts” or “one more dice in the gamble” — the avowed object of the amendment (and of Koppi Setty, c2).
  • “Involves” implies a considerable element of necessity — the question must arise in the case and be necessary to decide it; that a party raised the question is not enough (SBI v. S.N. Goyal, (2008) 8 SCC 92; Pankaj Bhargava v. Mohinder Nath, (1991) 1 SCC 556; Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari, (2001) 3 SCC 179).
  • Formulation is a condition precedent — sine qua non for exercising the power; it is “not a mere formality, but is meant to be adhered to” (Singaram v. Ramanathan, 2021, as extracted in the DU solved papers; Dnyanoba v. Maroti, (1999) 2 SCC 471: SQLs must be framed at admission and the appeal heard only on them).
  • No interference with pure questions of fact — the HC’s jurisdiction under s. 100 “does not confer any jurisdiction to interfere with pure questions of fact” (Roop Singh v. Ram Singh, (2000) 3 SCC 708, para 7 — quoted in Q.3/2019).
  • Concurrent findings of the courts below are not to be disturbed on second appeal — disturbing them without formulating an SQL invites the Supreme Court to set the HC aside and remit (Koppi Setty).
  • s. 100A kills the LPA in second appeals — a Single Judge’s decision in a second appeal admits of no further appeal: it would amount to a “third appeal”.

“Substantial question of law” — the anatomy of the test

The phrase is undefined, but the Supreme Court has built a layered test, and DU has examined every layer. The full judgement anatomy is in Chunilal V. Mehta (c1); the map is here:

Layer 1 — Raghunath Prasad, AIR 1927 PC 110
Substantial means “substantial as between the parties” — a question of general importance is neither necessary nor sufficient. If the decision does not turn on it, it is not substantial as between the parties.
Layer 2 — Dinkarrao v. Rattansey, AIR 1949 Nag 300 (Bose CJ)
A question of law is substantial as between the parties “if the decision turns one way or another on the particular view taken of the law.” Note: Chunilal found this too wide standing alone — “so defined, it would cover every question of law” — but the decision-turns idea survives inside the larger test. Q.4(a)/2018 asks you to take exactly this position.
Layer 3 — Madras FB, R. Subba Rao, AIR 1951 Mad 969
The question must be “fairly arguable” — open, not free from difficulty, capable of a difference of opinion. Approved by the Supreme Court.
The Chunilal composite test (AIR 1962 SC 1314, para 6)
A question of law is substantial if — (A) it is of general public importance, OR it directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties; AND (B) it is either open (not finally settled by the SC / Privy Council / Federal Court) or not free from difficulty or calls for discussion of alternative views. NOT substantial: question settled by the highest court; well-settled principles merely to be applied (misapplication is not enough); or a palpably absurd plea.
Not a SQL — checklist (Takwani)
concurrent findings of fact · a first-appellate finding of fact · two views possible on evidence · new case / new plea in second appeal · omnibus or general grounds · inference drawn on record evidence · question concluded by SC/PC/FC · finding attacked as merely “erroneous” (not perverse).
Is a SQL — checklist (Takwani)
conflict of judicial opinion · finding recorded without any evidence · legal effect / inference from proved or admitted facts · disregard of relevant/admissible or admission of irrelevant/inadmissible evidence · misconstruction of documents · interpretation of material documents · admissibility of evidence · onus placed on the wrong party · disposal of appeal while an O.XLI r. 27 application was pending.

Hypothetical problem — apply s. 100 to this fact pattern (the kind DU asks)

Facts: A’s suit for declaration and possession is dismissed after full trial. The District Judge (first appeal) re-appreciates the evidence and dismisses the appeal. A files a second appeal in the High Court stating as the sole ground: “The findings of the courts below are erroneous, perverse, and against the weight of evidence.” The High Court, without framing any question, re-appreciates the evidence, reverses both courts, and decrees A’s suit. B appeals to the Supreme Court. Advise B.
Model answer skeleton
  • The memorandum is defective: s. 100(3) requires the memorandum to precisely state the substantial question of law. “Findings are perverse” is the classic omnibus ground — on the settled checklist it is not an SQL (attack on a finding as erroneous, not perverse-on-record; no question of law set out).
  • The High Court’s duty at admission: under s. 100(1)–(4) the HC could act only if satisfied the case involved an SQL — satisfaction imports necessity — and it was then bound to formulate that question. Formulation is “sine qua non and a condition precedent” (Santosh Hazari; Singaram 2021: not a mere formality).
  • The hearing transgressed s. 100(5): the appeal must be heard on the question formulated — the HC instead held a third trial on facts, which the 1976 amendment was designed to prevent (“no third trial on facts, no one more dice in the gamble” — Law Commission rationale applied in Koppi Setty).
  • Concurrent findings: two courts agreed on the facts after hearing them twice — the two-hearing rule. Interference requires a formulated SQL; none existed.
  • Conclusion: B will succeed — the Supreme Court will set aside the High Court’s judgment and remit the case for a fresh decision after formulation of the SQL, if any (exactly the course taken in Koppi Setty and the line of cases it cites — Dnyanoba, Roop Singh). Note for completeness: if, properly formulated, no SQL exists, the second appeal falls at the gate; B should also plead delay and expense as the very mischief the amendment targets.

Powers of the Appellate Court — Section 107 and Order XLI

Four powers, one duty: as far as possible, draw the final curtain on the litigation — the appellate court is not a spectator but a court of the case
Section 107 — Powers of Appellate Court (bare Act text)
(1) Subject to such conditions and limitations as may be prescribed, an Appellate Court shall have power —
(a) to determine a case finally;
(b) to remand a case;
(c) to frame issues and refer them for trial;
(d) to take additional evidence or to require such evidence to be taken.
(2) Subject as aforesaid, the Appellate Court shall have the same powers and shall perform as nearly as may be the same duties as are conferred and imposed by this Code on Courts of original jurisdiction in respect of suits instituted therein.
s. 108: the provisions of Part VII relating to appeals from original decrees apply, so far as may be, to appeals from appellate decrees and to orders under this Code or special/local laws (unless a different procedure is provided).

s. 107(2) — parity with the trial court: the appellate court has the same powers and duties as a court of original jurisdiction — it may, for instance, decide the whole case, take accounts, or grant any relief the trial court could have granted. That is why an appeal is “a continuation of the suit”, and why the appellate court in Chunilal (c1) could itself quantify the liquidated damages and dismiss the appeal with a money decree already embedded.

Additional Evidence at the Appellate Stage — Order XLI, Rule 27

The general rule is exclusion; the Code carves three narrow gateways; the discretion is the court’s, not the party’s — and the reasons must be recorded
Order XLI, Rules 27–29 (bare Act text; cl. (aa) inserted by Act 104 of 1976, w.e.f. 1-2-1977)
r. 27(1). The parties to an appeal shall not be entitled to produce additional evidence, whether oral or documentary, in the Appellate Court. But if —
(a) the Court from whose decree the appeal is preferred has refused to admit evidence which ought to have been admitted; or
(aa) the party seeking to produce additional evidence establishes that, notwithstanding the exercise of due diligence, such evidence was not within his knowledge or could not, after the exercise of due diligence, be produced by him at the time when the decree appealed against was passed; or
(b) the Appellate Court requires any document to be produced or any witness to be examined to enable it to pronounce judgment, or for any other substantial cause,
the Appellate Court may allow such evidence or document to be produced, or witness to be examined.
r. 27(2). Wherever additional evidence is allowed, the Court shall record the reason for its admission.
r. 28 (mode). The Appellate Court may either take such evidence itself, or direct the trial court (or any other subordinate court) to take it and send it up.
r. 29 (points). The Appellate Court shall specify the points to which the evidence is to be confined and record them on its proceedings.

The doctrine in five propositions

  • General rule — appeal runs on the record: the appellate court decides on the evidence led before the trial court (Soonda Ram v. Rameshwarlal, (1975) 3 SCC 698); s. 107(1)(d) is the exception, operating only through the gateways of r. 27 (K. Venkataramiah v. A. Seetharama Reddy, AIR 1963 SC 1526).
  • “Additional” ≠ “better”: the term does not mean evidence over and above what the party led below; a party who led no evidence below is not disqualified — the question is only whether a gateway is satisfied (Jaipur Development Authority v. Kailashwati Devi, (1997) 7 SCC 297).
  • Shivajirao Nilangekar Patil v. Dr. Mahesh Madhav Gosavi, (1987) 1 SCC 227 — the three principles: (1) the applicant must establish that with his best efforts the evidence could not have been adduced at the first instance; (2) the opposite party must have an opportunity to rebut it; (3) the evidence must be relevant for determination of the issue.
  • Clause (b) is for the court, not the party: it is only where the Appellate Court “requires” it — “it must be the Court that requires it” — that additional evidence comes in; the clause is not intended to allow a litigant who has failed below to patch up the weak parts of his case and fill up omissions (Parsotim Thakur v. Lal Mohar Thakur, AIR 1931 PC 143). The evidence must be needed to enable the court to pronounce judgment — never to enable it to pronounce the judgment a particular way (Municipal Corpn. of Greater Bombay v. Lala Pancham, AIR 1965 SC 1008, Constitution-strength 5-Judge Bench).
  • “Any other substantial cause”: includes removing obscurities to make the judgment “more satisfactory” (K. Venkataramiah) — but the cause must be substantial, and the discretion must be exercised on sound judicial principles, in the interests of justice, with reasons recorded (r. 27(2)).

Exam trap — the “I realised its importance late” plea
Gill & Co. rejects precisely this: “no justification for permitting additional evidence admittedly in the possession of the appellants on the flimsy ground that they did not realise its importance till an adverse finding was given.” The lateness of the insight is a reason against admission, not for it.

Hypothetical problem — the O.XLI r. 27 trap, DU-style

Facts: In a suit for recovery against P, the trial court decrees on the basis of P’s ledgers. P’s first appeal is pending before the District Judge. P now tenders (i) a bundle of his own account books seized by the Income-Tax Department in a raid after the trial began — P never sought their production from the Department below; and (ii) an affidavit of his cousin, a former employee, who “now remembers” a material payment. P says he did not realise the importance of either until the decree went against him. The appellate court refuses both. P argues refusal violates natural justice. Decide.
Model answer skeleton
  • Gateway (a) — fails: neither item was ever tendered below, so there was no “refusal to admit evidence which ought to have been admitted” (Gill & Co.: evidence never tendered cannot have been improperly refused).
  • Gateway (aa) — fails: the seizure was within P’s knowledge (it happened during the trial), yet he neither requisitioned nor summoned the records — no “due diligence”; and the cousin was always within reach. Due diligence is judged on what the party did, not on what he later wished he had done.
  • Gateway (b) — fails: the requirement belongs to the court, not the party; there is no lacuna in the record the court needs filled to pronounce judgment — only weak parts of P’s own case seeking a patch (Parsotim Thakur; Lala Pancham: the clause exists to enable the court to pronounce judgment, not to pronounce it a particular way; evidence from the party’s own possession/relations also risks being self-serving).
  • “Did not realise its importance” is the flimsiest of grounds — Gill & Co. holds exactly this plea insufficient; if anything, it shows the evidence was available and the failure deliberate.
  • Natural justice is not engaged: r. 27 refusal is a discretionary case-management order; where additional evidence is admitted, the other side’s right to rebut is the natural-justice element (Shivajirao Patil principle 2). Refusal here protects, not violates, fair play — the opposite party won on the record both parties made.
  • Conclusion: refusal justified and liable to be affirmed; if the appellate court had allowed the evidence, it was bound to record reasons (r. 27(2)), specify points (r. 29), and choose the mode (r. 28).

Chunilal V. Mehta & Sons Ltd. v. The Century Spinning & Manufacturing Co. Ltd.
AIR 1962 SC 1314 · 1962 Supp (3) SCR 549 · Supreme Court of India · J.C. Shah & V. Mudholkar JJ. (Mudholkar J. for the Court) · Appeal by special leave against refusal of an Art. 133(1)(a) certificate by the Bombay High Court · DU Case Material (LB-302), Topic 6, Case 6 · Also the anchor case for DU PYQs Q.3/2019 & Q.4(a)/2018

FFacts

  • By a managing agency agreement dated 15 June 1933, the appellant firm Chunilal V. Mehta & Sons became managing agents of the respondent mill company, Century Spinning & Manufacturing Co., for 21 years (the company was incorporated in March 1933).
  • cl. 10: remuneration — Rs. 6,000 per month, plus a top-up so that the total did not fall short of 10% of the gross profits each year. cl. 12: the remuneration accrued from day to day. cl. 14: if the company determined the agreement without the agents’ default, the company would pay, by way of liquidated damages, “a sum equal to the aggregate amount of the monthly salary of not less than Rs. 6,000” payable for the unexpired term.
  • On 23 April 1951 the company terminated the agency. The termination’s wrongfulness was admitted — the only question was quantum: the agents claimed Rs. 2,34,000 under cl. 14 (Rs. 6,000 × 39 months unexpired), and in the alternative damages beyond it.
  • The trial court and the Bombay High Court decreed Rs. 2,34,000 with interest but refused a certificate of fitness under Art. 133(1)(a) of the Constitution (the question — construction of cl. 14 — was, it thought, not a substantial question of law of general importance). The agents appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave, on quantum and on the certificate.

IIssues

  1. Whether the construction of cl. 14 of the managing agency agreement — on which the agents’ claim to Rs. 2,34,000 (and the company’s liability capped at it) turned — was a substantial question of law of general importance so as to attract Art. 133(1)(a) (the Court chose to re-state the law of “substantial question of law” generally, for the benefit of the High Courts).
  2. On merits: whether “the aggregate amount of the monthly salary of not less than Rs. 6,000” in cl. 14 meant the fixed Rs. 6,000 (yielding Rs. 2,34,000), or imported the 10%-of-profits top-up of cl. 10; and whether the clause was exhaustive or the agents could claim damages beyond it.

AArguments of the parties

Appellants (managing agents) — N.A. Palkhivala
  • The phrase “not less than Rs. 6,000” shows the parties contemplated the salary could be more — i.e., it incorporated the cl. 10 top-up to 10% of gross profits, so liquidated damages should be computed on the higher, profits-linked salary.
  • cl. 14 was not exhaustive: being a stipulation “in addition to” and not “in lieu of” damages, it did not exclude the general measure of damages under s. 73, Contract Act; the agents could prove and recover more for the unexpired term.
  • In any event, the question of its construction was of general importance and the High Court erred in refusing the certificate.
Respondent (the mill company)
  • cl. 14 was a genuine liquidated-damages clause — a fair pre-estimate of the loss of the agency for the residue of the term — and under s. 74, Contract Act, where the contract itself specifies a sum payable on breach, that sum (and no more) is recoverable, without proof of actual loss.
  • “Not less than” merely emphasised the irreducible minimum — the fixed Rs. 6,000 — and cl. 12’s day-to-day accrual could fit only a sum certain: profits are known only at the year’s end, so the parties cannot have intended the damages to fluctuate with profits.
  • The decree of the courts below should stand.

DDecision

  • Holding 1 — the law of substantial questions of law restated (Mudholkar J., para 6): “The proper test for determining whether a question of law raised in the case is substantial would, in our opinion, be whether it is of general public importance or whether it directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties and, if so, whether it is either an open question in the sense that it is not finally settled by this Court or by the Privy Council or by the Federal Court or is not free from difficulty or calls for discussion of alternative views. If the question is settled by the highest court or the general principles to be applied in determining the question are well-settled and there is a mere question of applying those principles or that the plea raised is palpably absurd, the question would not be a substantial question of law.”
  • The Court surveyed the earlier tests: Raghunath Prasad v. Commr. of Pratapgarh, AIR 1927 PC 110 (substantial “as between the parties”, not general importance); Dinkarrao v. Rattansey, AIR 1949 Nag 300 (Bose CJ — substantial if the decision turns one way or the other on the view of law) — found too wide (“so defined, it would cover every question of law”); and the Madras Full Bench in R. Subba Rao v. B. Sundara Raghavan, AIR 1951 Mad 969 (fairly arguable / room for difference of opinion) — approved.
  • Construction of a document of title which is the foundation of the parties’ rights necessarily raises a question of law; applied here, the construction of cl. 14 of the 1933 agreement — with a stake of over Rs. 26 lakh — was substantial; the Bombay High Court’s refusal of the Art. 133(1)(a) certificate was erroneous (though the Supreme Court was entertaining the matter by special leave, the discussion was written to guide High Courts and future benches).
  • Holding 2 — merits: Palkhivala’s construction rejected. “Not less than” only emphasises the irreducibility of the Rs. 6,000; the day-to-day accrual under cl. 12 presupposes a sum certain (the 10% top-up is knowable only at year-end); and cl. 14 being a liquidated-damages clause, s. 74 of the Contract Act excludes any claim to unascertained damages under s. 73. Rs. 2,34,000 with interest — affirmed; appeal dismissed with costs.

PPrinciple & exam use

The Chunilal composite test for a substantial question of law: substantial = (general public importance) OR (directly & substantially affects the parties’ rights) AND (open / not free from difficulty / calls for discussion of alternative views). Not substantial: settled law; well-settled principles merely applied (misapplication is not enough); palpably absurd pleas. A question of law can be substantial as between the parties without being of general importance — the decision-turns (Dinkarrao) and fairly-arguable (Madras FB) formulations are absorbed, not rejected wholesale. Second string for contract answers: a true liquidated-damages clause (s. 74) excludes proof of further s. 73 damages; and the construction of a foundational document is itself a question of law.
Why this case is prescribed here: the certificate jurisdiction (Art. 133(1)(a)) and the second appeal (s. 100 CPC) share the very same phrase — “substantial question of law”. Chunilal is thus the authoritative dictionary of the term that opens (or shuts) the High Court’s gate under s. 100. DU’s Q.3/2019 and Q.4(a)/2018 both answer themselves out of para 6 of this judgment (see §8).

Koppi Setty v. Ratnam & others v. Pamarti Venka & others (the DU material’s case head)
2007 RLR 27 (NSC) as per the LB-302 file header — citation caution: the DU syllabus page prints 2009 RLR 27; cite as “2007 RLR 27 (NSC) / 2009 RLR 27” with the discrepancy noted · Supreme Court of India (SLP against a second-appeal judgment of a High Court under s. 100 CPC) · DU Case Material (LB-302), Topic 6, Case 7 · The anchor case for the “discipline” side of s. 100 — Q.3/2019’s centrepiece

FFacts

  • A suit fought through trial and first appeal ended in concurrent findings of fact against the party who later sought to escalate.
  • That party went to the High Court in a second appeal under s. 100 CPC. The High Court set aside the concurrent findings of fact of the courts below — without formulating any substantial question of law, the step the amended s. 100(4) makes mandatory (the DU PYQ book names the party as Koppisetty Venkat Ratnam v. Pamarti Venkayamma and records the point expressly: the appeal lay “without formulating substantial question of law which is mandatory under the amended section 100”).
  • The aggrieved party filed a Special Leave Petition in the Supreme Court complaining precisely of this: that the High Court had no jurisdiction to disturb concurrent findings of fact under s. 100 and none to hear the second appeal at all without first formulating the substantial question of law.

IIssues

  1. Whether the High Court could, under the amended s. 100, set aside concurrent findings of fact of the courts below without formulating a substantial question of law.
  2. What is the rationale of confining the second appeal to substantial questions of law — and what discipline does that rationale impose on the High Courts at the admission and hearing stages?

AThe competing positions

The appellant (before the SC)
  • The amended s. 100 (post-1976, on the Law Commission’s 54th Report) permits only questions of law; concurrent findings of fact are beyond reach absent a formulated SQL.
  • Formulation is mandatory — a condition precedent to hearing; a second appeal heard without it is a jurisdictional illegality, not a curable irregularity.
  • The High Court’s conduct — re-appreciating evidence in the garb of a second appeal — makes the second appeal the “third trial on facts” the amendment abolished.
The position the judgment rejects
  • That s. 100 still leaves the High Court a general power to see that “substantial justice” is done on the facts whenever the findings appear to it to be wrong.
  • That formulating the question is a technicality which can be supplied later or read into the judgment.
  • That every perceived perversity in a finding of fact is, by that very fact, a question of law.

DDecision

  • The Supreme Court allowed the challenge: the High Court was wrong to set aside the concurrent findings under s. 100 without formulating a substantial question of law — the very exercise was without jurisdiction.
  • The rationale, in the Court’s own architecture (as extracted in the DU material): (1) only two fact-hearings — the trial and the first appeal; the doctrine of finality serves certainty and combats delay; (2) the first appeal exists for the litigant’s satisfaction; the second appeal is in the nature of a “luxury” — conferred not for the litigant but for the law; (3) a law-only second appeal preserves uniformity of law throughout the State — the High Court’s rulings bind all subordinate courts, tribunals and authorities within its jurisdiction, so that judge-made law is laid down “by one court” — this is implicit in any system where higher courts make binding decisions on questions of law; (4) High Court arrears are fed by laxly-admitted second appeals.
  • The discipline restated: the High Court may admit a second appeal only where a SQL is involved; the memorandum must precisely state it; the Court must formulate it before hearing; the appeal is heard only on it. (The DU PYQ answer adds the corroborating line of authority: Dnyanoba v. Maroti, (1999) 2 SCC 471 — SQLs to be framed at admission; Roop Singh v. Ram Singh, (2000) 3 SCC 708, para 7 — no jurisdiction over “pure questions of fact” under s. 100.)
  • The recurring mischief named: “a matter of common experience that despite clear enunciation of law in a catena of cases, a large number of cases come up where High Courts under s. 100 disturb the concurrent findings of fact without formulating the substantial question of law” — compelling the Supreme Court to set such judgments aside and remit for decision de novo after formulation; several years are lost; litigation becomes “extremely expensive and time consuming” — “one of the main reasons of delay in the administration of justice in civil matters”.
  • The case was accordingly remitted to the High Court for early disposal on the correct lines.

PPrinciple & exam use

The Koppi Setty discipline: s. 100 post-1976 is a drastically curtailed jurisdiction; formulation of the SQL at admission is mandatory and jurisdictional; the second appeal exists for State-wide uniformity of law, not for a dissatisfied litigant’s third shot at the facts; disturbing concurrent findings without a formulated SQL will be set aside by the Supreme Court and remitted — at the litigants’ and the system’s expense. Phrases to quote: “third trial on facts” · “one more dice in the gamble” · second appeal as “luxury” · first appeal as the litigant’s satisfaction.
How to deploy it: whenever a problem gives you a High Court reversing two courts below “in second appeal”, your answer has four beats — (1) s. 100 as amended; (2) concurrent findings immune without SQL; (3) formulation mandatory (Dnyanoba, Singaram); (4) consequence: SC sets aside and remits (Koppi Setty). This is precisely the skeleton the DU examiners rewarded in Q.3/2019 (see §8).

Gill & Co. (P) Ltd. & Anr. v. Bimla Kumari Jolly
1986 RLR 370 · Delhi High Court · J.D. Jain, J. · Second appeal under s. 39(2), Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958 (mirroring s. 100 CPC) · DU Case Material (LB-302), Topic 6, Case 8, paras 1–24 · The anchor case for DU PYQ Q.4(b)/2018 — additional evidence under O.XLI r. 27

FFacts

  • Premises — a portion of property No. A-41, Kirti Nagar, New Delhi — were let to appellant No. 1, M/s Gill & Co. (P) Ltd. (head office Bombay), at Rs. 750 per month in 1966. Appellant No. 2, Sohan Lal Ahuja, the company’s Manager at Delhi, was put into occupation for residence in his capacity as Manager — from the very inception of the tenancy.
  • Ahuja’s service was terminated on 31 March 1972, but he continued in occupation. The appellants’ case: he then acted as the company’s local representative at Delhi, negotiating business deals; his occupation was permissive; legal possession remained with the company throughout.
  • On 12 February 1975 the landlady, Bimla Kumari Jolly, sought eviction on four grounds: (a) non-payment of rent; (b) mis-user; (c) bona fide requirement (cl.(e)); (d) subletting, assignment or parting with possession in favour of Ahuja (cl.(b), proviso to s. 14(1), Delhi Rent Control Act).

IIssues

  1. Whether the Tribunal was right in refusing additional evidence under O.XLI r. 27 read with s. 151 CPC (the application of 24 March 1981).
  2. Whether the notice to produce documents (O.XII r. 8 CPC / s. 66, Evidence Act) was duly served on the company, so as to justify the adverse inference drawn below for withholding documents.
  3. On merits: whether the company had sublet, assigned or otherwise parted with possession of the premises in favour of Ahuja within cl.(b) of the proviso to s. 14(1) of the Delhi Rent Control Act — and what an appellate court under s. 39(2) may do with concurrent findings on that question.

AArguments of the parties

Appellants (Gill & Co. and Ahuja)
  • On r. 27: the documents could not be produced earlier because the IT Department seized the records (August 1976); copies of the 1972–74 account statements were found only later “lying in some very old papers”; they “woke up” to the need for them only when the ARC drew an adverse inference from the alleged O.XII r. 8 notice — a substantial cause under r. 27(1)(b) (K. Venkataramiah: removing obscurity for a more satisfactory judgment).
  • On service: the notice copy (X1), postal receipt (X2) and A.D. (X3) were produced only during Ahuja’s cross-examination, never duly proved, and no opportunity to rebut was given — so the adverse inference for “withholding documents” was illegal.
  • On merits: Ahuja remained a licensee — local representative earning brokerage and commission; account No. 3184, though in his name, was operated solely for the company, all money coming from Bombay; possession therefore never left the company.
Respondent (landlady Bimla Kumari Jolly)
  • On r. 27: reliance was never placed on these documents below; no effort was made to produce them or to summon them from the IT Department despite opportunities; the fault was the appellants’ own — the application was dilatory, to fill up gaps deliberately left at trial.
  • On service: registered A.D. letter to the correct Bombay address, duly delivered — presumption of service arises and mere denial does not rebut it.
  • On merits (fallback): even if the notice was not served, the best-evidence rule applies — a party withholding material documents in his power invites an adverse inference regardless of onus (Gopal Krishnaji Ketkar v. Mohamed Haji Latif, SC).
  • Ahuja ran his own brokerage (M/s Eskay Cotton Links), was remunerated independently, and the Ex. R13 letter shows that business was addressed at the suit premises; his own letter (Ex. R7) sought transfer of rent receipts to his name — possession had passed.

DDecision — J.D. Jain J., on the three issues in sequence

Issue 1 — r. 27 application rightly refused

  • Gateway (i) (refusal below): not available — the evidence was never tendered below, so there was no improper refusal.
  • Gateway (ii) (knowledge / due diligence): not available — the appellants well knew the IT Department had seized the records and could have requisitioned them or summoned the officials; no such effort was made; and, tellingly, the documents now sought were not the seized ones at all — they came from the appellants’ own possession (the seized records had not been released).
  • Gateway (iii) (court’s requirement / substantial cause): Parsotim Thakur v. Lal Mohar Thakur, AIR 1931 PC 143 — the rule is “clearly not intended to allow a litigant who has been unsuccessful in the lower Court to patch up the weak parts of his case and fill up omissions in the Court of appeal”; it is only where the Appellate Court “requires” it — “it must be the Court that requires it” — and the legitimate occasion is “when on examining the evidence as it stands, some inherent lacuna or defect becomes apparent”. K. Venkataramiah (SC) and Municipal Corpn. of Greater Bombay v. Lala Pancham (5-Judge SC) hold to the same effect — the court may require evidence to enable it to pronounce judgment, or to fill an obscurity for a more satisfactory judgment, but the requirement is the court’s, not the party’s.
  • Content test: apart from the pass books, account statements and a couple of letters, the documents were 1976–78 correspondence between the appellants inter se — i.e., dealings after the eviction petition was filed — “a lurking danger of self-serving evidence being created by the parties in order to bolster up their case to the prejudice of the opposite party”; the record as it stood was already “quite sufficient for recording a proper and satisfactory judgment”.
  • Held: “absolutely no justification for permitting the additional evidence, admittedly in the possession of the appellants, on the flimsy ground that they did not realise their importance till adverse finding was given by the trial Court” — the Tribunal’s refusal affirmed.

Issue 2 — the deemed-service correction

  • X2 (postal receipt) and X3 (A.D. receipt) proved a registered A.D. letter to the company’s correct Bombay address, delivered to someone for the addressee — raising the presumption of due official acts under s. 114, illustration (f), Evidence Act, and the (mandatory) deemed service under s. 27, General Clauses Act: properly addressed, prepaid, registered post — service deemed effected unless the contrary is proved.
  • The presumption is rebuttable, but mere denial is not enough — the addressee must prove circumstances showing the notice never reached him (Madan Lal Sethi v. Amar Singh Bhalla, 1980 (2) RCJ 543: mere denial by the tenant does not rebut the presumption; evidence of interruption of the usual course of post is needed). Ahuja could not even say whose signatures were on X3.
  • But the critical question — whether the envelope contained the notice (X1) — had “not an iota of evidence”: the notice copy was produced only at Ahuja’s cross-examination; no witness testified that the registered envelope contained the original of which X1 was a copy. Both courts below therefore “slipped into a grave error” in presuming service of the X1 notice itself.
  • Consequence: no adverse inference could be drawn against the appellants for “withholding” documents pursuant to a notice not proved served — cutting the ground from under that part of the findings below.
  • Best-evidence fallback (Gopal Krishnaji Ketkar): a party withholding the best evidence in his possession cannot hide behind the “abstract doctrine of onus of proof” — but whether such a presumption is well warranted on the facts is a separate question (answered on the merits below: the documents already on record sufficed).

Issue 3 — merits: “parted with possession” stands

  • The three concepts distinguished (Hazari Lal & Ram Babu v. Gian Ram, 1972 RCR 74, Delhi DB): sublet — full tenancy incidents between tenant and sub-tenant (transfer of interest, rent, right to possession against the tenant); assigned — tenant divests himself of all tenant rights; otherwise parted with possession — the tenant vests possession in another by divesting himself of not only physical possession but the right to possession. Mere user by others is not parting; a guest, even with exclusive use, is not parting so long as the tenant can claim possession back at will; a licence revocable at the tenant’s “sweet will and pleasure” is not parting.
  • Service licence doctrine: Denning LJ in Torbett v. Faulkner (1952) 2 TLR, approved by the Supreme Court in B.M. Lall v. Dunlop Rubber Co. (India) Ltd. — a servant in occupation may be tenant or licensee; exclusive possession is not conclusive; “the distinction depends on the truth of the relationship and not on the label which the parties choose to put upon it”. A service licence is personal and dies with the service — and that is why the company’s own plea (Ahuja stayed as ex-employee representative) was double-edged: a licence tied to employment ended in March 1972; the continued occupation then needed explanation.
  • Onus: a person let into exclusive possession is prima facie a tenant / transfer is prima facie made out; the onus then shifts to the tenant, in whose special knowledge the explanatory facts lie (Kishan Chand v. Kundal Lal, per I.D. Dua J. — subletting is proved “by necessary inference”, on probability, the landlord being a stranger to the tenant-subtenant arrangement; also Abdul Aziz, Abu, Dharam Chand — burden is not static).
  • The appellants’ proof failed: (i) no material on the terms of the post-March-1972 arrangement — not even one director or senior official of the Bombay company was examined; (ii) the remittances to account No. 3184 matched the brokerage/commission admittedly paid to Ahuja for services — probative of a business relationship, not of retained possession; (iii) Ahuja admittedly ran his own brokerage, M/s Eskay Cotton Links, and denied conducting it at the premises — but the letter Ex. R13 (25 January 1975) was addressed to Eskay Cotton Links at A-41, Kirti Nagar: unrebutted except by bare denial; (iv) the company’s telegram (6-1-1975) and letter (8-1-1975) promised to vacate by 31-1-1975, then revoked on 22/23-1-1975 amid “serious differences”; and (v) Ahuja’s own letter of 2-1-1975 to the landlady’s husband (Ex. R7): “I can have the house only if you transfer the rent receipts in my name.” The initial presumption of parting with possession therefore stood “absolutely unrebutted”.
  • The second-appeal interdict: s. 39(2) DRC Act (the rent-control twin of s. 100 CPC) confines the High Court to substantial questions of law — no re-appreciation of evidence, no reversal of findings of fact unless arrived at by error of law, without evidence, or perversely (Vinod Kumar v. Ajit Singh Ahluwalia, 1969 (1) RCR 181). The power of attorney of 11-1-1979 (Ex. PW4/1) was of “hardly any consequence” — a much later document.
  • Held: “this appeal is devoid of any merit. It is accordingly dismissed with costs.”

PPrinciple & exam use

Gill & Co. is three doctrines in one judgment: (1) r. 27 discipline — knowledge of a seizure is not due-diligence shown unless the party actually tried to get the documents; documents from one’s own possession cannot enter through the “seizure” story; the court’s requirement is the court’s alone (Parsotim Thakur); late-realised importance is a flimsy ground; inter-party correspondence created during litigation is suspected as self-serving. (2) Proof of service — envelope proved ≠ contents proved: s. 27 General Clauses Act deems service of the letter, but the party relying on the notice must connect the notice to the envelope; adverse inferences for withholding follow only from a proved notice. (3) Parting with possession — the Hazari Lal three-concept distinction; the truth-of-the-relationship test (label is nothing); exclusive possession raises a presumption which the tenant, holding the facts in special knowledge, must discharge — and appellate courts will not re-try these facts in second appeal.
Why prescribed for Topic 6: the whole appeal is a working demonstration of the syllabus — an appellate court’s treatment of an O.XLI r. 27 application (refused, correctly), the limits of appellate interference with concurrent findings, and the r. 27 gateways applied one by one to a live set of documents. Q. 3 / 2019CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
What are the tests to determine a substantial question of law for the purpose of second appeal? Discuss with the help of case laws.
Model answer

Second Appeal — s. 100. The law relating to appeals from appellate decrees (second appeals) is contained in ss. 100 to 103, 107, 108 and Order XLII CPC: s. 100 defines the second appeal; ss. 100A, 101, 102 deal with its limitations; s. 103 with determination of issues of fact; ss. 107–108 and O.XLII with the powers of the appellate court and the procedure in such appeals.

Statutory provision — s. 100 (as amended by the Amendment Act, 1976, on the Law Commission’s 54th Report)
(1) An appeal lies to the High Court from every decree passed in appeal by a court subordinate to it, if the High Court is satisfied that the case involves a substantial question of law. (2) The appeal may lie from an appellate decree passed ex parte. (3) The memorandum shall precisely state the substantial question of law. (4) The High Court shall formulate that question where satisfied it is involved. (5) The appeal is heard on the question so formulated, the respondent being free to argue that the case involves no such question — proviso: the Court may, for reasons recorded, hear the appeal on any other substantial question of law not formulated, if satisfied the case involves it.

The amendment drastically curtailed the second appeal, with the avowed object that it may not become a “third trial on facts” or “one more dice in the gamble”. Consequences: (1) HC’s satisfaction that an SQL is involved; (2) precise statement in the memorandum; (3) formulation by the HC at admission; (4) hearing confined to that question; (5) respondent may contest involvement; (6) other SQLs only for recorded reasons.

The concept — “substantial question of law”. The phrase is undefined. “Substantial” means of substance — real, of sound worth, important. The Legislature deliberately did not add “of general importance” (contrast s. 109 CPC and Art. 133(1)(a) of the Constitution).

  • The governing test — Chunilal V. Mehta & Sons v. Century Spg. & Mfg. Co., AIR 1962 SC 1314: the construction of a document of title which is the foundation of the rights of the parties necessarily raises a question of law; the proper test — the question is substantial if it is of general public importance, or directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties, and is either an open question (not finally settled by the SC/PC/FC) or not free from difficulty or calls for discussion of alternative views. It is not substantial if the question is settled by the highest court, or well-settled principles merely have to be applied (misapplication is not enough), or the plea is palpably absurd.
  • As between the parties: Raghunath Prasad v. Commr. of Pratapgarh, AIR 1927 PC 110 — a question of general importance is neither necessary nor sufficient; the question must be substantial as between the parties. A question is substantial as between the parties if the decision turns one way or the other on the particular view taken of the law (Dinkarrao v. Rattansey, AIR 1949 Nag 300, Bose CJ — see Q.4(a)/2018 below for the rider). Where the question is fairly arguable or admits of a difference of opinion, it is substantial (R. Subba Rao, AIR 1951 Mad 969, FB, approved in Chunilal).
  • Koppi Setty v. Ratnam / Koppisetty Venkat Ratnam v. Pamarti Venkayamma (2007 RLR 27 (NSC); DU syllabus prints 2009 RLR 27): SLP against a High Court setting aside concurrent findings of fact under s. 100 without formulating any substantial question of law — mandatory under the amended section. The SC explained the rationale: there are only two fact-hearings (trial + first appeal); finality means certainty and anti-delay; the first appeal exists for the litigant’s satisfaction, the second appeal is a “luxury” conferred to maintain uniformity of law throughout the State — the High Court’s rulings bind all courts, tribunals and authorities within its jurisdiction, one court laying down judge-made law for all. HC arrears are fed by laxly-admitted second appeals. Corroborating line: Dnyanoba v. Maroti, (1999) 2 SCC 471 — SQLs must be framed at admission and the appeal decided only on them; Roop Singh v. Ram Singh, (2000) 3 SCC 708 (para 7) — s. 100 confers no jurisdiction to interfere with pure questions of fact. The Court noted with regret that HCs keep disturbing concurrent findings without framing SQLs, compelling the SC to set aside and remit — years lost, expense multiplied — a main cause of delay in civil justice.
  • Recent affirmation: Singaram v. Ramanathan (2021) — framing the SQL is not a mere formality but is meant to be adhered to.

Conclusion — the tests in one frame: (a) is it a question of law at all (construction of documents, legal effect of admitted facts, finding without evidence, onus, admissibility)? (b) is it substantial — general importance, or direct and substantial effect on the parties’ rights, and open / difficult / arguable? (c) does the case involve it — necessity, not mere possibility? (d) has it been formulated by the High Court? Only when all four answers are “yes” does the second-appeal gate open.

Q. 4 (a) / 2018CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
In the case of Dillkarrao [Dinkarrao] v. Rattansey AIR 1949 Nag 300, the High Court held that “A question of law is substantial as between the parties if the decision turns one way or another on the particular view taken of the law”. Do you agree with the view? Explain with case law.
Model answer

Short answer: agree in substance, but not as a standalone definition — the Supreme Court in Chunilal V. Mehta found the Nagpur formulation, standing alone, too wide (“so defined, it would cover every question of law”), and absorbed the decision-turns idea into a larger composite test.

  • Context: a second appeal lies only on a substantial question of law (s. 100, post-1976; before the amendment, on the three old grounds). Whether a question is “substantial” is the gatekeeper question.
  • The Nagpur view (Bose CJ): substantiality is relational — a question is substantial as between the parties when the decision turns on the view of law taken. This correctly rejects the notion that only questions of general public importance are substantial — in line with the Privy Council in Raghunath Prasad, AIR 1927 PC 110, and echoed by the Law Commission’s 54th Report: limiting the second appeal to questions of general importance would wrongly disable the High Court from correcting subordinate courts that violate its own settled law.
  • The correction — Chunilal V. Mehta, AIR 1962 SC 1314 (para 6): the proper test is whether the question is of general public importance or directly and substantially affects the rights of the parties — and is open, or not free from difficulty, or calls for discussion of alternative views. If settled by the highest court, or only well-settled principles need application, or the plea is palpably absurd — not substantial. So “decision-turns” identifies which questions matter to the parties, but cannot by itself certify substantiality — otherwise every question of law would qualify.
  • The Madras Full Bench gloss (R. Subba Rao, AIR 1951 Mad 969), approved in Chunilal: a question is substantial where it is fairly arguable, where there is room for a difference of opinion, or an alternative view is equally possible.
  • Conclusion: the Dinkarrao proposition is a necessary condition — if the decision does not turn on the question, it cannot be substantial as between the parties — but it is not a sufficient one. Agree, therefore, with the qualification supplied by Chunilal; and add the discipline of Koppi Setty — the High Court must actually formulate the question before it may act on it.
Q. 4 (b) / 2018CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
The general rule is that an appellate court shall decide an appeal on the evidence led by the parties before the Lower Court and shall not admit additional evidence for the disposal of an appeal. Does the Code of Civil Procedure allow for an exception to this rule? Explain the relevant Order and Rule with help of case law.
Model answer

Yes — s. 107(1)(d) CPC read with Order XLI, Rules 27–29, is the exception to the general rule of exclusion.

The general rule and its statutory exceptions — O.XLI r. 27
r. 27(1) opens with the rule: “The parties to an appeal shall not be entitled to produce additional evidence, whether oral or documentary, in the Appellate Court.” But the Appellate Court may allow it where: (1) the lower court improperly refused to admit evidence which ought to have been admitted; (2) the additional evidence was not within the party’s knowledge or could not, after due diligence, be produced at the time the decree was passed (cl. (aa), inserted 1976); or (3) the Appellate Court itself requires the document or witness (a) to enable it to pronounce judgment, or (b) for any other substantial cause. r. 27(2): reasons for admission must be recorded; r. 28 (mode): taken by the appellate court itself or sent down to a subordinate court; r. 29: the points to which the evidence is confined must be specified and recorded.
  • Meaning: “additional evidence” does not mean evidence over and above what the party led below — a party who led no evidence below can also apply (Jaipur Development Authority v. Kailashwati Devi, (1997) 7 SCC 297).
  • Object & nature: justice and fairness; the power is discretionary, to be exercised on sound judicial principles — the applicant must show the evidence could not, with best efforts, have been adduced at the first instance; the opposite party must have an opportunity to rebut; the evidence must be relevant to the issue (Shivajirao Nilangekar Patil v. Dr. Mahesh Madhav Gosavi, AIR 1987 SC 294).
  • Leading case — Gill & Co. v. Bimla Kumari Jolly, 1986 RLR 370 (Del HC, J.D. Jain J.): during the first appeal the tenant company applied under O.XLI r. 27 to produce pass books, account statements and correspondence, pleading that its records had been seized by the Income-Tax Department. Held — refusal correct: (i) the evidence was never tendered below, so there was no improper refusal; (ii) the seizure was within the appellants’ knowledge — they could have requisitioned or summoned the records but made no attempt; the documents in fact came from their own possession; (iii) the requirement must be the court’s: the rule “is clearly not intended to allow a litigant unsuccessful below to patch up the weak parts of his case and fill up omissions” (Parsotim Thakur v. Lal Mohar Thakur, AIR 1931 PC 143); and most of the documents were 1976–78 correspondence between the appellants themselves — a “lurking danger of self-serving evidence”. Conclusion: “no justification for permitting additional evidence admittedly in the possession of the appellants on the flimsy ground that they did not realise their importance till an adverse finding was given.”
  • Same line of authority: Municipal Corpn. of Greater Bombay v. Lala Pancham, AIR 1965 SC 1008 (5-Judge) — additional evidence only to enable the court to pronounce judgment, not to enable it to pronounce a particular judgment; K. Venkataramiah v. A. Seetharama Reddy, AIR 1963 SC 1526 — filling an obscurity for a “more satisfactory judgment” is an “other substantial cause”, but the requirement is the court’s; Shivajirao Patil (India Today cuttings obtainable earlier — refused); refusal below of late-produced evidence will not be disturbed (Pramod Kumari v. Om Prakash, (1980) 1 SCC 412).
  • Conclusion: the Code does allow the exception — but through three narrow gateways and under judicial discretion exercised with recorded reasons; an appellate litigant gets a second look at the record, never a second chance to make his case.
Q. 3 (b) / 2017CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (10 marks)
“The substantial question of law is not necessarily a question which is of public importance. It must be a substantial question of law as between the parties in the case involved.” Discuss the statement.
Model answer (the DU book cross-refers to Q.3/2019 — the standalone frame is below)
  • The statement is correct and traces to the Privy Council: in Raghunath Prasad Singh v. Commr. of Pratapgarh, AIR 1927 PC 110, the Board held that for s. 100 a question need not be of public importance; it must be substantial “as between the parties” — the phrase carries the emphasis: substantiality is measured by the question’s impact on this case, not its headline value.
  • Legislative confirmation: the amended s. 100 (1976, on the 54th Report) dropped the earlier drafts’ flirtation with “general importance” — the Law Commission expressly said: “we are not limiting the scope of second appeal to questions of law of general importance… if the law has been clearly laid down by the High Court and the decision of the subordinate court is in clear violation of it, the power of the High Court to correct it should be left intact.”
  • Judicial confirmation — Chunilal, AIR 1962 SC 1314: the test is disjunctive at the first limb — general public importance OR direct and substantial effect on the parties’ rights — followed by the qualitative limb (open / difficult / alternative views). And the Dinkarrao gloss (Bose CJ): substantial as between the parties if the decision turns on the view of law taken; Madras FB: substantial if fairly arguable.
  • Illustrations: construction of a private managing-agency clause was held a substantial question in Chunilal (₹26-lakh stake between two parties, no public importance) — while questions settled by the highest court, or a mere application of settled principles, are not substantial however loudly they are argued. Conversely, a question may be of public importance and still fail (if already concluded by the SC).
  • Caveat — the other half of the statement is equally true: a question of general importance that does not affect the parties’ rights is not “involved” in the case at all (“involves” implies necessity — SBI v. S.N. Goyal, (2008) 8 SCC 92; Pankaj Bhargava, (1991) 1 SCC 556). And in every case the High Court must formulate the question before hearing (Koppi Setty; Dnyanoba; Singaram, 2021).
  • Conclusion: the statement accurately states Indian law: substantiality for s. 100 is relational, not reputational — measured as between the parties, tested by openness and difficulty, and exercised only through the discipline of formulation.
Q. 6 (a) / 2017CPC & Limitation — IIIrd Term (short note)
Write short notes on: (a) Doctrine of Merger.
Model answer
  • Meaning: the order of an inferior tribunal merges in the order of the superior tribunal passed in appeal or revision. Where an appeal is provided against a decree and the appeal is pressed, it is the decree of the appellate court which is operative in law and enforceable; the trial court’s decree ceases to have existence in the eyes of law and is superseded.
  • Rationale: there cannot be, at one and the same time, more than one operative decree governing the same subject-matter.
  • Not rigid or universal: its application depends on the nature of the appellate or revisional order in each case and the scope of the statutory provisions conferring the appellate or revisional jurisdiction (State of Madras v. Madhuri Mills Co., AIR 1967 SC 681; CIT v. Amritlal Bhogilal & Co., AIR 1958 SC 868 — if an appeal is provided against an order, the appellate authority’s decision is the operative decision in law; if it modifies or reverses, it substitutes; if it dismisses the appeal, the lower order merges in it and survives through it).
  • Consequences: execution proceeds on the appellate decree; grounds decided and grounds available but not taken merge too (subject to the rules of constructive res judicata in appeal); and after merger, attack on the decree must be against the appellate court’s decision, not the merged order.

ss. 96–99 — first appeal

s. 96
Appeal from every decree of a court of original jurisdiction → the authorized appellate court. Exceptions: “save as otherwise provided”; ex parte decree — appeal may lie (s. 96(2)); consent decree — never (s. 96(3)); Small Causes suits ≤ ₹10,000 — only on a question of law (s. 96(4)).
ss. 97–99
s. 97: no challenge to preliminary decree in the final-decree appeal if the first appeal was skipped. s. 98: majority decides; even bench may refer a point of law. s. 99: no reversal for misjoinder/non-joinder or error not affecting merits or jurisdiction (unless necessary party not joined); s. 99A: s. 47 orders protected unless decision prejudicially affected.

ss. 104–106 — orders

The exhaustive list
Only orders named in s. 104 (ff, ffa, g, h, i) or O.XLIII r. 1 are appealable; no appeal from an order passed in appeal under s. 104 (s. 104(2)). Errors in other orders travel as grounds of objection in the decree-appeal (s. 105(1)); remand orders not appealed become final (s. 105(2)); forum per s. 106.
Doctrine of merger
One operative decree at a time — the lower decree merges in the appellate decree; application depends on the nature and scope of the appellate order (Madhuri Mills, AIR 1967 SC 681).

s. 100 — second appeal

The single gateway
HC satisfied the case involves a substantial question of law → memo states it precisely (s. 100(3)) → HC formulates it (s. 100(4)) → heard only on it (s. 100(5)) → respondent may deny involvement → proviso: other SQLs for recorded reasons. s. 100A: no LPA. s. 101: no other grounds. s. 102: bar in money suits ≤ ₹25,000. s. 103: facts may be determined in the stated situations.
Chunilal test (AIR 1962 SC 1314)
Substantial = general public importance OR directly & substantially affects parties’ rights + open / not free from difficulty / calls for alternative-view discussion. Not substantial: settled law; settled principles merely applied; palpably absurd. As between the parties (PC, Raghunath Prasad); decision-turns (Dinkarrao — necessary, not sufficient); fairly arguable (Madras FB).
Koppi Setty discipline
No disturbance of concurrent findings without a formulated SQL — else SC sets aside and remits. Second appeal = uniformity of law State-wide; first appeal = litigant’s satisfaction; second = “luxury”; no “third trial on facts”, no “one more dice in the gamble”.

s. 107 + O.XLI r. 27

Four powers (s. 107(1))
(a) determine finally (r. 24) — draw the final curtain; (b) remand (rr. 23, 23A); (c) frame issues & refer (r. 25); (d) take or require additional evidence (rr. 27–29); plus r. 33 residual power. s. 107(2): appellate court = trial court’s powers and duties.
r. 27 gateways
(a) improperly refused below (must have been tendered!) · (aa) due diligence — not within knowledge / not producible when decree passed (1976) · (b) court requires it to pronounce judgment or any other substantial cause — the court’s requirement, not the party’s (Parsotim Thakur PC: no patching weak parts; Lala Pancham 5J: pronounce judgment, not a particular judgment; K. Venkataramiah: obscurity-removal = substantial cause). Shivajirao Patil trio: best efforts + opportunity to rebut + relevancy. r. 27(2): reasons mandatory; r. 28 mode; r. 29 points.
Gill & Co. one-liners
Seizure known ≠ due diligence shown; own-possession documents ≠ new discovery; inter-party wartime correspondence = self-serving danger; “did not realise importance till adverse finding” = flimsy ground; envelope proved ≠ contents proved (s. 27 GCLA deems service of the letter, not of the notice); parting with possession = divesting physical + legal possession (Hazari Lal trio); truth of relationship beats label (Torbett/B.M. Lall).
