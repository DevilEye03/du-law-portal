SECTION 1

The Concept of a Company Meeting

“Two heads are always better than one.” When two or more persons come together, deliberate upon a common matter and reach a decision, there is a meeting. In company law the institution has a precise constitutional function: the members (real owners) cannot individually bind the company, but assembled in a properly convened and conducted meeting, their collective decision becomes the will of the company itself.

  • General definition: “Any gathering, assembling or coming together of two or more persons for the transaction of some lawful business of common concern is called a meeting” (P.K. Ghosh); a “concurrence or coming together of at least a quorum of members in order to transact either ordinary or special business” of the company.
  • Why meetings are constitutionally vital: a company is an abstraction that can think only through humans. Day-to-day power sits with the Board; but the ultimate control and destiny of the company lies with shareholders, and the general meeting is the only regular forum where shareholders can convene to review the directors' work, re-elect or refuse to re-elect them, approve accounts, and declare dividends. It is the chief institution of shareholder protection — and the counterweight to the management's information advantage (cf. Percival v. Wright's “knowledge of directors' powers” principle in Topic 5).
  • The two families of meetings in a company's life: (i) Meetings of members (general meetings) — AGM, EGM, class meetings, Tribunal-ordered meetings — the focus of this topic, governed by Ss. 96–122; (ii) Meetings of the Board & its committees — governed by Ss. 173–174 (brief comparative table in §2, since “company meetings” in the syllabus text embraces them) — plus (iii) meetings of creditors/members in compromises (S.230) and winding-up contexts.
One meeting ≠ several people in a room. A “meeting” in law requires: (a) more than one person assembling (Sharp v. Dawes, Case 3); (b) lawful convening (proper authority + proper notice); (c) deliberation on the business notified; and (d) a decision by the requisite majority. Meetings of minds through connected rooms need a working audio-visual link (Byng v. London Life Assn.). Absent any of these, what happened is — in the phrase the courts use — a nullity or an incapable-of-adjournment gathering (Harben v. Phillips).
SECTION 2

Kinds of Meetings — The Complete Map

Company meetings fall into two broad families: meetings of members (which control constitution and capital) and meetings of directors (which control management). Within the members' family, the 2013 Act recognises three principal general meetings — the AGM, the EGM, and the class meeting — supplemented by Tribunal-convened meetings and statutory context meetings. Under the 2013 Act there is deliberately no separate “statutory meeting”: its work (freshers'-day formalities on incorporation) has been absorbed into the first AGM&Board-regime, and a company limited by shares is now registered with subscribers deemed members with immediate rights (S.9/S.36 read with the incorporation scheme).

Fig. 1 — The complete map of company meetings under the Companies Act, 2013 (Articles of Table F, cl. 42: “All general meetings other than annual general meetings shall be called extraordinary general meetings”).

2.1 The Members' Family — Explained

Annual General Meeting (S.96)
The pivotal yearly meeting — called expressly as “AGM” in the notice. Transacts the ordinary business statutorily reserved to it (accounts, dividend, directors' rotation, auditors) plus any special business. Detailed in §3 below.
Extraordinary General Meeting (S.100)
Any general meeting — other than the AGM — called for:
  • any urgent or special business which cannot be postponed till the next AGM; or
  • any business proposed by directors, or by requisitionists (S.100).

Formally: “any general meeting other than an AGM” is an EGM — including an adjourned AGM. Detailed in §4 below.

Class Meeting (S.48 / S.106 / S.116)
A meeting of a particular class of shareholders (equity or preference), held whenever the rights of that class are to be varied. Exception, one person could validly hold an entire class meeting (East v. Bennett Bros., Case §6).
Tribunal-ordered Meeting (S.98)
Where it is “impracticable” to call or conduct a meeting in the ordinary way, the Tribunal (NCLT) may, suomotu or on application of a director/member entitled to vote, order a meeting to be called/held/conducted as it thinks fit — even directing that one member present in person or by proxy shall be a meeting.
OPC relief (S.122)
Ss. 98 and 100–111 do not apply to a one-person company: its “resolutions” are simply communicated and entered in the minutes book, with the date of entry deemed the date of the meeting.
Emerging fourth pillar (2013 Act)
Decisions without an assembled meeting — postal ballot (S.110), e-voting (S.108) and written-in-minutes OPC decisions (S.122) — now carry substantial business that once required physical assembly (see §8–9).

SECTION 3

Annual General Meeting — Section 96 [the classic short-note]

The AGM is the pivotal institution of corporate life. Directors may lawfully possess huge discretionary power between meetings, but once in every year the company must submit itself, its accounts and its stewardship to the members. Parliament has therefore made the AGM compulsory for every company — public or private, and there is no provision even for a Government company to be exempt from holding it; exemption under S.96(2) third proviso lets Government companies only choose a different place.

3.1 When must it be held? — the three clocks

ClockRule — S.96(1)Proviso / consequence
First AGMWithin 9 months of the close of the first financial year of the company.No AGM at all is needed in the year of incorporation / the next calendar year (provided first AGM held in time).
Annual recurrenceThereafter, one AGM in each calendar year.Not in the year of incorporation itself.
Outer limitWithin 6 months of close of the relevant financial year, and the gap between two AGMs ≤ 15 months.RoC may, for special reason, extend the time by up to 3 months (2nd proviso) — this extension cannot be used for the first AGM.
Fig. 2 — The AGM clocks under S.96: the 9 / 6 / 15 month discipline with the RoC's limited extension and the Tribunal's default powers (Ss. 97–99).

3.2 Where and when within the day? — S.96(2)

  • Called during business hours — between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m.
  • Held on a day that is not a national holiday.
  • At the registered office of the company or at some other place within the city/town/village in which the registered office is situated.
  • First proviso: the AGM of an unlisted company may be held at any place in India, if consent (writing/electronic) is given by all members in advance.
  • Third proviso: the AGM of a Government company / wholly-owned subsidiary of a company incorporated outside India — at such other place as the Central Government may approve.

3.3 Tribunal's default powers — Ss. 97 & 98 (the safety valves)

  • S.97 — Power of Tribunal to call AGM. If default is made in holding the AGM, the Tribunal may, on the application of any member (note: company itself cannot apply — Cannanore Whole Body CT Scan), call or direct the calling of an AGM and give consequential directions, including that one member present in person or proxy shall be the AGM; a meeting so held is deemed a meeting called under S.96.
  • S.98 — “Impracticable” meetings. If for any reason it is impracticable to call a meeting other than an AGM — or to hold or conduct it — the Tribunal may, suo motu or on application of any director/member entitled to vote, order a meeting and issue ancillary directions, including the one-person quorum direction (El Sombrero, L. Opera Photographic: where a 49% minority boycotts meetings to defeat the statutory right of a 51% majority to remove directors, the Tribunal will convene a one-man meeting — the right is statutory and cannot be vetoed by quorum tactics). The S.98 power is exclusive to the Tribunal: courts have no inherent jurisdiction to convene company meetings. “Impracticable” = impracticable from a reasonable, prudent, common-sense businessman point of view (Indian Spg. Mills v. Lt.-Gen. Madan; Malhati Tea) — recipe: try S.100 self-help first (B. Mohandas).
  • S.99 — sanction. Default in complying with Ss. 96–98: the company and every officer in default — fine up to ₹1,00,000, plus ₹5,000/day continuing default. Non-holding of the AGM is not a ground for winding up; defences: medical emergency of the officer in default dirige (Kastoor Mal Banthiya), but not “books withheld by court” or successive adjournments (Brahmanbaria Loan; Bejoy Kumar Karnani).

3.4 What happens at the AGM — ordinary business (S.102(2))

  1. Consideration of the financial statements + Board's and auditors' reports;
  2. Declaration of dividend;
  3. Appointment of directors in place of those retiring by rotation;
  4. Appointment of / fixing remuneration of auditors.
Function, not just form: The AGM is where the general body “controls the destiny of the business” — Lord Sumner's phrase in Shaw & Sons v. Shaw — rotating directors, approving accounts, the auditors. And crucially: failure to lay accounts does NOT invalidate the meeting (Sunil Dev v. DDCA) — the AGM remains valid; the default attracts S.129/S.137 consequences instead. Nor does non-payment of dividend validly declared get vitiated by later events at the meeting.

3.5 Must the calendar-year meeting literally be held in the year? — the Meenakshi Mills rule

Section 96 says “once in every year” in addition to the 15-month gap rule. So a company that held its December 1934 meeting adjourned to March 1935 had not thereby held a 1935 meeting: there must be one meeting per year, and as many meetings as there are years — Sree Meenakshi Mills v. RoC (Case 1). (For the debate on whether an EGM can count as the year's meeting: Emperor v. Nasurbhai (Bom) yes (criticised); Lachmi Narain v. Emperor (PC confirmation via amended words "in addition to" of the 1913 Act) — modern reading: the AGM alone satisfies S.96.)

SECTION 4

Extraordinary General Meeting & Requisition — Ss. 100, 97–99

Between two AGMs a company cannot be left without a voice. The EGM is the reserve forum: any general meeting that is not an AGM — the annual audit of stewardship having happened, the EGM handles the unexpected and the urgent: removal of a director, a crisis restructuring, shifting the registered office, a takeover defence, a members' show of no-confidence.

4.1 Who can call an EGM?

Forum of callingBasis & discipline
1 · By the Board suo motuS.100(1): the Board may, whenever it deems fit, call an EGM. The Board's power is fiduciary-tinged: it should be exercised for the corporate purpose of informing/consulting the body that owns the company.
2 · By the Board on requisitionS.100(2): the Board “shall” call an EGM on requisition by holders of ≥ 1/10 of paid-up voting capital (companies with share capital) or ≥ 1/10 of total voting power (no share capital). The requisition must set out the matters for which the meeting is called, be signed, and deposited at the registered office.
3 · By the requisitionists themselvesS.100(4): Board must move within 21 days and convene within 45 days of deposit. Failing that, requisitionists (or a majority of them by value ≥ 1/10 threshold) may themselves call and hold the meeting within 3 months — reasonable expenses repaid by the company, recoverable from defaulting directors' fees/remuneration.
4 · By the TribunalS.98 — where calling/holding/conducting is impracticable (see §3.3): ordered meeting + any directions, including one-member quorum.
Fig. 3 — The S.100 requisition clock: 21 days to move, 45 days to hold, 3-month self-help window, expenses recoupment.

4.2 Doctrine notes (the exam's favourite traps)

  • Business scope is frozen by the requisition: no item beyond the matters set out may be transacted — where a removal item was added later, the meeting was restrained from taking it up (Ball v. Metal Industries).
  • Reasons not required: requisitionists proposing removal of directors need state no reasons — the power to requisition and to vote are incidents of membership, not accountable to a “reasonableness” test (LIC v. Escorts Ltd., SC 1986). But the special-notice discipline of Ss. 115+169 applies: absent special notice, the meeting may be valid yet the removal resolution invalid (Queens Kuries & Loans).
  • No offence in refusal, but consequences elsewhere: directors' refusal to call on requisition is not itself a criminal default (Anantha R. Hegde) — the statute's answer is requisitionist self-help plus expense recoupment; mala fide refusal can still feed S.97/S.98 Tribunal relief and oppression remedies.
  • Venue flexibility: unlike the AGM, an EGM need not be at the registered office or the same city/town — S.96(2)'s venue fetters do not reach S.100.
One-line for the short-note slot: “Every general meeting other than an AGM is an EGM (Table F, cl. 42). The Board may call it; ≥ 1/10 voting holders may requisition it; the Board must move in 21 days and convene in 45; else the requisitionists may themselves hold it within 3 months at the defaulting directors' eventual cost; the Tribunal (S.98) remains the residual convenor where the ordinary machinery is impracticable.”
SECTION 5

Notice of Meeting — Ss. 101–102 [the validity hinge]

Notice is the jurisdictional foundation of the meeting: it tells every member when, where, and — most importantly for shareholder protection — what business will be transacted, so that a member can intelligently decide whether to attend, write a proxy, or accept the outcome. A meeting convened on defective notice is, as a rule, no meeting at all in the eyes of the law for the business affected (cf. Harben v. Phillips: such a meeting cannot even lawfully adjourn itself).

5.1 Length — 21 clear days, S.101(1)

Rule
A general meeting may be called by giving not less than 21 clear days' notice, in writing or electronic mode. “Clear days” means excluding both the day of service (deemed 48 hours after posting) and the day of the meeting.
Shorter notice possible
Consent of ≥ 95% of members entitled to vote (AGM: 95% of members entitled to vote; other meetings: majority in number holding ≥95% of paid-up capital / voting power). Consent may precede or follow the notice (Self-Help Industrial Estate); omission of a member's consent defeats the short-notice tooth for that member's challenge.
Fig. 4 — Clear-days arithmetic. Certificate of posting is not conclusive (Hardeep Kaur); a postal strike defeats the deemed-service presumption (Bradman v. Trinity Estate); fax/e-mail service is valid (PNC Telecom).

5.2 Contents of the notice

  • Place, day and hour of the meeting — the “when and where” (S.101(1); change of venue/day after issue effectively requires fresh notice + 3-day newspaper advertisement discipline for adjourned meetings, S.103(3)).
  • Statement of business: ordinary business (at the AGM: accounts & reports, dividend, rotation of directors, auditors) vs special business — everything else at an AGM, and all business at an EGM (S.102(1)-(2)).
  • Omitted special business = void business: where the notice of an AGM said only “routine business” and capital was issued at it, the meeting and the issue were invalid (Martin Castelino v. Alpha Omega Shipman).

5.3 Explanatory statement — S.102(1): the anti-ambush weapon

For every item of special business an explanatory statement must be annexed, setting out: (i) all material facts concerning each item, including the nature of the concern or interest — financial or otherwise — of every director, manager, KMP and their relatives; (ii) the extent of shareholding interest ≥ 2% in any other company affected; (iii) time/place where documents can be inspected. SC restraint orders, subsisting arrangements, are “material facts”.

  • Sanction for concealment: benefit accruing to a promoter/director/KMP or relative by reason of non-disclosure is held in trust for the company + S.102(5) penalty ≤ ₹50,000 or 5× the benefit, whichever more.
  • Kaye v. Croydon Tramways (Case 2): a notice selling the undertaking while hiding the directors' compensation was not fair disclosure — the fullest candour is demanded.
  • Narayanlal Bansilal v. Maneckji Petit: “new articles will be adopted” without particulars of important changes = invalid resolutions.
  • Bimal Singh Kothari v. Muir Mills: printed copies must be sent; making them available at the office is deficient where members are scattered.
  • Cure by knowledge: a member aware of the real effect cannot complain of technical defect (Parsuram v. Tata Industrial Bank, PC); likewise consent/acquiescence or accepting benefits under the resolution cures directory defects (Bailey Hay; Shailesh Harilal Shah).
Exam architecture: A “notice” answer = length → mode → content → explanatory statement → consequences of defect, in that order. Examiners reward the clear-days arithmetic and the concealment-trust sanction with authority (Kaye, Narayanlal).
SECTION 6

Quorum — Section 103

Quorum = the minimum number of members whose personal presence is necessary to constitute a valid meeting. It is the law's answer to a simple risk: without a floor, a handful of insiders could meet at midnight and bind thousands. “Quorum” must be present before business validly begins; and (on the stricter English view, London Flats) for textured answers, through-out the meeting.

6.1 The statutory numbers — S.103(1)

CompanyQuorum for general meeting (unless articles require larger)
Public company5 members personally present if ≤1,000 members · 15 if 1,001–5,000 · 30 if >5,000
Private company2 members personally present
“Personally present”: a body's representative under S.113 counts; trustees of corporations can count personally; but proxies do not count toward quorum (they are not “personally present”); joint shareholders count as one; preference members count only when the resolution affects them (restricted voting under S.47).

6.2 The 30-minute rule & consequences — S.103(2)-(3)

Fig. 5 — The quorum waterfall: requisitioned meetings die; others sleep for a week and then wake with whoever turns up.

6.3 Doctrine — counting and collapse

  • One person is not a “meeting”: Sharp v. Dawes (Case 3) — Lord Coleridge CJ: “the word 'meeting' prima facie means a coming together of more than one person”. Exception — a class meeting may consist of the sole holder of the class (East v. Bennett Bros.); and Tribunal under S.97/98 can direct one-member quorum.
  • Quorum at the start — two views: Hartley Baird (quorum present when meeting opened suffices; later departure does not vitiate) vs London Flats (proceedings after the second member walked out were a nullity). Present both; most examiners reward noting that the safer management practice is to ensure quorum persists, and articles are construed contextually.
  • Interested member may count toward quorum (subject to articles); a member who stays away cannot complain that what passed is void after acquiescing and taking benefits.
SECTION 7

Chairman & Adjournment — S.104

“That person who presides over the conduct of the meeting at the time when the meeting is deliberating” — the Chairman is the meeting's umpire, clock-keeper and guardian of order. He decides on the spot emergent questions of procedure; the law clothes his good-faith rulings with prima facie finality (Mahaliram, Case 5); but he is no dictator: he cannot stop a lawful meeting at will.

7.1 Appointment — S.104(1)

  • Articles usually name who chairs (usually the Board chairman); otherwise members personally present elect one of themselves as chairman, on a show of hands, and if a poll is demanded on the election it is taken forthwith.
  • Where the appointed chairman is absent or unwilling within the time, members present elect one of themselves.

7.2 Powers

Guaranteed powers
Conduct & order of debate; closure; putting resolutions to vote; demand/take poll (S.109); decide points of order; casting vote (whoever occupies the chair, when articles/Act allow — S.104 scenario per Table F); sign minutes later.
Limits (doctrine)
Cannot himself postpone a properly convened meeting; may adjourn for disorder only as long as necessary and must tell the meeting; cannot dissolve to frustrate business — if he walks out on losing a vote, remaining quorum may elect another chairman and validly continue (Narayana Chettiar v. Kaleeswara Mills, Case 6).

7.3 Adjournment doctrine

  • By the meeting, not the chairman's whim: adjournment is primarily the meeting's decision. Chairman's residuary power survives (Byng v. London Life) but must pass a judicial-review style reasonableness test — used bona fide, not to gag business.
  • Byng v. London Life Assn. (CA): meeting in too-small a venue, audio link failed; chairman purported to move time/place without taking the sense of the meeting → continuation "meeting" at a new venue invalid.
  • Harben v. Phillips: a meeting void ab initio (notice omitted to members) cannot adjourn itself — “a meeting which is not a meeting can do nothing”.
  • Effect: the adjourned meeting is a continuation of the original; only business on the original agenda may be transacted (subject to articles); resolutions date on actual passing (S.116); poll demand lapses; proxies already lodged remain good unless revoked.
  • Protection & liability of the chair: Ram Narain v. Ram Kishen — chairman is neither wholly ministerial nor judicial; bona fide error in excluding a voter is not actionable in damages; but wilful wrong or bad-faith exclusion election-remedies lie.
SECTION 8

Proxy — S.105; Voting Machinery — Ss. 106–113

The modern shareholder is “a kind of investor, not an owner” — scattered, passive, always voting “yes” on the proxy card. The statute therefore builds the full machinery: proxies to carry the absent member's will; show of hands for speed; poll for precision; e-voting & postal ballot for scale; corporate representatives for institutional blocks.

8.1 Proxy — S.105

RulePosition
Right to appointAny member entitled to attend and vote may appoint another person (whether member or not) as proxy. Not available to companies without share capital (unless articles allow).
Proxy's powersVote only on a poll (unless articles extend to show of hands); no right to speak; acts as agent within instructions — excess votes void, rest stand (Bidwell Bros., re principle).
Form & depositWriting, signed by appointer or attorney; deposited 48 hours before the meeting; longer article-period is read down to 48h [S.105(4)]; notice of meeting must state the proxy right; blank proxy forms supplied, no pre-suggested names.
Volume capOne proxy may act for ≤ 50 members and such shareholding % as prescribed (S.105(1) proviso; Rule 19 — not more than aggregate 10% of total share capital voting rights holders lump under one proxy, exempting one-member instances).
RevocationAlways revocable (even an “irrevocable” proxy is revocable in law); revocation must be received per articles/before meeting; Narayana Chettiar: revoked before the final poll → effective; member personally attending and voting supersedes the proxy.
Court-controlChairman's good-faith decision rejecting defective proxies final per articles (Mahaliram); wrongful mass rejection to carry business = invalid business (Namita Gupta, CLB); inspection of proxy documents by members during prescribed window.

8.2 The four voting channels

① Show of hands — S.107
Default method; one member — one vote (head-count, ignoring shares); chairman's declaration of result is conclusive unless a poll is demanded (re Horbury Bridge line; Dhakeswari Cotton Mills: not conclusive where chairman computed figures and declared wrong-on-law). Proxies cannot vote on show of hands (unless articles allow).
② Poll — S.109
Demandable before/after show-of-hands result: by ≥ 1/10 total voting power or holders of ≥ ₹5,00,000 paid-up. Order of business: adjournment/chair-election polls forthwith; others within 48 hours as chairman directs. Votes count proportional to paid-up capital; vote-splitting allowed; result = decision of meeting; scrutineers + chairman's declaration prima facie final (Mahaliram).
③ E-voting — S.108
Prescribed class of companies (listed + ≥1,000-member companies) must offer electronic voting; members who vote remotely cannot again vote at the meeting; central to listed-company AGMs/postal ballots today.
④ Postal ballot — S.110
For items the Government prescribes to be transacted only by postal ballot (e.g. certain related-party, buy-back composition items per rules) and any item the Board optionally routes so (other than the four ordinary-business items + S.180 restricted). Includes e-voting (S.2(65)); membes vote by post/electronic; scrutiniser + result announcement.

Related guard-rails: S.106 — restriction on voting rights only where articles/statute restrict (calls in arrears); S.111 — members' resolution circulation (1/10 voting power; 6 weeks' deposit; expenses); S.112 — President/Governor nominees; S.113 — corporate representation: a body corporate shareholder may authorise a representative by Board resolution who votes, counts for quorum, and appoints proxies as if individual member.

SECTION 9

Resolutions, Circulation & Minutes — Ss. 111–122

9.1 Resolutions — the vocabulary of decisions (S.114)

TypeTestUsed for (examples)
Ordinary resolution (OR)Votes for > votes against (simple majority of votes cast, in person/proxy/postal/e-voting)Ordinary AGM business; removal of director (S.169); approval of accounts
Special resolution (SR)(i) intention specified as SR in notice; (ii) votes for ≥ 3× votes againstAlter articles (S.14); shift registered office across states (S.12); reduce capital (S.66); S.180 borrowing/disposal; voluntary winding-up (S.304); S.62 non-pre-emptive issue
OR requiring special notice — S.115Members holding ≥ 1% voting power or ≤₹5,00,000 paid-up may serve special notice ≥ 14 days before; company notifies members ≥ 7 days before meetingRemoval of director before tenure ends (S.169); auditor change scenarios (S.140); appointment of a director in place of one removed

9.2 Amendments & the notice-lock

A resolution passed must be substantially the same as the one proposed in the notice; an amendment that changes the substance so that a member who stayed away would “reasonably wish he had not” is void (Torbock v. Lord Westbury principle; text discussion). Clerical/grammatical corrections are fine; where substantive amendment is likely, the notice itself should warn.

9.3 Circulation of members' resolutions — S.111

Requisitionists (the S.100-type threshold of ≥1/10 voting power) may deposit, six weeks ahead, requisition + expenses, and the company must then circulate their resolution/statement to members. The Central Government/Tribunal may relieve the company where the right is abused for needless publicity of defamatory matter; costs may fall on requisitionists even if non-parties [S.111(3)-(4)].

9.4 Registration with RoC — S.117

Copy of every special resolution (and the classes of Board/member agreements listed in S.117(3): MD appointment/variation, class-binding resolutions, unanimous substitutes for SRs, S.180 consents…) + the S.102 explanatory statement must be filed within 30 days; every post-alteration copy of the articles issued must embody the amendment; default fine ₹5–25 lakh (company) / ₹1–5 lakh officers.

9.5 Minutes — Ss. 118–121 (the meeting's memory)

  • S.118 — keeping: minutes within 30 days in a bound book, pages consecutively numbered, handwritten (typed-not-pasted; loose-leaf is irregular — Gluco Series, Cal; Delhi more lenient — Edward Keventer); each page initialled/signed and last page signed+dated by the Chairman of that or the next meeting; fair and correct summary; all officer-appointments recorded; Board minutes must name dissents; Chairman's absolute discretion to exclude defamatory/irrelevant/detrimental matter; S.118(10) secretarial standards ICSI binding; default penalty ₹25,000 (company) + ₹5,000 (officers); tampering = imprisonment ≤2 yrs + fine ≤₹1,00,000 [S.118(12)].
  • Evidentiary presumption: minutes duly kept are evidence of the proceedings; meeting/recording presumed duly called/held, appointments presumed valid — rebuttable presumption; adverse inference where the minutes book is suppressed (V.G. Balasundaram); the burden lies on the challenger (B. Sivaraman); no such presumption for requisitioned meetings (Bhankerpur Simbhaoli Beverages).
  • S.119 — inspection: GM minutes at the registered office; open to members free, business hours, ≥ 2 hours/day; copies within 7 working days at prescribed fee; Tribunal may order immediate inspection/copy.
  • S.120: electronic maintenance/inspection permitted as prescribed.
  • S.121 — AGM report: every listed public company files a report on each AGM with the RoC within 30 days (compliance confirmation); default fine ₹1–5 lakh / officers ₹25,000–1 lakh.
  • S.122 — OPC exemption: Ss. 98 & 100–111 do not apply; resolutions communicated and entered in the minutes; entry date = meeting date.
SECTION 10

Essential Conditions of a Valid Meeting — Master Synthesis

“The following are the requisites of a valid meeting: the meeting must be (1) called by proper authority; (2) convened by a proper notice served on every member in the prescribed manner; (3) must have a quorum; (4) presided over by a chairman; (5) must transact only the business stated in the notice; (6) conducted by the statutory voting machinery; and (7) evidenced by minutes. Default in any condition vitiates the meeting or the affected business.”

Supporting Authority · AGM — S.96 “once in every year”

Sree Meenakshi Mills Co. Ltd. v. Registrar of Joint Stock Companies

AIR 1938 Mad 640 · 8 Comp Cas 175
Madras High Court

① Facts

The company was required to hold an AGM “once at least in every year” (S.76, Indian Companies Act, 1913 — ancestor of S.96, 2013). Its AGM for 1934 was convened in December 1934 but was adjourned for want of readiness of accounts, and the adjourned sitting was actually held in March 1935. No other general meeting was held during 1935. The Registrar prosecuted the company and its officers for failing to hold a meeting in the calendar year 1935.

② Issue: Does an adjourned sitting of the 1934 meeting, genuinely held in 1935, satisfy the statutory command of “one meeting in every year” — or must a fresh meeting be duly convened (notice etc.) within each calendar year itself?
For the company
• The adjourned meeting was, in substance, a meeting “held in” 1935 — members assembled and acted in March 1935.
• An adjournment is a continuation, but its physical occurrence was in 1935; the law asks for a meeting per year, not a fresh notice per year.
• The 15-month gap rule was admittedly honoured — prosecution over both limbs is duplicative.
For the Registrar / Crown
• An adjourned meeting is a continuation of the original meeting; legally it “took place” on the date of the original (compare S.116 logic).
• “Once in every year” is an additional, independent limb to the gap rule — the words “in addition to” make each calendar year demand its own properly-convened meeting.
• Otherwise companies could perpetually shuffle one meeting across years by adjournments and never face general scrutiny.

④ Decision CONVICTION UPHELD

Held: there must be one meeting per year, and as many meetings as there are years. The 1935 gathering was merely the continuation of the 1934 meeting; no 1935 meeting was “held”. The duty has two cumulative limbs — (a) not more than 15 months between meetings and (b) one meeting in each calendar year — breach of either is default. Convictions of the company and its agents affirmed (fine moderated by the court acknowledging bona fides).

⑤ Principle evolved: The AGM obligation is calendar-year-absolute: an adjourned continuation belongs to the year of the original meeting. Corporate management cannot defeat the yearly accountability cycle by procedural continuance; both limbs (per-year + gap-limit) are independently enforceable. This is the canonical footnote for “once in every year … in addition to any other meeting” under S.96(1).
Visual — why an adjournment cannot “migrate” a meeting into the next calendar year.
Anchor: S.96(1) CA 2013 · use in AGM short-note + “default” limb answers.
Supporting Authority · Notice — S.102 disclosure

Kaye v. Croydon Tramways Co.

(1898) 1 Ch 358 · 67 LJ Ch 222 (Court of Appeal)
English CA

① Facts

Croydon Tramways convened a shareholders' meeting to approve the sale of the company's undertaking to the British Electric Traction group. The notice disclosed the sale and the price — but concealed that the sale agreement contained a provision for payment of a large compensation sum to the directors for their loss of office. Kaye, a shareholder, discovered this and sued to restrain the company from acting on the resolution passed at that meeting.

② Issue: Is a notice of meeting adequate if it states the nature of the business in broad terms but withholds a material fact (directors' personal interest/compensation) which would influence a reasonable shareholder's decision whether to attend and how to vote?
Company / directors
• The notice tracked the articles: it did state the “nature of the business” — sale of undertaking.
• Shareholders could inspect the agreement at the office; diligence was on members.
• The court should not police the adequacy of disclosure, only the existence of notice.
Shareholder (Kaye)
• Notice is fiduciary communication from the company's agents: it must be frank, full and fair.
• The directors' compensation was the one fact most likely to affect votes — hiding it poisoned the meeting.
• “Available for inspection” cannot rescue a notice calculated to keep members at home.

④ Decision INJUNCTION GRANTED — RESOLUTION RESTRAINED

The Court of Appeal held the notice insufficient: the meeting was convened to approve a transaction whose true character — including directors' compensation — had not been disclosed. Lindley MR's classic formulation: the notice must give shareholders enough information to decide whether to attend; a disguised or partial invitation procures a decision that equity will not let the company enforce.

⑤ Principle evolved: Notice of special business must make a full and frank disclosure of all material facts, above all the personal interest of directors. This case is the doctrinal parent of S.102's explanatory statement and its “material facts / interest of directors & KMP” content — and of the trust-over-benefit sanction for concealment [S.102(4)-(5)].
Visual — the “anti-ambush” logic now codified in S.102's explanatory statement.
Anchor: S.102(1),(4),(5) · use in Notice/Explanatory statement short notes; pair with Narayanlal Bansilal & Bimal Singh Kothari.
Supporting Authority · Quorum — S.103 “meeting”

Sharp v. Dawes

(1876) 2 QBD 26 · 46 LJ QB 104
Court of Appeal (England)

① Facts

A general meeting of the Eden-on-Sea Silversmiths' company was duly convened (in part to authorise a call). Only one shareholder — one Sharp — attended. Holding proxies of several others, he took the chair, proposed and passed the call resolution, voted, and even recorded a vote of thanks to himself as chairman. Dawes, a shareholder, was sued on the call and objected: there was no “meeting” at all.

② Issue: Can one person (even holding proxies) constitute a “meeting” and pass binding resolutions — i.e., is “meeting” satisfied by one physical person, or does it inherently require a plurality of persons?
Argument for validity (company)
• The convening was regular; every member had notice and chose to stay away.
• Attendance by one + proxies channelled many wills — plurality existed in law.
• Invalidating rewards apathy: absent members should bear what properly-convened business produces.
Argument for Dawes (member)
• “Meeting” in ordinary language = a coming together of more than one person; deliberation requires at least two minds meeting.
• Proxies speak only at a meeting that exists — they cannot create it (and here could not vote on show of hands).
• Statutes fixing quorum confirm plurality as the norm.

④ Decision CALL RESOLUTION INVALID

Held (Lord Coleridge CJ): “the word 'meeting' prima facie means a coming together of more than one person.” One shareholder, even armed with proxies, cannot alone hold a general meeting; the call was invalid. Mellish LJ agreed; the result is a default rule — subject to specific exceptions (sole member of a class: East v. Bennett Bros.; statutory directions by the Tribunal, Ss.97–98; OPCs, S.122).

⑤ Principle evolved: Pluralitas est de essentia — plurality is of the essence of a meeting; hence S.103's quorum floor and the doctrine that resolutions passed inquorate are void ab initio (reinforced in London Flats: continuation after departure = nullity). The case is the load-bearing authority for every “essential conditions” answer (pillar ③, Fig. 6 above).
Visual — why plurality ≠ paper: proxies ride on a meeting; they cannot be its body.
Anchor: S.103 · “meeting” definition · quorum short-note; contrast East v. Bennett Bros. & S.98 one-person direction.
Supporting Authority · Notice length — S.101 “clear days”

Balwant Singh Sethi v. Sardar Zorawar Singh Hushnak Singh Anand

(1988) 63 Comp Cas 310 (Bom)
Bombay High Court

① Facts

Notices of a company meeting were posted on 31 August / 1 September, and the meeting was held on 21 September. Counting either date of posting and excluding the day of the meeting, members (assuming deemed delivery 48 hours after posting, per the Act's service rules) did not have the requisite 21 clear days between deemed service and the meeting. Certain resolutions were challenged.

② Issue: How is “twenty-one clear days' notice” computed — do the day of service and the day of the meeting count — and what is the fate of a meeting (and resolutions) held on a short count?
Company
• Twenty-one days elapsed between posting and the meeting if counted inclusively; members had actual knowledge anyway.
• Service rules deem posting effective; the intention of advance intimation was substantially served.
• At worst a directory irregularity — no prejudice shown.
Members (challengers)
• “Clear days” is a defined concept: both endpoints are excluded — day of deemed service (48h after post) and day of meeting.
• Counting gives 19 days: the meeting is short by two clear days.
• Notice is jurisdictional; shorter notice only via the statutory consent route, which was never taken.

④ Decision MEETING & RESOLUTIONS INVALID

The Bombay High Court read “clear days” strictly — the day of service and the day of the meeting are both excluded — and, adding the deemed-48-hours delivery, found the notice short. The meeting and the resolutions passed at it were invalid. (The statutory regime, however allows statutory cure by the 95% consent route and for waiver/acquiescence on directory readings in other contexts.)

⑤ Principle evolved: A “clear day” count is arithmetically exact, endpoint-exclusive and includes deemed-service time; short notice is fatal unless the S.101(1) proviso consent discipline (95%) is strictly complied with. Compute first, convene later — the two-line arithmetic is an examiner's favourite trap.
Visual — the endpoint-exclusive arithmetic that decided the case.
Anchor: S.101(1) · use in Notice short-notes; pair with “shorter-notice consent 95%” proviso + Hardeep Kaur (certificate of posting not conclusive).
Supporting Authority · Poll & chairman's declaration — Ss.107–109

Mahaliram Santhalia v. Fort Gloster Jute Manufacturing Co. Ltd.

AIR 1955 Cal 132 · 58 CWN 715
Calcutta High Court

① Facts

At a contested general meeting of Fort Gloster Jute, resolutions were passed on a poll. The validity was assailed on the ground that the chairman had improperly rejected certain proxies. The articles contained the familiar clause: disputed-admission decisions of the chairman “made in good faith shall be final”. Documentation was imperfect; the challengers wanted the whole voting exercise reopened in court.

② Issue: Is the chairman's on-the-spot ruling on admission/rejection of votes and his declaration of the poll result open to collateral re-litigation, or is the burden on the challenger to displace it — and on what showing?
Challengers (minority)
• Rejected proxies materially shifted the numbers — the “result” is arithmetically wrong.
• Finality clauses cannot sanctify error of law or bad faith; courts supervise meetings.
• Statutory rights of members to vote would be null if curb-side rulings are unreviewable.
Company / chairman
• The articles' good-faith finality clause is a valid contract binding every member.
• A chairman must decide emergent questions on the spot; meetings would be paralysed if every ruling awaited a suit.
• No bad faith alleged/proved: ruling and declaration stand, per S.109(7) prima facie effect.

④ Decision CHALLENGE FAILS

S.R. Das Gupta J: “The Chairman, by virtue of his position and the nature of his duties, has to decide on the spot all emergent questions that arise at the meeting, and it is not desirable to reduce the prima facie authority of his verdict… Hence the burden of proof lies on the other party to show that the decision of the chairman was wrong.” Absent demonstrated bad faith or patent error of law, the declaration governs; the challenge was dismissed.

⑤ Principle evolved: The chairman's good-faith ruling on votes/proxies and his declaration of result enjoy prima facie conclusiveness; courts intervene only for bad faith, fraud or clear error of law — otherwise the meeting's autonomy and decisiveness prevail. This is the doctrinal twin of S.107(6) (conclusive declaration on show of hands) and S.109(7) (poll result = decision of the meeting).
Visual — the deference pipeline: on-the-spot ruling → filtered review → stability.
Anchor: Ss.107(6), 109(5)-(7) · chairman/poll & proxy short notes; pair with Dhakeswari Cotton Mills (limits of “conclusive”).
Supporting Authority · Chairman — proxy revocation — Ss.104–105

Narayana Chettiar v. Kaleeswara Mills Ltd.

AIR 1952 Mad 515 · ILR 1952 Mad 218
Madras High Court

① Facts

At a stormy general meeting of Kaleeswara Mills, the sitting chairman — facing defeat on a poll about the managing directorship — purported to dissolve the meeting and walked out with his group. The remaining members (quorum intact) elected another chairman and continued. Separately, a shareholder who had given a proxy revoked it after the first poll but before a later poll; the company argued the earlier-deployed proxy was spent but the revocation came too late for the later round.

② Issues: (a) May a frustrated chairman dissolve a quorate meeting at will and nullify its continuance? (b) Is a proxy irrevocably “used up” once deployed in an earlier poll, or may the principal revoke it before a subsequent poll/round of the same continued meeting?
Sitting chairman / company
• The chairman controls the meeting's conduct; his declaration of dissolution ends it.
• Continuation under a self-appointed chairman is usurpation; its acts bind no one.
• A proxy voted once stands as given; else oscillation and confusion in successive polls.
Majority members
• Dissolution is the meeting's will, not the chairman's weapon; with quorum present, members may elect a new chairman and proceed.
• The chair cannot be used to veto democracy.
• Agency 101: a proxy is revocable like any authority, until actually relied upon for that act — the later poll had not happened when revoked.

④ Decision CONTINUATION VALID · REVOCATION EFFECTIVE

The Madras High Court upheld both propositions: (a) where the chairman abandons the chair from motives hostile to the meeting's purpose, the meeting does not die — the members deputise another chairman and lawfully continue; (b) the proxy having been revoked before the later poll, the later vote by the erstwhile proxy was invalid — revocation of agency is effective against future acts even within a continuing meeting.

⑤ Principle evolved: The chairman is trustee of the meeting's process, not its master: he cannot dissolve it to dodge defeat; and the shareholder-agency law governs proxies — revocable until exercised for the act in question. Together these supply the two classic qualifications to Ss.104–105 (chairmanship and proxy) that every “essential conditions” answer must state.
Visual — the two doctrines of Kaleeswara Mills: democracy outlives the walkout; authority dies at revocation.
Anchor: Ss.104–105 · Chairman & proxy short notes; pair with Byng v. London Life (disorderly-venue adjournment discipline) & Harben v. Phillips (a void meeting can do nothing).

PYQ 1 · Short Note (4 marks)

“Write a short note on the Annual General Meeting.” DU-pattern — repeatedly asked

Marks split: № 1 definition+compulsory · № 1 timing clocks · № 1 venue/hours · № 1 business + default remedies.

The AGM, S.96 CA 2013, is the compulsory yearly meeting of members of every company, expressly so called in the notice, at which the company submits its annual stewardship to the owners. Timing: the first AGM within 9 months of the close of the first financial year; thereafter one in each calendar year, within 6 months of the financial year's close, gap ≤ 15 months; the Registrar may extend only subsequent AGMs by ≤ 3 months for special reason. Venue/hours: business hours (9–6), not a national holiday, at the registered office or same city/town — an unlisted company may meet anywhere in India with advance consent of all members [S.96(2)]. Business: the four ordinary items of S.102(2) — accounts & reports, dividend, directors' rotation, auditors — plus any special business with an explanatory statement. The obligation is two-limbed and calendar-year absolute: an adjourned continuation belongs to the original year (Sree Meenakshi Mills v. RoC, AIR 1938 Mad 640). Default: a member (not the company) may move the Tribunal (S.97); fine on company & officers in default (S.99) — but non-holding is no ground for winding up, and failure to lay accounts does not vitiate the meeting itself (Sunil Dev v. DDCA).

Examiner hook: The 9/6/15 numbers + Meenakshi Mills ratio. If asked “can an EGM count as the year's AGM?” — answer with Lachmi Narain/Emperor v. Nasurbhai debate and conclude: the modern reading is NO; the AGM must be specifically convened as one.
PYQ 2 · Medium (7–8 marks)

“Explain the law relating to Extraordinary General Meetings and the members' right of requisition under the Companies Act, 2013.” DU-pattern — frequently asked

Marks split: № 2 definition & who may call · № 3 mechanics of requisition & 21/45/3-month clocks · № 2 doctrine edges (scope frozen; no-reasons; self-help before S.98) · № 1 synthesis/figure.

Definition. Every general meeting other than the AGM is an EGM (Table F, cl. 42) — the reserve forum for business that cannot wait for the annual audit-cum-review. Who may call: (i) the Board suo motu, S.100(1); (ii) the Board on requisition, S.100(2); (iii) the requisitionists themselves in default, S.100(4); (iv) the Tribunal where impracticable, S.98. Requisition mechanics: holders of ≥ 1/10 of paid-up voting capital (or 1/10 voting power, no-share-capital companies) deposit a signed requisition stating the matters at the registered office; the Board must “proceed” within 21 days to call, and convene within 45 days; failing which the requisitionists (majority in value ≥ threshold) may themselves hold it within 3 months, with expenses reimbursed by the company and recoverable from defaulting directors. Doctrine edges: (a) scope frozen — no item beyond the stated matters (Ball v. Metal Industries); (b) the Board cannot refuse merely because the proposed resolution might fail (Cricket Club of India v. M.L. Apte); (c) removal-requisitions need no reasons (LIC v. Escorts, SC), though S.115 special notice discipline still guards the removal resolution (Queens Kuries); (d) refusal is not itself an offence (Anantha R. Hegde) — self-help, not prosecution, is the statute's answer; (e) try S.100 first — S.98 is residual (B. Mohandas). An EGM's venue is free of the S.96(2) venue-fetters. Conclusion: S.100 converts shareholder voice from a courtesy into an enforced duty: 21/45/3-month clocks make the Board's obligation self-executing.

Examiner hook: The 1/10 → 21 → 45 → 3-month chain recited in one sentence, plus Ball (scope) and LIC v. Escorts (no-reasons) as the two edges.
PYQ 3 · Long (8 marks)

“What are the essential requisites/conditions of a valid meeting? Discuss with reference to the Companies Act, 2013 and decided cases.” DU-pattern — the unit's flagship question

Marks split: № 1 framing · № 5 — seven pillars (1+1+1+1+1 combined voting/minutes) · № 2 authorities + consequence-table + crisp ending.

A company's gathering is a “meeting” in law only if seven cumulative conditions hold; defect in the early pillars kills the meeting, defect in the later ones kills the affected transaction (structure as in Fig. 6 — reproduce it in 6 boxes). (1) Proper authority: only the Board's resolution (suo motu or on S.100 requisition) or the Tribunal (Ss.97–98) can convene; a director alone cannot. (2) Proper notice: 21 clear days to every member, auditor and directors (S.101), stating place/day/hour and business, with the S.102 explanatory statement for special business — length arithmetically strict (Balwant Singh Sethi), disclosure full and frank (Kaye v. Croydon Tramways; Narayanlal Bansilal); short notice only by 95% consent; an unnotified meeting cannot even adjourn (Harben v. Phillips). (3) Quorum: 5 (public, scaled 15/30) / 2 (private), personally present (S.103); one man is no meeting (Sharp v. Dawes); absence → requisitioned meeting dissolves, others auto-adjourn a week, and then members present count. (4) Chairman: per articles, else elected by members present (S.104); he rules on the spot and his good-faith rulings carry prima facie finality (Mahaliram) but he cannot dissolve at will (Narayana Chettiar v. Kaleeswara Mills) or adjourn a lawful meeting arbitrarily (Byng v. London Life). (5) Business only per the notice: requisitions freeze scope (Ball v. Metal Industries); unnotified capital issue void (Martin Castelino). (6) Statutory voting machinery: show of hands first (S.107), poll on demand thresholds (S.109), e-voting (S.108) & postal ballot (S.110) for scale; proxies within the S.105 discipline. (7) Minutes & filings: bound signed minutes within 30 days (S.118) carrying a rebuttable presumption of validity; S.117/121 filings close the loop; tampering is criminal. Ending: each pillar is individually enforceable because the meeting is not a social event but the company's act of will — courts invalidate not to frustrate members but to keep the franchise honest.

Examiner hook: Seven pillars named in order + one authority per pillar. This structure converts any version of this question (and PYQ 8's problem) into assembling pre-fabricated parts.
PYQ 4 · Short Note (4 marks)

“Write a short note on Quorum.” DU-pattern — repeatedly asked

Marks split: № 1 meaning + numbers · № 1 personally-present rules · № 1 30-minute waterfall · № 1 Sharp v Dawes + exceptions.

Quorum is the minimum number of members whose personal presence constitutes the meeting. S.103(1): public company — 5 (≤1,000 members), 15 (1,001–5,000), 30 (>5,000); private — 2; articles may demand more but not fewer. Proxies do not count; joint holders count as one; a S.113 representative counts. If quorum is not forthcoming within 30 minutes: a requisitioned meeting stands dissolved; any other stands adjourned to the same day/time/place next week — and if no quorum then, members present are the quorum [S.103(2)-(3)]. The floor exists because “meeting” means a coming together of more than one person (Sharp v. Dawes) — caveat: sole-class-holder meetings (East v. Bennett Bros.) and Tribunal-directed one-person meetings (S.98: El Sombrero logic against quorum-veto). Start-time quorum suffices per Hartley Baird, but London Flats invalidates what passes after collapse — safest practice, keep quorum throughout.

Examiner hook: The 5/15/30 scale (often omitted, earns the mark) + the dissolve/adjourn fork.
PYQ 5 · Short Note (4 marks)

“Notice of meeting and explanatory statement — discuss.” DU-pattern — repeatedly asked

Marks split: № 1 length/mode · № 1 contents · № 1 explanatory statement contents+sanction · № 1 Kaye + defect consequences.

Notice is the jurisdictional foundation of the meeting. Length: 21 clear days (both endpoints excluded; 48h deemed service), shorter only with ≥95% consent (AGM: of members entitled to vote; others: of paid-up voting strength) — Balwant Singh Sethi makes the arithmetic fatal when fudged. Mode: writing or electronic; certificate of posting not conclusive (Hardeep Kaur); postal strike defeats deemed-service presumption (Bradman); fax/e-mail fine (PNC Telecom). Contents: place/day/hour + statement of business; everything except the four S.102(2) AGM items (and all EGM business) is special business; unnotified special business is void (Martin Castelino). Explanatory statement must annex to each special item all material facts, interests of directors/KMP/relatives, ≥2% shareholding links, inspection time/place — concealment imports a trust over the benefit plus penalty [S.102(4)-(5)]. The gold-standard authority is Kaye v. Croydon Tramways: hiding the directors' compensation behind a sale-resolution notice invalidated the approval — notice must be full, frank, unambiguous (Narayanlal Bansilal; Bimal Singh Kothari: copies must reach members, not rest at the office). Cure routes: member-with-knowledge cannot complain (Parsuram, PC); acquiescence/benefit-taking cures directory lapses (Shailesh Harilal Shah).

Examiner hook: Kaye ratio + the trust-over-benefit sanction: the two least-quoted, highest-yield facts.
PYQ 6 · Short Note (4 marks)

“Write a short note on Proxy.” DU-pattern — repeatedly asked

Marks split: № 1 nature (agency) & who · № 1 powers/limits · № 1 form/48h/50-cap/revocation · № 1 authority.

A proxy is the member's agent at the meeting — “a person representative of a shareholder… to carry out a course which the shareholder has himself decided upon” (S.105). Any member entitled to attend & vote may appoint any person (member or not); the proxy votes only on a poll (per statute — articles may extend to show of hands), has no right to speak, and votes in excess of authority are void while the rest stand. Form & timing: written, signed instrument deposited 48 hours before the meeting (longer article-periods read down); the notice must advertise the proxy right; blank forms issued without suggested names. Caps & control: one proxy ≤ 50 members (and prescribed capital %), chairman's good-faith rejection/acceptance decisions are final (Mahaliram); proxies do not count for quorum. Revocable always — the member attending in person supersedes; revocation before a later poll in a continued meeting is effective (Narayana Chettiar v. Kaleeswara Mills).

Examiner hook: 48-hour deposit + 50-member cap + Kaleeswara Mills revocation rule.
PYQ 7 · Medium (7–8 marks)

“Distinguish ordinary and special resolutions; also show of hands, poll and postal ballot.” DU-pattern — comparison format

Marks split: № 3 resolutions table (test, uses, filing, amendment-lock, special notice) · № 3 voting channels · № 2 unifying close + authorities.

Resolutions (S.114): an ordinary resolution requires votes-for > votes-against (of votes cast); powers the four AGM ordinary items, director-removal (with special notice), routine approvals; no filing generally required. A special resolution requires (a) the intention stated as SR in the notice, and (b) votes-for ≥ 3× votes-against; it drives constitutional change — articles (S.14), inter-state registered-office shift (S.12), capital reduction (S.66), S.180 disposals/borrowings, non-pre-emptive issues (S.62), voluntary winding-up (S.304) — and must be filed with the RoC within 30 days (S.117) and embodied in subsequent copies of the articles. Both are frozen by the notice: amendments changing substance void them (would a member who stayed away reasonably wish he hadn't?). A third hybrid — OR by special notice (S.115) — guards destabilising moves (removal of directors/auditors): 1%-holders' notice 14 days ahead, company's circulation 7 days ahead.

Voting channels: show of hands (S.107) is head-count — one member, one vote, chairman's declaration conclusive bar poll (Dhakeswari: unless wrongly computed); poll (S.109) is share-weighted — demanded by 1/10 voting power or ₹5-lakh holders, chairman-bound if demanded, adjournment/chair-polls forthwith, others within 48h, result = decision of meeting, declaration prima facie final (Mahaliram); e-voting (S.108) enfranchises scale in listed/large companies; postal ballot (S.110) — voting by post/electronics for statutorily reserved items and optional items — skips assembly itself (with scrutiniser and results process). Close: the channels form one system: hands for speed, poll for precision, e-vote/postal for reach — proxies ride all except show of hands (unless articles allow).

Examiner hook: SR's two-limb test + 3× arithmetic + S.117 filing — the three most frequently missed points.
PYQ 8 · Problem (8 marks)

“A Ltd. posted AGM notices 28 days before, but two members never received theirs. At the meeting: quorum fell below five mid-way; the chairman, losing a removal poll, declared the meeting dissolved and left; the remaining four elected a chairman, continued, and passed the removal — afterwards a lone member seeks to undo everything. Advise.” DU-pattern composite — problem format

Marks split: № 2 notice limb · № 2 quorum limb · № 2 chairman-dissolution limb · № 2 removal-resolution validity + conclusion.

Issue 1 — notice: 28 days from posting easily satisfies the 21-clear-day rule even with the 48-hour deemed-service addition (Balwant Sethi arithmetic). Non-receipt by two members: the Act operates on proper despatch — but proof of posting is not conclusive (Hardeep Kaur); if the company proves correct despatch, isolated non-receipt is generally a directory lapse not vitiating the meeting (Shailesh Harilal Shah), absent prejudice-cum-design (Harben v. Phillips applies to wholesale omission). Issue 2 — quorum: S.103 requires 5 (listed-scale aside); Hartley Baird validates what passed while quorum existed, but London Flats invalidates post-collapse business; on the stricter (and safer) view, what the four passed after the fall is vitiated — UNLESS the meeting was “adjourned” by the articles/continuance doctrine, which cannot rescue that same session. Issue 3 — chairman's dissolution: he cannot dissolve at will to dodge defeat (Narayana Chettiar v. Kaleeswara Mills); the members' election of a new chairman was valid — provided quorum existed. Issue 4 — removal resolution: director-removal needs S.115 special notice + natural-justice hearing (S.169); if the special-notice cycle wasn't run, the resolution fails on that ground alone (Queens Kuries) regardless of the meeting's validity; requisitionists need no reasons (LIC v. Escorts). Conclusion: the meeting itself likely stands (notice sound), the chairmanship succession is valid, but the four-person continuation sits on the London Flats fault-line and the removal resolution collapses for want of special notice; advise the member that his strongest points are the quorum-collapse frame and the S.115 defect — while the company's best answer is Hartley Baird + proof of proper despatch.

Examiner hook: Issue-splitting discipline (IRAC per limb) + citing both London Flats and Hartley Baird teaches the examiner you hold the whole doctrine, not half of it.
