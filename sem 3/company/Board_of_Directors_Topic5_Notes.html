SECTION 1

Corporate Governance — The Concept

A company is “an artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law… it must act through living persons” — the directors. Corporate governance is the system of rules, practices and processes by which those human agents are directed, supervised and held accountable so that the corporation is run for its proper purpose and not for the private benefit of its controllers.

Classic definition (Cadbury Committee, UK, 1992): “Corporate governance is the system by which companies are directed and controlled.” It concerns the structure of the Board, the flow of honest information, and the balance of power between shareholders, the board and the professional managers.

1.1 Why does the topic begin here?

All shareholders cannot take part in management. Ownership (shareholders) is therefore separated from control (directors) — the celebrated separation of ownership and control. This separation creates the agency problem: directors (agents) may advance their own interest against that of the company (principal). Corporate governance is the set of safeguards erected against that abuse, and this whole Topic 5 — appointment rules, DIN, independent directors, duties, liabilities, the five cases and the Satyam case study — is simply the Indian legal architecture of those safeguards.

1.2 The Four Pillars of Good Corporate Governance

① Accountability

Directors must answer for every exercise/designation of power — to shareholders, auditors, regulators and ultimately the courts (fiduciary duties, S.166; misfeasance proceedings).

② Transparency

True and timely disclosure — audited accounts, Board's report, Directors' Responsibility Statement (S.134(5)) and fraud reporting by auditors (S.143(12)).

③ Independence

An independent element on the board (independent directors, S.2(47)/149; audit committee, S.177) capable of questioning dominant executives and promoters.

④ Fairness / Responsibility

Balancing the interests of members, employees, the community and the environment — expressly embedded in S.166(2) of the Companies Act, 2013.

SECTION 2

The Director — Concept & Legal Position

2.1 Who is a Director? — Definition

S. 2(34), Companies Act, 2013

“‘Director’ means a director appointed to the Board of a company.” — The Act does not precisely define the office; what matters is the nature of the position occupied and the duties performed. A person who performs the functions of a director is a director in the eyes of the law even if called by another name (de facto director); and minutes books and statutory returns are important evidence of the fact. Section 2(10): “‘Board of Directors’ or ‘Board’, in relation to a company, means the collective body of the directors of the company.”

A company, being a legal abstraction, must act through human agency: “A living person has a mind which can have knowledge or intention and hands to carry out his intention. A corporation has none of these; it must act through living persons” (Lord Reid in Tesco Supermarkets Ltd. v. Nattrass). Hence the necessity of directors — the “directing mind and will” of the company.

2.3 Kinds of Directors (Companies Act, 2013)

Independent Director — S.2(47), 149(4)-(12)

Non-executive, integrity-based outsider; mandatory 1/3rd in listed companies. The centrepiece of §6.

Managing Director — S.2(54)

Entrusted with substantial powers of management (by articles/agreement/resolution); dual capacity of director + employee; exercises delegated powers subject to Board superintendence.

Whole-time Director — S.2(94)

A director in the whole-time employment of the company; dual capacity like the MD.

Woman Director — S.149(1) proviso

Short-note favourite. Every listed company & public companies with paid-up capital ≥ ₹100 crore or turnover ≥ ₹300 crore must have at least one woman director (need not be independent).

Resident Director — S.149(3)

Every company must have at least one director who has stayed in India ≥ 182 days in the previous calendar year — so that one key person is amenable to Indian jurisdiction.

Additional / Alternate / Nominee Director — S.161

Additional by Board (till next AGM); alternate, in place of a director absent from India 3+ months (if articles/resolution authorise); nominee, appointed by institutions/Government/others (S.161(3)).

Small Shareholders' Director — S.151

For prescribed listed companies (paid-up ≥ ₹5 crore with ≥ 1,000 “small shareholders” holding ≤ ₹20,000 nominal value each) — minority representation on the Board.

De facto / Shadow Director

“By whatever name called” — a person in accordance with whose directions professional directors act (professional advisers acting purely professionally excluded). Important for liability, especially under S.2(60) “officer who is in default”.

Executive vs Non-Executive

Executives manage; NEDs monitor. S.149(12) fixes limited liability for NEDs/independent directors — only for acts with their knowledge (attributable through Board processes), consent/connivance, or where they did not act diligently.

First Directors — S.152(1)

Named in articles; else the subscribers to the memorandum (individuals) deemed first directors until the first AGM.

SECTION 3

Constitution of the Board — Section 149

S. 149 — Company to have Board of Directors
  • Only individuals can be directors — a company or firm cannot be a director of another company (one “evil” abolished by the Act).
  • Minimum: 3 (public co.) · 2 (private co.) · 1 (One Person Company). Maximum: 15; more only by special resolution.
  • Woman director: prescribed classes must have ≥ 1 [proviso to S.149(1) + Rule 3: listed cos.; public cos. with paid-up capital ≥ ₹100 cr or turnover ≥ ₹300 cr].
  • Resident director: ≥ 1 director resident in India (≥ 182 days stay in previous calendar year) [S.149(3)].
  • Compliance timeline for existing companies: one year from commencement/notification [S.149(5)].
S. 172 — Punishment for non-observance of Chapter XI composition rules

If a company contravenes the composition provisions (Ss.149–172 where no specific penalty is provided), the company and every officer in default is punishable with fine of ₹50,000 – ₹5,00,000 (post-2019 “Decriminalisation” amendments the Chapter uses penal fines rather than imprisonment for these defaults).

SECTION 4

Appointment of Directors — Sections 152, 160–165

Appointment is the shareholders' primary instrument of control: “Election of directors is the primary managerial function of stockholders in business corporations, and one that needed careful regulation in their interest.” The Act therefore regulates appointment strictly and blocks management by “undesirable persons”.

4.1 Modes of Appointment — the statutory map

4.2 Minimum Director Identification Number first - S.152(3)

No person shall be appointed as a director unless he has been allotted a DIN under S.154 (or any other number prescribed under S.153). This is the gateway between appointment law and the DIN regime (§5).

4.3 Qualifications & Disqualifications — Section 164

The Act prescribes no academic or professional qualification for directors (though articles may insist on qualification shares). Instead, S.164(1)-(2) supplies a negative list of minimum eligibility requirements:

S.164(1) — A person shall NOT be eligible for appointment as director if he:

  1. is of unsound mind and a competent court so finds (finding in force);
  2. is an undischarged insolvent;
  3. has applied to be adjudicated an insolvent and the application is pending;
  4. has been convicted of an offence (whether involving moral turpitude or otherwise) and sentenced to imprisonment for ≥ 6 months, and 5 years have not elapsed since expiry of the sentence. Proviso: conviction with imprisonment ≥ 7 years → permanent disqualification from directorship in any company;
  5. is disqualified by an order of a court or Tribunal, order in force;
  6. has not paid calls on shares held by him (alone or jointly) and 6 months have passed from the payment date;
  7. has been convicted of the offence of related-party transactions (S.188) within the last 5 years;
  8. has not complied with S.152(3) — no DIN.

S.164(2) — “Failed company” disqualification (5-year bar)

A person who is or has been a director of a company which: (a) has not filed annual accounts/returns for 3 continuous years; or (b) has failed to repay deposits/interest, redeem debentures, or pay declared dividend, and such failure continues for 1 year or more — shall not be eligible for appointment/re-appointment in that or any other company for 5 years. (A private company may add further disqualifications by its articles — S.164(3).)

Limit on directorships — S.165: a person may be director in at most 20 companies (dormant companies excluded), of which at most 10 public companies (private subsidiaries of public companies counted). DIN is the mechanism by which the RoC polices this cap — hence its purpose is described as “checking the violation of S.165”. Penalty (S.165(6)): ₹2,000 per day of default, capped at ₹2 lakh.

SECTION 5

Director's Identification Number (DIN) — Sections 153–159 [ classic SHORT NOTE ]

DIN is a unique identification number allotted by the Central Government to every individual who is, or intends to be, a director. Its purpose: to give the regulator a traceable identity key so that the S.165 ceiling on the number of directorships (20 / 10) can actually be enforced, and so that the track-record of every director is transparent across companies.

SECTION 6

Independent Director — Section 2(47) [ classic SHORT NOTE / LONG Q ]

S. 2(47) — Definition

“‘Independent director’ means an independent director referred to in sub-section (6) of section 149.” — In literal terms: a director who has no material/pecuniary relationship with the company, its promoters or management, and who brings objective, external judgment to the boardroom.

6.1 Why the institution exists

To make Indian boards consistent with international capital-market standards, the 2013 Act made independent directors mandatory for listed and large public companies. Independent directors evaluate the performance of the board and management; safeguard all stakeholders (particularly minority shareholders); moderate conflicts of interest; and are the statutory antidote to promoter-dominated decision-making — the precise pathology behind Satyam (§9).

6.2 The mandatory numbers — S.149(4) + Rule 4

  • Every listed public company: at least one-third of total directors independent (fractions rounded up).
  • Prescribed unlisted public companies [Rule 4]: at least 2 independent directors if — paid-up share capital ≥ ₹10 crore, or turnover ≥ ₹100 crore, or aggregate outstanding loans/debentures/deposits ≥ ₹50 crore.
  • Transitional: existing companies got 1 year to comply [S.149(5)]. Default → company + officer in default: fine ₹50,000–₹5,00,000 (S.172).

6.3 The eligibility code — S.149(6) {(a)–(f)} memorised as “I-N-P-P-E-K-Q”

An independent director, in relation to a company, means a director other than an MD, WTD or nominee director —

  1. Integrity (I): who, in the opinion of the Board, is a person of integrity possessing relevant expertise and experience;
  2. Non-promoter (N): who is/was not a promoter of the company or its holding, subsidiary or associate company;
  3. Pecuniary wall (P): who has/had no pecuniary relationship with the company/H/S/A company, their promoters or directors during the two immediately preceding financial years or the current financial year;
  4. Relatives' ceiling (P): none of whose relatives has/had pecuniary relationships/transactions with those entities amounting to ≥ 2% of gross turnover/total income, or ₹50 lakh (or higher prescribed sum, whichever is lower) in the same look-back periods;
  5. Employment/KMP bar (E): neither he nor his relatives — (i) holds/has held KMP or employment in the company/H/S/A in the 3 preceding financial years; (ii) is/has been employee/proprietor/partner of the auditors, CS, or cost auditors of the company, or legal/consulting firms with ≥ 10% of gross turnover from the company, in those 3 years; (iii) holds, with relatives, ≥ 2% voting power; (iv) is CEO/director of an NPO receiving ≥ 25% of receipts from the company;
  6. Qualifications (Q): possesses other prescribed qualifications (e.g., proficiency test under the 2019 data-bank rules).

A major post-Satyam assurance designed to restore willingness to serve on boards.

6.5 Schedule IV — the “Code for Independent Directors” (know the skeleton)

Part I · Guidelines of Professional Conduct

Uphold ethical standards of integrity & probity; act objectively/constructively with independent judgment; devote sufficient time; not abuse position; not allow extraneous considerations to cloud judgment.

Part II · Role & Functions

Bring independent judgment on strategy, performance, risk, resources, key appointments & standards of conduct; scrutinise management's performance & financial information; safeguard interests of all stakeholders, especially minority shareholders; balance conflicting interests; moderate/arbitrate in conflicts between management and shareholders.

Part III · Duties

Undertake induction & skill-refresh; seek clarification/information (including professional advice at company expense); attend Board/committee meetings & GMs; keep informed; keep confidential info confidential; report concerns about unethical behaviour/actual/suspected fraud or violations of the company's code/ethics policy; not to obstruct functioning; protect interests of the company, shareholders & employees.

Part IV · Appointment / Manner

Nomination & Remuneration Committee (S.178) identifies & recommends; appointment formalised by letter; disclosed on company's website & in Board report; separate meetings of independent directors (at least once a year, without non-independent directors/management); performance evaluation — Board evaluates independent directors who are themselves excluded from their own evaluation.

SECTION 7

Duties of Directors — Section 166 (with the case-law skeleton)

“Corporate executives are today possessed of immense power which must be regulated not only for the public good, but also for the protection of those whose investments are involved.” Traditionally, directors' duties were forged by the common law in the cases below; the Companies Act, 2013 codified them in Section 166 — a statutory formulation in six commands.

7.1 The six statutory duties — text, meaning, and the case that teaches each

Duty (S.166)Text (verbatim)Meaning & doctrinal anchor
(1) Act within the articles“…shall act in accordance with the articles of the company.”The all-pervading principle: articles are the director's charter of power; transgression → intra vires challenge, personal liability for ultra vires acts. Renders void any exercise of power beyond the articles' mandate.
(2) Good faith to promote the company's objects for all stakeholders“…shall act in good faith in order to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, the shareholders, the community and for the protection of environment.”The fiduciary core — “greatest good faith is expected”. Beneficiaries: the company as a whole + the whole body of shareholders, NOT individual shareholders (Percival v. Wright). Powers must be used for their proper purpose for the company's benefit — see Burland v. Earle (Case 20) for permissible margins of internal management.
(3) Due & reasonable care, skill, diligence; independent judgment“…shall exercise his duties with due and reasonable care, skill and diligence and shall exercise independent judgment.”The negligence head: common-law standard descending from Romer J. in Re City Equitable (Case 21) — now a statutory objective-reasonableness floor. Factors: nature of work; division of powers; customs of the business; gratuitous vs paid service. Not liable for mere errors of judgment. Attend meetings able to be attended; 12-month unauthorised absence = vacation (S.167).
(4) No conflict of interest“…shall not involve in a situation in which he may have a direct or indirect interest that conflicts, or possibly may conflict, with the interest of the company.”The no-conflict rule — the equity spine of Regal (Hastings) (Case 22) and IDC v. Cooley (Case 23): “a man must not be allowed to put himself in a position in which his fiduciary duty and his interests conflict”. Disclose interests at the first Board meeting (Ss.184, 188) — undisclosed interested contract is voidable at the company's option (S.188) + penalty ₹1 lakh.
(5) No undue gain“…shall not achieve or attempt to achieve any undue gain or advantage either to himself or to his relatives, partners, or associates… liable to pay an amount equal to that gain to the company.”The no-secret-profit rule — codification of Regal (Hastings) & Cooley: the fiduciary must disgorge to the company. Liability arises from the mere fact of profit made by the fiduciary position — bona fides no defence.
(6) No assignment of office“…shall not assign his office and any assignment so made shall be void.”Delegates non potest delegare: duties are personal. Limited delegation of functions is permissible where authorised by the Act/articles or justified by business exigency — but never a total abdication of the Board's collective responsibility.
S.166(7) — Penalty: contravention of S.166 → fine not less than ₹1,00,000, extending to ₹5,00,000, plus the civil remedies below (disgorgement, damages, rescission) and Schedule IV consequences for independent directors.

7.2 The common-law folder underneath S.166 (quote-earners)

  • Trustees of company assets — make good misapplied money (Re Lands Allotment Co. (1894)).
  • Proper-purpose doctrine — powers (calls, forfeiture, further issue of capital, transfer refusal) are “powers in trust”, to be used for corporate ends, not sectional interest.
  • No profit from office — if asked to buy property for the company and he buys for himself and resells to the company at profit → account (Boston Deep Sea Fishing v. Ansell; Regal line).
  • Disclosure & abstention in interested contracts — the company is entitled to the unbiased advice of every director; where the whole board knows, no formal disclosure needed (Guinness plc v. Saunders — director ordered to hand over benefits).
  • Duty to attend meetings — continuous absence is evidence of failure of the S.166(3) duty and triggers S.167 vacation.
  • Delegation controlled — permissible where authorised/exigent, but a director is in breach if he leaves to others what only the Board can do (Joint Stock Discount Co. v. Brown).

8.4 Vicarious Liability — the judicial doctrine (crucial for problem questions)

Starting rule: directors are NOT vicariously liable for the company's crimes merely by virtue of office. For a director to be criminally liable, ALL of the following must coexist — (i) the company itself is liable for the offence; (ii) a statute specifically creates vicarious liability of directors (e.g., S.141, NI Act; S.23C Drugs & Cosmetics Act); (iii) specific, substantiated allegations attributing a particular role to that director; (iv) direct involvement/personal conduct linking him to the company's liability; (v) criminal intent (mens rea). Mere authorisation, supervisory role, routine functions or general knowledge are not enough. (Sanjay Dutt v. State of Haryana, SC, 2 Jan 2025; Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI (2015) 4 SCC 609.)

Post-Satyam relaxation for independent/NEDs sits here too: S.149(12) limits independent and non-executive director liability to acts with knowledge/consent/connivance or lack of diligence — preventing “automatic” prosecution of outside directors.

9.5 Where exactly did governance fail? (the analysis markers want)

① Board / Independent Directors

The board included globally reputed names — a dean of ISB, a Harvard professor — yet none demanded independent bank confirmations or questioned the Maytas transaction until investors revolted. They had delegated away verification: the exact City-Equitable blind spot, decades after the exemption clauses died.

② Auditors

Price Waterhouse accepted management-supplied FD receipts and confirmations instead of writing to banks directly — the most elementary audit step. (SEBI 2018: the firm “was defeated/meant to be defeated” but failed basic scepticism.)

③ Promoter incentive structure

A promoter with a small, fully-pledged holding had every incentive to keep the stock price artificially high — inflation of results was survival. Hence S.166(2)'s duty to members “as a whole” fights exactly this alignment failure.

④ Institutional passivity & weak enforcement pre-2013

Analysts relied on audited numbers; the 1956 Act lacked: codified directors' duties, mandatory independent-director regime with enforceable code (Sch. IV), vigil mechanism, audit rotation, and a fraud-specific offence. The 2013 Act is, in large part, Satyam's legislative reply.

Case No. 19 · Duty towards whom?

Percival v. Wright

(1902) 2 Ch. 421 · Chancery Division · Swinfen Eady J.
CH D · 1902

Material facts

  1. The company, Nixon's Navigation Co. Ltd. (a colliery company), had few shareholders; shares were transferable only with Board approval and had no market quotation. Its memorandum empowered sale of the whole undertaking, but only with a special resolution of shareholders.
  2. On 8 October 1900 the plaintiffs (holders of 253 shares of £10 each, £9 8s paid up) wrote to the company secretary: do you know anyone willing to buy our shares? After quoting £12.5s (backed by an independent valuation they themselves had commissioned) and finally £12.10s, the chairman and two directors bought — 85 shares to the chairman, 84 each to two co-directors — transfers duly approved by the Board.
  3. Unbeknownst to the plaintiffs, one Holden had simultaneously been approaching the Board proposing to purchase the whole undertaking at prices implying considerably more than £12.10s per share. But no firm offer ever matured that the Board could place before shareholders; the negotiations ultimately proved abortive, and the Court was not even satisfied the Board ever intended to sell.
  4. The shareholders sued to set aside the sale, complaining of non-disclosure of the Holden negotiations.
Visual A — the sellers set the price; the buyers (directors) stayed silent about inconclusive takeover chatter.
Issue: Whether directors purchasing shares from shareholders owe those individual shareholders a fiduciary duty of disclosure regarding pending negotiations for sale of the company's undertaking?
For the plaintiffs (sellers)
  • Directors are fiduciaries “both for the company and for the shareholders who are the real beneficiaries”; during sale negotiations they become “trustees for sale” and cannot buy a beneficiary's interest without full disclosure.
  • In equity a share is merely a proportion of the undertaking; shareholders inter se stand like partners — incorporation does not disturb that inner relationship, though it changes external relations.
  • The tacit release of disclosure applies only to ordinary management information (a new vein, a good dividend) — not to a negotiation for the whole undertaking; had they known, plaintiffs would have held on to their shares.
  • Conceded: no allegation of unfair dealing or under-value purchase — only breach of fiduciary non-disclosure.
For the defendants (directors)
  • Even if trustees for sale of the undertaking, they were not trustees for sale of the plaintiffs' shares.
  • Equity has never been applied between a director and an individual shareholder; every director-buyer necessarily buys from a shareholder.
  • The company is a legal entity quite distinct from shareholders (citing the principle that a sale by company to shareholder isn't impeached merely because the shareholder's votes carried the resolution).
  • Imposing the duty would, in substance, re-write the relation between every director and every shareholder in every share-deal.

Decision — Swinfen Eady J.: DIRECTORS WINPLAINTIFFS' CASE “WHOLLY FAILS”

The learned judge reviewed the classic statements — Lord Selborne L.C. in Great Eastern Ry. v. Turner (directors are “the mere trustees or agents of the company”, not of individuals); Jessel M.R. in Re Forest of Dean Coal Mining Co. (trustees of assets under control, but “only the managing partners”); Lindley L.J. in Re Lands Allotment Co. (treated as trustees of money actually in their hands). Those cases show directors must act bona fide and give best terms for the company (e.g., not allot shares to themselves cheaply) — but the plaintiffs' contention “goes far beyond this”.

No authority supports the proposed line — that a disclosure-duty springs up once sale-negotiations begin. The true rule is the opposite: “a shareholder is fixed with knowledge of all the directors' powers, and has no more reason to assume that they are not negotiating a sale of the undertaking than to assume that they are not exercising any other power.” Imposing the duty would place directors in a “most invidious position” — forced to disclose negotiations whose premature publication might itself injure the company. Crucially, the shareholders approached the directors and named their price; there was no unfair dealing. Action dismissed with costs.

Principle evolved (the ratio): Directors' fiduciary duties are owed to the company alone (and, in the aggregate sense, the whole body of shareholders) — not to individual shareholders. Absent special circumstances (special trust/agency reposed in the director by the shareholder, imminent concluded contract, or fraud), directors may purchase shares without disclosing abortive sale-negotiations. Followed in Peskin v. Anderson (2000); the rule admits narrow exceptions (Allen v. Hyatt; cf. Coleman v. Myers (NZ) — family-company special facts).
Indian & exam relevance: approved in Ramaswamy Iyer v. Brahamayya & Co. (1966) 1 Comp LJ 107 (Mad); foundational for analysing S.166(2) — “for the benefit of its members as a whole”. Note the factual armour that saved the directors in any event: no solicitation by buyers + independent valuation + sellers-initiated approach.

Case No. 20 · Internal management vs. fiduciary accountability

Burland v. Earle (Consolidated)

(1902) AC 83 : (1900-03) All ER Rep. 1452 · Privy Council (on appeal from Ontario) · Lord Davey
PRIVY COUNCIL · 1902

Material facts

  1. British American Bank Note Co. (incorporated in Canada, 1866 — engraving/printing of banknotes, stamps, bonds) was “extraordinarily successful”: 30-year average dividend > 40% p.a. Burland was president-manager and largest shareholder (1,077 shares); Earle and co. were dissentient shareholders (433 shares combined).
  2. To the credit of profit-and-loss stood $264,167 of undivided profits (no formal reserve fund), invested substantially in bank shares and mortgages — mostly in Burland's own name. After losing a Dominion Government contract, profits shrank; the minority sued (7 Dec 1897) attacking four things, and the four questions reached the Judicial Committee:
#ComplaintCourts belowPC answer
1Accumulating a reserve/undivided-profit fund instead of distributing dividends is ultra viresCh. Justice: distribute all; CA: only “fair & reasonable” reserveIntra vires purely internal management
2Investment of the fund, in Burland's own nameCA: sweeping injunctionsImprudent, not unlawful injunctions discharged

Issues: (i) May a company lawfully retain undivided profits as a reserve instead of distributing all? (ii) May it invest them (even in the name of its president)? (iii) Is a director who bought assets on his own account and resold them to the company accountable for the profit as a “trustee”? (iv) Was Burland within the ambiguous salary resolution?
Minority shareholders (Earle etc.)
  • Companies paying dividends > 40% have no business hoarding $264,167 — forced distribution; the accumulation is ultra vires or at least reviewable.
  • Investing in Burland's personal name invites misappropriation — restrain it.
  • Burland bought the lithographic assets intending to resell to the company, at whose instance both courts found the resale was made, without disclosing his purchase price — a classic fiduciary secret profit.
  • The 1888 “5% on your stock” resolution cannot include the president — otherwise he wrote himself the biggest cheque.
Burland / the company
  • Retention and investment of profits are “questions of internal management which the shareholders must decide for themselves” — courts have “no jurisdiction to control or review their decision”.
  • Investments were disclosed year after year in balance-sheets placed before general meetings — bank shares/mortgages, permanent in character, no “trafficking or speculation”.
  • “No evidence whatever of any commission or mandate to Burland to purchase on behalf of the company, or that he was in any sense a trustee for the company of the purchased property.” His resale intention was his own — “which he was at liberty to carry out or abandon at his own will”.
  • The salary increment is a question of construction of the resolution only.

Decision — Lord Davey delivers the Judicial Committee's advice

Lord Davey first restated three elementary principles: (1) “the court will not interfere with the internal management of companies acting within their powers, and in fact has no jurisdiction to do so”; (2) for a wrong done to the company the action belongs prima facie to the company itself (Foss v. Harbottle), the minority action being a mere procedural exception confined to acts fraudulent or ultra vires — e.g., majority appropriating to themselves the company's property (as in Menier v. Hooper's Telegraph Works); mere informalities remediable by majority cannot be sued on (Macdougall v. Gardiner); (3) a shareholder may vote with personal interest unless the company's regulations forbid it (North-West Transportation Co. v. Beatty).

Applying them: (1) Reserve fund: “No principle compels a joint stock company, while a going concern, to divide the whole of its profits… These are questions for the shareholders to decide” — accumulation and investment intra vires; therefore also no basis for the sweeping injunctions. (2) Investments in Burland's own name: “unwise and imprudent”, but known to fellow directors for years; not ultra vires; he must of course account for company money in his hands (accounts directed). (3) Lithographic profit: courts below misdirected the relief — at most the company might once have sought rescission, not forced profit-sharing; quoting Lord Cairns L.C. in Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co.: a purchaser for himself, though intending resale, is “perfectly free to do with the [asset] whatever they liked… and to sell it how and to whom and for what price they liked” — accounting claim unsupportable. A man of “more refined self-respect” might have offered the company the benefit, “but their Lordships have not to decide questions of that character. The sole question is whether he was under any legal obligation to do so.” (4) Salaries: the 1888 resolution is ambiguous; “staff” cannot sensibly be grade-ranked by stockholding; Burland, its architect and chief beneficiary, must bear construction contra proferentem — he repays ≈ $53,000; J.H. Burland, drawing the same salary continuously despite the office-shuffle, keeps it.

Principles evolved: (a) Non-interference doctrine — intra vires internal decisions (dividend retention, investment) are unreviewable by courts; (b) a director's own-account purchase + later resale to the company is not trust property; accountability requires a mandate, trust or fiduciary relation at the moment of purchase (contrast Regal (Hastings), where the asset was bought through the fiduciary role); (c) self-drafted, self-benefiting resolutions are construed contra proferentem; (d) shareholders may vote their own interest. Read together with Case 22: motive is irrelevant only when the opportunity came through the office; motive-independent profit is fine only when the asset was never the company's.

Case No. 21 · Standard of care, skill & diligence

In re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. Ltd.

(1925) Ch. 407 · Court of Appeal · Romer J. (below), confirmed in appeal
C.A. · 1925

Material facts

  1. City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. went into liquidation. A searching investigation revealed that funds the company should have possessed were short by over £12,00,000 (one million two hundred thousand pounds) — from bad investments, bad debts and misappropriation.
  2. The executives had left the management almost entirely in the hands of its chairman/MD, “B” (Bevan), who secured loans to himself and to firms in which he was a partner; he was convicted of fraud on the company.
  3. The Official Receiver brought disqualification/negligence proceedings against the other directors, alleging that had they exercised ordinary attention, they would have discovered the fraud and prevented the loss.
  4. Crucial fact: the company's articles contained an exemption clause — directors were to be liable only for “gross negligence”.
Visual C — one fraudulent chairman + a trusting board + an article-shield = the modern reform trigger.
Issues: (i) Were the other directors negligent in leaving the company's affairs to the Chairman's unsupervised hands? (ii) Even if so, was their conduct “gross negligence” within the articles' exemption? (iii) What is the true measure of a director's duty of care, skill and diligence and his right to delegate?
Official Receiver (liquidator)
  • The directors abdicated supervision: cheques signed, investments and loans approved without scrutiny; the loans to “B” and his firms should have put the Board on inquiry at once.
  • Reasonable attention to the accounts and to Board business would have uncovered the pattern years before the crash — the loss is therefore attributable to them.
  • An insurance company handles the public's money: the standard must be higher than indulgent.
Defendant directors
  • No dishonesty is alleged — at most honest errors of judgment; the law does not require a director to show “all possible care or to act with best care”.
  • Business cannot run if directors must personally verify every transaction: delegation to apparently trustworthy, competent officials is part of ordinary management
  • The articles contractually cap liability at gross negligence — and the evidence at most shows ordinary negligence, if anything.
  • The Chairman was of ostensibly unblemished reputation; suspicion is a creature of hindsight.

Decision — Romer J.'s classic formulation (affirmed on appeal): DIRECTORS EXONERATED

Though the case was decided adversely to the Official Receiver upon the articles' gross-negligence clause, the enduring gift is Romer J.'s three propositions on the common-law standard (drawn from the celebrated passage of the judgment reproduced in every standard work on the subject):

  1. Skill is subjective. “A director need not exhibit in the performance of his duties a greater degree of skill than may reasonably be expected from a person of his knowledge and experience.” He does not owe the company the duty to take all possible care, or to act with best care; he must show only what an ordinary man might be expected to show in the same circumstances on his own behalf — directors are not liable for mere errors of judgment. The measure varies with the nature of the company's business and the internal distribution of work.
  2. Duties are intermittent. A director exercises his office at periodic Board meetings; he is not bound to attend every meeting — though he ought to attend whenever he reasonably can. Non-attendance alone is not negligence.
  3. Delegation is permissible. Having regard to the exigencies of business, duties may properly be left to some other official; absent grounds for suspicion, a director is justified in trusting that official to perform them honestly. “Total abrogation” of responsibility would still be a breach — the line lies between proper delegation and abandonment.
Principle evolved: the subjective standard of care + qualified right of delegation. Two statutory overturnings to note for exams: (i) the exemption-clause device used in this very case to exonerate the directors was rendered void by S.201 Companies Act 1956 (the 2013 Act has no exemption route; only S.463 judicial relief for honest & reasonable conduct); (ii) the low subjective bar of this case was progressively hardened (Dorchester Finance v. Stebbing) and finally replaced in India by the statutory objective duty of S.166(3). “The case is the fossil and S.166(3) is the living law — write both.

Case No. 22 · The no-profit rule at its hardest

Regal (Hastings) Ltd. v. Gulliver

(1942) 1 All ER 378 : (1967) 2 A.C. 134 (H.L.) · House of Lords · Viscount Sankey, Lord Russell of Killowen, Lord Macmillan, Lord Wright, Lord Porter
HOUSE OF LORDS · 1942

Material facts

  1. Regal (Hastings) Ltd., owner of a profitable freehold cinema at Hastings, schemed in 1935 to acquire leases of two more cinemas (Elite, Hastings; De Luxe, St. Leonards) through a wholly-owned subsidiary, Hastings Amalgamated Cinemas Ltd. (registered 26 Sept 1935, capital £5,000) — Regal to hold the shares, so the whole benefit would flow to Regal's shareholders, and then sell all three.
  2. The landlord of the two cinemas demanded a rent guarantee by the directors; unwilling to give it, at the crucial 2 October 1935 joint board-meeting (Regal + Amalgamated minutes blurred) the directors changed the scheme: Regal would subscribe only £2,000 (all it could honestly manage) and the five directors would take 500 shares each, while chairman Gulliver would find outsiders (Seguliva A.G., South Downs Land Co., Miss Geering) for the remaining 500; the company solicitor Garton was invited to take 500 and consented at the Board's request.
  3. Within weeks, the asset-sale mutated into a share-sale: an outside group bought all shares of both companies (24 Oct 1935). The 3,000 Amalgamated shares not held by Regal fetched £3 16s. 1d. per share against £1 paid — a profit of £2 16s. 1d. each. Regal's former directors made £7,018 8s. 4d.; Garton made £1,402 1s. 8d.
  4. The incoming controllers caused Regal itself to sue its five former directors and the solicitor to recover that profit (plus Garton's bill of costs), with alternative claims of misfeasance/damages.
  5. Both the trial judge (Wrottesley J.) and the Court of Appeal held for the defendants: no fraud was pleaded or proved — the directors acted bona fide, in Regal's interest; the Court of Appeal added that once they decided bona fide that Regal could provide only £2,000, “their obligation to refrain from acquiring these shares came to an end”.
Visual D — scheme-switch, share-profit, and the House of Lords' split verdicts.
Issue: Are directors (and the solicitor) who made a profit by taking up shares in the company's subsidiary accountable to the company, although they acted honestly and the company itself could not have afforded the shares?
Regal (appellant, new board)
  • Fraud is expressly abandoned: the claim rests on strict fiduciary accountability. The directors got the shares “by reason, and only by reason of the fact that they were directors of Regal” and in the course of executing that office — Garton's own September letter shows the scheme was for Regal to hold “virtually the whole of the capital”.
  • They used exclusive knowledge and their position to alter the scheme and divert £3,000 of the investment to themselves; Regal's shareholders were deprived of exactly that portion.
  • Trust-principles (Keech v. Sandford (1726); Ex parte James) apply to agents acting in a fiduciary capacity, i.e., directors and solicitors; no proof of mala fides, loss or company's inability is needed.
Directors & solicitor (respondents)
  • They confessed the general rule but sought to “confess and avoid” it: on a true perspective of the facts they were under no equity to account.
  • Regal was honestly unable to put up more than £2,000; it would have been a breach of trust to overreach; the only way to save the scheme was for them to subscribe personally — the deal benefitted Regal, which got its lease and sold at full value.
  • Wrottesley J. asked the right question wrongly answered in the courts below: plaintiff must show the defendants ought and could have taken the shares for Regal, and that failure caused loss — neither was shown.
  • Gulliver: the shares were bought by, and proceeds went to, third parties (Seguliva, South Downs, Miss Geering) — he made no profit at all. Garton: he subscribed at the express invitation and with the full knowledge and consent of the Regal board.

Decision — House of Lords: FOUR DIRECTORS LIABLEGULLIVER & GARTON FREE

Viscount Sankey: the rule of equity — “no one who has duties of a fiduciary nature to perform is allowed to enter into engagements in which he has or can have a personal interest conflicting with the interests of those whom he is bound to protect” — applies to agents such as directors and solicitors, and its application “does not depend upon proof of mala fides”. Nor is it an answer that putting up the money themselves was the only financing route; that predicament does not take the fiduciary outside the rule: “They sought no authority from the company to do so… they are liable to account to the company.” Gulliver's evidence — that the subscribers paid and received their own money — “it is clear that he made no profits for which he is liable to account”; Garton took the shares “with the full knowledge and consent of Regal”, falling within the recognised exception.

Lord Russell of Killowen delivered the set-piece: the liability “in no way depends on fraud, or absence of bona fides; or upon such questions or considerations as whether the profit would or should otherwise have gone to the plaintiff, or whether the profiteer was under a duty to obtain the source of the profit for the plaintiff, or whether he took a risk or acted as he did for the benefit of the plaintiff, or whether the plaintiff has in fact been damaged or benefited by his action. The liability arises from the mere fact of a profit having, in the stated circumstances, been made. The profiteer, however honest and well-intentioned, cannot escape the risk of being called upon to account.” On the facts, the shares were acquired “by reason, and only by reason of the fact that they were directors of Regal, and in the course of their execution of that office”. Keech v. Sandford (trustee who took the market-lease for himself when renewal for the infant was refused — “this may seem hard… but it is very proper that the rule should be strictly pursued”) and Ex parte James (no purchase by trustees “however honest the circumstances; the general interests of justice requiring it to be destroyed in every instance”) show the uncompromising pedigree. One escape route Lord Russell expressly noted: the directors could have protected themselves by a resolution of the Regal shareholders in general meeting — obtainable then, though the company could not itself release them afterwards.

Order: appeal allowed against the four directors (Bibby (“Bobby”), Griffiths, Bassett, Bentley) — £7,018 8s. 4d. to be accounted; appeal dismissed against Gulliver and Garton with costs. “Windfall to the new owners” was, Lord Russell said, immaterial: the rule is of “vital importance”.

Principle evolved (the strict no-profit rule): a fiduciary who makes a profit by reason and in course of his office must account to the principal — irrespective of good faith, the company's own ability to take the opportunity, risk undertaken, or the company's not having suffered any loss. Defences: (i) informed consent of the principal (Garton) — ideally of the shareholders in general meeting; (ii) the profit genuinely never reached the fiduciary (Gulliver); (iii) opportunity wholly outside the fiduciary nexus. The company may ratify prospectively by disinterested shareholder approval. Now reflected in S.166(4) & (5) and the corporate-opportunity analyses under S.184/188.
Nuance for distinction essays: Regal punished honest men; hence the controversy and the later flexibilities (e.g., Boardman v. Phipps retaining strictness; Queensland Mines v. Hudson releasing after full disclosure). Contrast Burland (Case 20): there the asset was never the company's (purchased at public auction without mandate); in Regal the very opportunity belonged to the fiduciary relationship. And see Cooley (Case 23) for the managing-director variant where intent was dishonest.

Case No. 23 · Conflict of interest — the managing director who defected

Industrial Development Consultants Ltd. v. Cooley

(1972) 1 WLR 443 · Birmingham Assizes · Roskill J.
QB (ASSIZES) · 1972

Material facts

  1. Neville Cooley, an architect of distinction and former chief architect of the West Midlands Gas Board, joined Industrial Development Consultants Ltd. (IDC) as managing director in February 1968 (salary £6,000; no written service agreement ever executed — an important technicality he later exploited). He was hired precisely to win public-sector gas-board work.
  2. In 1968, pitching for IDC, he courted the Eastern Gas Board for a contract to design and construct four depots — and was rejected: the Board disapproved of IDC's corporate set-up.
  3. May–13 June 1969: EGB's new deputy chairman Mr. Smettom tentatively approached Cooley privately: the Board was back in the market (Letchworth depot worth ~£250,000 within a scheme of ~£1.7 million) — the timetable urgent, but Cooley must first be free of all obligations to IDC.
  4. 16 June 1969: Cooley told IDC's chairman Mr. Hicks a lie — that his health no longer allowed him to continue. Believing him seriously ill, Hicks released him from 1 August 1969. The story of ill health was found “untrue to the defendant's knowledge and thus dishonest… a pretext”.
  5. 6 August 1969: Cooley, trading as “Design Group for Industry”, signed to build the very four depots for the Eastern Gas Board — “substantially the same business which the plaintiffs had been trying to get in 1968”.
  6. On 2 December 1969 IDC sued for declarations that he was a trustee of the contract(s), an account of profits, alternatively damages for breach of duty.
Visual E — the four months in which duty and interest collided and interest won.
Issue: Is a managing director who used information received privately, then obtained his release by dishonest misrepresentation and took for himself a contract his company had sought, accountable to the company — although the company arguably could not itself have obtained that contract?
IDC (plaintiffs)
  • Cooley got the contract “as a result of work which he did whilst still the plaintiffs' managing director” — information about EGB's renewed urgency, the money, the timetable (13 June), and his weekend of preparatory documents (sent 17 June) were all deployed while in office (Regal test satisfied).
  • His knowledge of EGB and standing in the gas industry were “of value to the company and were acquired in the course of serving it” — corporate information, not his private asset.
  • At minimum an honest managing director would have disclosed the approach; instead he obtained release “by fair means or, if necessary, by foul” — the release itself is a fraudulent misrepresentation.
Mr. Davies for Cooley
  • Smettom consulted Cooley “not as managing director of IDC but in a private capacity”; information received qua private person carries no fiduciary debt to the company.
  • Two classes of contract (per Lord Blanesburgh in Bell v. Lever Bros): (i) contracts in which the company is concerned — account; (ii) contracts with third parties exclusively — no account unless company property or confidential information was abused. This was class (ii), and no company property/information was used.
  • IDC could never have got the work — Smettom and Lacey rejected IDC's set-up in principle; therefore no duty to account; at most a damages claim which must fail for want of loss. “The whole action is misconceived.”

Decision — Roskill J.: COOLEY LIABLE — ACCOUNT ORDERED

Roskill J. refused the capacity-splitting: whatever the supposed label on 13 June, the defendant was still MD when he learned what IDC “would have wished to possess”, and when he chose (by deceit of 16 June) to escape rather than disclose. Even accepting that a private approach can sometimes fall outside the duty, “the relevant information which the defendant obtained on June 13 and which he acted upon… vitally concerned the plaintiffs,” and his duty was to hand it to them.

On Lord Blanesburgh's dichotomy, Roskill J. found the case squarely within the third admitted class — misuse of the fiduciary position. He defined the true principle not by clocking how the information arrived, but by the conflict: “An over-riding principle of equity… a man must not be allowed to put himself in a position in which his fiduciary duty and his interests conflict… The variety of cases where that can happen is infinite.” That defendants “substitute[d] himself as an individual for the company… to which he owed a fiduciary duty.”

The conclusion was reached with less reluctance because the fraud on IDC enabled the breach: “a release was obtained by the defendant from a binding contractual obligation by the dishonest and untrue misrepresentations made to Mr. Hicks on June 16.” Order: account of all profits (made and to be made).

Fallback (ratio-protecting): if account were wrong, Roskill J. would have awarded damages for the lost chance: EGB might conceivably have been persuaded — the defendant's own evidence made a 10% probability; so 10% of the contract's value as damages. The irony was noted: it would have been his duty to persuade the Board to change its mind for IDC.

Principle evolved: (a) the no-conflict rule is not confined to assets or formally corporate approaches — information, opening and opportunity obtained while in office as a fiduciary suffice, however labelled “private”; (b) resignation cannot launder a fiduciary breach hatched in office — “the fiduciary obligation does not cease with resignation” as to opportunities matured out of it (a fortiori where resignation was procured by deceit); (c) the company's own improbable chance of winning is irrelevant to the account, going only to damages; (d) distinguishing Regal is unnecessary: Cooley shows the principle operating with dishonesty, Regal shows it operating without any — together they define the rule's full strictness.

★ Standard Chartered Bank v. Pakistan National Shipping Corpn. (2003) 1 All ER 173 (HL)

Fraud of a director = personal tort of deceit No contributory negligence in deceit

Facts: Mr. Mehra, MD of Oakprime Ltd, beneficiary of a letter of credit issued by Incobank (Vietnam) and confirmed by Standard Chartered Bank (SCB), London, for sale of Iranian bitumen — the credit required shipment by 25 October 1993. Loading was late; the shipowner (Pakistan National Shipping Corporation) and Mehra agreed to issue back-dated bills of lading (dated 25 Oct, actually issued 8 Nov; goods shipped 9 Nov). Oakprime presented the doctored documents to SCB under Mehra's covering letter, where he falsely stated compliance. SCB paid and sought reimbursement from Incobank claiming documents were presented in time — also false; Incobank rejected. SCB sued PNSC, Oakprime and Mehra personally in deceit.

Held (HL): Mehra personally liable. Cresswell J.'s finding affirmed: it is irrelevant that the misrepresentation was made “on behalf of” the company — “No one can escape liability for his fraud by saying that he was committing the fraud on behalf of someone else.” He was sued not for the company's tort but for his own; every element (knowledge of falsity — he procured the back-dating; intent that SCB rely; reliance; loss) was made out. The Hedley Byrne “assumption of responsibility” filter has no application to fraud. And contributory negligence is no defence to deceit: the Law Reform (Contributory Negligence) Act 1945 was never intended to excuse fraud, and the very purpose of the tort would be undone if the deceiver could blame the deceived for believing him (Lord Hoffmann).

Principle: the corporate veil does not immunise a director's own intentional wrongdoing; personal knowledge + intent + personal participation = personal liability in deceit, concurrent with the company's. Pairs with Sanjay Dutt below to draw the criminal/civil fork: fraud = personal; negligence-by-position ≠ liability.

★ Sanjay Dutt & Ors. v. State of Haryana & Anr., SC, 2 January 2025 [2025 INSC 34]

No vicarious criminal liability without statute Newest suggested case (2025 syllabus)

Facts: A complaint under the Punjab Land Preservation Act, 1900 (Ss.4/19) alleged illegal uprooting of 250+ trees at a site in Gurugram developed by Tata Realty & Infrastructure Ltd./Tata Housing. Three senior officials — including MD Sanjay Dutt — were arrayed as accused by designation alone; the company itself was not made an accused. The Special Environment Court took cognizance; the Punjab & Haryana High Court refused to quash. Appeal to the Supreme Court.

Held (SC): complaint quashed. The 1900 Act contains no vicarious-liability provision; criminal liability thereunder is individual. Restating the doctrine of Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI (2015) 4 SCC 609 and Sharad Kumar Sanghi v. Sangita Rane: there is “no presumption that every officer of a company knows about the transaction”. Directors may be held criminally liable only where (i) the company is itself liable, (ii) the statute specifically creates vicarious liability of directors, (iii) specific, substantiated averments ascribe a distinct role to the director, (iv) his personal conduct directly connects to the wrongdoing and (v) mens rea exists. “Mere authorization of an act at the behest of the company or the exercise of a supervisory role… is not enough.” The allegations were “absolutely vague”.

Principle: office ≠ guilt. In corporate criminal law, liability travels along express statutory channels (e.g., S.141 NI Act) or proven personal involvement — never by bare designation. This is the criminal-side counterpart of the civil agencies/fiduciary analysis in the five prescribed cases, and the post-Satyam S.149(12) statutory shield for independent/NEDs embodies the same philosophy.

◆ Two doctrines invoked throughout the casebook (learn their one-liners)

  • Keech v. Sandford (1726) Sel. Cas. Ch. 61 — trustee took renewal of a market-lease for himself after the lessor refused to renew for the infant beneficiary: ordered to assign to the infant. “This may seem hard, that the trustee is the only person of all mankind who might not have the lease: but it is very proper that the rule should be strictly pursued.” — the ancestor authority of Regal's strictness.
  • Cook v. Deeks [1916] 1 AC 554 (PC) — three directors took a railway-construction contract in their own names and then used their majority votes to declare the company “not interested”: held, they must account; a resolution by wrongdoers to ratify their own breach is a fraud on the minority and void (Lord Buckmaster: “he must not without the knowledge and sanction of the company appropriate to himself a business opportunity which the company is entitled to pursue”). — use with Regal to show the ratification boundary: disinterested majority may sanction (Garton), wrongdoers' own votes may not (Cook).

PYQ 1 · DU Dec 2024 · Q.4A · 20 marks

PROBLEM — “The directors of ABC Ltd decided to buy a property which the company wanted but could not afford; later they sold it at a profit, without disclosing to or taking approval of the company. They also intended to sell it back to the company someday, and property values had risen sharply. No formal board resolution authorised the purchase. Advise/decide liability.”

Issue-spotting target: corporate opportunity doctrine · fiduciary accountability under Regal + Cooley · S.166(2)/(4)/(5) · ratification (Cook v. Deeks) · company's inability to buy (irrelevant per Regal) · remedy: account of profits / constructive trust; S.166(7) penalty; S.184 disclosure default.

Issue: Are directors who personally purchased property the company had identified but could not then afford, and resold it at profit without disclosure or sanction, liable to the company though they claim good faith, no loss to the company, and an intention ultimately to resell to it?

Rule: (1) Directors stand in a fiduciary position toward the company (Great Eastern Ry. v. Turner; S.166(2)); utmost good faith is expected (Bank of Poona Ltd. v. Narayandas, AIR 1961 Bom 252). (2) The rule of equity “which insists on those, who by use of a fiduciary position make a profit, being liable to account for that profit, in no way depends on fraud, or absence of bona fides;… or whether the profit would or should otherwise have gone to the plaintiff… or whether he took a risk… or whether the plaintiff has in fact been damaged or benefited by his action. The liability arises from the mere fact of a profit having, in the stated circumstances, been made” — Lord Russell of Killowen in Regal (Hastings) Ltd. v. Gulliver (1942) 1 All ER 378 (HL). (3) IDC v. Cooley (1972) 1 WLR 443 extends this to information/opportunity acquired while in office even where the company itself may not have succeeded, and condemns disguised self-substitution; “a man must not be allowed to put himself in a position in which his fiduciary duty and his interests conflict”. (4) Statutorily, S.166(4) (no conflict situation), S.166(5) (no undue gain — pay “an amount equal to that gain”), S.166(7) (fine ₹1–5 lakh), S.184 (disclosure of interest), and S.4 of Schedule IV for independent directors, all converge. (5) Ratification is possible only by the company in general meeting through disinterested votes; wrongdoers cannot vote for self-absolution (Cook v. Deeks [1916] 1 AC 554; contrast Garton in Regal who had board-level consent).

Application: (i) The property was squarely within the company's line of business and prior intention — information about its availability came to the directors qua directors; the “we meant well / company couldn't pay” defence is precisely the one rejected in Regal where “it would have been a breach of trust for the directors to invest more… and the only way to finance the matter was for them to advance the money themselves” — still “it is impossible to maintain that [this fact]… brought the respondents outside the general rule.” (ii) The company's inability to fund the purchase is irrelevant to the account, as Regal and Cooley both hold; it was the directors' job at most to recommend the opportunity to the shareholders in general meeting for informed consent — the Romer/Russell safety-valve they never used. (iii) The absence of a formal board resolution and the absence of disclosure compound what was already liable-making; absence of a resolution is no defence — it is an omission of the very S.184 machinery. (iv) Intent to “sell back to the company someday” is the same unverifiable mental reservation Lord Davey surplus-refused to enforce in Burland — but here, unlike Burland v. Earle, the opportunity itself belonged to the company (Burland bought at a public auction without mandate; these directors intercepted an identified corporate target), so Burland cannot save them. (v) Rising property values prove yet more profit now attached to a fiduciary position; equity follows the asset: a constructive trust lies over it or its proceeds. (vi) Nor can self-ratification by their own votes validate the transaction — fraud on the minority (Cook v. Deeks).

Conclusion: The directors are liable to account to the company for the entire profit (property or proceeds traced via constructive trust), face S.166(7) fines (₹1–5 lakh each); the undisclosed interested transaction is voidable at the company's option (Ss.184/188); and no defence of good faith, company inability, or future re-sale intention avails. The single compliant path — disclosure + informed general-meeting consent — was open and unused.

Examiner's ear: deploy the Lord Russell sentence verbatim once; contrast Burland properly (asset never the company's there); name the remedy in a separate concluding line. That trio alone typically earns the “well-structured answer” band.
PYQ 2 · DU Dec 2024 · Q.4B · 20 marks

“Discuss the role and importance of Independent Directors in a company. Explain the statutory provisions relating to their appointment, qualifications, tenure and liability under the Companies Act, 2013.”

Marking spine (from checklist's answer guide): definition (2(47)/149(6)) → numerical mandate (149(4) + Rule 4) → 149(6) conditions → declaration (149(7)) → data bank (150) → tenure (149(10)-(11), 149(13)) → remuneration (149(9)/197(5)) → Schedule IV roles → committees (177/178/135) → liability cap (149(12)) → SC & Satyam valediction.

Concept & definition: Under S.2(47), an independent director is “an independent director referred to in sub-section (6) of section 149” — literally, a director with no material pecuniary relationship with the company, introduced to bring external, objective judgement into promoter-heavy boards and to align Indian boards with international capital-market standards.

Mandate: S.149(4) requires every listed public company to have at least one-third independent directors (fractions rounded up); Central Government-prescribed public companies (paid-up capital ≥ ₹10 cr / turnover ≥ ₹100 cr / loans-debentures-deposits ≥ ₹50 cr) need at least 2; default punishable under S.172 (₹50,000–5,00,000).

Eligibility code, S.149(6): a director other than MD/WTD/nominee — (a) person of integrity + expertise + experience in Board's opinion; (b) not a promoter of company/H/S/A; (c) no pecuniary relationship during two preceding financial years/current year; (d) relatives' dealings below 2% of turnover/income or ₹50 lakh; (e) neither he nor relatives was KMP/employee in preceding 3 years, nor partner/employee of auditors/CS/cost-auditors or consulting firms at ≥10% turnover, nor holds ≥2% voting power, nor CEO/director of an NPO receiving ≥25% receipts from the company; (f) other prescribed qualifications. Annual declaration of independence must sit on Board record (S.149(7)).

Selection & appointment: from a notified data bank (S.150(1)) — due diligence is the company's; appointment by general meeting, the notice's explanatory statement justifying the choice (S.150(2)); identified by the NRC (S.178); inducted & evaluated per Schedule IV.

Tenure & remuneration: up to five consecutive years; second five-year term only by special resolution with disclosure in the Board's report (S.149(10)); maximum two consecutive terms, then a three-year cooling-off with zero association (S.149(11)); exempt from rotation (S.149(13)). No stock options; only sitting fees, expense reimbursement and members-approved profit-commission (S.149(9), S.197(5)).

Functions (Schedule IV): bring independent judgement to strategy/risk/resources; scrutinise management performance and financial information; safeguard all stakeholders, especially minority shareholders; arbitrate management-shareholder conflict; insist on integrity of controls; meet separately as independent directors at least annually; submit to performance evaluation. They anchor the Audit Committee (S.177 — majority, chair), NRC (S.178 — majority, chair) and sit on the CSR Committee (S.135) and vigil mechanism oversight.

Liability (the Satyam safety-valve): S.149(12) — an independent/NED is liable only for company acts/omissions occurring (i) with his knowledge (attributable through Board processes), (ii) with consent/connivance, or (iii) where he did not act diligently. This caps the City-Equitable fear-factor and preserves willingness to serve — while Sanjay Dutt v. State of Haryana (SC, 2025) reiterates that criminal liability never attaches by designation alone but only by specific statute, specific allegations and proven personal involvement.

Appraisal: Independent directors are the board's institutional conscience — the check whose abdication explains Satyam (eminent IDs certified fiction for years) and whose empowerment marks the 2013 Act. The Supreme Court in TCS v. Cyrus Investments (2021) 9 SCC 449 underscored their centrality to listed-company governance. Their effective performance closes the entire answer: independence of mind, not merely of status, is what the statute commands.

PYQ 3 · Short Note (Q.8 type) · 8/10 marks

“Write a short note on Director's Identification Number (DIN) under Sections 153–159 of the Companies Act, 2013.”

Concept & object: DIN is a unique identity number allotted by the Central Government to every individual who is or intends to become a director. Its purpose is regulatory traceability: the cap on directorships (S.165 — 20 companies, of which ≤ 10 public) and regulators' ability to track a person's directorial record are unenforceable without a unique key; DIN is that key. No person can be appointed director without a DIN — S.152(3); non-compliance is itself a S.164 disqualification.

  • S.153 — application to the Central Government in prescribed form/ manner/ fee (e-filing; Form DIR-3).
  • S.154 — allotment within one month.
  • S.155 — prohibition: once allotted, no individual may apply for or possess another DIN — one person, one identity.
  • S.156 — every existing director must intimate his DIN to each company of which he is a director within one month.
  • S.157 — the company must furnish the DIN to the Registrar within 15 days; default → company & officer in default: fine ₹25,000–₹1,00,000 (S.157(2)).
  • S.158 — DIN must be quoted on every return/information/particulars referring to the director — the filing system thus becomes fully traceable.
  • S.159 — penalty for contravening Ss.152, 155, 156: imprisonment up to six months or fine up to ₹50,000, with additional daily fine for continuing default.

Significance: DIN underpins appointment discipline (S.152(3)-(4)), disqualification monitoring (S.164), the S.165 ceiling, and director-KYC (DIR-3-KYC annually). In the governance scheme of Topic 5, it is the small provision with systemic effect: identity, accountability and traceability of every “directing mind”.

PYQ 4 · Short Note (Q.8 type) · 8/10 marks

“Write a short note on ‘Independent Director’ under Section 2(47) of the Companies Act, 2013.”

Definition: S.2(47): an independent director is one “referred to in sub-section (6) of section 149” — substantively, a non-executive director devoid of pecuniary/material ties with the company, its promoters and management, who owes the board objective, outsider judgement.

Who qualifies (S.149(6)): a director other than MD/WTD/nominee — of integrity, relevant expertise and experience (Board's opinion); not a promoter of the company or its holding/subsidiary/associate; with no pecuniary relationship in the two preceding financial years and the current year; relatives' transactions within the 2% / ₹50-lakh ceiling; neither he nor relatives was KMP/employee in the last 3 years or connected with auditors/consultants at ≥ 10% level, or holds ≥ 2% voting power, or heads a dependent NPO; and prescribed additional qualifications.

Framework: listed companies — ⅓ of the Board (S.149(4)); annual declaration of independence (S.149(7)); governed by the Schedule IV Code (S.149(8)); selection from the notified data bank with company due diligence and general-meeting approval (S.150); tenure of 5 years, one renewal by special resolution, 3-year cooling-off (S.149(10)-(11)); rotation-free (S.149(13)); no stock options — only sitting fee/reimbursement/approved commission (S.149(9)).

Protection & importance: liability confined by S.149(12) to knowing/consenting/undiligent participation. Independent directors give the board its checks-and-balances core — chairing audit and nomination committees, guarding minority interests (their failure in Satyam is the cautionary tale; TCS v. Cyrus Investments (2021) affirms their structural centrality). A one-line closer that examiners reward: “independently-minded generalists in the boardroom; the institutional answer to the agency problem.”

PYQ 5 · Long Answer · 20 marks

“Duties of directors were fashioned by the common law and are now codified. Explain the statutory duties of directors under Section 166 of the Companies Act, 2013, with the help of decided cases.”

Introduction: Directors wield “immense power which must be regulated not only for the public good, but also for the protection of those whose investments are involved”. The doctrinal trilogy — fiduciary loyalty, reasonable care, controlled delegation — was hammered out in the cases (Percival; Burland; City Equitable; Regal; Cooley). The 2013 Act codified this inheritance in S.166. A director who contravenes is liable to a fine of ₹1–5 lakh (S.166(7)), besides civil remedies.

(1) Duty to act within the articles — S.166(1): articles are the director's charter; action beyond them is challengeable as ultra vires and the default of a self-drafted ambiguity suffers contra proferentem reading (Burland v. Earle, salary-resolution point).

(2) Duty of good faith toward the company and all stakeholders — S.166(2): the fiduciary heart. Directors are “trustees” of company funds and “agents” of the company (Lord Selborne, Great Eastern Ry. v. Turner, quoted in Percival v. Wright), owing duty to the company as a whole and the members as a body — not to individual shareholders (Percival v. Wright (1902) 2 Ch 421). “Greatest good faith” is demanded (Bank of Poona v. Narayandas); 2013 text expands beneficiaries to employees, community and environment.

(3) Duty of due and reasonable care, skill, diligence and independent judgment — S.166(3): the negligence head. The classical subjective standard (Re City Equitable Fire Insurance Co. (1925) Ch 407 per Romer J. — no greater skill than reasonably expected of one of his knowledge and experience; intermittent duties; delegation justified absent suspicion) has been replaced by the statutory floor of reasonable care; exemption articles are dead (cf. S.201 of 1956 Act), leaving relief only via S.463 for honest-and-reasonable conduct. Failure of oversight amounting to fabricated figures/false disclosure is judicially condemned as a “failure of corporate governance” (N. Narayanan v. SEBI (2013) 12 SCC 152).

(4) Duty to avoid conflict of interest — S.166(4): the no-conflict rule. In Regal (Hastings) Ltd. v. Gulliver (1942) 1 All ER 378 (HL), directors who bona fide took subsidiary shares were ordered to account: the liability “in no way depends on fraud or absence of bona fides… [it] arises from the mere fact of a profit having, in the stated circumstances, been made” (Lord Russell of Killowen). In IDC v. Cooley (1972) 1 WLR 443, the MD who obtained release by feigned illness and took the gas-board contract personally was held accountable: the over-riding equity principle is that “a man must not be allowed to put himself in a position in which his fiduciary duty and his interests conflict”.

(5) Duty against undue gain — S.166(5): the statutory secret-profit bar; disgorgement of “an amount equal to that gain” — the codified echo of Regal and Cooley, cutting the contrary Burland point at its joint: where the opportunity/asset belonged to the company or arose through office, personal profit is disgorged; where it genuinely did not (own-account purchase without mandate — Burland v. Earle), it is not.

(6) Duty not to assign office — S.166(6): delegation beyond what the Act/articles or business exigency permits is void; delegation is permissible control, abdication is breach (Joint Stock Discount Co. v. Brown; City Equitable's qualified permission under suspicion-absence has hardened into the S.166(3) standard).

Conclusion: S.166 is the codification, not the creation, of fiduciary discipline: the five DU cases supply its grammar — Percival fixes to whom, City Equitable the care, Regal/Cooley the loyalty, Burland the boundary. Good answers name all five, quote one line from each, and tie every limb to a numbered sub-section.

PYQ 6 · Long Answer · 20 marks

“Discuss the civil and criminal liabilities of directors under the Companies Act, 2013. Are directors automatically liable for corporate offences? Refer to recent authority.”

Civil liability — to the company: (i) breach of fiduciary duty → account/disgorgement (Regal; Cooley; S.166(4)-(5)); (ii) negligence → compensation (City Equitable; S.166(3)); (iii) ultra vires misapplication and (iv) mala fide acts → restoration and compensation; with the S.463 judicial relief for honest-and-reasonable conduct and S.197(13) D&O insurance permissibility.

Civil liability — to third parties: ordinarily shielded by the Salomon principle; exceptions — personal guarantees; misstatement in prospectus (Ss.34-35 compensation); own fraud in deceit (Standard Chartered Bank v. PNSC (2003) — “no one can escape liability for his fraud by saying that he was committing the fraud on behalf of someone else”); winding-up misfeasance (Ss.336–339).

Criminal liability — the statutory catalogue: S.447 fraud (6 months–10 years; public-interest cases min 3 years; fine up to 3× amount); S.448-449 false statements/evidence; S.34 prospectus; S.159 DIN defaults (≤ 6 months' imprisonment/fine); S.166(7) duty contravention; S.172 composition defaults; SFIO obstruction (S.217); winding-up offences (Ss.336-339). Post-2020 decriminalisation converts several defaults into penal fines, but fraud-related provisions retain full teeth.

No vicarious liability by office alone: The bedrock doctrine — directors are not criminally liable for corporate offences automatically. Per Sanjay Dutt v. State of Haryana (SC, 2 Jan 2025, 2025 INSC 34) (the 2025-syllabus suggested case under Topic 5): there must exist (1) liability of the company, (2) a specific statutory provision creating directors' vicarious liability (e.g., S.141, NI Act), (3) specific and substantiated averments attributing a defined role, (4) direct personal involvement connecting conduct to the company's liability, and (5) mens rea. “Mere authorization… or the exercise of a supervisory role… is not enough”; there is “no presumption that every officer of a company knows about the transaction” (Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI (2015) 4 SCC 609). The complaint there — naked accusations against Tata officials under the Punjab Land Preservation Act — was quashed.

The independent-director shield: S.149(12) repeats the philosophy in civil-regulatory form: independent and non-executive directors answer only for knowing/consenting or non-diligent participation attributable through Board processes — the post-Satyam assurance that outside directors will not be trapped by designation.

Conclusion: civil liability follows the duty breached; criminal liability follows the statute breached plus personal involvement; neither follows from bare office (voir Regal for civil strictness, Sanjay Dutt for criminal restraint). The two tracks can run concurrently on the same facts — Satyam's prosecutions (S.447/IPC/CBI convictions, 2015) alongside SEBI's ₹1,848.93-crore disgorgement (2014) are the standard illustration.

PYQ 7 · Short Note / Essay · 8–20 marks

“Directors are trustees for the company, not for individual shareholders.” — Examine the proposition with reference to Percival v. Wright. Are there exceptions?

The proposition stated and applied: In Percival v. Wright (1902) 2 Ch 421, shareholders who had themselves approached the company secretary and named their price (after taking an independent valuation) sought to set aside the sale of their shares to the chairman and two directors, because the Board had not disclosed abortive takeover talks with one Holden that implied a higher per-share value. Swinfen Eady J. dismissed the action: the trusteeship that equity imposes runs to the company — “trustees of the company's money and property — agents in the transactions which they enter into on behalf of the company” (Lord Selborne in Great Eastern Ry. v. Turner, adopted). No line was drawn at takeover negotiations: a shareholder “is fixed with knowledge of all the directors' powers”, and compelling disclosure would put directors in a “most invidious position”, since premature revelation could itself injure the company. No unfair dealing existed: the sellers set the terms; the buyers never solicited.

Doctrinal location: fiduciary duty → to the company as a whole (and the members as a body — now textually S.166(2)'s “members as a whole”); the judgment was cited with approval in India (Ramaswamy Iyer v. Brahamayya) and reaffirmed in Peskin v. Anderson (2000): “ordinarily the directors are not agents or trustees of members or shareholders and owe no fiduciary duties to them.”

Exceptions (mark-fetchers): (i) where a special trust/confidence was reposed in the director as agent of the shareholder (Allen v. Hyatt (1914) — directors soliciting share-options to earn secret commissions accountable); (ii) close family-company relationships invoking legitimate expectations (Coleman v. Myers (NZ, 1977) — nondisclosing takeover compelled by dominant relatives breached duty to sellers); (iii) where the sale-negotiations are concluded/imminent contract or disclosure is statutorily compelled (insider-trading regime — SEBI (PIT) Regulations, 2015, making uninformed insider dealing itself unlawful); (iv) fraud or active concealment.

Evaluation: the rule balances two equities — the seller's information-deficit vs the company's need for confidential negotiation. Modern securities law answers at the market-place end (insider-trading liability), leaving the corporate-law baseline intact: Percival speaks for internal relations; insider-trading law polices the trade.

PYQ 8 · Essay / Case Study · 20 marks

“The Satyam episode is described as the failure of corporate governance in India.” Discuss the facts, the failures of the Board and auditors, and the legislative response contained in the Companies Act, 2013.

Facts in brief: On 7 January 2009, B. Ramalinga Raju, founder-chairman of Satyam Computer Services Ltd (India's 4th-largest IT firm), confessed to a ₹7,136-crore fraud: ₹5,040 crore of fictitious cash/bank balances, ₹376 crore of fictitious accrued interest, ₹1,230 crore of understated liabilities and ₹490 crore of overstated debtors; Q2 FY09 revenue was shown as ₹2,700 crore against the true ₹2,112 crore, margins ₹649 crore against ₹61 crore. The fraud ran on 7,561 fictitious invoices, forged bank statements/FDRs shown to auditors, ~13,000 ghost employees, and a promoter pledge-spiral which the aborted $1.6 bn Maytas related-party acquisition (16 Dec 2008 – “the last attempt to fill the fictitious assets with real ones”) detonated.

The governance failures: (1) Board/Independent Directors — eminent names (an ISB dean among them) approved the Maytas deal and never demanded independent bank confirmations; supervisory abdication recalling City Equitable; under S.166(3) this is precisely a “failure of corporate governance” (N. Narayanan phrase). (2) Auditors — Price Waterhouse relied on company-supplied confirmations; SEBI (2018) barred the network for two years and ordered ₹13.09 crore disgorgement (SAT later modified). (3) Promoter capture — a small pledged holding inflating results to survive margin calls. (4) Institutional passivity — analyst/ investor deference to audited fictions; (5) legal vacuum of the 1956 Act — no codified duties, no compulsive audit rotation, no statutory vigil mechanism, no fraud-specific offence.

State response and salvage: the Central Government superseded the entire board and installed an interim board (S. 397/408 of the 1956 Act; recorded in the Taxmann material as Union of India v. Satyam Computer Services Ltd. [2009] 96 SCL 367 (CLB) and [2010] 97 SCL 49 (CLB)); auction delivered the company to Tech Mahindra (Apr 2009, 31% at ₹58/share); CBI prosecution ended in conviction of Raju and nine others on 9 April 2015 (7 years' RI + ₹5.5 cr fine); SEBI's 2014 order demanded ₹1,848.93 cr disgorgement with 12% interest plus 14-year market bans; ED attached assets under PMLA; SEC/PCAOB and US class actions followed; SFIO obtained statutory status thereafter.

Legislative response — Companies Act, 2013 (map each limb): codified duties (S.166); independent directors with real teeth and a limited-liability shield (SS.149(4)-(12), S.150, Sch. IV); DIN identity (SS.152-160); Directors' Responsibility Statement (S.134(5)); audit rotation (S.139(2)); fraud reporting by auditors (S.143(12)); vigil mechanism (S.177(9)-(10)); audit/nomination committees manned by independents (Ss.177-178); the fraud offence (S.447); SFIO (Ss.211-212, 217); SEBI LODR 2015 tightening listed boards.

Conclusion: Satyam was not a failure of law so much as of vigilance at every checkpoint the law had provided; the 2013 Act rebuilt those checkpoints. For a company lawyer, Topic 5 — duties, appointment discipline, independent directors, DIN, liability — is Satyam's institutional memorial; and the examiner who asks for “corporate governance” is really asking you to narrate this autopsy and its remedy.
