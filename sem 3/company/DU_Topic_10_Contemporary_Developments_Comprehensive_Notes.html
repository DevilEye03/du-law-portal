01 · Reading map, scope and source discipline

Contemporary company law asks four connected questions: How should corporate wealth be used? Who answers for wrongdoing? How can a business be reorganised or rescued? And what does sustainable governance require?

This guide covers DU Topic 10: Contemporary Developments in the uploaded 2025 Company Law syllabus: (a) CSR and corporate criminal liability; (b) compromises, arrangements and amalgamations; (c) IBC; and (d) ESG. It explains the prescribed statutory core—section 135, the amended CSR Rules and Schedule VII; Chapter XV, especially sections 230–232; and the key IBC concepts—with worked problems and detailed model answers.

02 · CSR: meaning, need and the corporate-responsibility debate

Meaning: broad concept and narrower statutory category

Corporate social responsibility, in its broad sense, concerns the responsibilities of an enterprise towards people, communities and the environment affected by its business. It includes questions of ethical conduct, stakeholder well-being, sustainable operations and social contribution. Statutory CSR under section 135, however, is a specific legal framework applying to qualifying companies, with prescribed activities, governance, expenditure, treatment of unspent money and disclosure.

Thus an ethical business practice may be socially responsible without being expenditure that can be counted towards the statutory two-per-cent obligation. Paying legally required wages, installing legally mandated pollution-control equipment, or improving employee safety to satisfy statutory duties cannot simply be relabelled qualifying CSR expenditure.

Why CSR is needed

  1. External costs of enterprise. Business can generate pollution, resource depletion, displacement and infrastructure pressure. Market prices do not always reflect those costs. Social responsibility asks the enterprise to recognise effects beyond its own balance sheet.
  2. Unequal distribution of economic gains. Corporate growth can coexist with weak access to education, health, skills and livelihoods. Carefully designed projects can address capability gaps rather than offer only occasional donations.
  3. Stakeholder dependence. Employees, suppliers, communities and consumers make enterprise possible. Long-term corporate success depends on the resilience of those relationships.
  4. Institutional capacity. Companies can contribute finance, project-management capability, technology and partnerships. This does not mean that companies replace Government’s responsibilities or gain authority to dictate public policy through philanthropy.
  5. Trust and legitimacy. Transparency and credible social performance support public confidence. A publicity campaign without results may instead deepen distrust.
  6. Long-term business resilience. Skills, health, local infrastructure and ecological stability can improve the conditions in which business operates. Nevertheless, a project whose real nature is product marketing remains subject to the CSR exclusion.
  7. Accountability for deployment of corporate resources. Mandatory governance and disclosure reduce the risk that “charity” becomes a promoter’s personal preference, a related-party benefit or disguised diversion.

Arguments for and against mandatory spending

Argument Strength Qualification
Mandatory CSR mobilises corporate resources for public purposes Predictable minimum commitment by qualifying companies Amount spent does not prove social impact
It embeds responsibility at board level Policy, oversight and reporting make responsibility institutional Board approval can become a formality unless monitoring is credible
Companies possess implementation expertise Project skills may improve delivery Commercial expertise does not automatically equal community-development expertise
CSR is an indirect additional tax Mandatory expenditure reduces managerial discretion Unlike a general tax, the scheme leaves structured choices of lawful projects and implementation
It can distract from core business Poorly governed projects may waste resources Strategic social programmes can be professionally managed without substituting marketing for CSR
It risks “charity instead of compliance” Real danger of reputational laundering The Rules expressly exclude fulfilment of other statutory obligations and marketing sponsorship

Balanced examination conclusion: CSR is defensible as a governance and social-investment mechanism, but must be judged by lawful purpose, additionality, accountable implementation and outcomes—not only the percentage spent.

Charity, CSR, ESG and compliance are not synonyms

  • Charity: a voluntary donation may or may not qualify under Schedule VII and the Rules.
  • Statutory CSR: the section 135 spending/governance framework for qualifying companies.
  • ESG: environmental, social and governance factors integrated into operations, strategy, investment analysis and reporting.
  • Legal compliance: duties imposed by employment, environmental, tax, company and other laws. These do not disappear because the company undertakes CSR.

Example: A manufacturer must install an effluent-treatment system required by environmental law. That is compliance. It may separately fund eligible community drinking-water infrastructure under Schedule VII. Its total water use, discharge quality and exposure to water scarcity are ESG matters. These connected facts do not make the three legal categories identical.

Evolution of the Indian framework

India’s framework moved from largely voluntary corporate giving towards statutory governance and spending under the 2013 Act, with section 135 effective from 1 April 2014. Later amendments changed the applicability language, tightened treatment of unspent money, introduced a civil-penalty framework for transfer defaults, allowed specified excess-spend set-off, and added reporting/impact-assessment obligations. The 2026 amendment adds a limited Social Stock Exchange instrument route, discussed separately below.

Historical trap: “Spend or explain” describes an earlier stage of the framework more accurately than the complete amended regime. Today an explanation in the Board’s report does not replace required transfers of unspent amounts.

03 · Section 135: every subsection explained

Statutory core • Companies Act, 2013 • read with CSR Rules and Schedule VII

Section 135(1) — Which companies are covered?

A company satisfies the financial trigger if, during the immediately preceding financial year, it has any one of:

Test Threshold Logical relationship
Net worth ₹500 crore or more Alternative trigger
Turnover ₹1,000 crore or more Alternative trigger
Net profit ₹5 crore or more Alternative trigger

The word is OR, not AND. A company with ₹6 crore qualifying net profit may be covered even though its turnover and net worth are far below the other limits. The test is not limited to listed companies. Qualifying private companies, section 8 companies and relevant foreign companies are not automatically excluded merely because of their form; apply the Act and Rule 3 to the particular entity.

Group distinction: A subsidiary does not inherit its parent’s financial trigger simply because it is a subsidiary. Assess the statutory criteria for the relevant company. A common group project is possible subject to proper allocation, reporting and the Rules; double counting the same expenditure is not.

Two different calculations: The immediately preceding year tests applicability. The average of the relevant three preceding years ordinarily determines the spend base. Do not substitute one for the other.

Committee composition and exceptions

The default committee consists of three or more directors, including at least one independent director. Where the company is not required to appoint an independent director under section 149(4), the statutory proviso permits two or more directors. Rule 5 accommodates a private company with only two directors and specifies the foreign-company arrangement: at least two persons, including the designated India-resident service/acceptance person under section 380(1)(d), with another nominated by the foreign company.

Under section 135(9), where the required amount to be spent does not exceed ₹50 lakh, the separate committee requirement is inapplicable and the Board discharges its functions. This is relief from a committee, not relief from spending, transfer or reporting.

Unspent-account qualification: Rule 3’s 2022 proviso expressly requires a company having an amount in its Unspent CSR Account to constitute a committee and comply with subsections (2)–(6). Read this alongside the small-obligation exemption instead of announcing that a company with a small current spend can ignore continuing unspent-project governance.

Section 135(2) — Disclose committee composition

The Board’s report under section 134(3) must disclose the CSR Committee’s composition. The point is accountability: readers should be able to identify who is charged with recommending and monitoring CSR. Where the Board performs the functions under subsection (9), reporting should accurately reflect the applicable arrangement rather than inventing a committee that does not exist.

Section 135(3) — What the CSR Committee does

The committee must:

  1. Formulate and recommend the CSR Policy indicating activities in the areas/subjects in Schedule VII.
  2. Recommend expenditure on those activities.
  3. Monitor the policy from time to time.

Rule 5 adds the annual action-plan framework. The committee’s role is not exhausted by signing a list of donations at year end. It should connect project selection, budgets, implementation, timelines and monitoring. It recommends; the Board retains the Act’s approval and implementation responsibilities.

Section 135(4) — What the Board must do

The Board must consider the committee’s recommendations, approve the policy, disclose its contents in its report and place it on the company website, if any, in the prescribed manner. It must ensure that the approved activities are undertaken.

The Board cannot escape statutory responsibility merely by outsourcing implementation to an eligible agency. Under the ordinary Rule 4 mechanism it must satisfy itself about utilisation, supported by certification from the CFO or financial-management-responsible person. The special 2026 instrument route has its own stated exceptions, discussed in Chapter 4.

Section 135(5) — The two-per-cent obligation

The Board must ensure expenditure of at least 2% of average net profits during the three immediately preceding financial years. Where the company has not completed three financial years since incorporation, use the immediately preceding financial years it has completed, as provided by the subsection.

Formula:

CSR obligation = 2% × average qualifying net profits of the relevant preceding financial years.

Net profit is a legal computation. The explanation sends calculation to section 198, with the prescribed exclusions. It is not automatically 2% of turnover, profit after tax, cash surplus, group consolidated profit or the current year’s projected profit. Rule 2 excludes overseas-branch profits and the specified dividends from Indian companies covered by and complying with section 135. Foreign-company computation follows the relevant section 381 accounts read with section 198. For a numerical problem, state that the profits supplied are qualifying section 198/CSR figures; do not silently apply the percentage to a different accounting number.

Local-area preference

The company must give preference to its local area and areas around its operations. Preference is not an absolute prohibition on eligible projects elsewhere in India. The project must still satisfy Schedule VII and the Rules; spending near a factory does not automatically make advertising or a statutory compliance expense eligible.

Non-ongoing unspent amount: subsection (5)

If the company does not spend the required amount, the Board must explain the reasons in its report. Unless the unspent amount relates to a qualifying ongoing project, it must be transferred to a Schedule VII fund within six months of the financial year’s expiry.

An eligible Schedule VII activity is not the same as a specified Schedule VII fund. A late transfer to a random NGO implementing an otherwise eligible activity is not automatically the statutory fund transfer required by subsection (5). Rule 10 supplies the stated interim fund mechanism.

Ongoing-project unspent amount: subsection (6)

For a qualifying ongoing project:

  1. Transfer the unspent amount within 30 days after financial-year end.
  2. Transfer it to a special account in a scheduled bank, opened for that financial year and called the Unspent Corporate Social Responsibility Account.
  3. Spend it on the CSR obligation within three financial years from the transfer.
  4. If it remains unspent, transfer it to a Schedule VII fund within 30 days after completion of the third financial year.

Not a loophole: Merely describing an unplanned donation as “ongoing” does not satisfy the project definition. Rule 2 requires a genuine multi-year project with permitted timelines, including a justified Board extension where a project originally approved for one year becomes multi-year.

Section 135(7) — Penalties for the specified defaults

For default under subsections (5) or (6):

  • Company: penalty of twice the amount required to be transferred, or ₹1 crore, whichever is less.
  • Every officer in default: penalty of one-tenth of the amount required to be transferred, or ₹2 lakh, whichever is less.

The relevant denominator is the amount required to be transferred, not necessarily the company’s entire CSR budget. The provision uses penalty, and its present form is not the earlier imprisonment formula. A false report or diversion involving fraud may independently engage other laws; that does not turn the transfer-default penalty itself into automatic imprisonment.

Sections 135(8) and (9)

Subsection (8): Government may issue general/special directions to a company or class of companies necessary to ensure compliance; they must comply.

Subsection (9): required spend not exceeding ₹50 lakh removes the separate committee requirement and gives the Board its functions, subject to reading the continuing-unspent-account rule explained above. A company whose obligation is exactly ₹50 lakh falls within the subsection’s numerical wording; one at ₹50,00,001 does not.

Excess spend and the explanation

The third proviso to subsection (5) permits prescribed set-off of excess spending. Rule 7(3) permits set-off up to the immediately succeeding three financial years, with a Board resolution, excluding surplus generated by CSR activities. Excess spending and CSR-generated surplus are different categories. The section’s explanation also confirms section 198-based net-profit computation with prescribed exclusions.

Exam structure for section 135: Applicability → committee/Board → policy/action plan → calculation → permitted activity → spending/utilisation → unspent classification and transfer → reporting → penalty. This structure is more reliable than memorising “2%” alone.

04 · CSR Rules, 2014, as amended: implementation and accountability

Rule 1 — Title and commencement

The Rules are the Companies (Corporate Social Responsibility Policy) Rules, 2014, effective from 1 April 2014. Later amendments must be read into the operative text. An old textbook reproduction is not enough to establish today’s administrative-overhead, impact-assessment or implementation rule.

Rule 2 — Definitions and exclusions

The six excluded classes

Exclusion What it prevents Important qualification
Normal course of business Reclassifying ordinary commercial activity as CSR The COVID R&D exception was expressly limited to FY 2020–21, 2021–22 and 2022–23 and its conditions; not a permanent R&D exemption
Activity outside India Exporting the statutory social-spending programme without the permitted link Exception for training Indian sports personnel representing a State/UT nationally or India internationally
Direct/indirect political contribution Treating political finance as social responsibility Section 182 compliance does not itself make expenditure CSR
Activities benefiting employees within the rule Substituting employee-benefit expenditure for public-oriented CSR Distinguish a genuine public project from an employee-targeted scheme; incidental employee benefit is not the same factual situation
Sponsorship for marketing benefits Using CSR as an advertising budget A logo or acknowledgement is not the sole test; examine substance, purpose and benefit
Other statutory obligations Double-credit for legal compliance Mandatory pollution controls, labour-law duties and similar obligations remain separate

Administrative overheads

These are expenses of general management and administration of CSR functions, not directly incurred design, implementation, monitoring or evaluation costs of a particular project. The distinction affects the five-per-cent cap: a project-specific health survey may be a project cost, whereas general CSR-office administration is overhead. Merely renaming head-office salaries “project support” does not alter their real character.

CSR policy and ongoing project

The policy states the Board’s approach and direction, informed by committee recommendations, and guiding principles for selection, implementation, monitoring and the annual action plan. It is not just a catalogue of last year’s cheques.

An ongoing project is a multi-year project undertaken in fulfilment of CSR obligations, with timelines not exceeding three years excluding the financial year of commencement. A Board may, on reasonable justification, extend an initially non-multi-year project beyond one year. The Rules do not authorise indefinite rollover.

Two clocks: the project-definition maximum and the subsection (6) deadline for money transferred to an Unspent CSR Account are connected but not identical. Always date the project and the relevant unspent-fund transfer separately.

Rule 3 — Applicability and continuing obligations

Rule 3 includes qualifying holding/subsidiary companies and relevant foreign companies with an Indian branch/project office. Each must meet the statutory trigger in the relevant manner. The old rule concerning three consecutive financial years of ceasing to qualify was omitted in 2022. Do not use it as an unrestricted current exit rule. Existing unspent-account balances trigger the continuing committee and compliance requirement in the added proviso.

Rule 4 — Ordinary implementation routes

The company may implement directly or use the prescribed eligible entities:

  1. A section 8 company, registered public trust or registered society established by the company singly or jointly with another company, satisfying the rule’s tax-status requirements.
  2. A section 8 company, registered trust or society established by the Central or State Government.
  3. A qualifying statutory body constituted under an Act of Parliament or State legislature to undertake Schedule VII activities.
  4. An independent section 8 company/public trust/society satisfying the tax-status requirements and possessing at least three years’ track record in similar activities.

The 2022 wording recognises the specified section 10(23C) exempt categories or the stated 12A registration and 80G approval route under the referenced Income-tax Act. This reproduces the CSR rule’s eligibility formulation; for present-day tax-registration advice, check the applicable tax enactment, transitions and valid registration/approval documents. It is not enough that an organisation calls itself an NGO.

CSR-1 registration

An implementing entity within the rule intending to undertake CSR activities must register through Form CSR-1, effective 1 April 2021, subject to the saving for projects approved earlier. The electronic form is digitally verified by a practising CA, CS or cost accountant; a unique registration number is generated. Registration is an eligibility/compliance step, not Government certification that every proposed expenditure or project is sound.

International organisations and collaboration

A company may engage the specified international organisations for design, monitoring, evaluation and capacity building. This is not unrestricted permission to replace the eligibility rules with any foreign implementing agency. Companies may collaborate, provided each can report separately on its contribution and programme. Maintain an allocation trail to prevent duplicate attribution of expenditure.

Utilisation and ongoing-project supervision

Under Rule 4(5), the Board must satisfy itself that disbursed funds were utilised for approved purposes and in the approved manner, supported by CFO/financial-management certification. Sending money to an agency is therefore not equivalent to proving its appropriate use. Under Rule 4(6), ongoing projects are monitored against timelines and year-wise allocations, with justified modifications within the overall permissible period.

Rule 4A — New 2026 Social Stock Exchange instrument route

Later-law update • G.S.R. 415(E), 27 May 2026 • not part of the original 2014 framework

The amendment adds definitions for Not for Profit Organization and zero coupon zero principal instrument and permits CSR through such instruments issued by an NPO registered with the Social Stock Exchange segment of a recognised exchange, under SEBI’s framework.

The new route has four important components:

  • Expenditure on the instrument must not exceed 10% of total CSR expenditure for that financial year.
  • The subscribing company is exempt from impact assessment of a project funded through that instrument—not from every impact assessment across its CSR portfolio.
  • The issuing NPO must undertake the project within not more than three succeeding financial years from issue; on termination of listing it must transfer unspent funds to a Schedule VII fund and report compliance to SEBI.
  • Rule 4 applies except sub-rules (5) and (6). Do not apply the ordinary CFO-certification/ongoing-project sub-rules mechanically to this special route or, conversely, claim that all section 135 responsibilities vanish.

Conceptual explanation: “Zero coupon” means no interest coupon and “zero principal” indicates no principal repayment in the instrument’s social-finance design; it is not an ordinary return-bearing corporate bond. Its classification as a security does not make every securities investment eligible CSR. Amendment reproduction; consolidated Rules.

Rule 5 — Committee and annual action plan

The annual action plan should include:

  1. projects/programmes in Schedule VII areas;
  2. manner of execution;
  3. fund-utilisation modalities and implementation schedules;
  4. monitoring and reporting mechanisms;
  5. need/impact assessment details, where relevant.

The Board may alter the plan during the financial year on committee recommendation and reasonable justification. It is a managed programme, not a document frozen regardless of changed needs—but flexibility must not conceal diversion or perpetual delay.

Rule 6 — Omitted

The former standalone Rule 6 on CSR policy was omitted in 2021. Policy duties remain through section 135, definitions and other operative rules. An omitted rule does not mean the company no longer needs a policy.

Rule 7 — Expenditure: four separate controls

Administrative overheads: not more than 5% of total CSR expenditure in the financial year. The denominator is expenditure, not automatically the statutory obligation.

Surplus: any surplus from CSR activities is not business profit. It must be ploughed into the same project, transferred to the Unspent CSR Account and used in accordance with policy/action plan, or transferred to a Schedule VII fund within the stated six-month financial-year-end period. A project-generated surplus is not a credit for the company’s own excess contribution.

Excess spend: permissible set-off against the immediately succeeding three financial years, excluding CSR surplus and supported by a Board resolution. There is no universal retrospective set-off of every donation ever made.

Capital assets: assets created/acquired with CSR funds should be held by the eligible section 8 entity/public trust/society with charitable objects and CSR registration, beneficiaries in the permitted collective forms, or a public authority. They are not simply retained as an ordinary company-owned commercial asset while being booked as CSR. The rule also had a time-bound transitional arrangement for pre-2021 assets; do not use that historical transition as an indefinite fresh grace period.

Rule 8 — Reporting and impact assessment

The Board’s report includes the annual CSR report in the applicable annexure; the foreign-company reporting route is also specified. Impact assessment is required where:

  • the company’s average CSR obligation is ₹10 crore or more over the three immediately preceding financial years;
  • the relevant project’s outlay is ₹1 crore or more; and
  • the project was completed not less than one year before the study.

Assessment must be through an independent agency. Reports are placed before the Board and linked/annexed/disclosed through the applicable reporting requirements. The 2022 changes include the executive-summary/web-link presentation in the annual reporting format.

The permissible amount booked for impact assessment is capped at 2% of total CSR expenditure for the financial year or ₹50 lakh, whichever is higher. The older 5% or ₹50 lakh, whichever is less formula is obsolete. Actual expenditure is still needed; the ceiling is not an automatic spending credit. Remember the special Rule 4A exemption for instrument-funded projects.

Rules 9 and 10; CSR-2 distinction

Rule 9: disclose committee composition, policy and Board-approved projects on the company website, if any, for public access.

Rule 10: until a fund is specified for the section 135(5)/(6) purpose, transfer unspent amounts to a fund included in Schedule VII.

CSR-2: the reporting form belongs to the Companies (Accounts) Rules framework. It is distinct from CSR-1, which registers implementing entities. This guide does not invent one permanent CSR-2 filing date: year-specific extensions and filing arrangements must be checked for an actual return.

Worked problem 1 — Trigger versus spending base

Facts: A company’s immediately preceding-year net worth is ₹420 crore, turnover ₹850 crore and qualifying net profit ₹6 crore. Qualifying profits for the three preceding years are ₹4 crore, ₹5 crore and ₹6 crore.

Solution: It meets the net-profit trigger alone. Average = ₹15 crore ÷ 3 = ₹5 crore. Two per cent = ₹10 lakh. This is not 2% of ₹850 crore and not 2% of only the latest ₹6 crore. Under section 135(9), the Board ordinarily performs committee functions because the obligation does not exceed ₹50 lakh; check any continuing Unspent CSR Account qualification.

Worked problem 2 — Split the unspent balance

Facts: Obligation ₹60 lakh. Eligible utilised expenditure ₹40 lakh. Of the ₹20 lakh shortfall, ₹12 lakh concerns a qualifying ongoing project and ₹8 lakh does not. Assume year ends 31 March 2026.

Solution: Transfer ₹12 lakh to that year’s Unspent CSR Account within 30 days, ordinarily by 30 April 2026. Transfer the non-ongoing ₹8 lakh to a Schedule VII fund within six months, ordinarily by 30 September 2026. Explain reasons in the Board report. The ongoing balance must follow the three-financial-year utilisation and final-transfer rules. One unclassified ₹20 lakh bank reserve does not comply with both routes.

Worked problem 3 — Penalty arithmetic

If the amount required to be transferred is ₹12 lakh, the company’s subsection (7) penalty is the lower of ₹24 lakh and ₹1 crore: ₹24 lakh. Each officer in default faces the lower of ₹1.2 lakh and ₹2 lakh: ₹1.2 lakh. The underlying transfer obligation does not disappear because the penalty is paid.

If the defaulted transfer is ₹80 lakh, the company figure is lower of ₹1.6 crore and ₹1 crore = ₹1 crore; officer figure is lower of ₹8 lakh and ₹2 lakh = ₹2 lakh. Do not multiply the officer amount by the number of directors unless the problem establishes who is an officer in default.

Worked problem 4 — Overheads, surplus and excess

Suppose actual total CSR expenditure is ₹1 crore. General administrative overheads may not exceed ₹5 lakh. A directly attributable project evaluation cost is classified on its substance, not automatically as general overhead.

If qualifying spend exceeds the obligation by ₹8 lakh, that excess may be set off within the next three financial years on the prescribed conditions and Board resolution. If the project separately generates ₹2 lakh surplus, that surplus must be treated under Rule 7(2); it cannot be added to the ₹8 lakh credit as though the company spent another ₹2 lakh from its own funds.

Worked problem 5 — Impact-assessment ceiling

Company’s average CSR obligation is ₹12 crore; project outlay ₹1.5 crore; completed eighteen months earlier. The threshold conditions for independent assessment are met, subject to any special applicable exception. If actual total CSR expenditure is ₹20 crore, 2% = ₹40 lakh; the alternative is ₹50 lakh. The booking ceiling is therefore ₹50 lakh, not ₹40 lakh, because the rule says higher. At ₹40 crore total expenditure the ceiling becomes ₹80 lakh. The company cannot book the whole ceiling if it incurred only ₹18 lakh.

Worked problem 6 — The 2026 instrument cap

If total CSR expenditure is ₹2 crore, Rule 4A’s instrument expenditure cannot exceed ₹20 lakh. A ₹30 lakh subscription cannot all qualify through that ten-per-cent route on these figures. The project-specific impact-assessment exemption does not exempt unrelated large CSR projects from Rule 8.

What a defensible CSR compliance file contains

A good file links: trigger computation; section 198 working; committee/Board basis; approved policy and annual action plan; Schedule VII mapping and exclusion analysis; agency eligibility and CSR-1; budgets and fund trail; ordinary-route utilisation certification; progress reports; overhead/surplus/excess-spend ledgers; unspent classification and bank/fund transfer evidence; impact assessment where required; Board report/website/CSR-2 records. For Rule 4A subscriptions, add instrument/NPO eligibility, percentage calculation and the special route’s documentation instead of mechanically copying the ordinary implementation checklist.

Final distinction: A technically correct percentage with fictitious utilisation is not compliance. CSR is a chain of lawful decisions, deployment, outcomes, transfers and disclosures.

06 · Corporate criminal liability: concepts and statutory structure

Why can an artificial person commit an offence?

A company is a legal person which acts through human beings. Its artificial character does not create general immunity from prosecution. The real questions are: Does the offence apply to a company? What act or omission is required? Is a mental element necessary? Whose conduct/knowledge is attributable? What punishment is legally possible?

The older judgments discuss IPC section 11, which included companies within “person”. The present Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, also defines person to include a company/association/body of persons, whether incorporated or not. Keep historical IPC/CrPC references intact when explaining older judgments; do not rewrite their dates and statutory language as though the new enactments already existed.

Identification or directing-mind doctrine

For relevant offences, the acts and mental state of persons who embody the company’s directing mind and will may be attributed to the company. Iridium rejects the idea that a company necessarily lacks mens rea because it has no biological mind. But it does not say every junior employee’s knowledge is automatically the company’s intention for every offence. Attribution depends on function, authority, the legal rule and statutory context.

Statutory vicarious liability

Some laws expressly make the company and persons in charge/responsible for its business liable, often with conditions relating to consent, connivance, neglect, knowledge or due diligence. Apply the particular clause. The general criminal law does not impose personal guilt on every director solely from their office.

Direction matters: human guilty mind → company attribution can be legally justified. Company allegation → automatic guilt of every director is not the converse rule. Sunil Bharti Mittal is the key corrective.

Strict liability, mens rea and civil penalties

A statute may dispense with some mental-element requirements. That depends on its wording and construction, not a general rule that all economic offences are strict liability. Likewise, a civil administrative penalty under company law is not automatically the same as conviction for a criminal offence. Decriminalisation of a specified compliance default changes that default’s sanction; it does not legalise false evidence, fraudulent diversion or intentional deception.

Companies Act section 2(60): “officer who is in default”

The definition identifies categories including whole-time directors, key managerial personnel, specified directors where KMP is absent, persons charged with relevant responsibilities who authorise/participate/knowingly permit the default, persons whose advice/directions the Board is accustomed to follow (subject to the professional-capacity qualification), directors with the stated knowledge/participation/consent or connivance, and the specified persons concerning share issue/transfer matters. It is a statutory responsibility map, not “every employee” and not “only the managing director.”

Use it with the charging provision. If section 135(7) imposes a penalty on an officer in default, establish the person’s statutory connection. If a separate fraud prosecution requires dishonest intent, citing section 2(60) alone does not prove every ingredient of that offence.

Section 149(12): limited protection, not complete immunity

An independent director, or a non-executive director who is not a promoter/KMP, is liable under the section’s formulation only for acts of omission/commission which occurred with knowledge attributable through Board processes and consent/connivance, or where the director did not act diligently. This is a calibrated protection for the identified class, not permission to ignore serious warning signs and not a universal immunity under all other enactments.

Section 447: fraud in company law

Fraud in relation to a company/body corporate includes acts, omissions, concealment of facts or abuse of position by a person, or another with connivance, with intent to deceive, gain undue advantage or injure the interests of the company, shareholders, creditors or another person, whether or not wrongful gain/loss actually occurs.

For fraud involving at least ₹10 lakh or 1% of turnover, whichever is lower, the provision prescribes imprisonment from six months to ten years and fine from the amount involved up to three times that amount; where public interest is involved, the minimum imprisonment is three years. The lesser-amount, no-public-interest proviso has a different sanction: imprisonment up to five years, or fine up to ₹50 lakh, or both. Verify the statutory amount/public-interest branch before quoting one universal fraud punishment.

Company and individual: A corporation cannot physically undergo imprisonment, but relevant corporate prosecution/fine principles remain important. Individuals whose guilt is proved may face imprisonment according to the offence; the corporation’s physical impossibility does not remove their sentencing exposure.

Compliance design is preventive, not exculpatory by itself

Board oversight, segregation of duties, whistleblowing, training, transaction approvals and reliable records can reduce wrongdoing and provide evidence relevant to diligence/knowledge. A written policy does not automatically erase intentional fraud. Conversely, hindsight alone cannot substitute for evidence that a particular director had the required involvement or statutory responsibility.

Case A — Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement (2005)

Identity: Supreme Court Constitution Bench, 5 May 2005, reported (2005) 4 SCC 530. This is not the UK Pakistan National Shipping deceit case.

Material facts and procedural setting: The connected litigation concerned prosecution of corporate bodies, including the bank, under economic-offence statutes. The central example was section 56 of FERA, 1973, whose serious-offence branch prescribed imprisonment and fine. The corporate appellants argued that a company could not be prosecuted where a mandatory prison sentence could not physically be served. The earlier majority in Velliappa Textiles had accepted that obstacle in the tax-offence context; doubt about that ruling led to the Constitution Bench reference.

Issue: Does impossibility of imprisoning a company bar prosecution where the statute prescribes mandatory imprisonment together with fine?

Argument against prosecution: Strict construction of penal law requires the court to impose the sentence Parliament prescribed. If imprisonment and fine are mandatory, imposing only fine would rewrite the statute. Parliament knew companies cannot be imprisoned; the court should not fill a legislative gap by changing the punishment. Some counsel relied on the earlier Velliappa majority and unimplemented Law Commission recommendations.

Argument supporting prosecution: Corporate persons are covered by the statutory prohibition; the inability to execute one form of punishment cannot create immunity for serious economic crimes. The court should impose the legally executable fine on the company. Otherwise the absurd result would be prosecution for a minor offence punishable with fine but immunity for a graver offence whose punishment adds compulsory imprisonment. The judgment records that Ram Jethmalani, appearing for an appellant in a connected matter, supported prosecutability, so the arguments cannot accurately be divided into “all appellants versus all respondents.” The Additional Solicitor General opposed the immunity argument.

Decision and reasoning: By majority, the Court rejected the corporate-immunity argument and displaced the contrary Velliappa position. A company may be prosecuted and, if convicted in the imprisonment-plus-fine setting, subjected to the fine which can operate against it. The law does not compel physical impossibility, but that does not require defeating the legislature’s prohibition.

What was not decided: The leading opinion expressly left the broad mens rea attribution question outside the issue being decided. It is therefore wrong to cite this case alone as though it supplied the complete later Iridium analysis. Nor did the reference itself prove the bank guilty of all underlying allegations. Do not generalise the precise fine solution into a court’s unrestricted power to invent a fine for every offence whose statute prescribes imprisonment alone.

Principle: Distinguish amenability to prosecution from the practical form of punishment. Corporate personality is not a safe harbour against serious statutory offences merely because the company has no body to imprison.

Hypothetical: Atlas Finance Ltd. is accused under an applicable economic statute prescribing imprisonment and fine. Its plea “we cannot be jailed, therefore no prosecution is possible” fails under this principle. The prosecution must still prove the offence; responsible individuals do not inherit the company’s inability to serve imprisonment.

Case B — Iridium India Telecom Ltd. v. Motorola Incorporated

Identity: Supreme Court, 20 October 2010, reported (2011) 1 SCC 74; B. Sudershan Reddy and Surinder Singh Nijjar JJ. The judgment year and report year explain why both 2010 and 2011 appear in accounts.

Facts: The dispute arose from investment in the Iridium satellite communications project. Motorola was alleged to have promoted, controlled and supplied the venture and to have induced investment through representations about its technical/commercial capabilities. The complainant alleged misleading statements and concealment of material limitations in a project involving a constellation of satellites and worldwide communications. A criminal complaint alleged cheating and conspiracy under IPC sections 420 and 120B. The Bombay High Court quashed the proceedings; the complainant appealed.

Issues: Could the corporate accused be prosecuted for a mens-rea offence? Had the High Court exceeded the proper limits of quashing by deciding disputed facts and evaluating the defence at the threshold?

Complainant’s arguments: Ram Jethmalani emphasised that quashing at the cognizance stage is exceptional; the complaint should be read as a whole. A complaint need not mechanically recite every legal ingredient if it contains the necessary factual foundation. Allegations of knowing deception and material concealment could not be converted into a mere failed investment by evaluating contested defence documents as though trial had already occurred.

Motorola’s arguments: Ashok Desai argued that Motorola had not made the representations alleged in the required sense; the project involved distinct corporate entities and strategic investors. Statements were forward-looking projections concerning uncertain technology and feasibility, accompanied by risk warnings. Commercial failure and an investor’s disappointment did not establish dishonest intention at inception; the dispute was not a proper basis for criminal proceedings. These were defences, not judicial findings that all warnings were necessarily sufficient.

Decision: The Supreme Court allowed the appeal and set aside the High Court’s quashing judgment. It rejected the proposition that a company is incapable of mens rea. The relevant guilty mind of those who are its directing mind and will can be attributed to the company. Corporate criminal liability is not confined to offences dispensing with mental elements.

Reasoning on procedure: The High Court had stated the restraint applicable at the quashing stage but then effectively evaluated disputed merits. The Court distinguished whether a complaint discloses a prosecutable allegation from whether the prosecution ultimately proves it. Risk disclosures and documents may matter at the proper stage; they are not a universal threshold immunity from an adequately alleged fraudulent inducement.

Principles and limits: (1) Corporate mens rea can be established through attribution; (2) a factual commercial context does not automatically exclude a criminal offence; (3) business failure alone is not fraud; and (4) restoration of proceedings is not conviction. The judgment does not attribute every employee’s mental state to the company indiscriminately.

Hypothetical: A technology company’s controlling officers knowingly conceal a product’s serious limitations while inducing investment. The company cannot answer that artificial persons have no intention. But the claimant must ultimately prove the required deceptive conduct and mental element; an honest forecast which later fails is different.

Case C — Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI (2015)

Identity: Supreme Court, 9 January 2015, reported (2015) 4 SCC 609. The case concerns summoning individuals, not a blanket immunity for company directors.

Facts: In proceedings concerning allocation of additional telecom spectrum, the charge-sheet named corporate accused and a public official. The Special Judge additionally summoned senior corporate individuals, including Sunil Bharti Mittal and Ravi Ruia, although they were not named as accused in that charge-sheet. The stated reasoning treated senior controlling officers as the companies’ alter ego and attributed company acts to them. Two summoned individuals challenged the order.

Issues: Can the identification doctrine be reversed to impose criminal responsibility on senior directors simply because their companies are accused? What must support a summons to an individual not named in the charge-sheet?

Appellants’ arguments: Their counsel contended that identification ordinarily attributes the relevant individual’s acts/mind to the company, not company guilt automatically to the individual. High office and control are not substitutes for specific involvement, requisite intent or a statute imposing vicarious liability. The summoning reasoning did not identify an adequate individual basis merely by using the phrase “alter ego.”

CBI’s arguments: The CBI relied on the charge-sheet, surrounding circumstances and alleged meetings/decision-making links, arguing that companies act through human agencies and the trial court was entitled to summon persons beyond the investigating agency’s named accused if material justified it. It resisted reading the order’s references to material as unrelated to the summoned individuals. Thus the prosecution did not simply argue that every director in every case is guilty; it attempted to connect control and surrounding evidence to the case.

Decision and reasoning: The Supreme Court set aside the impugned summoning order insofar as it concerned the appellants. The identification principle had been applied in the wrong direction. An individual may be prosecuted where there is sufficient evidence of an active role with criminal intent, or where the applicable statute creates vicarious liability and its conditions are met. Mere corporate status is insufficient. The Court did not rule that a trial court can never summon someone omitted from a charge-sheet; the legal power must be supported by the required material and reasoned application.

Principle: Company liability and director liability are separate inquiries. Attribution to an artificial person is not automatic reverse attribution to every person controlling it. Setting aside this summons does not grant lifelong immunity from proceedings founded on legally sufficient material.

Hypothetical: A company is accused of intentional deception. Its non-executive director is summoned only because “a company must act through directors.” Ask for the individual’s role, knowledge, intent or the precise statutory vicarious-liability clause. Without the necessary basis, status alone does not justify that conclusion.

The three-case synthesis

Question Authority Answer
Can a company be prosecuted despite inability to serve imprisonment? Standard Chartered Yes in the relevant imprisonment-plus-fine setting; enforceable fine is not excluded
Can the company possess legally attributable mens rea? Iridium Yes; apply appropriate directing-mind/attribution principles
Are its directors therefore automatically guilty? Sunil Bharti Mittal No; establish personal involvement/intent or applicable statutory vicarious liability

These are complementary propositions. None says a company, promoter or director is convicted merely because a commercial project failed.

08 · Chapter XV: compromises, arrangements and amalgamations

Why restructuring law exists

A financially healthy company may reorganise to combine businesses, separate divisions, simplify capital, alter shareholder rights or integrate operations. A distressed company may restructure debt to preserve enterprise value. Ordinary contracts struggle where thousands of stakeholders must consent individually and a small minority can block a beneficial transaction. A statutory scheme combines collective decision-making, disclosure, class protection and Tribunal sanction so an approved arrangement can bind the relevant class, including dissenters.

A scheme is not synonymous with insolvency. Sections 230–232 can operate outside IBC. Conversely, a scheme used in an IBC liquidation cannot ignore the insolvency framework merely because its procedural vehicle comes from the Companies Act. That is the central lesson of Jagatramka.

  1. Identify the commercial objective and companies involved.
  2. Determine whether the transaction is a contractual acquisition, a section 230/232 scheme, a qualifying section 233 fast-track transaction, or an insolvency resolution.
  3. Identify stakeholders whose legal rights change and the correct classes.
  4. Identify sectoral, competition, securities, foreign-exchange and tax consequences.
  5. Apply the correct route’s approval thresholds, disclosures and sanctions.

Example: Two healthy unlisted companies merging do not become subject to IBC solely because they use a Tribunal. Conversely, a promoter barred by section 29A cannot assume an IBC-liquidation scheme is just a solvent-company private bargain.

09 · Section 230: the scheme-making process, subsection by subsection

Section 230(1) — Applicants, subject and class meetings

Where a compromise/arrangement is proposed between a company and its creditors or a class of them, or its members or a class of them, the Tribunal may order a meeting in the prescribed manner. Applications may be made by the company, creditor, member, or, in the company’s winding-up situation, the specified liquidator, including one appointed under the IBC.

The explanation includes reorganisation of share capital through consolidation or division into different classes. “Arrangement” is consequently broader than settlement of a disputed debt.

Class formation: People whose rights are sufficiently similar to consult together should ordinarily form a class. Identify legal rights, security, priority and the proposed alteration; labels such as “all creditors” cannot cure a meeting which combines materially conflicting legal interests. Equally, differences of commercial preference do not necessarily create a separate class. This is a principle of scheme fairness and representation, not a statutory instruction to create one class per creditor.

Section 230(2) — Disclosure by affidavit

The applicant must disclose all material facts relating to the company, including the latest financial position, latest auditor’s report and pending investigations/proceedings; any reduction of share capital included in the scheme; and the particular information required for a corporate debt restructuring scheme.

For the specified corporate debt restructuring proposal consented to by not less than 75% of secured creditors in value, the additional matters include:

  • creditor-responsibility statement in the prescribed form;
  • safeguards for other secured and unsecured creditors;
  • auditor’s report on the relevant fund requirements/liquidity position against the commercial restructuring estimates;
  • statement regarding proposed adoption of RBI’s restructuring guidelines;
  • valuation report of the relevant shares/property/assets, tangible/intangible, movable/immovable, by a registered valuer.

Do not merge thresholds: This 75%-secured-value condition is attached to the described restructuring disclosure framework. It is not the whole of section 230(6)’s meeting vote and not IBC’s 66% CoC vote.

Section 230(3) — Individual notice and explanatory material

Notice must be sent individually to creditors, members and relevant debenture holders at their registered addresses, with the scheme terms, explanatory statement, valuation report if any, and effect on creditors, KMP, promoters, non-promoter members and debenture holders. Material interests of directors/debenture trustees must be explained. The notice/explanatory material is also placed on the website, if any; listed companies must follow the required SEBI/stock-exchange dissemination, and prescribed advertisement requirements apply.

The substance is informed consent. A bare invitation stating “approve restructuring” without explaining dilution, security changes or promoter interests defeats that purpose. Beneficial ownership/control conflicts should not be hidden behind a favourable aggregate valuation.

Section 230(4) — Voting and objection standing

The notice must state the voting avenues—in person, proxy or postal ballot—within the prescribed statutory framework. The subsection refers to voting within one month from receipt of notice; the Tribunal order and the applicable CAA Rules govern meeting administration.

The proviso allows an objection only by persons holding at least 10% of shareholding, or having outstanding debt amounting to at least 5% of total outstanding debt according to the latest audited financial statement.

Three cautions: These are objection-standing thresholds, not the vote required to approve a scheme. A creditor tests the debt limb, not a shareholding limb imposed by analogy. And restricted objection standing does not eliminate the Tribunal’s own duty to test legality, disclosure and fairness.

Section 230(5) — Regulatory notice and the thirty-day window

The notice and relevant documents go to the Central Government, income-tax authorities, RBI, SEBI, Registrar, stock exchanges, Official Liquidator, Competition Commission if necessary, and other sectoral regulators/authorities likely to be affected, as applicable. They may make representations within 30 days of receipt; otherwise they are presumed to have no representations on the proposal.

Presumed absence of a representation is not a universal waiver of an independent statutory approval requirement. For example, a transaction requiring competition approval cannot be defended solely by saying the regulator remained silent in the scheme-notice process.

Section 230(6) — Approval and binding force

The scheme must be agreed to by a majority of persons representing three-fourths in value of the relevant creditors/class or members/class voting, in person/proxy/postal ballot, and be sanctioned by the Tribunal. It then binds the company and relevant class; in winding up, it also binds the liquidator and contributories as specified.

Dual test: a majority in number and at least 75% in value of the relevant persons voting. Do not write merely “75% shareholders must approve” or calculate against the wrong class or an unspecified entire issued capital base.

Numerical example: Ten creditors of one proper class vote. Six support; their voting debt value is ₹70 lakh out of ₹1 crore voted. The headcount limb passes, the value limb fails. Seven supporters holding ₹80 lakh pass both limbs, subject to proper process and Tribunal sanction. A majority vote is necessary, not self-executing judicial approval.

Tribunal scrutiny: neither rubber stamp nor business manager

The Tribunal examines statutory compliance, adequate disclosure, class representation, bona fides, legality and fairness; it must not sanction an unlawful scheme merely because a majority wants it. At the same time, it ordinarily does not substitute its own preferred commercial bargain for an informed, fairly obtained class decision simply because another exchange ratio seems arguable.

Minority protection therefore includes correct classes, disclosure of interests, objection opportunity within the statutory framework, valuation evidence and independent sanction. “Majority rule” does not mean a dominant group may confiscate another class’s legal rights without the Act’s safeguards.

Section 230(7) — Matters the sanction order may address

The order may provide for:

  • conversion of preference shares into equity, with the prescribed choice between dividend arrears in cash and equity corresponding to their value;
  • protection of creditor classes;
  • variation of shareholder rights in accordance with section 48;
  • abatement of the stated proceedings before the erstwhile BIFR where the specified creditor condition is met—a legacy provision requiring historical context, not a claim that BIFR is the present insolvency forum;
  • other matters including an exit offer to dissenting shareholders if necessary for effective implementation.

Mandatory accounting safeguard: A scheme cannot be sanctioned unless the company’s auditor certifies that proposed accounting treatment conforms to standards prescribed under section 133. A commercial valuation and an accounting-standards certificate serve different purposes.

Section 230(8) — File the order

The Tribunal’s order must be filed with the Registrar within 30 days of receipt. Distinguish receipt-triggered filing from the meeting date and the scheme’s appointed date.

Section 230(9) — Waiver of creditor meeting

The Tribunal may dispense with calling a meeting of creditors/class where creditors having at least 90% value agree and confirm by affidavit. This is a specific creditor-meeting dispensation, not a general “90% is the scheme approval threshold” rule and not an automatic shareholder-meeting waiver under this subsection.

Sections 230(10)–(12) — Buy-back and takeover

Subsection (10): no compromise/arrangement in respect of a buy-back unless it complies with section 68.

Subsection (11): a scheme may include a takeover offer in the prescribed manner; listed-company takeover offers follow the SEBI framework.

Subsection (12): a party aggrieved by the relevant takeover offer of an unlisted company may apply to the Tribunal in the prescribed manner, which may make an appropriate order. Do not treat this as a replacement of SEBI’s listed-company regime.

Final explanation: Section 66 does not apply to reduction of share capital effected pursuant to the Tribunal’s order under section 230. This prevents duplicative reduction procedure; it is not exemption from scheme disclosure, creditor protection, accounting or other applicable laws.

Model file sequence

Application and affidavit → correct classes and meeting directions → scheme/explanatory notices and regulator service → voting/affidavit dispensation where lawful → voting-result and compliance evidence → sanction hearing → reasoned order and accounting compliance → ROC filing → implementation and monitoring. A missing stage is not cured merely by drawing a flowchart; each has a distinct protective function.

10 · Sections 231 and 232: enforcement and merger machinery

Section 231(1) — Supervision and modification

After sanction under section 230, the Tribunal may supervise carrying out the compromise/arrangement and, when making the order or later, issue directions or make modifications necessary for proper implementation.

This recognises that a scheme is a continuing legal programme, not a single vote. Operational obstacles may require directions. But supervision is not an unrestricted power to replace the essential bargain with a fundamentally different arrangement while bypassing required stakeholder protections.

Example: A sanctioned asset transfer needs clarified sequencing of documents and instalments. An implementation direction may be appropriate. Replacing agreed secured repayment with an entirely new unsecured haircut is not necessarily a mere administrative clarification.

Section 231(2) — When failure can lead to winding up

The Tribunal may order winding up where satisfied that:

  1. the compromise/arrangement cannot be implemented satisfactorily, with or without modifications; and
  2. the company is unable to pay its debts as per the scheme.

It may act suo motu or on the application of an interested person. The resulting order is deemed an order under section 273.

The conjunction matters. Do not write that any missed milestone automatically causes winding up, or that section 231 recreates a universal standalone Companies Act debt-recovery/winding-up ground after the IBC amendments. The provision concerns a failed sanctioned scheme and the stated debt inability.

Section 231(3) — Legacy schemes

The section also applies, so far as may be, to companies in respect of which compromise/arrangement orders were made before the 2013 Act’s commencement. This preserves enforcement continuity; it does not mean every legacy scheme must be re-approved from scratch.

Section 232(1) — When merger provisions attach

Where an application under section 230 concerns a scheme for reconstruction, merger or amalgamation, involving transfer of all/part of an undertaking, property or liabilities to another company, or division and transfer to two or more companies, section 232 supplies the additional machinery. The Tribunal may order meetings, and section 230(3)–(6) apply mutatis mutandis.

Thus section 232 does not float independently of disclosure, regulator notice, class voting and sanction. It builds transfer/vesting consequences onto that framework.

Section 232(2) — Additional documents for the meeting

The merging companies or companies subject to division must circulate:

  • draft terms drawn up and adopted by their directors;
  • confirmation that the draft scheme was filed with the Registrar;
  • a directors’ report explaining effects on each class of shareholders, KMP, promoters and non-promoter shareholders, including the share-exchange ratio and any special valuation difficulties;
  • the expert valuation report, if any;
  • a supplementary accounting statement where the latest annual accounts end more than six months before the first meeting summoned to approve the scheme.

Valuation logic: A ratio determines participation in the resulting enterprise, not merely the nominal face value of shares. Relevant factors may include assets, earnings, business risks, cash flows and method suitability. The notes do not present one universally mandatory valuation formula. The legal requirement is the proper valuation/disclosure framework, including registered-valuer requirements where applicable.

Section 232(4) — Statutory vesting

Where the order provides for transfer, the property vests and liabilities become liabilities of the transferee by virtue of the order. If the order so directs, property may be freed from a charge which ceases under the scheme. “Property” includes rights and powers of every description; “liabilities” includes duties.

This machinery avoids an impractical need to recreate every corporate relationship individually, but is not a statement that stamp duty, registration, sectoral consents or every third-party legal restriction can always be ignored. Examine the relevant law and the order’s scope.

Section 232(5) — Filing; subsection (6) — appointed date

Each company to which the order relates must file a certified copy with the Registrar within 30 days of receipt.

The scheme must clearly indicate an appointed date, and is deemed effective from that date, not a later date. In transactional practice distinguish the appointed date for scheme effect/accounting attribution from the effective-date conditions used to operationalise the scheme and the order/filing dates. Their relationship must follow the scheme, Act and applicable guidance; “all four dates are always identical” is incorrect.

Section 232(7) — Annual completion statement

Until completion, each company files an annual statement in the prescribed form/time, certified by a practising CA, cost accountant or CS, indicating whether the scheme is being complied with according to the Tribunal’s orders. Sanction is therefore followed by an implementation-reporting obligation, not regulatory silence.

Section 232(8) — Present penalty

For non-compliance, the company and every officer in default face ₹20,000, and continuing failure a further ₹1,000 for each day after the first, subject to ₹3 lakh maximum. Older imprisonment/fine formulations should not be reproduced as the current subsection.

A complete merger hypothetical

Aster Manufacturing Ltd. transfers its undertaking to Beacon Industries Ltd. Shareholders receive Beacon shares on the disclosed ratio. Employee transfer, creditor liabilities, pending suits, a listed/unlisted issue and a security charge must be addressed.

The answer should identify: section 230 application/classes/notices/vote; section 232 draft terms/directors’ report/valuation/supplementary accounts; auditor’s section 133 certificate; regulatory approvals; Tribunal scrutiny; vesting/share allotment/self-holding cancellation; employee and litigation continuity; dissenters’ protection; dissolution without winding up; filing and annual compliance. A statement that “75% approved, so Aster automatically disappears” omits most of the law.

11 · Arun Kumar Jagatramka: the prescribed bridge case

DU-suggested authority • Supreme Court • 15 March 2021 • (2021) 7 SCC 474 / 2021 SCC OnLine SC 220

The uploaded DU list’s “AIR 2021 SC 136” appears truncated. Use the verified case name/date and the reported citations above rather than perpetuating a doubtful page reference. The dossier below is based on the full judgment, not a nonexistent reproduced DU extract.

1. Lead case: material and procedural facts

Gujarat NRE Coke Ltd. (GNCL) entered insolvency proceedings. The CIRP did not produce an approved resolution plan within the relevant 270-day period then in play; liquidation was ordered on 11 January 2018. Arun Kumar Jagatramka was a promoter and was ineligible under section 29A to submit a resolution plan.

He sought a compromise/arrangement under section 230 during liquidation. The NCLT’s order of 15 May 2018 permitted the scheme-meeting process. Jindal Steel and Power Ltd., an operational creditor, challenged that course. The NCLAT on 24 October 2019 held that a person ineligible under section 29A could not regain the company through this liquidation-stage scheme route. Jagatramka appealed to the Supreme Court.

2. Connected proceedings and regulatory challenge

The Court also considered connected Su-Kam proceedings involving promoter Kunwer Sachdev, a proposed joint resolution with Phoenix ARC, and a section 29A(h) disqualification which was not challenged in the relevant manner. Liquidation was ordered on 3 April 2019. A later section 230 attempt met the same eligibility objection, and the NCLAT ruled on 19 December 2019.

A connected challenge attacked the proviso to Regulation 2B(1) of the IBBI Liquidation Process Regulations, inserted in January 2020, which excluded persons ineligible to submit a resolution plan from participating in the compromise/arrangement process. Its validity and the scope of IBBI’s regulation-making power were therefore directly implicated.

3. Legal issues

  1. Can someone disqualified under IBC section 29A propose a section 230 scheme in IBC liquidation?
  2. Does section 35(1)(f), prohibiting sale to an ineligible person in liquidation, inform that answer?
  3. Is Regulation 2B’s ineligibility proviso ultra vires the parent statutes or unconstitutional?
  4. Is a liquidation-stage scheme equivalent to a section 12A withdrawal/settlement, so that the latter’s rationale permits the former?
  5. How should two statutes governing one insolvency-related transaction be read together?

4. Appellants’ case: strongest version

Textual autonomy: Section 230 itself did not expressly incorporate section 29A. The Companies Act provides a distinct route; an IBC disqualification should not be judicially inserted into another Act.

Different consequences: A successful resolution applicant can acquire the enterprise through a binding plan with reduced liabilities/clean-slate consequences. A scheme proponent argued that it takes the company subject to the negotiated scheme and existing obligations, not necessarily free of encumbrances. Therefore treating the two routes identically allegedly ignored material differences.

Revival: Allowing the promoter’s scheme could preserve jobs and business rather than force piecemeal liquidation. The Tribunal’s sanction requirement itself supplied safeguards against unfairness.

Section 12A analogy: Withdrawal after settlement returns the company to its former management; a consensual section 230 arrangement should not be barred merely because that result includes promoter return.

Delegated legislation: IBBI could not use a regulation to amend the Companies Act or enlarge section 29A beyond the statutory policy. Constitutional objections included arbitrariness and rights-based challenges under Articles 14, 19 and 21 as raised in the connected challenge.

Insertion argument: The need to insert an express regulatory proviso in 2020 was said to indicate that the earlier law contained no such prohibition.

5. Respondents’ case: strongest version

Unified insolvency policy: The relevant transaction occurs because the company is in liquidation under IBC. Section 230 cannot be isolated from that context. Parliament introduced section 29A and the corresponding section 35(1)(f) prohibition to prevent prohibited persons from retaking the enterprise through resolution or liquidation asset purchase.

No backdoor: Permitting the same person to obtain control through a scheme would defeat the bar through a third route. Form cannot neutralise the substance of the insolvency policy.

Not all promoters, and not merely this company’s misconduct: Section 29A contains diverse disqualifications, not a universal ban on promoters. Some concern a person’s relation to other companies or disqualifying circumstances. The relevant question is statutory ineligibility, not a general hostility to entrepreneurship.

Dual role of NCLT: In this setting the NCLT is both the IBC adjudicating authority and the Companies Act scheme Tribunal. It can insist that a liquidation-stage scheme genuinely revive the company and comply with the insolvency eligibility standard. Earlier scheme jurisprudence in liquidation required more than private settlement of one isolated class.

Regulation gives effect to the Code: The proviso implemented the parent framework rather than creating an unrelated policy. A section 230 scheme is not necessarily comprehensive merely because it uses that label; it can bind a class and must be evaluated in context.

6. Decision

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeals/writ challenge. Paragraph 91 records the central conclusion: a person ineligible under section 29A is also barred from proposing a compromise/arrangement under section 230 in the context of IBC liquidation. The section 35(1)(f) prohibition supports that reading. The Regulation 2B proviso was upheld as constitutionally valid and within the regulatory framework.

7. Reasoning, step by step

First: identify the statutory objective. IBC seeks timely resolution, value maximisation and a disciplined process, not a mechanism by which ineligible persons benefit from the company’s distress.

Second: connect resolution and liquidation. The statutory disqualification operates at resolution through section 29A and at liquidation sale through section 35(1)(f). Liquidation is not a legal vacuum in which the policy reverses.

Third: locate the scheme within that process. The section 230 application is being used for a company undergoing IBC liquidation. Although the procedural source is another Act, the commercial/legal result must remain consistent with the surrounding insolvency regime.

Fourth: reject circumvention. It would be incoherent to prohibit direct resolution and direct liquidation purchase but permit the same prohibited acquisition via a scheme.

Fifth: distinguish withdrawal. The judgment examined the then-existing section 12A route as withdrawal of the collective proceeding, contrasted with a scheme embedded in continuing liquidation. Their legal character and statutory architecture differ. A superficial similarity—old management might resume control—does not make their conditions interchangeable.

Sixth: sustain the regulation. The proviso concretised a disability flowing from the integrated scheme of the legislation; it did not independently invent an alien Companies Act restriction.

8. Ratio and boundaries

Ratio: A person barred under section 29A cannot evade that disqualification by proposing a section 230 compromise/arrangement for the corporate debtor during liquidation under IBC.

Do not overstate it: The Court did not ban all promoters, all schemes, or every solvent-company arrangement under section 230. Section 29A has specific categories, qualifications and exceptions, including the relevant MSME context. Nor did the Court hold that creditor commercial preference cures statutory ineligibility.

Policy observations: Paragraph 90’s discussion of policy suggestions is expressly not a decision on their merits. Do not turn recommendations discussed by the Court into enacted rules.

9. Hypothetical visual and application

Problem: Rohan is an ineligible promoter of Delta Steel Ltd., now in IBC liquidation. He cannot submit a resolution plan or purchase assets in the prohibited manner. He proposes an 80%-creditor-supported section 230 scheme through which he will regain control.

Answer: First verify the actual section 29A disqualification and any applicable exception—promoter status alone is insufficient. If the disability exists, Jagatramka bars the liquidation-scheme workaround. An apparently attractive price or creditor majority cannot override eligibility. Also do not mistake “80% creditors” for section 230’s separate class/headcount/value requirements.

10. Historical holding versus 2026 law

The case’s explanation of section 12A concerns 2021 law. The 2026 substituted section 12A changes the withdrawal window and is set out in Chapter 16. Do not lift the older procedural description into a present-day answer unqualified.

The 2026 Act also changes CoC involvement, liquidator appointment and liquidation timelines. This guide does not claim, without a verified applicable regulatory text, that the section 230 liquidation route has been generally abolished. For an actual post-amendment transaction, check the current Liquidation Regulations and transitional directions separately. The prescribed judgment remains essential for the anti-circumvention principle; contemporary procedure must be independently verified.

11. Examination paragraph to remember

“Jagatramka requires the Companies Act scheme mechanism to be read in the insolvency setting in which it is invoked. A person disabled by section 29A cannot achieve indirectly through a liquidation-stage section 230 arrangement what sections 29A and 35(1)(f) prohibit directly. The Court upheld Regulation 2B’s proviso as implementing that policy. The rule targets statutory ineligibility in IBC liquidation, not all promoters or all solvent-company schemes.”

12 · IBC: concepts, evolution and economic importance

DU-prescribed handbook lens • conceptual explanation supplemented by dated statutory updates

Insolvency, bankruptcy, liquidation and dissolution

Insolvency describes financial distress/inability to meet obligations in the legally relevant sense. A cash-flow problem and a balance-sheet deficit are conceptually different. Default, the corporate IBC entry concept, is non-payment of debt when due and payable; an applicant need not simply prove that liabilities exceed assets.

Bankruptcy commonly refers to a formal legal status/process, particularly for individuals under the Code’s architecture. It should not be used carelessly as a synonym for every corporate CIRP. Liquidation realises assets and distributes value according to law. Dissolution terminates legal existence. A company can be insolvent without yet being dissolved, and a company can be dissolved through merger without conventional liquidation.

Why a collective law is necessary

Suppose ten lenders can each seize the assets essential to a factory. The first seizure may destroy the operating business and reduce everyone’s eventual recovery. This is the common-pool/creditor-race problem. A collective moratorium preserves the pool while a coordinated process tests rescue and allocates loss.

Distress also creates information asymmetry: insiders know more about assets, receivables and related-party transactions than dispersed creditors. Professional management, information utilities, claims processes and a common information memorandum reduce that asymmetry. Timelines matter because machinery deteriorates, customers leave and employees depart while proceedings linger.

From fragmentation to an integrated framework

Before IBC, company winding up, industrial-sickness rehabilitation, debt-recovery tribunals and secured-creditor enforcement operated through multiple enactments and institutions. SICA/BIFR, company courts, debt-recovery law and SARFAESI served different functions, often producing delay, overlap and strategic litigation. IBC, enacted in 2016, consolidated and amended insolvency laws around time-bound resolution, value maximisation, entrepreneurship, availability of credit and balancing stakeholder interests, including altered priority of Government dues.

The corporate framework became operational through staged commencement. Later amendments addressed promoter eligibility, homebuyers, creditor voting, withdrawal, treatment of approved plans, pandemic defaults and pre-packaged resolution for eligible MSMEs. 2026 is another amendment stage, not the year the Code first began.

The shift in control

Ordinary corporate governance places business control with the Board. Ordinary CIRP displaces Board management powers to the IRP/RP, while the CoC exercises specified commercial decision-making. This is commonly described as creditor-in-control, but creditors do not become managers of every purchase order. The RP operates the company; the CoC supervises/approves specified decisions; the NCLT adjudicates; IBBI regulates the profession/framework.

Pre-packaged insolvency has a different debtor-in-possession-with-creditor-control design in its eligible setting. Do not impose the ordinary CIRP management-transfer description on every special process.

Scope and important entry exclusions

Part II principally governs corporate persons within the Code’s scope. Ordinary financial service providers are excluded from the corporate-person definition, subject to the notified section 227 special framework for relevant classes. Do not assume every bank or insurer enters the same ordinary company CIRP.

For ordinary Part II corporate defaults, the notified minimum is ₹1 crore following the 24 March 2020 notification, replacing the earlier ₹1 lakh threshold. Special routes such as eligible MSME pre-packs have their own conditions/threshold framework; do not mechanically apply this general figure to them.

Section 10A bars initiation under sections 7, 9 and 10 for defaults arising in the protected pandemic period beginning 25 March 2020, as extended to 24 March 2021. The permanent bar concerns initiating CIRP for those protected defaults, not every default the company ever commits. A later unprotected default requires its own analysis.

IP, IRP and RP: correct terminology

IP = insolvency professional, the regulated profession. IRP = interim resolution professional, the initial office. RP = resolution professional, the professional continuing/appointed to conduct CIRP. The same individual may serve as IRP and then RP if lawfully selected, but the terms identify different roles/stages.

The syllabus’s broad phrase concerning “insolvency resolution professionals” should not lead to expanding the abbreviation IRP incorrectly. A liquidator is another statutory office. Under the 2026 section 34 change, the CIRP RP is not to be appointed as that debtor’s liquidator; older textbook automatic-continuation descriptions must be dated.

NCLT and appellate architecture

Under section 60, the NCLT having territorial jurisdiction over the corporate person’s registered office is the corporate adjudicating authority. The connected personal/corporate guarantor rules direct relevant matters to the appropriate NCLT in the circumstances specified. Section 60(5) grants broad insolvency-connected jurisdiction, not authority to rewrite every public-law licence decision. Topic 9’s Embassy principle remains relevant: identify the precise issue, not merely the debtor’s insolvency status.

For ordinary IBC appeals: section 61 → NCLAT, 30 days, with up to 15 additional days for sufficient cause; section 62 → Supreme Court on a question of law, 45 days, with up to 15 additional days for sufficient cause. Actual computation depends on the legal trigger/exclusions. Do not use the Companies Act’s different 45/60-day appeal periods merely because both regimes use NCLT/NCLAT.

Debt classification precedes the application

A financial debt generally involves disbursal against consideration for the time value of money and the inclusive statutory categories. Substance matters; not every payment labelled “loan” necessarily satisfies the definition, and certain statutorily included transactions require their own analysis. Allottees in real-estate projects fall within the relevant deemed-financial-debt treatment.

An operational debt concerns goods/services, employment and the specified statutory dues. Classification affects initiation, CoC participation and minimum plan protection. Secured/unsecured is a separate dimension: a creditor is not automatically financial merely because security exists.

Section 7 — Financial-creditor initiation

A financial creditor may apply upon default, individually or jointly in the permitted manner, supplying evidence and proposed IRP details. The relevant allottees/creditor-class applications must meet the additional 100 or 10%, whichever is less, requirement within the statutory class/project framework; it is not a requirement that every bank application must have 100 lenders.

The adjudicating authority examines default, completeness and disciplinary proceedings against the proposed professional. A general assertion that the business might become profitable is not itself the statutory default test. The 2026 substitution of section 7(5) makes admission mandatory on the stated conditions and says no other rejection ground; see the precise update in Chapter 16. Historical discussions of admission discretion must not be treated as overriding that later text.

Sections 8 and 9 — Operational-creditor initiation

The operational creditor first delivers the statutory demand notice or invoice demanding payment. The corporate debtor has ten days to bring payment or the relevant existence of a dispute/pending proceeding to notice. If payment is not received and the statutory conditions permit, the creditor applies under section 9 with required documents.

A genuine pre-existing dispute, not a patently unsupported assertion invented after demand, matters at the admission stage. The NCLT is not to conduct a full civil trial of the disputed supply contract to make the insolvency route a substitute for ordinary debt adjudication. Conversely, a mere denial without a legally meaningful basis does not automatically defeat a proper application.

Example: A supplier demanded payment for machinery. Emails months before demand record serious performance complaints and a documented contractual dispute. That differs from a debtor first inventing “poor quality” after receiving the insolvency notice.

Section 10 — Corporate applicant

The corporate applicant may seek initiation upon default, with required books/information, proposed professional and corporate authorisation, including the special resolution of shareholders or resolution by at least three-fourths of total partners, as applicable. Management cannot lawfully use insolvency simply to defraud creditors or conceal assets. Statutory ineligibility, section 10A and other applicable conditions still apply.

Limitation and fraudulent initiation

Section 238A applies the Limitation Act as far as may be. The analysis often begins with the limitation period for an application and date of default, but acknowledgements, exclusions and legally relevant facts require separate examination. “A debt exists forever, therefore CIRP can always be filed” is wrong.

Section 65 addresses fraudulent/malicious initiation for a purpose other than resolution/liquidation. A statutory insolvency remedy cannot be weaponised by collusion. Allegation is not proof: identify supporting facts and the statutory consequence rather than casually labelling every disputed filing fraud.

Admission is the decisive transition

A demand notice or filing alone does not automatically impose the ordinary section 14 moratorium. Admission triggers the statutory commencement package: moratorium, public announcement and IRP appointment under the relevant provisions. Distinguish filing date, admission/insolvency commencement date and later CoC/plan dates.

14 · IRP/RP and Committee of Creditors: administration versus commercial control

IRP’s initial responsibilities

Under sections 16–20, read with current amendments and regulations, the IRP takes the initial statutory office, publicises commencement, receives/collates claims, collects information on assets/finances/operations, takes control/custody of qualifying assets, constitutes the CoC and endeavours to keep the debtor a going concern.

Section 17 suspends Board powers and vests them in the IRP. Officers/managers report and cooperate; banks act on the relevant instructions. This is a transfer of management powers, not immediate dissolution of the company and not a transfer of every shareholder’s property to the IRP personally.

Asset boundary: Assets owned by third parties and held under trust/contractual arrangements require careful classification under the Code’s exclusions. The professional cannot treat every item physically inside the factory as beneficially owned by the debtor.

Claims and cooperation after 2026

The amended section 18 expressly clarifies that in collating claims the IRP must verify, and if required determine the value of verified claims. This does not turn the RP into a court with unlimited adjudicatory jurisdiction.

Amended section 19 broadens/clarifies cooperation by present/former personnel, promoters, persons associated with management and persons engaged under a contract for service. The explanation extends the relevant IRP references to the RP. Failure to cooperate can be taken to the adjudicating authority through the statutory mechanism; obstruction is not cured by saying the old Board has been suspended.

Constitution of CoC — section 21

Ordinarily the CoC comprises financial creditors. Voting share is based on the relevant financial debt, not headcount. A related-party financial creditor is generally excluded from representation/participation/voting, subject to the statutory exception for the specified regulated creditor whose related-party status arises solely from the identified conversion/substitution circumstances. This guards against insider control over the creditor vote.

Creditors in a class participate through the authorised representative framework. The rules for instructions and class votes matter; the representative is not free to substitute a private preference for the prescribed class mechanism. Where the company has no eligible financial-creditor committee, the specified regulatory alternative composition applies. “CoC always consists exclusively of banks” is therefore inaccurate in several ways.

Operational creditors: attendance versus voting

Operational creditors are not ordinarily voting CoC members. Under section 24, representatives of operational creditors whose aggregate dues meet the 10% debt threshold receive the relevant meeting participation entitlement, without voting. Suspended directors/partners also have the statutory notice/attendance position without thereby regaining management or a CoC vote. Confidentiality and information-access questions must follow the governing law; attendance alone does not make a person a decision-making creditor.

Choosing the RP — section 22

The first CoC meeting is held within seven days of constitution. With the prescribed 66% voting share, creditors may continue the IRP as RP or resolve to replace the professional through the adjudicating-authority/IBBI mechanism. Replacement is an institutional decision subject to eligibility/consent, not a creditor’s unilateral dismissal by email.

RP’s continuing duties — sections 23 and 25

The RP conducts CIRP, manages operations, preserves/protects assets, represents the debtor, maintains claims information, convenes CoC meetings, prepares the information memorandum, invites eligible resolution applicants and places compliant plans before the CoC. Avoidance/fraudulent-trading issues must be examined and brought through the proper statutory process.

Professional independence: The RP owes duties to the process, not only the lender who proposed the name. Confidentiality, conflict management, accurate records and professional competence are essential. The RP may obtain professional assistance but cannot outsource personal statutory responsibility without limit.

Section 28 — Decisions requiring prior CoC approval

Specified significant actions require prior approval, ordinarily by 66% voting share: relevant interim-finance/security decisions, capital/ownership alterations, related-party transactions, specified account debits, delegation, constitutional changes and management/auditor changes within the section’s list. The exact proposal must be matched to the clause and approved limits. Routine operations are not all subject to a fresh supermajority, but major statutory reserved matters cannot be disguised as routine.

Commercial wisdom and its limits

The CoC assesses feasibility, viability, distribution and commercial terms within statutory boundaries. The RP does not choose the winning bidder by personal preference; the NCLT does not run an open-ended business auction merely because another plan might pay more. Yet neither “commercial wisdom” nor a high voting percentage validates an ineligible applicant, non-compliance with mandatory payment protection, fraud or an unlawful plan.

15 · CIRP and moratorium: the operational sequence

Statutory timeline: basic framework, not a guarantee of actual duration

Ordinary CIRP has a 180-day period from admission. A one-time extension up to 90 days may be granted on the prescribed CoC vote and statutory grounds. Section 12’s framework includes the 330-day outer-period formulation, including extension and time taken in legal proceedings, subject to the exceptional judicially recognised treatment of the mandatory limit. Avoid describing 330 days as an automatic extra period available on demand or as incapable of any legally recognised exception.

The 2026 section 33 restoration mechanism adds a separate conditional opportunity before liquidation, explained in the next chapter. Do not simply add every maximum in the statute as an entitlement to delay.

Section 14(1): four principal prohibitions

From the moratorium order:

  1. Institution or continuation of suits/proceedings against the corporate debtor, including execution of judgments, decrees or orders in the specified forums, is prohibited within the provision’s scope.
  2. The debtor cannot transfer, encumber, alienate or dispose of its assets or legal/beneficial interests as prohibited by the section.
  3. Actions to foreclose, recover or enforce security interests, including SARFAESI action, are stayed within the statutory scope.
  4. An owner/lessor cannot recover property occupied by or in possession of the corporate debtor in the prohibited manner.

The aim is a calm period: preserve assets and operations while the collective process decides the company’s future. It is not an adjudication that every creditor’s claim is invalid.

Essential and critical supplies

Specified essential goods/services are protected from termination, suspension or interruption during moratorium under section 14(2). The critical supply protection under section 14(2A) concerns supplies the IRP/RP considers critical to preserving/protecting value and managing the going concern, subject to the statutory current-dues/default and specified exceptions.

A supplier’s pre-CIRP arrears and payment for post-commencement supply are distinct. Moratorium is not a licence to consume everything indefinitely without paying current lawful charges. Analyse the category of supply and applicable condition instead of announcing that “all contracts must continue unchanged.”

Licences and Government grants

The section 14 explanation protects the relevant licence, permit, registration, quota, concession, clearance or similar grant/right from suspension/termination on the ground of insolvency, subject to payment of current dues for use/continuation during moratorium. It does not compel renewal regardless of every other substantive regulatory breach. The 2026 section 31(5) provision adds post-plan protection on its own conditions; do not collapse the two stages.

Important boundaries

  • The moratorium generally concerns the corporate debtor, not an automatic shield for its directors or a surety in a contract of guarantee; section 14(3) expressly matters.
  • It begins on admission/order, not mere demand or filing.
  • Claims are not extinguished merely because enforcement is stayed; creditors must use the insolvency process.
  • Proceedings by the debtor and proceedings against it are not automatically identical; examine the relief and provision.
  • Criminal/penal proceedings require analysis of their real nature and the governing law; do not say every criminal matter is stayed or every proceeding bearing a criminal label is necessarily outside section 14.
  • Third-party ownership, trust property, statutory exceptions and notified transactions require individual analysis.
  • Insolvency status does not give NCLT unlimited jurisdiction over every external regulatory decision.

When the CIRP moratorium ends

The section 14 moratorium continues until completion of CIRP, and ceases on the relevant plan-approval or liquidation order under subsection (4). Under the 2026 liquidation amendments, a liquidation-stage moratorium is separately provided on the specified terms. It is misleading to say that the original CIRP moratorium simply continues forever into liquidation unchanged.

Moratorium hypothetical

Facts: Sunrise Components enters CIRP on 1 July. A secured bank schedules asset enforcement on 5 July. A landlord seeks possession of occupied premises. The managing director faces an independent personal fraud allegation. An essential utility demands payment for current July supply.

Analysis: The bank’s security enforcement and recovery of occupied property engage section 14’s prohibitions. The director cannot assume a personal moratorium merely from the company’s admission. Current supply obligations must be examined under the essential/critical-supply rules, not treated as automatically forgiven. Claims predating CIRP go into the process; the moratorium is not a finding that the bank or landlord never had rights.

From information to plan

The RP prepares an information memorandum under section 29 and invites eligible applicants under the regulatory procedure. Confidentiality undertakings protect commercially sensitive data. Applicants develop plans for funding, restructuring, business operation and stakeholder treatment. Plans may change ownership, debt, capital or business structure; they are not limited to immediate cash repayment of all claims.

Under section 29A, eligibility is screened against specified disqualifications—undischarged insolvency, wilful-defaulter status, the relevant NPA category and cure, convictions, director disqualification, SEBI prohibition, implicated transactions/guarantees and connected-person categories, subject to their precise conditions and exceptions. It is unsafe to replace the section with “all promoters are barred.” Section 240A provides the relevant MSME relaxation for specified clauses, not wholesale suspension of eligibility law.

Plan scrutiny, vote and approval

The RP examines the plan against section 30(2): insolvency-process costs, statutory creditor-payment protections, management, implementation/supervision, compliance with law and other prescribed requirements. The CoC assesses feasibility/viability and approves with 66% voting share. The plan is then submitted for section 31 approval. NCLT approval makes it binding on the statutory stakeholder classes, including the relevant Government/local-authority dues, guarantors and others within the provision.

A resolution plan is not approved merely because the highest bidder offered the most money. Compliance, ability to implement and the lawfully exercised commercial judgment matter. A “haircut” is a reduction compared with claimed/admitted debt under the relevant comparison; it is not proof by itself of fraud or proof that the process succeeded.

Section 32A: conditional separation from earlier wrongdoing

Where statutory conditions concerning plan approval, change of control and the new controller’s independence from specified wrongdoing are met, section 32A provides the stated protection for the corporate debtor and property against prior-offence consequences. Former responsible individuals remain liable, and cooperation with investigating authorities remains important. Do not use “clean slate” to claim that every promoter, guarantor or accused person receives a personal amnesty.

Handbook Modules 5 and 6: why liquidation and avoidance still matter

The handbook treats liquidation as an organised process of realisation, claims and distribution, not the physical demolition of every asset. A business/asset sale can preserve productive value even when the corporate shell ultimately dissolves. But the handbook’s original liquidation procedure is historical where replaced by the 2026 amendments.

Module 6 discusses avoidance transactions and offences. Distressed management may prefer insiders, transfer assets cheaply, obtain extortionate credit or carry on business fraudulently/wrongfully. The law can reverse or compensate for specified transactions, restore value and impose relevant responsibility. Avoidance is not identical to criminal conviction: each cause of action has its own conditions, look-back period, defences and remedy.

Liquidation and the waterfall

Liquidation can follow failure to obtain an approved plan, the CoC’s qualifying decision, or the statutory consequences of plan contravention. The liquidator forms/administers the liquidation estate, realises assets and distributes under section 53, subject to secured-creditor choices under section 52 and the current moratorium/approval framework.

The broad waterfall remains a crucial concept: process/liquidation costs first; then the specified workmen’s dues and relinquishing secured creditors; then employee dues; unsecured financial debt; the specified Government dues and residual secured shortfall; remaining debts; preference shareholders; equity shareholders/partners. Statutory look-back periods and equal-ranking rules matter. The 2026 text further addresses priority and contractual arrangements, so an actual distribution must be calculated against the amended section rather than a mnemonic alone.

Section 52 choice: A secured creditor may relinquish security and enter the relevant waterfall tier or realise security in accordance with the statutory framework. It cannot simultaneously recover the full claim twice. Costs, workmen-related contributions and shortfall treatment require the applicable legal calculation.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026, Act 6 of 2026, received assent on 6 April 2026. S.O. 2625(E), dated 22 May 2026, appointed 26 May 2026 for specified provisions. It did not commence every part of the Act simply because assent had occurred.

Update A — Admission under substituted section 7(5)

The adjudicating authority must, within 14 days of receipt, admit where default has occurred, the application is complete and no disciplinary proceeding is pending against the proposed RP; otherwise it rejects on the statutory basis, with a seven-day defect-cure opportunity before rejection for incompleteness. Delay requires written reasons. It shall not reject on any other ground. The specified financial-institution default record with an IU is sufficient for ascertainment under the amended formulation.

Exam effect: Do not rely on an older open-ended admission-discretion summary without mentioning the substituted mandatory text. Evidence, completeness, limitation and applicable statutory bars still need proper analysis; “mandatory” is not “admit every unsupported demand.”

Update B — Substituted section 12A: narrower withdrawal window

An application for withdrawal is made by the RP, with 90% CoC voting approval. It is not permitted before constitution of the CoC or after the first invitation for submission of a resolution plan. The adjudicating authority should decide within 30 days, recording reasons for delay.

This is materially different from older textbook/case discussions of pre-CoC withdrawal and flexibility after invitation. It does not mean parties are prohibited from settling an ordinary dispute before any CIRP is admitted; it governs withdrawal of the admitted process under the substituted provision. Jagatramka’s 2021 discussion must be dated accordingly.

Update C — Continuing CoC supervision in liquidation

New section 21(11) provides that the CoC constituted under section 21 also supervises liquidation, with sections 21/24 applying as context requires. Specified other creditor classes may attend without voting. The explanatory transition covers newly initiated liquidation and ongoing liquidation where no section 54 dissolution application has been made, with the committee continuing for the remaining process.

Amended section 35(2) expressly assigns CoC supervision. The committee is not merely an optional historical stakeholder consultation committee under older regulations. Operational execution remains with the liquidator.

Update D — Different liquidator and replacement

Under amended section 34, the NCLT seeks IBBI’s recommendation and appoints an eligible IP. The CIRP RP cannot be appointed/replaced as the liquidator of that debtor. IBBI proposes an eligible different professional with consent within the stated ten-day reference period. The RP must hand over records.

New section 34A allows CoC replacement of the liquidator by 66% voting share, with written consent of the proposed replacement, an application to the NCLT and the no-pending-disciplinary-proceeding condition. An older sentence that “the RP automatically becomes liquidator unless replaced” is no longer a safe current statement.

Update E — Plan protection and approval mechanics

The amended section 30(2)(ba) protects financial creditors not voting in favour by a minimum based on the lower of the specified liquidation-waterfall amount and the amount obtained by distributing the plan value under section 53 priority. Do not apply this lower-of formula to operational creditors, whose separate section 30(2)(b) protection remains a distinct test. The amendment includes express transitional exclusions where the specified plan/liquidation milestones had already occurred on/before commencement.

Section 30 also provides for an implementation/supervision committee consisting of the relevant professional, creditor representatives and resolution applicant under specified conditions, and requires CoC reasons for approval.

Amended section 31 introduces the stated 30-day decision period, written reasons for delay, an opportunity to notify the CoC to rectify defects before rejection, and a conditional mechanism to approve implementation first and distribution thereafter within the prescribed thirty-day framework on the requisite 66% CoC-backed RP application. It also moves the specified CCI-approval timing to before submission of the plan to the adjudicating authority.

Update F — Grants, extinguishment and persons not released

New section 31(5) protects the specified public/regulatory grants/rights during their remaining subsistence after plan approval, subject to compliance with obligations for that remaining period.

New section 31(6) expressly addresses extinguishment of prior claims against the corporate debtor/assets unless otherwise provided in the plan, and bars proceedings based on those claims including assessment. Its explanations preserve claims/proceedings against promoters/management/control persons, guarantors and persons with joint or joint-and-several liability, and address extinguishment of a paying co-obligor’s indemnity against the debtor. The stated retrospective deeming is subject to matters attaining finality.

Crucial distinction: A debtor’s approved-plan protection is not automatic release of the personal guarantor. Nor does this monetary-claim rule substitute for every criminal-liability condition in section 32A.

Update G — Restoration before liquidation and new liquidation moratorium

Amended section 33 permits the adjudicating authority, before ordering liquidation on the specified grounds, to consider a 66%-CoC application to restore CIRP, with a period not exceeding 120 days and the stated stage/conditions. Restoration under that mechanism is available only once. The transition covers eligible existing CIRPs where liquidation has not yet been ordered, not every already-liquidated debtor.

The liquidation order now declares the specified moratorium applying section 14(1)(a) and (c), read with section 14(3), mutatis mutandis, subject to section 52 and the notification exception. The old section 33(5) is omitted; substituted subsection (6) requires leave for the liquidator to commence/continue the relevant suits/proceedings on behalf of the debtor. These are not identical to saying all four CIRP prohibitions simply survive unchanged.

Update H — Claims and the 180-day liquidation period

Amended section 35 maintains an updated claims list; sections 38–42 are omitted in the new framework, with express preservation for liquidation/voluntary liquidation initiated on or before commencement as specified. Do not apply the omission retroactively to erase every pending legacy claims process.

Substituted section 54(1) requires completion of liquidation and a dissolution application within 180 days from liquidation commencement, extendable by the adjudicating authority for sufficient reasons by not more than 90 days. Provisions address continuing avoidance/proceeds litigation and appropriate distribution arrangements without needlessly holding dissolution hostage. This is a separate liquidation clock from the 180-day CIRP clock.

17 · ESG: meaning, materiality and sustainability in business

ESG is a framework, not one statute or one score

ESG means Environmental, Social and Governance factors used to evaluate and manage an enterprise’s conduct, impacts, risks and opportunities. It appears in board strategy, investor analysis, lender decisions, supply-chain requirements, reporting and regulation. Different users may define materiality and weight factors differently; there is no single universal ESG score which conclusively proves legal compliance or sustainability.

Sustainability concerns maintaining the ecological and social conditions for present and future well-being while conducting economically viable activity. ESG can operationalise parts of that ambition through governance, metrics and accountability. But a favourable rating does not necessarily show that a company’s absolute environmental footprint has fallen or that all affected people are protected.

Environmental pillar

Environmental matters include climate emissions, energy use and transition, water withdrawal/consumption/discharge, waste and circularity, pollution, biodiversity, land use and resource efficiency.

  • Physical climate risks: heat, floods, drought and extreme weather can damage facilities, disrupt workers and suppliers and reduce productivity.
  • Transition risks: policy, technology, litigation and consumer shifts associated with decarbonisation can change costs and strand assets.
  • Opportunities: cleaner processes, efficiency, resilient products and reduced resource dependence can create value.

Emissions vocabulary: Scope 1 concerns direct emissions from owned/controlled sources; Scope 2 purchased-energy indirect emissions; Scope 3 other indirect value-chain emissions. Boundaries, methods and assurance matter. Reporting only factory electricity while ignoring a material supply-chain footprint can produce an incomplete picture.

Intensity versus absolute performance: Emissions per unit of product can fall while total emissions rise because output expands. Both indicators may be relevant; one should not be used to conceal the other.

Social pillar

Social factors include working conditions, wages, occupational health/safety, diversity and equal opportunity, human rights, child/forced labour, supply-chain labour practices, customer health/privacy, community impacts and access/inclusion.

A company may have excellent employee policies but procure from suppliers with serious labour abuses. The value-chain perspective asks how procurement incentives, contracts, monitoring and remediation affect those outcomes. A charitable school cannot offset forced labour in production as a legal or ethical defence.

Just transition: Environmental changes can impose costs on workers and communities dependent on older industries. Sustainable strategy should consider retraining, consultation, alternative livelihoods and distribution of burdens rather than treating affected people as an afterthought.

Governance pillar

Governance concerns board competence/independence, accountability, conflicts, remuneration, risk controls, audit, anti-corruption, shareholder rights, responsible tax conduct, whistleblowing and the reliability of disclosure.

It is the decision-making infrastructure of E and S. A carbon target without Board ownership, budget and verification may be little more than advertising. A governance structure that suppresses complaints or rewards only short-term output can undermine otherwise impressive policies.

Three useful materiality perspectives

Perspective Main question Example
Financial materiality How does an ESG issue affect enterprise value/risk/return? Water scarcity increases manufacturing costs
Impact materiality How does the enterprise affect people and environment? The plant depletes water available to nearby communities
Double materiality Consider both directions, recognising connections Community water harm triggers regulation, conflict and operating risk

These are conceptual tools; the exact reporting obligation depends on the applicable standard/jurisdiction. Do not assert that every Indian disclosure rule automatically adopts every feature of an overseas double-materiality regime.

How ESG is applied in modern business

  1. Governance: identify Board/committee and executive responsibility.
  2. Stakeholder mapping: identify affected workers, communities, customers, suppliers, investors and regulators.
  3. Risk/impact assessment: determine material issues across operations and value chain.
  4. Baseline: establish reliable data boundaries and measurement methods.
  5. Strategy and capital allocation: fund operational improvements rather than merely publish intentions.
  6. Targets: use time-bound, measurable, credible milestones with both leading and outcome indicators.
  7. Due diligence and controls: identify, prevent/mitigate, monitor and account for adverse impacts; provide appropriate remediation.
  8. Incentives: align remuneration and procurement with sustainable conduct while controlling gaming.
  9. Reporting and external assessment: explain methods, limitations, progress and setbacks.
  10. Review: revise strategy in light of evidence, stakeholder input and changing law.

A textile-company example across all three pillars

Environmental: meter water, reduce hazardous discharge, verify wastewater performance and assess absolute/production-adjusted emissions.

Social: ensure safe conditions, lawful wages, credible grievance channels, freedom from forced labour and realistic delivery schedules for suppliers.

Governance: assign responsibility, verify data, link incentives carefully and investigate complaints independently.

CSR connection: A separate community education project may count under section 135 if eligible. Pollution-law compliance and required worker protections do not become CSR merely because they support ESG. IBC connection: if the company later enters CIRP, environmental obligations, permit continuity and business viability must be analysed under their actual laws; a rescue plan cannot casually assume regulatory risk disappears.

Greenwashing and measurement weaknesses

Greenwashing includes vague “eco-friendly” assertions without evidence, selective presentation, hiding poor absolute performance behind intensity gains, presenting legal compliance as exceptional philanthropy, overstating offsets, or treating a narrow assurance opinion as validation of every sustainability claim.

Ratings can diverge because providers select different indicators, boundaries, weights and treatment of controversies. Aggregation may allow strong governance scores to compensate numerically for serious environmental harm. The correct response is not to reject all measurement, but to examine methodology, comparability, evidence, conflicts and real-world results.

18 · Prescribed reading: MacNeil and Esser’s financial-to-entity model

Iain MacNeil & Irene-marié Esser • European Business Organization Law Review 23 (2022), 9–45 • DOI 10.1007/s40804-021-00234-y

Nature of the reading

“From a Financial to an Entity Model of ESG” is an academic article developing a normative corporate-governance argument. It has no litigating parties, pleadings or judicial decree. To honour the user’s request for depth without fabricating case features, this dossier explains its problem, thesis, opposing model, mechanisms, critique and implications. Its references to anticipated European developments around 2021 belong to its historical writing context, not verified September 2026 European law.

The central problem

The authors argue that the dominant financial model of ESG places too much reliance on the investment chain to produce sustainable corporate behaviour. Institutional investors, asset managers, ratings, benchmarks and disclosure translate ESG into portfolio decisions. This can help integrate risk, but may subordinate operational corporate accountability to investor preferences and imperfect market signals.

The crucial distinction is between managing exposure to an externality and changing the activity that produces it. A fund can reduce its portfolio’s reported carbon exposure by selling shares in a polluter. The factory may still emit the same amount under another owner. Financial-market adjustment is not automatically real-world environmental improvement.

From CSR to the financial model of ESG

Traditional CSR often emphasised a company’s voluntary responsibility and social contribution. ESG developed a more investment-oriented language: non-financial factors could affect risk, return and long-term financial performance. That language attracted mainstream finance, but also shaped the objective: sustainability could be pursued primarily where financially material to investors rather than because of its effects on stakeholders.

The authors do not simply say “CSR was good, ESG is bad.” Their analysis traces a shift in focus, channel, implementation, metrics and reporting, then proposes another shift locating responsibility more firmly within the enterprise.

The financial model: how it works

Focus: portfolio risk and return, mediated by indicators, benchmarks and indices.

Channel: institutional investors and the investment chain—asset owners, managers, advisers, ratings and stewardship.

Implementation: portfolio selection, exclusions, tilting, engagement and voting.

Metrics: multiple methodologies, custodians and legal effects; limited verification and another layer of complexity from ratings.

Reporting: company disclosures and financial-channel disclosures help transmit information and investment preferences.

This model can price risks and exert pressure. Yet the chain contains principal-agent problems: the ultimate saver’s preferences may be imperfectly transmitted through managers and products. Short horizons, fees, mandates, benchmark tracking and divergent ratings can interfere with sustainability aims.

Why market-based ESG may underperform

  1. Financial materiality is narrower than social harm. A serious externality may remain profitable if its cost is borne by others.
  2. Portfolio change is not operational change. Selling an asset can alter investor exposure without reducing the underlying harmful activity.
  3. Data is not action. A company can improve disclosure faster than it improves environmental/social performance.
  4. Fragmented metrics weaken accountability. Different ratings may support inconsistent claims of leadership.
  5. Investment-chain incentives can dilute the mandate. The actor bearing the long-term harm may be far from the intermediary making short-term decisions.
  6. Boards may become reactive. They respond to investor signals rather than own a coherent sustainability strategy integrated with operations.
  7. Coverage is limited. Public capital-market channels may not adequately govern private or otherwise less market-visible enterprises and systemic externalities.

The proposed entity model

The entity model places primary accountability within the corporate entity, particularly board decision-making, with stakeholder integration and mechanisms to internalise externalities. The board should own operational sustainability alongside strategy, remuneration and resource allocation.

Internalising externalities means bringing costs/impacts otherwise imposed on outsiders into corporate decision-making and responsibility. It does not mean the company must literally pay every imaginable social cost regardless of causal connection; the proposed governance and due-diligence mechanisms organise how relevant impacts are identified and addressed.

Mechanism 1 — A duty of due diligence

The authors discuss due diligence as a business process/procedural requirement, a standard of conduct, or a combination. A serious duty would require identifying, preventing/mitigating and accounting for adverse impacts, rather than only checking whether a report contains a paragraph about risk.

Process alone: A company performs a supplier questionnaire and files it. If the questionnaire ignores obvious abuses or no action follows, formal procedure may fail to protect people.

Conduct plus process: The company uses evidence, investigates credible risks, changes procurement where necessary, monitors outcomes and provides appropriate remediation. The precise legal liability standard would depend on the enacted regime; the article’s proposal is not itself a binding Indian cause of action.

The authors see this as a more focused mechanism than merely adding broad sustainability words to directors’ duties while established shareholder-primacy pressures remain unchanged.

Mechanism 2 — Stakeholder board committee

A stakeholder committee can give relevant interests an institutional channel into governance and support due diligence. Its value depends on representation, information, authority and connection to actual Board decisions. A ceremonial committee meeting once a year without access to operational evidence would not achieve the proposed shift.

This is not the same as India’s statutory CSR Committee. The CSR Committee recommends/monitors a defined CSR policy and expenditure framework. The article’s stakeholder committee is a proposed mechanism for wider ESG governance and externality internalisation. Do not claim section 135 already enacts the whole entity model.

Mechanism 3 — Strategy, remuneration and operational metrics

Boards should connect sustainability with investment, operations and remuneration. If executives are rewarded only for short-term production while costs of safety or pollution are deferred, a separate ESG statement may be ineffective.

ESG-linked remuneration also needs scrutiny: easily met targets, selective baselines or opaque discretion can reward superficial improvement. Relevant metrics should reflect operational impact, not merely publication of a policy or inclusion in an index.

Mechanism 4 — Reporting is supporting infrastructure

The entity model does not abolish reporting. It reduces reliance on disclosure as the primary transmission mechanism and aligns reporting with due diligence, strategy and actual decisions. Investors still need information and stewardship retains a residual role. The proposal is therefore not “ban ESG funds” or “ignore all financial materiality.” It changes the centre of accountability.

Critical evaluation

Strengths: clearer Board responsibility; closer connection to actual impacts; broader reach than capital-market screening; more structured stakeholder voice; integration with incentives; less faith that perfect disclosure alone changes behaviour.

Difficulties: selecting legitimate stakeholder representatives; resolving conflicts among workers, communities, consumers and investors; avoiding box-ticking due diligence; measuring causal impact across long supply chains; enforcement cost; managerial discretion and accountability; risks of overburdening smaller businesses; interaction with public regulation such as carbon pricing.

Balanced conclusion: The article offers a persuasive governance correction, not a claim that company boards should replace democratically accountable regulators. Entity-level responsibility and market discipline may complement legal standards, public policy and stakeholder remedies.

An exam-ready application

A fund sells shares in a coal-intensive company and reports a lower portfolio carbon footprint. That is a financial-model action; it may change finance costs, but does not itself prove lower emissions. An entity-model response asks whether the company’s board changes investment and production, protects affected workers, conducts due diligence, measures absolute impacts and is accountable for progress. The strongest answer recognises that investor pressure can support this change, but should not be mistaken for the change itself.

19 · Indian ESG application: company duties, BRSR and CSR distinctions

Existing company-law connection: section 166(2)

A director must act in good faith to promote the company’s objects for members as a whole and in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders and community, and for environmental protection under section 166(2)’s formulation. This gives Indian company law an express stakeholder/environmental reference. It does not automatically create the precise stakeholder-committee/due-diligence regime proposed by MacNeil–Esser or grant every stakeholder the same enforcement route.

Board risk management, audit, related-party controls and truthful reporting also support ESG governance. Companies Act duties, securities disclosures, environmental licences, labour protections and consumer/data laws remain distinct but connected obligations.

BRSR: reporting architecture

SEBI’s Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) replaced the earlier BRR framework, becoming mandatory for the top 1,000 listed entities by market capitalisation from FY 2022–23 under the applicable listing framework, with the earlier voluntary transition. This is not a statement that every Indian company must file BRSR solely because it satisfies the CSR threshold.

BRSR contains:

  • Section A: general disclosures about the entity, products/operations, employees and related profile information;
  • Section B: management and process disclosures—policies, governance and implementation;
  • Section C: principle-wise performance disclosures aligned with the nine National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct principles, using essential and leadership indicators.

The nine principles cover ethical/transparency/accountability conduct; safe/sustainable goods/services; employee and value-chain worker well-being; stakeholder interests; human rights; environment; responsible public-policy engagement; inclusive growth/equitable development; and responsible consumer engagement.

BRSR Core: focused assessment/assurance

BRSR Core is a specified subset of key indicators within the wider BRSR framework, not a substitute for all reporting. It covers key areas such as emissions, water, energy, circularity, employee well-being, gender diversity, inclusive development, fairness with customers/suppliers and openness of business under the prescribed indicator structure.

The 28 March 2025 SEBI circular, SEBI/HO/CFD/CFD-PoD-1/P/CIR/2025/42, permits assessment or assurance for the relevant Core framework and retains the phased entity coverage:

The 2025 circular introduces voluntary green-credit disclosures under Principle 6 leadership indicators from FY 2024–25, addressing the entity and the top-ten value-chain-partner context in the stated formulation. A green credit is not automatically a carbon offset, an ESG rating, or eligible CSR expenditure. Its legal nature and any CSR treatment require separate analysis.

PYQ 1 — 2017 Q8(a): “Corporate Social Responsibility”

Model answer, appropriate to the 2017 legislative setting:

Corporate social responsibility expresses the principle that a company’s responsibilities extend beyond producing profits to the social and environmental context in which it operates. The Companies Act, 2013 introduced a statutory framework under section 135, read with the CSR Policy Rules, 2014 and Schedule VII, effective from 1 April 2014.

In the original framework relevant to this historical question, the section applied to companies meeting the prescribed ₹500 crore net-worth, ₹1,000 crore turnover or ₹5 crore net-profit threshold, using the then-applicable financial-year wording and Rules. The alternatives were disjunctive; all three did not need to be satisfied. Later amendments changed the temporal formulation to the immediately preceding financial year, so that phrase should not be retroactively assumed to have been the original 2017 text.

The company had to constitute a CSR Committee with the statutory composition and applicable rule-based exceptions. The committee formulated and recommended a policy covering Schedule VII activities, recommended expenditure and monitored the policy. The Board approved and disclosed the policy and ensured implementation. The spending norm was at least 2% of average net profits of the three immediately preceding financial years, calculated according to the Act rather than as a percentage of turnover. Local areas and areas surrounding operations were to receive preference.

Schedule VII included fields such as poverty/hunger relief, healthcare, education, gender equality, environmental sustainability, heritage, eligible sports, rural development and specified funds/research activities as then framed. Ordinary business expenditure, political contributions and employee-only benefits could not simply be relabelled CSR under the applicable Rules. The framework sought organised, accountable social contribution rather than occasional promotional philanthropy.

At that historical stage, where the prescribed amount was not spent the Board had to specify reasons in its report—the familiar spend-or-explain formulation. The later mandatory Unspent CSR Account/fund-transfer machinery, present penalty formula, impact-assessment rules and 2026 instrument route must not be stated as though they already governed the 2017 paper.

CSR is important because business depends on communities, natural resources and stakeholders and may generate external costs. Its success nevertheless depends on genuine projects, transparent implementation and outcomes, not merely a two-per-cent arithmetic exercise.

Current-law addendum for a present-day revision answer: Today use the immediately preceding-year trigger, the applicable Board substitute where required spend does not exceed ₹50 lakh, current Rules and continuing-unspent-account qualification. Distinguish ongoing-project transfers within 30 days to the special account from non-ongoing transfers within six months to a Schedule VII fund. Add the civil-penalty formula and, if relevant, the 2026 Rule 4A route. This addendum is not part of the historical 2017 law.

PYQ 2 — 2015 Q8(b): “Corporate social responsibility”

Model answer, short-note form with historical accuracy:

CSR is the responsibility of an enterprise to contribute to society and consider the wider effects of its activity. India gave it a specific statutory form through section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013, supported by the 2014 CSR Rules and Schedule VII. It was a significant development because qualifying companies were required to organise CSR through board-level governance and a statutory expenditure norm.

The financial triggers were net worth ₹500 crore, turnover ₹1,000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore, read with the then-operative applicability wording. A qualifying company constituted the CSR Committee in accordance with the statutory/rule-based composition requirements. Its functions were to recommend a Schedule VII-based policy, recommend spending and monitor implementation. The Board approved the policy, disclosed it through the prescribed channels and ensured the approved activities were undertaken.

The Board was to ensure expenditure of at least 2% of average net profits during the three immediately preceding financial years. This was not 2% of sales. The company was to give preference to local areas and places around its operations, without treating local preference as a universal ban on lawful projects elsewhere. If expenditure fell short, the Board’s report had to explain why under the then-existing framework.

Eligible subjects included education, health, poverty alleviation, gender equality, environmental protection and other Schedule VII heads as then amended. A marketing expense or fulfilment of another ordinary business responsibility could not automatically be booked as CSR. The legal framework needed to be distinguished from philanthropy at large: eligibility, governance and disclosure mattered.

The rationale was to link corporate growth with inclusive development and responsible use of resources. Criticism included reduced managerial discretion and the risk of box-ticking or publicity-led spending. A balanced view is that statutory CSR can mobilise resources and accountability, but meaningful impact depends on responsible project design and monitoring.

Historical exclusions from this answer: Do not add PM CARES, the later disaster-management head, the ₹50 lakh committee exemption, present unspent transfers or 2026 Rule 4A as if they existed in 2015. A present-day answer should explicitly label those later developments rather than falsify chronology.

PYQ 3 — “2015 Business Association II”, Q6(b): CSR

Model answer, fuller version for the separately headed source entry:

Corporate social responsibility concerns the relationship between corporate enterprise and the society which supplies its labour, resources, markets and institutional support. Its normative foundation is that corporate decision-making should not be indifferent to external costs and community welfare. The 2013 Act moved beyond a purely voluntary model by creating section 135, effective with the 2014 Rules from 1 April 2014.

The provision selected companies by alternative financial thresholds: ₹500 crore net worth, ₹1,000 crore turnover or ₹5 crore net profit, subject to the contemporary wording and Rules. It placed responsibility on a CSR Committee and the Board. The committee recommended a policy of eligible activities, proposed expenditure and monitored the policy. The Board considered those recommendations, approved and disclosed the policy and ensured implementation.

The expenditure benchmark was 2% of average net profits for the preceding three financial years, using statutory computation. Local-area preference recognised the company’s immediate social setting but did not reduce CSR to unrestricted local sponsorship. Schedule VII channelled spending towards defined social/developmental subjects such as education, health, environment and disadvantaged groups. The 2014 Rules supplied implementation and exclusion safeguards. Governance mattered because a donation beneficial to a promoter’s reputation or commercial sales could differ from a genuine qualifying social programme.

In the original regime relevant to a 2015 paper, a shortfall required reasons in the Board’s report. It is historically inaccurate to insert the later mandatory transfer-account regime into that account. The legal evolution since then can be mentioned only as a separately dated postscript in a modern revision exercise.

CSR supports inclusive growth, stakeholder trust and the use of corporate organisational skills for social purposes. But statutory spending cannot replace compliance with labour, environmental and other laws. Nor does it immunise corporate wrongdoing. The best assessment therefore combines the legal requirements with a critical concern for measurable outcomes, transparency and genuine public benefit.

Practice 1 — Explain amended section 135 and treatment of unspent CSR

Model answer: Section 135 applies where the immediately preceding financial year shows any one of net worth ₹500 crore, turnover ₹1,000 crore or net profit ₹5 crore. The default CSR Committee has three or more directors including an independent director; the Act/Rules provide exceptions, and required spend not exceeding ₹50 lakh ordinarily permits the Board to perform committee functions. Continuing Unspent CSR Account obligations require separate attention under Rule 3.

The committee recommends policy, expenditure and monitors; the Board approves, discloses and ensures implementation. The annual obligation is at least 2% of the relevant three-year average section 198-based net profit, with the shorter-history rule. Projects must fit Schedule VII and avoid the Rules’ exclusions. The annual action plan, eligible agency/CSR-1, utilisation, overhead and reporting rules operationalise the obligation.

Unspent money is divided by project character. Non-ongoing unspent amounts go to a Schedule VII fund within six months of year end. Ongoing amounts go to the financial-year-specific Unspent CSR Account in a scheduled bank within thirty days and must be spent within the next statutory three-financial-year window; the residue goes to a Schedule VII fund within thirty days after that window. Merely explaining the shortfall does not replace these transfers.

For the specified defaults the company penalty is lower of twice the transfer amount and ₹1 crore; each officer in default faces lower of one-tenth and ₹2 lakh. This is not automatic imprisonment. Excess spending can be set off for the immediately succeeding three financial years on Rule 7 conditions; project surplus is not business profit or the same credit. Administrative overheads are capped at 5% of total expenditure. Impact-assessment thresholds and the current higher-of ceiling apply; the 2026 Rule 4A instrument route is limited to 10% and has its own conditions.

Conclusion: CSR compliance requires lawful activity, accountable use, timely transfers and accurate reporting, not merely approving a budget equal to 2%.

Practice 2 — “A company has neither body nor mind and therefore cannot commit crime.” Discuss

Model answer: The proposition wrongly converts artificial personality into immunity. Corporate prosecution first requires an offence applicable to the company, proof of prohibited conduct and any required mental element. A company acts through human beings; legally appropriate attribution can connect their conduct and knowledge to it.

In Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement, the Constitution Bench rejected immunity based on inability to serve mandatory imprisonment where imprisonment and fine were prescribed. The executable fine could be imposed on the corporation; the impossibility of prison did not erase the prohibition. The case did not settle every mens rea attribution issue or itself convict the bank of all underlying allegations.

In Iridium India Telecom v. Motorola, the Court rejected the argument that corporate bodies cannot possess mens rea. The relevant directing mind and will may be attributed to the company. It also reversed premature quashing where allegations of dishonest inducement required proper examination. Restoration of prosecution was not proof of guilt, and honest commercial failure remained distinct from fraud.

However, Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI prevents automatic reverse attribution. Company liability does not make every director guilty. The individual must have the requisite active involvement/intent or fall within an express statutory vicarious-liability provision whose conditions are met. Section 2(60), section 149(12) and the particular charging clause must be read carefully.

Conclusion: Separate corporate prosecutability, corporate mens rea and individual responsibility. Each has a legal basis and evidentiary burden; neither blanket immunity nor automatic director guilt is correct.

Practice 3 — Explain section 230 and minority protection

Model answer: Section 230 permits a compromise/arrangement between the company and creditors/members or proper classes. The company, creditor, member or relevant liquidator applies. The Tribunal directs meetings after material disclosures, including financial position, investigations, capital reduction and special restructuring information where applicable.

Stakeholders receive scheme terms, explanatory disclosures, valuation material and relevant interests/effects. Regulators receive notice and have thirty days for representations. Classes must permit fair consultation among sufficiently similar legal rights; a manipulated class vote cannot substitute for substantive protection.

Approval requires a majority of persons representing at least three-fourths in value of the relevant class voting, followed by Tribunal sanction. Objection standing of at least 10% shareholding or 5% total debt is separate from voting approval. Creditor-meeting dispensation under subsection (9) requires at least 90% value agreement confirmed by affidavits; it is not a universal shareholder waiver.

The Tribunal examines legality, disclosure, class fairness and bona fides without simply substituting its commercial preference. Auditor certification under section 133 is mandatory. Buy-back must comply with section 68; share-right variation, dissenters’ protection and takeover rules retain their respective safeguards. The order is filed within thirty days of receipt and remains subject to section 231 supervision.

Conclusion: The scheme is a collective-binding mechanism constrained by disclosure, proper classes, dual voting requirements and independent sanction. Majority approval cannot legalise confiscation, statutory breach or insolvency circumvention.

Practice 4 — Distinguish sections 230, 231 and 232

Model answer: Section 230 creates the general procedure for a compromise/arrangement: application, disclosures, class meetings, regulator notices, vote, sanction and binding force. Section 231 concerns what happens after sanction: supervision, directions and necessary implementation modifications. If satisfactory implementation is impossible with or without modifications and the company cannot pay debts under the scheme, winding up may be ordered with the stated section 273 consequence.

Section 232 addresses reconstruction, merger/amalgamation and division-related transfers. It applies section 230(3)–(6) with necessary adaptations and adds draft terms, directors’ reports, exchange-ratio/valuation material and supplementary accounts where the six-month test is met. Its order can vest property/liabilities, allot securities, cancel prohibited self-holdings, continue proceedings, transfer employees and dissolve the transferor without winding up. Listed/unlisted and dissenting-member safeguards apply. The accounting certificate, thirty-day filing and annual completion statement remain important.

Conclusion: Think 230 = create and bind; 231 = supervise and enforce; 232 = implement structural integration/transfers. They are related stages/functions, not interchangeable numerical references.

Practice 5 — Can an ineligible promoter use section 230 in IBC liquidation?

Model answer: The first question is whether the person is actually ineligible under section 29A, including relevant exceptions; promoter status alone is insufficient. If ineligibility is established, Arun Kumar Jagatramka governs the liquidation-scheme workaround.

GNCL entered liquidation after CIRP failed. Its ineligible promoter proposed a section 230 scheme, initially permitted at the meeting stage by NCLT but barred by NCLAT. The Supreme Court considered the connected proceedings and upheld Regulation 2B’s proviso. The appellants relied on section 230’s independent text, different consequences from a clean-slate resolution plan, revival and the section 12A settlement analogy. Respondents emphasised coherent insolvency policy and the need to prevent backdoor re-entry.

The Court held that section 230, when used in IBC liquidation, must be read with sections 29A and 35(1)(f). A prohibited person cannot secure indirectly what resolution and liquidation-sale rules prohibit directly. The regulation implemented that policy. The judgment does not ban all promoters or solvent-company schemes, and a creditor majority cannot cure statutory ineligibility.

For a present transaction, separately check current regulations and 2026 transitions; the case’s older section 12A procedural discussion is not the substituted current withdrawal rule. Conclusion: An ineligible promoter cannot rely solely on the section 230 label to defeat the IBC bar.

Practice 6 — Explain the CoC and RP, including current developments

Model answer: The RP administers CIRP and manages the debtor under the Code; the CoC exercises collective commercial decision-making. The IRP initially takes control, receives/verifies claims, gathers information and constitutes the committee. The CoC ordinarily consists of financial creditors, with related-party exclusions and class-representative/alternative-composition provisions. Operational-creditor attendance in the specified threshold case does not confer a vote.

The committee’s voting share reflects debt, not headcount. Ordinary decisions require 51% unless a different provision applies; major decisions such as plan approval, specified reserved actions and professional replacement require 66%; section 12A withdrawal requires 90% within the current window. The RP must act independently, preserve assets, maintain the going concern, prepare information and place compliant plans before creditors. Neither role replaces NCLT’s legal scrutiny.

The 2026 amendments clarify claim verification and cooperation, require reasons for plan approval, create plan-supervision machinery and continue CoC supervision into liquidation on the stated transitional basis. The CIRP RP can no longer simply be described as automatically becoming liquidator; current section 34 requires a different eligible professional, and section 34A permits CoC-backed replacement with 66% approval through NCLT.

Conclusion: The Code separates operational professionalism, commercial judgment and adjudication. This separation promotes expertise and accountability; “creditor control” is not unlimited creditor power.

Practice 7 — Explain moratorium and its limits

Model answer: The section 14 moratorium begins with the admission/order in CIRP, not merely a demand notice or application. It supplies a calm period protecting collective value from a creditor race. It prohibits specified proceedings against the debtor, prohibited asset dispositions, security enforcement and owner/lessor recovery of occupied/in-possession property.

Essential and critical supplies receive statutory protection, subject to the relevant current-dues and other conditions. Public grants/rights cannot be terminated merely on insolvency in the explanation’s circumstances, but every regulatory obligation is not waived. Claims survive for treatment in the process; moratorium is not debt forgiveness.

The protection is not automatically personal to directors or guarantors. Third-party assets, notified exceptions and the real nature of proceedings require attention. A criminal label alone does not settle every moratorium question. NCLT’s insolvency jurisdiction also does not become unlimited public-law jurisdiction.

The CIRP moratorium ceases on the relevant plan approval/liquidation event under subsection (4). The 2026 law creates a separate, specified liquidation moratorium subject to section 52 and exceptions. Conclusion: Always identify the person, property, proceeding, date and statutory exception rather than asserting a blanket stay of everything connected to the company.

Practice 8 — Explain and evaluate the entity model of ESG

Model answer: MacNeil and Esser contrast a financial model centred on investor risk/return with an entity model centred on Board responsibility, stakeholder integration and operational impact. The financial model transmits ESG through institutional investors, asset managers, ratings, benchmarks, portfolio choices and disclosure. It can price risks and motivate engagement, but may change portfolio exposure without reducing real-world harm.

The entity model seeks to internalise externalities through corporate decision-making. A due-diligence duty, stakeholder committee, integration with strategy/remuneration and operationally useful metrics would make the company more directly accountable. Reporting remains important but ceases to carry the entire burden of producing sustainability; stewardship retains a residual role.

Its strengths are a clearer accountable actor, attention to impacts beyond financial materiality and stronger linkage to actual operations. Challenges include stakeholder representation, conflicting interests, cost, measurement, enforcement and box-ticking. It complements rather than necessarily displaces public regulation and investor pressure.

In India, section 166(2), CSR and BRSR provide relevant but distinct points of connection. They do not already enact every element of the authors’ proposed model. A company may comply with the two-per-cent CSR requirement while causing environmental harm; an impressive BRSR does not prove remediation. Conclusion: The model shifts the question from “which securities look sustainable?” towards “how is this enterprise governed to reduce harm and create sustainable outcomes?”

Practice 9 — Integrated problem: CSR, merger, fraud and ESG

Facts: Nova Ltd. has ₹5.5 crore qualifying profit in the preceding year and ₹4 crore average qualifying profit. It spends ₹3 lakh on an eligible completed project, leaves the rest unspent without an ongoing project, pays for mandatory pollution controls and calls those CSR. Its directors propose a merger, claim that dissolution will erase past false-statement liability and advertise an excellent ESG score.

Model answer: Nova meets the ₹5 crore profit trigger. Required spend = 2% of ₹4 crore = ₹8 lakh. On the facts, mandatory pollution-control expenditure is excluded from CSR, leaving ₹5 lakh non-ongoing shortfall. It must explain and transfer to the proper Schedule VII fund within six months of year end. Committee relief may apply at this small obligation, but not relief from Board functions/transfers, and continuing unspent-account facts must be checked.

A merger requires the applicable section 230/232 process, proper disclosures, voting, sanction, accounting compliance and filing; it is not automatically effective because directors propose it. Section 240 prevents the stated pre-merger officer-offence liability from disappearing merely through amalgamation. Personal criminal responsibility still requires the charging provision’s ingredients and individual basis; corporate accusation does not automatically convict every director.

The ESG claim must be tested against methodology and actual performance. Compliance costs are not CSR donations, the score is not immunity from pollution law, and the Board should address operational impacts rather than rely on branding. Conclusion: Separate the spending calculation, transfer duty, scheme validity, offence liability and sustainability claim; each requires its own legal test.

Answer-writing technique: a reliable sequence

For a statute: purpose → trigger → actors → procedure → thresholds → safeguards → consequence → short example → current-law qualification.

For a case: identity → material/procedural facts → issues → strongest arguments on both sides → actual order → reasoning/ratio → limits → application. Do not describe allegations as proved facts or a restored prosecution as conviction.

For the ESG article: problem → financial model → critique → proposed entity model → mechanisms → evaluation → Indian connection → proposal-versus-law distinction.

For a problem: state the date; classify facts; identify legal provision; calculate using the correct denominator; apply exceptions; reach a qualified but clear conclusion. A concise legally correct answer scores better than a long list of unrelated section numbers.
