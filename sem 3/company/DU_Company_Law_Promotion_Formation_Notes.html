Promotion of Company & the Promoter — S. 2(69)

What "promotion" means, what a promoter does, who is (and who is not) a promoter, and the statutory definition in S. 2(69) of the Companies Act, 2013.

🔎 1.1 Meaning of "promotion" — preliminary steps to bring the company into existence

  • Promotion is the first stage in the life of a company. Before a company can be formed "there must be some persons who have the intention to form a company, and who take the necessary steps to carry that intention into operation; such persons are called 'promoters'" (Charlesworth & Morse, Company Law — quoted in Avtar Singh).
  • Judicial description — Bowen LJ in Whaley Bridge Calico Printing Co. v. Green (1880) LR 5 QBD 109 (CA): "promoter" is "a term not of law, but of business", usefully summing up in a single word "a number of business operations, familiar to the commercial world, by which a company is generally brought into existence". The emphasis, therefore, is on the business operations, not on legal technicality — which is why the term was long left undefined by statute.
  • Judicial description — Cockburn CJ in Twycross v. Grant (1877) LR 2 CPD 469 (CA): a promoter is "one who undertakes to form a company with reference to a given project, and to set it going, and who takes the necessary steps to accomplish that purpose." (This definition was adopted by the Madras High Court in Weavers Mills Ltd. v. Balkis Ammal, AIR 1969 Mad 462.)
  • Lindley J in Emma Silver Mining Co. v. Lewis (1879) LR 4 CPD 396: "any person who undertakes to take part in the forming of a company… is prima facie a promoter of the company" — i.e., participation in the formation is the test, not the label.
  • Taxmann (Ch. 4.1): "Promotion is a term of wide import denoting the preliminary steps taken for the purpose of registration and floatation of the company." A promoter may be an individual, a syndicate, an association, a partner or a company itself.
  • In the USA: SEC Rule 405(a) defines a promoter as a person who, acting alone or in conjunction with others, directly or indirectly takes the initiative in founding or organising the business enterprise.
🧾
The definitional difficulty — "not a term of art, nor a term of law, but of business" The Companies Act uses the word "promoter" at several places to impose liability (e.g., Ss. 2(69), 32, 34, 35, 39, 52, 102, 238), yet, as Avtar Singh records, it "has never been clearly defined either judicially or legislatively" — until the 2013 Act. Most definitions are in terms of the categories of work that promoters perform. Hence Avtar's warning: "Whether a person is a promoter or not is a question of fact in each case. Much depends upon the nature of the role played by him in the promotion of business."

❓ 1.2 Who is a promoter — the functional test (Twycross v. Grant as the classic illustration)

In Twycross v. Grant (1877) LR 2 CPD 469 (CA), the court described the defendants in words that have become the standard functional test of a promoter:

"the defendants were the promoters of the company from the very beginning can admit of no doubt. They framed the scheme, they not only provisionally formed the company, but were, in fact, to the end its creators, they found the directors, and qualified them, they prepared the prospectus, they paid for printing and advertising, and the expenses incidental to bringing the undertaking before the world."Cockburn CJ, Twycross v. Grant (1877) — quoted in Avtar Singh

The court added that the functions of promoters come to an end as soon as they hand over the company to a governing body, like a Board of directors — the moment at which promotion ceases and management begins.

🏗️ What promoters typically do (the functions)

  1. Originate the scheme — conceive and investigate the business idea/project.
  2. Get together the subscribers to the memorandum (7 for public, 2 for private, 1 for OPC).
  3. Get the memorandum and articles prepared, executed and registered (through legal experts).
  4. Reserve the company's name (S. 4 — RUN / SPICe+ Part A).
  5. Find the bankers, brokers and legal advisers for the proposed company.
  6. Locate the first directors and settle the terms of preliminary contracts with vendors and agreements with underwriters.
  7. Arrange the preparation, advertisement and circulation of the prospectus and the placement of the capital.
  8. Hand over the company to its board — after which his functions end (Twycross).

📌 Two common Indian patterns of promotion

(i) The promoter of an entirely new venture: the promoter or the principal of them usually secures for themselves the management of the company being formed.

(ii) The conversion of an existing business: a person converts his own private business into a limited company (public or private) and secures for himself, more or less, a controlling interest in the company's management (Avtar Singh, quoting A. Ramaiya).

A person may become a promoter even after the formation of the company, for example by becoming a party to share issues or to procuring subscriptions (Lagunas Nitrate Co. v. Lagunas Syndicate (1899) 2 Ch. 392 — a person who subsequently helps to arrange the floating of the company's capital is equally a promoter).

📜 1.3 The statutory definition — S. 2(69) of the Companies Act, 2013

The 2013 Act at last gives a statutory definition, in functional terms. It provides that "promoter" means a person —

① Limb (a) — named / identifiedA person named as such in a prospectus, or identified by the company in the annual return referred to in S. 92.
② Limb (b) — controlA person who has control over the affairs of the company, directly or indirectly, whether as a shareholder, director or otherwise.
③ Limb (c) — de facto controlA person in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the Board of directors of the company is accustomed to act.
⚠️
The proviso — professionals excluded "However, a person who is acting merely in a professional capacity shall not be treated as a promoter." Thus a solicitor who prepares, on behalf of the promoters, the primary documents of the proposed company is not a promoter; similarly an accountant or a valuer who helps the promotion in his professional capacity is not a promoter. But any such person may become a promoter if he helps the formation by doing an act outside the scope of his professional duty — e.g., helping to get a purchaser for the company's patent, getting subscribers for shares, or getting personnel for the company. (Avtar Singh: a servant or agent of a promoter is likewise not a promoter for that reason alone — Re Great Wheat Polgooth Co. (1883).)

Avtar Singh's comment: the definition is "more or less in terms of functional categories". Its advantage is that only those who fulfil the functional concept of the term are subjected to the strict duties of a promoter (following Jacobus Marler Estates Ltd. v. Marler (1913) 85 LJ PC 167). Category (c) also feeds into the definition of related party in S. 2(76)(vii) — the effect is that the restrictions on related-party transactions under S. 188 apply to promoters (see §03.7).

02

Position & Powers of the Promoter

The promoter is not a trustee and not an agent of the company — but he stands in a fiduciary position towards it. This section states exactly what that means and what the promoter is (and is not) empowered to do.

📌 2.1 The fiduciary position — the classic statement of Lord Cairns in Erlanger

The place to begin every answer is Lord Cairns LC in Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co. (1878) 3 AC 1218 (case 14 of the DU list):

"It is now necessary that I should state to your Lordships in what position I understand the promoters to be placed with reference to the company which they proposed to form. They stand, in my opinion, undoubtedly in a fiduciary position. They have had in their hands the creation and moulding of the company; they have the power of defining how, and when, and in what shape, and under what supervision it shall start into existence and commence to act as a trading corporation. If they are doing all this in order that the company may, as soon as it starts into life, become, through its managing directors, the purchaser of the property of themselves, the promoters, it is in my opinion incumbent upon the promoters to take care that in forming the company they provide it with an executive, that is to say, with a board of directors who shall both be aware that the property which they are asked to buy is the property of the promoters, and shall be competent and impartial judges whether the purchase ought or ought not to be made."Lord Cairns LC, Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co. (1878) 3 AC 1218 — the headnote adopts the same words

The headnote of the case (as printed in the DU casebook) distils the ratio in four propositions:

  1. The position of promoters vis-à-vis the company they promote is not that of trustees to a cestui que trust, but they are in a fiduciary position towards the company.
  2. Where promoters sell property to the company, the burden is on them of showing that they have not taken any unfair advantage resulting from their relations with the company.
  3. It is their duty to nominate independent directors who are capable of acting impartially in defence of the company's interests and will be competent and impartial judges whether the purchase ought to be made; and to disclose all material facts relating to the transaction (though not what they paid for the property, provided they are not guilty of unfair concealment).
  4. Where the company is not given an opportunity, through independent directors, to exercise a fair and independent judgment on the purchase, the court may order the contract to be rescinded and the purchase price to be returned.

🎭 Position changes at the moment the prospectus goes out

Lord Gordon in Erlanger shows that the fiduciary relation is not merely contractual — it is generated by the act of promotion itself:

"Having acquired the property, they resolved to form a company… They became promoters of the company, and prepared the necessary documents for its formation, and issued a prospectus to the public with the view of inducing the public to take up its shares. In doing this the syndicate changed the position it originally held, and put itself in a fiduciary relation to the company which it was engaged in forming."Lord Gordon, Erlanger — (1874-80) All ER Rep. 271

Consequence: the promoter may hold property for the proposed company in equity from the very first day — in Vali Pattabhirama Rao v. Ramanuja Ginning and Rice Factory (P) Ltd. (1986) 60 Comp Cas 568 (AP), a promoter acquired a leasehold for a company he intended to form; he first formed a partnership and then converted it into a company; it was held that the lease became the property of the company without any formality of conveyance.

⚖️ Because the duty is fiduciary — the standard is strict

Lord O'Hagan in Erlanger on the strictness and moral quality of the obligation:

"But the privilege given to them for promoting such a company for such an object involved obligations of a very serious kind. It required, in its exercise, the utmost good faith, the completest truthfulness, and a careful regard to the protection of the future shareholders. The power to nominate a directorate is manifestly capable of great abuse, with very evil consequences to multitudes of people who have little capacity to guard themselves. It should be watched with jealousy, and restrained from employment in such a way as to mislead the ignorant, the unwise, or the unwary… The fiduciary obligation may be violated, though there may be no intention to do injustice."Lord O'Hagan, Erlanger — (1874-80) All ER Rep. 271

Key point for answers: breach of fiduciary duty does not require fraud or dishonest intention — mere failure to protect the company's interests suffices ("This does not necessarily imply the imputation of evil purpose or conscious fraud").

🧩 2.2 Why the promoter is NOT a trustee — and NOT an agent

❌ Not a trustee

  • First head of Lord Hatherley's speech in Erlanger: the syndicate "were in no such sense as that trustees for the company" — the property belonged absolutely to them; they bought it as a syndicate and were entitled to deal with it as they thought proper. The rule that no profit can be derived by a trustee out of the property of his cestui que trust has no application, because there was no trust property and no cestui que trust.
  • Omnium Electric Palaces Ltd. v. Baines (1914) 1 Ch. 332 (CA): promoters are not trustees in the real sense of the word, because the company may not be in existence as a legal person when the property is acquired — there is no one to be a cestui que trust. The fiduciary position emerges from agency.
  • Yet the substance is close: "The promoter is in the situation akin to that of a trustee of the company, and his dealings with it must be open and fair" (Morawetz, Corporations § 546, quoted in Avtar Singh; accepted in Weavers Mills Ltd. v. Balkis Ammal, AIR 1969 Mad 462).

❌ Not an agent

  • No principal in existence: an agent presupposes a principal; before incorporation "the company, before incorporation, is a non-entity"; "two consenting parties are necessary to a contract" — so no contract made before incorporation can bind the company (Avtar, Ch. 2).
  • No authority, no ratification: "The company could not be sued in law for those expenses, inasmuch as it was not in existence at the time when the expenses were incurred… and ratification was impossible" (Re English & Colonial Produce Co. (1906) 2 Ch. 435 — solicitor who prepared documents and paid the registration fee could not recover from the company).
  • But the law gives him a fiduciary cloak, not an immunity: because he cannot hide behind a non-existent principal, a person who contracts on behalf of an unincorporated company is personally liable on the contract (Kelner v. Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174 — see §04).
🧾
The label the DU class notes use — "quasi-trustee" (Dr. Megh Raj, Unit 2) "A promoter is neither an agent nor a trustee of the company under incorporation, but certain fiduciary (founded on trust) duties have been imposed on him. He is not an agent because there is no principal born by that time, and he is not a trustee because there is no cestui que trust in existence. Hence he occupies the peculiar position of a 'quasi-trustee'." And the classic modern definition of a fiduciary — Bristol and West Building Society v. Mathew (1998), Lord Millett: "A fiduciary is someone who has undertaken to act for and on behalf of another in a particular matter in circumstances which give rise to a relationship of trust and confidence." Conduct of a fiduciary may be treated as constructive fraud where it rests on acts, omissions or concealment considered fraudulent.

⚡ 2.3 Powers of the promoter — what he can (and cannot) do

✅ Legitimate powers (derived from his role)

  • To create and mould the company: define "how, and when, and in what shape, and under what supervision" the company starts into existence (Cairns LC — Erlanger).
  • To frame its constitution: get the memorandum and articles prepared (through professionals), executed and registered; fix the objects, capital, directorate in the documents.
  • To nominate the first board of directors — and thereby determine who first governs the company.
  • To settle preliminary contracts with vendors, bankers, brokers, underwriters; to procure subscribers for shares.
  • To arrange the capital: prepare, advertise and circulate the prospectus; place the capital.
  • To hold property and preliminary advantages for the proposed company in equity — which the company may later adopt (Vali Pattabhirama Rao, above).
  • To be indemnified for legitimate expenses and to receive remuneration where agreed (exam checklist — "right to indemnity for legitimate expenses; may receive remuneration if agreed").
  • To subsequently adopt and enforce a contract made before incorporation for the purposes of the company, if warranted by the terms of incorporation (S. 15, Specific Relief Act, 1963 — see §04).

🚫 Limits on those powers

  • Cannot bind the company before incorporation. The company is a "non-entity"; a contract made in its name before registration binds nobody but the maker (Kelner v. Baxter; Re English & Colonial Produce Co.).
  • Cannot claim indemnity/expenses from the company merely because he promoted it — "It is not desirable to saddle the corporation with burdens imposed upon it in advance by over-optimistic promoters" (Re English & Colonial Produce Co.; Re Northumberland Avenue Hotel Co. (1886) 33 Ch. D. 16 — an implied contract to pay arises only if the company acts on the contract, not when it acts under a mistaken belief of being bound).
  • Cannot make any profit in secret. He may profit — but must disclose profit minus the expenses of earning it (Lydney and Wigpool Iron Ore Co. v. Bird (1886) 33 Ch. D. 85 (CA)); and he must account for the profit (Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres of Varieties (1902) 2 Ch. 809 (CA)).
  • Cannot give the company a board of his own creatures — he must provide an independent, competent executive (Erlanger: the board must be "competent and impartial judges").
  • Cannot procure the company's money by unfair concealment — e.g., a prospectus that states the purchase "was made by the directors" when the true parties were the promoters (Erlanger).
  • Cannot plead his own promotion to escape liability for the company's debts or for misstatements in the prospectus (S. 34, 35, 339, 447 — §04).
🧠
When does the promoter's position end? (i) As soon as the company is handed over to a governing body — the board (Twycross v. Grant); (ii) but his fiduciary duty continues even after incorporation until the profits are fully disclosed and fully accounted for (Emma Silver Mining Co. v. Lewis — duty continues until the profits are fully disclosed and accounted); (iii) and his statutory liabilities (prospectus: Ss. 34–35; fraud: Ss. 7(5)-(6), 339, 447) survive well past formation. Where rescission is not possible — the company is not in a position to return the property — it can recover damages for breach of the duty of good faith, the measure being the profit the promoter made from the breach (Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres).

🗣️ 3.2 The hardest question — to WHOM must disclosure be made?

Avtar Singh calls this "the only difficult question": it was suggested by the House of Lords in Erlanger that disclosure should be made to an independent and competent Board of directors — i.e., the promoters must provide the company with an executive body which is (i) aware that the property offered is the promoter's property, and (ii) competent and impartial to judge whether the purchase ought to be made.

But subsequent experience showed that this is not always possible — in a private company, or a public company which, like that of Salomon & Co., consists of only the family members, it is just not possible to constitute an independent Board. In such a case the promoter should disclose his interest and profit to the shareholders of the company.

🚨
BUT — "disclosure to the parties" is not disclosure to the company: Gluckstein v. Barnes (1900) AC 240 (HL) A syndicate raised a fund, bought the "Olympia" property, and resold it to a company which they had formed and of which they were the first directors. They disclosed the two prices (£1,40,000 bought; £1,80,000 sold) but not the bonus of £20,000 they had made by buying up charges on the property below their value. Held: the promoters ought to have disclosed the £20,000. Rejecting the argument that the fact was known to the parties to the transaction, Lord Halsbury LC said:
"It is too absurd to suggest that a disclosure to the parties to this transaction is a disclosure to the company. They were there by the terms of the agreement to do the work of the syndicate, that is to say, to cheat the shareholders; and this, forsooth, is to be treated as a disclosure to the company, when they were really there to hoodwink the shareholders, and so, far from protecting them, were to obtain from them the money, the produce of their nefarious plans."Lord Halsbury LC, Gluckstein v. Barnes (1900) AC 240, 247 — quoted in Avtar Singh
"Disclosure is not the most appropriate word to use when a person who plays many parts announces to himself in one character what he has done and is doing in another."Lord Halsbury LC, Gluckstein v. Barnes

Practical rule: it will not be enough to disclose the truth to the first few shareholders; disclosure must be made to the whole body of persons who are invited to become shareholders — "the duty continues even after incorporation until the profits are fully disclosed and fully accounted for" (Emma Silver Mining Co. v. Lewis).

🛡️ 3.3 Duty to give the company an independent and competent board — Erlanger in detail

The second limb of the Erlanger ratio is the duty of the promoter in framing the constitution of the company: he must take care, when selling his property to it, that he sells it "through the medium of a board of directors who can and do exercise an independent and intelligent judgment on the transaction, and are not left under the belief that the property belongs, not to the promoter, but to some other person."

Applying that test to the facts, Cairns LC found the September 29, 1871 board meeting — attended by only Dakin, Macdonald and Evans (the vendor himself), with the syndicate's solicitor (Westall, "virtually another vendor") present and two statutory directors (de Lhuys, Eastwick) absent — to be:

"a meeting at which two of the principal directors did not and could not attend — at which one who did attend, and take part in the deliberations, was at once a person buying and selling — where the legal adviser present and assisting was virtually another vendor, and where the two remaining directors are not shown to have had the means of exercising, or to have exercised, any intelligent judgment on the subject — as little else than a mockery and a delusion."Lord Cairns LC, Erlanger (1878) 3 AC 1218

And the answer to the argument that the articles allowed a quorum of two — "a provision which, in my opinion, could not be held to remedy defects such as I have pointed out as going to the entire constitution of the board."

Lord O'Hagan added the policy reason — the power to nominate a directorate "is manifestly capable of great abuse, with very evil consequences to multitudes of people who have little capacity to guard themselves… In all such cases the directorate nominated by the promoters should stand between them and the public, with such independence or intelligence that they may be expected to deal fairly, impartially, and with adequate knowledge… If they have not those qualities, they are unworthy of trust; they are the betrayers and not the guardians of the company they govern, and their acts should not receive the sanction of a court of justice."

✅
What disclosure does NOT require (and what it does) Per Lord Hatherley in Erlanger, a vendor-promoter need not disclose what he paid for the property in his own anterior purchase before asking an enhanced price; but he must not be guilty of any conduct which amounts to unfair concealment of the real facts of the case, which ought in common fairness to be disclosed to a person seeking to purchase or entering into a treaty with him. The headnote of the case repeats this: "They need not disclose what they paid for the property which they are selling to the company, but they must not be guilty of any conduct which amounts to unfair concealment of the real facts."

🔁 Why the fiduciary duty matters — the burden of proof

From the headnote of Erlanger: "where the promoters of a company sell property to the company the burden is on them of showing that they have not taken any unfair advantage resulting from their relations with the company." This inversion of the ordinary burden of proof is the practical teeth of the fiduciary principle: the promoter does not have to prove fraud against himself — he must prove his own fairness.

Example of the doctrine applied: if he conceals any fact in relation to the character or value of the property, or his personal interest in the proposed sale, the company is entitled to set aside the transaction or recover compensation; he is guilty of a breach of trust if he sells property to the company without informing it that the property belongs to him, or if he accepts a bonus or commission from a person who sells property to the company (Avtar Singh).

🎓
Model sentence to quote in the answer "Subject to such disclosures, it is quite lawful for a promoter to sell his property to the company at a profit to himself" — Omnium Electric Palaces Ltd. v. Baines (1914) 1 Ch. 332 (CA); "There is no duty that the promoter must restrain himself from making any profit whatsoever in the business of promotion." The law strikes only at secret profits, and requires that the profit be disclosed minus the expenses in earning it (Lydney & Wigpool Iron Ore Co. v. Bird (1886) 33 Ch. D. 85 (CA)).

Liabilities of the Promoter

The DU class notes divide this head as "liability for negligence and personal profits"; for exam purposes it unfolds into three main heads: (i) pre-incorporation contracts (personal liability — Kelner), (ii) misstatements in the prospectus (Ss. 34–35 — Twycross), (iii) fraud in promotion / negligence and breach of duty (Ss. 7(5)-(7), 338–341, 447). Plus the promoter's right to indemnity and remuneration, and his accountability for secret profits.

📄 4.1 Pre-incorporation contracts — the starting principles

A pre-incorporation (preliminary) contract is a contract purporting to be made on behalf of a company before the company is incorporated. The fundamental rule is stated by Avtar Singh: "No contract can bind a company before it becomes capable of contracting by incorporation. Two consenting parties are necessary to a contract, whereas the company, before incorporation, is a non-entity." A company has no status prior to incorporation — it can have no income before incorporation for tax purposes; shares cannot be acquired in its name before incorporation (a transfer form is liable to be rejected where the name of a proposed company is entered in the column of transferee).

👎 The company CANNOT be sued on it

Rule: "It is not desirable to saddle the corporation with burdens imposed upon it in advance by over-optimistic promoters."

  • Re English & Colonial Produce Co. (1906) 2 Ch. 435: a solicitor prepared documents on instructions, obtained registration and paid the registration fee — the company was held not bound to pay: "The company could not be sued in law for those expenses, inasmuch as it was not in existence at the time when the expenses were incurred… and ratification was impossible."
  • An arbitration agreement was held to be non-existent when the company with which it was made was incorporated subsequent to it (Avtar, Ch. 2).
  • Kelner v. Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174: the company never came into existence — but the individuals who contracted, having taken delivery, must pay (see box below).

👎 The company CANNOT sue on it

Rule: "A company cannot by adoption or ratification obtain the benefit of a contract purporting to have been made on its behalf before the company came into existence."

  • Natal Land & Colonisation Co. v. Pauline Colliery Syndicate (1904) AC 120 (PC): N. Co. agreed with C., who acted on behalf of a proposed syndicate, to give the syndicate a lease of coal-mining rights. The syndicate was then registered, struck a seam of coal and claimed the lease; N. Co. refused. An action for specific performance or damages was held not maintainable — the syndicate was not in existence when the contract was signed.
  • Similarly, shares cannot be acquired in the name of a company before incorporation (Avtar).
  • Rover International Ltd. v. Cannon Films Sales Ltd. (1987) 1 WLR 1597: "somebody who does not exist cannot contract."

🔓 4.2 The escape routes — Specific Relief Act, 1963 (Ss. 15 and 19)

"Thus, so far as the company is concerned, it is neither bound by, nor can have the benefit of, a pre-incorporation contract. But this is subject to the provisions of the Specific Relief Act, 1963" (Avtar Singh).

✅ S. 15 — the company may adopt & enforce

Where the promoters of a company have made a contract before its incorporation, for the purposes of the company, and the contract is warranted by the terms of incorporation, the company may adopt and enforce it.

  • "Warranted by the terms of incorporation" means within the scope of the company's objects as stated in the memorandum.
  • The contract must be for the purposes of the company — a contract to allot shares after incorporation is not "for the purposes of the company", so the company cannot enforce it against the other party (Avtar).
  • Adoption need not be express or in the articles: Jai Narain Parasrampuria v. Pushpa Devi Saraf (2006) 7 SCC 756 (SC) — the very fact that the company was seeking a declaration of its ownership of the property which the directors had purchased for it before incorporation was sufficient to signify acceptance of the transaction.
  • A new contract may also be made by the company after incorporation in terms of the earlier contract — Howard v. Patent Ivory Mfg. Co. (1888) LR 38 Ch. D. 156; and an implied contract arises if the company after incorporation acts on the contract — but not when it acts under a mistaken belief of being bound: Re Northumberland Avenue Hotel Co. (1886) 33 Ch. D. 16 (CA).

✅ S. 19 — the other party may also enforce

S. 19 of the same Act provides that the other party can also enforce the contract against the company — if two cumulative conditions are satisfied (per the Taxmann problem hint, S. 19(e)):

  1. the company has adopted the contract after incorporation; and
  2. the contract is warranted by the terms of incorporation.

Unless the company adopts, the other party cannot enforce against it — but the promoters can be held personally liable.

Application — Vali Pattabhirama Rao v. Ramanuja Ginning and Rice Factory (P) Ltd. (1986) 60 Comp Cas 568 (AP): a promoter acquired a leasehold interest for a company he intended to form, held it for a time for a partnership firm, and converted the firm into a company which adopted the lease — the lessor was held bound to the company under the lease; from the very first day of purchase the interest was in equity the property of the company.

👤 4.3 Personal liability of the contracting agent (the promoter himself)

For contracts that do not fall within Ss. 15/19, the question is whether they can be enforced by or against the agent who acted on behalf of the projected corporation. "The answer will depend upon the construction of the contract. If the contract is made on behalf of a company not yet in existence, the agent might incur personal liability. For, where a contract is made on behalf of a principal known to both the parties to be non-existent, the contract is deemed to have been entered into personally by the actual maker." Similarly, "where a person purports to contract as agent he may nevertheless disclose himself as being in truth a principal, and bring an action in his own name" (Avtar Singh).

📢 4.4 Liability for misstatements in the prospectus — Ss. 34 and 35 of the Companies Act, 2013

This is the second head of the promoter's liability, and it rests on the classic authority of Twycross v. Grant (1877) LR 2 CPD 469 (CA): the promoters were the creators of the company; "they prepared the prospectus"; and they were held liable for the false statements in the prospectus — the doctrine being that a person who issues, or authorises the issue of, a prospectus containing false statements is liable to those who subscribe on the faith of it, and cannot escape by pointing to the directors as the formal signatories. (Avtar: "S. 35 makes liable for mis-statements in prospectus every person who is promoter of the company"; the stringent provisions of the Companies Act and allied Acts "have virtually eliminated fraudulent practices in company promotions.")

⚖️ S. 34 — criminal liability (misstatement in prospectus)

  • Where a prospectus includes any statement which is false, or omits anything likely to mislead, every person who authorised the issue of the prospectus is liable for fraud.
  • Punishment is that of S. 447: imprisonment of not less than 6 months which may extend to 10 years, and fine not less than the amount involved in the fraud, which may extend to three times that amount.
  • It is no answer that the misstatement was made by the board: the promoter who framed the scheme and prepared the prospectus is within "every person who authorised the issue" (Twycross v. Grant).

🛡️ S. 35 — civil liability for untrue statements

  • Where a person has subscribed for securities in reliance upon an untrue statement included in the prospectus, the company and every person who was a director at the time of issue, or a promoter, or who authorised the issue are jointly and severally liable to compensate the subscriber for the loss or damage sustained.
  • Defences (S. 35(2)): reasonable ground to believe the statement true; withdrawal of consent before issue; the issue without his knowledge or consent.
  • Related safeguards: S. 26 (matters to be stated and reports to be set out), S. 31 (shelf prospectus), S. 32 (red herring prospectus), S. 33 (abridged prospectus), S. 25 (deemed prospectus — "document offering securities for sale"), S. 36 (damages for fraudulent inducement, S. 447 punishment), S. 40 (public offer only after registration).

🕳️ 4.5 Fraud in promotion and winding-up liability

  • False particulars at the time of incorporation — S. 7(5): "if any person furnishes any false or incorrect particulars of any material information of which he was aware", he is liable for action under S. 447 (fraud).
  • Incorporation obtained by fraud — S. 7(6): if after incorporation it is proved that the incorporation was obtained by furnishing false or incorrect information, or by suppressing any material fact or information, or by any fraudulent action, the promoters, the persons named as first directors and those making the declaration are also liable for action under S. 447; and an application may be made to the Tribunal (S. 7(7)) which may — after giving the company a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and taking into account transactions entered into by the company — (a) regulate the management (including changes in the memorandum and articles) in public interest or the interest of the company, members and creditors; (b) direct that the liability of the members shall be unlimited; (c) direct removal of the company's name from the Register of Companies; (d) order winding up; or (e) pass such other orders as it deems fit.
  • Fraudulent conduct of business — S. 339 (the "fraud in promotion… liable under S. 339" point of the exam checklist): if in the course of the winding up it appears that the business of the company has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, the personally liable persons — including a promoter who was a party — may be declared personally liable without any limitation of liability for all or any of the debts of the company; the court may also direct contribution (S. 339(2)).
  • Misfeasance / assessment of damages — S. 340: the Tribunal may assess damages against delinquent officers (successor to s. 543 of the 1956 Act) for any loss caused by their misfeasance, which "does not necessarily involve" fraud; the section's operation extends to those concerned in the promotion and formation of the company.
  • Public examination in winding up — S. 300: the Tribunal has the power to order public examination of promoters, directors, etc. in the course of winding up — the direct statutory hook by which a promoter is publicly examined and thereafter held liable if fraud is found.
  • Secret profits: the promoter must account for all benefits derived from promotion (duty to account); if the company has gone into liquidation, the liquidator can still recover the undisclosed profit made on the formation (Gluckstein v. Barnes — recovery in liquidation; Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres).
  • SEBI discipline: promoters of listed companies must comply with the SEBI guidelines relating to their contribution to the public issue and the lock-in period (Avtar fn. 21).
🔎
The statutory-liability list (as Dr. Megh Raj's class notes enumerate it — all 2013 Act numbers) "There are various statutory provisions imposing liability on the promoters. Some of them are — Ss. 7(6), 26, 35(1), 36, 34, 39, 340 and 300, etc." Read with Ss. 7(5), 338, 339, 341 and 447: S. 7(6) incorporation obtained by fraud → S. 447 + Tribunal orders; S. 26 matters to be stated/reports in the prospectus; S. 34 criminal liability for false statements; S. 35(1) civil liability for untrue statements; S. 36 damages for fraudulent inducement (with S. 447); S. 39 allotment/public-offer compliance; S. 340 misfeasance damages (old s. 543); S. 300 public examination of promoters in winding up; S. 339 fraudulent conduct of business → personal liability without limit; S. 341 extends this liability to partners/directors of firms; S. 447 the general fraud penalty (6 months–10 years; fine ≥ amount involved, up to 3×).
💵
Rights of the promoter (the other side of liability) (i) Indemnity for legitimate expenses — but only where the company, after incorporation, adopts the contract or picks up the expense; not merely because he acted as promoter (Re English & Colonial Produce Co.). (ii) Remuneration — the promoter may receive remuneration if agreed; the agreement must be made after incorporation or be adopted by the company, otherwise the "over-optimistic promoter" cannot saddle the company with it. (iii) He may sell his own property to the company at a profit — subject to full disclosure (S. 188 also requires special resolution and valuation for related-party transactions: see §03.7).

Formation of Company — S. 3 & the Procedure of Registration (including online)

Who may form a company; the name; the memorandum and articles; the S. 7(1) filing; the Registrar's scrutiny; and the modern SPICe+ online route with DIN, DSC, e-MoA/e-AoA and AGILE-PRO.

👥 5.1 Formation under S. 3 — "for any lawful purpose"

S. 3(1) of the Companies Act, 2013: a company may be formed for any lawful purpose by the following —

① PUBLIC companySeven or more persons — where the company to be formed is to be a public company.
② PRIVATE companyTwo or more persons — where the company to be formed is to be a private company.
③ ONE PERSON COMPANYOne person — where the company to be formed is to be a One Person Company, "that is to say, a private company with one member".
  • They have to subscribe their names to the memorandum of the company and comply with the requirements of the Act in respect of registration.
  • A company formed under S. 3 may be limited by shares, limited by guarantee, or an unlimited company.
  • The OPC and its nominee: the memorandum of a One Person Company has to indicate the name of another person, with his prior written consent in the prescribed form, who is to become a member of the company in the event of the subscriber's death or his incapacity to contract. The consent must be filed with the Registrar at the time of incorporation along with the memorandum and articles. The nominee may withdraw consent in the prescribed manner; the member may change the nominee by following the prescribed procedure, intimate the change to the nominee (by indicating in the memorandum or otherwise) and notify the Registrar — such change is not to be taken as an alteration of the memorandum (Avtar Singh, Ch. 2).
  • Lawful purpose: S. 12(1) echoes the requirement — "persons associated for any lawful purpose may… form an incorporated company"; a company cannot properly be registered for an unlawful purpose, and an illegal object does not become legal by the issue of the certificate (§06.5 below).
💡
Minimum members, maximum members Public company: min. 7 (max. unlimited). Private company: min. 2, max. 200 (S. 2(68)) — excluding employee-members and excluding joint holders. OPC: min. and max. 1. In Salomon the "seven subscribers" were satisfied by a family of six plus Salomon — a point which is now the private-company pattern; and the "two adult persons and a guardian" problem in Moosa Goolam Ariff shows what happens when the minimum is not really complied with — the certificate is still conclusive (§06.4).

🗂️ 5.2 What must be done BEFORE filing — type, name, memorandum, articles

📝 Step A — Decide the type & reserve the name (S. 4)

  • Type of company: the first thing the promoters must decide (public / private / OPC; limited by shares or guarantee or unlimited) — Taxmann 4.2-1a.
  • Name: no company shall be registered by a name which (a) is identical with or resembles too nearly the name of an existing company registered under this Act or any previous company law; or (b) is such that its use — (i) will constitute an offence under any law for the time being in force; or (ii) is undesirable in the opinion of the Central Government.
  • Application — RUN or SPICe+ Part A: under S. 4(4) a person may apply through the web service at www.mca.gov.in using the form RUN (Reserve Unique Name), or through SPICe+ Part A, to the Registrar, Central Registration Centre (CRC), for reservation of a name — either as the name of the proposed company or the name to which it proposes to change.
  • Validity: the CRC may reserve the name for twenty days (new company) / sixty days (change of name by an existing company), from approval of the application. If the name was applied for with wrong or incorrect information: if the company is not yet incorporated the reserved name is cancelled and the applicant is liable to a penalty up to ₹1,00,000; if incorporated, the Registrar may direct change of name within 3 months (ordinary resolution), or strike off, or petition for winding up.
  • (Under the older INC-1 route the promoters gave up to six proposed names in order of preference.)

📝 Step B — Prepare the memorandum and articles

  • Memorandum — S. 4(6) read with Schedule I: in the form given in Tables A, B, C, D and E as applicable; Articles — S. 5: in the form in Tables F, G, H, I and J of Schedule I.
  • Stamping: the memorandum and articles have to be stamped as per the applicable State stamp laws, and duly printed (print or computer printing by laser — but not xerox: Selvarajan v. Registrar of Companies (1986) 3 Comp LJ 275 (Mad)).
  • Signing: S. 7 and Rule 13 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014 — the memorandum and articles shall be signed by each subscriber, who shall add his name, address, description and occupation, if any, in the presence of at least one witness, who shall attest the signature and likewise sign and add his particulars, and verify his ID.
  • Not necessary that the promoters themselves sign the memorandum and articles; an illiterate subscriber affixes his thumb impression or mark, described as such by the person writing for him, who places the subscriber's name and the number of shares taken, and reads and explains the contents, endorsing that fact on the documents (Taxmann 4.2-1c).
  • Other documents: agreement, if any, with a person for appointment as managing/whole-time director or manager; particulars of the registered office; and the professional's engagement.

📮 5.3 The application — S. 7(1) and the documents and declarations

S. 7(1): there shall be filed with the Registrar within whose jurisdiction the registered office of the company is proposed to be situated the following documents and information for registration:

  1. the memorandum and articles of the company duly signed by all the subscribers to the memorandum in the prescribed manner;
  2. a declaration in the prescribed form by (i) an advocate, chartered accountant, cost accountant or company secretary in practice who is engaged in the formation of the company, and (ii) a person named in the articles as a director, manager or secretary of the company, that all the requirements of this Act and the rules made thereunder in respect of registration and matters precedent or incidental thereto have been complied with;
  3. an affidavit from each of the subscribers to the memorandum and from persons named as first directors, if any, in the articles:
  • that he has not been convicted of any offence in connection with the promotion, formation or management of any company, or found guilty of any fraud or misfeasance or of any breach of duty to the company under the Act or any previous company law during the preceding five years;
  • that the documents filed for registration contain correct and complete information and true to the best of his knowledge and belief;
  1. the address for correspondence until the company's registered office is established;
  2. particulars of every subscriber — name including surname or family name, residential address, nationality and such other particulars alongwith proof of identity as prescribed (where the subscriber is a body corporate, the prescribed particulars);
  3. particulars of the first directors mentioned in the articles — name including surname or family name, Director Identification Number (DIN), residential address, nationality and other particulars including proof of identity; and their interests in other firms or bodies corporate, alongwith their consent to act as directors.

Certificate of Incorporation & the Effects of Registration — S. 9

The certificate is the company's "birth certificate". What it contains, why the date on it is conclusive, the six effects of registration under S. 9, and the great rule — the certificate is conclusive evidence — with its limited exceptions.

🎫 6.1 The certificate — issue, contents, and the conclusive date

  • Issue (S. 7(2)): if the Registrar finds the documents satisfactory, he registers the documents and enters the name of the company in the Register of Companies, and issues a certificate called the certificate of incorporation. The certificate is issued electronically under the digital signature of the Registrar (MCA practice).
  • CIN (S. 7(3)): the Registrar then allots to the company a Corporate Identity Number — a distinct identity for the company — and this number has to be included in the certificate.
  • What the certificate testifies: (i) that the company has been incorporated on the date mentioned; (ii) that the subscribers have taken the number of shares set out against their names and have paid for them; and (iii) — the conclusive-evidence rule, below — that all requirements of the Act as to registration and matters precedent or incidental thereto have been complied with.
  • The date is conclusive: "The company's life commences from the date mentioned in the certificate of incorporation and the date appearing on it is conclusive, even if wrong" (Avtar). Sections 8 and 9 both key off "the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation".
  • One certificate, no more questions: "When once the certificate of incorporation is given nothing is to be inquired into as to the regularity of the prior proceedings" (Lord Cairns, Peel's case — §6.4).

⚙️ 6.2 Effects of registration — the words of S. 9

S. 9 describes the effects of registration in the following words (as reproduced in the prescribed Taxmann text):

"From the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, such subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons, as may, from time to time, become members of the company, shall be a body corporate by the name contained in the memorandum, capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company under this Act and having perpetual succession and a common seal, with power to acquire, hold and dispose of property, both movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, to contract and to sue and be sued, by the said name."Section 9, Companies Act, 2013

🪞 6.3 The certificate is conclusive evidence — the classical rule

Not only does the certificate create the company — it is also "the conclusive evidence that all the requirements of this Act have been complied with in respect of registration and matters precedent and incidental thereto and that the association is a company authorised to be registered and duly registered under this Act". In other words, the validity of the certificate cannot be disputed on any grounds whatsoever (Avtar Singh, Ch. 2 — quoting the statutory formula).

⚖️ Re Barned's Banking Co., Peel's case (1867) LR 2 HL 325

After signature and before registration a proposed memorandum was altered without the authority of the subscribers, so materially that the alteration "entirely neutralised and annihilated the original execution and signature" of the document. The company was nevertheless registered and a certificate issued. It was objected that the memorandum had not been signed by seven (or any) subscribers.

Lord Cairns assented to that proposition — but: "the certificate of incorporation is not merely a prima facie answer, but a conclusive answer to such objection…. When once the certificate of incorporation is given, nothing is to be inquired into as to the regularity of the prior proceedings."

⚖️ Oakes v. Turquand & Harding (1867) LR 2 HL 325 — Lord Chelmsford

To the same effect: "I think the certificate prevents all recurrence to prior matters essential to registration… and that it is conclusive… that all previous requisites have been complied with."

⚖️ Moosa Goolam Ariff v. Ebrahim Goolam Ariff (1913) — Privy Council

The memorandum was signed by two adults and by a guardian for five minors (a separate signature for each minor); the Registrar nonetheless registered the company and issued the certificate; the plaintiff sought a declaration that the certificate was void. Lord Macnaghten:

"Their Lordships will assume that the conditions of registration prescribed by the Indian Companies Act were not duly complied with; that there were not seven subscribers to the memorandum and that the Registrar ought not to have granted the certificate. But the certificate is conclusive for all purposes."

⚖️ Cotman v. Brougham (1918) AC 514 (HL) — from the DU casebook

The memorandum contained objects which were wholly outside the company's power; but the Registrar accepted the memorandum, granted the certificate — and the validity of the memorandum could not thereafter be challenged. Lord Wrenbury, explaining the Registrar's function and the effect of the certificate: the certificate is "conclusive evidence that all the requirements of this Act in respect of registration and of matters precedent and incidental thereto have been complied with"; and the Registrar "ought to consider whether the requirements have been complied with, and to refuse registration if not" — for there is a twofold purpose of the objects clause: (i) the investor must know the field of risk in which he is venturing, and (ii) the person dealing with the company must know the contractual relation into which he is entering.

⚖️ T.V. Krishna v. Andhra Prabha (P) Ltd. (AIR 1960 AP 123) — the Indian summary

A company was formed when Express Newspapers sold its undertaking to Andhra Prabha Private Ltd.; it was alleged that the new company was formed for the illegal purpose of evading wage-board obligations and that the registration should be declared void. The court refused: the purpose was not unlawful; but even if some objects were illegal, the legal persona of the company could not be extinguished by cancelling the certificate — the remedy would be to wind up the company.

"If a company is born, the only method to get it extinguished is not by assailing its incorporation, but by resorting to the provisions of enactments which provide for the winding up of the companies."Chandra Reddy CJ

Accord: Universal Mutual Aid & Poor Houses Assn. v. A.D. Thappa Naidu (AIR 1933 Mad 16) — the Registrar can refuse to register a memorandum with unlawful objects, and if he happens to do so, his certificate is not conclusive as to the lawfulness of the objects. So the "conclusive" rule is about compliance with registration requirements, not about validity of objects.

🧷 6.4 The limits of conclusiveness

⚠️ Where the certificate does NOT protect

  • Illegal objects are not validated: "The illegal objects, however, do not become legal by the issue of the certificate" — Performing Right Society Ltd. v. London Theatre of Varieties (1922) 1 KB 539; R. v. Registrar of Joint Stock Companies (1931) 2 KB 197 (CA) (company registered for an unlawful object ordered to be struck off); Bowman v. Secular Society Ltd. (1917) AC 406 (HL): "a company cannot properly be registered for an unlawful purpose; the registration may be cancelled by appropriate proceedings."
  • Registration of a trade union as a company: certificate not conclusive — British Assn. of Glass Bottle Mfrs. Ltd. v. Nettlefold (1911) 27 TLR 27.
  • Incomplete registration: the conclusive presumption may not be raised where non-compliance with a material statutory provision leaves the registration an "incomplete fact" — Caiman v. National Assn. of Mental Health (1971) 1 Ch. 317.
  • Judicial review: English courts have explored the possibility of reviewing the Registrar's certificates — a company registered for an unlawful object was ordered to be struck off; but the Kerala High Court has held that a writ cannot be issued to cancel the registration of a company under the Companies Act — Maluk Mohamed v. Capital Stock Exchange Kerala Ltd. (1991) 72 Comp Cas 333 (Ker.).
  • Fraud at the time of incorporation (2013 Act): S. 7(5)-(7) — false particulars or fraud in obtaining incorporation invite S. 447 and the Tribunal's powers (unlimited liability of members, removal of name, winding up) — a modern statutory carve-out from the 19th-century absolute rule.

🧠 Why the law insists on conclusiveness (the policy)

  • Security of transactions with third parties: everyone dealing with the company must be able to rely on its existence; the company's contracts, seals and property with third parties cannot be undone years later because a signature was defective.
  • Investor protection: the certificate marks the point beyond which subscribers' money is committed; the contract with the company has to be capable of being enforced (note the twofold purpose of the objects clause in Cotman — investor's risk field and dealer's contractual relation).
  • Clean-cut remedy: if a company is wrongly born, the remedy is the winding-up machinery, not a collateral attack on the certificate — T.V. Krishna.
  • Finality for the promoter's own position: once the company is born, its contracts and acquisitions are its own — and the promoter's pre-incorporation liabilities are confined to the S. 15/19 machinery (contrast with the Salomon-line of cases, where the company's separate existence itself is beyond challenge).

⚖️ 7.1 Case 14 (prescribed) — Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co., (1878) 3 AC 1218 · (1874-80) All ER Rep. 271 (HL)

🧷 Case 14 · Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co. — the promoter's fiduciary position · secret profit · rescission
(1878) 3 AC 1218 (HL)(1874-80) All ER Rep. 271Promoter — fiduciary positionDuty of disclosureRescission + return of price

Procedural history (from the report): bill filed by the respondent company in the Chancery Division to rescind the contract for purchase of the island — Malins V.C. decreed rescission; Court of Appeal (Sir George Jessel M.R., James and Baggallay L.JJ.) affirmed; the defendants (the syndicate/promoters) appealed to the House of Lords, which affirmed the Court of Appeal — Lord Cairns LC dissenting only on the question of the remedy (he would have refused rescission on the ground of laches and impossibility of restoring the island). The headnote is printed in the DU casebook and is the exam's most quotable paragraph.

① Facts
  • The property. Sombrero is a small island in the West Indies (about 1¼ miles long) containing beds of phosphate of lime. It belonged to the Crown; a lease was made of it for 21 years from 16 March 1865 at a rent of £1,000.
  • The Old Sombrero Co. took an assignment of the lease, paying £100,000 and taking it subject to a mortgage of £12,400. That company was wound up by the Court of Chancery, and in 1871, in the winding up, the lease came to be sold.
  • The syndicate. The appellants — headed by Baron Erlanger — with Thomas Westall, a solicitor, formed a syndicate to buy the lease. On 30 August 1871 they agreed to buy it from the official liquidator for £55,000, the contract being made in the name of Westall on behalf of his principals.
  • The company. Shortly before 20 September 1871 the syndicate determined to form a joint-stock company, to be registered on 21 September, and to sell the island to it for £110,000 (£80,000 cash + £30,000 in fully paid-up shares). The memorandum stated the object as the purchasing, leasing and working of mines or quarries of phosphate of lime in the island; the articles (art. 65) fixed four to seven directors, two a quorum, and (art. 82) expressly empowered the directors to adopt the contract of 20 September 1871.
  • The contract. By the contract of 20 September 1871 John Marsh Evans agreed to sell, and Francis Pavy agreed to purchase, the lease and property for £110,000. Evans was trustee or agent for Baron Erlanger and other members of the syndicate; Pavy was a person whose name was introduced "as a matter of form, to represent the company about to be created" in case it should adopt the contract. The memorandum was signed by Evans and six other persons, all nominees of the syndicate, none of whom was in a condition to afford disinterested protection to the company's interests.
  • The first directors (art. 65): M. Drouyn de Lhuys (asked by Erlanger to act; "trusting entirely to Baron Erlanger"; his appointment "afforded no protection to the company"), E.B. Eastwick (had asked Erlanger to let him join; absent in Canada — did not take his seat till end of December 1871), Sir Thomas Dakin (disinterested, embarked his own money — but made no inquiry though he knew the promoters were the vendors), John Marsh Evans (himself the vendor — "whether… beneficially interested or as trustee for the syndicate is immaterial"), and Rear-Admiral R.J. Macdonald (per Lord Blackburn, "evidently come into the company with a foregone conclusion that everything his friend Erlanger had done was right").
  • The board meeting of 29 September 1871. Present: Sir Thomas Dakin, Admiral Macdonald, Evans — and Westall, the solicitor to the syndicate (who had a £500 interest in the syndicate). A prospectus was produced, ready for issue, stating that the contract for purchase had been made by the directors; and the first resolution — approving and confirming the contract — was carried, without any inquiry into facts and figures.
  • Aftermath. A considerable number of shareholders came forward; in November 1871 the purchase price was paid. In February 1872 the first shareholders' meeting took place; in June 1872 a committee of shareholders was appointed to investigate; counsel's advice was taken; and on 24 December 1872 the company filed the bill to rescind.
② Issues
  1. Did the promoters stand in a fiduciary position towards the company they formed, so as to be accountable for the profit they made on the sale of the island to it — or were they merely vendors who had bought the property for themselves, free to sell at any price they pleased?
  2. Was the sale liable to be set aside because the company did not get the opportunity, through an independent and competent board of directors, of exercising an intelligent judgment on the purchase (the prospectus saying only that the purchase "had been made by the directors")?
  3. Had the company lost its right to rescind by laches or acquiescence — the bill having been filed 14 months after the purchase and about two months after the shareholders "must have known" of the facts (23 October 1872)? Given the wasting nature of the property (a 21-year mineral lease, since worked), could the parties still be restored to their original positions?
  4. What relief, if the contract fell — rescission with return of the purchase money, or only damages?
③ Arguments — for the promoters/appellants (the syndicate)
  • No trust: the syndicate bought the lease from the liquidator for its own absolute benefit; they were never trustees of the company — which was not even in existence when they bought — and a trustee's disability to profit cannot apply.
  • Non-disclosure of the price need not be disclosed: before the company was formed the property was their own; a vendor is under no duty to disclose what he paid for his property (the bill's own assertion to the contrary was misconceived).
  • The company had notice — through the prospectus and the contract itself — that the purchase was from the syndicate; the directors knew the sellers; the shareholders sanctioned the purchase; and Erlanger was not shown to have misrepresented anything.
  • Laches and impossibility: the island had been worked — the phosphate extracted — for over a year; the lease was wasting; the parties could not be restored in statu quo; and the company had waited until the concern appeared to be failing, holding on while it was prosperous. Relief in equity was therefore barred.
③ Arguments — for the company/respondent
  • Fiduciary position: the promoters "had in their hands the creation and moulding of the company"; they selected the directors, settled the purchase contract, and then procured its adoption — they were, in substance, both buyer and seller.
  • No independent judgment: of the five directors, two (de Lhuys, Eastwick) could not attend; Evans was the vendor; the solicitor present (Westall) was virtually another vendor; only Dakin was disinterested — and even he made no inquiry. The board "ratified" a pre-arranged bargain — a body appointed solely to adopt the promoters' terms.
  • The prospectus misled: it stated that the contract for purchase "had been made by the directors" — concealing the real history — and the shareholders were induced to subscribe without knowing the material facts.
  • No laches: the shareholders' committee was investigating; negotiations for a compromise were in progress; the bill was filed reasonably promptly (24 December 1872), and there was no acquiescence with knowledge and free choice (Selborne's two conditions were absent).
④ Observations of the Court
  • Lord Cairns LC (on the promoter's position — the passage to quote): "They stand, in my opinion, undoubtedly in a fiduciary position. They have had in their hands the creation and moulding of the company… I do not say that the owner of property may not promote and form a joint-stock company and then sell his property to it, but I do say that, if he does, he is bound to take care that he sells it to the company through the medium of a board of directors who can and do exercise an independent and intelligent judgment on the transaction, and are not left under the belief that the property belongs, not to the promoter, but to some other person."
  • Lord Cairns LC (on the board meeting of 29 September): "I cannot but regard a meeting at which two of the principal directors did not and could not attend — at which one who did attend, and take part in the deliberations, was at once a person buying and selling — where the legal adviser present and assisting was virtually another vendor, and where the two remaining directors are not shown to have had the means of exercising, or to have exercised, any intelligent judgment on the subject — as little else than a mockery and a delusion." And on the quorum argument: "a provision which, in my opinion, could not be held to remedy defects such as I have pointed out as going to the entire constitution of the board."
  • Lord Cairns LC (dissenting only on relief): "if within a proper time after the completion of this purchase a bill had been filed by the company… the purchase must have been set aside"; but given "the very peculiar nature of the property and the utter impossibility of restoring the property… and looking to the amount of notice which the company had by the prospectus, and to the knowledge which they might have obtained", he would have held it "contrary to the principles of equity to give to the company the relief which at an earlier period they might have obtained."
  • Lord Hatherley (three heads): (1) the syndicate were not trustees — the trust principle had no application, since "the syndicate never constituted themselves trustees, but intended to sell and did sell this property to the new company…"; (2) the purchase was nevertheless liable to be impeached on other grounds — on that point all the judges were unanimous; (3) no laches: "We have the company quite right… down to Oct. 23… The bill was filed on Dec. 24"; considering the magnitude of the case, the information to be obtained and the committee's efforts at compromise — no such laches as to waive the right; the appeal "ought to fail".
  • Lord Hatherley (on the extent of disclosure — vendor and purchaser): "a vendor need not do what was at one time asserted by this bill, namely, disclose what he has paid in effecting his own anterior purchase before asking an enhanced price from him to whom he seeks to sell the property; but he must not be guilty of any conduct which amounts to unfair concealment on his part of the real facts of the case, which ought in common fairness to be disclosed to a person seeking to purchase or entering into a treaty with him for that purpose."
  • Lord O'Hagan (on the privilege and its price): "The privilege given to them for promoting such a company for such an object involved obligations of a very serious kind. It required, in its exercise, the utmost good faith, the completest truthfulness, and a careful regard to the protection of the future shareholders.… The power to nominate a directorate is manifestly capable of great abuse… It should be watched with jealousy… In all such cases the directorate nominated by the promoters should stand between them and the public… If they have not those qualities, they are unworthy of trust; they are the betrayers and not the guardians of the company they govern." On the facts: the island's value was judicially ascertained at £55,000; "a few days after, circumstances remaining wholly unchanged, a contract for the sale of it at £110,000 was ratified by three of the five directors…assisted by their solicitor, who was a member of the syndicate"; the directors "were nominated merely to ratify any terms the promoters might dictate". Crucially: "This does not necessarily imply the imputation of evil purpose or conscious fraud… The fiduciary obligation may be violated, though there may be no intention to do injustice."
  • Lord Selborne (on acquiescence): even a wasting mineral lease "of speculative value" can be rescinded — "It was the act of the vendors to put their property, being of that character, in such a position"; the company had managed the property properly (appointed a fit manager when the old one failed); nothing done or left undone stood in the way. "Two things were generally necessary" for acquiescence: (i) sufficient knowledge of the facts on which the equity depended; and (ii) where rescission is sought, substantial freedom of choice and action, independent of the original influence under which the voidable contract was made — neither existed here.
  • Lord Gordon (the change of position): the property "belonged absolutely to the members of the syndicate"; but "in issuing a prospectus to the public with the view of inducing the public to take up its shares… the syndicate changed the position it originally held, and put itself in a fiduciary relation to the company which it was engaged in forming. It thus became incumbent on the promoters, not only to make a full disclosure of the position they as owners held, "but also to make arrangements, by the appointment of competent officials and otherwise, for enabling the company to form an independent judgment as to the propriety of purchasing the property of the promoter." On laches: "The onus lay on the appellants of showing that there had been such laches… I think that the appellants have failed."
⑤ Decision
  • The appeal was dismissed and the decree affirmed (by majority; Lord Cairns LC dissenting only on the ground that rescission had become inequitable — he thought the company's remedy, if any, should have been pursued earlier).
  • The contract was rescinded and the purchase price ordered to be returned to the company — the relief the headnote states: "Where a company is not given by the promoters an opportunity of exercising, through independent directors, a fair and independent judgment on the subject of the purchase by the company of property from the promoters, the court may order the contract to be rescinded and the purchase price to be returned."
  • Lord Hatherley, Lord O'Hagan, Lord Selborne and Lord Gordon concurred; Lord Blackburn (who tried the case at first instance on evidence) had held that Macdonald came "with a foregone conclusion" — his finding was accepted throughout.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The promoter's position: not a trustee of the company he promotes (he owns the property he sells), but in a fiduciary position towards it — with obligations of "the utmost good faith, the completest truthfulness, and a careful regard to the protection of the future shareholders."
  • Burden of proof: where promoters sell property to the company, the burden is on them of showing that they have not taken any unfair advantage resulting from their relations with the company.
  • Duty to give an independent board: a promoter selling his own property to the company must sell it "through the medium of a board of directors who can and do exercise an independent and intelligent judgment on the transaction" — directors aware of the promoter's ownership, competent and impartial as to price; a board of the promoter's nominees makes ratification "a mockery and a delusion"; a two-director quorum will not cure a defect "going to the entire constitution of the board".
  • Duty of disclosure — with limits: the promoter must disclose all material facts relating to the transaction (his interest, the real character of the deal); he need not disclose what he paid, but must not be guilty of unfair concealment of facts which ought in common fairness to be disclosed.
  • Fault not required: the fiduciary duty may be violated without intent to defraud — "the fiduciary obligation may be violated, though there may be no intention to do injustice."
  • Remedy: if the company is not given the opportunity, through independent directors, of exercising a fair and independent judgment, the court may rescind the contract and order the purchase price returned, even though the property is a wasting mineral lease; acquiescence requires (i) sufficient knowledge of the facts and (ii) substantial freedom of choice and action; laches must be proved by the promoter, and a two-month gap filled with negotiations is not laches.
  • The modern significance: this is the fount of the Indian law of promoters — adopted in Weavers Mills Ltd. v. Balkis Ammal (AIR 1969 Mad 462) — and its "independent board" requirement is the ancestor of today's related-party-transaction regime (S. 188) and independent-director provisions.

🔗 The Erlanger–Salomon boundary (from the DU casebook's discussion of Salomon)

The casebook expressly distinguishes Erlanger while discussing Salomon v. Salomon & Co. (1897): in Erlanger the "vendor… got up the company, with the view of selling his adventure to it, attracted shareholders by a prospectus which was essentially false", and the directors were "virtually his nominees", so that the shareholders sanctioned the purchase "without being aware of the real facts". By contrast, in Salomon the contract between Salomon and the company was approved with full knowledge — the company and the shareholders knew exactly what they were doing — and therefore Erlanger "has no application".

Exam use: whenever an answer on Salomon's separate entity is challenged with Erlanger (or vice versa), give this distinction: separate entity is one thing; a promoter's obligation of fair dealing is another. The company's separate personality is no shield for a promoter who sold it its own property through a puppet board.

🧷 Twycross v. Grant (1877) LR 2 CPD 469 (CA) — the creator of the company and liability for the prospectus
(1877) LR 2 CPD 469 (CA)Who is a promoterProspectus liability
① Facts
  • Grant and another undertook to form a company "with reference to a given project" and to set it going; the scheme, the provisional formation, the finding and qualification of the directors, the preparation of the prospectus, and the payment of the printing and advertising expenses were all their work.
  • The prospectus contained false statements; persons subscribed for shares on the faith of it and suffered loss.
  • Action by the subscribers against Grant and his co-promoter for the loss — the defendants answering that the directors, not they, were the authors and signatories of the prospectus.
② Issues
  • Who is a "promoter" for the purpose of liability?
  • Is a promoter liable for misstatements in a prospectus which he caused to be issued, though he was not one of the formal signatories?
③ Arguments — for the subscribers (plaintiffs)
  • The defendants "framed the scheme", "provisionally formed the company", "found the directors and qualified them", "prepared the prospectus" — they were the creators of the company from the very beginning to the end; they, not the figurehead directors, issued the document to the world.
  • One who issues or authorises the issue of a prospectus with false statements must answer for the loss of those who subscribed relying on it.
③ Arguments — for the promoters (defendants)
  • The prospectus was issued by the directors of the company; the defendants were not directors and did not sign it; no fraud was alleged against them personally.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Cockburn CJ (adopted in Weavers Mills Ltd. v. Balkis Ammal, AIR 1969 Mad 462): a promoter is "one who undertakes to form a company with reference to a given project, and to set it going, and who takes the necessary steps to accomplish that purpose".
  • On the facts: "that the defendants were the promoters of the company from the very beginning can admit of no doubt. They framed the scheme, they not only provisionally formed the company, but were, in fact, to the end its creators, they found the directors, and qualified them, they prepared the prospectus, they paid for printing and advertising, and the expenses incidental to bringing the undertaking before the world."
  • The court added that the functions of promoters come to an end as soon as they hand over the company to a governing body, like a Board of directors.
⑤ Decision
  • The promoters were held liable for the loss caused by the false statements in the prospectus — the formal signature of the directors was no answer.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The functional test of a promoter: he is the author of the scheme, the creator of the company, the selector of the directors, the issuer of the prospectus.
  • Liability for the prospectus follows authorship, not signature — the ancestor of Ss. 34–35 of the Companies Act, 2013 ("every person who authorised the issue of the prospectus").
🧷 Whaley Bridge Calico Printing Co. v. Green (1880) LR 5 QBD 109 (CA) — "a term not of law, but of business"
(1880) LR 5 QBD 109 (CA)Definition of promotionSecret profit
① Facts
  • Green and others, who held an option over certain assets, formed a company to take over those assets — the vendor-option holders also becoming directors of the new company — and the assets were sold to the company at a price which yielded them a profit.
  • The company sued to recover the profit as a secret profit made in the course of the promotion.
② Issues
  • Were Green and co. promoters, so as to be accountable for the profit?
  • What is the meaning of the word "promoter" in law?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The promoters bought cheaply through their option and resold to the company at an enhanced price without disclosing the margin; they were in a fiduciary position and must account.
③ Arguments — for the defendant promoters
  • The transaction was known to and approved by the company's board; they were vendors, not trustees; the profit was their own.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Bowen LJ: the term "promoter" is "a term not of law, but of business", usefully summing up in a single word "a number of business operations, familiar to the commercial world, by which a company is generally brought into existence".
  • This is why the term long defied precise definition — the law looks at what the person did, not at what he called himself.
⑤ Decision
  • The defendants, standing in the position of promoters, were held accountable for the undisclosed profit made on the sale of the assets to the company (the classic statement of the "promoter must not make an undisclosed profit" rule).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Promotion is a business concept; the promoter is identified by his function — bringing the company into existence; and having brought it into existence, he cannot make a profit from the formation without disclosing it.
🧷 Emma Silver Mining Co. v. Lewis (1879) LR 4 CPD 396 — "prima facie a promoter" · the duty to account
(1879) LR 4 CPD 396Prima facie promoterDuty continues after incorporation
① Facts
  • Lewis took part in the formation of a mining company and received or was concerned in a benefit from the promotion (a commission/profit from the sale of the mine to the company), which was not disclosed to the company.
  • The company, after incorporation and issue of shares, sought to recover the undisclosed benefit.
② Issues
  • Was Lewis a "promoter" — having merely taken part in a part of the formation?
  • Did his duty to account end with incorporation?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • Anyone who takes part in the formation of the company is a promoter; the fiduciary duty attaches from that participation and the benefit obtained must be accounted for.
③ Arguments — for the promoter
  • The company once formed, he ceased to be concerned with it; the benefit was earned before incorporation and was his own; he had no fiduciary relation.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Lindley J: "any person who undertakes to take part in the forming of a company… is prima facie a promoter of the company"; the term "has no very definite meaning", and the advantage of the flexible definition is that only those who fulfil the functional concept are subjected to the strict duties (per Jacobus Marler Estates Ltd. v. Marler (1913) 85 LJ PC 167).
  • One who leaves the task of promotion to others and sits behind to share their profits is likely to be regarded as a promoter.
  • The authority is the foundation of the rule (adopted in Avtar Singh) that the duty continues even after incorporation until the profits are fully disclosed and fully accounted for.
⑤ Decision
  • The promoter was held accountable for the undisclosed benefit obtained from the promotion, notwithstanding that the company had by then been incorporated and was trading.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Participation in the formation is enough to make one prima facie a promoter; incorporation does not discharge him — the duty to disclose and account continues until the profits are fully disclosed and accounted for.
🧷 Lagunas Nitrate Co. v. Lagunas Syndicate (1899) 2 Ch. 392 (CA) — promoter after formation · independent board
(1899) 2 Ch. 392 (CA)Promoter need not be founderOpen dealing
① Facts
  • The Lagunas Syndicate promoted the Lagunas Nitrate Co.; the properties (nitrate grounds) were sold by the syndicate's nominees to the new company at a price which included the promoters' profit; the syndicate's directors also became directors of the new company.
  • The company later sought relief, alleging that the price was excessive and that the promoters had not made proper disclosure.
② Issues
  • Must a person be connected with the initial formation to be a promoter — or does one who helps to float the capital / carries on the promotion count?
  • When is a promoter's profit recoverable, and when not?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The sale was by promoters to their own company; the profit should be disgorged or the difference in value refunded.
③ Arguments — for the syndicate
  • The transaction was gone into openly by a board which knew of the promoters' interest; the company had the property and used it; the price was a matter of commercial judgment for that board.
④ Observations of the Court
  • To be a promoter one need not necessarily be associated with the initial formation — one who subsequently helps to arrange the floating of its capital will equally be regarded as a promoter (Taxmann, citing this case).
  • Where the promoters' interest is known to, and the contract is considered by, an independent-minded board (and where the company, with knowledge, takes the benefit), the transaction stands; the promoter is not liable to make good an honest business judgment.
⑤ Decision
  • Relief was refused — the sale was upheld; the profit made by the promoters on a sale to a company with full knowledge on the part of the board was not recoverable.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Promotion is a process, not a moment — the promoter's role (and his duties) can continue after the initial formation; but if the bargain is made openly before an independent board, the promoter's profit is his — Erlanger protects the company that is not given a chance of independent judgment, not the company that, knowing the facts, takes the bargain.

💰 7.3 The secret-profit cases

🧷 Gluckstein v. Barnes (1900) AC 240 (HL) — "a disclosure to the parties is not a disclosure to the company"
(1900) AC 240 (HL)Secret profitDisclosure to whom
① Facts
  • A syndicate was formed to raise a fund, buy a property called "Olympia" and resell it to a company. They first bought up some of the charges upon the property for sums below what the charges afterwards realised, thereby making a profit of £20,000.
  • They then bought the property for £1,40,000, formed a limited company, and resold the property to it for £1,80,000 — of which company they were the first directors.
  • The prospectus invited applications and disclosed the two prices (£1,40,000 and £1,80,000), but not the £20,000 profit on the charges. Shares were issued, and the company afterwards went into liquidation.
② Issues
  • Was the £20,000 profit a secret profit which the promoters were bound to disclose and account for?
  • Was it enough that the profit was known to the parties to the transaction (the syndicate's own nominees)?
③ Arguments — for the liquidator/company
  • The £20,000 was an undisclosed gain made by the promoters in the course of the promotion; the two prices disclosed did not reveal it; the promoters were bound to account even in liquidation.
③ Arguments — for the promoter (defendant)
  • The fact was known to the parties to the transaction — every member of the syndicate and every director was aware of it; there was therefore no "concealment" from anybody who counted.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Lord Halsbury LC: "It is too absurd to suggest that a disclosure to the parties to this transaction is a disclosure to the company. They were there by the terms of the agreement to do the work of the syndicate, that is to say, to cheat the shareholders; and this, forsooth, is to be treated as a disclosure to the company, when they were really there to hoodwink the shareholders…"
  • Lord Halsbury LC: "Disclosure is not the most appropriate word to use when a person who plays many parts announces to himself in one character what he has done and is doing in another."
⑤ Decision
  • Held: the promoters ought to have disclosed the £20,000; the defendant, one of the promoters, was liable to account for it to the company (in the winding up).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Disclosure to the promoters' own men is no disclosure to the company; the relevant audience is the body of shareholders/investors invited to subscribe. A profit made on the promotion — however "known" within the syndicate — must be disclosed to the company and, failing that, accounted for; liquidation does not bar the claim.
🧷 Lydney and Wigpool Iron Ore Co. v. Bird (1886) LR 33 Ch. D. 85 (CA) — disclose the profit minus the expenses
(1886) LR 33 Ch. D. 85 (CA)Net profitMeasure of disclosure
① Facts
  • Bird was a promoter (and director) of the Lydney and Wigpool Iron Ore Co.; he participated in the sale of property/mining rights to the company at a price above what the sellers had paid, and claimed to be entitled to keep the whole margin.
  • The company's suit challenged the profit; the question was what the promoter could lawfully retain.
② Issues
  • May a promoter make a profit at all — and, if so, of what amount must he account?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The entire margin on the resale was the promoter's gain from the promotion and must be disgorged.
③ Arguments — for the promoter
  • He had laid out his own money and risk in acquiring the property; the margin was his reward, and he had (in substance) accounted.
④ Observations of the Court
  • The promoter's profit is not forfeited merely because there is a profit — he is bound to disclose his profit minus the expenses incurred in earning it; what he may not do is retain a profit which has never been disclosed.
⑤ Decision
  • The promoter was held entitled to the profit to the extent it had been disclosed/accounted (net of expenses), and accountable beyond that — the case fixing the measure of the disclosure: profit minus expenses.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • A promoter may lawfully profit from the promotion, but the profit disclosed and accounted for must be net — after deducting the expenses of earning it; he cannot round up the benefits and keep the difference undisclosed.
🧷 Omnium Electric Palaces Ltd. v. Baines (1914) 1 Ch. 332 (CA) — promoter is not a trustee; profit is lawful if disclosed
(1914) 1 Ch. 332 (CA)Not a trusteeDisclosed profit is lawful
① Facts
  • Baines and another promoted the Omnium Electric Palaces Ltd. and, at the formation, sold to it (or procured the sale of) property — machinery/land for an electric-theatre business — at a figure which included a profit for themselves.
  • The company, later in liquidation or suit by shareholders, sought to recover the profit, arguing that the promoters were trustees and could not profit at all.
② Issues
  • Are promoters trustees of the company so as to be disabled from any profit?
  • Where does the promoter's fiduciary position come from?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • As promoters/directors they stood in a fiduciary position; the profit belonged in equity to the company.
③ Arguments — for the promoters
  • They were not trustees — the company was not in existence when the property was bought — and the arrangement was known to and approved by the board and shareholders.
④ Observations of the Court
  • "They are not trustees in the real sense of the word, because the company may not be in existence as a legal person" — there being no trust property and no cestui que trust. “The fiduciary position emerges from agency.”
  • "There is no duty that the promoter must restrain himself from making any profit whatsoever in the business of promotion"; subject to disclosure, it is quite lawful for a promoter to sell his property to the company at a profit to himself.
⑤ Decision
  • The claim failed — the promoters had made their profit openly and the company, with knowledge, took the benefit; there was no secret profit to be recovered.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Promoter ≠ trustee (no company in existence, no cestui que trust), though his position is akin to a trustee's and his dealings must be open and fair; the fiduciary position emerges from agency; and a disclosed profit is a lawful profit — the vice is secrecy, not profit.
🧷 Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres of Varieties Ltd. (1902) 2 Ch. 809 (CA) — damages where rescission is impossible
(1902) 2 Ch. 809 (CA)RemedyMeasure of damages = profit
① Facts
  • Promoters sold a theatre/variety-hall property to a company they had formed at a price yielding them a large profit; the purchase was sanctioned by the shareholders without the material facts; the company went into liquidation.
  • By the time of the liquidator's claim the property had been enjoyed and disposed of — the parties could not be restored to their original positions, so the contract could not be rescinded.
② Issues
  • Where rescission is impossible, can the company still recover anything from the promoters who breached their duty of good faith?
  • If yes, what is the measure?
③ Arguments — for the liquidator
  • The promoters breached a fiduciary duty; the profit is the fruit of the breach and must be restored; impossibility of rescission should not immunise them.
③ Arguments — for the promoters
  • Rescission being barred, no remedy survives; the company took the property and ran the business; there is no loss to measure.
④ Observations of the Court
  • The company was not in a position to return the property, so rescission was out of the question; but the promoter's breach of the duty of good faith still gave a right to compensation — the measure of damages being the profit which the promoter made from the breach of his duty.
⑤ Decision
  • The liquidator's claim against the promoters succeeded for the amount of the undisclosed profit.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Where rescission is not possible (the company cannot return the property), the company can recover damages for breach of the duty of good faith, and the damages are measured by the profit the promoter made from the breach — the profit is the quantification of the fiduciary's liability.

📄 7.4 Pre-incorporation contract cases

🧷 Kelner v. Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174 — where the company never came into existence
(1866) LR 2 CP 174Personal liability of agentNo principal = contract with the agent
① Facts
  • Kelner (the plaintiff) intended to sell wine to a company which was to be formed, but under the contract he agreed to sell to the proposed directors (Baxter and others) of the company.
  • The proposed directors intended to buy the wine on behalf of the company; but as the company was not in existence when the contract was made, they took delivery personally (and drank/used it); the company was never incorporated, and the wine was not paid for.
  • Kelner sued the individuals for the price.
② Issues
  • Can a contract made "on behalf of" a company not yet in existence bind the company? (No — but that was not the question on which the case turned.)
  • Are the persons who made the contract personally liable to the seller, having contracted for a principal that did not exist?
③ Arguments — for the seller
  • The wine was delivered and consumed; the buyers were the only persons who could have been liable; they cannot escape by saying they meant to bind somebody else who never existed.
③ Arguments — for the proposed directors
  • They contracted as agents only; the contract was expressed to be for the company; an agent incurs no personal liability on a contract made within his authority.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Where a contract is made on behalf of a principal known to both parties to be non-existent, the contract is deemed to have been entered into personally by the actual maker — there is no other person who could possibly be bound, and the agent cannot be heard to say that he meant to bind a non-entity.
⑤ Decision
  • Held: the proposed directors, having contracted on behalf of a principal who did not exist and having received the wine, must pay for it — personal liability.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The class-room rule for every answer on pre-incorporation contracts: an agent contracting for a non-existent principal is himself the contracting party — personally liable (and personally entitled), subject only to the S. 15/19 Specific Relief Act machinery once the company is incorporated and adopts.
🧷 Newborne v. Sensolid (Great Britain) Ltd. (1954) 1 QB 45 (CA) — the contrast: contract with the "company" only
(1954) 1 QB 45 (CA)Form of signatureContrast with Kelner
① Facts
  • Newborne contracted for the sale of goods, signing "for and on behalf of Sensolid (Great Britain) Ltd." — a company which in fact never was incorporated.
  • The goods were delivered; the price was not paid; the seller sued the signatory personally (the company of course could not be sued).
② Issues
  • Did the signatory contract personally, or only as agent for the named (unborn) company?
③ Arguments — for the seller
  • There was no company, so the individual must be liable on the contract, in line with Kelner v. Baxter.
③ Arguments — for the signatory
  • The contract on its face was with the company — he signed only as its agent, and did not purport to undertake any personal obligation.
④ Observations of the Court (Lord Goddard CJ)
  • The whole point is the construction of the contract: if the paper contract is expressed to be made by the company (though unborn), the person signing as its agent has not contracted personally — he is not liable, though of course the "company" cannot enforce or be made liable either.
⑤ Decision
  • The seller's action failed — no personal liability of the signatory (an unhappy result for the seller, and the reason English law later enacted s. 36(2) of the Companies Act 1985, now s. 51 of the Companies Act 2006, making the agent of an unformed company personally liable).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Kelner and Newborne together give the Indian rule: it depends on the construction of the contract — personal liability where the parties dealt with the maker personally; no liability where the contract is expressed to be with the company alone. In India the would-be creditor's protection is (i) suing the promoter personally under Kelner, and (ii) enforcing against the company under S. 19 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 after adoption.
🧷 Natal Land & Colonisation Co. v. Pauline Colliery Syndicate (1904) AC 120 (PC) — the company cannot adopt and sue
(1904) AC 120 (PC)No adoption at common lawSpecific performance refused
① Facts
  • N. Co. entered into an agreement with one C., who acted on behalf of a proposed syndicate, under which N. Co. was to give the syndicate a lease of coal-mining rights.
  • The syndicate was then registered as a company, struck a seam of coal, and claimed the lease. N. Co. refused.
  • The company sued for specific performance of the agreement, or in the alternative damages.
② Issues
  • Can a company, after incorporation, adopt or ratify a contract purporting to have been made on its behalf before it existed, and enforce it?
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The agreement was made for its benefit; it has come into existence and now adopts the contract; the lessor should be held to it.
③ Arguments — for N. Co.
  • The syndicate was not in existence when the contract was signed; there was no party to contract with; adoption of a contract by a person who did not exist at its date is impossible.
④ Observations of the Court
  • "A company cannot by adoption or ratification obtain the benefit of a contract purporting to have been made on its behalf before the company came into existence" — the company is not a party to the contract, and there is nothing it can ratify.
⑤ Decision
  • The action was held not maintainable — no specific performance, no damages.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • At common law a pre-incorporation contract is void and incapable of adoption in favour of the company. The company's only route is statutory — S. 15, Specific Relief Act, 1963 (company may enforce if the contract was made for its purposes and is warranted by the terms of incorporation) — hence the celebrated problem-question: "A company cannot ratify a pre-incorporation contract, though it is open to it to enter into a fresh one."
🧷 Re English & Colonial Produce Co. (1906) 2 Ch. 435 — no indemnity for the promoter's expenses
(1906) 2 Ch. 435No reimbursementRatification impossible
① Facts
  • A solicitor, on the instructions of certain gentlemen, prepared the necessary documents and obtained the registration of a company; he paid the registration fee and incurred the incidental expenses of registration.
  • The company refused to pay him for those services and expenses, and he claimed reimbursement.
② Issues
  • Is the company liable for expenses incurred on its behalf before its incorporation — can it be sued for them, or can it ratify the expenditure?
③ Arguments — for the solicitor
  • His services were rendered for the company, which has taken the benefit of the registration; justice requires reimbursement.
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The company "could not be sued in law for those expenses, inasmuch as it was not in existence at the time when the expenses were incurred… and ratification was impossible."
④ Observations of the Court
  • "It is not desirable to saddle the corporation with burdens imposed upon it in advance by over-optimistic promoters." The promoter (and his agent) must look to the incorporation to perfect his arrangements, or make a new contract with the company afterwards (Howard v. Patent Ivory Mfg. Co.).
⑤ Decision
  • The claim was rejected — the company was not bound to pay.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • A pre-incorporation contract cannot be enforced against the company — neither can it be ratified by it; the promoter's remedy is to secure a fresh agreement after incorporation, or an adoption under S. 15 of the Specific Relief Act. Half the examination problem-questions on pre-incorporation contracts turn on this pair: Kelner (promoter liable) + English & Colonial (company not liable).

🎫 7.5 Certificate of incorporation cases

🧷 Re Barned's Banking Co., Peel's case (1867) LR 2 HL 325 — "conclusive answer to such objection"
(1867) LR 2 HL 325Conclusive evidenceNo inquiry into prior proceedings
① Facts
  • After signature and before registration a proposed memorandum of association was altered without the authority of the subscribers, so materially that the alteration "entirely neutralised and annihilated the original execution and signature" of the document.
  • The company was, nevertheless, registered, and the Registrar gave his certificate of incorporation.
  • It was objected in the winding up that the memorandum had not been signed by seven, or indeed by any, subscribers and that the provisions of the Act had not been complied with — so the whole registration was bad.
② Issues
  • When the certificate of incorporation has been issued, may the court inquire into the regularity of the prior proceedings — here, the obliterated signature of the memorandum?
③ Arguments — for the objectors
  • The memorandum was not signed by the statutory number (indeed by anyone) in the form in which it was registered; a company registered on such a document is not a company at all.
③ Arguments — for the company/certificate
  • The statute confides the preliminary examination to the Registrar; the certificate is the statutory answer; otherwise no one could safely deal with the company.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Lord Cairns assented to the proposition that the memorandum had not been signed as required — but "the certificate of incorporation is not merely a prima facie answer, but a conclusive answer to such objection.… When once the certificate of incorporation is given, nothing is to be inquired into as to the regularity of the prior proceedings."
⑤ Decision
  • The objection failed; the incorporation stood notwithstanding the defective execution of the memorandum.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The certificate is conclusive: defects in the documents and in the steps antecedent to registration are shut out by the certificate. This is the earliest and the classic authority for the "conclusive answer" doctrine which later reached India.
🧷 Moosa Goolam Ariff v. Ebrahim Goolam Ariff (1913) LR 40 IA 219 (PC) — the Indian locus classicus
(1913) LR 40 IA 219 (PC)Conclusive for all purposesMinors · guardian signatures
① Facts
  • The memorandum of association of a company was signed by two adult persons and by a guardian of the other five members, who were minors at the time, the guardian making a separate signature for each of the minors.
  • The Registrar, however, registered the company and issued under his hand a certificate of incorporation.
  • The plaintiff contended that this certificate of incorporation should be declared void — the condition of seven subscribers having never been satisfied.
② Issues
  • Where the conditions of registration were not in fact complied with (no seven effective subscribers), is the certificate of incorporation still conclusive — can the company's existence be collaterally destroyed?
③ Arguments — for the plaintiff
  • The statutory minimum of subscribers was not truly satisfied; the guardian's signatures were not the signatures of members; the Registrar ought to have refused the certificate; the certificate is therefore void.
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The Registrar has issued the certificate; the Act makes it conclusive; the proper remedy, if any, is collateral — not a declaration of nullity.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Lord Macnaghten: "Their Lordships will assume that the conditions of registration prescribed by the Indian Companies Act were not duly complied with; that there were not seven subscribers to the memorandum and that the Registrar ought not to have granted the certificate. But the certificate is conclusive for all purposes."
⑤ Decision
  • The plaintiff's claim was rejected — the certificate could not be impeached.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The Indian locus classicus: even a defective memorandum (fewer than seven genuine subscribers, guardians signing for minors) cannot nullify incorporation once the certificate is issued; the certificate is conclusive for all purposes — a company born cannot be annihilated except by the winding-up machinery (T.V. Krishna — see below).
🧷 Cotman v. Brougham (1918) AC 514 (HL) — the Registrar's certificate and ultra vires objects
(1918) AC 514 (HL)Memorandum validityTwofold purpose of objects
① Facts
  • A company (the "Cotman" line of cases) had a memorandum containing objects widely beyond its powers; share subscription contracts and dealings were entered into which depended on the validity of those objects.
  • The Registrar had accepted the memorandum and granted the certificate of incorporation; and the memorandum had been registered and acted upon for years.
  • The parties later sought to challenge the validity of the memorandum/objects and to escape liability on the footing that the whole incorporation was bad.
② Issues
  • Can the validity of the memorandum be challenged after the Registrar has accepted it and the certificate has been granted?
  • What is the Registrar's function, and what is the purpose of the objects clause?
③ Arguments — for the challengers
  • The memorandum is void in part; the certificate cannot make a void document valid; the company's capacity was never what the memorandum said.
③ Arguments — for the certificate/company
  • The Registrar examined and accepted the memorandum; the certificate is conclusive evidence of compliance; the objects stand as registered.
④ Observations of the Court
  • The Registrar's function: he "ought to consider whether the requirements have been complied with, and to refuse registration if not" — the certificate being "conclusive evidence that all the requirements of this Act in respect of registration and of matters precedent and incidental thereto have been complied with" (Lord Wrenbury).
  • The twofold purpose of the objects clause: (i) the investor — "the person who takes shares knows what is the field of risk in which he is venturing"; and (ii) the dealer — the person dealing with the company "knows the contractual relation into which he is entering".
  • Effect: the Registrar accepted the memorandum; the certificate was granted; and the validity of the memorandum could not be challenged.
⑤ Decision
  • The memorandum stood as registered; the challenge failed (and the substratum/ultra vires litigation was confined to the specific transactions).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Registration cures formal invalidity of the constitution: once the Registrar has considered and accepted the memorandum and the certificate has been issued, the validity of the memorandum is not open to collateral attack. The certificate is conclusive as to compliance with the requirements of the Act in respect of registration and matters precedent or incidental thereto — though (per the other side of the line of authority) it does not make lawful what is unlawful.
🧷 T.V. Krishna v. Andhra Prabha (P) Ltd., AIR 1960 AP 123 — "if a company is born… the only method is winding up"
(AIR 1960 AP 123)Certificate cannot be cancelledRemedy: winding up
① Facts
  • Express Newspapers (P) Ltd., leading publishers, sold its undertaking to a new company, Andhra Prabha Private Ltd.
  • The Government had adopted certain recommendations of a Wage Board improving the terms of service and salaries of working journalists; it was alleged that the new company was formed for the illegal purpose of evading the new responsibility imposed by the Wage Board, and that therefore the registration of the company should be declared void.
② Issues
  • Can the registration of a company be declared void because of the (allegedly unlawful/evasive) purpose for which it was formed?— i.e., can the certificate be cancelled or the incorporation collaterally annulled in an Indian court?
③ Arguments — for the attack on registration
  • The incorporation was a device to escape wage-board obligations; the purpose was unlawful or opposed to public policy; the registration should be declared void.
③ Arguments — for the company
  • The purpose was not unlawful; in any event the certificate of incorporation is conclusive and the company, once born, can be dissolved only by the modes provided by law.
④ Observations of the Court (Chandra Reddy CJ)
  • The court did not assent to the proposition that the purpose for which the company was formed was unlawful or opposed to public policy — the company was validly incorporated.
  • "Thus the position is firmly established that if a company is born, the only method to get it extinguished is not by assailing its incorporation, but by resorting to the provisions of the enactments which provide for the winding up of the companies."
  • Even if some of the objects were illegal, the legal persona of the company could not have been extinguished by cancelling the certificate; the certificate is conclusive; the remedy is to wind up the company. But the illegal objects do not become legal by the issue of the certificate.
⑤ Decision
  • The challenge failed; the company's registration was upheld.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The Indian summary of the whole doctrine: the certificate of incorporation is conclusive; incorporation is not open to collateral attack; the only road to extinction is the statutory winding-up machinery — but an illegal object is not validated by the certificate (the avenue there being refusal by the Registrar, or winding up, or challenge where the object is unlawful: Performing Right Society; R. v. Registrar of Joint Stock Companies; Bowman v. Secular Society).

DU Semester PYQs + Best Model Answers

Questions drawn from the DU exam checklist for this topic (promotion/promoters; pre-incorporation contracts; registration; certificate of incorporation; S. 9), each with a full model answer in the structure the examiner rewards: definition → statutory text → case law → application.

Q1. "What is promotion? Who is a promoter? Discuss the legal position of a promoter in relation to the company he promotes." 10–15 marks · long answer
Part I — Promotion. Promotion is the first stage in the life of a company: the preliminary steps taken for the purpose of the registration and floatation of the company (Taxmann). Bowen LJ in Whaley Bridge Calico Printing Co. v. Green (1880) LR 5 QBD 109 describes "promoter" as "a term not of law, but of business", summing up "a number of business operations, familiar to the commercial world, by which a company is generally brought into existence." The functions of promotion: originating the scheme; getting together the subscribers; having the memorandum and articles prepared, executed and registered; finding bankers, brokers and legal advisers; locating the first directors; settling preliminary contracts with vendors and underwriters; and arranging the preparation and circulation of the prospectus and the placement of capital. Part II — Who is a promoter. Cockburn CJ in Twycross v. Grant (1877): "one who undertakes to form a company with reference to a given project, and to set it going, and who takes the necessary steps to accomplish that purpose" — adopted in Weavers Mills Ltd. v. Balkis Ammal (AIR 1969 Mad 462). Lindley J in Emma Silver Mining Co. v. Lewis (1879): anyone who "undertakes to take part in the forming of a company" is prima facie a promoter. S. 2(69), Companies Act, 2013 now gives the statutory definition — a person (a) named as such in a prospectus or identified by the company in the annual return (S. 92); or (b) having control over the affairs of the company, directly or indirectly, as shareholder, director or otherwise; or (c) in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the Board is accustomed to act — excluding persons acting merely in a professional capacity. A promoter may be an individual, syndicate, association, firm or company; he may become a promoter even after incorporation (Lagunas Nitrate); and "whether a person is a promoter or not is a question of fact in each case." His functions end when he hands over the company to the board (Twycross). Part III — Legal position. The promoter is not a trustee: in Erlanger v. New Sombrero Phosphate Co. (1878) 3 AC 1218, Lord Hatherley held the syndicate were "in no such sense trustees for the company", the property belonging to them absolutely; Omnium Electric Palaces Ltd. v. Baines (1914) 1 Ch 332 adds that there can be no trust because the company is not in existence when the property is acquired — the fiduciary position "emerges from agency". Nor is he an agent: a company before incorporation is a non-entity and cannot be bound. But he stands in a fiduciary position: "They stand, in my opinion, undoubtedly in a fiduciary position. They have had in their hands the creation and moulding of the company…" (Cairns LC). Consequences: the burden of showing no unfair advantage lies on him; he must disclose all material facts; he must give the company a board which can exercise an independent and intelligent judgment; he must not make secret profits (he may profit, but only openly — Omnium; Lydney & Wigpool — profit minus expenses; Gluckstein v. Barnes — disclosure to the parties is not disclosure to the company). His liabilities: personally liable on pre-incorporation contracts (Kelner v. Baxter) until the company adopts (Ss. 15, 19 Specific Relief Act, 1963); civil and criminal liability for misstatements in the prospectus (Ss. 34–35); liability for fraud in promotion (Ss. 7(5)-(6), 339, 447). He has a right to indemnity for legitimate expenses and to remuneration if agreed.
Q2. "The promoter is not an agent or trustee for the company he promotes; but stands in a fiduciary position towards it." Comment. 10 marks
Statement of the proposition (true in law): the promoter of a company is neither a trustee (Erlanger — Lord Hatherley; Omnium Electric Palaces) nor an agent (Kelner v. Baxter; Re English & Colonial Produce Co.) of the company — for the simple reason that the company does not exist when the promotion happens; there is no principal and no cestui que trust. Yet equity places him in a fiduciary position: he has "in his hands the creation and moulding of the company", and the power to define how, when, in what shape and under what supervision it starts into existence (Erlanger, Cairns LC). The obligations are "of a very serious kind… the utmost good faith, the completest truthfulness, and a careful regard to the protection of the future shareholders" (Lord O'Hagan). Powers. To form and mould the company; to frame its constitution; to nominate the first board; to settle preliminary contracts; to arrange capital and issue the prospectus; to hold property for the company in equity (Vali Pattabhirama Rao); and to be indemnified for legitimate expenses and remunerated if agreed. But he cannot bind the unborn company, cannot claim reimbursement as of right (English & Colonial), and cannot make secret profits. Liabilities. (i) Pre-incorporation contracts — personally liable (Kelner v. Baxter; contrast Newborne v. Sensolid), company neither bound nor entitled unless the Specific Relief Act, 1963 applies (S. 15 — company may enforce if contract for its purposes and warranted by the terms of incorporation; S. 19 — other party may enforce after adoption). (ii) Prospectus — S. 34 (criminal, S. 447 punishment) and S. 35 (civil compensation), grounded in Twycross v. Grant. (iii) Fraud — S. 7(5)-(6) (false particulars/fraudulent incorporation → S. 447; Tribunal orders: unlimited liability, removal of name, winding up), S. 339 (fraudulent conduct of business — personal liability without limit), S. 340. (iv) Account for secret profits (Gluckstein; Leeds & Hanley — damages measured by the profit). Conclusion: the statement is accurate and is the foundation of modern rules — the promoter is treated as a fiduciary who may deal with the company but must deal openly, must not take unfair advantage, and bears the burden of proving his own fairness.
Q3. Discuss the duties of a promoter. What are the remedies of the company where the promoter has made a secret profit? 10 marks
Duties. (1) Fiduciary duty — utmost good faith and complete truthfulness (Erlanger); (2) duty of disclosure — of his position, profit and interest in the property — to an independent board (Erlanger) or, where that is impossible (private/family companies), to the whole body of shareholders; disclosure to the "parties to the transaction" is not disclosure to the company (Gluckstein); he need not disclose what he paid, but must not unfairly conceal (Lord Hatherley); (3) duty of good faith — no unfair concealment, no misleading prospectus; (4) no secret profits — profit may be made but must be disclosed net of expenses (Omnium; Lydney & Wigpool); (5) duty to provide an independent and competent board when selling his property to the company (Erlanger — Cairns LC: "a mockery and a delusion"); (6) duty to account — continuing even after incorporation until profits are fully disclosed and accounted for (Emma Silver Mining Co.); (7) duty of truth in the prospectus (Twycross); and (8) statutory duties — S. 188 related-party discipline, SEBI contribution/lock-in norms. Remedies. (1) Rescission of the contract and return of the purchase price — the primary remedy (Erlanger: "the court may order the contract to be rescinded and the purchase price to be returned"); (2) account of the secret profit — even in liquidation (Gluckstein v. Barnes); (3) damages where rescission is impossible, measured by the profit made (Re Leeds & Hanley Theatres); (4) recovery of compensation where property is bought from him without disclosure of his interest; (5) criminal and civil remedies under the Act for prospectus fraud (Ss. 34–35, 447) and fraudulent conduct of business (S. 339); and (6) under the 2013 Act, the company can invoke S. 188 where the promoter is a related party.
Q4. "A certificate of incorporation is conclusive evidence that all the requirements of the Companies Act have been complied with." Comment. 10–15 marks
The rule. Once the Registrar registers the documents and issues the certificate (S. 7(2)) bearing the CIN (S. 7(3)), the certificate is the company's birth certificate and is conclusive evidence that all the requirements of the Act in respect of registration and of matters precedent or incidental thereto have been complied with — the validity of the certificate cannot be disputed on any ground of irregularity (Avtar Singh). The authorities. Re Barned's Banking Co., Peel's case (1867) LR 2 HL 325 — Lord Cairns: the certificate is "not merely a prima facie answer, but a conclusive answer"; "when once the certificate of incorporation is given nothing is to be inquired into as to the regularity of the prior proceedings" (even where the memorandum had been altered after signature, nullifying the execution). Oakes v. Turquand — Lord Chelmsford: "the certificate prevents all recurrence to prior matters essential to registration." Moosa Goolam Ariff v. Ebrahim Goolam Ariff (1913) — PC (Lord Macnaghten): even assuming there were not seven subscribers and the Registrar ought not to have granted the certificate, "the certificate is conclusive for all purposes". Cotman v. Brougham (1918) AC 514: the Registrar "ought to consider whether the requirements have been complied with, and to refuse registration if not" — but once accepted and certified, the validity of the memorandum cannot be challenged. T.V. Krishna v. Andhra Prabha (P) Ltd. (AIR 1960 AP 123): "if a company is born, the only method to get it extinguished is not by assailing its incorporation, but by resorting to the provisions… which provide for the winding up of the companies." The limits. (i) The certificate does not validate unlawful objects — "illegal objects… do not become legal by the issue of the certificate" (Performing Right Society v. London Theatre of Varieties (1922); R. v. Registrar of Joint Stock Companies (1931); Bowman v. Secular Society (1917)); the Registrar may refuse a memorandum with unlawful objects (Universal Mutual Aid v. Thappa Naidu (AIR 1933 Mad 16)). (ii) Registration of a trade union as a company is not protected (British Assn. of Glass Bottle Mfrs. v. Nettlefold (1911)). (iii) In England the Registrar's certificates may be judicially reviewed; in India a writ cannot issue to cancel registration (Maluk Mohamed v. Capital Stock Exchange Kerala Ltd. (1991) 72 Comp Cas 333 (Ker.)). (iv) Under the 2013 Act, fraud at incorporation is a statutory carve-out: S. 7(5)-(7) — false particulars or fraudulent incorporation attract S. 447 and the Tribunal's orders (unlimited-member-liability, removal of name, winding up). Conclusion. The statement is correct as to compliance and regularity; the exceptions are confined to the lawfulness of objects and fraud — in both cases the remedy is winding up or statutory action, not a collateral attack on the certificate.
Q5. "A company cannot ratify a pre-incorporation contract, though it is open to it to enter into a fresh contract." Comment. 10 marks
Statement. Before incorporation the company is a non-entity; "two consenting parties are necessary to a contract" (Avtar). Hence at common law: (i) the company cannot be sued on a pre-incorporation contract — Re English & Colonial Produce Co. (1906) 2 Ch 435 (solicitor who paid the registration fee could not recover; "ratification was impossible"); and (ii) the company cannot sue on it — "a company cannot by adoption or ratification obtain the benefit of a contract purporting to have been made on its behalf before the company came into existence" (Natal Land & Colonisation Co. v. Pauline Colliery Syndicate (1904) AC 120 — action for specific performance of a lease not maintainable). The promoter who made the contract is personally liable: where a contract is made on behalf of a principal known to be non-existent, "the contract is deemed to have been entered into personally by the actual maker" (Kelner v. Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174 — the wine case). Newborne v. Sensolid (1954) 1 QB 45 shows the converse: if the paper contract is expressed to be made with the company alone, the signatory is not personally liable (which is why English law later enacted s. 36(2) CA 1985 / s. 51 CA 2006; India has no such general provision). Statutory exception. S. 15, Specific Relief Act, 1963: where promoters have made a contract before incorporation for the purposes of the company, and the contract is warranted by the terms of incorporation (i.e., within the objects in the memorandum), the company may adopt and enforce it. S. 19: the other party may also enforce the contract against the company if the company has adopted it after incorporation and it is within the terms of incorporation. Adoption need not be formal: Jai Narain Parasrampuria v. Pushpa Devi Saraf (2006) 7 SCC 756 — a company seeking a declaration of ownership of property purchased for it before incorporation had thereby accepted the transaction; Vali Pattabhirama Rao v. Ramanuja Ginning & Rice Factory (1986) 60 Comp Cas 568 — the lessor was bound where the firm was converted into a company which adopted the lease; Howard v. Patent Ivory Mfg. Co. (1888) — a fresh contract may be made after incorporation in terms of the earlier one; Re Northumberland Avenue Hotel Co. (1886) — an implied contract arises where the company acts on the contract, not where it acts under a mistaken belief of being bound. Conclusion. The comment is correct: the company can neither be bound nor benefit at common law, and cannot "ratify" in the technical sense — but the statutory machinery of Ss. 15 and 19 gives adoption (a fresh statutory act) the practical effect of ratification, and till then the promoter bears the personal liability.
Q6. Explain the procedure for registration of a company, including online registration. 10–15 marks
I. Preliminary. (1) S. 3: seven or more persons (public), two or more (private), one person (OPC) may form a company for any lawful purpose by subscribing to the memorandum; the OPC memorandum names a nominee who (with prior written consent filed at incorporation) becomes a member on the subscriber's death or incapacity. The company may be limited by shares, limited by guarantee, or unlimited. (2) S. 4 — name: must not be identical with or too nearly resemble an existing company's name, or be an offence, or be undesirable; application for reservation through RUN or SPICe+ Part A to the Registrar, Central Registration Centre (CRC) — reserved for 20 days (60 days for a change of name); wrong information → cancellation + penalty up to ₹1,00,000 (or post-incorporation: change of name within 3 months / striking off / winding-up petition). II. Documents. (3) The memorandum (Tables A–E, Schedule I) and articles (Tables F–J) — stamped as per State stamp law, signed by each subscriber in the presence of at least one witness who attests and verifies identity; an illiterate subscriber affixes his thumb impression with the prescribed endorsements. (4) S. 7(1) filing with the Registrar of the State of the registered office: memorandum and articles; a declaration by an advocate/CA/CS in practice engaged in the formation and a person named as director/manager/secretary in the articles that all requirements have been complied with; affidavits of subscribers and first directors (no conviction for promotion/formation/management offences or fraud/misfeasance in the preceding five years; correctness of documents); correspondence address until the registered office is established; particulars (name, surname, residential address, nationality, proof of identity) of subscribers and of first directors with DIN and interests in other bodies, with consent to act. III. Registration. (5) The Registrar registers the documents, enters the name in the Register of Companies, allots the CIN (S. 7(3)), and issues the certificate of incorporation (S. 7(2)) — the company is born on the date in it (S. 9). Where an object is banking, insurance, or practising chartered accountancy/cost accountancy/company secretaries/architecture, incorporation requires the regulator's/institute's in-principle approval/NOC. IV. Online route (SPICe+). (6) DIN for each proposed director (allotted through Part B; standalone DIR-3) and a DSC from a licensed Certifying Authority (with MCA portal Role Check). (7) SPICe+ (INC-32) Part A — up to two proposed names, fee ₹1,000, CRC approval in 1–2 working days, 20-day validity. (8) Part B — eight sections: company details (type/category/sub-category), registered office with proof, capital structure (minimum subscribed: ₹1 OPC / ₹2 private / ₹7 public — there is no minimum paid-up capital under the 2013 Act; Nidhi ₹5 lakh), director details (up to 3), subscriber details, stamp duty (state-wise, auto-calculated), professional details, attachments (PDF). (9) Linked forms: e-MoA INC-33, e-AoA INC-34, AGILE-PRO (GSTIN, EPFO, ESIC, profession tax, bank account), INC-9 (declaration), INC-10 (verification of signatures), URC-1 (conversion of an existing entity); OPC uses SPICe+ with the nominee consent; companies with more than seven subscribers (or foreign subscribers) use INC-7. (10) Affix DSCs, pay the filing fee and e-stamp duty, submit; the form is processed in Non-STP mode and on approval the certificate of incorporation is issued electronically with the CIN and simultaneously PAN and TAN (and GSTIN etc., as applied). (11) Post-incorporation: a company having share capital must file the S. 10A declaration of commencement of business within 180 days of incorporation (penalty for default) — the old "certificate of commencement of business" is gone. V. Consequence. (12) The effects of registration follow from S. 9 (see Q7), and the certificate is conclusive evidence of compliance (see Q4).
Q7. What are the effects of registration of a company under S. 9 of the Companies Act, 2013? 6–10 marks
Section 9: "From the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, such subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons, as may from time to time become members of the company, shall be a body corporate by the name contained in the memorandum, capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company under this Act and having perpetual succession and a common seal, with power to acquire, hold and dispose of property, both movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, to contract and to sue and be sued, by the said name." The effects, in order: (1) Body corporate / separate legal entity — the company becomes a legal person distinct from its members — the Salomon principle; (2) a name — it is known and suable by the name in the memorandum; (3) perpetual succession — members may come and go but the company goes on, and can be extinguished only by law (winding up/striking off) — T.V. Krishna; (4) common seal — the mode of corporate action (after the 2015 Amendment the seal is optional: a company "may have a common seal"; where it has none, signatures of authorised persons suffice); (5) property — may acquire, hold and dispose of property, movable and immovable, tangible and intangible; (6) contract — capacity to contract in its own name; the memorandum and articles bind the company and members (S. 10); (7) sue and be sued — a litigant in its own name, and can sue for defamation etc.; and (8) all the functions of an incorporated company — borrowing, issuing shares, employing, limited liability of members. The date on the certificate is conclusive even if wrong, and the certificate itself is conclusive evidence of compliance (the old s. 34(2) 1956-Act formula — S. 7(2) + S. 9 + the case law) — the two ideas together ("birth + valid birth") are what the examiner wants.
Q8. Short notes (any two): (a) Promoter under S. 2(69); (b) Secret profits of promoters; (c) Pre-incorporation contracts; (d) Certificate of incorporation. 5–6 marks each
(a) S. 2(69). "Promoter" means a person (i) named as such in a prospectus or identified by the company in the annual return (S. 92); or (ii) having control over the affairs of the company, directly or indirectly, as shareholder, director or otherwise; or (iii) in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the board is accustomed to act; but not a person acting merely in a professional capacity. It is a functional definition — the judicial descriptions (Whaley Bridge: "a term not of law, but of business"; Twycross: "one who undertakes to form a company… and to set it going"; Emma Silver: any person who "undertakes to take part in the forming of a company") identify the role; S. 2(69) fixes the liability end. Category (c) makes the promoter a related party (S. 2(76)(vii)) with all the S. 188 consequences; the question "who is a promoter" is a question of fact in each case. (b) Secret profits. The promoter may sell his property to the company at a profit (Omnium Electric Palaces) — but it must be a disclosed profit: disclosure of profit minus expenses (Lydney & Wigpool); disclosure to the company's independent board or the whole body of shareholders, not to the parties to the transaction (Erlanger; Gluckstein v. Barnes — "disclosure is not the most appropriate word when a person who plays many parts announces to himself in one character what he has done and is doing in another"). The duty continues until profits are disclosed and accounted (Emma Silver). Remedies: rescission and return of price (Erlanger); account even in liquidation (Gluckstein); damages measured by the profit where rescission is impossible (Leeds & Hanley). (c) Pre-incorporation contracts. The company is a non-entity before registration; it can neither be bound nor enforce such contracts (English & Colonial Produce; Natal Land); the maker is personally liable (Kelner v. Baxter; contrast Newborne). Statutory exceptions: S. 15 (company may adopt and enforce if the contract was for the company's purposes and is warranted by the terms of incorporation) and S. 19 (other party may enforce after adoption) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963; adoption may be implied (Jai Narain Parasrampuria; Vali Pattabhirama Rao); or a fresh contract in terms of the old one (Howard v. Patent Ivory). (d) Certificate of incorporation. Issued by the Registrar (S. 7(2)) upon registration, containing the CIN (S. 7(3)); the company is born on the date mentioned in it (S. 9). It is conclusive evidence of compliance with the requirements of the Act as to registration and matters precedent or incidental thereto — Peel's case (1867); Moosa Goolam Ariff (1913); Cotman v. Brougham (1918); T.V. Krishna (AIR 1960 AP 123) — and is now issued electronically. Limits: unlawful objects are not validated; trade-union registration is excluded; and fraud at incorporation attracts S. 7(5)-(7) with S. 447.
Q9. Problem: The promoters of a company incorporated on 9 April 2014 had entered into a contract with M on 8 March 2014 for supply of goods. After incorporation the company does not wish to proceed with the contract. Advise the management. problem question
Advice. The contract of 8 March 2014 is a pre-incorporation contract. The company was not in existence when it was made; "two consenting parties are necessary to a contract, whereas the company, before incorporation, is a non-entity" (Avtar). Consequently:
  • The company is not bound by the contract and cannot be sued on it — the contract in general is void ab initio as against the company (Re English & Colonial Produce Co.; Rover International Ltd. v. Cannon Films Sales Ltd. — "somebody who does not exist cannot contract"). The date of the certificate (9 April 2014) does not retrospectively bind the company; a contract of 10 October made after incorporation would bind it, but a contract of 8 March does not.
  • The promoters are personally liable to M — the contract is deemed to be made with the actual makers (Kelner v. Baxter) — unless it was signed in a form making the company alone the contracting party (Newborne v. Sensolid).
  • Will the company's refusal be effective? Yes, as against the contract — but only if the company has not adopted it. If the company after incorporation has acted on the contract (taken delivery, paid, or sued on it), an implied adoption arises (Re Northumberland Avenue Hotel Co.) and a fresh contract in the terms of the old one may be spelt out. If the company seeks to enforce the contract (or has done so), the other party can hold it under S. 19, Specific Relief Act, 1963.
  • Risk to be flagged: if the company's board now adopts the contract — e.g., by resolution or by conduct — M gets a direct right against the company under S. 19 (adoption + warranty by the terms of incorporation: the supply must be within the company's objects). Since the company is not proceeding, the promoters should negotiate a release with M and ensure the company (through the board) formally declines adoption.
Conclusion: subject to adoption, the company can refuse to proceed; the promoters remain personally exposed to M (Kelner); the cleanest course is a fresh agreement or a mutual rescission with M, since the company cannot "ratify" but can adopt (S. 15) or novate.
Q10. Problem: Six of the seven signatures to the memorandum of association were forged; the company was registered and the certificate of incorporation issued. Can the registration be challenged? problem question
Answer: No — the certificate is conclusive; the registration cannot be challenged by a collateral attack. The rule (S. 7(2) read with the authorities, inheriting the old s. 34(2) 1956-Act formula): once the Registrar registers the documents and issues the certificate, it is conclusive evidence that all the requirements of the Act in respect of registration and matters precedent or incidental thereto have been complied with. Re Barned's Banking Co., Peel's case (1867): the certificate is "not merely a prima facie answer, but a conclusive answer"; "when once the certificate of incorporation is given, nothing is to be inquired into as to the regularity of the prior proceedings" — and there the memorandum had been materially altered after signature. Moosa Goolam Ariff (1913): even where — as here — there are not seven genuine subscribers and the Registrar ought not to have granted the certificate, "the certificate is conclusive for all purposes"; forgeries and defects of signature are precisely the class of "prior matter" the certificate closes. Cotman v. Brougham (1918): the Registrar must consider and may refuse, but once he accepts and certifies, the validity of the memorandum cannot be challenged. T.V. Krishna (AIR 1960 AP 123): the only mode of extinguishing a born company is the statutory winding-up machinery. Caveats. (i) If the certificate was obtained by fraud, the 2013 Act provides the modern statutory answer: S. 7(5)-(7) — false/incorrect particulars or suppression invite S. 447 against the promoters, persons named as first directors and the declarants, and the Tribunal may order regulation of management, make members' liability unlimited, direct removal of the company's name, or order winding up. (ii) Unlawful objects are not validated by the certificate (Performing Right Society; R. v. Registrar of Joint Stock Companies). (iii) In England the certificates may be judicially reviewed; in India a writ will not lie to cancel registration (Maluk Mohamed). — Hence the clean answer for a 5–6 mark problem: the company is validly born; the forged signatures are immaterial to its existence; the appropriate remedies are winding up (or, post-2013, the S. 7(6)-(7) Tribunal route).
