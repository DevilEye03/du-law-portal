SECTION 1

Majority Rule → Minority Vulnerability: Why This Topic Exists

“The protection of the minority shareholders within the domain of corporate activity constitutes one of the most difficult problems facing modern company law.” A company acts through majority will expressed in general meetings (Topic 6): that is efficient — but the same machinery can be turned into an instrument of tyranny of the majority. This unit is the law's answer: first a judge-made set of exceptions to majority supremacy, then — since the Cohen Committee (1945) called the winding-up remedy “often worse than the disease” — a statutory chapter of qualified minority rights (Ch. XVI, Ss. 241–246) that lets the Tribunal salvage the company instead of killing it.

Fig. 1 — The conceptual spine of Topic 7: entity + majority rule → potential abuse → layered correction (common law first, statute next).

1.1 Who is the “minority” here — and who can actually complain?

  • No word “minority” in S.241: the section speaks of “any member”. So even a majority group may invoke it where control rests elsewhere and the oppression is exercised by “those in control even though they may lack majority holding” — e.g., rival boards, two registered offices, unauthorised persons holding assets (DU notes: “oppression of majority by minority is not barred”). The company itself cannot be an applicant.
  • “Qualified minority rights”: Chapter XVI confers rights that open only at a threshold (S.244 numbers) or on Tribunal waiver — deliberately filtering grievance-shopping while preserving genuine complaint.
  • Representation on the Board — S.151: listed companies may have one director elected by small shareholders (≤ ₹20,000 nominal value) — a structural, preventive protection alongside the curative S.241 remedy.
One-line thesis for every answer in this unit: “Corporate democracy runs on majority will, but Company Law reserves two corrective systems — the exceptions to Foss v. Harbottle (judiciary-made) and Chapter XVI (legislature-made) — to ensure that the majority's legitimate power is never converted into an illegitimate tyranny: remedy with salvage, not winding-up.”
SECTION 2

The Rule in Foss v. Harbottle & Its Exceptions

The rule, restated authoritatively by Jenkins LJ in Edwards v. Halliwell (1950), has two limbs: (1) the proper-plaintiff limb — the proper plaintiff in respect of a wrong done to a company is, prima facie, the company itself (it is a distinct legal person; the injury is to the corporation, not to the shareholders qua individuals); and (2) the internal-management limb — where the alleged wrong is a transaction which a simple majority of members might make binding on the company (i.e., “cadit quaestio” — the question falls), no individual member may sue about it, because the majority could simply ratify, making the litigation futile. Wigram VC's own policy reasons: multiplicity of futile suits, and the pointlessness of litigating what a general meeting can regularise.

The rule rewards
Ratifiable irregularities: appointment of managing agents later confirmed (V.N. Bhajekar v. K.M. Shinkar); directors' unissued-capital manoeuvres later absolved on full disclosure (Hogg v. Cramphorn; Bamford v. Bamford); remuneration increases challengeable only before the general body (Normandy v. Ind Coope).
The rule's logic in one formula
MacDougall v. Gardiner (Mellish LJ): if what was done irregularly, the majority could do regularly — “there can be no use in having litigation about it, the ultimate end of which is only that a meeting has to be called, and then ultimately the majority gets its wishes.”

2.1 The six exceptions — where the majority CANNOT ratify

Fig. 2 — The exception map. Rule applies only where the majority could lawfully ratify; where no majority can cure (ultra vires, fraud, special-majority bypass, wrongdoer control, personal rights) every single member has standing.

SECTION 3

The Statutory Framework — Ss. 241–246, Chapter XVI

Chapter XVI (Ss. 241–246) supplies the modern machinery: a member may move the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) against oppression (S.241(1)(a)) and mismanagement (S.241(1)(b)); the Tribunal has a wide order-menu (S.242); specific standing rules (S.244); the Central Government may itself apply where public interest is prejudiced (S.241(2)) and may direct termination of offending managerial agreements (S.243); the chapter carries the fraud-investigation provisions with it (S.246); and a collective class action mechanism stands alongside (S.245).

3.1 Section 241 — the gateway and the DUAL TEST (S.242(1))

S.241(1)
Any member of a company who complains —
(a) that the affairs of the company have been or are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest or in a manner oppressive to him or any other member or members or in a manner prejudicial to the interests of the company; or
(b) that a material change has taken place in the management or control of the company (by alteration in the Board, or manager, or in the ownership of shares, or membership, or in any other manner) — not being a change brought about by or in the interest of any creditors including debenture-holders, or any class of shareholders — and that by reason of such change it is likely that the affairs of the company will be conducted in a manner prejudicial to its interests or its members or any class of members,
may apply to the Tribunal for an order under this Chapter, provided such member has a right to apply under Section 244.
S.241(2)
The Central Government, if it is of the opinion that the affairs of the company are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest, may itself apply to the Tribunal for an order under this Chapter.

The dual test the Tribunal must pass (S.242(1))

Fig. 3 — The dual test affirmed in TCS v. Cyrus (2021) ¶16.2: limb-1 conduct plus limb-2 just-and-equitable/unfair-prejudice calculus. Indian law is thus stricter than the current English S.994 (which has dropped limb 2).
Lineage (TCS ¶15, legislative history): Cohen Committee 1945 → UK S.210 of 1948 ("alternative remedy to winding up in cases of oppression") → India S.222 of 1913 Act (S.153C+Courts' order Regs.) → Ss. 397/398 of the 1956 Act (oppression / mismanagement) → Ss. 241/242 of 2013 (adding "prejudicial to the interests of the company" and the S.245 class action). Thread constant across all enactments: orders must be “with a view to bringing to an end the matters complained of”. Meanwhile England moved (Jenkins Committee 1962) from "oppressive" to "unfairly prejudicial" (S.459 of 1985 → S.994 of 2006) and dropped the winding-up limb — India consciously retains it, making our provision “more stringent” (your checklist's phrasing).

3.3 Section 242 — the Tribunal's order-menu

General power
S.242(1): “such order as it thinks fit” framed to end the matters complained of — including interim orders, appointment of administrators/special officers/Chairman, Board reconstitution, supersession mismanagement salvage (Rajahmundry), validation/proportionate remedies found wanting elsewhere (Needle Industries: “the court is not powerless to do substantial justice”).
S.242(2) enumerated
(a) regulation of future conduct of affairs; (b) purchase of shares/interests of any members by other members or by the company (with consequent capital reduction, (c)); (d) restrictions on transfer/allotment; (e) termination, setting aside or modification of agreements with MD/directors/managers (with notice to them, and with leave for shareholders'/third-party agreements); (f) termination/modification of company-third-party agreements; (g) setting aside fraudulent/unlawful transfer/delivery of goods/property/execution; (h) recovery of undue gains by MD/manager from misapplication/breach of trust; (i) imposition of costs… (menu is non-exhaustive).
Consequential discipline: alterations to memorandum/articles ordered under S.242 take effect as if made by the company — and the company cannot itself make inconsistent alterations without leave of the Tribunal (certified copy filed with the RoC). S.243: where an order ends/sets aside/modifies an agreement with an MD/manager, no compensation claim lies against the company for that termination; and S.243-type orders are subject to notice to the affected manager/director. S.246: Ss. 337–341 (fraud investigation/procedures) apply mutatis mutandis to applications under Ss. 241–242.
SECTION 4

“Oppression” — The Jurisprudence Ladder

The statute deliberately does not define “oppressive”. The courts have built a cumulative jurisprudence — each generation tightening the elements. Learn this ladder as a sequence: it is the entire intellectual content of S.241(1)(a).

Fig. 4 — Six stops on the oppression ladder — cite them in order in any long answer.

4.1 The four consolidated elements (Shanti Prasad formula)

  1. Manner of management: the oppression must relate to the manner in which the company's affairs are conducted — by the majority exercising predominant voting power “as shareholders” in that conduct.
  2. Continuous course: events considered not in isolation but “as a part of a consecutive story” — a continuing act up to the date of the petition (“affairs are being conducted” — present, persisting course).
  3. Burdensome, harsh and wrongful: involving at least an element of lack of probity or fair dealing toward a member in the matter of his proprietary rights as a shareholder (visibility-of-unfair-dealing standard of Elder/Meyer).
  4. Qua member + the winding-up calculus: the petitioner must suffer as member; the facts must justify a just-and-equitable winding up while winding up would unfairly prejudice (limb 2). Mere lack of confidence is insufficient unless it springs from oppression.

SECTION 5

Mismanagement — S.241(1)(b) [flagged LONG-answer]

The mismanagement limb protects the company itself (and through it, all stakeholders) where a material change in management or control makes future prejudicial conduct likely. It differs from oppression in beneficiary, timing, and trigger.

Instances of mismanagement (DU notes): group in power conspiring to defraud members; properly appointed directors not permitted to join and function; in-fighting among directors seriously prejudicing the company; MD continuing after expiry of term without re-appointment meeting; directors benefiting themselves by neglect at the company's expense. And the Rajahmundry doctrine: the rule of internal management applies only to a running concern — once a winding-up-caliber case is made out, vesting management in administrators is not “interference” but salvage (see Case 26).
SECTION 6

Class Action Suit — S.245 [flagged SHORT-answer]

A 2013 innovation: a requisite number of members or depositors with a common grievance may, on behalf of the whole class, sue the company, its directors, its auditors (including audit firm and the partner involved), any expert/advisor/consultant/any other person — for restraint orders, declarations, damages or compensation — where the company's management or conduct of affairs is “prejudicial to the interests of the company or its members or depositors”. Unlike S.241 (individual protective jurisdiction), S.245 is a collective compensatory and restraining jurisdiction.

Case 24 · The foundation — proper plaintiff & majority supremacy

Foss v. Harbottle

(1843) 2 Hare 461 · (1843) 67 ER 189 · Sir James Wigram VC
Court of Chancery (England)

① Facts (as stated in the bill, from the case material)

In September 1835 an association was conceived to buy ~180 acres of land near Manchester, enclose and plant it ornamentally, erect houses with gardens and pleasure-grounds, and dispose of them. Promoters bought parcels — some buying from others at profit — and, to escape ordinary partnership liability, procured a private Act of Parliament (Royal Assent 5 May 1837) incorporating “The Victoria Park Company” with perpetual succession, common seal, power to sue and to hold lands. Richard Foss (2 shares) and Edward Starkie Turton (12 shares of £100) were subscribers. In October 1842 they filed a bill “on behalf of themselves and all other the shareholders” against the five directors — Thomas Harbottle, Joseph Adshead, Henry Byrom, John Westhead, Richard Bealey (three of whom had become bankrupt) — plus a shareholder, the solicitor and the architect, charging them with concerting fraudulent and illegal transactions: the promoters had sold their own land to the company at inflated prices (a self-dealing profit), the company's property had been misapplied, aliened and wasted, and mortgages/incumbrances had been created without authority; further, by death and bankruptcy there was no sufficient board, no clerk, no office — so the proprietors could not themselves set the corporate machinery in motion. The bill prayed that the defendants be made to restore the property/monies and that a receiver be appointed. The defendants demurred — i.e., even assuming the bill's facts, the form of the suit was bad in law.

② Issues: (1) Can two shareholders sue, in a representative bill, for a wrong done to the company? (2) Will equity interfere in the internal administration of a corporation whose members retain power to convene, control acts, and confirm or repudiate the impugned transactions? (On demurrer, every intendment runs against the pleader.)
Foss & Turton (minority shareholders)
• The fiduciary promoters sold their own land to their company — a fraud the company should undo; the defendants engineered the corporation precisely to escape partnership liability.
• The corporate machinery is paralysed: no valid board (bankruptcies disqualify), no clerk or office on whom S.48 notices must be served, no power to call the meeting that alone could act.
• Unless members may sue in this form, the wrongdoers are insulated: the corporation is in practical dissolution, and equity will not suffer wrong without remedy (Wallworth v. Holt-type reasoning).
Harbottle & others (defendants / demurrants)
• The injury alleged is to the corporation, not to the plaintiffs personally — only the company can sue on its own cause.
• The company's supreme body — proprietors in general meeting — retains full power to control the directors and to confirm or repudiate the impugned acts; the bill nowhere avers that convening a meeting was attempted and failed.
• Transactions like the land purchases are at worst voidable, ratifiable; if the proprietors confirm them, the litigation collapses — hence the suit is futile at inception.
• On demurrer, facts not averred are intended against the pleader; the bill itself shows meetings were in fact held in 1840–41 and business carried on through the solicitor's office.

④ Decision DEMURRERS ALLOWED — BILL DISMISSED

Wigram VC held for the defendants on every point: (i) a board of directors de facto must be intended from the bill's own statements (meetings were held; business was carried on; bankrupts executed deeds in the character of directors); (ii) the S.48 impediments (no clerk/office) were at most directory difficulties — a notice in substance would do, and misconduct of the board cannot be allowed to paralyse the proprietors' statutory power; (iii) most fundamentally: “the result of these clauses is that the directors are made the governing body, subject to the superior control of the proprietors assembled in general meetings”; while that body retains its functions, the very power to confirm the avoidable transactions “is decisive to show that the frame of this suit cannot be sustained”. Even the unauthorised-mortgage charge (arguably void, incapable of confirmation against dissenters — Preston v. Grand Collier Dock principle) failed here: the mortgagees were not parties, the bill did not seek to avoid the securities, and the company — having received and used the money — could not both keep the benefit and visit the consequences personally on the directors in this form of action. Demurrers allowed. (But the VC himself planted the exception: if a case arises where no adequate remedy remains except suit by individual corporators, “the claims of justice would be found superior to any difficulties arising out of technical rules”.)

⑤ Principle evolved (the twin rule): (i) Proper-plaintiff rule: the company, as separate legal person, is the proper plaintiff for injury to itself — “the corporation should sue in its own name and in its corporate character”; (ii) Majority-rule / internal-management principle: courts will not interfere in irregularities or transactions which the general meeting can lawfully ratify — for litigation would only force a meeting that hands the majority its wish (MacDougall v. Gardiner's “cadit quaestio”; restated by Jenkins LJ in Edwards v. Halliwell). Corollary: where no majority can confirm (ultra vires), where wrongdoers control, where fraud on the minority or personal membership rights or special-majority bypass are in play — the rule yields (§2.2).
Visual — the door that closed on Foss & Turton, and the crack the Vice-Chancellor left open.
Anchor: foundation of §2; paired with Bharat Insurance (exception ①) in your case list; read with Edwards v. Halliwell restatement.
Case 25 · The meaning of “oppression” — continuous, burdensome, qua shareholder

Shanti Prasad Jain v. Kalinga Tubes Ltd.

AIR 1965 SC 1535 · (1965) 35 Comp Cas 351 · K.N. Wanchoo J.
Supreme Court of India

① Facts

Kalinga Tubes was floated as a private company (1 Dec 1950; authorised ₹25 lakh), shares held roughly equally by the Patnaik and Loganathan groups, with ₹36 lakh debentures guaranteed by the Orissa Government. In administrative and financial distress, the appellant (Shanti Prasad Jain) was invited in 1954 to finance and guide the company; the 27 July 1954 agreement (between Jain and the two groups — the company not a party) promised Jain shares equal to each of the other two groups, equal Board representation (2 each) and the chairmanship. Resolutions of 16 Aug 1954 substantially implemented the terms; capital rose to ₹61 lakh, split in thirds (barring a French company's ₹4 lakh holding) — and when Rath's 250 shares were sold, the three groups bought them equally (one odd share held jointly). The articles, however, were never amended to embed the agreement. In January 1957 the company was converted into a public company to qualify for an Industrial Finance Corporation loan. When ₹39 lakh of new shares came up for issue (March 1958), war broke out: Jain wanted them offered to existing shareholders under S.81; the Patnaik–Loganathan majority feared that (lacking funds) they could not subscribe their two-thirds and Jain would swallow the whole issue and capture control. The general meeting of 29 March 1958 rejected Jain's resolution and resolved to allot privately at the directors' discretion. Jain sued in Cuttack for declaration/injunction; an interim injunction was vacated on 30 July 1958 and the shares were allotted the same day in haste, only 15% of the money coming up front; six of the seven allottees borrowed from a bank; ₹7.65 lakh remained unpaid even at the S.397 petition. Post-issue, Patnaik–Loganathan held ₹38 lakh, Jain ₹19 lakh, new allottees ₹39 lakh — no group had a majority. Jain's Ss. 397/398/402/403 petition was rejected by the High Court; he appealed to the Supreme Court.

② Issues: (1) Did the affairs of the company's conduct — the by-passing of S.81-type proportionate allotment, the private allotment, its haste, the unpaid calls, the ₹7 lakh withdrawal by the majority groups — amount to a course of oppression of the minority under S.397? (2) Independently, was there mismanagement under S.398 (prejudice to the company)? (3) Would the facts justify a just-and-equitable winding up, with winding up unfairly prejudicing the minority?
Appellant (Jain group)
• The 1954 agreement was the sheet-anchor: the three groups were to remain equal forever; the majority used voting power to destroy that parity when the company prospered — a classic “treating the company as their own property”.
• The allotment design — refusal of rights issue, discretion-allotment to friends, indecent haste after the injunction fell, 15% money — proves a scheme to push the majority over 75% voting strength (special-resolution control).
• ₹7 lakh was pulled from the company's coffers by the two groups; the company lost the appellant's support — prejudice to the company itself (S.398).
Respondents (Company; Patnaik & Loganathan groups)
• The agreement never bound the company (not a party), covered no future capital rise, and was a deal with a then non-member; a public company is still less bound; the fifth term deals with the French holding only.
• The 1958 resolutions complied with S.81 as it then stood (regulation 42 of the 1913 Act was repealed); the general meeting could direct otherwise than rights-issue.
• Haste was caused by the appellant's own litigation (fear of fresh injunctions); the company needed the issue to unlock the IFC loan; the slow calls prove nothing where allottees were not benamidars.
• ₹7 lakh was owed to the former managing agent — repayment is not prejudice; the appellant's proprietary rights as shareholder are untouched.

④ Decision APPEAL DISMISSED — NO OPPRESSION, NO MISMANAGEMENT

Wanchoo J., synthesizing Elder, Scottish Co-op, Meyer and Harmer, laid down the controlling test (paraphrased): it is not enough to show that just-and-equitable grounds for winding up exist; it must further be shown that the conduct of the majority was oppressive to the minority as members — events viewed “not in isolation but as a part of a consecutive story”, a continuous act up to the petition date; conduct “burdensome, harsh and wrongful”; mere lack of confidence insufficient unless it springs from oppression involving “at least an element of lack of probity or fair dealing to a member in the matter of his proprietary rights as a shareholder”. Applying it: the agreement did not bind the (now public) company; the allotment was lawful and produced a broad-based company (no group's 75%, new allottees not stooges — “if shares are issued privately they are bound to go to friends of the directors”); preventing one group's domination was not oppression of the minority; ₹7 lakh was a genuine debt; the support withdrawn was no longer needed; post-July 1958 management changes promised no future prejudice. Petition under both Ss. 397 and 398 failed.

⑤ Principle evolved — the Indian definition of oppression: (1) oppression relates to the manner of management by predominant voting power as shareholders; (2) demands a continuous course concluded at petition date; (3) is burdensome, harsh and wrongful, importing at least lack of probity/fair dealing in proprietary membership rights; (4) must be suffered qua shareholder, with (5) the alternative-winding-up calculus satisfied. Majority action taken lawfully to prevent one shareholder from seizing control — resulting in a more democratised register — is not “fraud on the minority”. Equally: a shareholders' agreement never embedded in the articles does not bind the company; expectations outside the corporate constitution don't generate oppression.

Case 26 · Just-and-equitable limb + consent standing + salvage by administrators

Rajahmundry Electric Supply Corpn. Ltd. v. A. Nageshwara Rao

AIR 1956 SC 213 · (1956) 26 Comp Cas 91 · Venkatarama Ayyar J.
Supreme Court of India

① Facts

A member of the Rajahmundry Electric Supply Corporation petitioned for winding up under S.162(v)&(vi) of the 1913 Act — gross mismanagement, ₹3,10,175 owed to the Government for energy, misappropriation by directors, a majority directorate “riding roughshod” over shareholders — and in the alternative for remedial orders under S.153-C (the statutory alternative-remedy ancestor of S.241). Standing was attacked: S.153-C(3)(a)(i) required written consent of ≥100 members or ≥ one-tenth of members, whichever less. The petitioner claimed 80 consents; opponents countered that 13 consenters were not members, 2 signed twice, and 13 had withdrawn after filing — leaving 52, below the line. Excluding only the 13 non-members and 2 duplicates left 65 of 603 members — more than one-tenth. On merits, courts below had found: the Vice-Chairman grossly mismanaged and drew company funds for personal purposes; machinery was in disrepair; arrears to Government mounted; the directorate was attenuated; “a powerful local junta was ruling the roost”; shareholders outside the Chairman's group were apathetic and powerless. The trial court ordered winding up on the just-and-equitable clause and, under S.153-C, appointed two administrators in supersession of the directorate (Chairman contested; affidavit affirmed on appeal).

② Issues: (1) Is the petition's standing to be judged as at presentation, so later withdrawals of consent are irrelevant? (2) Must S.153-C relief be preceded by proof of facts justifying winding up under S.162? (3) Is directors' misconduct/mismanagement — nothing more — a “just and equitable” ground (does the clause read ejusdem generis with the earlier clauses)? (4) Is appointing administrators an unlawful interference in the company's “internal management”?
Company / Chairman (appellants)
• With the 13 withdrawals the consents fell to 52 — below the statutory number: petition not maintainable.
• No S.153-C relief without a S.162 case; and “just and equitable” must be read ejusdem generis — mere misconduct of directors is internal management, to be cured within the company (removed Vice-Chairman; clean-up underway).
• Administrators superseding the elected directorate is an impermissible judicial intrusion into internal administration.
Petitioning member (respondent)
• Standing existed on presentation day (65/603); post-filing withdrawals cannot retroactively unmake jurisdiction.
• The findings — misappropriation, derelict plant, Government arrears, junta, powerless members — cross the just-and-equitable threshold; the clause is not cabined by the earlier clauses (Halsbury).
• On a winding-up petition the whole point is to terminate the existing management — the “don't interfere” maxim has no traction in that forum.

④ Decision APPEAL DISMISSED — ADMINISTRATORS AFFIRMED

The Supreme Court (Venkatarama Ayyar J.) held: (1) standing is tested at presentation — “a petition which was valid when presented cannot cease to be maintainable by reason of events subsequent to its presentation”; the 13 withdrawals were irrelevant. (2) S.153-C presupposes S.162 facts — the alternative remedy exists to salvage a company that otherwise qualifies for liquidation: “whereas prior to its enactment the Court had no option but to pass an order for winding up when the conditions mentioned in S.162 were satisfied, it could now… make an order for its management by the Court with a view to its being ultimately salvaged.” (3) “Just and equitable” is NOT ejusdem generis; directors' misappropriation alone may not justify killing a sound company, but here “in addition to such misconduct, circumstances exist” — junta rule, shattered machinery, helpless shareholders — making winding-up-just, and hence S.153-C salvage proper. (4) The internal-management immunity applies only to a running concern: a winding-up forum's very object is to terminate the articles-based management; administrators do what a liquidator could. Appeal dismissed.

⑤ Principle evolved: (i) Standing freezes at filing — the canonical rule for every threshold-based corporate petition (S.245 class actions included); (ii) the alternative-remedy architecture: oppression/mismanagement relief is an escape valve from liquidation, granted only where winding-up facts exist but destruction would unfairly prejudice — the ancestor of today's S.242(1)(b) dual test; (iii) “just and equitable” is residual and independent; (iv) in salvage mode the court may supersede the board with administrators — the direct doctrinal grandfather of Tribunal powers under S.242.

Case 27 · The ultra-vires exception to Foss — single member's declaration

Bharat Insurance Co. Ltd. v. Kanhaiya Lal Gauba

AIR 1935 Lah 792 · Sale J.
Lahore High Court

① Facts

Mr. Kanhaiya Lal Gauba — shareholder, policy-holder and director of Bharat Insurance — found that a considerable portion of the company's life-insurance fund was invested in undertakings controlled by the Chairman, Lala Harkishan Lal, and complained that several investments were made by the directors without adequate security, contrary to clause 3(d) of the Memorandum: “To advance money at interest on the security of land, houses, machinery and other property situated in India and to invest money not immediately required upon such securities and Bank Deposits as may be from time to time determined.” He sued for (1) a declaration that the company could invest/advance only against specified securities — not mere personal security of the borrower — and (2) a perpetual injunction restraining loans/investments in specified concerns except on proper security with a valid Board quorum. The Subordinate Judge (ex parte against the company) granted the declaration, and an injunction — but against the directors (who were not parties). The company appealed. Two threshold points were fought: maintainability (can one member sue at all?) and the construction of clause 3(d).

② Issues: (1) Is a single member's suit maintainable — or should the grievance have gone to the general body (Foss)? (2) Must the company (and the directors, for an injunction) be parties? (3) On the true construction of clause 3(d), are advances in the nature of loans confined to real security, while “investments” may rest on securities of the directors' choosing?
Company (appellant)
• Internal management is for the general body: a dissatisfied director-shareholder should take the matter there; courts do not supervise the officers' commercial judgment (Foss rule).
• On construction, the two halves of cl. 3(d) divide long-term vs short-term deployment: long advances need real security; short advances may go even on personal security — so nothing was ultra vires. (Also, the directors — targets of the injunction — were never impleaded.)
Gauba (respondent)
• The application of corporate assets against the Memorandum's limits is not “mere internal management”: directors acting ultra vires can be restrained at the suit of any one member; he seeks no judgment on past acts — only authoritative construction for the future.
• “Advance money at interest” (loans) and “invest money” are distinct acts; reading loans into “invest” would make the first limb redundant. Loans need real security per cl. 3(d); “securities” needs no ejusdem-generis squeeze across the two limbs.

④ Decision DECLARATION AFFIRMED (modified) INJUNCTION SET ASIDE

Sale J. held: (1) maintainability YES — “the broad rule… in all matters of internal management of a company, the company itself is the best judge” BUT “application of the assets of a company is not a matter of mere internal management… alleged… directors… acting ultra vires in their application of the funds of the company. Under these circumstances, I have no doubt that a single member can maintain a suit for a declaration as to the true construction of the article in question.” The company was a necessary party (Brice on Ultra Vires); the injunction against un-impleaded directors could not stand — and, on merits, the Court refused to assume future disobedience: once construction was declared, directors would comply, so prospective suppression of the company's discretion was unwarranted. (2) Construction: the two limbs operate independently — “a loan is not the same thing as an investment… The question must be determined by the real nature of the transaction”: temporary loans fall in limb-1 and “can only be made on the security of land, houses, machinery and other property situated in India”; genuine investments (e.g., Government securities, stock) may rest on such security as directors think adequate. Appeal allowed in part: declaration modified accordingly; injunction set aside; parties to bear their own costs.

⑤ Principle evolved — the ultra-vires exception, Indian-chapter: Where the complaint is that directors are deploying corporate assets beyond the Memorandum's allocation, the question is not internal management: each single member has standing for declaration/injunction (the rule's inner limit — “the rule of Foss applies only as long as the company is acting within its powers”). Second principle: declaratory construction is the appropriate relief against recurrent ultra-vires risk; courts will not fetter future discretion by injunction on the assumption the constitution will again be breached. Third: necessary-party discipline — the company for declarations, and the implicated governing body for injunctions.

Case 28 · Tribunal not powerless even without proved oppression — deadlock & substantial justice

M.S.D.C. Radharamanan v. M.S.D. Chandrasekara Raja

(2008) 6 SCC 750 · AIR 2008 SC 1738 · S.B. Sinha J.
Supreme Court of India

① Facts

M/s Shree Bhaarathi Cotton Mills (P) Ltd. had 2,84,000 equity shares — 2,83,999 held by a father (Respondent 1, MD) and his son (appellant, Director); the 1 odd share with Visva Bharathi Textiles, itself split equally between the same two. A company in substance owned by two warring individuals. The father petitioned the CLB under Ss. 397/398 alleging oppression by the son. The CLB found NO oppression and NO mala fide by the son — but noted a deadlock in the company's affairs and ordered the son to purchase the father's shares at a chartered valuer's price. The Madras High Court (S.10-F appeal), taking a “holistic” quasi-partnership view — acrimony, criminal complaint for ₹8,15,000 misappropriation, a partition suit pending — modified the mechanism (son to buy first; father to buy out on son's default; third-party transfer if both fail). The son appealed to the Supreme Court: can the Tribunal order a buy-out where the threshold finding of oppression is absent?

② Issues: (1) Can the CLB/Tribunal pass any order under S.402 r/w Ss. 397–398 without a finding of oppression by the respondent? (2) Does a deadlock in a two-member quasi-partnership company, with just-and-equitable considerations, justify a share-transfer/buy-out direction “in the interest of the company”? (3) Limits: must relief have a direct nexus with the company's affairs and business conduct; can the party's professed lack of funds block the order?
Appellant — son (C.A. Sundaram)
• Condition precedent missing: CLB itself exonerated him of oppression; S.402 powers are consequential on Ss. 397–398 findings — the order is ferrum sine capite.
• The High Court reversed findings of fact without an appeal by the father.
• Relief must serve the company's interest with direct nexus to its affairs — a private family buy-out does not; an additional Director would have sufficed; and he lacks funds to purchase.
Respondent — father (K. Parasaran)
• This private company is for all purposes a quasi-partnership: equities — not technicalities — govern; the jurisprudence (Needle Industries onwards) empowers substantial justice even absent a formal oppression finding.
• No funds-plea was ever raised in the SLP.
• The offer of an additional Director was rejected by the appellant; deadlock relief is the only realistic protection for the company.

④ Decision APPEAL DISMISSED — BUY-OUT UPHELD (with costs)

The Supreme Court (S.B. Sinha J.), after a grand review — Elder, H.R. Harmer (“oppressive” = “burdensome, harsh and wrongful”; “mere loss of confidence or pure deadlock does not come within S.210″ — but oppression meaning “visible departure from the standards of fair dealing…”), Shanti Prasad, and especially Needle Industries (Chandrachud CJ: even where oppression is not proved, “the court is not powerless to do substantial justice between the parties”) — held that the Tribunal's jurisdiction under Ss. 397/398 r/w S.402 cannot be disabled by “sheer technicality” where its absence would mean “complete mismanagement in regard to the affairs of the company”. It approved the equitable superimposition: the just-and-equitable principle (from Ebrahimi and winding-up jurisprudence — Yenidje Tobacco's deadlock; relief as last resort when other remedies are insufficient) may inform S.397/398 relief “subject to applicability of the well-known judicial safeguards”. It accepted Sangramsinh Gaekwad's glosses (burden on the applicant, but court examines the entire record; an unlawful act is not per se oppressive; clean-hands and acquiescence qualifications from London School of Electronics/R.A. Noble), and it rejected the over-broad reading of Kilpest as contrary to larger Benches. On facts: two shareholders-two directors, personal bitterness, direct impact on the company's conduct — CLB/HC rightly exercised jurisdiction; the funds-plea came too late; the additional-director route was spurned by the appellant. Article 136 interference declined.

⑤ Principle evolved: (1) The Tribunal's jurisdiction so long as the Ss. 397/398 gate is invoked is not hostage to a positive oppression-finding: it “would not be powerless” to pass orders in the interest of the company — including buy-out directions in deadlock. (2) Indian law absorbs just-and-equitable equity into oppression/mismanagement relief — protection of the company & shareholders as a whole is the lodestar (need for dual-test facts persists, though: see the 2021 tightening in Cyrus). (3) Doctrine guards: burden on applicant (whole-record view), illegality-not-per-se-oppressive, clean hands, acquiescence, cautious quasi-partnership ( Kilpest bare-proposition rejected), relief must direct nexus with company's affairs.

Case 29 · The modern restatement — dual test, removal-≠-oppression, Art. 75 exit, no reinstatement

Tata Consultancy Services Ltd. v. Cyrus Investments (P) Ltd.

(2021) 9 SCC 449 · Civil Appeals (arising from NCLAT order of 18.12.2019) · V. Ramasubramanian J. (3-Judge Bench)
Supreme Court of India

① Facts (background of the litigation, from the case material)

Tata Sons (incorporated 8.11.1917 as a private limited company) is the group's principal holding company. Two Shapoorji Pallonji Group companies — Cyrus Investments and Sterling Investment Corporation — began with 48 preference + 40 equity shares inherited from Mrs. Rodabeh Sawhney and grew to 18.37% of Tata Sons' paid-up capital; the two Tata Trusts held ~65.89%, operating companies 12.87%. Cyrus Pallonji Mistry (CPM) — whose father Pallonji Mistry had sat on the Board 1980–2004 — joined as a Non-Executive Director in August 2006; he was made Executive Deputy Chairman (16.03.2012, five-year term from 01.04.2012), then Executive Chairman w.e.f. 29.12.2012 with Ratan N. Tata (RNT) becoming Chairman Emeritus. On 24.10.2016 the Board replaced CPM with RNT as interim Non-Executive Chairman (CPM could remain Non-Executive Director); shareholders of Tata Industries, TCS and Tata Teleservices thereafter removed him from those boards; CPM resigned from other operating companies ahead of removal resolutions. On 20.12.2016 the two SP companies (together ~2% of issued capital) petitioned the NCLT under Ss. 241/242 r/w 244 — unfair prejudice, oppression, mismanagement — seeking (inter alia) that affairs were run as RNT's “proprietary concern”, that winding-up-grade facts existed but liquidation would unfairly prejudice them, plus relief. They first needed the proviso waiver of S.244(1)(a)'s 1/10 threshold; NCLT (06.03.2017 & 17.04.2017) dismissed the petition as non-maintainable and refused waiver; NCLAT (21.09.2017) granted the waiver and remanded. Meanwhile (16.02.2017) an EGM removed CPM as Director of Tata Sons. On merits, NCLT (09.07.2018) dismissed the petition after point-wise findings. NCLAT (18.12.2019) reversed wholesale: set aside the 24.10.2016 Board proceedings, “restored” CPM as Executive Chairman “for rest of the tenure” (declaring his successor's appointment illegal), directed RNT/Trust-nominee directors to desist from advance decisions requiring Board/AGM majority, enjoined Tata Sons' Article 75 share-transfer power against the minority except in “exceptional circumstances” with recorded reasons, and declared illegal the RoC conversion of Tata Sons back into a private company. Twelve appeals by the Tata side (Tata Sons, RNT, the Trustees, three operating companies) plus one by the complainants (seeking more — proportional representation / restricting Article 121 affirmative votes) reached the Supreme Court; the RoC's “Private”-word grievances were also before it.

② Issues (questions of law framed at ¶14.2): (1) Could NCLAT lawfully hold the affairs “prejudicial and oppressive” and winding-up-justifiable without overturning NCLT's fact-findings individually — the dual-test discipline? (2) Were NCLAT's reliefs — including reinstatement of CPM as Chairman/Director — consonant with the pleadings, prayers, and S.242(2)? (3) Could NCLAT mute Article 75 (the compulsory-transfer/exit option) without striking it down? (4) Was the characterisation of the Trusts' affirmative voting rights (Arts. 121/121A read with 104B) as oppressive justified after the SP Group had abandoned the challenge to them? (5) Did Tata Sons' re-conversion to private company need S.14 CA 2013 approval or S.43A(4) CA 1956 action?
Tata Sons, RNT, Trustees (appellants)
• NCLT dealt with every allegation (Siva/Sterling transactions, Air Asia, Mehli Mistry dealings, Corus, Nano…) and recorded findings; NCLAT overturned none individually — its one-paragraph ¶183 conclusion cannot found the dual opinion.
• Removal of a director/chairman per se is never oppression (Hanuman Prasad Bagri); the Tribunal cannot sit in appeal over the removal's legal validity — only ask whether the conduct was oppressive/prejudicial to some members. CPM was till 24.10.2016 only removed as Chairman, not as Director.
• S.242(2) is addressed to the future conduct of the company; there is no reinstatement power, certainly not “for rest of the tenure” after the term has run (29.12.2017–timeline) and never prayed for.
• Articles already waived by SP attack cannot be re-opened; Arts. 75/121/121A bind subscribers (S.10); Article 75 is an exit option, to be exercised/valued by the parties, not judicially muted; reconversion complied with law.
SP Group / complainant companies (respondents)
• Tata Sons is run as the proprietary concern of RNT, Board as puppets: articles 86, 104B, 118, 121, 121A concentrate control in Trust-nominee Directors and their “pre-consultation/pre-clearance” with RNT/Trustees — prejudicial to members and to public interest.
• CPM's removal was engineered overnight without notice months after the NRC praised his performance (pay-hike recommendation 29.6.2016) — probity collapse; then he was hounded off every operating-company board.
• Dubious transactions (Nano, Corus, DoCoMo, IL&FS guarantee, Mehli Mistry flat, Air Asia) show mismanagement; a quasi-partnership of 18.37% family group vs Trusts deserves the equitable superimposition (Ebrahimi; Radharamanan; Needle Industries).
• Relief sought as alternative: fair-value separation/exit of SP's ownership via capital-reduction or Article 75-route with compensation (IA before SC).

④ Decision SP GROUP APPEAL DISMISSED TATA APPEALS ALLOWED — NCLAT SET ASIDE; COMPANY PETITION DISMISSED

(Q1 — dual test): S.241(1)(a) r/w S.242(1)(a)–(b) requires the Tribunal's opinion that (1) affairs have been/are being conducted prejudicially to members/public interest/company or oppressively to any member(s), and (2) facts justify just-and-equitable winding up but winding up would unfairly prejudice the members. NCLAT, a final fact-forum, did not set aside NCLT's findings; findings not specifically reversed attained finality — an appellate concocted “oppression” without that groundwork is legally infirm. Crucially (¶16.21–16.28): CPM was removed only from the Chairmanship on the petition date — “the mere termination of Directorship cannot be projected as something that would trigger the just and equitable clause… or relief under Ss. 241–242”; and even removal from Directorship “can never be held to be an oppressive or prejudicial conduct” (Hanuman Prasad Bagri). The Tribunal asks not whether the removal was legally valid or justified but whether it tantamounts to oppressive/prejudicial conduct to some members. The SP case was internally inconsistent: mutual-admiration-NRC praise till 29.6.2016 negates a long course of oppression; their nominees had subscribed to all the Articles. (Q2 — reinstatement): S.242(2) menu neither mentions reinstatement nor can it be implied — reinstatement ≠ the natural method of ending a matter of “past” conduct; never specifically prayed; “for rest of the tenure” was incoherent after the term expired. NCLAT granted it gratis — unsustainable. (Q3 — Article 75): it's an exit-option article binding under S.10; NCLAT could not enjoin its exercise without striking it down (challenge expressly given up); parties left to take “the Article 75 route or any other legally available route” for fair-value separation. (Q4 — Arts. 121/104B affirmative votes): objections abandoned; S.47(1)(b) preference-style protections and proportional representation are lawful; pre-consultation reflects the Trusts' selfless philanthropy model (66% holding funnels dividends to charity via Trust deeds — “romantic idealism” arguments cannot rewrite subscribed articles); the nominee directors' duty analysis from Ss. 135/149/151/161/166/177 changes nothing. (Q5 — conversion): the conversion to private followed the transitional scheme; NCLAT's illegality finding error. Result: all Tata appeals allowed; C.P. No. 82 of 2016 dismissed; SP's appeal (C.A. 1802/2020) dismissed; the later separation-of-ownership IA (adjudication on facts/valuation) not entertained at S.423 questions-of-law stage. No costs.

⑤ Principle evolved — the 2021 restatement of Indian minority law: (1) The dual test is mandatory and cumulative: limb-1 conduct + limb-2 winding-up-us-just-but-unfair — a consciously stricter scheme than UK S.994; (2) appellate interference with the dual opinion requires specific dislodgment of the fact-findings that reject oppression, or a demonstrated perversity; (3) removal of a director/chairman, per se, is never oppression, and the Tribunal doesn't retry removal legality — it asks the conduct-question; — failed business decisions likewise cannot be dressed as oppression; (4) relief under S.242 looks forward to “bring to an end” the matters complained of — no implied reinstatement power; (5) subscribed articles bind (S.10): affirmative-vote and exit-option articles (like Tata's Arts. 121/75) are enforceable till lawfully struck down; (6) “divorce without acrimony” — buy-out/exit at fair value — is the encouraged end-state of corporate family fights in both jurisdictions.

PYQ 1 · Long (Dec-2024 · Q5)

“Discuss the law relating to prevention of oppression and mismanagement, as a protection of minority shareholders under the Companies Act, 2013. When can the Tribunal grant relief and what orders may it pass?” mapped from Dec-2024 Q5 checklist

Marks split: № 2 concept framing (majority rule → qualified minority rights) · № 2 S.241 grounds + mismanagement limb · № 2 dual test + threshold S.244/waiver · № 2 S.242 order-menu + Ss. 243–246 + cases (Shanti Prasad → Radharamanan → Cyrus).

Framing. Corporate democracy vests control in the majority forum, but Company Law corrects its excesses at two levels — judge-made exceptions to Foss v. Harbottle, and the statutory chapter XVI (Ss. 241–246), an “ingenious remedy” whose mere availability deters management. These are qualified minority rights — open to “any member” (even a majority group, where control lies elsewhere), screened by threshold numbers. Grounds (S.241): (a) affairs conducted — or having been conducted — prejudicially to public interest, oppressively to any member(s), or prejudicially to the company (the oppression/prejudice track, requiring the Shanti Prasad course: continuous, burdensome-harsh-wrongful conduct with a lack-of-probity/fair-dealing element, suffered qua member); (b) a material change in management or control (excluding creditor/shareholder-class–beneficial changes) making future prejudice likely (the mismanagement track, relief running to the company). The dual test (S.242(1)): the Tribunal must opine BOTH that limb-1 conduct exists and that facts justify a just-and-equitable winding up while winding up would unfairly prejudice the members — Indian law's deliberate extra stringency over UK S.994 (TCS v. Cyrus, ¶16.2). Standing (S.244): 100 members / 1/10 of members (whichever less) / 1/10 issued capital; 1/5 for no-capital companies; consent in writing; joint holders one; Tribunal may waive if just and equitable (granted in Cyrus; standing frozen at filing — Rajahmundry). The Central Government may itself apply under S.241(2). Powers (S.242): any order to end the matters complained of — future-conduct regulation, buy-outs by members/company (with capital reduction), transfer restrictions, agreement terminations (with notices; S.243 consequences), setting aside fraudulent transfers, recovery of undue gains — plus interim orders, board supersession/administrators (Rajahmundry), and order-driven article alterations that bind as if the company's own (no inconsistent reversal without leave). Calibration by the Supreme Court: relief may issue even without a formal oppression-finding where the company's interest demands and deadlock strangles it (Radharamanan, echoing Needle Industries' “not powerless”), but removal of a director per se is never oppression and S.242 is forward-looking — no implied reinstatement (Cyrus). Conclusion: Ch. XVI is a salvage jurisdiction: its remedial generosity serves one end only — “bringing to an end the matters complained of”, protecting the minority without burying the company in which they invested.

Examiner hook: Recite the dual test verbatim once; name the waived-threshold in Cyrus (2% holders) and the standing-frozen rule in Rajahmundry (65/603) — details most scripts omit.
PYQ 2 · Long

“State the rule in Foss v. Harbottle and the exceptions recognised to it. Is the rule still alive in India after Chapter XVI?” DU-pattern — frequently asked

Marks split: № 2 rule (two limbs + policy) · № 4 the five/six exceptions with authorities · № 1 Foss facts/decision summary · № 1 vitality answer.

The rule. In Foss v. Harbottle (1843) 2 Hare 461, two shareholders of the Victoria Park Company complained that promoter-directors had sold their own land to the company, misapplied funds and created unauthorised mortgages. Demurrers succeeded: Vice-Chancellor Wigram held that (i) the injury was to the corporation — the company alone is the proper plaintiff (“in law the corporation and the aggregate of members are not the same thing”); and (ii) the proprietors in general meeting retained power to control the board and ratify avoidable transactions, so members' suit was futile (“…the very fact that the governing body… may bind even a reluctant minority is decisive”). Jenkins LJ later condensed it (Edwards v. Halliwell): proper plaintiff = company; ratifiable-by-simple-majority wrongs cannot found individual actions (MacDougall v. Gardiner: “no use in having litigation… ultimately the majority gets its wishes”). Policy: avoid multiplicity of futile suits and respect internal democracy. Exceptions — where no majority can ratify, and every member may sue (relief to the company — the ‘derivative action’): (1) Acts ultra vires the memorandum (Bharat Insurance v. Kanhaiya Lal, Sale J: application of assets against the MoA “is not a matter of mere internal management” — single member may sue for declaration); (2) fraud on the minority — discriminatory majority self-dealing (Menier v. Hooper's Telegraph; Cook v. Deeks; Brown v. British Abrasive Wheel; test in Greenhalgh/Allen v. Gold Reefs: benefit of the company as a whole); (3) acts requiring special majority purported by ordinary resolution (Dhakeswari Cotton Mills; Nagappa Chettiar); (4) wrongdoers in control — Foss itself reserved this (“claims of justice… superior to technical rules”; Glass v. Atkin; control = decisive influence making a meeting futile); (5) individual membership rights (Pender v. Lushington — vote must be recorded; Joseph v. Jos). A sixth, institutional exception is Chapter XVI itself (Kanika Mukherji v. Rameshwar Dayal Dubey, Cal — Ss. 241–246 are “an exception to the rule… which lays down the sanctity of the majority rule”). Vitality today: the rule persists as the default for ratifiable, intra vires irregularities — statutory standing thresholds (S.244) reinforce its screening logic; but wherever the conduct crosses into unratifiable territory or oppression/mismanagement, statute gives the member a direct door to the Tribunal. It survives as a boundary doctrine, not a tyranny-license.

Examiner hook: The Foss-bill details (180 acres, bankrupt directors, meeting-power surviving) show mastery of the case material; the “cadit quaestio” quote closes the answer with style.
PYQ 3 · Long (flagged in DU notes)

“Explain the scheme of Sections 241 to 244 of the Companies Act, 2013 for prevention of oppression and mismanagement.” Meghraj Unit 8 — Long Answer flag

Marks split: № 2 S.241 tracks · № 2 S.242 powers · № 2 S.244 thresholds/waiver · № 2 S.243/CG/S.246 + case support.

S.241 — the gateway, two tracks: (1)(a) lets a member complain of present/past prejudicial-to-public-interest, oppressive-to-member(s), or prejudicial-to-company conduct; (1)(b) covers mismanagement — a material change in management/control (Board, manager, share ownership, membership, “in any other manner whatsoever”), not being a creditor/debenture/class-shareholder interested change, by reason whereof prejudicial conduct is likely — preventive in design, relief running to the company. The Central Government may itself apply (S.241(2)). S.242 — the remedy engine: on forming the dual opinion — limb-1 conduct + facts justifying just-and-equitable winding up yet winding up would unfairly prejudice — the Tribunal may make “such order as it thinks fit” to end the matters complained of; S.242(2) enumerates (without exhaustion): regulation of future affairs; share purchases by other members or the company (and consequential capital reduction, S.242(2)(c)); transfer/allotment restrictions; termination/modification of agreements with MD/director/manager (after due notice) and even with other parties (leave-based); setting aside fraudulent/unlawful transfers and executions; recovery of gains from misapplication/breach of trust; costs. Tribunal-ordered article amendments bind as the company's own and cannot be reversed without leave. S.243 — consequential platform: when orders terminate/mofify managerial agreements, affected officers are heard first, and compensation claims against the company for such termination are barred. S.244 — standing & waiver: share-capital companies — 100 members / 1/10 of members (whichever less) / holders of 1/10 issued capital (calls paid, joint holders = one); no-capital — 1/5 of members; the proviso waiver empowers the Tribunal to admit lower-number members “if… just and equitable” (Cyrus: 2% SP Group admitted on waiver). Standing is frozen at presentation (Rajahmundry); “consent” under S.244(2) requires like-minded written consent. Equator cases: Shanti Prasad (oppression elements), Radharamanan (order despite failed oppression-finding, for the company's good), TCS v. Cyrus (dual test + forward-looking relief + articles-bind). S.246 pulls in Ss. 337–341 (fraud-investigation machinery) mutatis mutandis.

Examiner hook: The track-distinction table + the phrase “qualified minority rights” + waiver authority (2%) = full marks territory.
PYQ 4 · Short Note (4–5 marks)

“Write a short note on class action suits under Section 245.” Meghraj Unit 8 — Short Answer flag

Marks split: № 1 concept · № 1 standing · № 2 grounds/factors/orders · № 1 guards.

Concept (2013 innovation): A requisite number of members or depositors may apply to the NCLT on behalf of the whole class where the company's management/affairs are prejudicial to the interests of the company or its members/depositors — seeking restraint, declarations, and damages/compensation against the company, directors, auditors (audit firm + the partner involved), experts, advisors, consultants or any other person for fraudulent/unlawful/wrongful or misleading conduct. Standing (S.245(3)): 100 members or prescribed % (whichever less) or prescribed % of issued capital — calls/due sums paid; 1/5 of members for no-capital companies; 100/prescribed % depositors or prescribed % of outstanding deposits. Grounds (S.245(1)): restrain ultra-vires or MoA/AoA-breaching acts; declare resolutions void when passed suppressing material facts; restrain acts against law or members' resolutions; claim damages for the chain's wrongful conduct/statements. Tribunal filters (S.245(4)): applicant's good faith; third-party involvement; whether he could sue in his own right; views of disinterested members; whether the act could have been authorised/ratified. Procedure: public notice on admission (English + vernacular newspapers — Rule 87, within 7 days); consolidation; class may choose (else Tribunal appoints) a lead applicant; no two applications for the same cause; costs borne by the company or the person responsible; order binds company + all members/depositors/auditors/experts. Guards: frivolous/vexatious applications rejected with recorded reasons, costs ≤ ₹1,00,000; non-compliance attracts ₹5–25 lakh (company) and ₹25,000–1 lakh (officers). Close: imported from the US (and post-Satyam legislative impulse), S.245 arms the small investor-collective who individually lacks means to sue institutional fraud — complementing the individual protective jurisdiction of S.241.

Examiner hook: “Audit firm + every involved partner liable [S.245(2)]” and the ₹1-lakh frivolity cap are the least-written facts.
PYQ 5 · Medium

“Who can apply for relief under Section 241? Is a member below the S.244 threshold remediless? Discuss with authority.” DU-pattern

Marks split: № 2 thresholds · № 1 consent discipline · № 2 waiver + Rajahmundry/Cyrus · № 1 conclusion.

Threshold (S.244): for share-capital companies, the application must proceed from ≥100 members or ≥1/10 of total members, whichever less, or member(s) holding ≥1/10 of issued share capital; for no-capital companies, ≥1/5 of members. Joint holders count as one member (Explanation); applicants must have paid calls/dues (share-application money pending allotment can still support membership in substance where the company has so treated the investor). “Consent” under S.244(2) demands written consent upon the same thing in the same sense — signatures obtained for a different purpose (e.g., requisitioning a meeting) don't count; GPA-signed consent can (P. Punniah v. Jeypore Sugar, SC). Below the line — the waiver door: the proviso lets the Tribunal admit any member(s) short of the threshold where “circumstances exist which make it just and equitable to do so” — exercised in TCS v. Cyrus where NCLAT admitted the SP Group holding ~2% (a waiver the Supreme Court left undisturbed, deciding instead on merits). Timing: standing is judged at presentation — Rajahmundry Electric (65 valid consents out of 603 members; 13 later withdrawals irrelevant: “a petition which was valid when presented cannot cease to be maintainable”). Even zero-threshold routes exist: Central Government under S.241(2), and class action S.245 with its own numbers. Conclusion: the threshold screens, not bars — between waiver, CG reference and S.245, a genuine grievance is never remediless.

Examiner hook: “standing frozen at filing” + waiver numbers from Cyrus — precision wins marks.
PYQ 6 · Medium

“‘Oppression’ is not defined in the Companies Act. How have the courts understood it? Explain with authorities.” DU-pattern — frequently asked

Marks split: № 1 non-definition + purpose approach · № 2 Scottish/English definitions · № 2 Shanti Prasad four elements · № 2 later refinements (Needle/Radharamanan/Cyrus) + gallery example.

Point of departure: Parliament left “oppressive” undefined because, as Lord Cooper noted in Elder v. Elder & Watson Ltd. (1952), “the circumstances in which oppression may arise being so infinitely various… impossible to define them with precision.” Courts therefore build the meaning fact-wise. Foundational formulae: Elder — the conduct must involve “at the lowest… a visible departure from the standards of fair dealing, and a violation of the conditions of fair play on which every shareholder who entrusts his money to a company is entitled to rely”; and the petitioner's complaint fails where he suffers not as shareholder but as director/employee (the qua-member requirement). The House of Lords in Scottish Co-operative Wholesale Society v. Meyer (1959) adopted the dictionary test — “burdensome, harsh and wrongful” — holding oppression where the majority reduced minority directors-nominees to “mere ciphers”. Indian crystallisation (Shanti Prasad Jain v. Kalinga Tubes, AIR 1965 SC 1535): (i) conduct in the manner of management by predominant voting power; (ii) a continuous course concluding at the petition date (“events not in isolation but as part of a consecutive story”); (iii) burdensome, harsh and wrongful, importing lack of probity or fair dealing in the member's proprietary rights — mere lack of confidence insufficient “unless it… springs from oppression of a minority by a majority”; (iv) the just-and-equitable/unfair-prejudice envelope. Refinements: Needle Industries (1981) — “a resolution may be perfectly legal and yet oppressive”, and conversely an isolated unlawful act without mala fide policy is not oppression; an unwise/careless act is not oppression; even exoneration does not render the court “powerless”. Radharamanan (2008) — burden on applicant but the whole record is weighed; clean hands and acquiescence (London School of Electronics; R.A. Noble) temper relief; equitable superimposition permissible. TCS v. Cyrus (2021) — dual test mandatory; removal of a director/chairman per se never oppression; the Tribunal asks whether removal was oppressive conduct to some members, not whether it was procedurally legal. Gallery anchor: compare Hindustan Co-operative Insurance (compensation diverted to new objects by special resolution while meetings suppressed — oppression) with Shanti Prasad itself (preventing a single group's capture by discretionary allotment — not oppression). Conclusion: oppression is unfairness-with-conduct — sustained, prejudicial use of corporate power over proprietary membership rights — wider than illegality, narrower than disappointment.

Examiner hook: Quote Elder (“visible departure…”) + Meyer (B-H-W) + Shanti Prasad's “consecutive story” verbatim — three quotations lift the answer to the top band.
PYQ 7 · Long (flagged in DU notes)

“Explain the concept of mismanagement and the remedy available against it under Section 241(1)(b). How does it differ from oppression?” Meghraj Unit 8 — Long Answer flag

Marks split: № 2 concept + material change · № 2 instances + Rajahmundry · № 2 oppression-vs-mismanagement table · № 2 relief mechanics + conclusion.

Concept: S.241(1)(b) is the Act's preventive limb. Where a material change has taken place in the management or control of the company — by alteration in the Board of directors, the manager, the ownership of shares, its membership (if without share capital), “or in any other manner whatsoever” — and by reason of that change it is likely that the affairs will be conducted prejudicially to the company or its members or any class of members, relief can be sought before the harm matures. Two exclusions mark the limb: changes brought about by or in the interest of creditors, debenture-holders or any class of shareholders do not count; and the likelihood of prejudice must be shown — not mere apprehension. In the 1956 framework (S.398) the section came into play “when there is actual mismanagement or apprehension of mismanagement” (Shanti Prasad) — 2013 keeps that architecture in 241(1)(b) while folding the conduct-track into 241(1)(a). Instances (DU notes gallery): group in power conspiring to defraud members; properly appointed directors prevented from joining and functioning; director in-fighting seriously prejudicing the company; MD staying on after term-expiry without appointment process; directors benefiting by neglect at the company's cost. Classic authority — Rajahmundry Electric (AIR 1956 SC 213): gross personal drains on funds, derelict plant, Government arrears, a “local junta ruling the roost” with powerless members = just-and-equitable facts; the SC affirmed two administrators appointed in supersession — and settled that the “don't-interfere-in-internal-management” rule shields only a running concern, not a company whose winding up is otherwise justified. Differences from oppression: (i) trigger — ongoing course of management-conduct vs a change-triggered risk; (ii) timing — accumulated past/present wrongs vs likelihood of future prejudice; (iii) beneficiary — the wronged members qua members vs the company itself (relief “runs in favour of the company and not to any particular member”); (iv) evidential color — qua-member proprietary unfairness vs conduct-with-potential-to-harm the corporate body. Relief mechanics: same S.242 order-menu and S.242(1) opinion discipline (winding-up facts + unfair prejudice), same S.244 standing; Tribunal may supersede management (Rajahmundry), reconstitute boards, regulate future conduct, terminate tainted managerial agreements (S.242(2)(e)–(f) with S.243 notice-consequences). Conclusion: mismanagement relief is the company's own insurance — it catches the downward trajectory before the members' losses crystallise into “oppression”.

Examiner hook: The two exclusions (creditor/debenture/shareholder-class changes) and “relief runs to the company” are the distinctions most scripts miss.
PYQ 8 · Problem (8 marks)

“P and Q hold 12% and 8% of X Ltd. The board, dominated by group R (60%), proposes a fresh private allotment to R's associates, passes it in a general meeting, diluting P and Q; months later P is removed as a director; meanwhile the company has stopped calling AGMs and the two blocks refuse to co-operate, paralysing the Board. P and Q (together 20%) want Tribunal relief but fear they fall short of S.244; they want P reinstated and the allotment undone. Advise.” DU-pattern composite

Marks split: № 2 standing/waiver · № 2 allotment-dilution limb · № 2 removal limb · № 2 deadlock/relief menu + conclusion.

Limb 1 — standing: P+Q together hold 20% of issued capital: they comfortably clear S.244's 1/10-issued-capital route (calls paid), so no waiver is even needed; had they fallen short, the proviso lets the Tribunal admit them where just and equitable (Cyrus: 2% holders admitted). Standing is assessed at filing (Rajahmundry). Limb 2 — the dilutive allotment: this is their strongest card. Allotment engineered to destroy minority voting strength and squeeze them out can be oppression where mala fide purpose colors the power's exercise (checklist's own example; cf. the doctrinal line from Menier/Cook v. Deeks against majority self-advantage). They must however answer Shanti Prasad: if the company needed funds and the issue leaves the register more broad-based — and preventing one group's capture is genuine — the majority's self-defence is not oppression. Evidence of associates-stooge allotments, slow calls, suppression at the meeting (S.102-type non-disclosure) tips the balance back for P/Q; restraining/setting aside transfers fits S.242(2)(d)&(g). Limb 3 — P's removal: weak. Hanuman Prasad Bagri and Cyrus hold removal of a director per se is never oppression, and the Tribunal will not sit on the removal's legality — only ask whether the conduct is oppressive to members; and there is no implied reinstatement power under S.242(2), which looks forward. Advise dropping reinstatement; the AGM-boycott + refusal-to-co-operate story, however, may ground the mismanagement track (S.241(1)(b), material change + likely prejudice). Limb 4 — deadlock & relief menu: the paralysed two-block Board is Radharamanan territory: even absent a full oppression-finding, the Tribunal “is not powerless” where the company's interest demands — it may regulate future conduct, reconstitute the Board, appoint administrators (Rajahmundry), or order a buy-out (the “divorce without acrimony” the SC commended in Cyrus) at fair value. Conclusion: proceed on oppression (dilution with mala fide design) + mismanagement tracks; frame the dual test (just-and-equitable winding-up facts + unfair prejudice from liquidation); seek forward-looking orders under S.242 — withdrawal/validation of the allotment, board reform, and in the alternative buy-out of P/Q at fair value. Drop reinstatement of P; it is doctrinally dead.

Examiner hook: Explicitly dropping the weak reinstatement prayer (with Cyrus ¶16.28) shows judgment — examiners reward tactical honesty.
VAULT

Rapid Revision — Doctrines, Numbers & Quotables

Foss formula
Proper plaintiff = company · ratifiable-by-majority wrongs = no individual action · exceptions ① ultra vires ② fraud on minority ③ special-majority bypass ④ wrongdoer control ⑤ personal rights ⑥ Ch. XVI statute itself.
Oppression formula
Manner-of-management + continuous-course-to-petition-date + burdensome/harsh/wrongful + lack of probity/fair dealing + qua member + just-&-equitable/unfair-prejudice envelope (Shanti Prasad ¶19).
Standing (S.244)
100 members / 1⁄10 members (lesser) / 1⁄10 issued capital · 1⁄5 no-capital · joint holders = 1 · calls paid · consent in writing · waiver if just & equitable (Cyrus 2%) · frozen at filing (Rajahmundry 65/603).
Dual test (S.242(1))
Limb 1 conduct (oppressive/prejudicial) AND Limb 2 winding-up-just-but-unfair — stricter than UK S.994. Both mandatory (Cyrus ¶16.2).
S.242 menu
Future regulation · buy-outs (+capital cut) · transfer blocks · agreement termination (notice; S.243 bars comp-claims) · undo fraudulent transfers · recover undue gains · administrators possible · amendment = company's own; leave needed to reverse.
Mismanagement
Trigger = material change in mgmt/control → likely prejudice · excludes creditor/debenture/class-shareholder changes · relief runs to the COMPANY · Rajahmundry: running-concern rule yields to salvage.
Class action (S.245)
100 or prescribed% members/depositors · calls paid · grounds: ultra vires/breach/void resolutions/damages vs company-directors-auditors-experts · filters S.245(4) · lead applicant; one cause one action; notice 7 days; costs ≤ ₹1 lakh if frivolous; bind-the-class effect.
Removal ≠ oppression
Terminating a director/chairman is per se never oppression; Tribunal doesn't retry legality; S.242 forward-looking — no implied reinstatement (Bagri; Cyrus ¶16.21–16.28, ¶17).
Equitable escape
“Court not powerless” to do substantial justice despite failed oppression-finding (Needle Industries); equity may be superimposed on law with judicial safeguards (Radharamanan); clean hands, acquiescence, quasi-partnership caution (Kilpest capped).

Cyrus 2021 — dual test cumulative; removal-≠-oppression; no reinstatement; articles (Arts. 75/121) bind; divorce-without-acrimony exit.
