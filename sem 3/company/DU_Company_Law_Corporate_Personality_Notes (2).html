Company, Legal Personality & the Theory of Corporate Personality

Meaning · legal-person concepts · Sec. 2(20) · Sec. 9 · characteristics · theories of corporate personality.

1.1 What is a "Company"? — Definition & Nature

Statutory definition — Sec. 2(20), Companies Act 2013

S. 2(20) "Company" means a company incorporated under this Act or under any previous company law.

Body corporate S. 2(11) — includes a company incorporated outside India; excludes a co-operative society and any body corporate notified by the Central Government.

Read with S. 1(4), the Act applies to companies under this Act, insurance companies, banking companies, electricity companies, companies under special Acts, and notified bodies corporate.

Common-law (juristic) meaning

In common law a company is a legal person — a "legal entity separate from, and capable of surviving beyond the lives of, its members" (Avtar Singh, citing Salomon). It is:

  • "an artificial, invisible, intangible person, existing only in the contemplation of law" — Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward (1819) 17 US 518 (Marshall C.J.);
  • a legal device for attaining social/economic ends — "an intricate, centralised, economic-administrative structure run by professional managers who hire capital from investors";
  • a combination of a political, social, economic and legal institution.
📜
Sec. 9 — Effect of Registration (the bedrock provision) From the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons who from time to time become members of the company shall be a body corporate by the name contained in the memorandum, capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company, and having perpetual succession, with power to hold property, to contract, to sue and be sued in its own name, and with a common seal (if any).

Nature / essential characteristics of a company

CharacteristicWhat it means
1. Incorporated associationMust be registered under the Act (S. 3 read with S. 7); registration is conclusive (S. 7(9) — no company can challenge its own incorporation; Jubilee Cotton Mills principle).
2. Separate legal entityDistinct from members — Salomon; S. 9. Company can own property and incur liabilities of its own.
3. Perpetual succession"Members may come and go, but the company can go on forever." Death/insolvency of members does not affect it.
4. Limited liabilityMembers liable only to the unpaid amount on shares (or guarantee amount) — except where the veil is lifted.
5. Separate propertyShareholders have no legal or equitable interest in company property; no insurable interest in it.
6. Transferable sharesS. 44 — shares are movable property, transferable as per articles (subject to restrictions in private companies).
7. Capacity to sue & be suedIn its own name, even against its own members.
8. Common seal (optional)Official signature; since 2015 amendment optional (S. 12 read with S. 9).
9. Artificial juristic personNo body, mind or conscience — acts through human agents (directors); not a citizen.
10. Separate taxationCompany assessed to tax separately; member's receipt of income is a separate taxable event.

Legal person — the stepping stone

Gray: a person is "an entity to which rights and duties may be attached". Salmond: any being whom the law regards as capable of rights and duties. Every such being is a person — human or not.

  • Natural persons — citizens and aliens/foreigners.
  • Artificial (juristic) persons — created or recognised by law: companies, statutory corporations, universities, idols,
    (an idol is a legal person — Pramatha Nath Mullick v. Praduman Kumar Mullick (1925) PC; Guru Granth Sahib — SGPC v. Somnath Das).

Three pre-conditions of juristic personality: (i) a group of persons associated for a purpose; (ii) an organ through which it functions; (iii) attribution of legal persona by law.

Types of corporation: corporation aggregate (a company — group incorporated) and corporation sole (an incorporated series of successive persons, e.g. the British Crown).

Note: a person may have double capacity (e.g. director + trustee) but never double personality — a point that resolves the puzzle in Lee (see §2.5).

1.2 Theories of Corporate Personality

Why does the law treat a group as a "person"? Five classical theories answer the question. Mention 3–4 in a 10-mark answer; know the proponents.

① Fiction TheorySavigny · Salmond · Blackstone — Personality is a fiction of law; only human beings are real persons, the corporation is a legal make-believe. By a second fiction the corporation is clothed with the will of its members. Its root is the Papal maxim (Pope Innocent IV, 13th c.) that corporate bodies exist only in abstracto and so cannot be excommunicated — the Supreme Court has called this enunciation the foundation of the separate-entity principle.
② Realist (Organic) TheoryGierke — The group has a real (psychologically real) personality: a real mind, real will, real power of action, independent of the will of individual members; the corporate will is the will of the organ.
③ Bracket (Symbolist) TheoryIhering — Personality is only a symbol-technique, a bracket put round the rights of the members to facilitate dealings; nothing exists behind the bracket except the members.
④ Concession Theory — Personality is a concession (privilege) granted by the State; no incorporation without the State's permission. Supports the requirement of registration.
⑤ Purpose TheoryBrins · Barker — Personality is conferred for a special limited purpose and operates only within that purpose (ties in with the doctrine of ultra vires — an act outside the object clause is void).
⑥ Kelsen's Normative / Hohfeld's view — Personality is only the legal order's way of imputing obligations and rights to a complex of facts — a purely juridical construct, useful to mention as modern analysis.
✍️
Exam tip Do not stop at names. Connect each theory to a doctrine: Fiction ← certificate of incorporation; Concession ← registration (S. 7); Purpose ← ultra vires; Realist ← corporate will of the Board; Symbolist ← the veil and the real people behind it.

1.3 Historical evolution (one-paragraph frame)

1844 Joint Stock Companies Act (UK) — registration without Royal Charter; 1855–56 — limited liability recognised; 1862 — consolidated Act (the Act under which Salomon arose).
1850 & 1857 India's first company legislation — Act of 1850 (registration, no limited liability); Act of 1857 (limited liability).
1866 → 1913 Acts of 1866, 1882, 1895, 1908 and the Indian Companies Act, 1913 (consolidation; the Act under which TELCO was incorporated).
1956 Companies Act, 1956 — long-standing, ~700 sections; the Act under which most pre-2013 cases were decided.
2013 Companies Act, 2013 — repealed 1956 Act (S. 1); introduces OPC, CSR (S. 135), SFIO, class actions, independent directors, NCLT/NCLAT; far fewer sections, principles-based drafting.
2015–2023 Amendments of 2015 (common seal relaxed, OPC eased), 2017, 2020 (decriminalisation, CSR), 2021 (OPC reforms, multiple changes), plus the IBC 2016 taking over insolvency, and the LLP Act 2008 as a parallel form.

1.4 Advantages & disadvantages of incorporation (framework)

Advantages

  1. Independent corporate existence — from the date on the certificate (S. 9); a one-man company is still a distinct entity;
  2. Limited liability — members never personally liable for company debts (unless veil lifted);
  3. Perpetual succession — Gower: even a bomb on a general meeting cannot destroy the company;
  4. Separate property — shareholders have no right to company assets;
  5. Transferable shares — liquidity (S. 44);
  6. Capacity to sue / be sued — including against its own members;
  7. Professional management — Board of directors, hired expertise;
  8. Large capital — the only form able to invite the public (public offers, debentures).

Disadvantages

  1. Lifting of the corporate veil — the principal advantage can be taken away when the entity is abused (LIC v. Escorts Ltd. (1986) 59 Comp Cas 548 SC);
  2. Formalities and expense — registration (S. 7), annual filings (Ss. 92, 137), audit, ROC documents are public;
  3. Corporate governance burden — Board management, meetings, statutory records; decisions are tedious in large companies;
  4. Public disclosure — accounts and returns are open to inspection; loss of privacy;
  5. Separate taxation + double incidence — company taxed, then dividends taxed in members' hands (Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT);
  6. Borrowing and security complexities — charges registration, debenture formalities;
  7. Strangers to their own enterprise — directors owe fiduciary duties; members cannot use company assets as their own.

The Doctrine of Separate Legal Entity — Salomon, its Consequences and Lee

The "cardinal distinction" between the company and its members · Salomon in full · consequences · Indian recognition · Lee's dual capacity.

🧭
The doctrine stated Once a company is legally incorporated, it becomes a person in the eyes of the law, distinct and independent from its members. The company may own property, contract, sue and be sued in its own name; the members are neither owners of the corporate undertaking nor liable for corporate debts; the motives, intentions, schemes and conduct of the shareholders are legally irrelevant. The only guide is the statute itself (S. 9). This is the foundation of the whole of company law — and its chief exception is the doctrine of lifting the corporate veil (§04).

2.1 Salomon v. Salomon & Co., Ltd. (1897) A.C. 22 (H.L.) — six-part brief of the case that founded everything

Complete brief in the six-part format required for the exam — full quotations and the litigation history; the same case also appears as Case 1 in the Case Library (§06) with extended quotations, and as the anchor of the debate on limited liability (§4.12).

① Facts
  • Aron Salomon — for many years a prosperous leather merchant and boot manufacturer at 113 High Street, London, trading as "A. Salomon & Co."; the business was sound, solvent and yielded profits that maintained the family and added to his capital.
  • July 1892: he incorporated "Aron Salomon & Co., Ltd." under the Companies Act, 1862 — nominal capital £40,000 in 40,000 £1 shares; the seven subscribers to the memorandum were Salomon, his wife, daughter and four sons, each taking one share (purely to satisfy the statutory minimum); 20,007 shares were issued — Salomon 20,001, the family 6. Salomon and two sons were the directors.
  • Sale of the business (20 July / 2 Aug 1892): the business was sold to the company for £38,782 — resolved to pay £6,000 in cash and £10,000 in debentures (secured by a charge on the company's assets, ranking before all unsecured creditors); the remaining 20,000 shares were allotted to Salomon. (The debentures were later mortgaged to Edmund Broderip for £5,000 and, after default, re-issued to him at 8%.)
  • Failure: in October 1893 a winding-up order followed; the evidence of Emanuel Salomon attributed the failure to "a succession of strikes in the boot trade" and there was "not a tittle of evidence" to the contrary (Lord Watson).
  • Winding-up arithmetic: assets realised about £6,000; claims — secured debenture holder £10,000; unsecured creditors £7,733 → nothing for the unsecured creditors. The liquidator (in the company's name) sued Salomon for rescission, cancellation of the debentures, repayment of the price and indemnity of £7,733, alleging the price exceeded the value by over £8,200, that the scheme was "a fraud upon the creditors", and that there was no independent board.
② Issues
  1. Was the company a real, separate person, or was it merely the "nominee and agent" of Salomon — "whether, in truth, that artificial creation of the legislature should be treated as a real thing"?
  2. Could Salomon lawfully stand as both shareholder and secured debenture-holder of his own company, so that his £10,000 charge took priority over the unsecured trade creditors?
  3. Was the company entitled to an order that Salomon indemnify it against the claims of creditors (£7,733), as its alleged principal?
③ Arguments — company side (liquidator; Vaughan Williams J.; CA)
  • The business was "Salomon's business and no one else's" — the company was his agent; an agent must indemnify the principal.
  • The seven subscribers were mere "dummies"; the scheme was "contrary to the true intent and meaning of the Companies Act, 1862… in fraud of creditors" (CA order, as quoted by Lord Watson); Lindley L.J.: a "device to defraud creditors".
  • Price of £38,782 was exorbitant (exceeding value by £8,200+); debentures to the vendor himself were colourable; there was no independent board.
③ Arguments — Salomon's side (appealing to the House of Lords)
  • The Act's requirements were fully complied with; "the Act… has said that one share is sufficient to constitute a shareholder" (Halsbury) — six one-share members are as good as six substantial ones.
  • A company cannot be "a real person and nothing to be an agent at all" — the CA's agent-theory is self-contradictory (Halsbury).
  • The business was solvent at transfer, the price honest, the failure caused by strikes; all the members knew and accepted the terms (Watson); no evidence of absence of an independent board was led.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Halsbury L.C.: "I have no right to add to the requirements of the statute, nor to take from the requirements thus enacted. The sole guide must be the statute itself.… It is essential to the artificial creation that the law should recognise only that artificial existence — quite apart from the motives or conduct of individual corporators… once the company is legally incorporated it must be treated like any other independent person with rights and liabilities appropriate to itself."
  • Halsbury L.C. (again): "If it was a real thing; if it had a legal existence… it appears to me to follow as a consequence that it is impossible to deny the validity of the transactions into which it has entered" — and on the judges below: they "appear to me not to have been absolutely certain in their own minds whether to treat the company as a real thing or not".
  • Lord Macnaghten: "The company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers to the memorandum… the company is not in law the agent of the subscribers… nor are the subscribers as members liable in any shape or form, except to the extent and in the manner provided by the Act." And on one-man companies: "It has become the fashion to call companies of this class 'one man companies'. That is a taking nickname, but it does not help one much in the way of argument."
  • Lord Watson: the allegations of fraud and of absence of a board "were not supported by evidence" — "No evidence was led tending to support the allegation that no board of directors was ever appointed"; the liquidator admitted the business was in a sound condition with a substantial surplus at transfer.
⑤ Decision
  • Vaughan Williams J. (first instance): for the company — Salomon as agent must indemnify; price "exorbitant" though not fraudulent vis-à-vis shareholders. Court of Appeal (Lindley, Lopes, Kay L.JJ., 1895): affirmed — "the object of the whole arrangement is to do the very thing which the legislature intended not to be done"; a "device to defraud creditors"; the company "a mere sham" and its members "dummies".
  • House of Lords (Halsbury L.C., Watson, Herschell, Macnaghten — unanimously): appeal allowed, the CA reversed. The company was validly incorporated and was a separate person; the £10,000 debentures were valid and secured; Salomon was not liable to indemnify the company; the unsecured creditors, who dealt with the company on the faith of its registered documents, must bear the loss.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Separate legal personality: a company incorporated under the statute is a legal person distinct from its members, even where one man owns all but six of the shares; the company can contract with its promoter/member, hold security against him, and sue and be sued.
  • The statute is the sole guide; motives are irrelevant: courts may not add unlegislated conditions ("genuine subscribers", "independent directors") to incorporation; the shareholders' motives (even tax-planning or protection of the promoter) do not affect the validity of a properly incorporated company.
  • Limited liability as a legislative choice: even an honest trader may use the Act to limit his liability and take priority as a secured creditor; if this causes hardship to creditors, the remedy is reform of the Act — not judicial legislation. (This is why the counter-doctrine of lifting the veil and the statutory exceptions of §4.12 exist; that is the other side of the same coin.)
  • One-man companies are legitimate — "one share is enough"; Lee v. Lee's Air Farming (§2.4) and IRC v. Sansom are direct applications of this proposition.

2.2 The doctrine recognised in India — before and after Salomon

Kondoli Tea Co. Ltd., In re (ILR (1886) 13 Cal. 43) — the first Indian decision (even before Salomon): joint owners of a tea estate formed a company and transferred the estate to it, claiming exemption from ad valorem duty because they were the shareholders. Held: "The company was a separate person, a separate body altogether from the shareholders, and the transfer was as much a conveyance… as if the shareholders had been totally different persons." Duty payable. (Also holds that members cannot ask the veil to be lifted.)
T.R. Pratt (Bombay) Ltd. v. E.D. Sasoon & Co. Ltd. AIR 1936 Bom. 62 — a "one-man company" is still "a distinct entity; it is not permissible or relevant to enquire whether the directors belonged to the same family". Followed in Praga Tools Corpn. v. C.A. Imanual (1969) 1 SCC 585.
Dhulia-Amalner Motor Transport Ltd. v. Raychand Rupsi Dharamsi AIR 1952 Bom. 337 — partners sold buses to their own private company; the other partners sued for accounts of the firm's business carried on by the company. Held: the company was "altogether a third person"; buses were the company's property; motive of becoming shareholders is not a field of enquiry — the law recognises the company irrespective of the motives, intentions, schemes or conduct of individual shareholders.
Modern echoes — State of Karnataka v. Selvi J. Jayalalitha (2017) 201 Comp Cas 230 (SC): company is separate from members "subject to the exception when corporate entity is a mere cloak or sham used to misdirect shareholders and authorities"; a company cannot be convicted of conspiracy with its sole director (only one mind); Vodafone International Holdings BV v. UOI (2012) 6 SCC 613: group entities distinct, veil lifted only in exceptional/sham cases.

2.3 Consequences of separate legal personality (the 10-point answer template)

ConsequenceExplanation & authority
1. Company owns its own propertyMembers have no legal or equitable interest; no insurable interest. Kondoli Tea; Dhulia-Amalner.
2. Company contracts in its own nameEven with its own members/directors. A suit for wages lies against the company, not its directors — Abdul Haq v. Das Mai (1910).
3. Company sues and is suedIn its own name; may sue a member; may sue in forma pauperis (Mamta Papers (P) Ltd. v. State of Orissa (2000)).
4. Limited liability of membersNo member bound to pay more than unpaid amount on shares (or guarantee); a share transfer does not discharge the transferor's liability to the company for unpaid calls.
5. Perpetual successionMembers may come and go — the company goes on forever; total change of membership leaves the same entity with the same privileges and possessions (Blackstone; Gower's bomb illustration).
6. Company is not the agent/trustee of membersSalomon (Macnaghten); Freewheels (India) Ltd. v. Veda Mittra AIR 1969 Del. 258 (a 52% subsidiary is still a separate entity); J.H. Rayner (Mincing Lane) Ltd. v. Deptt. of Trade (1990) 2 AC 418 (HL).
7. Members cannot claim company's rightsA shareholder cannot sue for violation of the company's fundamental rights (Chiranjit Lal Chowdhury v. UOI AIR 1951 SC 41 — as the majority view; Mukherjea J. dissented); cannot claim the company's property on dissolution matters (Pierce Leslie & Co. v. Violet Ouchterlony Wapshare AIR 1969 SC 843).
8. Company's debts ≠ members' debtsDecree against one company cannot be executed against another, even with the same management; a holding company is not liable for the provident fund dues of its subsidiary (§04, ground 5).
9. Criminal liability is the company'sBooks of the company held by a managing director in his official capacity cannot be compelled from him personally; but consent of all directors to misapplication of company money is not the company's consent — they can be prosecuted for theft (A-G's Reference (No. 2 of 1983), (1984) QB 456).
10. Company has nationality, domicile & residenceIt is not a citizen but has these attributes (see §03: Casque v. IRC; De Beers Consolidated Mines v. Howe (1906)).
🔁
The same coin, two sides Because the company is a separate person, a member who sells property to his company makes a real sale (taxable) — CIT v. Associated Clothiers Ltd. AIR 1963 Cal. 629. Kahn-Freund: "the corporate entity sometimes works like a boomerang and hits the man who was trying to use it."

2.4 Lee v. Lee's Air Farming Ltd. (1960) 3 All ER 420 (PC) — six-part brief: the logical extreme of Salomon (master and servant in one person)

Complete brief (Case 7 in §06 carries the extended quotations, including the articles and the New Zealand judgments).

① Facts
  • In 1954, Geoffrey Woodhouse Lee of Christchurch, New Zealand, formed "Lee's Air Farming Ltd." to carry on the business of aerial top-dressing: nominal capital £3,000 in 3,000 shares of £1 — Lee was allotted 2,999; the remaining share was, per the memorandum, to be taken by a solicitor.
  • Under Art. 32 Lee was appointed governing director for life with "the full government and control of the company" vested in him, with power to exercise all the powers of the directors and of the company (his signed minute having the effect of a resolution); under Art. 33 "the company shall employ the said Geoffrey Woodhouse Lee as the chief pilot… at a salary of £1,500 per annum… and in respect of such employment the rules of law applicable to the relationship of master and servant shall apply as between the company and the said Geoffrey Woodhouse Lee" (amended 16 Aug 1954 by a minute signed by Lee to "a salary to be arranged by the governing director").
  • Lee piloted the company's aircraft for the farmers, with whom the company alone contracted; his wages were recorded in the company's wages book; the company paid the premiums on personal-accident policies on his life (debited to his personal account).
  • On 5 March 1956 Lee was killed in a crash while aerial top-dressing. His widow Catherine Lee claimed £2,430 (for herself and four infant children) plus funeral expenses under s. 3(1) of the New Zealand Workers' Compensation Act, 1922 — "worker" being defined in s. 2 as "any person who has entered into or works under a contract of service… with an employer".
  • The company denied that Lee was a "worker": he was at the time the controlling shareholder and governing director. On a Case Stated, the New Zealand Court of Appeal (North J., 18 Dec 1958) answered No: the powers were delegated to him for life; "he became in effect both employer and worker… the two offices are clearly incompatible. There could exist no power of control and therefore the relationship of master-servant was not created." The widow appealed to the Privy Council.
② Issues
  1. Was the deceased a "worker" — i.e. had he entered into or worked under a contract of service with an employer — within the Workers' Compensation Act, 1922?
  2. Can one person be, in relation to the same company, both governing director/controlling shareholder (the "employer" side) and servant (the "employee" side) — is such duality of capacity legally possible?
  3. Does the contract of service satisfy the requirement of a right of control in the employer?
③ Arguments — for the widow (appellant)
  • The company is a separate legal entity; "it was never suggested (nor could it reasonably have been suggested) that the respondent company was a sham or a mere simulacrum" — real companies can make real contracts with their controllers.
  • Lee contracted as agent of the company to employ himself; a director may serve his company (Fowler v. Commercial Timber Co.); the contract in IRC v. Sansom (sale of business to Sansom's own company, he governing director) was upheld as the company's.
  • What matters is the company's right of control, which the company possessed; capacity (director/employee) is not the same as personality — Salomon itself made such dual relations possible.
③ Arguments — for the company (as the NZ CA held)
  • A contract of service needs two parties and a master's power of control; Lee had the whole government and control for life; "on him lay the duty both of giving orders and obeying them"; there was no one to control him, so no master-servant relation.
  • The offices of governing director and servant are incompatible; the company had no independent will to employ, direct or dismiss him; he would in effect be contracting with himself.
④ Observations of the Court (Privy Council — Lord Morris of Borth-y-Gest)
  • "The relationship came about because the deceased, as one legal person, was willing to work for and to make a contract with the respondent company which was another legal entity. A contractual relationship could only exist on the basis that there was consensus between two contracting parties."
  • "It is well established that the mere fact that someone is a director of a company is not an impediment to his entering into a contract to serve the company." Quoting Salomon (Halsbury): "If it was a real thing; if it had a legal existence… it is impossible to deny the validity of the transactions into which it has entered"; and Macnaghten: the nickname "one man company" "does not help one much in the way of argument".
  • "If… the deceased was a party to a contract of service with the respondent company, then the respondent company had a right of control. The manner of its exercise would not affect or diminish the right to its exercise.… Just as the respondent company and the deceased were separate legal entities so as to permit of contractual relations being established between them, so also were they separate legal entities so as to enable the respondent company to give an order to the deceased."
  • Approving IRC v. Sansom (Younger L.J.) — the contracts of such a company are "ex facie regular… regarded as the company's" — and Fowler v. Commercial Timber Co. (Scruton L.J. on the two positions of a managing director).
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed; the question answered in the affirmative: Lee was a "worker" within s. 2; he had worked under a contract of service with the company; the accident arose out of and in the course of that employment; the widow was entitled to compensation under s. 3(1) (payable through the company's insurers).
  • "L.'s special position as governing director and principal shareholder did not preclude him from making on the company's behalf a contract of employment with himself, nor preclude him from entering into, or working in the capacity of servant under, a contract of service with the company."
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Capacity vs. personality: one natural person may act in two capacities (governing director/agent and servant) towards one separate legal person; the "magic of corporate personality enabled him to be master and servant at the same time" (Gower).
  • Salomon applied, not relaxed: Lee is the logical consequence of separate personality — and is therefore the best answer to "can a one-man company employ its owner?"
  • The test of employment: existence of the right of control in the employer; how it is exercised (even by the same natural person in another capacity) is immaterial.
  • Practical importance: companies may employ their controlling shareholders/directors; employee-welfare, tax (deductible remuneration), insurance, PF/ESI and compensation liabilities attach to the company — the very point that welfare statutes are designed to secure.

2.5 Limits of the doctrine — where the courts will NOT lift the veil

The theory "cannot be pushed to unnatural limits" — but equally, in several situations the courts insisted on keeping the veil intact, and you must be able to cite the negative side:

  • No veil-lifting at the shareholder's request: Kondoli Tea (need duty — transfer is transfer); Premlata Bhatia v. UOI (2004) — a licencee transferred premises to her wholly-owned company without consent and pleaded that she and the company were one; the plea was rejected — the doctrine is a defence, not a weapon for members.
  • Landlord and tenant: a landlady's business carried on through her company did not give her personal "occupation" rights — Tunstall v. Steigmann (1962) 2 QB 593.
  • Tax — the member cannot identify with the company: Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT AIR 1955 SC 74 — dividend from a tea company is not "agricultural income" in the member's hands; CIT v. Associated Clothiers — sale to one's own company is a real sale.
  • Group entities remain distinct absent compelling circumstances: Freewheels; Industrial Development Corpn. of Orissa v. Regional PF Commissioner (2002) — holding company not the employer of subsidiary's workers; Polly Peck International plc (No. 3) (1996) — court looks at legal substance, not economic substance; Balwant Rai Saluja v. Air India Ltd. (2014) 9 SCC 407 (subsidiary with no control exercised by Air India); Alembic Glass Industries v. CCE (2002).
  • Race/restrictive covenant cases: People's Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder (1908) — transfer to a company of "coloured" members did not breach a covenant against transfer to coloured persons: "the corporation has a distinct existence… a stranger to its own members."

"Company is not a Citizen" — Citizenship, Fundamental Rights & the Shareholders' Rights Doctrine

STC v. CTO (1963) · TELCO v. State of Bihar (1965) · R.C. Cooper (1970) · Bennett Coleman (1972) — the four-case chain that every DU answer must run. (Citation note: the DU list quotes STC as "AIR 1963 SC 811"; the standard reporting is AIR 1963 SC 1811, (1963) 33 Comp Cas 1057, (1964) 4 SCR 99 — quote either, but be consistent.)

3.1 Why the question arises — two kinds of fundamental rights

Part III of the Constitution — two baskets

Rights of "citizens" onlyArt. 15, 16, 19, 29 — e.g. Art. 19(1)(a)–(g): speech & expression; assembly; association; movement; residence; profession/trade/business.
Rights of "any person" (citizen + foreigner + juristic person)Art. 14 (equality), 20 (protection in convictions), 21 (life & liberty), 22, 25, 26 (subject to text), 31 (repealed), etc.

"Citizen" is defined by Arts. 5–11 and the Citizenship Act, 1955 — and both contemplate only natural persons: an artificial person cannot be a citizen. The scheme of the Constitution (Part II) speaks of "citizens" — natural persons by birth, descent, registration etc.

🧪
Test yourself 7 Indians incorporate a company — is the company a "citizen"? No (it is an Indian national, and a company). 7 foreigners incorporate in India — the company is still an Indian company (and not a citizen). Citizenship of members never passes to the company.

Nationality vs. citizenship

Nationality of a company is determined by the place of incorporation; domicile/residence by the place of central management and control (De Beers Consolidated Mines v. Howe (1906) AC 455; Casque v. IRC (1940) 2 KB 80). A company cannot change its nationality as a natural person can.

Citizenship is a distinct, narrower concept: it is the political membership of the State, carrying fundamental rights (Art. 19) and duties. The Supreme Court held nationality and citizenship to be distinct and separate concepts — the word "citizen" means the same thing in Part II and in Art. 19; and an incorporated company can never be a citizen.

Consequence: a company can claim rights available to "persons" (Art. 14, 21, and — after Kharak Singh/Maneka jurisprudence — it may invoke Art. 21 through its officers) but cannot invoke Art. 19 on its own. The hardship is mitigated by: (i) the general protection of Arts. 14/19(1)(f)/(g)-type freedoms given to all persons within India except citizens-only rights; and (ii) the shareholder-rights doctrine (below).

3.2 Case 1 — State Trading Corporation of India Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer, AIR 1963 SC 811 — six-part brief

Extended brief with fuller quotations: Case 2 in the Case Library (§06).

① Facts
  • STC was a private company registered under the Companies Act, 1956, registered office New Delhi, formed by the Government of India for foreign trade; its shares stood in the names of the President of India and two Union Secretaries in their official capacities.
  • Sales tax was assessed against it; STC moved the Supreme Court under Art. 32 (writs against the CTO, Visakhapatnam and the State of Bihar) complaining of infringement of Art. 19(1)(f) and (g).
② Issue

Is an incorporated company a "citizen" under Part II of the Constitution and Art. 19, and can it invoke Art. 19(1)(f)/(g)?

③ Arguments — STC
  • Not defined in Part II → wide construction; an association of citizens, incorporated in India, is an Indian national — nationality = citizenship.
  • Otherwise whole areas of commerce would be unprotected by Part III.
③ Arguments — Revenue/State
  • Part II and the Citizenship Act, 1955 contemplate only natural persons (birth, descent, registration, naturalisation); a company cannot be a citizen.
  • Company's personality (Salomon) keeps members' character distinct; members held shares in official, not personal, capacity.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Majority: neither Part II nor the Citizenship Act confers citizenship on "any person other than a natural person"; "citizen" means the same thing in Part II and in Art. 19; nationality and citizenship are distinct concepts; an incorporated company is a person, not a citizen.
  • Hidayatullah J. (concurring): "…if all of them (the members) are citizens of India, the company does not become a citizen of India any more than, if all of them are married, the company would be a married person."
  • Reassurance: companies are protected as "persons" (Art. 14; Art. 265; Art. 301; Art. 300A) — and the Court was not invited to "tear the corporate veil"; the citizen-members' own rights are not lost.
⑤ Decision

The writ petitions were dismissed: STC is not a citizen and cannot invoke Art. 19 (the question whether a one-man/all-citizen company's member could sue was expressly left open).

⑥ Principle evolved

A company is not a citizen. It has personality, nationality (place of incorporation) and residence (place of central management and control — De Beers), but no citizenship and no Art. 19 rights. Classification of rights: citizens-only (Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29) vs. all-persons (Arts. 14, 20, 21, 25, 26) — companies claim only the latter directly. Chain-link 1 of 4 (STC → TELCO → Cooper → Bennett Coleman).

3.3 Case 2 — Tata Engineering & Locomotive Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar, AIR 1965 SC 40 — six-part brief

① Facts
  • TELCO, registered under the Companies Act, 1913 (head office Bombay), manufactured trucks/bus chassis and spare parts at Jamshedpur (Bihar).
  • Bihar levied sales tax; TELCO filed a writ under Art. 32 claiming violation of Art. 19(1)(f) and (g), inviting the court — as the company is not a citizen — to lift the corporate veil and protect the citizens behind it.
② Issue

Can the court lift the veil of incorporation to allow a company to claim the citizens' rights under Art. 19 on behalf of its shareholders?

③ Arguments — TELCO (N.A. Palkhivala)
  • Citizens may exercise Art. 19(1)(f)/(g) through the medium of a company; otherwise the State could do indirectly what it cannot do directly.
  • The court should disclose the "real facts behind the corporate entity" as American and Indian cases do.
③ Arguments — State of Bihar
  • STC is conclusive: no citizenship, no Art. 19.
  • The veil is a weapon against abuse (fraud, evasion), not a shield for the company to claim what the law denied it; shareholders may sue in their own right but are not parties here.
④ Observations of the Court
  • “This Court said that a company was distinct and separate entity from shareholders” (as Bennett Coleman summarises): “the corporate veil… could be lifted in cases where the company is charged with trading with the enemy or perpetrating fraud on the Revenue authorities.”
  • The court refused to disregard the separate existence for the company's own benefit: Art. 19 rights cannot be asserted by the company either directly or through identification with the shareholders; the shareholders are free to assert their own rights.
⑤ Decision

The company's petition dismissed — the veil is not lifted to enable a company to claim citizens' fundamental rights; the shareholders' position was left open (resolved later in Cooper and Bennett Coleman).

⑥ Principle evolved

The negative rule: veil-lifting is a defensive instrument against abuse, not an offensive instrument to confer rights on the company. “What the company cannot do directly, it cannot do indirectly.” Chain-link 2 of 4 — and the seed of the later distinction between lifting in favour of citizens (Cooper/Bennett) and lifting against wrongdoers (Gilford/Daimler/Dinshaw).

3.4 Case 3 — R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case), (1970) 3 SCR 530 — six-part brief

① Facts
  • The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance/Act, 1969 nationalised 14 major banks (deposits above ₹50 crore); under Schedule II compensation was to be decided by a Tribunal but paid only after 10 years.
  • Rustom Cavasjee Cooper — shareholder, director and depositor in the Central Bank of India and Bank of Baroda — filed under Art. 32, alleging violation of Arts. 14, 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g) and 31(2). The Union contended he had no locus standi.
② Issues
  1. Can a shareholder/director/depositor challenge an Act affecting the company's undertaking? What is the test of “impairment of the shareholder's rights”?
  2. On merits: did the Act offend Arts. 19(1)(f)/(g), 14, 31(2)?
③ Arguments — Cooper
  • His bundle of shareholder rights (dividends, vote, surplus, carrying on business through the agency of the company) was destroyed; he suffered direct financial loss.
  • The compensation was illusory; the classification irrational; the Act beyond competence.
③ Arguments — Union of India
  • The undertaking belonged to the companies, not shareholders; a shareholder has no interest in company property; companies themselves cannot invoke Art. 19 (STC/TELCO).
  • Nationalisation was a legislative policy measure in public interest, not for judicial substitution.
④ Observations of the Court (Shah J.)
  • The locus test: “A measure… may impair the rights of the company alone… or of the shareholders as well as of the company. Jurisdiction of the Court to grant relief cannot be denied, when by State action the rights of the individual shareholder are impaired, if that action impairs the rights of the company as well.”
  • The famous sentence: “The test in determining whether the shareholder's right is impaired is not formal; it is essentially qualitative; if the State action impairs the right of the shareholders as well as of the company, the Court will not, concentrating merely upon the technical operation of the action, deny itself jurisdiction to grant relief.”
  • Direct-operation test: what matters is the effect of the law on the right, not the object of the authority or the form of action (adopted in Sakal and Bennett Coleman).
  • Merits: the compensation clause (10-year postponement; no just basis) did not provide a just equivalent; the Act infringed Art. 19(1)(f)/(g) and 31(2). (A.N. Ray J. dissented — 10:1.)
⑤ Decision
  • Locus standi upheld (qualitative test); the petition went to the merits, where the Act was struck down as void for infringing Arts. 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g), 14 and 31(2) (the measure was later re-enacted after changes).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Shareholder standing — qualitative test: a shareholder may sue where the State action impairs his rights as well as the company's; the test is of substance, not form.
  • “The fundamental rights of shareholders as citizens are not lost when they associate to form a company” (as Bennett Coleman later phrased it) — the bridge between TELCO's refusal and Bennett Coleman's relief. Chain-link 3 of 4.

3.5 Case 4 — Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India, (1972) 2 SCC 788 — six-part brief: the veil lifted FOR the shareholders

① Facts
  • The Newsprint Import Policy 1972–73 (under the Import Control Order, 1955, itself under ss. 3–4-A, Imports and Exports Control Act, 1947) and the Newsprint Control Order, 1962 (under s. 3, Essential Commodities Act, 1955) were challenged as violating Art. 19(1)(a) and Art. 14.
  • Four features attacked: (i) no new paper/edition by a common-ownership unit even within quota; (ii) maximum 10 pages with no adjustment between circulation and pages; (iii) no interchangeability of quota between papers/editions of a unit; (iv) 20% page increase up to 10 pages only for papers below 10 pages.
  • Petitioners were companies joined with citizens: shareholders, editors, printers, publishers and readers (Bennett Coleman group: company + shareholder + reader + 3 editors; Hindustan Times: shareholder-Deputy Director, Deputy Editor, printer-publisher, reader; Express: company + Chief Editor; The Hindu: shareholder, Managing Editor, publisher). The Additional Solicitor-General raised two demurrers: (i) companies cannot invoke fundamental rights; (ii) Art. 358 bars the challenge.
② Issues
  1. Can shareholders, editors, printers, publishers invoke Art. 19(1)(a) where the State action impairs the freedom of the press exercised through newspaper companies?
  2. Does the presence of the company among the petitioners bar relief — i.e. when can the veil be lifted in favour of the persons behind the company?
  3. Are the page ceiling, growth prohibition and non-interchangeability proportionate under Art. 19(2)? Is the policy in pith and substance a newspaper-control order?
③ Arguments — petitioners
  • “The individual rights of freedom of speech and expression of editors, directors and shareholders are all exercised through their newspapers through which they speak… The shareholders speak through their editors.” Restraints on pages, circulation, advertisements = restraints on propagation, publication, circulation under Art. 19(1)(a).
  • The effect/direct operation test (Cooper): the object and form are immaterial; the policy is a newspaper-control order in the guise of import control; Express Newspapers and Sakal Papers show the Court's settled practice.
③ Arguments — Union
  • Demurrers: companies are not citizens (STC/TELCO — cf. Mukherjea J.'s dissent in Chiranjit Lal); Art. 358 suspends Art. 19.
  • Merits: a legitimate import-control measure for a scarce essential (newsprint) and textbook paper; restrictions reasonable and temporary; no unlimited right to expand circulation at national cost.
④ Observations of the Court (Ray J. for the majority; Beg J. concurring; Mathew J. separate)
  • The STC/TELCO summary: “This Court in State Trading Corporation… and Tata Engineering… expressed the view that a corporation was not a citizen… In the State Trading Corporation case the Court was not invited to ‘tear the corporate veil’. In the Tata Engineering case this Court said that a company was distinct and separate entity from shareholders. The corporate veil it was said could be lifted in cases where the company is charged with trading with the enemy or perpetrating fraud on the Revenue authorities.”
  • The veil lifted for the shareholders: “The Bank Nationalisation case has established the view that the fundamental rights of shareholders as citizens are not lost when they associate to form a company… The shareholders' rights are equally and necessarily affected if the rights of the company are affected. The rights of shareholders with regard to Article 19(1)(a) are projected and manifested by the newspapers owned and controlled by the shareholders through the medium of the corporation.”
  • Standing: “The fact that the companies are the petitioners does not prevent this Court from giving relief to the shareholders, editors, printers who have asked for protection of their fundamental rights… The presence of the company is on the same ruling not a bar to the grant of relief.”
  • Pith and substance: “The Newsprint Control Policy is found to be newspaper control order in the guise of framing an Import Control Policy for newsprint”; the machinery of import control “cannot be utilised to curb or control circulation of growth or freedom of newspapers in India”.
  • Disproportionate classification: 28 of 35 newspapers above 10 pages benefited; 7 big dailies were hit — no intelligible differentia; freedom to publish pages/circulate is “an integral part of the freedom of speech and expression”.
⑤ Decision
  • The Newsprint Policy 1972–73 (10-page ceiling, growth prohibition, non-interchangeability) struck down for violating Art. 19(1)(a) (and Art. 14), not saved by Art. 19(2); relief granted to the companies jointly with shareholders/editors/printers.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Shareholders' rights doctrine (complete): citizens do not lose Art. 19 rights by associating into a company; where State action hits the company and thereby equally and necessarily the shareholders, the court lifts the veil to protect the shareholders' rights “projected and manifested through the corporation”.
  • Standing mechanics: company + citizen member/editor = maintainable (Godhra Electricity (1975) follows); the STC/TELCO rule is untouched — the company still cannot claim as a citizen.
  • Freedom of the press: every restriction on pages, circulation, quota and newsprint is tested by direct effect, and pith-and-substance prevents use of import control as press control. Chain-link 4 of 4.

3.6 The synthesis — a decision tree the examiner loves

Other cases that complete the picture

  • Chiranjit Lal Chowdhury v. UOI AIR 1951 SC 41 — majority: a shareholder cannot sue for infringement of the company's rights (Mukherjea J.'s dissent foreshadowed Cooper).
  • Express Newspapers (P) Ltd. v. UOI (1958) & Sakal Papers (P) Ltd. v. UOI (1962) — relief granted to newspaper companies and their shareholders/editors on Art. 19(1)(a); the Supreme Court's consistent practice.
  • Godhra Electricity Co. Ltd. v. State of Gujarat (1975) 1 SCC 199 — a company gets standing by impleading a shareholder.
  • Hyderabad (Sind) Electric Supply Co. v. UOI AIR 1959 Punj. 199 — a change in the shareholders' nationality does not change the company's nationality.
  • Residence / domicile: De Beers — "A company resides where its real business is carried on… where the central management and control actually resides."

Lifting the Corporate Veil — Statutory & Judicial Grounds

Meaning · who may plead it · classification · every statutory ground (Companies Act 2013 + other statutes) · every judicial ground with cases · statutory exceptions to limited liability.

4.1 Concept, metaphor and terminology

Definition

Lifting (piercing/cracking open) the corporate veil means disregarding the corporate entity and paying regard, instead, to the individual members/persons behind the "smoke-screen". The court either statute itself contemplates the lifting, or the corporate personality is being misused for an unlawful or improper purpose (tax evasion, fraud, avoidance of welfare legislation). The fundamental principle may be disregarded in the interest of justice.

Synonyms used by judges: piercing the veil cracking open the corporate shell disrespecting the separate corporate personality tearing the veil — and the three techniques: penetration (ignore the company, fix liability on members), extension (treat a group as one entity), ignorance (non-recognition of the company at all).

Foundation — Sec. 9, not its negation

Lifting the veil presupposes the separate entity: there must be a veil to lift. Therefore:

  • The doctrine does not invalidate the company — after the veil is lifted, the company remains a perfectly valid company.
  • It requires strong reasons: "adherence to the Salomon principle will not be doggedly followed where this would cause an unjust result" (Avtar Singh). The matter is largely in the discretion of the court, governed by "the underlying social, economic and moral factors as they operate in and through the corporation".
  • The Supreme Court: "It is the duty of the court, in every case where ingenuity is expended to avoid taxing and welfare legislation, to get behind the smoke-screen and discover the true state of affairs. The court is not to be satisfied with form and leave alone the substance of a transaction." — Workmen v. Associated Rubber Industry Ltd.

4.2 Who can plead the doctrine? (the "standing" rules)

✅ Who CAN

  • Creditors, revenue authorities, workmen, the State — those who are defrauded or prejudiced by the misuse of the corporate form;
  • Third parties dealing with the company, where the company is a sham device;
  • Courts suo motu, in the interest of justice, and the statute itself (statutory exceptions).

The doctrine operates in defence proceedings: it is raised against a person who relies on the company's separate personality as a shield to cover his wrongdoing.

❌ Who CANNOT

  • Shareholders cannot ask for the veil to be lifted for their own benefit — Kondoli Tea Co., In re (they wanted the transfer duty exemption); Premlata Bhatia v. UOI (2004) (licencee who transferred premises to her own company without consent: "If the doctrine is applied, only she will be found" — rejected);
  • Members cannot claim to be economically identical with the company where this harms revenue — Bacha F. Guzdar; CIT v. Associated Clothiers;
  • Persons who created the sham cannot complain;
  • No lifting in cases where the company's separate existence is legally required to be maintained (e.g. licensing, tenancy), absent fraud.

4.5 Judicial ground ① — Determination of the true character of a company (enemy character)

📌 Full six-part brief: Case 6 — Daimler in §06 · companion contrast: People's Pleasure Park below.

A company is an artificial person: it has no mind or conscience and can be neither loyal nor disloyal, neither friend nor enemy. But in times of war the court may examine the character of the persons in de facto control — if they reside in, or act under the control of, an enemy country, the company itself will be treated as an enemy company, and it cannot sue or be paid, because that would amount to trading with the enemy.

① Facts
  • Continental Tyre (G.B.) Ltd. — registered in England — marketed tyres made by a German parent; 23,398 of 25,000 shares and the three directorships were held by Germans resident in Hanover; the secretary was a naturalised German.
  • After war broke out the English company sued Daimler for £5,605 16s. and sought leave to sign final judgment; Daimler pleaded the company was in truth an alien enemy and the action incompetent under the Trading with the Enemy Act, 1914.
② Issues
  • Does an English-registered company whose shares and directors are enemy-controlled assume enemy character in wartime?
  • Was the secretary authorised to sue and give a valid discharge after the outbreak of war?
③ Arguments — Daimler (appellant)
  • “The names and the incorporation were but the machinery by which the purpose (giving moneys to the enemy) would be accomplished” (Halsbury).
  • Trading with the enemy is prohibited in all its forms, direct or indirect; the prohibition cannot be evaded by a corporate screen.
  • No authority to sue: enemy directors cannot act; the secretary had “not a scrap of writing” of authority.
③ Arguments — Continental (respondent)
  • It was “an English company, being registered at Somerset House”; the proclamation said “enemy character attaches only to those incorporated in an enemy country”.
  • The Act itself provided for inspection and controller of enemy-shareholder companies — Parliament had not made them enemies.
  • The company cannot be identified with its shareholders (Macnaghten in Janson); Lush J. had found the secretary trustworthy.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Halsbury L.C.: “when the object to be obtained is unlawful the indirectness of the means… will not get rid of the unlawfulness” — “the mere machinery to do an illegal act will not purge its illegality — fraus circuitu non purgatur”; the company is “a partnership in all that constitutes a partnership except the names”; it is not “a live thing” capable of loyalty or disloyalty.
  • Lord Atkinson: the secretary’s authority was unproved — burden on the respondent; he declined on that ground to identify the entity with shareholders. Lord Shaw concurred in the result (no authority to sue) but thought English incorporation excluded enemy character.
  • Received ratio (majority per Lord Parker): a company may assume an enemy character where the persons in de facto control are resident in, or act under the control of, the enemy.
⑤ Decision
  • The remaining appeal was allowed: the suit was dismissed as incompetent — no authority to sue could lawfully be exercised for the company.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ①: courts look behind incorporation to the real character of the company — nationality, residence, and in war, enemy character derived from de facto control.
  • Indirectness is no defence to an unlawful object; the corporate form cannot legalise what the law forbids.
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 6 in §06.
People's Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder (1908) 109 Va. 439 — the contrast Land was conveyed subject to a covenant not to sell to coloured persons; the transferee sold to a company whose members were exclusively negroes. The court refused to lift the veil: "the corporation has a distinct existence — an existence separate from that of its shareholders"; members individually or collectively are not the corporation. Because there was no danger to public interest, the veil stayed.

Also on this ground: Sovfracht v. Van Udens Scheepvaart (1943) AC 203 (HL) (enemy character again); Kuenigl v. Donnersmarck (1955); New Horizons Ltd. v. UOI AIR 1994 Del. 126 — a blacklisted contractor cannot hide his disqualification by tendering through a company (reversed on facts in New Horizons Ltd. v. UOI (1995) 1 SCC 478, which used the veil to see the constituents' experience). Licensing/disqualification cases show the modern use of this ground.

4.6 Judicial ground ②— Prevention of fraud or improper conduct (sham / cloak)

📌 Full six-part briefs: Case 11 — Gilford Motor v. Horne and Case 12 — Subhra Mukherjee in §06.

"The corporate entity is wholly incapable of being strained to an illegal or fraudulent purpose. The courts will refuse to uphold the separate existence of the company where it is formed to defeat or circumvent law, to defraud creditors or to avoid legal obligations." — Avtar Singh. This is the widest judicial ground: fraud, misrepresentation, diversion of funds, evasion of contractual or statutory obligations.

① Facts
  • Horne, ex-joint managing director of Gilford Motors, was bound by clause 9 not to solicit the company’s customers during/after service or compete within 3 miles for 5 years.
  • Fourteen days before the covenant’s protective period ended he incorporated “J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd.” — shares 999 to his wife, 1 to an employee; the other director his son; run from his own address; the company at once circularised Gilford’s customers and undercut Gilford.
② Issues
  • Was clause 9 a valid and reasonable covenant (and not released by the Nov 1931 settlement)?
  • Could the injunction bind the company — a separate person that never covenanted — if it was Horne’s cloak?
③ Arguments — Gilford Motor Co.
  • The covenant is definite in date and necessary to protect Gilford’s connection; solicitation is admitted both before and after incorporation.
  • Farwell J.: the company was “the channel through which the defendant Horne was carrying on his business”; the company’s only purpose was to let him breach clause 9 — a cloak, per Lindley L.J. in Smith v. Hancock.
  • The Nov 1931 agreement dealt with term-shortening and compensation only — no release of clause 9.
③ Arguments — Horne & the company
  • Covenant in restraint of trade is prima facie void; too wide in area/time; “casual customers” outside it.
  • The company is a separate legal entity; it never covenanted; the injunction cannot run against an innocent separate person.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Hanworth M.R.: “I am quite satisfied that this company was formed as a device, a stratagem, in order to mask the effective carrying on of a business of Mr. E.B. Horne… under what is a cloak or a sham”.
  • “I do hold that the company was ‘a mere cloak or sham’; I do hold that it was a mere device for enabling Mr. E.B. Horne to continue to commit breaches of clause 9, and under those circumstances the injunction must go against both defendants.”
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed — injunction against both Horne and J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd., with costs.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ②: a company created as a “cloak or sham”, a mere device to evade an existing legal obligation, is disregarded — relief runs against the company itself.
  • Separate personality protects honest users; it does not shelter breaches of covenant/statute committed “through” the company.
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 11 in §06.
Jones v. Lipman (1962) 1 WLR 832 Lipman agreed to sell land to Jones for £5,250, changed his mind, and — to avoid specific performance — sold the land to a company formed for that very purpose (he controlled it; the company was a "mask"). Held: the court ordered the company itself to transfer the land to Jones — the corporate form was used as a device to defeat a legal obligation; the veil was lifted and the decree operated on the company.
① Facts
  • M/s Nichitpur Coal Co. (P) Ltd. (Serial No. 133, Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act, 1973) “sold” its bungalow-land for ₹5,000/₹7,000 to the wives of its directors (the directors were real brothers; the wives had no income) by an antedated resolution of 21-9-1970, receipt of 30-12-1970 and sale deed of 20-3-1972 — months before vesting on 1-5-1973.
② Issues
  • Was the sale bona fide and genuine, or sham/bogus?
  • If sham: did the property vest in the Centre under s. 3(1)?
  • May the court pierce the veil to see who were “the real parties to the sale”?
③ Arguments — Appellants (the wives)
  • Resolution, receipt and registered sale deed are all proved; no rebuttal evidence; the company is a separate legal entity — Salomon repeated; husband–wife relation and price are irrelevant.
③ Arguments — BCCL (respondent)
  • Antedated resolution; unexplained raise of price ₹5,000→₹7,000; unregistered agreement; no possession/exercise of ownership till the suit; connections obtained during the suit; property maintained by the company for the directors till vesting — a transaction to defeat vesting.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Quadri J.: the conclusion that the resolution was an antedated document “appears to be irresistible” on intrinsic evidence; the wives had no independent source of income, “which cannot be ignored altogether as irrelevant”.
  • “Lifting the veil of incorporation… is an equally settled position of law… the court will be justified in piercing the veil of incorporation to ascertain the true nature of the transaction — who were the real parties to the sale… behind the façade of separate entity of the company.”
  • On burden: genuineness must first be proved by the parties asserting it; if intrinsic evidence shows it is not bona fide, “it is unnecessary for the court to find out whether the respondent has led any evidence to show that the transaction is sham”.
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal dismissed — the sale conferred no title; the property vested in the Central Government under s. 3(1) (supported by Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. v. Madanlal Agrawal).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ② (Indian application): family transactions behind the corporate façade, timed to defeat statutory vesting/nationalisation, are shams — the veil is pierced to identify the real parties.
  • Sham markers: family relationship, no independent income, antedated docs, unexplained price changes, unregistered agreement, non-exercise of ownership.
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 12 in §06.
More on this ground PNB Finance Ltd. v. Shital Prasad Jain (1981) — companies created only to hoodwink the lending company; their assets attached. DDA v. Skipper Construction Co. (1997) 11 SCC 430 — veil lifted to expose persons using the company to defraud plot/flat allottees; human agents not absolved. Jai Narain Parasrampuria v. Pushpa Devi Saraf (2006) 7 SCC 756 — promoters estopped from denying the company's ownership. Barakot Ltd. v. Epiettee Ltd. (1997) — loan agreement pre-dating incorporation dismissed (res judicata).

4.7 Judicial ground ③— Prevention of tax evasion / benefit of the revenue

📌 Full six-part briefs: Case 8 — Dinshaw Petit and Case 9 — CIT v. Meenakshi Mills in §06.

"The court has the power to disregard the corporate entity if it is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligation… in certain exceptional cases the Court is entitled to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade."— CIT v. Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 819 (the DU casebook's own formulation)
① Facts
  • Assessed on ₹11,35,302; disputed ₹3,90,804 (interest + dividends) which he claimed belonged to four “family” companies (Petit Ltd. etc.) he formed in 1921 — all shares but ₹30-worth in his name/those of his three subordinates; the dividends were credited to the companies and the same day debited to him as “loans”; no interest, no repayment, no resolutions, no dividends declared.
② Issues
  • Were the transfers/trusts and the “loans” genuine — or, in substance, withdrawals of income disguised as loans?
  • Was the income assessable to super-tax in his hands under the IT Act, 1922?
③ Arguments — Assessee (Sir Dinshaw Petit)
  • He is only a trustee; the companies may lawfully accumulate and lend; they paid tax on the interest — estoppel; and (alternatively) if not loans, the sums were the companies’ money wrongly received by him, hence not taxable.
③ Arguments — Revenue
  • The dispositions are a sham; the companies do no business and are only his agents; the “loans” are not genuine — the sums are his income (ss. 2(15), 3, 6, 12, 55, 56, 58).
④ Observations of the Court
  • Kemp J.: the companies were formed “purely and simply as a means of avoiding super-tax… created… as legal entities to ostensibly receive the dividends and interests and to hand them over to the assessee as pretended loans”.
  • Marten C.J.: though the presumption of genuineness favours the taxpayer, here “we have not got a company carrying on an open business like a shop”; the three shareholders were subordinates, unpaid preference dividends, no protest; “the same day debited to the assessee by way of loan”; paying tax on the interest “was much cheaper for the assessee than paying super-tax on the dividends themselves”; “plausible paper schemes under the Companies Acts have not stood the test of examination in a Court of Law”; no estoppel against the Crown.
⑤ Decision
  • Questions answered: (1) affirmative — assessable; (2) & (3) negative; (4) the “loans” were “not genuine loans but merely withdrawals of income disguised as loans”.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ③: companies created purely as tax-avoidance screens are disregarded; paper loans (credit-and-debit entries) from closely-held companies are the shareholder’s income.
  • Genuineness is presumed but tested by substance; prior acceptance by the revenue creates no estoppel. (Now codified: IT Act ss. 2(22)(e), 179, GAAR.)
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 8 in §06.
Apthorpe v. Peter Schoenhofen Brewing Co. (1899) 4 TC 41 — agency case Aliens could not hold land in New York; an English company bought the business and assets of a New York brewery; the American company was kept alive only to hold the land, the business being run by the English company. Held: the American company was the English company's agent; the whole profit was taxable as the English company's income — despite the Salomon argument. Followed in Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Llewellin (1957) 1 WLR 464 (subsidiary as the parent's European trading agency).
① Facts
  • Three public companies (cotton yarn, Pudukottai) had sale proceeds deposited and loans taken against those deposits, the group’s common director sitting on the bank’s board; the interest was claimed as deductible, and the department invoked s. 42(1), IT Act 1922, treating the arrangement as a colourable/indirect device.
② Issues
  • Can the authorities disregard the corporate entity for tax purposes, and on what conditions?
  • Did s. 42(1) tax the interest, and was the Tribunal’s finding sustainable in law?
③ Arguments — Assessee company
  • Genuine banking transactions at arm’s length; separate legal entities; the Tribunal’s findings of fact are final; earlier years accepted.
③ Arguments — Revenue
  • The deposit–loan structure was circular: real outgo none, deduction manufactured; s. 42(1) catches income arising “directly or indirectly”; the veil should be lifted to see the reality.
④ Observations of the Court
  • The classic formulation: “In certain exceptional cases the Court is entitled to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade. For example… the Court has power to disregard the corporate entity if it is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligation” (with Apthorpe and Firestone as illustrations of subsidiaries as mere agencies).
  • But the power is exceptional: the Tribunal’s finding (genuine transaction; s. 42(1) not attracted) was a finding of fact not shown perverse — the assessee succeeded on the facts.
⑤ Decision
  • Commissioner’s appeal dismissed — s. 42(1) not applied — yet the doctrine was affirmed and is the standard citation for the “economic realities” test (developed in McDowell (1985) and Vodafone (2012)).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ③ (formulation): veil may be lifted where the entity is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligation, but only in exceptional cases; the revenue must show the device — genuine commercial structures are respected.
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 9 in §06.
The reverse side — members cannot use the veil either Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT AIR 1955 SC 74: a shareholder's dividend from a tea company is not "agricultural income" (even though 60% of the company's income was) — the company's income and the shareholder's income are different legal persons' incomes. CIT v. Associated Clothiers Ltd. AIR 1963 Cal. 629: selling property to one's own company is a real commercial sale; the profit is assessable. Juggilal Kamlapat v. 4.8 Judicial ground ④— Avoidance of welfare legislation

📌 Full six-part brief: Case 10 — Workmen v. Associated Rubber in §06.

"It is the duty of the court, in every case where ingenuity is expended to avoid taxing and welfare legislation, to get behind the smoke-screen and discover the true state of affairs. The court is not to be satisfied with form and leave alone the substance of a transaction."— Workmen v. Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. (1985) 4 SCC 114 (SC, O. Chinnappa Reddy J.)
① Facts
  • A.R.I. Ltd. (Bhavnagar) held INARCO shares (₹4,50,000) whose dividends were included in its P&L and in the gross profits for bonus. In 1968 it transferred the shares to Aril Holdings Ltd. — a wholly-owned subsidiary with no other capital, no business, no income except those dividends. The dividends left A.R.I.’s accounts: bonus fell from 16% to 4% for 1969.
② Issues
  • Are the subsidiary’s dividends part of the parent’s gross profits under the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965?
  • Was the transfer a device to avoid bonus — and will the court disregard the subsidiary’s separate personality?
③ Arguments — Workmen
  • A shell subsidiary with no business or purpose save stripping income; the “facts speak for themselves”; welfare rights cannot be defeated by a corporate manoeuvre.
③ Arguments — Company
  • Distinct legal entities (Salomon); Aril’s profits are its own; no evidence of a device; the 1971 amalgamation shows reorganisation, not avoidance.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Chinnappa Reddy J.: “distinct legal entities… but that was not an end of the matter. It is the duty of the court, in every case where ingenuity is expended to avoid taxing and welfare legislation, to get behind the smoke-screen and discover the true state of affairs… not to be satisfied with form”.
  • “A new company… serving no purpose whatsoever except to reduce the gross profits… These facts speak for themselves.… It is such an obvious device that no further evidence, direct or circumstantial, is necessary.” (Adopting Meenakshi Mills and McDowell.)
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed: the INARCO dividend must be included in A.R.I.’s gross profits; the workmen are entitled to 16% bonus for 1969.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ④: the veil is lifted where corporate structures are used to evade welfare/tax legislation; substance over form; intent may be inferred where the structure serves no purpose except avoidance.
  • Group realities: a wholly-owned shell subsidiary is part of the parent for bonus/welfare purposes (cf. KCP, Pinn & Wheeler, Renusagar).
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 10 in §06.
Allied cases KCP Ltd. v. KCP Employees' Association AIR 1969 Mad. 370 — holding company and subsidiaries treated as one group for bonus. National Dock Labour Board v. Pinn & Wheeler Ltd. 1989 BCLC 647 — three companies at one dock shown to be one undertaking to avoid the Dock Labour Scheme. Chitra Kumar Basu v. Property Development Trust Ltd. (1988) 93 CWN 725 — evasion of welfare legislation. State of U.P. v. Renusagar Power Co. (1988) 4 SCC 59 — subsidiary generating power exclusively for the parent treated as one unit for excise, to prevent evasion of duty. Merchandise Transport Ltd. v. British Transport Commission — licences obtained through a subsidiary with no business of its own set aside.

Why this ground is the most-tested It unites Salomon (distinct entities), the veil (substance over form), welfare law (bonus) and a 10-line quotable judgment (Chinnappa Reddy J.). It was the December 2024 DU question 2 — see §07.

4.9 Judicial ground ⑤— Company as agent / surrogate / instrumentality of the State (government companies)

📌 Full six-part brief: Case 13 — Kapila Hingorani in §06 · companion contrast: Som Prakash Rekhi below.

A government company is not a department or extension of the State — its employees are not civil servants; prerogative writs do not issue against it (Praga Tools Corpn. v. C.A. Imanual (1969); Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union v. State of Bihar (1969)). But when the company is in substance an agent, surrogate or instrumentality of the State, the veil is lifted and the State's obligations — including fundamental rights of employees — bind it. The test is functional, not formal: "merely because a company… has functional and jural individuality for certain purposes, it does not necessarily follow that for the effective enforcement of fundamental rights we should not scan the real character of that entity."

Som Prakash Rekhi v. UOI (1981) 1 SCC 449 An employee of Burmah Shell (Oil Storage) was entitled to pension/provident fund; the undertaking was acquired and vested in Bharat Petroleum Corporation Ltd. (a government company) under the Burmah Shell (Acquisition of Undertakings in India) Act 1976; BPCL reduced his pension. The court (Krishna Iyer J.) looked behind the company: "This is the well-worn legal strategy for government to run economic enterprises… Law does not hoodwink itself; what is but a strategy cannot be used as a stratagem." Where the company was created to perform a public function with the State as "the true owner, the real operator… the effective collectorate", writ lies, and the veil is lifted for enforcement of rights. The corporate form only assures commercial flexibility — it does not insulate the State from accountability.
① Facts
  • Bihar government corporations — State-controlled instrumentalities — were unable to pay their employees’ salaries/dues; the employees (Kapila Hingorani and others) sought writs against the State, which pleaded the separate personality of the companies.
② Issues
  • Whether the State can be compelled to discharge the wage liabilities of its companies, or is the separate entity an absolute answer?
  • When is the veil lifted to fix State liability for a government company?
③ Arguments — Employees
  • The companies are the State’s creations and instrumentalities; the State controlled management, finance and appointment; the failure is the State’s own; Art. 21 (livelihood) and the State’s public obligations forbid hiding behind the legal fiction.
③ Arguments — State of Bihar
  • A government company is a separate legal entity; its employees are not State employees; statutory remedies (winding up) exist; no contract claim lie against the exchequer; fiscal autonomy must be respected (Praga Tools, Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union, A.K. Bindal).
④ Observations of the Court
  • The Court accepted the general rule — the State is not ordinarily liable for the company’s debts or contracts — but the veil is lifted where the corporation is the State’s instrumentality/alter ego and the separate personality is pleaded “to take shelter behind the legal fiction… while refusing to discharge its obligation to its employees” . The employees’ right to livelihood (Art. 21) and the State’s control of the corporations justified the directions.
  • Boundary (same period): A.K. Bindal v. UOI (2003) 5 SCC 163 — where the company is genuine and the State has not used it to evade responsibility, the veil is preserved.
⑤ Decision
  • Petitions allowed in part: the State was directed to ensure payment of the employees’ dues in a phased scheme, with consequential directions (priority to wages; revival/winding-up).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Ground ⑤ (constitutional): the veil of a government company can be lifted where it is the State’s surrogate and its personality would defeat justice, public policy or fundamental rights — the State cannot escape wage/public obligations through its creature (contrast: genuine independence → no lifting).
  • Read with Som Prakash Rekhi (1981): “Law does not hoodwink itself; what is but a strategy cannot be used as a stratagem.”
▶ Extended verbatim brief: Case 13 in §06.

4.11 Judicial ground ⑦ — Economic realities / group entities & the judge-made situational tests

Where a group of companies is used as a single economic unit to evade obligations, courts look at substance. Avtar Singh's four recurring situations: (i) partnership of two corporate entities with one acting as the alter ego; (ii) actual control by the parent of the subsidiary's affairs (subsidiary as branch/agent/trustee); (iii) the company's name being just a sham; (iv) two companies interlinked (common management, finance, operations).

  • State of U.P. v. Renusagar Power Co. (1988) 4 SCC 59 — subsidiary supplying power exclusively to parent = one entity for excise (avoidance of duty).
  • DHN Food Distributors Ltd. v. Tower Hamlets LBC (1976) 1 WLR 852 — wholly-owned subsidiary treated as part of the parent's business for compensation; contrasted with Woolfson v. Strathclyde Regional Council 1978 SLT 159 (no lifting where group structure was a matter of form and no injustice resulted).
  • Vodafone International Holdings BV v. UOI (2012) 6 SCC 613 — the current benchmark: the State can look through "colourable" devices; but a genuine, legally structured transaction (Hutchison → Vodafone transfer offshore) is not to be rewritten; "lifting is an exception and not the rule".
  • State of Karnataka v. Selvi J. Jayalalitha (2017) 201 Comp Cas 230 (SC) — separate entity subject to exception where the entity is a mere cloak or sham; company cannot conspire with its sole responsible person.
  • Park Bisleri (P) Ltd. v. CCE (2010) 14 SCC 378 — three interrelated companies' production clubbed for exemption limits; Bharat Steel Tubes v. IFCI (2011) 11 SCC 385.
  • Contrasts (veil preserved): Freewheels (India) Ltd. v. Veda Mittra AIR 1969 Del. 258 (52% subsidiary not the parent); Industrial Development Corpn. of Orissa v. Regional PF Commr. (2002) (holding company not the employer); Polly Peck International plc (No. 3) (1996) — courts look at legal, not economic, substance; Balwant Rai Saluja v. Air India Ltd. (2014) 9 SCC 407 (no control exercised → no liability); Alembic Glass Industries v. CCE (2002).

5.1 The classification tree

5.2 Public company — Sec. 2(71)

Definition (S. 2(71))

"Public company" means a company which is not a private company and includes a company which is a subsidiary of a public company.

Deemed public company (proviso): a company that is a subsidiary of a company not being a private company is deemed a public company for the purposes of the Act, even if its own articles continue to be those of a private company. (Hence the "public" tag travels down the chain.)

Incidents — minimum 7 members (S. 3(1)(a)); minimum 3 directors (S. 149(1)); may issue prospectus / invite public (but then must comply with Ss. 23–40); not less than two-thirds of the directors liable to retire by rotation (S. 152(6)); no restriction on transfer of shares; independent directors & committees where thresholds met; stricter deposits, loans, accounts regime.

Listed company (S. 2(52)) — a sub-species

A company having listed equity shares on a recognised stock exchange (or whose shares are listed). The listing brings SEBI (LODR) Regulations, disclosure and governance obligations. Distinguish: a public company need not be listed; a listed company is necessarily public.

Private company's requisites that a public company must observe

  • public cannot restrict transferability of shares;
  • cannot limit members to 200 — it is open-ended;
  • must comply with prospectus rules before inviting public.

5.3 Private company — Sec. 2(68)

Definition: "private company" means a company having a minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed (requirement deleted by the 2015 Amendment) and which by its articles:

① Restricts transferability restricts the right to transfer its shares (any restriction enabling directors to maintain the 200-member ceiling suffices; not required for a private company not limited by shares)
② Limits members to 200 exclusive of present/former employees; joint holders = one member; OPC excluded
③ Prohibits public invitation prohibits any invitation to the public to subscribe to shares/debentures, and any invitation or acceptance of deposits from persons other than members, directors or their relatives

Conditions precedent: minimum 2 members (S. 3(1)(b)); minimum 2 directors (S. 149(1)); must have articles (to contain the three restrictions); may commence business immediately on incorporation, being exempt from the prospectus/minimum-subscription regime that governs public issues.

🔁
Conversion
Private → Public: by special resolution deleting the s. 2(68) restrictions from the articles; from the date of alteration it becomes a public company and must comply with public-company requirements (3 directors, 7 members, etc.). Public → Private: special resolution to insert the s. 2(68) restrictions — but such alteration shall not have effect unless approved by the Tribunal (S. 14(1) proviso); a copy with the Tribunal's order must be filed with ROC within 15 days. Conversion never changes the company's identity — it is the same legal person (All India Reporter Ltd. v. Ramchandra AIR 1961 Bom. 292).

5.4 Holding company (S. 2(46)) & subsidiary company (S. 2(87))

The two definitions

S. 2(46): "holding company", in relation to one or more other companies, means a company of which such companies are subsidiary companies.

S. 2(87): "subsidiary company" in relation to any other company (the holding company) means a company in which the holding company —

  • (i) controls the composition of the Board of directors or
  • (ii) holds more than half in nominal value of its equity share capital — that is, controls more than half of the total voting power.

Board-composition control (Explanation): the holding company has the power to appoint or remove a majority of directors; it is deemed to have it where a person's appointment as director cannot be made without its support, or automatically follows on appointment as its director/manager, or the directors are its nominees or its subsidiaries' nominees.

Further rules of the game

  • Chain rule (Explanation to S. 2(87)): subsidiary of a subsidiary is a subsidiary of the ultimate holding company. Illustration in the section: B is a subsidiary of A; C of B; therefore C is a subsidiary of A; D (subsidiary of C) is a subsidiary of B, and consequently of A, and so on.
  • Nominee rule: shares held or power exercisable by a nominee of the holding company (or of its subsidiary) count for the test.
  • Disregarded shareholding (must quote in exam): shares held / power exercisable (a) in a fiduciary capacity; (b) under the provisions of debentures or a trust deed securing debentures; (c) by a lending company by way of security in the ordinary course of business — are ignored.
  • Layers: holding companies of prescribed classes cannot have subsidiaries beyond the prescribed number of layers (Rule — not more than 2 layers, excluding a layer of wholly-owned subsidiaries).
  • S. 19: a subsidiary cannot be a member of its holding company; allotment/transfer is void (exceptions: legal representative, trustee, pre-existing shareholding, amalgamation scheme).
  • S. 129(3): holding company must prepare consolidated financial statements of the whole group.
⚖️
Control ≠ merger of personality A 52% subsidiary (Freewheels (India) v. Veda Mittra), a wholly-owned subsidiary with its own business (Balwant Rai Saluja v. Air India), and a parent cannot be substituted as the employer of subsidiary workers (IDC of Orissa v. Regional PF Commissioner). The veil is lifted for a group only when the subsidiary is a mere branch/agent/trustee (Renusagar; DHN; KCP) or an obvious device (Associated Rubber).

5.5 Limited by shares · Guarantee · Unlimited — Secs. 2(22), 2(21), 2(92)

Company limited by shares — S. 2(22)

The liability of its members is limited by the memorandum to the amount, if any, unpaid on the shares respectively held by them. The overwhelming majority of companies — including every listed company — are of this kind. The member's liability is satisfied by paying the issue price; nothing more can be called from him (except in winding-up calls made under the Act, within the same limit).

Company limited by guarantee — S. 2(21)

Liability is limited by the memorandum to such amount as the members respectively undertake to contribute to the assets of the company in the event of its being wound up. Features:

  • May have no share capital (typical of clubs, associations, stock exchanges, charitable bodies);
  • If it has share capital, reduction rules apply (S. 66) and it cannot buy back its own shares (S. 67);
  • Articles must state the number of members with which it is registered;
  • S. 4(7): any provision in MOA/articles or resolution purporting to give a non-member a right to participate in divisible profits is void;
  • Membership transfer involves admission to the organisation, so the rights of the body to refuse are wider than in a share company (Narendra Kumar Agarwal v. Saroj Maloo (1995) 6 SCC 114);
  • Guarantee without share capital may also be formed as a Section 8 charitable company.

Unlimited company — S. 2(92)

"Unlimited company" means a company not having any limit on the liability of its members. Features:

  • Articles must state the number of members and, if it has share capital, the amount of share capital;
  • Members are liable like partners but creditors cannot sue them directly — the liability is realised through winding up (the liquidator calls on members to contribute — S. 333);
  • Need not have share capital; can reduce capital freely; can purchase its own shares (S. 67 doesn't apply);
  • May re-register as limited (S. 18) — existing debts/liabilities unaffected;
  • Rarely chosen now — suited to family enterprises where public confidence in unlimited credit matters.

5.6 Small company — Sec. 2(85)

Definition: a company, other than a public company, whose —

paid-up share capital does not exceed ₹4 crore (statutory base ₹50 lakh; raised by notification to ₹2 crore in 2018 and to ₹4 crore by the 2022 Rules)
or turnover as per the last profit & loss account does not exceed ₹40 crore (statutory base ₹2 crore; raised to ₹20 crore, then ₹40 crore by the 2022 Rules)

Proviso — exclusions: the definition does not apply to (i) a holding or subsidiary company; (ii) a company registered under Section 8 (charitable); (iii) a company/body corporate governed by a special Act.

Why it matters: small companies enjoy lighter compliance — one board meeting each half-year (S. 173(5)), audit-committee/CSR inapplicability, exemption from cash-flow statements, easier annual-return filing, less-rigorous board/committee requirements for private small companies.

5.7 One Person Company (OPC) — Sec. 2(62) read with S. 3(1)(c)

The concept

S. 2(62) "one person company" means a company which has only one person as a member. S. 3(1)(c) — a company may be formed for any lawful purpose by one person, where the company is to be a one person company, i.e., a private company. Introduced on the J.J. Irani Committee recommendation — India was among the first jurisdictions to allow a single-member limited-liability company.

Features: the member is a natural person (Rules; no minor); the OPC is a private company with separate personality and limited liability; it must designate a nominee.

The nominee mechanism

  • The memorandum must state the name of another person who, with his prior written consent filed with the ROC, shall become the member in the event of the member's death or incapacity to contract;
  • The nominee may withdraw consent; the member may substitute another nominee by notice; any change does not amount to alteration of the memorandum (2021 Amendment liberalised repeated changes);
  • The company must inform the ROC of the change;
  • On death/incapacity, the nominee becomes the member.
Governance & privileges One director suffices (S. 149(1)(a)); the member is deemed the first director; only one board meeting per half-year (gap ≥90 days) (S. 173(5)); S. 174 quorum doesn't apply; no AGM requirements of a general nature (exemptions under 2015/2021 notifications); relaxed compliance.
Conversion & checks Can convert to private/public company and vice-versa (S. 18 & Rules; the old mandatory conversion on capital/turnover thresholds was replaced by voluntary conversion from 2021); OPC cannot be a Section 8 company; only a natural person can be member; no OPC for charitable objects.
🧩
Connections to this unit An OPC is the modern statutory answer to the Salomon one-man company: the legislature now encourages what the House of Lords merely tolerated — while S. 3A still exposes members when a company is carried on below its minimum membership.

5.8 Government company — Sec. 2(45)

Definition (S. 2(45))

"Government company" means any company in which not less than fifty-one per cent of the paid-up share capital is held by — the Central Government, or any State Government(s), or partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments, and includes a company which is the subsidiary of a company so defined.

Tests: (i) shareholding threshold — 51%+ paid-up capital (50% exactly is not enough — TM Devassy v. Periyar Latex); (ii) ownership by government(s) in the defined combinations; (iii) subsidiary-chain inclusion. A takeover of management under IDRA does not make a company a government company (Kulbir Singh v. UOI (1997)).

Special provisions (Ss. 394–395 & audit)

  • Auditor appointed/re-appointed by the CAG (S. 139(5)); CAG may direct the manner of audit, order supplementary/test audit, and comment on the audit report (S. 143(5)–(7));
  • Annual report on the working and affairs of the company within 3 months of the AGM, laid before both Houses of Parliament (or the State Legislature) with the audit report and CAG comments (S. 394); applies even in liquidation; exemptions may be notified (S. 462);
  • Non-government "public sector" character: employees are not civil servants; but the company may be an instrumentality of the State under Art. 12 for writ purposes — see §4.9 (Som Prakash Rekhi; Kapila Hingorani; Mysore Paper Mills) — while Praga Tools; Heavy Engg. Mazdoor Union; A.K. Bindal; LIC v. Escorts hold otherwise on their facts.

5.9 Foreign company — Sec. 2(42) (read with Ss. 379–392)

The two-limb definition

"Foreign company" means any company or body corporate incorporated outside India which — (a) has a place of business in India, whether by itself or through an agent, physically or through electronic mode; and (b) conducts any business activity in India in any other manner.

Electronic mode (Rules) covers e-commerce, offers/invitations to Indian persons to subscribe to securities, financial settlements, web-based marketing, teleservices, etc. A share transfer/share registration office in India is a place of business.

Key point: a company incorporated outside India with only foreign shareholders but no Indian presence is not a "foreign company" under the Act; conversely a company incorporated in India with 100% foreign shareholders is an Indian company, not a foreign company.

Obligations (S. 380–392) and consequences

  • S. 380: file with the ROC (within 30 days of establishing its place of business) — certified copy of charter/constitution (with certified translation if not in English), address of registered/principal office abroad, list of directors & secretary, particulars of the person(s) authorised to accept service in India, address of the Indian office deemed principal place of business, particulars of opening/closing of earlier places of business, a declaration that none of the directors/representatives has been convicted or debarred, and prescribed information; notify changes (S. 380(3));
  • S. 381: file balance sheet & P&L in the prescribed form every calendar year;
  • S. 382: conspicuously exhibit its name and country of incorporation (English + regional language) outside every place of business, and state the same (with the fact of limited liability) on all business letters, bill-heads, notices, publications; failure does not invalidate contracts but (as the Act provides) affects the company's right to sue or claim set-off until compliance;
  • Prospectus (Ss. 390–391), service of process on the authorised person, and winding up provisions apply (Re Senator Hanseatische (1997) — jurisdiction from a UK business club).

5.10 Other classes you may be asked to name (and the body-corporate family)

Section 8 company — formed to promote commerce, art, science, religion, charity, sports, education, research, social welfare, environmental protection etc., with profits (if any) applied only to these objects and no dividend to members; requires special licence; exempted from "Ltd./Pvt. Ltd." suffix and from many provisions (e.g., S. 2(85) proviso).
Producer company (S. 378A) — a body corporate of primary producers (agriculture, horticulture, forestry, dairy, fishery etc.); a hybrid of co-operative ethos and company form; membership limited to active producers.
Associate company (S. 2(6)) — a company in which another has significant influence (≥20% of voting power or control of business decisions under an agreement) but which is not a subsidiary; includes joint ventures; relevant for S. 129(3) "subsidiary" explanation and S. 185/186 limits.
Dormant company (S. 455) — registered for future projects or holding assets/IP with no significant accounting transaction; may be declared dormant; benefits: reduced compliance.
Nidhi company (S. 406) — notified class of companies for mutual benefit (accepting deposits from and lending to members); regulated by Nidhi Rules, 2014.
Statutory company — incorporated by a special Act of Parliament/State (e.g., LIC, RBI are statutory corporations; FCI; SEBI) — governed by its own statute, not by the Companies Act.

🧷 Case 1 · Salomon v. Salomon & Co., Ltd. (1897) A.C. 22 (H.L.) — the foundation of corporate personality
(1897) AC 22 (HL)All ER Rep 33Separate legal entityFoundational / exam-must

Where it is treated in this file: doctrine §2.1 · consequences §2.2–2.3 · veil contrast §4.11 · PYQ model answer §7.

① Facts
  • Aron Salomon carried on for many years a prosperous leather-merchant and boot-manufacturing business at 113 High Street, London, trading as "A. Salomon & Co." The business was perfectly solvent — the liquidator himself admitted "that the business when transferred to the company was in a sound condition, and that there was a substantial surplus".
  • July 1892: to convert his business into a limited liability company he incorporated "Aron Salomon & Co., Ltd." under the Companies Act, 1862. The seven subscribers to the memorandum were Salomon, his wife, his daughter and his four sons — each subscribing for one share, purely to satisfy the statutory minimum of seven subscribers. Nominal capital: £40,000 in 40,000 shares of £1.
  • 20,007 shares were issued: Salomon took 20,001; each of the six family members took 1. Salomon and two of his sons were the directors (the two sons, "the managing director and the other director") — i.e. a "one-man company" in everything but form.
  • Sale of the business (agreements of 20 July and 2 August 1892): the business and goodwill were sold to the company for £38,782 — of which about £16,000 was payable; the company paid £6,000 in cash and issued £10,000 in debentures, the debentures being secured by a charge on the company's assets and ranking ahead of all unsecured creditors. The remaining 20,000 shares were allotted to Salomon.
  • Salomon later mortgaged the debentures to Edmund Broderip for £5,000; default having occurred, Broderip's solicitor obtained a winding-up order (October 1893) — the debentures were then re-issued to Broderip.
  • The failure was not Salomon's fault: the evidence of Emanuel Salomon (employed in the business for nearly 20 years) attributed the non-success and insolvency to "a succession of strikes in the boot trade", and "there is not a tittle of evidence tending to modify or contradict his statement" (Lord Watson).
  • State of affairs on winding up: assets realised about £6,000; claims — the secured debenture holder £10,000 (which would swallow the assets); unsecured creditors (other than Salomon) £7,733. The company (through its liquidator) therefore sued Salomon for (a) rescission of the July/August 1892 agreements, (b) cancellation of the debentures, (c) repayment of sums paid under those agreements, and (d) a lien/indemnity — alleging the price paid exceeded the real value by upwards of £8,200, that the arrangement was "a fraud upon the creditors", and that no independent board ever existed.
② Issues
  1. Was the company at law a separate person from Aron Salomon, or was it merely "the nominee and agent" of Salomon — i.e. "whether, in truth, that artificial creation of the legislature should be treated as a real thing" at all? (Lord Halsbury framed the question exactly so.)
  2. If it was a separate person, could the money-lending/security arrangement — £10,000 debentures issued to Salomon against the very business he had himself sold (he was secured creditor and shareholder in the same company) — stand, even though it left trade creditors unpaid on liquidation?
  3. Was the company justified in asking the court to declare Salomon bound to indemnify it against the creditors' claims (£7,733), on the footing that he was its agent and the real owner of the business?
  4. Was a false certificate (misdescription) — the debenture certificate stated that £30,000 of debentures had been issued when only £10,000 had been — fatal?
③ Arguments — for the Company (the liquidator; affirming courts below)
  • Salomon was the real owner; the company was "a mere nominee and agent" for him. Vaughan Williams J. below had said "the business was Salomon's business and no one else's" — "the company was the agent of the appellant". An agent is bound to indemnify its principal; Salomon therefore owed the company £7,733.
  • The seven subscribers were "dummies": his wife, daughter and sons each held one share; the company had "no board of directors... independent" — it was "a scheme to enable the appellant to carry on business in the name of the company with limited liability, contrary to the true intent and meaning of the Companies Act, 1862, and in fraud of creditors" (this is the very wording of the Court of Appeal's order, as reproduced in Lord Watson's speech).
  • The consideration of £38,782 was exorbitant (exceeding the real value by over £8,200); the issue of debentures to the vendor himself was colourable; the whole arrangement was "a device to defraud creditors" (Lindley L.J. in the CA).
  • Even the CA accepted the company's incorporation was not open to challenge — but urged that the court "look at the thing as a whole" and refuse effect to an arrangement whose "object... is to do the very thing which the legislature intended not to be done".
③ Arguments — for Mr. Salomon (appellant)
  • Every requirement of the Companies Act, 1862 had been complied with — memorandum, articles, certificate of incorporation, registration; the Act "required seven members", and "has said that one share is sufficient to constitute a shareholder, though the shares may be... held by a member who... has no interest except in the shares" (Halsbury).
  • The company was not an agent: "it is impossible to say at the same time that there is a company and there is not a company; that the company is a real person and nothing to be an agent at all" (Halsbury, exposing the CA's contradiction).
  • The price was honestly agreed; the business was solvent at transfer; "the honesty of the sale" was not really in contest, and the company got full value — the later insolvency was due to external causes (strikes), not overpricing.
  • All members knew and accepted the terms of the 1892 agreements (Lord Watson: "all the members of the company were fully cognisant of the terms... and... were willing to accept and did accept those terms") — so no fraud on the company; and if the arrangement was honest in the company, it was honest vis-à-vis creditors who lent on the faith of the registered documents.
  • The alleged defect (no independent board) was wholly unproved: "No evidence was led tending to support the allegation that no board of directors was ever appointed" (Watson).
④ Observations of the Court (House of Lords — Halsbury L.C., Watson, Herschell, Macnaghten)
  • On the only guide the court may use: "I have no right to add to the requirements of the statute, nor to take from the requirements thus enacted. The sole guide must be the statute itself." — Lord Halsbury L.C.
  • On the artificial person: "It is essential to the artificial creation that the law should recognise only that artificial existence — quite apart from the motives or conduct of individual corporators… once the company is legally incorporated it must be treated like any other independent person with rights and liabilities appropriate to itself, and the motives of those who took part in the promotion of the company are absolutely irrelevant." — Halsbury L.C.
  • On "real thing" / agent: "The learned judges appear to me not to have been absolutely certain in their own minds whether to treat the company as a real thing or not. If it was a real thing; if it had a legal existence, and if consequently the law attributed to it certain rights and liabilities in its constitution as a company, it appears to me to follow as a consequence that it is impossible to deny the validity of the transactions into which it has entered." — Halsbury L.C. (quoted with approval by the Privy Council in Lee).
  • On the CA's invented requirement: the judges below "wanted to do what Parliament never intended" — they refused to accept a company "where all the shares but one are held by one person", but "the Act does not say" that; "one share is sufficient to constitute a shareholder" (Halsbury).
  • Lord Macnaghten's immortal words: "The company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers to the memorandum… and though it may be that after incorporation the business is precisely the same as it was before… the liability of the members… is… limited… the company is not in law the agent of the subscribers… nor are the subscribers as members liable in any shape or form, except to the extent and in the manner provided by the Act."
  • On "one-man companies" and motive: "It has become the fashion to call companies of this class 'one man companies'. That is a taking nickname, but it does not help one much in the way of argument… If it merely means that there is a predominant partner possessing an overwhelming influence and entitled practically to the whole of the profits, that is no reason in the world why the company should not be a company legally incorporated." — Macnaghten.
  • On the alleged fraud: "The company was bound to take the business… at a proper valuation" and the debenture-holder "was entitled to enforce the security"; on the evidence "there was no fraud" — the creditors who dealt with the company did so knowing its capital and its debenture charge; the "object of the legislature" in permitting limited liability was precisely to let honest traders (not just financiers) enjoy it (Watson).
  • On misdescription (false certificate): an objection taken in the CA that the debenture certificate was false (stating £30,000 issued when £10,000 were) was disposed of: the appellant was not party to the fraud, and the point "does not help the company"; "the honesty of the issue of the debentures... is not attacked" (Watson).
⑤ Decision
  • House of Lords (unanimous): appeal allowed; the order of the Court of Appeal reversed. The company was validly incorporated; Salomon was not liable to indemnify it; the £10,000 debentures were valid and, being secured, ranked first on the company's assets — so the £6,000 of assets went to the secured creditor (Broderip) and the unsecured creditors got nothing.
  • Forensic history worth quoting: Vaughan Williams J. (first instance) held for the company (Salomon as agent, bound to indemnify — price "exorbitant"); Court of Appeal [Lindley, Lopes, Kay L.JJ., 1895] affirmed in emphatic language — Lindley L.J. called the arrangement "a device to defraud creditors" and said the company was a mere "sham" whose members were "dummies"; the H.L. unanimously reversed — the most famous reversal in company law.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The cardinal principle: from the moment of incorporation, a company is a legal person distinct from its members — it may own property, contract, sue and be sued in its own name; its members generally have no rights in its property and no liability for its debts (beyond their shares).
  • The statute is the sole guide: courts cannot import policy requirements ("independent directors", "genuine subscriber" etc.) that the legislature has not written; the validity of an incorporation is tested by compliance with the statute alone, and the motives of the corporators are irrelevant.
  • One-man companies are lawful — "one share is enough"; a company controlled by a single member is none the less a legal person.
  • Even the vendor of his business to his own company may lawfully be its secured creditor (debenture-holder) — and priority of debentures over unsecured trade creditors does not constitute fraud.
  • Corollary (equally examinable): because the company is a separate person, limited liability can be abused — hence the doctrine of lifting the corporate veil (§04) and the statutory exceptions (§4.12) are the necessary counterweight, but every lifting is measured against Salomon, which the courts will not lightly displace.
One-line for revision: "The company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers" — once incorporated it is an independent person; the statute, not the court's sense of fairness, is the sole guide.
🧷 Case 2 · State Trading Corporation of India Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer, AIR 1963 SC 811
AIR 1963 SC 811(1964) 4 SCR 99(1963) 33 Comp Cas 1057Company is not a citizenShort-answer staple
① Facts
  • The State Trading Corporation of India Ltd. was a private company registered under the Companies Act, 1956, with its registered office in New Delhi; it was formed by the Government of India to conduct foreign trade.
  • The corporation's shares were held — as the corporation itself asserted — by the President of India and two Union Secretaries in their official capacities, i.e. every member was a citizen of India.
  • Sales tax assessments were made against it; the corporation filed writ petitions under Art. 32 before the Supreme Court (against the Commercial Tax Officer, Visakhapatnam and the State of Bihar), complaining of the levy and seeking certiorari — claiming that the impugned provisions infringed its rights under Art. 19(1)(f) (property) and 19(1)(g) (trade/business).
② Issues
  1. Is an incorporated company a "citizen" within the meaning of Part II of the Constitution and of Art. 19, so as to be entitled to the fundamental rights conferred on citizens?
  2. If not, can the court nonetheless look through the corporate veil to the members — who were admittedly Indian citizens — so that the company may assert "their" Art. 19 rights?
  3. Could the corporation, at any rate, claim the protection of Art. 19 as "citizen" by virtue of its Indian nationality, on the footing that nationality and citizenship are the same?
③ Arguments — for STC (petitioner)
  • "Citizen" is not defined anywhere in Part II of the Constitution; the definition must be gathered from the scheme — and a company incorporated in India, all of whose members are citizens, should qualify; the corporation is "an association of three citizens" and thus itself a citizen.
  • Nationality and citizenship are used interchangeably in international and constitutional law; the company — incorporated and functioning in India, and an Indian national — must therefore be a citizen.
  • The word "citizen" should receive a wide construction so that economic activity carried on through incorporated bodies is not left unprotected; otherwise huge areas of commerce would be beyond the reach of Part III.
③ Arguments — for the Revenue / the State (respondents)
  • Part II of the Constitution (Arts. 5–11) deals with "citizens" in the sense of natural persons — acquisition by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation or incorporation of territory — concepts which can never apply to an artificial person; a company cannot be born, be registered as a citizen, or be naturalised.
  • The Citizenship Act, 1955 likewise recognises only natural persons; the company's separate personality (Salomon) means the character of its members cannot be imputed to it.
  • Even the petitioner's own pleading showed the shares stood in the name of the President and Secretaries in their official capacities — so the members were not even in their personal capacity "citizens" behind the company.
④ Observations of the Court
  • The majority: whether this or that theory of corporate personality be accepted, neither Part II of the Constitution nor the Citizenship Act confers citizenship on, or recognises as a citizen, any person other than a natural person. The framers used "citizen" in Art. 19 in the same sense as in Part II; an incorporated company is a person, but not a citizen, and cannot enforce Art. 19.
  • Nationality ≠ citizenship: a company looks to the country of incorporation for its nationality and to the place of its central management and control for its residence (De Beers) — but citizenship is a different, narrower concept — the political membership of the State, with rights and duties of an individual; the two must not be confused.
  • Hidayatullah J. (concurring) — the analogy you must quote: "If all of them (the members) are citizens of India, the company does not become a citizen of India any more than, if all of them are married, the company would be a married person."
  • On the veil: in this case the Court was not invited to "tear the corporate veil" — the argument was not that the company was a mere agent or alter ego; and even the widest veil-lifting would only identify the members, whose rights as citizens could then be asserted personally.
  • The reassurance: companies are not defenceless — they claim the rights given to "persons" (Art. 14; Art. 21 through natural persons; Art. 265; Art. 301; Art. 300A), and a citizen member whose own rights are impaired may sue in his own right; "there is no doubt that the corporation in most cases will share the benefit" of such protection.
⑤ Decision
  • The writ petitions were dismissed: STC, though a legal person and an Indian national, is not a citizen, and the impugned sales-tax provisions could not be struck down on a company's invocation of Art. 19.
  • Shah J. (with the majority) and Hidayatullah J. (concurring separately) both held: a corporate body is not a citizen; rights under Art. 19 belong to natural persons. The reference to "one-man companies, in which the sole member or the majority of members consist of citizens" was expressly left open — the Court did not decide whether the member could sue in such a case.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • A company is not a citizen — no incorporated body can be; citizenship attaches only to natural persons (Part II + Citizenship Act, 1955).
  • Capabilities of the company distinguished: nationality and residence (yes, by incorporation and central control) · legal personality (yes) · citizenship and political/fundamental rights of citizens (no).
  • Fundamental rights are of two classes — those of citizens (Arts. 15, 16, 19, 29) and those of all persons (Arts. 14, 20, 21, 25, 26…) — and a company can invoke only the second class directly; the first class it can reach only through its citizen-members (which the next cases develop).
  • This is the first step of the four-case chain: STC (no citizenship) → TELCO (no veil-lifting for the company's benefit) → R.C. Cooper (qualitative test for shareholders) → Bennett Coleman (veil lifted for shareholders/editors).
🧷 Case 3 · Tata Engineering & Locomotive Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar, AIR 1965 SC 40
AIR 1965 SC 40(1964) 6 SCR 885Veil not lifted for company's benefitChain-link 2/4
① Facts
  • TELCO was incorporated under the Companies Act, 1913 with its head office in Bombay; its factory and works were at Jamshedpur (Bihar), where it manufactured diesel trucks and bus chassis and spare parts (a substantial part of its production being from the Jamshedpur works).
  • The State of Bihar levied sales tax on the company's transactions; TELCO challenged the levy by a writ petition under Art. 32, contending that it infringed the company's rights under Art. 19(1)(f) and 19(1)(g).
  • The petitioners urged that even if a company is not a citizen, the court should lift the corporate veil and protect the citizens — the shareholders — who stood behind the company.
② Issues
  1. Can a registered company itself invoke Art. 19, given STC?
  2. If not, can the court, by lifting the veil of incorporation, treat the company as merely the medium through which citizen-shareholders exercise their rights, and uphold the petition on their behalf?
  3. Are the two "veil" functions distinguishable — lifting against a company (fraud, enemy character, revenue) versus lifting for it (to give it citizens' rights)?
③ Arguments — for the company (Mr. N.A. Palkhivala, for TELCO)
  • The right under Art. 19(1)(f)/(g) belongs to a citizen; a citizen may exercise his right to carry on business through the medium of a company — the company is but the vehicle and the shareholders the real beneficiaries.
  • To hold otherwise would be to permit the State to do indirectly what it cannot do directly — evade constitutional restraints merely because citizens choose the corporate form for their commerce.
  • The American and Indian cases on the "corporate personality" show the courts disclosing the true facts behind the corporate entity and refusing to let legal technicality defeat constitutional protection.
③ Arguments — for the State of Bihar
  • STC is conclusive: a company is not a citizen; Art. 19 does not protect it; the petition is not maintainable.
  • The corporate veil is a judicial tool for the protection of third parties and the public (fraud, evasion, enemy taint) — it is not a shield for the company itself to claim what the law has not given it; the shareholders are not parties to the petition and cannot be substituted.
  • If the shareholders' rights are violated they may sue for themselves; the company cannot press their claim as its own.
④ Observations of the Court (per majority; the chain is summarised in Bennett Coleman, para 11)
  • The doctrine stated: "a company is distinct and separate entity from its shareholders" — the company's personality is not the personality of the shareholders; the "corporate veil" can only be lifted in well-recognised exceptional situations.
  • The recognised exceptions named (this enumeration became the stock formula): "The corporate veil… could be lifted in cases where the company is charged with trading with the enemy or perpetrating fraud on the Revenue authorities" — i.e. veil-lifting is for protection against abuse.
  • No lifting to confer citizens' rights: the court refused to disregard the company's separate existence for the company's own benefit; Art. 19 rights cannot be asserted by the company either directly or by equating it with its shareholders.
  • If the impugned law affects the shareholders' rights, the remedy belongs to the shareholders — "individual members… are free to assert their rights"; the company's petition, however, fails. (The Supreme Court in Bennett Coleman characterised the TELCO approach exactly thus: "This Court said that a company was distinct and separate entity from shareholders.")
⑤ Decision
  • The petition was dismissed — TELCO, being not a citizen, could not complain of violation of Art. 19(1)(f) and (g); the court did not lift the veil to allow the company to claim the shareholders' fundamental rights.
  • The judgment left the position of the shareholders open — they could vindicate their own rights, and the following decisions (Cooper, Bennett Coleman) did exactly that.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The veil is lifted only to prevent misuse (enemy taint, fraud on revenue, evasion of law) — not to confer on the company rights of citizens. "What the company cannot do directly, it cannot do indirectly through the veil."
  • The negative rule: a company cannot maintain a petition for a violation of Art. 19 — neither as "citizen" (STC) nor through veil-lifting (TELCO).
  • The route left open: citizen-members may sue in their own right for impairment of their rights — the seed of the qualitative test (Cooper) and of the shareholders' rights doctrine (Bennett Coleman).
  • Doctrinal point: the case draws the classic line — veil-lifting is a defensive instrument against abuse, not an offensive instrument for the company. This is why the same court later refused to lift the veil at the member's own request (Kondoli Tea; Premlata Bhatia).
🧷 Case 4 · Rustom Cavasjee Cooper v. Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case), (1970) 3 SCR 530
(1970) 3 SCR 530AIR 1970 SC 564(1970) 1 SCC 248Qualitative testChain-link 3/410:1 majority
① Facts
  • The Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance, 1969 (later replaced by the Act of 1969) nationalised the 14 major Indian banks — banks with deposits of over ₹50 crore — transferring their undertakings to the Union.
  • Compensation under Schedule II was to be fixed by a Tribunal, but was payable to the banking company only after 10 years; management and control passed immediately; the compensation provisions were the most fiercely attacked.
  • Rustom Cavasjee Cooper — a shareholder, a director and a depositor in the Central Bank of India and Bank of Baroda — filed a petition under Art. 32, claiming that the Ordinance/Act violated Arts. 14, 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g) and 31(2).
  • The Union raised a preliminary objection: Cooper, a shareholder, had no locus standi — the rights infringed were those of the banking companies, not of a shareholder or depositor.
② Issues
  1. Can a shareholder, director and depositor maintain a petition complaining of an Act which takes over the banking company's undertaking — or does the separate personality of the company exclude him? (The corporate-entity question.)
  2. What is the test for deciding whether the shareholder's rights are "directly" impaired — a formal test (company's rights only) or a substantive one?
  3. On the merits: did the Act (esp. Schedule II and s. 15) offend Art. 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g), 14 and 31(2)?
③ Arguments — for the petitioner (Cooper)
  • A shareholder's bundle of rights includes the right to dividends, to vote, to share in surplus, and to carry on business through the agency of the company; nationalisation destroyed the company's business and thereby his own rights — "he suffered financial loss", his right to vote and dividends ceased, and he was deprived of the right as shareholder to carry on business through the agency of the company.
  • The Act was beyond legislative competence; the compensation was illusory (payable after 10 years, without interest); the classification of banks (above/below ₹50 crore deposits) was irrational; the guarantee of trade freedom was infringed.
  • A procedure that "made the Tribunal a puppet" and fixed a wholly inadequate amount denied the guarantee of Art. 31(2); Art. 19(1)(f) was hit because the acquisition destroyed the value of the property (shares) itself.
③ Arguments — for the Union of India
  • Locus standi: the undertaking belonged to the banking companies, not to their shareholders; a shareholder has no interest in the company's property; Cooper was at best a creditor/depositor and a shareholder — neither's right was "directly" affected; only the companies could sue, and they could not invoke Art. 19 (STC/TELCO).
  • The classic rule: the company is a separate person; the shareholders' rights are confined to their shares, and unless the company's rights are also impaired and the shareholder's right to dividends/votes is gone, the shareholder cannot complain.
  • Nationalisation was in public interest and was a legislative measure of economic policy, which the courts must not substitute their judgment for.
④ Observations of the Court (Shah J. for the majority)
  • The principle of locus standi restated but qualified: "A shareholder is not entitled to move the Court for enforcement of the company's rights" — but the court must examine the substance: "A measure executive or legislative may impair the rights of the company alone, and not of its shareholders; it may impair the rights of the shareholders not of the company; it may impair the rights of the shareholders as well as of the company. Jurisdiction of the Court to grant relief cannot be denied, when by State action the rights of the individual shareholder are impaired, if that action impairs the rights of the company as well."
  • The holding of the court on locus: "The test in determining whether the shareholder's right is impaired is not formal; it is essentially qualitative; if the State action impairs the right of the shareholders as well as of the company, the Court will not, concentrating merely upon the technical operation of the action, deny itself jurisdiction to grant relief."
  • The two tests from Cooper's case that Bennett Coleman later applied: (i) it is not the object of the authority making the law nor the form of action that determines the invasion of the right; (ii) it is the effect of the law and action upon the right — the direct operation upon the rights — which attracts the jurisdiction of the court.
  • On the merits: the guarantee of compensation in Art. 31(2) required a just equivalent; a compensation clause postponing payment for 10 years and fixing no just basis was not compensation at all in the constitutional sense; the Act also contravened Art. 19(1)(f)/(g) in so far as it destroyed the rights of the shareholders of the acquired banks.
  • A.N. Ray J. dissented (one dissent in a court of 11): he would have held the compensation constituted under the Act not violative of Art. 31 and upheld the measure.
⑤ Decision
  • Held (10:1): the petitioner's locus standi was upheld — it was not necessary that the shareholder's rights alone be impaired, if the State action impaired his rights along with the company's; the petition proceeded to the merits.
  • Merits: the impugned Act (in particular the compensation provisions of Schedule II and s. 15, conferring arbitrary discretion on the Central Government in making the scheme) was struck down as void for infringing Arts. 19(1)(f), 19(1)(g) and 31(2) (the Act was later re-enacted after amendment as the 1970 Act, which survived — but the principle stands).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The qualitative test: whether a shareholder may sue depends not on form but on the substance and quality of the injury — if the State action impairs the rights of the company and thereby of the shareholder, the court will not deny relief merely because the company alone is the formal object.
  • The rights of citizens are not lost by association into a company — the seed of the shareholders' rights doctrine: "the fundamental rights of shareholders as citizens are not lost when they associate to form a company" (as Bennett Coleman put it).
  • Test of direct operation: for judging the impact of State action on fundamental rights, the court looks at the effect, not the object or form, of the measure (also applied in Sakal, Express Newspapers, Bennett Coleman).
  • Doctrinal note: Cooper is thus the bridge between TELCO ("no veil for the company") and Bennett Coleman ("veil lifted for the shareholders") — the veil is lifted not to make the company a citizen but to protect the citizens behind it.
🧷 Case 5 · Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India, (1972) 2 SCC 788 — the veil lifted FOR the shareholders
(1972) 2 SCC 788AIR 1973 SC 106Art. 19(1)(a) · press freedomMajority: Sikri C.J., Ray, Reddy JJ. (Ray J.); Beg J. concurring; Mathew J. separate
① Facts
  • The petitions challenged the Import Policy for Newsprint, 1972–73 (framed under the Import Control Order, 1955, itself under ss. 3 and 4-A of the Imports and Exports Control Act, 1947) and certain provisions of the Newsprint Control Order, 1962 (under s. 3, Essential Commodities Act, 1955), as infringing Art. 19(1)(a) (freedom of speech and expression) and Art. 14.
  • The three principal changes of 1972–73 attacked: (i) no new newspaper/edition could be started by a common ownership unit even within its authorised quota; (ii) maximum page level of 10 pages, with no adjustment between circulation and pages to enable increase of pages; (iii) no interchangeability of quota between papers/editions of a common ownership unit and no facility to increase pages even by reducing circulation.
  • Petitioners were companies plus natural persons: in the Bennett Coleman group — the company, a shareholder, a reader and three editors of the three dailies; in the Hindustan Times case — a shareholder (also Deputy Director), a Deputy Editor, the printer-publisher and a reader; in Express Newspapers — the company and the Chief Editor; in The Hindu — a shareholder, the Managing Editor and the publisher. Every one of them claimed that the policy "imposed heavy financial loss and impaired their right to carry on the business of printing and publishing the dailies through the medium of the companies".
  • Two preliminary pleas in demurrer were raised by the Additional Solicitor-General: (i) the petitioners were companies and could not invoke fundamental rights; (ii) Art. 358 barred any challenge.
② Issues
  1. Can the shareholders, editors, printers and publishers — though they speak through newspaper companies — invoke Art. 19(1)(a) when the State action impairs the freedom of the press exercised through those companies?
  2. Does the presence of the company among the petitioners bar relief — i.e. when can the court lift the corporate veil in favour of the persons behind the company?
  3. Do the Newsprint Policy and the 1962 Order's restrictions (page ceiling, no interchangeability, no adjustment between pages and circulation) directly and disproportionately restrict the freedom of the press, or are they a reasonable regulation under Art. 19(2)?
  4. Was the policy in substance a newspaper-control order in the guise of an import policy?
③ Arguments — for the petitioners (companies + shareholders + editors)
  • Our case is "not the case of a company claiming citizens' rights as a company"; the petitioners include citizens — shareholders, editors, printers, publishers — and "the individual rights of freedom of speech and expression of editors, directors and shareholders are all exercised through their newspapers through which they speak. The press reaches the public through the newspapers. The shareholders speak through their editors."
  • The newsprint restrictions are a direct and disproportionate burden on circulation — "a restraint on the number of pages, a restraint on circulation and a restraint on advertisements would affect the fundamental rights under Article 19(1)(a)"; the object of the authority and the form of the action are immaterial; the direct operation/effect is the test (R.C. Cooper).
  • The policy is, in pith and substance, a newspaper-control order — it uses the import machinery to curb circulation and growth of newspapers, which is beyond the Import Control Act; the 10-page ceiling classifies arbitrarily (35 newspapers above 10 pages; big dailies hit, small ones given free growth).
  • Sakal Papers and Express Newspapers already granted relief to newspaper companies and their shareholders; the practice of this Court is settled (in Express Newspapers the company was the petitioner and the Court said the Act was "liable to be struck down" if it fell within the mischief of Art. 19(1)(a); in Sakal "relief was granted to the shareholders and the company").
③ Arguments — for the Union of India
  • Demurrer 1: "the petitioners were companies and therefore they could not invoke fundamental rights" — STC and TELCO: corporations are not citizens; cf. Mukherjea J.'s minority view in Chiranjit Lal that an incorporated company can come to court for enforcement of fundamental rights.
  • Demurrer 2: Article 358 (suspension of Art. 19 in times of war/emergency) barred the challenge — the proclamation of emergency was in force.
  • On merits: the policy was a legitimate import-control measure to conserve a scarce essential commodity (newsprint) and secure equitable distribution; the need was for textbook paper; a larger quota of imported newsprint was given in 1971–72 (1.40 → 1.80 lakh tonnes) — the restrictions were reasonable, necessary and temporary.
  • Freedom of the press does not guarantee an unlimited right to newsprint or to expand circulation at the cost of scarce national resources.
④ Observations of the Court (Ray J. for the majority; Beg J. concurring)
  • The two demurrers disposed of: (i) on Art. 358 — the Court did not accept that the challenge was barred; (ii) on the company question — "this Court in State Trading Corporation… and Tata Engineering… expressed the view that a corporation was not a citizen… The result was that an incorporated company could not be a citizen so as to invoke fundamental rights. In the State Trading Corporation case the Court was not invited to 'tear the corporate veil'. In the Tata Engineering case this Court said that a company was distinct and separate entity from shareholders. The corporate veil it was said could be lifted in cases where the company is charged with trading with the enemy or perpetrating fraud on the Revenue authorities."
  • But the veil is lifted for the shareholders here: "The Bank Nationalisation case has established the view that the fundamental rights of shareholders as citizens are not lost when they associate to form a company. When their fundamental rights as shareholders are impaired by State action their rights as shareholders are protected. The reason is that the shareholders' rights are equally and necessarily affected if the rights of the company are affected. The rights of shareholders with regard to Article 19(1)(a) are projected and manifested by the newspapers owned and controlled by the shareholders through the medium of the corporation."
  • On standing of the company joined with citizens: "The fact that the companies are the petitioners does not prevent this Court from giving relief to the shareholders, editors, printers who have asked for protection of their fundamental rights… The locus standi of the shareholder petitioners is beyond challenge after the ruling of this Court in the Bank Nationalisation case. The presence of the company is on the same ruling not a bar to the grant of relief."
  • On the pith-and-substance: "The Newsprint Control Policy is found to be newspaper control order in the guise of framing an Import Control Policy for newsprint"; "the machinery of the Import Control cannot be utilised to curb or control circulation of growth or freedom of newspapers in India."
  • On the ceiling: "freedom of a newspaper to publish any number of pages or to circulate it to any number of persons has been held… to be an integral part of the freedom of speech and expression"; the restrictions on growth, the 10-page ceiling and the non-interchangeability were disproportionate — "a restraint on the number of pages… would affect the fundamental rights under Article 19(1)(a) on the aspects of propagation, publication and circulation" — and the classification of dailies (those over 10 pages vs. those under) had no rational basis (28 of 35 papers above 10 pages benefited; 7 big dailies were hit).
  • Mathew J. delivered a separate opinion (examined the "shareholders' rights are projected and manifested through the corporation" doctrine with greater caution), but the majority's reasoning carried the case; Ray J. (who later sat in the majority) wrote for the majority.
⑤ Decision
  • The Newsprint Policy 1972–73 was struck down as violative of Art. 19(1)(a) (and Art. 14) in so far as it imposed the 10-page ceiling, prohibited growth and denied interchangeability of quota; the restrictions were not saved by Art. 19(2).
  • Relief was granted to the petitioner companies jointly with their shareholders, editors, printers and publishers — the corporate veil being lifted, not to make the company a citizen, but to protect the citizens whose rights were projected and manifested through the corporation.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • The shareholders' rights doctrine in full bloom: citizens do not lose Art. 19 rights by associating into a company; where the State action hits the company, and thereby equally and necessarily the shareholders, the court will lift the corporate veil to protect the shareholders' rights projected and manifested through the corporation.
  • Who may sue: the company acquires standing by joining a citizen shareholder/editor (and by the qualitative test of Cooper); the presence of the company among the petitioners is no bar. (Followed in Godhra Electricity Co. v. State of Gujarat (1975) 1 SCC 199.)
  • Freedom of the press: a company newspaper's freedom is the citizens' freedom of expression; restrictions on pages/circulation/quotas must be tested by their direct operation, not the form or object of the measure.
  • Complete the four-case chain: STC (no citizenship) → TELCO (no veil for the company) → Cooper (qualitative test; shareholders' rights may be asserted) → Bennett Coleman (veil lifted; relief to shareholders/editors/printers).
🧷 Case 6 · Daimler Co., Ltd. v. Continental Tyre and Rubber Co. (G.B.), Ltd., (1916) 2 AC 307 (HL) — the enemy-character case
(1916) 2 AC 307 (HL)[1916-17] All ER Rep 191True character of companyJudicial ground ①
① Facts
  • Continental Tyre and Rubber Co. (G.B.), Ltd. was a private company registered in England (under the Companies (Consolidation) Act, 1908) to market in England the motor-tyres manufactured by a German concern; its capital was 25,000 shares.
  • Ownership: 23,398 of the 25,000 shares were held by a German parent company incorporated and resident in Hanover; three German subjects resident in Hanover held 1,600 shares between them; the remaining two shares were held by the secretary, Hans Frederick Wolter (a German, naturalised as a British subject on 1 Jan 1910), and the managing director, Paul Scharnhorst Brodtmann.
  • All the three directors (besides the managing director) were German subjects resident in Hanover; “with the exception of the secretary, all the directors and shareholders are German subjects”.
  • After the outbreak of the First World War (Aug 1914) the English company sued Daimler Co., Ltd. (also English) on a specially indorsed writ (23 Oct 1914) for £5,605 16s. — the amount due on three bills of exchange — and applied under R.S.C. Ord. 14 for leave to sign final judgment.
  • Daimler's defence: it was ready and willing to pay — but only on conditions: (i) that in paying it was not contravening the Trading with the Enemy Act, 1914, and (ii) that the respondent company could institute the action and give a good discharge. It averred that the parent company controlled the respondent; that the parent and all the officers of the respondent were alien enemies; that alien-enemy officers could not act for the company; and that the proceedings were consequently wrongly instituted. Daimler sought unconditional leave to defend.
  • Master Macdonell granted leave to sign final judgment (24 Nov 1914) on the affidavits; Scrutton J. (on affidavit evidence not before the master) affirmed; the Court of Appeal affirmed (19 Jan 1915) — holding that an English-registered company could not be an enemy. Daimler appealed to the House of Lords. (A parallel action, Continental v. Thomas Tilling, was tried before Lush J.)
② Issues
  1. When war is declared and the majority of a company's shares and all its directors are alien enemies resident in the enemy country, does the English-registered company take on an enemy character so that paying it (or permitting it to sue) amounts to trading with the enemy — notwithstanding the certificate of incorporation?
  2. Was the secretary clothed with authority to institute the action and to give a valid discharge for the debt, after the war had begun?
  3. Does the indirectness of the means adopted (the interposition of an English company) purge the illegality of paying money to enemies?
③ Arguments — for Daimler (the appellant company)
  • The substance, not the form: the company is but the “machinery” — “the names and the incorporation were but the machinery by which the purpose (giving moneys to the enemy) would be accomplished”; the respondent company was controlled by and was the channel of the German parent; the real parties in interest were enemies.
  • Public policy: “to enable thousands of pounds to be paid to the King's enemies” could not be permitted; trading with the enemy is illegal in every sense, and no subject could escape by pleading that he was “acting merely as the hand of others” — the prohibition “is binding in regard to all action direct or indirect, personal or representative”.
  • No valid authority to sue: there was “not a scrap of writing” proving the secretary had power to institute actions; the authority was in the enemy directors, who could not exercise it in wartime; the secretary’s affidavit (which said only “English company, registered at Somerset House… I am a British subject”) said nothing of where the business was conducted or from where it was directed, and never alleged authority — “his silence… is inexplicable”.
③ Arguments — for Continental Tyre (the respondent company)
  • The certificate is conclusive of character: the company was “an English company, being registered at Somerset House under the Companies (Consolidation) Act, 1908”; its secretary was a naturalised British subject; the proclamation of 9 Sept 1914 itself said: “In the case of incorporated bodies, enemy character attaches only to those incorporated in an enemy country.”
  • Statutory surveillance shows the legislative policy: the Trading with the Enemy Act 1914 itself provided (s. 2(2)) for inspection and (s. 3) for appointment of a controller where a third or more of the share capital or directorate is held by alien enemies — “to carry the legislation… it was clear that the case of companies held by a majority or even by a minority of alien enemies was put under surveillance”; so Parliament had not declared such companies enemies, and “it is… unsound for a court of law to announce that… the shareholding of a company incorporated in England has to be investigated”.
  • Identification argument rejected: “the legal entity the company cannot be so identified with its shareholders, or the majority of them, as to make their nationality its nationality or their status its status, so completely as to make it an alien enemy because they are alien enemies”; Lord Macnaghten in Janson v. Driefontein Consolidated Mines: “If all its members had been subjects of the British Crown the corporation itself would have been none the less a foreign corporation”.
  • Lush J. had found the secretary a “truthful, though a forgetful and inaccurate, witness” and had held he had authority — the company relied on that finding.
④ Observations of the Court
  • Earl of Halsbury (the dictum to quote): “I am of opinion that this judgment should be reversed. In my opinion, the whole discussion is solved by a very simple proposition that in our law when the object to be obtained is unlawful the indirectness of the means by which it is to be obtained will not get rid of the unlawfulness, and in this cause the object of the means adopted is to enable thousands of pounds to be paid to the King's enemies.”
  • Halsbury on the corporate creature: “It is, in fact, a partnership in all that constitutes a partnership except the names… No one can doubt that the names and the incorporation were but the machinery by which the purpose (giving moneys to the enemy) would be accomplished… the mere machinery to do an illegal act will not purge its illegality — fraus circuitu non purgatur.”
  • Halsbury on the “live thing” / liability of the enemy: “I observe the Lord Chief Justice says that the company is a live thing. If it were, it would be capable of loyalty and disloyalty. But it is not; and the argument of its being incapable of being loyal or disloyal is founded on its not being ‘a live thing.’ Neither is the bag in my illustration ‘a live thing.’” And again: “The limited liability was a very useful introduction into our system, and there was no reason why foreigners should not, while dealing honestly with us, partake of the benefits of that institution, but it seems to me too monstrous to suppose that for an unlawful, because, after a declaration of war, a hostile, purpose the forms of that institution should be used, and enemies of the State, while actually at war with us, be allowed to continue trading and actually to sue for their profits in trade in an English court of justice.”
  • Lord Atkinson on the facts: “there was not a scrap of writing of any kind given in evidence to prove that any power to institute actions or give receipts for money recovered was ever conferred upon the secretary”; the only document relied on “contradicted every statement made by him on the point”; “The burden of proving that the secretary had power and authority to institute the present action some months after the outbreak of the war rested on the respondent company… they had not discharged that burden.”
  • Lord Atkinson (narrow ground), declining to identify company with shareholders: “the question of residence of the company apart, I do not think that the legal entity the company can be so identified with its shareholders, or the majority of them, as to make their nationality its nationality or their status its status, so completely as to make it an alien enemy because they are alien enemies, or to give it an enemy character because they have that character” — and he approved the order proposed by Lord Parker (the majority’s leading speech) on the authority point.
  • Lord Shaw agreed in the result but expressly disagreed with the majority’s enemy-character reasoning: “the country of incorporation of the company if English excludes the company from being either an enemy company or of an enemy character”; yet on authority: “since the outbreak of war it is not… competent for enemy directors or shareholders to have anything to do with the management of this company's affairs in England… The point against agency and authority to take these particular legal proceedings has been taken; and I do not differ from the view of your Lordships that it is well founded. I agree, accordingly, to the suit being dismissed upon that ground.”
  • The received ratio of the majority (Lord Parker): an English company may assume an enemy character where the persons in de facto control of its affairs are resident in an enemy country, or, wherever resident, are acting under the control of enemies — the court will look through the legal personality to the real controlling minds; a company “can be neither loyal nor disloyal — neither friend nor enemy” in itself, but its character is derived from those who control it.
⑤ Decision
  • The appeal was allowed; the orders below were reversed; the action was dismissed (the writ/proceedings being incompetent), and leave to defend was not the remedy — the proper order was that the suit could not proceed, since no authority to sue could lawfully be exercised on behalf of the company in the circumstances.
  • Authority to sue was not established (burden on the respondent, not discharged); and the courts below erred in refusing to investigate the true character of the company in wartime: to allow the company to sue and receive payment would have been to use “the names and the incorporation… as the machinery by which the purpose (giving moneys to the enemy) would be accomplished”.
  • In the associated action, the claim was likewise not allowed to proceed; and payment by English debtors was required to await the war-time machinery (custodian etc.).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ① — true character of the company: the court can look behind incorporation to determine the real character of a company — nationality, residence and, in time of war, enemy character — by reference to the persons in de facto control; a company acts through human beings, and the law will see who those human beings are.
  • Indirectness is no defence: an unlawful object performed through legal machinery remains unlawful — “fraus circuitu non purgatur”; the corporate form cannot be used to do what the law forbids being done directly.
  • Company vs. members — both aspects: incorporation is real (the company is not automatically an “alien” because members are aliens — Janson), yet the company is not a “live thing” capable of loyalty/disloyalty: its loyalty, residence and character are found in its controllers. (Contrast: People's Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder (1908) — where no public danger exists, courts refuse to identify members with the company.)
  • Trading with the enemy test: trading with a company resident or controlled in enemy territory is as much prohibited as trading with the enemy individual; payments to such a company are payments to enemies.
  • Significance for India: the same doctrine applies to trading with the enemy during war/emergency and, in the modern cases, to the true ownership/control inquiries (economic sanctions, beneficial ownership, resident-status questions: New Horizons Ltd. v. UOI (1994) 1 SCC 478 line; Vodafone (2012)).

(1960) 3 All ER 420 (PC)[1961] AC 12Separate entity appliedResult: widow compensated

Lee was appointed governing director for life under Art. 32, with “the full government and control of the company” vested in him, able to exercise all the powers of the directors and of the company not required by statute to be exercised in general meeting; his signed minute had “the effect of a resolution of the company”.
  • Art. 33 provided: “The company shall employ the said Geoffrey Woodhouse Lee as the chief pilot of the company at a salary of £1,500 per annum from the date of incorporation… and in respect of such employment the rules of law applicable to the relationship of master and servant shall apply as between the company and the said Geoffrey Woodhouse Lee.” (On 16 Aug 1954 the words “a salary of £1,500 per annum…” were deleted and replaced by “a salary to be arranged by the governing director” — the amendment effected by a minute signed by the deceased.)
  • The company owned an aircraft equipped for top-dressing; Lee was a duly qualified pilot; he piloted the aircraft, received wages (recorded in the company's wages book), and the company was paid by farmers with whom the company alone contracted. Personal-accident policies were taken out on Lee's life by the company secretary, premiums being paid by the company and debited to Lee's personal account.
  • On 5 March 1956, Lee was killed while piloting the aircraft in the course of aerial top-dressing. His widow, Catherine Lee, claimed £2,430 compensation (for herself and four infant children) plus funeral expenses, under s. 3(1) of the New Zealand Workers' Compensation Act, 1922, which makes the employer liable where personal injury by accident arises “out of and in the course of” the employment of a “worker” — defined in s. 2 as “any person who has entered into or works under a contract of service… with an employer”.
  • The company denied that Lee was a “worker”: he was at the time of the accident the controlling shareholder and governing director. On a Case Stated (Archer J., Compensation Court) the question went to the New Zealand Court of Appeal (Gresson P., North and Cleary JJ.), which — per North J., 18 Dec 1958 — answered in the negative: although a director may properly have a service agreement with his company, Lee had all the powers delegated to him for life, “there remained with the company no power of management whatsoever”; “he became in effect both employer and worker… the two offices are clearly incompatible. There could exist no power of control and therefore the relationship of master-servant was not created.” Laura Lee appealed to the Privy Council.
② Issues
  1. Was the deceased a “worker” (i.e. a person who had entered into or worked under a contract of service with an employer) within the Workers' Compensation Act, 1922, so as to entitle the widow to compensation?
  2. Can a man who is the governing director and controlling shareholder of a company simultaneously serve the company as a servant/employee — i.e., can one natural person occupy the two capacities of employer and employee vis-à-vis the same company?
  3. Is a contract of service possible between a company and its own controller, given the requirement of two distinct contracting parties and of a right of control?
③ Arguments — for the appellant widow (Mrs. Catherine Lee)
  • Under Salomon the company is a separate legal person; it was never suggested the company was “a sham or a mere simulacrum”; the contract of service was expressed in Art. 33 to attract master-and-servant law; the deceased worked as pilot, was paid wages, and was subject to the company's right of control.
  • The right of control is what matters — not how it is exercised: Lee as governing director appointed himself pilot as agent of the company; the company, not Lee, had the right to control; the two offices are functions of the same single personality of the company as a separate entity, and his shareholding/directorship did not preclude the existence of a contract of service with it (a director may contract to serve his company — Fowler v. Commercial Timber Co. supports the duality).
  • Capacity is not personality: one person may act in two capacities; the contract was made between him and the company — two legal persons — and the separate-entity principle exists precisely to make such arrangements possible (cf. IRC v. Sansom (1921) 2 KB 492, where such a company's contracts with its sole governing director were upheld as the company's).
③ Arguments — for the respondent company (as found by the NZ Court of Appeal)
  • A contract of service requires two parties and a right of control in the master; Lee was the company — all powers were vested in him for life; “on him lay the duty both of giving orders and obeying them”; “there could exist no power of control and therefore the relationship of master-servant was not created”.
  • The “two offices are clearly incompatible”: he would be contracting with himself; the company had no independent will that could employ or dismiss him.
  • The Court of Appeal accepted that a director may enter into a service agreement with his company — but not when he is the governing director in whom the full government and control is vested.
④ Observations of the Court (Privy Council — Lord Morris of Borth-y-Gest)
  • The framework: “The substantial question which arises is… whether the deceased was a ‘worker’ within the meaning of the Workers' Compensation Act, 1922… Was he a person who had entered into or worked under a contract of service with an employer?”
  • The decisive identification of the employer: “It cannot be suggested that, when engaged in the activities above referred to, the deceased was discharging his duties as governing director. Their Lordships find it impossible to resist the conclusion that the active aerial operations were performed because the deceased was in some contractual relationship with the respondent company. The relationship came about because the deceased, as one legal person, was willing to work for and to make a contract with the respondent company which was another legal entity. A contractual relationship could only exist on the basis that there was consensus between two contracting parties. It was never suggested (nor… could it reasonably have been suggested) that the respondent company was a sham or a mere simulacrum.”
  • Directorship is no impediment: “It is well established that the mere fact that someone is a director of a company is not an impediment to his entering into a contract to serve the company. If, then, it be accepted that the respondent company was a legal entity, their Lordships see no reason to challenge the validity of any contractual obligations which were created between the respondent company and the deceased.”
  • Salomon applied: quoting Halsbury L.C. in Salomon: “If it was a real thing; if it had a legal existence… it appears to me to follow as a consequence that it is impossible to deny the validity of the transactions into which it has entered”; and Macnaghten's “one man companies… that is a taking nickname, but it does not help one much in the way of argument”.
  • On the right of control: “If… the deceased was a party to a contract of service with the respondent company, then the respondent company had a right of control. The manner of its exercise would not affect or diminish the right to its exercise. But the existence of a right to control cannot be denied if once the reality of the legal existence of the respondent company is recognised. Just as the respondent company and the deceased were separate legal entities so as to permit of contractual relations being established between them, so also were they separate legal entities so as to enable the respondent company to give an order to the deceased.”
  • Authority cited with approval: Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Sansom (1921) 2 KB 492 (Younger L.J.: “so long as such a company as this is recognised by the legislature there can be no reason why the contracts and engagements made in its name or entered into on its behalf, and themselves ex facie regular, should not everywhere until the contrary is alleged and proved be regarded as the company's”); and Fowler v. Commercial Timber Co. (1930) 2 KB 1 (Scruton L.J. on the two positions of a managing director).
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed — the question was answered in the affirmative: the deceased was a “worker” within s. 2 of the Act; he had entered into and worked under a contract of service with the respondent company; his death arose out of and in the course of the employment.
  • The widow was entitled to compensation under s. 3(1) of the Workers' Compensation Act, 1922 (in practice the amount was paid by the insurers, since the company had taken out accident policies on Lee).
  • The New Zealand Court of Appeal's answer in the negative was reversed: “his special position as governing director and principal shareholder did not preclude him from making on the company's behalf a contract of employment with himself, nor preclude him from entering into, or working in the capacity of servant under, a contract of service with the company” (the Held in the report).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Capacity vs. personality: one natural person can occupy two capacities (director/agent and servant) in relation to one separate legal person (the company); “the magic of corporate personality enabled him to be master and servant at the same time” (Gower). The company is not a sham, and the contract is valid.
  • Test of master-servant: the existence of a right of control in the employer is what counts; the manner of its exercise is irrelevant — and the right exists once the company is a real legal person with the contract.
  • Salomon vindicated, not relaxed: Lee is the logical consequence of separate personality — a one-person arrangement is still a two-person relationship in law; hence it is cited as the strongest application (not an exception) of Salomon.
  • Practical effects: controlling shareholders/directors can be employees of their own companies — important for workmen's compensation, income-tax (remuneration deductible), ESI/EPF, insurance and insolvency-priority questions.
  • Contrast for the answer: where a company is a mere sham (Gilford; Subhra Mukherjee) the separate entity is disregarded; where it is a real company, even total control by one man does not merge him with it (Lee; Salomon; Sansom).
🧷 Case 8 · In re Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit, Bari (Income-tax reference), AIR 1927 Bom. 371
AIR 1927 Bom 371Bom HC — Marten C.J. & Kemp J.Tax evasion · sham companiesJudicial ground ③
① Facts
  • For the financial year 1925–26, Sir Dinshaw Petit was assessed to super-tax on an aggregate income of Rs. 11,35,302 arising in the previous year. He objected to Rs. 3,90,804 — made up of Rs. 2,76,800 (Government and other fixed-interest-bearing funds) and Rs. 1,14,004 (dividends in companies).
  • Admittedly, the assessee was the legal owner of most of these funds (standing in his name, interest and dividends paid to him direct); as to the rest, the apparent legal owners were his nominees; "he has retained all the above interest and dividends and applied the same to his own use".
  • In 1921 he had formed four private "family" companies — (i) Petit Ltd., (ii) the Bombay Investment Co. Ltd., (iii) the Miscellaneous Investment Co. Ltd., (iv) the Safe Securities Ltd. — although "in fact no other member of his family took any direct benefit thereunder". Each took over a particular block of the assessee's investments.
  • Petit Ltd. (the typical case): incorporated 12 April 1921, nominal capital Rs. 10,000,000 — 9,99,900 ordinary shares of Rs. 10 and 100 preference shares of Rs. 10 (6% cumulative). Issued/subscribed: 3,48,604 fully paid ordinary shares, all held by the assessee, and three preference shares held by three of "his subordinates… entirely under his control" — the Secretary of the Petit Charities, the Secretary of the four companies, and a clerk of the same companies ("not one of them… paid their preference dividends, and no protest on their part is on record").
  • The machinery: (1) on 12 Apr 1921 the assessee purported to sell to Petit Ltd. 498 shares in the Maneckji Petit Manufacturing Co. Ltd. (worth Rs. 7,000 each) for Rs. 34,86,000, in consideration of an allotment of the company's shares; (2) a declaration of trust of the same date (the shares standing in the vendor's/nominees' names until called upon to transfer); (3) thereafter all dividends and interest from the blocks were credited to the company in account and — the same day — debited to the assessee by way of "loan": "none of the dividends or interest ever reached the company. Only credit and debit entries were made. Nor was any interest paid on the amount of the loan… but the interest was credited every year to the company in the account."
  • Over the years the assessee's current account for these alleged loans stood at Rs. 7,14,103-8-11; nothing was ever repaid; no interest was paid in cash; no resolutions were passed; the fixed preference dividends remained unpaid and a large sum of back income accumulated in the assessee's hands. No dividend was ever declared by the companies.
  • The Commissioner of Income-tax stated a case with four questions (para 15) — (1) whether the alleged dispositions/trusts were real; (2) whether the companies' business was really the assessee's; (3) whether the sums were assessable as his income; (4) the genuineness of the alleged loans.
② Issues
  1. Were the transfers and declarations of trust in favour of the four family companies genuine — or a sham/paper transaction, the companies being mere screens for the assessee?
  2. Were the alleged "loans" of the dividends/interest back to the assessee real loans, or were they merely withdrawals of income disguised as loans?
  3. If the companies were not real, were the disputed sums (Rs. 3,90,804) part of the assessee's taxable income for super-tax under the Indian Income-tax Act, 1922 (ss. 2(15), 3, 6, 12, 55, 56, 58)?
  4. (Subsidiary) Could the Revenue, having previously taxed the interest/dividends in the companies' hands, be estopped from now assessing the assessee?
③ Arguments — for the assessee (Sir Dinshaw Petit)
  • He is only a trustee for the family companies; the interest and dividends are theirs, not his; he has credited them in account, and "though he has had the benefit of them in specie this is because the family companies have lent him these moneys at interest which he has credited to them in account although he has not actually paid the interest in cash".
  • The family companies are under no obligation to declare a dividend and are entitled to lend out their income in this way, even though over a series of years the fixed preference dividends went unpaid and a large sum of back income accumulated in his hands.
  • The companies were duly incorporated under the Companies Act; Salomon says the company is a distinct person; the shares stood in the nominees' names; the companies paid tax on the interest credited to them — indicating the Revenue itself treated them as the owners; the Revenue is estopped from changing its stand.
  • And (alternatively) if the moneys were not loans, they were moneys wrongfully received by him, which he must refund to the company — on which, therefore, he cannot be assessed.
  • It was argued (citing English experience) that legislation alone (not the court) could defeat the device of accumulating profits and refusing to declare dividends.
③ Arguments — for the Revenue (Advocate-General / Commissioner)
  • The alleged disposition in favour of each family company is a sham, as is the declaration of trust; the transactions are all paper transactions and not real.
  • If the family company carries on any business, it does so solely as the agent of the assessee; and in any event the alleged loans are not genuine loans — so the sums are the assessee's taxable income under ss. 2(15), 3, 6, 12, 55, 56 and 58 of the 1922 Act.
  • The three shareholders were his servants, entirely under his control; no one else of the family took any benefit; the price of Rs. 34,86,000 for the 498 shares was not fully paid and the "sale" was concealed; no dividends were ever declared, no resolutions passed, no interest paid — the "companies" had no real business, no real income and no real existence apart from the assessee's tax-saving design.
④ Observations of the Court (Marten C.J. and Kemp J.)
  • The right starting point — and its limits: "If it were merely a question of the genuineness, the transactions between the assessee and the family company, it would be quite wrong to start with the presumption that those transactions are sham ones. On the contrary one should start with the presumption that they are genuine, and throw the onus on the Crown… But there may nevertheless come a time when the onus is discharged" — and here it was, by "clear and definite evidence that the Company is carrying on a genuine business as a separate entity" being absent.
  • Contrast with genuine cases: in IRC v. Sansom there was a timber business "carried on openly in the usual way"; the English Special Commissioners found the loans genuine because they were "in point of fact" loans (there the Crown failed). Here "we have not got a company carrying on an open business like a shop. The circumstances, therefore, are more unfavourable to the assessee"; and "the part played by the quarrelsome and independent Mr. Hime in Sansom's case falls to the lot of three subordinates of the assessee. But they can hardly be said to be in the same independent position… They have not even been paid their preference dividends, and no protest on their part is on record."
  • The finding on the companies: "There are other facts which suggest that the company in this case was formed by the assessee purely and simply as a means of avoiding super-tax and that the company was nothing more than the assessee himself. It did no business but was created purely and simply as a legal entity to ostensibly receive the dividends and interest and hand them over to the assessee as pretended loans." (Also: "out of the total subscribed capital of over thirty to forty lacs of each company, only shares of the face value of Rs. 30 were not in the assessee's name".)
  • On the "loans": "A perusal of the current account in the company's books shows that the dividends and interest alleged to have been received by the company were credited in the… account of the company and the same day debited to the assessee by way of loan"; "We have here a feature which is not present in the other cases, viz., that the alleged transfer or declaration of trust is itself challenged"; "the fact that the family company has paid tax on the interest credited to it by the assessee in respect of the alleged loans does not necessarily involve the conclusion that the loans were genuine, nor estop the Crown from now showing that these loans were illusory. Paying tax on the alleged interest arising from the loan was much cheaper for the assessee than paying super-tax on the dividends themselves."
  • On "legislation alone": "This is not the first time when plausible paper schemes under the Companies Acts have not stood the test of examination in a Court of Law" — the court is not asked to do what legislation alone can do; it is only dealing with a sham transfer and a sham loan, which no legislation is needed to defeat. (Even if the payments were illegal without declaration of dividend, the assessee could be taxed: Partridge v. Mallandaine; Birendra Kishor Manikya.)
  • On the alternative plea (no assessment because liable to refund): rejected — "I am not prepared, in any case, to accede to this argument where, if the company be regarded as carrying on its own business separate to that of the assessee, it has made no attempt and apparently does not intend to recover such sums from the assessee"; nor can the sums be treated as dividends, "for the company paid no dividends and the moneys are not entered in its books as such".
  • Kemp J. (concise formulation): the four companies were "formed… purely and simply as a means of avoiding super-tax… they did no business, but were created simply as legal entities to ostensibly receive the dividends and interests and to hand them over to the assessee as pretended loans".
⑤ Decision
  • Per curiam — the four questions answered: (1) In the affirmative — the sums were taxable in the assessee's hands (the disposition/trust was not genuine); (2) and (3) No (the companies' business was not in truth their own, and the sums were not the companies' income); (4) the loans "were not genuine loans but merely withdrawals of income disguised as loans".
  • The assessment of super-tax on the aggregate income including the disputed Rs. 3,90,804 was upheld.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ③ — tax evasion: the court will pierce the veil of companies created merely as screens to avoid tax — here four companies with no business except to receive the assessee's dividends and return them to him as "loans"; the "income" was held to be the assessee's.
  • Sham "loans" to shareholders are not loans: credit-debit entries without payment, interest, security, repayment or even a resolution — are withdrawals of income disguised as loans (the ancestor of s. 2(22)(e) IT Act, 1961 — deemed dividend).
  • Genuineness is presumed, but proof is of substance, not form: the Crown must prove the sham, but formal compliance (incorporation, accounts, nominees, even paying tax at the lower rate) does not make a paper scheme real; "economic realities behind the legal facade" (Meenakshi Mills) are decisive.
  • No estoppel against the Revenue for prior assessments made on the same facts; and no immunity from tax for a person who receives company funds unlawfully without declaring dividends.
  • Position in India today: the same logic is codified in s. 2(22)(e) (deemed dividend on payments by closely-held companies), s. 179 (directors' liability for private company's tax), ss. 68/69 (unexplained credits), and GAAR (ss. 95–102); the classical case remains Dinshaw Petit.
🧷 Case 9 · Commissioner of Income-tax v. Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 819
AIR 1967 SC 819(1967) 1 SCR 934(1967) 63 ITR 609Economic realities · anti-avoidanceJudicial ground ③ · the classic formulation
① Facts
  • Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd. and two associated public limited companies carried on the business of manufacturing and selling cotton yarn at Pudukottai (then in Madras State).
  • The taxing authorities invoked s. 42(1) of the Indian Income-tax Act, 1922 — which taxes income which "arises, directly or indirectly" through or from any money lent at interest by a person in the taxable territories to a person outside the taxable territories, "provided that" the money was brought into the taxable territories or the lender's business was carried on there, and the money was "in truth" employed in such a way that the interest would not have arisen but for the bringing in / the lending from within.
  • The arrangement in question: the companies (assessed in British India) had substantial sale proceeds credited in a bank account; deposits were made outside the taxable territories (at the bank's Pudukottai branch treated as outside for the purpose, or similar), and loans were taken against those deposits; the group's common director sat on the bank's board; interest paid was claimed as a deductible business expenditure.
  • The department's case: the deposits-and-loans arrangement was a colourable/indirect device — the interest "arising indirectly" from money lent — and the true picture was that of a circular arrangement designed to manufacture deductible interest; the Income-tax Officer and the Appellate Assistant Commissioner found against the assessee on the question of the genuineness of the arrangement (in particular, the AAC's finding on the Pudukottah branch deposits).
  • On appeal, the Income-tax Appellate Tribunal reversed in part — holding that s. 42(1) did not apply — and the High Court answered the reference in the assessee's favour. The Commissioner appealed to the Supreme Court.
② Issues
  1. Whether, on the facts, the interest arose directly or indirectly from money lent by the assessee-companies within the meaning of s. 42(1) of the 1922 Act (s. 42(1) being a charging provision and also the provision under which the courts stated the veil doctrine).
  2. Whether the income-tax authorities can disregard the corporate entity of the companies and go behind the apparent transactions to the "economic realities" — and if so, on what conditions can that exceptional power be exercised?
  3. Did the arrangement constitute a device to evade tax — the sum being taxable under s. 42(1) — the Tribunal having erred in law?
③ Arguments — for the assessee company
  • Each transaction (sale, deposit, loan, payment of interest) was a genuine banking transaction at arm's length; the money was really deposited and really lent; s. 42(1) contemplates lending to a person outside the taxable territories, and here the borrower and the interest-bearing arrangement were within the taxing net; the interest was paid in the ordinary course of business and was deductible.
  • The companies are separate legal entities (Salomon); the common directorate does not make them one; the revenue cannot recharacterise the parties' lawful transactions; the assessments for earlier years had been accepted.
  • The Tribunal — the final fact-finding authority — had found the arrangement genuine and s. 42(1) inapplicable; the High Court rightly answered in favour of the assessee; the Supreme Court should not disturb concurrent findings.
③ Arguments — for the Revenue
  • At the AAC stage the record showed: the fixed deposits at the "outside" branch were made with the group's moneys, loans were taken against them, and the assessor's own common director controlled both sides — the moneys never really left the group; the interest paid on the loans and received on the deposits cancelled each other out, the net result being a deduction without any real outgo — a wholly circular, colourable device.
  • On this basis the authorities invoked s. 42(1): the interest "arose indirectly" through money lent by the assessee to the person outside, and the arrangement was entered into in connection with the bringing of the money in — in fact, the whole thing was an arrangement to cream off a deduction.
  • The Tribunal's finding proceeded on a wrong legal approach and misread the AAC's finding; the Tribunal should have disregarded the facade — the revenue is entitled to lift the veil where corporate entities are used to evade tax.
④ Observations of the Court (Supreme Court)
  • The doctrine — the sentence every answer must quote: "It is true that from the juristic point of view the company is a legal personality entirely distinct from its members and the company is capable of enjoying rights and being subjected to duties which are not the same as those enjoyed or borne by its members. But in certain exceptional cases the Court is entitled to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade. For example, the Court has power to disregard the corporate entity if it is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligation."
  • Illustrations given by the Court (from the casebook's own text): Apthorpe v. Peter Schoenhofen Brewing Co. (1899) 4 TC 41 — the New York company "had only been kept in being to hold the land, since aliens were not allowed to do so under New York law"; "if the business was technically that of the New York company, the latter was merely the agent of the English company"; the Court of Appeal, "despite the argument based on Salomon's case", held the New York business to be that of the English company. Firestone Tyre & Rubber Co. v. Llewellin (1957) 1 WLR 464 — the wholly-owned English subsidiary sold to the distributors; "it was conceded that the subsidiary was a separate legal entity and not a mere emanation of the American parent… Nevertheless… it was held that the substance of the arrangement was that the American company traded in England through the agency of its subsidiary."
  • But the power is exceptional: the Court approved the settled approach — disregard of the entity requires clear evidence of a device; the Tribunal's function is to find whether the transaction was a genuine commercial one; where the facts are not sufficient to form that conclusion, the ordinary rule (entity respected) applies. On the facts (the Tribunal's finding being one of fact and the AAC's findings having been set aside on the legal test), the Court held that the Tribunal's conclusion that s. 42(1) was not attracted could not be disturbed — the assessee succeeded on the facts.
  • Hedayatullah J. (who wrote the judgment) — the framing: the question of the validity of the assessment depends on the finding whether there was a real lending; the power to lift the veil is real but the case must fall within the exceptional category; courts will not assist a colourable device, but will also not rewrite a genuine commercial transaction.
⑤ Decision
  • The appeal of the Commissioner was dismissed: on the facts found (the Tribunal's finding that the arrangement was a genuine transaction and that s. 42(1) was not attracted — a finding of fact not shown to be perverse), the amount could not be taxed.
  • The doctrine of lifting the veil for tax evasion was, however, affirmed in the widest terms — the case is today cited not for its facts but for its principle: "in certain exceptional cases the Court is entitled to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade."
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ③ (revenue) — the standard formulation: where a company is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligations, the court/authority may disregard the corporate entity and pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade.
  • But the power has limits: it is exceptional; the burden is on the revenue to establish the device; genuine commercial transactions are not to be rewritten (Meenakshi on facts; later Vodafone (2012) 6 SCC 613 — "lifting is an exception and not the rule"; McDowell (1985) 3 SCC 230 — courts will not give "judicial benediction" to "new and sophisticated legal devices to avoid tax").
  • Method: look to the substance — who really earns, who really controls, what really happens to the money — not to the documentation; subsidiaries that are mere agents/channels of the parent are treated as such (Apthorpe; Firestone).
  • Three conditions for s. 42(1)-type avoidance findings (from the statutory scheme the case applied): (i) money lent from within to outside; (ii) arrangement to bring it back/employ it; (iii) interest which "but for" the arrangement would not have arisen. The same tripartite structure is tested in every "indirect income" problem.
🧷 Case 10 · Workmen of the Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. v. The Associated Rubber Industry Ltd., (1985) 4 SCC 114
(1985) 4 SCC 114Chinnappa Reddy J.Welfare legislation · subsidiary as deviceJudicial ground ④ · bonus 16% restored
① Facts
  • The Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. (Bhavnagar) had, some years before, purchased shares of INARCO Ltd. by investing Rs. 4,50,000. It received annual dividends on those shares, shown year after year in its profit and loss account and taken into account for calculating the bonus payable to its workmen.
  • Some time in 1968, the company transferred the INARCO shares to Aril Bhavnagar Ltd. (renamed Aril Holdings Ltd.) — a wholly-owned subsidiary of the parent. Aril Holdings had no other capital except those shares, and no other business or source of income except the dividend on the INARCO shares.
  • The dividend income was no longer transferred to the parent, so it disappeared from the parent's profit and loss account; the available surplus for bonus shrank. The net result: bonus at 4% only for 1969 instead of the 16% to which the workmen would otherwise have been entitled.
  • The workmen raised an industrial dispute, claiming bonus at 16% for 1969; their case was that the transfer to Aril Holdings was "no more than a device to avoid payment of higher bonus".
  • The Industrial Tribunal and then the High Court of Gujarat (under Art. 226) held for the company: the two companies were independent companies with separate legal existence, so Aril's profits could not be treated as the parent's for computing gross profits; and there was no evidence that the transfer was a device. The workmen appealed to the Supreme Court.
  • (Later fact: Aril Holdings Ltd. was itself wound up in 1971 and amalgamated with the parent — relied on by the company as showing the creation was not a device.)
② Issues
  1. Whether the gross profits of the parent company, for computing the rate of bonus under the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 (ss. 4–7, Schedule I/II), include the dividend income earned by its wholly-owned subsidiary — the shares having been transferred to the subsidiary shortly before the accounting year.
  2. Whether the use of a wholly-owned subsidiary with no business of its own to strip dividend income out of the parent's accounts was a device to avoid payment of bonus to the workmen — and, if so, whether the court will disregard the separate personality of the subsidiary.
  3. Whether affirmative direct evidence of the device is required, or whether the facts themselves may speak.
③ Arguments — for the workmen (appellants)
  • The transfer of the INARCO shares to a wholly-owned subsidiary with no assets, business or income of its own was a mere paper transaction — the only purpose of Aril Holdings was to receive the dividend and keep it out of the parent's accounts; the dividend "did not find place in the profit and loss account of the company with the result that the available surplus… became reduced".
  • Bonus is a welfare measure; the workmen's right cannot be defeated by a corporate manoeuvre; the court must look at the substance and treat the parent and the subsidiary as one economic unit for bonus purposes.
  • "These facts speak for themselves" — direct evidence of motive is rarely available and never required where the inevitable effect is the reduction of the bonus; the amalgamation in 1971 did not prove that the original creation was honest — it showed that even the device was abandoned.
③ Arguments — for the company (respondent)
  • The two companies are distinct legal entities with separate existence (Salomon); the profits of Aril Holdings are its own, not the parent's; gross profits for the Bonus Act must be computed on the parent's own accounts.
  • There is no evidence (direct or circumstantial) that the transfer was a device to avoid bonus — the Tribunal and High Court so found; the transfer was a legitimate corporate reorganisation; the later winding up and amalgamation of Aril (1971) showed there was no scheme of avoidance.
  • A company is free to reorganise its holdings; the workmen have no right to any particular level of profit in the parent, and no claim against the subsidiary.
④ Observations of the Court (Chinnappa Reddy J.)
  • The duty of the court: "It is true that in law the Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. and Aril Holdings Ltd. were distinct legal entities having separate existence. But, in our view, that was not an end of the matter. It is the duty of the court, in every case where ingenuity is expended to avoid taxing and welfare legislations, to get behind the smoke-screen and discover the true state of affairs. The court is not to be satisfied with form and leave well alone the substance of a transaction."
  • The Meenakshi formulation adopted: "In certain exceptional cases the Court is entitled to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade… the Court has power to disregard the corporate entity if it is used for tax evasion or to circumvent tax obligation" (quoted with the Apthorpe and Firestone illustrations).
  • McDowell & Co. v. CTO (1985) 3 SCC 230 followed: "It is up to the Court to take stock to determine the nature of the new and sophisticated legal devices to avoid tax and consider whether the situation created by the devices could be related to the existing legislation… to expose the devices for what they really are and to refuse to give judicial benediction."
  • The "facts speak for themselves" finding: "A new company is created wholly owned by the principal company, with no assets of its own except those transferred to it by the principal company, with no business or income of its own except receiving dividends from shares transferred to it by the principal company and serving no purpose whatsoever except to reduce the gross profits of the principal company. These facts speak for themselves. There cannot be direct evidence that the second company was formed as a device to reduce the gross profits… An obvious purpose that is served and which stares one in the face is to reduce the amount to be paid by way of bonus to workmen. It is such an obvious device that no further evidence, direct or circumstantial, is necessary."
  • On the 1971 amalgamation: "Probably, after Aril Holdings Ltd. was created, some unforeseen difficulties arose… and it became necessary to wind it up and amalgamate it" — the circumstance did not negative the device.
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed. The dividend received by Aril Holdings Ltd. from INARCO Ltd. "should be taken into account in assessing the gross profit of the Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. for the purpose of calculating the rate of bonus payable to the workmen".
  • Declared: "the workmen of the Associated Rubber Industry Ltd., Bhavnagar are entitled to be paid bonus at the rate of 16% for the year 1969" (instead of 4%).
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ④ — avoidance of welfare legislation: the corporate veil will be lifted where a company is used to evade taxing or welfare statutes — the court gets "behind the smoke-screen" and looks at substance, not form; the separate existence of a wholly-owned subsidiary is no answer.
  • No direct proof of intent needed: where the structure created serves no purpose except to reduce the parent's profits (or otherwise evade the statute), "these facts speak for themselves" — the device is apparent from its inevitable effect. (This is the key evidentiary principle: intent may be inferred from the objective design.)
  • Group as one economic unit: a wholly-owned subsidiary with no independent business/income is treated as part of the parent for welfare-legislation purposes — the ancestor of the "economic realities / group entity" ground ⑦ (cf. KCP Ltd.; Renusagar; DHN).
  • Bonus computation: "available surplus" and "gross profits" under the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965 (ss. 4–7, Sch. I–II) are to be computed on the true economic position of the employer-unit, not on a doctored corporate structure.
🧷 Case 11 · Gilford Motor Co., Ltd. v. Horne, (1933) 1 Ch. 935 (CA) — the cloak or sham
(1933) 1 Ch 935 (CA)Hanworth M.R., Lawrence L.J., Romer L.J.Fraud / breach of covenantJudicial ground ② · injunction against company
① Facts
  • The plaintiff company, Gilford Motor Co., Ltd., bought the various parts of motor vehicles from manufacturers, assembled them on its premises and sold the products under the name "Gilford"; it also supplied spare parts for Gilford vehicles and repaired them.
  • By an agreement of 30 May 1929, Edward Bert Horne was employed as joint managing director for six years from 1 Sept 1928 (he was "of primary importance in the business"). Clause 9 of the agreement provided that he would not, during his employment or within 5 years after ceasing to be employed, solicit or endeavour to entice away from the company any person, firm or company "who at any time during or at the date of the termination of his employment" was "in the habit of dealing with the company", and would not within five years be engaged "directly or indirectly" in any similar business within a radius of three miles of the company's premises.
  • Difficulties arose; on 17 Nov 1931 Horne resigned on agreed terms (compensation for the shortened term; no release of clause 9). He then set up in business as "E.B. Horne" — "there is no doubt that his… business was properly described as the business of E.B. Horne" — and sent out circulars (through solicitors) to customers of Gilford Motors.
  • On 8 April 1932 he incorporated a private company, "J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd.": the stationery previously "E.B. Horne" was altered by blacking out "E.B." and inserting "J.M." (the initials of Horne's wife, Jessie May Horne); the company's address was 170 Hornsey Lane — Horne's own address; the 1,000 shares were held 999 by Mrs. Horne and 1 by A.V. Howard (an employee of Gilford Motors "associated with Mr. E.B. Horne"); the other director was Horne's son J.M. Horne; the company was run from Horne's premises.
  • The company sent out circulars to customers of Gilford Motors, supplied spare parts and undercut Gilford's prices. Farwell J. (trial) found: "the defendant company is obviously carried on wholly by the defendant Horne"; Mrs. Horne took no part; the son was in a subordinate position; Howard was an employee; "as one of the witnesses said… the 'boss' or the 'guv'nor'… was the defendant Horne"; "the defendant company was the channel through which the defendant Horne was carrying on his business"; "one of the reasons for the creation of that company was the fear of Mr. Horne that he might commit breaches of the covenant". Farwell J. granted an injunction against Horne only, holding the company not bound. The plaintiffs appealed against the company; Horne cross-appealed.
② Issues
  1. Was the covenant in clause 9 valid and enforceable — or void as an unreasonable restraint of trade? Does the protection of "customers" extend to persons merely in the habit of dealing with the company at the material time?
  2. Did the events of 17 Nov 1931 (resignation, new agreement, compensation) amount to a release or discharge of clause 9?
  3. The corporate question: can the injunction extend to "J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd." — a company which is a separate legal person and never a party to the covenant — where the company is merely the defendant's cloak, sham, device or stratagem for continuing the very breaches the covenant forbade?
③ Arguments — for Gilford Motor Co. (plaintiffs / appellants on the company point)
  • Clause 9 is a reasonable covenant to protect the plaintiff's business connection — its customers are a definite class identifiable on the relevant date ("persons… in the habit of dealing with the company" during the employment); it is definite in date, not uncertain; a covenant whose breach is prevented by injunction is the accepted mode of enforcement.
  • The company is Horne: "the defendant company is the channel through which the defendant Horne was carrying on his business" (Farwell J.); the formation was in the teeth of clause 9 — described by the trial judge as motivated by "the fear of Mr. Horne that he might commit breaches of the covenant"; Horne admitted soliciting customers both before and after the company's formation. Sir Walter Greaves-Lord conceded in argument (citing Lindley L.J. in Smith v. Hancock) that if the company were "a mere cloak or sham", the injunction could go against it.
  • No true release: the Nov 1931 arrangement dealt only with the shortening of the term and compensation; clause 9 was untouched and continued to bind.
③ Arguments — for Horne and J.M. Horne & Co. (defendants)
  • Restraint of trade: a covenant in restraint of trade is prima facie unenforceable; this one is too wide — "customers" is vague, the area and time restrictions excessive; it was not necessary for the protection of the plaintiff's business.
  • Release: the agreement of 17 Nov 1931 (with letters and minute-book entry) cancelled the old agreement and substituted a new one — the release of the restrictive covenant necessarily followed (Mr. Collier's argument); and "casual customers" are outside the clause.
  • Separate personality: "J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd." is a distinct legal entity; it never covenanted; Horne's covenant cannot bind it; and the company's circulars were its own acts — "in law the defendant company is a separate entity from the defendant Horne"; any breach is Horne's, not the company's; an injunction against the company would wrongly restrain an innocent third party's business.
  • Lush J.'s findings (in a related action) that Horne's dealings were honest and that the company was real were pressed; the 1971-type "legitimate organisation" argument is unavailable — but it was urged that forming a company for one's own trade is lawful.
④ Observations of the Court (Court of Appeal — Hanworth M.R.)
  • On the covenant: "I accept the proposition that a covenant in restraint of trade is prima facie one which the law will not enforce", but here it is limited and reasonable: "The covenant is definite in date; it is not uncertain… you have got a covenant which is reasonable in the sense of being necessary for the protection of the plaintiff company's business"; "The defendant has, by his own admission, solicited persons who come within the ambit of the covenant." The release argument failed: "the purpose of the second agreement was to deal with the question of the shortening of the term of the employment, and the compensation to be paid… and was not intended to deal with or release the defendant from the restrictive covenant."
  • Adopting Farwell J.'s findings verbatim: "I have recalled that portion of the judgment of Farwell J., and I wish in clear terms to say that I agree with every word of it." And then the famous conclusion: "I am quite satisfied that this company was formed as a device, a stratagem, in order to mask the effective carrying on of a business of Mr. E.B. Horne. The purpose of it was to try to enable him, under what is a cloak or a sham, to engage in business which, on consideration of the agreement which had been sent to him just about seven days before the company was incorporated, was a business in respect of which he had a fear that the plaintiffs might intervene and object."
  • Lindley L.J.'s test applied: "If the evidence admitted of the conclusion that what was being done was a mere cloak or sham and that in truth the business was being carried on… by the defendant… I certainly should not hesitate to draw that conclusion, and to grant the plaintiff relief accordingly. I do draw that conclusion; I do hold that the company was 'a mere cloak or sham'; I do hold that it was a mere device for enabling Mr. E.B. Horne to continue to commit breaches of clause 9, and under those circumstances the injunction must go against both defendants."
  • On the admissions: "Admission has been made quite frankly and candidly in this Court… that there have been circulars sent out to the customers of the Gilford Motor Company"; "there is no defence at all to the claim… unless the conduct of the two defendants can be excused" on the two grounds (validity/release), both rejected.
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal allowed; the injunction granted by the trial court was extended against both defendants — Horne and J.M. Horne & Co., Ltd. — "in the terms asked in the prayer in the statement of claim", with costs here and below.
  • Ratio of the result: Gilford succeeded at the hands of the CA not merely because Horne had broken a covenant, but because the company was a cloak — a mere device — for the evasion of an existing legal obligation; against a cloak, the court ignores the separate entity and restrains the company itself.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ② — fraud/improper conduct: where a company is created as a "cloak or sham", a mere "device, a stratagem" to enable a person to do what the law forbids him to do (break a covenant, defraud creditors, evade an obligation), the court will disregard its separate personality and grant relief against the company itself — even though the company never contracted the obligation.
  • Test: look at who really controls and benefits ("the boss or the guv'nor was the defendant Horne"), why the company was formed (fear of the covenant), and the effect (the company as "the channel" of the wrongdoing). Smith v. Hancock (Lindley L.J.): "a mere cloak or sham" in truth carrying on the defendant's business = relief.
  • The company is not faultless automatically: separate personality protects honest users (Salomon); it is no protection for the dishonest one. The veil does not shelter breaches of contractual or legal obligations carried out "through" the company.
  • Remedial consequence: injunctions and (in subsequent cases) specific performance/custody orders can run against the shell company; the wrongdoer cannot seek to exclude relief by interposing a corporation (Jones v. Lipman (1962) — company formed to defeat specific performance; and cf. Indian cases DDA v. Skipper, PNB Finance v. Shital Prasad Jain).
🧷 Case 12 · Subhra Mukherjee v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd., (2000) 3 SCC 312 — sham sale to directors' wives
(2000) 3 SCC 312S.S.M. Quadri J.Fraud on nationalisation · shamJudicial ground ② · Indian must-know
① Facts
  • The suit property was owned by M/s Nichitpur Coal Company Private Ltd. (registered under the Companies Act) — a coal company listed at Serial No. 133 of the Schedule to the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act, 1973, whose right, title and interest in the coal mines were to vest in the Central Government with effect from 1 May 1973.
  • By a Board resolution dated 21 Sept 1970, the Company resolved to sell the suit property to the appellants (Mrs. Subhra Mukherjee and others) for a consideration of Rs. 5,000. The appellants paid Rs. 7,000 to one of the directors under a receipt dated 30 Dec 1970; an agreement to sell for Rs. 7,000 (Rs. 5,000 for the bungalow + Rs. 2,000 for the land) was executed on 3 Jan 1971; and the Company executed a sale deed on 20 March 1972.
  • The appellants were the wives of the Directors of the Company, who were real brothers; the wives had no independent source of income.
  • On 1 May 1973 the Act came into force and the property vested in the Central Government; under an order of the Central Government, the vested properties stood transferred to the government company M/s Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (BCCL).
  • As the appellants did not hand over possession, BCCL initiated proceedings against them on 15 Oct 1976 under the Public Premises (Eviction of Unauthorised Occupants) Act, 1971; the appellants thereupon sued BCCL for declaration of their title. BCCL resisted: the property vested in it; the sale was sham, collusive, without consideration, brought into existence to avoid vesting.
  • Procedural history: Trial court (22 Sept 1977) — no title, suit dismissed. District Judge, Dhanbad (6 Oct 1978) — appeal allowed, sale upheld. Patna High Court (Ranchi Bench) (7 Oct 1985) — second appeal dismissed. Supreme Court (17 Aug 1993) — set aside, remitted for decision of two points: (1) whether the transaction was "bona fide and genuine" or "sham, bogus and fictitious"; (2) whether, on s. 3(1) read with s. 2(h)(xi) and Serial No. 133, the property vested in the Central Government free of all encumbrances. After remand, the High Court decided point 2 first (by Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. v. Madanlal Agrawal (1997) 1 SCC 177) and then, on point 1, restored the trial court's finding — sham and bogus — and allowed BCCL's second appeal (11 Nov 1997). The appellants came to the Supreme Court.
② Issues
  1. Was the sale of the suit property by the Company to the wives of its directors a bona fide and genuine transaction, or sham, bogus and fictitious?
  2. On the answer to point 1: did the property vest in the Central Government under s. 3(1) read with s. 2(h)(xi) and Serial No. 133 of the Schedule — free of all encumbrances?
  3. In deciding point 1, can the court pierce the veil of incorporation to see who the real parties behind the sale were — the directors and their wives — despite the Company's separate legal entity?
  4. (Procedural) Did the High Court err in deciding point 2 before point 1, against the earlier remand order, causing prejudice?
③ Arguments — for the appellants (the wives — Mr. A.K. Srivastava, senior counsel)
  • Three facts stood proved: (i) the Board resolution of 21-9-1970; (ii) payment of Rs. 7,000 to one director on 30-12-1970 (receipt Ext. 10, supported by PW 8, the company accountant, and entries in the books); (iii) the registered sale deed of 20-3-1972. In the absence of rebuttal evidence, the sale must be held genuine.
  • The finding that the resolution was antedated was unsupported: the government authorities held all the company's records and should have produced the original register; without that, the charge of antedating cannot stand.
  • The Company is a separate legal entity independent of its directors and shareholders — "repeatedly referred to the oft-quoted decision in Salomon v. Salomon & Co."; the relation of husband-wife between sellers' and buyers' side, and the smallness of the price, are irrelevant; undue emphasis was given to them; and (on procedure) the High Court should have decided point 1 first as directed.
③ Arguments — for BCCL (respondent — Mr. Anip Sachthey)
  • The suit property is in the midst of the colliery; the Directors of the Company and the appellants are no other than husbands and wives; the transaction was entered into to save the suit property from vesting in the Central Government under s. 3 of the 1973 Act — the sale was sham, collusive and without consideration (as BCCL had pleaded at every stage).
  • Intrinsic evidence of sham: the resolution of 21-9-1970 is antedated; the consideration was raised from Rs. 5,000 (resolution) to Rs. 7,000 (receipt) with no explanation; the agreement for sale was not registered though it recited payment of the full price on the very day; neither the agreement nor the sale deed followed the resolution; the purchasers never exercised ownership rights until the suit; water and electricity connections were obtained during the pendency of the suit; the property was maintained by the Company for the use of the Directors till vesting.
  • Point 2 was settled by Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. v. Madanlal Agrawal; once the sale is a sham, the property vested in the Centre free of encumbrances.
④ Observations of the Court (S.S.M. Quadri J.)
  • On the antedating: "the resolution dated 21-9-1970 was an antedated document" — on the intrinsic evidence: no mention in the registers or in the sale deed; "On the basis of the intrinsic evidence, pointed out above, the conclusion that the resolution was an antedated document appears to be irresistible." (The absence of the original records with the government could not help the appellants, since intrinsic evidence — the company's own documents — established it.)
  • Other circumstances: "though the resolution mentions the sale consideration as Rs. 5000 there is no explanation as to why it was enhanced to Rs. 7000"; "the appellants did not exercise their rights as purchasers over the suit property till the date of the filing of the suit; the water and electricity connections were obtained during the pendency of the suit…; further till the date of vesting… it was maintained by the Company for the use of the Directors"; "the agreement for sale of the suit property is not a registered document; it recites that the suit property will be sold for Rs. 7000 even though the consideration of Rs. 7000 was paid on 30-12-1970 itself and neither the agreement nor the sale deed is in terms of the resolution"; "the transaction of sale was between the husbands and the wives and… they had no independent source of their income, which cannot be ignored altogether as irrelevant".
  • Salomon acknowledged — and distinguished: "The principle laid down in Salomon's case more than a century ago in 1897 by the House of Lords that the company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers… is the foundation of joint stock company… Lifting the veil of incorporation under statutes and decisions of the courts is an equally settled position of law. This is more readily done under American law. To look at the realities of the situation and to know the real state of affairs behind the façade of the principle of the corporate personality, the courts have pierced the veil of incorporation. Where a transaction of sale of its immovable property by a company in favour of the wives of the Directors is alleged to be sham and collusive… the court will be justified in piercing the veil of incorporation to ascertain the true nature of the transaction — as to who were the real parties to the sale and whether it was genuine and bona fide or whether it was between the husbands and the wives behind the façade of separate entity of the company. That is what was done by the High Court in this case."
  • On the burden of proof: "There can be no dispute that a person who attacks a transaction as sham, bogus and fictitious must prove the same. But a plain reading of Question 1 discloses that it is in two parts; the first part says… 'whether the transaction in question is a bona fide and genuine one' which has to be proved by the appellants. It is only when this has been done that the respondent has to dislodge it by proving that it is a sham and fictitious transaction. When the circumstances of the case and the intrinsic evidence on record clearly point out that the transaction is not bona fide and genuine, it is unnecessary for the court to find out whether the respondent has led any evidence to show that the transaction is sham, bogus or fictitious."
  • On procedure: though the High Court decided point 2 first, the ultimate result flowed from the finding on point 1; the Court found no prejudice in the circumstances (and the point-2 ratio was settled in Madanlal Agrawal).
⑤ Decision
  • Appeal dismissed. The High Court was right: the sale in favour of the appellants was neither bona fide nor genuine and conferred no right or title on them.
  • In view of the finding on point 1, "the suit property remained the property of the Company and, therefore, it vested in the Central Government under Section 3(1) of the Act of 1973" — supported by Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. v. Madanlal Agrawal (1997) 1 SCC 177. BCCL's title and the eviction proceedings stood.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Fraud on a statute / nationalisation: a sale by a company to the wives of its directors (real brothers; wives without independent income) months before nationalisation, for a paltry price, with an antedated resolution, unregistered agreement and no exercise of ownership rights — is a sham; the veil is pierced to see "who were the real parties to the sale" — the husbands and wives behind the façade.
  • Salomon's limit: the separate entity is the foundation; but "lifting the veil… is an equally settled position of law" — the entity does not protect transactions which are shams designed to defeat statutory vesting (or any public obligation).
  • Burden of proof nuances: the party asserting genuineness must first establish it; only then does the opponent have to prove sham — but where intrinsic evidence and circumstances show the transaction is not bona fide, no further "positive" proof of sham is required (a point routinely tested in problem questions: "who has to prove what?").
  • Evidentiary markers of a sham (checklist for answers): family relationship of the parties · no independent income · antedated documents · unexplained price escalation · unregistered agreement · non-exercise of ownership rights · property kept for the seller's/directors' use till vesting. These markers convert a "proved" paper transaction into a sham.
🧷 Case 13 · Kapila Hingorani v. State of Bihar, (2003) 6 SCC 1 — the State cannot hide behind its company
(2003) 6 SCC 1V.N. Khare C.J. & Ruma Pal J. (two judgments)State instrumentality · wages liabilityJudicial ground ⑤ · constitutional use of the doctrine
① Facts
  • A group of petitions (led by Kapila Hingorani, and including the Bihar finances crisis petitions) concerned the collapse of several government-owned corporations in Bihar — multi-sector State corporations and subsidiaries (among them the Bihar State Water Development Corporation and similar public-sector undertakings — bodies set up under or controlled by the State of Bihar and its departments) — which had accumulated enormous unpaid dues and could no longer pay their employees' salaries and statutory dues.
  • The employees (assistant engineers, officers, workmen and other staff) sought a direction that the State of Bihar, as the real master behind these corporations, be made liable to pay their wages/salaries and terminal dues — the companies having been reduced to shell-like inability, largely because the State controlled their finances, appointments and management.
  • The State resisted: the corporations were separate legal entities; the State was not their employer; the employees' remedy lay against the corporations (in winding up or before the appropriate forums); the State could not be directed to pay out of the public exchequer.
  • Earlier jurisprudence laid the frame: Som Prakash Rekhi v. UOI (1981) 1 SCC 449 (the State's company is an instrumentality; veil lifted where the corporation is used as "the alter ego" or the State takes shelter behind it); State of UP v. Renusagar Power Co.; and the Art. 12 tests of Ramana v. IAAI (1979); contrast the line of cases holding a genuine government company distinct: Praga Tools Corpn. v. Imanual (1969), Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union v. State of Bihar, A.K. Bindal v. UOI (2003) 5 SCC 163.
② Issues
  1. Whether, in the extraordinary circumstances — public companies of the State unable to pay their employees — the State could be directed to discharge the wage liabilities of its corporations, or whether the separate legal personality of the companies is an absolute answer.
  2. When can the court lift the corporate veil to fix liability on the State for the obligations of a government company — what is the test (control, instrumentality, public function, abuse of the corporate form)?
  3. Is the claim a matter of welfare/constitutional rights (right to livelihood, dignity — Art. 21) such that technical separateness must yield?
③ Arguments — for the employees (Kapila Hingorani and other petitioners)
  • The corporations were created, financed and controlled by the State; the State appointed/dismissed their officers, dictated their finances, and used them as its departments in fact; the companies are the State's alter ego and instrumentality; the failure to pay was the consequence of the State's own mismanagement.
  • Employees are not in a position to recover from insolvent shells; the right to livelihood (Art. 21) and the State's constitutional obligations (Directive Principles; the obligation to act fairly) require the State, which created and controlled the corporations, to stand behind them.
  • The separate entity cannot be used as a shield against public obligations — "the State cannot be permitted to take shelter behind the legal fiction of a separate entity of the company while refusing to discharge its obligation to its employees" (the proposition the Court accepted on the line of Som Prakash Rekhi, Haryana SEB cases).
③ Arguments — for the State of Bihar (and the corporations)
  • Under Salomon and the Indian authorities (Praga Tools; Heavy Engineering Mazdoor Union; Freewheels), a government company is a separate legal person; its employees are employed by the company, not by the State; the State is not an employer and cannot be substituted.
  • Winding-up and other statutory remedies exist; an Art. 32 writ cannot be used to enforce private contract claims for wages against the State; payment from the exchequer outside statutory channels is impermissible; the State's financial crisis and fiscal autonomy should not be overridden by judicial directions.
  • Where the company is reasonably administered and genuinely independent, the veil must be respected (A.K. Bindal — State not liable for staff salaries of a company that was a separate entity).
④ Observations of the Court (V.N. Khare C.J.; Ruma Pal J. — separate but concurring reasoning)
  • The doctrine acknowledged on both sides: a government company is normally a distinct entity — the State is not in general liable for its debts or its employees' contracts; the separate personality is real and is not to be disturbed merely because the State holds shares.
  • But the veil yields to public policy and constitutional duties: where the corporation is the State's instrumentality/surrogate, where the State has controlled and managed its affairs and the corporation's inability to pay its employees is the product of that management, the court will not permit the State "to take shelter behind the legal fiction of a separate entity of the company while refusing to discharge its obligation to its employees".
  • The constitutional framing: the right to livelihood and the dignity of labour under Art. 21 read with the Directive Principles; employees unable to realise their wages from moribund State corporations are entitled to look to the real master — the State — which created, funded and controlled the corporations; the court issued directions for payment of arrears of salary/dues by a staged scheme, and directed the State to take over/revive or wind up the corporations without evading its responsibility.
  • The boundary (to avoid overstatement): in A.K. Bindal v. UOI (2003) 5 SCC 163 (decided the same period), where the company was genuinely independent and the State had not created the structures or taken the benefit, the Court refused to fix the State with the company's staff liabilities — the veil is preserved where no abuse and no public obligation is defeated; it is lifted where the State uses the company to avoid public responsibility.
⑤ Decision
  • The petitions were allowed in part: the Court held the State of Bihar responsible to ensure payment of the employees' dues of the government corporations, directed payment/settlement of arrears by a phased programme with timelines, and gave consequential directions (reporting to the Court, sale/revival/winding-up of the companies, priority to wages).
  • The veil of the government companies was lifted to the extent necessary to protect the employees — the State could not disown the obligations of corporations that were in substance its own instrumentalities.
⑥ Principle evolved
  • Judicial ground ⑤ — company as agent/surrogate of the State: the veil of a government company can be lifted where the corporation is the State's instrumentality/alter ego and its separate personality is pleaded to defeat justice, public policy or constitutional rights — the State cannot hide behind its creature to avoid paying wages and dues.
  • The two-limbed test: (i) control/instrumentality — is the company in substance the State's department/altered ego (shareholding, control of management and finance, public functions)? (ii) abuse/consequence — is the separate personality being used to evade a public obligation or to produce an unjust result? Both must point the same way (Som Prakash Rekhi; Mysore Paper Mills; New Tirupur; contrast Praga Tools, LIC v. Escorts, A.K. Bindal, Balmer Lawrie on the "no" side).
  • The constitutional dimension: this is the modern, "social" use of the doctrine — Art. 21 (livelihood) and welfare obligations give the court jurisdiction to pierce even where no fraud exists; the veil protects honest commerce, not public irresponsibility.
  • Indian government companies: the same doctrine applies across the public sector — often coupled with the Art. 12 "instrumentality" test of Ramana; and with the statutory accountability of ss. 2(45), 394–395 (CAG audit, reports to legislature) of the Companies Act, 2013.

7.1 Actual PYQ (December 2024, DU — Question 1): Companies and Fundamental Rights under Article 19(1)(g)

Source: Company-Law-Exam-Checklist (prepared from the December 2024 LB-303 paper) — Question 1 of that paper tested exactly this unit's citizenship/rights chain, with the facts of a State action affecting a company and its shareholders.

Model answer — "A State measure restricts the trade/business activity of a company. Can the company, or its shareholders, invoke Article 19(1)(g)? Advise." 10–15 marks
(I) Introduction — the two questions

The problem raises two distinct questions: (i) whether the company — an artificial juristic person — is a "citizen" entitled to invoke Art. 19(1)(g); and (ii) whether the citizens who stand behind it (shareholders, directors, editors) may assert their own fundamental rights when the State action strikes at the company through which they carry on their trade or business.

(II) The company is not a citizen — but it is a person

"Citizen" in Part II (Arts. 5–11) and the Citizenship Act, 1955 contemplate only natural persons. In State Trading Corporation of India Ltd. v. CTO, AIR 1963 SC 811, a company all of whose shares were held by the President of India and two Secretaries was held not to be a citizen: "Neither the provisions of the Constitution, Part II, nor of the Citizenship Act either confer the right of citizenship on, or recognise as citizen, any person other than a natural person." Hidayatullah J. put it memorably — "if all of them are citizens of India, the company does not become a citizen… any more than, if all are married, the company would be a married person." Following this, in TELCO v. State of Bihar, AIR 1965 SC 40, the company's own petition under Art. 19(1)(f) and (g) failed: the company is distinct from its shareholders, and the veil is not lifted to give the company citizens' rights — "what the company cannot do directly, it cannot do indirectly." Hence the company itself cannot invoke Art. 19(1)(g); it can, however, claim rights guaranteed to "all persons" (Art. 14; Art. 21 through its agents; no taxation without authority — Art. 265).

(III) The shareholders' right — the qualitative test

The rigour of STC/TELCO was relaxed in R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (Bank Nationalisation case), (1970) 3 SCR 530 (10:1). A shareholder, depositor or director cannot petition merely because the company's rights are infringed; but the test is qualitative, not formal — "if the State action impairs the right of the shareholders as well as of the company, the Court will not, concentrating merely upon the technical operation of the action, deny itself jurisdiction to grant relief." In Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India, (1972) 2 SCC 788, the Newsprint Policy (page limits and circulation restrictions) was struck down as violating Art. 19(1)(a), and the locus standi of shareholders/editors was upheld: "the fundamental rights of citizens are not lost when they associate to form a company"; the shareholders' rights are "equally and necessarily affected if the rights of the company are affected"; the rights are "projected and manifested" through the corporation, and "the shareholders speak through their editors". The corporate veil can be lifted to see whether the shareholders' rights are impaired.

(IV) Application to the problem

For the company: no direct Art. 19(1)(g) claim — the petition must be examined for impairment of the shareholders' rights. For the shareholders (and directors/managers who are citizens): if the measure hits the company's business and thereby the shareholders' right to carry on business through it, the qualitative test is satisfied and the petition lies — the company may acquire standing by impleading a shareholder with itself (Godhra Electricity Co. Ltd. v. State of Gujarat (1975) 1 SCC 199). If the restriction is a reasonable one within Art. 19(6), no relief lies; if arbitrary/disproportionate, the measure will fall, as in Bennett Coleman.

(V) Conclusion

A company is a legal person but never a citizen; yet the citizens behind it do not forfeit their Art. 19 rights by associating into a company. The company cannot invoke Art. 19(1)(g) directly; the shareholders can, provided the State action impairs their rights as well (Cooper's qualitative test) — the corporate veil being lifted for that limited purpose (Bennett Coleman).

7.2 Actual PYQ (December 2024, DU — Question 2): Workers' bonus and transfer of assets to a wholly-owned subsidiary

Model answer — "The principal company transfers its dividend-earning shares to a newly created wholly-owned subsidiary; the dividend income disappears from its P&L account; the bonus rate falls. Can the workmen claim that the dividend income be included in the gross profits? Advise." 10–15 marks
(I) The facts in law

Under the Payment of Bonus Act, 1965, bonus is computed on the gross profits (Schedule I/II), reduced to "available surplus" and then to "allocable surplus" (67% for companies). Dividend income credited to the P&L account forms part of the gross profits. If the company transfers the dividend-earning assets to a wholly-owned subsidiary that has no business, no assets and no income of its own, the dividends vanish from the parent's P&L and the bonus payable falls. The subsidiary is, formally, a separate legal entity — the question is whether the court will respect that separateness.

(II) The doctrine

A company is a separate person (Salomon v. Salomon & Co. (1897) AC 22 (HL)); but the separateness fails when the entity is used as a device to avoid statutory obligations. The Supreme Court is entitled, "in certain exceptional cases… to lift the veil of corporate entity and to pay regard to the economic realities behind the legal facade" — CIT v. Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 819. And "it is the duty of the court, in every case where ingenuity is expended to avoid taxing and welfare legislation, to get behind the smoke-screen and discover the true state of affairs" — Workmen v. Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. (1985) 4 SCC 114.

(III) The authority — Workmen v. Associated Rubber Industry Ltd.

On exactly these facts (INARCO dividends moved to wholly-owned Aril Holdings Ltd.; bonus falling from 16% to 4%), Chinnappa Reddy J. held that although the two companies were distinct legal entities, "that was not an end of the matter". The subsidiary had "no assets of its own except those transferred to it by the principal company, no business or income of its own except receiving dividends… serving no purpose whatsoever except to reduce the gross profits of the principal company. These facts speak for themselves… It is such an obvious device that no further evidence, direct or circumstantial, is necessary." The dividends were added back and the workmen were awarded bonus at 16%.

(IV) Application & conclusion

Applying the same test, the transfer is not "restructuring" but avoidance: the subsidiary had no independent business purpose, the dividend income alone explains it, the timing coincides with the bonus computation, and the result is a reduced statutory payment. The veil is lifted; the dividend income is included in the gross profits; the workmen are entitled to bonus at the earlier (higher) rate. Advise the company: a genuine commercial purpose (independent business, real assets, arm's-length terms) and disclosure would have been the only safe course; advise the workmen: the claim succeeds.

7.3 Standard long-answer PYQ 1 — "The rule in Salomon's case is the foundation of company law, yet it is not absolute. Discuss the doctrine of separate legal personality and its limits."

Model answer 15 marks · Long answer
(I) Introduction

The cardinal distinction of company law is that a company, upon incorporation, becomes a legal person separate and distinct from its members. Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013 gives statutory force to this: from the date in the certificate of incorporation the subscribers "shall be a body corporate… with perpetual succession, power to hold property, contract, sue and be sued". The leading authority is Salomon v. Salomon & Co., Ltd. (1897) AC 22 (HL).

(II) The rule — Salomon

Salomon incorporated a company for his boot business; he held 20,001 of 20,007 shares and £10,000 secured debentures as part of the price; the company failed and unsecured creditors (£7,733) were left with assets of about £6,000. The Court of Appeal held the company a "device to defraud creditors" (Lindley L.J.), but the House of Lords reversed: "the company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers to the memorandum… the company is not in law the agent or trustee of the subscribers… one share is enough" (Lord Macnaghten); "the sole guide must be the statute itself… motives of those who took part in the promotion are absolutely irrelevant" (Lord Halsbury L.C.). The debentures were valid; Salomon was not liable. Ratio: a legally incorporated company is an independent person; the statute enacts nothing as to the balance of power or the independence of the seven members.

(III) Consequences

Separate property (shareholders have no interest in it — Kondoli Tea Co., In re (1886) 13 Cal 43, the first Indian decision); limited liability; perpetual succession ("members may come and go, the company can go on for ever"); capacity to sue and be sued; separate taxation (Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 74); the company is not an agent or trustee of its members (Freewheels (India) Ltd. v. Veda Mittra, AIR 1969 Del 258). Lee v. Lee's Air Farming Ltd. (1960) 3 All ER 420 carries the principle to its logical end: Lee, governing director and 2,999/3,000 shareholder, could validly be employed by his own company as chief pilot, and his widow recovered workmen's compensation — one person, two capacities, one personality.

(IV) The limits — lifting the veil (judicial)

"Adherence to the Salomon principle will not be doggedly followed where that would cause an unjust result." The court disregards the company where the corporate form is abused:

  1. Determination of character: Daimler Co. v. Continental Tyre & Rubber Co. (1916) AC 307 — an English company controlled by Germans resident in Germany during WWI was an "enemy"; the courts look at persons in de facto control. Contra: People's Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder (1908).
  2. Fraud/improper conduct: Gilford Motor Co. v. Horne (1933) 1 Ch 935 — company a "cloak or sham" to breach a covenant; injunction against it; Jones v. Lipman (1962); Subhra Mukherjee v. BCCL (2000) 3 SCC 312 — sale to directors' wives to defeat nationalisation.
  3. Tax evasion: In re Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit, AIR 1927 Bom 371 — four companies created to split income; Apthorpe v. Schoenhofen Brewing Co. (1899); power affirmed in CIT v. Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 819.
  4. Avoidance of welfare legislation: Workmen v. Associated Rubber Industry Ltd. (1985) 4 SCC 114 — subsidiary created to cut bonus; "get behind the smoke-screen"; bonus restored at 16%.
  5. Company as agent/surrogate of the State: Som Prakash Rekhi v. UOI (1981) 1 SCC 449; Kapila Hingorani v. State of Bihar (2003) 6 SCC 1 — the State cannot shelter behind a government company to avoid paying wages.
  6. Protection of shareholders' fundamental rights: R.C. Cooper (1970) 3 SCR 530; Bennett Coleman (1972) 2 SCC 788.
  7. Economic realities/group entities: State of U.P. v. Renusagar Power Co. (1988) 4 SCC 59; Vodafone (2012) 6 SCC 613.
(V) Statutory exceptions

The legislature itself pierces the veil: S. 3A (minimum members — several liability), S. 12(9) (misdescription of name), S. 339 (fraudulent trading), Ss. 7(5)–(7) (fraud in incorporation), Ss. 34–36 (prospectus), S. 127 (deposits), S. 447 (fraud), S. 129(3) (consolidated accounts); and under other statutes (IT Act s. 179; s. 85 IT Act; EP Act s. 16 etc. — "person in charge" liability).

(VI) Conclusion

The separate legal entity is a privilege (concession theory) extended for the encouragement of enterprise; it is neither absolute nor a licence. The courts lift the veil when the entity is a mere cloak or sham, or when its recognition would defeat justice, revenue or welfare — the company itself remains valid, only the immunity is withdrawn (Premlata Bhatia v. UOI (2004); Vodafone). Salomon stands, but not as a fortress.

7.5 Short-answer PYQs with model answers (5–6 marks each)

SA-1 · "A company is not a citizen of India." Discuss. (Very frequently asked)

Answer. A company is a legal person but not a citizen — neither under the Constitution (Part II, Arts. 5–11) nor under the Citizenship Act, 1955, both of which deal only with natural persons. In State Trading Corporation v. CTO, AIR 1963 SC 811 — a company whose shares were held by the President of India and two Secretaries — the Supreme Court held that a corporation has nationality, domicile and residence but cannot be a citizen: "if all of them are citizens of India, the company does not become a citizen… any more than, if all are married, the company would be a married person" (Hidayatullah J.). In TELCO v. State of Bihar, AIR 1965 SC 40 the court reaffirmed that the company is distinct from shareholders and the veil cannot be lifted to let the company claim citizens' rights — what is not allowed directly is not allowed indirectly. Consequence: a company cannot invoke Art. 19; but it can claim rights available to all persons (Art. 14; Art. 21; Art. 265 guarantees no taxation without authority), and its citizen-shareholders may assert their own rights under the R.C. Cooper qualitative test and Bennett Coleman (veil lifted to protect them). Conclusion: nationality is a function of incorporation; citizenship is a political status of natural persons; the two never merge.

SA-2 · "It is a logical consequence of Salomon's case that a man may be both master and servant." Explain with Lee v. Lee's Air Farming Ltd.

Answer. In Lee v. Lee's Air Farming Ltd. (1960) 3 All ER 420 (PC), Geoffrey Lee held 2,999 of 3,000 shares and was the governing director of the company, and was appointed chief pilot under Art. 33. He was killed in a crash; his widow claimed under the New Zealand Workers' Compensation Act, 1922; the insurers urged that a man cannot employ himself. The Privy Council held that the company and Lee were separate legal persons: Lee could act for the company and the company could employ him. "In law the company appointed him"; the contract of service was valid; Lee was a "worker"; compensation was payable. Ratio: separate personality creates two capacities in one natural person — as director he acts for the company, as employee the company is his master; capacity is distinct from personality. The case is thus an application, not an exception, of Salomon, and shows the practical value of the doctrine: companies may employ their controlling shareholders, and statutory employee welfare protections apply to such employees.

SA-3 · "Can a company be an 'enemy'?" — Daimler and determination of character

Answer. A company is a creature of law — "it can be neither loyal nor disloyal; it can be neither friend nor enemy". But in Daimler Co. Ltd. v. Continental Tyre & Rubber Co. (G.B.) Ltd. (1916) AC 307 (HL), the English company's shares were overwhelmingly held by, and all its directors were, Germans resident in Germany during WWI. The House of Lords held that where the persons in de facto control reside in, or act under the control of, an enemy country, the company itself assumes an enemy character, and it cannot maintain an action — to allow it would be to use the company "as a machinery" for giving money to the enemy, "monstrous and against public policy". Principle: the court lifts the veil to determine the character of the company from its real controllers. The exercise is discretionary: in People's Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder (1908) the court refused to treat a company of coloured members as a "coloured person" for a restraint covenant — no public danger, veil kept. Conclusion: the enemy-character doctrine is the classic instance of "determination of character" as a ground of veil-lifting; the same technique is used today in licensing, disqualification and public-interest cases.

SA-4 · "The machinery of incorporation cannot be used to defraud creditors or defeat the law." — the fraud ground (Gilford / Subhra Mukherjee)

Answer. The corporate entity "is wholly incapable of being strained to an illegal or fraudulent purpose"; where it is formed to defeat or circumvent the law, to defraud creditors, or to evade obligations, the courts disregard it. In Gilford Motor Co. v. Horne (1933) 1 Ch. 935, a former managing director bound by a non-solicitation covenant carried on business through a company he controlled; the court called it "a device, a stratagem… a mere cloak or sham" and granted an injunction against both. In Jones v. Lipman (1962), a company formed only to defeat specific performance was ordered to convey. In Subhra Mukherjee v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (2000) 3 SCC 312, the Supreme Court pierced the veil where a coal company, just before nationalisation, sold its property to the wives of its directors for a nominal price on an antedated resolution: the sale was a sham to defeat vesting under the Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act, 1973. Ratio: Salomon is not absolute — it "will not be applied where it would be used as a vehicle for perpetrating fraud or defeating statutory schemes". Conclusion: fraud (like tax evasion and welfare-avoidance) is the classic judicial trigger; the court examines the real parties, timing, consideration and purpose behind the transaction.

SA-5 · Short notes on kinds of companies — OPC · Small company · Government company · Holding & subsidiary · Foreign company · Guarantee company
One Person Company — S. 2(62), S. 3(1)(c) A company with one person as member, necessarily a private company; the memorandum names a nominee (with written consent) who becomes member on the member's death/incapacity; nominee may be changed (2021 Amendment); one director suffices (S. 149); one board meeting per half-year (S. 173(5)); may convert to private/public voluntarily; only natural persons (not minors) may be members; liability limited; introduced on the J.J. Irani Committee recommendation.
Small company — S. 2(85) A non-public company with paid-up capital ≤ ₹4 crore or turnover ≤ ₹40 crore (statutory base ₹50 lakh/₹2 crore, raised by notification; 2022 Rules). Excluded: holding/subsidiary companies, S. 8 companies, bodies governed by special Acts. Privileges: less onerous meetings, audit, accounts and committee requirements.
Government company — S. 2(45) ≥ 51% of paid-up share capital held by the Central Government, State Government(s), or jointly, including a subsidiary of such a company. Auditor appointed by CAG (S. 139(5)); CAG comments on audit; annual report laid before Parliament/Legislature (S. 394). Separate entity — employees are not civil servants (Praga Tools); but an instrumentality of the State may be sued by writ (Som Prakash Rekhi; Kapila Hingorani).
Holding & subsidiary — Ss. 2(46), 2(87) Holding = company of which others are subsidiaries. Subsidiary = company whose board composition the holding controls, or in which it holds more than half the equity/voting power; nominees count; chain rule (subsidiary of subsidiary); shares held in fiduciary capacity, under debenture/trust deeds, or by way of security by a lender are disregarded; layer limits; consolidated accounts (S. 129(3)); subsidiary cannot hold shares of holding (S. 19); entities remain distinct absent abuse (Freewheels; Associated Rubber).
Foreign company — S. 2(42) Company incorporated outside India which (a) has a place of business in India (by itself or through an agent, physically or through electronic mode) and (b) conducts business activity in India. Obligations: file constitution/directors'/service documents within 30 days (S. 380); accounts yearly (S. 381); display name + country of incorporation (S. 382); prospectus rules (S. 390); non-compliance bars suits until cured. Incorporated-in-India companies with foreign shareholders are not foreign companies.
Guarantee company — S. 2(21) Liability limited to the amount members undertake to contribute on winding up (fixed in memorandum). Often without share capital (clubs, associations, stock exchanges, S. 8 charities); articles must state member count; S. 4(7): any provision giving non-members a right to participate in profits is void; reduction rules apply if it has share capital; cannot buy back shares (S. 67); membership admission is a matter of the organisation's discipline (Narendra Kumar Agarwal v. Saroj Maloo (1995) 6 SCC 114).

7.6 Problem-type PYQ — the Dinshaw Petit pattern (exam favourite)

Problem — "R, a wealthy businessman with dividend and interest income of ₹2 crore, forms six private companies, transfers his investments to them, and the companies pay the income back to him as 'loans', thereby reducing his tax. The department assesses the whole income in his hands. He challenges the assessment, relying on Salomon. Decide." 10 marks
Answer framework
  1. R's argument: each company is a separate person (S. 9; Salomon); the income belongs to the companies; the amounts received by him are loans; the assessment on him is bad.
  2. The department's answer: the companies are a pretence; the "loans" have no resolutions, no interest, no security, no vouchers — the receipts are his income under a different label.
  3. The authority: In re Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit, AIR 1927 Bom. 371 — four companies created "purely and simply as a means of avoiding super-tax… they did no business, but were created simply as a legal entity to ostensibly receive the dividends and interests and to hand them over to the assessee as pretended loans"; the whole income was taxable in his hands. The court will look at economic realities (CIT v. Sri Meenakshi Mills Ltd., AIR 1967 SC 819), and colourable devices get no judicial benediction (McDowell & Co. v. CTO (1985) 3 SCC 230; Vodafone (2012) — but only where the arrangement is a sham/device; a genuine structure is not rewritten).
  4. Application: six companies, no business of their own, income routed back as unsecured "loans" — on the Dinshaw Petit tests (no board resolutions, no interest, no repayment terms) the veil is lifted; alternatively the receipts are deemed dividend under s. 2(22)(e) IT Act where the companies are closely held.
  5. Conclusion: the assessment stands; the companies' separate personality is disregarded for this purpose; R pays tax on the entire income. But if R had shown genuine purpose, bona fide business and real loan documentation, the veil would not have been lifted (Vodafone; contrast Bacha Guzdar).

7.7 Problem-type PYQ — the creditor / one-man company pattern

Problem — "X, a sole trader, incorporates a private company, transfers his business to it, and takes back 99% of the shares plus a charge on the assets for the price. The company becomes insolvent; trade creditors are unpaid. Advise the creditors." 10 marks

Advise as follows. (i) On Salomon, the creditors lose: the company is validly incorporated, X's 99% holding and the secured debentures/charge are unimpeachable; the secured creditor is paid before unsecured creditors. The company is not X's agent; his motives are irrelevant (Lord Halsbury; Lord Macnaghten). (ii) Exceptions to explore: fraud in incorporation (S. 7(5)–(7)) — available only if the incorporation itself was fraudulent; fraudulent trading (S. 339) — if the business was carried on with intent to defraud creditors (Re William C. Leitch Bros.); minimum-members default (S. 3A) — if the company was carried on below two members for over six months; wrong name (S. 12(9)). (iii) If the transfer was a sham to defeat existing creditors with no genuine business purpose, the veil may be lifted in fraud cases (Gilford; Jones v. Lipman; Subhra Mukherjee) — but on facts like Salomon itself (a genuine surrender of the business for a full price; failure due to trade conditions) there is no fraud. (iv) Conclusion: the creditors' real lesson is the pricing and security structure of promoter-financed acquisitions; absent fraud, the Salomon shield stands.

8.4 Self-test — 20 rapid questions (tap to reveal)

Q1–Q5
  1. Who said "the sole guide must be the statute itself"? — Lord Halsbury L.C. in Salomon.
  2. What did Lord Macnaghten say about one share? — "One share is enough"; the statute enacts nothing about balance of power or independence of subscribers.
  3. Which section gives effect of registration? — S. 9, Companies Act 2013.
  4. Was Salomon's business loss his fault? — No; strikes in the boot trade (the liquidator's own witness).
  5. First Indian case on separate entity? — Kondoli Tea Co., In re, ILR (1886) 13 Cal 43 (predates Salomon).
Q6–Q10
  1. Which case says a company cannot be a "married person"? — STC v. CTO (Hidayatullah J.).
  2. What did TELCO say about indirect claims? — What the company cannot do directly it cannot do indirectly.
  3. The "qualitative test" is from which case? — R.C. Cooper v. UOI (Bank Nationalisation), (1970) 3 SCR 530.
  4. The four restrictions of the 1972–73 Newsprint Policy? — no new paper/edition; 10-page ceiling; no circulation-page adjustment; no inter-changeability.
  5. In Bennett Coleman, who "speaks through the editors"? — The shareholders ("the individual rights of freedom of speech and expression of editors, directors and shareholders are all exercised through their newspapers").
Q11–Q15
  1. Who were the shareholders of Continental Tyre (GB)? — A German parent holding the bulk; all but one of the remaining shares and all directorships held by Germans resident in Germany.
  2. What does Daimler say a company CAN be? — Neither friend nor enemy — but it may assume an enemy character via de facto control.
  3. Why did Lee's widow win? — The company and Lee were separate persons; the company, through Lee as agent, validly employed Lee; he was a "worker".
  4. How many companies did Sir Dinshaw form, and why? — Four private companies, purely to avoid super-tax by splitting income and routing it back as pretended loans.
  5. Which section did Meenakshi Mills involve? — S. 42(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1922 (income deemed to arise in the taxable territory).
Q16–Q20
  1. What was the bonus rate before and after the Aril Holdings device? — 16% → 4%; restored to 16% after lifting the veil.
  2. Was there direct evidence of the device in Associated Rubber? — No; it was "such an obvious device that no further evidence… is necessary".
  3. In Gilford v. Horne, what was the company called? — "A mere cloak or sham… a device, a stratagem"; an injunction issued against both Horne and the company.
  4. To whom did Nichitpur Coal sell, when, and for how much? — To the wives of its directors (real brothers), deed dated 20-3-1972, for ₹7,000, on an antedated resolution, to defeat vesting under the 1973 Act (effective 1-5-1973).
  5. What does Kapila Hingorani add to the veil doctrine? — Government companies can be pierced when the separate personality defeats justice/ public policy; the State cannot plead lack of resources to avoid wages.
