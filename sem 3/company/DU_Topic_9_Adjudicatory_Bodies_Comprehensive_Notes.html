01 · How to study this topic

A tribunal must be independent enough to adjudicate, but it must also remain within the jurisdiction Parliament has given it. These are the two organising ideas of DU Topic 9.

This guide follows the prescribed syllabus: National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT)—constitution, powers, jurisdiction, procedure and judicial review; Companies Act, 2013, sections 407–434. The two prescribed decisions are Madras Bar Association v. Union of India & Anr., WP(C) 1072/2013, 14 May 2015, and Embassy Property Developments Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka & Ors., MANU/SC/1661/2019.

Source hierarchy and the different layers of law

  1. DU-first case analysis. The uploaded LB-303 Company Law, 2025 Final extracts are the primary teaching source. The MBA dossier follows the issues, submissions and reasoning reproduced there. The Embassy dossier follows DU’s selected paragraphs, with clearly identified full-judgment supplementation for omitted arguments and procedural steps.
  2. Statutory explanation. Sections 407–434 are explained individually, with conditions, consequences, exceptions and applications. The uploaded textbook discussion is supplementary; separately checked section texts correct stale details in it.
  3. Historical holding is not today’s appointment notification. The 2015 judgment tests the then-existing Act against the binding 2010 directions. Later amendments and tribunal-reform legislation must not be attributed to the 2015 Court. The parent-Act appointment and service provisions are therefore explicitly labelled.
  4. Later-law appendix. There are important developments after the 2025 DU compilation, including the November 2025 tribunal judgment and the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026. These are isolated in Chapter 17. Do not use the unrevised 2021 wording of section 417A as an unqualified statement of present NCLAT service law.

02 · Institutional foundations: sections 407, 408 and 410

What is an adjudicatory body?

An adjudicatory body resolves disputes by applying legal standards to facts and issuing authoritative decisions affecting rights, liabilities or status. NCLT and NCLAT are statutory judicial/quasi-judicial institutions, not company-management departments. Their orders may alter membership rights, sanction a restructuring, grant oppression relief, commence or supervise insolvency, or wind up a company—but only within the jurisdiction conferred by the relevant enactment.

The specialist system brings together legal expertise and relevant commercial, financial and accounting expertise. Its aims include concentration of company-law work, consistent decisions, accessible specialist adjudication and speed. These are institutional objectives, not proof that every case is decided faster or that every dispute involving a company belongs before NCLT.

Section 407 — Definitions: get the institutional vocabulary rightSource ↗

Section 407 applies to Chapter XXVII unless the context otherwise requires. It defines five expressions:

  • Chairperson: the Chairperson of the Appellate Tribunal.
  • Judicial Member: a member of NCLT or NCLAT appointed as such; includes the President or Chairperson, as appropriate.
  • Member: either a Judicial or Technical Member of the Tribunal/Appellate Tribunal; includes its President/Chairperson.
  • President: the President of the Tribunal, i.e. NCLT.
  • Technical Member: a member of NCLT or NCLAT appointed as such.

Why the inclusions matter: A provision concerning “Members” may extend to the institutional head because the statutory definition includes that office. Equally, a provision specifically distinguishing the President from other members must be read according to its context. Calling the NCLT head “Chairperson” and the NCLAT head “President” reverses the Act’s terminology.

Section 408 — Constitution of National Company Law TribunalSource ↗

The Central Government must constitute NCLT by notification, effective from the date specified in the notification. It consists of a President and such number of Judicial and Technical Members as the Central Government considers necessary; appointments are also by notification.

The operative jurisdictional phrase is that NCLT exercises the powers and functions conferred by or under the Companies Act or any other law for the time being in force. This is why NCLT can act as an adjudicating authority under the IBC. It does not mean that NCLT automatically acquires every power found in every law: a legal source must actually confer the particular jurisdiction.

Distinguish three events: enactment of an enabling section; appointment/constitution through notification; actual institutional functioning. The 2015 MBA judgment concerns a time when the bodies were not yet functioning. The tribunals were constituted with effect from 1 June 2016. It is historically wrong to say the Supreme Court was reviewing an established NCLT’s 2015 case-management performance.

Section 410 — Constitution of National Company Law Appellate TribunalSource ↗

The Central Government constitutes NCLAT by notification. It consists of a Chairperson and Judicial and Technical Members appointed by the Government. Its principal Companies Act role is to hear appeals from NCLT, with other appellate work conferred by law.

The checked amended text of section 410 also refers to orders of the National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) and to Competition Commission decisions/directions/orders within the appeal framework identified by section 53A of the Competition Act, 2002. Its IBC appellate jurisdiction comes from IBC section 61, not from treating all insolvency appeals as ordinary Companies Act appeals.

Textbook correction: The former ceiling of eleven members in section 410 was omitted in 2021. It is not an enduring current cap. Older passages referring to Competition Act section 53N in this context also require correction; the amended reference is section 53A. Neither change is a holding of the prescribed 2015 case.

Historical consolidation: understand it, do not oversimplify it

The tribunal project sought to consolidate company-law adjudication previously distributed among the Company Law Board, courts and industrial-rehabilitation institutions. But saying “everything before every old authority automatically moved to NCLT” is inaccurate. Section 434 and the transfer rules govern categories and stages of pending court proceedings. The old sick-industrial-company regime underwent separate repeal/IBC-related changes; the former Companies Act revival chapter is not a live substitute for CIRP.

DU connection: MBA explains why replacement of court work demands credible judicial standards. Embassy explains why receiving specified court-like functions does not transform NCLT into a superior constitutional court.

03 · Qualifications and selection: sections 409, 411 and 412

Parent-Act framework • read with the 2015 constitutional decision • NCLAT later-service override in Chapter 17

Section 409 — Qualification of President and Members of NCLTSource ↗

President

The President must be a person who is or has been a Judge of a High Court for five years. This is not the same formulation as being merely qualified for appointment as a High Court judge. Nor is the requirement simply five years of legal practice.

Judicial Member: three alternative routes

A Judicial Member must be a person who:

  1. is or has been a Judge of a High Court; or
  2. is or has been a District Judge for at least five years; or
  3. has, for at least ten years, been an advocate of a court.

The explanation concerning the advocate route permits the specified periods of judicial office, tribunal membership or a Union/State post requiring special knowledge of law, held after becoming an advocate, to be counted in computing that qualifying period. Do not convert this explanation into a standalone power to appoint any legal-service official as a judicial member merely by designation.

Difference from the President: section 409(1) expressly requires five years as a High Court judge for President. Section 409(2)(a), the Judicial Member route, does not repeat that five-year requirement for a High Court judge.

How to apply alternatives: A candidate needs to satisfy a legally available route, not all six simultaneously. A cost accountant is not required additionally to have held Secretary rank. Conversely, a Joint Secretary cannot rely on the professional-practice clauses merely because official work involved reading balance sheets.

Why technical members are constitutionally sensitive

Technical expertise is justified when it assists adjudication of specialised issues; it is not a licence to replace the judicial institution with a bureaucracy. A civil servant’s administrative competence, a professional’s technical skill and a judge’s adjudicatory independence are related but different qualities. Where jurisdiction previously exercised by courts moves to a tribunal, the qualifications must preserve the standard and independence appropriate to that work.

Section 411 — Qualifications of Chairperson and Members of NCLATSource ↗

The parent-Act baseline, relevant to understanding the prescribed case, is:

  • Chairperson: a person who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court or Chief Justice of a High Court.
  • Judicial Member: a person who is or has been a High Court judge, or is a Judicial Member of NCLT for five years.
  • Technical Member: proven ability, integrity and standing, with special knowledge and professional experience of not less than twenty-five years in industrial finance, industrial management, industrial reconstruction, investment and accountancy.

The original broad specialist wording was constitutionally challenged in MBA; the later amended parent-Act wording narrows the fields. For an actual NCLAT appointment today, stop here and consult section 417A and the later framework, rather than treating this table as the complete operative recruitment notice. Chapter 17 explains the 2026 replacement and its rule-making structure.

Section 412 — Selection of members: corrected parent-Act architectureSource ↗

Consultation route under subsection (1)

The President of NCLT, Chairperson of NCLAT and Judicial Members of NCLAT are appointed by the Central Government after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, under the parent scheme. This must be distinguished from the selection-committee route in subsection (2).

Committee route under subsection (2)

The corrected parent provision provides for NCLT members and NCLAT Technical Members to be appointed on the recommendation of a selection committee consisting of:

  1. Chief Justice of India or nominee — Chairperson.
  2. A senior Supreme Court judge or Chief Justice of a High Court — Member.
  3. Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs — Member.
  4. Secretary, Ministry of Law and Justice — Member.

The Chairperson has a casting vote under subsection (2A). The MCA Secretary is the convener. The committee determines its procedure for recommending suitable candidates; an appointment is not invalid merely because of a vacancy or defect in the committee’s constitution.

Original defect, judicial remedy, statutory correction

The 2013 scheme had five members: two judicial and three executive, including the Secretary in the Department of Financial Services, Ministry of Finance. This allowed executive numerical dominance and lacked the constitutionally required assurance of judicial primacy. MBA rejected the argument that a history of unanimous decisions could substitute for a sound statutory structure. The amended four-member structure and casting vote are a legislative correction, not proof that the original five-member committee was valid.

Hypothetical application: Four selectors divide two–two between candidates. A judicial Chairperson’s casting vote matters precisely at this point. If the law instead gives three executive members a routine numerical majority, saying “we usually reach consensus” does not remove the structural vulnerability. Constitutional safeguards are designed for disagreement, not only harmonious practice.

High-scoring distinction: Government appointment is not, by itself, executive control of adjudication. The constitutional question concerns the effective selection structure, judicial primacy, qualifications, tenure and independence. Also distinguish the 2015 parent-Act remedy from the separate 2026 NCLAT search-cum-selection framework.

04 · Tenure, independence and institutional continuity: sections 413–418

Section 413 — Term of office: parent-Act textSource ↗

The parent Companies Act provides the following scheme. Its NCLAT limb must be read with section 417A; it is included here to explain the syllabus provision, not to suppress subsequent legislation.

Office Parent-Act term Upper age in parent text Reappointment
NCLT President Five years from entering office 67 Eligible for another five-year term, subject to age
NCLT other member Five years from entering office 65 Eligible for another five-year term, subject to age
NCLAT Chairperson Five years from entering office 70 Parent text permits another five-year term; later regime governs where applicable
NCLAT other member Five years from entering office 67 Same temporal qualification

The section’s parent text also states a minimum age of fifty for appointment as a member and restricts retention of a lien with the parent cadre/ministry/department to not more than one year. The parent text and the separate tribunal-reform minimum-age litigation must not be collapsed into one undifferentiated table. The 2026 common framework does not reproduce the invalidated general fifty-year floor; NCLT is not automatically brought into that common framework. For appointment advice, use the particular institution’s governing law and applicable judicial directions, not this educational baseline alone.

Constitutional significance of tenure and lien

A very short term may discourage good candidates, undermine institutional experience and make the prospect of reappointment a source of pressure. A continuing link with the parent executive department may affect the member’s perceived independence. MBA’s discussion of the 2010 directions therefore treats meaningful tenure and a limited lien as structural safeguards. Reappointment eligibility is not a right to reappointment. Age limits also constrain how much of a nominal term can actually be served.

Section 414 — Salary, allowances and other service conditionsSource ↗

Salaries, allowances and other conditions of service are prescribed by rules. The essential protection is that neither salary and allowances nor other service conditions may be varied to a member’s disadvantage after appointment.

The provision does not itself contain a universal rupee salary figure. Quoting an old pay table as though section 414 fixes it forever is inaccurate. The principle is protection against detrimental alteration after appointment; applicable rules and later overriding provisions must also be checked.

Illustration: A Government unhappy with a member’s orders cannot treat a discretionary reduction in that member’s service conditions as ordinary administrative management. Security of conditions protects institutional independence, not merely personal financial comfort.

Section 415 — Acting President or ChairpersonSource ↗

Two situations are distinguished:

  1. Vacancy through death, resignation or otherwise: the senior-most Member acts as President/Chairperson until a duly appointed successor enters office.
  2. Temporary inability owing to absence, illness or another cause: the senior-most Member discharges the functions until the head resumes duties.

Acting service ensures continuity; it is not a permanent appointment to the head’s office. The language here is “senior-most Member.” Do not silently insert “senior-most Technical Member” or reverse the two institutions’ titles.

Section 416 — ResignationSource ↗

The President, Chairperson or member may resign by a written notice under his hand addressed to the Central Government. Under the parent provision, the person continues until the earliest of:

  • expiry of three months from Government’s receipt of the notice;
  • the duly appointed successor entering office; or
  • expiry of the resigning person’s term.

Date problem: If the resignation is received on 1 January but the successor joins on 15 February, the successor event ends the continuation before the three-month point. “Three months” is not a compulsory additional tenure in every case. Read the NCLAT service override where applicable.

Section 417 — Removal: accountability with safeguardsSource ↗

Subsection (1): specified grounds and consultation

The Central Government may, after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, remove a President, Chairperson or member who:

  • has been adjudged insolvent;
  • has been convicted of an offence which, in Government’s opinion, involves moral turpitude;
  • has become physically or mentally incapable of acting;
  • has acquired financial or other interests likely prejudicially to affect the functions of office; or
  • has abused the position so that continuation is prejudicial to the public interest.

For grounds (b)–(e), the proviso expressly requires a reasonable opportunity of being heard. A precise answer should not omit the distinction in the text or confuse the section with removal of company directors.

Subsection (2): proved misbehaviour or incapacity

Without prejudice to subsection (1), removal on this route requires a Central Government order after an inquiry by a Supreme Court judge nominated by the CJI, on a reference made by Government. The concerned office-holder must be informed of the charges and given a reasonable hearing. Thus “the Ministry considers the member unsuitable” is not a substitute for the prescribed inquiry.

Suspension and inquiry rules

Under subsection (3), suspension pending the inquiry requires concurrence of the CJI, not merely an executive decision to write to the CJI. Subsection (4) contemplates rules on the inquiry procedure made by Government after consultation with the Supreme Court.

Examination distinction: Appointment consultation, removal consultation and suspension concurrence are different statutory safeguards. Do not use the three expressions as synonyms. For NCLAT, also identify the applicable later service-removal regime.

Section 417A — The special NCLAT service-law bridgeSource ↗

This inserted provision lies within the user’s section range and cannot be skipped. It was introduced through the Finance Act, 2017, later referred to the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021, and has now been substituted through the 2026 legislation. Its subject is the Chairperson and Members of the Appellate Tribunal, not an automatic wholesale replacement of the NCLT service provisions.

The 2026 substituted text sends NCLAT qualifications, manner of selection, appointment, salaries and allowances, resignation, removal, other service conditions and reappointment eligibility to the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026. Read Chapter 17 for the enacted text, commencement report, selection structure, tenure and transitional cautions. This is later-law supplementation, not part of the 2015 judgment.

Section 418 — Staff of the Tribunal and Appellate TribunalSource ↗

The Central Government must, in consultation with the two bodies, provide necessary officers and employees. Their functions are discharged under the general superintendence and control of the President/Chairperson, or another member to whom such supervisory powers are delegated. Their salaries, allowances and service conditions are prescribed.

This section matters constitutionally because independence needs working registries, support staff, budgets and infrastructure. A tribunal can be formally independent yet operationally dependent if its personnel or facilities are inadequate. However, staff provision by Government must not be confused with Government deciding the merits of pending cases.

DU link: The closing portion of MBA records the Government’s preparations for premises, staffing and infrastructure and urges prompt corrective action so that the tribunals can start functioning. It is not a holding that every administrative arrangement then proposed had become immune from constitutional review.

05 · Benches, allocation and differences of opinion: section 419

Section 419 — Benches of NCLTSource ↗

Number and principal seat

Under subsection (1), the Central Government specifies the number of benches by notification. The Principal Bench is at New Delhi, presided over by the President under subsection (2). Local territorial allocation depends on the applicable notifications and jurisdictional provisions; the statute is not a reason to invent a permanent number of operational benches.

Ordinary bench: one Judicial + one Technical Member

Subsection (3) provides for powers to be exercised by a two-member bench containing one Judicial and one Technical Member. This combines adjudicatory and relevant specialist expertise while preserving judicial participation.

Authorised exception: A single Judicial Member may function as a bench for the specified class of cases/matters authorised by the President’s general or special order. There is no matching generic permission in this provision for a single Technical Member to decide those cases alone.

If, during the hearing, the single Judicial Member considers that the matter ought to be heard by a two-member bench, the President may transfer it, or the member may refer it to the President for transfer to an appropriate bench.

Subsection (4): IBC benches

The amended subsection requires the Central Government to establish, by notification, the benches considered necessary to exercise the adjudicating authority’s powers under Part II of the IBC. Older descriptions of subsection (4) as invariably requiring a three-or-more-member revival/restructuring special bench reproduce superseded language.

06 · Jurisdiction: what NCLT may decide—and what it may not

Jurisdiction is conferred, not assumed

Section 408 creates an institution which can receive jurisdiction from several enactments. It is not a self-contained list of every remedy. A strong answer names the source provision, the eligible applicant, the conditions for relief and the nature of the proposed order.

Subject matter, standing and relief are separate filters

Suppose a member alleges oppression. The subject may fall within sections 241–242, but the applicant must still satisfy the standing/waiver framework in section 244. Merely using the word “oppression” does not establish either the merits or standing. Similarly, a resolution professional may have authority to act for a company but still be approaching the wrong forum for a mining-law remedy.

Three different propositions: “This company has an asset”; “the RP must protect that asset”; and “NCLT can adjudicate this dispute about that asset.” The first two do not logically prove the third.

IBC section 60: the Embassy jurisdictional setting

Under section 60(1), NCLT is the relevant adjudicating authority in relation to insolvency resolution and liquidation of corporate persons, including corporate debtors and personal guarantors in the statutory framework, with the territorial connection identified by the provision. Section 60(4) addresses powers associated with the DRT framework for the purposes specified there. It is not a general constitutional writ jurisdiction clause.

Section 60(5), notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in any other law, gives NCLT jurisdiction to entertain or dispose of:

  • an application or proceeding by or against the corporate debtor or corporate person;
  • a claim made by or against it, including claims by or against its subsidiaries situated in India; and
  • a question of priorities or a question of law or fact arising out of or in relation to insolvency resolution or liquidation under the Code.

The language is broad because collective insolvency requires an effective forum. But breadth must be read in its insolvency setting. It is not a provision transferring all statutory appeals, regulatory permissions and constitutional review proceedings involving the debtor to NCLT.

The proper reading of “arising out of or in relation to”

An operational-debt claim against an insolvent company has a direct insolvency function: the claim must be admitted, ranked or dealt with in the collective process. By contrast, the correctness of a statutory tax assessment is adjudicated through the tax-law hierarchy; the debtor’s CIRP does not give NCLT a general tax-appellate role. Likewise, a statutory mining-extension refusal cannot be overturned merely because the mine is valuable to a proposed resolution plan.

This does not mean Government can never be a party before NCLT. Government may be a creditor, stakeholder or party to a matter properly assigned to the tribunal. The nature of the issue and the relief—not the public identity of one party—determines the jurisdictional analysis.

What overriding effect does—and does not—do

IBC section 238 gives the Code overriding effect over inconsistent laws and instruments in the circumstances of its operation. But an override provision is not an independent grant of every imaginable adjudicatory power. First locate the Code’s substantive jurisdiction, then identify any inconsistency. Saying “IBC overrides” cannot replace those steps.

Duties of the resolution professional are not jurisdiction-conferring provisions

The DU Embassy extract considers duties under IBC sections 18, 20 and 25: preserving value, managing operations and protecting the corporate debtor’s assets. Section 25(2)(b) expressly contemplates representing the debtor before judicial, quasi-judicial and arbitral forums. That is a strong textual indication that some disputes must be pursued outside NCLT in the competent forum.

Memorise this formulation: An RP’s responsibility to preserve property establishes the need to pursue a remedy. It does not establish the jurisdiction of the particular forum in which that remedy is sought.

07 · Procedure, evidence, orders and representation

Section 420 — Orders of NCLTSource ↗

Hearing before an order

NCLT may pass such orders as it thinks fit after giving parties a reasonable opportunity of being heard. “As it thinks fit” is controlled by the statute, jurisdiction, the relief properly sought, fair procedure and reasoned judicial decision-making. It is not a general equitable licence to rewrite rights outside the Act.

Rectification of an apparent mistake

Within two years from the date of the order, NCLT may amend an order to rectify a mistake apparent from the record. It shall make the amendment if such a mistake is brought to its notice by the parties. The proviso bars this amendment route for an order against which an appeal has been preferred under the Act.

Elements to state in an answer:

  1. There is an existing NCLT order.
  2. The proposed correction identifies an apparent record mistake.
  3. The statutory two-year period is observed.
  4. The appeal proviso does not bar the amendment.
  5. Rectification is not used as a disguised rehearing of contested merits.

Illustration: A self-evident computational transcription error reflected in the order and underlying record may invite rectification. A party’s argument that the tribunal adopted the wrong interpretation after considering two competing views ordinarily seeks appellate correction, not a new merits hearing dressed as rectification.

NCLT must send a copy of every order under the section to all concerned parties. Communication is also important to the statutory appeal framework, although the exact trigger must be read from the appeal provision itself.

Section 424 — Procedure before NCLT and NCLATSource ↗

The foundational rule: flexible procedure, mandatory fairness

The tribunals are not bound by the procedure laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, but are guided by the principles of natural justice. They may regulate their own procedure subject to the Companies Act, the IBC where applicable, and the rules.

This sentence contains three cumulative limits, not an exemption from all law. A tribunal cannot use procedural autonomy to disregard an express filing period, deny a fair hearing or invent jurisdiction. Conversely, every departure from the CPC’s ordinary suit procedure is not automatically an illegality.

Natural justice in a company dispute

  • Notice: the affected party must understand the case and relief sought.
  • Opportunity to answer: a meaningful chance to present material and meet adverse submissions.
  • Impartial adjudication: a disqualifying personal interest or bias is incompatible with fair decision-making.
  • Reasoned determination: the decision should disclose why material issues are resolved as they are, making review and appeal effective.
  • Context-sensitive procedure: fairness does not necessarily require an identical oral-trial format in every petition; the statutory scheme and dispute determine what is appropriate.

Do not overstate: These are explanatory natural-justice principles, not a claim that section 424 contains this exact five-item enumeration.

  • for an order against another person, that person voluntarily resides, carries on business or personally works for gain.

The provision gives machinery for enforcement; it does not turn NCLT into a civil court of unlimited subject-matter jurisdiction.

Proceedings are deemed judicial proceedings for the IPC purposes specified in the text (sections 193, 228 and, for its purposes, 196). A limited statutory fiction must not be expanded into all-purpose High Court status or unrestricted criminal trial power.

Section 426 — Delegation for inquiry and reportSource ↗

The Tribunal/Appellate Tribunal may, by general or special order, direct an officer, employee or another authorised person to inquire into a matter connected with a proceeding/appeal and report to it, in the manner and subject to conditions specified in the order.

Crucial distinction: This provision concerns an inquiry and report. It is not a blanket power to hand final adjudication of a company dispute to an outside consultant, nor merely a section about delegating financial administration. A report supplies material for the tribunal’s judicial decision; the tribunal remains responsible for deciding within its statutory role and observing fairness when relying on adverse material.

Section 432 — Right to legal representationSource ↗

A party may appear in person or authorise one or more:

  • chartered accountants;
  • company secretaries;
  • cost accountants;
  • legal practitioners; or
  • any other person,

to present the case before NCLT/NCLAT. The statute’s representation rule is broader than “only advocates may appear.” Applicable authorisation and procedural requirements still matter.

Do not confuse representation with adjudication: A professional’s right to represent a party under section 432 does not establish eligibility to be appointed a tribunal member under sections 409/411 or the later service framework. The function, qualifications and governing provisions differ.

Section 433 — LimitationSource ↗

The Limitation Act, 1963 applies “as far as may be” to proceedings and appeals before NCLT/NCLAT. The qualifier matters. Read the general limitation framework alongside express special periods and outer condonation limits such as section 421; it is not a licence to extend every statutory deadline indefinitely.

Distinguish condonation of delay from legally available exclusion/computation of time. An apparent elapsed-day total cannot be assessed responsibly without identifying the statutory trigger, applicable exclusions, sufficient cause where relevant, and the outer limit on the particular forum’s condonation power. A pending rectification request is not a general assurance that the appeal deadline has stopped.

08 · Appeals, rectification and disposal targets: sections 421–423

Section 421 — Appeal from NCLT to NCLATSource ↗

Who, from what, to where?

Any person aggrieved by an order of NCLT may appeal to NCLAT. This statutory phrase requires a grievance of the kind the law recognises; it is not an invitation for a complete stranger to appeal simply because company affairs interest them.

Exception: No section 421 appeal lies against an order made by NCLT with the consent of the parties. Do not confuse dissatisfaction after agreeing with an ordinary contested order. A genuine allegation that the supposed consent was never given requires careful identification of the appropriate remedy; the bare consent-appeal bar cannot simply be ignored.

Limitation and form

The normal period is 45 days from the date on which a copy of the NCLT order is made available to the person aggrieved. The appeal must be in the prescribed form with the prescribed fees.

NCLAT may admit the appeal after those 45 days within a further period not exceeding 45 days, if satisfied that sufficient cause prevented timely filing. The extension is discretionary, not automatic. The outer condonation period is itself limited.

Appellate powers and communication

After giving a reasonable hearing, NCLAT may confirm, modify or set aside the appealed order. It must send its order to NCLT and the parties to the appeal. The Companies Act first appellate tier is not restricted by section 421 to questions of law alone; that restriction appears in section 423 for the next statutory appeal.

Section 422 — Expeditious disposalSource ↗

Every application/petition before NCLT and appeal before NCLAT should be disposed of as expeditiously as possible. Every endeavour should be made to dispose of it within three months from presentation/filing.

If that does not happen, the body must record reasons. The President/Chairperson may, considering those reasons, extend the period by a further period not exceeding ninety days.

How to describe its effect: It is an expeditious-disposal mandate and an accountability mechanism. The section does not say every proceeding automatically abates, is allowed, or becomes void on the day after three months. Do not confuse a disposal target imposed on the tribunal with an appeal limitation imposed on a litigant.

Section 423 — Appeal from NCLAT to the Supreme CourtSource ↗

A person aggrieved by an NCLAT order may appeal to the Supreme Court on any question of law arising out of that order, within 60 days from receipt of the order. On sufficient cause, the Supreme Court may allow a further period not exceeding 60 days.

The expression is “question of law,” not necessarily the “substantial question of law” formula used in other appellate contexts. Nor is this a right to a complete third trial of disputed facts. Identify the actual legal error: statutory interpretation, the legal test applied, jurisdictional scope or another question of law arising from the order.

09 · Enforcement, institutional protection and the civil-court bar

Section 425 — Power to punish for contemptSource ↗

NCLT and NCLAT have the same jurisdiction, powers and authority in respect of contempt of themselves as the High Court, and may exercise powers under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971, subject to the statutory adaptations:

  • references to High Court include NCLT and NCLAT;
  • the reference to Advocate-General in section 15 of that Act is read as a reference to the Law Officers specified by the Central Government.

The correct Act year is 1971, not 1970. The power protects the administration of justice and obedience to lawful orders. It must be exercised within the applicable contempt law; mere disagreement with reasoning is not identical to contempt.

DU connection: MBA rejected the challenge to section 425 because the petitioner failed to establish its unconstitutionality. The Court did not say the power makes NCLT a High Court for all constitutional purposes. Embassy illustrates the converse: pressure to comply through contempt cannot supply the missing jurisdiction to grant the underlying public-law relief.

Section 427 — Public-servant statusSource ↗

The President, Members, officers and employees of NCLT, and the Chairperson, Members, officers and employees of NCLAT are deemed public servants under the section’s inherited IPC reference (section 21). This is a statutory status and accountability provision, not a declaration that adjudicators must obey executive instructions on case outcomes. Read present-day criminal-law application with the replacement enactment/savings framework noted under section 424.

Section 428 — Protection for action taken in good faithSource ↗

No suit, prosecution or other legal proceeding lies against the specified tribunal/appellate institution and its office-holders, staff, liquidator or other authorised person for loss or damage caused or likely to be caused by an act done or intended in good faith in pursuance of the Companies Act.

A complete explanation identifies:

  1. the persons/institutions covered;
  2. the connection to discharge of functions under the Act;
  3. an act done or intended in pursuance of the Act;
  4. good faith; and
  5. the loss/damage-related proceeding against which protection is claimed.

This is not absolute immunity for bad faith, and not a statutory extinction of appeal or constitutional review of an order. Protection against personal proceedings for protected action and correction of an adjudicatory order are different legal questions.

Section 429 — Assistance to obtain property, accounts and documentsSource ↗

The amended section operates in Companies Act winding-up proceedings or proceedings under the IBC. To take into custody or control property, books of account or other documents, NCLT may make a written request to the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Chief Judicial Magistrate or District Collector within whose jurisdiction the material is situated/found.

The official must take possession and cause the material to be entrusted to NCLT or its authorised person. Necessary steps and force may be used to secure compliance. Subsection (3) protects acts done in pursuance of the section from being questioned in any court/authority on the broad terms used there.

Read constitutionally: This assistance/protection mechanism is not a licence for action outside the statute or a power in ordinary legislation to abolish constitutional judicial review. Also, taking custody of an asset does not decide a contested entitlement to a statutory licence or extension of a lease. Embassy’s jurisdictional distinction still matters.

Illustration: Former management refuses to hand over books required for a pending insolvency proceeding. The statutory assistance mechanism addresses physical possession. It does not allow the District Collector or NCLT to grant the company a mining permission which the relevant law requires another authority to decide.

Section 430 — Civil courts not to have jurisdictionSource ↗

The bar has two limbs:

  • Suit/proceeding bar: no civil court may entertain a suit or proceeding concerning a matter NCLT/NCLAT is empowered to determine under the Companies Act or another law.
  • Injunction bar: no court or other authority may grant an injunction against action taken or to be taken by NCLT/NCLAT in pursuance of powers conferred by the Act or another law.

The words that do the work: “empowered to determine”

The bar tracks the tribunal’s assigned jurisdiction. First establish that the matter falls within that jurisdiction. One cannot reason backwards that, because civil courts are barred in assigned matters, every dispute involving a company must be assigned to NCLT.

The constitutional distinction

A High Court exercising Articles 226/227 is not merely an ordinary civil court hearing a substitute suit. Constitutional judicial review cannot be extinguished by an ordinary statutory jurisdiction bar. Nonetheless, a High Court ordinarily respects effective statutory appellate remedies and does not act as a routine additional NCLT appellate tier.

Correct combined proposition: Section 430 protects the specialist forum’s statutory field; constitutional judicial review controls the legality of the exercise of that field and its limits. Embassy applies the inherent-lack-of-jurisdiction exception, rather than announcing that every error in a tribunal order warrants a writ.

Section 431 — Vacancy/defect not, by itself, to invalidate proceedingsSource ↗

An act or proceeding of NCLT/NCLAT is not invalid merely because of a vacancy or defect in the institution’s constitution. The word “merely” is crucial. The provision prevents routine institutional disruption; it should not be read as curing every unconstitutional appointment structure, every absence of a required bench member, or a complete want of subject-matter jurisdiction.

Compare: Section 412’s saving addresses an appointment and defects/vacancies in the selection committee. Section 431 protects the institution’s acts/proceedings against the stated objection. Neither makes the constitutional reasoning in MBA pointless.

10 · Transfer of pending proceedings: section 434

Section 434 — A transition rule, not universal retrospective migrationSource ↗

Section 434 explains how identified pending matters move from older authorities/courts to NCLT, and which old matters continue under the old system. It matters because the institutional reform did not require every proceeding at every stage to restart from zero.

Clause (1)(a): Company Law Board matters

On the notified date, matters, proceedings and cases pending before the Company Law Board, constituted under section 10E of the 1956 Act, stand transferred to NCLT. NCLT disposes of them according to the Companies Act, 2013. This limb became operative on 1 June 2016.

Clause (1)(b): appeal from a pre-transfer CLB order

A person aggrieved by a CLB decision/order made before the relevant date may appeal to the High Court, on a question of law, within 60 days from communication. On sufficient cause, the High Court may permit a further period not exceeding 60 days.

Why this matters: “All company-law appeals now go to NCLAT” is too broad. A saved appeal from an old CLB order uses the special transitional route, not an invented retrospective section 421 appeal.

Clause (1)(c): court proceedings

Proceedings under the 1956 Act pending before District Courts/High Courts, including those relating to arbitration, compromise, arrangements, reconstruction and winding up, fall within the transfer framework, subject to its provisos. NCLT may deal with transferred matters from their pre-transfer stage. Clause (c) took effect on 15 December 2016.

Winding-up stage qualification

Only winding-up proceedings at the prescribed stage transfer through the stage-based mechanism. The Companies (Transfer of Pending Proceedings) Rules, 2016, as amended, supply the necessary details. Service/stage and the particular ground of winding up matter; one should not reduce the complete rule framework to “all cases transferred on 15 December.”

Party-initiated transfer under the 2018 proviso

A party or parties to a winding-up proceeding pending before a court immediately before commencement of the specified 2018 Ordinance may apply for transfer. The court may transfer the proceeding to NCLT; a proceeding transferred under this proviso is dealt with as an application for initiation of CIRP under the IBC.

Notice the difference between a statutory transfer operating on a prescribed category and a transfer dependent on a court order on an application. A party’s request does not itself accomplish migration, and this is not permission to disregard the applicable transfer jurisprudence.

Non-winding-up cases reserved for orders

The transfer provisos distinguish non-winding-up proceedings in which the High Court has already reserved orders. Such reserved matters are not transferred through the general mechanism described in the proviso. The saved categories—including winding-up matters not transferred—continue under the Companies Act, 1956 and Companies (Court) Rules, 1959, as specified.

Saved voluntary winding up

Where notice of the voluntary-winding-up resolution had been given by advertisement under 1956 Act section 485(1) and the company had not been dissolved before 1 April 2017, the section preserves treatment under the 1956 Act and 1959 Rules. This is a saving for old proceedings, not the current general procedure for starting a new voluntary liquidation.

Subsection (2): rule-making for timely transfer

The Central Government may make rules consistent with the Act to ensure timely transfer of pending matters from the CLB/courts. The requirement of consistency matters: a rule administers the statutory transition and cannot legitimately contradict it.

11 · Case dossier 32: Madras Bar Association (2015)

Prescribed DU case 32 • WP(C) No. 1072 of 2013 • Supreme Court • 14 May 2015 • Judgment by A.K. Sikri J. • DU printed pp. 294–314

The creation of a specialist tribunal and the constitutional adequacy of its structure are different questions. Parliament may change the forum; it cannot dilute the independence appropriate to the judicial work transferred.

The statement above is an explanatory synthesis, not a verbatim quotation.

A. Identity and why DU prescribes this case

Madras Bar Association v. Union of India & Anr. is a constitutional challenge to the NCLT/NCLAT scheme in the Companies Act, 2013. The judgment is a sequel to Union of India v. R. Gandhi, President, Madras Bar Association (2010). It must also be distinguished from the 2014 National Tax Tribunal case, and later cases with the same association’s name concerning tribunal service conditions.

The decision teaches both sides of tribunalisation: specialist adjudication is constitutionally permissible, but adjudicators who take over court functions need judicially credible qualifications, selection and independence. It is not an anti-tribunal judgment; it insists on constitutionally adequate tribunals.

B. Material facts: a constitutional case has institutional facts

This was not a dispute about one company’s debt, share register or winding up. The facts concern the legislative design of institutions:

  1. The Companies (Second Amendment) Act, 2002 inserted provisions in Parts IB and IC of the Companies Act, 1956 for NCLT and NCLAT.
  2. Madras Bar Association challenged the transfer of jurisdiction and the bodies’ constitution, qualifications and service arrangements before the Madras High Court.
  3. On 30 March 2004, the High Court upheld the creation of the specialist system in principle but identified structural provisions offending separation of powers. Defects had to be removed before the bodies could constitutionally exercise the transferred jurisdiction.
  4. Both sides appealed: the association objected to the acceptance of tribunal creation; the Union objected to the adverse findings on structure.
  5. The 2010 Constitution Bench upheld the creation of both bodies and agreed that significant corrections were needed. Paragraph 120 tabulated the safeguards.
  6. Parliament enacted the Companies Act, 2013, with Chapter XXVII governing NCLT/NCLAT. The association alleged that important defects condemned in 2010 had reappeared in the new legislation.
  7. The present writ petition challenged the new scheme, particularly provisions identified in the prayer, as contrary to Article 14 and constitutional judicial-independence requirements.
  8. A related WP 267/2012 was not finally decided with this petition: it required a further Government response. The Court proceeded separately with WP 1072/2013.

Procedural posture: constitutional adjudication by the Supreme Court, not an appeal under section 421 or 423 against an NCLT/NCLAT merits order. At the close of the judgment, the Court expressly noted that the tribunals’ functioning had not yet started.

C. The actual scope of challenge

The DU extract records a broad prayer referring particularly to sections 408, 409, 411(3), 412, 413, 425, 431 and 434. But a prayer is not the same as an argued and decided issue. Paragraph 8 records that no arguments were addressed on several provisions, including 415, 418, 424, 426, 431 and 434, and the Court therefore did not discuss them substantively.

Paragraph 9 groups the live challenge into three compartments:

  1. Constitutional validity of NCLT/NCLAT as institutions.
  2. Qualifications and service-related prescriptions for their heads/members.
  3. Structure of the selection committee.

There were additional objections to contempt power and Government’s power to constitute benches. A sound case note distinguishes those ancillary objections from the main three issues and does not invent holdings on provisions not argued.

E. Issue 1: was the creation of NCLAT constitutionally invalid?

Petitioner’s argument, as recorded in DU

Senior counsel Arvind P. Datar made no serious renewed attempt to overturn NCLT’s validity, acknowledging the 2010 decision. The principal institutional attack was on NCLAT. Although its validity had also been upheld in 2010, counsel argued that there had been no adequate independent discussion of it; the conclusion should therefore not foreclose a fresh challenge.

The petitioner relied on the 2014 Madras Bar Association decision striking down the National Tax Tribunal (NTT) scheme, arguing that similar treatment should follow for NCLAT. The larger objection to tribunalisation invoked impartiality, fairness, rule of law, separation of powers and the judicial nature of the work being transferred.

Pleading nuance: The Court noted that the prayer expressly attacked section 408 but conspicuously omitted section 410, despite the oral emphasis on NCLAT. This was not the sole ground of rejection; the Court examined the earlier decision and the proposed NTT analogy.

Respondent’s position and the Court’s response

The Union defended the statutory institutions. The DU extract’s detailed treatment centres on the binding force of the 2010 judgment, rather than setting out a separate lengthy Union speech for every sub-issue. It would be inaccurate to manufacture one.

The Court held that the 2010 litigation had expressly included and decided NCLAT’s validity. The petitioner had challenged both institutions on common grounds, the earlier Court had examined those arguments, and the operative conclusion specifically upheld both. The association could not strip that conclusion of binding force by saying one institution had received insufficient separate discussion.

Two related legal reasons mattered:

  • Res judicata/finality between the parties: the earlier institutional challenge had been decided.
  • Binding precedent and coordinate-bench discipline: the later Bench could not treat the earlier Constitution Bench’s explicit ruling as if it did not exist.

These principles do not make every later, altered statutory provision immune from scrutiny. Indeed, the Court went on to test the 2013 provisions against the standards already laid down. They prevent the same resolved institutional issue from being relitigated as though no binding decision existed.

Why the National Tax Tribunal analogy failed

The Court distinguished the nature of jurisdiction:

Holding on Issue 1

The renewed challenge to the constitutional creation of NCLT/NCLAT failed. The 2010 validation stood. The Court did not abolish NCLAT or transfer all of its future appeals back to High Courts.

F. Issue 2: were the technical-member qualifications constitutionally adequate?

Identify what was actually contested

Paragraph 19 records that judicial-member qualifications were not challenged in the relevant argument. The contested issue was principally the technical-member qualification scheme, especially the reintroduced civil-service route and overbroad specialist criteria. The heading concerning qualifications/service conditions is broader than the precise operative defects ultimately addressed.

Petitioner’s case

The association argued that Parliament had effectively repeated qualifications disapproved in 2010. The earlier directions required standards appropriate to the judicial functions transferred, including seniority for the civil-service technical route and genuinely relevant specialised experience. Appointment criteria could not be diluted simply because a new Act replaced the old one.

Government’s specific defence

The Union argued that sufficient Additional Secretary-level officers in the relevant company-law service were not available. It submitted that Additional Secretary and Joint Secretary functions were similar and that Joint Secretary-level officers possessed valuable knowledge of company operations and company law. Their expertise would benefit NCLT.

This was a practical staffing argument: the Government defended the variation as workable and functionally justified. The Court did not accept staffing convenience as a reason to disregard a binding constitutional safeguard.

Judicial reasoning: why rank and experience mattered

The Court returned to the 2010 discussion, especially paragraphs 108–119 and the corrections in paragraph 120. The analysis was institutional, not a personal criticism of every civil servant:

  • When High Court work moves to a tribunal, the substitute should have members as nearly equivalent in rank, capacity and status as the nature of that work requires.
  • Paying a person a High Court judge’s salary does not itself create equivalent judicial standing, maturity or independence.
  • Administrative decision-making and adjudication between parties are different functions. Departmental experience is not automatically the same as judging disputes impartially.
  • A technical member should bring expertise genuinely connected with the tribunal’s subject, not just incidental exposure to company files.
  • The danger is a gradual lowering of standards, shrinking judicial space and enlarging the executive’s role in deciding matters previously decided by courts.

The DU extract reproduces the Court’s historical examples of progressively diluted tribunal qualifications. Those examples explain the structural concern; they are not a licence to call any non-judge unfit for all tribunal work.

The civil-service rule must not be applied to the wrong category

For the civil-service technical route, the Court insisted on the Secretary/Additional Secretary-level standard and rejected the renewed Joint Secretary route. This does not mean that every accountant, company secretary or other professional technical member must have been a Government Secretary. Separate legitimate expertise routes must be kept distinct.

I. Issue 3: was the five-member selection committee valid?

Original statutory structure

The 2013 committee comprised:

  1. CJI or nominee as Chairperson;
  2. a senior SC judge or HC Chief Justice;
  3. MCA Secretary;
  4. Law and Justice Secretary; and
  5. Secretary, Department of Financial Services, Ministry of Finance.

This yielded two judicial and three executive members. It also omitted the specified assurance of a judicial Chairperson’s casting vote.

Petitioner’s case

The 2010 directions had already settled the structure needed for judicial primacy. A five-member committee with executive numerical superiority reinstated the vice; the new Act was not free to treat the directions as optional drafting advice.

Government’s recorded arguments

The Union offered several justifications:

  • The earlier recommended composition was only in “broad terms.”
  • The additional Financial Services Secretary was relevant because BIFR and AAIFR, associated with that department, were to be subsumed in the institutional reorganisation.
  • Selection committees had developed healthy, robust practices, with recommendations ordinarily unanimous.
  • There was no known material disagreement or actual voting in the cited MCA committees.
  • Similar statutory bodies did not always provide a casting vote.
  • The committee could determine its own modalities/procedure.

These are arguments actually reflected in the DU extract. They should be presented fairly: the Government did not expressly claim a right to appoint biased judges; it defended a design which the Court nevertheless held structurally incompatible with judicial independence.

Why the Court rejected them

A constitutional safeguard must govern the possibility of disagreement. Past unanimity cannot replace a legal assurance of judicial primacy. The 2010 directions were binding precedent; the CJI/nominee’s final say, secured by the casting vote in the prescribed composition, formed part of their ratio. Procedure-making power could not amend away the committee’s numerical structure.

The Court also addressed the drafting ambiguity in the earlier phrase referring to the Finance and Company Affairs Ministry: it did not permit expansion into an extra executive seat defeating the mandated four-member arrangement. The relevant “and” was treated in context as a typographical issue, not a warrant for executive predominance.

Holding on Issue 3

Section 412(2), as then framed, was invalid. The Court directed correction in accordance with paragraph 120(viii) of the 2010 judgment. The later amended parent provision reflects a four-member structure and a casting vote, as explained in Chapter 3. Do not attribute the separate 2026 committee to this 2015 holding.

J. Ancillary challenges and final order

Contempt: The objection to section 425 was rejected. Calling the power “not healthy” did not establish constitutional invalidity.

Bench constitution: The objection to Government’s statutory power to constitute benches also failed; the petitioner had not shown why the legislative arrangement was unconstitutional. Do not confuse creating/locating benches with deciding individual cases on executive instructions.

Disposition: The writ petition was partly allowed in the manner stated. The institutions survived; defective qualifications and selection provisions required correction.

Implementation observations: The Government’s May 2015 affidavit described approved posts, proposed rules, staffing, premises and budget preparations. The Court urged timely remedial measures so that adequately staffed NCLT/NCLAT could begin functioning. These observations explain the urgency of implementation; they are not an order closing existing tribunals or a merits ruling in an insolvency dispute.

Problem: Parliament creates a new specialist tribunal to decide company disputes previously heard by a High Court. D. The two questions framed by the Supreme Court

  1. Alternative remedy and writ jurisdiction: Should the High Court interfere under Articles 226/227 with an NCLT order made in an IBC proceeding despite the statutory NCLAT appeal, and in what circumstances?
  2. Fraud jurisdiction: Can NCLT/NCLAT examine allegations of fraud in IBC proceedings, including fraudulent initiation of CIRP?

The questions interact but have different answers. The High Court’s interference was justified because of the public-law jurisdictional excess, not because the insolvency tribunals lacked power to consider insolvency fraud.

E. Appellants’ arguments: full-judgment supplementation

Resolution applicant: K.V. Viswanathan

The applicant relied on the effective alternative remedy under IBC section 61. Where a specialist statutory forum exists, High Courts should not ordinarily entertain writs against its orders. Counsel also raised the fact that the order came from the Chennai NCLT bench.

Substantively, the Code seeks to revive the corporate debtor as a going concern and maximise asset value. The RP must be able to obtain relief protecting the debtor’s property. The Code was characterised as a unified umbrella with pervasive, exclusive remedies, so challenges to the RP’s NCLT relief should remain within that architecture.

Resolution applicant: Mukul Rohatgi

Counsel did not go so far as to say High Court jurisdiction was completely ousted. Instead, he argued for a “hands off” approach in this type of matter. He relied on approval of the resolution plan on 12 June 2019 and argued against interference that would damage or unravel the approved plan.

Resolution professional: Kapil Sibal

The RP emphasised:

  • the time-bound resolution objective and the disruption caused by collateral writ litigation;
  • the overriding effect of IBC section 238;
  • the State’s earlier participation/remand position, which allegedly prevented it from now denying NCLT’s jurisdiction;
  • the broad definition of property in section 3(27), including various present, future, vested or contingent interests;
  • the RP’s duty to preserve that property;
  • the breadth of section 60(5)(c) and NCLT’s competence to examine fraud.

The argument linked NCLT’s undoubted insolvency role to the asserted lease-extension right. The Court accepted the fraud-jurisdiction component but rejected the leap from property preservation to public-law judicial review.

Committee of Creditors: Arvind P. Datar and E. Om Prakash

The CoC argued that IBC is a complete code and orders should be challenged through its own routes. It characterised the requested relief as mere recognition of an already existing statutory right to deemed extension under MMDR section 8A, rather than an exercise of judicial review not vested in NCLT.

Why this detail improves an answer: The appellants did not simply ask for a discretionary commercial favour. They argued that the extension was already a statutory entitlement whose recognition was necessary to preserve value. The Court’s answer was that the correctness of the statutory authority’s refusal still had to be tested by the competent public-law forum, not that commercial value was irrelevant to insolvency generally.

F. State’s arguments: expressly recorded in DU paragraph 9

Attorney General K.K. Venugopal argued that an order made with inherent lack of jurisdiction is amenable to High Court review despite an appeal provision. The inferior forum cannot force a party through an appellate route before a superior court can address a nullity.

The State characterised the mining decision as a statutory/public-law matter under the MMDR Act and rules, not merely an inter-party contract. It read section 60’s contours and associated powers as insufficient to confer judicial review over such a decision.

The State relied on Barnard v. National Dock Labour Board and State of U.P. v. Mohammad Nooh for the principle that an inferior tribunal’s jurisdictional nullity need not invariably be challenged only through the statutory appeal.

It also pointed to the CoC meeting of 27 February 2019: another company had made a better offer, but the selected applicant was willing to take the risk of non-renewal. A party which accepted that risk should not shelter behind approval of the resolution plan to compel renewal. This was a recorded submission, not the central ratio or a freestanding finding that every risk-taking resolution applicant loses public-law rights.

Fraud allegation: The State alleged that related actors had orchestrated CIRP to corner the mine/lease, rather than genuinely resolve insolvency. That allegation presented the second jurisdictional question; it was not treated as already proved.

G. Reasoning I: the lease refusal belonged to the public-law domain

The land in question belonged to Karnataka. The lease was granted under the statutory mining regime and governed by the Mineral Concession Rules, 1960, with rights and restrictions defined by the lease and public law. The Court referred to the constitutional/statutory setting of mines and minerals, including Union List Entry 54 and the MMDR Act section 2 declaration concerning public-interest Union control.

Thus the relationship was not just contractual. The challenged refusal was a decision by a statutory authority exercising powers under the mining regime. Reviewing its correctness required public-law judicial review by a superior court competent to exercise it, rather than an insolvency tribunal borrowing that power from the financial importance of the asset.

DU paragraph 28 supplies the central formulation: NCLT is a creature of a special statute for specific functions and cannot be elevated into a superior court with judicial review over administrative action.

H. Reasoning II: NCLT is not a general civil court

The Court distinguished NCLT’s specified statutory functions from the general civil jurisdiction associated with section 9 CPC. Even decree-like enforcement and civil-court procedural powers do not create all-purpose subject-matter jurisdiction.

The tribunal needs authority both to decide the question and to grant the relief sought. It is not enough that the company’s insolvency is properly before it. A lawful CIRP does not authorise every order which would make the CIRP more commercially successful.

I. Reasoning III: section 60(5) is broad but not unlimited

The Court resisted reading “any question of law or fact” in isolation. Its context is insolvency resolution or liquidation. A dispute with a public authority does not become an insolvency question solely because an adverse decision would reduce the estate’s value.

DU’s tax illustration: If a company needs to challenge an income-tax assessment, that challenge does not automatically move to NCLT because CIRP has begun. A tax demand may give rise to a claim to be dealt with in insolvency; that is different from NCLT replacing the tax appellate machinery and deciding the assessment’s correctness.

This distinction avoids two extremes:

  • It rejects “NCLT can decide every dispute touching the debtor.”
  • It also rejects “NCLT can never decide a matter involving Government.”

The proper inquiry is functional: what precise issue is being adjudicated, under which statutory power, and through what relief?

J. Reasoning IV: the RP’s preservation duty requires the proper forum

Appellants relied on sections 18, 20, 25 and the broad property definition. The Court’s answer was not that an RP should let valuable rights lapse. The RP must protect and represent the debtor, but through the legally competent forum.

Section 25(2)(b) expressly requires the RP to represent the debtor and exercise its rights in judicial, quasi-judicial and arbitration proceedings. That language would be unnecessary if every claim could simply be rerouted to NCLT by section 60(5). DU paragraphs 39–40 stress that the RP cannot use CIRP as a bypass around the public-law route.

K. Additional full-judgment principles omitted from DU’s selected run

Consent cannot confer subject-matter jurisdiction: paragraph 43

The State’s agreement to return to NCLT on remand did not clothe it with a jurisdiction absent from statute. A statutory tribunal’s subject-matter competence cannot be created by party concession. This answers the RP’s participation/estoppel argument without deciding that every procedural objection survives any form of waiver.

Moratorium preserves; it does not create a new lease right: paragraph 44

The Court stated that the moratorium’s purpose is to preserve the status quo, not create a new right. Protection against recovery/dispossession under section 14(1)(d) did not amount to a right to renewal of this mining lease. The particular mining grant and retained State rights also informed the discussion of possession.

Temporal caution: This is the 2019 judgment’s analysis of the legal text and facts before it. Later IBC amendments concerning licences, permits and similar grants must not be retroactively attributed to the Court or turned into a claim that every present-day licence cancellation case is already decided by this paragraph. The enduring lesson here is to distinguish preservation of an existing right from creation/enlargement of a right and to identify the competent forum.

L. Holding on the first question: writ intervention was justified

DU paragraph 45 holds that NCLT lacked jurisdiction to entertain the application directing Karnataka to execute supplementary mining lease deeds. Because NCLT exercised a jurisdiction not vested in it, it was coram non judice in relation to that relief. The High Court could entertain the writ notwithstanding the NCLAT appeal.

Meaning of coram non judice: the matter/relief was before an authority without the necessary jurisdiction. It does not mean that NCLT was not validly constituted at all, or that its CIRP jurisdiction vanished for all purposes.

M. Reasoning and holding on fraud: appellants were right on this point

The Court then separately asked whether NCLT could investigate fraud in CIRP. In DU paragraph 49, it expressly held the appellants’ objection on this point well founded.

Section 65: fraudulent or malicious initiation

The provision addresses initiation of insolvency resolution/liquidation fraudulently or with malicious intent for a purpose other than genuine insolvency resolution/liquidation. The adjudicating authority may impose a penalty of at least ₹1 lakh and up to ₹1 crore. The quoted subsection (2) separately concerns voluntary liquidation initiated with intent to defraud.

The State’s allegation—that one person in different corporate “avatars” used CIRP to capture the mine rather than genuinely resolve insolvency—would, if established, fall within the mischief of section 65(1). Hence NCLT was capable of examining it.

Sections 66 and 69

DU paragraph 50 also invokes section 66, dealing with fraudulent trading and the relevant statutory contribution framework, and refers to section 69, concerning punishment for transactions defrauding creditors. These provisions demonstrate that the Code anticipates fraudulent conduct rather than leaving every such issue outside its institutions.

Important criminal-law boundary: The reference to punishment in section 69 does not confer universal criminal trial jurisdiction on NCLT. Distinguish NCLT’s statutory inquiry/penalty/contribution functions from prosecution and trial under the competent criminal/Special Court framework.

Consequence for appeal and writ

Since NCLT can inquire into the relevant insolvency fraud, NCLAT can consider it on appeal. Fraudulent initiation, standing alone, is therefore not a reason to bypass IBC section 61. “Fraud vitiates everything” cannot replace analysis of which forum has statutory power to decide the alleged fraud.

O. Hypothetical visual: one insolvency, two legal routes

Hypothetical: Meridian Minerals Ltd. enters CIRP. The State refuses statutory extension of a mining lease. Its RP asks NCLT both to (a) punish a collusive creditor for malicious initiation and (b) quash the State refusal and compel a fresh mining entitlement.

Analysis: Split the reliefs. Alleged malicious initiation falls within IBC section 65 and can be examined by NCLT, subject to proof; an adverse order uses the IBC appeal route. Public-law review of the statutory refusal is not conferred on NCLT merely by section 60(5) or the RP’s preservation duty. The appropriate statutory/public-law forum must be used. A High Court challenge to NCLT’s assumption of that absent jurisdiction is not barred merely because NCLAT exists.

P. Ratio, limits and application language

Ratio: NCLT/NCLAT have the insolvency jurisdiction and fraud-related powers assigned by the Code, but no general power of judicial review over public-law decisions of statutory/quasi-judicial authorities such as the mining-extension refusal. Where NCLT acts outside that jurisdiction, High Court intervention is permissible notwithstanding the statutory alternative remedy. Fraud within CIRP does not itself justify bypassing that remedy because the IBC forums can examine it.

Application sentence: “The decisive fact is not simply that the debtor is in CIRP or that fraud is alleged; it is that the relief asks an insolvency tribunal to review a statutory administrative decision outside its conferred jurisdiction.”

Exam capsule: A Karnataka mining-lease debtor entered CIRP. After the State refused deemed extension, NCLT directed supplementary lease deeds; the High Court stayed the direction. SC dismissed appeals from that intervention. It held that NCLT cannot exercise the superior courts’ public-law judicial review, although it can examine fraudulent CIRP initiation under section 65 and the associated IBC fraud framework.

A six-step method for a forum problem

  1. Disaggregate the prayers. “Protect the company” may conceal a money claim, statutory-permission dispute, fraud allegation and request for an injunction.
  2. Identify the source of each claimed right. Companies Act, IBC, contract, mining statute, tax law or the Constitution?
  3. Find the source of NCLT’s jurisdiction. Section 408 is the enabling architecture; the specific legal provision must confer the issue/relief.
  4. Test the procedural route. Correct bench, standing, limitation, service and fair hearing.
  5. Choose the corrective mechanism. Rectification, procedural recall/restoration, Companies Act appeal, IBC appeal or exceptional constitutional review.
  6. State a qualified conclusion. Do not let the result on one prayer answer all other prayers automatically.

Hypothetical lab 1: the executive-majority committee

Facts: A statute transfers commercial court functions to a tribunal. Its committee contains two judges and three departmental secretaries. Government says the committee has never voted and all appointments will be excellent.

Answer: Apply MBA’s structural reasoning. The validity of the specialist institution is distinct from the validity of appointment design. Past harmony does not answer what happens when judges and officials disagree. A binding requirement of judicial primacy cannot be replaced by an assurance of good practice. The defect lies in the arrangement’s legally permitted operation, not a need to prove that every past appointee actually decided cases under Government pressure.

Hypothetical lab 2: the mixed mining/fraud application

Facts: The RP asks NCLT to declare the State’s mining refusal invalid and to impose a section 65 penalty on a collusive creditor. The State argues that both matters must be kept out of NCLT because fraud is serious.

Answer: Separate the issues. Under Embassy, the public-law challenge to this type of statutory refusal lies outside NCLT’s conferred review powers. But section 65 expressly permits examination of fraudulent/malicious initiation; seriousness of fraud is not a reason to strip the tribunal of a power the Code gives it. Findings of fraud require proof and hearing. An adverse fraud decision ordinarily goes to NCLAT under section 61; the public-law jurisdictional excess may support a writ.

Hypothetical lab 3: an ordinary insolvency error labelled “jurisdictional”

Facts: A creditor says NCLT gave the wrong priority to another admitted claim. It files a writ, asserting that “every wrong application of the Code is without jurisdiction.”

Answer: Priority is expressly within the insolvency jurisdictional field. A wrong conclusion reached while deciding an entrusted issue ordinarily raises appellate error, not inherent want of jurisdiction. The party should use the appropriate IBC appeal. Embassy is not an invitation to rename every merits complaint as a jurisdictional nullity. A genuinely distinct constitutional defect must be specifically established if invoking writ jurisdiction.

Hypothetical lab 4: the procedural-power fallacy

Facts: A debtor argues that, because section 424 gives NCLT powers of a civil court and section 425 gives High Court-like contempt power, it must also be able to quash every regulatory decision affecting the company.

Answer: The conclusion does not follow. Evidence collection, execution and contempt are powers serving assigned adjudication. They do not grant general civil jurisdiction or constitutional judicial review. A legal fiction applies for its specified purpose. Cite Embassy and identify the missing substantive power to review the regulatory refusal.

Hypothetical lab 5: consent to the wrong forum

Facts: The State participates on remand before NCLT, then objects that NCLT cannot decide a public-law mining refusal. The RP alleges waiver.

Answer: Full-judgment paragraph 43 of Embassy rejects conferral of absent subject-matter jurisdiction by concession. Participation may have procedural consequences in other contexts, but it cannot create a statutory tribunal’s missing adjudicatory field. The answer should identify subject-matter jurisdiction specifically rather than claim no procedural objection can ever be waived.

Hypothetical lab 6: natural justice and independent design

Facts: A properly constituted bench receives a confidential inquiry report under section 426 and decides against a party on decisive allegations never disclosed to it.

Answer: Proper appointment does not cure a procedurally unfair decision. Sections 420 and 424 require a reasonable hearing and natural justice. Section 426 permits inquiry/report, not determination through undisclosed adverse material without the fairness the circumstances require. Identify the prejudice, the governing procedure and appropriate remedy. The institutional independence issue in MBA and procedural fairness in an individual case are related but distinct.

PYQ 1 — 2017, Question 8(b): National Company Law Tribunal

Reported wording: “Explain briefly the following: … (b) National Company Law Tribunal.”

Scope: A short note. The source does not establish a separate mark allocation for this subpart, so none is invented. The following answer is deliberately fuller than a few lines; shorten according to the actual marks and available time. It is written as a 2017-oriented answer, without attributing Embassy or later amendments to that examination date.

Model answer

The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) is the specialist statutory adjudicatory body constituted under section 408 of the Companies Act, 2013. It consists of a President and Judicial and Technical Members appointed by the Central Government through notification. It was constituted with effect from 1 June 2016 to exercise powers conferred under the Companies Act and other applicable laws.

Constitution and independence. The President must have been a High Court judge for the prescribed five years. Judicial-member routes include a High Court judge, a District Judge with five years’ service, or an advocate with ten years’ experience, as provided by section 409. Technical qualifications must supply genuine relevant expertise and conform to constitutional standards. In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2015), the Supreme Court maintained the validity of NCLT/NCLAT but required correction of defective technical qualifications and the executive-dominant selection structure in accordance with the binding 2010 judgment. Thus tribunalisation is permissible only with adequate judicial independence.

Jurisdiction. NCLT decides company-law matters specifically entrusted to it, including relevant share-register/transfer disputes, schemes, oppression and mismanagement, class actions, restoration and winding up. It also acts as adjudicating authority in the corporate-insolvency framework under the IBC, 2016. It is not a forum for every dispute merely because a company is a party.

Benches and procedure. Section 419 provides for benches, with the Principal Bench at New Delhi and the ordinary two-member combination of one Judicial and one Technical Member; an authorised single Judicial Member may hear specified classes. Under section 424, NCLT is not bound by CPC procedure but is guided by natural justice and the governing statute/rules. It has specified civil-court powers for evidence and enforcement. Section 420 requires a reasonable hearing and permits correction of an apparent record mistake within two years, subject to its appeal proviso. Parties may appear personally or through representatives allowed by section 432.

Appeal and control. Under section 421, an aggrieved person may appeal to NCLAT within 45 days of the statutory copy-availability trigger; a further period up to 45 days requires sufficient cause. Consent orders are excluded from that appeal. NCLT also has contempt powers under section 425, while section 430 excludes ordinary civil-court jurisdiction over matters the tribunal is empowered to determine. Constitutional judicial review is not extinguished.

Conclusion. NCLT combines specialist expertise and a judicial process to concentrate company-law adjudication, but its legitimacy depends on independent constitution, fair procedure and adherence to statutory jurisdiction.

Use six headings or six compact paragraphs: meaning; constitution; independence; jurisdiction; procedure; appeal. Cite MBA briefly. Avoid reproducing every service condition unless the question specifically asks for constitution in detail. If answering this as a contemporary practice question rather than reconstructing the 2017 paper, add one sentence on Embassy’s jurisdictional limit and distinguish the current NCLAT service regime.

Crossover PYQ — 2017, Question 6(b): who can seek oppression relief?

Reported wording: “Who can apply under section 241 of the Companies Act, 2013 to tribunal for relief in case of oppression?”

Classification: This is principally an oppression/standing question, included because it tests access to the adjudicatory body. It is not represented as a separate direct question on Chapter XXVII’s institutional structure.

Model answer

The right to seek relief under section 241(1) must be read with section 244. An eligible member may complain that the company’s affairs have been or are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to the public interest, oppressive to members, or prejudicial to the company’s interests, within the statutory formulation. The section also addresses the qualifying material-change situation and the apprehended prejudicial manner of conducting affairs.

For a company having share capital, section 244 ordinarily permits an application by:

  • at least 100 members, or one-tenth of the total number of members, whichever is less; or
  • a member or members holding at least one-tenth of the issued share capital, provided the required calls and other sums due on their shares have been paid.

For a company without share capital, at least one-fifth of its total members ordinarily qualify. Joint holders are counted as one member for these purposes. The Tribunal may, on application, waive all or any of the section 244(1) requirements to enable members to apply under section 241. Where several members qualify, one or more may apply on behalf of the others with their written consent under section 244(2).

Separately, the Central Government may apply under section 241(2) if it considers that the company’s affairs are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to the public interest. Therefore, an answer limited to the numerical member thresholds misses this distinct governmental route.

Conclusion: Identify both the members’ standing framework, including waiver, and the Central Government route. Satisfying standing is not the same as proving oppression or obtaining section 242 relief.

Practice 1 — “Discuss the constitutional validity of NCLT and NCLAT in light of Madras Bar Association.”

Model answer

The constitutional issue must be divided into the permissibility of transferring adjudicatory jurisdiction to tribunals and the adequacy of their composition and safeguards. The fact that Parliament creates a specialist body does not make it unconstitutional, but specialisation cannot be a pretext for executive control over judicial functions.

In Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010), the Constitution Bench upheld the creation of NCLT/NCLAT while requiring corrections to their structure under the 1956 Act. In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India, WP(C) 1072/2013, decided 14 May 2015, the association argued that the Companies Act, 2013 repeated important defects.

Institutional validity. The petitioner sought to reopen NCLAT’s validity by contending that the 2010 judgment had not sufficiently discussed it and by relying on the 2014 National Tax Tribunal decision. The Court rejected this. NCLAT’s creation had expressly been contested and upheld in 2010; finality and binding precedent applied. The NTT comparison also failed because the nature of the transferred jurisdiction differed: NCLT acts at first instance, NCLAT supplies a specialist first appeal, and section 423 provides an SC appeal on a question of law. A tribunal replacing adjudication of substantial tax-law questions presents a different institutional setting.

Qualifications. Technical members must possess genuinely relevant expertise, and the civil-service route must retain the seniority appropriate to the transferred court functions. The renewed Joint Secretary-level route conflicted with the Secretary/Additional Secretary standard laid down in 2010. Government’s reliance on officer shortages and functional similarity did not justify dilution. Judicial status is not created by equivalent salary alone; rank, maturity, adjudicatory suitability and independence matter. The Court required correction of the impugned technical-qualification provisions, including section 411(3) as then worded.

Selection. Section 412(2) then provided a five-member committee with two judicial and three executive members. The Court rejected the defence that the committee normally worked unanimously and could regulate its own procedure. Constitutional design must secure judicial primacy in the event of disagreement. The required corrected arrangement was a four-member committee with the CJI/nominee as Chairperson and casting vote. Past consensus was not a substitute for this safeguard.

Other matters and result. Challenges to contempt powers and Government’s power to constitute benches failed for want of a demonstrated constitutional defect. Several broadly challenged provisions were not argued and did not receive separate merits analysis. The writ petition was partly allowed; the bodies were not abolished. The Court called for prompt corrective steps so that they could function.

Conclusion: MBA accepts specialist tribunalisation while insisting that transferred judicial work retain the independence and standards of a credible judicial institution. The historical holding must be distinguished from subsequent statutory amendments and the present NCLAT service framework.

Practice 2 — “An NCLAT appeal exists. Is a High Court writ against an NCLT order therefore never maintainable?”

Model answer

No. The existence of an effective statutory appeal ordinarily calls for judicial restraint, not extinction of the High Court’s constitutional jurisdiction. Articles 226/227 and the tribunal’s statutory appeal system serve different functions. A High Court should not become a routine additional appellate forum, but a case of inherent want of jurisdiction may justify intervention.

In Embassy Property Developments v. State of Karnataka (2019), a corporate debtor held a statutory mining lease. During CIRP, Karnataka refused deemed extension. The RP obtained NCLT directions setting aside the refusal and requiring supplementary lease deeds. After intervening proceedings and remand, NCLT reiterated the relief; the High Court stayed it. The resolution applicant, corporate debtor through its RP, and CoC argued that IBC section 61 supplied the proper appellate remedy and that writ intervention would disrupt time-bound resolution.

The Supreme Court distinguished insolvency adjudication from public-law judicial review. The refusal arose under the MMDR Act and mining rules. It was not merely a private contract claim. NCLT is a special statutory institution and cannot exercise a superior court’s review over administrative decisions merely because their outcome affects the insolvent company’s assets.

Although section 60(5) is broad, it must be read in the insolvency/liquidation context. The RP’s duties to preserve assets under sections 18, 20 and 25 do not enlarge NCLT’s jurisdiction. Section 25(2)(b) itself contemplates representation in other judicial, quasi-judicial and arbitral forums. Nor can participation or concession confer absent subject-matter jurisdiction. NCLT was coram non judice as to the mining-review relief, so High Court intervention was justified despite the appeal.

The Court separately held that fraudulent CIRP initiation can be examined by NCLT/NCLAT. Section 65 expressly addresses it; sections 66 and 69 also show that the Code deals with fraudulent conduct. Thus the State’s fraud allegation alone would not justify bypassing section 61. It was the independent public-law jurisdictional excess that supported the writ.

Conclusion: State the exact defect. A wrong priority decision within NCLT’s entrusted field ordinarily belongs in appeal; an order assuming public-law jurisdiction not conferred by statute may warrant constitutional review. Embassy supports this distinction, not indiscriminate writ challenges to all NCLT orders.

Practice 3 — “Explain the powers and procedure of NCLT/NCLAT under sections 420–433.”

Model answer

NCLT/NCLAT combine flexible procedure with enforceable judicial authority, but their powers remain controlled by the governing statute and natural justice.

Orders and correction: Section 420 requires NCLT to afford a reasonable opportunity of hearing before passing orders. Within two years it may correct an apparent mistake from the record, and must do so when such a mistake is brought to its notice by parties, subject to the proviso excluding orders appealed under the Act. This is rectification, not unrestricted merits review. Copies go to concerned parties.

Procedure and evidence: Under section 424 the bodies are not bound by CPC procedure, but must follow natural justice and the Act/IBC and rules. They may summon witnesses, examine on oath, order discovery/production, receive affidavit evidence, requisition public records subject to the stated privilege qualification, issue commissions, dismiss for default, proceed ex parte and set aside those procedural orders, besides prescribed powers. Their proceedings have the limited judicial/civil-court deeming status specified in subsection (4).

Inquiry and representation: Section 426 permits authorised persons to inquire and report on a matter connected with a proceeding. It does not authorise surrender of final adjudication. Section 432 permits personal appearance or authorised representation by the listed professionals and other persons. Section 433 applies the Limitation Act as far as may be, subject to special statutory limits.

Execution and contempt: Orders may be enforced as decrees under section 424(3), including through the competent local court. Section 425 confers High Court-like powers in respect of contempt of themselves under the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 with stated modifications. Under section 429, NCLT can seek written assistance from the designated Magistrate/Collector for possession of property/books/documents in winding up or IBC proceedings.

Appeals and speed: Section 421 provides the Companies Act NCLAT appeal: 45 days with a further 45-day maximum condonation on sufficient cause, excluding consent orders. Section 423 provides an SC appeal on a question of law: 60 days plus a possible further 60. Section 422 seeks disposal within three months, with reasons for delay and a further extension up to ninety days. The IBC has distinct appeal periods.

Protection and limits: Sections 427–428 concern public-servant status and good-faith protection; section 431 prevents invalidation merely for the specified institutional vacancy/defect. Section 430 bars civil-court proceedings in the tribunal’s assigned field. None supplies universal civil jurisdiction or abolishes constitutional review. As Embassy shows, strong procedural/enforcement machinery does not confer a missing public-law jurisdiction.

Practice 4 — “Distinguish NCLT from NCLAT and explain the appellate structure.”

Model answer

NCLT is constituted under section 408 and headed by a President; NCLAT is constituted under section 410 and headed by a Chairperson. Both have Judicial and Technical Members, but their institutional roles differ. NCLT is predominantly the first-instance specialist adjudicator in matters assigned by the Companies Act and the IBC. NCLAT exercises the appellate jurisdiction conferred by those and other enactments.

Under the parent Act, the NCLT President requires five years as a High Court judge; section 409 sets judicial and technical routes for members. Section 411 sets the parent NCLAT qualifications, including a Supreme Court judge/former judge or High Court Chief Justice for Chairperson. The current NCLAT appointment/service regime must additionally be read through section 417A and the later legislation; it should not simply be assumed identical to NCLT’s.

NCLT’s ordinary bench under section 419 contains one Judicial and one Technical Member, with an authorised single Judicial Member exception for specified classes. Its Principal Bench is at New Delhi. Jurisdiction is matter-specific: a share rectification application, scheme, oppression proceeding or CIRP must satisfy its own enabling provisions.

For Companies Act orders, section 421 permits an aggrieved person to appeal to NCLAT within 45 days of the prescribed copy-availability trigger. A further period up to 45 days requires sufficient cause. A consent order does not attract that appeal. NCLAT may confirm, modify or set aside after hearing the parties. From its order, section 423 allows an appeal to the Supreme Court on a question of law, within 60 days of receipt, with a possible further 60 days on sufficient cause.

For IBC orders, the relevant routes instead are sections 61 and 62: 30 days plus a maximum additional 15 to NCLAT, and 45 plus a maximum additional 15 to the Supreme Court. The institution remains the same, but the jurisdictional source changes the appeal regime. NFRA and competition appellate work likewise must be located in the relevant statutory provisions, rather than assigned a universal section 421 calendar.

Both institutions follow natural justice and have specified civil-court procedural, execution and contempt powers. Neither is thereby a constitutional High Court. MBA establishes independent institutional design; Embassy preserves the boundary between statutory appeal and exceptional constitutional review.

Practice 5 — “NCLT cannot investigate fraud; therefore every fraud allegation requires a writ. Discuss.”

Model answer

The proposition is incorrect. A fraud allegation must be classified by its statutory setting. Embassy Property Developments (2019) expressly holds that NCLT/NCLAT can investigate the relevant fraud issues within the IBC, including fraudulent initiation of CIRP.

Karnataka alleged that related actors had used the debtor’s insolvency process to corner a mining lease. The appellants answered that insolvency fraud fell within NCLT’s powers and should be addressed through the Code, including appeal to NCLAT. The Supreme Court accepted that part of their argument.

Section 65 directly addresses insolvency resolution/liquidation initiated fraudulently or with malicious intent for a collateral purpose rather than genuine insolvency resolution/liquidation. The adjudicating authority may impose a penalty between ₹1 lakh and ₹1 crore. The quoted voluntary-liquidation limb addresses initiation with intent to defraud. The Court also referred to section 66 concerning fraudulent trading and to the punishment framework in section 69.

Accordingly, if the alleged use of different corporate avatars to capture the mine were proved, it could fall within section 65. The Court did not itself find the fraud established. Competence to inquire and proof of the alleged wrongdoing are different matters. Likewise, reference to the Code’s criminal punishment provision does not turn NCLT into a court with universal criminal trial jurisdiction.

The writ in Embassy was nevertheless justified on a separate ground: NCLT had purported to overturn a statutory mining-law refusal and compel lease deeds, a public-law review function outside its jurisdiction. Its competence to investigate CIRP fraud did not cure this lack of authority over the mining decision.

Conclusion: Fraud is neither a magic word excluding NCLT nor a magic word expanding it. Relevant IBC fraud belongs within the conferred insolvency framework; review of an external statutory administrative decision must be pursued before the competent forum. Fraudulent initiation alone is not a reason to bypass the section 61 appeal.

Practice 6 — “Explain section 430 with reference to judicial review.”

Model answer

Section 430 protects the specialist tribunal’s allocated field by excluding ordinary civil-court jurisdiction over matters which NCLT/NCLAT is empowered to determine under the Companies Act or another law. It also prohibits injunctions by courts or other authorities against action taken or to be taken by those bodies in pursuance of their conferred powers.

The statutory condition is important. Before invoking the bar, identify the nature of the suit and whether the tribunal has authority over that matter and relief. The section does not transfer every contractual, tortious, tax or regulatory dispute involving a company to NCLT. A bar against competing civil adjudication in an assigned field is different from the positive grant of that field.

Constitutional judicial review also remains distinct. High Courts’ powers under Articles 226/227 cannot be extinguished by ordinary legislation, although a High Court normally respects an effective statutory remedy and does not rehear tribunal cases as a routine additional appellate court. The question is whether the complaint concerns a normal error within jurisdiction or a defect justifying constitutional intervention.

In Embassy, the Code entrusted NCLT with insolvency matters, including fraud issues, but not public-law review of the statutory mining-extension refusal. When NCLT nevertheless set aside the State’s refusal and directed supplementary lease deeds, it acted coram non judice regarding that relief. The High Court could entertain the writ despite the section 61 appeal.

Conclusion: Section 430 and judicial review are complementary controls: the former avoids parallel ordinary litigation over assigned tribunal matters; the latter ensures the institution remains within constitutional and statutory bounds. Neither a litigant’s preference for civil court nor routine dissatisfaction with an order defeats the statutory specialist route.

Practice 7 — “Write a reasoned note on tenure, removal and independence.”

Model answer

Judicial independence depends not only on who is appointed but on the security under which adjudicators serve. A short insecure tenure, continuing dependence on a parent department, disadvantageous changes to service conditions or executive control of removal can weaken both actual autonomy and public confidence.

The parent section 413 provides five-year terms with further-term eligibility and office-specific age limits; it also limits lien with the parent cadre/department. Section 414 protects salary, allowances and other conditions from disadvantageous change after appointment. Sections 415–416 maintain continuity during vacancies, temporary inability and resignation. These mechanisms should be read as institutional safeguards, not merely employment benefits.

Under the parent section 417, Government may remove on specified grounds after CJI consultation, with the stated hearing safeguards. The proved-misbehaviour/incapacity route requires inquiry by an SC judge nominated by the CJI, notice of charges and hearing. Suspension under that inquiry route needs CJI concurrence. A mere departmental view that a member’s orders are inconvenient is not one of these statutory procedures.

In MBA (2015) the Court reaffirmed the 2010 concern that meaningful tenure is necessary to build expertise and attract candidates; very short terms can make tribunals post-retirement extensions rather than durable judicial institutions. It also stressed limiting parent-cadre lien, secure suspension arrangements and appropriate administrative support. Equal salary alone cannot compensate for inadequate qualifications or structural dependence.

For accuracy, distinguish these parent-Act provisions from the later common tribunal-service regime linked to NCLAT by section 417A. As explained in Chapter 17, the 2026 Act now supplies that common framework; NCLT must not automatically be treated as included. Historical constitutional reasoning remains important, but an actual appointment requires the correct institution, date, applicable rules and transitional law.

Conclusion: Independence and accountability are not opposites. Clear grounds, an impartial process, adequate tenure and protection from arbitrary disadvantage make accountability compatible with independent adjudication.

Practice 8 — “Does section 434 transfer every pending company matter to NCLT?”

Model answer

No. Section 434 is a differentiated transitional framework, not a universal migration provision stripped of its provisos.

Pending Company Law Board matters transfer under subsection (1)(a). But appeals from pre-transfer CLB orders use subsection (1)(b): appeal to the High Court on a question of law, ordinarily within 60 days of communication and with up to a further 60 days on sufficient cause. This route must not be mistaken for a section 421 appeal.

Court proceedings under the 1956 Act fall within subsection (1)(c), including the identified scheme/reconstruction and winding-up categories, and NCLT may proceed from the pre-transfer stage. However, winding-up transfer depends on the prescribed stage and the applicable transfer rules. Non-winding-up proceedings already reserved for orders are distinguished by the provisos. Saved court proceedings continue under the 1956 Act and Companies (Court) Rules, 1959 as specified.

The 2018 proviso permits parties to the specified pending winding-up proceedings to apply for transfer; the court may transfer them, and proceedings so transferred are dealt with as a CIRP application under the IBC. A party’s request is not itself a completed transfer order.

The section also saves the identified old voluntary-winding-up proceedings where the section 485(1) advertisement had been given but dissolution had not occurred before 1 April 2017. New voluntary liquidations cannot simply be initiated under that historical saving.

Conclusion: Identify the old forum, nature of the proceeding, date, stage and proviso before selecting NCLT or the saved court route. Section 434 balances consolidation with continuity; it does not confer general constitutional judicial review on the new tribunal.

Twelve mistakes to avoid

  1. “NCLT is unconstitutional.” MBA validates the institution while correcting design.
  2. “Every technical member must be a Secretary.” The seniority rule concerns the civil-service route; independent professional routes remain distinct.
  3. “Joint Secretary remains the corrected 409(3)(a) rule.” That was the condemned dilution, not the amended parent standard.
  4. “The selection committee has five members and consensus solves everything.” Not the corrected parent section 412 structure.
  5. “Section 413 governs removal.” Removal is section 417.
  6. “NCLAT has a permanent eleven-member statutory cap.” That cap was omitted.
  7. “NCLT has all CPC powers and all High Court jurisdiction.” Specific procedural/deeming/contempt powers do not establish general jurisdiction.
  8. “All NCLT appeals allow 45 + 45 days.” IBC section 61 is 30 + at most 15.
  9. “Every NCLAT–SC appeal allows 60 + 60.” IBC section 62 is 45 + at most 15.
  10. “Embassy says fraud is outside NCLT.” It expressly holds the relevant IBC fraud inquiry is within it.
  11. “A moratorium creates whatever new right the resolution plan needs.” Not the reasoning of Embassy.
  12. “The 2015 judgment decided the 2026 service framework.” Keep historical ratio, amendments and current-law supplementation separate.

Self-test: answer before opening

1. Does a right to appear as a company secretary under section 432 establish eligibility as a Judicial Member?

No. Representation and appointment are different functions governed by different provisions. The judicial-member qualification routes must independently be met.

2. Does section 420 permit review of any debatable legal conclusion within two years?

No. It permits rectification of a mistake apparent from the record, subject to its conditions and appeal proviso. A general merits rehearing cannot be disguised as rectification.

3. Can a single Technical Member rely on the single-member proviso in section 419(3)?

No. That exception identifies an authorised single Judicial Member for specified classes of cases/matters.

4. What is wrong with saying that section 422 automatically dismisses every case not decided in three months?

The section directs expedition, an endeavour to meet the target, recorded reasons and possible extension. It does not prescribe automatic dismissal or allowance for crossing the target.

5. Why did the appellants lose Embassy despite winning the fraud-jurisdiction argument?

NCLT still lacked jurisdiction to exercise public-law review over the State’s statutory mining refusal. Its competence over insolvency fraud did not cure that independent jurisdictional excess.

6. Can the parties confer NCLT’s missing subject-matter jurisdiction by consent?

No. Full-judgment paragraph 43 of Embassy states that a statutory tribunal cannot be clothed with absent jurisdiction by a party’s concession.

7. What does the word “merely” do in section 431?

It limits the institutional saving. A vacancy/defect by itself should not invalidate proceedings; the provision is not a universal cure for unconstitutional design, absence of required jurisdiction or any other serious legal defect.

8. Is NCLT automatically included whenever a common tribunal-service law covers NCLAT?

No. Section 417A concerns the Appellate Tribunal. The 2026 First Schedule includes NCLAT, not NCLT. Identify the institution and legislative link separately.

The two-case memory formula

MBA = lawful transfer + independent design.

Embassy = lawful insolvency jurisdiction − public-law overreach + power over IBC fraud.

Building a strong answer in limited time

For a doctrinal essay, use definition → statute → issue → case reasoning → qualification → conclusion. For a factual problem, use issue → source of jurisdiction → governing rule → fact-specific application → remedy. Spend more words on the disputed condition than on uncontested facts. Quote a short decisive statutory phrase, such as “empowered to determine,” “mistake apparent from the record,” or “arising out of or in relation to”, and explain what that phrase excludes.

Avoid a catalogue of sections without application. An answer explaining why the RP’s duty does not create mining-review jurisdiction is stronger than one listing every power of NCLT but never identifying the defect.

Supplementary current-law context • researched 8 September 2026 • not part of the prescribed 2015 or 2019 holdings

Why this appendix is necessary

Tribunal appointment law has changed repeatedly. The uploaded textbook and online consolidated Companies Act pages can retain historical language. This guide therefore explains the parent provisions for the syllabus while isolating the later NCLAT framework. The appendix is not an exhaustive recruitment manual: institution-specific rules, current notices and individual transitional entitlements still need checking for an actual appointment.

The intervening 2025 constitutional judgment

In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India, 19 November 2025, 2025 INSC 1330, the Supreme Court addressed reintroduction of tribunal-service provisions previously invalidated. The judgment criticised the renewed minimum-age, truncated-tenure and selection provisions and reaffirmed that Parliament may cure a defect but cannot merely reproduce invalidated substance and declare judicial directions irrelevant.

Paragraph 151 directed that the principles in the earlier MBA IV and V decisions govern appointment/service issues pending legislation faithfully addressing the constitutional defects. Paragraphs 152–153 directed establishment of a National Tribunals Commission, with a four-month period. Paragraphs 154–155 addressed protection of the identified appointments and transitional cohorts. Read these as the 2025 judgment’s directions, not as if no subsequent legislation could occur. Full judgment.

The 2026 Act: enactment and commencement

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026, Act 17 of 2026, received assent on 13 August 2026. The Gazette copy was examined for this appendix; section 18 and the Second Schedule substitute Companies Act section 417A. The reported commencement notification brings all provisions into force from 25 August 2026. The Gazette enactment establishes the text; the commencement date here is supported by the SCC Times notification report. 1

Text examined: Gazette copy of the 2026 Act, hosted with the Bar & Bench report. Do not substitute an introduced Bill for the enacted text when giving a final legal proposition.

The substituted section 417A

The new section provides, notwithstanding the Companies Act, that NCLAT Chairperson/member qualifications, manner of selection, appointment, salaries and allowances, resignation, removal, other service conditions and reappointment eligibility are governed by the Tribunals Reforms Act, 2026.

Institutional scope: NCLAT appears in the First Schedule, entry 13. NCLT does not appear there. This is why it is wrong to announce that the entire NCLT service system has automatically been replaced merely because section 417A changed. A later amendment to the schedule would itself require attention; this appendix reports the enacted framework checked.

Selection under section 13: count votes, not just people

For a tribunal Chairperson, the committee is headed by the Commission Chairperson; for a tribunal member, it is headed by a Commission Judicial Member. It also includes a Commission Technical Member, the designated retired judicial member (a retired HC Chief Justice for chairperson selection; a retired HC judge for member selection), a Government Secretary, two empanelled experts and the Commission Secretary as Member Secretary.

The crucial qualification: Under section 13(5), the expert members and Member Secretary have no vote. The Chairperson has a casting vote under section 13(4). Merely counting all seven participants without distinguishing voting status gives a misleading comparison with MBA’s executive-majority problem.

Section 14(6) provides for a suitable candidate and one additional waiting-list name per vacancy. The Secretariat communicates the recommendation within three days; Government must make the appointment within three months of receipt, under subsections (7)–(8). The waiting-list arrangement is not properly described as unqualified executive freedom to choose either of two coequal recommended candidates.

Transitional care under section 24

The Act expressly addresses several cohorts, including:

  • appointments on/after its commencement under the new Act/rules;
  • the defined older appointments made on or before 1 October 2021, based on selection/recommendation under the relevant parent enactments/rules;
  • other specified 2021-Act appointees appointed on/after 4 April 2021 and still in office immediately before commencement, for whom the five-year/age-limit rule is stated;
  • protection against disadvantageous service changes for the specified continuing cohort;
  • continuation of identified pre-Commission selection committees and completion of pending selection processes, with the specified new-law consequences.

The Act’s commencement is not, by itself, proof that the Commission has been fully staffed or every implementing rule notified. No such operational-status claim is made here. Nor does a five-year term mean every existing appointee receives five fresh years from 25 August 2026; the statutory cohort and original appointment date matter.

How to use later law in a DU answer

For “Discuss MBA, decided 14 May 2015”, give the historical facts, issues and holdings first. Add a short separately labelled update only if useful. Do not rewrite the 2015 dispute around a Commission created by later law.

For “Explain current constitution/service conditions of NCLAT”, identify the parent architecture under sections 410–411, then section 417A’s operative link, the 2026 framework, applicable rules and savings. Do not stop at a stale parent-Act table.

For “Judicial review—Embassy”, the appointment reforms do not replace the core jurisdictional distinction between insolvency adjudication and public-law review. Explain the 2019 ratio on its own terms.
