01 · How to use this DU-first guide

Winding up is not merely the closure of a business. It is a supervised, collective process for administering a company’s affairs, protecting its assets, settling claims and ultimately ending its legal existence. The central exam question is: when does failure, conflict or misconduct justify ending the company rather than correcting the problem?

02 · Concepts, boundaries and the statutory map

Meaning of winding up

A liquidator takes charge of the company’s property and affairs, identifies and realises assets, determines liabilities, settles claims according to legal priorities, adjusts the rights of contributories and distributes any remaining surplus. The process culminates, ordinarily, in dissolution. The liquidator acts within a statutory office under supervision, not simply as an agent of the shareholder who filed the petition. Source: T, Chapter 24, opening discussion; ss. 278, 283, 290 and 302.

The core components are:

  1. A legal ground and competent petitioner: proving a grievance does not by itself establish standing or a statutory basis for winding up.
  2. Judicial assessment: the Tribunal assesses evidence and exercises a judicial discretion; it does not mechanically execute a shareholder’s preference.
  3. Collective administration: the company’s assets are administered for all persons with legally recognised claims, not appropriated by the first petitioner.
  4. Preservation and realisation: assets may be sold separately or as a going concern where appropriate; winding up does not invariably require immediate destruction of productive value.
  5. Claims and contribution: creditors establish claims; persons liable for unpaid capital or guarantees contribute within statutory limits.
  6. Distribution and dissolution: claim priorities must be respected before members receive the residual estate; legal personality ends through dissolution, not merely presentation of the petition.

Why the historical cases still matter

German Date Coffee and Yenidje are English decisions. Seth Mohan Lal arose under the 1913 Act; Aluminium arose under the 1956 Act. They do not literally interpret present section 271. They are prescribed because they illuminate the concepts of substratum, deadlock, judicial discretion and proper use of the remedy. In a modern answer, give the current section first, explain the historical holding accurately, and then make the doctrinal connection.

03 · Grounds: sections 270 and 271 in depth

Section 270 — the gateway, not a list of two present modesSource ↗

The provisions of Part I apply to winding up by the Tribunal under the Act. Its present role is to identify the applicable statutory regime. The former wording describing both Tribunal and voluntary winding up was substituted following the IBC changes. An answer repeating the old two-mode text as current section 270 would be inaccurate.

Section 271(a) — special resolution for winding up by the TribunalSource ↗

Elements: there must be a company resolution; it must be a special resolution; and it must resolve that the company be wound up by the Tribunal. A directors’ resolution alone is not the special resolution contemplated by this clause. Under the general rule in s. 114(2), votes in favour must be not less than three times votes against, together with the applicable notice requirements. This is not “75% of all issued shares regardless of attendance”.

Legal effect: the resolution supplies a ground for a petition; it does not itself dissolve the company. The Tribunal retains discretion under s. 273 and must consider the statutory purpose and affected interests. A resolution to pursue a qualifying voluntary liquidation under the IBC is a different route.

Illustration: members resolve to seek Tribunal winding up because the venture has served its purpose. The liquidator does not acquire office merely on the vote; the Tribunal process and appointment must follow. Source: s. 271(a); T, para. 24.3.1.

Section 271(b) — acts against specified national and public interestsSource ↗

The company must have acted against the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency; or morality. The statutory words matter: this is not simply any commercial conduct that someone dislikes or loosely calls “against public interest”. Identify the act, the protected interest and the connection between them.

Section 272(1)(f) expressly enables the Central Government or a State Government to petition in a case falling under this clause. Read that alongside the rest of s. 272; do not use a textbook paraphrase to delete other statutory classes without analysing the section. The Tribunal’s adjudicatory function remains essential; a government allegation is not itself a winding-up order.

Section 271(c) — fraud, unlawful formation, misfeasance or misconductSource ↗

This clause has a special applicant requirement and a substantive judicial satisfaction requirement.

Who initiates the clause-specific application? The Registrar or another person authorised by Central Government notification under the Act.

What must the Tribunal find? One of the following statutory situations:

  • The affairs of the company have been conducted in a fraudulent manner; or
  • The company was formed for a fraudulent and unlawful purpose; or
  • Persons concerned in its formation or management have been guilty of fraud, misfeasance or misconduct in connection therewith;
  • And it is proper that the company be wound up.

The final “proper” requirement is important. The statute does not say that mentioning the word fraud automatically ends the company. Explain the conduct, its connection with formation or affairs, the admissible material and why corporate winding up is justified.

Distinguish the concepts: fraud concerns dishonest deception or abuse; misfeasance is wrongful performance of a duty or misuse of entrusted powers; misconduct is a wider category of improper conduct in the relevant corporate connection. These explanatory meanings do not replace statutory definitions or proof requirements in a specific proceeding. A weak business decision is not necessarily fraud.

Devas connection: the 2021 High Court decision principally concerns the validity of authorisation and whether Registrar-specific pre-sanction representation requirements extend to the authorised-person route. It does not establish that executive authorisation conclusively proves s. 271(c) fraud. See the detailed brief below.

Section 271(d) — five consecutive years of filing defaultSource ↗

Elements: default in filing with the Registrar the company’s financial statements or annual returns for the immediately preceding five consecutive financial years. Note the disjunctive “or”: do not rewrite the clause as requiring default in both documents. The years must be consecutive, and the immediately preceding period must be identified.

Not enough under this clause: an isolated late filing; five scattered defaults separated by compliant years; a failure to hold an AGM described without establishing the specified filing default. These facts may have other statutory consequences but must not be treated as automatically satisfying s. 271(d).

Reason for the ground: persistent absence of corporate disclosure undermines supervision and accountability. Nevertheless, the Tribunal must deal with the petition judicially under the statutory framework.

Section 271(e) — just and equitableSource ↗

This is a residuary equitable ground, not an unlimited licence to dissolve companies whenever shareholders disagree. Its content is developed through circumstances in which continued corporate existence has become fundamentally inconsistent with the basis of the association, the conduct expected in it, or the legitimate interests affected.

Five questions for any problem:

  1. What was the company’s real foundation: a particular project, multiple genuinely independent objects, or a relationship of personal participation?
  2. What has failed: the essential undertaking, only one line of business, or mutual cooperation at management level?
  3. Is the failure substantial and enduring, rather than temporary or speculative?
  4. What do the evidence, assets, liabilities, remaining powers and realistic future possibilities show?
  5. Is winding up a justified response, having considered stakeholders and s. 273(2) alternatives?

A. Loss of substratum

Substratum means the real foundation or substantial purpose of the company. The DU extract of Seth Mohan Lal formulates disappearance of substratum where the object of incorporation has substantially failed, where business cannot be carried on except at a loss, or where existing and possible assets are insufficient to meet existing liabilities. Those formulations arose historically; use them with the present distinction between equitable winding up and the IBC debt route.

Do not equate dominant activity with the only permissible object. A company may currently trade mainly in one commodity while its constitution genuinely permits several independent lines. Conversely, pages of general objects do not necessarily transform a specific-purpose venture into an unrestricted enterprise. The memorandum must be construed as a whole.

German Date Coffee: a German-patent venture without a German patent had lost its foundation even though another form of date-coffee business remained possible. Seth Mohan Lal: restriction of gur futures did not destroy a multi-commodity exchange’s foundation. The distinction is one of substantive objects and realistic capacity, not merely the number of clauses in a memorandum.

B. Deadlock in management

A deadlock must be more than disagreement. Relevant features include equal voting power, inability to pass necessary resolutions, no effective casting vote or workable exit, entrenched hostility, and inability to cooperate in the expected management relationship. Yenidje shows that profits do not necessarily save a company whose management relationship has irretrievably broken down.

Proof checklist: identify board composition, shareholding, quorum and voting requirements, disputes over essential decisions, attempts at arbitration or settlement, and whether practical business can continue on a stable legal basis. One lost vote is not a deadlock. A minority that lacks sufficient votes cannot merely relabel majority control as “deadlock”.

C. Quasi-partnership considerations

A company remains a separate legal person. In an appropriate closely held company, however, equitable expectations based on a personal association may supplement formal rights. Personal mutual confidence, an understanding of participation in management, and restrictions on withdrawal may make a purely formal approach unjust. Yenidje illustrates partnership reasoning in a company effectively built around two equal participants.

Do not say every private company is a partnership. The DU discussion of Hind Overseas elsewhere in the case compilation cautions against automatic transplantation of partnership law. Identify why the particular association has the relevant personal and management foundation.

D. Loss of confidence based on lack of probity

Distinguish justifiable loss of confidence in honest management from resentment at being outvoted. Persistent diversion of assets, concealment of accounts and oppressive exclusion may support equitable intervention. Ordinary policy differences, isolated procedural errors or unproved accusations do not inevitably justify ending the company. The appropriate response may instead be accounts, recovery, correction of management or relief under ss. 241–242.

E. Persistent losses, illegality and a sham enterprise

A sustained inability to conduct the company’s actual undertaking except at a loss may be relevant, but a bad year does not establish hopeless failure. A legal prohibition may destroy a sole purpose, but may leave genuine independent objects untouched. A company with no real business foundation may present a different case from a genuine enterprise temporarily inactive. Analyse evidence, rather than treating “losses”, “ban” or “bubble company” as self-proving labels.

The limiting provision: section 273(2)

Where the petition is based on just-and-equitable winding up, the Tribunal may refuse if it considers both that another remedy is available and that the petitioners are acting unreasonably in seeking winding up instead of pursuing it. The bare existence of some imaginable remedy is not sufficient analysis; explain its availability, adequacy and the unreasonable choice of the drastic remedy.

Possible alternatives, where legally available, include ss. 241–242 relief, a purchase of shares, enforcement of an agreed exit, rectification, accounting, or civil proceedings over a genuine debt dispute. Do not promise a buyout in every case; the legal basis and practical feasibility must be shown.

04 · Petition, hearing and immediate safeguards: sections 272–274

Section 272 — who may petition?Source ↗

Clause Eligible petitioner Qualification / caution
(1)(a) The company Company petition must be accompanied by a prescribed statement of affairs under (4)
(1)(b) Any contributory or contributories Apply the special rules in (2); a fully paid shareholder is not automatically excluded
(1)(c) All or any of the persons in (a) and (b) Permits the specified joint presentation
(1)(d) Registrar Prior Central Government sanction and company representation safeguard under (3)
(1)(e) Person authorised by Central Government Distinct route considered in Devas; authority must be established

Creditors: the current list does not contain the old general category “any creditor, including contingent or prospective creditor”. A creditor may have another statutory capacity, but a debt alone is not current s. 272 standing. This is a major historical/current-law trap.

Contributory conditions under (2): relevant shares, or some of them, must either have been originally allotted to the petitioner; or held by and registered in the petitioner’s name for at least six months within the eighteen months immediately before commencement; or devolved through the death of a former holder. These are alternative limbs, not a universal six-month condition. The section expressly permits petitioning despite fully paid shares, no company assets, or no surplus for members after liabilities are satisfied.

Registrar safeguards under (3): the Registrar may petition under s. 271 except on the special-resolution ground in clause (a). Obtain previous Central Government sanction; the Government cannot accord that sanction without giving the company a reasonable opportunity to make representations. The former exclusion of the just-and-equitable ground was removed by the 2019 amendment. Do not carry that old restriction into a present answer.

Copies and views under (5): a copy must also be filed with the Registrar; the Registrar must submit views to the Tribunal within 60 days of receipt. This is different from permission to initiate a Registrar petition, and different from the liquidator’s 60-day report under s. 281.

Section 273 — orders available to the TribunalSource ↗

On receiving the petition, the Tribunal may:

  1. Dismiss it, with or without costs.
  2. Make an interim order it thinks fit.
  3. Appoint a provisional liquidator until a winding-up order is made.
  4. Order winding up, with or without costs.
  5. Make any other order it thinks fit within its statutory jurisdiction.

The statutory direction is to make the order within 90 days of presentation. That is not a rule that the entire liquidation must finish in 90 days. The judicial function must still respect evidence, natural justice and the applicable statute.

Before appointing a provisional liquidator: normally give notice and a reasonable opportunity for company representations. Notice may be dispensed with for special reasons recorded in writing. The provisional office is designed to protect the estate pending decision, not to presume a final merits result.

No-assets proviso: the Tribunal must not refuse a winding-up order merely because assets are mortgaged to their full value or more, or because the company has no assets. This does not mean “no assets automatically requires winding up”; the other statutory conditions remain necessary.

Alternative remedy: s. 273(2), discussed above, is specifically directed to the just-and-equitable petition. Its wording does not dispense with the need to assess the proper use of other statutory grounds.

Section 274 — statement of affairs and booksSource ↗

When a person other than the company petitions: if a prima facie case is made out, the Tribunal directs the company to file objections and its statement of affairs within 30 days of the order. It may allow another 30 days in a contingency or special circumstances. It may require the petitioner to deposit reasonable security for costs before issuing those directions.

Non-compliance: failure to file the statement means the company forfeits its right to oppose the petition; responsible directors and officers face the consequences under (4). This does not convert false factual allegations into proved facts or eliminate the Tribunal’s duty to apply the law.

After the winding-up order: within 30 days, directors and other officers must submit, at company cost, books completed and audited up to the date of the order, to the liquidator as the Tribunal specifies.

Section 274(4): the defaulting director/officer is punishable with imprisonment up to six months, or fine from ₹25,000 to ₹5 lakh, or both. Under (5), the Registrar, provisional liquidator, Company Liquidator or a Tribunal-authorised person may complain before the Special Court. Do not confuse this still-operative provision with the substituted cooperation mechanism in s. 284.

Evidence-building illustration: the statement of affairs reveals assets, liabilities and operations. It may show that an alleged sole-business failure is actually only temporary, or that proclaimed solvency is unsupported. It is therefore both a compliance document and a foundation for an informed winding-up decision.

05 · Liquidator, consequences and asset protection: sections 275–284

Section 275 — appointment, authority, fees and independenceSource ↗

The Tribunal appoints an Official Liquidator or a liquidator under the statutory framework as Company Liquidator when it orders winding up. The amended subsection (2) uses insolvency professionals registered under the IBC for the provisional/Company Liquidator appointment route. The old professional-panel wording based merely on ten years’ experience must not be repeated as current (2).

A provisional liquidator ordinarily has the same powers as a liquidator unless the appointment order or a later order limits them. The Tribunal specifies appointment terms and fees with regard to tasks, experience, qualification and company size. Within seven days of appointment, the liquidator files a declaration disclosing conflict of interest or lack of independence; the obligation continues throughout the appointment. A provisional liquidator may be appointed Company Liquidator on the final winding-up order. Subsection (4), relating to the former panel-removal mechanism, is omitted.

Why it matters: independence is not merely checked on day one. A subsequent financial interest or relationship must be addressed; the liquidator’s job is to administer the estate impartially.

Section 276 — removal and replacementSource ↗

The Tribunal may remove the provisional or Company Liquidator on reasonable cause and recorded reasons for: misconduct; fraud or misfeasance; professional incompetence or failure of due care and diligence; inability to act; or conflict of interest/lack of independence. The affected liquidator must receive a reasonable opportunity of being heard.

Death, resignation or removal requires transfer of the unfinished work to another liquidator by a reasoned order. Where fraud, misfeasance or failure of due care and diligence has caused loss or damage to the company, the Tribunal may direct recovery from the liquidator and make appropriate orders. Removal of an office-holder and recovery of compensation are related but distinct responses.

Section 277 — notification and winding-up committeeSource ↗

Seven-day intimation: the Tribunal must cause intimation of provisional appointment or winding up to be sent to the liquidator and Registrar within seven days of the order. The Registrar records the fact, notifies the Official Gazette and, for a listed company, informs the stock exchange(s).

Employment consequence: the winding-up order is deemed notice of discharge to officers, employees and workmen, except where the business continues. This is not a rule extinguishing accrued wage or other legal claims.

Within three weeks of the winding-up order: the Company Liquidator applies to constitute a winding-up committee, comprising the Official Liquidator attached to the Tribunal, a secured-creditor nominee and a professional nominated by the Tribunal. The Company Liquidator convenes meetings.

The committee assists and monitors: taking over assets; examining the statement of affairs; recovering property and benefits; reviewing audits and accounts; selling assets; finalising creditors and contributories; compromising, abandoning or settling claims; paying dividends; and other directed functions. Monthly reports with signed minutes go to the Tribunal. The liquidator prepares a draft final report for committee consideration and approval, then submits the approved final report for dissolution.

Section 278 — a representative, collective orderSource ↗

The winding-up order operates in favour of all creditors and all contributories, as though made on their joint petition. Thus a petitioner cannot treat the liquidation as private execution for exclusive advantage. This statutory collective character helps explain the attention to other creditors and workers in Aluminium Corporation.

Section 279 — stay of suits and other proceedingsSource ↗

After a winding-up order or provisional-liquidator appointment, no suit or other legal proceeding is to be commenced or, if pending at the order date, proceeded with by or against the company except with Tribunal leave and on its terms. A leave application must be disposed of within 60 days. Proceedings pending in appeal before the Supreme Court or a High Court are excepted by subsection (2).

Purpose: preserve orderly administration and avoid uncontrolled litigation draining the estate. Limits: do not present it as an indiscriminate extinction of every criminal, regulatory, public-law or special-statute proceeding. The nature of the proceeding and any overriding statutory regime must be examined. Merely filing the winding-up petition does not itself trigger the precise s. 279 stay.

Section 280 — Tribunal jurisdiction in winding upSource ↗

Notwithstanding other law, the Tribunal has jurisdiction to entertain or dispose of suits/proceedings by or against the company; claims by or against it, including claims involving its Indian branches; applications under s. 233; and questions of priorities and other questions of law or fact arising out of or relating to winding up. The provision applies whether matters were instituted or arose before or after the order, under the temporal wording of the section.

Doctrinal caution: jurisdiction is connected to the statute. Do not turn “any question of law or fact” into unlimited authority over every governmental public-law decision. The uploaded DU adjudicatory-bodies materials themselves distinguish statutory Tribunal jurisdiction from constitutional judicial review.

Section 281 — initial liquidator’s report: 60 daysSource ↗

Within 60 days from the relevant order, the liquidator reports to the Tribunal. A strong answer organises the contents into five groups:

Group Required detail
Estate Nature, location and value of assets; cash in hand and bank separately; negotiable securities; valuation by registered valuers
Capital and obligations Issued, subscribed and paid-up capital; existing and contingent liabilities; creditor identities; secured/unsecured amounts separately; security particulars, values and dates; guarantees
Recoveries and membership Debts owed to company, debtor identities and likely realisations; contributories, dues and unpaid calls
Business architecture Trademarks and intellectual property; subsisting contracts, joint ventures and collaborations; holding and subsidiary companies; legal cases by or against company
Assessment Promotion/formation and suspected fraud; viability or steps to maximise value; other directed or necessary information

Further reports are permissible. Persons describing themselves in writing as creditors or contributories may inspect at reasonable times and obtain copies/extracts on payment of prescribed fees. The report is not just a balance sheet: it combines asset identification, recoverability, misconduct assessment and a realisation strategy.

Section 282 — action on the reportSource ↗

The Tribunal fixes a time limit for completion and dissolution. It may revise the limit where continued proceedings are not advantageous or economical, after the required hearing. It may order sale of the company as a going concern or of all/part of its assets, and constitute a sale committee of such creditors, promoters and officers as it decides.

On a report of fraud from the liquidator, Central Government or another person, the Tribunal orders investigation under s. 210 without prejudicing liquidation. After the investigation report, it may give directions under ss. 339–342 or direct a criminal complaint. It may also direct steps to protect, preserve or enhance asset value, and make other appropriate orders. An initial suspicion, an investigation and a final liability finding are different stages.

Section 283 — custody is not automatic personal ownershipSource ↗

Upon winding up or provisional appointment, the liquidator takes custody/control of property, effects and actionable claims to which the company is or appears entitled, as the Tribunal directs. Company property is deemed in the Tribunal’s custody from the winding-up order date. Persons such as contributories, trustees, bankers, agents and officers may be ordered to pay, deliver, surrender or transfer company money, property, books and papers to the liquidator.

Legal title is not thereby beneficially transferred to the liquidator personally. A third party’s property does not become the company’s simply because it is physically present in company premises. Title, security and competing claims may need adjudication.

Section 284 — full cooperation, including former insidersSource ↗

Promoters, directors, officers and employees, present or former and persons acting or associated with the company within the section, must extend full cooperation. If a required person fails to assist, the liquidator may apply to the Tribunal; the Tribunal directs compliance with the liquidator’s instructions and cooperation in performance of duties.

Amendment alert: the former six-month/₹50,000 criminal-punishment wording in subsection (2) was replaced in 2020 by this directions mechanism and subsection (3). That does not immunise fraud, concealment or disobedience from other applicable law; it means the old penalty is not the current wording of s. 284.

06 · Contributions, supervision and accounts: sections 285–298

Section 285 — list of contributories and application of assetsSource ↗

After the winding-up order, the Tribunal settles the list, rectifies the members’ register where required and applies assets to discharge liabilities. Settlement may be dispensed with if there is no need to make calls or adjust contributories’ rights. Distinguish contributories in their own right from representatives of others.

Current and former members: the list may include persons who are or have been members, subject to strict limits:

  • A former member is not liable if membership ended one year or more before commencement.
  • A former member is not liable for debts contracted after departure.
  • A former member is called on only if present members cannot satisfy required contributions.
  • For a company limited by shares, ordinary membership contribution cannot exceed the unpaid amount on the shares.
  • For a guarantee company, contribution cannot exceed the undertaking; where it also has share capital, unpaid share liability is additional.

Numerical illustration: a member holds 1,000 shares of ₹10 each, with ₹7 paid. The ordinary outstanding share contribution is at most ₹3,000, not the full company deficit. A ₹50 lakh shortfall does not itself lift this limit. A separate fraud-based liability, if established under the applicable law, is a different issue.

Former member illustration: X transfers shares eight months before commencement. Liability is not automatic: check pre-transfer debt, present members’ inability and unpaid capital. Y left eighteen months before commencement and falls outside the former-member contribution window under this provision.

Section 286 — directors/managers whose liability is unlimitedSource ↗

In a limited company, a director or manager whose liability is unlimited under the Act may have to make a further contribution as though a member of an unlimited company at commencement, in addition to ordinary membership contribution.

The former office-holder is protected where office ended at least a year before commencement, and against liabilities contracted after leaving. Subject to the articles, further contribution is required only where the Tribunal considers it necessary to meet debts, liabilities and winding-up costs. Do not say every director automatically has unlimited liability on liquidation. This provision addresses a specified class.

Section 287 — advisory committeeSource ↗

The Tribunal may direct an advisory committee to advise the liquidator and report on specified matters. It comprises not more than twelve members, drawn from creditors, contributories or others in the proportions the Tribunal directs. The liquidator convenes a creditors’ and contributories’ meeting within 30 days of the winding-up order to enable the Tribunal to determine membership.

The committee may inspect accounts, documents, assets and property at reasonable times. Meetings are chaired by the liquidator and follow prescribed procedure. Its purpose is advice and reporting, not replacing the Tribunal’s ultimate control.

The liquidator reports periodically and at the end of each quarter on winding-up progress in the prescribed form and manner. On the liquidator’s application, the Tribunal may review its orders and modify them. Section 289 — omittedSource ↗

The earlier section entitled “Power of Tribunal on application for stay of winding up” has been omitted following the IBC amendments. An exam answer should identify this status, not recite a repealed stay-and-revival procedure as present law. This does not mean no interim or appellate relief can ever exist under another legal provision; it means s. 289 is not the present source.

Section 290 — powers and duties of Company LiquidatorSource ↗

Subject to Tribunal directions and overall control, the liquidator has the following statutory toolkit:

  1. Carry on business so far as necessary for beneficial winding up; this is not unrestricted permission for a new speculative venture.
  2. Execute deeds, receipts and documents in the company’s name and on its behalf, using its seal where necessary.
  3. Sell movable/immovable property and actionable claims, by public auction or private contract, together or in parcels.
  4. Sell the whole undertaking as a going concern, preserving value where appropriate.
  5. Raise money on security of company assets where required for the administration.
  6. Institute or defend civil/criminal suits, prosecutions and other proceedings in the company’s name.
  7. Invite and settle claims of creditors, employees and others, and distribute sale proceeds according to Act priorities.
  8. Inspect records and returns with the Registrar or another authority.
  9. Prove and rank in a contributory’s insolvency, receiving appropriate dividends on the balance owed.
  10. Draw, accept, make and endorse negotiable instruments, including cheques, bills, hundis and promissory notes, in the company’s name.
  11. Obtain letters of administration in the official name for a deceased contributory and take necessary steps to recover estate liabilities where recovery cannot conveniently be pursued in the company’s name. The limited statutory deeming of the debt as due to the liquidator facilitates this recovery; it is not a personal windfall.
  12. Obtain professional assistance and appoint an agent for tasks the liquidator cannot perform personally and for protection of assets.
  13. Sign and verify required papers and instruments for winding up, distribution and discharge of duties.
  14. Apply for Tribunal orders/directions needed for winding up.

The Tribunal may specify further duties. The liquidator must use powers for the statutory purpose, not for private preference. A sale might be within the listed powers but still challengeable if undertaken improperly or contrary to directions.

Section 291 — professional assistanceSource ↗

With Tribunal sanction, the liquidator may appoint one or more chartered accountants, company secretaries, cost accountants, legal practitioners or other professionals to assist. Appointed professionals must disclose forthwith to the Tribunal, in the prescribed form, any conflict of interest or lack of independence in respect of the appointment. Read this specific safeguard with s. 290’s assistance power; delegation does not eliminate the liquidator’s accountability.

Section 292 — directions, discretion and challengeSource ↗

In administration and distribution, the liquidator must have regard to directions by creditor/contributory resolutions at general meetings and by the advisory committee. Where these conflict, the general-meeting directions override advisory-committee directions. This does not place either above the statute or Tribunal.

The liquidator may summon meetings to ascertain wishes, and must do so when directed by resolution or requested in writing by not less than one-tenth in value of creditors or contributories, as applicable. Subject to the Act, the liquidator retains administrative discretion. Any person aggrieved by an act or decision may apply to the Tribunal, which may confirm, reverse, modify or make further just orders.

Section 293 — books of proceedingsSource ↗

Maintain prescribed books with entries/minutes of meeting proceedings and other prescribed matters. Subject to Tribunal control, creditors and contributories may inspect personally or through an agent. This creates an audit trail of decisions; it differs from the financial accounting and audit obligations under s. 294.

Section 294 — accounts and auditSource ↗

Maintain proper regular books, including receipts and payments. Present verified accounts in duplicate and at least twice each year during tenure. The Tribunal causes audit, can require vouchers and information and inspect books. One audited copy is filed with the Tribunal; the other is delivered to the Registrar and is open to inspection by creditors, contributories and interested persons.

For a government company, the relevant Central/State Government member receives a copy; both receive it if both are members. Audited accounts or a summary are printed and sent by post to creditors and contributories unless the Tribunal dispenses with that requirement. Accuracy point: this is oversight of liquidation accounts; it is not simply the ordinary company’s pre-liquidation annual audit.

Section 295 — debts from contributories and set-offSource ↗

After the order, the Tribunal may direct a listed contributory to pay money due to the company from that person or the represented estate, excluding money due by virtue of a call, which is dealt with separately.

Set-off is carefully limited: for an unlimited company, independent contractual/dealing debts owed to the contributory may be allowed, but not member entitlements to dividends or profits. A limited-company director/manager whose liability is unlimited may receive the corresponding set-off. Once all creditors are paid in full, in any company, money due on any account to a contributory may be allowed against a subsequent call.

Illustration: “The company owes me a dividend, so I will not pay my capital contribution” is not an unrestricted defence against the creditors’ estate. Separate ordinary debt recovery, capital calls and final member adjustment.

Section 296 — calls by the TribunalSource ↗

Before or after assets’ sufficiency is ascertained, the Tribunal may make calls on contributories to the extent of their liability for money necessary to meet debts, liabilities, costs and adjustment of contributory rights, and may order payment. It may take account of the probability that some contributories will not pay in full or at all. This practical allowance does not enlarge another member’s legal maximum liability.

Section 297 — adjustment and surplusSource ↗

The Tribunal adjusts contributories’ rights among themselves and distributes any surplus among those entitled. Ordinary shareholders do not share the gross asset pool equally with creditors merely because they own the company. Class rights, paid-up amounts and the constitution may affect the residual allocation; equal division is not universal.

Section 298 — costs where assets are insufficientSource ↗

If the assets are insufficient to satisfy liabilities, the Tribunal may order payment out of the assets of winding-up costs, charges and expenses in the priority it considers just. This is an administration-cost provision, not a complete statutory ranking of every claim. Distribution answers must also consider ss. 326–327 and the applicable security/priority framework.

07 · Examination, enforcement and dissolution: sections 299–303

Section 299 — information and property recoverySource ↗

After provisional appointment or a winding-up order, the Tribunal may summon officers; persons known or suspected to possess company property/books/papers; persons known or suspected to owe the company money; and persons capable of giving relevant information about formation, promotion, business, dealings or affairs.

It may examine on oath orally, through written interrogatories or on affidavit, reduce oral answers to writing and require signatures. Books and papers may be required. Claimed liens are preserved pending determination; production does not automatically waive them.

The Tribunal may call for the liquidator’s report concerning third-party debts/property. If indebtedness or possession of company property is found, it can order payment or delivery on just terms, with appropriate examination costs. Unexplained failure to attend can attract costs. Payment/delivery orders are executable like CPC decrees; compliance ordinarily discharges liability concerning that debt/property unless the order directs otherwise.

Distinction: suspicion permits inquiry; it does not itself prove the final debt or ownership claim.

Section 300 — examination following a fraud reportSource ↗

Trigger: a winding-up order plus a liquidator’s report expressing the opinion that fraud has been committed in promotion, formation, business or conduct of affairs. After considering it, the Tribunal may require the person/officer to attend examination concerning the relevant conduct.

The liquidator participates and may use specially authorised legal assistance. The examinee answers on oath. Before examination, the person receives the report at their own cost and may retain authorised professional representation to ask permitted questions explaining or qualifying answers.

A person may seek exculpation from the charges; the liquidator must draw relevant matters to the Tribunal’s attention. If exculpation is granted, appropriate costs may be awarded. Notes are written, read over/to the person, signed and supplied in copy, may be used in evidence and are inspectable by creditors/contributories. Examination can be adjourned or delegated to an authorised person/authority, which exercises examination powers but not the costs power.

Major correction: s. 300 is an examination provision. It is not the general provision imposing unlimited personal liability for fraudulent trading; the relevant neighbouring substantive provisions include ss. 339–340.

Section 301 — preventing evasionSource ↗

Before or after the winding-up order, the Tribunal may act where satisfied that a contributory or person possessing company property/accounts/papers is about to leave India, abscond, remove or conceal property to evade calls or avoid examination. The section provides for detention of the contributory and seizure/safe keeping of books, papers and movable property until the Tribunal orders otherwise.

The evasive purpose and statutory conditions must be established. Ordinary travel or an unpaid claim does not alone justify this coercive power. An answer should identify the power precisely rather than suggest arbitrary arrest of all directors.

Section 302 — dissolutionSource ↗

Once affairs are completely wound up, the Company Liquidator applies for dissolution. On that application, or where the Tribunal considers dissolution just and reasonable in the circumstances, it orders that the company be dissolved from the date of the order.

Under the amended subsection (3), within 30 days the Tribunal forwards a copy to the Registrar and directs the liquidator to forward a copy; the Registrar records the minute of dissolution. The former daily-default fine in subsection (4) was omitted in 2020. Do not reproduce the old subsection as current law.

Exam conclusion: the company is not dissolved merely because members voted, a petition was filed, a liquidator was appointed or assets were sold. Identify the legal dissolution event.

Section 303 — historical orders and appealsSource ↗

The Chapter does not affect operation or enforcement of court winding-up orders made immediately before commencement of the Act; appeals against those orders go to the authority competent before commencement. This is a saving/transitional provision, not the general modern appeal section.

For present Tribunal orders, the general appeal framework is in s. 421 (NCLAT) and s. 423 (Supreme Court on a question of law), subject to their conditions. Section 421 ordinarily uses 45 days with a further period not exceeding 45 days for sufficient cause; s. 423 ordinarily uses 60 days with a further period not exceeding 60 days. Consent orders have the statutory restriction under s. 421(2). Keep Topic 9 institutional detail separate from this Topic 8 saving clause.

08 · Case dossier I — German Date Coffee

DU PRIMARY · D25 printed pp. 278–290 / PDF pp. 284–296

In re German Date Coffee Company (1882) 20 Ch D 169

Court and procedural setting: English winding-up proceedings before Kay J, followed by an appeal. The DU extract reproduces the first-instance reasoning and appellate reasoning, including Baggallay LJ and Lindley LJ. Core subject: failure of substratum and construction of the memorandum’s dominant purpose. The appellate court affirmed the winding-up order.

Facts: the investment was in a protected German undertaking

  1. The company was registered on 16 February 1881, with capital of £100,000 in £1 shares.
  2. The memorandum’s first and central object was to acquire and exploit inventions for manufacturing a coffee substitute from dates, for which a patent had been or would be granted by the German Empire to T. F. Henley.
  3. Other clauses concerned using or improving the invention, manufacturing and selling preparations, licensing, obtaining related patents, acquiring cognate inventions and importing/exporting food produce. Land, buildings and machinery powers were also included.
  4. A named agreement provided for acquisition of the German rights for £50,000, payable partly in cash and partly in shares. The prospectus presented the venture as one to purchase and work Henley’s German patent.
  5. The German patent was never granted. An appeal against refusal failed. Around 27,000 shares were surrendered by shareholders withdrawing after the misrepresentation became apparent.
  6. A substituted agreement of 12 October 1881 proposed rights connected with the English patent for use in Germany and assignment of a Swedish patent, while maintaining the substantial purchase consideration. Shareholders approved it on 13 October by 8,979 votes to 2,075.
  7. The company spent about £3,000 on a Hamburg manufactory and claimed profitable production of date coffee there without a German patent. It was solvent; a large majority of continuing shareholders wanted to proceed.
  8. Two shareholders, holding 100 and 10 shares, petitioned on 30 January 1882, before the first anniversary of incorporation, alleging that the objects had entirely failed.

Why the details matter: this was not a company with no cash, no product and no supporters. The crucial failure was the specific protected undertaking for which investors subscribed. Solvency and some profitable activity did not answer that objection.

Issues

  • Was the company a general date-coffee/food enterprise, or a company whose essential object was working a particular German patent?
  • Could broadly worded ancillary objects sustain continuation after the German patent became unobtainable?
  • Did obtaining a Swedish patent or continuing unpatented production in Hamburg amount to carrying on the same undertaking?
  • Could the majority compel the minority to remain in the altered venture?
  • Was the petition premature because it was filed within a year of incorporation?

Petitioning shareholders’ arguments — recorded in the DU extract

The German patent was the main object/substratum for which money had been subscribed. The petitioners had not joined to manufacture unpatented date coffee. Once the patent became unattainable, the contemplated business could no longer be carried on. Partnership principles and authorities such as Baring v Dix and Re Suburban Hotel supported dissolution where the whole foundation failed. Majority approval did not authorise compelling the dissenters to undertake a different business.

Territorial point: the value of a German patent was exclusivity under German law. A right under English or Swedish patent law could not supply that German exclusivity merely by being assigned to a German company.

Company / continuation side — recorded in the DU extract

The company relied on the memorandum’s wide words: power to use inventions, manufacture preparations, acquire improvements and cognate inventions, trade in food produce and acquire property. It maintained that some contemplated business could still be conducted without the German patent and with majority approval. The undertaking was solvent and, it claimed, profitable.

It relied on Re Langham Skating Rink and Re Middlesborough Assembly Rooms, where continuing only part or a modified scale of an undertaking did not justify ignoring the majority. It also argued that statutory provision for a year to commence business indicated that the petition came too early. An affidavit referred to a renewed patent application.

Decision and reasoning

Holding: the substratum had failed; winding up was just and equitable. The appellate challenge failed and Kay J’s order stood. Do not confuse dismissal of the appeal with dismissal of the winding-up petition.

1. The memorandum must be construed as a whole. Kay J treated the German patent as the foundation shown by the memorandum and the named transaction. Broad wording was ancillary or auxiliary to that scheme, not authority to reinvent the enterprise entirely.

2. A smaller version of the same project differs from a different project. In Langham Skating Rink, a smaller rink was still within the real contemplated undertaking. Here the indispensable German monopoly right was missing; producing the substance without it was not simply a cheaper scale of the same protected investment.

3. The substituted patent did not cure the failure. The Swedish patent gave no German monopoly. The proposed English-patent permission was similarly ineffective as a substitute for German protection. The decisive question was not “Can anybody still manufacture this product?” but “Can this company achieve the essential object for which it was constituted?”

4. General words must not trap subscribers. Lindley LJ warned against construing general objects literally so that subscribers in an enterprise to manufacture one thing could be bound into an unrelated trading venture. This is a contextual construction principle; it is not a rule that every later-listed object in every memorandum is necessarily subordinate.

5. Minority rights survived majority approval. The majority’s preference was relevant but could not change the fundamental bargain by retaining minority capital in an essentially different undertaking. The statutory equitable remedy existed to address that injustice.

6. No need to wait for an anniversary where failure is already clear. A reasonable commencement period protects a venture still capable of implementation. It does not require pointless delay after the essential undertaking is demonstrably abortive. The asserted renewed application did not establish a realistic possibility of obtaining the required patent on the evidence.

Principle and limits

Where a company’s distinct dominant purpose, as properly construed, has completely failed, general ancillary objects, solvency and majority support do not necessarily prevent winding up on the just-and-equitable ground.

Ratio/application today: use the case under s. 271(e) to analyse a sole or dominant indispensable object. Apply s. 273(2) and current procedural requirements separately.

Not decided: that all unsuccessful patents require winding up; that all broad objects are ineffective; that a company can never alter its objects lawfully; or that commercial disappointment alone is sufficient. The result depends on a foundation that cannot be realised, not merely disappointing profits.

Case-use capsule

Trigger words: sole licence, indispensable concession, named patent, unavailable essential right, substitute business, minority objection. Exam sentence: “As in German Date Coffee, the ability to conduct some profitable activity is not decisive if it is not the undertaking that constituted the company’s substratum.”

Hypothetical variation: a company is genuinely constituted for several independent food businesses, one of which uses a German patent. Patent refusal alone may not establish loss of substratum; that version is closer to the reasoning in Seth Mohan Lal. This is an explanatory hypothetical, not an additional reported fact.

09 · Case dossier II — Seth Mohan Lal

DU PRIMARY · D25 printed pp. 291–293 / PDF pp. 297–299

Seth Mohan Lal v Grain Chambers Ltd., AIR 1968 SC 772

Court: Supreme Court of India; judgment in the supplied extract delivered by J. C. Shah J. Historical statute: Indian Companies Act, 1913, including s. 162. Core subject: a legal prohibition affecting one principal activity does not necessarily destroy the substratum of a company with other genuine objects and assets.

Facts and procedural history

  1. Grain Chambers Ltd., Muzaffarnagar, was incorporated to conduct an exchange in grain, cotton, sugar, gur, pulses and other commodities. In 1949–1950 its business was principally futures in gur.
  2. On 14 March 1949, the board sanctioned gur futures for the Phagun settlement, stated in the extract as 4 March 1950.
  3. On 9 August 1949, Seth Mohan Lal & Co. acquired a share, qualified for membership and began dealing in gur futures. The extract records 1,136 bijaks of sales in the firm’s own name and a further asserted 2,137 bijaks in the benami names of five other members.
  4. During sharp price fluctuations in January 1950, the board resolved on 7 January that settlements would not be accepted above ₹17-8-0 per maund, with margin deposits required. The appellants deposited ₹5,26,996-14-0 on their own transactions and alleged additional benami deposits of about ₹7 lakh.
  5. On 15 February 1950, the Government extended the Sugar (Futures and Options) Prohibition Order to gur under the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act. The prohibition was against entering future transactions after the appointed day; the case did not establish that all existing contracts became void.
  6. That day the board fixed outstanding Phagun settlement at the previous closing rate, ₹17-6-0 per maund. Its resolution recorded special invitees, including Mohan Lal. Against the appellants’ margin balance the company debited ₹5,15,769-5-0 as adjusted loss, leaving ₹11,227-9-0 to their credit.
  7. On 22 February 1950, the appellants and their partner sought winding up: alleged inability to pay, fraud/misappropriation by management and destruction of substratum.
  8. The single judge dismissed the petitions; the Allahabad High Court affirmed. Appeals reached the Supreme Court, which dismissed them.

Reading note: the extract uses old rupees–annas–pies notation and more than one settlement reference. The legal answer does not require silently “correcting” these historical details or converting them to modern decimal amounts. The central issues are the effect of prohibition, legitimacy of settlement and survival of the corporate objects.

Issues

  • Did the prohibition make outstanding gur contracts void, so that settlement debits/payment of others were wrongful?
  • Were allegations concerning benami transactions, fraud, misapplication of funds and board irregularities proved?
  • Did the loss of gur-futures trading destroy the company’s substratum?
  • Were liabilities unpaid and assets inadequate, or could the company pursue remaining objects?
  • Could later inactivity caused by prolonged litigation establish a winding-up ground that did not exist when the petition was filed?

Appellants’ contentions — as reported in D25

They challenged the settlement transactions and alleged misuse of funds, including amounts said to belong to them through benami dealings. They asserted that the government prohibition had destroyed the business, liquid assets had been disposed of and no reasonable prospect of restarting remained. Winding up was sought both on financial grounds and as just and equitable.

Company’s position — drawn from the findings upheld in D25

The supplied excerpt is not a verbatim transcript of respondent counsel’s speech. Its reported defence/outcome supports these propositions: outstanding contracts were not rendered void; directors prudently settled at the relevant prevailing rate; benami claims and mala fide payment allegations were not proved; the board was properly constituted and directors’ trading violated no relevant company-law provision; the objects were wider than gur futures; and usable assets survived.

Argument attribution discipline: present these as the respondent’s case reflected in the findings, not invented quotations or a purported complete counsel transcript.

Decision and reasons

The appeals failed; winding up was refused.

1. Existing transactions were not automatically annihilated. The High Court’s view that the notification did not invalidate the outstanding futures was upheld in the supplied account. Settlement of obligations on the stated basis was prudent and in the interests of company and shareholders, not proof of fraud.

2. Allegations required proof. The asserted benami transactions and mala fide settlements were not established. Separate suits based on those transactions were dismissed. Winding up was not justified by simply asserting that funds had been misapplied.

3. The incorporation objects had not substantially failed. Gur futures were the main current activity, not the exclusive corporate purpose. The company could engage in other commodities within its objects. The case therefore differs from a company whose sole indispensable patent never existed.

4. Financial and organisational capacity survived. The company possessed immovable property and liquid assets valued at about ₹2,54,000. Inability to pay was not shown; the Court’s view left no creditors with the asserted outstanding debts. Discharging brokers did not destroy the organisation irretrievably because their services could be obtained again.

5. Losses were not inevitably permanent. There was no proof that all permitted business could be conducted only at a loss, or that assets were insufficient to meet liabilities. A legal ban on one class of transaction did not prove that broader conclusion.

6. Time of assessment matters. Primarily, circumstances existing at the petition date determine whether a just-and-equitable case is made out. Business inactivity caused by long litigation could not itself justify winding up when the original case failed.

The three substratum indicators stated by the Court

The DU extract states that substratum disappears when:

  1. The object of incorporation has substantially failed; or
  2. It is impossible to carry on the business except at a loss; or
  3. Existing and possible assets are insufficient to meet existing liabilities.

Read these as the Court’s historical formulation. In a modern answer, do not use the third limb to smuggle a routine debt-default petition back into current s. 271; explain the IBC transition and substantiate an actual equitable ground.

Principle, qualifications and use

Loss of the company’s principal current activity is not necessarily loss of its substratum where genuine remaining objects, assets and a realistic ability to resume business survive.

The Court considered both shareholders’ and creditors’ interests. The decision is not an absolute rule that several objects always defeat winding up, nor that temporary cash assets conclusively establish viability. The key is whether the company can genuinely pursue the business for which it was incorporated.

Trigger words: regulatory prohibition, commodity exchange, multiple genuine objects, temporary closure, assets available, litigation-caused paralysis. Exam sentence: “The question is not whether the formerly dominant business has stopped, but whether the company’s substantive objects and realistic capacity to pursue them have substantially disappeared.”

10 · Case dossier III — Aluminium Corporation

DU PRIMARY · D22 printed pp. 322–331 / page markers 328–337 · Suggested case in D25

Aluminium Corporation of India Ltd. v Lakshmi Ratan Cotton Mills Co. Ltd., AIR 1970 All 452

Court: Allahabad High Court, M. H. Beg J, decision 28 July 1969. Historical framework: ss. 433, 434 and 443 of the Companies Act, 1956. The name appears with “Aluminum/Aluminium” and “Ratan/Rattan” variants across the supplied materials. The syllabus’s AIR 1970 All 452 reference is the correct one for the discussion here; do not adopt a website heading incorrectly saying AIR 1978.

Facts: restitution, industrial rivalry and a proposed revival

The Gupta and Singhania industrial groups had once jointly controlled the cotton-mill company, the aluminium corporation and a related firm. Their affairs had been intertwined. Following separation under an award, the corporation went to the Singhanias and the company/firm to the Guptas.

The cotton-mill company sued the corporation on accounts and obtained a decree for about ₹2,82,734, with costs and interest. The limitation issue turned on whether a corporation secretary’s letter validly acknowledged liability. The trial court accepted implied authority; the High Court reversed, holding the acknowledgement ineffective for extending limitation. The original decree had meanwhile been satisfied through a deposit and set-off arrangement.

After reversal, the corporation obtained a restitution order dated 15 April 1967 for ₹4,11,554. The company appealed the underlying judgment to the Supreme Court, but had no stay of restitution and did not separately appeal the restitution order. Rather than establishing unsuccessful execution of process, the corporation served a three-week demand on 11 May 1967 and filed for winding up on 9 August 1967.

The mill had closed on 6 September 1966. Evidence concerned depression in the cotton industry, labour trouble, substantial liabilities, assets and possible government financial support. The petitioning corporation had sought to restrain the company from taking a proposed ₹40 lakh government loan intended to help restart the mill. The company asserted that the mill had resumed working profitably. Other creditor claims were also before the Court.

Four framed issues

  1. Was the company commercially insolvent and liable to winding up?
  2. Had it suspended business for a whole year so as to attract the historical statutory ground?
  3. Was winding up otherwise just and equitable?
  4. Was the petition mala fide and liable to dismissal on that ground?

An important preliminary issue concerned whether the Court was confined to evidence accompanying the original/amended petition or could receive later affidavits in the representative proceeding.

Corporation’s arguments — recorded in D22

  • An unsatisfied judgment/restitution debt plus failure to comply with the statutory demand should entitle the creditor to winding up ex debito justitiae.
  • Without an appellate stay, a pending appeal should not ordinarily excuse non-payment of an adjudicated debt.
  • The restitution order had become final and supplied an immediately enforceable liability.
  • Commercial insolvency, closure, other debts and alleged corporate irregularities supported the winding-up request.
  • The petitioner invoked the recognised idea of winding up as a form of equitable execution rather than merely an ordinary suit.

Company’s arguments — recorded in D22

  • The debt was bona fide disputed on substantial grounds, despite the existing judgment and restitution order; material from the underlying judgments and appeal showed more than a sham appeal.
  • The statutory fiction of neglect to pay should not arise where a genuine substantial dispute existed.
  • No execution process had been shown returned unsatisfied so as to establish the separate statutory route under s. 434(1)(b).
  • Commercial insolvency was not proved by an adequate comparison of current assets and liabilities, ignoring potential profitable working.
  • The closure had explanation and credible prospects of reversal; a whole year had not elapsed when the petition was filed.
  • Ordinary enforcement and other remedies were available, and the petitioner’s attempt to obstruct a revival loan undermined its claimed equitable purpose.
  • The company also objected to new evidence beyond the petition; that restrictive procedural objection was rejected.

Reasoning: equitable jurisdiction requires a real balance of interests

1. Later evidence was not categorically excluded. A prima facie case should accompany the petition, but the representative nature of winding up and the applicable procedure permit later affidavits and creditor evidence. Expedition does not justify a rigid rule that only the original affidavit can ever be considered.

2. “May” means judicial discretion. Even proved inability does not automatically compel a winding-up order. Justice and equity permeate the jurisdiction, not only the separately named just-and-equitable ground. The creditor’s claimed right becomes compelling only when the established equities warrant the collective remedy.

3. A decree is strong evidence, not an answer to every possible substantial dispute. The presumption of correctness remains until reversal, but the Court can examine whether the challenge is substantial. A bare appeal is insufficient. Here, the different judgments, paper-book material and proposed appeal grounds established a genuine controversy over repayment liability.

4. Distinguish the historical s. 434 routes. Under (1)(a), a genuine substantial dispute can rebut the inference of neglect following demand. Under (1)(b), the separate question is whether execution or process has actually been returned unsatisfied; the mere dispute is not the same answer to that circumstance. In the case, the petitioner had not established returned-unsatisfied process. The Court did not declare every appealed decree unenforceable.

5. Commercial insolvency was not proved adequately. The petitioners had not shown current assets below current liabilities and inability to pay through the profitable operations asserted. The Court did not accept that the evidence compelled closure in creditors’ interests.

6. Closure was explicable and revival plausible. The petition preceded the first anniversary of mill closure. More importantly, depression and labour trouble explained suspension, and the possibility of working with government assistance weighed against treating the business as irretrievably dead.

7. Equities must relate to legitimate creditor interests. The remedy is not an opportunity to fling accusations in an industrial feud. The proposed injunction against revival finance was relevant to whether winding up actually served creditors. Workers could lose employment rather than gain by the company’s liquidation.

Actual order — the point most summaries miss

Under s. 443(1)(b) of the 1956 Act, the Court postponed the final decision for one year, on condition that the parties take steps to establish a clear balance of equities for or against winding up.

It was not an immediate winding-up order, and the DU extract does not end with a final unconditional dismissal. Other substantial indebtedness meant the petition could not simply be disposed of as though the petitioner’s disputed debt were the only issue.

Principles and present relevance

  • Winding up is an equitable, collective remedy, not automatic debt collection.
  • Judicial discretion must be reasoned, not arbitrary.
  • Bona fide dispute must be substantial, not an assertion designed to manufacture delay.
  • Even a judgment debt calls for careful analysis of the type and effect of the evidence in the historical jurisdiction.
  • Temporary closure and viable revival differ from destruction of substratum.
  • The petitioner’s conduct and the interests represented by the petition matter.

Modern-law boundary: do not tell a present creditor to issue a three-week notice under the old s. 434 and file under current s. 271 for inability to pay. Preserve the case’s historical holding; apply its caution about drastic relief to current equitable analysis. The one-year adjournment under old s. 443 also cannot simply override present statutory timing requirements.

Exam capsule: “Aluminium Corporation illustrates judicially controlled equitable discretion; importantly, the Court postponed the final decision for one year rather than ordering immediate liquidation or finally dismissing the petition.”

11 · Case dossier IV — Yenidje Tobacco

DU-SUGGESTED · Detailed supplement: M PDF pp. 134–135; A PDF pp. 52–53 · External report-citation check

Re Yenidje Tobacco Co. Ltd. [1916] 2 Ch 426

Court: English Court of Appeal; Lord Cozens-Hardy MR, Pickford LJ and Warrington LJ. Core subject: deadlock and a partnership-like private company. The DU syllabus prints the case at “2 Ch. D. 169”; the uploaded Meghraj account and external report references identify 2 Ch 426. 3

Facts supported by the uploaded summaries

Two tobacco/cigarette manufacturers combined their businesses in a private company. They were its only two shareholders and only two directors, with equal voting power. Their relationship deteriorated so seriously that they would not speak to each other and communicated through the secretary. Disagreement affected important management and staffing matters. Yet the company remained solvent and profitable.

The ASIM summary notes an articles-based arbitration arrangement. The quoted appellate reasoning available externally treats arbitration as a means of resolving particular disputes, not as a workable substitute for the entire ongoing relationship of management and reasonable cooperation. A compulsory winding-up petition followed the complete breakdown. Source boundary: the uploaded summaries do not safely establish a complete chronology of every arbitral step or an authoritative allocation of every procedural role between the two principals; those details are not invented here. 3

Issues

  • Could a profitable, solvent company be wound up because its equal controllers could no longer cooperate?
  • Could the just-and-equitable jurisdiction use principles analogous to dissolution of a partnership?
  • Did continued trading/profit or an arbitration clause answer the breakdown?
  • Was just-and-equitable winding up confined to closed categories such as financial collapse or loss of commercial substratum?

Winding-up side — explanatory reconstruction, not a counsel transcript

The strongest case for the petitioner is that the equal-control arrangement assumed continuing reasonable cooperation. Irretrievable hostility and the absence of an effective internal resolution destroyed that basis. Requiring the company to survive solely because profits existed would imprison the participants in an unworkable association. Arbitration of individual disputes did not supply a continuous substitute management structure.

Continuation side — partly reflected in appellate discussion; otherwise reconstructed

The strongest contrary case is that the corporation remained legally distinct and commercially successful, so personal hostility should not dictate its destruction; dispute-resolution machinery should be used; and winding up should be reserved for established exceptional grounds. These are the legal positions to evaluate. The uploaded materials do not reproduce full submissions by counsel, so they must not be presented as verbatim reported arguments.

Decision and principle

The company was ordered to be wound up on the just-and-equitable ground. The court treated the essential relationship as a partnership in the guise of a private company: the same fundamental inability to cooperate that could justify dissolving that personal association could justify corporate winding up. Prosperity did not cure the institutional and relational failure. 5

The appellate reasoning emphasised that the parties expected reasonable conduct and courtesy in working together. A mechanism for arbitrating specific disputes did not mean they intended the company to operate indefinitely through hostility and repeated arbitration. The court also resisted treating the examples of substratum failure and complete deadlock as exhaustive limits of equity. 3

Analytical significance and limits

Profitability and governability are distinct. Employees can continue generating revenue for a time while the constitutional decision-making relationship is broken. Therefore, proof of profits defeats an insolvency allegation but does not necessarily defeat an equitable-deadlock case.

Not every quarrel qualifies. The equal-control structure, entire collapse of cooperation and lack of an effective resolution mattered. Ordinary disagreements, a casting vote that works, or a realistic contractual exit can alter the result. In a current Indian problem, expressly analyse s. 273(2), rather than assuming the old English result is automatic.

Not ordinary veil lifting: partnership reasoning does not erase separate personality for all purposes. It is an equitable basis for evaluating whether continuation should be compelled.

Exam capsule: “Yenidje demonstrates that financial prosperity is not an absolute defence where a quasi-partnership company’s mutual-cooperation basis and management machinery have irretrievably failed.”

Devas Employees Mauritius Private Ltd. v Union of India

Karnataka High Court · W.P. No. 6191 of 2021 · 28 April 2021 · P. S. Dinesh Kumar J. The report is also identified as [2021] 127 taxmann.com 108 (Karnataka). Principal provisions: ss. 271(c), 272(1)(e), 272(3); constitutional challenge and judicial review of Central Government authorisation. 2

Facts and procedural setting

The petitioner, a Mauritius company, held 3.48% in Devas Multimedia Pvt. Ltd. On 28 January 2005, Devas and Antrix Corporation entered into an agreement for lease of space-segment capacity on ISRO/Antrix S-band spacecraft. Antrix terminated the agreement in 2011. Devas pursued ICC arbitration and obtained an award for USD 562.5 million. These facts are recorded in the uploaded ASIM account, printed pp. 108–109.

On 14 January 2021, Antrix’s Chairman-cum-Managing Director sent the Ministry of Corporate Affairs a detailed request for authority to seek winding up, setting out the transaction and allegations. Central Government authorisation followed on 18 January 2021. Antrix petitioned the NCLT; admission and provisional-liquidator orders were made, and the shareholder pursued the NCLAT route and an application to participate before NCLT. The writ sought to challenge the statutory authorisation route and undo the authorisation and resulting proceedings. 2

Precise issues — avoid replacing them with the later appeal

  1. Was s. 272(1)(e), allowing a Central Government-authorised person to petition, unconstitutional?
  2. Should the company’s pre-sanction representation protection in the second proviso to s. 272(3) for a Registrar petition be read into the authorised-person route?
  3. Was the authorisation of 18 January 2021 mala fide, arbitrary or otherwise liable to interference?
  4. Was the writ an appropriate parallel proceeding where the shareholder was already participating in the Tribunal process? 2

Petitioner’s arguments — reported positions

  • The absence of the Registrar-route pre-sanction hearing in the authorised-person route created an objectionable difference and called for constitutional invalidation or reading the hearing safeguard into it.
  • Devas had not been given an opportunity before authorisation was granted.
  • Antrix was an award debtor; seeking the award creditor’s winding up was alleged to be a device for avoiding payment.
  • The agreement had been terminated on force majeure, not fraud; the later fraud case was said to be designed to defeat legitimate arbitral dues.
  • The speed of authorisation was cited as evidence of a mala fide exercise of power. 2

Respondents’ arguments — reported positions

The respondents relied on the different statutory positions of the Registrar and a specially authorised applicant; the material placed before Government in the detailed request; serious allegations relating to formation, promised technology, approvals and movement of funds; and investigation material. They argued that fraud could not be neutralised merely by reliance on termination terminology or an award, and that the shareholder could contest the matter before the NCLT/NCLAT. 2

Decision and reasoning

Outcome: the High Court rejected the constitutional challenge, declined interference with authorisation and dismissed the writ with ₹5 lakh costs, payable within four weeks according to the uploaded ASIM summary. It treated the proceedings as an abuse of process in the circumstances. A, printed pp. 108–109, corroborated by the reported extract. 2

1. Different statutory categories were not interchangeable. The Registrar, who receives corporate information in an official capacity, occupies a different position from a person authorised to present a petition. The provision prescribing prior sanction and representation for the Registrar did not automatically extend to s. 272(1)(e). The reported conclusions at paragraphs 51–52 reject unconstitutionality and the requested reading down. 2

2. Permission to approach the Tribunal is not itself winding up. The company could contest the case in adjudicatory proceedings. The authorisation did not amount to the final civil consequence of dissolution or a conclusive determination of all fraud allegations. The High Court’s reasoning stressed the opportunity to present objections before the competent forum. 2

3. Mala fides were not established merely by chronology. The detailed request and material could not be brushed aside solely because authorisation came quickly, or because Antrix faced an award. The petitioner failed to establish the constitutional infringement alleged. 2

4. Litigation conduct mattered. The shareholder had pursued the Tribunal route and sought participation, then initiated the writ immediately before the date fixed for final hearing. The Court characterised the parallel attack as abuse of process/proxy litigation in the particular circumstances. This is not a universal bar on constitutional challenges to company legislation. 2

  • Authorised-person standing is constitutionally valid on the challenge examined.
  • Registrar-specific safeguards cannot simply be transplanted into a distinct statutory category.
  • Authorisation and merits adjudication are different. NCLT must decide the statutory case; the executive does not dissolve a company by authorising a petition.
  • Alleged mala fides require substantiation; a quick decision or an existing arbitral award is not by itself the complete answer.
  • Equitable/constitutional process cannot be used abusively to derail proceedings already being contested.

A1 · 2017 Q7 — spouses, equal votes and eighteen months of paralysis

Reported question, lightly normalised: What is meant by winding up? What are the modes and grounds? H and W, married under the Special Marriage Act, 1954, incorporated a private company in 2000 as its only directors and shareholders with equal voting rights. Marital differences arose in 2015. They have not spoken since 2015 and business has ceased for eighteen months. W petitions for winding up. Decide. Transcription gap: the source says “W files for” without supplying what follows; no missing proceeding is assumed. 3

Model answer

Meaning and framework. Winding up is the collective process by which a liquidator administers a company’s assets, settles its liabilities and distributes any surplus before dissolution. The company does not cease to exist merely because the petition is filed. Under the amended Companies Act, Part I of Chapter XX governs winding up by the Tribunal. Corporate insolvency resolution and liquidation, and eligible voluntary liquidation under s. 59, belong to the IBC framework. The former statutory voluntary winding-up provisions in ss. 304–323 have been omitted.

The present grounds in s. 271 are: a special resolution for winding up by Tribunal; conduct against the specified national/public interests; qualifying fraud, unlawful formation or connected misconduct on the stipulated application where winding up is proper; failure to file financial statements or annual returns for the immediately preceding five consecutive financial years; and the Tribunal’s opinion that winding up is just and equitable.

Issue and standing. W’s likely ground is s. 271(e), not the mere fact of a matrimonial dispute. As a shareholder, W may petition as a contributory subject to s. 272(2). The long-standing equal membership described strongly supports satisfaction of the relevant shareholding condition, although the actual holding/allotment should be identified. W need not prove insolvency merely to invoke equity.

Petitioner’s case. The company is controlled entirely by two equal participants who no longer communicate. Unlike an ordinary majority/minority disagreement, neither can resolve governance by a majority decision. Eighteen months of business cessation supports the inference that hostility has disabled management, rather than merely made meetings unpleasant. If their personal association was the foundation of equal participation, its complete breakdown reinforces the claim.

Applicable case. In Re Yenidje Tobacco Co., two equal shareholder-directors would not cooperate and communicated through a secretary. Although the business was profitable, the company was wound up as just and equitable because the partnership-like relationship and workable management had broken down. The equitable jurisdiction was not confined to financial failure.

Company/H’s answer. Separate corporate personality means marital discord does not itself dissolve the company. The Tribunal should inspect the articles for a casting vote, arbitral mechanism, buyout or other effective way forward. If a realistic purchase of W’s shares or an appropriate management remedy is available, ending the business might unnecessarily prejudice creditors, employees and the remaining member.

Discretion and alternatives. Section 273(2) permits refusal where another remedy is available and W acts unreasonably by seeking winding up instead. Therefore, an answer should not stop at “Yenidje applies”. It should ask whether the deadlock is genuine, entrenched and incapable of resolution by an adequate alternative. Also, eighteen months of inactivity is evidence here, not an independent current statutory ground of one-year suspension.

Conclusion. On the stated facts, W has a strong just-and-equitable case: two equal controllers, complete communication breakdown and prolonged cessation. Unless a workable alternative or contrary governance evidence is shown, the Tribunal would be justified in winding up. The legally decisive reason is the demonstrated corporate deadlock, not the marriage’s failure in isolation. Dissolution follows the statutory liquidation process rather than occurring immediately on W’s petition.

What makes this a strong answer: identifies current law; establishes contributory standing; uses Yenidje by factual analogy; distinguishes matrimonial facts from corporate consequences; handles s. 273(2); avoids the obsolete suspension ground.

A2 · 2015 Company Law Q6 — meaning and grounds

Reported question: “What do you understand by the winding up? What are the grounds of compulsory winding up of a company?” 3

Model answer — current-syllabus version with historical qualification

Winding up is the legal administration of a company’s affairs for closure: assets are taken into control and realised, liabilities are established and discharged according to law, contributory rights are adjusted and surplus is distributed. Dissolution terminates the corporate personality; winding up is the process leading to it. A solvent company may be wound up, so winding up is wider than insolvency.

Statutory basis. For the present DU syllabus, s. 270 applies Part I to Tribunal winding up and s. 271 supplies five grounds. Because this question is attributed to 2015, an historical answer must additionally recognise the then-operative 1956 winding-up machinery; current amended provisions should not be projected backwards without explanation.

First, special resolution. Under s. 271(a), the members must pass a special resolution specifically for winding up by the Tribunal. The resolution is a ground, not dissolution itself, and the Tribunal’s judicial discretion remains.

Second, specified national/public interests. Section 271(b) concerns acts against sovereignty and integrity, State security, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality. A concrete act and statutory connection must be shown, rather than a vague assertion of public disapproval.

Third, qualifying fraud or connected wrongdoing. Section 271(c) requires application by the Registrar or a Central Government-authorised person and satisfaction concerning fraudulent affairs, formation for fraudulent and unlawful purpose, or fraud, misfeasance or misconduct by persons involved in formation/management. It must also be proper to wind up. Executive authority to file is distinct from proof of the case. The Karnataka High Court’s 2021 Devas decision explains the validity of the authorised-person route and its distinction from the Registrar’s sanction safeguard.

Fourth, repeated filing default. Section 271(d) requires failure to file financial statements or annual returns for the immediately preceding five consecutive financial years. One late return does not suffice under this ground.

Fifth, just and equitable. Section 271(e) addresses grave situations including substantial failure of the company’s foundation or irretrievable deadlock. In German Date Coffee the German patent was indispensable, and the Swedish patent did not cure its absence; the winding-up order was affirmed despite solvency and majority support. In Seth Mohan Lal the ban on gur futures did not destroy a genuinely multi-commodity exchange’s objects and restart capacity, so winding up was refused. In Yenidje, equal directors’ total inability to cooperate justified winding up although the business was profitable.

Judicial control. Winding up is not automatic on accusation or commercial disappointment. Section 273 enables dismissal, interim relief, provisional appointment or winding up and directs an order within 90 days. Under s. 273(2), another available remedy and unreasonable insistence on winding up can justify refusal of a just-and-equitable petition. The historical Aluminium Corporation case likewise demonstrates equitable discretion, although its debt machinery is not today’s s. 271 route.

Conclusion. Compulsory winding up is a supervised remedy founded on statutory grounds and exercised judicially for the collective interests affected. Inability to pay debts, formerly a separate ground under s. 433(e), is not an independent current s. 271 ground after the IBC changes. A complete modern answer must make that distinction rather than blend successive Acts.

Historical adaptation: for the original 2015 legal position, use the historical grounds set out in A4/A5 and explain the phased commencement, rather than claiming the present five-clause scheme was already the operative 2015 test.

A3 · 2015 Business Association II Q3 — statutory grounds with cases

Reported question: “Discuss the grounds of compulsory winding-up of a company with reference to statutory provisions and decided cases.” 3

Model answer — historical framework, followed by the present position

Compulsory winding up means winding up under a judicial order rather than merely by voluntary internal decision. It is a collective administration of the company’s estate and does not itself mean that the company ceased to exist on the petition date. The company ordinarily continues for winding-up purposes until dissolution.

Historical statutory grounds. In the 1956 Act framework relevant to old Company Law papers, s. 433 included special resolution; specified default in the statutory report/statutory meeting requirements; failure to commence business within a year or suspension for a whole year; reduction of members below the statutory minimum; inability to pay debts; and just-and-equitable winding up. The historical text also contained added grounds concerning specified sovereignty/security/public interests and five consecutive financial years of filing default. Statutory amendments must be read with their commencement: a provision inserted in an amendment is not necessarily operative merely because it appears in a printed consolidation.

Debt ground and its safeguards. Section 434 supplied the historical machinery: failure following the statutory demand, returned-unsatisfied execution, or proof of inability with contingent and prospective liabilities considered. It was not intended as pressure to collect a genuinely disputed debt. In Aluminium Corporation v Lakshmi Ratan Cotton Mills, the Court examined the underlying decree dispute, evidence of assets and liabilities, closure and revival prospects. It held that judicial discretion remained and postponed the final decision for one year. Thus a creditor could not convert an asserted judgment debt into an automatic entitlement to destroy the undertaking.

Failure of foundation. Under the just-and-equitable clause, the real undertaking must be identified from the company’s constitution and evidence. In German Date Coffee, the company was constituted to work a German patent that could not be obtained. General objects and a Swedish patent did not preserve the essential venture. The order was affirmed notwithstanding solvency and majority wishes. Conversely, in Seth Mohan Lal, the principal current activity of gur futures was prohibited, but the multi-commodity exchange’s genuine wider objects and resources survived. Substratum had not disappeared and the appeals seeking winding up failed.

Deadlock. In Yenidje, equality of voting power combined with total breakdown of cooperation made continued management untenable. Partnership-like equitable considerations applied even though the company was prosperous. That case establishes why inability to pay is not a necessary ingredient of every winding-up ground.

Discretion and proper purpose. The just-and-equitable jurisdiction is not confined mechanically by the preceding grounds, but it requires serious proved circumstances, not mere dissatisfaction. The historical alternative-remedy principle in s. 443(2) cautioned against unreasonably insisting on winding up where another remedy was available. Creditors’, members’ and other affected interests had to be considered.

Present-law qualification. Today the statutory answer should begin with s. 271(a)–(e): special resolution; specified public/national interests; qualifying fraud/unlawful purpose/connected misconduct; five-year filing default; and just and equitable. The old debt route has shifted to the IBC framework, and old one-year suspension/member-number grounds are not independent current s. 271 grounds. Petitioners and powers are now in ss. 272–273.

Conclusion. The cases explain continuing equitable principles, but the grounds and procedural entitlements must be taken from the statute actually governing the question. A sound answer combines historical accuracy with a clear statement of present law, not a hybrid list from different eras.

A4 · 2013 Q6 — modes and compulsory court winding up

Reported question: “Explain the winding up of the company. Write down the different modes available for winding up a company with special reference to compulsory winding up by court.” 3

Model answer

Winding up is the process of collecting and administering company property, satisfying liabilities, adjusting members’ rights and distributing surplus, ordinarily followed by dissolution. The liquidator replaces ordinary management for the purposes of the process, but winding up and the extinction of personality are conceptually distinct.

The historical modes. This reported 2013 question belongs to the 1956 Act setting and itself refers to court winding up. The traditional s. 425 scheme distinguished: (i) winding up by the court; (ii) voluntary winding up; and (iii) winding up subject to court supervision. Voluntary winding up historically included members’ and creditors’ forms, differentiated by the statutory solvency-declaration framework and creditor involvement. These are historical modes, not the present amended s. 270 text.

Compulsory grounds. The central historical s. 433 grounds included special resolution; statutory report/meeting default; non-commencement for one year or suspension for a year; membership below the prescribed minimum; inability to pay; and just and equitable, together with applicable later additions concerning specified national/public interests and persistent filing default. The effect and commencement of amendments must be checked when stating the complete historical consolidation.

Petition and hearing. The historical law identified permitted petitioners under s. 439, including creditors and contributories subject to qualifications. The court could dismiss, adjourn, give interim protection or order winding up under s. 443. A petitioner had to establish a legal ground and appropriate use of the remedy; mere assertion of commercial failure was not enough.

Decided cases. German Date Coffee shows that a solvent company can be wound up where its essential German-patent purpose became impossible; general ancillary powers and majority support could not save the failed foundation. Seth Mohan Lal provides the counter-example: a prohibition affecting gur futures did not annihilate the objects of an exchange that could genuinely conduct other commodity business with surviving assets. Yenidje shows the distinct ground of irretrievable deadlock between equal shareholder-directors. Aluminium Corporation illustrates restraint where the alleged debt was substantially disputed, commercial failure was unproved and revival remained realistic; the final decision was postponed, not automatically granted to the creditor.

Effect of a compulsory order. The process becomes collective. Assets are brought under liquidation control, claims are dealt with in accordance with their legal priorities and contributories may be called upon within their liabilities. Legal proceedings and dispositions are subject to the relevant statutory controls. The liquidator’s administration leads to an application/order for dissolution; the initial order is not identical to the end of personality.

Current comparison. Under the amended 2013 Act, Part I applies to Tribunal winding up and s. 271 contains five grounds. Sections 304–323, which had dealt with statutory voluntary winding up, are omitted. Eligible voluntary liquidation is now under IBC s. 59; default-based corporate resolution/liquidation is governed by the Code. Therefore the historical three-mode answer must be labelled as historical if used in a present semester examination.

Conclusion. Compulsory winding up is distinguished by a judicial order and statutory collective administration, not simply by insolvency or a shareholder vote. The modern and historical modes should be contrasted explicitly because institutional and insolvency reforms have changed the route while preserving important equitable principles.

A5 · 2012 Q7 — when is winding up just and equitable?

Reported question: “What are the grounds of compulsory winding up of a company? When courts have considered it just and equitable to wind up company? Refer statutory provisions and decided cases in support of your answer.” 3

Model answer

The historical compulsory grounds under s. 433 of the Companies Act, 1956 encompassed special resolution, specified statutory-report/meeting default, failure to commence or year-long suspension, reduction below the minimum membership, inability to pay debts and just-and-equitable winding up, with applicable later additions concerning protected national/public interests and persistent filing default. The central focus of this question is the equitable ground in s. 433(f), now reflected in s. 271(e).

Nature of the jurisdiction. “Just and equitable” is a residuary standard, not a phrase mechanically limited to circumstances identical to the earlier enumerated grounds. At the same time, equity is judicial, evidence-based and sensitive to the gravity of liquidation. It is not authority to close a company whenever a minority dislikes management or profitability disappoints.

Failure of substratum. The substantial purpose of incorporation must have failed or become impossible. In German Date Coffee, the real foundation was acquisition and exploitation of a particular German patent. It could not be secured. Unpatented manufacture and a Swedish patent were not equivalent to the protected German enterprise. The general words in the memorandum were ancillary to the dominant purpose and could not bind the minority to another venture. Winding up was upheld even though the company was solvent and many shareholders wished to continue.

Not every stopped business is a failed company. In Seth Mohan Lal the company traded mainly in gur futures, but had been incorporated for an exchange in several commodities. Government prohibition of new gur-futures business did not invalidate every outstanding contract or destroy all corporate objects. Assets survived, brokers could be re-engaged and inability to pay was not shown. Primarily the petition-date circumstances were relevant; litigation-induced later inactivity could not retrospectively supply the missing ground. Winding up was refused.

Deadlock and personal association. In Yenidje Tobacco, two equal shareholder-directors became incapable of cooperation, communicating only through an intermediary. Although trading was profitable, the management relationship had broken down. In a company effectively constituted as a partnership-like association, the equitable principles which justify ending an irretrievably broken relationship may justify corporate winding up. This does not convert every private company into a partnership.

Lack of probity and oppression. Serious dishonest conduct and oppressive exclusion may make continuation unjust. But loss of confidence must relate to probity or the legitimate basis of the association, not mere resentment at being defeated on policy. Less destructive statutory relief must be considered where available.

Discretion and alternatives. In Aluminium Corporation, the Court insisted on a real balance of equities and examined the substantial dispute, employment and revival prospects. The Court postponed the final decision one year. Under historical s. 443(2), and now s. 273(2), a just-and-equitable winding-up request may be refused where another remedy is available and the petitioner acts unreasonably by insisting on liquidation.

Conclusion. Courts have applied the clause to substantial foundational failure, irretrievable management deadlock and other grave equitable breakdowns. Categories guide, not exhaust, the jurisdiction. The correct test combines real objects, evidence, remaining possibilities, stakeholder interests and alternatives. In a modern answer, cite s. 271(e) and do not list inability to pay as a separate surviving Companies Act ground.

A6 · 2011 Q8 — German patent fails; Swedish patent obtained

Reported question, normalised: A company’s main object is manufacture of coffee from dates under a German patent to be acquired. It fails to acquire that patent but begins manufacturing under a Swedish patent. A contributory seeks winding up. Decide, and discuss compulsory grounds with statute and cases. 3

Model answer

Issue. The principal question is whether failure to acquire the German patent has destroyed the company’s substratum, or whether obtaining the Swedish patent permits continuation of the same corporate undertaking. Because this is a reported 2011 question, the historical Indian statutory ground is s. 433(f) of the 1956 Act. Its present counterpart is s. 271(e) of the 2013 Act. Contributory standing must be addressed under the applicable petition provision.

Rule. Where the real foundation or essential object of incorporation becomes impossible, winding up may be just and equitable. The memorandum must be construed as a whole to identify the genuine undertaking. Wide ancillary objects do not necessarily authorise a fundamentally different venture. Conversely, a reduction in scale or failure of only one among several truly independent objects is not automatically destruction of substratum.

Authority. In Re German Date Coffee Company, the company was formed to exploit a German patent for making coffee from dates. The patent was refused; an alternative Swedish patent and profitable unpatented manufacture in Hamburg did not save the venture. Kay J’s winding-up order was affirmed. The object was a protected German undertaking, not any date-coffee trade anywhere. The majority could not compel dissenters to continue in an essentially altered enterprise merely by invoking general clauses.

Application for the contributory. The problem expressly identifies the German-patent manufacture as the main object. Patent rights are territorial. The Swedish patent does not, just by its existence, confer the German monopoly that was the commercial and constitutional basis of the investment. Therefore the substitute does not cure the failure. The facts closely parallel the prescribed case; solvency or continuing sales, if asserted, would not alone answer the foundational defect.

Company’s counterargument. It may contend that manufacture from dates is the real object and Germany only one contemplated means, or that the memorandum genuinely authorises independent businesses of which the Swedish activity is one. It may invoke majority support and existing operations. The Court must examine the actual memorandum and surrounding scheme, rather than deciding solely by the company name. But on the stated facts the indispensable German patent is the foundation, so this defence is weak.

Distinguishing authority. In Seth Mohan Lal, the prohibition concerned gur futures while genuine other commodity objects and assets survived. That was not complete destruction of the company’s undertaking. Here no comparable independent object is supplied; replacing the specified patent undertaking is not the same as continuing a surviving original object.

Other grounds and discretion. Under historical s. 433, identify the other ordinary grounds summarised in A4/A5, including inability to pay and the non-commencement/suspension provisions. Under current s. 271, instead state the five operative clauses. In either framework, winding up is a judicial remedy, not a shareholder’s automatic command. The alternative-remedy provision must be considered; a current answer should apply s. 273(2) expressly.

Conclusion. On the given facts, the contributory’s petition should succeed on loss of substratum, subject to standing, evidence and the relevant alternative-remedy inquiry. German Date Coffee is the direct authority. The correct reason is failure of the indispensable corporate purpose, not inability to make any product or the mere fact that the company has changed patents.

15 · Additional exam practice — not verified PYQs

These questions are newly drafted to cover the rest of the statutory range. They are not represented as DU past questions. Suggested answer lengths are study choices, not official marking guidance.

Practice 1 · “A solvent company cannot be wound up.” Critically examine.

Model answer. The proposition is incorrect. Winding up is a legal process, whereas insolvency is a financial condition. Section 271 permits Tribunal winding up on grounds independent of debt default, including special resolution, protected public/national interests, qualifying fraud, persistent filing default and just-and-equitable circumstances. Indeed, inability to pay is no longer an independent current s. 271 ground.

German Date Coffee directly demonstrates the distinction: the company was solvent and claimed profitable operations, yet the German-patent foundation had failed. The question was whether the undertaking subscribers joined could still be performed, not whether a bank balance remained. Yenidje likewise involved prosperity but complete breakdown between equal controllers, justifying equitable winding up. By contrast, in Seth Mohan Lal, surviving assets supported the conclusion that the company could genuinely pursue its other objects; they did not establish an absolute immunity enjoyed by all solvent companies.

A judicial discretion remains. The Tribunal should ask whether the ground is proved and the collective remedy proper; under s. 273(2), an adequate available remedy and unreasonable insistence may justify refusal. Thus solvency is relevant evidence in some cases, not a universal bar. A reasoned conclusion should identify the specific non-financial ground and explain why available assets do or do not address it.

Practice 2 · A fully paid shareholder who acquired shares two months ago seeks winding up. Is the petition maintainable?

Model answer. Fully paid status is not disqualifying. Section 272(2) expressly allows a contributory to petition despite fully paid shares, no assets or no likely shareholder surplus. The separate question is the statutory acquisition/holding qualification.

If the relevant shares were originally allotted to the petitioner, that alternative limb may be met without six months’ holding. If they devolved through a former holder’s death, the succession limb must be examined. If they were simply bought and registered by transfer two months ago, and no qualifying shares satisfy an alternative limb, the requirement of at least six months in the preceding eighteen months is not met. The answer therefore depends on the mode of acquisition, not the paid-up status alone.

Maintainability also requires an actual s. 271 ground and compliance with petition procedure. This distinction between standing and merits is essential: qualifying as contributory does not prove substratum failure, just as proving a business problem does not cure absent standing.

Practice 3 · A liquidator sells a factory and refuses to show any records. Explain powers and accountability.

Model answer. Section 290 allows sale of movable/immovable property and actionable claims through public auction or private contract, including sale in parcels or sale of the undertaking as a going concern. The power is subject to Tribunal directions and overall control. The mere use of a private contract is not automatically unlawful; the sale’s compliance with the order, purpose, valuation and applicable procedure must be examined.

Sections 281–282 connect realisation to registered valuation, reporting, preservation of value and possible sale-committee assistance. Section 293 requires records of proceedings, with creditor/contributory inspection subject to Tribunal control. Section 294 requires proper accounts, at-least-twice-yearly presentation and audit. Section 287 permits advisory-committee inspection; s. 292 allows aggrieved persons to ask the Tribunal to confirm, reverse or modify the liquidator’s act or decision.

A blanket refusal of all statutory access cannot be justified simply by saying that the liquidator replaces the board. If misconduct, fraud, lack of diligence, incompetence or conflict is established, removal and possible recovery for loss may arise under s. 276. The correct approach is to specify the applicant’s inspection entitlement, request the relevant records and invoke Tribunal supervision, not assume every sale is void or that the liquidator is unaccountable.

Practice 4 · Distinguish sections 299 and 300.

Model answer. Both assist inquiry, but their triggers and purpose differ. Section 299 operates after provisional appointment or winding-up order. It reaches officers, persons possessing company property or records, suspected debtors and other information-holders. Examination can occur on oath through oral questions, interrogatories or affidavit; records can be compelled subject to lien questions. Proven debts/property may lead to payment/delivery orders executable as decrees.

Section 300 requires a winding-up order and a liquidator’s fraud report. It is directed to examination concerning promotion, formation, business or affairs and the person’s relevant conduct. It contains express procedural protections: access to the report at cost, representation, explanatory questioning, an exculpation application, signed notes and regulated use of evidence. Examination can be delegated, but not the costs power.

Thus s. 299 is a broad information-and-recovery tool; s. 300 is a fraud-report-based examination mechanism. Neither should be mistaken for automatic criminal conviction, and s. 300 is not itself the general fraudulent-trading personal-liability rule. Those distinctions prevent a common mixing of investigative and substantive provisions.

Practice 5 · The Government authorises an applicant without a Registrar-style pre-sanction hearing. Must the proceedings fail?

Model answer. Identify which statutory route is used. Section 272(3) requires previous sanction for a Registrar’s petition and a reasonable opportunity for company representations before the Government accords it. Section 272(1)(e), by contrast, permits a person authorised by Government to present the petition.

In Devas Employees Mauritius v Union of India (Karnataka HC, 28 April 2021), the court rejected the constitutional attack on the authorised-person route and declined to extend the Registrar-specific proviso automatically to it. The reported reasoning distinguishes the categories and emphasises the ability to contest the matter in the Tribunal proceedings. The writ was dismissed with costs in its particular procedural circumstances. See Chapter 12 and its source citations.

Accordingly, absence of that particular prior safeguard does not by itself invalidate every authorised-person petition. However, valid authority must exist, the relevant s. 271 ground must be established and the adjudicatory process must follow its own safeguards, including the applicable notice requirements for provisional appointment under s. 273. Mala fides or a jurisdictional defect, if substantiated, cannot simply be dismissed as irrelevant. The key distinction is that authorisation confers permission to approach the Tribunal; it does not decide the merits or dissolve the company.

Practice 6 · Explain the procedure from petition to dissolution.

Model answer. First identify a ground under s. 271 and an eligible petitioner under s. 272. A company petition requires a statement of affairs; copies go to the Registrar for views within 60 days. On an external petition with a prima facie case, s. 274 provides for objections and a statement within 30 days, extendable by a further 30 in specified circumstances.

Under s. 273 the Tribunal may dismiss, make interim orders, appoint a provisional liquidator or order winding up, with the statutory 90-day direction for the petition order and notice safeguards. Section 275 governs the liquidator’s appointment, terms and continuing independence disclosure. Section 277 governs intimation and committee mechanisms, while s. 278 gives the order collective effect. Litigation controls arise under s. 279, assets under s. 283 and cooperation under s. 284.

The liquidator reports under s. 281 within 60 days, enabling directions, value preservation and sale under s. 282. Sections 285–286 determine contribution; ss. 290–292 provide powers and supervision; ss. 293–294 require records and audit; ss. 295–298 deal with recovery, calls, adjustment and costs. Sections 299–301 assist examination, recovery and prevention of evasion. Periodic and committee reporting continue; statutory priorities govern distribution.

Finally the liquidator applies for dissolution under s. 302 when affairs are wound up, with the alternative just-and-reasonable dissolution power preserved. The company dissolves from the order date and the statutory communication to the Registrar follows. Section 303 saves specified pre-commencement orders and their appeal routes. A complete answer distinguishes each stage and does not equate presentation, winding-up order and dissolution.

16 · High-value revision, traps and answer architecture

Twenty errors to eliminate

  1. Citing present just-and-equitable winding up as s. 271(g).
  2. Listing old inability-to-pay machinery as an independent current s. 271 ground.
  3. Treating every creditor as automatically entitled under current s. 272.
  4. Saying fully paid shareholders cannot petition.
  5. Applying the six-month rule to originally allotted shares without examining the alternative limbs.
  6. Treating five isolated filing defaults as five consecutive immediately preceding years.
  7. Requiring default in both annual returns and financial statements despite the statutory “or”.
  8. Assuming a special resolution itself dissolves the company.
  9. Treating provisional appointment as the final merits decision.
  10. Saying the entire winding-up process must finish within 90 days.
  11. Treating the winding-up, advisory and sale committees as the same body.
  12. Confusing monthly committee reports, quarterly progress reports and twice-yearly accounts.
  13. Reciting the omitted s. 289 as operative.
  14. Saying all directors become unlimited contributories under s. 286.
  15. Describing s. 300 as the general fraudulent-trading liability provision.
  16. Treating any failed business line as total loss of substratum.
  17. Reporting winding up as granted in Seth Mohan Lal.
  18. Reporting Aluminium Corporation as a final unconditional dismissal.
  19. Confusing the Devas 2021 writ with the Supreme Court’s 2022 winding-up appeal.
  20. Labelling a model practice question or third-party transcription as an authenticated official DU PYQ.

Structure for a substantial analytical answer

Opening: define winding up and distinguish dissolution in two or three precise sentences. Law: identify the current section, or the appropriate historical provision if expressly required. Elements: break the ground into components. Authority: explain the decisive facts, issue, holding and ratio of the closest case. Application: use each important problem fact and fairly address the opposite side. Discretion: consider stakeholders, s. 273(2) and genuine alternatives. Conclusion: state the specific order justified and any genuinely missing fact.

Do not spend most of a problem answer listing every liquidator power if the question is about a German patent. Conversely, a procedure question should not become only a recital of the five famous cases. Coverage and relevance must work together.

Five one-minute recall cards

German Date Coffee: particular German patent → refused → Swedish/unpatented substitute inadequate → solvency and majority not decisive → substratum lost → winding up affirmed.

Seth Mohan Lal: multi-commodity exchange → gur-futures ban → other genuine objects and ₹2.54 lakh assets survive → allegations unproved → petition-date focus → winding up refused.

Aluminium Corporation: restitution decree → substantial dispute + unproved commercial collapse + revival prospect → equitable discretion → final decision postponed one year.

Yenidje: two equal owners/directors → complete hostility and broken cooperation → profit does not cure deadlock → partnership-like equity → winding up.

Devas 2021: Government-authorised applicant → challenge to s. 272(1)(e)/authorisation → Registrar proviso not automatically extended → merits forum remains available → writ dismissed with ₹5 lakh costs.

Closed-book self-test

1. Is it enough to show that a company’s present main business has stopped?

No. Identify the actual objects and whether the company’s foundation as a whole has substantially failed. Contrast German Date Coffee with Seth Mohan Lal; test permanence and realistic remaining operations.

2. Which provision makes a winding-up order collective?

Section 278: it operates in favour of all creditors and contributories as if made on their joint petition.

3. What two findings support refusal under section 273(2)?

Another remedy is available, and the petitioner is acting unreasonably by seeking just-and-equitable winding up instead of pursuing that remedy.

4. Name the three different 60-day requirements.

Registrar’s views under s. 272(5); disposal of a leave application under s. 279; liquidator’s initial report under s. 281. Their triggers are different.

5. Does liquidation permit shareholders to take the factory ahead of creditors?

No. Company assets are administered collectively. Contribution, claims and statutory priority rules apply; members receive the residual estate according to their rights only after the relevant liabilities are addressed.
