Orientation

The topic in one sentence

The Memorandum states the company’s constitutional identity and outer capacity; the Articles prescribe its internal machinery. Section 10 gives the registered documents contractual force between the company and its members; ultra vires polices the outer boundary, while the rule in Turquand protects a bona fide outsider from undisclosed defects inside that boundary.

Outer question

Can the company do this at all? Read the Act and Memorandum. If no, the act is ultra vires the company.

Authority question

Who may do it? Read the Articles, board delegation and agency rules. An internal excess may be capable of ratification or Turquand protection.

Procedure question

Was the required internal step taken? A missing resolution, quorum or delegation is often an “indoor” matter—unless the outsider knew, ought to inquire, or the defect is visible on the face of the document.

The master distinction to write in every problem answer

Capacity is not authority; authority is not procedure. First ask whether the company has capacity. Only after that ask whether the director/agent had authority and whether the internal procedure was regular. This sequence resolves the apparent tension between Ashbury, Turquand, Freeman & Lockyer, Kotla and Dehradun-Mussoorie.

Part II · Memorandum

Memorandum of Association — the company’s charter and outer boundary

DU Megh Raj notes · Topic 3 pp. 47–54 Avtar Singh · Ch. 3 pp. 49–78

The Memorandum is the foundational constitutional statement through which a proposed company identifies itself, tells the public its field of activity, and fixes key conditions of incorporation. The classic formulation is useful: it is the company’s charter; it is not just an internal rulebook.

Why it matters to investors

Subscribers and later shareholders can see the field for which capital is being risked. The objects clause limits diversion of corporate funds to an unannounced enterprise.

Why it matters to outsiders

Creditors and counterparties can test whether a contemplated transaction lies within corporate capacity. This public-facing function explains both constructive notice and the historic harshness of ultra vires.

4

Section 4(1): the six memorandum statements

Name clause

The name must end in Limited for a public limited company or Private Limited for a private limited company, subject to the § 8 exception.

  • Must satisfy §§ 4(2)–(3).
  • Specified IFSC companies have additional suffix rules.

Registered-office State

State the State in which the registered office is to be situated—not the full street address.

  • Full office compliance sits in § 12.
  • State-to-State migration engages § 13.

Objects clause

State the objects for which the company is proposed to be incorporated and matters considered necessary in furtherance of them.

  • It frames capacity.
  • Do not confuse an object with a mere power or method.

Liability clause

State whether liability is limited or unlimited. A shares company says liability is limited to unpaid amount on its shares; a guarantee company states each member’s undertaking.

  • Guarantee covers winding-up debts, costs and adjustment among contributories.

Capital / subscription clause

If there is share capital, state its amount, division into fixed-value shares, and the number each subscriber intends to take (at least one).

  • Textbooks often call the subscriber undertaking the association/subscription clause.
  • Capital alteration is principally § 61 territory.

OPC nominee

An OPC must name the person who, on the subscriber’s death, will become the member.

  • Read with the applicable incorporation rules and consent requirements.

Section 4(2)–(5): name integrity before incorporation

Objects clause: how to read it

Read the language fairly, in context and as a whole. A company has power to do what is expressly authorised and what is reasonably incidental or naturally conducive to achieving an authorised object. But a remote commercial advantage is not enough. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar is the Indian Supreme Court authority for the “reasonably proximate connection” idea; Cotman and Bell Houses show why drafting and express independent objects matter.

Part III · Articles

Articles of Association — the internal operating code

DU Megh Raj notes · Topic 3 pp. 54–56 Avtar Singh · Ch. 4 pp. 79–86

If the Memorandum says what the company is constituted to do, the Articles generally say how it will be managed. They are its internal regulations or by-laws: governance, allotment, transfer, meetings, officers, delegation, dividends, borrowing procedure and winding-up mechanics.

§ 5(1)–(2)

Articles must contain regulations for management and prescribed matters; a company may include additional management matters it considers necessary.

§ 5(3)–(5)

Entrenchment can make named provisions harder to alter than a special resolution. It needs all-member agreement in a private company and a special resolution in a public company, plus Registrar notice.

§ 5(6)–(9)

Use the appropriate Schedule I model form; adopt all/some regulations; unexcluded model regulations apply to post-Act companies; old-company Articles remain unless amended.

Part IV · Registration, effect & alteration

From draft constitution to binding instrument

It is useful to separate incorporation, registration, binding force and alteration. A document may be drafted by promoters, but its special constitutional effect arises once the company is registered under the Act.

6

Section 6 — the statutory override clause

Save where the Act expressly provides otherwise, the Act prevails over anything inconsistent in the MOA, AOA, a company agreement or a resolution of the general meeting / Board—whether made before or after the Act’s commencement. The inconsistent part becomes void. It follows that neither an unanimous shareholder resolution nor an artfully worded Article can validate what the Act prohibits.

Write this hierarchy in a problem answer

Act → Memorandum → Articles → Board resolution / officer action. A lower instrument cannot contradict a higher one. This is the statutory foundation for the statement that Articles cannot expand objects, as confirmed in Dr A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar.

10

Section 10 — effect of Memorandum and Articles

§ 10(1): deemed covenants

Once registered, MOA and AOA bind the company and its members as if each had signed them and covenanted to observe every provision. This is a statutory contractual effect—not a normal multilateral contract with every person who might benefit.

§ 10(2): monetary consequence

All money payable by a member to the company under the Memorandum or Articles is a debt due from that member to the company. This is a distinct subsection and an easy precision mark.

Section 11: omitted. Section 10A: separately requires a company incorporated after the statutory commencement and having share capital to meet declaration/registered-office conditions before commencing business or exercising borrowing powers. It is useful background, but it does not replace § 10’s constitutional binding rule.

Do not conflate § 12 and § 13

The MOA contains the State of the registered office. An ordinary intra-State/local administrative shift is principally § 12 territory; a shift from one State to another alters the Memorandum and activates § 13(4)–(7).

Section 13 — how the Memorandum may be altered

Baseline — § 13(1)

Save as provided by § 61, alteration needs a special resolution and compliance with § 13. Capital changes are not to be casually analysed as § 13 alterations.

Name — § 13(2)–(3)

Meet § 4(2)–(3), obtain Central Government written approval unless only adding/deleting “Private” on class conversion, and await a fresh certificate. The name change is complete only when that certificate issues.

State — § 13(4)–(7)

For a move from one State to another, seek Central Government approval. It must decide within 60 days and consider creditor/debenture-holder consent, discharge or adequate security. File the order with both State Registrars; the destination Registrar issues a fresh certificate.

Objects — § 13(8)–(10)

Special resolution is the base. If public money raised through a prospectus remains unutilised, add prescribed newspaper/website disclosure with justification and an exit opportunity for dissenting shareholders through promoters/control shareholders under SEBI regulations. No effect until registration.

Guarantee/no share capital — § 13(11)

An alteration purporting to give a non-member a right to divisible profits is void.

Filing and effect

File the special resolution and any required approval in the statutory manner. For an objects alteration, the Registrar registers and certifies within 30 days from filing of the special resolution. Registration is a condition of effect.

Section 14 — alteration of Articles

Subject to the Act and any conditions in the Memorandum, a company may alter its Articles by special resolution. This includes private-to-public or public-to-private conversion. If a private company removes the restrictions/limitations that make it private, it ceases to be private from the date of alteration. Public-to-private conversion requires an order of the Central Government on the prescribed application; transitional Tribunal matters are dealt with under the relevant proviso. File the alteration, the approval order where applicable and a copy of the altered Articles with the Registrar within the statutory period; once registered, it is valid as if originally in the Articles.

Statutory limit

It must remain within the Act and Memorandum. Section 6 defeats a contradictory article.

Judicial limit

Exercise alteration power bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole; it must not operate as a fraud on the minority or unfairly destroy protected rights.

Contract limit

An alteration may change constitutional rights, but cannot be used to avoid liability for an independent pre-existing contract. The company may still owe damages for breach.

Section 15: every alteration to the MOA/AOA must be noted in every copy issued. Company and officer in default each incur a ₹1,000 penalty for every unamended copy issued. Section 16: is explained just below because it completes the name-control story.

Member qua member test

A shareholder who claims a voting, dividend, transfer, meeting or share-related right normally sues as a member. The same individual claiming a job, solicitor’s retainer, supply contract or separate service relationship is an outsider for that purpose. Say this expressly before applying Eley.

Rights that are normally constitutional

  • vote and meeting participation;
  • dividend according to the Articles;
  • share transfer, lien and forfeiture rights;
  • member/director obligations where they attach to shareholding;
  • pre-emption or compulsory transfer mechanisms, subject to law and valid alteration.

Rights needing a separate contract

  • employment, solicitor, supplier or consultant rights;
  • rights of a stranger named in an Article;
  • private shareholder agreements not incorporated into the Articles;
  • an unusual office-holder benefit that is not accepted as a contractual appointment.

Binding-force qualifications and exceptions

  1. Act and Memorandum prevail. No § 10 contract can enforce something § 6 makes void or the Memorandum does not permit.
  2. Membership capacity is essential. A member does not gain enforcement merely because they happen also to be solicitor, employee, director or creditor.
  3. Outsiders do not get a statutory contractual benefit. They need a separate agreement, though its terms may be drawn from the Articles after appointment/acceptance.
  4. Inter-member enforcement is nuanced. Articles regulate the relationship; direct enforcement depends on the nature of the membership right and the procedural posture, rather than a simplistic “all members contract with all members” statement.
  5. Alteration is built into the statutory contract. Members take shares subject to the valid power of § 14 alteration, but a company cannot use an alteration to repudiate an independent contract without consequences.
  6. Entrenchment and class-right rules matter. An Article may impose a stricter constitutional threshold, and changes affecting class rights may additionally trigger statutory protections outside this Topic’s core sections.
Part VI · Corporate capacity

Doctrine of ultra vires — definition, consequences and critical analysis

Ultra vires means “beyond powers.” In company law, its strict form means an act beyond the company’s statutory or Memorandum capacity. It is not merely a bad business decision or a director’s procedural mistake: it is an act the artificial legal person lacks power to undertake.

Ultra vires the Act

Contrary to an express statutory prohibition. § 6 prevents constitutional documents or resolutions from validating it.

Ultra vires the company / MOA

Beyond express objects and what is reasonably incidental/naturally conducive. The classic Ashbury situation: void at source.

Ultra vires directors / AOA

Within company capacity but beyond an officer’s authority or internal procedure. Usually a different problem: ratification, agency and Turquand may matter.

The capacity–authority–procedure decision tree

What counts as “within” an object?

  • Express authority: the act falls in the ordinary/literal meaning of an object clause.
  • Necessary or reasonably incidental authority: it is naturally conducive to the express object, not merely profitable or remotely advantageous.
  • Clear independent objects: a separate object can stand on its own if validly and clearly drafted (Cotman), although labels do not convert every facilitating power into an object (Re Introductions).
  • Subjective connection clauses: where wording commits the connected/ancillary judgment to the board’s bona fide opinion, that opinion can be dispositive within the clause’s limits (Bell Houses).
  • Articles cannot enlarge: a power in the Articles is useful only if the company already has Memorandum capacity to pursue the object (Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar).

Do not overstate “void”

A void ultra vires contract is not enforceable as such. That does not mean a court automatically leaves a dishonest recipient with traceable company property or ignores a separate restitution/subrogation claim. State the narrow contractual conclusion first, then identify any reserved proprietary or restitutionary issue. Beauforte is the authority for this sophistication.

Critical analysis — why the doctrine both matters and disappoints

Its constitutional value

  • Protects capital contributed for a disclosed venture from managerial diversion.
  • Provides creditors and investors an ascertainable outer limit.
  • Preserves the distinction between corporate capacity and internal authority.
  • Allows minority members to restrain an act that a majority cannot ratify.
  • Disciplines directors: broad AOA powers cannot trump the stated objects.

Its practical weaknesses

  • Drafting can make objects extremely broad, independent and commercially elastic.
  • It may punish innocent counterparties who never actually read the constitutional documents.
  • It encourages long boilerplate clauses rather than intelligible disclosure.
  • It causes hard line-drawing between an “object” and a “power.”
  • Modern companies need flexibility to diversify; § 13 permits prospectively altering objects.

Balanced conclusion: In India, § 4(c) still requires objects and § 13 still regulates their alteration, so capacity analysis remains doctrinally real. But it is no longer sensible to call it an absolute commercial shield: wide modern drafting, independent-object language, bona fide opinion clauses and statutory alteration make it a targeted constitutional control. It has greatest force where a transaction is truly alien to the company’s disclosed purpose, where directors deploy funds for a remote end, or where a lender knows the funds finance an unauthorised venture.

Part VII · Case dossiers

The six ultra vires authorities

Each card follows a DU-friendly order: facts → issue → both sides → decision → reasoning → ratio → significance/application. Read the ratio box aloud before memorising facts.

1Ashbury Railway Carriage and Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche

(1875) L.R. 7 H.L.; (1874–80) All E.R. Rep. 2219 (HL)

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 80–89

Material facts

The company’s objects included making/selling/hiring railway plant; carrying on mechanical engineering and “general contractors”; dealing in mines/minerals and specified materials. Riche held a Belgian concession to build the Antwerp–Tournay railway. The company contracted to acquire the concession, form and finance a Belgian société anonyme, take its shares/bonds and give Riche the supply business. Financial difficulty followed. Even though the Articles purported to permit business expansion by special resolution, no such resolution was passed; shareholder arrangements later tried to shift the burden to directors.

Issue

Was the concession/finance agreement within the Memorandum, particularly the words “general contractors”? If not, could shareholder assent, an Article, later arrangements or ratification validate it?

Riche’s argument

“General contractors” was broad enough to cover the railway venture. In any event, the shareholders had knowledge/assented and could ratify the transaction; the company’s internal arrangements with its directors should not defeat his claim.

Company’s argument

The words had to be read with “mechanical engineers,” not as a licence for every kind of commercial contract. The company was purchasing and financing a foreign railway enterprise—not supplying its own railway plant. Articles could not extend the Memorandum and shareholders could not authorise what the company itself lacked capacity to do.

Decision & reasoning

The House of Lords found for the company. The contractual scheme was outside the objects clause. “General contractors” was contextually limited to contracts connected with mechanical engineering; otherwise the object statement would become meaningless. The Memorandum was the company’s charter and limitation of powers; Articles were subordinate internal regulations. The statutory incorporation scheme protected not only existing shareholders, but future shareholders and the dealing public. A company cannot do indirectly through an Article or unanimously consent to what the statute/Memorandum says it cannot do.

Ratio / principle evolved

A contract wholly beyond the objects in the Memorandum is ultra vires the company, void from inception and incapable of ratification even by every shareholder. An Article cannot enlarge corporate capacity.

Significance & exam application

Begin any capacity answer with Ashbury. Then distinguish it from an act merely ultra vires the directors. If the company could have contracted but a resolution was missing, move to Turquand; if it could not have contracted at all, stop at Ashbury.

2Cotman v. Brougham

(1918–19) All E.R. Rep. 265 (HL)

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 90–95

Material facts

Essequibo Rubber and Tobacco Estates Ltd. had a Memorandum containing about 30 wide objects. Its concluding clause said each sub-clause was independent and not restricted by the first object or company name. It sub-underwrote 20,000 shares in Anglo-Cuban Oil, Bitumen and Asphalt Co.; 17,200 shares were allotted and calls became due. In the liquidation, Essequibo’s liquidator sought removal from the contributories list, saying the unrelated underwriting was ultra vires.

Issues

Could a party challenge the validity of the already registered Memorandum itself? On its true construction, did the independent object clauses cover underwriting/acquiring shares? Must capacity be measured by the main object/substratum?

Liquidator’s argument

The sprawling clause failed to genuinely delimit a field of activity and the oil-company underwriting was unrelated to a rubber/tobacco business. The sub-clauses should be treated as ancillary to a dominant object; the company’s name and substratum supported a narrow reading.

Opposing argument

The Registrar’s certificate was conclusive as to incorporation requirements. The text expressly authorised promoting companies and acquiring/dealing in their shares, and the independence clause prevented those powers being cut down by the first object or name.

Decision & reasoning

The House of Lords dismissed the appeal: the transaction was intra vires. Once the Memorandum had been accepted and a certificate issued, this private proceeding could not reopen its registrability. Clauses authorising promotion and acquisition/dealing in shares were textually wide enough; the independent-object language had to be given effect. The Court separated a legal capacity issue from the equitable question whether a company’s substratum had failed for winding-up purposes. Lord Wrenbury strongly criticised the drafting practice, but the Court had to construe the incorporated document as it stood.

Ratio / principle evolved

Clear, separately stated object clauses may be construed as independent rather than as merely ancillary to a “main” object. A registered certificate is conclusive as to pre-incorporation compliance; capacity turns on construction of the operative Memorandum.

Significance & exam application

Use Cotman against an automatic “main object only” argument. But add the boundary from Re Introductions: merely calling a facilitative power an “independent object” does not necessarily turn it into a substantive corporate end. Source alert: the isolated word “ultra vires” in the reader’s headnote is inconsistent with the report’s result; the correct outcome is intra vires.

3In re (Jon) Beauforte (London) Ltd.

[1953] 1 Ch. 131

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 96–99

Material facts

The company was authorised to run clothing/costume-related businesses. It embarked instead on manufacturing veneered panels, admittedly outside those objects, and built a Bristol factory. In liquidation, the liquidator rejected proofs by (i) builders who had a consent/default judgment, (ii) veneer suppliers who had a summary judgment, and (iii) a coke supplier. The creditors had no actual knowledge of the constitutional mismatch; the coke correspondence openly described the company as a veneer-panel manufacturer.

Issues

Could a judgment, consent order, estoppel or failure to plead ultra vires make an ultra vires contract enforceable? Could a supplier say goods might have been used for a legitimate business? What residual remedies survived rejection of the proof?

Creditors’ argument

The debts had merged in judgments or a compromise and should bind the company. They had no actual knowledge that the business was unauthorised. The coke supplier argued that fuel could have been applied to the company’s legitimate trade.

Liquidator’s argument

The transactions were admittedly ultra vires; consent or a procedural judgment cannot create capacity. Creditors had constructive notice of the Memorandum, and the coke supplier had direct information that the fuel served the veneer business.

Decision & reasoning

The proofs were rejected. A company cannot create a power it lacks by consenting in litigation, allowing default judgment or treating an ultra vires agreement as valid. A judgment founded on such a contract is secure only where it embodies a judicial determination of the ultra vires issue or a genuine compromise of that issue. The court also held the fuel supplier fixed with clear notice of the actual venture. Crucially, the order preserved possible tracing claims and the possibility of sharing in any surplus after proving creditors and liquidation costs were met.

Ratio / principle evolved

An ultra vires contract does not become intra vires through estoppel, ratification, acquiescence, lapse of time, an unargued/default judgment or a consent based on the mistaken premise of capacity.

Significance & exam application

Use this case when a problem says “but the company signed a consent decree” or “the judgment is final.” Separate contractual enforcement from tracing/restitution/surplus; that final qualification earns depth marks.

4Bell Houses, Ltd. v. City Wall Properties, Ltd.

[1966] 2 All E.R. 674

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 100–109

Material facts

Bell Houses was a property-development company. Its chairman, Mr Bell, controlled day-to-day administration under Board delegation. Clause 3(c) permitted any trade/business which, in the opinion of the board, could advantageously be carried on in connection with or as ancillary to the listed/general business. Bell introduced City Wall to a source of £1 million bridging finance and claimed a £20,000 commission. City Wall resisted, late in the proceedings, by calling the arrangement ultra vires “mortgage broking.”

Issues

Was the isolated finance-introduction agreement connected/ancillary to the property-development business? Did the clause make a bona fide Board opinion sufficient? Did absence of a formal opinion-resolution defeat the claim?

City Wall’s argument

The transaction was a new and unrelated mortgage-broking business. The board’s opinion could not alone make it intra vires; it had to be objectively correct, and no formal board resolution recorded such an opinion.

Bell Houses’ argument

Development depended on access to finance. The introduction was an isolated opportunity arising from its general business, created goodwill and built a valuable funding relationship. The Articles permitted delegation; Mr Bell’s bona fide opinion was evident from his authorised conduct.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Appeal allowed Bell Houses’ appeal. It rejected the artificial characterisation as a wholly new mortgage-broking business. On the actual facts the finance source and goodwill were connected with and advantageous to property development. The wording made the board’s bona fide opinion meaningful; it need not be displaced by an objective judicial re-evaluation. Mr Bell had delegated management authority and the evidence showed his opinion. Other clauses allowing the company to turn assets/information to account strengthened the conclusion. Because the transaction was intra vires, the Court did not need to decide the abstract question whether the defendant could invoke ultra vires as a defence.

Ratio / principle evolved

Where a Memorandum expressly makes the bona fide opinion of the board the criterion for connected/ancillary business, that opinion can be sufficient to establish capacity, provided the clause’s limits and the factual connection are genuinely met.

Significance & exam application

Use Bell Houses to show why the doctrine can be diluted by careful drafting. Do not turn it into a licence for any business: the activity still had a factual connection to the general business and the opinion had to be bona fide. Contrast Re Introductions.

5Re Introductions, Ltd. v. National Provincial Bank Ltd.

[1969] 1 All E.R. 887

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 110–112

Material facts

Introductions began by serving Festival of Britain visitors, later operated a deck-chair business and was dormant for a period. A new board then used it for pig breeding—conceded to be outside its Memorandum. Its bank allowed an overdraft and received debentures over company assets. Before accepting security, the bank had received the MOA/AOA and knew that pig breeding was the company’s sole activity. It relied on a broadly worded borrowing/security clause and a declaration that preceding sub-clauses were independent objects.

Issue

Could a general power to borrow and issue secured debentures be treated as a separate corporate object, so that the security was valid despite the bank’s knowledge that the borrowing funded an ultra vires pig-breeding venture?

Bank’s argument

The Memorandum expressly authorised borrowing and charging assets. The independent-object declaration elevated this to an independent end. A lender should only need to see an express borrowing power, not investigate the commercial purpose—even where it knew the business being conducted.

Liquidator’s argument

Borrowing is a means to a corporate end, not an end “in the air.” Independent-object boilerplate cannot change the character of a genuinely ancillary power. The bank’s actual knowledge of the unauthorised purpose prevented reliance on any innocent-lender rationale.

Decision & reasoning

The appeal was dismissed; the bank could not rely on its debentures against the liquidator. The court accepted that an ignorant lender ordinarily need not investigate a permitted borrowing’s application, but this bank knew the purpose. A borrowing power necessarily means borrowing for purposes of the company. Calling each sub-clause an “independent object” could not convert an instrumental power into a self-standing object. Because the borrowing was for a purpose outside corporate capacity and that fact was known, the security failed.

Ratio / principle evolved

A power to borrow/secure money is ordinarily ancillary to lawful corporate purposes. It cannot be made an unlimited substantive object merely by drafting language, particularly where the lender knows the funds finance an ultra vires activity.

Significance & exam application

This is the disciplined counterweight to Cotman and Bell Houses. In a loan problem, ask why the money was borrowed and what the lender knew. Do not say that every lender must investigate: the knowledge finding is central.

6Dr A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar v. Life Insurance Corporation of India

AIR 1963 SC 1185

Primary DU case material 2022 · pp. 129–134 Listed reference in 2025 DU syllabus

Material facts

United India Life Assurance Co. carried on life insurance. At a shareholder meeting it resolved to donate ₹2 lakh from a “Shareholders’ Dividend Account” to the M.Ct.M. Chidambaram Chettyar Memorial Trust. The trust had broad charitable/educational objects, one of which included technical/business knowledge in insurance. Article 93(t) authorised donations for charitable, benevolent, public, general or useful objects. After nationalisation, LIC—into which the insurer’s controlled-business assets/liabilities vested—sought recovery. The Life Insurance Tribunal ordered repayment.

Issues

Was the donation within the insurer’s Memorandum or naturally incidental/conducive to life insurance? Could Article 93(t), a shareholder resolution, or the special dividend account support it? Were those responsible personally accountable?

Appellants’ argument

The Articles expressly empowered charitable/public donations. The account belonged beneficially to shareholders rather than the company, and shareholders had decided its destination. Training related to insurance/business could benefit the insurer; trustees said they should not be personally liable.

LIC’s argument

The Memorandum did not authorise the donation and no direct/substantial corporate advantage existed. The wide trust objects did not oblige spending on insurance education. The alleged future benefit was remote. Articles and shareholder resolution could not enlarge corporate objects.

Decision & reasoning

The Supreme Court held the payment ultra vires and upheld recovery. It treated the meeting as a meeting of the company, not shareholders privately disposing of personal money. A Memorandum is fairly construed, and powers include what is incidental/naturally conducive to authorised objects; but the benefit here was too indirect and remote. The Articles may assist in explaining an ambiguity or supplementing silence, but cannot extend a clear Memorandum. Article 93(t) was therefore usable only if the company already had Memorandum capacity. Directors responsible for authorising the unlawful disbursement had to make it good; the Tribunal could address responsibility/benefit of trustees under the LIC Act.

Ratio / principle evolved

A company may do what its Memorandum expressly authorises and what has a reasonably proximate, natural connection with those objects. Articles and even unanimous shareholder approval cannot enlarge a clear objects clause; a remote hoped-for benefit is insufficient.

Significance & exam application

This is the leading Indian authority on the line between an object and a power. Use it for donations, scholarships, social expenditure, diversification and Board powers. Modern note: do not mechanically use it to deny an expenditure expressly mandated/authorised by contemporary statute (for example, applicable § 135 CSR obligations); first identify the independent statutory basis.

Part VIII · Outsider protection

Constructive notice and the doctrine of indoor management

Avtar Singh · Ch. 4 pp. 90–103 DU 2025 case material · pp. 107–141

These doctrines are complementary—not contradictory. Constructive notice makes an outsider take the registered constitution as it stands. The Turquand rule then protects a bona fide outsider from undisclosed failures in the company’s internal machinery.

1. Constructive notice — the external-facing doctrine

The Memorandum and Articles are registered and publicly inspectable. Traditionally, a person dealing with the company is deemed to have read and correctly understood their proper legal meaning whether or not they actually inspected them. The person is therefore fixed with notice of the company’s capacity, of limits on an officer’s outward authority, and of a prescribed mode of execution. The doctrine reflects disclosure: a company should not have to bear a contract that its public constitution unmistakably says it could not make in that way.

What an outsider must take notice of

  • the company’s objects/capacity and express prohibitions;
  • an Article that says a deed must be signed by specified officers;
  • the fact that a director/manager role can or cannot exist under the constitution;
  • statutorily registrable power-affecting documents—classically, special resolutions and charge particulars—where the doctrine applies.

What is not constructively known

  • whether a board or general meeting was actually held;
  • whether notice, quorum, voting or minute requirements were properly met;
  • whether a permissible delegation was actually made;
  • unfiled internal instructions, private defects or the day-to-day “indoor” history.

Limits and criticism of constructive notice

It is a technical presumption, not evidence that a businessperson truly read or relied on every filing. Freeman & Lockyer describes it as a negative doctrine: it stops a contractor from denying an adverse constitutional limitation; it does not automatically let a contractor rely positively on an unusual delegation clause they never knew about. The old doctrine is commercially unrealistic and has been heavily curtailed in some jurisdictions, but it remains important in Indian teaching and is applied forcefully in Kotla.

2. Indoor management / the Turquand rule

Classical formulation: if the public constitution gives a company/officer power subject to an internal preliminary (for example, a general-meeting or Board resolution), a person dealing in good faith may assume that the preliminary was duly performed. The outsider must read the public rule; the outsider need not enter the boardroom to verify compliance.

Why the rule exists

Minutes, voting, quorum and appointment processes are usually unavailable to an outsider. Requiring every supplier, lender and customer to audit the company’s internal machinery would freeze commerce. As the DU extract puts it, an outsider knows the constitution, “but not what may or may not have taken place within the doors that are closed to him.”

Exceptions / boundaries — use every relevant one in a problem answer

1 · Actual knowledge

No protection where the outsider actually knows the resolution, appointment or procedural requirement was not satisfied. An insider participating in the irregularity cannot invoke it.

2 · Suspicion / duty to inquire

Unusual, improvident or facially doubtful circumstances put the outsider on inquiry. One cannot deliberately shut one’s eyes. The inquiry is contextual, not a general duty to inspect internal minutes.

3 · Forgery / no genuine act

A forged instrument is ordinarily outside the classic rule: it is not merely an irregular genuine company transaction. Ruben v Great Fingall is the standard authority. Separate estoppel/apparent-authority facts can nevertheless matter.

4 · No possible / apparent authority

The rule does not make an accountant a land-selling agent or a random employee a company borrower. The claimed act must fall within the ordinary or held-out scope of that office.

5 · Public constitutional bar

Where the MOA/AOA itself shows that the act/mode is impermissible, constructive notice applies. Kotla: a deed visibly lacking a mandatory third signature fails.

6 · No company capacity / statutory illegality

Indoor management cannot override an ultra vires transaction or a statutory/external condition precedent. Dehradun-Mussoorie is particularly instructive: company-law regularity did not cure missing Government consent under the Tramway Order.

7 · No positive reliance on an unknown Article

Where the outsider did not know of the Article/power at all, the outsider cannot ordinarily invoke it positively as the foundation for an assumption that it was regularly exercised. Rama Corporation v. Proved Tin is the usual authority. Test any independent holding-out case separately.

Refinement: “Forgery is always an exception” is a safe examination shorthand, but the underlying inquiry is whether the company itself made a representation or held out the purported actor. A company may on distinct facts be estopped by its own representation; do not confuse that with treating a secretary’s unauthorised forgery itself as internal regularity.

7Royal British Bank v. Turquand

(1856) 6 E. & B. 327; 119 E.R. 886

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 113–115

Material facts

The registered Cameron’s Coalbrook Steam, Coal, and Swansea and London Railway Company gave Royal British Bank a bond. It acknowledged liability of £2,000 and secured the running account up to £1,000. Its deed of settlement authorised directors to borrow on bond only such sums as a general resolution of the company authorised. The company, through its official manager, pleaded that no such shareholder resolution existed and therefore the sealed bond was not the company’s deed.

Issue

Was the bank, which was deemed to know the deed of settlement, also bound to prove that the internal general-meeting resolution had actually been passed before it could enforce the bond?

Company’s argument

Companies have only statutory/deed powers, unlike ordinary partners. The deed expressly made a general resolution a condition to borrowing on bond. No resolution meant no authority and no company deed; the bank had notice of this from the registered document.

Bank’s argument

The deed did not prohibit borrowing. It permitted borrowing once a resolution was passed. Whether the resolution was passed was an internal matter, and the outward sealed bond appeared to be the type of transaction the directors could lawfully carry out.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Exchequer Chamber affirmed judgment for the bank. Jervis CJ accepted that a contractor must read the statute and deed, but “not … more.” On reading it, the bank saw a power capable of completion by a resolution. It could infer that the resolution authorising an apparently legitimate bond had been passed. The Court also considered that the pleaded general resolution might itself have been sufficiently broad, but its enduring holding is the right to assume internal regularity.

Ratio / principle evolved

Where a public constitutional document permits an act subject to an internal authorisation, a bona fide outsider may presume that the internal authorisation has been duly obtained. This is the doctrine of indoor management / the Turquand rule.

Significance & exam application

Apply only after confirming company capacity and a power that could have been regularised. The bank was not excused from knowing the condition; it was excused from verifying its internal fulfilment. Compare Kotla, where the external document itself displayed non-compliance.

8Freeman & Lockyer (a firm) v. Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd.

[1964] 1 All E.R. 630 (CA)

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 116–126

Material facts

Kapoor and Hoon formed the defendant to buy and profitably resell Buckhurst Park Estate. The Board comprised them and nominees; the Articles allowed appointment of a managing director and delegation, but required a quorum of four. No formal resolution appointed Kapoor managing director. Hoon was often abroad. With the Board’s knowledge Kapoor ran the venture, sought purchasers/development approval and instructed Freeman & Lockyer, architects and surveyors, to work on planning matters. Their £291 6s fee was undisputed; the company denied being bound.

Issues

Did Kapoor have actual authority? If not, had the company held him out as managing director with ostensible authority? Must the architects have read/rely on the Articles before using a delegation power?

Company’s argument

Kapoor was only a director. There was no Board resolution appointing him managing director or authorising the surveyors; communications often used his personal name. The architects had not inspected the Articles or shown reliance on a formal appointment/delegation.

Architects’ argument

Kapoor instructed them for the company’s estate and they performed the work. The Board knowingly allowed him to conduct the company’s business as managing director—dealing with agents, disposal and planning. Hiring surveyors was an ordinary incident of that held-out office and property-development venture.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Appeal dismissed the company’s appeal. There was no actual authority: no valid appointment/delegation resolution. But the Board’s knowing acquiescence constituted a representation by the company that Kapoor was managing director. The plaintiffs relied on that representation; employing surveyors for planning/sale was within an ordinary managing director’s apparent authority. The Articles gave the Board capacity to appoint/delegate, so the constitution did not bar that authority. The plaintiffs were not required to investigate whether Kapoor had been properly appointed.

Ratio / principle evolved

A company is bound by an officer’s act where (i) a representation that the officer had authority was made by persons with actual authority to manage, (ii) the representation induced the contractor to contract, and (iii) the act fell within the authority that could lawfully be delegated and within the apparent ordinary scope of the held-out office. Apparent and actual authority are distinct.

Significance & exam application

This is not merely a Turquand case—it is the modern architecture of ostensible authority. State the four elements. Then say why it fails if the MOA/AOA made the act incapable of delegation or if the only “representation” came from Kapoor himself.

9Kotla Venkataswamy v. Chinta Ramamurthy

AIR 1934 Mad. 579

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 127–128

Material facts

Venkatamma took a ₹1,000 mortgage bond from South Indian Agricultural and Industrial Improvement Co. Ltd. and assigned it to the plaintiff. The company later went into voluntary liquidation and its property was sold. Article 15 required all deeds and instruments to be signed by the managing director, secretary and working director. The mortgage bore only the secretary’s and working director’s signatures. Evidence that the managing director had been dismissed/prosecuted did not establish any special authority for the two signatories.

Issues

Did the two officers validly execute the mortgage? Could the mortgagee invoke good faith/indoor management despite the visibly absent managing-director signature? Could later ratification or an equitable charge be considered?

Mortgagee’s argument

The debt was regularly incurred under Articles/resolutions; she acted in good faith and the funds were used for company purposes. She also raised ratification and an equitable-charge theory based on benefit to the company.

Company/purchaser’s argument

Article 15 was a public and mandatory execution requirement. The document was signed by only two of the three named officers. The alleged special authorisation was unproved; alternative ratification/equitable points were never properly made issues at trial.

Decision & reasoning

The Madras High Court dismissed the appeal. Article 15 was not a trivial formality: it empowered the three named officers together to execute on behalf of the company; without it, authority remained in the Board as a whole. The lender was deemed to have read the Articles and would have discovered the three-signature requirement. This was not a hidden internal defect. Even good faith and use of money for company purposes did not cure the invalid bond. Ratification/equitable charge questions were not entertained because they had not been framed and tried as issues.

Ratio / principle evolved

Constructive notice fixes an outsider with a public Article prescribing the essential mode of executing a deed. The Turquand rule does not protect a person against an irregularity apparent on the face of the instrument when read with the Articles.

Significance & exam application

Use Kotla as the standard limit to indoor management. Highlight the missing signature in the facts. It does not say all execution defects are fatal: ask whether the public constitution makes the form mandatory and whether a genuine separate authorisation/agency case is pleaded.

10Dehradun-Mussoorie Electric Tramway Co. v. Jagmandar Das & Others

AIR 1932 All. 141

Primary DU case material 2025 · pp. 123–141

Material facts

The tramway company’s managing agent, Beltie Shah, obtained a ₹25,000 bank overdraft urgently needed to pay for machinery/stores; the money was admittedly used for company purposes. He then executed a 19 June 1923 mortgage over Khazanchi Bagh land to secure it. The Articles prohibited the Board from delegating a general borrowing power and required a Board procedure; the minute recording mortgage authority dated 2 June was not from a properly convened meeting. The bank had been shown the minute book and believed the resolution genuine. Separately, clause 37 of the statutory Tramway Order required previous Local Government assent before transfer of the undertaking; none was obtained for this mortgage.

Issues

(i) Was the temporary overdraft/mortgage authorised or ratified under company law? (ii) Could the bank rely on internal regularity despite the defective Board meeting? (iii) Was the land part of the tramway undertaking and was the missing Government consent merely voidable or absolutely fatal?

Liquidators’ argument

Beltie Shah lacked delegated borrowing power; the 2 June resolution was bogus/improperly convened and the mortgage execution defective. The bank’s letters showed it knew approval was not regular. Most importantly, clause 37 made a transfer without prior Government assent void; the land was part of the undertaking.

Bank’s argument

The agent faced an emergency and had broad management powers; the loan was necessary and later ratified in directors’ reports/accounts. The bank acted bona fide, was deceived by a plausible managing agent and could assume proper internal procedure. It contended no proof of missing Government consent, and argued the company may not have become a statutory “promoter” or the land was not part of the undertaking.

Decision & reasoning

The Allahabad High Court made a vital two-stage distinction. Company-law stage: the emergency temporary loan was within agency authority (and in any event was ratified); although no properly convened Board meeting passed the 2 June resolution, the bank had no reason to suspect that internal defect. Under Turquand, the company was bound by the mortgage so far as company law was concerned. Statutory stage: the company had become a “promoter” (the 9 July letter was sufficient prior assent for transfer to it); the depot land was part of the undertaking because it belonged to, was suitable for and was used for tramway purposes. But the mortgage itself lacked prior Government assent under clause 37. The Tramway Order had force of law, so the mortgage was absolutely void, not merely voidable, and could not be ratified or supported by estoppel. The bank received a simple money decree and ranked as an unsecured creditor in liquidation (₹29,773-4-3, with the stated interest treatment).

Ratio / principle evolved

An innocent outsider may invoke indoor management against an unobservable defect in Board procedure, but that doctrine cannot cure contravention of an independent statutory condition precedent. A transfer forbidden by a statutory order without prior governmental consent is void and incapable of ratification/estoppel.

Significance & exam application

This is an excellent high-mark authority because it prevents a false either/or answer. Write: “The bank wins on the internal-management point, but loses the security on the external statutory-consent point.” Then preserve the unsecured debt remedy. Do not reduce the case to a bare application of Turquand.

DU LB-303 · December 2024 · Question 3 Recovered / reconstructed issue statement

Doctrine of indoor management, Board resolutions, constructive notice, apparent authority and exceptions. The checklist’s preserved cue is that ABC Finance relied on a director’s apparent authority.

Reconstructed issue statement

Explain the doctrine of indoor management and its relationship with Board resolutions and constructive notice. Apply the doctrine to the ABC Finance scenario, subject to the facts available. Important: this is a reconstruction from the uploaded checklist, not the original exam wording.

Model answer

The doctrine of indoor management, also known as the rule in Royal British Bank v. Turquand, protects a bona fide person dealing with a company against a defect in the company’s internal procedure. Its starting point is the apparently harsh doctrine of constructive notice: an outsider is deemed to know the registered Memorandum and Articles and therefore cannot say that an express public restriction on corporate capacity or mode of execution was unknown. But the outsider is not required to find out whether a Board meeting was properly convened, whether quorum existed, whether a resolution was actually passed, or whether a permissible appointment or delegation was regularly made. In short: the outsider must read the public rule, but need not audit its indoor observance.

What constructive notice does

ABC Finance is fixed with the contents of the Memorandum and Articles. Thus, it must take notice if the documents say, for example, that borrowing is outside the company’s objects, that a particular officer can never borrow, or that a deed must visibly bear the signatures of three specified office-holders. A transaction visibly beyond those limits cannot be saved merely by calling the defect “internal.”

What Turquand supplies

If the Articles permit borrowing subject to a Board/general-meeting resolution, that resolution is an internal preliminary. A lender dealing honestly may assume that it has been passed. In Turquand, the public deed permitted borrowing subject to a resolution; the bank was entitled to assume the resolution had been made. The company, rather than the outsider, bears the risk of its secret procedural failure.

The same result may arise through apparent (ostensible) authority. In Freeman & Lockyer v. Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd., Kapoor had not been validly appointed managing director, but the Board knowingly permitted him to act as one. The Court of Appeal held that the company’s representation, the contractor’s reliance, and the ordinary scope of a managing director’s work could bind the company even without actual authority. Diplock LJ’s structure is valuable here: the representation must come from persons with actual authority to manage the company; it must induce the transaction; and the act must be one that the company could lawfully delegate and that falls within the represented office’s apparent scope.

Application to ABC Finance on the surviving facts

  1. Identify the public constitutional rule. Ask first whether the Memorandum permits the relevant finance/borrowing transaction. If it is outside the company’s objects or prohibited by statute, it is ultra vires the company; neither a Board resolution nor indoor management can create missing capacity.
  2. Classify the alleged defect. If borrowing was permitted but required a Board resolution, then absence, defective notice, lack of quorum, or an irregularly recorded resolution is normally an indoor defect. ABC Finance may assume regular compliance, provided it was acting bona fide.
  3. Test the director’s position. The checklist states that ABC Finance relied on the director’s apparent authority. If the Board held that person out as managing director or as a person who ordinarily arranged finance, and borrowing was within the possible authority of that office, Freeman & Lockyer supports liability. The director’s own unsupported assertion is insufficient; the company’s representation is essential.
  4. Apply the limits. ABC Finance loses the protection if it actually knew that no required resolution existed; if circumstances were so suspicious that a reasonable financier should have inquired; if the document was forged and there was no genuine company act; if it seeks to rely positively on an Article/power it did not know; if the director’s act lay outside any possible/apparent authority; if the Articles made a public execution requirement visibly unmet; or if an external statutory condition precedent was absent.
  5. State a conditional conclusion. If the only defect was the company’s failure to pass or properly record an internally required Board resolution, and the director was held out with ordinary finance authority, ABC Finance should succeed against the company. If the alleged defect was instead visible from the Articles/instrument or went to capacity/statutory legality, it should not.

Authorities on the limiting branches: Kotla Venkataswamy v. Chinta Ramamurthy denies relief where the Articles publicly required three signatures but the mortgage visibly had only two. Dehradun-Mussoorie Electric Tramway Co. v. Jagmandar Das shows the subtler result: the bank could rely on indoor management concerning a defective Board meeting, but the security still failed because prior Government assent under a statutory Tramway Order was missing. Thus, a good answer must not treat Turquand as a universal cure.

Conclusion in one examinable paragraph

The doctrines are complementary. Constructive notice charges ABC Finance with the public constitutional boundary; indoor management relieves it from proving compliance with a hidden Board-resolution requirement inside that boundary. Subject to good faith, a company representation and possible ordinary authority, the company is bound despite an undisclosed procedural irregularity. Actual knowledge, suspicion, forgery, a positive reliance claim based on an Article/power never known to the outsider, a facial public defect, lack of capacity or an independent statutory prohibition lead to the opposite conclusion.

1. Distinguish between “Memorandum of Association” and “Articles of Association”. Professional practice

Answer spine: Begin with hierarchy: the Act is supreme by § 6; the Memorandum is the company’s charter and outer boundary; the Articles are internal regulations. Under § 4, the Memorandum states the name, State of registered office, objects, liability, capital and (for an OPC) nominee. Under § 5, Articles regulate management, allocation of powers, meetings, delegation, share machinery and internal procedure. A Memorandum-capacity breach is generally ultra vires and cannot be ratified; an Article/authority breach may be cured by ratification, apparent authority or indoor management if the company had capacity. Alteration is governed principally by § 13 for the Memorandum and § 14 for Articles. Finally, under § 10 both documents bind company and members in their membership capacity, but neither automatically creates a contract with an outsider. See full MOA analysis and AOA analysis.

2. “The Articles of Association play a subordinate role to the Memorandum of Association.” Comment. Professional practice

Answer spine: Agree, but qualify. An Article is subordinate first to the Act (§ 6) and then to the Memorandum. It can distribute powers, set procedures and supply operational detail; it cannot confer an object that the Memorandum has withheld. In Dr A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar, a wide Article authorising charitable donations could not enlarge the insurer’s objects or validate a remote donation. The Articles can clarify an ambiguity or govern the manner in which an existing power is exercised; they cannot create corporate capacity. Therefore an Article contrary to the Act/Memorandum is inoperative to that extent, and a valid § 14 alteration still cannot override § 6 or retrospectively cure a transaction void for want of capacity. Apply the capacity–authority–procedure test.

3. Write a short note on the doctrine of indoor management. Professional practice

Model short note: The rule in Royal British Bank v. Turquand permits a bona fide outsider to assume that internal requirements, such as a necessary Board or general-meeting resolution, have been observed. It is the counterweight to constructive notice: an outsider is deemed to know the filed Memorandum and Articles, but not to know facts locked inside the company. The rule only operates where the company had capacity and the public constitution made the act possible; it does not validate a facial breach of the Articles, actual knowledge of irregularity, suspicious circumstances, forgery, absence of any possible/apparent authority or statutory illegality. Add Kotla for visible execution defects and Dehradun-Mussoorie for an external statutory-consent bar. See the full doctrine and exception table.

4. “The doctrine of indoor management is a ‘silver lining’ to strangers dealing with the company.” Comment. Professional practice

Answer spine: The metaphor is apt because constructive notice would otherwise force strangers to suffer for secret failures in meetings, minutes and delegations. Turquand shifts that internal risk to the company and makes commerce workable. It is not a blank cheque: the stranger must be bona fide and the transaction must be intra vires and within possible/apparent authority. A lender cannot ignore a public three-signature rule (Kotla), rely on a forged company act, or bypass mandatory Government consent (Dehradun-Mussoorie). Conclude that the doctrine is a calibrated commercial protection, not an override of the statute or constitution.

5. Discuss the binding effect of Memorandum and Articles when registered, on shareholders and outsiders. Professional practice

Answer spine: Quote the substance of § 10: once registered, the Memorandum and Articles bind the company and its members as if each had signed them and covenanted to observe them. The statutory contract is enforceable by company against member and member against company when the claimant asserts a right qua member—such as voting, transfer or meeting rights. Articles may regulate relations among members, but should not be described uncritically as a free-standing contract between every pair. An outsider obtains no right merely because the Articles mention or benefit that person: Eley v. Positive Government Security Life Assurance Co. is the classic boundary. A separate contract may nevertheless arise by ordinary contract principles. Use the binding-force matrix.

6(a). “Articles constitute a contract between the company and the members and members inter se.” Discuss. Professional practice

Answer spine: State § 10 and distinguish its legal fiction from ordinary bilateral contracting. The strongest proposition is company–member reciprocity in membership matters. A member may enforce an Article against the company and is bound by it in that capacity. Articles also organise member rights inter se, and courts may permit direct relief in a suitable membership dispute; but orthodox analysis resists treating every Article as an independent bilateral promise by each member to every other. Identify the right, identify the proper claimant/defendant, and apply the qua-member test. An employment, professional or supplier benefit calls for a separate contract, as Eley illustrates.

6(b). “The altered Articles will bind the members just as the original Articles did, but the alteration is not retrospective.” Comment. Professional practice

Answer spine: A valid § 14 special-resolution alteration, once registered, becomes part of the statutory contract and binds existing as well as future members in their membership capacity. The qualification is crucial: it governs the constitutional relationship prospectively; it does not retrospectively manufacture capacity for a void old transaction or simply extinguish an accrued independent contractual liability. The alteration must also remain within the Act and Memorandum, be bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole, and not be an oppressive or fraudulent use of majority power. Thus, “binding” does not mean “free to rewrite history.”

7. “The power of altering the Articles is wide, yet subject to a large number of limitations.” Examine. Professional practice

Answer spine: Lead with § 14: alteration normally requires a special resolution and must be filed/registered; conversion from public to private company has the statutorily required Central Government approval. Then organise limitations: (i) § 6—the alteration cannot override the Act; (ii) it cannot contradict the Memorandum or extend corporate capacity; (iii) statutory procedures, class-right safeguards and filing requirements must be observed; (iv) it must be bona fide for the company as a whole, not an instrument of oppression, fraud or personal expropriation; (v) it cannot unlawfully increase a member’s liability without the required consent; and (vi) it cannot retrospectively cure a void act or evade an independent contract. A valid exclusion of members who compete with the company can be justified where genuinely directed to the company’s welfare; a merely punitive alteration cannot.

8. Can shareholders enter into an agreement amongst themselves inconsistent with the Articles? Professional practice

Answer spine: Shareholders may make a private agreement among themselves, but a company’s constitutional action must conform to the Act, Memorandum and Articles. A shareholders’ agreement that conflicts with the Articles does not, merely by private consent, authorise the company or its organs to act contrary to the registered constitution. It may create personal obligations between its parties according to ordinary contract law, depending on its terms, but should be incorporated by a valid Article alteration if the parties want it to bind the company in its constitutional capacity or be enforceable as a membership right. Identify whether the company is a party, whether a statutory rule is involved, and whether a § 14 amendment is necessary.

9. The benefit of the doctrine of indoor management is not available under certain circumstances. Comment. Professional practice

Answer spine: Enumerate rather than merely name the rule: no benefit where the outsider knew the irregularity; where surrounding facts create a duty to inquire; where the supposed company act is forged; where the outsider seeks to invoke positively an Article/power it did not know; where there is no possible or apparent authority; where a public constitutional restriction or prescribed execution mode is visibly ignored; or where the transaction is ultra vires/statutorily illegal. Explain the result with cases: Kotla (facial execution defect), Freeman & Lockyer (need company representation and possible authority), and Dehradun-Mussoorie (mandatory external consent). Finish by distinguishing an internal missing resolution, which is ordinarily protected, from these disqualifying defects.

10. “Constructive notice and indoor management are conflicting doctrines.” Examine, including matters not covered by each. Professional practice

Answer spine: Reject the word “conflicting.” They operate on different information. Constructive notice covers registered, public documents: the company’s objects, express external limits, and a visible prescribed mode of execution. Indoor management covers unobservable compliance with internal preconditions: actual passing of a resolution, quorum, notice, minutes, appointment and delegation. The outsider knows what the rule says, not whether it was observed. Neither doctrine covers a lack of corporate capacity or statutory illegality. The strongest concluding sentence is: “Notice fixes the boundary; Turquand protects regularity within it.”

11. Enumerate the exceptions to the doctrine of indoor management. Professional practice

Model list: (1) actual knowledge of the irregularity; (2) suspicious circumstances or negligence creating a duty to inquire; (3) forgery or no genuine company act; (4) no positive reliance on an Article/power that the outsider did not know; (5) absence of possible/apparent authority or a representation by the company; (6) a public restriction or execution defect apparent from the Articles and instrument; and (7) absence of corporate capacity or violation of an independent statute/mandatory condition. For full marks, add a one-line reason and authority to at least three items instead of presenting a bare list. See the exception matrix.

12. Is it legally compulsory for a company to have its own Articles? What restrictions must Articles provide for private-company status? Professional practice

Answer spine: Every company operates under Articles, but a company limited by shares may adopt all or part of the applicable model Articles; if it does not register its own bespoke Articles, the applicable model regulations operate to the extent applicable. Do not confuse “own specially drafted Articles” with “Articles at all.” For a private company, § 2(68) requires constitutional restrictions: transfer of shares must be restricted; membership must be limited to 200 (with statutory exclusions, including prescribed employee categories); and invitation to the public to subscribe to securities must be prohibited. State that statutory conditions and the company’s actual constitutional form matter; an inaccurate label cannot defeat the Act.

13. Explain the rule in Royal British Bank v. Turquand and state its exceptions. Professional practice

Model answer: In Turquand, the company’s deed authorised borrowing subject to approval by a general resolution. The bank was entitled to assume that the internal resolution had been passed. The rule protects an outsider from an unobservable failure to observe internal formalities, while leaving the outsider charged with the constitution’s public content. Its conditions are bona fide dealing, corporate capacity, a power capable of regular exercise, and an officer/organ acting within actual or apparent possible authority. Its main exceptions are actual knowledge, suspicion, forgery, no positive reliance on an unknown Article/power, no genuine/possible authority, a facial public defect, and statutory illegality. Link the rule to Kotla, Freeman & Lockyer and Dehradun-Mussoorie for a complete answer.

Supplementary practical problems — answer templates

Practical 1 · Named solicitor cannot be removed except for misconduct

Answer: The company may ordinarily remove Mr Srivastava despite the Article if he relies only on the Article in the capacity of an outsider/solicitor. Articles do not themselves create an enforceable contract with a stranger merely because they mention or benefit him. This is the Eley v. Positive Government Security Life Assurance Co. point. Check separately for an accepted appointment or another express/implied service contract; that independent contract, not § 10 alone, might create a claim.

Practical 2 · Competing member compelled to transfer shares after alteration

Answer: The member is bound if the Article was validly altered and the compulsory-transfer power is genuinely for the benefit of the company as a whole—for example, safeguarding it against competition by members—and operates on fair terms. The relevant limit is bona fides, statutory compliance and absence of oppressive personal advantage. The fact pattern mirrors the familiar principle that an alteration directed to the company’s welfare can bind the affected member, not merely future entrants.

Practical 3 · Borrowing document with forged supporting director signatures

Answer: Separate the forged instrument from the underlying debt. Forgery ordinarily supplies no genuine company act and cannot be cured through indoor management; a security whose validity depends on forged signatures is therefore highly vulnerable. But do not end there. If the managing director was actually or apparently authorised to borrow, the company held him out, the lender acted bona fide and the money was received/used for the company, the company may still be liable on the underlying borrowing or by agency/estoppel principles. The uploaded Taxmann hint reaches that nuanced company-liability result; plead and analyse the mode of execution separately.

Practical 4 · Bankrupt member’s shares offered to other shareholders

Answer: The provision is generally binding as an incident of membership because a share is held subject to the Articles. Borland’s Trustee v. Steel Brothers & Co. Ltd. supports enforcement of such an Article against the member/trustee. Still test whether the clause was validly adopted, applies according to its terms and is not contrary to statute or vulnerable as an oppressive/fraudulent alteration.

Practical 5 · Director could have been authorised but was not; outsider never saw Articles

Answer: On the stated facts, the company is not bound merely because the director could have been authorised. The rule in Turquand cannot be used positively by someone who did not know of the constitutional power on which the assumption is founded; this is the point of Rama Corporation v. Proved Tin & General Investments Ltd. The cheque’s misappropriation strengthens the need to test actual/apparent authority. A different result is possible only if the company independently held the director out as having authority and the other elements of ostensible authority are proved.

Common traps—and the correction that earns marks

Trap“Every act outside the Articles is ultra vires and void.”
Correction: distinguish company capacity from directors’ authority and internal procedure. An Article-level breach may be ratified or bind through agency/Turquand.
Trap“Turquand means outsiders never need to read Articles.”
Correction: outsiders are fixed with public constitutional limits. Turquand excuses proof of hidden compliance, not reading the rule.
Trap“A Board resolution cures an ultra vires company act.”
Correction: no organ can ratify what the company itself lacks capacity to do. Use Ashbury.
Trap“A benefit to the company is enough for any donation.”
Correction: the benefit must be reasonably proximate/naturally conducive to the authorised objects; remote advantage fails under Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar.
Trap“A named outsider automatically gets an Article-based contract.”
Correction: § 10 protects members in membership capacity. Test for a separate contract; use Eley.
Trap“Forged signatures always end all company liability.”
Correction: forgery normally defeats reliance on the forged instrument, but analyse any separate debt, receipt of money, ratification or genuine apparent-authority representation separately.
Trap“Dehradun-Mussoorie is simply a case where the bank won.”
Correction: bank won on internal-management/debt logic, but lost the security because statutory prior consent was absent; it ranked unsecured.
