What this file does — and why its structure matters

This is not a loose collection of definitions. Topic 3 becomes easy once one question is asked in the right order: Did the company have capacity? If yes, was its internal machinery regularly used? If not, can the outsider nevertheless rely on the company’s representation? The entire topic, including the cases, fits around that sequence.

Part I · Memorandum

Memorandum of Association (MOA): the corporate charter

Statutory definition

“Memorandum” means the memorandum of association of a company as originally framed or as altered from time to time in pursuance of any previous company law or of this Act.Companies Act, 2013, s.2(56)

The definition tells us what document it is. Its juridical significance comes from ss.4, 6, 10 and 13, and from the classic capacity cases.

Working definition for exams

The MOA is the company’s public charter. It identifies the company, marks the field within which corporate action may occur, and states foundational conditions on which incorporation is granted. It is not merely a business plan: it sets a boundary of corporate capacity.

Lord Cairns’ formulation in Ashbury remains memorable: the memorandum defines the limits on the company’s powers, affirmatively and negatively.

Why the memorandum matters

1 · Identity and disclosure

Name, State of registered office, liability and capital/subscription data make the corporate person intelligible to members, investors, creditors and regulators.

2 · Capacity boundary

The objects clause identifies what business/object the company may pursue. An act outside this field is company-level ultra vires.

3 · Allocation of risk

Subscribers see the field in which their capital may be used; outsiders know the public perimeter of a proposed transaction.

Core distinction: a board may exceed its authority while the company remains capable of ratifying the act. But where the company itself lacks capacity under its MOA, it cannot manufacture capacity by a board resolution, articles, consent or unanimous shareholder vote.

Section 4 — formation, form and required content

s.4

What the memorandum must state

At incorporation, the memorandum must state the prescribed name, the State in which the registered office is to be situated, objects to be pursued on incorporation and matters necessary in furtherance of those objects, liability, and (where there is share capital) capital/subscriber information. An OPC must nominate the person who becomes member on death/incapacity, subject to the statutory scheme. It is drawn in the applicable Schedule I form and filed through the incorporation process.

  • Form: Schedule I Tables A–E provide memorandum forms for the different liability/capital structures. They are a drafting starting point, not a licence to disguise unlimited objects in boilerplate.
  • Name reservation: s.4 also addresses unacceptable names — including names identical with/too nearly resembling existing company or registered trade-mark names, prohibited names, and names considered undesirable. The precise application/validity process is subject to current incorporation rules.
  • Execution: subscriber particulars, number of shares agreed to be taken where applicable, signatures and witnessing follow the prescribed incorporation form/manner. The association/subscription undertaking is embodied in the Schedule form.
  • Objects drafting: distinguish the objects to be pursued from matters/powers necessary in their furtherance. A clause such as “to borrow” generally describes a power, not an independent commercial object: Re Introductions.

Part II · Articles

Articles of Association (AOA): the internal constitution

“Articles” means the articles of association of a company as originally framed or as altered from time to time or applied in pursuance of any previous company law or of this Act.Companies Act, 2013, s.2(5)

Articles are the company’s internal regulations. If the MOA says what zone the company may enter, the AOA says who decides, by what procedure, and on what terms within that zone.

Section 5 in one view

Articles contain regulations for management and prescribed matters; a company may include additional management provisions. They may also contain entrenchment provisions — clauses that can be altered only through a procedure more restrictive than the ordinary statutory special-resolution route.

For entrenchment: introduce it at formation, or later by unanimous agreement in a private company / special resolution in a public company, then notify the Registrar in the prescribed manner.

What articles usually regulate

Capital and membership

Classes of shares, calls, liens, transfers/transmission, certificates, forfeiture and member rights.

Meetings and voting

Notice, quorum, proxies, voting, general meetings and resolutions.

Board governance

Appointment, powers, delegation, managing director, conflicts, board meetings and execution of instruments.

Financial administration

Dividends, reserves, accounts, audit, bank operations and use of any seal.

Internal remedies

Share-transfer machinery, arbitration clauses where valid, and relationship-management provisions.

Winding-up architecture

Internal rules consequential on winding up, subject always to the Act and insolvency law.

Registration, effect & statutory contract

What registration changes: a public document becomes a binding constitution

s.10

The statutory contract

Registered memorandum and articles bind the company and its members as if each had been signed by the company and each member, and contained covenants by each member, heirs and legal representatives, to observe every provision. Money payable by a member to the company under the memorandum or articles is a debt due from that member to the company.

Who may enforce an article? Ask: “Is the asserted right held qua member?”

Supporting authorities on binding nature

Wood v Odessa Waterworks (1889)

Articles required dividends in cash. Directors proposed debentures instead. A member secured an injunction: articles bind company to a member qua member.

Borland’s Trustee v Steel Bros (1901)

A compulsory-transfer article on a shareholder’s bankruptcy bound the shareholder’s trustee. A share is held subject to the constitution.

Eley v Positive Government Security (1876)

Articles named Eley as company solicitor. He could not enforce that appointment through the articles: he sued as an outsider/solicitor, not as a member.

Browne v La Trinidad (1887)

An article does not, without more, give an outsider a contractual right against the company merely because it mentions or benefits that person.

Rayfield v Hands (1960)

Articles required directors to take shares offered by a member. The court enforced the member-related transfer obligation; directors were treated within the statutory-membership arrangement.

V.B. Rangaraj v V.B. Gopalakrishnan (SC)

Useful Indian reinforcement: a share-transfer restriction not contained in the articles is generally not enforceable as a company-law restriction.

Phrase to use in an exam: “Section 10 creates a statutory contract, but it is not an all-purpose contract. The claimant must show that the right is asserted qua member, rather than in an independent outsider capacity.”
s.17

Copies on request

On a member’s request and payment of the prescribed fee, the company must send a copy of its memorandum, articles, and applicable resolutions/agreements referred to in s.117 within seven days. Current s.17(2) provides a penalty of ₹1,000 for every day of continuing default, subject to a maximum of ₹1 lakh for the company and every officer in default. This is distinct from Registrar inspection under s.399.

s.14

Alteration of articles

Subject to the Act and conditions in the memorandum, articles are altered by special resolution. An alteration converting a public company into a private company does not take effect without Central Government approval. Under s.14(2), file the altered articles and the approval order, where applicable, with the Registrar within 15 days; once registered, the altered provision is valid as if originally contained in the articles, subject to the Act.

Substantive limits on s.14

Articles cannot conflict with the Act or MOA, cannot authorise illegality, and must be exercised bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole. A section 8 company has its own Central Government approval discipline for constitutional changes.

Classic judicial control

Allen v Gold Reefs of West Africa: alteration must be bona fide for the benefit of the company as a whole. It cannot be a colourable device to oppress a member or confiscate rights arbitrarily.

s.15

Notation on every copy

Every alteration made in the memorandum or articles must be noted in every copy issued after the alteration. The current penalty is ₹1,000 for every copy issued without the alteration, on the company and every officer in default. It is not a per-day penalty.

s.16

Rectification of name on Central Government direction

Where a name is identical with/too nearly resembles an existing company’s name, or on a registered trade-mark proprietor’s application made within three years of incorporation/registration/change of name, the Central Government may direct a change. The company changes its name within three months of the direction after adopting an ordinary resolution, then gives notice of the change/order to the Registrar within 15 days under s.16(2). Continuing default attracts the current daily penalty, capped at ₹1 lakh for the company and each officer in default.

Section-number precision: s.10 creates the statutory contract; s.11 is omitted; s.12 concerns the registered office; s.13 MOA alteration; s.14 AOA alteration; s.15 notation of copies; s.16 rectification of name; s.17 copies for members. This sequence is worth memorising verbatim.

Part III · Corporate capacity

Doctrine of ultra vires: a critical, usable analysis

Ultra vires means “beyond powers.” In company law, the decisive question is: beyond whose power? The legal consequence changes radically depending on whether the Act, the MOA, the AOA, a board delegation, or an external statute has been exceeded.Use this distinction before citing any case
Type of excessMeaningConsequenceIllustration
Ultra vires the Act / external lawThe law itself prohibits the act or makes approval a condition.Void/ineffective; neither articles, estoppel nor Turquand can override law.Dehradun-Mussoorie (external Tramway Order).
Ultra vires MOA / companyThe company lacks corporate capacity for the object/transaction.Void ab initio; no ratification even by all shareholders.Ashbury; Lakshmanaswami; Jon Beauforte.
Ultra vires AOA / boardCompany could do it, but its organ/agent acted outside internal authority.May be ratified by company if otherwise lawful; bona fide outsider may invoke indoor management.Turquand; contrast Kotla.

The core rule

A company can carry out its express objects and acts that are fairly incidental, naturally conducive or reasonably proximate to attainment of those objects. It cannot undertake a wholly foreign enterprise merely because the board expects a possible benefit. A company-level ultra vires transaction is void from the beginning, not merely voidable; it cannot be ratified by unanimous shareholder assent, altered articles, acquiescence, lapse of time or estoppel.

How to construe the objects clause

Express object

A clearly worded, genuinely independent object is effective as written. Cotman rejects cutting it down merely because it is not connected to an assumed dominant business.

Incidental / conducive act

The connection must be reasonably proximate and natural. An indirect, remote possible benefit will not do: Lakshmanaswami.

Power is not object

Borrowing, investing or acquiring property may facilitate valid objects. Calling a power an “independent object” does not make it an end in itself: Re Introductions.

Board-opinion clause

A valid clause may make the board’s bona fide opinion decisive where the activity is in connection with/ancillary to the business: Bell Houses.

New business

Starting a distinct enterprise outside the described field is not made ancillary merely by commercial convenience: Ashbury; Jon Beauforte.

Winding-up substratum is different

Finding a company’s dominant substratum for winding-up is a different inquiry from deciding a third-party ultra vires challenge: Cotman.

Effects and remedial consequences

What cannot happen

  • The company cannot sue or be sued upon the ultra vires contract as such.
  • All shareholders cannot ratify a company incapacity: Ashbury; Lakshmanaswami.
  • Articles cannot enlarge a plain MOA object: Lakshmanaswami.
  • A consent/default judgment that never determines the ultra vires issue does not cure it: Jon Beauforte.

What may still be available

  • A member may seek an injunction before performance.
  • Directors who improperly disburse company assets may have to make good the loss.
  • Identifiable money/property may support tracing or restitutionary arguments; Jon Beauforte expressly reserved such rights.
  • A lender ignorant of an unlawful application may have a different position from one who knows the money is for an ultra vires enterprise: contrast Re David Payne principle with Re Introductions.

Critical analysis: why the doctrine remains examinable

Protective rationale

It protects the investment bargain and creditors’/outsiders’ reliance on a disclosed field of activity; it disciplines managers who would divert company funds to a different venture.

Commercial criticism

Rigid application can defeat honest outsiders and encourages encyclopaedic object clauses. It may turn a public filing fiction into a harsh loss-allocation rule.

Modern Indian position

The 2013 Act still requires objects in s.4 and regulates alteration in s.13. The doctrine is therefore not abolished. Broad but intelligible drafting and prospective alteration reduce, but do not remove, its practical force.

Comparative perspective

UK Companies Act 2006 generally gives unrestricted objects unless articles restrict them and protects third-party validity; Australia and modern US corporate statutes also substantially curtail external ultra vires invalidity. India retains the classic doctrinal relevance for MOA capacity.

High-scoring conclusion: The modern practical question is not whether ultra vires has disappeared, but whether the transaction is truly outside a fairly construed MOA/Act boundary. If it is, internal consent cannot cure a corporate incapacity; if it is merely an internal departure, the analysis shifts to ratification, apparent authority and indoor management.

Part IV · Public documents

Doctrine of constructive notice: the outsider is deemed to know the public limit

Once constitutional documents are registered and accessible, a person dealing with the company is treated as having notice of their public contents, whether or not that person actually read them. It is a legal attribution, not a factual assertion of actual knowledge.

What the doctrine does

  • Charges an outsider with the external contents of MOA/AOA filed with the Registrar.
  • Prevents an outsider from enforcing a transaction facially beyond company capacity or facially contrary to a known signing/authority restriction.
  • Explains why public constitutional documents must be read before reliance is placed on an officer’s act.

What it does not do

  • It does not require outsiders to inspect board minutes, verify notice, quorum, voting or internal approval.
  • It does not make a public document a contract with every outsider.
  • It does not enable a party to rely on an unusual AOA provision they never actually relied upon; see the nuance in Freeman & Lockyer.

Correction to an overbroad note-formula: “Ignorance of the MOA/AOA” is not a free-standing exception that automatically defeats indoor management. The outsider is deemed to know public limits, but need not prove actual inspection of every internal record. The safe exam formulation is: read (or be deemed to know) the public constitution; assume hidden internal regularity only if the act appears constitutionally possible and the outsider acts bona fide.

Kotla Venkataswamy in one line

Where articles required a mortgage instrument to bear the signatures of the managing director, secretary and working director, a mortgage signed by only two officials was facially non-compliant. The lender had constructive notice; it could not invoke Turquand to cure an irregularity apparent from the public document itself.

Part V · Commercial protection

Doctrine of indoor management: the Turquand rule and its limits

A person who reads the public constitution and sees that an act could be completed by an internal resolution or formality is entitled, in good faith, to assume that the internal step was properly taken. The outsider is not required to conduct an audit of the company’s indoor management.Royal British Bank v Turquand

The operating conditions

  1. Corporate capacity exists. The transaction must be within the Act and MOA; Turquand cannot turn an ultra vires corporate act into a valid one.
  2. Public documents permit the kind of act. The constitution must disclose a power that is capable of being completed by an internal approval.
  3. The alleged defect is internal. Missing resolution, defective meeting, quorum or hidden delegation is indoor; a patent signature defect is not.
  4. The outsider is bona fide. No actual knowledge of irregularity and no circumstances that reasonably demand inquiry.
  5. No forgery or external legal prohibition. A forged instrument is not a mere irregular exercise of a genuine power; a statute outside the constitution cannot be waived by apparent regularity.

Exceptions / boundaries

BoundaryWhy protection failsAuthority / illustration
Actual knowledgeA person who knows the internal resolution/appointment is defective cannot claim an assumption opposite to the truth.General rule; compare bank knowledge in Re Introductions.
Suspicion / abnormal transactionThe doctrine protects good faith, not a person who deliberately shuts eyes to an obvious need for inquiry.Facts must support suspicion; it is not a vague “negligence” label.
ForgeryForgery is a nullity, not merely an irregular use of a valid corporate power.Ruben v Great Fingall Consolidated (supporting authority).
Facial violation of AOAConstructive notice already reveals that the required outward form was not met.Kotla.

Turquand and apparent authority are related but not identical

Turquand question

“The public document permits directors/board to act if an internal resolution exists. May the outsider assume it exists?” This is an internal regularity question.

Freeman question

“Did the company, through someone with actual authority, represent that this officer had authority, and did the outsider rely on it?” This is an ostensible authority / estoppel question.

One-sentence conclusion: Turquand is a shield for a bona fide outsider against hidden procedural irregularity; it is not a magic wand against public constitutional limits, forgery, an officer’s self-created authority, knowledge of irregularity, or external law.

Case 1 · corporate ultra vires

Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche

[1875] LR 7 HL 653; [1874–80] All ER Rep 2219 (HL)
DU primary case materialMOA objectsno ratification

Material facts

The company’s memorandum authorised it to make, sell or hire railway carriages, wagons, railway plant and rolling stock; to carry on the business of mechanical engineers and general contractors; and to deal with specified mines, minerals, land and materials. Riche had obtained a Belgian Government concession for a railway from Antwerp to Tournay. The directors contracted to take over the concession, establish a société anonyme, raise and contribute construction capital, take bonds/shares, and give Riche the supply business for iron and rolling stock.

When finance trouble arose, an investigating committee reported that the arrangement was ultra vires. Shareholders later allowed accounts to pass and made arrangements under which directors would bear the burden between themselves and shareholders. Riche nevertheless sued the company for non-performance/damages.

Issues

  • Did “mechanical engineers and general contractors” authorise taking over/funding a foreign railway construction concession?
  • If not, could shareholder assent or later arrangements ratify the company’s contract?

Riche / contractor

“General contractors” should be read broadly enough to cover the Belgian railway arrangement. The related opportunity to supply rolling stock showed commercial connection. In any event, shareholder acts and later arrangements adopted or ratified the bargain.

Company / liquidator position

“General contractors” takes colour from “mechanical engineers”; it means contracts of the mechanical-engineering business, not every imaginable commercial undertaking. Acquisition and financing of a foreign railway company was outside the stated field. An act the corporation cannot do cannot be ratified.

Decision & reasoning

The House of Lords held the contract ultra vires and void. Lord Cairns construed “general contractors” in its immediate context: an unlimited reading would make the specification of objects meaningless. The real substance was not making railway equipment but purchasing a concession and finding capital for a foreign railway company. The memorandum was the company’s fundamental law; the company existed for its stated objects, not with unlimited common-law trading capacity. The later deed/arrangements could not validate a corporate act beyond capacity. Riche could not recover contractual damages from the company.

Principle evolved

Company-level ultra vires is absolutely void and incapable of ratification even by unanimous shareholder consent. Distinguish this from an act merely beyond directors’ authority: the latter may be internally cured if the company had capacity; the former cannot.

Use when: a fact pattern asks whether an ostensibly broad phrase authorises a distinct new business, or whether a shareholder resolution can cure an act outside the MOA. First line: “On Ashbury, I must distinguish the company’s capacity from a director’s authority.”

Case 2 · independent objects

Cotman v. Brougham

[1918] AC 514 (HL); [1918–19] All ER Rep 265
DU primary case materialobjects constructionintra vires

Material facts

Essequibo Rubber & Tobacco Estates Ltd. had a sprawling objects clause with 30 heads. Though one early head concerned foreign property development, later heads expressly authorised promotion of companies and dealing in their shares/securities. A concluding clause declared every sub-clause independent: none was to be limited by the first object, another sub-clause or the company’s name.

The company sub-underwrote 20,000 shares in Anglo-Cuban Oil, Bitumen and Asphalt Co. and took 17,200 shares. After the latter company’s liquidation, Essequibo was placed on the contributories list for unpaid calls of roughly £14,000. Its liquidator sought removal, arguing the underwriting was ultra vires.

Issues

  • Could an unrelated share-underwriting transaction be cut down by a supposed main object?
  • Could validity of the accepted memorandum be attacked after incorporation?

Liquidator / appellant

The real business was rubber/tobacco/property; stock underwriting in an unrelated oil company was not ancillary. An indiscriminate list of “objects” failed the statutory purpose of identifying a field of industry and should not defeat the main-object approach.

Respondent position

Clauses authorising promotion and acquisition/dealing in shares were express and clear. The independence clause prevented their being read as merely auxiliary to a first head. The certificate of incorporation foreclosed a collateral attack on the registered memorandum’s validity; the court must construe its text as it stands.

Decision & reasoning

The House of Lords dismissed the appeal: the underwriting was intra vires. Clauses 3(8) and 3(12), read with the independence declaration, plainly covered it. The certificate of incorporation was conclusive as to registration requirements in the procedural posture of the case; the court could not re-open whether the Registrar should have accepted the memorandum. Lord Parker separated the question whether a transaction is ultra vires from the equitable/substratum question relevant to winding up.

Several judges criticised the drafting practice of burying the real business beneath a mass of clauses. That criticism did not allow the court to rewrite an unambiguous accepted memorandum.

Principle evolved

A genuinely independent, expressly authorised object operates independently. For a transaction challenge, do not invent a dominant object to cut down clear words. The “main object”/substratum inquiry may matter in another context, but not where the MOA’s express independent object authorises the act.

Reading alert: a shorthand/headnote line in one course extract appears to say “so the transaction was ultra vires.” The report’s holding and appeal dismissal are the opposite: the underwriting was intra vires. Write the actual ratio.

Use when: a company’s MOA has clear, independently drafted object clauses and someone argues that an act is invalid merely because it is unrelated to a “main” activity or the company’s name.

Case 3 · ultra vires debts and judgments

In re Jon Beauforte (London) Ltd.

[1953] 1 Ch 131
DU primary case materialconstructive noticetracing reserved

Material facts

The company was authorised to carry on the business of costumiers, gown/dress makers and related businesses, together with business which in the board’s opinion could be advantageously carried on in connection with or ancillary to that business. It decided to manufacture veneered panels — admittedly ultra vires — and erected a factory near Bristol.

After compulsory liquidation, three representative claims were rejected: builders who constructed the factory claimed £2,078 and had obtained a consent/default mechanism; veneer suppliers claimed £1,011 after a default judgment; and a coke supplier claimed £107, saying fuel might have been used legitimately. None claimed actual knowledge of the ultra vires position. The coke correspondence, however, was on company paper headed “Veneered Panel Manufacturers.”

Issues

  • Can suppliers prove debts arising from an admittedly ultra vires business?
  • Do default/consent judgments, absence of actual knowledge, acquiescence or estoppel cure the defect?
  • What residual remedies remain?

Creditors

They supplied value without actual knowledge; the company visibly carried on the veneer trade. The existing judgments or compromise should bind the liquidator. The coke might have had a legitimate use and should not be irretrievably characterised as ultra vires.

Liquidator

The MOA gave constructive notice of the company’s limited objects. These particular factory contracts were admittedly ultra vires. A company cannot make an invalid contract valid by consenting to judgment or failing to raise the defence; the coke supplier had clear notice of the factory’s true purpose.

Decision & reasoning

The applications were dismissed and proofs rejected. Roxburgh J held that a judgment founded on an ultra vires contract cannot stand merely because the company consented, defaulted or chose not to plead the defect. It is sustainable only if it embodies an actual judicial decision on the ultra vires issue or a compromise of that issue. The builders’ compromise proceeded on the premise that the contract was intra vires, and the veneer judgment never determined capacity. The coke supplier’s headed correspondence gave clear notice of the veneer factory purpose.

The order expressly preserved possible rights to trace money/property into an asset or proceeds, and to participate in any residual surplus after proper provable creditors and liquidation expenses were satisfied.

Principle evolved

Ultra vires cannot be cured by estoppel, lapse, ratification, acquiescence, default or a consent judgment that has not actually resolved the capacity issue. Yet invalidity of the contractual proof does not erase every possible proprietary/restitutionary remedy.

Use when: a supplier says “I did not know,” a creditor has a default judgment, or an ultra vires company has gone into liquidation. Add the remedial nuance: contract proof fails, but tracing may remain open.

Case 4 · ancillary business and board opinion

Bell Houses Ltd. v. City Wall Properties Ltd.

[1966] 2 All ER 674
DU primary case materialincidental objectintra vires

Material facts

Bell Houses’ actual business was property/housing-estate development. Its chairman, Mr Bell, effectively administered that business pursuant to board delegation. The company’s memorandum included a clause allowing any other trade or business which, in the opinion of the board, could advantageously be carried on in connection with or as ancillary to its businesses/general business.

Knowledge of finance sources was commercially valuable to Bell Houses because its developments needed bridging finance. When City Wall needed approximately £1 million bridging finance, Bell introduced it to a financing source (Nestlé’s Pension Trust). Bell Houses claimed an agreed £20,000 procuration/commission fee. City Wall resisted, characterising the transaction as ultra vires mortgage broking. At first instance the preliminary ultra vires point succeeded.

Issues

  • Was the introduction-for-finance transaction a new unrelated business or sufficiently connected/ancillary to property development?
  • What effect should be given to the bona fide opinion-of-board clause?

Bell Houses

This was an isolated commercial use of a valuable finance connection, not a launch into general mortgage broking. It produced commission, goodwill with financier and customer, and valuable knowledge for its own development operations. Mr Bell acted for the company, not personally, and the clause made bona fide board opinion decisive.

City Wall

Facilitating finance for a third party was distinct mortgage broking, not property development. A director’s subjective opinion could not by itself create corporate capacity; it had to be objectively and genuinely ancillary. The benefit, if any, was too indirect.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Appeal allowed Bell Houses’ appeal. The transaction was within the company’s powers. It was an isolated, commercially connected use of information/assets acquired in the ordinary property-development business, rather than a deliberate entry into a wholly new trade. The clause expressly asked whether, in the board’s bona fide opinion, the activity could advantageously be carried on in connection with/ancillary to the business. Mr Bell’s authorised management and the facts supported that opinion. The court therefore did not need to decide wider questions about whether City Wall could raise ultra vires as a defence.

Principle evolved

Not every transaction outside the company’s routine activity is ultra vires. A commercially connected, ancillary act can be intra vires; a valid clause may make directors’ bona fide opinion material or decisive. The case is not authority for a free-standing power to begin any new business.

Use when: a company undertakes an isolated side transaction linked to its existing business and the MOA contains “in connection with,” “ancillary,” “incidental,” or bona fide board-opinion language. Contrast Ashbury and Jon Beauforte.

Case 5 · borrowing power is not an object

Re Introductions Ltd. / Introductions Ltd. v. National Provincial Bank Ltd.

[1969] 1 All ER 887
DU primary case materialbank securityknowledge of purpose

Material facts

The company began in connection with the Festival of Britain, later operated a deck-chair business, then had a period of inactivity. A new board used it for pig breeding. That business was admittedly outside the MOA. The bank opened an account, allowed an overdraft, and took two debentures secured on company assets. Crucially, before taking security it had a copy of the MOA/AOA and express knowledge that pig breeding was the company’s sole activity.

The MOA contained a general clause authorising borrowing/raising money, including through debentures and charges. It also said prior sub-clauses were to be construed independently as objects. In liquidation, the liquidator challenged the debentures.

Issues

  • Can a general power to borrow and give security stand as an independent object?
  • Does the bank’s knowledge that borrowing would finance ultra vires pig breeding invalidate its security?

Bank

It needed only to see an express borrowing/debenture clause. The independence declaration “elevated” borrowing into an object. Following Cotman, it need not investigate equities or the use of money despite knowledge of the business.

Liquidator

Borrowing is a means, not an end; it must be for a lawful company purpose. Labels cannot transform an ancillary power into an autonomous corporate object. The bank was not an innocent lender — it knew the sole destination was ultra vires pig breeding.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Appeal held the debentures void against the liquidator. The underlying pig-breeding activity was ultra vires and there was no appeal on that point. A borrowing clause cannot “mean something in the air”: it necessarily means borrowing for purposes of the company. The independence wording did not change the true character of a power into an object. A lender who does not know the unlawful intended use need not ordinarily inquire; but this bank did know and could not rely on its debentures.

Principle evolved

Power ≠ object. A general borrowing/security power is impliedly confined to intra vires company purposes. The innocent lender principle does not protect a lender with actual knowledge that money/security supports an ultra vires business.

Use when: a lender relies on a broad borrowing clause while knowing the money will fund an unauthorised activity. Contrast an unknown internal misapplication with known ultra vires purpose.

Case 6 · Indian Supreme Court authority

A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar v. Life Insurance Corporation of India

AIR 1963 SC 1185
Indian authorityDU primary case material (2022 full report)articles cannot enlarge MOA

Material facts and timeline

United India Life Assurance Co. Ltd. was incorporated to carry on life-insurance business in all branches, indemnity and guarantee business, investments and matters incidental/conducive to those objects. At an extraordinary general meeting on 15 July 1955, shareholders resolved to donate ₹2 lakh from the Shareholders’ Dividend Account to the proposed M.Ct.M. Chidambaram Chettyar Memorial Trust. The trust’s numerous charitable objects included scholarships, education, art/science/industrial/technical/business knowledge, including banking and insurance.

Directors paid ₹5,000 initially and ₹1,95,000 in December 1955. On statutory vesting of insurers’ controlled business in LIC, LIC demanded repayment and sought relief under s.15 of the Life Insurance Corporation Act, 1956. Directors/trustees argued that article 93(t) authorised charitable/public/general/useful payments and that the dividend account belonged beneficially to shareholders, not the company.

Issues

  • Was the ₹2 lakh donation within the company’s objects or naturally incidental/conducive to life-insurance business?
  • Could article 93(t) or shareholder control over the dividend account enlarge the MOA?
  • Were directors/trustees personally answerable for repayment?

LIC / Corporation

The MOA did not authorise this donation. The trust was broadly charitable, with no obligation to spend any part on insurance education; any benefit to insurance staffing/business was remote. The meeting was a company meeting, not a personal distribution by individual owners of a separate fund. Article 93(t) was subordinate to the MOA. Under the LIC Act, recovery could be ordered from parties responsible for or benefited by an imprudent/non-consideration payment.

Directors / trustees / appellants

Article 93(t) expressly empowered charitable, benevolent, public and useful payments. The trust included insurance knowledge and therefore could ultimately benefit the company. The Shareholders’ Dividend Account was said to be exclusively shareholders’ property, held by the company for them, so unanimous shareholder resolution could direct its destination. Trustees also denied personal liability.

Decision & reasoning

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal. The meeting was a meeting of the company convened to approve a company donation. The MOA was fairly construed: its investment clause was a power to invest/deal with company funds, not a free charitable object. Article 93(t) could operate only within MOA capacity; articles may assist where a memorandum is ambiguous or silent, but cannot extend its plain scope.

The test for an incidental/conducive act requires a reasonably proximate connection. Here the trustees had no obligation to use the money for insurance education, and even such education would not necessarily benefit the company. The hypothesised gain was too indirect and remote. The donation was therefore ultra vires, absolutely void and incapable of ratification even by all shareholders. Directors responsible for the wrongful disbursement had to make good the company’s money. Trustees, as beneficiaries/representatives of the trust, could be ordered to repay depending on responsibility, benefit and notice; the fund had not been disposed of before LIC’s demand.

Principle evolved

Articles cannot enlarge an unambiguous memorandum; a remote possible commercial benefit is not “incidental or conducive.” A company donation outside objects is void despite shareholder approval. The case also grounds personal accountability for directors who cause unauthorised disbursement.

Use when: a company makes a charity/CSR-like payment under a broad AOA clause, or argues an indirect reputational/training/market benefit. State the reasonably proximate connection test and distinguish statutory CSR compliance from an unauthorised historical donation.

Case 7 · origin of indoor management

Royal British Bank v. Turquand

(1856) 6 E & B 327; [1843–60] All ER Rep 435
DU primary case materialindoor managementbond

Material facts

The Cameron’s Coalbrook Steam, Coal, Swansea and London Railway Co. gave the Royal British Bank a company-sealed bond. Its registered deed of settlement allowed directors to borrow on bond, but only such sums as were authorised from time to time by a general resolution of the company. The company, through its official manager, pleaded that no such shareholder resolution had authorised the bond.

The bank’s case was that it had taken the instrument in good faith. The public deed showed not an outright prohibition on borrowing, but a power that could be completed by the required internal resolution.

Issue

Must a lender verify, beyond the public constitutional document, that the required general meeting resolution was in fact passed before relying on directors’ sealed bond?

Bank

The deed gave directors power to borrow if authorised internally. Once the public document showed that the authority could be completed by a general resolution, an external lender could assume the internal condition had been fulfilled. It was not required to inspect the company’s internal proceedings.

Company / official manager

Companies have only statutory/deed powers, unlike ordinary partners. The public deed made the general resolution a precondition; the bank was charged with its contents, and no such resolution meant the bond lacked corporate authority.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Exchequer Chamber affirmed judgment for the bank. Jervis CJ reasoned that parties dealing with registered companies must read the statute and deed of settlement, but need do no more. On reading the deed, the bank found permission to borrow subject to a resolution; it was entitled to infer that the resolution authorising an apparently legitimate bond had been passed. The reported pleadings also contained a broad general-resolution allegation, but the enduring rule does not depend on auditing its factual correctness.

Principle evolved

The Turquand rule: an outsider may assume that internal acts and formalities required to make an externally permitted transaction effective have been duly performed. It is the counterweight to constructive notice.

Use when: an AOA/deed says “directors may borrow/act with a resolution/approval,” but the alleged missing step is inside the company. Then immediately test good faith, facial regularity, capacity and external prohibition.

Case 8 · apparent authority

Freeman & Lockyer (a firm) v. Buckhurst Park Properties (Mangal) Ltd.

[1964] 1 All ER 630
DU primary case materialostensible authorityfour-part test

Material facts

Buckhurst Park Properties owned an estate bought for profitable resale. Kapoor, a director, was never formally appointed managing director; there was no board resolution authorising him to engage the claimant architects/surveyors. Yet the board knew he was acting as managing director, conducting the estate’s business, employing agents and seeking planning permission/sale. The articles gave the board management power and allowed appointment/delegation to a managing director/single director.

In 1959 Kapoor instructed Freeman & Lockyer to do planning and surveying work on the company’s property. The work was done and the £291 6s fee was undisputed. The company contended Kapoor had no authority and that correspondence suggested he was acting personally.

Issues

  • Did Kapoor have actual authority? If not, did he have apparent/ostensible authority?
  • When does a corporate representation estop a company from denying an agent’s authority?

Architects

Kapoor was actually authorised by inference from the facts; alternatively, the board knowingly allowed him to act as managing director and thereby held him out. Employing professionals for planning/sale was within ordinary apparent authority, and the architects relied on that representation in contracting.

Company

No formal managing-director appointment, specific resolution or valid delegation existed; Kapoor was only a director. The architects’ correspondence referred to him personally, they did not prove reliance on a company representation, and the board had not authorised the particular contract.

Decision & reasoning

The Court of Appeal held the company liable. Actual authority failed: there was no appointment or resolution. But the board, which had actual management authority, knew and permitted Kapoor to act as managing director in the relevant part of the company’s business. That conduct represented to outsiders that he had the ordinary authority of that role. The architects were induced to believe and rely on it; obtaining planning permission and professional services was ordinary for disposal/development of the company property. The articles did not make the company incapable of appointing/delegating in this way.

Diplock LJ’s four conditions

  1. A representation that the agent had authority to enter the relevant kind of contract.
  2. The representation was made by a person/body with actual authority to manage that part of the company’s business (usually the board).
  3. The contractor was induced by and relied on the representation in entering the contract.
  4. The company had capacity to enter the transaction and capacity to delegate/hold out that authority.

Apparent authority is an estoppel between principal and contractor. It cannot cure an act the company itself could not do, or an authority the constitution makes incapable of delegation.

Use when: an officer was never formally appointed but has been allowed by the board to act in a role. Apply all four conditions separately; do not merely write “indoor management applies.”

Case 9 · patent irregularity

Kotla Venkataswamy v. Chinta Ramamurthy

AIR 1934 Mad 579
Indian authorityDU primary case materialconstructive notice

Material facts

A mortgage bond for ₹1,000 was purportedly executed for South Indian Agricultural and Industrial Improvement Co. Ltd. in favour of Venkatamma, whose interest was assigned to the plaintiff. The company later went into voluntary liquidation. Article 15 provided that all deeds, hundies, cheques, certificates and other instruments had to be signed by the managing director, secretary and working director on behalf of the company.

The mortgage was signed only by the secretary and working director. The plaintiff asserted regular authority/special resolutions and later sought to rely on ratification and equitable charge arguments. The managing director was said to have been unavailable, but that did not change the article.

Issue

Can a mortgagee enforce an instrument whose face reveals non-compliance with the AOA’s required three-signature execution rule, by invoking Turquand/good faith?

Mortgagee / plaintiff

The loan was regularly contracted or specially authorised; at minimum the company should be bound because the officers appeared authorised, the lender acted in good faith and money may have benefited the company. Later ratification/equity should assist.

Company / purchaser

The AOA was public and clearly required three named signatories. The two officers were not competent alone. The lender was deemed to know that defect, so this was not a hidden internal irregularity. Alternative factual claims were not properly tried/proved.

Decision & reasoning

The Madras High Court held the mortgage invalid and dismissed the appeal. Article 15 was not merely a formal signing rule detached from power; absent special proof, execution authority remained with the board/constitution. If the mortgagee had informed herself of the articles, she would have seen that the managing director’s signature was missing. Unlike Turquand, illegality/non-compliance appeared on the face of the outward instrument when read against the public articles. Good faith and possible application of money to company purposes did not make the mortgage enforceable on the pleaded issue.

Principle evolved

Indoor management does not cure a patent irregularity disclosed by public constitutional documents. Constructive notice defeats the outsider where the outward execution itself falls short of a known mandatory article.

Use when: articles specify a number/name of signatories and the document visibly bears fewer/different signatures. Contrast Turquand: missing unseen resolution versus Kotla: visible outward defect.

Case 10 · the boundary of Turquand

Dehradun-Mussoorie Electric Tramway Co. v. Jagmandar Das & others

AIR 1932 All 141
Indian authorityDU primary case materialexternal statute prevails

Material facts

The company obtained an emergency overdraft of ₹25,000 from Bhagwan Das & Co., Bankers, through managing agent Bertie Shah, to pay for machinery/stores urgently needed for the tramway. The money was admittedly used for necessary company purposes. A mortgage dated 19 June 1923 over Khazanchi Bagh land secured the overdraft. Articles gave the board power to borrow/mortgage but prohibited delegation of borrowing power; the managing agent had broad management/contract powers.

A minute book contained a 2 June resolution authorising the security, but the court found there had been no properly convened board meeting passing it. The bank manager had seen the apparently signed minute and had no reason at the time to suspect fraud. Later directors’ reports acknowledged the debt/security. Separately, clause 37 of the 1921 Tramway Order — having force of law — required prior Government assent for transfer/mortgage of the tramway “undertaking.” No prior assent was obtained for this mortgage.

Issues

  • Company-law issue: did the agent/board/mortgage bind the company despite internal irregularity?
  • External-law issue: was the land part of the statutory “undertaking,” making Government assent indispensable?
  • If security failed, what was the bank’s remedy?

Bank / respondents

The emergency justified a temporary loan under ordinary agency principles; directors later recognised/ratified the loan. The board could authorise execution of security for an already incurred debt. A seal defect was immaterial if a sealed deed was not legally necessary. The bank was bona fide, saw a minute-book resolution, and could assume internal regularity. It also argued absence of proof of required Government sanction and/or that the land was not within the undertaking.

Company / liquidators

The non-delegation article barred the agent from borrowing; the June resolution was bogus/improperly convened and the document execution defective. The bank’s letters put it on notice. More fundamentally, no prior Government approval was obtained as clause 37 required, and the depot/car-shed land was suitable for and used for the tramway undertaking; the mortgage was therefore void.

Decision & reasoning — two layers

Layer 1: company law. The Allahabad High Court found that the emergency could authorise temporary accommodation to protect company interests; later reports also recognised the loan. The board could delegate execution of a mortgage securing an existing loan even though it could not delegate a general power to borrow. The June board meeting had not been properly convened, but the bank had no actual knowledge or reason to suspect that defect. It was protected by the indoor-management principle, and the mortgage was binding so far as company law was concerned.

Layer 2: external statute — the ultimate result. The company had validly become the promoter after earlier Government assent to transfer the concession, but the mortgaged land was part of the “undertaking”: it was acquired for depot/office/car-shed purposes, was suitable, and was used to store sleepers. No previous Government assent to mortgage it existed. Clause 37 had force of law. Mortgage without required prior assent was absolutely void, not merely voidable; it could not be ratified and estoppel could not validate it.

Principle evolved and remedy

Turquand cures hidden internal irregularity, not breach of an external statutory prohibition. The bank lost its security but not its underlying money claim. The court substituted a simple money decree of ₹29,773-4-3 against the liquidators, to rank as an unsecured debt in liquidation (with the stated interest treatment).

Important correction: a short-note version may stop after saying indoor management protected the bank. The DU report’s final holding is more nuanced: it protected the bank on the company-law question, but the mortgage was ultimately void under the external Tramway Order. This is the best case to show the outer boundary of Turquand.

Use when: an outsider wins on internal authority but an external statute/licence/order requires prior approval. Always run a second, separate statutory-prohibition inquiry.

Official DU PYQ · Dec. 2015 · Q.2

“Explain the doctrine of ultra vires with reference to Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche and other cases. Compare current scenario of it in Indian context & other countries?”

The paper instructed candidates to attempt five questions carrying equal marks (100 marks / five questions).

Best model answer — deploy as a 20-mark answer

Open / close the model answer

Introduction. The doctrine of ultra vires means that a company cannot validly act outside the powers conferred by the statute and its memorandum of association. “Ultra” means beyond and “vires” means powers. Its central purpose is to protect members, creditors and persons dealing with a limited-liability corporation by confining corporate funds to the objects publicly stated at incorporation. The doctrinal question must, however, be framed accurately: an act may be ultra vires the Act, the memorandum/company, the articles, or merely the directors. Only the second is the classic corporate ultra vires rule.

Nature of the rule. Under the Companies Act, 2013, s.4 requires the memorandum to state the objects pursued at incorporation and matters necessary for their furtherance. A company may carry out express objects and acts naturally/ reasonably incidental or conducive to them. But where an act is beyond the company’s MOA capacity, it is void ab initio. It cannot be ratified by directors, by articles, or even by unanimous shareholder consent. An internal excess of director authority is different: if the company itself had capacity, shareholders may ratify and a bona fide outsider may sometimes invoke indoor management.

Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. v. Riche. This is the leading authority. The company’s MOA authorised railway carriages/rolling stock, mechanical engineering and general contracting. Its directors agreed to acquire and finance a Belgian railway concession, establish a foreign company and take its shares/bonds, with Riche to receive the equipment supply business. Riche argued that “general contractors” was wide enough and that shareholder conduct later ratified the bargain. The company replied that the words had to be read with “mechanical engineers” and did not authorise an unlimited foreign railway-financing business. The House of Lords accepted the latter position. Lord Cairns held that the memorandum was the fundamental law of the corporation; the Belgian railway arrangement was outside it. The contract was void and no shareholder assent could validate it. The case establishes the difference between an original limit on corporate capacity and a merely unauthorised act of directors.

Later applications and qualifications. In re Jon Beauforte (London) Ltd. applies the strict rule to a costumier/gown-maker company that ran a veneered-panel factory. Builders and suppliers could not prove their contract debts in liquidation, even though some had no actual knowledge and judgments had been obtained; consent, default, estoppel and lapse did not cure company ultra vires. The court nevertheless reserved possible tracing and residual-surplus rights. A. Lakshmanaswami Mudaliar v LIC is the important Indian authority. An insurance company’s ₹2 lakh donation to a broad charitable trust was ultra vires: a possible remote insurance-education benefit was not reasonably proximate to the insurance objects, and an AOA clause authorising charity could not enlarge the MOA. Responsible directors had to make good the funds.

The doctrine is not applied mechanically. Cotman v Brougham shows that plainly worded, independently drafted object clauses must be given effect: underwriting/acquiring shares was intra vires because expressly authorised, even though unrelated to a supposed main business. Bell Houses v City Wall Properties similarly upheld an isolated finance-introduction commission as connected/ancillary to a property developer’s business under a bona fide board-opinion clause. In contrast, Re Introductions held that a general borrowing/debenture clause is a power, not a free-standing object. A bank that knew its security funded ultra vires pig breeding could not enforce the debentures.

Effects. A company-level ultra vires agreement is unenforceable as a contract. A member may restrain a threatened act by injunction. Directors who misapply company money may be personally liable to restore it, as in Lakshmanaswami. Identifiable property/money may support restitutionary/tracing relief. A lender without knowledge of an improper application may have a different result from a lender with actual knowledge; the distinction in Re Introductions is therefore vital.

Present Indian position. India has not abolished the significance of corporate objects. The 2013 Act continues to require objects in s.4; s.13 permits prospective alteration by special resolution and, where public-prospectus money is unutilised, imposes additional disclosures and dissentient exit protection. No s.13 alteration takes effect until registration. Broad, carefully drafted objects and an available alteration process may reduce the frequency of classical disputes, but they do not retrospectively validate an already ultra vires transaction. Sections 5–6 and 10 also preserve the hierarchy: articles are subordinate to the Act/MOA and cannot enlarge corporate capacity.

Comparison with other jurisdictions. The traditional English rule originated in Ashbury, but modern UK law has largely removed its external sting: under the Companies Act 2006, a company’s objects are unrestricted unless its articles specifically restrict them, and third-party validity is protected even where constitutional capacity is exceeded. Australian corporations law and modern US model corporate statutes likewise broadly confer natural-person capacity and largely confine ultra vires objections to internal/shareholder or state proceedings. India remains closer to the classical memorandum-based approach, although practical drafting and statutory alteration have softened rigidity.

Conclusion. Ultra vires continues to matter in India as a discipline of corporate capacity and investor/creditor protection. The correct answer is not that every unusual act is void: construe the MOA fairly, recognise genuine independent objects and legitimate incidental acts, but deny validity where a company is asked to enter a wholly foreign enterprise or deploy funds for a remote/non-authorised purpose. That is the enduring lesson of Ashbury and Lakshmanaswami.

Official DU PYQ · Dec. 2015 · Q.3(a)

“Define the doctrine of ‘Indoor management’ in reference to rules laid down in Royal British Bank v. Turquand?”

Part (a) of Question 3. The companion part asked about public and private companies; the answer below is confined to Topic 3(a).

Best model answer

Open / close the model answer

Meaning and rationale. The doctrine of indoor management, known as the Turquand rule, protects a bona fide outsider dealing with a company from hidden irregularities in its internal management. It is the equitable/commercial counterweight to constructive notice. Constructive notice deems an outsider to know the registered MOA and AOA; indoor management says that, after respecting those public limits, the outsider need not inspect minutes, check quorum, verify notice, or ensure that an internally required resolution was actually passed.

Royal British Bank v Turquand. The company’s registered deed allowed directors to borrow on bond only to the extent authorised by a resolution at a general meeting. Directors gave the bank a company-sealed bond, and the company later pleaded that no required general resolution existed. The bank argued that the public deed showed borrowing was permitted if internally authorised; it was entitled to assume that the internal condition had been performed. Jervis CJ held for the bank. A person dealing with the company must read the statute and constitutional document, “but is not bound to do more.” The outsider could infer the existence of a resolution authorising an act that appeared legitimate on the face of the public constitution.

Conditions for application. First, the transaction must be within the company’s capacity under the Act and MOA. Secondly, the public documents must make the transaction facially possible; the missing matter must be an internal formality such as a resolution, quorum or proper appointment. Thirdly, the outsider must act in good faith, without actual knowledge of irregularity and without suspicious circumstances calling for inquiry. Finally, there must be no forgery and no external statutory prohibition.

Important limits. In Kotla Venkataswamy v Chinta Ramamurthy, articles required the managing director, secretary and working director all to sign an instrument. A mortgage bore only two signatures. The lender had constructive notice of this patent public-document defect and could not use Turquand. Thus the rule protects against an unseen missing resolution, not visible non-compliance with a required outward form. In Freeman & Lockyer v Buckhurst Park Properties, the board knowingly allowed Kapoor to act as managing director though he was never formally appointed. The company was bound because it had held him out; Diplock LJ’s apparent-authority analysis requires a representation by an actually empowered organ, reliance by the outsider, and company capacity to transact/delegate. An agent cannot create authority by their own assertion.

The strongest boundary is Dehradun-Mussoorie Electric Tramway Co. v Jagmandar Das. The bank was protected on the company-law issue despite an irregular board meeting because it acted bona fide and had no reason to suspect the internal defect. Yet the mortgage ultimately failed because an external Tramway Order required prior Government approval to mortgage the undertaking, and none existed. Indoor management cannot override a statute or validate an act ultra vires the company/external law.

Conclusion. The rule promotes commercial certainty: outsiders need not become company auditors. But it is deliberately narrow. They are deemed to know public constitutional limits and lose protection where there is actual knowledge, suspicion, forgery, a patent AOA breach, absence of a company representation, company-level ultra vires, or external statutory illegality. Therefore, the correct formulation is: public limits must be respected; hidden internal regularity may be presumed.

Documented topic only · Dec. 2024 checklist

“Question 3: Doctrine of Indoor Management and Board Resolutions.”

The supplied checklist identifies this title and the associated concepts/cases, but does not reproduce the full original question. The answer below is a model answer to the documented issue, not a fabricated verbatim PYQ.

Model response to the documented board-resolution issue

Open / close the model answer

Where a company’s articles permit a transaction only after a board or general-meeting resolution, the answer should begin by separating capacity from regularity. If the Act and MOA permit the underlying transaction, a missing resolution is generally an indoor matter. Under Royal British Bank v Turquand, a bona fide financier may assume the resolution was properly passed; it need not inspect minute books or test quorum/notice. Therefore, an ABC Finance-type outsider can argue that it relied on a transaction which the constitution made facially possible and that the company should bear the risk of its internal failure.

However, that conclusion is conditional. If the document itself discloses a required execution form that was not met — for example articles require three signatures and only two appear — Kotla denies protection because constructive notice reveals the defect. If the officer was not formally appointed but the actual board knowingly allowed the officer to act as managing director, apply Freeman & Lockyer: ask whether an authorised corporate organ represented authority, whether the outsider relied, and whether the company had capacity to transact/delegate. If the financier knew the resolution was not passed or facts were so unusual as to require inquiry, good faith fails. Finally, no board resolution or ostensible authority can cure company-level ultra vires or an external statutory condition: Dehradun-Mussoorie is decisive on that boundary.

Conclusion for a problem: “ABC Finance succeeds only if the transaction was within corporate capacity, outwardly regular under public documents, and it acted bona fide without knowledge/suspicion. The board-resolution defect is then indoor and presumed regular. Otherwise state the exact exception and deny protection.”

Mini applications

Problem A — a charity payment under broad articles

Facts: An education-technology company’s MOA is to develop and sell educational software and things incidental. Articles allow directors to contribute to charitable and public objects. Directors donate substantial funds to a general arts trust, arguing better art education may improve the company’s brand.

Answer: Start with MOA capacity, not AOA permission. The general trust has no obligation to use funds for the company’s educational-software objectives; the reputational benefit is remote. Applying Lakshmanaswami, the article cannot enlarge the MOA and a remote benefit is not naturally conducive. The donation is likely company-level ultra vires, void and not ratifiable merely by shareholder approval. Directors may face accountability. If a modern statutory CSR duty/policy is pleaded, analyse that independent statutory basis separately rather than assume the AOA clause is enough.

Problem B — loan signed by two instead of three prescribed officers

Facts: Articles say every mortgage must be signed by the managing director, CFO and company secretary. A bank accepts a mortgage bearing only CFO and secretary signatures. It never checks articles.

Answer: This is Kotla, not Turquand. Constructive notice fixes the bank with the public signature requirement. The defect is visible when document and articles are compared; it is not a hidden missing board-resolution irregularity. Indoor management will not validate it.

Problem C — no board minute, but managing director role is held out

Facts: Articles permit appointment/delegation to an MD. No appointment resolution exists. The whole board nevertheless allows X for years to negotiate projects, retain lawyers and sign routine contracts as “Managing Director.” X appoints consultants for a project squarely within company objects.

Answer: Actual authority may be absent, but use Freeman & Lockyer. The board has actual managerial authority and represents by conduct that X is MD; the consultants rely in a normal transaction; company capacity and delegability exist. The company is estopped from denying X’s apparent authority. If X enters an extraordinary transaction beyond ordinary MD scope, reliance/suspicion requires closer scrutiny.

Problem D — required Government approval missing

Facts: A statutory licence requires prior regulator approval before charging a licensed asset. The board resolution is irregular, but lender sees a signed minutes extract and acts in good faith. No regulator approval exists.

Answer: The lender may invoke Turquand only for the board-resolution defect. Apply Dehradun-Mussoorie separately: an external statutory condition cannot be assumed away through indoor management. Determine whether the statute makes the security void/voidable and what unsecured/restitutionary remedy survives.

Common exam mistakes — avoid all eight

  1. Writing “all ultra vires acts can never be cured” without distinguishing an act beyond MOA from an act merely beyond AOA/director authority.
  2. Calling Cotman an ultra vires holding. It is an intra vires independent-objects case.
  3. Writing that an article can authorise what the MOA does not. Lakshmanaswami says the opposite.
  4. Applying Turquand before testing Act/MOA capacity and external legal restrictions.
  5. Using Kotla as a case favouring a lender. It favours the company because the outward document was facially defective.
  6. Stopping Dehradun-Mussoorie at the internal-management holding and missing the ultimate void-security result.
  7. Calling the s.10 statutory contract a contract with all outsiders. It binds company/members in membership capacity.
  8. Citing “s.11” as the present statutory effect of constitutional documents. Use s.10; s.11 is omitted.
