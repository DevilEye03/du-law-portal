§ 00

Why this Act exists

Before 2006, food regulation in India was scattered across at least eight central enactments and a web of state rules. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 was enacted to consolidate this into one science-based, single-window code — and it is squarely a white-collar crime statute, because almost every offence it creates is committed by a food business operator in the ordinary, outwardly legitimate course of trade.

The pre-2006 patchwork

Before consolidation, food safety regulation in India was governed by a fragmented set of laws, each administered by a different ministry and enforced by different officers, with inconsistent standards and overlapping jurisdiction: the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954; the Fruit Products Order, 1955; the Meat Food Products Order, 1973; the Vegetable Oil Products (Control) Order, 1947; the Edible Oils Packaging (Regulation) Order, 1998; the Solvent Extracted Oil, De-Oiled Meal and Edible Flour (Control) Order, 1967; the Milk and Milk Products Order, 1992; and provisions of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955 insofar as they touched food. This multiplicity meant a single food business could simultaneously need clearances from, and answer to, half a dozen different inspecting authorities applying different standards to the same product.

An Act to consolidate the laws relating to food and to establish the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India for laying down science based standards for articles of food and to regulate their manufacture, storage, distribution, sale and import, to ensure availability of safe and wholesome food for human consumption and for matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.

The Law Commission's diagnosis

The Krishna Murari Yadav compilation (relying on the Law Commission of India's 264th Report, 2017) records that Parliament's own stated reasons for enacting the FSS Act, 2006 were: (i) the existing food laws had become fragmented and outdated, having grown organically over decades without a unifying philosophy; (ii) there was no single, science-based standard-setting body — standards were fixed by different committees under different orders, often without a common risk-assessment methodology; (iii) enforcement was in the hands of multiple, poorly coordinated agencies, producing both regulatory gaps and duplicated compliance burdens; and (iv) India needed a framework aligned with international food safety practice (Codex Alimentarius-style science-based standards) to support both consumer protection and export competitiveness. The Act therefore repeals the PFA Act, 1954 and the other food control orders listed above, and vests standard-setting, licensing, and enforcement in one integrated hierarchy running from the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) down to the Food Safety Officer in the field.

WHY THIS TOPIC BELONGS IN A WHITE-COLLAR CRIME COURSE

Food adulteration and misbranding fit Sutherland's definition of white-collar crime with unusual precision: the offender is almost always a "person of respectability and high social status" — a manufacturer, packer, or corporate food business — who commits the offence "in the course of [their] occupation," using the outward legitimacy of a licensed trade to conceal a breach of trust against an anonymous class of consumer-victims. Unlike street crime, the harm is diffuse (millions of small doses of injury rather than one visible victim), the offender's intent is commercial rather than malicious, and detection depends entirely on regulatory sampling and laboratory science rather than a complaining witness — exactly the enforcement-dependency pattern McBarnet identifies as the structural advantage of white-collar offenders.

§ 01

Key Definitions — Section 3

Section 3 is the vocabulary on which every offence in Chapter IX is built. A prosecution under Section 59 (unsafe food) or Section 52 (misbranded food) lives or dies on whether the product fits these statutory definitions — so examiners test them constantly, and they recur inside every case dossier below.

ADULTERANT S.3(a)

Any material which is or could be employed for making the food unsafe or sub-standard or misbranded or containing extraneous matter.

CONTAMINANT S.3(f)

Any substance, whether or not added to food, but which is present in such food as a result of the production (including operations carried out in crop husbandry, animal husbandry or veterinary medicine), manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packing, packaging, transport or holding of such food, or as a result of environmental contamination and does not include insect fragments, rodent hair and other extraneous matter.

FOOD S.3(j)

Any substance, whether processed, partially processed or unprocessed, which is intended for human consumption and includes primary food, genetically modified food, infant food, packaged drinking water, alcoholic drink, chewing gum, and any substance used as an ingredient in the manufacture of food; but does not include animal feed, live animals unless prepared for market for human consumption, plants prior to harvesting, drugs/medicinal products, cosmetics, narcotic or psychotropic substances.

PRIMARY FOOD S.3(zk)

An article of food, being a produce of agriculture or horticulture or animal husbandry and dairying or aquaculture, in its natural form, resulting from the growing, raising, cultivation, picking, harvesting, collection or catching in the hands of a person other than a farmer or fisherman.

FOOD BUSINESS S.3(n)

Any undertaking, whether for profit or not, and whether public or private, carrying out any of the activities related to any stage of manufacture, processing, packaging, storage, transportation, distribution of food, import, and includes food services, catering services, sale of food or food ingredients.

FOOD BUSINESS OPERATOR S.3(o)

A person by whom the business is actually carried on or by whom the affairs of the business are conducted — i.e., ownership/control-based liability, which is how Section 66 (offences by companies) fastens onto directors and managers "in charge of and responsible to the company for its conduct."

MISBRANDED FOOD S.3(zf)

An article of food is "misbranded" if it is (i) mis-described, mis-stated, or false/misleading in its label particulars; (ii) sold under the name of another article; (iii) an imitation of another food without disclosure; (iv) sold in a package with false/misleading label or one not conforming to Act/Rules; (v) sold contrary to representation on the label; (vi) not labelled per Act/Rules requirements; (vii) not conforming to a claim on the label (e.g. "enriched"); or (viii) any other condition prescribed as constituting misbranding.

SUB-STANDARD FOOD S.3(zx)

An article of food is "sub-standard" if it does not meet the specified standard but does not pose a health risk — the least serious of the four defective-food categories.

UNSAFE FOOD S.3(zz)

An article of food is "unsafe" if it is injurious to health by reason of composition, contamination, decomposition, adulterants, toxins, disease-carrying constituents, or if the harm from ordinary handling/storage/consumption outweighs any beneficial effect — the most serious category, and the trigger for Section 59's graded, injury-linked penalty scale.

EXTRANEOUS MATTER S.3(i)

Any matter contained in an article of food which may be carried from the raw materials, packaging material or process systems used for its manufacture or which is added to it, but such matter does not render such food unsafe.

CODEX ALIMENTARIUS COMMISSION S.3(e)

The joint FAO/WHO body that sets international food standards, referenced repeatedly in Section 18 and the Nestlé judgment as the benchmark FSSAI standards are meant to track.

TRIBUNAL S.3(zy)

The Food Safety Appellate Tribunal established under Section 70 — the specialised first-appeal forum from an Adjudicating Officer's order.

EXAM ANGLE — THE FOUR TIERS OF "DEFECTIVE" FOOD

The Act does not use one undifferentiated idea of "bad food." It grades defects on a rising scale of seriousness, and each tier has its own offence provision: extraneous matter (S.3(i) → S.54, mildest, fine only) → sub-standard food (S.3(zx) → S.51, quality falls short but not unsafe) → misbranded food (S.3(zf) → S.52, a labelling/description defect) → unsafe food (S.3(zz) → S.59, the gravest, with imprisonment scaled to actual injury or death caused). Examiners frequently ask you to classify a given fact pattern into the correct tier — this is precisely the exercise the Nestlé case performs when it asks whether excess lead made Maggi noodles "unsafe" rather than merely "misbranded" for want of MSG disclosure.

§ 02

Offices & Officers, Part I — The National Apex Body

The Act builds a two-tier machinery: a single national standard-setter (FSSAI) at the top, and a state-level enforcement hierarchy underneath it that actually inspects, samples, licenses and prosecutes. This part covers the apex body; the enforcement chain follows in the next section.

Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) — Sections 4–17

The Central Government shall, by notification, establish a body to be known as the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India — a body corporate with perpetual succession and a common seal, competent to acquire and hold property and to sue or be sued in its own name. Its head office is at Delhi, with power to open offices elsewhere in India.

S.5 & S.6 — Composition, qualifications, and the Selection CommitteeSTRUCTURE

FSSAI consists of a Chairperson and twenty-two Members (at least one-third women): seven ex-officio Joint-Secretary-rank members representing Agriculture, Commerce, Consumer Affairs, Food Processing, Health, Legislative Affairs and Small Scale Industries; two from the food industry (one from small-scale industry); two from consumer organisations; three eminent food technologists/scientists; five rotating zonal members representing States/UTs; two representing farmers' organisations; and one representing retailers. The Chairperson must be a person of eminence in food science, or a retired/serving officer of Secretary rank who has handled the subject, and cannot hold any other office while in post.

Appointments (other than ex-officio members) go through a Selection Committee chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, with the Secretary in charge of the administering Ministry as convener, plus Secretaries of Health, Legislative and Personnel departments, the Chairman of the Public Enterprises Selection Board, and one eminent food technologist nominated by the Centre. The Committee must recommend a panel of two names for every vacancy, and must first satisfy itself the candidate has no conflicting financial interest.

S.7 & S.8 — Tenure and removalSECURITY OF TENURE

Members hold office for three years, renewable once; the Chairperson cannot continue past age 65. The Central Government may remove the Chairperson or a Member for insolvency, conviction involving moral turpitude, physical/mental incapacity, acquisition of a disqualifying financial interest, or abuse of position — but removal on the latter two grounds requires a reasonable opportunity of hearing first, a due-process safeguard built directly into the statute.

S.9 & S.10 — Chief Executive OfficerEXECUTIVE HEAD

The CEO, not below Additional Secretary rank, is FSSAI's Member-Secretary and legal representative, running its day-to-day administration: drawing up work programmes, executing the budget, and reporting annually to the Authority and Central Government. Critically, S.10(5) makes the CEO exercise the powers of the Commissioner of Food Safety in matters concerning food safety of specified articles — a functional bridge between the national and state tiers.

S.11–S.15 — Central Advisory Committee, Scientific Panels & Scientific CommitteeSCIENCE INPUT

The Central Advisory Committee (S.11–12) links FSSAI to enforcement agencies and stakeholders (industry, agriculture, consumers, laboratories, every State Commissioner of Food Safety), advising on work-programme priorities, risk identification, and knowledge-pooling; it must meet at least three times a year.

Scientific Panels (S.13) are made up of independent scientific experts and cover eight named domains: food additives/processing aids; pesticide/antibiotic residues; GMOs; functional foods/nutraceuticals; biological hazards; contaminants; labelling; and sampling/analysis methods.

The Scientific Committee (S.14) sits above the Panels — chaired collectively by the Panel Chairpersons plus six independent experts — and gives opinions on cross-cutting issues that fall between or outside individual Panels' remits, organising public hearings where necessary.

DUTIES & FUNCTIONS OF THE FOOD AUTHORITY — SECTION 16

Section 16(1) casts FSSAI's core duty in a single sentence: "to regulate and monitor the manufacture, processing, distribution, sale and import of food so as to ensure safe and wholesome food." Sub-section (2) then lets it specify, by regulation, the actual machinery for doing this — standards and enforcement systems (a); tolerance limits for additives, contaminants, pesticide/veterinary-drug residues, heavy metals, myco-toxins, antibiotics and irradiation (b); laboratory accreditation (c),(e); import quality control (d); sampling and inter-agency information exchange (f); enforcement surveys (g); food-labelling standards (h); and risk-analysis methodology (i). Sub-section (3) layers on a further set of "shall also" functions — scientific advisory support to governments, data collection on consumption risk and contaminants, crisis-management planning, international coordination, consumer/panchayat outreach, training, and general food-safety awareness.

STRUCTURE TO REMEMBER

S.16(1) = the duty (one line). S.16(2) = regulation-making powers (9 heads, a–i). S.16(3) = additional functions (14 heads, a–n). S.16(4) = transparency obligations (must publish Scientific Committee opinions, conflict-of-interest declarations, study results, annual report). S.16(5) = FSSAI may bind State Commissioners of Food Safety with directions. S.16(6) = confidentiality carve-out for commercially sensitive information, subject to a public-health override.

GENERAL PRINCIPLES — SECTION 18

Section 18 is a compact, three-clause provision that courts treat as the interpretive lens for the entire Act. Krishna Murari Yadav's teaching diagram frames it as three tracks: principles to be followed during implementation (clause 1), principles for the Food Authority while framing regulations/standards (clause 2), and a carve-out excluding farmers and fishermen from the Act's reach at the primary-production stage (clause 3).

Achieve an appropriate level of protection of human life/health and consumer interests (a); carry out risk management based on risk assessment (b); adopt provisional precautionary measures where harm is suspected but scientific uncertainty persists (c), provided such measures are proportionate and no more trade-restrictive than necessary (d) and are periodically reviewed (e); proactively inform the public of identified health risks (f); and presume an entire batch/lot/consignment non-compliant once part of it is shown to be, until the contrary is proved (g).

Take account of prevalent domestic practice and international standards (a); base standards on risk analysis (b); conduct risk assessment independently, objectively and transparently (c); hold open public consultation before regulations (d, urgency exception capped at 6 months); protect consumer interests and informed choice (e); and prevent both unfair trade practices and unsafe/sub-standard food (f).

S.18(3) — THE FARMER/FISHERMAN CARVE-OUT

The Act does not apply to a farmer, fisherman, or their farming/fishing operations, crops, livestock, or aquaculture, nor to supplies used or produced in farming, nor to a farmer's or fisherman's own-produced crops/catch. Regulatory responsibility attaches only once produce passes into the hands of a food business — precisely why the definition of "primary food" (S.3(zk)) speaks of produce "in the hands of a person other than a farmer or fisherman."

Both prescribed and supplementary DU case material read Section 18 the same way — as a set of guiding principles, not an independent source of power. See the Association of the Traders v. Union of India and Nestlé India Ltd. v. FSSAI dossiers below for the exact judicial language.

The Full Enforcement Hierarchy — Organogram

One diagram tying the national and state tiers together, from the Central Government's notification establishing FSSAI down to the Food Safety Officer standing in a shop with a sampling kit.

Fig. 1 — FSSAI & state enforcement hierarchy, with the two parallel post-contravention tracks: civil penalty (Adjudicating Officer → FSAT → High Court) and criminal prosecution (Magistrate/Special Court), per Sections 4, 30, 36–37, 45, 68, 70–71 & 42, 73–74.
WHY TWO TRACKS?

The FSS Act splits consequences into a civil/administrative track (Chapter IX offences that carry only a monetary "penalty" — Ss.50–58, 60–67 — go to the Adjudicating Officer, then FSAT, then High Court) and a criminal track (offences that carry "punishment" including imprisonment — chiefly S.59, unsafe food — go before a Judicial Magistrate or Special Court under ordinary criminal procedure, per the routing decision the Designated Officer and Commissioner make under Section 42(3)–(4)). Confusing "penalty" sections with "punishment" sections is one of the most common examination errors on this topic.

§ 04

Offices & Officers, Part II — The State Enforcement Chain

This is where the Act actually touches a shopkeeper, a factory floor, or a restaurant kitchen. Four officers do all the work — the Commissioner sets state policy and sanctions prosecution, the Designated Officer runs each district, the Food Safety Officer does the sampling in the field, and the Food Analyst turns the sample into scientific evidence.

Commissioner of Food Safety — Section 30STATE HEAD

The State Government appoints the Commissioner of Food Safety for efficient implementation of the Act within the State.

Functions — S.30(2):

  • Prohibit, in the public interest, the manufacture/storage/distribution/sale of any food article across the State or any part of it, for up to one year (notified in the Official Gazette);
  • Survey industrial units to check compliance with FSSAI-notified standards;
  • Conduct training programmes for departmental staff and industry segments;
  • Ensure efficient, uniform, objective, accountable and transparent implementation of standards;
  • Sanction prosecution for offences punishable with imprisonment (the Commissioner's single most exam-tested power);
  • Any other function the State Government prescribes in consultation with FSSAI.
S.30(3) — THE ONE POWER THE COMMISSIONER CANNOT DELEGATE

The Commissioner may delegate any power or function to a subordinate officer — except the power to appoint the Designated Officer, Food Safety Officer, or Food Analyst. Appointment of the three field-level offices is reserved non-delegably to the Commissioner personally.

Under Section 29(6), both the Commissioner and the Designated Officer additionally exercise the same powers as, and follow the same procedure as, a Food Safety Officer — so the hierarchy is cumulative, not exclusive.

Designated Officer — Section 36DISTRICT HEAD

"Designated Officer" means the officer appointed under Section 36. The Commissioner appoints, by order, a Designated Officer — not below the rank of a Sub-Divisional Officer — to be in charge of food safety administration in a specified area. There must be one Designated Officer for each district.

Functions — S.36(3):

  • Issue or cancel food business licences (a);
  • Prohibit sale of any article of food contravening the Act (b);
  • Receive reports/samples from the Food Safety Officer and get them analysed (c);
  • Recommend prosecution sanction to the Commissioner where imprisonment is possible (d);
  • Sanction or launch prosecution directly where only a fine is involved (e);
  • Maintain records of FSO inspections and action taken (f);
  • Investigate written complaints of contravention (g), and written complaints made against the FSO (h);
  • Perform any other duty entrusted by the Commissioner (i).

Qualification (FSS Rules, 2011, Para 2.1.2): a whole-time officer, not below Sub-Divisional Officer rank, holding at minimum a Bachelor's degree in Science with Chemistry, or an equivalent FSO qualification; must complete training within six months of appointment; functions under the overall supervision of the District Collector/Magistrate. The DO also refunds analysis fees to a purchaser under Section 40(1) and has emergency administrative power to suspend/cancel a licence where the Food Analyst's report shows grave threat to public health.

Food Safety Officer — Sections 37–39FIELD INSPECTOR

"Food Safety Officer" means an officer appointed under Section 37. The Commissioner appoints, by notification, persons having the prescribed qualification as FSOs for assigned local areas; a State Government may additionally authorise any qualified State officer to perform FSO functions within a specified jurisdiction.

Qualification (FSS Rules, 2011, Para 2.1.3): a whole-time officer holding a degree in Food Technology, Dairy Technology, Biotechnology, Oil Technology, Agricultural Science, Veterinary Science, Bio-Chemistry, Microbiology, a Master's in Chemistry, a degree in Medicine, or any other Centrally-notified equivalent qualification.

Powers — Section 38 (the working toolkit)
  • Sample (S.38(1)(a)): take a sample of any food intended for/sold for human consumption, or found on suspect premises, where it may be needed as evidence;
  • Seize (S.38(1)(b)–(c)): seize contravening food and keep it in the food business operator's safe custody against a bond, sending a sample for analysis;
  • Enter and inspect (S.38(2)): enter and inspect manufacturing/storage/sale premises and take samples of food or adulterant;
  • Destroy perishables (S.38(4)): destroy a seized perishable article found unfit for consumption, after written notice to the FBO;
  • Search per CrPC (S.38(5)): follow CrPC search-warrant procedure "as far as may be" when entering and inspecting;
  • Seize unexplained adulterant + documents (S.38(6)): seize adulterant found in a manufacturer/dealer's possession that they cannot satisfactorily account for, plus relevant books of account (only with superior's prior approval), and place the burden of proof on the possessor to show it was not meant for adulteration (S.38(9));
  • Witness requirement (S.38(7)): call independent witnesses to be present and sign whenever sampling, seizing, or destroying;
  • Return documents within 30 days (S.38(8)) after taking certified copies.

An FSO who vexatiously and without reasonable ground seizes food/adulterant, or otherwise injures a person without reasonable belief the act was necessary for duty, is personally guilty of an offence — penalty up to ₹1 lakh. Mirror-image protection: a person who makes a proven false complaint against an FSO is punishable with a fine of not less than ₹50,000, extendable to ₹1 lakh — a deliberate two-way check against both officer overreach and vexatious trade complaints.

Food Analyst — Sections 45–46LABORATORY SCIENTIST

The Commissioner of Food Safety appoints, by notification, qualified persons as Food Analysts for assigned local areas. Critically, no person with any financial interest in the manufacture or sale of any food article can be appointed — a direct conflict-of-interest bar. Different analysts may be appointed for different categories of food.

Functions — Section 46:

  • On receiving a sealed sample, compare its seal against the specimen impression and note the seal's condition; if the container is broken/unfit, inform the Designated Officer within 7 days and requisition the second part of the sample (S.46(1));
  • Analyse every sample received (S.46(2));
  • Report within 14 days of receipt — four copies to the Designated Officer (with a copy to the Commissioner) for samples taken under S.38/S.47, or a copy to the purchaser (with a copy to the DO) for purchaser-initiated samples under S.40 (S.46(3)); if analysis cannot finish in 14 days, must inform the DO and Commissioner with reasons and a revised timeline;
  • Appeal (S.46(4)): an appeal against the Food Analyst's report lies to the Designated Officer, who may refer the matter to the notified referral food laboratory for a fresh opinion.
EXAM ANGLE — THE 4-WAY SAMPLE SPLIT (SECTION 47)

When an FSO takes a food sample, it is divided into four parts: one goes to the Food Analyst for testing, two stay with the Designated Officer in safe custody (as a reserve for referral testing), and the fourth goes to an accredited private laboratory only if the food business operator requests it. If the FBO's private lab result conflicts with the Food Analyst's result, the Designated Officer sends a reserve part to the referral laboratory, whose finding is final (S.47(1)(c) proviso). This four-way split is a favourite short-answer question because it builds in a check against a lone government analyst's finding being unchallengeable.

§ 05

Licensing & Registration — Section 31

No person may run a food business without either a licence or a registration — the Act uses a two-track model calibrated to the scale of the business.

General rule (S.31(1)): no person shall commence or carry on any food business except under a licence.

Exception — registration instead (S.31(2)): a petty manufacturer who himself manufactures/sells food, a petty retailer, hawker, itinerant vendor, temporary stall-holder, or small-scale/cottage/tiny food business operator need only register with the prescribed authority in the prescribed manner — a lighter-touch compliance track for the smallest operators, without compromising the availability of safe food or consumer interests.

THE LICENSING PROCEDURE, STEP BY STEP

  1. Application (S.31(3)): the applicant applies to the Designated Officer in the prescribed form, with prescribed particulars and fees.
  2. Decision (S.31(4)): the DO either grants the licence, or — after giving the applicant a hearing and recording reasons in writing — refuses it in the interest of public health, supplying a copy of the order.
  3. Deemed licence on delay: if the DO neither issues nor rejects the application within two months, the applicant may start business after that period; the DO cannot then refuse the licence outright but may issue an Improvement Notice under Section 32 instead.
  4. Form of licence (S.31(5)–(7)): a single licence can cover multiple food articles and multiple establishments in the same area; separate applications/licences are required for premises in different areas.
  5. Appeal (S.31(8)): an appeal against refusal to grant a licence lies to the Commissioner of Food Safety.
  6. Validity & renewal (S.31(9)): the licence runs for the specified period; if a renewal application is filed before expiry, the licence continues in force until the renewal application is decided.
  7. Survivorship (S.31(10)): on the licensee's death, the licence subsists for the benefit of the personal representative/family member for three months (extendable by the DO).
Improvement Notices & Prohibition Orders — Sections 32–34GRADED ENFORCEMENT

Improvement Notice (S.32): where the DO has reasonable grounds to believe an FBO has failed to comply with applicable regulations, he serves a notice stating the grounds, the specific failure, the corrective measures required, and a deadline (not less than 14 days). Non-compliance → licence suspension; continued non-compliance after a show-cause opportunity → licence cancellation (though the DO may suspend forthwith in the public interest, recording reasons). An aggrieved person may appeal to the Commissioner within 15 days, whose decision is final.

Prohibition Order (S.33): where a court convicts an FBO and is satisfied a health risk exists, it may — after a hearing — prohibit use of the process/treatment, the premises/equipment for that business or any similar business, or even bar the individual from managing any food business. Knowing contravention of such an order is punishable with a fine up to ₹3 lakh. The order lifts once the FBO satisfies the concerned FSO (with DO approval) that sufficient corrective measures have been taken, or on a court application made no earlier than six months after the order.

Emergency Prohibition Notice/Order (S.34): where the DO is satisfied a health-risk condition exists, he applies to the Commissioner for an emergency prohibition — at least one day's prior notice to the FBO is mandatory. Knowing contravention of an emergency order carries imprisonment up to 2 years and fine up to ₹2 lakh.

§ 06

From Sampling to Prosecution — Sections 38, 40, 42 & 47

This is the procedural spine examiners test most often, because it is the sequence that actually convicts (or acquits) an accused: exactly how a sample becomes evidence, and exactly who decides whether the case goes to a magistrate or to the Adjudicating Officer.

THE PURCHASER'S PARALLEL ROUTE — SECTION 40

Nothing in the Act stops any purchaser (other than an FSO) from privately getting a food article analysed by the Food Analyst on payment of a fee, provided the purchaser informs the food business operator at the time of purchase of the intention to do so. If the resulting report shows non-compliance, the purchaser is entitled to a refund of the analysis fee. Where the Food Analyst's report confirms a contravention, it is forwarded to the Designated Officer, who follows the ordinary Section 42 prosecution procedure exactly as if an FSO had drawn the sample — giving an ordinary consumer a direct, self-service trigger for enforcement action, independent of waiting for a regulatory inspection.

§ 07

Offences & Penalties — Chapter IX (Sections 48–67)

Twenty sections, all reproduced from the bare Act. Two general provisions frame the chapter; eighteen create specific offences graded from a mild extraneous-matter fine to life imprisonment for causing death through unsafe food.

S.48 & S.49 — The two framing provisionsGENERAL

S.48 — General provisions relating to offences: defines the four ways food is rendered injurious — adding a substance, using a substance as an ingredient, abstracting a constituent, or subjecting food to any other process — done with knowledge it may be sold/distributed for consumption. S.48(2) lists the factors relevant to whether food is "unsafe": normal conditions of use and handling; label/consumer information; probable cumulative toxic effects; sensitivities of the category of consumer targeted; and cumulative effect of ordinary-quantity consumption. A proviso protects primary food whose quality has fallen below standard solely due to natural causes beyond human control — such food is not deemed unsafe/sub-standard/extraneous-matter-containing.

S.49 — General provisions relating to penalty: when fixing the quantum of penalty, the Adjudicating Officer or Tribunal must have regard to: the gain/unfair advantage made (a); the loss caused to any person (b); the repetitive nature of the contravention (c); whether the contravention was without the accused's knowledge (d); and any other relevant factor (e) — a structured sentencing-guideline template unusual for Indian regulatory legislation of this vintage.

EXAM ANGLE — SECTION 59 IS THE ONLY SECTION THAT SCALES PUNISHMENT TO HARM

Every other Chapter IX offence carries a single fixed monetary ceiling regardless of actual consequence. Section 59 alone has a four-step ladder tied strictly to the injury actually caused — no injury, non-grievous injury, grievous injury, or death — culminating in a mandatory minimum of seven years, extendable to life imprisonment, with a mandatory minimum fine of ₹10 lakh where death results. This is precisely the provision the Supreme Court held, in Ram Nath v. State of U.P. (2024) — see the case dossier below — must be used in place of IPC Sections 272–273 wherever both could apply, because it is "more stringent" and Section 89 FSSA gives it an overriding effect.

S.66 — OFFENCES BY COMPANIES, IN DETAIL

Where a company commits an offence, every person in charge of, and responsible to, the company for the conduct of its business at the time — as well as the company itself — is deemed guilty and liable to be proceeded against. Where the company has separate establishments/branches/units, the Head or in-charge nominated by the company as responsible for food safety for that unit is liable for contraventions there. A due-diligence/no-knowledge defence is available.

Independently of sub-section (1), where it is proved the offence was committed with the consent or connivance of, or is attributable to neglect on the part of, any director, manager, secretary or other officer, that individual is also deemed guilty — regardless of whether they were "in charge" under sub-section (1). "Company" includes a firm; "director" in relation to a firm means a partner.

Adjudication & the Food Safety Appellate Tribunal — Chapter X (Ss. 68–76)

Chapter IX creates the offences; Chapter X builds the specialised forum that decides them and the appeal ladder above it.

Adjudicating Officer — Section 68FIRST FORUM

S.68(1): an officer not below the rank of Additional District Magistrate of the district where the alleged offence occurred is notified by the State Government as the Adjudicating Officer.

S.68(2): after giving the person a reasonable opportunity to make representations, and if satisfied on inquiry that a contravention has occurred, the Adjudicating Officer imposes such penalty as he thinks fit under the relevant Chapter IX provision.

S.68(3): the Adjudicating Officer has the powers of a civil court; all proceedings before him are deemed judicial proceedings within the meaning of Sections 193 and 228 IPC (giving false evidence / intentional insult or interruption to a public servant sitting in judicial proceeding), and he is deemed a court for the purposes of Sections 345–346 CrPC (compounding of offences).

S.68(4): in fixing the penalty quantum, he must apply the Section 49 guidelines (gain made, loss caused, repetitiveness, knowledge, other relevant factors).

Power to Compound Offences — Section 69SETTLEMENT

The Commissioner may empower the Designated Officer to accept, from petty manufacturers, retailers, hawkers, itinerant vendors and temporary stall-holders reasonably suspected of an offence, a composition sum in lieu of prosecution. On payment, any person in custody is discharged and no further proceedings follow. The composition sum is capped at ₹1 lakh, fixed with regard to the Section 49 guidelines. Crucially — no offence carrying a prescribed imprisonment can be compounded; compounding is available only for the smallest operators' fine-only contraventions.

Food Safety Appellate Tribunal — Sections 70–72APPELLATE FORUM

The Central or State Government may, by notification, establish one or more Food Safety Appellate Tribunals to hear appeals from Adjudicating Officer decisions under Section 68. The Tribunal consists of a single Presiding Officer, who must be, or have been, a District Judge. Qualifications, appointment, tenure, salary and removal are centrally prescribed.

Procedure and powers (S.71): the Tribunal is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 but must be guided by principles of natural justice, and may regulate its own procedure including venue. It has the civil-court powers to summon and examine witnesses on oath, order discovery/production of documents (including electronic records), receive affidavit evidence, issue commissions for examining witnesses, review its own decisions, and dismiss for default or decide ex parte. Every Tribunal proceeding is a judicial proceeding under Sections 193/228 IPC, and for Section 196 IPC purposes it is deemed a civil court under Section 195 and Chapter XXVI CrPC. An appellant may appear in person or through a legal practitioner/authorised officer. The Limitation Act, 1963 applies to Tribunal appeals.

Any person aggrieved by a Tribunal decision may appeal to the High Court within 60 days of communication of the order (extendable by a further 60 days on sufficient cause) on any question of fact or law. Section 76 additionally provides that such an appeal is disposed of by a High Court bench of not less than two judges — a two-judge minimum unusual for a regulatory appeal and a strong signal of the seriousness Parliament attached to these disputes.

Civil court barred (S.72): no civil court has jurisdiction over any matter the Adjudicating Officer or Tribunal is empowered to determine, and no court may grant an injunction against action taken under the Act — an ouster clause that channels every dispute through the Act's own forum.

  • S.74 — Special Courts and Public Prosecutor: Central/State Governments may, in the public interest, notify Special Courts (particularly for offences involving grievous injury or death) and appoint a Public Prosecutor.
  • S.75 — Power to transfer cases to regular courts.

S.89 — THE OVERRIDING-EFFECT CLAUSE

Tucked into Chapter XII (Miscellaneous), Section 89 provides that the FSS Act's provisions have effect "notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in any other law for the time being in force." This one line is the entire basis of the Supreme Court's 2024 ruling in Ram Nath v. State of Uttar Pradesh that Section 59 FSSA displaces Sections 272–273 IPC in food-adulteration prosecutions — discussed in full in the IPC/BNS section below and in its own case dossier.

WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED IN THE BNS RECAST

The BNS keeps the same conduct elements and the same imprisonment ceilings for food-adulteration offences (Ss.274–275 BNS) — the only change is that fines were quintupled (₹1,000 → ₹5,000) to correct for decades of monetary inflation since 1860. None of these figures come close to the FSS Act's own penalty scale — which is precisely the doctrinal problem the courts and the Law Commission have had to resolve.

Case Dossier — Ram Nath v. State of Uttar Pradesh

Ram Nath v. The State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors.
Supreme Court of India · Criminal Appeal No. 472 of 2012 (with connected appeals) · 2024 INSC 138 / 2024 SCC OnLine SC 177 · decided 21–23 February 2024 · Bench: Abhay S. Oka & Sanjay Karol, JJ.
SUPPLEMENTARY — NOT IN DU MATERIAL
FACTS

On 11 May 2010, the Uttar Pradesh Government issued an order directing prosecuting authorities to launch parallel prosecutions against food-business operators under both the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 (and, after 2011, the FSS Act, 2006) and Sections 272/273 IPC for the very same act of food adulteration. The appellant and similarly situated accused persons across several consolidated Criminal Appeals and one Special Leave Petition challenged this dual-track prosecution, arguing that once the FSSA — a complete, self-contained code enacted specifically to regulate food safety — occupied the field, a simultaneous IPC prosecution for the same conduct was legally impermissible.

ISSUE
Does the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 override Sections 272 and 273 of the IPC in matters of food adulteration, precluding simultaneous prosecution under both statutes for the same act?
APPELLANTS' ARGUMENT
  • The FSSA, 2006 is an exhaustive, self-contained regulatory code covering every stage of food production, sale and safety, with its own detailed procedural machinery (sampling, analysis, adjudication, appeal).
  • Section 89 FSSA expressly gives the Act an overriding effect over "any other law for the time being in force," which necessarily includes the IPC's general provisions on food/drink adulteration.
  • Section 59 FSSA covers the identical wrong (unsafe food) far more comprehensively and far more severely than Sections 272–273 IPC, so continuing an IPC prosecution alongside an FSSA one amounts to double jeopardy in substance and legislative redundancy in form.
STATE'S ARGUMENT
  • The IPC is a general penal code of general application; nothing in its text is expressly repealed by the FSSA, and general and special statutes can validly operate in their own spheres side by side.
  • Sections 272 and 273 IPC and Section 59 FSSA have different ingredients (IPC requiring specific intention/knowledge as to noxiousness; the FSSA turning on the statutory definition of "unsafe food"), so there is no true "inconsistency" triggering Section 89.
  • Simultaneous prosecution safeguards the State's ability to secure a conviction under whichever provision the evidence better supports.
DECISION & REASONING

The Supreme Court held that Section 89 FSSA gives the Act an overriding effect over all other food-related laws, drawing an analogy to Section 26 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 (which permits punishment under either of two laws for the same act but bars punishment twice for the same offence). The Court reasoned that because the FSSA contains exhaustive substantive and procedural provisions specifically dealing with unsafe food — and because Section 59 FSSA is, on the Court's own assessment, "more stringent" than Sections 272–273 IPC for the same underlying wrong — the two cannot run in parallel. In the Court's words (as widely reported), "we have no manner of doubt that by virtue of Section 89 of the FSSA, Section 59 will override the provisions of Sections 272 and 273 of the IPC. Therefore, there will not be any question of simultaneous prosecution under both the statutes." The Court quashed the pending IPC prosecutions but left the authorities at liberty to proceed against the accused under the FSSA for offences under Section 59.

PRINCIPLE ESTABLISHED

Where an act of food adulteration attracts both Sections 272–273 IPC (now Ss.274–275 BNS) and Section 59 of the FSS Act, 2006, the FSSA — being a complete, subsequent, and more stringent special code — overrides the IPC by virtue of Section 89 FSSA, and prosecution must proceed under the FSSA alone; simultaneous prosecution under both statutes for the same act is impermissible.

SO WHY STUDY IPC 272–276 / BNS 274–278 AT ALL?

Three reasons an examiner can still test them even after Ram Nath: (1) they remain good law on the statute books and apply of their own force to any food/drug adulteration that falls outside the FSS Act's field (e.g., non-food noxious drink cases, or drug adulteration where the Drugs and Cosmetics Act rather than the FSSA is the specialised code); (2) the Law Commission's 264th Report (below) specifically recommended reforming these very IPC sections, so they are part of the reform history of this topic; and (3) understanding why the FSSA needed an overriding clause in the first place requires understanding what it was overriding.

Nestlé India Ltd. v. The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India & Ors.
High Court of Bombay · W.P.(L) No. 1688 of 2015 · Coram: V.M. Kanade & B.P. Colabawalla, JJ. · decided 13 August 2015
PRESCRIBED — DU OFFICIAL SYLLABUS
BACKGROUND — HOW MAGGI ENDED UP BANNED

In May 2015, the Uttar Pradesh Food Safety Authority reported finding lead well above the permissible limit, and monosodium glutamate (MSG) despite a "No Added MSG" label, in samples of Nestlé's Maggi 2-Minute Noodles. On 4 June 2015 Nestlé voluntarily issued a press release announcing withdrawal of the product pending clarification, while maintaining the product was safe. The very next day, 5 June 2015, the CEO of FSSAI (Respondent No.2) passed an order (Exhibit-A) directing Nestlé to stop manufacture, sale and distribution of nine Maggi variants nationwide, and the Commissioner of Food Safety, Maharashtra (Respondent No.4) passed a parallel order (Exhibit-B). Nestlé challenged both orders by writ petition under Article 226 before the Bombay High Court.

FACTS

The petitioner sought quashing of the CEO's 5 June 2015 order and the Maharashtra Commissioner's parallel order, challenging them on five principal grounds: (i) both orders were passed in complete violation of natural justice — no show-cause notice was issued and no particulars of the adverse laboratory findings were disclosed before the ban; (ii) the food laboratories that tested the samples were either not NABL-accredited or not notified under Section 43 of the Act, and even where accredited, lacked specific accreditation for lead-testing in this product category; (iii) the product had not been tested according to its intended use (i.e., as reconstituted noodles, not the dry noodle cake alone); (iv) Nestlé's own accredited-laboratory tests showed lead well within permissible limits; and (v) an appeal under Section 46(4) was not a real alternative remedy since the impugned final orders had already pre-determined the very issue an appeal would raise.

ISSUES FRAMED BY THE COURT
I. Is the writ petition under Article 226 maintainable despite the alternative remedy of appeal under Section 46(4)?
Held, yes. The impugned order was a ban, not merely a show-cause notice — the show-cause portion for cancelling product approval was only consequential to a ban already imposed in the same order. Alternative remedy is not an absolute bar where natural justice has been violated (relying on Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trade Marks).
II. Did Nestlé suppress facts or attempt to destroy evidence, disentitling it to relief?
Held, no. Minutes of meetings — tendered across the bar and undisputed — showed Nestlé destroyed stock only pursuant to the Food Authority's own directions.
III. Could the ban rest on lead exceeding what Nestlé had represented in its product-approval application (0.1 ppm), even though it was below the Regulations' actual ceiling (2.5 ppm)?
Held, no — answered in the negative. The Court called the Respondents' argument "preposterous," holding that Parliament-approved Regulations under Sections 92–93 cannot be overridden by a manufacturer's own product-approval representation; the statutory limit governs, not a self-declared figure.
IV. Does the Food Authority have unfettered discretion to fix ad hoc standards for proprietary foods, unbound by the Regulations' Schedules?
Held, no — answered in the negative. Regulation 2.1.1(2) of the Contaminants, Toxins and Residues Regulations, 2011 fixes lead at 2.5 ppm for "foods not specified" (a residuary category covering proprietary foods); accepting FSSAI's argument would render this entry — and the entire Parliamentary-approval mechanism under Section 93 — otiose.
V. Did Section 22 impose a complete ban on sale of the products in question?
Left open — the Court found no need to decide this given its findings on other issues, expressly leaving it "to be argued in an appropriate case."
VI & VII. Was there a natural-justice violation, and what was the source of statutory power for the impugned orders?
Held, yes to the violation — no material was disclosed to Nestlé before the ban, and no real opportunity to respond was given. On source of power, the Court held the orders could only draw on Sections 10(5), 16(1), 16(5), 18, 22, 26, 28 and 29 read together, and that the impugned orders had not properly invoked or satisfied any of them.
VIII–XI. Were the testing laboratories properly accredited, could Nestlé's own lab reports be relied on, and was misbranding (the "No Added MSG" claim) established?
The labs that tested the FSSAI samples were not shown to hold NABL accreditation for lead-testing specifically, so their reports could not be relied upon under Sections 3(p) and 43. On misbranding, the Court found no material that Nestlé had deliberately added MSG — glutamate occurs naturally in many foods — and noted Nestlé had already agreed to delete the "No Added MSG" claim, so the misbranding allegation could not survive; in any event, the maximum penalty for misbranding under Section 52 is only ₹3 lakh, disproportionate to a nationwide, indefinite product ban.
XII. Was the ban arbitrary, capricious and violative of Articles 14 and 19(1)(g)?
Held, yes — answered in the affirmative. Combining the natural-justice violation, the invalid lab reports, non-compliance with the mandatory sampling procedure under Section 47(1), and the fact that Nestlé had already voluntarily recalled the product a day earlier, the Court found the ban arbitrary, non-transparent, and unconstitutional.
ARGUMENTS OF THE PARTIES
NESTLÉ (PETITIONER) — SR. COUNSEL MR. IQBAL CHAGLA
  • No show-cause notice or disclosure of adverse material before the ban — a clear breach of natural justice.
  • The testing laboratories lacked NABL accreditation specifically for lead-testing, so their reports were legally unreliable.
  • The product should have been tested as consumed (post-preparation), not merely as a dry noodle cake.
  • Nestlé's own accredited-lab results showed lead within the permissible 2.5 ppm limit.
  • The Section 46(4) appeal was not a genuine alternative remedy since the final orders had already pre-judged the very question an appeal would raise.
FSSAI / STATE (RESPONDENTS) — SR. COUNSEL MR. DARIUS KHAMBATTA; MR. MEHMOOD PRACHA FOR RESPONDENT NO.2
  • A manufacturer's own representation in its product-approval application (0.1 ppm) creates a "trust," breach of which justifies a ban even if the statutory ceiling (2.5 ppm) was not technically breached.
  • For proprietary/non-standardised foods under Section 22, the Food Authority has unfettered discretion over applicable safety limits, independent of the Regulations' general Schedules.
  • MSG was found despite the "No Added MSG" declaration, amounting to misbranding under Section 52.
  • One of the nine variants (Maggi Vegetable Atta Noodles) lacked product approval altogether.

The Court described the Respondents' "unfettered discretion" argument as one that left it "amazed and astonished," holding it would render the Parliament-approved Regulations under Sections 92–93 meaningless.

DECISION

The Bombay High Court allowed the petition and quashed both impugned orders, holding them arbitrary and violative of Articles 14 and 19(1)(g), for four cumulative reasons: (a) breach of natural justice, compounded by the fact that Nestlé had already voluntarily recalled the product; (b) reliance on laboratory reports from labs not properly accredited for lead-testing; (c) non-compliance with the mandatory four-way sampling procedure under Section 47(1); and (d) the misbranding allegation could not independently justify an indefinite nationwide ban given its modest ₹3 lakh statutory ceiling under Section 52.

However, mindful of public health, the Court did not simply let Nestlé resume sale unconditionally. It directed fresh testing: five preserved samples of each batch were to be sent to three named NABL-accredited laboratories (Vimta Lab Hyderabad, Punjab Biotechnology Incubator Mohali, and CEG Test House Jaipur), following the Section 47(1) procedure properly this time. Only if lead was found within permissible limits could Nestlé resume manufacture — and newly manufactured stock had to clear the same three-laboratory test again before sale was permitted. The Vegetable Atta Noodles variant, lacking approval altogether, would need to go through the ordinary product-approval process afresh. Since Nestlé had already agreed to delete the "No Added MSG" claim, the misbranding allegation was treated as resolved.

PRINCIPLES ESTABLISHED

(1) A food-safety ban is quasi-judicial state action and must satisfy natural justice — disclosure of the material relied upon, and an opportunity to respond, before (not merely after) the ban. (2) The Food Authority has no unfettered discretion to override Parliament-approved Regulations even for proprietary/non-standardised food under Section 22 — the Section 92–93 Regulations, once laid before Parliament, bind the Authority itself. (3) The mandatory sample-testing procedure under Section 47(1) (the four-way split, notified accredited laboratories) is not a mere formality — non-compliance vitiates the resulting order. (4) A disproportionality principle: the severity of executive action (an indefinite, nationwide product ban) must be proportionate to the maximum statutory penalty Parliament actually prescribed for the underlying contravention (here, ₹3 lakh for misbranding under S.52) — a ban cannot be used to achieve indirectly what the penalty structure itself would not support directly. (5) Article 226 remains available despite a statutory appeal remedy where natural justice has been breached or the order is wholly without jurisdiction.

Swami Achyutanand Tirth & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors.
Supreme Court of India · AIR 2016 SC 3626 · Full Bench: T.S. Thakur, C.J.I., R. Banumathi & U.U. Lalit, JJ. · decided 5 August 2016 · "The Milk Adulteration Case"
DU SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL
FACTS

The petitioners — residents of Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Haryana and NCT of Delhi — filed writ petitions in the Supreme Court relying on FSSAI's own "Executive Summary on National Survey on Milk Adulteration, 2011" (dated 2 January 2011), which had found that 68.4% of milk sold nationally was adulterated, with several States — Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, West Bengal, Mizoram, Jharkhand, and Daman & Diu — showing adulteration up to 100%, and Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh at 88%. The petitioners alleged that both the concerned State Governments and the Union of India had failed to take effective measures against adulteration of milk with hazardous substances such as urea, detergent, refined oil, and caustic soda, and sought directions compelling effective enforcement. Since food safety is a matter of national importance, the Union of India was joined as a party-respondent alongside the States.

ARGUMENTS / GROUNDS URGED
PETITIONERS
  • Mass adulteration of milk with hazardous chemicals violates the fundamental right to life and health under Article 21 of the consuming public at large.
  • Existing enforcement under the FSS Act, 2006 (Sections 50–65) and Sections 272–273 IPC has proved wholly inadequate given the scale FSSAI's own survey revealed.
  • Effective directions are needed for testing infrastructure, spot-checking, public awareness, and inter-agency accountability.
UNION OF INDIA / STATES
  • The existing statutory and regulatory framework (the FSS Act and the Food Safety and Standards (Food Products, Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, 2011, which define categories of milk products) was represented as adequate in principle, with implementation gaps attributable to State-level enforcement capacity.
GUIDELINES ISSUED

Rather than adjudicating a lis in the ordinary adversarial sense, the Court issued a comprehensive set of continuing-mandamus directions to the Union and States, including: (1) more effective implementation of the FSS Act, 2006; (2) informing dairy operators/retailers that stringent action will follow if chemical adulterants (pesticides, caustic soda, etc.) are found; (3) identifying high-risk areas and high-risk periods (e.g., around festivals) for intensified sampling; (4) ensuring adequate, NABL-accredited laboratory testing infrastructure at State and district level; (5) special spot-testing measures including Mobile Food Testing Vans with primary testing kits; (6) periodic (not one-off) snapshot surveys at both State and national level by FSSAI; (7) constitution of State-level committees (headed by the Chief Secretary or Dairy Department Secretary) and District-level committees (headed by the District Collector) to review anti-adulteration work, modelled on Maharashtra's practice; (8) a dedicated website in each State publicising food safety authorities' functions and complaint mechanisms, with toll-free and online complaint facilities and published contact details of Food Safety Commissioners; (9) consumer-awareness measures under Section 18(1)(f), including school workshops teaching simple adulteration-detection techniques (e.g., milk adulteration detection strips); and (10) a mechanism for the Union/States to address corruption or unethical practice among Food Authority officers themselves.

PRINCIPLE ESTABLISHED / SIGNIFICANCE

Widespread food adulteration engages the fundamental right to life under Article 21, and the Supreme Court can issue structured, continuing-mandamus directions compelling proactive, not merely reactive, enforcement of the FSS Act — reinforcing Section 18(1)(f)'s duty on authorities to inform the public of health risks. The judgment directly triggered legislative reform: it is the proximate cause of the Law Commission of India's 264th Report (2017), which the Court had itself effectively invited by calling on the Union to bring IPC penalties for food adulteration in line with the FSS Act and with the more stringent State amendments already made by Odisha, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal.

M. Mohammed v. Union of India
High Court of Madras · Writ Appeal No. 1491 of 2014 · Division Bench (affirming a Single Bench decision) · decided 2015
DU SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL
FACTS

M. Mohammed imported 50,942 kg of raw, ungarbled Betel Nut (Areca Nut/Supari) from Sri Lanka, submitting a Bill of Entry at Chennai on 19 June 2014. The Commissioner of Customs, Chennai, passed an Examination Order the same day directing that, to obtain customs clearance, the consignment required a test report and a No Objection Certificate (NOC) from the Second Authorised Officer, FSSAI, Chennai. Mohammed challenged this direction by writ petition before a Single Judge of the Madras High Court, principally contending that raw, unprocessed Betel Nut was not "food" within the meaning of the FSS Act, 2006 and so could not be subjected to FSSAI clearance requirements at all.

The Single Bench held that Areca Nut, being an agricultural product, fell within the definition of "primary food," which is itself covered by "food" under Section 3(j) of the FSS Act, 2006, and dismissed the writ petition — also noting that the definition of "food" under the (then-repealed) Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 differed from, and was narrower than, the definition under the FSS Act. Mohammed then filed an intra-court Letters Patent (Writ) Appeal before a Division Bench.

ISSUES & THE COURT'S ANSWERS
1. Is "ungarbled Betel Nut" the same thing as "Areca Nut/Betel Nut"?
No — they are different (ungarbled/raw nut becomes Areca Nut only after processing) — but both fall within the statutory definition of "Food." Section 3(j) expressly covers substances "whether processed, partially processed or unprocessed," so the unprocessed raw nut is still "Food."
2. Does "ungarbled Betel Nut" fall under the definition of "Food"?
Yes — as unprocessed food.
3. Is there a difference between "Food" under the PFA Act, 1954 and the FSS Act, 2006?
Yes — the FSS Act, 2006 definition is significantly wider than the PFA Act's.
4. Does the ratio in Al Marwa Traders v. Assistant Commissioner of Imports (2007) apply?
No — that case interpreted "food" under the narrower PFA Act, 1954, not the FSS Act, 2006, and is therefore distinguishable.
5. Can goods for which the FSS Act prescribes no specific standard still be withheld from release if found contaminated, unfit for consumption, or injurious to life?
Yes — the State's constitutional duty to protect public health and life (drawing on Centre for Public Interest Litigation, Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa, and Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, the last two cited for the proposition that international law may guide interpretation in the absence of specific domestic provision) justifies withholding release even absent a codified standard for that specific product.
ARGUMENTS
APPELLANT (M. MOHAMMED)
  • Raw/ungarbled Betel Nut is a raw commodity, not "Food" as commonly understood, and should not require FSSAI clearance for customs release.
  • The narrower PFA Act, 1954 precedent (Al Marwa Traders) should govern the meaning of "food" for import-clearance purposes.
RESPONDENT (UNION OF INDIA / CUSTOMS / FSSAI)
  • Section 3(j)'s definition of "Food" is deliberately broad, covering unprocessed substances intended for human consumption, and expressly includes "primary food" under Section 3(zk).
  • FSSAI clearance is a legitimate, statutorily-grounded precondition to import clearance under Section 25, regardless of whether the specific product has a codified standard.
DECISION

The Division Bench rejected the appellant's arguments and dismissed the appeal, affirming the Single Bench's finding that raw, ungarbled Betel Nut is "Food" within Section 3(j) FSS Act, 2006, and upholding the requirement of an FSSAI test report and NOC as a precondition to customs clearance. Mohammed obtained no remedy.

PRINCIPLE ESTABLISHED

The FSS Act, 2006's definition of "Food" under Section 3(j) is deliberately expansive — covering processed, partially processed, and unprocessed substances intended for human consumption — and is significantly wider than the definition previously used under the repealed Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954. Consequently, raw agricultural commodities not yet processed for sale (here, raw Areca Nut) still qualify as "primary food" under Section 3(zk) and attract full FSSAI regulatory jurisdiction, including import-clearance requirements under Section 25, even where no product-specific standard has yet been codified for that commodity.

The Association of the Traders & Ors. v. Union of India
High Court of Bombay · decided 16 September 2015
DU SUPPLEMENTARY MATERIAL
CONTEXT & HOLDING

This case is preserved in the DU compilation specifically for its interpretation of Section 18 (general principles), decided one day before the Nestlé judgment by the same High Court. The Bombay High Court articulated the purpose and legal character of Section 18 in terms later echoed almost verbatim in the Nestlé decision:

"A perusal of Section 18 reveals that the foundation is preservation of human life and health while protecting the consumer's interest and by having fair practices in all kinds of food and trade and maintenance of food safety and standards and practices. A significant feature to be noted in this provision is that the entire endeavour is to take care of the harmful effects of food on the basis of scientific studies and taking appropriate measures to ensure availability of safe food for human consumption... The Parliament, in exercise of its legislative powers as conferred in Entry 18 of List III [Concurrent List — "Adulteration of foodstuffs and other goods"], has enacted the FSS Act in the interest of public health and to safeguard the constitutional guarantee to the citizens as conferred under Article 21 of the Constitution... It is clear that the authorities entrusted with the duty of implementing the [Act] are not merely to perform the negative duty of preventing food adulteration, but are required to play a very pro-active role for ensuring safe and wholesome food and to prevent and eliminate risk to health caused by unsafe food. It is, therefore, clear that [the FSS Act, 2006] is the comprehensive single special legislation for all food products on the subject of safety and standards."

PRINCIPLE ESTABLISHED

Section 18 is not a mere administrative checklist — it embodies the Act's constitutional foundation in Article 21 and casts food-safety authorities in a proactive, not merely reactive, role. The FSS Act, 2006 is confirmed as "the comprehensive single special legislation for all food products on the subject of safety and standards" — the doctrinal basis later relied on, both in Nestlé (to hold that Section 18 lays down guiding principles rather than an independent source of power) and, more consequentially, in Ram Nath v. State of U.P. (2024) to justify treating the FSS Act as displacing the IPC's older, general adulteration provisions.

Law Commission of India, Report No. 264 (2017)

"The Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill, 2017 — Provisions Dealing with Food Adulteration." The direct legislative aftershock of Swami Achyutanand Tirth, and the missing link between the FSS Act's own graded penalty structure and the IPC's century-old flat fine.

HOW THE REPORT CAME TO BE WRITTEN

In Swami Achyutanand Tirth, the Supreme Court had — in earlier orders dated 5 December 2013 and 10 December 2014, reiterated in the 2016 judgment — observed that it was "desirable to make penal provisions of IPC at par with the provisions contained in the State Amendments" made by Odisha, Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal, which had already enhanced the punishment for food adulteration under their state amendments to imprisonment for life, and fine. The Court further suggested the Union revisit the FSS Act's own penalty structure to make it more deterrent where the adulterant could adversely affect health. Pursuant to this, the Ministry of Home Affairs referred the specific question of amending Sections 272 and 273 IPC to the Law Commission by letter dated 2 November 2016, and the Commission — already engaged in a comprehensive review of the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act — submitted Report No. 264 in January 2017.

THE COMMISSION'S DIAGNOSIS AND RECOMMENDATION

THE COMMISSION'S OWN WORDS

"After detailed discussions, the Commission felt that the punishment provided for is too inadequate in the present scenario. Therefore, the Commission is of the view that there must be more stringent punishment in offences relating to adulteration of food, which is a threat to human life. The Commission feels that the punishment must be seen in light of the harm caused to the consumer by consuming adulterated food items."

Rather than simply raising the flat penalty ceiling across the board, the Commission deliberately proposed a graded punishment structure — calibrated to (i) whether the adulteration was intentional or otherwise, and (ii) the actual extent of harm suffered by victims — explicitly reasoning that "merely raising the overall punishment may leave the matter free for courts to adopt lenient... attitudes from time to time," whereas a harm-graded structure forecloses that judicial leniency and "brings home to the criminal the reality of the consequences of crime." This is, notably, structurally identical to the graded model the FSS Act itself already used in Section 59 (no injury / non-grievous / grievous / death) — the Commission's recommendation was essentially to import the FSS Act's own architecture into the IPC.

THE REPORT ALREADY SAW THE S.89 PROBLEM

Report 264 itself expressly discusses Section 89 FSSA (overriding effect) alongside Section 97 (repeal and savings), engaging with the very question of how a reformed IPC would sit alongside the FSS Act's own more comprehensive scheme. In other words, the doctrinal tension the Supreme Court would eventually resolve in Ram Nath v. State of U.P. (2024) — seven years later — was already flagged, though not conclusively settled, in this 2017 Report.
