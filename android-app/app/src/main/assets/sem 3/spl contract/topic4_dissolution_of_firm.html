The Five Routes, and the Court — Chapter VI, ss. 39 to 44

s. 39 tells you what dissolution is. ss. 40 to 43 give you four routes that need no court. s. 44 gives you the fifth — a decree. Read them in that order and the chapter stops being a list.

§1.0 — Entering Chapter VI: what this topic is really about

Everything you studied in Chapters III, IV and V assumed a firm that exists. Chapter VI is about what happens when it stops existing. That sounds like an ending, but it is not: the Act's central insight is that a dissolved firm does not simply vanish. It lingers, in a limited and carefully controlled way, for exactly as long as it takes to settle its affairs — and it is that lingering that generates almost every dispute in this topic.

1 A firm is not a legal entity

s. 4 defines partnership as a relation between persons; the firm name is merely "a compendious name" for those persons (S.V. Chandra Pandian v. S.V. Sivalinga Nadar, ¶ 8). So on dissolution the assets vest in all the partners, not in some abstract body — which is why the distribution of those assets is a mutual adjustment of rights and not a transfer.

2 Dissolution is a date, not a process

In this chapter "when" matters more than "whether". The date of dissolution starts the limitation clock (Art. 5), fixes the cut-off for s. 45(1) liability, and opens the window for winding up under ss. 46 to 47. Most DU problems are really about a date.

3 Winding up is the second act

Dissolution ends the partnership relation; winding up ends the business. The two are distinct, and the second can last years. s. 47 keeps the partners' authority alive for exactly that interval — "so far as may be necessary … but not otherwise".

✱ A real-life running example

Keep this one in your head; it will be reused throughout the notes. Anita, Bhaskar and Chandra run "ABC Foods", a registered partnership making packaged pickles from a factory in Ghaziabad. Their deed, executed in 2021, says three things: the firm is for seven years; the death of a partner shall not dissolve the firm; and the firm may be terminated at any time by mutual agreement. Bhaskar dies in a road accident in 2025. Because of the death clause, s. 42(c) is excluded and the firm continues — Bhaskar's widow Geeta is admitted in his place. That is a reconstitution, not a dissolution (s. 17(a); Sharad Vasant Kotak). In 2027 Anita wants out and serves a notice on Chandra. That notice dissolves nothing, because the deed provides for determination and the firm is therefore not at will (s. 7) — her remedy is retirement with consent (s. 32(1)(a)) or a suit under s. 44. Finally, in 2028 all three agree to stop: that is dissolution by agreement under s. 40, and it immediately triggers s. 46 (winding up), s. 47 (authority to finish the pickling contracts already booked), s. 45 (liability until public notice is given under s. 72) and s. 48 (creditors, then advances, then capital, then the residue split in the profit ratio). One firm, four sections — that is the whole chapter in a paragraph.

§1.1 — Section 39: What "dissolution of a firm" means

§ The section, verbatim

39. Dissolution of firm.— "The dissolution of partnership between all the partners of a firm is called the 'dissolution of the firm'."

Eighteen words, and three of them do all the work: "between all the partners". Those three words are the whole distinction between this topic and Topic 3. If all the partners go out together, the firm is dissolved and Chapter VI applies. If one goes out and the rest carry on, the firm survives and Chapter V (ss. 31–38) applies. The Act calls the first "the dissolution of the firm" and the second, by implication, merely "a dissolution of partnership".

1 Element one — a partnership must exist

There can be no dissolution of what never was. s. 4 requires an agreement to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all, and s. 5 insists that the relation arises from contract and not from status. Where no firm existed, the remedy is not dissolution but accounts — see Dayalal v. Harjeevan, AIR 1983 NOC 20 (Guj).

2 Element two — "between all the partners"

The entire web of relationships must snap. It is not enough that two of three partners fall out, or that a partner sells his share: the firm must lose all of its members simultaneously. Hence s. 41(a) — adjudication of all the partners or of all but one — is enough, because one person cannot be "partners".

3 Element three — it is a name, not a separate event

s. 39 does not dissolve anything. It is a definition section. The dissolving is done by ss. 40 to 44. Writing "the firm is dissolved under s. 39" is the single most common error in DU scripts on this topic — s. 39 only tells you what to call it.

Fig. 2 — One distinction, six consequences. Learn the first row ("all the partners") and the rest follows by itself.

Six reasons the distinction matters in a DU answer

  1. Different sections, different chapters. Chapter V (ss. 31–38) for an exit; Chapter VI (ss. 39–48) for a dissolution. Mis-citing is an instant deduction.
  2. Only a dissolution triggers winding up. ss. 46 to 48 have no application where a single partner merely retires — his remedy is accounts under s. 26 read with s. 48, subject to the terms of the retirement deed.
  3. s. 42(c) and (d) convert one into the other. Where the deed so provides, the death or insolvency of a partner dissolves the firm; without such a clause, ss. 34 and 35 apply and the firm continues. This is the pivot of Saligram Ruplal Khanna v. Kanwar Rajnath and of DU 1994 Q.7(a).
  4. Limitation runs differently. A suit for an account of a dissolved partnership and for a share of its property is governed by Article 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 — three years from the date of dissolution. Once the firm is dissolved, the clock starts; while it subsists, it does not. This is precisely what defeated the plaintiffs in Saligram.
  5. The registration bar is lifted. s. 69(3)(a) expressly preserves a suit for dissolution, for accounts of a dissolved firm, and for realising its property — so even an unregistered firm can be wound up through the court (DU 2013 OC Q.4(b); 2003 Q.6(b)).
  6. Tax and stamp consequences differ. On a true dissolution the distribution of assets is a mutual adjustment and not a transfer (see S.V. Chandra Pandian, case 18) — so no capital gains, no stamp duty under Article 12 of Schedule I of the Indian Stamp Act, and no registration under s. 17 of the Registration Act, 1908.

§1.2 — Section 40: Dissolution by agreement

§ The section, verbatim

40. Dissolution by agreement.— "A firm may be dissolved with the consent of all the partners or in accordance with a contract between the partners."

i Limb one — consent of all

A fresh agreement, made now, to bring the firm to an end. The four elements are: (1) an agreement; (2) between all the partners — not a majority (contrast s. 23, which lets a majority decide ordinary differences); (3) to dissolve; and (4) no form is prescribed.

ii Limb two — by an existing contract

The firm ends because an event the partners provided for in advance has happened: "this firm shall stand dissolved on 31 March 2027", or "on the sale of the Delhi branch", or "if the licence is not renewed". The dissolution is then automatic on that event — no further act is needed.

Five points that carry marks

  1. "Consent of all" is the same standard as s. 31(1) and s. 11(1). The Act does not permit a majority to dissolve the firm by agreement, any more than it permits a majority to admit a partner or to vary the mutual rights and duties. A dissolution resolution passed 3–1 is a nullity as against the dissenter.
  2. No writing, no form, no registration is required. Unlike a transfer of immovable property (s. 54, T.P. Act 1882 read with s. 17, Registration Act 1908), a dissolution may be oral, or may be inferred from conduct. But note the evidentiary trap: in Saligram Ruplal Khanna v. Kanwar Rajnath, AIR 1974 SC 1094 (case 15) the plaintiffs alleged an oral agreement to continue the firm after the lease had been cancelled, and the Supreme Court upheld the concurrent finding that it had not been proved. An oral dissolution is lawful but difficult to establish.
  3. Consent may be express or implied. s. 11(1) recognises a course of dealing as a source of implied agreement; and a dissolution may be inferred where the partners have all acted on the footing that the business is over — closing the bank account, returning the licence, delivering possession. In Juggilal Kamlapat v. Sew Chand Bagree, AIR 1960 Cal 463 (case 16) G.K. Mitter J. held the firm dissolved on precisely such material: a deed of agreement prepared by solicitors, the issue of a trade licence by the Corporation, the opening of a new bank account, and a letter to the bankers.
  4. A dissolution deed is nonetheless advisable — and where it deals with immovable property worth ₹100 or more, the question of registration arises. It does not arise where the deed merely distributes the residue after settlement of accounts in accordance with s. 48, because the residue is money in the eye of the law (S.V. Chandra Pandian v. S.V. Sivalinga Nadar, (1993) 1 SCC 589, case 18).
  5. s. 40 is subject to the general law of contract. The agreement must be lawful (s. 23, ICA 1872), free of fraud, coercion, undue influence and misrepresentation (ss. 14–19, ICA), and — where it is a compromise of pending litigation — must comply with O. 23 r. 3, CPC 1908.
✱ Real-life illustration

Priya, Quentin and Rahul run "PQR Design Studio" from a leased studio in Hauz Khas. Clause 22 of their deed: "This partnership shall stand dissolved on 31 March 2028 or on the expiry or termination of the lease of the studio premises, whichever is earlier." In January 2027 the landlord terminates the lease. The firm stands dissolved under the second limb of s. 40 — automatically, on the event, without any further agreement. If instead the three of them meet in February 2027 and simply decide to stop, that is dissolution under the first limb — and it must be the decision of all three. If Priya alone wants out and the other two wish to continue, s. 40 is of no use to her at all: her remedies are retirement under s. 32 (if the firm is at will, or with consent), or a suit for dissolution under s. 44 on one of the seven grounds.

! Three traps
  1. "Dissolution by mutual agreement" in a deed is not s. 43. Where the deed says the firm may be terminated only by mutual agreement, a single partner cannot dissolve it by notice — the firm is not at will. (DU 2001 Q.5(b); 2017 OC Q.6(b).)
  2. A clause that death or retirement "shall not dissolve the firm" is a contract to the contrary under s. 42 — and it also means the firm is not at will. (DU 2004 Q.3(ii); 1994 Q.7(a).)
  3. Agreement to dissolve ≠ agreement to distribute. The second is governed by s. 48, whose waterfall cannot be contracted out of as against creditors — though it is "subject to agreement by the partners" as between themselves.

§1.3 — Section 41: Compulsory dissolution

§ The section, verbatim

41. Compulsory dissolution.— "A firm is dissolved —

  (a) by the adjudication of all the partners or of all the partners but one as insolvent, or

  (b) by the happening of any event which makes it unlawful for the business of the firm to be carried on or for the partners to carry it on in partnership:

Provided that, where more than one separate adventure or undertaking is carried on by the firm, the illegality of one or more shall not of itself cause the dissolution of the firm in respect of its lawful adventures and undertakings."

Note the absence of the words "subject to contract between the partners". s. 41 is not subject to contract: unlike s. 42, the partners cannot contract out of compulsory dissolution. That single drafting difference is the surest way to distinguish the two sections in an answer.

Fig. 3 — Two limbs, one proviso, and no room for the partners to contract out. Note that s. 41 has no "subject to contract" opening — that is what makes it compulsory.

Reading limb (a) precisely

  1. "Adjudication", not "insolvency" in the commercial sense. A partner who cannot pay his debts but has not been adjudicated does not dissolve the firm. The event is the order of the court.
  2. The date is the date of the order — the same rule as s. 34(1). Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, the analogous steps for an individual partner are the application by creditors under ss. 95 to 100 and the bankruptcy order; the Presidency-Towns Insolvency Act, 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 continue to apply where the IBC does not. And s. 69(3)(b) expressly preserves the powers of the Official Assignee, the Receiver and the court to realise the property of an insolvent partner, notwithstanding the registration bar.
  3. "All the partners or of all the partners but one." The arithmetic is worth stating explicitly in the exam: in a two-partner firm, the insolvency of either dissolves it; in a five-partner firm, four must be adjudicated. The reason is conceptual — one person cannot be "partners" with himself, so there is no relationship left for the law to dissolve.
  4. Contrast s. 42(d), which dissolves the firm on the insolvency of one partner — but only "subject to contract between the partners". s. 41(a) needs all but one; s. 42(d) needs one. Both can be pleaded in the alternative.

Reading limb (b) and the proviso

  • "Unlawful for the business … to be carried on" covers illegality attaching to the business: the excise licence is cancelled, the drug licence lapses, the trade is prohibited by a new statute, the import licence is not renewed.
  • "…or for the partners to carry it on in partnership" covers illegality attaching to the persons: on the outbreak of war a partner becomes an alien enemy; a partner is convicted and disqualified from the profession; a statutory bar disqualifies a partner (compare the now-repealed s. 11 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 on competency).
  • The proviso is a severance rule. If the firm carries on several separate adventures, the illegality of one does not infect the others. The test is whether the undertakings are genuinely separate — separate capital, separate accounts, separate management — or merely departments of one business.
  • Related authority and allied law: AIR 1933 Patna 239 (all partners, or all but one, adjudicated insolvent — firm dissolved); the Indian Contract Act, 1872, s. 23 (unlawful consideration and object) and s. 56 (agreement to do an impossible act becomes void — the contractual parallel of s. 41(b)); the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 and the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 where a licence or registration is the thing withdrawn; and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, s. 79 for what counts as a bankruptcy debt.
✱ Real-life illustration

Kabir and Lalit run two businesses under one deed: a licensed country-liquor warehouse in Gurugram and an unlicensed packaging unit in Baddi. In March the excise authorities cancel the warehouse licence for a licensing breach. The packaging unit is a separate adventure: under the proviso to s. 41(b) the firm is not dissolved as to it, provided the two genuinely are separate undertakings with separate books. If instead the firm had only the warehouse business, the cancellation would dissolve it at once, and — critically — the partners would remain liable to third parties for acts done afterwards until public notice under s. 45(1).

§1.4 — Section 42: Dissolution on the happening of certain contingencies

§ The section, verbatim

42. Dissolution on the happening of certain contingencies.— "Subject to contract between the partners a firm is dissolved —

  (a) if constituted for a fixed term, by the expiry of that term;
  (b) if constituted to carry out one or more adventures or undertakings by the completion thereof;
  (c) by the death of a partner; and
  (d) by the adjudication of a partner as an insolvent."

The opening words — "Subject to contract between the partners" — are the most heavily examined words in Chapter VI. They mean that clauses (a) to (d) are default rules. Every one of the four can be excluded or modified by the partnership deed. Most well-drawn deeds exclude (c) and (d).

§ The three-question test for any s. 42 problem
  1. What does the deed say? This comes first because s. 42 is subject to contract. If the deed excludes death or insolvency, ss. 42(c) and (d) are simply not in play.
  2. If the deed is silent — which clause applies? Fixed term (a), single adventure (b), death (c), insolvency (d). State the date on which it bites.
  3. What follows? If the firm is dissolved: winding up (ss. 46–47) and settlement of accounts (s. 48), with Article 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 starting to run from the date of dissolution. If the firm continues: ss. 34, 35, 37 apply, and the outgoing partner or his estate has the profits-or-6% election.
✱ Real-life illustration — the fork in the road

Meera, Naveen and Omar are partners in "MNO Exports", constituted in 2019 "for a period of ten years", with clause 19 providing that "the death of a partner shall not dissolve the firm, and the surviving partners shall continue". Naveen dies in 2024. Because of clause 19, s. 42(c) is excluded: the firm is not dissolved. Naveen's estate is not liable for acts done after his death (s. 35), and it may elect a share of profits attributable to the use of Naveen's share of the firm property or 6% per annum (s. 37). The firm will still dissolve on 31 December 2028 by the expiry of the fixed term under s. 42(a) — unless all three originally agreed, or the survivors agree, to extend it. Delete clause 19, and Naveen's death would dissolve the firm there and then under s. 42(c), sending the parties straight into ss. 46 to 48.

§1.5 — Section 43: Dissolution by notice, of a partnership at will

§ The section, verbatim

43. Dissolution by notice of partnership at will.—
(1) Where the partnership is at will, the firm may be dissolved by any partner giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention to dissolve the firm.

(2) The firm is dissolved as from the date mentioned in the notice as the date of dissolution or, if no date is so mentioned, as from the date of the communication of the notice.

1 The firm must be "at will"

This is the gateway. s. 7: a partnership is "at will" where no provision is made by contract for the duration of the partnership or for its determination. Either limb is enough.

2 Notice must be IN WRITING

Oral notice is worth nothing. Asha Ram v. Ram Chander, (1993) (1) WLN 388. A resolution is not a notice; a lawyer's letter, an e-mail or a registered letter is.

3 To ALL the other partners

Service on one or two of three partners dissolves nothing. Every partner must receive it — this is the single most common defect in DU problems.

4 Intention to dissolve

The notice must express an intention to dissolve. A notice of retirement, or of an intention to retire, is not a notice of dissolution of the firm.

Fig. 5 — One gateway (s. 7), four ingredients (writing, to all, intention, at will), one date rule (s. 43(2)). Miss any one and there is no dissolution.

When is a firm "at will"? — the s. 7 test, applied

  • No clause on duration. The deed is silent on how long the firm will last → at will.
  • No clause on determination. The deed fixes a term but says nothing about how the firm may be brought to an end → at will, because s. 7 uses "or".
  • Not at will where the deed fixes a term AND provides for determination — e.g. "ten years, terminable earlier by mutual agreement". Then s. 43 is excluded and the aggrieved partner must rely on s. 40, s. 42 or s. 44. This is exactly DU 2001 Q.5(b) and 2017 OC Q.6(b).
  • Not at will where the deed excludes death or insolvency as a ground of dissolution — the clause is a provision for determination, so s. 7's second limb is not satisfied (DU 2004 Q.3(ii); 1994 Q.7(a)).
  • A firm for a fixed term that continues to trade after the expiry of the term becomes, in the absence of any agreement to the contrary, a partnership at will as regards its mutual rights and duties (s. 17(b)) — and thereafter s. 43 applies to it.
  • A partnership at will may later be converted into one for a fixed term, or vice versa, by a contract between the partners (s. 11(1)) — but only by the consent of all of them.
  • An illegal firm is not a partnership at will — it is no firm at all (s. 23, ICA 1872), and the court will not dissolve what does not exist.
✱ Real-life illustration

Vidya, Wasim and Zoya open "VWZ Café" in Lajpat Nagar. The deed is four pages long and says nothing about duration and nothing about how the firm may be ended. On 1 June 2026 Vidya posts a registered letter to Wasim stating: "I wish to withdraw from the café with effect from 1 July 2026." Zoya receives no notice. Two defects. First, the notice expresses an intention to retire, not an intention to dissolve the firm — s. 43(1) is not satisfied. Second, the notice did not go to all the other partners. A correct notice would read: "I hereby give you notice under s. 43 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 of my intention to dissolve the firm VWZ Café with effect from 1 July 2026", and would be served in writing on both Wasim and Zoya. Then the firm dissolves on 1 July 2026 — the date mentioned in the notice (s. 43(2)) — and ss. 46 to 48 follow.

§1.6 — Section 44: Dissolution by the Court

§ The section, verbatim

44. Dissolution by the Court.— "At the suit of a partner, the Court may dissolve a firm on any of the following grounds, namely:—

(a) that a partner has become of unsound mind, in which case the suit may be brought as well by the next friend of the partner who has become of unsound mind as by any other partner;
(b) that a partner, other than the partner suing, has become in any way permanently incapable of performing his duties as partner;
(c) that a partner, other than the partner suing, is guilty of conduct which is likely to affect prejudicially the carrying on of the business, regard being had to the nature of the business;
(d) that a partner, other than the partner suing, wilfully or persistently commits breach of agreement relating to the management of the affairs of the firm or the conduct of its business, or otherwise so conducts himself in matters relating to the business that it is not reasonably practicable for the other partners to carry on the business in partnership with him;

(f) that the business of the firm cannot be carried on save at a loss; or
(g) on any other ground which renders it just and equitable that the firm should be dissolved."

§ Five structural points about s. 44
  1. The suit must be "at the suit of a partner". A creditor, an assignee, or an heir who has not been admitted as a partner cannot invoke s. 44. This is why the plea in Juggilal Kamlapat v. Sew Chand Bagree (case 16) mattered — the question was who was still a partner liable on the award, not who could sue for dissolution.
  2. Grounds (b) to (e) are asymmetric. Each is directed at "a partner other than the partner suing". The section is a weapon against the guilty partner, not a licence for a defaulting partner to walk out of his own breach. Ground (a) is the exception — it is neutral, and the next friend of the partner of unsound mind may sue.
  3. Dissolution is discretionary. "The Court may dissolve". Even where a ground is made out, the court may refuse the decree — for instance where the dominant purpose of the suit is to escape a liability, or where a lesser remedy (an injunction, an account, a receiver) will do.
  4. Ground (g) is residuary, not free-standing. The words "any other ground" take their colour from (a) to (f). A mere quarrel, or a fall in profits, is not enough; the ground must render it just and equitable in a sense analogous to the enumerated ones.
  5. The date of dissolution is the date of the decree — not the date of the suit. Until the decree is passed, the firm subsists, and s. 45(1) continues to bind the partners for acts done in the meantime.
✱ Real-life illustration — DU 1998 Q.6(b) / 2017 OC Q.3(b), worked

Facts. A, B and C are partners in a firm of pharmaceutical distributors. B is found guilty of adultery at a private party. A sues for dissolution under s. 44.

Answer. The relevant ground is s. 44(c) — conduct "likely to affect prejudicially the carrying on of the business, regard being had to the nature of the business". Two steps. First, is the conduct proved? Yes, on the given facts. Second — and this is where marks are won — does it affect the business? The Act does not say "conduct of which the other partners disapprove"; it says conduct likely to affect the carrying on of the business, having regard to its nature. In a firm whose stock-in-trade is reputation and regulatory trust — a bank, a school, a hospital, a licensed distributor — a partner's public disgrace may well be prejudicial. In a firm of timber merchants it probably is not.

Conclusion. Argue both ways; then hold that on these facts the better view is that the ground is made out given the fiduciary and reputational character of the business, but that dissolution remains discretionary and the court may instead grant a lesser remedy. Contrast the ticketless-travel conviction (DU 1997 Q.3(b); 2014 OC Q.4(b)): dishonesty in a banking firm goes directly to fitness for the business, and s. 44(c) is more clearly attracted there.

The Aftermath — ss. 45 to 48, and the allied ss. 49 to 55

Dissolving the firm is only half the chapter. Once it is dissolved, four questions remain: who is still liable to outsiders (s. 45), who may insist on winding up (s. 46), how far does the partners' authority survive (s. 47), and in what order is the money paid out (s. 48). §2.1 — Section 45: Liability for acts done after dissolution

§ The section, verbatim

45. Liability for acts of partners done after dissolution.—
(1) Notwithstanding the dissolution of a firm the partners continue to be liable as such to third parties for any act done by any of them which would have been an act of the firm if done before the dissolution, until public notice is given of the dissolution:

Provided that the estate of a partner who dies, or who is adjudicated an insolvent, or of a partner who, not having been known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner, retires from the firm, is not liable under this section for acts done after the date on which he ceases to be a partner.

(2) Notices under sub-section (1) may be given by any partner.

✓ Why s. 45 exists

Dissolution is a private event; third parties are not party to it. A customer who has dealt with "M/s Sharma & Sons" for years cannot be expected to know that the firm was dissolved last Tuesday. s. 45 therefore keeps the partners holding out as partners to the outside world until they tell the world. The section is the dissolution analogue of s. 32(3) (retirement) and of s. 28(1) (holding out) — learn the three together.

Fig. 7 — One rule (liability until public notice) and three automatic exceptions. Category ③ is the one that carries Juggilal Kamlapat (case 16).

The five ingredients of s. 45(1)

  1. The firm has been dissolved — by any of the five routes. If it has not been dissolved, s. 45 has no application and ordinary ss. 18–22 liability applies instead.
  2. An act is done by "any of them" — by any one partner. It need not be by all, and it need not be authorised by the others.
  3. The act is one "which would have been an act of the firm if done before the dissolution" — i.e. it must be within s. 19(2) implied authority, or expressly authorised, or at least within the ordinary course of the firm's business. A partner's wholly private borrowing after dissolution binds nobody.
  4. Liability is "as such" — the partners are liable as partners, i.e. jointly and severally for all the acts of the firm (s. 25), and the creditor may sue any or all of them (s. 43, ICA 1872).
  5. It continues only "until public notice is given" — the cut-off is public notice, not private knowledge. A creditor who happens to have heard of the dissolution is still entitled to rely on the absence of public notice, unless the partner proves he had actual knowledge at the time of the dealing.
§ What "public notice" means — s. 72

72. Mode of giving public notice.— A public notice under this Act is given—
(a) where it relates to the retirement or expulsion of a partner from a registered firm, or to the dissolution of a registered firm, or to the election to become or not to become a partner in a registered firm by a person attaining majority who was admitted as a minor to the benefits of partnership, by notice to the Registrar of Firms under section 63, and by publication in the Official Gazette and in at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district where the firm to which it relates has its place or principal place of business; and, by clause (b), by publication in the Official Gazette and in such a newspaper where the firm is unregistered.

So there are three steps for a registered firm: (1) a notice to the Registrar under s. 63; (2) publication in the Official Gazette; and (3) publication in at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district. Missing any one of them is fatal — the liability continues.

✱ Real-life illustration — the classic DU pattern

Facts. M, K and L are partners in "M/s Sunrise Traders", a registered firm. M retires in April; K is taken in; no notice is given to the Registrar and no newspaper advertisement is published. X, an old customer, sells goods worth ₹8 lakh to the firm in August on credit, believing M is still a partner. The firm goes bankrupt. X sues M.

Answer. Under s. 45(1) M continues liable "as such" to X for the August sale, because no public notice of the change was given as required by s. 72(a). M's only escape is the proviso: that he was a partner "not having been known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner" who has retired. Whether X knew M to be a partner is a question of fact and onus — and on the authority of Juggilal Kamlapat v. Sew Chand Bagree, the onus lies on M, who must show that X did not know him to be a partner. Mere entries in the Register of Firms do not discharge that onus: Rameshwar Agarwalla in that case admitted he did not know Manik Chand and Moti Chand to be partners until he consulted the Registrar's records after the dispute arose, and that is exactly what protected them. But — and this is the twist DU 1998 Q.5(b) turns on — if X had dealt with the firm through M for years, M loses.

Practical lesson. A retiring or outgoing partner should always give public notice under s. 72 and, separately, send individual written notice to every existing customer — because s. 32(3) and s. 45 protect him only from those to whom he was not known, and every customer who knew him must be told personally.

§2.2 — Section 46: Right of partners to have the business wound up

§ The section, verbatim

46. Right of partners to have business wound up after dissolution.— "On the dissolution of a firm every partner or his representative is entitled, as against all the other partners or their representatives, to have the property of the firm applied in payment of the debts and liabilities of the firm, and to have the surplus distributed among the partners or their representatives according to their rights."

1 A right, not a discretion

The word is "entitled". No partner can be compelled to leave the assets in another partner's hands, and no partner can appropriate them. This is the statutory answer to the recurring DU question "Does dissolution result in the winding up of the firm?" (2014 Q.5; 2012 Q.5) — yes, and every partner has an enforceable right to insist on it.

2 "Every partner OR HIS REPRESENTATIVE"

The legal representatives of a deceased partner (ss. 2(c), 35, 42(c)), and the Official Assignee or Receiver of an insolvent partner (ss. 34, 41(a), 69(3)(b)), stand in his shoes. They may demand winding up even though they are not partners.

3 Two limbs: debts, then surplus

First, the property must be applied in payment of debts and liabilities. Only then is the surplus distributed "according to their rights" — and what those rights are is spelled out in s. 48. s. 46 and s. 48 are therefore read together.

✓ What "winding up" actually involves — the practical list
  1. Realise the assets — collect book debts, sell stock, close the lease, sell the goodwill (s. 55(1)).
  2. Pay the creditors of the firm, in the order set by s. 48(b)(i) and s. 49.
  3. Complete transactions begun but unfinished (s. 47).
  4. Render accounts — each partner may demand a full account (ss. 9, 26; O. 20 r. 15, CPC).
  5. Distribute the residue in the profit-sharing proportions (s. 48(b)(iv)).
  6. Give public notice of the dissolution (ss. 45, 72).

§2.3 — Section 47: Continuing authority of partners for purposes of winding up

§ The section, verbatim

47. Continuing authority of partners for purposes of winding up.— "After the dissolution of a firm the authority of each partner to bind the firm, and the other mutual rights and obligations of the partners, continue notwithstanding the dissolution, so far as may be necessary to wind up the affairs of the firm and to complete transactions begun but unfinished at the time of the dissolution, but not otherwise:

Provided that the firm is in no case bound by the acts of a partner who has been adjudicated insolvent, but this proviso does not affect the liability of any person who has after the adjudication represented himself or knowingly permitted himself to be represented as a partner of the insolvent."

Fig. 8 — The authority that survives dissolution is a residual authority to finish the job, not an authority to start a new one. The four words "but not otherwise" are the limit.

Five points on s. 47

  1. Both the authority AND the mutual obligations continue. The section says "the authority of each partner to bind the firm, and the other mutual rights and obligations of the partners". So ss. 9 to 17 (diligence, indemnity, the duty to render true accounts, the duty not to compete) continue to apply during the winding-up period.
  2. The test is necessity. "So far as may be necessary". Convenience is not necessity. In Saligram Ruplal Khanna the Supreme Court held that consenting to the Morarji Desai award was necessary — the transaction had been begun while the firm subsisted and had to be carried through.
  3. "Transaction" is a wide word. Rejecting a narrow construction, the Court (per H.R. Khanna J.) quoted Lindley on Partnership, 13th Edn., p. 251 and Halsbury's Laws of England, 3rd Edn., Vol. 28, p. 573 for the proposition that "transaction" is not confined to commercial purchase and sale — it extends to whatever is necessary to wind up.
  4. The proviso is a carve-out and a sting. The firm is "in no case" bound by the acts of an insolvent partner. But anyone who holds himself out, or knowingly lets himself be held out, as a partner of the insolvent, remains liable — the s. 28(1) principle applied to the insolvent's firm.
  5. Procedural consequence. Because the authority continues for winding-up purposes, a surviving partner may continue a suit already filed by the firm. Abdul Rehman v. Rameshwar Dayal, 1994 (2) WLC 260 (Raj): on the death of one of two partners who had sued on a pronote, the surviving partner was held competent to continue the suit.

§2.4 — Section 48: Mode of settlement of accounts between partners

§ The section, verbatim

48. Mode of settlement of accounts between partners.— "In settling the accounts of a firm after dissolution, the following rules shall, subject to agreement by the partners, be observed:—

(a) Losses, including deficiencies of capital, shall be paid first out of profits, next out of capital, and, lastly, if necessary, by the partners individually in the proportions in which they were entitled to share profits;

(b) the assets of the firm, including any sums contributed by the partners to make up deficiencies of capital, shall be applied in the following manner and order:—
(i) in paying the debts of the firm to third parties;
(ii) in paying to each partner rateably what is due to him from the firm for advances as distinguished from capital;
(iii) in paying to each partner rateably what is due to him on account of capital; and
(iv) the residue, if any, shall be divided among the partners in the proportions in which they were entitled to share profits."

Fig. 9 — Four rungs, in a fixed order: creditors → advances → capital → residue. Note that (ii) and (iii) are rateable, while the residue and the losses follow the profit-sharing ratio.

Worked example — the arithmetic DU examiners expect

✱ Settle the accounts

Facts. A, B and C share profits equally. On dissolution the position is:

  • Capital: A ₹2,00,000; B ₹1,00,000; C ₹60,000
  • Advances (loans) by partners: A ₹50,000; C ₹30,000
  • Trade creditors: ₹1,40,000
  • Cash realised from assets: ₹3,00,000

Step 1 — s. 48(b)(i), debts to third parties. Pay the creditors ₹1,40,000. Remaining: ₹1,60,000.

Step 2 — s. 48(b)(ii), advances, rateably. A is owed ₹50,000 and C ₹30,000; total ₹80,000. The fund (₹1,60,000) is sufficient, so both are paid in full: A ₹50,000, C ₹30,000. Remaining: ₹80,000.

Note the point of "rateably": had only ₹40,000 remained, A and C would share it 5 : 3 (in proportion to their advances) — A ₹25,000 and C ₹15,000 — and not equally.

Step 3 — s. 48(b)(iii), capital, rateably. Capital totals ₹3,60,000 (2,00,000 + 1,00,000 + 60,000) but only ₹80,000 remains. So the partners share the shortfall 2,00,000 : 1,00,000 : 60,000, i.e. 10 : 5 : 3. Of ₹80,000: A gets ₹44,444; B ₹22,222; C ₹13,334.

Step 4 — s. 48(b)(iv), the residue. Nothing remains, so there is no residue. Had there been one, it would have been divided equally (the profit-sharing ratio), not in the 10:5:3 capital ratio.

Step 5 — s. 48(a), the losses. The unpaid capital is a deficiency of capital, which is a loss. It is borne first out of profits (none here), then out of capital (exhausted), and lastly by the partners individually in the profit-sharing proportions — equally. The creditors have already been paid in full at step 1, so this is purely an inter se adjustment.

! Four traps in s. 48
  1. "Advances as distinguished from capital." A partner who has both a capital account and a loan account is paid twice — first as a creditor (ii), then as a partner (iii). This is a favourite of examiners.
  2. "Rateably" ≠ equally. It means in proportion to the respective amounts due.
  3. The residue follows profits, not capital. s. 48(b)(iv) says "in the proportions in which they were entitled to share profits" — and s. 13(b) presumes equality where the deed is silent.
  4. s. 48 is "subject to agreement by the partners". Partners may agree a different order inter se, but they cannot contract out of paying the firm's creditors first — those creditors are not parties to the agreement, and s. 49 and the general law protect them.

§2.5 — The allied provisions: ss. 49 to 55, covered in place

! Why these seven sections are here at all

The DU syllabus for Chapter VI names only ss. 39 to 48. But the Act does not stop at s. 48 — ss. 49 to 55 are the consequences of dissolution, and DU problems routinely turn on them without naming them. They are therefore set out here, in place, rather than in an appendix: read them immediately after s. 48 and the chapter becomes a single continuous story.

Fig. 10 — The seven consequences. Learn s. 49 with s. 48(b)(i), s. 50 with s. 47, and ss. 53 to 55 as the "firm name and goodwill" trio.

The seven sections, one by one

§ s. 49 — Payment of firm debts and of separate debts

"Where there are joint debts due from the firm, and also separate debts due from any partner, the property of the firm shall be applied in the first instance in payment of the debts of the firm, and, if there is any surplus, then the separate property of any partner shall be applied first in the payment of his separate debts and the surplus, if any, in the payment of the debts of the firm."

The rule in one line: firm property → firm debts first; a partner's separate property → his separate debts first; only the surplus crosses over. This is the "two estates" rule, and it dovetails with s. 48(b)(i) and with O. 21 r. 49, CPC (a creditor of a partner can only attach that partner's share, not the firm's assets). Contrast ss. 60 and 61 of the CPC and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, ss. 140–142, where firm creditors prove against the firm estate first.

§ s. 50 — Personal profits earned after dissolution

"Subject to contract between the partners, the provisions of clause (a) of section 16 shall apply to transactions by any surviving partner or by the representatives of a deceased partner, undertaken after the firm is dissolved on account of the death of a partner and before its affairs have been completely wound up."

So s. 16(a) — a partner making personal profit from a transaction of the firm, or from the use of its property, name or business connection, must account for it — continues to apply during the winding-up period after a death-dissolution. This is the companion of s. 37, which gives the deceased partner's estate the election between a share of profits and 6% per annum. Note the opening words "subject to contract between the partners".

§ s. 51 — Return of premium on premature dissolution

"Where a partner has paid a premium on entering into partnership for a fixed term, and the firm is dissolved before the expiration of that term otherwise than by the death of a partner, he shall be entitled to repayment of the premium or of such part thereof as may be reasonable, regard being had to the terms upon which he became a partner and to the length of time during which he was a partner, unless— (a) the dissolution is mainly due to his own misconduct, or (b) the dissolution is in pursuance of an agreement containing no provision for the return of the premium or any part of it."

Four conditions: (1) a premium was paid; (2) the firm was for a fixed term; (3) it was dissolved before the term expired; and (4) the dissolution was not by death of a partner. Two exceptions: his own misconduct, and an agreement that says nothing about returning the premium. The measure is what is reasonable — ordinarily the unexpired proportion of the term.

§ s. 52 — Rights where the partnership contract is rescinded for fraud or misrepresentation

"Where a contract creating partnership is rescinded on the ground of the fraud or misrepresentation of any of the parties thereto, the party entitled to rescind is, without prejudice to any other right, entitled—

(a) to a lien on, or a right of retention of, the surplus of the assets of the firm remaining after the debts of the firm have been paid, for any sum paid by him for the purchase of a share in the firm and for any capital contributed by him; (b) to rank as a creditor of the firm in respect of any payment made by him towards the debts of the firm; and (c) to be indemnified by the partner or partners guilty of the fraud or misrepresentation against all the debts of the firm."
Allied law: ss. 17 to 19 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (fraud and misrepresentation); s. 19-A (power to set aside a contract induced by undue influence); s. 27 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 (rescission); and s. 62 of the ICA (novation).

§ s. 53 — Right to restrain the use of the firm name or firm property

"After a firm is dissolved, every partner or his representative may, in the absence of a contract between the partners to the contrary, restrain any other partner or his representative from carrying on a similar business in the firm name or from using any of the property of the firm for his own benefit, until the affairs of the firm have been completely wound up: Provided that where any partner or his representative has bought the goodwill of the firm, nothing in this section shall affect his right to use the firm name."
Allied law: the Trade Marks Act, 1999, ss. 27, 28, 29, 134, 135 (passing off and infringement); the Specific Relief Act, 1963, ss. 38, 39 (perpetual injunction); and O. 39 rr. 1–2, CPC for the interim injunction.

§ s. 54 — Agreement in restraint of trade

"Partners may, upon or in anticipation of the dissolution of the firm, make an agreement that some or all of them will not carry on a business similar to that of the firm within a specified period or within specified local limits, and notwithstanding anything contained in section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, such agreement shall be valid if the restrictions imposed are reasonable."
This is the statutory exception to s. 27 of the ICA, which otherwise voids every agreement in restraint of trade. Three points: (1) the agreement must be made upon or in anticipation of the dissolution; (2) the restraint must be limited by period or local limits — and 1969 WLN 570 holds that the word "or" cannot be read as "and", so a restraint specifying only a period, or only a locality, is good; and (3) the restriction must be reasonable.

§ s. 55 — Sale of goodwill after dissolution

(1) "In settling the accounts of a firm after dissolution the goodwill shall, subject to contract between the partners, be included in the assets, and it may be sold either separately or along with other property of the firm."

(2) "Where the goodwill of a firm is sold after dissolution, a partner may carry on a business competing with that of the buyer and he may advertise such business, but, subject to agreement between him and the buyer, he may not— (a) use the firm name, (b) represent himself as carrying on the business of the firm, or (c) solicit the custom of persons who were dealing with the firm before the dissolution."

So a selling partner keeps three freedoms (compete, advertise, and — by implication — deal with new customers) and loses three (the name, the representation, and solicitation of old customers). Allied law: the Trade Marks Act, 1999; s. 27 of the ICA read with s. 54 of the Partnership Act; and the Income-tax Act, 1961, ss. 45, 48, 55(2)(a) on the capital-gains treatment of goodwill.

§2.6 — Procedure, limitation, court-fees, stamp and registration

⚖ The suit for dissolution — the procedural checklist
  1. Forum and valuation. A suit for dissolution and accounts is a civil suit under s. 9, CPC. Court-fees are governed by the Court-Fees Act, 1870, s. 7(iv)(f) and the Suits Valuation Act, 1887, ss. 7 and 11 — the plaintiff states the amount at which he values the relief.
  2. Parties. All the partners must be before the court; a suit for dissolution cannot proceed in the absence of a necessary party. Sue or be sued in the firm name under O. 30 rr. 1–3, CPC, and disclose the partners.
  3. Interim relief. A receiver under O. 40 r. 1; an injunction under O. 39 rr. 1–2; attachment before judgment under O. 38.
  4. The decree. O. 20 r. 15 — the court must declare the proportions of the partners' shares, direct the taking of accounts, and pass a preliminary decree first. The final decree follows the accounts.
  5. Execution. The assets are realised, the creditors paid, and the residue distributed in the s. 48 order.
  6. Arbitration. Where the deed contains an arbitration clause, the court may refer the parties under s. 8 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, and the award itself may dissolve the firm — Chandra Pandian (case 18) is precisely such a case.
⚖ Limitation — the provision that decides most of these cases
  1. Art. 5, Limitation Act 1963. "For an account and a share of the profits of a dissolved partnership" — three years, and time runs from the date of the dissolution.
  2. This is why Saligram failed. The firm dissolved on 30-08-1957; the suit was filed on 20-12-1960 — more than three years later. Everything else in that litigation was, in a sense, surplusage.
  3. The date of dissolution is therefore the single most important fact in the chapter. s. 42(a) — expiry of the term; s. 42(c) — death; s. 42(d) — adjudication; s. 43(2) — the date in the notice, or the date of communication; s. 44 — the date of the decree.
  4. Art. 113 (three years from when the right to sue accrues) governs a creditor's claim; Art. 137 applies to applications; and s. 5 allows condonation of delay on sufficient cause.
  5. Art. 12, Sch. I, Indian Stamp Act 1899. Held not attracted to a distribution of the residue under s. 48(b)(iv) — Chandra Pandian.
  6. s. 17, Registration Act 1908. Likewise not attracted — the residue is money, i.e. movable property; the same case.

CASE 15 · Saligram Ruplal Khanna and Pessumal Atalrai Shahani v. Kanwar Rajnath
AIR 1974 SC 1094 · Supreme Court (H.R. Khanna J.) · DU reader p. 77
[Partnership for a fixed term — dissolution on expiry of the term: sections 42 and 47] — the leading authority on when a firm dissolves, and on how far a partner's authority survives afterwards.
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The two plaintiff-appellants, Saligram Ruplal Khanna and Pessumal Atalrai Shahani, sued Kanwar Rajnath for dissolution of partnership and rendition of accounts. The firm carried on business as "Shri Ambernath Mills Corporation" (SAMCO). Its property comprised three mills at Ambernath — a woollen mill, a silk mill, and an oil and leather-cloth factory with land, bungalows and chawls — plus a bobbin factory at Taradeo with offices at Bombay, Ahmedabad and elsewhere. Together these were described, as in the High Court, as "Ambernath Mills".

The background. The mills originally belonged to Ahmed Abdul Karim Bros. Private Ltd. They were declared evacuee property in September 1951 and the Custodian took over management under the Administration of Evacuee Property Act, 1950. It was decided that the mills should be managed by displaced persons who had been industrialists in Pakistan. A private limited company of 31 persons was formed, each contributing ₹25,000. The appellants and the respondent were members. The respondent was a big industrialist with a verified claim of ₹23 lakhs; Appellant No. 1 had a verified claim of ₹22,000 and a disputed claim for industrial properties; Appellant No. 2 had a verified claim of about ₹30,000.

The two documents of 30 August 1952. By August 1952 all other members had dropped out, and the Custodian granted a lease of the Ambernath Mills to the respondent and the two appellants. On that day two documents were executed: (i) an agreement of partnership between the three for carrying on the business of Ambernath Mills under the lease, in the name of SAMCO; and (ii) the agreement of lease, executed by the Custodian as lessor and the three partners as lessees. The lease was to run five years from the date possession was handed over, subject to sooner determination on the contingencies in Clause 21, or on breach of condition, or "in the event of any dispute among the lessees resulting in the closure of the mills". Rent was ₹6,00,000 a year in four quarterly instalments of ₹1,50,000, and the lessees were to furnish a bank guarantee for ₹7,00,000.

Under the partnership deed each partner was to contribute ₹1,00,000 (the ₹25,000 already paid to the Custodian counting as part payment). Each had a one-third share, adjustable if fresh partners were taken in. The respondent was to be managing partner, and the appellants agreed not to interfere directly or indirectly with his management and control. Critically, the period of the partnership was five years, "being the period of said lease" (Clause 16).

Possession and the second deed. Possession was taken on 31 August 1952. Appellant No. 1 was put in charge of administration at Ambernath; Appellant No. 2, an engineer, of the properties, machinery and stores; the respondent was in overall charge. Stocks were valued by the Custodian's auditor at ₹30 lakhs. The partnership could not pay the ₹7 lakhs (or furnish the guarantee) and the sixth rent instalment was partly dishonoured — ₹1,00,000 was arranged but ₹50,000 remained unpaid.

On 12 February 1954 the Custodian served a show-cause notice. The partnership filed a writ petition on 16 February 1954 to quash it. Meanwhile, by letters of 8 February 1954, both appellants offered to have their shares reduced. On 24 February 1954 a second partnership deed was executed: Appellant No. 1 to have 3 annas, Appellant No. 2 1 anna, the respondent 12 annas; the appellants were to have no right, title or interest in the name, capital assets or goodwill; the new firm was deemed formed from 1 October 1953; the respondent was managing partner and to get 6% interest on finances arranged by him; and — the clause that decides the case — the period of partnership was to be "the outstanding period of the lease" (Clause 17).

The lease collapses. The writ petition succeeded before a Single Judge on 31 March 1954, but a Division Bench set it aside on 13 April 1954. This Court granted a certificate and a stay on 5 May 1954. The Custodian cancelled the lease on 25 May 1954, and the partnership voluntarily delivered possession on 30 June 1954. On 10 November 1955 this Court held there was no legal infirmity in the Custodian's notice. On 10 March 1955 the Central Government notified acquisition of the mills under s. 12 of the Displaced Persons (Compensation and Rehabilitation) Act, 1954. An injunction suit by all three against the Central Government and the Custodian was dismissed by the City Civil Court and, on appeal, by a Division Bench of the Bombay High Court on 14 January 1957 — the purchase agreement in Clauses 17 to 21 being held indefinite and vague and not capable of specific performance, and the acquisition being free of encumbrances. No appeal was filed, so that judgment became final.

The arbitration and the award. Arbitration proceedings had been started by an application under s. 20 of the Arbitration Act on 21 April 1955, while SAMCO was in existence. The Custodian claimed ₹30,00,000; SAMCO counter-claimed ₹17,67,080 by written statement dated 18 December 1956. On 13 November 1957 the respondent, on behalf of SAMCO, consented to an award of ₹18,00,000 by Mr Morarji Desai in favour of the Custodian. The respondent later entered an agreement with the President of India dated 14 August 1957, and the grant of the mills was made to him on 21 April 1960; he mortgaged the mills for ₹48,11,000 and took possession on 22 April 1960, the mills having lain idle for nearly six years since 30 June 1954. On 7 May 1960 he circularised the displaced persons whose claims had been transferred to him; Appellant No. 1 received a statement of account and, in September 1960, a cheque for ₹204 by way of interest.

The plaint. The appellants alleged that after 25 May 1954 the three had orally agreed not to dissolve the partnership notwithstanding the termination of the lease, and that the partnership should continue "for the purpose of acquiring on behalf and for the benefit of the said partnership the properties (Ambernath Mills) and to exploit the said industries". They pleaded that the respondent had represented he was acquiring the mills on behalf of the partnership (the Central Government desiring to deal with one individual only), that he had admitted using ₹2,00,000 of partnership funds for earnest money, that he stood in a fiduciary character and could not gain a pecuniary advantage for himself, and that the properties and profits acquired were for the benefit of the partnership. The original prayers were declarations that the partnership was still subsisting, that the mills belonged to it, and for rendition of accounts; by amendment a prayer was added that the partnership be dissolved from the date of the filing of the suit.

The written statement. The respondent denied the oral agreement. According to him the partnership stood dissolved on 10 March 1955 when the Central Government acquired the mills; the partnership funds had been used to pay creditors and were insufficient for the rest; Appellant No. 1 knew the mills were being acquired for the respondent alone; the respondent denied ever saying the ₹2,00,000 earnest money came from partnership funds; and he alleged Appellant No. 1 had asked for some benefit by way of an agency. He pleaded that the claim for accounts was barred by limitation. By an affidavit of 11 January 1961 he added that even if an oral partnership agreement were held proved, it should be taken to have been dissolved.

The courts below. The trial Judge held the oral agreement not proved; that there was no express or implied agreement to acquire the mills as a partnership asset; that s. 88 of the Indian Trusts Act could not be invoked; and that the claim was barred by limitation because the firm dissolved on 25 May 1954, or in any case on 14 January 1957 or 30 August 1957. The Division Bench repelled all four contentions, held the firm dissolved at the latest on 10 November 1955, and held the suit filed on 20 December 1960 — more than three years later — barred. Hence this appeal by special leave.

Issues
  1. Was there an express oral agreement of 25 May 1954 to continue the partnership for acquiring and exploiting the mills, so that the mills became an asset of the partnership?
  2. If not, can an implied agreement to that effect be inferred from the conduct of the parties and the correspondence?
  3. Even absent such an agreement, are the rights acquired by the respondent under his agreement of 14 August 1957 and the Presidential grant impressed with a trust in favour of the partnership under s. 88 of the Indian Trusts Act?
  4. Even if the mills are not a partnership asset, are the plaintiffs entitled to accounts of the partnership which admittedly existed under the deeds of 30 August 1952 and 24 February 1954 — and is that claim within time?
For the plaintiff-appellants (S.T. Desai)
  • At the outset, a frank concession: Mr Desai conceded he could not challenge the concurrent findings that the express oral agreement of 25 May 1954 was not proved. He also flagged, and then did not press, the s. 88 Indian Trusts Act contention.
  • The surviving case on implied agreement. The appellants no longer claimed any interest in the ownership of the mills, which now vested in the respondent. But an agreement should be inferred from the parties' conduct that the mills would be run by the respondent in partnership with the appellants, even if ownership vested in him alone.
  • The main burden of the argument: limitation. There had been no dissolution prior to the institution of the suit, so the suit for rendition of accounts was not barred. The High Court was in error in holding otherwise.
  • The consent to the award of 13 November 1957 shows that SAMCO had not been dissolved before that date — a dissolved firm does not consent to arbitration awards. The suit having been filed on 20 December 1960, three years had not run from November 1957.
  • The proposition of law relied on: a dissolution does not necessarily follow because a partnership has ceased to do business. The mills may have been idle, but the firm subsisted.
  • The equity of the case. Between June and September 1957 Appellant No. 1 entertained hopes of a benefit if the respondent acquired the mills; the respondent "while not belying those hopes and expectations took care not to make any commitment", and after the acquisition gave him a cold rebuff.
For the defendant-respondent (Mr Cooper)
  • No oral agreement. The alleged agreement of 25 May 1954 was denied outright; no such case had even been set up in the trial court, whether in the plaint or otherwise, nor before the Division Bench. What was contended below was an agreement to acquire the mills as an asset of the partnership.
  • That stand is contradicted by the lease itself. The agreement of lease showed the mills would become the absolute property not only of the three but of all persons associated with the lessees, in proportion to their compensation claims; and the lessees' rights were distinct from the proprietary interest, the lessees being at liberty to continue the lease for the unexpired residue.
  • The respondent's representation of 9 August 1954 to the Custodian enclosed letters of authority and particulars of verified claims of 30 displaced persons — implicitly, on transfer the mills would vest in all thirty, not in the three partners.
  • Two documents run counter to the appellants' case. First, the agreement of 20 September 1957 and the bond of 21 September 1957, by which Appellant No. 1 agreed to have his compensation claim of only ₹6,994 adjusted towards the price and received 6% interest on it, with an option to buy shares in a company the respondent might promote. Had he any interest in the mills, there could have been no occasion for these documents — and the amount was "wholly insignificant" compared with the price. No question would have arisen of the respondent borrowing from Appellant No. 1 to pay the price if the mills were being acquired for both. Second, the letter of 18 December 1959 to the Collector of Bombay, in which Appellant No. 1 said the sales-tax arrears were the respondent's responsibility and that he was "no more in picture" — a repudiation of liability by disclaiming his connection with the business.
  • Limitation. The firm was dissolved at the latest on 10 November 1955; the suit of 20 December 1960 was more than three years out of time.
Decision (H.R. Khanna J.) — appeal dismissed

On the implied agreement (¶¶ 20–24): rejected. No such case had been set up in the trial court or before the Division Bench. What was contended there was an agreement to acquire the mills as a partnership asset, and that stand "could plainly be not accepted when one keeps in view the agreement of lease dated August 30, 1952 as well as other documents on record". The respondent was right on both documents.

"Had Appellant No. 1 any interest in the Ambernath Mills which were being acquired by the respondent there could arise no occasion for the execution of the agreement dated September 20, 1957 and the bond dated September 21, 1957. All that was agreed by the respondent in those two documents was that in case he promoted a company for owning, running and managing of the Ambernath Mills, Appellant No. 1 would get a share of the value of half of his claim compensation of Rs 6,994. The said amount when compared to the price of Ambernath Mills was wholly insignificant."¶ 21

On the date of dissolution (¶ 25) — the heart of the case. Possession under the lease was delivered on 31 August 1952, so the five-year term expired on 30 August 1957.

"As the partnership was for a fixed period, the firm would in normal course dissolve on the expiry of the period of five years on August 30, 1957. No agreement between the partners to keep the firm in existence after the expiry of the fixed term of five years has been proved. The above provision [of section 42] makes it clear that unless some contract between the partners to the contrary is proved, the firm if constituted for a fixed term would be dissolved by the expiry of that term."¶ 25

On the consent to the award (¶¶ 30–31) — the s. 47 point. The consent of 13 November 1957 does not show the firm was undissolved. The arbitration had been commenced on 21 April 1955 when SAMCO was a running concern, and the consent was given "with a view to get the dispute between SAMCO with the Custodian finally settled. This was a necessary step for the purpose of winding up the affairs of SAMCO and to complete transaction of arbitration proceedings which had been begun but remained unfinished at the time of dissolution."

"According to Section 47 of the Indian Partnership Act, after the dissolution of a firm the authority of each partner to bind the firm, and the other mutual rights and obligations of the partners, continue notwithstanding the dissolution, so far as may be necessary to wind up the affairs of the firm and to complete transactions begun but unfinished at the time of the dissolution, but not otherwise. The word 'transaction' in Section 47 refers not merely to commercial transaction of purchase and sale but would include also all other matters relating to the affairs of the partnership. The completion of a transaction would cover also the taking of necessary steps in connection with the adjudication of a dispute to which a firm before its dissolution is a party."¶ 30

Quoting Lindley on Partnership, 13th Edn., p. 251:

"Notwithstanding a dissolution each partner can pay, or receive payment of, a partnership debt; for it is clearly settled that payment by one of several joint debtors, or to one of several joint creditors, extinguishes the debt irrespective of any question of partnership. So, again, it has been held that a continuing or surviving partner may issue a bankruptcy notice in the firm name in respect of a judgment obtained before the dissolution, and that notice to him of the dishonour of a bill of exchange is sufficient, and that he can withdraw a deposit or sell the partnership assets, or pledge them for the purpose of completing a transaction already commenced, or of securing a debt already incurred, or the overdraft on the partnership current account at the bank."Lindley, 13th Edn., p. 251, quoted at ¶ 30

And Halsbury's Laws of England, 3rd Edn., Vol. 28, p. 573:

"The proposition, in our opinion, cannot be disputed that after dissolution, the partnership subsists merely for the purpose of completing pending transactions, winding up the business, and adjusting the rights of the partners; and for these purposes, and these only, the authority, rights, and obligations of the partners continue."¶ 31

On the appellants' legal proposition (¶ 32): "a dissolution does not necessarily follow because a partnership has ceased to do business" — true, but unhelpful, because "we are not basing our conclusion of the dissolution of the firm of the parties upon the fact that the partnership had ceased to do business. On the contrary, we have arrived at the above conclusion in accordance with the principle of law that a firm constituted for a fixed term shall stand dissolved, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, on the expiry of that term."

On the "carrot dangling" (¶ 33): the respondent "kept some kind of carrot dangling before Appellant No. 1 during the delicate stage of his negotiations with the Government for the acquisition of the mills lest Appellant No. 1 did something to sabotage those efforts. After acquisition of the mills by the respondent his attitude changed and he gave a cold rebuff to Appellant No. 1." But that "may have a bearing on the question of the award of costs, but it cannot affect our decision on the point as to whether the suit is within limitation or not." The appeal was dismissed.

Principles evolved
  1. A firm constituted for a fixed term stands dissolved, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, on the expiry of that term — s. 42(a). This is the leading Indian authority. Nothing more is required: no notice, no resolution, no agreement, no decree.
  2. Where the term is defined by reference to another instrument, that instrument governs. Here the period was "five years, being the period of said lease" and later "the outstanding period of such lease" — so the five years ran from 31 August 1952, the date possession was delivered, and expired on 30 August 1957.
  3. "Transaction" in s. 47 is a wide word. It is not confined to commercial purchase and sale; it includes every matter relating to the affairs of the partnership, and the taking of necessary steps in the adjudication of a dispute to which the firm was a party before dissolution — including consenting to an arbitral award.
  4. After dissolution the partnership subsists for three purposes only — completing pending transactions, winding up the business, and adjusting the rights of the partners — "and for these purposes, and these only".
  5. Article 5 of the Limitation Act is decisive. Once the date of dissolution is fixed, three years runs from it, and everything else in the litigation becomes academic. This case is the textbook illustration.
  6. An implied agreement to continue is a question of fact, and the burden is heavy. The Court examined the contemporaneous documents and found two that "run counter" to the alleged agreement. A party who disclaims liability to a tax authority is poorly placed to assert he remained a partner.
  7. Unconscionable conduct affects costs, not limitation. The "carrot dangling" observation is a classic illustration of the difference between a moral claim and a legal one.
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

Deploy it whenever the question is "has the firm been dissolved, and if so when?" — DU 2017 Q.7, 2011 Q.7(b), 2003 Q.6(a), 2014 OC Q.4(b), 2001 Q.5(a). Write: "A firm constituted for a fixed term stands dissolved on the expiry of the term by virtue of s. 42(a), unless a contract to the contrary is proved — Saligram Ruplal Khanna v. Kanwar Rajnath, where a five-year partnership tied to a lease was held dissolved on 30 August 1957 and the suit of 20 December 1960 was held barred under Article 5 of the Limitation Act. Acts done after that date do not revive the firm if they are necessary to wind up or to complete a transaction begun but unfinished, for s. 47 preserves the partners' authority for those purposes and those only (ibid., consenting to an arbitral award on 13 November 1957)." Then add the s. 47 quote from Lindley if you have room — it is worth two marks on its own.

CASE 16 · M/s. Juggilal Kamlapat v. M/s. Sew Chand Bagree
AIR 1960 Cal 463 · Calcutta High Court (G.K. Mitter J.) · DU reader p. 87
[Dissolution of firm — no public notice under section 45(1)] — the leading authority on the proviso to s. 45(1): a partner who was not known to the person dealing with the firm is protected, and the proviso covers dissolution, not merely retirement.
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

This was an application in execution of a decree on an award made by the Bengal Chamber of Commerce on 14 June 1950, on a dispute between Juggilal Kamlapat (the award-holder) and Sew Chand Bagree, against whom the award was made. The decree was passed on 28 May 1951 for a total sum of over ₹31,000.

The award arose out of a contract entered into between Sew Chand Bagree and Juggilal Kamlapat on 25 September 1948.

The opposition. The application was opposed by Manik Chand Bagree and Moti Chand Bagree, whose case was that the firm of Sew Chand Bagree was dissolved in October 1945 by mutual consent of its partners, and that thereafter their brother Jankidas Bagree started a new business in the name of Sew Chand Bagree with which they had no concern. Sew Chand Bagree the individual was the father of the three.

The Register of Firms. From a copy of the entries in the Register of Firms maintained by the Registrar of Firms, West Bengal, it appeared that the business of Sew Chand Bagree was established in 1924, that it was formerly a joint Hindu family business, and that the partnership firm was started on 28 October 1933. The three partners shown were Manik Chand, Moti Chand and Jankidas Bagree. The document did not show any change in the constitution of the firm ever since its inception — even though dissolution was alleged to have taken place in 1945, some fourteen years before the execution proceedings.

How the dissolution was proved. The Court accepted the oral testimony of the Bagrees, corroborated by: (i) a deed of agreement prepared by Messrs Dutt and Sen and signed by the Bagree brothers; (ii) the issue of a trade licence by the Corporation of Calcutta; (iii) the opening of an account with Hindustan Commercial Bank Ltd.; (iv) a letter written to Bank of Baroda Ltd.; and (v) the contracts of Juggilal Kamlapat with Manik Chand Bagree in a name and style other than Sew Chand Bagree, which tended to prove the disruption in the family.

The admission that decided the case. Rameshwar Agarwalla (for the decree-holder) admitted that he did not know Manik Chand Bagree and Moti Chand Bagree to be partners of Sew Chand Bagree until six months or a year before the hearing — and even then only from a copy of the entries in the Register of Firms.

Issue

The Court framed it itself: "On this finding [that the firm was dissolved] the question is whether sub-section (1) of Section 45 of the Partnership Act is brought into play or whether the point is covered by the proviso to the said sub-section." Two sub-questions followed: (a) was the firm in fact dissolved in 1945? and (b) does the proviso, which speaks of a partner who "retires from the firm", apply at all where the firm has been dissolved?

For the decree-holder / award-holder (Mr Tibrewalla)
  • No dissolution was established at all. No attempt had been made to get any alteration in the constitution of the firm noted in the records of the Registrar of Firms up to the year 1959, although dissolution was said to have taken place in 1945.
  • No publicity whatsoever. The Bagrees had examined no disinterested third party to show that the dissolution was known to outsiders; no advertisement had appeared in any newspaper; there was no evidence of any circular; and no broker other than Sriratan Damani had been examined.
  • Adverse inference from the non-production of the books of account. He relied strongly on the absence of the books of Sew Chand Bagree and contended that, if produced, they would have established that the firm had never been wound up. The Court acknowledged there was "certainly some force in these contentions, specially the comment on the non-production of the books of account".
  • On construction — the main legal argument. "Mr Tibrewalla argued that the exception, if any, is limited to the case of a partner who 'retires from the firm' and does not apply to the case of a dissolution of the firm whereby the relationship of all the partners inter se is put an end to for ever."
  • Reliance on the Contract Act antecedent. He referred the Court to Garth C.J. in Chundee Churn Dutt v. Eduljee Cowasjee Bijnee, ILR 8 Cal 678, which turned on s. 264 of the Contract Act, 1872: "Persons dealing with a firm will not be affected by dissolution of which no public notice has been given unless they themselves had notice of such dissolution."
For the objectors (Manik Chand and Moti Chand Bagree)
  • The firm was dissolved in October 1945 by mutual consent — i.e. squarely within the first limb of s. 40 (consent of all the partners).
  • Thereafter Jankidas alone traded in the name of Sew Chand Bagree; the other two brothers had no concern with it.
  • They were never known to the decree-holder to be partners — a fact the decree-holder's own witness, Rameshwar Agarwalla, ultimately conceded, admitting he came to know of them only from the Register of Firms, and only six months to a year before the hearing.
  • Therefore the proviso to s. 45(1) applies, and they cannot be made liable for the decretal amount.
Decision (G.K. Mitter J.) — application dismissed against Manik Chand and Moti Chand

On dissolution — held proved. Despite the force in Mr Tibrewalla's criticisms, "I must hold on a consideration of the entire evidence adduced that the firm had been dissolved." The deed prepared by Messrs Dutt and Sen, the Corporation trade licence, the new bank account, the letter to Bank of Baroda and the contracts in a different name "all corroborate the oral testimony".

On the scheme of the Act (¶ 13). The Court set out the registration framework: registration is not compulsory, but an unregistered firm cannot sue to enforce a right arising out of a contract; the application must comply with s. 58; registration is effected under s. 59; alterations in name, changes in partners, and changes in and dissolution of the firm are recorded under ss. 60 and 63; s. 68 makes recorded statements conclusive proof against the signatory; and s. 72 prescribes the mode of public notice — notice to the Registrar, the local Official Gazette, and at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district.

On the English comparison (¶ 19). s. 45(1) without the proviso is "somewhat similar to" s. 36(1) of the English Partnership Act, 1890, but the provisions are not identical:

"Under Sec. 36(1) of the English Act an apparent member continues to be liable to an outsider unless the latter has notice of the change in the firm. But even if there be no such notice a partner who was not known to the outsider as such ceases to be liable after his retirement under Sub-sec. (3) of Sec. 36. In the Indian Act the proviso replaces sub-sec. (3) of the English section. The only difference between Sec. 36 sub-sec. (1) of the English Act and Sec. 45 sub-sec. (1) of the Indian Act seems to be that under the former any one who is an apparent member continues liable while under the latter any one who was a member, whether apparently so or not remains liable until public notice of dissolution is given."¶ 19

On the function of a proviso (¶ 20). Quoting Lord Macmillan in M. and S. M. Rly. Co. v. Bazwada Municipality, AIR 1944 PC 71:

"The proper function of a proviso is to except and deal with a case which would otherwise fall within the general language of the main enactment, and its effect is confined to that case. But for the proviso the dissolution of a firm would not have affected the liability of a partner who had gone out of it or of a dormant partner until public notice of the dissolution was given. The effect of the proviso is to except the case of a partner who was not known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner and who has retired from the firm without any public notice of dissolution being given."¶ 20, quoting AIR 1944 PC 71

On the facts (¶ 21): "These persons, therefore, were not known to Juggilal Kamlapat to have been partners of the firm and they had gone out of the firm before the contract in this case was entered into. Clearly the proviso is attracted to the facts of this case and Manik Chand Bagree and Moti Chand Bagree cannot be made liable for payment of the decretal amount."

On Tibrewalla's construction argument (¶ 22) — the heart of the judgment:

"In my view, this contention has no substance because the case of a retiring partner is expressly provided in Sec. 32 of sub-sec. (3) and the proviso to the said sub-section. It certainly would have been better if instead of the words 'retires from the firm', the legislature had used the expression 'severs his connection with the firm'. Probably the actual words used have been taken from the English Act. Without entering into speculation of this kind it is not difficult to find out what the legislature intended. It appears to me, however, that the contingencies of death, insolvency, retirement and even expulsion of a partner having already been provided for by the Indian Act in Secs. 35, 34, 32 and 33 of the Act respectively, Sec. 36 might well have dealt with the case of a dissolution of firm simpliciter."¶ 22 (section numbering as printed in the report)

On the Register of Firms (¶ 23) — the evidentiary point: "The fact that the entries in the record of the Registrar of Firms still show that Manik Chand Bagree and Moti Chand Bagree [are partners], does not help the decree-holder in this case. If the decree-holder had adduced evidence to the effect that these records had been scrutinized by it before the transaction was entered into the position might have been different."

Disposal (¶ 25): "The application will, therefore, be dismissed with costs as against Moti Chand Bagree and Manik Chand Bagree. There will be an order in terms of prayer (a) as against Jankidas Bagree."

Principles evolved
  1. The proviso to s. 45(1) covers dissolution, not merely retirement. Because retirement is already dealt with by s. 32(3), the proviso to s. 45 would be otiose if it were confined to retirement. The better expression would have been "severs his connection with the firm". This is the single most quotable holding of the case.
  2. Three categories are protected automatically, without notice — a partner who dies, one who is adjudicated insolvent, and one who, not having been known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner, retires (or, per this case, goes out on dissolution).
  3. The onus is on the person dealing with the firm to prove knowledge, in practice. The protection is lost only if the outsider knew him to be a partner. Here the outsider's own witness conceded he did not.
  4. Mere entries in the Register of Firms do not prove knowledge. The outsider must show that he scrutinised the register before contracting. Registration is not constructive notice to the world of who the partners are.
  5. The Indian s. 45(1) is wider than the English s. 36(1). Under the English provision only an apparent member continues liable; under the Indian provision "any one who was a member, whether apparently so or not" remains liable until public notice.
  6. A proviso carves out of the main enactment a case that would otherwise fall within it (Lord Macmillan). This is the standard rule of construction, and it is how Mitter J. reached his result.
  7. Dissolution may be proved by conduct. A solicitor's draft deed, a municipal trade licence, a new bank account, a letter to the bankers and contracts in a different name were enough — even though the Register of Firms was never altered and no newspaper advertisement was published.

This is the authority for every "retiring partner and the old customer" problem — DU 1998 Q.5(b) (M retires, K joins, no notice, X supplies goods and sues M), 2010 Q.6, 1992 Q.7(b). Write: "Under s. 45(1) the partners continue liable to third parties for acts which would have bound the firm before dissolution until public notice is given under s. 72. But the proviso exempts a partner who was not known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner, in respect of acts done after he ceased to be one; and although the proviso speaks of retirement, Mitter J. held in Juggilal Kamlapat v. Sew Chand Bagree that it extends to dissolution as well, since retirement is already provided for in s. 32(3) and the better expression would have been 'severs his connection with the firm'. Mere entries in the Register of Firms do not establish knowledge unless the outsider shows he scrutinised them before contracting." Then apply the onus to the given facts.

CASE 17 · Sharad Vasant Kotak v. Ramniklal Mohanlal Chawda
(1998) 2 SCC 171 · Supreme Court (K. Venkataswami J.) · DU reader p. 90
[Section 46 / registration] — induction of a partner is reconstitution, not dissolution; a reconstituted firm does not need fresh registration; and s. 69(2-A) (Maharashtra) requires only that the firm is registered and that the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms.
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The appellants are partners of a firm called "M/s Paramount Builders". The partnership was entered into on 29 November 1979 with seven individuals as partners. (The judgment elsewhere refers to the first deed as dated 28 November 1979 — both dates appear in the report.)

The firm was registered on 15 December 1980 under Registration No. 158675 with the Registrar of Firms. On 6 May 1986 Shri Mohanlal Hinji Chawda, a partner, died, and in his place his widow Smt Jijiben Mohanlal Chawda was admitted as a partner. After her admission, another deed of partnership was executed, consisting of the six old partners and the newly admitted partner — dated 20 October 1986. The induction was not brought to the notice of the Registrar of Firms by forwarding the required particulars.

Still later, on 3 November 1992, a third deed was brought into existence consisting of the same partners. Yet another partner, Smt Hemkuver B. Kotak, died in September 1994 — and that death too was not intimated to the Registrar.

The suit. While so, the first respondent gave a notice of dissolution of the firm to the appellants and filed Suit No. 5016 of 1994 on 15 December 1994 in the High Court of Judicature at Bombay on its original side, for dissolution of the partnership firm and accounts.

The amendment application. Initially the plaint did not raise the constitutional validity of s. 69(2-A) of the Act, as amended by the Maharashtra Act. The first respondent then took out Chamber Summons No. 301 of 1997 seeking permission to amend. Two amendments were sought: (i) a factual plea that the changes under the deeds of 20 October 1986 and 3 November 1992 are merely changes/modifications which do not affect the registration of the firm, so as to entitle a partner to sue for reliefs on dissolution; and (ii) in the alternative, a challenge to the vires of s. 69(2-A) as in force in Maharashtra.

The Single Judge rejected the amendment, accepting the appellants' objections: s. 69(2-A) "creates a bar on the threshold of the filing of the suit", the suit was "incompetent", and so the amendment could not be permitted. The Division Bench reversed: registration continues in force notwithstanding reconstitution, and even when dissolution takes place the registration of the firm continues; s. 69(2-A) requires registration of a firm and does not require a fresh registration each time a reconstitution or dissolution takes place. It allowed the factual amendment and refused the constitutional one as unnecessary. Hence this appeal by special leave.

The statutory provision. s. 69(2-A) (Maharashtra): "No suit to enforce any right for the dissolution of a firm or for accounts of a dissolved firm or any right or power to realise the property of a dissolved firm shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of any person suing as a partner in a firm against the firm or any person alleged to be or to have been a partner in the firm, unless the firm is registered and the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner in the firm: Provided that the requirement of registration of firm under this sub-section shall not apply to the suits or proceedings instituted by the heirs or legal representatives of the deceased partner of a firm for accounts of a dissolved firm or to realise the property of a dissolved firm."

Issue

As framed by the Supreme Court (¶ 9): "Whether on the facts of this case the suit for dissolution and account of partnership is hit by Section 69(2-A) of the Act as amended in the State of Maharashtra?" The Court noted at ¶ 11 that it was concerned with "a suit filed by a partner for dissolution and accounts" and that "no third-party rights or liabilities are involved", and at ¶ 12 that it would "confine ourselves strictly to the facts of the case … because the maintainability of the suit is the sole issue".

For the appellants (Mr Nariman, Senior Counsel)
  • When a new partner was inducted under the deed of 20 October 1986, the firm registered under the 1979 deed ceased to be on the records of the Registrar of Firms, and the registration already given will not enure to the benefit of the partnership entered on 20 October 1986. Hence s. 69(2-A) is not satisfied and the suit is not maintainable.
  • Reliance on s. 4. Bearing in mind the definition of "partnership" in s. 4, the partners — including the second respondent (the widow) — will collectively be a firm, and that firm is not registered, since the second respondent's name does not appear in the Register. The mere fact that the plaintiff's name appears is not sufficient when one partner's name is absent.
  • Reliance on the language of ss. 31 and 32. A comparison of those provisions shows that whenever a partner is inducted, the old firm ceases to exist and an altogether new firm comes into existence from the date of induction — and that new firm must obtain fresh registration.
  • Reliance on clauses 4 and 5 of the 1986 deed ("Commencement" and "Accounting Year"). Since the 1992 deed expressly used the word "reconstituted" in its preamble while the 1986 deed did not, the understanding under the 1986 deed was that the old firm ceased and a new firm was brought into existence.
  • Reliance on Lindley on the Law of Partnership, 15th Edn., p. 374: "after the admission of a new member, a new partnership is constituted, and thus special circumstances are required to be shown before the debts and liabilities of the old partnership are treated as having been undertaken by the new partnership."
  • Purposive argument. The object with which the Maharashtra Legislature introduced s. 69(2-A) would be lost if the respondents' view were accepted.
  • Procedure. Without impleading the State of Maharashtra and the Union of India, the vires of s. 69(2-A) cannot be challenged.
For the first respondent (Mr Soli J. Sorabjee, Senior Counsel)
  • There is a well-recognised distinction between dissolution and reconstitution. In the case of an incoming or outgoing partner there is only a reconstitution; in all other respects the existing firm continues with the old and new partners.
  • The structure of the Act proves it. Chapter V deals with "Incoming and Outgoing Partners" while Chapter VI separately deals with "Dissolution of a Firm". "The two are totally different concepts and cannot in law be equated with each other."
  • Even dissolution does not deregister a firm. "even when there is a dissolution of a firm, it does not cease to be a registered firm but for the purposes of Partnership Act it continues to be registered" — registration is valid till it is cancelled in a manner known to law.
  • Non-compliance attracts penalties, not deregistration. Non-compliance with ss. 61, 62 and 63 (as amended in Maharashtra) attracts s. 69-A penalties and nothing more. "As the consequence of deregistration is a drastic one, it is impermissible to hold that non-compliance with Sections 63(1) and 63(1-A) would lead to deregistration of a firm in the absence of express and clear legislative provision to that effect."
  • Substance, not phraseology. "it is the substance of the matter that is relevant to be looked into and not the phraseology employed by the parties" — the test is whether, after the 1986 deed, for all intents and purposes the firm as reconstituted was a different unit or remained the same unit despite the change in constitution. Looked at that way, the unit remained the same.
  • The dissimilarities are consequential. Clauses 4 and 5 of the 1986 deed are "really not dissimilarities but consequential and incidental changes".
  • The Rules and Forms support him — the Maharashtra Rules of 1989 and Forms E, G and H in particular.
  • Reply on purpose: failure to comply with ss. 61, 62 or 63 attracts penalties, not deregistration — the object of the amendment is not defeated.
Decision (K. Venkataswami J.) — appeal dismissed; suit held maintainable

The two conditions of s. 69(2-A) (¶ 13): (1) the firm must be registered; and (2) the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner. Both were satisfied: the 1979 partnership was duly registered and a certificate granted; the plaintiff was a founder partner whose name found a place in the register; and "there is nothing to show that at any point of time, his name has been removed from the register of firms."

On the deeds (¶ 20) — the decisive clause. "It is relevant here to note that in both the deeds it was expressly made that the death, insolvency or retirement of any partner shall not dissolve the partnership firm. On the other hand, the partner shall be entitled to carry on the partnership business on the terms and conditions mutually agreed upon by the said partners (vide clause 11). Therefore, it cannot be contended by the appellants that by reason of death of one of the partners, the existing firm stands dissolved."

On clauses 4 and 5 (¶ 20): "Clauses 4 and 5 relate to commencement of the partnership and accounting year. These are minimal changes introduced in the second deed of partnership by reason of the introduction of a new partner … and in other respects, namely, the name of the partnership firm, the address and location of the firm, the business carried on and shares allotted among the partners and duration of the partnership, are identical. Moreover a careful reading of clauses 5 and 6 of the second partnership deed will give an impression that the partners have agreed to continue the existing firm. The profits or losses for the period prior to and up to the death of the deceased partner is dealt with and provided. There is no indication that the old firm was dissolved." The word "reconstituted" in the preamble of the 1992 deed "does not make any substantial difference when we look into the substance of the three deeds".

On induction being dissolution (¶ 22):

"The contention of the learned counsel for the appellants that the induction of the new partner will result in dissolution of the firm is not also acceptable. Reliance placed on the language of Sections 31 and 32 of the Act to support the said contention will be of no avail if we look into Section 17 of the Act. Section 17(a) of the Act suggests only reconstitution of the firm where a change occurs in the constitution of the firm. Otherwise, the old firm remains the same."¶ 22

On fresh registration (¶ 25):

"A close perusal of these provisions [Sections 58, 59 and 63, Rules 3, 4, 6 and 17] with Forms 'A', 'E', 'G' and 'H' will show that there is a definite distinction between the Certificate of Registration given to the firm and any alterations to be entered in the Register of Firms. This will suggest in no uncertain terms that the changes in the constitution of the firm will not affect the registration once made. In other words, it is not required that every time a new partner is inducted, fresh registration has to be applied and obtained. However, information about changes have to be given. Failure to comply attracts penalties under Section 69-A of the Act."¶ 25

Reliance on Pratapchand Ramchand & Co., AIR 1940 Bom 257 (¶¶ 27, 29). The Bombay High Court (Bhandarkar/Beaumont C.J. line) there held, dealing with s. 63(1), that the section "evidently contemplates in the case of a dissolution of a firm by death that notwithstanding the death the firm should still be treated for the purpose of the Act as still registered"; that "No doubt it would have been logical having regard to Section 42 if the Act had so provided. But in fact it has not"; that no time-limit is fixed in any of ss. 60 to 63 for giving notice of alterations; and that on the facts the firm "remained registered notwithstanding the death of one of the original partners". The Supreme Court: "In our opinion, the view taken by the Bombay High Court and followed by the other High Courts is the right view."

On the s. 4 argument (¶ 32): "We are also not impressed by the arguments … that if the definition of Section 4 is applied to Section 69(2-A) then unless the names of all the partners find a place in the Register of Firms, the suit filed by the plaintiff cannot be sustained." The newly inducted partner's absence from the register means she may incur disabilities — "and that will not disable the plaintiff to press the suit against the firm, which was registered against the persons whose names find a place in the Register of Firms". "For the purpose of Section 69(2-A), the partnership firm will mean the firm as found in the certificate of registration and the partners as found in the Register of Firms maintained as per rule in Form 'G'."

Conclusion (¶¶ 33–34): "Our conclusion is that on the induction of the second respondent, the existing firm was only reconstituted on the facts of this case and, therefore, there is no necessity to get a fresh registration. If by virtue of non-compliance of certain mandatory provisions in not informing the Registrar of Firms about the change in the constitution of the firm, certain penalties provided in the Act alone are attracted and that will not lead to the conclusion that the registration of the firm ceased." The suit was not hit by s. 69(2-A); the appeal was dismissed, with no order as to costs.

Principles evolved
  1. Induction of a partner is reconstitution, not dissolution. s. 17(a) says only that a change in the constitution produces a reconstitution; "otherwise, the old firm remains the same". This is the direct link between Topic 3 (Chapter V) and Topic 4 (Chapter VI).
  2. A clause that death, insolvency or retirement shall not dissolve the firm is a contract to the contrary that excludes s. 42(c) and (d). Here clause 11 of both deeds did exactly that — so the death of Mohanlal did not dissolve the firm, and the induction of his widow was a mere reconstitution.
  3. No fresh registration is required on a change in the constitution. There is "a definite distinction between the Certificate of Registration given to the firm and any alterations to be entered in the Register of Firms". Information must be given under ss. 60 to 63, but non-compliance attracts penalties under s. 69-A — not deregistration.
  4. Deregistration is a drastic consequence and requires express legislative provision. Courts will not infer it from silence.
  5. Substance, not phraseology, is the test for whether a new firm has come into existence — compare the name, address, business, shares and duration; the words "reconstituted" or their absence are not decisive.
  6. s. 69(2-A) requires only two things — the firm is registered, and the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms. The absence of another partner's name does not bar the plaintiff.
  7. A firm remains registered even after dissolution. Following Pratapchand Ramchand & Co., "even when there is a dissolution of a firm, it does not cease to be a registered firm" — which is why s. 63(1) contemplates a notice of dissolution being recorded against an existing entry.
✓ How to use this case in a DU answer

Use it for three recurring questions. (a) "Is the firm at will?" (DU 2004 Q.3(ii), 1994 Q.7(a), 2001 Q.5(b)): a deed providing that death, insolvency or retirement shall not dissolve the firm is a provision for determination, so the firm is not at will and s. 43 is unavailable — Sharad Kotak applied clause 11 for exactly this purpose. (b) "Does the firm need fresh registration after a change?" (the Topic 3 / Topic 4 crossover): "A change in constitution is a reconstitution under s. 17(a), not a dissolution; no fresh registration is required and non-compliance attracts only s. 69-A penalties — Sharad Vasant Kotak v. Ramniklal Mohanlal Chawda." (c) "Is a suit for dissolution barred by s. 69?": under the Central Act, s. 69(3)(a) expressly preserves such suits even for an unregistered firm; under the Maharashtra s. 69(2-A), the two conditions are registration of the firm and the plaintiff being shown in the Register — and reconstitution does not undo the registration.

CASE 18 · S.V. Chandra Pandian v. S.V. Sivalinga Nadar
(1993) 1 SCC 589 · Supreme Court (A.M. Ahmadi J.) · DU reader p. 98
[Mode of settlement of accounts — section 48(b)(iv)] — the residue falling to be divided on dissolution is money in the eye of the law, so allocating specific properties against a partner's share is not a partition, transfer or extinguishment, and s. 17 of the Registration Act is not attracted.
Facts (verbatim from the DU case material)

The four appellants and respondents 1 and 2 were brothers carrying on business in partnership in the name and style of Messrs Sivalinga Nadar & Brothers and S.V.S. Oil Mills, both partnerships being registered under the Partnership Act, 1932. Most of the properties were acquired by the firm of Sivalinga Nadar & Brothers. The firm of M/s S.V.S. Oil Mills merely had leasehold rights in a parcel of land belonging to the first-named firm, on which the superstructure of the oil mill stood. Both partnerships were of fixed durations.

The arbitration agreement. Disputes arose between the six brothers regarding the business carried on in the aforesaid two names. For their resolution they entered into an arbitration agreement dated 8 October 1981:

"We are carrying on business in partnership together with other partners under several partnership names. We are also holding shares and managing the Public Limited Company, namely, the Madras Vanaspati Ltd., at Villupuram. Disputes have arisen among us with respect to the several business concerns, immovable and moveable properties standing in our names as well as other relatives. We are hereby referring all our disputes, the details of which would be given by us shortly to you, namely, Sri B.B. Naidu, Sri K.R. Ramamani and Sri Seetharaman. We agree to abide by your award as to our disputes."Arbitration agreement dated 8 October 1981

The award. The arbitrators directed that "the firms of M/s Sivalinga Nadar & Bros. and M/s S.V.S. Oil Mills and also the joint house property Rent Account be dissolved as at the close of business on 14 July 1984." The award was made on 9 July 1984. The arbitrators set out the properties belonging to or claimed to belong to the two firms in paras 6 to 24. Para 25 was a residuary clause: any asset left out or realised thereafter, or any liability found due other than those reflected in the account books, was to be divided and/or borne equally among the disputants. Paras 26 and 27 dealt with the use of the firm names. Para 29 referred to businesses carried on by relatives in the names of Sri Brahmasakthi Agency and Srimagal Finance Corporation — even though not carried on by the disputants, the arbitrators thought it desirable to dissolve those firms too w.e.f. 24 July 1984 "in the larger interest of peace and amity among the disputants and their relatives". Para 30 referred to properties standing in the name of the father of the six disputants. The award set out the share of the disputants, and the properties were allotted by Schedules A to F.

Para 10(c) — the clause the Division Bench relied on:

"(c) Other Lands and Buildings and House properties belonging to S.V. Sivalinga Nadar & Bros. standing in the name of the firm and/or otherwise jointly owned by the disputants. These have been allotted by us to one or other or jointly to some of the disputants as per schedules annexed hereto."Paragraph 10(c) of the award

The procedural history. After the award, O.P. No. 230 of 1984 was filed by S.V. Chandrapandian and others for a direction to the arbitrators to file the award in court, which was done. The applicants then filed Miscellaneous Application No. 3503 of 1984 for a decree in terms of the award. Before orders could be passed, O.P. Nos. 247 and 275 of 1984 were filed by S.V. Sivalinga Nadar and S.V. Harikrishnan under s. 30 of the Arbitration Act to set aside the award.

The Single Judge made the award a rule of the Court. On the stamp and registration objection he noted that the award "has been submitted for registration long ago on October 27, 1984 itself and it is stamped and if there is any deficiency, the registering authority could direct proper stamp to be affixed and therefore I feel there could be no impediment for the award being made a rule of the Court". He allowed Application No. 3505 of 1984, dismissed the setting-aside applications, and directed the parties to take steps for getting the award registered.

The Division Bench reversed. It concluded that the award required registration under s. 17(1) of the Registration Act — reasoning, at para 39, that the allotments are "exclusive to the brothers and they get independent rights of their own under the award in the properties allotted under the schedule and hence it is not a case purely of assignment of the shares in the partnership but it confers exclusive rights to the allottees"; and, at para 42, that the award also partitions certain immovable properties jointly owned by the disputants, relying on para 10(c). Since the award was not registered it could not be made the rule of the Court.

Note on the pleadings (¶ 9). Ahmadi J. recorded that "No contention was raised regarding the want of registration of the award. However, being a question of law, the learned Single Judge entertained the plea and rejected it but it found favour with the Division Bench." The objections actually taken under ss. 30 and 33 of the Arbitration Act concerned (i) the conduct of the arbitrators (alleged negligence, bias, breach of natural justice); (ii) the deliberate omission of certain properties — the shareholdings of Madras Vanaspati Ltd., stock-in-trade and cash deposits, the properties of Velayudha Perumal Nadar; and (iii) the failure to grant the objector a higher share.

Issue

As the Court framed it (¶ 9): "The question which requires determination is whether on the dissolution of the partnership the distribution of the assets of the firm comprising both moveable and immovable properties after meeting its obligations on settlement of accounts amongst the partners of the firm in proportion to their respective shares amounts to a partition of immovable properties or a relinquishment or extinguishment of a share in immovable property requiring registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act if the allocation includes immovable property of the value of Rs 100 and above? In other words the question to be considered is whether the interest of a partner in partnership assets is to be treated as moveable property or both moveable and immovable depending on the character of the property for the purposes of Section 17 of the Registration Act?"

For the appellants (those seeking to make the award a rule of the Court)
  • The award distributes the residue on dissolution, and the residue is money. The mode of settlement in s. 48 requires the partnership asset in its entirety to be converted into money, and only then disbursed; the residue under s. 48(b)(iv) is therefore cash, i.e. movable property.
  • Reliance on Addanki Narayanappa v. Bhaskara Krishtappa, AIR 1966 SC 1300 — the share of a partner in the assets of a partnership, even where the assets include immovable property, is movable property, and its assignment does not require registration.
  • Reliance on CIT v. Juggilal Kamalapat, AIR 1967 SC 401 — a deed of relinquishment of individual interests in the assets of a firm did not require registration even though the assets included immovable property.
  • Reliance on CIT v. Dewas Cine Corporation, AIR 1968 SC 676 — on dissolution, theatres returned to their original owners were returned in satisfaction of a claim to a share in the residue; there was "no sale or transfer".
  • Reliance on CIT v. Bankey Lal Vaidya, AIR 1971 SC 2270 — a partner paid an amount in lieu of his share is not taxed on capital gains; it is not a sale, exchange or transfer.
  • Reliance on Malabar Fisheries Co. v. CIT, AIR 1980 SC 176 — the firm is not a distinct legal entity; on dissolution the distribution "is nothing but a mutual adjustment of rights between the partners" and there is no transfer under s. 2(47) of the Income-tax Act.
  • On the facts: the arbitrators confined themselves to the properties belonging to the two firms and scrupulously avoided dealing with properties not belonging to the firm (see paras 15 to 18 of the award).
For the respondents (S.V. Sivalinga Nadar and others)
  • The award involves a partition of immovable properties as a consequence of the dissolution of the firms, and the value of the immovable properties which are the subject-matter of the award indisputably exceeds ₹100, so the award was compulsorily registrable in view of the mandatory language of s. 17(1) ("shall be registered").
  • The allotments are exclusive. As the Division Bench put it at para 39, "the allotments are exclusive to the brothers and they get independent rights of their own under the award in the properties allotted under the schedule and hence it is not a case purely of assignment of the shares in the partnership but it confers exclusive rights to the allottees."
  • The award also partitions jointly-owned immovable property (para 42 of the Division Bench judgment), relying on para 10(c) of the award, which refers to properties "otherwise jointly owned by the disputants".
  • Reliance on Ratan Lal Sharma v. Purshottam Harit, (1974) 1 SCC 671 — an award making an exclusive allotment of partnership assets including a factory to one partner for consideration was held compulsorily registrable.
  • Reliance on Lachhman Dass v. Ram Lal, (1989) 3 SCC 99 — an award transferring a half share in land worth more than ₹100 required registration.
  • Procedurally: the respondent objected to registration before the Sub-Registrar through his lawyer, which was why registration was withheld.
Decision (A.M. Ahmadi J.) — appeal allowed; Division Bench set aside; matters remitted

The statutory survey (¶ 7). The Court walked through ss. 4, 14, 15, 18, 19, 22, 29, 30, 31 to 38, 40 to 44, 48 and 49, and then stated the core proposition (¶ 8):

"It is well settled that the firm is not a legal entity, it has no legal existence, it is merely a compendious name and hence the partnership property would vest in all the partners of the firm. Accordingly, each and every partner of the firm would have an interest in the property or asset of the firm but during its subsistence no partner can deal with any portion of the property as belonging to him, nor can he assign his interest in any specific item thereof to anyone. … His right is merely to obtain such profits, if any, as may fall to his share upon the dissolution of the firm which remain after satisfying the liabilities set out in the various sub-clauses (i) to (iv) of clause (b) of Section 48 of the Act."¶ 8

The decisive reasoning (¶ 16) — the passage to quote:

"The mode of settlement of accounts set out in Section 48 clearly indicates that the partnership asset in its entirety must be converted into money and from the pool the disbursement has to be made as set out in clause (a) and sub-clauses (i), (ii) and (iii) of clause (b) and thereafter if there is any residue that has to be divided among the partners in the proportions in which they were entitled to a share in the profits of the firm. So viewed, it becomes obvious that the residue would in the eye of law be moveable property i.e. cash, and hence distribution of the residue among the partners in proportion to their shares in the profits would not attract Section 17 of the Registration Act."¶ 16
"Each and every partner of a firm has an undefined interest in each and every property of the firm and it is not possible to say unless the accounts are settled and the residue or surplus determined what would be the extent of the interest of each partner in the property. … In that residue … every partner has an interest and when a particular property is allocated to him in proportion to his share in the profits of the firm, there is no partition or transfer taking place nor is there any extinguishment of interest of other partners in the allocated property in the sense of a transfer or extinguishment of interest under Section 17 of the Registration Act."¶ 16

On Malabar Fisheries (¶ 15):

"the consequence of the distribution, division or allotment of assets to the partners which flows upon dissolution after discharge of liabilities is nothing but a mutual adjustment of rights between the partners and there is no question of any extinguishment of the firm's rights in the partnership assets amounting to a transfer of assets within the meaning of Section 2(47) of the Act."Malabar Fisheries Co. v. CIT, AIR 1980 SC 176, quoted at ¶ 15

On Ratan Lal Sharma (¶ 17) — distinguished. There "the award did not seek to assign the share of the respondent to the appellant but on the contrary made an exclusive allotment of the partnership asset including the factory and liabilities to the appellant, thereby creating an absolute interest on payment of consideration of Rs 17,000 plus half the amount of the realisable debts, it was held to be compulsorily registrable." But "The Court did not depart from the principle that the share of a partner in the asset of the partnership inclusive of immovable properties, is moveable property and the assignment of the share on dissolution of the partnership did not require registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act. The decision, therefore, turned on the interpretation of the award."

On Lachhman Dass (¶ 17) — held to have no bearing: that was "not a case of assignment of partnership property under a dissolution deed"; it concerned benami land, and the award "transferred half share of the appellant to the respondent and since the value thereof exceeded Rs 100, it was held that it required registration."

On the reading of the award (¶ 18). The Division Bench's conclusion "appear[s] to be based on an erroneous reading of the award". A careful reading shows the arbitrators "confined themselves to the properties belonging to the two firms in question and scrupulously avoided dealing with the properties not belonging to the firm".

"Mere statements that a certain property will now exclusively belong to one partner or the other, as the case may be, cannot change the character of the document or the nature of assignment because that would in any case be the effect on the distribution of the residue. The property falling to the share of the partner on the distribution of the residue would naturally then belong to him exclusively but so long as in the eye of law it is money and not immovable property there is no question of registration under Section 17 of the Registration Act."¶ 18

Disposal (¶ 19): "For the above reasons, we allow these appeals and set aside the impugned orders of the Division Bench and remit the matters to the Division Bench for answering the other contentions which arose in the appeal before it but which were not decided in view of its decision on the question of registration of the award. We also make it clear that the award which is pending for registration may be registered by the Sub-Registrar notwithstanding the objection raised by one of the partners, S.V. Sivalinga Nadar through his lawyer if that is the only reason for withholding registration. The appeals are allowed accordingly with costs."

Principles evolved
  1. The partnership asset in its entirety must be converted into money. That is what the s. 48 waterfall requires — which is why the residue is cash.
  2. The residue under s. 48(b)(iv) is movable property in the eye of the law, regardless of the character of the assets out of which it arose.
  3. Allocating a specific property to a partner against his share is a mutual adjustment of rights — not a partition, not a transfer, not a sale, and not an extinguishment of interest. Hence s. 17(1) of the Registration Act, 1908 is not attracted, and neither is Article 12 of Schedule I of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899.
  4. A partner's interest is a fluctuating, undefined interest in each and every asset. "He cannot predicate an earmarked share" in any specific item; the extent of his interest cannot be determined until the accounts are settled and the residue ascertained.
  5. A firm is not a legal entity — it is a compendious name. Its assets vest in all the partners jointly, which is why the "transfer" analysis fails: there is no separate owner to transfer from.
  6. An award may itself dissolve a firm — here, as at the close of business on 14 July 1984. This matters for Article 5 of the Limitation Act and for the date from which s. 45(1) liability ceases.
  7. Character follows substance. "Mere statements that a certain property will now exclusively belong to one partner … cannot change the character of the document." This is the same "substance, not phraseology" test that laterappears in Sharad Kotak.
  8. Tax consequence: no capital gains under s. 45 read with s. 2(47) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (Malabar Fisheries; Bankey Lal Vaidya).

Model Answer — Part III: Does dissolution result in winding up?

Yes — and every partner has an enforceable right to it. s. 46: on the dissolution of a firm "every partner or his representative is entitled, as against all the other partners or their representatives, to have the property of the firm applied in payment of the debts and liabilities of the firm, and to have the surplus distributed … according to their rights." Six steps: realise the assets; pay the creditors (s. 48(b)(i)); complete unfinished transactions (s. 47); render accounts; distribute the residue (s. 48(b)(iv)); and give public notice (ss. 45, 72).

Contrast the retirement of a single partner: no winding up follows, because the firm continues. That is the practical consequence of the s. 39 distinction.

1. The gateway question is always s. 7. Note the word "or": satisfying either limb is enough to make the firm one at will. It follows that a deed which provides for determination — in whatever form — takes the firm out of s. 7, and therefore out of s. 43.

  2. To all the other partners — service on one of three dissolves nothing.
  3. An intention to dissolve the firm — a notice of retirement is not a notice of dissolution. This is the most common defect in DU problems.

Model Answer — Part I: Compulsory dissolution, s. 41

Two limbs, one proviso, and no contracting out.

  1. (a) Insolvency of all, or of all but one. The trigger is the adjudication, not mere inability to pay. The arithmetic matters: in a two-partner firm the insolvency of either dissolves it; in a five-partner firm, four must be adjudicated. The reason is conceptual — one person cannot be "partners" with himself. The date is the date of the order of adjudication. Allied: IBC 2016, ss. 95–100 (creditors' application), s. 79 (bankruptcy debts); Provincial Insolvency Act 1920; Presidency-Towns Insolvency Act 1909; and s. 69(3)(b), which preserves the Official Assignee's and Receiver's powers.
  2. (b) Supervening illegality. Any event making it unlawful for the business of the firm to be carried on (the licence is cancelled, the trade is banned by a new statute) or for the partners to carry it on in partnership (a partner becomes an alien enemy, or is disqualified by a professional conduct rule). Allied: s. 23, ICA 1872 (unlawful object); s. 56, ICA (impossibility).
  3. The proviso — a severance rule. Where more than one separate adventure or undertaking is carried on, the illegality of one does not dissolve the firm as respects the lawful ones. The test is whether the undertakings are genuinely separate — separate capital, books and management.

The distinguishing feature: s. 41 has no "subject to contract" opening. s. 42 does. That single drafting difference is the fastest way to separate the two sections in an answer, and it is what makes s. 41 compulsory.

Model Answer — Part III: The two problems

2011 Q.3(a) — firm of four, three adjudicated insolvent, D carried on. Answer: The firm stood dissolved on the date of the order of adjudication of the third partner, by virtue of s. 41(a) — "all the partners or all the partners but one". A suit for dissolution is therefore not maintainable as a suit for dissolution: there is nothing left to dissolve. But that is not the end of it. Three points earn the marks: (i) what D did afterwards was, if at all, a step in winding up under s. 47 — and any business he carried on beyond winding up or completion of unfinished transactions was his personal venture; (ii) a suit for accounts of the dissolved firm is nonetheless maintainable, and is expressly saved from the registration bar by s. 69(3)(a); and (iii) the suit must be filed within three years of the date of dissolution under Article 5 of the Limitation Act — and this, on the Saligram pattern, is usually the real answer.

2014 OC Q.4(b) / 1997 Q.3(b) — banking firm; partner convicted of ticketless travel. Answer: Work through it in order.

  1. Not s. 41(b). s. 41(b) requires an event making it unlawful for the business to be carried on. A ticketless-travel conviction does not make banking unlawful.
  2. Not s. 42. None of the four contingencies is present.
  3. Not s. 43, unless the firm is at will — check s. 7.
  4. s. 44(c) is the provision. The relevant ground is "conduct which is likely to affect prejudicially the carrying on of the business, regard being had to the nature of the business". A banking firm is a business of trust; a conviction involving dishonesty goes directly to fitness. Argue both ways and then conclude that the better view is that the ground is made out — but note that the remedy is discretionary ("the Court may dissolve"), and that the dissolution, if decreed, dates from the decree.
  5. Crucially, the firm does not dissolve automatically. Until a decree is passed it subsists, and s. 45(1) continues to bind all the partners for acts done in the meantime.

Three years of loss in a ten-year firm is a strong case; fifteen years in a twenty-year firm is stronger still. But note: (i) the remedy is discretionary, so the court may refuse where the losses are temporary or remediable; and (ii) a mere fall in profits is not enough — the test is loss, not reduced profit. Model Answer — Part I: The rule, s. 45(1)

"Notwithstanding the dissolution of a firm the partners continue to be liable as such to third parties for any act done by any of them which would have been an act of the firm if done before the dissolution, until public notice is given of the dissolution." Five ingredients: (i) the firm has been dissolved; (ii) an act is done by any partner; (iii) the act is one which would have bound the firm before dissolution (s. 19(2) implied authority); (iv) liability is "as such", i.e. jointly and severally (s. 25); and (v) it continues only until public notice.

Model Answer — Part II: The proviso — the three automatic exceptions

The estate of a partner who (1) dies; (2) is adjudicated an insolvent; or (3) not having been known to the person dealing with the firm to be a partner, retires from the firm — "is not liable under this section for acts done after the date on which he ceases to be a partner." The protection is automatic: no notice is required.

§ The construction point that decides these problems

The proviso says "retires from the firm". In M/s Juggilal Kamlapat v. M/s Sew Chand Bagree, AIR 1960 Cal 463 (case 16), Mr Tibrewalla argued that the exception is "limited to the case of a partner who 'retires from the firm' and does not apply to the case of a dissolution of the firm whereby the relationship of all the partners inter se is put an end to for ever". G.K. Mitter J. rejected this: the case of a retiring partner is expressly provided for in s. 32(3) and its proviso, "[i]t certainly would have been better if instead of the words 'retires from the firm', the legislature had used the expression 'severs his connection with the firm'", and the contingencies of death, insolvency, retirement and expulsion being already dealt with in ss. 35, 34, 32 and 33, s. 45 must deal with "the case of a dissolution of firm simpliciter".

2010 Q.6 — first limb. Model Answer — Part I: s. 46, the right to winding up

"On the dissolution of a firm every partner or his representative is entitled, as against all the other partners or their representatives, to have the property of the firm applied in payment of the debts and liabilities of the firm, and to have the surplus distributed among the partners or their representatives according to their rights."

  1. The word is "entitled" — a right, not a discretion. No partner can be compelled to leave the assets in another's hands.
  2. "Every partner or his representative" — the legal representatives of a deceased partner (ss. 2(c), 35, 42(c)) and the Official Assignee or Receiver of an insolvent partner (ss. 34, 41(a), 69(3)(b)) stand in his shoes.
  3. Two limbs — debts first, then the surplus — and what "their rights" are is spelled out in s. 48. ss. 46 and 48 are read together.

The six steps of winding up: (1) realise the assets, including goodwill (s. 55(1)); (2) pay the creditors (s. 48(b)(i), s. 49); (3) complete unfinished transactions (s. 47); (4) render accounts (ss. 9, 26; O. 20 r. 15, CPC); (5) distribute the residue (s. 48(b)(iv)); and (6) give public notice (ss. 45, 72).

Model Answer — Part II: s. 47, the continuing authority

"After the dissolution of a firm the authority of each partner to bind the firm, and the other mutual rights and obligations of the partners, continue notwithstanding the dissolution, so far as may be necessary to wind up the affairs of the firm and to complete transactions begun but unfinished at the time of the dissolution, but not otherwise."

§ The authority for the width of "transaction"

In Saligram Ruplal Khanna v. Kanwar Rajnath, AIR 1974 SC 1094 (case 15), the respondent had consented on 13 November 1957 to an arbitral award — after the firm had dissolved on 30 August 1957. It was argued that this kept the firm alive. H.R. Khanna J. held not: the consent was "a necessary step for the purpose of winding up the affairs of SAMCO and to complete transaction of arbitration proceedings which had been begun but remained unfinished at the time of dissolution", and "[t]he word 'transaction' in Section 47 refers not merely to commercial transaction of purchase and sale but would include also all other matters relating to the affairs of the partnership" — including "the taking of necessary steps in connection with the adjudication of a dispute to which a firm before its dissolution is a party". His Lordship quoted Lindley, 13th Edn., p. 251 and Halsbury, 3rd Edn., Vol. 28, p. 573 — the latter for the proposition that "after dissolution, the partnership subsists merely for the purpose of completing pending transactions, winding up the business, and adjusting the rights of the partners; and for these purposes, and these only, the authority, rights, and obligations of the partners continue."

The fresh-contract problem. Where a partner, after dissolution, enters a new contract, the firm is not bound — the four words "but not otherwise" end the authority. It is his personal act and he alone is liable. But if he did it within the apparent authority of the firm and the supplier had no notice of the dissolution (no public notice under s. 72), s. 45(1) makes all the partners liable to that third party — with a right of indemnity against the acting partner under s. 13(f). This is how ss. 45 and 47 interlock, and it is the point most answers miss.

  1. Quote "so far as may be necessary … but not otherwise". The necessity test is the section.
  2. Distinguish winding up from a new venture. Ask: was this step needed to finish something already begun, or was it something new?
  3. Cite Saligram for the width of "transaction" and the Lindley / Halsbury quotations.

Model Answer — Part VI: The registration question

No — the award is not compulsorily registrable. In S.V. Chandra Pandian v. S.V. Sivalinga Nadar, (1993) 1 SCC 589 (case 18), A.M. Ahmadi J. held that the s. 48 mode of settlement "clearly indicates that the partnership asset in its entirety must be converted into money", so "the residue would in the eye of law be moveable property i.e. cash, and hence distribution of the residue among the partners in proportion to their shares in the profits would not attract Section 17 of the Registration Act". Model Answer — Part I: The registration bar and its exceptions

The general rule — s. 69(1) and (2). No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract, and no suit by or on behalf of a firm against a third party, may be instituted by or on behalf of any person suing as a partner against the firm or any person alleged to be or to have been a partner, unless the firm is registered and the person suing is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner.

The exceptions — s. 69(3). The bar does not apply to:

  1. (a) the enforcement of any right or power to realise the property of a dissolved firm;
  2. (a) any suit or proceeding for dissolution of a firm, or for accounts of a dissolved firm;
  3. (b) the powers of an Official Assignee, Receiver or Court under the insolvency law to realise the property of an insolvent partner;
  4. (4)(a) a firm or partner who has no place of business in India; and
  5. (4)(b) suits or claims of the value of less than ₹100, where the firm has no place of business in the towns specified.

Answer to the question: YES, a suit for dissolution of an unregistered firm is maintainable — s. 69(3)(a) in terms preserves it. So does a suit for accounts of a dissolved firm. Add two refinements: (i) the same is true under the Maharashtra s. 69(2-A), provided the firm is registered and the plaintiff is shown in the Register — and registration is not lost by a reconstitution (Sharad Vasant Kotak, case 17); and (ii) the point was made expressly in Sharad Kotak at ¶ 30: "Section 69(3)(a) of the Central Act enables the partners of both registered and unregistered firms to file a suit for dissolution and/or accounts."

Model Answer — Part II: The Saligram pattern — limitation
  1. Find the date of dissolution. Clause 16 fixed the term at five years, "being the period of lease"; possession was delivered on 31 August 1952, so the term expired on 30 August 1957. Under s. 42(a) the firm "would in normal course dissolve on the expiry of the period of five years" — and "[n]o agreement between the partners to keep the firm in existence after the expiry of the fixed term … has been proved."
  2. Deal with the apparent act of continuance. The consent to the award three months later does not revive the firm: under s. 47 it was "a necessary step for the purpose of winding up the affairs" and to complete a transaction "begun but remained unfinished at the time of dissolution". The word "transaction" "refers not merely to commercial transaction of purchase and sale".
  3. Apply Article 5. Three years from the date of dissolution. A suit filed more than three years after 30 August 1957 is barred.
  4. Add the equity point and dismiss it. Any unconscionable conduct "may have a bearing on the question of the award of costs, but it cannot affect our decision on the point as to whether the suit is within limitation or not."
Model Answer — Part III: The Sharad Kotak pattern — reconstitution
  1. Is the induction a dissolution? No. "Section 17(a) of the Act suggests only reconstitution of the firm where a change occurs in the constitution of the firm. Otherwise, the old firm remains the same."
  2. Is fresh registration required? No. "there is a definite distinction between the Certificate of Registration given to the firm and any alterations to be entered in the Register of Firms … it is not required that every time a new partner is inducted, fresh registration has to be applied and obtained."
  3. What is the consequence of non-intimation? Only penalties under s. 69-A. "As the consequence of deregistration is a drastic one, it is impermissible to hold that non-compliance with Sections 63(1) and 63(1-A) would lead to deregistration of a firm in the absence of express and clear legislative provision to that effect."
  4. Is the suit maintainable? Yes. Following Pratapchand Ramchand & Co., AIR 1940 Bom 257, "even when there is a dissolution of a firm, it does not cease to be a registered firm", and the two conditions of s. 69(2-A) are satisfied.

  1. Step 1 — Has the firm been dissolved, and on what DATE? Run ss. 40 to 44 in order. Everything else depends on this.

The ten mistakes that cost the most marks

! Read this the night before
  1. Writing "the firm is dissolved under s. 39". s. 39 is a definition. Always cite the operating section — 40, 41, 42, 43 or 44.
  2. Forgetting that s. 42 is "subject to contract" and s. 41 is not. That is the whole difference between contingent and compulsory.
  3. Applying s. 43 without checking s. 7. If the deed provides for duration or determination, the firm is not at will and notice is useless.
  4. Treating s. 44 dissolution as automatic. It dates from the decree, and the remedy is discretionary.
  5. Overlooking "other than the partner suing" in grounds (b) to (e) of s. 44.
  6. Confusing retirement with dissolution in a s. 43 notice. The notice must express an intention to dissolve the firm.
  7. Not stating the three steps of public notice under s. 72 — Registrar (registered firms), Gazette, and a vernacular newspaper.
  8. Missing the onus under the proviso to s. 45(1). The partner must show the third party did not know him; register entries alone prove nothing.
  9. Running the s. 48 waterfall in the wrong order, or using the capital ratio for the residue. Creditors → advances → capital → residue; and the residue follows profits.
  10. Ignoring limitation. Article 5, three years from the date of dissolution. On the Saligram pattern this is usually the decisive point, and a whole answer can be thrown away by omitting it.
