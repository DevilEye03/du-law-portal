Part A — Who is an “Unpaid Seller”? The Rights Conferred by Chapter V

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 · Chapter V · Sections 45 and 46 · read with s. 2(8), (10), (11), (13) and ss. 55–61

A.1 Why the law gives the seller remedies against the goods

A contract of sale has two sides. The buyer's remedy for non-delivery is to sue the seller personally. But when the seller is the aggrieved party, a personal action against the buyer may be worthless — the buyer may be a man of straw, or may have become insolvent, or may be a company in liquidation. English commercial law therefore gave the unpaid seller a set of proprietary, self-help remedies that operate on the goods themselves, even though the property in those goods has already passed to the buyer and even though the seller has already parted with possession. Those remedies are codified, for India, in Chapter V, ss. 45 to 54 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.

The logic of the Chapter is captured in a single sentence: the seller may hold on to what is still in his hands (lien), may claw back what is still moving towards the buyer (stoppage in transitu), and may finally turn the goods into money (re-sale) — but the contract itself is not automatically wiped out by his doing so (s. 54(1)).

§A whole chapter in one line

“Notwithstanding that the property in the goods may have passed to the buyer, the unpaid seller of goods, as such, has by implication of law — (a) a lien on the goods for the price while he is in possession of them; (b) in case of the insolvency of the buyer a right of stopping the goods in transit after he has parted with the possession of them; (c) a right of re-sale as limited by this Act.” — s. 46(1), verbatim

Where the Chapter came from

When the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 was enacted it consolidated the scattered provisions of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 relating to sale, and in doing so it repealed ss. 76 to 123 of the Contract Act (originally through s. 65 of the Sale of Goods Act; s. 65 itself was later removed by the Repealing Act of 1938 as a spent provision). Sections 45–54 are substantially the same as ss. 38–48 of the English Sale of Goods Act, 1893 (now ss. 38–48 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1979). Indian courts therefore still read English decisions on those sections as highly persuasive, which is why Valpy v. Gibson, Whitehead v. Anderson, Kemp v. Falk, Lickbarrow v. Mason, Gallagher v. Shilcock and R.V. Ward Ltd. v. Bignall appear in every Indian textbook on this topic.

A.2 Section 45 — “Unpaid seller” defined ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 45 (verbatim)

45. “Unpaid seller” defined.—(1) The seller of goods is deemed to be an “unpaid seller” within the meaning of this Act—

(a) when the whole of the price has not been paid or tendered;

(b) when a bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument has been received as conditional payment, and the condition on which it was received has not been fulfilled by reason of the dishonour of the instrument or otherwise.

(2) In this Chapter, the term “seller” includes any person who is in the position of a seller, as, for instance, an agent of the seller to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed, or a consignor or agent who has himself paid, or is directly responsible for, the price.

Breaking the definition into its elements

ElementWhat it requiresWhy the courts read it that way
“The seller of goods”The person must be the seller, or a person expressly treated as a seller by s. 45(2). A buyer who has paid and later returns the goods is not an unpaid seller.Chapter V creates sellers' remedies; a buyer's remedy for money paid is restitution, not lien.
“Is deemed to be”The status is a legal fiction confined to “the meaning of this Act”. It is not the same thing as being a creditor for the price.It unlocks only ss. 46–54. Rights under other statutes (e.g. IBC, SARFAESI) are unaffected.
“The whole of the price has not been paid or tendered” (limb a)Even one rupee left unpaid keeps the seller within the section; and a mere offer to pay which is not legally a valid tender does not help the buyer.Lien under s. 47 secures the whole price; it is one indivisible lien for the price, and not a series of part-liens.
“Bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument received as conditional payment” (limb b)If the instrument was taken as conditional payment (the ordinary presumption), the seller is unpaid until it is honoured; if it was taken as absolute payment (rare, and needs proof), the seller loses the status.Payment by negotiable instrument is conditional until realisation; on dishonour the seller's rights revive as if the instrument had never been given.
“…or otherwise” in limb (b)The condition may fail for reasons other than dishonour — e.g. the instrument is void, the acceptor becomes insolvent, or the instrument becomes unenforceable.The words are wide enough to cover every failure of the conditional payment, not merely a technical dishonour.
“Position of a seller” (s. 45(2))An agent of the seller to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed; a consignor; or an agent who has himself paid, or is directly responsible for, the price.This mirrors s. 38(2) of the English Act. It protects financing middlemen — confirming houses, export agents, commission agents and freight-forwarders who pay the principal's supplier.

The words “insolvent”, “price”, “property” and “seller” — the four definitions that decide this topic

Chapter V uses words that are defined elsewhere in the Act. Examiners expect these definitions to be quoted.

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 2 (the clauses that matter here), verbatim

2(8) a person is said to be “insolvent” who has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business, or cannot pay his debts as they become due, whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not;

2(10) “price” means the money consideration for a sale of goods;

2(11) “property” means the general property in goods, and not merely a special property;

2(13) “seller” means a person who sells or agrees to sell goods;

2(4) “document of title to goods” includes a bill of lading, dock warrant, warehouse keeper's certificate, wharfingers' certificate, railway receipt, multimodal transport document, warrant or order for the delivery of goods and any other document used in the ordinary course of business as proof of the possession or control of goods, or authorising or purporting to authorise, either by endorsement or by delivery, the possessor of the document to transfer or receive goods thereby represented.

★The most under-used definition in this topic: s. 2(8)

Insolvency under the Sale of Goods Act does not require an adjudication. A buyer is “insolvent” if he has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business, or cannot pay his debts as they become due — “whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not”. So the seller does not have to wait for an insolvency petition to be filed, or for a company to be admitted to insolvency resolution. What the seller needs is evidence that the buyer has stopped paying debts as they fall due. This definition is the gateway to s. 47(1)(c) (lien) and s. 50 (stoppage in transit).

Modern overlay: for companies and LLPs, insolvency is now regulated by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (Part II for corporate debtors; Part III for individuals and partnerships, which is to be notified, until which the Presidency-towns Insolvency Act, 1909 and the Provincial Insolvency Act, 1920 continue). See Part F, F.6.

Is he an unpaid seller? — eight worked verdicts

2A delivers goods on 30-day credit. Fifteen days later, before the credit period expires, B is declared insolvent and its bank accounts are frozen.Unpaid seller, and the lien revives under s. 47(1)(c) notwithstanding the running credit — insolvency ends the period of grace.
3A takes B's cheque for ₹ 2,00,000 “in payment”. The cheque is dishonoured and returned with the remark “insufficient funds”.Unpaid seller under s. 45(1)(b). The cheque was conditional payment; the condition failed on dishonour. (See also ss. 138 and 118 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881.)
4A takes a bearer demand draft from B and hands over the goods. The bank later fails before paying A.Arguably an unpaid seller — the instrument was received as conditional payment and the condition “failed otherwise” than by dishonour, which is expressly covered by the words “or otherwise”. The seller's remedy is usually against the bank/issuer first.
5A has obtained a decree for ₹ 5,00,000 against B for the price, which is still unsatisfied. A still holds part of the goods.Unpaid seller. s. 49(2) confirms that a lien is not lost merely because the seller has obtained a decree for the price.
6A, an export house, buys goods from an Indian mill, endorses the bill of lading to itself and ships to a foreign buyer. The foreign buyer fails to pay A. A's own payment to the mill is outstanding.Unpaid seller for the limited purpose of s. 45(2) — an agent to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed, and a person “directly responsible for” the price, is placed in the position of a seller.

A holds the goods.A is not an unpaid seller of goods — the price was paid, so the Chapter does not apply. Ten instalments are unpaid.Not an “unpaid seller” — there was no sale: by s. 8 of the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 property passes to the hirer only on completion of the purchase. A.3 Section 46 — The unpaid seller’s rights ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 46 (verbatim)

46. Unpaid seller’s rights.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any law for the time being in force, notwithstanding that the property in the goods may have passed to the buyer, the unpaid seller of goods, as such, has by implication of law—

(a) a lien on the goods for the price while he is in possession of them;

(b) in case of the insolvency of the buyer a right of stopping the goods in transit after he has parted with the possession of them;

(c) a right of re-sale as limited by this Act.

(2) Where the property in goods has not passed to the buyer, the unpaid seller has, in addition to his other remedies, a right of withholding delivery similar to and co-extensive with his rights of lien and stoppage in transit where the property has passed to the buyer.

Breaking s. 46 into its elements

WordsEffectPoint to note
“Subject to the provisions of this Act”Chapter V rights are subject to the Act's own limits — e.g. s. 53 (sub-sale/pledge with a document of title defeats the lien), s. 49 (termination of lien), s. 54 (re-sale “as limited by this Act”).Never answer a lien question without asking whether s. 53 has intervened.
“and of any law for the time being in force”Other statutes prevail where they say so — e.g. a moratorium under s. 14 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016; the railway administration's own statutory lien under the Railways Act, 1989.A modern, high-scoring point (see Part F, F.6).
“notwithstanding that the property … may have passed to the buyer”The rights exist even though the buyer is now the owner. This is what makes them extraordinary: they cut across the buyer's ownership, not because the seller owns the goods, but because the price is unpaid.Contrast: where property has not passed, the seller needs no lien at all — s. 46(2).
“by implication of law”The rights are implied, not contractual. But being implied terms, they can be negatived or varied by express agreement, course of dealing or usage — s. 62.Hence a contract may provide “no lien”, or “payment against delivery only”, or “cash before despatch”.
(a) “a lien on the goods for the price while he is in possession of them”Practical, possessory remedy. Lasts only so long as possession lasts (ss. 47–49).The seller's lien is a special property in the goods, not the general property — s. 2(11).
(b) “in case of the insolvency of the buyer … stopping the goods in transit”Available only when the seller has parted with possession and only on the buyer's insolvency (s. 2(8)).Not available merely because the buyer is delaying payment while solvent.
(c) “a right of re-sale as limited by this Act”The re-sale right is not free-standing: it must be earned under s. 54 (perishable goods, or notice plus reasonable time) or reserved by contract under s. 54(4).Re-sale without complying with s. 54 has serious consequences — the buyer keeps the profit and the seller loses his damages.
s. 46(2) “right of withholding delivery”Where property has not passed, the seller may simply refuse to deliver until paid — “similar to and co-extensive with” lien and stoppage.Examiners love this sub-section because it is so often forgotten.

!The most common exam error in this topic

Students write that “the unpaid seller can sue for the price under s. 45”. There is no such right in s. 45. Section 45 only defines the unpaid seller; ss. 46–54 give him rights against the goods; the right to sue for the price is in s. 55, and the right to damages for non-acceptance is in s. 56. A second common error is to treat the re-sale right in s. 54 as an automatic consequence of the lien; it is not — s. 54(2) requires either perishable goods or a notice, plus refusal for a reasonable time, plus a re-sale within a reasonable time.

The three pre-conditions on which everything in Chapter V depends

1 · He must be “unpaid”

The whole price, or part of it, or a dishonoured instrument, or an unfulfilled condition of payment — s. 45(1). No unpaid price, no Chapter V.

2 · He must have an immediate right to sue for the price

The lien presupposes that the price is payable. While a credit term is running and the buyer is solvent, the lien is in suspense (Grice v. Richardson); the buyer's insolvency accelerates it (s. 47(1)(c)).

3 · The goods must still be reachable

In the seller's possession (lien), in transit with an independent carrier (stoppage), or lawfully recoverable after default (re-sale, s. 54). Once a third party has acquired rights under s. 53, Chapter V is exhausted.

DDeeper point: why this is a “self-help” remedy, and why it survives insolvency

The lien and the right of stoppage are possessory remedies. They give the seller something close to security without any registration, court order or contractual charge. That is precisely why they are valuable against an insolvent buyer: an ordinary unsecured creditor lines up with the general body of creditors, but an unpaid seller who has retained possession (lien) or who can lawfully recapture the goods while they are still in transit (stoppage) never lets the goods enter the insolvent estate at all. The seller is not recovering a debt; he is refusing to part with, or taking back, his own goods.

Two limits keep this from being unfair to the buyer's other creditors: (i) the seller must act before the buyer or his agent takes delivery (s. 51), and (ii) the seller must respect rights created by the buyer under a document of title (s. 53 proviso) — the point on which Morvi Mercantile Bank v. Union of India and Mercantile Bank of India Ltd. v. Central Bank of India Ltd. are the leading authorities.

Part B — The Unpaid Seller’s Lien: Sections 47, 48 and 49

Retaining possession until the price is paid — the first and strongest of the three rights against the goods

B.1 What a lien is — and what it is not

A lien is the right of a person in possession of goods to retain them until a debt due to him is paid. The unpaid seller's lien is therefore purely possessory. It gives him no right to sell, no right to use the goods, and no right to sue third parties in respect of them. It is a right to say one sentence: “not until I am paid.” Its value depends entirely on one physical fact: the seller must still have the goods.

It IS
  • A particular lien — it secures the price of those very goods, not a general balance of account (contrast the general lien of bankers, factors, wharfingers and attorneys under s. 171 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872).
  • A special property in the goods, not the general property (s. 2(11)).
  • Indivisible — the seller may retain the whole of the goods until the whole of the price is paid; the buyer cannot tender a proportionate sum and demand a proportionate part.
  • Available even if the seller holds the goods as the buyer's agent or bailee — s. 47(2).
It is NOT
  • A right to sell the goods. That is the separate right under s. 54, and it must be earned on its own terms. (Contrast the pawnee's power of sale under s. 176 of the Contract Act.)
  • A right that survives loss of possession — the lien dies with possession (s. 49).
  • A right against the buyer personally — that is s. 55 or s. 56.
  • Available when the credit period is still running and the buyer is solvent — during that window the lien is in suspense, not destroyed.

B.2 Section 47 — Seller’s lien ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 47 (verbatim)

47. Seller’s lien.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the unpaid seller of goods who is in possession of them is entitled to retain possession of them until payment or tender of the price in the following cases, namely:—

(a) where the goods have been sold without any stipulation as to credit;

(b) where the goods have been sold on credit, but the term of credit has expired;

(c) where the buyer becomes insolvent.

(2) The seller may exercise his right of lien notwithstanding that he is in possession of the goods as agent or bailee for the buyer.

The three cases — with the reason for each

CaseSituationReason and illustration
(a) No stipulation as to creditA pure cash sale: “goods to be paid for on delivery”.The buyer never acquired a right to possession without payment. The lien is the natural counterpart of a cash sale. Illustration: a shop sells a washing machine on “cash on delivery”; the delivery boy carries it back if the buyer does not pay.
(b) Credit term expiredGoods sold on 30 days' credit; the 30 days have run out and the price is still unpaid.Once the price becomes due and payable, the seller's right to be paid revives in the form of a possessory right. The credit was given on the faith of the buyer's solvency; when that fails, the seller is not bound to deliver goods for which he will merely have a claim in the insolvency. This is the case that Ex parte Chalmers settled in England. Illustration: a cement dealer sells on 45 days' credit; on day 20 the buyer's other cheques start bouncing and his factory is sealed; the dealer may retain the undelivered cement.

§Sub-section (2): lien in possession as agent or bailee

A seller who has sold the goods may nevertheless continue to hold them — in his own godown, in a warehouse, or as the buyer's caretaker — after the property has passed. Sub-section (2) makes sure that this change in the character of his possession does not destroy his lien. However, the sub-section applies only to possession as agent or bailee of the buyer in respect of the same goods: if the seller later takes the goods back for a different purpose (e.g. repairs), the lien does not revive — Eduljee v. Café John Bros., AIR 1940 Nag 311.

B.3 The case law on lien

Bloxam v. Sanders (1825) 4 B. & C. 941
Court of King’s Bench · Bayley and Holroyd JJ. · the fountainhead of the seller’s lien WEB
Property may have passed; possession is what pays.
Facts

Goods (dealings in hops) were sold and the property in them passed to the buyer, but the seller retained possession because the price had not been paid; the buyer gave bills which were dishonoured, and the buyer then went bankrupt. The trustee in bankruptcy demanded the goods from the seller, who refused to hand them over except against payment, and the dispute came up in the form of an action for conversion or trover.

Issue

Can an unpaid seller who has retained possession refuse to deliver the goods until the price is paid, even though the property in the goods has passed to the buyer and the buyer has become bankrupt?

Decision

Yes. The seller's lien was upheld. Bayley J. explained that a seller who has not been paid has a lien on the goods in his possession, and that the lien may be enforced notwithstanding that the property has passed and notwithstanding the buyer's bankruptcy; the buyer (or his assignee) cannot demand possession until he pays or tenders the price.

Principle evolved

The seller's lien is a possessory right which survives the passing of property, and it is not destroyed by the buyer's insolvency. This is the principle that ss. 46 and 47 of the Indian Act now codify — and the reason s. 47 lists the buyer's insolvency as a distinct ground for lien.

Credit is given on the faith of solvency; when solvency fails, credit fails with it.
Facts

A contract for the sale of iron provided for payment on a credit term. Before the term had expired the buyer committed an act of bankruptcy. The goods were still in the seller's hands when the trustee in bankruptcy claimed them for the estate, and the seller refused to deliver unless the price was paid.

Issue

Does a stipulation for credit survive the buyer's insolvency, so as to compel the seller to deliver without payment and then prove in the bankruptcy for the price?

Arguments
For the trustee in bankruptcy
  • The contract gave credit; the seller must perform his part and take his place among the creditors.
  • Bankruptcy does not put an end to the contract (semble), so the stipulation as to credit should be given effect.
For the seller
  • Credit was extended on the assumption that the buyer was solvent; the bankruptcy has destroyed the foundation of the arrangement.
  • The seller still had the goods; he was entitled to hold them until the price was paid.
Decision

The seller prevailed. The insolvency of the buyer revives the unpaid seller's lien, and the trustee cannot compel delivery without payment, even though the credit period has not yet run out.

Principle evolved

An agreement to give credit is not an absolute waiver of the lien; it merely suspends it for the period of credit on the assumption of the buyer's continued solvency. The buyer's insolvency therefore cancels the suspension and restores the lien — codified in s. 47(1)(c).

△Bloxam v. Sanders and Ex parte Chalmers in one sentence each

Bloxam v. Sanders: the lien exists even though the property has passed. Ex parte Chalmers: the lien, though suspended by an agreement to give credit, revives on the buyer's insolvency. Together they are the entire core of s. 47(1)(c).

B.4 Section 48 — Part delivery ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 48 (verbatim)

48. Part delivery.—Where an unpaid seller has made part delivery of the goods, he may exercise his right of lien on the remainder, unless such part delivery has been made under such circumstances as to show an agreement to waive the lien.

The section rests on a simple idea. Delivery of part of the goods is not a delivery of the whole, and the buyer cannot infer from it that the seller has abandoned his security over the rest. But if the circumstances of the part delivery show that the parties intended to give up the lien altogether — for example, where the seller delivers a part and says “the rest will follow, payment later” — the lien is gone for the residue as well. The burden of showing such an agreement lies on the party who asserts it.

≠Section 48 distinguished from s. 49(1)(b)

Section 48 is about part delivery by the seller himself; s. 49(1)(b) is about the buyer lawfully obtaining possession of “the goods”. Delivering 10 of 100 bags from the seller’s own godown leaves the lien intact on 90 bags; the buyer taking the 90 into his own godown by agreement ends the lien over them. Courts read the two sections together: what matters is whether possession of the remainder has passed.

Facts

Goods were sold and, at the buyer's request, were sent by the sellers to the buyer's shipping agents, who put them on board a ship. The goods were thereafter re-landed and returned to the sellers for the purpose of being re-packed. While the goods were still with the sellers for that purpose, the buyer became insolvent, and the sellers, still unpaid, refused to deliver them except against payment of the price, claiming to exercise their lien.

Issue

Had the sellers lost their lien by delivering the goods to the buyer's shipping agents; and if they had, did the lien revive when the goods came back into their hands for re-packing?

Arguments
For the sellers
  • The goods were back in their possession when the buyer became insolvent, so the lien, being a right founded on possession, should be available.
  • The possession was for the buyer's own benefit (re-packing), not a voluntary surrender of security.
For the trustee in bankruptcy
  • Delivery to the buyer's shipping agents was delivery to the buyer; the lien had been lost once and for all.
  • A lien, once lost, is not revived by a later, different possession; the seller's re-possession was for a limited purpose (re-packing), not for the purpose of the sale.
Decision

The sellers had lost their lien by delivering the goods to the buyer's shipping agents, and the refusal to deliver them to the trustee in bankruptcy was wrongful. A lien is not revived by the seller subsequently getting the goods into his hands for some other purpose.

Principle evolved

Three propositions, all now found in ss. 49 and 51: (i) delivery to the buyer's agent or carrier for transmission ends the lien; (ii) the lien, once lost, does not revive merely because the seller happens to hold the goods again; (iii) the seller's only remedy after parting with possession is stoppage in transit — and even that requires the goods to be in the course of transit, which they were not once the shipping agents had taken them (s. 51(1)).

No. Once delivery of the refrigerator had been made to the buyer, the right of lien came to an end, and it could not be revived by the seller again obtaining possession of the goods — and certainly not where the later possession was for a different purpose (repairs) altogether.

The lien attaches to possession held under the original contract of sale. Part C — Stoppage in transitu: Sections 50, 51 and 52

Recovering possession from the carrier when the buyer has become insolvent — “a right to regain” possession

C.1 The nature and history of the right

The right of stoppage in transitu (Latin: “in the course of passage”) is the unpaid seller's right, on the insolvency of the buyer, to resume possession of the goods while they are still on their way to the buyer and to retain them until the price is paid or tendered. It is the natural completion of the lien: the lien is a right to retain possession; stoppage is a right to recover possession.

The remedy was created by the English Chancery in the 18th century and was given a statutory shape by the Lickbarrow v. Mason line of cases (1787) and later by the Sale of Goods Act, 1893. Anson called it “a kind of equitable lien adopted by the law merchant”. Its justification is risk-reallocation: the goods have physically left the seller, but they have not yet reached the buyer's estate; if the buyer is insolvent, it is better that the goods return to the seller, who can still turn them into money, than that they be swallowed by the general body of creditors for a dividend of a few paise in the rupee.

Why the buyer's insolvency is the trigger

A solvent buyer's delay is a breach of contract, and the seller's remedy is a personal action (or lien, if he still holds the goods). But an insolvent buyer's delay threatens the seller with losing both the goods and the price. Hence the law allows possession to be pulled back before the goods reach the insolvent estate.

Why it is a drastic remedy

The seller reaches out and takes possession of goods in the hands of a carrier with whom he has no contract, and negates the delivery he himself made. That is why the Act surrounds it with conditions: insolvency, an independent carrier, and goods still “in course of transit”, plus strict rules on notice (s. 52).

C.2 Section 50 — Right of stoppage in transit ACT

DLien and stoppage are successive, never simultaneous

The carrier may hold the goods in one of three capacities, and the seller's remedy changes with it:

  • Carrier as the seller's agent (e.g. the seller's own lorry, his own godown-keeper, his own freight forwarder acting for him): the goods are still in the seller's possession — the lien applies, and stoppage is unnecessary.

C.3 Section 51 — Duration of transit ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 51 (verbatim)

51. Duration of transit.—(1) Goods are deemed to be in course of transit from the time when they are delivered to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, until the buyer or his agent in that behalf takes delivery of them from such carrier or other bailee.

Part D — Transfer of Goods by the Buyer and the Seller: Sections 53 and 54

Sub-sale and pledge by the buyer; and the unpaid seller’s right of re-sale — the two sections that complete Chapter V

D.1 The problem that s. 53 solves

The unpaid seller's lien and right of stoppage are useless if the buyer can destroy them by dealing with the goods. Suppose B buys goods from A on credit, and before paying for them resells them to C, or pledges them to a bank. Whose right prevails — A's lien, or C's purchase, or the bank's pledge? Section 53 answers in three tiers:

  1. The buyer's sub-sale or pledge of the goods alone does not affect the seller. A sub-purchaser “takes the property subject to the same restrictions that his immediate vendor held it under”, and a pledgee of the goods takes nothing more than the buyer had. So A's lien or stoppage survives (s. 53(1), main limb), unless A assented to the dealing.
  2. But a document of title changes everything. Documents of title — bills of lading, railway receipts, warehouse-keeper's certificates, dock warrants, delivery orders, multimodal transport documents — are the currency of commerce precisely because they can be transferred. If a document of title has been issued or lawfully transferred to the buyer (or owner) and he transfers it to a person who takes it in good faith and for consideration, then (a) a transfer by way of sale defeats the seller's lien and stoppage; (b) a transfer by way of pledge or other disposition for value leaves the seller's rights alive but subject to the rights of the transferee (the proviso to s. 53(1)).
  3. Even against a pledgee, the seller gets a small relief: under s. 53(2) he may require the pledgee to satisfy the amount secured by the pledge, as far as possible, first out of any other goods or securities of the buyer in the pledgee's hands and available against the buyer.

D.2 Section 53 — Effect of sub-sale or pledge by buyer ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 53 (verbatim)

53. Effect of sub-sale or pledge by buyer.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the unpaid seller's right of lien or stoppage in transit is not affected by any sale or other disposition of the goods which the buyer may have made, unless the seller has assented thereto:

Provided that where a document of title to goods has been issued or lawfully transferred to any person as buyer or owner of the goods, and that person transfers the document to a person who takes the document in good faith and for consideration, then, if such last mentioned transfer was by way of sale, the unpaid seller's right of lien or stoppage in transit is defeated, and, if such last mentioned transfer was by way of pledge or other disposition for value, the unpaid seller's right of lien or stoppage in transit can only be exercised subject to the rights of the transferee.

(2) Where the transfer is by way of pledge, the unpaid seller may require the pledgee to have the amount secured by the pledge satisfied in the first instance, as far as possible, out of any other goods or securities of the buyer in the hands of the pledgee and available against the buyer.

D.3 Morvi Mercantile Bank Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1965 SC 1954 WEB

Morvi Mercantile Bank Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1965 SC 1954 : (1965) 35 Comp Cas 620
Supreme Court of India · Constitution Bench · the leading Indian authority on documents of title, pledge and stoppage WEB
A railway receipt is a document of title: pledging the paper pledges the goods.
Facts

A firm consigned goods (menthol crystals, valued at about ₹ 35,500) by rail from Bombay for delivery at Delhi and obtained the railway receipts. To raise an advance of ₹ 20,000, the firm endorsed and delivered the railway receipts to the Morvi Mercantile Bank as security. The consignment was never delivered: the railway offered the bank certain boxes which, on inspection, contained a different commodity (caustic soda) and not the goods consigned. The bank, as endorsee of the receipts, sued the Union of India (the railway administration) for the value of the goods.

Issues
  • Is a railway receipt a “document of title to goods” within the meaning of the Contract Act and the Sale of Goods Act?
  • Does the endorsement of the railway receipt for consideration operate as a pledge of the goods represented by it, or only of the paper?
  • Can the endorsee-bank sue the railway for the loss of the consignment?
Arguments
For the bank (endorsee)
  • A railway receipt is expressly included in the definition of “document of title to goods” in s. 2(4) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, and in the Explanation to s. 178 of the Contract Act as it stood after the 1930 amendment; it is also a “mercantile document of title to goods” under s. 137 of the Transfer of Property Act.
  • Under s. 178 the owner of goods may pledge them by transferring the document of title; a pledge of the document is therefore a pledge of the goods, and the pawnee has the rights of a pledgee in possession, including a right to sue for their loss.
  • The older view that a railway receipt was a mere “symbol” and not a document of title was the English view, and India deliberately departed from it by including the railway receipt in the statutory definition.
For the Union of India (railway)
  • A railway receipt is not a bill of lading; it is not negotiable in the full sense, and the railway's obligation runs to the consignee named in it or his nominee, not to a stranger-endorsee.
  • Under the railway law and the conditions of carriage, a transfer of the receipt has effect only if the railway is notified and agrees to hold the goods for the transferee; merely endorsing the paper cannot bind the carrier.
  • The bank, if only a pledgee of the documents, must sue in the name of the consignor; it cannot claim the whole value of the goods as if it were the owner.
Decision

By a majority, the Supreme Court held that a railway receipt is a document of title to goods, that the firm had, by endorsing the receipts to the bank for consideration, pledged the goods covered by the receipts to the bank, and that the bank was the pawnee of the goods and could enforce its rights as such. The Court reasoned that the Sale of Goods Act, the Contract Act (as amended in 1930) and the Transfer of Property Act all treat the railway receipt as a mercantile document of title, and that if the owner could not pledge goods by transferring the document, the absurd result would follow that his agent could do what the owner could not.

Principle evolved
  • A pledge of a document of title is a pledge of the goods it represents; constructive delivery of the document is delivery of the goods for the purposes of ss. 172–179 of the Contract Act.
  • A railway receipt ranks with a bill of lading for the purposes of the right of stoppage in transit and of a valid pledge under s. 178 of the Contract Act — which is exactly what s. 53 proviso and s. 30(2) of the Sale of Goods Act assume.
  • Consequently, in a s. 53 dispute the question is never merely “who holds the goods?” but “who holds the document, in what capacity, for what consideration and in what good faith?”
Mercantile Bank of India Ltd. v. Central Bank of India Ltd., AIR 1938 PC 117 : (1938) 40 Bom LR 713
Privy Council · Lord Thankerton and Lord Roche · estoppel, pledges of railway receipts and the protection of the pledgee WEB
A pledgee who has not acted so as to mislead the world keeps his security, even against a later pledgee of the goods.
Facts

A firm of merchants borrowed from the Central Bank and pledged railway receipts to it, delivering also letters of lien and promissory notes. The merchants then discovered that they could take delivery of the goods by using the receipts themselves (the Central Bank having stamped them for the purpose of taking delivery in the ordinary course of business), and they proceeded to pledge the actual goods so obtained to the Mercantile Bank. The merchants became insolvent; both banks claimed the goods, and the Mercantile Bank argued that the Central Bank was estopped from setting up its earlier pledge because it had allowed the merchants to have possession of the receipts bearing its stamp.

Issue

Had the Central Bank, by allowing the merchants to deal with the railway receipts, lost the priority of its pledge or become estopped from asserting it against the later pledgee of the goods?

Arguments
Mercantile Bank (the later pledgee of the goods)
  • The Central Bank's stamp on the receipts was a representation to the world that the merchants had authority to obtain and deal with the goods.
  • Having put the merchants in a position to deceive, the Central Bank should be estopped from proving its earlier, superior pledge.
Central Bank (the earlier pledgee of the receipts)
  • It had taken a valid pledge of the documents and had not voluntarily parted with possession of them; the merchants took delivery fraudulently.
  • The receipt's form contained no representation that the merchants could pledge the goods, and no duty was owed to a stranger bank.
Decision

The Privy Council dismissed the appeal of the Mercantile Bank. The Central Bank had obtained a valid pledge of the goods by the pledge of the railway receipts and did not lose its rights as pledgee by what the merchants afterwards did. There was no duty or representation on the Central Bank's part which could found the estoppel asserted against it.

Principle evolved

A pledgee of a document of title is protected in his security; a later taker of the goods from the pledgor cannot claim priority merely because the earlier pledgee's document passed through the pledgor's hands in the ordinary course of business. The decision belongs to the same family as s. 53: he who takes a document of title honestly and for value gets a real right in the goods, but he who takes the goods from a dishonest buyer gets only what that buyer had.

D.4 Section 54 — Sale not generally rescinded by lien or stoppage in transit ACT

The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Section 54 (verbatim)

54. Sale not generally rescinded by lien or stoppage in transit.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this section, a contract of sale is not rescinded by the mere exercise by an unpaid seller of his right of lien or stoppage in transit.

(2) Where the goods are of a perishable nature, or where the unpaid seller who has exercised his right of lien or stoppage in transit gives notice to the buyer of his intention to re-sell, the unpaid seller may, if the buyer does not within a reasonable time pay or tender the price, re-sell the goods within a reasonable time and recover from the original buyer damages for any loss occasioned by his breach of contract, but the buyer shall not be entitled to any profit which may occur on the re-sale. If such notice is not given, the unpaid seller shall not be entitled to recover such damages and the buyer shall be entitled to the profit, if any, on the re-sale.

(3) Where an unpaid seller who has exercised his right of lien or stoppage in transit re-sells the goods, the buyer acquires a good title thereto as against the original buyer, notwithstanding that no notice of the re-sale has been given to the original buyer.

(4) Where the seller expressly reserves a right of re-sale in case the buyer should make default, and, on the buyer making default, re-sells the goods, the original contract of sale is thereby rescinded, but without prejudice to any claim which the seller may have for damages.

Sub-section by sub-section: what each part actually does

Sub-sectionRuleWhy it matters in an answer
s. 54(1)Neither the exercise of the lien nor the exercise of the right of stoppage rescinds the contract. D.5 The case law on resale

Stephens v. Wilkinson (1831) 2 B. & Ad. 320
Court of King’s Bench · the cautionary case on resale WEB
An unpaid seller in possession cannot simply treat the goods as his own and re-sell them; his rights are those the law gives him, no more.
Facts

Goods had been sold and the property in them had passed to the buyer, but the seller remained in possession because he had not been paid. The seller, treating himself as still the owner, purported to re-sell the goods to a third party.

Issue

Does an unpaid seller who still has possession have the right, merely because he is unpaid, to re-sell the goods and thereby divest the buyer's property?

Decision

No. The mere fact that the seller is unpaid and in possession does not give him a complete right of property in the goods, so as to divest the buyer of his property by a re-sale. (Contrast s. 54(2) of the Indian Act, which confers a statutory power of re-sale within strict limits.)

Principle evolved

The seller's possession after the property has passed is a special property — the lien (s. 2(11)) — and not the general property. Any power of re-sale must therefore be traced either to the statute (s. 54(2)) or to the contract (s. 54(4)), and must be exercised strictly within its terms. This is exactly why the Mysore Sugar decision examines whether s. 54(2) was complied with, and why a seller who re-sells outside s. 54 can find himself liable in damages to the original buyer.

Gallagher v. Shilcock [1949] 2 K.B. 765 WEB and R. V. Ward Ltd. v. Bignall [1967] 1 Q.B. 534 (CA) WEB
English authorities on the “rescission” debate under the parallel s. 48 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1893/1979 · Diplock L.J. WEB
Does a re-sale put an end to the original contract? The old answer was “no”; Diplock L.J. held “yes”.
Facts and issue

Under the English Act, where goods are perishable or where the unpaid seller gives notice and the buyer does not pay within a reasonable time, the seller may re-sell and recover damages. The question was whether that re-sale rescinded the original contract — and therefore whether the seller could keep any profit on the re-sale, or had to account for it to the buyer.

Arguments
The old view — Gallagher v. Shilcock
  • The statutory re-sale is not an election to rescind; the contract subsists, the seller is merely realising his security.
  • Being a realisation, the seller must account to the buyer for any surplus, exactly like a pledgee.
The modern view — Ward v. Bignall
  • A re-sale is an act “inconsistent with a subsisting sale to the original buyer”: the seller cannot both re-sell the goods and keep the original contract alive.
  • The re-sale therefore rescinds the contract even if the seller did not expressly say so, and the seller keeps the profit, being liable only for damages for non-acceptance.
Decision

Gallagher v. Shilcock held that the statutory re-sale did not rescind the contract, so the buyer could claim the profit; Ward v. Bignall disagreed and overruled that approach, holding that a re-sale puts it out of the seller's power to perform the original contract and therefore rescinds it, so that the seller keeps any profit.

Principle evolved, and the Indian position

Indian law does not have to choose between the two cases, because s. 54(2) settles the point in words: with notice (or for perishable goods) the seller keeps the profit — “the buyer shall not be entitled to any profit which may occur on the re-sale”; but if notice is not given, the seller loses his damages and “the buyer shall be entitled to the profit”. Section 54(4) goes further and expressly makes a re-sale under a reserved contractual right rescind the contract. In short: the Indian Act gives the buyer the profit only where the seller has failed to give the notice the Act requires.

Bhajan Singh Hardit Singh & Co. v. Karson Agency (India), decided 31 March 1967 (Punjab & Haryana High Court)
Indian authority on two limits of s. 54(2): no re-sale right where property has not passed, and limitation for the damages claim WEB
No re-sale power without a lien to exercise; and the cause of action arises at the breach, not at the re-sale.
Facts

Cloth was sold at ₹ 23/4 per yard with ₹ 1,000 paid in advance. The buyers refused to take delivery. After notice that the goods would be re-sold at the buyers' risk, the sellers re-sold the cloth in smaller lots and sued for the shortfall as damages. The suit was filed nearly three years later, and the question was whether it was within limitation — the courts below having held that the cause of action arose only when the last lot was re-sold.

Issues
  • Did the sellers have a right of re-sale under s. 54(2) at all, given that the property in the goods had not passed to the buyers?
  • When did the cause of action for damages for the buyers' refusal to take delivery accrue, for the purposes of limitation?
Decision

The Court held that there was no question of the seller having a right of re-sale under s. 54(2), because that sub-section presupposes an unpaid seller who has exercised a right of lien or stoppage — which presupposes that the property has passed to the buyer. Here the property had not passed: the goods remained the sellers', and the buyers' refusal to take delivery was a simple breach of contract. The suit for compensation was governed by the ordinary rule of limitation for breach of contract, the cause of action accruing when the breach occurred — not when the re-sale fixed the quantum of loss. The suit, filed too late, was therefore dismissed.

Principle evolved
  • The statutory power of re-sale in s. 54(2) exists only where the seller has a lien or right of stoppage to exercise, i.e. only where the property has already passed to the buyer. Where the contract is still executory, the seller's remedies are withholding delivery (s. 46(2)) and damages for non-acceptance (s. 56 with s. 73 of the Contract Act).
  • A re-sale is a step in the realisation of damages; it does not postpone the accrual of the cause of action.
🔗Cross-link to Topic 7 — the same section, decided differently on the facts

Case 34 (P.S.N.S. Ambalavana Chettiar & Co. v. Express Newspapers Ltd., AIR 1968 SC 741, p. 232) turned on the question whether the goods had been appropriated and the property had passed. Because 300 of the 415 tons of newsprint had never been appropriated to the contract, they remained unascertained; property had not passed; there was therefore no lien to be exercised and no re-sale power under s. 54(2), and the seller's remedy was damages under s. 73 of the Indian Contract Act (contract price minus market price on the date of breach). The Supreme Court also explained the notice limb of the re-sale right: where notice is given, the seller re-sells on his own behalf and not as the buyer's agent, so the buyer takes no profit — but where no notice is given, the buyer takes the profit and the seller loses his damages. Case 37 (Mysore Sugar) is the mirror image: the property had passed, so s. 54(2) governed, and the seller failed not because the section was inapplicable but because he delayed the re-sale beyond a reasonable time. Read together, they give you the whole of s. 54 in two decisions.

EWorked problem — the scooter (a real DU question, 2013 CT-OC)

Problem. A sold to B a scooter for ₹ 7,000. B paid the whole price except ₹ 3,000, which he promised to pay within 5 days. A kept possession until the balance was paid. Before the five days expired, B became insolvent. A re-sold the scooter to C for ₹ 7,500 without giving notice to B. B claims ₹ 500 from A as the profit on the re-sale. Is A liable?

Answer. Yes. (1) A is an unpaid seller under s. 45(1)(a): the whole of the price has not been paid. (2) On B's insolvency, A's lien was available under s. 47(1)(c), even though the five-day period had not expired — insolvency accelerates the lien. (3) But the scooter is not of a perishable nature, so A could re-sell under s. 54(2) only after giving B notice of his intention to re-sell and after B failed to pay within a reasonable time. No notice was given. (4) The consequence is laid down in the last sentence of s. 54(2): A “shall not be entitled to recover such damages” and “the buyer shall be entitled to the profit, if any, on the re-sale”. A is therefore liable to pay ₹ 500 to B (through his trustee or official assignee). (5) C still gets a good title to the scooter under s. 54(3) — the absence of notice does not hurt C; it hurts A.

Marks-winning extras: cite s. 2(8) for the meaning of insolvency; distinguish Gallagher v. Shilcock and Ward v. Bignall; and add that if the contract had contained an express reserved right of re-sale, the position would have been governed by s. 54(4), the contract would stand rescinded, and A would have kept the ₹ 500.

Part E — DU Case 37: Mysore Sugar Co. Ltd. v. Manohar Metal Industries

AIR 1982 Kant. 283 · Karnataka High Court · G.N. Sabhahit J. · DU case material, p. 247 · [Section 54 — Right of an unpaid seller]

Mysore Sugar Co. Ltd., Bangalore v. Manohar Metal Industries, Chikpet, Bangalore
AIR 1982 Kant. 283 · Karnataka High Court · G.N. Sabhahit J. · DU CASE 37 · p. 247 · headnoted in the Faculty’s reader as “[Section 54 — Right of an un-paid seller]”
An unpaid seller who re-sells three months late, in a falling market, cannot make the buyer pay for the delay.
§Why the Faculty put this case here

Chapter V gives the unpaid seller a right of re-sale under s. 54(2) — but that right is conditional. The buyer must be given the chance to pay within a reasonable time, and the re-sale itself must be effected within a reasonable time. Mysore Sugar is the Indian decision which examines both “reasonable time” requirements against a falling market, insists on the seller's duty to mitigate and on the seller's burden of proving damages, and holds that s. 54 — being the specific provision for moveable property sold — prevails over the general s. 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. It is the only case in the DU reader on Chapter V, and it comes to the High Court as an appeal from a dismissed suit.

Facts — as narrated in the Faculty’s case material (pp. 247–249)

The parties. The Mysore Sugar Company Ltd., Bangalore (the plaintiff) had at its works at Mandya certain items — copper ingots, copper scrap and brass tubes. Manohar Metal Industries, Chikpet, Bangalore (the defendant) was in the metal trade and wished to buy them.

The contract. The Company advertised the sale of these items by a notification dated 27 July 1966. The defendant, by its letter dated 30 June 1966 (as printed in the report), offered to purchase the items, and the plaintiff accepted the offer. On acceptance there was an unconditional contract of sale, with stipulations as to payment of the price and delivery of the goods to follow.

Performance begins — and stops. The defendant lifted some of the items on part-payment. When it came to lifting the copper ingots, he asked for time to pay the balance and to remove the ingots, which first had to be separated from other material with which they were mixed. Under the contract the defendant had to lift 2,000 kg of copper scrap and 2,000 kg of copper ingots, valued at ₹ 48,503·96.

The buyer’s complaint. The defendant wrote to the plaintiff on 28 April 1966 (as printed) raising objections about the percentage of copper content in the articles — his case being that what the advertisement offered was “copper scrap and copper ingots”, whereas what was found at the spot was an alloy and not pure copper.

The seller’s reply. By its intimation dated 12 September 1966 (Exhibit D-8) the plaintiff told the defendant that no certificate of purity of the metal would be given, that the material had been sold on an “as is and where is” condition, and that if the goods were not lifted within three days, the contract would be treated as cancelled.

Persistent default. In spite of repeated reminders and demands the defendant did not take delivery of the remaining goods and did not remit the value. A further letter dated 22 November 1966 (Exhibit D-10) also met with no favourable response; by that letter the plaintiff made its intention clear that the buyer must lift the goods on payment, failing which the goods would be re-sold and the defendant would be liable for any loss caused.

The re-sale. The plaintiff then re-sold the copper tubes and copper ingots through an advertisement dated 30 December 1966 to M/s Karnataka Hardware, Avenue Road, Bangalore. On the re-sale the plaintiff claimed to have incurred a loss of ₹ 8,643·96.

The suit. A legal notice was issued; the defendant did not make good the loss. The plaintiff therefore sued for recovery of ₹ 8,643·96 less ₹ 500 (the defendant's initial deposit), that is ₹ 8,143·96, together with costs and interest.

The defendant’s contentions (as recorded in the judgment)
  1. The suit was not tenable.
  2. What was offered in the advertisement was copper scrap and copper ingots, but what was found at the spot was an alloy and not pure copper; consequently the plaintiff itself had committed a breach of contract.
  3. The plaintiff had no right to re-sell; compensation, if any, could be recovered only under the general principles contained in s. 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872; s. 54 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 was not applicable.
  4. The defendant was therefore not liable to pay any damages — and, on the contrary, he claimed compensation of ₹ 1,000.
Arguments of the two sides before the High Court
For the plaintiff-Company (appellant)
  • There was an unconditional contract of sale; the property in the goods passed to the buyer; that is precisely the situation in which s. 54(2) applies.
  • The Company gave the buyer clear notice, by Exhibit D-10, of its intention to re-sell and of his liability for the loss; the condition in s. 54(2) was therefore satisfied.
  • The buyer defaulted despite repeated demands, so the Company was entitled to re-sell and recover the loss of ₹ 8,643·96 as damages, reduced by the ₹ 500 deposit.
  • The Company examined P.W. 2 — the purchaser in the re-sale — to prove the price realised; the shortfall is the measure of the loss.
For the defendant-respondent
  • The material was described as copper scrap and ingots but was in fact alloy; the Company having failed to supply what it advertised, there was no default by the buyer at all.
  • Section 54 of the Sale of Goods Act has no application; only s. 73 of the Contract Act governs, and under that section no loss has been proved.
  • In any event there was no re-sale within a reasonable time as s. 54(2) requires: the breach (if any) occurred in September 1966, but the re-sale advertisement appeared only on 30 December 1966.
  • P.W. 2 himself admitted that prices were higher about three months earlier and had since fallen; the Company waited three months although the market was falling, so the loss, if any, is the result of its own delay and not of the buyer's breach.
The points for determination (verbatim from the judgment)

“(1) Whether the Courts below were justified in holding that the defendant committed breach of contract? (2) Whether the learned Civil Judge was justified in dismissing the suit for damages?”

E.2 Principle evolved — the ratio decidendi

  1. Section 54(2) presupposes that the property has passed. Where the contract is unconditional and the property has passed to the buyer, the seller's remedy on default is not the general law of damages alone: he must work through the specific machinery of s. 54(2) — notice (unless the goods are perishable), a reasonable time for the buyer to pay, and a re-sale within a reasonable time.
  2. Notice must precede the re-sale, and once given it makes the time of payment of the essence of the contract; an unpaid seller who re-sells without the notice required by s. 54(2) forfeits his damages and hands the profit to the buyer.
  3. “Reasonable time” is a question of fact (s. 63) and its content depends on the commercial circumstances — above all on the movement of the market. Where prices are falling, a delay of three months after the breach is “inordinate” and the re-sale is not a re-sale “within a reasonable time” under s. 54(2).
  4. The seller has a duty to mitigate, which s. 54(2) does not displace; it is implicit in the section and is also the express requirement of the Explanation to s. 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (“the means which existed of remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance of the contract must be taken into account”).
  5. The burden of proving the loss lies on the seller. The price realised on a delayed re-sale is not evidence of the loss; the seller must prove the ruling market price at the time of the breach (or within a reasonable time of it). If he does not, the suit for damages fails — even though the buyer is in breach.
  6. Section 54 prevails over s. 73 of the Contract Act because it is the specific provision for a specific case — a contract of sale of moveable property — while s. 73 states the general law of compensation.
DA note for the careful student: the two notices in the judgment

The judgment works with two letters and the reader should keep them apart:

  • Exhibit D-8 (12-9-1966): the “as is and where is” reply, coupled with a warning that the goods must be lifted within three days or the contract would be treated as cancelled. The Court used it to fix the date on which “the contract came to an end on or about 15-9-1966”, i.e. to start the clock for the reasonable time within which the re-sale should have been advertised.
  • Exhibit D-10 (22-11-1966): the express statement of intention to re-sell and of the buyer's liability for the loss — held to “satisfy the condition mentioned in Section 54(2)”.

A critic may ask how there could be a statutory re-sale of goods under a contract which had already been “treated as cancelled” by the seller on 15-9-1966. The answer that makes the judgment coherent is that the Court read the two letters together as the seller's election to treat the buyer's default as a breach and to realise the goods, and that the real failure was not the mechanism but the timing and the proof. That is why the decision is remembered for the “reasonable time + burden of proof” point rather than for the s. 54(2) notice point, on which the seller actually succeeded.

F.1 The Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 — and why its section 8 belongs to this topic

The syllabus for Topic 8 lists s. 8 of the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 immediately after ss. 45–54 of the Sale of Goods Act. The reason is a contrast that examiners love: the owner under a hire-purchase agreement is never an “unpaid seller” at all. Under a hire-purchase agreement the goods are bailed to the hirer, who pays instalments; the property does not pass until the purchase is completed. There is therefore no “sale”, no “buyer” and no “unpaid seller” within Chapter V — the owner simply remains the owner, and resumes his own goods on default instead of exercising a lien or a right of re-sale.

The Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 — Section 8 (verbatim)

8. Passing of property.—Subject to the provisions of this Act, the property in the goods to which a hire-purchase agreement relates shall pass to the hirer only on the completion of the purchase in the manner provided in the agreement.

Read with s. 4 of the Act (the hire-purchase agreement must be in writing and signed by all parties, and must state the total hire-purchase price, the cash price, the date on which the hire-purchase agreement commences, the number of instalments and the amount of each instalment, and a description of the goods), s. 8 produces three consequences for an unpaid seller's analysis:

  1. No Chapter V rights for the owner. The owner needs none: he never parted with the property, so he has no need of a lien (s. 47), no need of stoppage (s. 50) and no need of a statutory re-sale (s. 54). On default he terminates the agreement under s. 10 and retakes his own goods. The hirer's position is that of a bailee in default.
  2. No nemo dat protection for a purchaser from the hirer. A hirer who sells the goods to a third party passes no title (Topic 7, s. 27); and because a hire-purchase agreement is not a sale, the buyer's exceptions in ss. 30(1) and 30(2) of the Sale of Goods Act do not apply to the hirer (he is not a “seller” or a “buyer” who has “bought or agreed to buy”).
  3. What the hirer does get is a statutory protection package: the right to complete the purchase at any time with a rebate (s. 9), the right to terminate the agreement at any time before the final payment on fourteen days' notice (s. 10), the right to appropriate payments among two or more agreements (s. 11), and the right to assign his interest with the owner's consent, which cannot be unreasonably withheld (s. 12).

F.3 The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — what “conditional payment” means

Section 45(1)(b) of the Sale of Goods Act turns on a negotiable instrument having been received as conditional payment and the condition having failed. The rules of conditional payment come from the general law and from the Negotiable Instruments Act:

  • s. 118 — statutory presumptions as to negotiable instruments, including that they were made or drawn for consideration;
  • s. 32 — the maker, acceptor or endorser of a negotiable instrument is bound to pay it according to its tenor on maturity, and on default the holder may sue;
  • ss. 76–78 — discharge of the instrument by payment, cancellation, release and so on;
  • s. 138 — the criminal remedy for the dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds, on the statutory notice and limitation conditions. (A seller may use s. 138 against the buyer and still remain an “unpaid seller” for the purposes of ss. 46–54: the two remedies are concurrent.)
§Conditional versus absolute payment — the modern cases

Conditional: Edwards v. Brewer (1837) 2 M&W 375 — the seller is unpaid where bills of exchange were taken as conditional payment and were not met; Gunn v. Bolckow Vaughan (1875) L.R. 10 Ch. App. 491 — “if the bill is dishonoured before delivery has been made, then the vendor's lien revives; or if the purchaser becomes openly insolvent before the delivery actually takes place, then the law does not compel the vendor to deliver to an insolvent purchaser.”

Absolute: Cowasjee v. Thompson (1845) 3 M.I.A. 422 — a seller who takes a negotiable security as absolute payment is no longer an unpaid seller and has no rights against the goods. And for modern payment systems see Re Charge Card Services Ltd. [1988] All E.R. 707 (CA) — there is no general principle that a payment through a third party is necessarily conditional; each method of payment (card, banker's credit, e-wallet) must be judged on its own contractual scheme. Man (E.D. & F.) Ltd. v. Nigerian Sweets & Confectionery Co. Ltd. F.6 Insolvency — the trigger of s. 47(1)(c) and s. 50, and the modern overlay

Two of the most important rights in Chapter V depend on the buyer's insolvency: the lien under s. 47(1)(c) and the right of stoppage under s. 50. Section 2(8) of the Sale of Goods Act supplies the test, and it is deliberately a commercial, not a procedural, test.

The test, again (s. 2(8) SOGA, verbatim)

A person is said to be “insolvent” who has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business, or cannot pay his debts as they become due, whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not.

Three consequences follow, and each is worth a mark in an answer:

  1. No adjudication is required. The seller does not have to wait for an insolvency petition, a winding-up order or an admission into insolvency resolution. Evidence that the buyer has stopped paying his debts as they fall due is enough — which is why stoppage in transit is a genuinely practical remedy.
  2. But mere default is not insolvency. A buyer who delays a particular payment, or disputes quality, is not “insolvent”. The seller who stops goods on that footing may be liable for wrongful stoppage.
  3. Insolvency is needed only for particular rights. The lien under s. 47(1)(a) and (b) and the right of re-sale under s. 54 do not require insolvency at all. Only s. 47(1)(c) and s. 50 do.

2017 LL.B. LB-304 · Q. 8(b) Set A · Unpaid seller — definition, stoppage, lien vs stoppage
Question “Who is an unpaid seller under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? When can an unpaid seller exercise the right of stoppage in transit? How does the seller's lien differ from the right of stoppage in transit? Discuss.”
Model answer

1. The definition — s. 45. Begin with the statutory text: a seller is deemed to be an unpaid seller (a) when the whole of the price has not been paid or tendered, or (b) when a bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument has been received as conditional payment and the condition has not been fulfilled by reason of dishonour or otherwise. Add s. 45(2): “seller” includes any person in the position of a seller — an agent to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed, a consignor, or an agent who has himself paid or is directly responsible for the price. Note the two practical points: part payment does not disqualify the seller (Longbottom & Co. v. Bass, Walker & Co. (1922) WN 245), and a seller who has obtained a decree for the price is still unpaid until it is satisfied (s. 49(2)).

2. The rights conferred — s. 46. Notwithstanding that the property has passed, the unpaid seller has, by implication of law, (a) a lien on the goods for the price while in possession; (b) in case of the buyer's insolvency, a right of stopping the goods in transit after parting with possession; and (c) a right of re-sale as limited by the Act. Where property has not passed, s. 46(2) gives a right of withholding delivery “similar to and co-extensive with” the lien and stoppage.

3. When stoppage in transit may be exercised — ss. 50–52. Four conditions: (i) the seller is unpaid; (ii) he has parted with possession (otherwise his lien suffices); (iii) the buyer has become insolvent — insolvency being defined by s. 2(8) as having ceased to pay debts in the ordinary course of business or being unable to pay debts as they become due, “whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not”; and (iv) the goods are in the course of transit, i.e. in the hands of a carrier or other bailee who is independent of both parties (s. 51(1)). The transit ends when the buyer or his agent takes delivery (s. 51(1)); it also ends on early delivery (s. 51(2)), on the carrier's acknowledgment to the buyer (s. 51(3)), or on the carrier's wrongful refusal to deliver (s. 51(6)) — but not on the buyer's rejection of the goods (s. 51(4)). Where the goods go into a ship chartered by the buyer, the character of the master's possession is a question of fact (s. 51(5); Kemp v. Falk (1882) 7 App Cas 573). The right is exercised by taking actual possession or by notice — to the person in actual possession or to his principal — given in such time and circumstances that the principal may, with reasonable diligence, prevent delivery (s. 52(1)); the carrier must then re-deliver to the seller, at the seller's expense (s. 52(2)); Litt v. Cowley (1816) 7 Taunt 169.

4. The differences — present them in a table. (1) Lien is a right to retain possession; stoppage is a right to regain it. (2) Lien operates while the goods are in the seller's own possession; stoppage operates while they are with an independent carrier. (3) Lien may be exercised even when the buyer is solvent — a cash sale or an expired credit term will do; stoppage requires the buyer's insolvency. (4) Lien requires no notice; stoppage usually requires a notice which must reach in time to prevent delivery. (5) Lien is lost by delivery to a carrier without reserving the right of disposal (s. 49(1)(a)) — precisely the moment at which the right of stoppage is born; and when the goods are successfully stopped and come back into the seller's hands, the lien revives. That is why the two rights are successive, and never simultaneous. (6) Expenses of re-delivery after stoppage fall on the seller (s. 52(2)); no such cost attaches to a lien.

5. Conclusion. Both rights are possessory, self-help remedies in favour of the seller; neither rescinds the contract (s. 54(1)); and both are defeated or subordinated by a transfer of a document of title to a good-faith transferee for value under s. 53 proviso.

Examiner’s expectation: the statutory text of s. 45 and s. 46; the four conditions of s. 50; s. 2(8); s. 51 in outline; s. 52(1); and a clear comparison table with the “successive not simultaneous” point. One case on each side is enough — Longbottom for part payment, Kemp v. Falk for the chartered ship.
2015 LL.B. LB-304 · Q. 1(a) Set B · Lien — when exercisable, when lost
Question “Define Unpaid Seller. When can an unpaid seller exercise his right of lien on the goods, and when does he lose his lien thereon?”
Model answer

Definition. Quote s. 45(1)(a) and (b) verbatim, and add s. 45(2) on the extended meaning of “seller”. Mention that the status depends on the price remaining unpaid or a conditional payment failing, and that it is a status “within the meaning of this Act”, unlocking the remedies in ss. 46–54.

When the lien may be exercised — s. 47(1). The unpaid seller in possession may retain the goods until payment or tender of the price in three cases: (a) the goods were sold without any stipulation as to credit; (b) they were sold on credit but the term of credit has expired; (c) the buyer has become insolvent — even during an unexpired credit term, because credit is given on the faith of solvency: Ex parte Chalmers (1873) L.R. 8 Ch. App. 289; Gunn v. Bolckow Vaughan (1875) L.R. 10 Ch. App. 491. Add s. 47(2): the lien is not lost merely because the seller is in possession as the buyer's agent or bailee. Add the two properties of the lien: it secures the whole price, and it is indivisible — the buyer cannot tender a proportionate sum and demand a proportionate part (Bloxam v. Sanders (1825) 4 B. & C. 941).

When the lien is lost — s. 49(1). (a) Where the seller delivers the goods to a carrier or other bailee for transmission to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal; (b) where the buyer or his agent lawfully obtains possession; (c) by waiver — express or implied, e.g. by taking security which is inconsistent with the lien, or by conduct showing that the lien is given up (Anguis v. McLachlan (1883) 23 Ch D 330; Jones v. Tarleton (1842) 9 M&W 675). Two additions: the lien is not lost by obtaining a decree for the price (s. 49(2)), and in practice it is also lost by estoppel — where the seller's conduct leads a third party to believe that no lien exists (s. 115 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872).

The non-revival rule. A lien once lost is not revived by the seller again getting possession for a different purpose: Valpy v. Gibson (1847) 4 C.B. 837 (goods returned for re-packing) and, in India, Eduljee v. Café John Bros., AIR 1940 Nag 311 (refrigerator returned for repairs — the seller's remedy there, if any, is the bailee's lien under s. 170 of the Contract Act). Note also s. 48: a part delivery does not destroy the lien on the remainder unless the part delivery shows an agreement to waive the lien (Shepherd v. Harrison (1871) L.R. 5 H.L. 116).

Examiner’s expectation: s. 45; s. 47(1)(a)–(c) with the insolvency rule; s. 47(2); s. 48; s. 49(1)–(2); and at least two cases. The highest marks go to the candidate who explains why insolvency revives a lien suspended by credit.
2015 LL.B. LB-304 · Q. 1(b) Set B · Problem — Valpy v. Gibson on the phone
Question “The goods sold were delivered to the buyer's shipping agents, who had put them on board a ship. But the goods were returned to the seller for packing. While they were still with the seller for this purpose the buyer became insolvent, and the seller, being an unpaid seller, claimed to retain the goods in the exercise of his lien. Advise the seller.”
Model answer

The issue. Has the seller's lien been lost by the delivery of the goods to the buyer's shipping agents, and, if so, did it revive when the goods came back to him for packing?

Provisions. Section 47(1)(c) gives a lien to an unpaid seller in possession where the buyer has become insolvent; but s. 49(1)(a) provides that the seller loses his lien when he delivers the goods to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal. The law is settled that a carrier or shipping agent of the buyer is a bailee for transmission, and that delivery to him is, in effect, delivery to the buyer.

Authority. This is the very problem decided in Valpy v. Gibson (1847) 4 C.B. 837. Goods were sent to the buyer's shipping agents and loaded on board; they were then re-landed and returned to the sellers for re-packing. While they lay with the sellers the buyer became insolvent, and the sellers refused to deliver them to the trustee except against payment. It was held that the sellers had lost their lien by delivering the goods to the buyer's shipping agents, and that their refusal to deliver was wrongful. A lien, once lost, is not revived by the seller's later possession for a different purpose. Follow Eduljee v. Café John Bros., AIR 1940 Nag 311.

Application. On the facts, the goods had already left the seller's possession for transmission to the buyer, and were being handled by the buyer's own shipping agents. The packing job was a fresh, limited purpose unconnected with the contract of sale. The seller's lien was therefore gone; the return of the goods did not restore it.

Advice. The seller cannot retain the goods under s. 47. His remedy, if any, is the right of stoppage in transit under s. 50 — but even that fails here, because the goods were re-landed and returned to the seller, so they were no longer “in the course of transit” in the hands of a carrier (s. 51(1)). If the buyer is insolvent and the goods have reached him, the seller is left with a personal claim in the insolvency; he must prove for the price along with the other creditors. The lesson for the trade is to reserve the right of disposal (for example, by taking the bill of lading to his own order or to a bank's order) so that s. 49(1)(a) never applies.

Examiner’s expectation: Valpy v. Gibson named and applied; s. 49(1)(a); the “no revival” rule; the fall-back to s. 50 and its failure on these facts; and a practical suggestion about reserving the right of disposal (s. 19(3)).
2015 LL.B. LB-304 · Q. 6 Set C · “Lien ends where the right of stoppage commences”
Question “By implications of law examine the validity of the following statement: Lien ends where the right of stoppage commences.”
Model answer

The statement is substantially true, and the Act is built on it. The two rights are successive stages of a single protective idea: so long as the unpaid seller holds the goods, he retains them (lien); once he has parted with possession for transmission to the buyer, the law gives him a right to recover them while they are still moving (stoppage in transit). They cannot sensibly co-exist, because the lien depends on possession and stoppage on the absence of possession.

The statutory mechanism. Section 49(1)(a) provides that the lien is lost “when he delivers the goods to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal of the goods”. Section 50 then confers the right of stoppage in the very situation created by that delivery — the seller has parted with possession and the goods are in course of transit. The hinge is therefore the word “transmission”: the same act that kills the lien creates the stoppage right. Section 46 itself lists the three rights in that order — lien, stoppage, re-sale — as a sequence. And when the seller successfully stops the goods and they come back into his possession, his lien revives, which is why s. 50 says he “may retain them until payment or tender of the price”.

Two qualifications that earn extra marks. First, the statement is true only where the seller delivers to a carrier without reserving the right of disposal. If he reserves the right of disposal — for example by taking the bill of lading or railway receipt to his own order under s. 19(3) — the property itself may not pass, and his lien, or rather his ownership, continues even during carriage. Second, the statement must not be read as saying that the two rights can never overlap in the hands of different persons: an agent to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed is treated as a seller under s. 45(2) and may exercise either right as the facts allow. There is also the case of the carrier who is the seller's own agent: the goods are still in the seller's possession in law, and the lien (not stoppage) applies.

Authorities. Bloxam v. Sanders (1825) 4 B. & C. 941 (the lien exists while possession remains); Valpy v. Gibson (1847) 4 C.B. 837 (lien lost on delivery to the buyer's agents; not revived); Litt v. Cowley (1816) 7 Taunt 169 and Whitehead v. Anderson (1842) 9 M&W 518 (the stoppage right, and the duty of reasonable diligence to prevent delivery).

Conclusion. The maxim is a sound statement of the scheme of Chapter V: the lien and the right of stoppage are complementary and successive, and the boundary between them is the delivery of the goods to a carrier for transmission to the buyer, without a reservation of the right of disposal.

Examiner’s expectation: s. 49(1)(a) quoted; s. 50 quoted; the revival of the lien on a successful stoppage; the “reserving the right of disposal” exception (s. 19(3)); and the carrier's three possible capacities (seller's agent, independent, buyer's agent).

2015 LL.B. LB-304 · Q. 8 (short notes) Set D · Short note — “Unpaid seller & his rights”, with case law
Question “Write notes on any two of the following, referring to case law to justify your answer: … (c) Unpaid seller & his rights.”
Model short note (about 350–450 words)

Who. Section 45(1) deems a seller to be an unpaid seller when the whole of the price has not been paid or tendered, or when a bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument has been received as conditional payment and the condition has failed by dishonour “or otherwise”. Section 45(2) extends the expression to any person in the position of a seller — an agent to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed, a consignor, or an agent who has himself paid or is directly responsible for the price. Part payment does not remove the seller from the section (Longbottom & Co. Ltd. v. Bass, Walker & Co. (1922) WN 245), and a seller who has a decree for the price remains unpaid until it is satisfied (s. 49(2)).

Rights against the goods — s. 46(1). Even though the property has passed to the buyer, the unpaid seller has, by implication of law, three rights: (a) a lien for the price while in possession (ss. 47–49); (b) a right of stoppage in transit where the buyer has become insolvent and the seller has parted with possession (ss. 50–52); and (c) a right of re-sale as limited by s. 54. If the property has not passed, s. 46(2) gives him a right of withholding delivery, co-extensive with those rights. These rights are possessory and self-help in character and are therefore valuable precisely when the buyer is insolvent, because the goods never enter the insolvent estate.

Rights against the buyer personally. The seller may sue for the price where the property has passed or the price is payable on a day certain (s. 55), sue for damages for non-acceptance (s. 56), treat the contract as repudiated before the due date (s. 60), and claim interest and special damages (s. 61) — all subject to s. 73 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, which requires him to mitigate.

Limits. The rights are subject to s. 53: a sub-sale of the goods does not affect them, but a transfer of a document of title to a good-faith transferee for value defeats them if it is by way of sale and subordinates them if by way of pledge (Morvi Mercantile Bank Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1965 SC 1954). Exercise of the lien or of stoppage does not rescind the contract (s. 54(1)); the buyer may still pay and claim the goods. And the re-sale power in s. 54(2) is conditional on perishability or notice, a reasonable time for payment and a re-sale within a reasonable time — as Mysore Sugar Co. Ltd. v. Manohar Metal Industries, AIR 1982 Kant. 283 (DU Case 37) demonstrates, where a three-month delay in a falling market cost the seller his damages.

Examiner’s expectation: a short note should have a definition, a three-part structure (rights against goods / against buyer / limits), four or five sections quoted precisely, and at least three cases — Longbottom, Morvi Mercantile Bank and Mysore Sugar would be ideal.
2012 CT-OC LL.B. (CT & OC) · Q. 6(a) and 6(b) Set E · Right of lien and right of re-sale; loss of lien
Question “(a) Discuss the right of lien and the right of re-sale of an unpaid seller. (b) How may the unpaid seller's right of lien be lost?”
Model answer

(a) The right of lien. Section 47(1): an unpaid seller in possession may retain the goods until payment or tender of the price — (a) where the goods were sold without any stipulation as to credit; (b) where sold on credit but the term of credit has expired; (c) where the buyer becomes insolvent. Section 47(2) preserves the lien although the seller is in possession as agent or bailee of the buyer. The lien is a particular lien for the price of those goods (contrast s. 171 of the Contract Act), it secures the whole price, and it is indivisible. Section 48 preserves it on the remainder after a part delivery, unless the part delivery shows an agreement to waive the lien. Authorities: Bloxam v. Sanders (1825) 4 B. & C. 941; Ex parte Chalmers (1873) L.R. 8 Ch. App. 289 (insolvency revives a lien suspended by credit); Shepherd v. Harrison (1871) L.R. 5 H.L. 116.

(a) The right of re-sale. Section 54(1): the contract is not rescinded by the mere exercise of the lien or of stoppage. Section 54(2): where the goods are perishable, or where the seller who has exercised the lien or stoppage gives the buyer notice of his intention to re-sell, the seller may — if the buyer does not, within a reasonable time, pay or tender the price — re-sell the goods within a reasonable time and recover damages for the loss occasioned by the breach; the buyer is not entitled to any profit on the re-sale. If the notice is not given, the seller cannot recover damages and the buyer is entitled to the profit. Section 54(3): the sub-purchaser acquires a good title against the original buyer even if no notice of the re-sale was given. Section 54(4): where the contract expressly reserves a right of re-sale, a re-sale after default rescinds the original contract, without prejudice to damages. Authorities: Stephens v. Wilkinson (1831) 2 B. & Ad. 320 (an unpaid seller in possession cannot, merely because he is unpaid, re-sell as if he were owner); Gallagher v. Shilcock [1949] 2 K.B. 765 and R. V. Ward Ltd. v. Bignall [1967] 1 Q.B. 534 (the “rescission” debate); Ambalavana Chettiar v. Express Newspapers Ltd., AIR 1968 SC 741 (the seller who gives notice re-sells on his own behalf; without notice the buyer takes the profit); Mysore Sugar, AIR 1982 Kant. 283 (DU Case 37) for “reasonable time” and proof of loss.

(b) Loss of the lien — s. 49(1). (i) Delivery of the goods to a carrier or other bailee for transmission to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal; (ii) the buyer or his agent lawfully obtaining possession; (iii) waiver, express or implied. Add: the lien is not lost merely because the seller has obtained a decree for the price (s. 49(2)); and a lien once lost is not revived by a later, different possession (Valpy v. Gibson; Eduljee v. Café John Bros.). Estoppel is the equitable fourth mode.

2011 CT-OC LL.B. (CT & OC) · Q. 6 Set F · Define unpaid seller; lien; termination
Question “Define ‘Unpaid Seller’. When can an unpaid seller exercise his right of lien on the goods, and when is this right terminated?”
Model answer — the “six-box” structure
  1. Definition (s. 45). Quote both limbs, add s. 45(2), and note that a seller who refuses a valid tender of the price is not an unpaid seller — the tender must be a proper offer of the whole price (Longbottom & Co.).

2013 CT-OC LL.B. (CT & OC) · Q. 1 Set G · Problem — the ₹ 500 profit on an unnotified re-sale
Question “A sold to B a scooter for ₹ 7,000. B paid the whole price except ₹ 3,000, which he promised to pay within 5 days. A held possession of the scooter until the rest of the money was paid. But before the expiry of the stipulated time B became insolvent. A re-sold the scooter to C for ₹ 7,500 without giving notice to B. B claims ₹ 500 from A, being the profit on the re-sale. Is A liable to pay ₹ 500 to B?”
Model answer (IRAC)

Issue. Is the seller liable to account for the profit made on a re-sale effected without notice to an insolvent buyer?

Rule. (i) Section 45(1)(a): the seller is an unpaid seller because the whole of the price has not been paid. (ii) Section 47(1)(c): the lien may be exercised where the buyer becomes insolvent — and insolvency is defined by s. 2(8) as having ceased to pay debts in the ordinary course of business or being unable to pay them as they become due, “whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not”. The insolvency also accelerates the lien, so that B's promise to pay “within 5 days” is immaterial. (iii) Section 54(2): the seller may re-sell on the buyer's failure to pay within a reasonable time only where the goods are perishable or where he has given the buyer notice of his intention to re-sell. A scooter is not perishable. The consequence of failing to give notice is expressly provided: the seller “shall not be entitled to recover such damages and the buyer shall be entitled to the profit, if any, on the re-sale”. (iv) Section 54(3): the sub-purchaser C nevertheless gets a good title against B even though no notice of the re-sale was given.

Application. A was an unpaid seller in possession. B's insolvency brought s. 47(1)(c) into play and permitted A to re-sell — but only on the conditions of s. 54(2). A gave no notice, and the goods were not perishable. The re-sale produced a profit of ₹ 500. By the express words of the second sentence of s. 54(2), that profit belongs to B (through his trustee/official assignee), and A cannot claim his damages either.

Conclusion. Yes — A is liable to pay ₹ 500 to B. Had the contract contained an express reserved right of re-sale, the position would have been governed by s. 54(4): the contract would have been rescinded on the re-sale and A would have kept the ₹ 500, remaining liable only for damages (of which there would be none). Had A given a notice and allowed B a reasonable time, A would have kept the profit under the first sentence of s. 54(2).

Examiner’s expectation: s. 45(1)(a) + s. 47(1)(c) + s. 2(8) + the second sentence of s. 54(2), applied in that order; and a note on the fate of C under s. 54(3). Mention of Ward v. Bignall or Gallagher v. Shilcock shows extra reading.

Question “Distinguish between sale and hire-purchase agreement.”
Model answer

The central distinction is the passing of property. In a sale (s. 4 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930), the seller transfers the property in the goods to the buyer for a price, either at once (a sale) or in the future (an agreement to sell). In a hire-purchase agreement, the owner bails the goods to the hirer for a series of periodical payments, and the hirer has an option to purchase; by s. 8 of the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972, “the property in the goods … shall pass to the hirer only on the completion of the purchase in the manner provided in the agreement”.

Why the distinction matters for this topic. Because a hire-purchase owner is not a seller, he cannot be an “unpaid seller” under s. 45, and he needs none of the Chapter V remedies. Three extra practice problems (not from past papers, but in the same format)

Practice problem 1 — the three-month delay

Problem. A sugar mill sells 5,000 bags of sugar to a trader. The property passes on acceptance, but the trader fails to lift the bags or pay. The mill sends a notice that it will re-sell, and the trader still does not pay. Sugar prices are crashing. The mill waits four months and then re-sells at a loss of ₹ 6,00,000, and sues. Advise the mill.

Answer. Applying Mysore Sugar (DU Case 37): the mill is an unpaid seller; the property has passed, so s. 54 applies and, being the special provision, prevails over s. 73 of the Contract Act. The notice satisfies the first limb of s. 54(2). But the re-sale must be within a reasonable time — a question of fact under s. 63 — and four months in a falling market will almost certainly be held inordinate, particularly since P.W. 2-type evidence of the earlier, higher price is readily available. The mill must also prove the ruling market price at the date of breach; the re-sale price realised four months later does not prove its loss. Advise an immediate mitigation and re-sale, with contemporaneous market evidence (trade association price lists, comparable sales).

Practice problem 2 — the bank’s pledge and the sub-sale

Problem. A consigns goods by rail to B and takes the railway receipt to B's name. B endorses the receipt to a bank for an advance and then, before the goods arrive, purports to sell them to C, who pays value without notice. A is unpaid and learns that B is insolvent. Advise A.

Answer. (i) A is an unpaid seller; but he has parted with possession, so his remedy is stoppage in transit (s. 50) — available because B is insolvent within s. 2(8). (ii) A railway receipt is a document of title (Morvi Mercantile Bank Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1965 SC 1954; s. 2(4) SOGA). (iii) Under the proviso to s. 53(1), the transfer of the document by B: if it was by way of pledge to the bank (the usual position where it secures an advance), A's right of stoppage survives but can be exercised only subject to the bank's rights — so A must pay or tender to the bank the amount secured by the pledge before taking the goods (Kemp v. Falk; Ramdas Vithaldas Durbar v. S. Amarchand & Co., (1916) 43 IA 164 (PC)). (iv) If the bank in turn transferred the document by way of sale to a good-faith purchaser for consideration, A's lien and stoppage would be defeated altogether. (v) The sub-sale of the goods to C is ineffective against A: a buyer's sub-sale does not affect the unpaid seller (s. 53(1) main limb), because C takes only what B had (McEwan v. Smith (1849) 2 H.L. Cas. 309). Answer: A may stop the goods, but on terms — first satisfying the bank, and not against any purchaser who has taken the document by sale in good faith and for value.

Practice problem 3 — the part delivery that looked like a waiver

Problem. A sells 100 bales of cotton to B for ₹ 10,00,000 on 30-day credit. After 20 days, A delivers 30 bales under a delivery order and tells B “the rest will follow as soon as your cheque clears”. Before the 30 days expire, B's bank returns his other cheques unpaid; A refuses to deliver the remaining 70 bales and B's receiver demands them. Advise A.

Answer. A is an unpaid seller in possession of 70 bales. Section 47(1)(c) applies because B has become insolvent — and insolvency revives a lien suspended by an unexpired credit term (Ex parte Chalmers). Section 48 preserves the lien on the remainder unless the part delivery shows an agreement to waive the lien; the words used (“the rest will follow as soon as your cheque clears”) indicate the opposite of a waiver — payment is the condition of further delivery. A may therefore retain the 70 bales until payment or tender of the price, and the lien secures the whole price (Shepherd v. Harrison). The receiver cannot compel delivery; but note that A cannot retain the 30 bales already delivered, since his lien over them is gone (s. 49(1)(b)) and he cannot revive it by re-possession (Valpy v. Gibson).

H.2 Mnemonics

The three rights — “L-S-R”

Lien → Stoppage → Re-sale. They are successive, and the fourth remedy — Withholding delivery (s. 46(2)) — applies where the property has not passed. Mnemonic: “L-S-R, and W when nothing has gone.”

The three cases for lien — “C-I-C”

Cash sale (no stipulation as to credit) · Installment of credit expired · Customer insolvent. All three are in s. 47(1)(a)–(c).

The three ways to lose the lien — “C-B-W”

Carrier without reserving the right of disposal · Buyer lawfully in possession · Waiver. (Plus the decree rule of s. 49(2) and estoppel.)

The four conditions of stoppage — “U-P-I-T”

Unpaid · Parted with possession · Insolvent buyer · Transit. Say it as a sentence: “Unpaid, parted, insolvent, in transit.”

The s. 54(2) profit rule — “Notice to the seller, no notice to the buyer”

With notice (or perishables): the seller takes the profit and his damages. Without notice: the buyer takes the profit and the seller loses his damages. Add “perishable = no notice needed”, and “s. 54(4) reserved right = rescission, seller keeps the profit”.

The two “reasonable times” — “Pay, then Sell”

First the buyer's reasonable time to pay or tender; then the seller's reasonable time to sell. Both are questions of fact (s. 63), and both were applied in Mysore Sugar (Case 37).

H.3 Ten common mistakes

#The mistakeThe correction
1“The unpaid seller may sue for the price under s. 45.”Section 45 only defines the status. Suit for the price is s. 55; damages for non-acceptance is s. 56.
2“Stoppage can be exercised because the buyer has not paid.”The buyer must be insolvent within s. 2(8). Non-payment alone is not enough.
3“An adjudication of insolvency is necessary.”s. 2(8) expressly says “whether he has committed an act of insolvency or not”.
4“The lien and the right of stoppage can be exercised together.”They are successive; delivering to a carrier ends the lien (s. 49(1)(a)) and creates the right of stoppage.
5“The seller can re-sell whenever he likes, being unpaid.”He must comply with s. 54(2) (perishables or notice, reasonable time to pay, re-sale within a reasonable time) or with s. 54(4) (reserved right). Stephens v. Wilkinson.
6“A re-sale without notice gives the seller his damages and the buyer the profit.”Wrong: without notice the seller loses his damages and the buyer takes the profit — the double penalty in the second sentence of s. 54(2).
7“The re-sale rescinds the contract.”Only a re-sale under an expressly reserved right does — s. 54(4). A statutory re-sale under s. 54(2) does not rescind (s. 54(1)).
8“A lien is a right to sell the goods.”A lien is only a right to retain; the power of sale is separate (s. 54; contrast s. 176 of the Contract Act for a pawnee).
9“The buyer's sub-sale destroys the seller's rights.”Not unless the seller assented — s. 53(1). Only a transfer of a document of title to a good-faith purchaser for value changes the result, and then only as the proviso provides.
10“The seller's burden of proof ends when he proves the re-sale price.”He must prove the ruling market price at the date of the breach (or within a reasonable time of it) and must have mitigated. Mysore Sugar, Case 37.
