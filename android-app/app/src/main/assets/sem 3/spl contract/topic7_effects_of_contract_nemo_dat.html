Transfer of Property & the Doctrine of Nemo Dat Quod Non Habet
The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 · Sections 18 to 30

A complete, exam-oriented study module on what a contract of sale actually does: when the property in the goods moves from seller to buyer (ss. 18–26) and whether a seller who is not the owner can pass a good title (ss. 27–30). The two questions are different and the Act answers them in different chapters — s. 18 says no property passes until the goods are ascertained; s. 19 makes intention decisive; ss. 20–24 supply the five default rules for discovering that intention; s. 25 lets the seller reserve the right of disposal; s. 26 makes risk follow property. §1.0 · Property, possession, risk and title — the four-way distinction

A contract of sale is defined by s. 4(1) as “a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price”. The whole of Chapter III of the Act is machinery for answering two questions about that transfer. Before using the machinery you must separate four ideas that students habitually run together:

☕ One story that separates all four

Ravi buys a second-hand Royal Enfield from a dealer for ₹1,20,000. He pays in full. The dealer says “collect it on Saturday, I will finish the service”. On Friday night a fire in the dealer's godown destroys the bike. On Saturday Ravi discovers the bike was stolen, and the true owner, Meena, claims it.

  • Possession — still with the dealer. He never delivered.
  • Property — passed to Ravi when the contract was made, because this was an unconditional contract for specific goods in a deliverable state (s. 20). Neither payment nor delivery is required for property to pass.
  • Risk — therefore on Ravi under s. 26. He must still pay the price (s. 26; the loss falls on the owner). This is the answer that surprises students: he never touched the bike and it burned down, yet he bears it.
  • Title — Meena wins the bike. The dealer had no title and, on these facts, none of the ss. 27–30 exceptions applies (the dealer is not a mercantile agent holding with the owner's consent — the thief had none). Ravi's remedy is against the dealer for total failure of consideration: he recovers the whole ₹1,20,000 (Rowland v. Divall).

The scheme of Chapter III, Part A, in one sentence each: s. 18 is a gateway (no property until the goods are ascertained); s. 19 is the governing principle (property passes when the parties intend); ss. 20–24 are the default rules the court uses when the parties' intention cannot be found; s. 25 is an override (the seller may reserve the right of disposal and thereby stop property passing even though he has handed the goods over); s. 26 is the consequence ( risk follows property). §1.1 · Section 18 — Goods must be ascertained

Section 18 — verbatim ACT
“Goods must be ascertained.—Where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods, no property in the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained.”

Twenty-nine words, and they defeat more sale-of-goods claims than any other sentence in the Act. s. 18 is not a rule about when property passes; it is a rule about whether it can pass at all. It is a condition precedent. If the goods are not ascertained, there is nothing for property to attach to, and the buyer has no proprietary interest whatsoever — not even an equitable one.

The three categories of goods — get these labels right
1 SPECIFIC GOODS

s. 2(14): “goods identified and agreed upon at the time a contract of sale is made”. The parties have pointed at this thing. ss. 20–22 and s. 25(1) apply to them directly.

2 UNASCERTAINED GOODS

Generic or future goods not yet identified. Future goods — s. 2(6): “goods to be manufactured or produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract of sale”. s. 23 is the rule that ascertains them.

3 ASCERTAINED GOODS

Goods that were unascertained when the contract was made but have since been identified and attached to it by appropriation. Once ascertained, ss. 19–25 apply to them as if they had been specific all along.

☕ Real examples
  • Specific: “my 2019 Maruti Swift, registration UP-70 AB 1234, standing in my garage”. Identified and agreed upon. s. 20 applies — property passes the moment the contract is made.
  • Unascertained (generic): “10 tonnes of 24-carat Gujarat mangoes from your next harvest”. Nothing is identified. Even if 10 tonnes are later loaded on a lorry, no property passes until those 10 tonnes are unconditionally appropriated to this contract (s. 23).
  • Unascertained (future): “the car you are going to manufacture for me in March”. By s. 6(3) a purported present sale of future goods “operates as an agreement to sell the goods”.
  • The exam favourite: “300 tons out of the 415 tons lying in my godown”. Although the stock is identified, the 300 tons is not. That is Case 34, and the Supreme Court held no property passed.
The one way round s. 18 — ascertainment ‘by exhaustion’

If the seller sells the whole of an identified bulk, no selection is needed and no selection is possible: the bulk identifies itself. This is called ascertainment by exhaustion. It is why Re Wait [1927] 1 Ch 606 and Wait & James v. Midland Bank (1926) 31 Com Cas 172 came out differently on almost identical facts:

! Two mistakes to avoid
  • “The stock is identified, so the goods are ascertained.” No. Identification of the source is not identification of the goods sold. Bayley B. said it best in Gillett v. Hill (1814) 149 ER 871, 873 — quoted with approval by the Supreme Court in Case 34 — “If I agree to deliver a certain quantity of oil as ten out of eighteen tons, no one can say which part of the whole quantity I have agreed to deliver until a selection is made. There is no individuality until it has been divided.”
  • “The buyer has an equitable interest in the bulk.” No, in India as in England before the 1995 amendments. Contrast the position in England today under ss. 20A–20B of the Sale of Goods Act 1979 (inserted by the Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1995), which give a buyer of an undivided share in an identified bulk a statutory co-ownership. India has no equivalent.

§1.2 · Section 19 — Property passes when intended to pass

Section 19 — verbatim ACT
“Property passes when intended to pass.—(1) Where there is a contract for the sale of specific or ascertained goods the property in them is transferred to the buyer at such time as the parties to the contract intend it to be transferred.

(2) For the purpose of ascertaining the intention of the parties regard shall be had to the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case.

(3) Unless a different intention appears, the rules contained in sections 20 to 24 are rules for ascertaining the intention of the parties as to the time at which the property in the goods is to pass to the buyer.”
★ The single most important sentence in Chapter III

It is s. 19(3)'s opening phrase: “Unless a different intention appears.” Sections 20 to 24 are not the law of passing of property. They are defaults — the machinery a court falls back on only when it cannot find the parties' actual intention. The Supreme Court said exactly this in Case 35: “This exercise has to be done to give effect to the opening words, namely, ‘Unless a different intention appears’ occurring in Section 19(3).” A student who answers “property passed under s. 20” without first looking for a contrary intention has skipped the step that carries the marks.

Ascertaining intention — the three sources in s. 19(2)
T 1 · TERMS OF THE CONTRACT

The written or spoken bargain. Does it say ‘property passes on shipment’? An F.O.B., C.I.F. or ex-works clause? A ‘no title until payment in full’ (Romalpa) clause? An express reservation under s. 25?

C 2 · CONDUCT OF THE PARTIES

What they did. Who took out insurance? Who paid the freight? Whose name is on the bill of lading? Was the buyer asked to approve the goods? Was the price paid into a bank as the seller's agent, or as the buyer's? Case 35 turns on this.

S 3 · CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CASE

The surrounding matrix: the trade usage, the nature of the goods, whether a document of title exists, whether the goods are on the seller's premises or in transit, whether the parties dealt at arm's length.

§ The two authorities on s. 19 the Supreme Court uses
  • Hoe Kim Seing v. Maung Ba Chit, AIR 1935 PC 182 — cited in Case 35: “intention of the parties was the decisive factor as to when the property in goods passes to the purchaser. If the contract is silent, intention has to be gathered from the conduct and circumstances of the case.”
  • Consolidated Coffee Ltd. v. Coffee Board, AIR 1980 SC 1468 — also cited in Case 35: in an auction-sale of chattels, property passes to the purchaser on the acceptance of his bid. The Supreme Court made a subtle point DU examiners like: this happens “not because of Section 64(2) but because of the rule contained in Section 20” — s. 64(2) fixes the moment the contract is complete; s. 20 fixes the moment property passes.
☕ Real example — the same facts, two different intentions

A Delhi dealer orders 200 mobile phones from a Noida warehouse. The goods are identified, packed and ready. Two scenarios:

  • Scenario A — credit sale, nothing said. Unconditional contract for specific goods in a deliverable state → s. 20: property passes when the contract is made. The phones burn in a warehouse fire that night. The loss is the buyer's (s. 26). He must still pay.
  • Scenario B — the invoice says ‘property passes on delivery to your premises’, the seller insures the goods in transit and charges the buyer for freight to destination. Different intention appears → ss. 20–24 are displaced. Property (and risk) pass only on delivery at the buyer's premises. The same fire in the seller's warehouse is now the seller's loss.

§1.3 · The five rules — Sections 20 to 24 at a glance

These five sections are the court's fallback. Remember the order in Figure 3: the court first looks for a real intention under s. 19(2); only if the contract is silent does it turn to these rules “for ascertaining the intention of the parties”. Two structural points before the detail:

  1. Rules 1–3 (ss. 20–22) are about specific goods. Rules 4–5 (ss. 23–24) are about unascertained / future goods and about goods the buyer has not yet committed himself to.
  2. Rules 1–3 are three different answers to one question: is anything left for the seller to do? Rule 1 — nothing left → property passes at once. Rule 2 — something left to the goods → wait. Rule 3 — something left to ascertain the price → wait. The recurring idea is that property cannot pass while the seller still has work to do that is essential to the goods.

§1.4 · Rule 1 — Section 20: specific goods in a deliverable state

Section 20 — verbatim ACT
“Specific goods in a deliverable state.—Where there is an unconditional contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state, the property in the goods passes to the buyer when the contract is made, and it is immaterial whether the time of payment of the price or the time of delivery of the goods, or both, is postponed.”
The three conditions, and the one consequence
i Condition 1 — UNCONDITIONAL

No term suspends the transfer. A condition precedent, a ‘subject to inspection’ clause, a reservation under s. 25, or a requirement that the seller do something first (taking you into s. 21 or s. 22) all make the contract conditional and take it out of s. 20.

ii Condition 2 — SPECIFIC GOODS

s. 2(14): “identified and agreed upon at the time a contract of sale is made”. Note that s. 20 also covers goods that were ascertained later, because s. 19(1) speaks of “specific or ascertained goods”. (The Supreme Court in Case 35 treats ss. 19 and 20 as applying to ‘specific or ascertained’ goods alike.)

iii Condition 3 — DELIVERABLE STATE

s. 2(3): goods are in a “deliverable state” when they are “in such state that the buyer would under the contract be bound to take delivery of them”. It is a contractual test, not a physical one: goods still needing packing may be deliverable if the contract does not require the seller to pack.

★ The consequence — learn this sentence verbatim

“…the property in the goods passes to the buyer when the contract is made, and it is immaterial whether the time of payment of the price or the time of delivery of the goods, or both, is postponed.” Three things follow immediately: (i) risk passes at once under s. 26, even though the buyer has neither paid nor taken delivery; (ii) the seller may sue for the price under s. 55 rather than merely for damages; (iii) the buyer can pass a good title to a sub-buyer immediately, and can sue a third party in conversion.

☕ Real examples
  • The haystack. Tarling v. Baxter (1827) 6 B & C 360: a haystack was sold on 27 December, to be paid for on 4 January and not to be cut until 1 May. It burned down on 22 January. The buyer bore the loss — property had passed on 27 December, and the postponement of payment and delivery were both immaterial.
  • The auction car. A buyer's bid is accepted at an auction. s. 64(2): “the sale is complete when the auctioneer announces its completion by the fall of the hammer”. s. 20: property passes at that same instant — per the Supreme Court in Consolidated Coffee Ltd. v. Coffee Board, AIR 1980 SC 1468, “not because of Section 64(2) but because of the rule contained in Section 20”.
  • The showroom scooter. You buy the display model, pay next week, collect next month. It is stolen from the showroom tonight. Unless the contract is conditional, the loss is yours — and the dealer can still sue you for the price.

§1.5 · Rule 2 — Section 21: specific goods to be put into a deliverable state

Section 21 — verbatim ACT
“Specific goods to be put into a deliverable state.—Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods and the seller is bound to do something to the goods for the purpose of putting them into a deliverable state, the property does not pass until such thing is done and the buyer has notice thereof.”

The structure is a mirror image of s. 20. s. 20 asks whether the goods are already deliverable; s. 21 asks whether the seller must still make them deliverable. Two elements must both be satisfied before property passes, and students routinely forget the second:

1 The act must be DONE

“such thing is done” — the seller must actually complete the work he is bound to do. Starting it, or nearly finishing it, is not enough.

2 The buyer must have NOTICE

“and the buyer has notice thereof” — the seller must communicate that the work is finished. Silence leaves the property with the seller. Notice need not be formal; it may be inferred from conduct.

☕ Real examples
  • The condensing engine. Underwood Ltd. v. Burgh Castle Brick & Cement Syndicate [1922] 1 KB 343: a 30-ton engine was sold “free on rail”. It was bolted to the floor and had to be dismantled and loaded before delivery. The seller dismantled it, but while loading it the crane broke and the engine was damaged. Held: the seller bore the loss. He was bound to put the machine on rail, and until that was done (and the buyer told) the property had not passed under s. 21. Scrutton L.J. stressed that the seller's obligation went to the goods, not merely to the price.
  • The unpolished furniture. A carpenter agrees to sell a specific dining table but must first apply the agreed finish and polish it. He polishes it on Monday and says nothing. On Tuesday the workshop floods. Property has not passed: the act was done, but the buyer had no notice.
  • A distinction to keep straight: if the seller's remaining work is only packing for transit and the contract does not make packing a condition of delivery, the goods are already in a deliverable state and s. 20, not s. 21, applies.

§1.7 · Rule 4 — Section 23: unascertained goods and appropriation

Section 23 — verbatim ACT
“Sale of unascertained goods and appropriation.—(1) Where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained or future goods by description and goods of that description and in a deliverable state are unconditionally appropriated to the contract, either by the seller with the assent of the buyer or by the buyer with the assent of the seller, the property in the goods thereupon passes to the buyer. Such assent may be express or implied, and may be given either before or after the appropriation is made.

Delivery to carrier.—(2) Where, in pursuance of the contract, the seller delivers the goods to the buyer or to a carrier or other bailee (whether named by the buyer or not) for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, and does not reserve the right of disposal, he is deemed to have unconditionally appropriated the goods to the contract.”
★ Why s. 23 is the most heavily examined rule

It is the bridge between s. 18 and everything else. s. 18 says no property passes while goods are unascertained; s. 23(1) is the machine that ascertains them and, in the same breath, passes the property. And s. 23(2) is the everyday commercial case — almost every sale involving a courier, a lorry or a ship is decided by it. Case 35 is an s. 23(2) case, and the Andhra Pradesh High Court's reasoning (approved by the Supreme Court) is the clearest statement of the rule you will find: “Where goods have been delivered to a common carrier to be sent to the person, by whom they have been ordered, the carrier becomes the agent of the vendee and such a delivery amounts to delivery to the vendee under Section 23(2) of the Sale of Goods Act.”

§ The five-point test for an effective appropriation

The classic modern statement is that of Pearson J. in Carlos Federspiel & Co. S.A. v. Charles Twigg & Co. Ltd. [1957] 1 Lloyd's Rep 240, and Indian courts use the same ideas. An appropriation must be:

  1. a differentiation of the goods from the bulk — the goods must be earmarked, packed, marked or otherwise set apart;
  2. an irrevocable act — the seller must have finally committed himself; an intention to change his mind defeats it;
  3. of goods conforming to the contract description;
  4. of goods in a deliverable state;
  5. made unconditionally — no reservation of disposal (see s. 25).
s. 23(2) — the carrier rule, and why the carrier is the buyer's agent

This fiction is one of the most useful shortcuts in the whole Act. Once the seller hands conforming goods to a carrier for transmission to the buyer, and does not reserve the right of disposal, the law deems an unconditional appropriation. The consequences are immediate:

1 Property passes

At the moment of delivery to the carrier — not on arrival.

2 Risk passes

Under s. 26, transit loss is the buyer's. This is the single biggest practical consequence.

3 The carrier is the buyer's agent

For the purpose of taking delivery — per the Andhra Pradesh High Court in Case 35.

4 Lien is lost

s. 49(1)(a): the unpaid seller loses his lien when he delivers to a carrier without reserving the right of disposal.

! The three words in s. 23(2) that decide the case

s. 23(2) is switched off by the phrase “and does not reserve the right of disposal”. In practice, reservation is usually achieved by taking the bill of lading or railway receipt in the seller's own name or to his order — which is then reinforced by s. 25(2), a prima facie presumption of reservation. That is precisely the mechanism in Case 33, and it is why the chromite was held to have been sold in London and not in Madras.

§1.8 · Rule 5 — Section 24: goods sent on approval or ‘on sale or return’

Section 24 — verbatim ACT
“Goods sent on approval or “on sale or return”.—When goods are delivered to the buyer on approval or ‘on sale or return’ or other similar terms, the property therein passes to the buyer—

(a) when he signifies his approval or acceptance to the seller or does any other act adopting the transaction;

(b) if he does not signify his approval or acceptance to the seller but retains the goods without giving notice of rejection, then, if a time has been fixed for the return of the goods, on the expiration of such time, and, if no time has been fixed, on the expiration of a reasonable time.”

The commercial point of s. 24. In an ordinary sale the buyer cannot hand the goods back. Here he can — he has possession but not property, and he holds an option to become the owner. The Act must therefore say when the option is exercised. It gives two routes, and they are cumulative, not alternative.

a Route (a) — ADOPTION

The buyer does something positive: he signifies approval or acceptance, or does “any other act adopting the transaction”. The classic adopting acts are reselling, pledging, consuming, or altering the goods beyond what trial requires. Kirkham v. Attenborough [1897] 1 QB 201: jewellery delivered on sale-or-return and pledged by the buyer — pledging was an act adopting the transaction, so property had passed and the pledgee defeated the seller.

b Route (b) — EXPIRY

The buyer does nothing. Silence is not neutral: if he retains the goods without giving notice of rejection, property passes on the expiry of the fixed time, or of a reasonable time where no time is fixed. What is reasonable is, by s. 63, a question of fact.

! Two traps on ‘on sale or return’
  1. Risk does not automatically follow. Property passes under s. 24, but s. 26 asks separately whether the goods were at the buyer's risk. Where goods are returned damaged without fault within the trial period, the loss stays with the seller — Head v. Tattersall (1871) LR 7 Ex 7 (a horse on trial was injured without anyone's fault; the buyer was entitled to return it and the seller bore the loss). Contrast Elphick v. Barnes (1880) 5 CPD 321, where the horse died on the third day without fault and, property not having passed, the seller bore the loss — res perit domino.
  2. ‘On approval’ vs ‘on sale or return’. Both are in s. 24 and both work the same way, but the purpose differs: ‘on approval’ means the buyer is testing the goods; ‘on sale or return’ means the buyer (often a retailer) takes them to try to resell them. In the second case the buyer may resell and, on doing so, adopts the transaction under limb (a).
☕ Real examples
  • The saree on approval. A boutique gives a customer a ₹25,000 saree “on approval — return by Saturday”. On Thursday the customer wears it to a wedding. On Friday she returns it, saying she does not like it. Wearing it to a function is an act adopting the transaction under s. 24(a): property passed on Thursday, she cannot return it, and she owes the price.
  • The book distributor. A publisher sends 200 copies to a retailer “on sale or return”, no time fixed. After six months the retailer has neither sold nor returned them. A reasonable time has expired under s. 24(b): property has passed and the publisher may sue for the price.
  • The jeweller's diamonds — a 2015 DU question. “A, a jeweller, delivers some diamonds to B on sale or return. B pledges the diamonds with C.” Under s. 24(a), pledging is an act adopting the transaction: property had already passed to B before the pledge, so B could validly pledge them and C gets a good pledge. A's claim against C fails; A's remedy is against B for the price. (Note how this differs from a case under s. 30(2), where the buyer has possession before property has passed.)

§1.9 · Section 25 — Reservation of the right of disposal

Section 25 — verbatim ACT
“Reservation of right of disposal.—(1) Where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods or where goods are subsequently appropriated to the contract, the seller may, by the terms of the contract or appropriation, reserve the right of disposal of the goods until certain conditions are fulfilled. In such case, notwithstanding the delivery of the goods to a buyer or to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, the property in the goods does not pass to the buyer until the conditions imposed by the seller are fulfilled.

(2) Where goods are shipped or delivered to a railway administration for carriage by railway and by the bill of lading or railway receipt, as the case may be, the goods are deliverable to the order of the seller or his agent, the seller is prima facie deemed to reserve the right of disposal.

(3) Where the seller of goods draws on the buyer for the price and transmits to the buyer the bill of exchange together with the bill of lading or, as the case may be, the railway receipt, to secure acceptance or payment of the bill of exchange, the buyer is bound to return the bill of lading or the railway receipt if he does not honour the bill of exchange; and, if he wrongfully retains the bill of lading or the railway receipt, the property in the goods does not pass to him.

Explanation.—In this section, the expressions “railway” and “railway administration” shall have the meanings respectively assigned to them under the Indian Railways Act, 1890 (9 of 1890).”
Sub-sections (2) and (3) were substituted by Act 33 of 1963, s. 4, with effect from 22-9-1963.ACT
★ The one-sentence idea, and the case that proves it

s. 25 lets the seller hand over the goods and still keep the property. Delivery is not ownership. And Case 33 is the Supreme Court's own illustration. S.R. Das J. put the point in a passage you should be able to reproduce: “Placing of the goods on board the steamer named by the buyer under a F.O.B. contract clearly discharges the contractual liability of the seller as seller and the delivery to the buyer is complete and the goods may thenceforward be also at the risk of the buyer … Prima facie such delivery of the goods to the buyer and the passing of the risk in respect of the goods from the seller to the buyer are strong indications as to the passing also of the property in the goods to the buyer but they are not decisive and may be negatived, for under Section 25 the seller may yet reserve to himself the right of disposal of the goods until the fulfilment of certain conditions and thereby prevent the passing of property in the goods from him to the buyer.”

§1.10 · Section 26 — Risk prima facie passes with property

Section 26 — verbatim ACT
“Risk prima facie passes with property.—Unless otherwise agreed, the goods remain at the seller's risk until the property therein is transferred to the buyer, but when the property therein is transferred to the buyer, the goods are at the buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not:

Provided that, where delivery has been delayed through the fault of either buyer or seller, the goods are at the risk of the party in fault as regards any loss which might not have occurred but for such fault:

Provided also that nothing in this section shall affect the duties or liabilities of either seller or buyer as a bailee of the goods of the other party.”

The Latin tag is res perit domino — the thing perishes to its owner. s. 26 is the Act's version of it, and it answers the question that arises in nearly every sale-of-goods problem paper: the goods have been destroyed or damaged, no one is at fault, and someone must bear the loss. The answer is: whoever owned them at the moment of destruction. Which means the whole of ss. 18–25 has been leading up to this.

1 The main rule

Before property passes → seller's risk. After property passes → buyer's risk, and the decisive words are “whether delivery has been made or not”. Delivery is irrelevant to risk. This is the sentence students forget: a buyer can be carrying the risk of goods he has never touched, and a seller can carry the risk of goods sitting in the buyer's warehouse.

2 The two provisos

Proviso 1 — fault. Where delivery is delayed by the fault of one party, that party bears the loss “as regards any loss which might not have occurred but for such fault”. Proviso 2 — bailment. s. 26 does not disturb the duties either owes the other as bailee (ss. 151–152, Contract Act): a seller holding the buyer's goods must still take reasonable care of them.

§2.1 to §2.11 · the rule, the estoppel limb, the five statutory exceptions, and the one exception India does not have

★ How Part B is arranged, and why

Chapter III of the Act has two parts, and this one is headed Transfer of title. Everything in Part A was about when ownership moves. Everything here is about whether a seller who is not the owner can pass ownership at all. The two questions are independent: a buyer may have taken ‘property’ under ss. 18–25 and yet be defeated by the true owner because the seller had no title to give.

DU has asked the nemo dat question in 2011, 2012, 2013 and twice in 2015 — it is the single most repeated question on this Topic. The answer has a fixed shape: (1) state the maxim; (2) set out s. 27 verbatim; (3) explain the estoppel limb inside the section; (4) run through the five statutory exceptions, each with its conditions and its leading case; (5) note that market overt is not part of Indian law; (6) close with what the disappointed buyer gets instead. §§2.1–2.11 build exactly that answer.

§2.2 · The maxim — origin, scope and justification

L The two Latin forms

Nemo dat quod non habet — “no one gives what he does not have”. The civilian parallel is nemo plus iuris ad alium transferre potest quam ipse habet — “one cannot transfer to another more right than he himself has”. Both express the same idea.

H Its history in one quotation

Denning L.J. in Case 36: “In the early days of the common law the governing principle of our law of property was that no person could give a better title than he himself had got, but the needs of commerce have led to a progressive modification of this principle so as to protect innocent purchasers.”

⚖ The problem it creates

Two innocent parties — the true owner (O) and the good-faith purchaser (P) — and one rogue (R) who has vanished. Someone must lose. Denning L.J. again in Case 36: “The cases show how difficult it is to strike the right balance between the claims of true owners and the claims of innocent purchasers.”

Why the law carves out exceptions — the three justifications
  1. Estoppel. Where the true owner has held the seller out as owner or as authorised to sell, it would be dishonest for him to deny it afterwards. This is the in-built limb of s. 27 and s. 115 of the Evidence Act.
  2. Apparent authority / agency. Where the owner has entrusted goods to a mercantile agent, he has clothed that agent with the appearance of authority. Denning L.J. in Case 36: “Parliament has not protected the true owner if he has himself consented to a mercantile agent having possession of them, because, by leaving them in the agent's possession, he has clothed the agent with apparent authority to sell them, and he should not, therefore, be allowed to claim them back from an innocent purchaser.”
  3. Commercial convenience. In a market economy buyers cannot investigate title in every transaction. Scrutton L.J. in Folkes v. King [1923] 1 KB 282, 305, quoted by Denning L.J. in Case 36, put the point in the language of the motor trade: if purchasers insisted on the registration book, “it would be very difficult to find a market for the cars which have lately been stolen in such large numbers”.
☕ Real examples — the rule operating where people least expect it
  • The stolen laptop. A thief sells a laptop on a marketplace site. The buyer is wholly innocent and pays full value. s. 27 applies: he gets nothing. The thief had no title and no mercantile agent was involved. The buyer must return the laptop to the true owner and sue the thief (if he can find him). This is the common, harsh case — and the reason the exceptions exist.
  • The phone on EMI. A buyer takes a phone on a hire-purchase or EMI plan. Until the last instalment, he is a hirer, not an owner (Hire-Purchase Act 1972, s. 8 — property passes only on the exercise of the option). He sells it to an innocent friend. s. 30(2) may or may not save the friend: s. 30(2) requires that the disposer “bought or agreed to buy” the goods; and in Worcester Works Finance Ltd. v. Cooden Engineering Co. Ltd. [1972] 1 QB 210 the Court of Appeal held that a hirer under a hire-purchase agreement does fall within it — but the finance company's own hire-purchase terms usually prevent it.
  • The warehouse receipt. A trader pledges goods with a bank by handing over a warehouse-keeper's certificate (a document of title under s. 2(4)). The bank sells. Because the trader consented to the bank's possession of a document of title, and the bank sold as a pledgee exercising its power, the buyer is protected by the general law, not by s. 27 — the bank was not a ‘person who is not the owner’.
  • The double sale. A sells his car to B (property passes under s. 20) but keeps the car — and then sells it to C, who knows nothing of B. This is s. 30(1): C gets a good title and B loses the car, though B has a claim against A.

§ The pair of cases that fix the boundary
  • Central Newbury Car Auctions Ltd. v. Unity Finance Ltd. [1957] 1 QB 371. The owner of a car allowed a rogue to have both the car and its registration book. Held: by parting with the registration book — the document that in the motor trade is treated as the badge of title — the owner made a representation that the rogue was entitled to sell, and was estopped from denying it. The innocent purchaser won.
  • Moorgate Mercantile Co. Ltd. v. Twitchings [1977] AC 890 (HL). The owner of a car let a dealer have possession only, without the registration book, and the dealer sold it. Held: no estoppel. Merely parting with possession is not a representation of ownership, and the owner owed no duty of care to a potential purchaser at large. (The decision is also the reason Hire-Purchase Information — the trade's register — came to matter so much.)
★ How estoppel and Case 36 fit together

Pearson v. Rose & Young is not an estoppel case — it is a case on the proviso to s. 27 (the mercantile-agent exception). But the same fact decides both: the plaintiff kept the registration book. Denning L.J. spells out why that matters: “So long as the owner retains the log book, the mercantile agent cannot dispose of the car in the ordinary course of his business, because it is not in the ordinary course of anyone's business to dispose of a second-hand car without a log book.” Retention of the log book therefore “effectually prevents” the exception from operating. Learn the two doctrines together: possession + log book with consent → protection; possession without log book → no protection.

§2.4 · Exception 1 — Sale by a mercantile agent (proviso to s. 27)

Proviso to Section 27 — verbatim ACT
“Provided that, where a mercantile agent is, with the consent of the owner, in possession of the goods or of a document of title to the goods, any sale made by him, when acting in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent, shall be as valid as if he were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same; provided that the buyer acts in good faith and has not at the time of the contract of sale notice that the seller has not authority to sell.”
Section 2(9) — ‘mercantile agent’ — verbatim ACT
““mercantile agent” means a mercantile agent having in the customary course of business as such agent authority either to sell goods, or to consign goods for the purposes of sale, or to buy goods, or to raise money on the security of goods.”
★ A mnemonic that also tells you the law

A mercantile agent has authority to S–C–B–R: Sell · Consign for sale · Buy · Raise money on the security of goods. A person who cannot do at least one of those four things in the customary course of his business is not a mercantile agent, and the proviso cannot apply to him. A carrier, a warehouseman, a repairer, a servant, or a friend looking after your buffaloes is not a mercantile agent.

The five cumulative conditions

All five must be satisfied. If any one fails, the buyer gets no title and the true owner recovers the goods.

§ Condition 4 in detail — what ‘ordinary course of business’ means

This is the condition DU has examined, and it is the heart of Case 36. Three propositions come out of the cases:

  1. It is an objective test about the transaction, not about the agent's honesty. The question is whether a sale of this kind is one that a mercantile agent would ordinarily make.
  2. Selling outside the owner's instructions is usually still within the ordinary course. That is the entire point of the exception: the agent is protected precisely because he disobeyed. Selling at a lower price than instructed, or keeping the proceeds, does not by itself take the sale outside the ordinary course — but see Lowther v. Harris below.
  3. Selling without the documents that the trade treats as essential is not. Vaisey J. in Case 36 put it vividly: a dealer “cannot be said to be ‘acting in the ordinary course of’ his business as such when selling a car without its log book, any more … than he would be so acting if he sold a car with only three wheels”.
! The 2017 DU question, answered

Q (2017, Q6(b)): “Mercantile agent was entrusted with car by G to sell the same at stated price. The agent sold the car to bonafide purchaser below stated price and misappropriated the proceeds. Whether buyer will acquire a good title of car under Sale of Goods Act, 1930? Refer case law.”

A: No. Conditions 1–3 and 5 are satisfied (he is a mercantile agent; the car was entrusted to him as such; with the owner's consent; the buyer is bona fide and without notice). The case fails on condition 4. Wright J. held in Lowther v. Harris [1927] 1 KB 393 that where an owner entrusts goods to a mercantile agent to be sold at a specified minimum price, a sale at a lower price is not a sale in the ordinary course of business; the agent is doing something of a different kind from that which he was authorised to do. The buyer therefore acquires no title and must return the car to G.

But add the refinement that earns the extra marks. Had G entrusted the car without stipulating a price, a sale below market value would still have been in the ordinary course — an agent is expected to sell, and the owner's private reserve price does not define the ‘ordinary course’ unless it was communicated to, and formed part of the terms of, the agency. Contrast Jerome v. Bentley & Co. [1952] 2 All ER 114, where a rogue instructed to sell a ring for not less than £550 sold it for £175 and the Court of Appeal held the sale was not in the ordinary course — the buyer should have been put on inquiry by the suspiciously low price.

☕ Real examples
  • The commission agent (protected). A farmer sends 200 bags of Basmati to a commission agent at the mandi with instructions to sell at ₹4,500 a quintal. The agent sells at ₹4,300 to a buyer who knows nothing. The buyer is protected: an agent entrusted with goods for sale who sells at a modest variance is acting in the ordinary course. The farmer's remedy is against the agent for the shortfall.
  • The serviced car (protected). You leave your car with a dealer for a service. The dealer is a mercantile agent (he sells cars in the customary course of his business), he has possession with your consent, and — if you left the registration certificate in the glove box — he has everything needed to sell. An innocent buyer gets a good title. Leave the registration certificate at home.
  • The bike left for safekeeping (not protected). You leave your motorcycle with a neighbour while you are abroad. He sells it. He is not a mercantile agent — safekeeping is not selling, consigning, buying or raising money. The buyer gets nothing.
  • The pawn (protected). The proviso covers “any sale made by him”. A pledge by a mercantile agent is protected by the Factors Act, 1889 s. 2(1) in England; in India, a pledge by a mercantile agent is dealt with under the general law of agency and s. 178/179 of the Contract Act, 1872 (which survive for pledges). Raise this only if asked; s. 27's proviso speaks of sale.

§2.5 · Exception 2 — Sale by one of joint owners (s. 28)

Section 28 — verbatim ACT
“Sale by one of joint owners.—If one of several joint owners of goods has the sole possession of them by permission of the co-owners, the property in the goods is transferred to any person who buys them of such joint owner in good faith and has not at the time of the contract of sale notice that the seller has not authority to sell.”
s. 28 “is a reproduction of Exception 2 to section 108 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872” and “has been applied to a joint Hindu family” — Mulla, The Sale of Goods Act, 11th ed., [s 28.1].WEB
The four elements
i JOINT OWNERSHIP

There must be several joint owners of the goods — co-owners, partners, co-sharers, or members of a joint Hindu family. Each owns the whole together with the others; none owns an identified share of the physical thing.

ii SOLE POSSESSION

One of them must have sole possession. If all the co-owners have the goods, or if the seller is only a part-possessor, s. 28 does not apply. The section protects buyers who deal with the person who appears to be in charge.

iii BY PERMISSION

The possession must be by permission of the co-owners. Permission may be express or implied, and may be inferred from the ordinary arrangements of the family or firm.

iv GOOD FAITH + NO NOTICE

The buyer must buy in good faith and without notice at the time of the contract of sale that the seller had no authority. This is the same formula as in the proviso to s. 27.

★ The surprising consequence — the whole property passes

Note the wording: “the property in the goods is transferred” — not “the seller's share”. A joint owner who sells with permission and in good faith transfers the entire property, defeating his co-owners. Their remedy is against him for their share of the proceeds, not against the buyer. This is what makes s. 28 a real exception to nemo dat rather than a mere restatement of it.

☕ Real examples
  • Mulla's own illustration. A, B and C own certain cattle in common. A is left by B and C in possession of a cow, which he sells to D. D purchases bona fide. The property in the cow is transferred to D. B and C must sue A for their shares.
  • The joint family tractor. Three brothers of a joint Hindu family own a tractor. The eldest keeps it at his house and uses it, with the others' consent. He sells it to a neighbour who knows nothing of the arrangement. The neighbour gets a good title under s. 28 read with the Hindu joint family.
  • The partnership van. Two partners own a delivery van. The managing partner has sole possession. He sells it and absconds. If the buying was in good faith and without notice, s. 28 protects the buyer — though note that a partner selling partnership property in the ordinary course would in any case bind the firm under s. 22 of the Partnership Act, 1932, so s. 28 is a second string to the bow.
  • Where it fails. A co-owner who is not in sole possession — say two brothers both keep the tractor at the family farm and both use it — cannot pass the whole property. A buyer from one of them takes only what that brother had: an undivided share, and no right to exclusive possession.
! Do not confuse s. 28 with Case 34

Case 34 sounds as if it should be a joint-ownership case — the trial judge held that after the variation the parties “became joint owners of the stock 415 tons” — but the Supreme Court rejected that analysis: “In our opinion, this was not the correct legal position. The parties did not intend that the appellants would buy undivided share in 415 tons of newsprint.” The truth was that after 26 November 1951, the bargain was to buy 300 tons out of a larger stock of 415 tons, which is a contract for unascertained goods governed by s. 18, not a case of joint ownership under s. 28. Keep the two apart: joint ownership is an interest in every particle; a portion out of a bulk is no interest in anything until selection.

§2.6 · Exception 3 — Sale by a person in possession under a voidable contract (s. 29)

Section 29 — verbatim ACT
“Sale by person in possession under voidable contract.—When the seller of goods has obtained possession thereof under a contract voidable under section 19 or section 19A of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (9 of 1872), but the contract has not been rescinded at the time of the sale, the buyer acquires a good title to the goods, provided he buys them in good faith and without notice of the seller's defect of title.”
s. 29 “is, in effect, the first part of Exception 3 to section 108 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 … The wording of the section is taken from section 23 of the English Sale of Goods Act, 1893” — Mulla, 11th ed., [s 29.1].WEB
★ The whole section in one distinction: VOIDABLE, NOT VOID

A contract induced by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused (s. 19 and s. 19A, Contract Act) — but it is valid until it is rescinded. So the rogue who buys goods by fraud does acquire a title: a defective but real title. If he resells before the defrauded seller rescinds, the sub-buyer gets a good title and the original owner loses his goods. The race is therefore won by whoever moves first — rescind first, or lose your goods.

§ The four cases that draw the void / voidable line
  • Cundy v. Lindsay (1878) 3 App Cas 459 (HL) — VOID. A rogue signing himself ‘Blenkarn’ ordered handkerchiefs from Lindsay & Co., who knew of a reputable firm called Blenkiron & Co. and believed they were dealing with that firm. The House of Lords held there was no contract at all — the Lindsay firm never intended to contract with the writer of the letters. Title never passed to Blenkarn, so the innocent sub-purchaser had to return the goods.
  • Phillips v. Brooks Ltd. [1919] 2 KB 243 — VOIDABLE. A rogue went into a jeweller's shop, said “I am Sir George Bullough” and produced a cheque. The jeweller intended to contract with the person physically in front of him; the mistake was as to an attribute (his creditworthiness), not his identity. The contract was merely voidable; the pledgee who took the ring before rescission got a good title.
  • Ingram v. Little [1961] 1 QB 31 — VOID, on a narrow view. Two sisters sold a car to a rogue who said he was ‘P.G.M. Hutchinson’ of a named address; one sister checked the telephone directory and confirmed it. The Court of Appeal held they intended to deal with that Hutchinson, not with the man in front of them. (Much criticised, and confined to its facts.)
  • Lewis v. Averay [1972] 1 QB 198 — VOIDABLE. A rogue posed as the actor Richard Greene and produced a studio pass. The Court of Appeal preferred Phillips v. Brooks to Ingram v. Little and held the presumption, in a face-to-face transaction, is that you intend to deal with the person present. The contract was voidable; the innocent sub-buyer was protected.
How rescission works — and how to make it effective
  1. Who may rescind? The party whose consent was caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation (s. 19A, Contract Act — the party whose consent was so caused “may … rescind”; and under s. 19 he may, at his option, insist on performance and be put in the position he would have been in had the representation been true).
  2. How is it rescinded? By notice to the guilty party (s. 66, Contract Act) or by filing a suit. Communication to the rogue is normally required — but see Car & Universal Finance Co. Ltd. v. Caldwell [1965] 1 QB 525, where the Court of Appeal held that where the rogue has disappeared, the defrauded owner may rescind by any overt act evidencing an intention to rescind — informing the police and the trade association was enough.
  3. Bars to rescission (ss. 25, 28, 63, 67, Contract Act): affirmation after discovering the truth; lapse of a reasonable time; restoration of benefits being impossible; and — decisively — the intervention of third-party rights. s. 29 is the statutory form of that last bar.
  4. The practical point. Notify the rogue, notify the police, and notify the trade immediately. Every hour of delay is an hour in which the rogue may sell to an innocent buyer.
☕ Real examples
  • The bounced cheque (protected buyer). A man walks into an electronics shop, buys a laptop with a cheque that bounces, and sells it the same evening to a colleague who knows nothing. The shop's contract with the rogue is voidable for fraud. The shop does not discover the dishonour for four days. By then the sale to the colleague has happened before rescission, so the colleague gets a good title under s. 29. The shop's only remedy is against the rogue.
  • The fake ID (unprotected buyer). A rogue emails a dealer, forging the identity of a well-known company with a real registered office, and orders goods on 30 days' credit. The dealer intends to deal with that company, not with the writer. On Cundy v. Lindsay principles the contract is void: the rogue acquires no title at all, s. 29 never applies, and a sub-buyer gets nothing.
  • The hire-purchase car (voidable, and time-sensitive). Butterworth v. Kingsway Motors Ltd. [1954] 1 WLR 1286: a hirer under a hire-purchase agreement who had not yet exercised his option sold the car. Until he exercised the option his title was voidable, not void; because the innocent sub-buyer bought before the finance company rescinded, he got a good title and the finance company lost the car.
  • The exam contrast. A rogue who buys by fraud has a voidable title (s. 29 protects the sub-buyer). A rogue who steals has no title (s. 27 defeats the sub-buyer). A rogue to whom the owner entrusted the goods as a mercantile agent can pass a title under the proviso to s. 27. Three rogues, three different answers.

§2.7 · Exceptions 4 and 5 — Seller or buyer in possession after sale (s. 30)

Section 30 — verbatim ACT
“Seller or buyer in possession after sale.—(1) Where a person, having sold goods, continues or is in possession of the goods or of the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or documents of title under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of the previous sale shall have the same effect as if the person making the delivery or transfer were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same.

(2) Where a person, having bought or agreed to buy goods, obtains, with the consent of the seller, possession of the goods or of the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person or by a mercantile agent acting for him, of the goods or documents of title under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of any lien or other right of the original seller in respect of the goods shall have effect as if such lien or right did not exist.”
30(1) SELLER in possession

The seller has sold (property has passed to B) but continues or is in possession of the goods or of a document of title. He then disposes of them to C. C gets a good title and B loses the goods. The disposition may be a sale, pledge or other disposition — the words are deliberately wide.

30(2) BUYER in possession

The buyer has bought or agreed to buy and has obtained possession with the seller's consent — but property has not yet passed to him. He then disposes of the goods to C. C takes free of the unpaid seller's lien or other right. The original seller loses his lien; his remedy is against B for the price.

! Note the difference between s. 30(2) and s. 53(1)

Both deal with a buyer who disposes of goods before paying. But s. 53(1) is the default: “the unpaid seller's right of lien or stoppage in transit is not affected by any sale or other disposition which the buyer may have made, unless the seller has assented thereto” — subject to a proviso where a document of title has been lawfully transferred to a person who transfers it in good faith and for consideration. s. 30(2) is the exception to that default where the buyer has possession with the seller's consent and the transferee is in good faith. In an exam, run s. 53(1) first and s. 30(2) second.

☕ Real examples
  • The double sale (s. 30(1)). A sells his motorcycle to B on Monday; property passes at once under s. 20. B says “I'll collect it at the weekend”. On Wednesday A sells the same motorcycle to C, who knows nothing, and hands it over. C gets a good title; B loses the bike and must sue A for the price (and damages for conversion).
  • The warehouse warrant (s. 30(1), documents of title). A sells 500 bales of cotton to B but keeps the warehouse-keeper's certificate. He then pledges the certificate with a bank. The bank takes in good faith. The bank's pledge is valid under s. 30(1), which expressly extends to “documents of title” and to a “pledge or other disposition”.
  • The machine on trial (s. 30(2)). A dealer delivers a photocopier to a firm “on approval” — the firm has possession with the dealer's consent but property has not passed (s. 24). The firm sells it to an innocent purchaser. Two answers: on one view this is an act adopting the transaction under s. 24(a), so property passed first and the firm sold its own goods; on another, if no adoption had occurred, s. 30(2) still protects the purchaser because the firm had “agreed to buy” and had possession with consent. Either way the purchaser wins — which is why the 2015 DU question on the jeweller's diamonds is best answered on both grounds.
  • The car on finance (s. 30(2)). A finance company lets X have a car under a hire-purchase agreement. Before paying the last instalment X sells the car to an innocent neighbour; property had not passed, because under s. 8 of the Hire-Purchase Act, 1972 it passes only when the hirer exercises his option in the manner specified. If X “agreed to buy” and had possession with consent, s. 30(2) protects the neighbour (Worcester Works Finance); if X was a mere hirer who had not exercised the option, the neighbour may lose (Stadium Finance v. Robbins).

§2.8 · Sale in Market Overt — the exception India does not have

! Start here: there is no market-overt provision in the Sale of Goods Act, 1930

The DU syllabus lists “sale in Market Overt” as a head under Topic 7. The Sale of Goods Act, 1930 contains no provision for it. If your answer opens with the Indian statutory text, you have made a mistake. Open instead with the absence: state that the rule exists in the English Sale of Goods Act 1893 (s. 22(1)), that the Indian legislature deliberately left it out, and that England has since abolished it. Then explain what the rule was. That is the answer that scores.

What the rule was

Market overt (Law French marché ouvert) was a medieval English institution. Where goods were openly sold in a legally constituted open market or fair, between sunrise and sunset, and in accordance with the usage of the market, a buyer who bought in good faith and without notice of any defect in the seller's title acquired a good title — even if the goods had been stolen. It was the most dramatic of all the nemo dat exceptions, because it protected the buyer even where the true owner had done nothing at all.

Why India never adopted it — the reason is worth quoting

Mulla records the history: “The Select Committee to which the Bill was referred objected to this clause, the ground of objection being substantially that the provision would make British India an asylum for cattle stealers from the native States. So the ancestor of ss. 27–30 — s. 108 of the Contract Act — is the market-overt clause, recast. Instead of protecting a buyer who buys in a market, the Indian legislature protected buyers who deal with a person the owner has armed: an agent, a co-owner, a rogue under a voidable contract, a seller or buyer in possession. The emphasis moved from where you bought to whom you bought from.

Why England abolished it

The Sale of Goods (Amendment) Act 1994 (c. 32) received Royal Assent on 3 November 1994 and came into force on 3 January 1995. Its long title is simply “An Act to abolish the rule of law relating to the sale of goods in market overt”. It repealed s. 22(1) of the Sale of Goods Act 1979 and s. 47 of the Laws in Wales Act 1542. The reasons were the growth of car-boot sales and similar venues where stolen property could be ‘fenced’ easily — Parliament called market overt a “thieves' charter”. §2.11 · What the disappointed buyer recovers

If s. 27 bites, the buyer loses the goods to the true owner. He is not without a remedy against his own seller — and the measure is more generous than students expect.

§ Rowland v. Divall — total failure of consideration

Rowland v. Divall [1923] 1 KB 500 (CA). The plaintiff bought a motor car from the defendant for £334 and used it for four months. It turned out to have been stolen; the plaintiff was compelled to return it to the true owner. He sued to recover the whole £334.

Held: he recovered the entire price, with no deduction for the four months' use. Scrutton L.J.: “He is not getting the car at all, and the consideration has wholly failed.” Atkin L.J.: the buyer contracted to buy not merely the physical thing but the property in it, and he had received nothing of what he bargained for. In India the same result is reached under s. 65 of the Contract Act, 1872 (obligation to restore an advantage received under a void or subsequently-voided contract) read with the implied condition as to title in s. 14(a) of the Sale of Goods Act — a breach of a condition, so the buyer may in addition treat the contract as repudiated under s. 12(2).

1 Against the seller

Recover the full price for total failure of consideration (s. 65 ICA; Rowland v. Divall), and damages for breach of the s. 14(a) implied condition as to title. Note that the two overlap — you cannot recover twice.

2 Against the true owner

Normally nothing — he must return the goods and may be liable in conversion for the period of detention. He may, however, claim for improvements he made in good faith (s. 51, Specific Relief Act, 1963) and, where he was a bona fide possessor, for necessary expenses.

3 Time limits

A suit for the price back is governed by Article 24 (money paid for a consideration that afterwards failed) or Article 26 of the Limitation Act, 1963 — three years from the date the consideration failed, i.e. from the date the true owner's claim was established.

☕ Real example — the second-hand luxury watch

You buy a second-hand luxury watch for ₹4.5 lakh from a dealer, wear it for eight months, and are then told it was stolen in Mumbai two years ago. The police seize it and return it to the insurer. Under s. 27 you never got any title at all. Against the true owner you have nothing. Against the dealer: ₹4.5 lakh back in full, with no deduction for eight months' wear (Rowland v. Divall), plus interest — and you should additionally plead breach of the s. 14(a) condition as to title and s. 14(b) warranty of quiet possession. If the dealer has vanished, your only remaining route is a consumer complaint under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019 for deficiency in service and unfair trade practice.

§3.1 · Case 33 — CIT v. Mysore Chromite Ltd. DU p. 227

33. Commissioner of Income Tax v. Mysore Chromite Ltd.
(1955) 1 SCR 849 : AIR 1955 SC 98 · Supreme Court of India · S.R. Das J. · DU Reader p. 227 · Income-tax reference under s. 66(1), Income Tax Act, from the Madras High Court (judgment dated 29 March 1951)
Under an F.O.B. contract the delivery and the risk may pass at the port of shipment while the property passes somewhere else entirely — because the seller, by taking the bill of lading in his own name and releasing it only against acceptance of the draft, reserves the right of disposal under s. 25. Appropriation that is conditional is not appropriation within s. 23(1).
Facts

The assessee. A private limited company registered in the Mysore State under the Mysore Company Regulations, with its registered office at Sinduvalli, Mysore State. The management and control of the company was vested in Messrs Oakley Bowden & Co. (Madras) Ltd., another private limited company incorporated under the Indian Companies Act, with its registered office at No. 15, Armenian Street, Madras.

The business. The assessee owned chromite mines in Mysore State. Chrome ore was extracted, converted into a merchantable product and sold to buyers mostly outside India. A very small proportion of the sales was effected in India, and for the purposes of the case that was left out of consideration: the sales were mostly to buyers in America and Europe.

The two selling channels.

  • European purchasers: the sales were put through in London by Bowden Oakley & Co. Ltd., London, the assessee's agent in Europe, holding a power of attorney from the assessee. The contracts were signed by Bowden Oakley & Co. Ltd. in London.
  • American purchasers: the sales were effected through Messrs W.R. Grace & Co., who bought for undisclosed principals. The contracts were signed by W.R. Grace & Co. (presumably in America) and by Oakley Bowden & Co. (Madras) Ltd. in Madras.

The terms. Specimen forms of both contracts were set out in the order of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal dated 22 January 1948, out of which the reference arose. Under both forms the price was F.O.B. Madras or Marmagoa (a very small quantity was sold F.O.B. Marmagoa and was left out of consideration). Provision was made for weighment, sampling and assay of the goods at destination.

The course of dealing, as found by the Appellate Tribunal. Before the goods were actually shipped, the buyers used to open a confirmed irrevocable bankers' credit with some first-class bank in London. Being informed of the opening of such credit, the Eastern Bank Ltd., London sent intimation to the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, which in turn passed the intimation on by letter to the assessee company. A specimen of that letter was also set out in the Tribunal's order. In it the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras informed the assessee that —

“in accordance with advices received by letter from our London Office, a confirmed and irrevocable credit has been opened in your favour by Messrs Morgan Grenfell & Co., Ltd., London, for account of Messrs W.R. Grace & Co., New York, for a sum not exceeding £7,300 (seven thousand three hundred pounds sterling) in all, available by delivery to us on or before 15 January, 1940 of the following documents…”

Towards the end of the letter the bank wrote that they were “prepared in our options as customary to negotiate drafts drawn in terms of the arrangement provided that the documents as abovementioned appear to us to be in order”. The letter concluded with a warning that the advice was “given for your guidance and without involving any responsibility on the part of this Bank”.

What the assessee then did. On receipt of such intimation the assessee company —

  1. placed the contracted goods on board the steamer at Madras and obtained a bill of lading in its own name (shipments were made principally at Madras Port);
  2. made out a provisional invoice on the basis of the bill of lading weight and the contract price for 48 per cent Cr2O3;
  3. drew a bill of exchange on the buyers' bank where the letter of credit had been opened — for 90 per cent of the amount of the provisional invoice payable at sight in the case of European contracts, and 80 per cent at 90 days' sight in the case of American contracts — in either case in favour of the Eastern Bank Ltd., London;
  4. negotiated the bill of exchange, together with the relative bill of lading endorsed in blank by the assessee and the provisional invoice, with the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, its own bankers, who credited the assessee with the amount of the bill of exchange;
  5. the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras then forwarded the documents to the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, which presented the bill of exchange to the buyers' bank in London and, upon the bill being accepted, delivered the bill of lading and the invoice to the buyers' bank. The buyers' bank in due course paid the amount of the bill to the Eastern Bank Ltd., London;
  6. thereafter, on arrival of the goods and after weighment and assay, the sale price was ascertained and the balance of the price, after deducting the payments made against the bill of exchange, was paid to the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, which was the assessee company's agent and banker in London.

The two questions referred under s. 66(1) of the Income Tax Act — both of which the Madras High Court answered in the affirmative, i.e. in favour of the assessee:

“(1) Whether on the facts and in the circumstances of the case the profits derived by the assessee company from sales made to European and American buyers arose outside British India?

(2) Whether on the facts and in the circumstances of the case the profits derived by the assessee company from sales made to European and American buyers were received outside British India?”

Issues
  1. Where did the property in the goods pass? The Revenue's case stood or fell on the property passing at Madras. If it passed in London, the sales took place outside British India and the profits arose outside British India.
  2. Specifically, was there an “unconditional appropriation” within s. 23(1) of the Sale of Goods Act merely because the assessee placed the goods on board the steamer named by the buyer at Madras Port under an F.O.B. contract?
  3. Did the assessee reserve the right of disposal under s. 25? — in particular, did taking the bill of lading in its own name have that effect?
  4. Were the profits received in British India? — i.e. was the 80%/90% paid by the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras a payment of the price, or something else?
Arguments
For the appellant (the Commissioner of Income Tax — the Revenue)

Learned Solicitor-General, on Question 1:

  • Having regard to the terms of the contracts, the sales must be regarded as having taken place in British India.
  • Three facts were strongly relied on: (i) the price and delivery of goods were on F.O.B. terms; (ii) in the European contracts the insurance, if any, was to be the concern of the buyers; and (iii) payment of the 80 per cent or 90 per cent, as the case may be, was made in Madras by the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, to the assessee company on the delivery of the documents.
  • All these facts taken together indicate that the property in the goods passed at Madras, and the sales were accordingly completed in British India.

Learned Solicitor-General, on Question 2:

  • Irrespective of where the sale may have taken place, the profits were received in Madras. After shipment the assessee, through its managing agent in Madras, prepared provisional invoices, drew bills of exchange for 80/90 per cent, handed them to the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, and received the amount of the bill of exchange from them in Madras.
  • That receipt “was really the receipt of the price of the goods and amounted to receipt of profits in Madras”.
  • Reliance was placed on the words “through the Eastern Bank Ltd.” appearing in the contract, and the words “available by delivery to us” appearing in the letter of intimation — suggesting the Eastern Bank was acting for the buyers.
For the respondent (the assessee company)
  • The contracts were always for the sale of unascertained goods, so no property could pass until ascertainment (s. 18).
  • Under the provision in the contract for weighment and assay — which was ultimately to fix the price unless the buyer rightly rejected the goods as not being in terms of the contract — the passing of property could not take place until the buyer accepted the goods and the price was fully ascertained after weighment and assay.
  • That being the position, the property in the goods passed and the sales were concluded outside British India, for the weighment, sampling, assay and the final fixation of the price could only take place, under all these contracts, outside British India.
  • On Question 2: the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras and the Eastern Bank Ltd., London were agents of the assessee company; neither had any relation with the buyers; a payment by them therefore cannot be a payment of the price.
Decision — Question 1: the property passed in LONDON

Appeal dismissed. Both questions answered in favour of the assessee. S.R. Das J., for the Court:

“We are unable to accept this line of reasoning.” The Court began with s. 4 of the Sale of Goods Act — a contract of sale is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods for a price; where the property is transferred the contract is called a sale, but where the transfer is to take place at a future time or subject to some condition thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is called an agreement to sell; and by s. 4(4) an agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled.

“Section 18 of the Act clearly indicates that in the case of sale of unascertained goods no property in the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained. In the present case, the contracts were always for sale of unascertained goods. Skipping over Sections 19 to 22 which deal with contract of sale of specific goods we come to Section 23 which lays down that where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained or future goods by description and goods of that description and in a deliverable state are unconditionally appropriated to the contract … the property in the goods thereupon passes to the buyer. It is suggested that as soon as the assessee company placed the goods on board the steamer named by the buyer at the Madras Port the goods became ascertained and the property in the goods passed immediately to the buyer. This argument, however, overlooks the important word ‘unconditionally’ used in the section. The requirement of the section is not only that there shall be appropriation of the goods to the contract but that such appropriation must be made unconditionally.” CIT v. Mysore Chromite Ltd., AIR 1955 SC 98, per S.R. Das J.

The Court then read s. 23 with s. 25:

“This is further elaborated by Section 25 which provides that where there is a contract for the sale of specific goods or where goods are subsequently appropriated to the contract, the seller may, by the terms of the contract or appropriation, reserve the right of disposal of the goods until certain conditions are fulfilled. In such a case, notwithstanding the delivery of the goods to the buyer, or to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, the property in the goods does not pass to the buyer until the conditions imposed by the seller are fulfilled. The question in this case, therefore, is: was there an unconditional appropriation of the goods by merely placing them on the ship?” Ibid.

And the answer:

“It is true that the price and delivery was F.O.B., Madras but the contracts themselves clearly required the buyers to open a confirmed irrevocable Bankers' credit for the requisite percentage of the invoice value to be available against documents. This clearly indicated that the buyers would not be entitled to the documents, that is, the bill of lading and the provisional invoice, until payment of the requisite percentage was made upon the bill of exchange. The bill of lading is the document of title to the goods and by this term the assessee company clearly reserved the right of disposal of the goods until the bill of exchange was paid.” Ibid.

Then the passage that every DU answer on s. 25 should quote — the rejection of the inference from delivery and risk:

“Placing of the goods on board the steamer named by the buyer under a F.O.B. contract clearly discharges the contractual liability of the seller as seller and the delivery to the buyer is complete and the goods may thenceforward be also at the risk of the buyer against which he may cover himself by taking out an insurance. Prima facie such delivery of the goods to the buyer and the passing of the risk in respect of the goods from the seller to the buyer are strong indications as to the passing also of the property in the goods to the buyer but they are not decisive and may be negatived, for under Section 25 the seller may yet reserve to himself the right of disposal of the goods until the fulfilment of certain conditions and thereby prevent the passing of property in the goods from him to the buyer.” Ibid.

The Court expressly declined to decide the wider argument that property could not pass at all until weighment, assay, acceptance and final ascertainment of the price at the destination:

“It is not necessary for us to express any opinion on this extreme contention. Suffice it to say, for the purposes of this case, that in any event upon the terms of the contracts in question and the course of dealings between the parties the property in the goods could not have passed to the buyer earlier than the date when the bill of exchange was accepted by the buyers' Bank in London and the documents were delivered by the assessee company's agent, the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, to the buyers' Bank. This admittedly, and as found by the Appellate Tribunal, always took place in London. It must, therefore, follow that at the earliest the property in the goods passed in London where the bill of lading was handed over to the buyers' Bank against the acceptance of the relative bill of exchange.” Ibid.

Accordingly the Appellate Tribunal and the High Court were “quite correct in holding that the sales took place outside British India and, ex hypothesi, the profits derived from such sales arose outside British India”.

Decision — Question 2: the profits were received in LONDON

(a) “through the Eastern Bank Ltd.” — the Court held these words did not help the Revenue:

“The words ‘through the Eastern Bank Ltd.,’ appear to us to go with the preceding words ‘to be advised to sellers’ which are put within brackets which seem to have been wrongly closed after the word ‘sellers’ instead of after the words ‘the Eastern Bank Ltd.’ Ordinarily, the buyer opens a letter of credit with his Bank in favour of the seller and the words ‘through the Eastern Bank Ltd.’ would be meaningless unless it was intended to mean that the irrevocable credit which was in favour of the assessee company was to be operated upon by the latter through the Eastern Bank Ltd. If that were the true meaning, then that certainly does not make the Eastern Bank Ltd. the agent of the buyers.” Ibid.

(b) “available by delivery to us” — likewise rejected, on the wording of the letter as a whole:

“The words ‘available by delivery to us’ occurring in the letter of the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, do not appear to us to indicate that this was any part of the terms of the letter of credit. This was an intimation in accordance with the advice received by the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, from the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, that the assessee company might avail itself of the letter of credit by delivery of the documents to the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras. This is made further clear by the latter part of the letter where the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, expressed their willingness at their option to negotiate the drafts drawn in terms of the arrangement provided that the documents were in order. The concluding sentence of that letter whereby the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, disown any responsibility in respect of the advice clearly militates against the suggestion of the learned Solicitor-General.” Ibid.

(c) The decisive point — who was the bank acting for?

“Normally, price is paid by or on behalf of the buyer. In this case the fact found is that the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, and the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, were agents of the assessee company. Neither of them had any relation with the buyers. Therefore, a payment by them cannot be regarded as a payment of the price.” Ibid.

The Court then adopted Lord Sumner's analysis of the documentary-credit mechanism in The Prinz Adalbert [LR (1917) AC 586, 589]:

“When a shipper takes his draft, not as yet accepted, but accompanied by a bill of lading indorsed in this way, and discounts it with a banker, he makes himself liable on the instrument as drawer, and further makes the goods, which the bill of lading represents, security for its payment. If, in turn, the discounting banker surrenders the bill of lading to the acceptor against his acceptance, the inference is that he is satisfied to part with his security in consideration of getting this further party's liability on the bill, and that in so doing he acts with the permission and by the mandate of the shipper and drawer.” Lord Sumner, The Prinz Adalbert [1917] AC 586, 589 — quoted and applied

Applying it:

“This payment by the Eastern Bank Ltd., Madras, therefore, is nothing but an advance made by them to their own customer on the security of the goods covered by the bill of lading reinforced by the benefit of the liability taken up by the assessee company as drawer of the bill which in its turn is backed by the confirmed and irrevocable credit of the buyers' London Bank. If this payment was on account of the price, why should the assessee company, as the seller, undertake any liability to the Eastern Bank Ltd., as the drawer of the bill of exchange? The truth of the matter is that the price was paid on behalf of the buyers by their respective London Banks in London to the Eastern Bank Ltd., London which was the agent of the assessee company. The first receipt of the price, therefore, as pointed out by the High Court, was by the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, on behalf of the sellers.” CIT v. Mysore Chromite Ltd., AIR 1955 SC 98

The balance of the price, ascertained after weighment and assay and after deducting the amount paid on the bill of exchange, was “similarly received in London by the Eastern Bank Ltd., London, on behalf of the assessee company”. And “the subsequent adjustment made in the books of the Eastern Bank Ltd., London did not operate as a receipt of profits in British India”.

Principles evolved
  1. F.O.B. fixes delivery and risk, not property. “Placing of the goods on board the steamer named by the buyer under a F.O.B. contract clearly discharges the contractual liability of the seller as seller” — but property is a separate question, governed by ss. 18–25. This is the single most quotable proposition in the case.
  2. ‘Unconditionally’ is a substantive requirement of s. 23(1), not a flourish. “The requirement of the section is not only that there shall be appropriation of the goods to the contract but that such appropriation must be made unconditionally.” An appropriation coupled with a reservation of disposal is not an appropriation at all.
  3. The bill of lading is the standard instrument of reservation. “The bill of lading is the document of title to the goods and by this term the assessee company clearly reserved the right of disposal of the goods until the bill of exchange was paid.” (Sub-section (2) of s. 25, inserted by Act 33 of 1963, now makes this a prima facie presumption — the judgment anticipated the amendment by eight years.)
  4. Delivery and the passing of risk are only indicia. “Prima facie … strong indications … but they are not decisive and may be negatived.” A student who stops at “F.O.B. therefore property passed at Madras” has been answered by this sentence.
  5. A banker's payment against documents is an advance, not a payment of price. Where the bank is the seller's banker, negotiating the seller's draft on the security of the documents, the price is paid only when the buyer's bank honours the draft. “Normally, price is paid by or on behalf of the buyer.”
  6. Judicial restraint on the wider question. The Court refused to decide whether property could pass at all before weighment, assay and final ascertainment of price at destination, disposing of the case on the narrower ground. Note the technique: decide only what is necessary.
  7. A sale-of-goods rule can decide a tax case. The location of a sale, and hence the accrual and receipt of profits, was determined entirely by ss. 18, 23 and 25 of the Sale of Goods Act. Allied statutes ride on this Topic.
★ How to use Case 33 in a DU answer
  • Cite it for s. 25 every single time. It is the only Indian Supreme Court authority in your syllabus on reservation of the right of disposal, and it is the natural answer to any question containing the words “F.O.B.”, “C.I.F.”, “bill of lading”, “documents against acceptance” or “letter of credit”.
  • Cite it for s. 23(1) too. The word ‘unconditionally’ is the bridge between the two sections, and the Court's treatment is the clearest exposition available.
  • Cite it for s. 26 whenever you need to show that risk and property can split.
  • Do not cite it for s. 18. Although the Court mentions s. 18, the goods did become ascertainable on shipment; the case was decided on the conditional character of the appropriation. For s. 18 use Case 34.
! Reader errata — two slips in the printed text
  • The reader's print of the judgment runs paragraphs 3, 4, 7, 8, 9 — paragraphs 5 and 6 are not printed in the Faculty's extract. Nothing turns on it, but do not be thrown by the numbering when you cite the case.
  • The specimen bank letter is printed as “prepared in our options as customary” where the sense plainly requires “prepared in our option as customary” — a typographical slip in the reader.

§3.2 · Case 34 — P.S.N.S. Ambalavana Chettiar v. Express Newspapers Ltd. DU p. 232

34. P.S.N.S. Ambalavana Chettiar and Company Ltd. v. Express Newspapers Ltd.
(1968) 2 SCR 239 : AIR 1968 SC 741 · Supreme Court of India · R.S. Bachawat J. · DU Reader p. 232 · Civil Appeals Nos. 165 and 166 of 1965, on certificate from a Division Bench of the Madras High Court (trial: Rajagopala Ayyangar J.)
A contract to buy 300 tons out of a larger stock of 415 tons is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods. No property passes until the portion is identified and appropriated (s. 18) — and until it does, the seller has no statutory power of resale under s. 54(2) and must fall back on damages measured by s. 73 of the Contract Act.
Facts

The dispute arose out of a contract between the appellants and the respondent entered into on 13 November 1951. Its terms were recorded in writing in the form of a letter written by the respondent to Appellant 1:

“Messrs. P.S.N.S. Ambalavana Chettiar and Company Ltd., 260, Angappa Naicken Street, Madras.

Dear Sirs,

We confirm having purchased from you and the Madras Paper Marketing Company, Madras, 500 tons of Russian Newsprint as per the following description: About 70 per cent in reels of 34 inches width, “15 per cent in reels of 22 inches width; 15 per cent in reels of 36 inches width” at annas 9 per lb. ex-wharf Bombay duty, etc., paid. The buyers are to take delivery within four days of the offer of delivery. Any wharfage, etc., up to the fourth day of the offer of delivery will be on seller's account and thereafter on buyer's account.

We have also sold you about 415 tons of Russian newsprint in sheets in size of about 30″ × 42″ (760 mm × 1085 mm) ex-godown, Madras at Re. 0-9-6 per lb. We will keep the stock of sheets in our godown on your account free of rent. We shall advance you moneys against this newsprint at annas 8 per lb. This advance will carry interest at 5 per cent per annum. We will also charge you the exact amount of insurance which we pay to our Insurance Company against the goods.

We shall pay Rs 5,60,000 to your Bankers in Bombay and take delivery of the 500 tons of newsprint from the harbour in Bombay. Accounts will be made on the basis of the above arrangement and whatever one party is liable to pay to the other will be adjusted subsequently.”

In substance, therefore, there were two cross-sales:

  • the respondent bought 500 tons of Russian newsprint in reels from the appellants at 9 annas per lb., ex-wharf Bombay, taking delivery on payment of Rs 5,60,000; and
  • the appellants bought 415 tons of Russian newsprint in sheets then lying in a godown in Madras at 9 annas 6 pies per lb., upon the terms that the appellants would pay the insurance charge and interest at 5 per cent per annum on an amount equivalent to the price calculated at 8 annas per lb.

The understanding was that the appellants would take delivery of the sheets in instalments within a reasonable time and the accounts would be finally adjusted on completion of the deliveries. Appellant 2 carried on business under the name and style of Madras Paper Marketing Company.

The variation. On 26 November 1951 the parties orally agreed that instead of 500 tons the respondent would buy 300 tons in reels, and instead of 415 tons the appellants would buy 300 tons in sheets — and that the terms of the contract dated 13 November 1951 would stand varied accordingly.

Performance and breach. On 5 December 1951 the respondent took delivery of 300 tons of newsprint in reels on payment of Rs 3,18,706-9-10, leaving Rs 57,816-13-2 due to the appellants on account of the price of those goods. Between 29 November 1951 and 27 February 1952 the appellants took delivery of 122,324 lbs. of newsprint in sheets on payment of Rs 63,032-15-9. They then refused to take delivery of the balance of 547,501 lbs. Counsel for the parties agreed before the Supreme Court that 29 March 1952 was the date of repudiation.

The resale. On 21 April 1952, after giving notice to the appellants, the respondent resold the balance goods to one G.R. Lala at 6½ annas per lb.

The litigation. On 18 April 1952 the appellants filed C.S. No. 175 of 1952 in the Madras High Court claiming Rs 57,816-13-2 — the balance price of the 300 tons in reels — with interest. The respondent admitted that claim. On 30 July 1952 the respondent filed C.S. No. 262 of 1952 claiming a decree for Rs 62,266-13-2: the balance price of the 122,324 lbs., the deficiency on the resale of the 547,501 lbs., interest and insurance charges, after setting off the Rs 57,816-13-2 due to the appellants. The appellants' principal defence was that the contract with regard to the 415 tons of newsprint in sheets was cancelled in November 1951 and that Appellant 2 was not a party to it; they also denied the factum and validity of the resale.

Rajagopala Ayyangar J. dismissed C.S. No. 175 of 1952 and decreed C.S. No. 262 of 1952. A Division Bench dismissed both appeals. The present appeals were filed on certificates granted by the High Court.

Concurrent findings, not challenged in the Supreme Court: (1) Appellant 2 was a party to the contract of purchase of 415 tons of newsprint in sheets; (2) on 26 November 1951 the parties orally agreed to reduce the quantity to 300 tons; (3) there was no cancellation of the contract as alleged; and (4) the resale held on 21 April 1952 was genuine, effected at a proper price, on due notice and after proper advertisement. Mr Gupte attempted to challenge the last of these, but the Court saw no reason to interfere.

Issues
  1. Had the property in the 300 tons of newsprint in sheets passed to the appellants before the resale of 21 April 1952? — the decisive question.
  2. If not, did the respondent have a right of resale — under s. 176 of the Contract Act (as pledgee), or under s. 54(2) of the Sale of Goods Act (as unpaid seller)?
  3. If the resale was invalid, what is the correct measure of damages?
Arguments
For the appellants (Ambalavana Chettiar & Co. — the buyers)

Mr Gupte:

  • The principal argument: “the property in the goods resold on April 21, 1952 had not passed to the appellants and the resale was consequently invalid.”
  • After the variation of 26 November 1951, the subject-matter of the sale was 300 tons out of a larger stock of 415 tons. Nothing had been identified, set apart or appropriated to the contract; therefore, under s. 18, no property had passed.
  • Since no property had passed, the respondent could not resell under s. 54(2), which presupposes that property has passed to the buyer subject to the unpaid seller's lien.
  • Nor could the respondent rely on s. 176 of the Contract Act as a pledgee: the arrangement was not a pledge at all — no loan had been made on the security of the goods.
  • Accordingly the claim for the deficiency on resale was unsustainable.
For the respondent (Express Newspapers Ltd. — the sellers)
  • Under the original contract of 13 November 1951 there was an unconditional contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state — the stock of 415 tons lying in the respondent's godown — and the property passed then.
  • The variation of 26 November 1951 merely reduced the quantity: the property in the entire 415 tons remained with the appellants, who were subsequently relieved of their liability to take 115 tons.
  • Alternatively (per Rajagopala Ayyangar J.), the variation made the appellants and the respondent joint owners of the stock of 415 tons.
  • Either way, property had passed, so the respondent as an unpaid seller with a lien was entitled to resell under s. 54(2) and to recover the deficiency.
  • Reliance was also placed on the clause “We shall advance you moneys against this newsprint at annas 8 per lb. This advance will carry interest at 5 per cent per annum” — suggesting a security arrangement.
Decision — the appeal was allowed in part

The Supreme Court accepted the appellants' principal argument. R.S. Bachawat J.:

Step 1 — there was no pledge, so s. 176 of the Contract Act is irrelevant.

“It is to be noticed that the contract did not envisage any loan of money by the respondent to the appellants on the security of the newsprint in sheets. The payment of Rs 3,18,706-9-10 was made by the respondent towards part discharge of its liability for the price of the newsprint in reels. No doubt, the contract stated: ‘We shall advance you moneys against this newsprint at annas 8 per lb. This advance will carry interest at 5 per cent per annum.’ But the real import of this clause was that the appellants would pay interest at 5 per cent per annum on an amount equivalent to the price of the newsprint in sheets calculated at 8 annas per lb. The respondent was not a pledgee of the newsprint in sheets and had no right to sell the goods under Section 176 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The real question is whether the respondent had the right to resell the goods under Section 54(2) of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.” AIR 1968 SC 741, ¶ 7

Step 2 — s. 54(2) requires that the property has passed.

“The seller can claim as damages the difference between the contract price and the amount realised on resale of the goods where he has the right of resale under Section 54(2) of the Sale of Goods Act. The statutory power of resale under Section 54(2) arises if the property in the goods has passed to the buyer subject to the lien of the unpaid seller. Where the property in the goods has not passed to the buyer, the seller has no right of resale under Section 54(2). The question is whether the property in the 300 tons of newsprint in sheets had passed to the appellants before the resale.” Ibid., ¶ 8

Step 3 — the original contract was for specific goods, and property passed on 13 November 1951.

“On November 13, 1951, the respondent agreed to sell to the appellants the stock of 415 tons of newsprint in sheets then lying in the respondent's godown in Madras. There was an unconditional contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state and the property in the goods then passed to the appellants.” Ibid., ¶ 9

Step 4 — but the variation substituted a contract for unascertained goods.

“But on November 26, 1951, the contract was varied in a material particular. The parties agreed that the appellants would buy only 300 tons of the stock of 415 tons of newsprint then lying in the respondent's godown. The result was that in place of the original contract for sale of specific goods a contract for sale of unascertained goods was substituted.” Ibid.

Step 5 — the two contrary views below were both rejected.

“Rajagopala Ayyangar, J., held that the effect of the variation of the contract on November 26, 1951 was that the appellants and the respondent became joint owners of the stock 415 tons. In our opinion, this was not the correct legal position. The parties did not intend that the appellants would buy undivided share in 415 tons of newsprint. On November 26, 1951 the bargain between the parties was that the appellants would buy and the respondent would sell 300 tons out of the larger stock of 415 tons.” Ibid., ¶ 10
“The appellate court held that the property in the entire 415 tons passed to the appellants who were subsequently relieved from their liability to take 115 tons and that the respondent could resell any 300 tons out of the larger stock of 415 tons. We are unable to accept this line of reasoning. It is true that originally the property in the entire 415 tons had passed to the appellants. But the result of the variation of the contract was to annul the passing of property in the goods. The effect of the bargain on November 26, 1951 was that the respondent would sell and deliver to the appellants any 300 tons out of the larger stock of 415 tons. As from November 26, 1951, the property in the entire stock of 415 tons belonged to the respondent. The parties did not intend that as from November 26, 1951 the property in any individual portion of the stock of 415 tons would remain vested in the appellants.” Ibid., ¶ 11

Step 6 — s. 18, and Bayley B. in Gillett v. Hill.

“Section 18 of the Sale of Goods Act provides that where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods no property the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained. It is a condition precedent to the passing property under a contract of sale that the goods are ascertained. The condition is not fulfilled where there is a contract for sale of a portion of a specified larger stock. Till the portion is identified and appropriated to the contract, no property passes to the buyer. In Gillett v. Hill [149 ER 871, 873] Bayley, B. said:—
‘Where there is a bargain for a certain quantity extra greater quantity, and there is power of selection in the vendor to deliver which he thinks fit, then the right to them does not pass to the vendee until the vendor has made his selection, and trover is not maintainable before that is done. If I agree to deliver a certain quantity of oil as ten out of eighteen tons, no one can say which part of the whole quantity I have agreed to deliver until a selection is made. There is no individuality until it has been divided.’” Ibid., ¶ 12 (quoting Gillett v. Hill (1814) 149 ER 871)

Step 7 — the resale was therefore invalid.

“No portion of 415 tons of the newsprint lying in the respondent's godown was appropriated to the contract by the respondent with the appellants' consent before the resale. On the date of the resale, property in the goods had not passed to the buyer. Consequently, the respondent had no right to resell the goods under Section 54(2). The claim to recover the deficiency on resale is not sustainable.” Ibid., ¶ 13

Step 8 — the correct measure: contract price less market price on the date of breach.

“The respondent is entitled to claim as damages the difference between the contract price and the market price on the date of the breach. Where no time is fixed under the contract of sale for acceptance of the goods, the measure of damages is prima facie the difference between the contract price and the market price on the date of the refusal by the buyer to accept the goods, see Illustration (c) to Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act. In the present case, no time was fixed in the contract for acceptance of the goods. On March 29, 1952, the appellants refused to accept the goods. The respondent is entitled to the difference between the contract price and the market price on March 29, 1952.” Ibid., ¶ 14

Counsel on both sides asked the Court, rather than remand the matter, to assess damages itself and finally dispose of it. Going through the materials with counsel's assistance, the Court assessed the market price of Russian newsprint in sheets on 29 March 1952 at 8 annas per lb. — a figure agreed by counsel on both sides. The respondent's claims for Rs 6,798-5-1 (interest) and Rs 1,119-6-0 (insurance charges) were admitted before the Court by Mr Gupte. Accordingly Civil Appeal No. 165 of 1965 was allowed in part and the decrees below were varied by substituting a decree for Rs 10,980-12-8 with interest at 6 per cent per annum from 30 July 1952; Civil Appeal No. 166 of 1965 was dismissed.

Principles evolved
  1. A contract for a portion out of a specified larger stock is a contract for unascertained goods. “The condition is not fulfilled where there is a contract for sale of a portion of a specified larger stock. Till the portion is identified and appropriated to the contract, no property passes to the buyer.” This is the proposition for which the case is prescribed.
  2. “There is no individuality until it has been divided.” Bayley B.'s sentence in Gillett v. Hill is the clearest statement of why, and it is worth committing to memory.
  3. A material variation may annul a passing of property. Property that had passed on 13 November 1951 was destroyed by the variation of 26 November 1951: “the result of the variation of the contract was to annul the passing of property in the goods”, and from that date the property in the entire stock revested in the seller.
  4. Joint ownership is a different thing from a share out of a bulk. The Court rejected the joint-ownership analysis: “The parties did not intend that the appellants would buy undivided share in 415 tons of newsprint.” Do not confuse this case with s. 28.
  5. s. 54(2) is a two-condition power. The goods must be perishable or notice of intention to resell must have been given, and the property must have passed to the buyer subject to the unpaid seller's lien. The second condition is easy to overlook and is fatal when it is missing.
  6. A label in the contract does not create a pledge. The word “advance” did not make the seller a pledgee: “the contract did not envisage any loan of money … on the security of the newsprint”. Look at the substance, not the vocabulary.
  7. Where there is no right of resale, damages are measured by s. 73 of the Contract Act — the difference between the contract price and the market price on the date of the breach, where no time was fixed for acceptance (Illustration (c) to s. 73).
  8. Appellate efficiency. Where the material on record is adequate and both counsel ask for it, the Court may assess damages itself rather than remand — a useful practical point on s. 107 CPC.
★ How to use Case 34 in a DU answer
  • It is the authority for s. 18. Whenever the facts show a quantity to be taken out of a larger bulk — “10 out of 18 tons”, “300 out of 415 tons”, “50 bags out of the 500 in my godown” — this is your case.
  • Pair it with Re Wait. Ambalavana Chettiar gives the rule; Re Wait [1927] 1 Ch 606 gives the consequence (no proprietary interest at all, not even an equitable one); Wait & James v. Midland Bank (1926) gives the exception (exhaustion).
  • Use it for s. 54(2) in any question on the unpaid seller's right of resale — it is one of the few Indian authorities that states the property-must-have-passed condition explicitly.
  • Do not cite it for s. 28 (joint owners). The Court expressly rejected the joint-ownership analysis. Citing it for s. 28 is a classic error that signals a careless answer.

§3.3 · Case 35 — Agricultural Market Committee v. Shalimar Chemical Works Ltd. DU p. 236

35. Agricultural Market Committee v. Shalimar Chemical Works Ltd.
(1997) 5 SCC 516 : AIR 1997 SC 2502 · Supreme Court of India · S. Saghir Ahmad J. · DU Reader p. 236 · Civil appeal from the Andhra Pradesh High Court
Where the A.P. Markets Act contains no definition of ‘sale’, the courts borrow ss. 19–23 of the Sale of Goods Act. The intention of the parties is decisive, gathered from terms, conduct and circumstances (s. 19(2)) — and the clearest conduct of all is who carried the risk of transit and who paid the insurance premium. A statutory presumption of ‘deemed sale’ from mere weighment cannot override the Act.
Facts

The parties. The Agricultural Market Committee — the appellant before the Supreme Court — is a statutory body created under the Andhra Pradesh (Agricultural Produce and Livestock) Markets Act, 1966. The respondent is a licensed trader dealing in ‘copra’ (dried coconut kernel), which it imports from various places in the State of Kerala for manufacturing coconut oil.

Why the dispute matters. ‘Copra’ is a notified agricultural produce. The Committee therefore has the right to levy and realise market fee on all transactions of purchase and sale provided the transactions take place within the notified area of the Committee. Everything turned, therefore, on a single question of sale-of-goods law: did the sale take place in Kerala, or at Hyderabad inside the notified area?

The assessment. By orders dated 2 March 1989 and 28 March 1989, the Assessing Authority — who is also the Secretary of the Committee — levied market fee on the respondent. The respondent challenged those orders in appeals filed under s. 12-E of the Act, but the appeals were dismissed on the technical ground of non-compliance with s. 12-E(2), under which the whole amount of market fee had to be deposited before filing the appeal.

The statutory presumption relied on. In order to levy market fee, the Assessing Authority had relied upon Rule 74(2) of the Andhra Pradesh (Agricultural Produce and Livestock) Market Rules, 1969 and the Explanation to Bye-law 24(5) of the Bye-laws of the Committee, which contain a statutory presumption that if a notified agricultural produce was weighed or measured within the notified area of the Committee, it shall be deemed to have been purchased or sold within that area. The appellate as well as the revisional authorities relied on this provision and held that since the copra imported from Kerala was admittedly weighed at Hyderabad, it shall be deemed to have been sold to the respondent at Hyderabad, and the respondent was accordingly liable to pay market fee on all transactions during the period in question.

The evidence before the Appellate Authority. During the pendency of the proceedings, the statement of Shri Somnath Bhattacharya, Director of the respondent company, was recorded. He stated that the copra was brought into the State of Andhra Pradesh from outside, was unloaded at the respondent's premises, and was there crushed and coconut oil extracted. He further stated:

“After the material comes to Hyderabad, we will weigh the same for the purpose of verification regarding the quantity despatched by the Kerala dealers. We have a running account with the dealers in Kerala State. The account of the dealers will be settled sometimes monthly and sometimes within two or three months from the date of despatch… Very rarely it is found on weighment at Hyderabad that the quantity despatched by the dealer at Kerala is less than the quantity mentioned in the invoice concerned and in such cases, the Hyderabad unit will send a report to our Head Office and the Head Office raises a debit note against the dealer for the shortage of copra.”

The documentary evidence. The High Court took into account the contents of the invoice and Form X. Invoice No. 357 dated 16 May 1985 showed that one Abdul Hameed despatched 200 bags of copra through Lorry No. MSQ 3971 from Alleppey in Kerala to Hyderabad, and that a demand draft for Rs 1,39,000 was forwarded to bank. The note to the invoice said that the despatch of the goods was made solely at the risk and responsibility of M/s Shalimar Chemical Works, and that Abdul Hameed takes “no responsibility or liability as to delayed despatches, losses due to theft, pilferage, rain or damage, leakage, wear and tear etc.”

Column 1 of the accompanying Form X named the consignor as Abdul Hameed. Clause 5 of Form X asked, in substance: if the consignor is transporting goods in pursuance of a sale for the purpose of delivery to the buyer, give the name and address of the person to whom the goods are sold. Against Column 5 it was mentioned that Shalimar Chemical Works is the person to whom the goods are sold. Column 7 asked for the address where goods are sent to an agent for sale or to a godown for storage — and against that column it was written ‘For Sale’. It was that entry on which the appellate authority founded its conclusion that the transport of copra was only to enable Shalimar to purchase the goods and that they had not been sold in Alleppey.

The High Court's finding. The High Court concluded that the weighment was done only for the satisfaction of the buyer and was not a condition of the contract, held the appellate authority's view on Form X to be “totally unsustainable”, and held that the sale took place in Kerala.

Issues
  1. Where did the sale of copra take place — in Kerala, or at Hyderabad within the Committee's notified area? This decided the liability to market fee.
  2. Can the statutory presumption in Rule 74(2) / Bye-law 24(5) — that produce weighed within the notified area is deemed to have been purchased there — override the Sale of Goods Act?
  3. What is the relevance of weighment at Hyderabad, of payment through a bank at Hyderabad, and of collection of documents from the bank there?
  4. Who is to be treated as the owner at the moment the goods are loaded on to the lorry at Alleppey?
Arguments
For the appellant (the Agricultural Market Committee)
  • “If an order was placed with a dealer in Kerala in pursuance of which goods were despatched by lorry to Hyderabad where the respondent, after making payment to and receiving documents from the bank, obtained delivery of goods, and that too, after weighment, the transaction cannot but be treated as sale at Hyderabad and not in the State of Kerala.”
  • Rule 74(2) and the Explanation to Bye-law 24(5) create a statutory presumption: the copra was admittedly weighed at Hyderabad, so it must be deemed to have been sold at Hyderabad.
  • Form X, Column 7 said ‘For Sale’ — which showed that the goods were being sent to enable Shalimar to purchase them, not because they had already been sold in Alleppey.
  • Payment was made through a bank at Hyderabad and documents were collected there; delivery was obtained at Hyderabad after weighment. All of that points to a sale at Hyderabad.
For the respondent (Shalimar Chemical Works Ltd.)
  • The goods were ascertained and in a deliverable state when the order was placed; the seller in Kerala loaded them on the lorry and despatched them to Hyderabad.
  • The contract expressly put the risk of transit on the buyer: the note to the invoice said the despatch was “solely at the risk and responsibility” of Shalimar, and the Kerala seller took “no responsibility or liability” for losses in transit.
  • Consistently with that, Shalimar insured the goods and paid the premium — conduct which, under s. 19(2), shows where the parties intended the property to be.
  • Weighment at Hyderabad was for verification only, and was not a condition of the contract; the accounts with the Kerala dealers were settled monthly over a running account.
  • Form X, Column 5 — the column that asks “if the consignor is transporting goods in pursuance of a sale for the purpose of delivery to the buyer” — named Shalimar as the person to whom the goods were sold. That is direct evidence of a completed sale in Kerala.
  • Once the goods were handed to a common carrier for despatch to the buyer, s. 23(2) applies: the carrier becomes the buyer's agent and delivery to the carrier is delivery to the buyer.
  • A deeming provision in subordinate legislation cannot displace ss. 19–23 of the Sale of Goods Act, which is the statute Parliament enacted on this very question.
Decision — appeal dismissed: the sale took place in KERALA

The Supreme Court agreed entirely with the High Court. S. Saghir Ahmad J.:

Step 1 — the A.P. Act has no definition of sale, so the Sale of Goods Act supplies it.

“We may, at this stage, consider certain provisions of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, specially as the Andhra Pradesh (Agricultural Produce and Livestock) Markets Act, 1966 does not contain any definition of sale or purchase. [Sections 19 and 20 of the Sale of Goods Act were quoted by the court.]” AIR 1997 SC 2502, ¶ 35

Step 2 — intention is the decisive factor.

“We may, before analysing the provisions of Sections 19 and 20, observe that the Indian Sale of Goods Act is based largely upon the English and American Acts. Under these Acts, namely, the English Sale of Goods Act, the American Uniform Sales Act and the Indian Sale of Goods Act, the relevant factor for determining where the sale takes place, is the intention of the parties. A contract of sale, like any other contract, is a consensual act inasmuch as parties are at liberty to settle, amongst themselves, any terms they may choose.” Ibid., ¶ 36

Step 3 — the role of ss. 19(2) and 19(3).

“Section 19 attempts to give effect to the elementary principle of the Law of Contract that the parties may fix the time when the property in the goods shall be treated to have passed. It may be the time of delivery, or the time of payment of price or even the time of the making of contract. It all depends upon the intention of the parties. It is, therefore, the duty of the court to ascertain the intention of the parties and in doing so, they have to be guided by the principles laid down in Section 19(2) which provides that for ascertaining the intention of the parties, regard shall be had to the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case.” Ibid., ¶ 37

Step 4 — s. 20 as the first rule, and the two conditions for its attraction.

“In order that Section 20 is attracted, two conditions have to be fulfilled: (i) the contract of sale is for specific goods which are in a deliverable state; and (ii) the contract is an unconditional contract. If these two conditions are satisfied, Section 20 becomes applicable immediately and it is at this stage that it has to be seen whether there is anything either in the terms of the contract or in the conduct of the parties or in the circumstances of the case which indicates a contrary intention. This exercise has to be done to give effect to the opening words, namely, ‘Unless a different intention appears’ occurring in Section 19(3). In Hoe Kim Seing v. Maung Ba Chit [AIR 1935 PC 182], it was held that intention of the parties was the decisive factor as to when the property in goods passes to the purchaser. If the contract is silent, intention has to be gathered from the conduct and circumstances of the case.” Ibid., ¶ 40

Step 5 — auction sales: s. 20, not s. 64(2).

“This Court in Consolidated Coffee Ltd. v. Coffee Board [AIR 1980 SC 1468], has held that in an auction-sale of chattels, property passes to the purchaser on the acceptance of his bid. This occurs not because of Section 64(2) but because of the rule contained in Section 20.” Ibid., ¶ 41

Step 6 — the decisive passage: conduct.

“In the instant case, the goods which were the subject-matter of sale were ascertained goods. They were also in a deliverable state. On the order being placed by the respondent, the seller in the State of Kerala, loaded the goods on the lorry and despatched the same to Hyderabad. It is at this stage that the conduct of the parties becomes extremely relevant. It was one of the terms of the contract between the parties that the seller would not be liable for any future loss of goods and that the goods were being despatched at the risk of the respondent. The respondent had also obtained insurance of the goods and had paid the policy premium. He, therefore, intended the goods to be treated as his own so that if there was any loss of goods in transit, he could validly claim the insurance money. The weighment of the goods at Hyderabad or the collection of documents from the bank or payment of price through the bank at Hyderabad were immaterial, inasmuch as the property in the goods had already passed at Kerala and it was not dependent upon the payment of price or the delivery of goods to the respondent.” Ibid., ¶ 42

Step 7 — s. 23(2), through the High Court's reasoning which the Court “fully” approved.

“One significant aspect to be noticed in this case is that after the stocks were loaded into the trucks, the sellers in Kerala had absolutely no liability with regard to any future losses. That is the reason why the goods were insured and the insurance premia were paid by the appellant. Where goods have been delivered to a common carrier to be sent to the person, by whom they have been ordered, the carrier becomes the agent of the vendee and such a delivery amounts to delivery to the vendee under Section 23(2) of the Sale of Goods Act. There was thus completed sale in Kerala State and no purchase in the State of Andhra Pradesh.” Andhra Pradesh High Court, quoted and approved, ibid., ¶ 33

Step 8 — conclusion.

“We are in full agreement with the view expressed by the High Court and are also of the opinion that having regard to the evidence on record which indicated that on the order placed by the respondent, the stocks were loaded into the trucks for despatch to Hyderabad with the clear stipulation that the despatch was at the risk of the purchaser and that the seller had no liability with regard to any future losses and that the stock was insured and the insurance premium was paid by the respondent, the sale took place in the State of Kerala and not at Hyderabad.” Ibid., ¶ 43

The Court added that the High Court had also rightly held the appellate authority's reading of Form X to be “totally unsustainable”: the entry ‘For Sale’ in Column 7 could not outweigh the express statement in Column 5 that the goods were sold to Shalimar. The appeal was dismissed.

Principles evolved
  1. Intention is the decisive factor; ss. 20–24 are only the fallback. “Under these Acts … the relevant factor for determining where the sale takes place, is the intention of the parties.” And the search is governed by s. 19(2): terms, conduct, circumstances.
  2. Risk and insurance are the best evidence of intention. The party who bears the transit risk and pays the insurance premium has “intended the goods to be treated as his own”. This is the practical test the case is prescribed for.
  3. Weighment, payment through a bank, and collection of documents are “immaterial” once the property has passed. “The weighment of the goods at Hyderabad or the collection of documents from the bank or payment of price through the bank at Hyderabad were immaterial.” Contrasting this with Case 33 is the single most instructive exercise on this Topic: in Case 33 weighment mattered because the seller had reserved disposal; here it did not because the parties' conduct showed an unconditional transfer.
  4. Delivery to a common carrier is delivery to the buyer (s. 23(2)). “The carrier becomes the agent of the vendee.” Once the goods are loaded for despatch to the buyer without reservation of disposal, the property passes — and the situs of the sale is the place of loading.
  5. Passing of title is not dependent on payment or delivery. “If the time for payment of price or the time for delivery of goods, or both, is postponed, it would not affect the passing of the title in the goods so purchased.”
  6. A deeming provision in subordinate legislation cannot override the parent statute. Rule 74(2) and the Explanation to Bye-law 24(5) deemed a sale wherever weighment occurred; the Supreme Court applied ss. 19–23 instead and the presumption collapsed. Rules must yield to the Act.
  7. Where a special statute is silent, the general statute fills the gap. The A.P. Markets Act contained no definition of sale or purchase, so the Court borrowed the Sale of Goods Act definition — a technique that recurs across tax and regulatory litigation.
  8. s. 64(2) vs s. 20 in auction sales. “Property passes … on the acceptance of his bid. This occurs not because of Section 64(2) but because of the rule contained in Section 20” — s. 64(2) fixes when the contract is complete; s. 20 fixes when the property passes.
★ How to use Case 35 in a DU answer
  • It is your authority for s. 19(2) — intention gathered from conduct. Use it whenever the facts tell you who insured the goods, who paid the freight, or at whose risk the despatch was made.
  • It is your authority for s. 23(2). The sentence “the carrier becomes the agent of the vendee and such a delivery amounts to delivery to the vendee under Section 23(2)” is the cleanest Indian statement of the rule.
  • Contrast it with Case 33 — this is the highest-value comparison in the Topic. Both are F.O.B.-style inter-State despatches; both raised the ‘where did the sale take place’ question; both mattered for tax. In Mysore Chromite the seller kept the bill of lading → reservation → property passed in London. In Shalimar the buyer carried the risk and the insurance and there was no reservation → property passed in Kerala. Two cases, opposite results, one principle: look for the intention, and the reservation is the surest sign of it.
  • Do not cite it for s. 18. The Court expressly held the goods were ascertained.

§3.4 · Case 36 — Pearson v. Rose & Young, Ltd. DU p. 240

36. Pearson v. Rose & Young, Ltd.
(1950) 2 Ch. D. 1027 · Court of Appeal (Somervell L.J., Denning L.J., Vaisey J.), reversing Devlin J. · DU Reader p. 240 · Action for damages for conversion of a motor car · English · Decided under the Factors Act, 1889, s. 2(1) — the exact counterpart of the proviso to s. 27 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930
A mercantile agent cannot pass a good title unless the owner consented to his possession of everything that makes the sale possible. In the motor trade that means the car and its registration book. A sale of a second-hand car without its log book is “not a sale in the ordinary course of business”, and retaining the log book “effectually prevents” the exception from operating.
★ Why an English case on the Factors Act is on your Indian syllabus

Section 2(1) of the Factors Act, 1889 is, word for word in all that matters here, the model for the proviso to s. 27 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. England kept the mercantile-agent exception in a separate Factors Act; India folded it into the Sale of Goods Act itself as a proviso to s. 27. Every proposition in this case about “consent of the owner”, “possession as a mercantile agent” and “ordinary course of business” therefore applies directly to the Indian proviso. Read the case as an Indian case.

Facts

The plaintiff, Pearson, owned a motor car — “admittedly up to March 17, 1949, the property of the plaintiff”. He gave possession of the car to Mr Hunt, who was admittedly a motor car dealer and a mercantile agent within s. 2(1) of the Factors Act, 1889. The purpose was limited and specific: the car was handed over “for the purpose of inviting offers to purchase it”. Hunt was not authorised to conclude a sale.

The trick. By means of a trick, Hunt induced the plaintiff to hand him the registration book relating to the car. The Court of Appeal's account of how this happened is critical: on March 18, 1949, the plaintiff “simply let Hunt have the log book in his hands to inspect it for a few moments” — he gave Hunt “the barest physical custody of it while he was still there himself”. Hunt then, “by a trick, managed to get the plaintiff called away while he, Hunt, still held the book”.

The sale and the chain. Later the same day, Hunt, acting without the authority or knowledge of the plaintiff, sold the car and handed the registration book to the fourth party, who “acted in good faith without notice of any absence of authority”. The fourth party subsequently sold the car to the third party, and the third party sold it to the defendants, Rose & Young, Ltd.

The windfall. Denning L.J. noted that the immediate buyer, Mr Marshall, was himself a motor car dealer, who bought the car from Hunt on 18 March 1949 for £450 and sold it on 21 March 1949 for £620 — a profit of £170 in three days.

The litigation. The plaintiff sued the defendants for damages for the conversion of the car. Devlin J. decided against the plaintiff. The plaintiff appealed; Somervell L.J. recorded that the “sole issue in the appeal is whether the fourth party did or did not get a good title under the Factors Act, 1889”.

Issues
  1. Did the plaintiff consent to Hunt's possession of the registration book? — the question Somervell and Denning L.JJ. treated as decisive.
  2. Was a sale of a car without its registration book a sale “in the ordinary course of business” of a mercantile agent within s. 2(1)?
  3. Does larceny by a trick necessarily exclude the Factors Act? — i.e. does the thief's criminal obtaining of possession destroy the owner's ‘consent’? On this point the authorities conflicted and there was “not clear decision on the point”.
  4. Consequent upon all this: did the fourth party — and through him the third party and the defendants — obtain a good title?
Arguments
For the plaintiff (Pearson — the true owner)
  • On Devlin J.'s findings, the only possible conclusion was that the car was obtained by larceny by a trick; and on that basis s. 2(1) of the Factors Act cannot apply, because the owner never consented to the possession.
  • Reliance was placed on Fletcher Moulton L.J. in Oppenheimer v. Frazer & Wyatt [1907] 1 KB 519: “A mercantile agent is as capable of stealing as any other man, and, if he has stolen the goods, there can be no question, in my opinion, that he must be taken to hold possession of them without the consent of the owner. … larceny by a trick … is in the eye of the law pure stealing.”
  • In any event, the plaintiff never consented to Hunt's possession of the registration book. Consent to possession of the car is not consent to possession of the log book; and without the log book Hunt could not have sold the car at all.
  • Further, a sale of a second-hand car without its log book is not a sale “in the ordinary course of business” — counsel accepted (and the Court accepted) that cars can be sold without log books, “but the price would be substantially reduced thereby”.
For the defendants and the third and fourth parties (the innocent purchasers)
  • Hunt was admittedly a mercantile agent, and the plaintiff admittedly consented to his possession of the car — indeed he handed the car over precisely so that offers could be invited.
  • The whole purpose of the Factors Act is to protect the purchaser in good faith carrying out an ordinary mercantile transaction with a person in the position of a mercantile agent. That is what the fourth party did.
  • Per Scrutton L.J. in Folkes v. King: where “the true owner intends to give him possession”, the fact that the agent “secretly intends to break his contract of agency” does not destroy the consent. “It appears to me to be enough to show that the true owner did intentionally deposit in the hands of the mercantile agent the goods in question.”
  • “I do not think Parliament had any intention of applying the artificial distinctions of the criminal law to a commercial transaction, defeating it if there were larceny by a trick, but not if there were only larceny by a bailee, or possession obtained by false pretences.”
  • If larceny by a trick does not exclude the Act, then the fourth party in good faith and without notice obtained a good title, and so did every buyer in the chain.
Decision — appeal allowed: the buyer got NO title

All three members of the Court held for the plaintiff, each by a slightly different route. Devlin J.'s decision was reversed. Somervell L.J. was explicit that he decided the case without needing the larceny point: “I have come to the conclusion that the appeal succeeds on the judge's findings irrespective of that point”.

(i) SOMERVELL L.J. — no consent to possession of the log book, and no sale in the ordinary course.

“It is, I think, clear that on the sale of a second-hand car the vendor will ordinarily deliver and the purchaser require the delivery of the registration, or ‘log’, book. Counsel for the plaintiff stated, and I accept it, that he did not contend that cars could not be sold without their log books, but the price would be substantially reduced thereby. This shows, and I so hold, that a sale of a car without its log book would not be a sale ‘in the ordinary course of business’. The findings of DEVLIN, J., as to Mr Hunt's reasons for getting hold of the log book support this view.” Somervell L.J., (1950) 2 Ch D 1027
“The transaction which the fourth party seeks to uphold is the sale of a car with its log book, a more valuable subject-matter than a car without its log book. Mr Hunt, it seems to me, was never in possession of the log book with the consent of the plaintiff. One can test it in this way. If immediately after the plaintiff had left the building he had been asked: ‘Did you mean to leave your car?’ he would have answered ‘Yes.’ If he had been asked ‘Did you mean to leave your log book?’ He would have answered ‘No’, and would have gone back and collected it. The ostensible authority which enabled Mr Hunt to effect a sale ‘in the ordinary course of business’ arose because of his possession of the log book without the consent of the owner. The relationship of the log book, which is, of course, not a document of title, to a motor car is one to which no useful analogy has occurred to me. I am, however, of opinion that on the conclusions as set out above the fourth party cannot claim the protection of the Factors Act, 1889.” Ibid.

On the larceny point, Somervell L.J. held, on Bankes L.J.'s analysis in Folkes v. King [1923] 1 KB 282, that there was larceny by a trick: “It seems to me clear that Mr Hunt had an animus furandi at the time he got possession of the car”. But he preferred the reasoning of Scrutton L.J. on its legal consequence:

“I have set out these passages at some length because I agree with the conclusion and the reasons given for it and do not desire to add to them.” Ibid., adopting Scrutton L.J. in Folkes v. King

(ii) DENNING L.J. — “goods” means the car together with the log book. His judgment opens with the passage every nemo dat answer should begin with:

“In the early days of the common law the governing principle of our law of property was that no person could give a better title than he himself had got, but the needs of commerce have led to a progressive modification of this principle so as to protect innocent purchasers. We have had cases in this court recently about sales in market overt and sales by a sheriff, and now we have the present case about sales by a mercantile agent. The cases show how difficult it is to strike the right balance between the claims of true owners and the claims of innocent purchasers.” Denning L.J., (1950) 2 Ch D 1027

He then stated the policy of the Act:

“Parliament has protected the true owner by making it clear that he does not lose his right to goods when they are taken from him without his consent … In that case the true owner can claim them back from any person into whose hands they come, even from an innocent purchaser who has bought from a mercantile agent. Parliament has not protected the true owner if he has himself consented to a mercantile agent having possession of them, because, by leaving them in the agent's possession, he has clothed the agent with apparent authority to sell them, and he should not, therefore, be allowed to claim them back from an innocent purchaser. The critical question, therefore, in every case is whether the true owner consented to the mercantile agent having possession of the goods.” Ibid.

And then the passage that makes the case famous:

“The registration book, or, as it is more commonly called, the ‘log book’, of a car: It is not a document of title, but it is the best evidence of title. Everyone who buys or sells a second-hand car knows that a clean title cannot be given or obtained without the log book. The instructions on every log book tell the owner to ‘keep this book in a safe place, not on the vehicle’ and that ‘on transferring the vehicle to another person you must hand over this book to the person acquiring the vehicle.’ All sensible owners pay heed to these instructions. If the seller can produce the book showing that his title is in order, he will receive the proper market price, but no wise buyer pays his money except against delivery, not only of the car, but also of the log book. If the seller cannot produce it, he will receive as a rule only a fraction of the proper price, because its absence is of itself notice of a defect of title.” Ibid.
“When the significance of the log book is realised, it becomes plain that the consent of the owner, which is necessary for the Factors Act to operate, is a consent to the possession by the mercantile agent, not only of the car, but also of the log book that goes with it. So long as the owner retains the log book, the mercantile agent cannot dispose of the car in the ordinary course of his business, because it is not in the ordinary course of anyone's business to dispose of a second-hand car without a log book. The retention by the owner of the log book, therefore, effectually prevents the Factors Act from operating. The handing over of the log book with the car enables the Act to operate. The owner then clothes the agent with apparent authority to dispose of the car … To put it shortly, in the case of a car, ‘goods’ in the Act means the car together with the log book.” Ibid.

Applying it to the facts:

“Did the plaintiff consent to Hunt having possession of the log book as well as the car? On the findings of fact by DEVLIN, J., the answer is clearly ‘No’. On March 18, 1949, the plaintiff simply let Hunt have the log book in his hands to inspect it for a few moments. The plaintiff gave Hunt the barest physical custody of it while he was still there himself. He never consented to Hunt having possession of it. Then Hunt, by a trick, managed to get the plaintiff called away while he, Hunt, still held the book. Armed thus with the log book, Hunt was able to sell the car on the very same day to an innocent purchaser, which, without it, he could not have done. On those facts the plaintiff no more consented to Hunt having possession of the log book than if Hunt had stolen it from his pocket. The Factors Act does not operate, therefore, to give a good title to the dealer who bought from Hunt, nor to the buyers in succession from him.” Ibid.

And the closing reflection, which explains why the Court did not strain to protect a dealer:

“I confess that I come to this conclusion with some reluctance, because I recognise that the legislature intended the courts to make every reasonable presumption in favour of the innocent purchaser: see s. 2(4) of the Act, but my reluctance is tempered by the reflection that in this case the buyer, Mr Marshall, was himself a motor car dealer, who bought the car from Hunt on March 18, 1949, for £450 and sold it on March 21, 1949, for £620. A dealer whose business enables him to make quick and large profits of that kind must not be surprised if he occasionally gets his fingers burnt.” Ibid.

(iii) VAISEY J. — “a car with only three wheels”.

“Where there are two innocent parties of whom one has to suffer for the criminal act of a third, no decision can be said to be satisfactory. This example of that familiar problem presents to my mind considerable difficulty. On the whole, having had the advantage of reading the judgments of my Lords which have just been delivered, I share their view that this appeal should succeed. … What strikes me as decisive of the present case is the fact, already pointed out by my Lords, that a dealer in cars, being a mercantile agent, cannot be said to be ‘acting in the ordinary course of’ his business as such when selling a car without its log book, any more, in my judgment, than he would be so acting if he sold a car with only three wheels. Suppose that a car with only three of its wheels had come into the dealer's possession with the owner's consent, and that the dealer had proceeded to steal the fourth wheel from the owner's garage, had placed it on the car, and had then sold the car. In such a case, I do not think that the totality of the four-wheeled vehicle could be said to have come into the dealer's possession with the owner's consent, so as to bring into operation s. 2(1) of the Facts Act, 1889. … That Act does modify and form an exception to the general law of property by conferring a title upon strangers in derogation of the rights of the true owner, and for myself I would be reluctant to extend its operation further than its terms necessitate.” Vaisey J., (1950) 2 Ch D 1027

Result: the fourth party got no title from Hunt; therefore neither did the third party; therefore the defendants had no title either, and the plaintiff succeeded in conversion.

Principles evolved
  1. Consent must cover everything that makes the sale possible. “The consent of the owner … is a consent to the possession by the mercantile agent, not only of the car, but also of the log book that goes with it.” Partial consent is not consent for the purposes of the section.
  2. ‘Goods’ is construed by the usage of the trade. “In the case of a car, ‘goods’ in the Act means the car together with the log book.” Where a trade treats a document as inseparable from the chattel, the statute follows the trade.
  3. ‘Ordinary course of business’ is an objective test about the transaction. “It is not in the ordinary course of anyone's business to dispose of a second-hand car without a log book.” A sale that fetches only “a fraction of the proper price” because a document is missing is, for that very reason, outside the ordinary course.
  4. Custody for inspection is not possession. “The barest physical custody” while the owner is still present is not the juridical possession the Act requires. This is the same distinction the Indian courts drew under the old s. 108 of the Contract Act — ‘possession’ meant juridical possession, not mere custody.
  5. Larceny by a trick does not automatically destroy consent — but the point is not settled. Somervell L.J. agreed with Scrutton L.J. (and with Bankes L.J.'s “state of mind of the owner” test) that where the owner intentionally deposits the goods with the agent, the agent's secret intention to steal does not destroy the consent: “I do not think Parliament had any intention of applying the artificial distinctions of the criminal law to a commercial transaction.” He nonetheless found animus furandi present, and disposed of the case on the log book in any event.
  6. A defect in title defeats the whole downstream chain. Because the fourth party got nothing, “the buyers in succession from him” got nothing either — nemo dat propagates down a chain.
  7. The exception is construed narrowly. Vaisey J.: the Act “confer[s] a title upon strangers in derogation of the rights of the true owner, and for myself I would be reluctant to extend its operation further than its terms necessitate”. Somervell L.J. likewise found no useful analogy for the log book and refused to invent one.
  8. The pro-purchaser policy has limits. Denning L.J. acknowledged s. 2(4)'s “every reasonable presumption in favour of the innocent purchaser” — but a dealer who turns a £450 purchase into a £620 sale in three days “must not be surprised if he occasionally gets his fingers burnt”.
★ How to use Case 36 in a DU answer
  • It is the only prescribed case on the proviso to s. 27. Any question on nemo dat and its exceptions, on a mercantile agent, or on ‘ordinary course of business’ must cite it.
  • Open your nemo dat answer with Denning L.J.'s first paragraph. “The governing principle … was that no person could give a better title than he himself had got, but the needs of commerce have led to a progressive modification of this principle” — and “how difficult it is to strike the right balance”. Nothing else frames the doctrine so well in three sentences.
  • Use it for the 2013 CT OC problem (“P left his car with a mercantile agent and authorised him only to receive an offer and not to sell. The agent obtained possession of the registration book from P without his consent and then promptly sold the car to D”) — that is this case almost verbatim. The answer is D acquires no title.
  • Contrast it with Central Newbury Car Auctions and Lowther v. Harris: consent to car plus log book → protection; consent to car but not log book → no protection; consent to sell at a stated price but sold below it → no protection.
  • Translate the log book into Indian terms. Today the equivalents are the Certificate of Registration (RC) under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, Form 29/30 transfer papers, and the insurance certificate. The reasoning applies unchanged: hand over the RC only when you intend the dealer to be able to sell.
! Two traps when citing this case
  • Do not call the log book a document of title. Somervell L.J. said expressly that it “is, of course, not a document of title”, and Denning L.J. said “It is not a document of title, but it is the best evidence of title”. The distinction matters: s. 27's proviso covers possession of “the goods or of a document of title to the goods”, and s. 2(4) of the Factors Act deems possession of documents of title to be with consent — but none of that applies to a log book. The case was decided on ‘ordinary course of business’ and on factual consent, not on the document-of-title limb.
  • The reader prints “Facts Act, 1889” in Vaisey J.'s judgment (“so as to bring into operation s. 2(1) of the Facts Act, 1889”) — a typographical slip for the Factors Act, 1889.

Set A — Nemo dat quod non habet: the rule, its rationale and its scope

2015LB-304 Q7(a) · the shortest form of the question
Question What do you mean by the doctrine of ‘Nemo dat quod non habet’?
Model answer

1. The maxim. Nemo dat quod non habet — literally, “no one gives what he does not have”. The civilian parallel is nemo plus iuris ad alium transferre potest quam ipse habet — “one cannot transfer to another more right than he himself has”. In the law of sale of goods it expresses the rule that a seller cannot pass to the buyer a better title than he himself possesses.

2. The statutory form — s. 27, verbatim. Set the section out in full (see §2.1). Three components must be noticed: (a) the opening reservation “Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time being in force”; (b) the trigger — a sale by a person who is not the owner and who sells neither under the authority nor with the consent of the owner; (c) the rule itself — “the buyer acquires no better title to the goods than the seller had”.

3. Two points of drafting that examiners reward.

  • “No better”, not “no”. s. 2(11) defines ‘property’ as the general property. If the seller had a limited interest — a pledgee's special property, an unpaid seller's lien, a co-owner's joint interest — the buyer takes exactly that and no more.
  • The estoppel limb is inside the section. “Unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct precluded from denying the seller's authority to sell.” That is s. 115 of the Evidence Act, 1872, written into s. 27 itself. It is not one of the ‘exceptions’ — it is the exception the section carries within it.

4. The policy. Denning L.J. in Pearson v. Rose & Young (Case 36): “In the early days of the common law the governing principle of our law of property was that no person could give a better title than he himself had got, but the needs of commerce have led to a progressive modification of this principle so as to protect innocent purchasers … The cases show how difficult it is to strike the right balance between the claims of true owners and the claims of innocent purchasers.”

5. Close with the consequence. Where the rule bites, the buyer must return the goods to the true owner, but recovers the full price from his seller for total failure of consideration — Rowland v. Divall [1923] 1 KB 500; s. 65, Contract Act, 1872.

Exam technique. A five-mark ‘what do you mean by’ question still wants: the maxim in Latin and in English, s. 27 verbatim, the estoppel limb, the policy, and one consequence. Do not write the exceptions here — that is a different question (see Set B). Answering the wrong question is the commonest way of losing marks on this Topic.

Question What do you understand by the maxim, ‘Nemo dat quod non habet’? Discuss the exceptions to this rule with decided cases.

Introduction. [Paras 1–4 of Set A1 above, compressed into six lines.] The maxim is enacted in s. 27 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930; it is subject, by the section's own opening words, to the five statutory exceptions in ss. 28–30 and to the mercantile-agent proviso.

Short answer: No.

1. Identify the governing provision. The proviso to s. 27 of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, read with the definition of ‘mercantile agent’ in s. 2(9). Quote both.

2. Run the five conditions.

The agent was doing “something of a different kind” from what he was authorised to do.
  • Jerome v. Bentley & Co. [1952] 2 All ER 114 (CA) — a rogue was instructed to sell a ring for not less than £550 and sold it for £175. Denning L.J.: the Act protects an innocent purchaser only where the owner has “clothed the agent with apparent authority”.
  • Contrast Heap v. Motorists' Advisory Agency [1923] 1 KB 577 — where the agent was selling as principal with the owner's assent after a part-exchange, the exception did not apply, because he was not acting as an agent.

4. The refinement that earns the extra marks. Not every departure from instructions takes a sale outside the ordinary course. An agent entrusted with goods without a stated price who sells a little below market value is still acting in the ordinary course — the exception exists precisely to protect buyers against the agent's disobedience. What takes the sale outside the ordinary course here is the combination of (i) an express minimum price communicated as a term of the agency, and (ii) a sale below it. The misappropriation of the proceeds is irrelevant — it is a matter between G and the agent, and does not affect the buyer's title either way.

5. Result and remedies. The buyer acquires no title and must return the car to G. The buyer can recover the full price from the agent for total failure of consideration (Rowland v. Divall [1923] 1 KB 500; s. 65, Contract Act, 1872), and G can sue the agent for breach of the agency and for money had and received (s. 218, Contract Act).

1. Identify the case. The facts are Pearson v. Rose & Young, Ltd. [1950] 2 Ch D 1027 — Case 36 on the DU syllabus — almost verbatim. Say so in your first sentence; the examiner has set the question precisely because the case is prescribed.

  1. Mercantile agent? Yes — a motor dealer is within s. 2(9).
  2. Possession as a mercantile agent? Yes — P left the car with him to invite offers.
  3. With the owner's consent? Only partly. P consented to his possession of the car, but not to his possession of the registration book, which the agent obtained “without his consent”. This is the decisive point.
  4. Ordinary course of business? No. “The sale of a car without the registration book relating to it was not a sale of goods in the ordinary course of business” (headnote). Somervell L.J.: a car without its log book fetches “substantially reduced” price; Vaisey J.: it is like “a car with only three wheels”.

“The consent necessary to pass a good title to a purchaser under s. 2(1) was a consent to possession of both the car and the registration book.” Pearson v. Rose & Young, Ltd. — headnote, (1950) 2 Ch D 1027
“To put it shortly, in the case of a car, ‘goods’ in the Act means the car together with the log book. … The retention by the owner of the log book, therefore, effectually prevents the Factors Act from operating.” Denning L.J., (1950) 2 Ch D 1027

5. Two additional points to add.

  • P authorised the agent only to receive an offer and not to sell. That limitation does not by itself defeat the exception — the exception exists precisely because agents exceed their instructions. What defeats it is the want of consent to possession of the registration book and the absence of an ordinary-course sale. Do not give the wrong reason.
  • On the findings there was also larceny by a trick, and Somervell L.J. found animus furandi present. But he expressly preferred Scrutton L.J.'s view in Folkes v. King that larceny by a trick does not by itself destroy the owner's consent: “I do not think Parliament had any intention of applying the artificial distinctions of the criminal law to a commercial transaction.” So the criminal-law route is not the reason the buyer loses.

6. Result. D acquires no title; P may recover the car (or damages for conversion) from D. D's remedy is against the agent for the full price — Rowland v. Divall.

2011CT · OC Q7 · the three-situation rule question
Question Discuss the rules regarding transfer of Property in the following transactions:
(a) In Contract for sale of specific goods in a deliverable state.
(b) Sale of unascertained goods.
(c) When goods are delivered to the buyer on approval or ‘on sale or return’.
Model answer

Preliminary — two sentences before you start. (i) The whole enquiry presupposes that the goods are ascertained: s. 18 provides that “where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods, no property in the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained”. (ii) The governing provision is s. 19(1): property passes “at such time as the parties to the contract intend it to be transferred”, that intention being gathered, by s. 19(2), from the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case. The rules in ss. 20–24 apply only “unless a different intention appears” (s. 19(3)).

(a) Specific goods in a deliverable state — Rule 1, s. 20. Quote s. 20 verbatim. Two cumulative conditions: an unconditional contract and specific goods in a deliverable state (s. 2(3): goods in such state that the buyer would under the contract be bound to take delivery of them). If both are satisfied, the property passes when the contract is made, and “it is immaterial whether the time of payment of the price or the time of delivery of the goods, or both, is postponed”. Consequences: risk passes at once (s. 26); the seller may sue for the price (s. 55); the buyer may sue a stranger in conversion. Authority: Tarling v. Baxter (1827) 6 B & C 360 (the haystack); Case 35 (ascertained goods, deliverable state, loaded on the lorry — property passed in Kerala); Consolidated Coffee Ltd. v. Coffee Board, AIR 1980 SC 1468 (auction — property passes on acceptance of the bid, “not because of Section 64(2) but because of the rule contained in Section 20”). Contrast: if the seller must still do something to the goods, s. 21 applies; if he must still weigh or measure to fix the price, s. 22 applies.

(b) Sale of unascertained goods — s. 18 and Rule 4, s. 23. No property passes until the goods are ascertained. Ascertainment happens in two ways: (i) appropriation under s. 23(1) — goods of the contract description, in a deliverable state, unconditionally appropriated to the contract by one party with the assent of the other (express or implied, before or after the act); (ii) ‘ascertainment by exhaustion’ — where the whole of an identified bulk has been sold, so no selection is needed (Wait & James v. Midland Bank (1926) 31 Com Cas 172). s. 23(2) supplies a deeming rule for the commonest case: delivery to the buyer, or to a carrier or other bailee for transmission, without reserving the right of disposal, is deemed an unconditional appropriation. Authorities: Case 34 (300 tons out of 415 tons — unascertained, no property, no resale); Re Wait [1927] 1 Ch 606 (no equitable interest either — the buyer is an unsecured creditor); Gillett v. Hill (1814) 149 ER 871 per Bayley B. (“There is no individuality until it has been divided”); Case 35 (s. 23(2)).

(c) Goods on approval or ‘on sale or return’ — Rule 5, s. 24. Quote s. 24. The buyer has possession but no property, and holds an option to become owner. Property passes on either of two events: (a) adoption — he signifies approval or acceptance, or does “any other act adopting the transaction” (reselling, pledging, consuming, wearing it to a function); or (b) expiry — he retains the goods without giving notice of rejection, on the expiry of the fixed time or, where none is fixed, of a reasonable time (a question of fact: s. 63). Authority: Kirkham v. Attenborough [1897] 1 QB 201 (pledge = adoption). Important qualification: property and risk are separate — where goods are returned damaged without fault within the trial period the loss stays with the seller (Head v. Tattersall (1871) LR 7 Ex 7; Elphick v. Barnes (1880) 5 CPD 321).

Close with the override. Even where a rule in ss. 20–24 would pass the property, s. 25 allows the seller to reserve the right of disposal, and then no property passes “notwithstanding the delivery of the goods to a buyer or to a carrier” — Case 33.

Model answer

1. Question Sale of unascertained goods. [Attempt briefly any two of: (a) Risk follows property · (b) Sale of unascertained goods · (c) Distinction between lien and stoppage in transit.]
Model answer

1. The rule — s. 18, verbatim. “Where there is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods, no property in the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the goods are ascertained.” This is a condition precedent, not merely a rule about timing.

2. What ‘unascertained’ means. Goods not “identified and agreed upon at the time a contract of sale is made” (s. 2(14), defining specific goods). Two kinds: generic goods not yet picked out (10 tonnes of wheat), and future goods — s. 2(6), “goods to be manufactured or produced or acquired by the seller after the making of the contract of sale”. By s. 6(3) a purported present sale of future goods “operates as an agreement to sell”.

3. How they become ascertained — s. 23(1). Goods of the contract description, in a deliverable state, unconditionally appropriated to the contract by one party with the assent of the other (express or implied, before or after the act). s. 23(2): delivery to the buyer, or to a carrier or other bailee for transmission, without reserving the right of disposal, is deemed unconditional appropriation. The second route is ascertainment by exhaustion — where the whole of an identified bulk is sold, so no selection is needed (Wait & James v. Midland Bank (1926) 31 Com Cas 172).

4. The consequences of remaining unascertained. The buyer has no proprietary interest at all — not legal, not equitable — even if he has paid in full. On the seller's insolvency he is an ordinary unsecured creditor (Re Wait [1927] 1 Ch 606). The seller commits no conversion by refusing to deliver. And, critically, the seller has no right of resale under s. 54(2) — Case 34.

5. The authority. Case 34, P.S.N.S. Ambalavana Chettiar v. Express Newspapers Ltd., AIR 1968 SC 741: a contract to buy 300 tons out of a larger stock of 415 tons is a contract for unascertained goods. “The condition is not fulfilled where there is a contract for sale of a portion of a specified larger stock. Till the portion is identified and appropriated to the contract, no property passes to the buyer.” Quoting Bayley B. in Gillett v. Hill (1814) 149 ER 871: “There is no individuality until it has been divided.” Note: India has no equivalent of ss. 20A–20B of the English Sale of Goods Act 1979, which give a prepaying buyer of an undivided share in an identified bulk a statutory co-ownership.

2012CT · OC Q5(b) · part delivery and severance
Question “A delivery of part of goods with an intention of severing it from the whole, does not operate as a delivery of the remainder.” Comment.
Model answer

1. The proposition. Delivery is, by s. 2(2), “voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another”. It is an act, and an act of a particular kind: a transfer of that which is delivered. Where the parties intend only to sever and hand over a part of a bulk, the delivery is of that part alone. The remainder is not delivered merely because it was once physically attached to the part that was.

2. The statutory home. Two provisions make the point:

  • s. 23(1) — property in unascertained goods passes only on an unconditional appropriation of goods of that description. A bulk that has not been divided is not appropriated; and a part-delivery is not an appropriation of the whole.
  • s. 48 — “Where an unpaid seller has made part delivery of the goods, he may exercise his right of lien on the remainder, unless such part delivery has been made under such circumstances as to show an agreement to waive the lien.” The Act assumes, as a matter of course, that part delivery leaves the remainder in the seller's hands.

Question X agreed to sell to Y 300 quintals of rice out of the 500 quintals lying in X's godown. Y paid the full price in advance and lifted 100 quintals. Y then refused to take the balance. After notice, X resold the remaining 200 quintals at a loss and sued Y for the deficiency on resale. X alternatively pleads that he was a pledgee of the rice because the contract provided that X would “advance” Y money against the rice carrying 5% interest. Decide.
Model answer

1. Frame the question. Two issues: (i) had the property in the 200 quintals passed to Y? (ii) if not, did X have any power of resale?

2. Issue (i) — s. 18 is decisive. The contract was for 300 quintals out of a larger stock of 500 quintals. On Case 34, that is a contract for the sale of unascertained goods: “The condition is not fulfilled where there is a contract for sale of a portion of a specified larger stock. Till the portion is identified and appropriated to the contract, no property passes to the buyer.” The 100 quintals Y lifted were appropriated by delivery and assent, so property in those passed. The remaining 200 quintals were never appropriated, so no property passed in them.

3. The pre-payment makes no difference. In Re Wait [1927] 1 Ch 606 a buyer who had paid in full for 500 tons out of a 1,000-ton cargo was held to have no proprietary interest, legal or equitable — he was an unsecured creditor. India has no statutory equivalent of ss. 20A–20B of the English Sale of Goods Act 1979.

4. Issue (ii) — s. 54(2) is unavailable. The statutory power of resale under s. 54(2) arises only where the property has passed to the buyer subject to the unpaid seller's lien. Here it had not. So X had no right of resale under s. 54(2), and “the claim to recover the deficiency on resale is not sustainable” (Case 34, ¶ 13).

5. The pledge argument fails. On the reasoning of Case 34, ¶ 7: the court must ask whether the contract “envisage[d] any loan of money by the seller to the buyer on the security of the goods”. The word ‘advance’ in the contract does not settle it: “the real import of this clause” has to be found. If the clause is in substance a mechanism for computing interest on the price, X was not a pledgee and had no right to sell under s. 176 of the Contract Act. Look at the substance, not the label.

6. What X can recover. Damages under s. 73 of the Contract Act, 1872: “the difference between the contract price and the market price on the date of the breach”. Where no time was fixed for acceptance, that date is the date of the buyer's refusal (Illustration (c) to s. 73). Alternatively, under s. 56 of the Sale of Goods Act, damages for non-acceptance — measured, by s. 56(2), on the same contract-minus-market basis. X may also recover interest and storage charges if they were agreed terms.

7. Note the irony worth stating in the answer. Because property never passed, X remained the owner of the 200 quintals; he was free to sell them to anyone. What he cannot do is charge Y with the loss on that sale, because the statutory resale machinery in s. 54(2) was not available to him.

2015LB-304 Q1(b) · statutory compulsion and consent
Question When goods are supplied under a statutory compulsion whether this results in sale or not and consent of the supplier is material or not? Discuss with case law to support your answer.
Model answer

1. The starting point — what a ‘sale’ requires. s. 4(1): “a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price”. ‘Contract’ carries with it, by s. 3 of the Act, the unrepealed provisions of the Contract Act, 1872 — and therefore s. 2(e) (agreement), s. 10 (free consent of parties competent to contract) and s. 13 (‘two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense’). The Supreme Court said as much in State of Madras v. Gannon Dunkerley & Co., AIR 1958 SC 560: a ‘sale of goods’ requires consensus ad idem — an agreement to transfer property for a price.

2. Is the consent of the supplier material? Yes — but it need not be express, and compulsion of law is not ‘coercion’. This is the crux of the modern law:

  • s. 14, Contract Act: consent is ‘free’ when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake.
  • s. 15: ‘coercion’ is the committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining of property. A statutory command is neither.

3. The leading authority — Andhra Sugars Ltd. v. State of A.P., AIR 1968 SC 599 : (1968) 1 SCR 705 (Wanchoo C.J., Bachawat, Ramaswami, Mitter and Hegde JJ.; judgment delivered 29 September 1967 by R.S. Bachawat J. — the same judge who decided Case 34). Under the Andhra Pradesh Sugarcane (Regulation of Supply and Purchase) Act, 1961, a cane-grower in a reserved area makes an offer to the factory and the factory is obliged to accept it and to execute a written agreement. The Court held:

“The consent of the occupier of the factory to the agreement is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake. His consent is free as defined in Section 14 of the Indian Contract Act though he is obliged by law to enter into the agreement. The compulsion of law is not coercion as defined in Section 15 of the Act. In spite of the compulsion, the agreement is neither void nor voidable. … The agreements are enforceable by law and are contracts of sale as defined in s. 4 of the Indian Sale of Goods Act.” Andhra Sugars Ltd. v. State of A.P., AIR 1968 SC 599, 706

The Court also stated the governing test: “It depends upon the facts of each case and the terms of the particular statute regulating the dealings whether the parties have entered into a contract of sale of goods.”

4. The older contrary authority, and its fate. New India Sugar Mills Ltd. v. Commissioner of Sales Tax, Bihar, AIR 1963 SC 1207: a sugar factory despatched sugar to the State of Madras pursuant to the directions of the Sugar Controller under the Sugar and Sugar Products Control Order, 1946. By a majority (Kapur and Shah JJ.; Hidayatullah J. dissenting) the Court held that the “transactions did not amount to sales”: the manufacturer was “left no volition: he could not decline to carry out the order”, and there was “no offer and acceptance and no contract”. Hidayatullah J.'s dissent — that consent “may be express or implied” and that parties trading under controls at a fixed price “must be deemed to have agreed” — was adopted in Andhra Sugars. Andhra Sugars held that New India Sugar Mills “should not be treated as an authority for the proposition that there can be no contract of sale under compulsion of a statute”.

5. The second leading authority — Coffee Board v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes. Coffee Board, Karnataka v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes, Karnataka (Case 35 cites the related decision in Consolidated Coffee Ltd. v. Coffee Board, AIR 1980 SC 1468). Under s. 25 of the Coffee Act, 1942, growers were compelled to deliver all their coffee (save an internal sale quota) to the Coffee Board, and the Board was compelled to buy it. The Board argued compulsory acquisition, or agency/trusteeship. Held: the deliveries are sales. The Court found “an element of consensuality” in the scheme — fixation of price is regulation, not acquisition; no time was fixed for delivery; the Act provided for periodical payments of price and for loans to growers. The governing principle was stated thus: “a merely regulatory law, even if it circumscribed the area of free choice, did not take away the basic character or core of sale from the transaction”. And the Board was not a trustee or agent of the growers — it sold in its own name and deducted excise and customs duties.

2015LB-304 Q3(b) · the exceptions to ‘owner bears the loss’
Question The general rule is that the owner has to bear the loss. What are the exceptions to it?
Model answer

1. State the rule and its source. Res perit domino: the loss of goods destroyed without anyone's fault falls on the owner. Enacted in s. 26, first limb — the goods remain at the seller's risk until the property is transferred, and are thereafter at the buyer's risk “whether delivery has been made or not”.

2. Exception 1 — agreement to the contrary. s. 26 opens “Unless otherwise agreed”. This is the widest exception, and in commerce it is almost always used: F.O.B. (risk on shipment), C.I.F. (risk on shipment although the seller pays freight and insurance), ex-works (risk on collection), ‘risk passes on delivery’, ‘at buyer's risk’. Case 33 and Case 35 both turned on such terms.

3. Exception 2 — fault (s. 26, first proviso). Where delivery has been delayed through the fault of either party, the goods are at the risk of the party in fault “as regards any loss which might not have occurred but for such fault”. Two consequences: (a) a buyer who is already the owner can still throw the loss back on the seller if the seller's delay caused it; (b) a buyer who is not the owner can still be made to bear it if his delay caused it. Demby Hamilton & Co. v. Barden [1949] 1 All ER 435: the buyer delayed taking delivery of apple juice; it fermented; the seller had to store it as bailee and the loss of the juice caused by the buyer's delay fell on the buyer.

4. Exception 3 — bailment (s. 26, second proviso). “Nothing in this section shall affect the duties or liabilities of either seller or buyer as a bailee of the goods of the other party.” So although the goods may be at the buyer's risk, a seller who retains possession is liable for negligence under ss. 151–152 of the Contract Act. Risk and liability for negligence are different questions. Illustration: a dealer sells a tractor (property passes under s. 20) but keeps it overnight for collection and leaves the keys in the ignition; it is stolen. The loss is the buyer's under s. 26, but the seller is liable as bailee.

5. Exception 4 — ss. 7 and 8 of the Act. Where specific goods perish before the risk has passed, the contract (s. 7) or the agreement (s. 8) is avoided without anyone's fault, and the loss stays with the seller even though property may have passed. This is a genuine exception: normally risk follows property, but here the Act steps in and reverses the incidence of the loss.

6. Exception 5 — sale on approval / sale or return (s. 24). Where goods delivered on approval are returned damaged without fault within the trial period, the loss remains the seller's even though the buyer had possession: Head v. Tattersall (1871) LR 7 Ex 7 (horse injured without fault — buyer entitled to return it); Elphick v. Barnes (1880) 5 CPD 321 (horse died on the third day without fault — seller bore the loss).

7. Exception 6 — insurance. Not strictly an exception to s. 26, but the practical answer to it: the party on whom the risk falls insures. That is why evidence of who paid the premium is such powerful evidence of where the parties intended the property to be — the reasoning of the Supreme Court in Case 35.

8. A caution. Do not list ‘delivery’ as an exception. Delivery is, by the express words of s. 26, irrelevant to risk. Confusing delivery with risk is the single commonest error in answers on this Topic.

2015LB-304 Q5(a) · delivery is not acceptance
Question “Delivery does not amount to acceptance of goods.” Discuss.
Model answer

1. The two concepts, separated. Delivery is, by s. 2(2), “voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another”. Acceptance is defined by s. 41: the buyer is deemed to have accepted the goods when he (a) intimates to the seller that he has accepted them, or (b) does any act in relation to the goods inconsistent with the ownership of the seller after they have been delivered to him, or (c) retains the goods after the lapse of a reasonable time without intimating to the seller that he has rejected them. Delivery is a physical event; acceptance is a legal one, and it can occur before, at, or long after delivery — or never.

2. Four reasons why delivery ≠ acceptance.

  1. Delivery may be to a carrier, not to the buyer (s. 23(2), s. 39(1)(b)): property passes and the buyer is at risk, yet he has not seen the goods and plainly cannot have accepted them.
  2. The buyer has a right to examine before accepting (s. 41(2) read with s. 40): “the buyer is deemed not to have accepted the goods until he has had a reasonable opportunity of examining them for the purpose of ascertaining whether they are in conformity with the contract”. Delivery is the precondition of that opportunity, not its conclusion.
  3. Acceptance is what extinguishes the right to reject. Until acceptance, the buyer may reject for breach of a condition (s. 12(2), s. 13). If delivery were acceptance, the buyer's principal remedy would vanish the moment the goods arrived — which is not the law.
  4. Acceptance can occur without delivery — under s. 41(1)(a), by an intimation; and under s. 24(a), by an act adopting the transaction.

3. Where delivery leads to acceptance — s. 41(1). Delivery is the usual trigger for the deeming rules. After delivery, the buyer accepts if he resells, consumes or alters the goods (an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership), or if he retains them beyond a reasonable time without intimating rejection. So delivery is the setting in which acceptance is tested, not acceptance itself.

4. Consequences of acceptance. Once the buyer has accepted:

  • he cannot reject the goods, and a breach of condition is treated as a breach of warranty — damages only (s. 13(1), s. 42);
  • but he retains his claim for damages (s. 59), and he does not lose it merely by paying the price unless he had a prior examination (s. 42(2)).

5. The link to Topic 6. s. 13(1) and s. 42 are the provisions that “downgrade” a condition to a warranty. So the delivery → examination → acceptance sequence is the mechanism by which the buyer's strongest remedy is lost. Note the rule DU examined in Topic 6, from Wallis v. Pratt [1911] AC 394: when a condition is reduced to a warranty the effect is not that the condition becomes a warranty — only the remedy changes.

6. Illustration. A consignment of 500 shirts is delivered to a retailer. He signs the delivery challan (delivery) but does not open the cartons. Three days later he opens them and finds the shirts are the wrong colour. He has had no “reasonable opportunity of examining” them (s. 41(2)), so he has not accepted and may still reject for breach of the s. 15 condition. Had he instead sold 100 shirts to a customer, that would be an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership — acceptance under s. 41(1)(b) — and he would be left with damages only.

Exam technique. Quote s. 41 verbatim — most candidates cannot, and it is the whole answer. Then the four reasons, then the consequences under ss. 13 and 42. Note that s. 40–42 belong to Topic 8; flag that you are going slightly beyond Topic 7, or the examiner will assume you have lost your place.
2015LB-304 Q8(b) · the jeweller's diamonds
Question A, a jeweller, delivers some diamonds to B on sale or return. B pledges the diamonds with C. B failed to pay the price of diamonds and A commenced an action against C to recover the diamonds. Will he succeed?
Model answer

Short answer: No — A's action against C fails. B had become the owner before he pledged, so B could validly pledge and C has a good pledge. A's remedy is against B for the price.

1. The governing provision — s. 24. Goods delivered on approval or ‘on sale or return’: the property passes to the buyer — “(a) when he signifies his approval or acceptance to the seller or does any other act adopting the transaction”.

2. Pledging is an act adopting the transaction. This is settled by Kirkham v. Attenborough [1897] 1 QB 201 (CA): jewellery was delivered by the plaintiff to a person on sale-or-return; that person pledged it with the defendant. Held: the pledging was an act adopting the transaction; the property had already passed to the intermediate person before the pledge; he was therefore able to pledge what was by then his own property, and the plaintiff's claim against the pledgee failed. The facts of the 2015 question are Kirkham almost exactly.

3. The reasoning to spell out.

  1. On delivery ‘on sale or return’, B had possession but no property; he held an option to become the owner.
  2. By pledging the diamonds, B did an act that is inconsistent with A's continued ownership — precisely the test in s. 41(1)(b) and the classic ‘act adopting the transaction’ under s. 24(a).
  3. Therefore property passed to B at the moment of the pledge, and before the pledge took effect.
  4. B accordingly pledged his own goods, and C obtained a valid pledge.
  5. A cannot recover the diamonds from C. Note that this is not a nemo dat case at all: there is no need to invoke s. 30(2), because B was the owner when he pledged.

4. The alternative route, which reaches the same result. If, contrary to the above, one held that B had not adopted the transaction, then B had ‘agreed to buy’ the diamonds within s. 30(2) and had obtained possession with the consent of the seller. A pledge by him to a person taking in good faith and without notice of any lien or other right of A would “have effect as if such lien or right did not exist”. So on either analysis C prevails. Say this — it is the mark of a complete answer.

5. The contrast that sharpens the point. Poole v. Smith's Car Sales (Balham) Ltd. [1962] 2 All ER 482: a car was delivered to a dealer on sale or return and the dealer, before paying, allowed a customer to take it on hire-purchase. The Court of Appeal held the dealer had not adopted the transaction — a negotiation was not an act of ownership — but that the finance company was protected by s. 25(2) of the English Act (our s. 30(2)). Note the difference: in Kirkham the pledge itself was the adopting act; in Poole the transaction was still at the negotiation stage and only the statutory exception saved the third party.

6. A's remedies. A may sue B for the price (s. 55 — property having passed under s. 24), or alternatively for damages for breach of contract. A cannot vindicate the diamonds against C. Practical moral: a jeweller who delivers on sale-or-return should stipulate in writing that no property passes until payment in full, thereby relying on s. 25(1) and on the express term defeating the s. 24 presumption.

Exam technique. Name Kirkham v. Attenborough in the first sentence — the facts are lifted from it. Give two routes to the same conclusion (s. 24(a) and s. 30(2)); that is what distinguishes a first-class answer from a pass. Then state clearly that A's remedy is in personam against B.

2015LB-304 Q5(b) · C.I.F. resale and rejection
Question The buyer of goods under a C.I.F. contract, soon after taking delivery of it, resold a part of the goods and despatched the same to the sub-buyers. Later on the buyer came to know that the goods were not in accordance with the contract and wanted to reject them. Is he entitled to do so? Explain.
Model answer

Short answer: No — not as to the goods he has already resold and despatched. He may still reject the remainder, if he acts in time and the breach is of a condition.

1. The C.I.F. contract: property passes on shipment, and documents are everything. Under a C.I.F. contract the seller's duty is to ship conforming goods, procure a bill of lading and a policy of insurance, and tender the documents. Property passes on shipment or on tender of documents, depending on the contract — and in the classic analysis a C.I.F. buyer may reject the documents if they are not in order, and reject the goods on arrival if they do not conform. But he must do so before acceptance.

2. The decisive provision — s. 41(1)(b). The buyer “is deemed to have accepted the goods … when the goods have been delivered to him and he does any act in relation to them which is inconsistent with the ownership of the seller”. Reselling the goods and despatching them to sub-buyers is the clearest possible act inconsistent with the seller's ownership. Compare Kirkham v. Attenborough [1897] 1 QB 201, where a mere pledge was held to be such an act; a fortiori an outright resale and despatch.

3. The consequence — s. 42 and s. 13(1). Where the buyer has accepted the goods, “the breach of any condition to be fulfilled by the seller can only be treated as a breach of warranty”, unless the contract provides otherwise. So the buyer loses the right to reject and is left with damages (s. 59) — the difference between the value of the goods as they should have been and their value as they are.

4. The three qualifications the examiner is looking for.

  • (a) Only the part resold is affected. Acceptance is severable in fact: the buyer's act relates to the goods he has resold and despatched. He may still reject the remainder, provided he rejects it within a reasonable time of discovering the defect (ss. 41(1)(c), 42, 63) and before he has done any adopting act in relation to that remainder. s. 38(2) is the analogous provision for instalment contracts.
  • (b) The buyer must have had a reasonable opportunity to examine. s. 41(2): “the buyer is deemed not to have accepted the goods until he has had a reasonable opportunity of examining them for the purpose of ascertaining whether they are in conformity with the contract.” If the defect was latent — one that no reasonable examination on delivery would reveal — then reselling and despatching before the defect could be discovered may not amount to acceptance, and the buyer may still reject. This is the point on which the answer turns, and it is the mark of a good one.
  • (c) He does not lose his damages claim by paying. s. 42(2): in the absence of a prior examination, payment of the price does not of itself amount to acceptance and does not bar damages.

5. Authorities. Kirkham v. Attenborough [1897] 1 QB 201 (pledge = acceptance) · Poole v. Smith's Car Sales [1962] 2 All ER 482 (mere negotiation is not) · the Wallis v. Pratt [1911] AC 394 rule (from Topic 6) that when a condition is reduced to a warranty the effect is not that the condition became a warranty — only the remedy changes. And the classic C.I.F. proposition: the buyer's right is to reject the documents if they are bad, and the goods if they do not conform — but in each case before acceptance.

6. Conclusion. As to the resold and despatched part, the buyer has accepted and cannot reject; his remedy is damages under s. 59. As to the remainder, he may reject if he acts within a reasonable time and the defect was not one a reasonable examination at delivery would have revealed.

Exam technique. Split the goods — that is the insight the question is testing. Then run s. 41(1)(b) → s. 42 → s. 59 for the resold part, and s. 41(2) → s. 42 for the remainder. Note that ss. 41–42 are Topic 8 material, so begin by saying “this question straddles Topics 7 and 8”.
2015LB-304 Q6 · lien and stoppage in transit — cross-topic
Question By implications of law examine the validity of the following statement: “Lien ends where the right of stoppage commences.”
Model answer
! Note

This question belongs to Topic 8 (Rights of an unpaid seller, ss. 45–54) rather than Topic 7. It is answered here because DU asked it in the same paper; prepare it properly under Topic 8.

1. The statement is substantially correct. Lien and stoppage in transit are the first two of the three rights of an unpaid seller against the goods (s. 46(1): lien, stoppage in transit, and resale). They are mutually exclusive in point of fact, because they operate at different stages of the carriage:

2. Why the statement is true. The moment the seller delivers the goods to a carrier for transmission to the buyer without reserving the right of disposal, two things happen simultaneously: his lien is lost (s. 49(1)(a)) and the goods are in transit (s. 51(1)), so that if the buyer is insolvent the right of stoppage arises (s. 50). One right ends exactly where the other begins. The same event — handing the goods to a carrier — is the terminus of the lien and the genesis of stoppage.

3. Three qualifications.

  1. Not every end of lien is a beginning of stoppage. Lien is also lost when the buyer lawfully obtains possession (s. 49(1)(b)) — in that case the transit is over too, so no stoppage arises. And lien is lost by waiver (s. 49(1)(c)) without any question of transit.
  2. Stoppage requires insolvency; lien does not. Lien is available whenever the price is unpaid and no credit was given or the credit has expired (s. 47(1)(a)–(b)). Stoppage is available only where the buyer has become insolvent (s. 50). So the two rights are not simply two halves of one continuous right — the trigger differs.
  3. Both presuppose that the property has passed. Where property has not passed, the unpaid seller's remedy is not lien or stoppage at all, but the “right of withholding delivery similar to and co-extensive with his rights of lien and stoppage” under s. 46(2). Case 34 is a reminder: there the property had never passed, so the seller had neither lien nor stoppage nor resale.

4. “By implications of law”. The question's opening words point to s. 46(1) itself, which says the unpaid seller has these rights “by implication of law” — they arise automatically, by operation of law, and do not have to be stipulated. But note that, being rights arising by implication of law, they are subject to s. 62 and may be negatived or varied by express agreement, course of dealing, or a usage binding on both parties.

Conclusion. The statement is valid as a general proposition about the carriage stage, subject to three qualifications: the lien may also end otherwise than by delivery to a carrier; stoppage additionally requires the buyer's insolvency; and where the property has not passed the seller is exercising the different right in s. 46(2).

Exam technique. Use the comparison table — it carries the whole answer. Then the three qualifications, then the s. 62 point prompted by the words ‘by implications of law’. Do not confuse lien (retain) with stoppage (resume): that one word difference is what the question tests.

Delivery is governed by ss. 33–39 — strictly Topic 8. It is set out here in outline because DU asked it alongside the Topic 7 questions; the full treatment belongs to Topic 8.

1. Definition and duty. s. 2(2): “delivery” means voluntary transfer of possession from one person to another. s. 31: “It is the duty of the seller to deliver the goods and of the buyer to accept and pay for them, in accordance with the terms of the contract of sale.” s. 32: unless otherwise agreed, delivery and payment are concurrent conditions.

  • s. 39(1)(b) read with s. 23(2) — delivery to a carrier or other bailee (whether named by the buyer or not) for transmission is prima facie deemed delivery to the buyer and deemed unconditional appropriation. Case 35: “the carrier becomes the agent of the vendee”.

  1. Delivery is not acceptance — s. 41; see Set E3.
  2. Delivery does not necessarily pass the property — s. 25, and Case 33.
  3. Delivery is irrelevant to risk — s. 26: “whether delivery has been made or not”.
  4. Delivery to a carrier without reserving disposal destroys the lien — s. 49(1)(a), the exact mirror of s. 23(2).

SynthesisedProblem The composite problem — ss. 20, 26, 27 and 30(1) in one
Question On 1 March, A agreed to sell his specific second-hand car to B for ₹4 lakh, payment and delivery both deferred to 15 March. On 5 March the car was destroyed in a fire in A's garage. A had no fire insurance. Separately, on 10 March — having forgotten the first sale — A sold and delivered the same car to C for ₹4.2 lakh, and C, who knew nothing of the sale to B, drove it away. Advise B and C. (Assume for the second part that the fire did not occur.)
Model answer

Part I — the fire on 5 March: who bears the loss?

1. Has the property passed? Yes. This is an unconditional contract for the sale of specific goods in a deliverable state. s. 20 therefore applies: “the property in the goods passes to the buyer when the contract is made, and it is immaterial whether the time of payment of the price or the time of delivery of the goods, or both, is postponed.” B became the owner on 1 March. Tarling v. Baxter (1827) 6 B & C 360.

2. Who bears the risk? B. By s. 26, once the property is transferred “the goods are at the buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not”. B never took delivery and never touched the car, and that is irrelevant.

3. The fault proviso. A's failure to insure is not ‘fault’: ‘fault’ means “wrongful act or default” (s. 2(5)), and there is no suggestion that the fire was A's doing. The proviso is not engaged. (Had the fire been caused by A's negligence, the second proviso would in any case have preserved A's liability as bailee of B's car under ss. 151–152 of the Contract Act.)

4. Result. B must still pay the ₹4 lakh and bears the loss of the car. A may sue for the price under s. 55 (property having passed and B having wrongly neglected to pay) rather than merely for damages.

Part II — the sale to C on 10 March: who owns the car?

1. B was already the owner. Property passed to B on 1 March under s. 20. When A “sold” to C on 10 March, A had no property left to transfer.

2. So A is “a person who is not the owner” — s. 27. The general rule would give C nothing. But look at s. 30(1):

“Where a person, having sold goods, continues or is in possession of the goods or of the documents of title to the goods, the delivery or transfer by that person … under any sale, pledge or other disposition thereof to any person receiving the same in good faith and without notice of the previous sale shall have the same effect as if the person making the delivery or transfer were expressly authorised by the owner of the goods to make the same.”

Every condition is satisfied: A had sold (to B) and continued in possession; he then delivered the car (an actual transfer is required — F. E. Hookway & Co. v. Alfred Isaacs & Sons [1954] 1 Lloyd's Rep 491) to C, who took in good faith and without notice of the previous sale.

3. Result. C gets a good title and keeps the car. B loses it.

4. B's remedy. B cannot recover the car from C — nemo dat is displaced by s. 30(1). B's remedies against A are:

  • the price B has paid, on the footing of a total failure of consideration (s. 65, Contract Act, 1872; Rowland v. Divall [1923] 1 KB 500);
  • damages for breach of the s. 14(a) implied condition as to title and the s. 14(b) warranty of quiet possession;
  • damages for conversion of the car, in tort.

5. The practical moral, and the point to add. Buyers of second-hand goods should take delivery immediately or, at the very least, take the registration certificate and the keys. A buyer who leaves the goods (and the documents) in the seller's hands is exposed to exactly this risk — and Case 36 shows the mirror-image rule: an owner who leaves a car with its registration book in a mercantile agent's hands is exposed in the same way.

Exam technique. In any composite problem, separate the questions: (i) where is the property? (ii) where is the risk? (iii) was there a valid transfer of title? Answer them in that order and in numbered paragraphs, and cite one section for each step. Never write a continuous narrative — the examiner cannot mark it.

✓ The last word

Topic 7 is, in substance, one question asked five ways: who owns the goods at the relevant moment? Everything else — risk, title, the right to resell, the right to reject, the right to sue for the price — is a consequence of that answer. Sections 18 to 25 tell you how to find it; section 26 tells you who suffers if the goods are lost; section 27 tells you what happens when the seller had no right to sell at all, and sections 28 to 30 tell you when the law nevertheless protects the buyer.

If you take one sentence into the examination hall, take this one, from the Supreme Court in Case 33: “the property in the goods passes when the parties intend it to pass” — and then find that intention in the terms of the contract, the conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case, which is exactly what s. 19(2) tells you to do.
