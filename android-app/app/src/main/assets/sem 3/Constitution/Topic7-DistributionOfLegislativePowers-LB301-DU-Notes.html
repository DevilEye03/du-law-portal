The spine of the whole topic (memorise this sentence): First ask whether the legislature’s territory touches the transaction (Art 245 + nexus); then whether its list covers the subject (Art 246 + plenary/ancillary reading); then whether a rival claim is reconciled by harmony, saved by pith and substance, or exposed as colourable; then whether the leftover belongs to Parliament (Art 248); and only if two competent laws on the same concurrent matter truly collide does Art 254 speak.

§2 Article 245 — Extent of Laws & the Doctrine of Territorial Nexus item (a)

⚖ Article 245 (verbatim — Constitution of India (2020))

245. Extent of laws made by Parliament and by the Legislatures of States.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this Constitution, Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India, and the Legislature of a State may make laws for the whole or any part of the State.

(2) No law made by Parliament shall be deemed to be invalid on the ground that it would have extraterritorial operation.

Reading clause (1): two legislatures, two territories, one proviso

🧩 Workings

  • “Subject to the provisions of this Constitution” — territorial extent is only the first gate; Part III, the lists (Arts 246/248), Art 254 etc. still apply. G.V.K.: Art 245(1)’s subject is “the whole or any part of the territory of India”, object is to empower Parliament; the linking word is the preposition “for” — Concise O.E.D. senses (“in the interest of, to the benefit of, in defence of … in respect of”) — which is why Parliament legislates for India’s benefit only.
  • Parliament: whole or any part of India — a State legislature: whole or any part of the State — plus Art 246(4)’s special allowance for non-State territories (§3).
  • Clause (2) is a shield, not a sword: it removes one ground of invalidation (“extraterritorial operation”) for laws Parliament has already validly made under clause (1). G.V.K. rejects the Attorney-General’s claim that 245(2) is an independent source of legislative power: “make laws” (legislature) — “operation” (executive) — “invalidate” (judiciary) are three different functions; clause (2) simply narrows judicial review by that one ground. Expressio unius: if power to legislate “for any territory” were hidden in clause (2), the words “for the whole or any part of the territory of India” in clause (1) would be surplusage.
  • Art 1(3)(c) completes the picture: territories acquired become part of India; only then may Parliament legislate for that territory (G.V.K. paras 98–100, citing Re Berubari). Art 260 is the only express provision for foreign territories (agreements with foreign governments).

🎯 What the DU casebook stresses

  • Charusila (53) quotes Art 245(1) and (2) verbatim in the parties’ arguments and then applies the nexus doctrine to uphold a State law whose effects reached Calcutta property.
  • G.V.K. (54) is a full Constitution Bench-style exposition on the outer limit of Parliament’s own power: no legislating for foreign territory without Indian nexus, and laws must be for the benefit of India.
  • Du cross-references the nexus test to income-tax, sales-tax and gambling-tax lines of cases — 51 Tata Iron (sales tax) and 52 R.M.D.C. (gambling).

The Doctrine of Territorial Nexus

A law made for a territory may operate beyond it. The doctrine polices State legislatures (Parliament having Art 245(2)): a State law with extraterritorial operation is saved only if a sufficient territorial connection exists between the State and the person/property/transaction regulated — and the liability imposed is pertinent to that connection.

🧾 The two-element test — as stated in DU’s own Charusila excerpt (p. 256)

“This Court has applied the doctrine of territorial connection or nexus to income tax legislation, sales tax legislation and also to legislation imposing a tax on gambling. In Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar [AIR 1958 SC 452] the earlier cases were reviewed and it was pointed out that sufficiency of the territorial connection involved a consideration of two elements, namely, (a) the connection must be real and not illusory and (b) the liability sought to be imposed must be pertinent to that connection.” — S.K. Das J. in State of Bihar v. Charusila Dasi.

And on Tata Iron’s own caveat: “It is not necessary for us … to lay down any broad proposition as to whether the theory of nexus, as a principle of legislation is applicable to all kinds of legislation … no apparent reason to confine its application to income tax legislation but … extended it to sales tax and to tax on gambling.”

🔬 The test, step by step

  1. Is there a connection between the taxing/regulating State and the subject? Candidates: presence of goods at the time of the agreement; manufacture/production in the State; situs of the trust/property/institution; the activity (gambling) occurring within the State; residence/operation of trustees within the State.
  2. Is it real and not illusory? (rational basis, not a fiction on paper)
  3. Is the liability pertinent to that connection? (the tax/regulation must relate to the connected activity — not an unrelated obligation piggy-backing on a thin link)
  4. Both satisfied → operation outside the State does not invalidate the law (no “extraterritoriality” strike-down). One fails → the law fails as applied.

Modern gloss (G.V.K. para 125): no quantitative “sufficiency/significance” thresholds for Parliament — “real and not illusory or fanciful” is enough; whether a given law shows the real connection is “a mixed matter of facts and of law.”

🧭 Two rules students confuse

  • Art 245(2) (Parliament): ground-level protection — a central law is never struck down merely for extraterritorial operation; but per G.V.K., Parliament still must have competence under (1) (nexus with India; benefit of India). A wholly foreign-territory law = ultra vires for want of power, not “extraterritorial”.
  • Nexus doctrine (States): a justification — the State proves its connection so that operation outside its borders stands. Also applied to central legislation’s situs questions (e.g., Bengal Immunity) but classically to State taxes.

Case 51 · Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar — AIR 1958 SC 452 RESEARCHED · DU mention

Doctrine: territorial nexus extended to sales tax · Bench: S.R. Das C.J., T.L. Venkatarama Ayyar, S.K. Das, A.K. Sarkar and Vivian Bose JJ. · decided 19-02-1958 · DU cross-ref: quoted in Charusila DU pp. 255–256
Facts

TISCO manufactured iron and steel at Jamshedpur (Bihar) and sold to buyers all over India, dispatching goods on its own railway receipts (TISCO as consignee, freight paid by it; receipts sent to branches/banks to be released on payment). Under the Bihar Sales Tax Act, 1947 (Bihar Act 19 of 1947), s. 4(1) read with s. 2(g) and the second proviso inserted by the Bihar (Amendment) Act 6 of 1949, the Sales Tax Officer disallowed TISCO’s deductions for sales made/ goods delivered & consumed outside Bihar and added the sales-tax realisations of ₹13,66,496 (first period) and ₹22,37,919 (second period) to the taxable turnover — assessing TISCO to ₹15,31,374-5-9 and ₹28,30,458-6-9 respectively. The Commissioner of Sales Tax (Chota Nagpur) dismissed TISCO’s appeals; the Patna High Court upheld the Act’s validity but framed references (including on whether tax collected from customers as such formed part of the taxable turnover). The matter reached the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Is the levy a tax on sale of goods (Entry 48, List II, GoIA 1935 ≡ Entry 54, Seventh Schedule) — or, in substance, an excise duty on production which the Province could not impose?
  2. Does the theory of territorial connection/nexus apply to sales-tax legislation at all? (Attorney-General relied on Bengal Immunity having left the question open; nexus cases so far were income-tax cases.)
  3. If it applies, is there a sufficient nexus where goods are manufactured/present in Bihar but the sale is completed and property passes outside Bihar?
  4. Does the retrospective reach of the 1949 proviso destroy the tax’s character or exceed competence? Do the claimed deductions (including railway freight) have to be allowed?
Arguments — TISCO (appellant)
  • The sales were inter-State: goods delivered and consumed outside Bihar; property passed outside; therefore Bihar’s territorial power does not reach them — “It has no power to tax extra territorially, therefore it can only tax sales that occur in the State itself.”
  • Nexus theory cannot be disaggregated across the “components” of a sale: the power is to tax the sale — locate the sale, and if it is not in Bihar, Bihar cannot tax it (this became Bose J.’s dissent).
  • The levy is really excise on manufacture (producers’ first sale), beyond provincial power; the nexus is “illusory”, not real; retrospective application destroys the indirect nature of the tax, converting it into a direct tax on the dealer.
  • Collection-of-tax-by-dealer amounts etc. should be excluded from the turnover; freight deductions allowed.
Arguments — State of Bihar (respondent)
  • Goods originated, were manufactured and were present in Bihar at the time of the contract; agreements of sale were executed around Bihar operations; the second proviso merely located (“deemed”) such sales in Bihar — it did not enlarge the definition of “sale”.
  • Nexus is real: manufacture/presence in the State + realisation of price through Bihar-based machinery; liability (tax on sale) is pertinent to that connection. A province may tax sales having territorial nexus even if property ultimately passes elsewhere (sale situs fiction well accepted in State practice).
  • First sale by the manufacturer is taxed qua seller, not qua manufacturer — hence sales tax, not excise. Multiple-taxation argument answered by Art 286/central legislation possibilities.
Decision (majority; Bose J. dissenting)
  1. It is a sales tax. Liability arises on “sale”, not on production; the proviso does not enlarge “sale” but, on the facts specified, locates the sale in Bihar.
  2. Nexus theory applies to sales tax — “it is too late in the day to contend that the theory of nexus does not apply to sales tax legislation at all”; examination of this Court’s decisions clearly recognises it (relying on the first-sale/explanation line, e.g., Poppatlal Shah-line cases).
  3. Sufficient nexus exists on two independent footings: (a) presence of the goods in Bihar at the date of the agreement for sale, and (b) manufacture/production of the goods in Bihar — even though property passed and delivery occurred outside.
  4. Retrospective operation of the amendment valid; does not alter the tax’s character. Costs against TISCO; appeals dismissed.
  5. Dissolution — Vivian Bose J.: a tax on a sale cannot be split into ingredients; “the power to tax is limited to the sale … Supreme Court shall determine where a sale is situated and once that is determined it cannot be situated anywhere else. If it does not happen to be in your territory you cannot tax it.” He warned of multiple taxation by several States — a concern that led Parliament to the Central Sales Tax Act, 1956.
Principle

(1) Territorial nexus is a general principle of legislation, not confined to income-tax — it governs sales-tax laws too. (2) The sufficiency of connection has two elements: the connection must be real and not illusory, and the liability must be pertinent to that connection (formulation quoted by the Supreme Court itself in Charusila, DU p. 256 — the anchor statement for syllabus item (a)). (3) Manufacture in, or presence of goods at, the taxing State supplies that connection. (4) A State law’s operation outside its borders does not, of itself, violate Art 245.

Case 52 · State of Bombay v. R. M. D. Chamarbaugwala — AIR 1957 SC 699; [1957] SCR 874 RESEARCHED · DU mention

Doctrine: territorial nexus upheld for gambling-tax; gambling = res extra commercium · Bench: decided 09-04-1957 (S.R. Das C.J. leading) · DU cross-ref: applied in Charusila DU pp. 256–257 (and see companion “R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India”, SCR 930, for the presumption-of-validity rule)
Facts

R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (first respondent) was managing director of a company (Star Publications Ltd.) incorporated in Mysore State, which ran a giant crossword-style prize competition — the “R.M.D.C. Cross-words” — through a weekly newspaper printed and published at Bangalore. Entries and entry fees flowed in from all over India, including from Bombay, where agents collected fees and the gambling activity (answering/pooling entries) occurred. The Bombay Lotteries and Prize Competitions Control and Tax Act, 1948 regulated such competitions; the Bombay Lotteries and Prize Competition Control and Tax (Amendment) Act, 1952 (Act XXX of 1952) widened the definition of “prize competition” in s. 2(1)(d) to include competitions run through newspapers printed and published outside the State and inserted s. 12A, levying a tax on the promoters calculated on sums collected through Bombay. The promoters moved the Bombay High Court under Art 226: the Act, as applied to them, was ultra vires (extra-territorial), and violated Art 19(1)(g) and Art 301; the tax was also attacked as a tax on their trade (Entry 60) rather than gambling (Entry 62), and as offending Arts 276(2)/304(b). The High Court (Court of Appeal) partly protected them; the State appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Are the prize competitions of a gambling nature (Entry 34) or protected trade/business (Entry 26/60)? Is s. 12A a tax on betting & gambling (Entry 62) or a tax on trade?
  2. Does the State legislature have competence to apply the Act to competitions conducted via a newspaper printed/published outside Bombay — i.e., is there a sufficient territorial nexus (Art 245/246)?
  3. If they are gambling: do Art 19(1)(g) and Art 301 protect the business? (And consequently, do Arts 304(b)/276(2) sanction questions arise?)
Arguments — State of Bombay (appellant)
  • The competitions, judged by the statutory definition and the actual schemes, were gambling — chance substantially predominated; gambling is outside “trade, commerce or intercourse” (res extra commercium), hence no Art 19(1)(g)/301 protection at all.
  • Entries 34 and 62, List II (betting & gambling; taxes on luxuries/entertainment/betting/gambling) squarely cover regulation + tax; s. 12A is levied on gambling — a percentage of entry fees remitted from Bombay — collected through promoters merely as a convenient mode (the promoter is not taxed qua trade).
  • Territorial nexus: Bombay is where entries are solicited, fees collected and the gambling undertaken; wide circulation + agents + remittances give a real connection (the exact point DU records: activities “which the gambler is ordinarily expected to undertake” took place mostly, if not entirely, in Bombay).
  • Corporation with Indian-citizen shareholders has no freestanding right wider than the activity’s status.
Arguments — Respondents/promoters (upholders of the High Court view)
  • The competitions demanded skill, knowledge and judgment — a bona fide business, not gambling; hence protected u/Art 19(1)(g); corporation (citizen shareholders) entitled to fundamental rights.
  • The State’s law cannot reach beyond its borders: the paper was printed/published outside; regulating out-of-State business = extra-territorial legislation violating Art 245; s. 12A is a tax on their trade (Entry 60), not gambling; restrictions unreasonable under Art 19(6); Art 301 free inter-State trade; Art 304(b) requires previous sanction of the President for discriminatory trade laws (not obtained); Art 276(2) bar on certain fees; windfall-levy argument (net profit only ~5%).
Decision
  1. Validity tested on three planes (the Court’s own scheme): (i) legislative competence — real nature of the levy is a tax on gambling under Entries 34/62, List II; “on ultimate analysis it is a tax on each entry fee received from each individual competitor who remits it from the State of Bombay”; collection from promoters ≠ incidence on promoters; (ii) territorial nexus — “There is sufficient territorial nexus to entitle the State Legislature [to] collect the tax from the petitioners who carry on the prize competitions through the medium of a newspaper printed and published outside the State of Bombay.” The connection = Bombay participation, fee-flows, agents, and the gambling consummated in Bombay — real and pertinent; (iii) constitutional limitations — gambling is not “trade, commerce or intercourse”; therefore no Art 19(1)(g) and no Art 301 protection (and the Art 304(b)/276(2) objections fall with it).
  2. Competitions held gambling in nature where chance predominates over skill; the Act’s regulatory and taxing provisions applied to them were within competence.
  3. Appeal allowed — High Court’s order set aside; petition dismissed with costs throughout.
Principle

(1) A State may validly regulate and tax activity that is consummated within its borders even though organised, printed and published outside — given a sufficient territorial nexus (entries solicited + fees remitted + participation within the State). This is the gambling-tax limb of the nexus doctrine, expressly relied upon by the Supreme Court in Charusila (DU p. 257): “all the activities which the gambler is ordinarily expected to undertake” took place mostly, if not entirely, in Bombay → sufficient territorial nexus → law not bad for extra-territoriality. (2) Gambling is res extra commercium — not trade, hence outside Arts 19(1)(g)/301. (3) The real nature of a tax (not who remits it) decides the entry.

Case 53 · State of Bihar v. Charusila Dasi — AIR 1959 SC 1002 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 252–257

[Doctrine of Territorial Nexus – Extra-territorial operation of a State legislation] · Bench: S.R. Das C.J. and S.K. Das, P.B. Gajendragadkar, K.N. Wanchoo, M. Hidayatullah JJ. (judgment by S.K. Das J.)
Facts (from the DU excerpt)

The Srimati Charusila Trust was created by a trust deed of 11 March 1938 by Srimati Charusila Dasi, widow of Akshaya Kumar Ghose, residing at Charu Niwas, Deoghar (district Santhal Perganas, Bihar). Trust properties: Schedule B — three bighas+ of land at Mohalla Karanibad, Deoghar (on which a twin temple “jugal mandir” and Nat Mandir were built); Schedule C — Charu Niwas, Deoghar; Schedule D — several houses and land in Calcutta, aggregate value ≈ ₹8,50,000. Annual income of all properties ≈ ₹87,839. Objects: install and worship the deity Iswar Srigopal and the image of her spiritual preceptor Sri Sri Balanand Brahmachari; fund daily puja/festivals (≤ ₹13,600 + ₹4,500 p.a.); and run the Akshaya Kumar Female Hospital for Hindu females with an outdoor dispensary for all, at ₹12,000 p.a. (temples built and deities installed; hospital not yet built). Five trustees including herself.

On 27-10-1952 the Superintendent, Bihar State Board of Religious Trusts, Patna, issued notice under s. 59 of the Bihar Hindu Religious Trusts Act, 1950 (Bihar 1 of 1951) demanding a return; she replied the trust was private, for a family idol, so the Act did not apply. The Board disagreed; on 5-2-1953 the President proposed to assess a fee under s. 70. She moved the Patna High Court under Art 226 (6-4-1953): (a) private trust → Act inapplicable; (b) Act ultra vires as violating Art 19. The High Court (5-10-1953) held the trust wholly private, quashed the s. 59/s. 70 proceedings (certiorari) and prohibited the Board (prohibition). The State + Board appealed on an Art 133 certificate.

Issues (the DU excerpt’s two questions, para 6)
  1. On a true construction of the 1938 deed, is the Charusila Trust a private endowment for a family idol (as the High Court found) — so that the Act does not apply?
  2. If not (i.e., it is a public religious trust): does s. 3 of the Act validly apply to trust properties situated outside Bihar (the Calcutta houses) — or is that extra-territorial legislation beyond the Bihar Legislature’s power under Arts 245/246?
Arguments — for the respondent (Charusila) on the nexus point
  • Text of Arts 245–246: Parliament may make laws for the whole/any part of India; a State Legislature for the whole/any part of the State; Art 245(2) saves only Parliament’s laws from the extraterritoriality ground — by implication a State law may not operate extra-territorially.
  • Art 246 distribution: State exclusive in List II, concurrent in List III; Item 28 List III = “Charities and charitable institutions, charitable and religious endowments and religious institutions.” The Bihar legislature, acting under that Item, had no power to make a law operating outside Bihar; only Parliament could legislate for institutions with property in several States.
  • Alternative: even if the Act applies where any part of the property is in Bihar (s. 3), its provisions cannot be applied to the Calcutta properties — such application would be ultra vires.
Arguments / approach — State (and the Court’s working of them)
  • Construction first: read s. 1(2) (“extends to the whole of the State of Bihar”) + s. 3 + the Preamble (“better administration of Hindu religious trusts in the State of Bihar and protection of properties appertaining to such trusts”) — the Act applies only where (a) the trust/institution is in Bihar AND (b) some part of its property is in Bihar. Presumption that the legislature did not exceed jurisdiction; a sovereign legislature’s Act must, if possible, be given an operative interpretation (In re Hindu Women’s Rights to Property Act; Re Special Reference u/s 213; R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. UOI, SCR 930).
  • In personam control: the Act operates by controlling the trustees in Bihar — beneficiaries’ rights are enforceable only where the trust is situate or the trustees reside (Dicey, Conflict of Laws, 7th edn., 506). “The trust being situate in Bihar the State has legislative power over it and also over its trustees or their servants and agents who must be in Bihar.”
  • Integrated whole: temples in Bihar, hospital to be in Bihar for the Bihar public → enough territorial connection; the institution and its appurtenant property “form one integrated whole”; liability on trustees necessarily affects trust property → liability pertinent to connection.
  • Relied on: Tata Iron two-element test; State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (gambling nexus) — “We do not see any reason why the principles … should not be followed in the present case.”
Decision
  1. The appeal is allowed; the High Court’s judgment (5-10-1953) set aside; Charusila’s petition dismissed with costs — i.e., the trust is not held to be a private endowment (it is a public religious trust within the Act’s definitional scheme, per the connected appeals of the same date: Act does not apply to private endowments; here the conditions for application are satisfied).
  2. Section 3, correctly construed, imposes the two cumulative conditions (institution in Bihar + some property in Bihar) — the Act therefore has no extra-territorial application as construed.
  3. On the Calcutta properties: valid — the religious endowment is in Bihar, trustees function there, the connection is real; liability on trustees pertinent; “If therefore any liability is imposed on the trustees, such liability must affect the trust property.” Following Tata Iron and R.M.D.C.: “the Act applies and the provisions of the Act cannot be struck down on the ground of extra-territoriality.”
  4. A related Art 32 petition (Bombay Public Trusts Act applied to a Banaras math) did not decide the point — the Attorney-General disclaimed extraterritorial application (para 16).
Principle

(1) Territorial nexus for State legislation: where the institution/trust and its trustees are in the State, the State may legislate about appurtenant property situated elsewhere — control exercised in personam on trustees within the State, plus the “integrated whole” of trust and property, supplies a real connection pertinent to the liability. (2) Two-element test (via Tata Iron): connection real, not illusory; liability pertinent to it. (3) Construction saves competence: read the enactment with its preamble and extension clause so as to exclude extra-territorial operation (presumption of constitutional validity). (4) Comparative/foreign authority (Dicey) on situs of trusts may be used to fix the connection.

Case 54 · G.V.K. Industries Ltd. & Anr. v. Income Tax Officer & Anr. — (2011) 4 SCC 36 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 258–270

Bench: S.H. Kapadia C.J. and B. Sudershan Reddy, K.S.P. Radhakrishnan, S.S. Nijjar, Swatantra Kumar JJ. (judgment by B. Sudershan Reddy J.)
Facts (DU excerpt, “Factual Background”)

The appellant company filed a writ petition in the Andhra Pradesh High Court against an order of the Income Tax Officers deciding it liable to withhold tax on monies paid to a foreign company under s. 9(1)(i) or s. 9(1)(vii)(b) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (tax on payments deemed to accrue/receive outside India). It also challenged the vires of s. 9(1)(vii)(b) for want of legislative competence and violation of Art 14. The High Court held s. 9(1)(i) inapplicable on the facts but upheld s. 9(1)(vii)(b) — its validity and its applicability — relying mainly on the ratio of the three-Judge Bench decision in Electronics Corporation of India Ltd. v. CIT, 1989 Supp (2) SCC 642 (“ECIL”). The company appealed.

The DU extract is the constitutional court’s grand exposition: the judgment first surveys what legislative competence means in a federation (paras 1–6), then frames two questions on the outer limits of Art 245(1), interprets the words of Art 245 textually, structurally (Arts 1, 2, 51, 246, 260) and historically (Draft cl. 179), and answers them. (The appeal was ultimately ordered to be listed before an appropriate Bench for disposal of the tax dispute — para 128.)

Issues (framed by the Court, para 6 — DU pp. 259–260)
  1. Is Parliament constitutionally restricted from enacting legislation with respect to extra-territorial aspects or causes that have no (expected) impact/effect/consequence on (a) India or any part, or (b) interests, welfare, well-being or security of inhabitants/Indians?
  2. Does Parliament have power to legislate “for” any territory other than India or any part of it?

The Court contrasted two dichotomous positions: the ECIL-line territorial-nexus restriction (in three strengths — strict “India-only”; “significant/sufficient impact”; “some impact”) vs the Attorney-General’s sovereign-legislature thesis (Parliament may legislate “for” any territory; courts cannot strike down extra-territorial laws even if unrelated or detrimental to India).

Arguments — Attorney-General (for Parliament’s absolute sovereignty)
  • Parliament is a “sovereign legislature” with full power to make extra-territorial laws”; courts lack power to declare such laws invalid on the ground of extra-territorial effect — even where the law concerns aspects with no nexus with India, is not intended to benefit India, or may even be detrimental to India.
  • Art 245(2) as an independent source of power: Draft cl. 179 was split into 179(1)/(2) → if Parliament expressly may make laws having extra-territorial operation, it may make extra-territorial laws (“effect” being wider than “operation”).
  • Relied on: British Columbia Electric Ry. v. King, [1946] AC 527; Governor-General in Council v. Raleigh Investment Co.; Wallace Brothers v. CIT; A.H. Wadia v. CIT; Shrikant Karulkar Bhalchandra v. Gujarat; State of A.P. v. NTPC.
Arguments — the Court’s reasoning against those propositions (DU paras 20–127)
  • ECIL approved: “unless such nexus exists Parliament will have no competence … The provocation for the law must be found within India itself … It is inconceivable that a law should be made by Parliament which has no relationship with anything in India.” The distinction between making laws and operation of laws is valid and correctly relates clauses (1) and (2).
  • Text: “for” (preposition) = in the interest of/benefit of (Concise O.E.D.); legislating for a territory = acting as trustee of that people — “the Parliament belongs only to India”; two limitations: all powers must be exercised for the benefit of India; extra-territorial aspects without Indian nexus are outside Art 245(1). Art 51 (DPSG) gives interpretive support (peace, just relations = interests of India).
  • Structural: “make laws” (clause 1) — “made” + “extra-territorial operation” (clause 2) — “invalidate” (judiciary): clause (2) is only a one-ground exception to judicial review, not a power-grant; reading it as an independent source would make clause (1)’s “for … territory of India” surplusage — expressio unius est exclusio alterius. An a-priori, strained inference expanding legislative power beyond judicial scrutiny “would be contrary to the basic structure”.
  • Wider structural analysis: Art 246(4) speaks of power “for any part of the territory of India not included in a State” — legislative powers are visualised as “for” Indian territory; Art 260 is the only provision dealing with foreign territories; Arts 1(3)(c) + 2 + Re Berubari: acquired territory becomes part of India first, only then may Parliament legislate for it.
  • On quantum: no a priori quantitative tests (“sufficiency”, “significance”) may be imposed — “All that would be required would be that the connection to India be real or expected to be real, and not illusory or fanciful” (para 125); whether a law shows the connection is “a mixed matter of facts and of law” (para 126). If Parliament posits a higher degree of relationship, courts enforce that as a matter of that law, not the Constitution.
Decision (answers to the two questions)
  1. Q1: YES — Parliament is restricted: it may legislate about extra-territorial aspects only when they have, or are expected to have, some impact on/effect in/consequences for India, its parts, inhabitants or Indians. Laws on wholly unrelated foreign aspects = ultra vires (laws made “for” a foreign territory).
  2. Q2: NO — Parliament may not make laws “for” any territory other than India or part of it (unless part of India under Arts 1–2).
  3. The appeal was directed to be listed before an appropriate Bench for disposal (para 128) — i.e., the Constitution Bench answered the constitutional questions and remanded the tax litigation.
Principle

Art 245(1) confers power “for” India — with a built-in nexus/benefit limit: (1) Parliament may regulate genuinely extraterritorial aspects only if they have a real (not illusory/fanciful) connection with India, and the law’s core purpose must be benefit to India; (2) Art 245(2) removes only the “extraterritorial-operation” ground of attack from otherwise competent laws — it is not a freestanding competence; (3) make/operate/invalidate are distinct functions of legislature/executive/judiciary; (4) no quantitative sufficiency thresholds; mixed question of law and facts. For States, the same real-not-illusory standard appears as the first element of the Tata Iron–Charusila nexus test.

Source: DU case-book excerpt, pp. 258–270 (paras cited by number as in the excerpt; ECIL quoted at para 20 of the DU extract).

§3 Article 246 — Subject-matter of Laws & the Position of Union Territories item (b)

⚖ Article 246 (verbatim)

246. Subject-matter of laws made by Parliament and by the Legislatures of States.—(1) Notwithstanding anything in clauses (2) and (3), Parliament has exclusive power to make laws with respect to any of the matters enumerated in List I (Union List).

(2) Notwithstanding anything in clause (3), Parliament, and, subject to clause (1), the Legislature of any State also, have power to make laws with respect to any of the matters enumerated in List III (Concurrent List).

(3) Subject to clauses (1) and (2), the Legislature of any State has exclusive power to make laws for such State or any part thereof with respect to any of the matters enumerated in List II (State List).

(4) Parliament has power to make laws with respect to any matter for any part of the territory of India not included in a State notwithstanding that such matter is a matter enumerated in the State List.

🪜 The non-obstante ladder (how supremacy is built into syntax)

  • 246(1): “Notwithstanding … (2) and (3)” — federal supremacy for List I. Hoechst para 41: these words lay down the principle of federal supremacy: in case of inevitable conflict, Union power (List I) prevails over States (Lists II & III); overlapping Lists III/II → III prevails. But the non-obstante operates only if reconciliation proves impossible — “as a matter of last resort” (C.P. & Berar’s “witness to imperfections …” rule applied to Art 246(1) in Hoechst para 49).
  • 246(2): “Notwithstanding … (3)” + “subject to (1)” — both may legislate List III, State subject to Union.
  • 246(3): “Subject to (1) and (2)” — State’s exclusive field, carved down by the Union fields. Gujarat Univ.: the words “subject to” in a List II entry (there Item 11 “subject to Items 63–66 of List I and 25 of List III”) mean that to the extent of the restriction, power is taken away from the State (quoting Hingir Rampur) — even if Parliament never exercises its power.
  • Hoechst’s three essentials (para 38): ① List I exclusive to Parliament; ② List II exclusive to States subject to (1) — irreconcilable conflict → Parliament’s power supersedes pro tanto; ③ List III concurrent.

🧭 “Law with respect to” — the width of each field

  • Art 246 speaks of laws “with respect to” a matter — Mar Appraem Kuri headnote (C): this is distinct from a law merely “affecting” a subject-matter. “With respect to” covers the field in pith and substance, together with all ancillary/incidental matters fairly comprehended (G. Chawla: “the power to legislate on a topic of legislation carries with it the power to legislate on an ancillary matter which can be said to be reasonably included in the power given”).
  • Entries demarcate; Art 246 confers (Mar Appraem (A); Dhillon para 22; Hoechst para 74 — entries are “fields”, not powers; taxation is a distinct competence and cannot be deduced from a general entry as ancillary power).
  • Priority when lists “come in conflict” (Prafulla via Hoechst para 52): List I > List III > List II — but “the priority of the Federal legislature would not prevent the Provincial Legislature from dealing with any matter within List II though it may incidentally affect any item in List I.”

Position of Union Territories under Art 246

① Textual route — Art 246(4)

Parliament may legislate on any matter — including State-List matters — for any part of the territory of India “not included in a State”. Every UT (Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Lakshadweep, Chandigarh, Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, Puducherry, Delhi, J&K after 2019) is territory not included in a State — so Parliament’s power over UTs extends across all three lists. Historically this clause carried the Part C States of the First Schedule (pre-1956), governed with their elected assemblies by the Government of Part C States Act, 1951 — the very statute whose limits were in issue in G. Chawla (case 58): s. 21 let the Ajmer Assembly legislate on Lists II & III “Subject to the provisions of this Act”, while s. 21(2) preserved Parliament’s constitutional power — and the Supreme Court then applied Art 246(4)’s predecessor constitutional route.

② Legislature route — Arts 239A, 240

For Puducherry and Delhi, Art 239A creates a legislature “as if it were a State”, with Council of Ministers + responsible government — but Parliament retains plenary power: under Art 246(4) (not in a State) Parliament may also make laws on List II matters for the UT, and Art 240 lets Parliament/President make regulations for other UTs. Where both Parliament and the UT legislature may legislate, Parliament’s law prevails — the UT scheme is “subject to” Parliament (the Union-Territory relationship is not the quasi-federal State relationship of Art 246(3)).

Delhi-specific overlay: NCT of Delhi Act 1991 + Government of NCT of Delhi v. UOI (2018) 9 SCC 1 — “services” lie with the Union (Entry 41 read with Lists I/II), an Art 246-field question in itself.

③ Exam formula

“Position of UTs” = (i) UTs are not States → Art 246(3)’s exclusive State power does not apply to them; (ii) Parliament legislates for them under Art 246(4) over all lists; (iii) where a UT has a legislature under Art 239A, that legislature exercises State-like powers subject to Parliament’s overriding competence; (iv) President/Parliament regulate others via Art 240; (v) during President’s Rule in a UT, the Lt. Governor exercises that law-making role (Art 239AA(7)(b) for Delhi). Cite G. Chawla for the classic judicial application.

Do not say “UT legislatures derive List II power from Art 246(3)”. Art 246(3) is for States. The UT power flows from Art 239A read with Art 246(4) — and remains Parliament-overridable. Also remember Art 246A: GST is a special concurrent taxing power notwithstanding Arts 246 and 254 (101st Amendment).

§4 Interpretation of Legislative Lists — Plenary & Ancillary Power · Non Obstante · Harmonious Construction item (c)

💪 Plenary power

“After the dictum of Lord Selborne in Queen v. Burah [(1878) 3 App Cas 889], oft-quoted and applied, it must be held as settled that the legislatures in our Country possess plenary powers of legislation. This is so even after the division of legislative powers, subject to this that the supremacy of the legislatures is confined to the topics mentioned as Entries in the lists.” — G. Chawla, para 8 (DU p. 301). Within its entry (and ancillary matters), the legislature is sovereign: it chooses policy, breadth, retrospection, penalties; courts do not sit in appeal over expediency (C.P. & Berar: “it is not for the Court to express, or indeed to entertain, any opinion on the expediency”).

🧩 Ancillary / incidental power

“The power to legislate on a topic … carries with it the power to legislate on an ancillary matter which can be said to be reasonably included in the power given” (G. Chawla para 8; Hoechst paras 53, 62). DU’s own applications: education-power includes medium of instruction (Gujarat Univ.); money-lending power includes limiting promissory-note defences (Prafulla); entertainment-tax power includes car admission to a drive-in (Drive-in); compensation power includes taking estate-management buildings as appurtenances (Gajapati para 25). Subsidiary propositions: a general power shall not be read to nullify a particular power in the same enactment (Citizens Insurance / Bank of Toronto line, quoted in C.P. & Berar and Hoechst para 50); taxing power is not ancillary — it is a distinct head-group (Hoechst paras 74–76).

⚡ Non obstante clause

“Notwithstanding …” words make a provision override conflicting provisions — but only after reconciliation fails. Gwyer C.J. in C.P. & Berar: if the two powers can be read together, do so; “If indeed such a reconciliation should prove impossible, then, and only then, will the non obstante clause operate … for the clause ought to be regarded as a last resource, a witness to the imperfections of human expression and the fallibility of legal draftsmanship.” Non-obstante clauses in this topic: Art 246(1) & (2) (federal supremacy), Art 248, Art 249(1), Art 250(1), Art 253, Art 252? (no — 252 is consent-based), Entry-wise “notwithstanding” savings (e.g., G. Chawla’s s. 21(2)), ECA s. 6 (via Harishankar Bagla: “by-pass … does not necessarily amount to repeal”).

Doctrine of Harmonious Construction — statement

🧭 The four steps (C.P. & Berar as completed by Hoechst para 41 and Gujarat Univ. para 23)

  1. Where two entries seem to conflict, read both together, in their broad and liberal sense, without giving a narrow/restricted sense to either — first ask whether they can be reconciled to avoid a conflict of jurisdiction.
  2. Interpret or modify the language of one entry by the other — give to the Union entry a meaning “which, if less wide than it might in another context bear, is yet one that can properly be given to it”, and equally to the State entry a meaning it can properly bear; assign common territory to one or the other by construing one more restrictedly (e.g., excise = production-stage only; sales tax = thereafter).
  3. General power vs particular power: “a general power ought not to be so construed as to make a nullity of a particular power conferred by the same Act and operating in the same field” (Bank of Toronto v. Lambe; Citizens Insurance; approved C.P. & Berar & Hoechst).
  4. Only if reconciliation is impossible does the non-obstante/“subject to” clause make the Union power prevail (and in a truly irreconcilable overlap → Art 246(1) supremacy). Apparent overlap + pith-and-substance showing one list = no conflict at all.

Case 55 · In Re: C.P. & Berar Sales of Motor Spirit & Lubricants Taxation Act, 1938 — AIR 1939 FC 1 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 271–283

[Doctrine of Harmonious Construction] · Federal Court · Sir Maurice Gwyer C.J., Sir Shah Sulaiman and M.R. Jayakar JJ. · Opinion of Gwyer C.J. (whole Court) · Reference by the Governor-General u/s 213, Government of India Act, 1935
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 271–273)

The Central Provinces and Berar Sales of Motor Spirit and Lubricants Taxation Act, 1938 imposed by s. 3(1) a 5% tax on retail sales of motor spirit and lubricants (“retail dealer”, “retail sale” defined in s. 2). Motor spirit was already subject to central excise duty under the Motor Spirit (Duties) Act, 1917. The Special Reference asked: “Is the … Act, or any of the provisions thereof, … ultra vires the Legislature of the Central Provinces and Berar?” Competing entries under the GoIA 1935: Entry (45), Federal List — “Duties of excise on tobacco and other goods manufactured or produced in India” vs Entry (48), Provincial List — “Taxes on the sale of goods and on advertisements”. Government of India: the tax, so far as it falls on Indian-origin goods, is an excise duty → intrusion into the exclusively Federal field; and even if otherwise intra vires, the non obstante in s. 100(1) (“Notwithstanding anything in sub-ss (2) and (3)”) makes federal power prevail on overlap; s. 100(3) is itself “subject to the two preceding sub-sections”. Provincial Government: the tax falls within Entry 48, exclusively provincial; the entries are mutually exclusive — no overlap. Common ground: if s. 3(1) falls, the whole Act falls (it is the machinery of the charging section).

Issue

Whether a provincial retail sales tax on motor spirit/lubricants (Entry 48) is repugnant to/overlapping with the federal power of excise on Indian-manufactured goods (Entry 45) — and, if the entries are capable of overlapping, which legislature’s power prevails under the non obstante clauses.

Three possible solutions framed by Gwyer C.J.: (1) provincial entry covers, federal does not → tax valid; (2) federal covers, provincial does not → tax invalid; (3) tax falls in both → real overlap → invalid by reason of the non obstante clause.

Arguments — Government of India
  • An excise duty may be imposed at any stage from production to consumption (Blackstone onwards) → federal power reaches retail stage → Entry 45 catches the provincial levy; s. 100(1)’s “notwithstanding” decides any overlap for the Centre.
  • Absent provincial field: turnover/sales taxes since the war have made provinces rich; the Centre must protect Entry 45’s exclusivity; s. 297-type discrimination arguments also raised.
Arguments — Provincial Government
  • “Taxes on the sale of goods” in its ordinary and natural sense covers a retail sales tax exactly — “it might indeed be difficult to find a more exact or appropriate formula” (Advocate-General’s challenge: what would be left to provinces under the Centre’s reading? only “commonly known turnover taxes”).
  • Entries mutually exclusive; read both together; “sales tax”/“turnover tax” have no settled meaning, so Parliament’s chosen words must be given their plain import.
Decision (Gwyer C.J., for the whole Court)
  1. Constitutional canons first (DU pp. 272–273): a Constitution “is not to be construed in any narrow and pedantic sense” (Lord Wright in James v. Commonwealth); “it is a Constitution, a mechanism under which laws are to be made and not a mere Act which declares what the law is to be” (Higgins J., AG NSW v. Brewery Employees’ Union); “a broad and liberal spirit should inspire” the interpreter — but no stretching or perverting language to supply omissions; a Constitution is “a living and organic thing … construed ut res magis valeat quam pereat”; foreign federal decisions are persuasive only — “in the last analysis the decision must depend on the words of the Constitution which the Court is interpreting”.
  2. The reconciliation: it is impossible to read “taxes on the sale of goods” as only turnover taxes; so read “duties of excise” restrictively — as power to tax at the stage of or in connection with manufacture/production, extending no further. Result: mutually exclusive spheres, no overlap — Centre taxes before goods “become part of the general stock of the Province”; Province taxes thereafter.
  3. Only if reconciliation fails may the non obstante clause operate — “a last resource, a witness to the imperfections of human expression and the fallibility of legal draftsmanship”. Federal supremacy is not the first tool.
  4. Support: ordinary meaning of the provincial entry; Bank of Toronto v. Lambe (general power not construed to nullify a particular power; literal meaning of Dominion powers sometimes restricted “to afford scope for powers … given exclusively to the Provincial Legislatures”); Isaacs J. in Commonwealth Oil Refineries (a licence/tax merely on sale, unconnected with production, is outside “excise duties”); Indian legislative practice — all central excises (motor spirit, kerosene, silver, sugar, matches, iron & steel) were payable by the manufacturer/producer on issue from the place of manufacture; Devolution Rules 1920; the “it would exclude provinces from an immense field” reductio; White Paper (Cmd. 4268) & Joint Select Committee history. Rejected: the “provincial autonomy requires every intendment in our favour” argument — the Court will not weigh Centre vs Province importance.
  5. Answer: the C.P. & Berar Act is NOT ultra vires — reported to the Governor-General accordingly (whole Court agreeing).
Principle

The doctrine of harmonious construction: where two legislative entries (or two powers) apparently conflict, the Court must (i) read them together, (ii) construe/modify the language of each by the other so as to allocate mutually exclusive fields, and (iii) apply the non obstante/supremacy clause only as a last resource when reconciliation is impossible. “It is a fundamental assumption that the legislative powers of the Centre and Provinces could not have been intended to be in conflict with one another, and therefore we must read them together and interpret or modify the language in which one is expressed by the language of the other.” — reaffirmed word-for-word by the Supreme Court in Hoechst para 45.

Case 56 · Gujarat University v. Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar — AIR 1963 SC 703 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 284–292

Bench: B.P. Sinha C.J. and S.J. Imam, K. Subba Rao, K.N. Wanchoo, J.C. Shah, N. Rajagopala Ayyangar JJ. (judgment by J.C. Shah J.) · DU note: “Entry 11, List II referred to in this case is presently entry 25 of List III” (as to vocational training) — the education entries are List II Entry 11 / List I Entries 63–66 / List III Entry 25
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 284–287)

Shrikant (son of Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar) passed the SSC (March 1960) studying in Marathi; joined St. Xavier’s College, Ahmedabad (University of Gujarat) First Year Arts in the English-medium section; on completing I.A. (March 1961) sought admission to Intermediate Arts classes in the English medium. The Principal refused without University sanction, citing the Gujarat University Act, 1949 and Statutes 207, 208, 209 (framed by the Senate in 1954, amended 1961): Statute 207 made Gujarati the medium of instruction and examination (English continuing ≤ 10 years, Hindi optional for non-Gujaratis); Statute 208 phased English out from June 1955/1956 course-wise; 209 listed examinations. The Registrar’s circular (22-6-1961) allowed English only to certain students; Shrikant (SSC in Marathi) did not qualify. The Gujarat University (Amendment) Act 4 of 1961 extended English beyond ten years (proviso to s. 4(27)) and inserted s. 38-A (penalty — affiliation withdrawn for contravention of media-of-instruction rules).

Krishna Mudholkar, for himself and minor Shrikant, moved the Gujarat High Court: declare ss. 4(27), 18(i)(xiv), 38-A of the 1949 Act and Statutes 207–209 void/inoperative; restrain the University/Vice-Chancellor; mandamus to admit Shrikant to the English-medium I.A. class. High Court issued the writs; University and State appealed to the SC on fitness certificates.

Issues (DU p. 284)
  1. Under the Gujarat University Act, 1949 (as amended), is it open to the University to prescribe Gujarati or Hindi or both as the exclusive medium of instruction and examination in affiliated colleges?
  2. Does legislation authorising the University to impose such media infringe Entry 66, List I — “coordination and determination of standards in institutions for higher education or research and scientific and technical institutions”?
Arguments — University / State of Gujarat
  • Power expressly conferred by diverse clauses of s. 4 (esp. s. 4(27)) exercised by the Senate u/s 18(xiv); Statutes 207–209 lawfully promulgated; in any event, a corporation controlling higher education has such power necessarily implied.
  • Medium of instruction falls within Item 11, List II (“education including Universities …”) — a State subject; “education” is of wide import; the State may prescribe regional/federal language media; Statement of Objects & Reasons (empower University to adopt Gujarati, English for ten years) shows legislative intent.
  • Even if Entry 66 overlaps, Union power arises only when it actually coordinates standards; no Union legislation on the point.
Arguments — Mudholkar (respondent)
  • No provision authorises an exclusive medium — s. 4(27) and the Senate’s power do not carry it; St. Xavier’s had English as its historic medium; students’ rights under Arts 19(1)(g)/29?
  • Entry 66, List I covers coordination/determination of higher-education standards — medium of instruction in universities directly bears on standards; making an exclusive regional medium compulsory in affiliated colleges impinges the Union field; Item 11 List II is expressly “subject to” Entries 63–66 List I.
Decision (Shah J.)
  1. No power to impose an exclusive medium. Neither the 1949 Act nor Act 4 of 1961 conferred on the University power to prescribe Gujarati/Hindi as exclusive media; the Senate’s statute-making must lie within the contours of the University’s statutory powers. Statements of Objects and Reasons are historical material only — ignored in interpretation (para 18). So far, HC correct.
  2. Legislative-entry analysis (the heart for item (c)):
    • Pre-Constitution provincial power under GoIA Item 17 List II was plenary over education; under the Constitution, Item 11 List II gives States “education including Universities subject to Items 63, 64, 65, 66 of List I and 25 of List III”.
    • “Subject to” = power taken away to the extent of the restriction (Hingir Rampur) — whether or not Parliament has legislated. Items 63–66 are carved out of education; on those matters power lies exclusively with Parliament.
    • Harmonious construction of Item 11 (II) and Item 66 (I): “The two entries undoubtedly overlap: but to the extent of overlapping, the power conferred by Item 66 List I must prevail over the power of the State under Item 11 List II.” A contrary reading would produce the “startling result” that the State controls medium of instruction even in national institutions on which it has no other power.
    • Medium of instruction is not a distinct head — it “falls within Item 11 as a necessary incident of the power to legislate on education; it also falls within Items 63 to 66”; as an incident of Entry 66 it is deemed included there and excluded from Item 11 List II.
    • State may prescribe syllabi/courses + medium as an incident — but Union has an overriding power where medium/syllabi “impair standards … or render coordination … impossible or even difficult”; validity judged by pith and substance — whether the State law “impinges on the field reserved for the Union under Entry 66”, “but not upon the existence of some definite Union legislation”. If Union legislates → Art 254(1) paramountcy; even without Union legislation, a State law trenching on the Union field is invalid (exclusive lists).
    • Entry 66 is wide: “coordination” = harmonising for concerted action, not mere evaluation; power is absolute and unconditional, includes preventing/removing disparities; not conditioned on an existing emergency of standards. Where absence of textbooks/journal/teachers/incapacity to understand in the mandated medium would lower standards, such legislation “necessarily falls within Item 66” (paras 25–26).
    • Act 4 of 1961 (extending the traditional English medium beyond ten years) is not invalid — continuing the pre-existing all-India medium cannot lower standards; no Entry 66 encroachment (para 29).
    • Result: HC’s order confirming invalidity of Statutes 207 & 209 (insofar as they impose Gujarati/Hindi as exclusive medium) and the enforcing circulars — CONFIRMED; appeal of University/State to that extent fails; Act 4/1961 amendment upheld.
Principle

(1) Harmonious construction of “subject to” entries: where a List II entry is expressly “subject to” List I entries, the overlap belongs to Parliament — the State power is restricted to the residue; the entries must be read together, with the Union entry prevailing to the extent of the overlap (no non-obstante needed). (2) Ancillary matters: medium of instruction is not a freestanding entry — it travels with whichever head the impugned law’s pith and substance touches (education = State; standards coordination = Union). (3) The Union’s field is not dormant: non-legislation by Parliament does not enlarge State power; State law is tested for real impingement on Entry 66, not on the existence of a Union statute. (4) Interpretive canons: Objects & Reasons excluded; plenary power within a head; “statutes stand or fall with the section containing the essence of the legislation”.

§5 Doctrine of Pith and Substance item (c) core

⚖ Statement of the doctrine — Gwyer C.J. in Subramanyam Chettiar (1940 FCR 188, 201), approved by the Privy Council in Prafulla Kumar and quoted verbatim in DU’s G. Chawla (p. 301) and Hoechst (pp. 350–351)

“It must inevitably happen from time to time that legislation, though purporting to deal with a subject in one list, touches also upon a subject in another list, and the different provisions of the enactment may be so closely intertwined that blind adherence to a strictly verbal interpretation would result in a large number of statutes being declared invalid because the Legislature enacting them may appear to have legislated in a forbidden sphere. Hence the rule which has been evolved by the Judicial Committee whereby the impugned statute is examined to ascertain its ‘pith and substance’ or its ‘true nature and character’, for the purpose of determining whether it is legislation with respect to matters in this list or in that.”

🔬 The test in operation

  1. Ignore the label/preamble/nomenclature. Examine the whole enactment — its object, scope and effect (Drive-in para 6; Gajapati: “the substance of the Act that is material and not merely the form”).
  2. Ask: what is the law’s pith and substance — its true nature and character? Attribute it to the list where that subject really lies (Prafulla’s three questions).
  3. If substantially within its own list → VALID, even if it incidentally trenches on a rival list (Prafulla para 43: the Act “is not void either in whole or in part”; Hoechst para 53: incidental encroachment does not affect competence).
  4. Degree of invasion matters only as evidence of true character: “the question is not, has it trespassed more or less, but is the trespass … such as to show that the pith and substance … is not money-lending but promissory notes or banking?” (Prafulla para 38, Lord Porter — quoted in Hoechst para 52).
  5. If truly outside its lists → void (entirely, or the offending part if severable — “to the extent of the repugnancy” logic).

🗺 Where it plugs into the scheme

  • Threshold gate for legislative competence — asked before repugnancy: “No question of conflict between the two Lists will arise if the impugned legislation, by the application of the doctrine of ‘pith and substance’, appears to fall exclusively under one list” (Hoechst para 41).
  • Also governs the repugnancy enquiry itself when the two laws are under different entries — Vijay Kr Sharma (64): “Not to apply the theory of pith and substance when the repugnancy between the two statutes is to be considered under Art 254 … would be illogical.”
  • Three questions from Prafulla (para 30): ① Does the Act deal in pith and substance with X? ② If so, is it valid though it incidentally trenches on reserved matters? ③ Once pith and substance is determined, is the extent of invasion material? (Answers: yes; yes; only as evidence of true character.)
  • Why three lists don’t break it (Prafulla paras 34–37): “It is not possible to make so clean a cut between the powers of the various legislatures: they are bound to overlap from time to time … much beneficent legislation would be stifled at birth.” The hierarchy (I > III > II on conflict) answers “priority in what respect” — not incidentally affecting matters, but true character attribution.
Mnemonic — the P&S syllogism: “Name ≠ nature; substance governs; incidental trenching is forgiven; degree is evidence, not a test; one list only must win — ask which list the Act, in substance, belongs to.”

Case 57 · Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee v. Bank of Commerce Ltd., Khulna — AIR 1947 PC 60 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 293–299

[Doctrine of Pith and Substance] · Judicial Committee of the Privy Council: Lord Wright, Lord Porter, Lord Uthwatt, Sir Madhavan Nair, Sir John Beaumont (judgment by Lord Porter)
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 293–294)

The Bengal Money-lenders Act, 1940 was challenged. Its s. 30 provided: “Notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force, or any agreement … (1) No borrower shall be liable to pay after the commencement of this Act … more than a limited sum in respect of principal and interest or more than a certain percentage of the sum advanced by way of interest” — retrospective, usable as a defence to a moneylender’s suit or by the borrower in a fresh suit. Relevant GoIA 1935 powers: s. 100(1)–(4) (quoted in DU — Federal List prevails “notwithstanding”; Provincial List “subject to”); Federal List: Entry 28 (cheques, bills of exchange, promissory notes …), Entry 33 (corporations incl. banking), Entry 38 (banking — conduct of banking business); Provincial List: Entry 27 (trade and commerce within the Province; money-lending and money-lenders). [DU cross-reference: Entries 28/33/38 List I ≡ now Entries 46/43/45 List I; Provincial Entry 27 ≡ now Entry 30 List II.] The respondents (moneylenders-bankers) argued the loans were secured by promissory notes and involved banking — exclusively federal fields — so the Act was wholly void (or void as to such loans). The Act had the assent of the Governor-General; the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (an “existing Indian law”, not a federal statute) was the alleged repugnant law. The Indian High Courts had upheld the Act; the Federal Court (1944 FCR 126) had held it “a clear interference” with federal entries insofar as it dealt with them, but not totally void; the Privy Council now decided the appeal.

The three questions (DU para 30)
  1. Does the Act deal, in pith and substance, with money-lending?
  2. If it does, is it valid though it incidentally trenches upon matters reserved for the Federal Legislature?
  3. Once pith and substance is money-lending, is the extent to which the federal field is invaded a material matter?
Arguments — appellants (upholding the Bengal Act)
  • Head 1: power to legislate on money-lending “necessarily imports the power to affect the lender’s rights against the borrower upon a promissory note given in the course of a money-lending transaction”; read the Constitution as a whole — Item 27 (Particular/Provincial) is an exception from the general Item 28 (Federal).
  • Head 2 (the pith-and-substance head): the Act is in pith and substance an Act with respect to money-lenders and money-lending and is not rendered void in whole or part because it incidentally touches matters outside the authorised field.
  • Head 3: on true construction, federal Entry 28 covers only the negotiability aspect of instruments, not their contractual aspect — that is “contract” (List III Entry 10); interest on promissory notes = contract. Hence no true conflict with the Negotiable Instruments Act; and by s. 107(2) (assent of Governor-General), the Bengal Act prevails over the existing Indian law (Negotiable Instruments Act).
Arguments — respondents (striking the Act down)
  • The GoIA differs from BNA/Australian Acts: three exhaustive lists with a strict hierarchy — s. 100: Federal List over Concurrent over Provincial; “the Provincial Legislature has not power” re List I — however incidental the interference. “No question could arise as to pith and substance”; the prohibition is absolute.
  • No “exception by particularity”: a matter in List I cannot be whittled by a more particular List II item; “money-lending” is no more particular than “bills of exchange, promissory notes…”; if money-lending = contract, still the federal existing law prevails (subject to s. 107(2) argument).
Decision (Lord Porter, for the Board)
  1. Q1 — YES. “All the Courts in India have held that the transactions … are in pith and substance money-lending transactions and their Lordships are of the same opinion. To take promissory note as security for a loan is the common practice of money-lenders; if a legislature cannot limit the liability of a borrower in respect of a promissory note … it cannot in any real sense deal with money-lending. All the lender would have to do … would be to continue his normal practice of taking the security of a promissory note … In truth … the substance is money-lending and the promissory note is but the instrument for securing the loan.” Banking similarly answered — supported by the Act’s exemption of scheduled/notified banks.
  2. Q2 — YES, valid. The respondents’ “strict hierarchy / no pith-and-substance” argument rejected: “To take such a view is to simplify unduly the task of distinguishing between the powers of divided jurisdictions. It is not possible to make so clean a cut between the powers of the various legislatures: they are bound to overlap from time to time.” The British Parliament knew the BNA/Australian experience — overlap is unavoidable; Gwyer C.J.’s passage (1940 FCR 188, 201) is quoted with approval as describing the rule’s foundation; it applies to Indian as well as Dominion legislation. “The overlapping of subject-matter is not avoided by substituting three lists for two, or even by arranging for a hierarchy of jurisdictions. Subjects must still overlap, and where they do the question must be asked what in pith and substance is the effect of the enactment … If these questions could not be asked, much beneficent legislation would be stifled at birth.”
  3. Q3 — extent is evidential only. Invasion degree matters “not … because the validity of an Act can be determined by discriminating between degrees of invasion, but for the purpose of determining what is the pith and substance” — “the question is not, has it trespassed more or less, but is the trespass … such as to show that the pith and substance … is not money-lending but promissory notes or banking? Once that question is determined the Act falls on one or the other side of the line.”
  4. Priority of the lists re-explained: List I > III > II “where they come in conflict” — but priority means not to prevent the Province dealing with List II matters that incidentally affect List I; attribute each Act by true character.
  5. Result: the Bengal Money-lenders Act “is not void either in whole or in part as being ultra vires the Provincial Legislature”; appeal allowed; no opinion on the negotiability-vs-contract point or Ordinance XI of 1945. (No need to decide whether federal “promissory notes” is confined to negotiability — the Act is valid because its pith and substance is money-lending.)
Principle

The foundational statement of pith and substance: (1) In a federation of enumerated lists, overlap is inevitable; the impugned statute is examined for its pith and substance / true nature and character to decide the list it belongs to. (2) A statute substantially within the enacting legislature’s field is valid despite incidental trenching on a reserved field. (3) The extent of trespass is relevant only to detect when the true character has actually changed. (4) The list-hierarchy decides conflicts, not incidental effects. (5) Reading a particular entry as an exception from a general rival entry is disfavoured — reconcile by substance instead.

Case 58 · State of Rajasthan v. G. Chawla — AIR 1959 SC 544 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 300–303

Bench: S.R. Das C.J. and S.K. Das, P.B. Gajendragadkar, K.N. Wanchoo, M. Hidayatullah JJ. (judgment by M. Hidayatullah J.) · Loudspeaker case — pith & substance in action
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 300–301)

The Ajmer Legislative Assembly enacted the Ajmer (Sound Amplifiers Control) Act, 1952 (Ajmer III of 1953), assented to by the President on 9-3-1953. After reorganisation, the State of Rajasthan was substituted as appellant. The respondents were prosecuted under s. 3 for breaching two permit conditions for using sound amplifiers on 15–16 May 1954: amplifiers audible beyond 30 yards (condition 1) and placed higher than 6 feet (condition 2) — they had been operating amplifiers for a Sammelan under permission. On a reference u/s 432 CrPC, the Judicial Commissioner of Ajmer held: the pith and substance of the Act fell within Entry 31, Union List (post and telegraphs; telephones, wireless, broadcasting and other like forms of communication), not Entry 6, State List (public health and sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries) as the State claimed; and the Act exceeded the Assembly’s powers u/s 21 of the Government of Part C States Act, 1951 — hence unconstitutional (certificate under Art 132 → SC appeal). The JC’s attention had not been drawn to Entry 1, State List — “Public order …”.

Statutory framework quoted in DU: Art 246(4) (then: Parliament may make laws for any part of India not included in Part A or B of the First Schedule notwithstanding State-List matters) and s. 21, Govt of Part C States Act: “(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the Legislative Assembly of a State may undertake laws for the whole or any part of the State with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the State List or the Concurrent List …; (2) Nothing in sub-section (1) shall derogate from the power conferred on Parliament …”. Competence of the Ajmer Assembly was therefore confined to the two non-Union lists: “If … the subject-matter of the Act falls substantially within an Entry in the Union list, the Act must be declared to be unconstitutional, but it is otherwise if it falls substantially within the other two lists, since prima facie there is no question of repugnancy to a Central statute or of an ‘occupied field’.”

Issue

In pith and substance, is the Ajmer (Sound Amplifiers Control) Act a law with respect to Union Entry 31 (broadcasting/communication) or with respect to State Entry 6 (public health), read with Entry 1 (public order) — and is the Act within the State legislature’s competence?

Arguments — State of Rajasthan (appellant)
  • The Act’s end and purpose is control of the use of amplifiers in the interests of health and tranquillity — squarely Entry 6 (and Entry 1) of the State List; amplifiers are used as nuisance-producing instruments (noise near hospitals, schools, offices, habitations), which public health demands regulating.
  • Plenary power (Queen v. Burah); entries are enumeratio simplex of broad categories which occasionally overlap; pith-and-substance test applies with the ancillary-power corollary; slight transgression upon a rival list is permissible.
  • Union counsel’s rival reading (amplifiers = instruments of broadcasting → Entry 31) confuses the thing with the use: manufacture/licensing of amplifiers, control of ownership/possession, regulation of trade in them = Entry 31 matters; controlling their use to the detriment of tranquillity, health and comfort = Entry 6 matter.
  • Relied on Umrigar for Entry 1 (alone or combined with Entry 6). No Union enactment occupies Entry 31’s field as to ownership/use-regulation — so no repugnancy, no “occupied field”.
Arguments — against (the Judicial Commissioner’s view, which the appeal reverses)
  • Amplifiers are “instruments of broadcasting and even of communication”; the Act regulates them; therefore its pith and substance lies in Union Entry 31 — the State legislature (confined to Lists II/III by s. 21 + Art 246(4) structure) had no power; Act unconstitutional.
Decision
  1. Plenary power settled: citing Lord Selborne in Queen v. Burah — legislatures possess plenary powers even after distribution, confined to their entries; entries “though meant to be mutually exclusive are sometimes not really so. They occasionally overlap, and are to be regarded as enumeratio simplex of broad categories.” Pith-and-substance + slight-transgression rule (Gwyer C.J. in Chettiar, approved in Prafulla) laid down in terms.
  2. Ancillary-power doctrine stated: “the power to legislate on a topic of legislation carries with it the power to legislate on an ancillary matter which can be said to be reasonably included in the power given.”
  3. Pith and substance = State: the gist of the prohibition is the “use” of an external sound amplifier (not a licensed wireless component) without written sanction/conditions — “The pith and substance of the impugned Act is the control of the use of amplifiers in the interests of health and also tranquillity, and thus falls substantially (if not wholly) within the powers conferred to preserve, regulate and promote them and does not so fall within the entry in the Union list, even though the amplifier … is an apparatus for broadcasting or communication.”
  4. Use vs thing distinction: manufacture/licensing/ownership/possession/trade in amplifiers (Union) ≠ control of use causing nuisance (State: Entry 6, conceivably Entry 1). “The end and purpose of the legislation furnishes the key to connect it with the State List.” Not an argument that leaving other loud noises uncontrolled defeats the Act.
  5. No occupied field: no Union enactment under Entry 31 burdened ownership/possession of amplifiers with such regulation — “no question of repugnancy or of an occupied field”.
  6. Result: JC’s order set aside; appeal allowed; the Act declared intra vires in all its parts; no retrial (matter 4 years old; State stated it did not propose to prosecute).
Principle

Pith and substance decides lists by legislative purpose, not by the instrument regulated: a law about an object that belongs to a Union field is nevertheless a State law when its true nature is regulating use for a State-List purpose (health/tranquillity/public order). Plus three standing rules: (i) plenary power within entries (Burah); (ii) entries = broad categories, occasionally overlapping (enumeratio simplex); (iii) ancillary matters ride with the head; (iv) absence of a covering Central law means no repugnancy/occupied-field objection. This case is the model answer to any “loudspeaker/amplifier vs broadcasting entry” problem.

Source: DU case-book excerpt, pp. 300–303.

Case 59 · State of Karnataka v. Drive-in Enterprises — (2001) 4 SCC 60 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 304–308

Bench: V.N. Khare and Ruma Pal JJ. (judgment by V.N. Khare J.)
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 304–305)

The respondent owned a drive-in theatre on the outskirts of Bangalore — open-air premises where viewers watch films sitting in motor cars (plus a small auditorium for others paying ₹3). “Drive-in-cinema” defined by Rule 111-A, Karnataka Cinemas (Regulation) Rules, 1971 (framed u/s 19 of the Karnataka Cinemas (Regulation) Act, 1964; capacity ≤ 1,000 cars). Under the Karnataka Entertainments Tax Act, 1958: s. 2(a) “admission”; s. 2(e) “entertainment” (incl. cinematography shows); s. 2(i) “payment for admission”; s. 3 = charging section — 70% entertainment tax on each payment for admission; s. 6 mode of payment. A car-owner paid the proprietor an extra ₹2 to take his car inside. The State levied entertainment tax on the admission of cars — challenged; the Single Judge and then the Division Bench of the Karnataka High Court struck the levy down as “not on a person entertained (i.e. car/motor vehicle)” → ultra vires. The Legislature then amended the Act by Act 3 of 1985, inserting s. 2(i)(v): “any payment for admission of a motor vehicle into the auditorium of a cinema known as drive-in-theatre”, with consequent amendments to ss. 4-A and 6. The re-challenged levy was again struck down by the High Court (writ u/Art 226) — sub-clause (v) held beyond State legislative competence. State’s appeal to the SC.

Issue (DU para 5)

Is the State Legislature competent — under Entry 62, List II (“taxes on luxuries and entertainments, amusements, betting and gambling”) — to levy entertainment tax on the admission of cars/motor vehicles into a drive-in theatre?

Arguments — State of Karnataka (appellant)
  • In pith and substance the levy is on the person entertained, not on admission of cars; after the 1985 amendment (s. 2(i)(v) + ss. 4-A, 6) the charge is squarely on “payment for admission” to entertainment; the State is “fully competent to impose such a levy”.
Arguments — Drive-in Enterprises (respondent)
  • Drive-in is a distinct category of cinema; the car’s entry is incidental and part of the concept of a drive-in; the film is the same as in any theatre.
  • The incidence of an “entertainment tax” is on entertainment, i.e., on persons entertained — State Legislatures can levy such tax on human beings and not on any inanimate object; a car is not a person entertained → levy beyond competence (Entry 62).
Decision
  1. Test stated (para 6): where vires are challenged, the Court ascertains the true nature and character of the enactment — examine the whole enactment, its object, scope and effect; if it substantially falls on a matter assigned to the legislature, it is valid even though the nomenclature suggests otherwise. “The nomenclature of a levy is not conclusive for determining its true character … the real nature of levy [is what matters] … its pith and substance.”
  2. Authority-spray (DU paras 6–10): Goodyear India v. Haryana (nomenclature of Act not conclusive); R.R. Engineering v. Zila Parishad, Bareilly (“the measure of the tax is not a true test of the nature of the tax” — income as yardstick ≠ income tax); Kerala SEB v. Indian Aluminium (“If in pith and substance a legislation falls within one list … some portion … incidentally trenches upon … another list, the Act as a whole would be valid”); Governor-General in Council v. Province of Madras (AIR 1945 PC 98) — “its real nature, its ‘pith and substance’ … a tax on the sale of goods”; (Morris) Leventhal v. David Jones (bridge tax upheld as land tax); Raza Buland Sugar v. Municipal Board, Rampur (water tax = levy on land and building).
  3. Application: Entry 62 covers taxes on luxuries/entertainment; incidence on entertainment, and “entertainment necessarily implies the persons entertained”. But the car does not go in by itself — “It is driven inside the theatre by the person entertained … the person entertained carries his car inside … in order to have better quality of entertainment”; the levy varies with the comfort/luxury enjoyed (car-sitter vs squatter). Real nature: a levy on the person entertained — on entertainment, not on the vehicle. “Whatever be the nomenclature of levy, in substance, the levy under heading ‘admission of vehicle’ is a levy on entertainment … ‘entertainment’ is wide enough to comprehend … the luxury or comfort with which a person entertains himself. Once … there is a nexus between the legislative competence and subject of taxation, the levy is justified and valid.”
  4. Result: s. 2(i)(v) within Entry 62; appeal allowed — HC judgment set aside; writ petition dismissed; levy valid.
Principle

Nomenclature is not the test — pith and substance is: the true nature of a levy, not its label (“admission of vehicles”) or its measure (cars), decides competence; a tax valid in substance under its entry survives any misleading caption, and measures/yardsticks do not change the tax’s character (Drive-in; R.R. Engineering; Goodyear). Contrast with colourable legislation (60): there the label is a disguise for a subject outside competence — here the label was merely inaccurate, the substance inside competence. Also illustrates ancillary reasoning: car-admission is an inseparable incident of drive-in entertainment.

§6 Doctrine of Colourable Exercise of Legislative Power item (c) core

⚖ The doctrine — in the DU casebook’s own words (Gajapati paras 9–12, pp. 313–315)

  • “The doctrine of colourable legislation does not involve any question of bona fides or mala fides on the part of the legislature. The whole doctrine resolves itself into the question of competency of a particular legislature to enact a particular law.”
  • “Whether a statute is constitutional or not is thus always a question of power” (Cooley’s Constitutional Limitations, Vol 1, p. 379). With a limited/qualified jurisdiction, transgression of constitutional limits “may be patent, manifest or direct, [but] it may also be disguised, covert and indirect — it is to this latter class … that the expression ‘colourable legislation’ has been applied.”
  • “Although apparently a legislature … purported to act within the limits of its powers, yet in substance and in reality it transgressed these powers, the transgression being veiled by what appears, on proper examination, to be a mere pretence or disguise.” (Duff J. in AG for Ontario v. Reciprocal Insurers [1924 AC 328, 337]: “Where the law making authority is of a limited or qualified character it may be necessary to examine with some strictness the substance of the legislation …”)
  • “It is the substance of the Act that is material and not merely the form … the form in which the law is clothed would not save it from condemnation. The legislature cannot violate the constitutional prohibitions by employing an indirect method.” Courts may examine effect/object/purpose/design — “but these are only relevant for the purpose of ascertaining the true character and substance … and not for finding out the motives.”
  • Lefroy: even if the legislature avows on the face of the Act an intent to legislate on a subject beyond its jurisdiction, “yet if the enacting clauses … bring the legislation within its powers, the Act cannot be considered ultra vires” (Canadian Constitution, p. 75).
  • The maxim (para 18): the doctrine is based on “you cannot do indirectly what you cannot do directly.”

🧭 When (and when not) a law is colourable

✗ COLOURABLE (invalid)✓ NOT COLOURABLE (valid)

Comparative anchors quoted in Gajapati: AG Alberta v. Pith and substance vs colourable — do not merge them. P&S asks: “Which list does this law really belong to?” (a labelling/attribution exercise; incidental trespass forgiven). Colourable asks: “Is this law a pretence for something the legislature may not do at all?” (a competency exercise; nothing forgives a real-object-ultra-vires). A law can fail both ways; a law with an ugly motive but a competent real object fails neither (Gajapati’s Orissa agricultural income-tax).

Case 60 · K.C. Gajapati Narayan Deo v. State of Orissa — AIR 1953 SC 375 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 309–323

Bench: M. Patanjali Sastri C.J. and B.K. Mukherjea, S.R. Das, Ghulam Hasan, N.H. Bhagwati JJ. (judgment by B.K. Mukherjea J.) · Six appeals from Art 226 petitions against the Orissa Estates Abolition Act, 1952
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 309–313)

Six appeals by permanently-settled estate proprietors in Orissa against the Orissa Estates Abolition Act, 1952 (introduced 17-1-1950, passed 28-9-1951, President’s assent 23-1-1952 → protected by Arts 31(4)/31(A), though not in the Ninth Schedule). The Act follows the Bihar/UP/M.P. pattern: abolish zamindari/intermediary interests (s. 2(g) “estate”, “intermediary” definitions; s. 3 vesting notification; s. 4 surrender agreements; s. 5 consequences — lands, forests, tanks, fisheries, bazars etc. vest absolutely in the State free of incumbrances; s. 6–7 homestead/agricultural retention), with compensation = a number of years’ purchase of net annual income — gross asset (rents+cesses) minus deductions (land revenue, agricultural income-tax, chowkidari/municipal taxes on estate buildings, management costs, other income-tax) payable in 30 annual instalments (s. 37), State option to pay lump sum.

The colourable attack centred on the Orissa Agricultural Income Tax (Second Amendment) Act, 1950: the original amendment bill (sponsored by Chief Minister H.K. Mahtab, who favoured “suitable compensation”) was published 8-1-1950 — five days after the Estates Abolition Bill (3-1-1950) — raising the top rate from 3 annas to 4 annas in the rupee and the top slab from ₹30,000 to ₹20,000; it was dropped, then a revised bill (22-7-1950, under Mahtab’s successor, “a champion of abolition … with little or no compensation”) became law on 10-8-1950 with the top rate at 12 annas 6 pies in the rupee for slabs above ₹15,000 — ostensibly to finance development schemes. Since s. 27(1)(b) of the Abolition Act makes agricultural income-tax deductible in computing net income (hence compensation), appellants said the tax law was a device to deflate compensation — a fraud on the Constitution, relying on the majority in State of Bihar v. Maharaja Kameshwar Singh [1952 SCR 889] (Bihar Land Reforms Act ss. 4(b), 23(f) held colourable/unconstitutional). Other heads: Madras Estates Land (Orissa Amendment) Act 1947 (ss. 168-A, 177(2) rent-settlement/retrospection — improper delegation/Art 14 — left open with consent); vesting of buildings (appurtenances) and private lands; s. 37 instalments (attack as colourable). At the High Court, Narasimham J. separately recorded suspicion that the 1950 amending Act was a colourable device, yet concurred in dismissal — “whenever there is any doubt regarding the constitutionality of an enactment, the doubt should always go in favour of the legislature”. Certificates under Arts 132/133 + s. 110 CPC → appeals.

Issues
  1. Is the Orissa Agricultural Income Tax (Amendment) Act, 1950 a colourable piece of legislation — ostensibly taxation (Entry 46, List II), in reality a device to reduce compensation (Entry 42, List III) — and therefore void (in whole or as affecting the abolition scheme)?
  2. Was the Court entitled to investigate the motives (synchronisation with the ministry change, the statement of objects) in asking that question?
  3. Are the buildings/private lands vesting and s. 37 mode of payment of compensation valid (and is inadequacy of compensation justiciable given Arts 31(4)/31A)?
Arguments — appellants (estate owners)
  • The 1950 Act “is not a bona fide taxation statute at all, but is a colourable piece of legislation, the real object of which is to reduce, by artificial means, the net income of the intermediaries” — the objects-reason claim (development finance) “wholly untrue” since those principally hit were the intermediaries whose class was about to be abolished; synchronised with the change of Chief Ministry; separate statute vs embedding in the Abolition Act “should not make any difference in principle”; Kameshwar Singh is the sheet-anchor (two Bihar provisions struck as colourable under Entry 42 + Art 31(1) “without authority of law”).
  • Buildings need not be “parts of the land” under Indian law; private lands of temporary tenants vest with no compensation for the tenant-interest; s. 37’s 30 instalments negate compensation.
Arguments — State / Attorney-General
  • The amendment is plainly agricultural income-tax legislation within Entry 46, List II — a head the State undeniably possessed; “unjust or inequitable” rates do not affect competence; agricultural income-tax is a pre-existing, relevant deduction (collected under the amended Act in the following year), unlike the Bihar “non-existent” items.
  • Motive irrelevant: if the ulterior end lies within the legislature’s power, no colourable exercise; the attack on the 1947 rent-amendment machinery (delegation/Art 14) is not a ground against the Abolition Act — objections can be raised when gross assets are computed; both sides agreed to leave them open.
  • Buildings = appurtenances of the estate → ancillary to acquisition, within Art 31(2)/(4); s. 37 = “form and manner” under Entry 42 squarely; inadequacy barred by Art 31(4) (“passed by such Legislature” includes passage with amendments — the Bill was pending at commencement and duly assented).
Decision
  1. Doctrine expounded (para 9) and applied: competency not motive; substance over form; disguise may be “covert and indirect”. But the doctrine’s premise is a power that lacks: “it would be a colourable legislation only if it is shown that the real object is not attainable to it by reason of any constitutional limitation or that it lies within the exclusive field of another legislature.”
  2. 1950 Amendment NOT colourable: it is agricultural income-tax legislation in form and substance under Entry 46 List II; rates may be unjust/inequitable — “but that does not affect the competency of the legislature”; unlike the Bihar case, not “based on something which was unrelated to facts and did not exist at all” — the tax was an existing liability when the 1952 Act commenced. Assuming an intent to inflate deductions: since Entry 42 permits any principle of compensation (restrictions only via Art 31(2)/(4)), inflated/deflated deductions “do not affect the constitutionality”; the High Court judge pursuing “what actually the motives were” followed “a wrong line of enquiry”.
  3. Kameshwar Singh distinguished: there ss. 23(f)/4(b) purported to be compensation “principles” while resting on fictitious items wholly unrelated to facts (no definable pre-existing liability for raiyat-benefit works; taking half of arrears “taking the half without any return”) — a colourable use of Entry 42 + Art 31(1) violation. No such fiction here.
  4. Other heads: buildings — appurtenant to the estate, valuation as part of estate OK; inadequacy no objection (Art 31(4)); private lands — entire proprietor’s interest acquired, compensation via net-income basis (Raja Suriya Pal Singh); Art 31(4): “passed by such Legislature” = passed with or without amendments in Art 107 procedure; s. 37 (30 instalments) squarely Entry 42 — “form and manner”; not a negation of compensation. (Madras Amendment delegation/Art 14 points left open with consent.)
  5. Result: all points fail; appeals dismissed (exceptions kept open earlier).
Principle

Colourable legislation = using an appearance of competence as a pretence for an object that is, in substance, beyond the legislature’s power — governed by the maxim “you cannot do indirectly what you cannot do directly.” (1) It is a question of power, not good faith; motive is irrelevant both ways — collateral motive does not invalidate a competent law, and honest avowal cannot validate an incompetent one. (2) Substance and real object decide (Reciprocal Insurers; Lefroy). (3) Not colourable when the real object, however unworthy, lies within the legislature’s own competence — Gajapati’s own facts prove it. (4) Degree of injustice in compensation cannot be litigated where Art 31(4)/Entry 42 apply.

§7 Residuary Power of Legislation — Article 248 item (d)

⚖ Article 248 (verbatim) & Entry 97, List I

248. Residuary powers of legislation.—(1) [Subject to article 246A,] Parliament has exclusive power to make any law with respect to any matter not enumerated in the Concurrent List or State List.

(2) Such power shall include the power of making any law imposing a tax not mentioned in either of those Lists.

Entry 97, List I: “Any other matter not enumerated in List II or List III …” (incl. residuary taxes — the textual companion of Art 248).

🧪 The Art 248 test (H.S. Dhillon para 21 — “widest possible terms”)

“The only question to be asked is: Is the matter sought to be legislated or included in List II or in List III or is the tax sought to be levied mentioned in List II or in List III? No question has to be asked about List I. If the answer is in the negative, then it follows that Parliament has power to make laws with respect to that matter or tax.” Corollaries from the case:

  • “The function of the entries/lists is not to confer powers; they merely demarcate the legislative field.” (para 22)
  • Words of exclusion inside List I entries (e.g., “exclusive of agricultural land” in Entry 86; “other than agricultural income” in Entry 82) do not cut down Art 248/Entry 97 — “the content of the residuary power does not change with its conferment on Parliament” (para 19); “any other matter” in Entry 97 refers to the topics of Entries 1–96 read as wholes, not to phrases excluded from them (para 20–21).
  • No principle permits reading a proviso into Art 248 — “We simply have not the power to add a proviso like this” (para 24).
  • Parliament may combine powers under specified entries with Entry 97/Art 248 (Aeronautics in Canada) — “Residuary power is as much power as the power conferred under Article 246” (paras 87, 90–91).

🆚 History & structure

  • GoIA 1935 gave the residue to NOBODY — s. 104’s deadlock reflected “profound cleavage of opinion” (Joint Committee Report Vol. I, para 56) — Dhillon paras 15–17. Our Constitution’s innovation: exclusive residue to Parliament (Ambedkar in CA: entry “to allay the fears of the Provinces and … the Indian States”; Canadian precedent of enumerating centre powers despite a residue).
  • CA debates reproduced in DU (paras 35–44): Sardar Hukam Singh (delete “other” — Lists II/III exhaustively cover the field); Naziruddin Ahmed (“Entries 1–90 would be rendered unnecessary … List I was inserted by way of afterthought”); Shibban Lal Saksena (residue “will strengthen the Centre”); Dr Ambedkar (entry defines “the limit or scope of List I”; equivalent formulation would be “Anything not included in List II or III shall be deemed to fall in List I”; Canadian Constitution also enumerates despite residue); T.T. Krishnamachari (“vesting of the residuary power is only a matter of academic significance” given exhaustive enumeration; the one unenumerated tax he foresaw = capital levy on agricultural land — precisely what later reached the Court in Dhillon!).
  • Two-route structure: Art 248 (substantive residuary power) + Entry 97 (textual companion); Art 246A (GST carve-out) now qualifies Art 248(1).

Case 61 · Union of India v. H.S. Dhillon — (1971) 2 SCC 779 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 324–335

[Residuary Power of Legislation] · Bench of 7: S.M. Sikri C.J., J.M. Shelat, G.K. Mitter, A.N. Ray, I.D. Dua, S.C. Roy, D.G. Palekar JJ. (opinion by S.M. Sikri C.J.)
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 324–325)

Section 24 of the Finance Act, 1969 amended the Wealth-tax Act, 1957 so as to include the capital value of agricultural land in “net wealth” (s. 2(c) “assets” = property of every description; s. 2(m) “net wealth”; s. 3 charging). The Punjab & Haryana High Court (Civil Writ 2291/1970, five Judges): four Judges held the amendment beyond Parliament’s competence (Pandit J. dissenting — intra vires), and further held the levy was not a law on Entry 49, List II (taxes on lands and buildings); the Court issued a direction that the Wealth-tax Act as amended (agricultural land in net wealth) was ultra vires. Union of India appealed. Background entries: Entry 86 List I — taxes on capital value of assets of individuals/companies “exclusive of agricultural land”; Entry 49 List II — taxes on lands and buildings; Entry 97 List I + Art 248 — residue; also exclusions in Entries 82 (agricultural income), 84 (alcoholic liquor/opium excise), 87 (agricultural land from estate duty), 88 (agricultural land from succession duty).

Issues (framed at paras 10–11)
  1. Is the impugned amendment a law with respect to Entry 49, List II (i.e., inside the State field)?
  2. If not, is it beyond Parliament’s competence — or valid under Entry 86 read with Entry 97 / Entry 97 by itself, read with Art 248, notwithstanding “exclusive of agricultural land” in Entry 86?

Methodology adopted: “the best way … is to ask ourselves two questions: first is the impugned Act legislation with respect to Entry 49, List II and secondly if it is not, is it beyond the legislative competence of Parliament.” (Setalvad’s “first test List II” vs Palkhivala’s objection that no such test appears in 20 years of this Court’s decisions and that the Canadian-origin test is inapposite.)

Arguments — Union of India (Setalvad, appellant)
  • Test validity of a central statute by first asking whether it is a law on any List II entry (incl. Entry 49); if not — no further question on State power — it “must necessarily fall within the legislative competence of Parliament under Entry 86 read with Entry 97 or Entry 97 by itself read with Art 248”.
  • “Exclusive of agricultural land” in Entry 86 cannot cut down Entry 97 or Art 248 — exclusions from List I entries are not prohibitions on Parliament.
Arguments — respondent & interveners (Palkhivala)
  • The Constitution’s scheme gives States exclusive power over agricultural land, agricultural income and taxes thereon; the specific exclusion of agricultural land from Entry 86 was intended to keep it out of Entry 97 and Art 248 as well — “exclusive of agricultural land” is a word of prohibition; “any other matter” cannot include matters specifically excluded from Entries 1–96 (excluded matters listed: Entries 82/84/86/87/88).
  • The High Court was wrong that the tax falls under Entry 49 List II (it should have been so held, helping the respondent’s cause).
  • The “first test List II” methodology is a Canadian import unsuited to our Constitution.
Decision (Sikri C.J.)
  1. Q1: NOT Entry 49. Approving Sudhir Chand Nawa (AIR 1969 SC 59) and reading Buckingham & Carnatic correctly: Entry 86 tax = on aggregate capital value (aggregation, debts deducted, no definable relation to any component); Entry 49 tax = on units of lands/buildings (three requisites, para 74: tax on units as such; not on totality; unconcerned with division of interests) — “not a personal tax but a tax on property”. Wealth-tax’s basic concept differs entirely → fields do not overlap. Caution (para 83): if a Central Act’s real effect were to impose an Entry 49 tax, it “may be bad as encroaching upon the domain of State Legislatures”.
  2. Q2: VALID under the residue. The scheme gives residuary powers to Parliament “beyond any dispute” (Art 248 + Entry 97); GoIA’s no-residue contrast noted. Exclusions in Entries 82/84/87/88 map onto specific List II entries (46/51/48/47) — but capital value of agricultural land matches no List II entry, so design cannot be inferred to keep it from Entry 97: “The content of the residuary power does not change with its conferment on Parliament” (para 19); “It would be arbitrary to say that it does not fall within Entry 97” (para 20).
  3. Whole-entry reading: “the matter in Entry 86 … is the whole entry” (illustrations: Entry 9 List I vs Entry 3 List III preventive detention; Entry 82’s “other than agricultural income” is not a separate matter). Art 246(1)+Entries 1–96 confer positive power — not intended as debarments from other constitutional powers (para 21).
  4. Interpretive guide: “the only safe guide … is the language employed, interpreted not narrowly but fairly in the light of the broad and high purposes of the Constitution, but without doing violence to the language” (para 32) — rejects the “was this tax known to the framers?” test (TTK’s capital-levy remark notwithstanding).
  5. CA debates used affirmatively (paras 33–44): Ambedkar’s “limit or scope of List I” + “all that is not in Lists II/III falls in List I” equivalence; enumeration = to allay provincial/Princely-State fears + Canadian convention (paras 41, 67: three lists make List II easier to construe).
  6. Canadian test accepted (paras 48, 66–67): lists exhaustive; scheme “essentially the same”; “if a Central Act is challenged as being beyond the legislative competence of Parliament, it is enough to enquire if it is a law with respect to matters or taxes enumerated in List II. If it is not, no further question arises.” Part III limitations are of a different character — “they have nothing to do with legislative competence” (para 47).
  7. Even the original Wealth-tax Act likely fell wholly under Entry 97 (not Entry 86) (para 86); powers under specified entries may be combined with Entry 97/Art 248 (para 87, citing In re Regulation and Control of Aeronautics in Canada); “Residuary power is as much power as the power conferred under Article 246” (para 90).
  8. Result: the impugned Act is VALID; appeal allowed; HC judgment set aside; writ dismissed, no order as to costs.
Principle

(1) Residuary power is Parliament’s by Art 248/Entry 97 — tested by a single negative question: is the matter/tax in List II or List III? If no, Parliament may legislate (the lists demarcate; they do not confer). (2) Words of exclusion inside List I entries do not withhold a topic from the residue — the entry is read as a whole; “the content of the residuary power does not change with its conferment”. (3) The validity of a Central Act challenged as incompetent is decided by asking only whether it invades List II (Canada-style exhaustive-list test, constitutionally orthodox here). (4) Entry 49 vs Entry 86: unit-based property tax vs aggregated capital-value tax — different basic concepts, no overlap. (5) Parliamentary powers under specified entries can be supplemented by the residuary power.

§9 Doctrine of Repugnancy — Article 254 item (f)

⚖ Article 254 (verbatim)

254. Inconsistency between laws made by Parliament and laws made by the Legislatures of States.—(1) If any provision of a law made by the Legislature of a State is repugnant to any provision of a law made by Parliament which Parliament is competent to enact, or to any provision of an existing law with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the Concurrent List, the law made by Parliament, whether passed before or after the law made by the State Legislature, or, as the case may be, the existing law, shall prevail and the law of the State Legislature shall, to the extent of the repugnancy, be void.

(2) Where a law made by the Legislature of a State with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the Concurrent List conflicts with any provision of a law made by Parliament … and the law made by Parliament has been reserved for the consideration of the President and has received the assent of the President, the law so passed by Parliament shall prevail and that of the State Legislature shall to the extent of the repugnancy be void.

Explanation — the expression “law made by Parliament …” in cl (2) includes an Act of Parliament previously passed. [Art 255: defects of procedure — recommendation/sanction — not fatal. The pre-Constitution model was s. 107(2), GoIA 1935, quoted in Zaverbhai: a reserved-and-assented Provincial law would prevail over an earlier Dominion law/existing law — “but nevertheless, the Dominion Legislature may at any time enact further legislation with respect to the same matter.”]

🧭 The repugnancy algorithm

  1. Gate 1 — subject-matter: is the field really Concurrent (List III)? Apply pith and substance first (Hoechst para 41: “No question of conflict between the two Lists will arise if the impugned legislation, by the application of the doctrine of ‘pith and substance’, appears to fall exclusively under one list”). A conflict between laws of different lists is not an Art 254 matter at all (Hoechst: List-II-vs-Union-list clash → the intruding law is simply ultra vires; Vijay Kr Sharma (64) applies pith & substance even to 254 enquiries across different Concurrent entries).
  2. Gate 2 — real conflict: does the State law actually conflict? The “irreconcilable / cannot both stand” test: overlap + harmonious coexistence possible → both valid (Hoechst; Zaverbhai para 8: if later legislation deals with distinct though cognate matters, 254 has no application).
  3. Gate 3 — priority: irreconcilable conflict on the same matter → Parliament prevails regardless of chronology (254(1) expressly covers laws “whether passed before or after”; Zaverbhai: implied-replenishment logic applies to 254(2)) — to the extent of the repugnancy the State law is void.
  4. Gate 4 — assent route (254(2)): a State law on the Concurrent field prevailing over a pre-existing Central law requires reservation + President’s assent; the proviso to 254(2) meanwhile lets Parliament add to, amend, vary or repeal the State law at any time (Zaverbhai para 7 — a power GoIA s. 107(2) did not confer: under AG Ontario v. AG Dominion (1896) AC 348 the Dominion could override but not directly repeal a provincial statute).
  5. Interpretive guides: presumption against implied repeal + presumption of harmony; non obstante = last resort (C.P. & Berar, adopted Hoechst para 45); “same matter” = penalty altered in degree not kind (Zaverbhai: Maxwell 10th edn 187–188; Smith v. Benabo (1937) 1 KB 518); extend both as far as possible before declaring conflict (Maxwell, via Zaverbhai para 15 / Hoechst).

Case 62 · Zaverbhai Amaidas v. State of Bombay — AIR 1954 SC 752 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 336–341

[Doctrine of Repugnancy] · Bench: M.C. Mahajan C.J., B.J. Mukherjea, V. Bose, B. Jagannadhadas and T.L. Venkatarama Ayyar JJ. · Opinion of T.L. Venkatarama Ayyar J. (as extracted in DU) · Appeal from Bombay HC under Art 132(1) certificate
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 336–339)

An appeal against the Bombay High Court’s dismissal of a revision petition against the appellant’s conviction under s. 7 of the Essential Supplies (Temporary Powers) Act, 24 of 1946 (Central). Charge: on 6 April 1951, Zaverbhai transported 15 maunds of juwar from his village Khanjroli to Mandvi without a permit, contravening s. 5(1) of the Bombay Food Grains (Regulation of Movement and Sale) Order, 1949. The Resident First Class Magistrate, Bardoli found him guilty — imprisonment till the rising of the Court + fine of ₹500; affirmed in appeal by the Sessions Judge, Surat.

Revision point: the appellant argued the Magistrate had no jurisdiction — under s. 2 of Bombay Act 36 of 1947 the offence was punishable up to 7 years, and by the Second Schedule CrPC only a Sessions Court could try it. State’s answer: after Central amendments in 1948, 1949 and finally the recast s. 7 by Central Act 52 of 1950, Bombay Act 36 of 1947 had become inoperative; the governing Act was Act 52 of 1950 under which the maximum was 3 years → the Magistrate had jurisdiction. High Court: Bavdekar J. — the amendments did not trench on Bombay Act 36 of 1947, which remained unaffected; Chainani J. — both Acts related to the same subject-matter and Act 52 of 1950 (later Central law) prevailed under Art 254(2). On difference under s. 429 CrPC, Chagla C.J. agreed with Chainani J. — repugnancy between s. 7 (Act 52/1950) and s. 2 (Bombay Act 36/1947), former prevailing → revision dismissed. Certificate under Art 132(1) → Supreme Court. Single point: is the contravention punishable under s. 2 of Bombay Act 36 of 1947 (trial illegal) or under s. 7 of the ESP Act as amended by Act 52 of 1950 (trial and conviction perfectly legal)?

Issue (DU para 8)

Whether the Central amendments of 1948, 1949 and 1950 are “further legislation” within s. 107(2) GoIA / “a law with respect to the same matter” within Art 254(2) — so that s. 2 of Bombay Act 36 of 1947 (avowedly repugnant to s. 7(1) of the 1946 Act) is void as against the recast s. 7; and whether the appellant’s contention (subject exclusively in the Provincial List → s. 107(2)/254(2), which apply only to Concurrent-matter conflicts, inapplicable) can succeed.

Arguments — appellant (State-side facts narrated; contentions at paras 5, 12)
  • (Bavdekar J.’s line, urged in revision): “enhanced penalty” under Act 36 of 1947 is a matter different from mere “punishment” under the ESP Act — so the subject-matter of Act 36/1947 remained untouched by Act 52/1950 except re possession exceeding twice the prescribed quantity; repugnancy not established on that footing.
  • Provincial-List argument (para 12): the subject-matter of Act 36 of 1947 lay exclusively in the Provincial List, so s. 107(2) and Art 254(2) — which apply only to Concurrent-List subjects — had no application; and there was no express repeal of the Bombay Act by Act 52 of 1950.
Arguments — State of Bombay (respondent)
  • There was “further legislation by the Central Legislature in 1948, in 1949 and again in 1950” (para 5) → s. 2 of Act 36 of 1947 became inoperative; the governing enactment is Act 52 of 1950; maximum sentence 3 years → the Resident First Class Magistrate had jurisdiction; conviction legal.
Decision (Venkatarama Ayyar J.)
  1. Art 254(2) traced (paras 6–7): the 1948–49 amendments came under GoIA s. 107(2); Act 52 of 1950 came after the Constitution, governed by Art 254(2) (text quoted: State law on Concurrent List + reserved + President’s assent → prevails; proviso preserves Parliament’s power to enact at any time any law with respect to the same matter “including a law adding to, amending, varying or repealing” the State law). “In substance a reproduction of s. 107(2)” with the concluding portion as a proviso plus additions. Under AG for Ontario v. AG for the Dominion [(1896) AC 348] (Lord Watson), a competent Dominion law overrides provincial law covering the same field, but the Dominion had no power to repeal a provincial statute directly — the s. 107(2) position. “By the proviso to Article 254(2), the Constitution has enlarged the powers of Parliament” — it may now add to, amend, vary or repeal a State Concurrent-field law. Even without express repeal, “the State law will be void under that provision if it conflicts with a later ‘law with respect to the same matter’” enacted by Parliament.
  2. The “same matter” test (para 8): “The important thing … is whether the legislation is ‘in respect of the same matter’. If the later legislation deals not with the matters which formed the subject of the earlier legislation but with other and distinct matters though of a cognate and allied character, then Article 254(2) will have no application.” Principle: where both Centre and Province legislate covering the same ground, both competent → the Centre’s law prevails.
  3. Application (paras 9–10): Bombay Act 36 of 1947’s preamble = “to provide for the enhancement of penalties for contravention of orders made under the [ESP Act, 1946]”. Act 52 of 1950’s new s. 7 is “a comprehensive code covering the entire field of punishment for offences under the Act, graded according to the commodities and the character of the offence” (textiles ≤ 3 yrs + fine + forfeiture; foodstuffs ≤ 3 yrs + fine unless fine alone meets justice + forfeiture; proviso: possession of foodgrains exceeding twice the maximum prescribed → up to 7 years + fine not less than 20 times the value + forfeiture of the excess, Explanation exempting ≤ 5 maunds over; other essential commodities ≤ 3 yrs; s. 7(4) non-compliance ≤ 3 yrs). “The subject of enhanced punishment that is dealt with in Act 36 of 1947 is also comprised in Act 52 of 1950, the same being limited to the case of hoarding of foodgrains” → agreement with Chagla C.J. and Chainani J.: Act 52 of 1950 is legislation in respect of the same matter as Act 36 of 1947.
  4. Bavdekar J.’s error (para 10): “The question of punishment for contravention of orders … both under Act 36 of 1947 and under Act 52 of 1950 constitutes a single subject-matter and cannot be split up”. On this rests the construction rule: “when the punishment or penalty is altered in degree but not in kind, the later provision would be considered as superseding the earlier one” (Maxwell on Interpretation of Statutes, 10th Edition, pp. 187–188). Goddard J. in Smith v. Benabo [(1937) 1 KB 518]: “if a later statute again describes an offence created by a previous one, and imposes a different punishment, or varies the procedure, the earlier statute is repealed by the later” (Michell v. Brown, per Lord Campbell).
  5. 254(1) vs 254(2) (para 11): under Art 254(1) “no question of repeal arises”; “but the principle on which the rule of implied repeal rests — namely, that if the subject-matter of the later legislation is identical with that of the earlier, so that they cannot both stand together, then the earlier is repealed by the later enactment — will be equally applicable to a question under Article 254(2) whether the further legislation by Parliament is in respect of the same matter.”
  6. Held: s. 2 of Bombay Act 36 of 1947 cannot prevail against s. 7 of the ESP Act as amended by Act 52 of 1950.
  7. Provincial-List point rejected (para 12): Act 36 of 1947 itself proceeded “on the basis that the subject-matter is in the Concurrent List”; the point had been raised and rejected by the Bombay High Court judges; and in the Art 132(1) leave application “the only ground that was put forward as involving a substantial question as to the interpretation of the Constitution was whether the Bombay Act 36 of 1947 was repugnant and void under Article 254” — declined to permit the new point.
  8. Result (para 13): the appeal fails and is dismissed — Magistrate’s jurisdiction and conviction lawful.
Principle

(1) Art 254 “same matter” test: it is the subject-matter, not the phraseology, that matters — where a later Central law re-describes the same offence-subject and alters the penalty only in degree, not in kind, it supersedes the earlier State provision (Maxwell; Smith v. Benabo), and the implied-repeal principle of “cannot both stand” informs 254(2) even though 254(1) involves no repeal. (2) Distinguished matters escape 254: later legislation on distinct though cognate matters does not touch the State law. (3) Proviso to 254(2) enlarged Parliament beyond GoIA s. 107(2): express addition/amendment/variance/repeal of State concurrent laws now lies in Parliament’s power — “enlarged the powers of Parliament.” (4) Procedure: grounds outside the Art 132(1) certificate/leave stage are not entertained.

Case 63 · Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. v. State of Bihar — (1983) 4 SCC 45 DU CASE MATERIAL · pp. 342–350

[Doctrine of Repugnancy – Article 254] · Bench: A.P. Sen, E.S. Venkataramiah and R.B. Misra JJ. · Opinion of A.P. Sen J. · Appeals from Patna HC (Arts 14, 19(1)(g), 246, 254 & Art 286 challenges to the Bihar Finance Act, 1981)
Facts (DU excerpt pp. 342–345)

The Bihar Finance Act, 1981: s. 5(1) — every dealer whose gross turnover in a year exceeds ₹5 lakhs shall pay, in addition to the tax payable under the Part, a surcharge up to 10% of the total tax as fixed by notification. s. 5(3) — “Notwithstanding anything to the contrary contained in this Part, no dealer mentioned in sub-section (1), who is liable to pay surcharge, shall be entitled to collect the amount of this surcharge.”

The parties: Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd and Glaxo Laboratories (India) Ltd — Companies Act, 1956 companies manufacturing and selling medicines/life-saving drugs throughout India including Bihar; branch/sales depot at Patna registered as dealer u/s 14 of the Act; sales via wholesale distributors/stockists → retailers → consumers. ~94% of their sales were at controlled prices exclusive of local taxes under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 1979, issued u/s 3(1) of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955; exceeding controlled prices was prohibited; the control orders allowed the manufacturer to pass tax liability on; printed price-lists (retail price inclusive of excise duty) and contracts provided “sales tax and local taxes will be charged wherever applicable”.

High Court’s findings (then under challenge): following S. Kodar v. State of Kerala (AIR 1974 SC 2272) — the surcharge is in reality an additional tax on the aggregate of annual sales (Entry 54, List II); a uniform 10% on the turnover-₹5-lakh class is not discriminatory (Art 14); banning collection of the surcharge is not an unreasonable restriction under Art 19(1)(g) (it is “not an essential characteristic of a sales tax that the seller must have the right to pass it on”; classification rests on capacity to pay); and there was no irreconcilable conflict between s. 5(3) and Paragraph 21 of the DPCO — both are capable of being obeyed. The appellants also failed before the HC on s. 6 ECA (occupied-field) and Art 286 arguments but renewed them in the SC.

Issues (DU para 3, 36)
  1. Occupied field: is price fixation of essential commodities/drugs an occupied field of Central control orders u/s 3(1) ECA (with s. 6’s non obstante), so that s. 5(3) of the Bihar Finance Act trenches on Parliament’s field and is void — or at least repugnant to Paragraph 21 of the DPCO under Art 254(1)?
  2. Scope of Art 254(1): does “a law made by Parliament which Parliament is competent to enact” include laws under List I (e.g., ECA under Entry 33 List III / price-control orders), making List-II-vs-Central-list conflicts repugnancy questions?
  3. Subsidiary: whether gross turnover for s. 5(1) may include inter-State/external/import-export transactions barred by Art 286 (and whether Entry 54 taxation supports s. 5(3)’s no-collection rule).
Arguments — appellants (manufacturers)
  • Occupied field (para 3): price fixation of essential commodities, drugs and formulations is occupied by control orders issued from time to time under s. 3(1) ECA (which permit passing tax liability on) → State legislature had no competence to enact s. 5(3); alternatively, if s. 5(3) covers sales of centrally-priced essential commodities, there is repugnancy with the control orders which under s. 6 ECA prevail (“notwithstanding any other law inconsistent therewith”).
  • Harishankar Bagla route (paras 29–30): s. 6’s non obstante gives Paragraph 21 overriding effect — s. 5(3) is “by-passed”. Purpose point: control orders exist for the manufacturer’s benefit (reasonable return argument: paras 25, 33 — bulk-drug prices allow “reasonable return on net worth” for efficient manufacturers); no-return undermines them.
  • Textual 254(1) point (para 64): “competent to enact” is prima facie wide enough to cover any Parliament law (Union List included) — so a valid ECA + DPCO triggers 254(1) against the State law.
  • Art 286 (para 3): turnover computation including inter-State/external sales is outside State taxing purview.
Arguments — respondents (State of Bihar)
  • Kodar line (paras 10–13): the surcharge is a sales tax under Entry 54, List II — classification by turnover valid; pass-on not an essential feature; s. 5(3) itself relatable to Entry 54 (fairly conceded by both sides, para 13).
  • Competence + ancillary power (para 28): “a surcharge partakes of the nature of sales tax”; a legislature competent to levy the tax may select the class of dealers and prohibit collection of the surcharge — an ancillary provision within its own entry.
  • No conflict on facts (paras 30–31): formulators are governed by Paragraph 24 (price to wholesaler inclusive of sales tax), not Paragraph 21 → no conflict at all; and even under Paragraph 21, the dealer may pass on sales tax and additional sales tax — s. 5(3) only blocks the surcharge, which cuts into the dealer’s profit while retail/controlled prices remain unchanged (consumer protected).
  • Purpose (paras 32–33): control orders serve consumers — equitable distribution and fair prices under scarcity; no entitlement to reasonable return (Shree Meenakshi Mills, AIR 1974 SC 366 — Ray C.J.).
  • Tax segregation (paras 73–76): the Constitution separates taxing power — List II Entries 45–63 taxes, List I Entries 82–92A taxes, List III has no tax entry (fees only) → a Union/Concurrent general-subject law cannot intrude into Entry 54 by “ancillary” tax power; taxing power cannot be deduced from a general entry.
Decision (A.P. Sen J.)
  1. Art 246 architecture (paras 37–43): validity is decided by pith and substance — is s. 5(3) relatable to Entry 54, List II? — not by repugnancy. The three essentials of Art 246 (List I exclusive; List II exclusive subject to (1) with Parliament prevailing only on irreconcilable conflict pro tanto; List III concurrent). Art 246 adopts s. 100, GoIA 1935 with immaterial alterations — “our Constitution was not written on a clean slate”; GoIA-era principles apply.
  2. Reconciliation first (para 41): federal supremacy under the non obstante of Art 246(1) “cannot be resorted to unless there is an ‘irreconcilable’ conflict”; read both entries together in broad sense → attempt fair reconciliation (Union language may be given a meaning “less wide than it might in another context bear, yet one that can properly be given”, and equally for State entries) → “The non-obstante clause in Article 246(1) must operate only if such reconciliation should prove impossible”; and “no question of conflict … will arise if the impugned legislation, by … ‘pith and substance’, appears to fall exclusively under one list, and the encroachment … is only incidental.”
  3. Authority chain (paras 45–53): Gwyer C.J. in C.P. & Berar (read together; “not how … theoretically capable of being construed, but how they are to be construed here and now”; non obstante as “last resource, a witness to the imperfections of human expression”); Earl Loreburn in AG Ontario [1912 AC 571]; Gwyer in Chettiar (pith-and-substance passage); Citizens Insurance (general power shall not nullify a particular power); Lord Simonds in GG in Council v. Province of Madras (“not the name of the tax but its real nature, its pith and substance”); Boddu Paidanna; Prafulla (degree of invasion immaterial except as evidence; List I > III > II only “where they come in conflict”); corollary: incidental encroachment does not affect competence (paras 53, 57, 62 — within competence to legislate matters “necessarily incidental to effective legislation … on a subject expressly enumerated in List II”).
  4. Harishankar Bagla distinguished (paras 29–31): s. 6 ECA merely by-passes inconsistent laws during the order’s continuance — “by-passing … does not necessarily amount to repeal”; and here the by-pass never engages because formulators are governed by Paragraph 24 (prices inclusive of sales tax) — “There being no conflict between sub-section (3) of Section 5 … and Paragraph 24 …, the question of non obstante clause to Section 6 … coming into play does not arise.” Even on Paragraph 21: dealer may pass on sales tax/additional sales tax; s. 5(3) only disallows the surcharge → dealer’s profit absorbs it; no clash (paras 31–33).
  5. Price-control purpose (paras 32–33): orders u/s 3(1) target consumers (equitable distribution, availability at fair prices); no right to a reasonable return unless the statutory scheme says so — Shree Meenakshi Mills followed.
  6. Art 254(1) confined to the Concurrent List (paras 64–70): the text is prima facie susceptible of the wider reading, “but if Article 254(1) is read as a whole, it will be seen that it is expressly made subject to clause (2) which makes reference to repugnancy in the field of Concurrent List — in other words, if clause (2) is to be the guide …, the repugnancy between Union and State law must be taken to refer only to the Concurrent field.” Settled: “with respect to … the Concurrent List” qualifies both limbs of 254(1); repugnancy arises “only when both the legislatures are competent to legislate in the same field”. A.S. Krishna two conditions (Venkatarama Ayyar J. on s. 107(1)): ① both laws in respect of a Concurrent-List matter; ② repugnant → only then void to the extent. Consequence: “Article 254(1) has no application to cases of repugnancy due to overlapping found between List II on the one hand and Lists I and III on the other” — such a State law is ultra vires (Art 246(1) non obstante + “subject to” in 246(3)) — it fails “not because of repugnance … but due to want of legislative competence.”
  7. Nicholas/Tika Ramji tests noted (para 66): (1) actual terms; (2) State law inoperative because the Central law is intended a complete exhaustive code; (3) both seek to exercise power over the same subject-matter — accepted in Ch. Tika Ramji (AIR 1956 SC 676).
  8. Art 254 structure (para 67): 254(1) = Union law prevails in concurrent field regardless of time; 254(2) = President-assented State law prevails only to the extent of inconsistency, in that State, over an earlier Union law; proviso lets Parliament repeal/amend directly or by a repugnant “same matter” law — State law becomes void when the later Parliament law is made, even without express repeal; repugnancy = direct conflict or same-field coexistence where both cannot stand.
  9. Application (paras 72–76): Entry 54 power is plenary including ancillary provisions (s. 5(3) fairly conceded within Entry 54); projecting Parliament’s Entry 33 power into Entry 54 would read Entry 54 as “taxes on sale … other than essential commodities” — no warrant; Entry 54 is subject only to Entry 92A List I. Taxation segregated: List II Entries 1–44 general / 45–63 taxes; List I 1–81 general / 82–92A taxes; List III has no tax entry — “in our Constitution, a conflict of the taxing power of the Union and of the States cannot arise”; entries = fields, powers come from Art 246; tax power cannot be deduced from a general entry as ancillary power. Final holding: the two laws operate on two separate and distinct fields and both are capable of being obeyed — no clash, repugnancy does not arise.
  10. Result: surcharge, no-collection rule and the challenged Bihar Finance Act provisions upheld; the appeals failed and were dismissed.
Principle

(1) Sequencing: pith and substance FIRST (is the State law within its own entry?) — only then repugnancy; and Art 254(1) is confined to same-field conflicts on Concurrent-List subjects (A.S. Krishna’s two conditions; clause (2) as the interpretive guide); List-II-vs-I/III overlaps are resolved by Art 246 supremacy → ultra vires, not repugnancy. (2) Reconciliation before supremacy: the non obstante of Art 246(1) operates only on irreconcilable conflict — last resort. (3) Taxation is segregated — no ancillary tax power from general entries; Union general-subject laws cannot intrude into Entry 54. (4) Non obstante by-pass ≠ repeal (Harishankar Bagla) and never engages without real conflict (Paragraph 24 vs 21). (5) 254(2): President’s assent makes the State law prevail only in that State and only to the extent of inconsistency; Parliament’s proviso power reverses it — express or implied.

Source: DU case-book excerpt, pp. 342–350 (doctrine paras 41, 49, 66–70, 76 as reproduced therein; M.P. Jain’s critique of para 69’s confinement is examined in PYQ Q3(a), §10).

Case 64 · Vijay Kr Sharma v. State of Karnataka — (1990) 2 SCC 562; AIR 1990 SC 2072 RESEARCHED · DU has no excerpt

W.P. Nos. 723, 238, 170, 759 & 1120 of 1989, decided 27-2-1990 (“Not Printed” in SCC; headnote reproduced) · Per Sawant J. (R. Misra J. concurring) · K. Ramaswamy J. dissenting
Facts

The Karnataka Contract Carriages (Acquisition) Act, 1976 (Karnataka Act 21 of 1976) nationalised contract-carriage services in the State: s. 14 froze private operation — it barred any person from applying for, and any officer/authority from entertaining or granting, an application for a permit to run a contract carriage (except for the State undertaking), while s. 20 provided the scheme machinery by which routes/areas could be reserved for the State Road Transport Undertaking to the exclusion of private operators. The Act had received the President’s assent. The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 (Act 59 of 1988), which came into force in 1989, enacted a liberalised permit regime — s. 73 (application for contract-carriage permit), s. 74 (grant of contract-carriage permits — the Regional Transport Authority “shall not ordinarily refuse to grant” an applied-for permit), and s. 80 (procedure; s. 80(2), 80(3) exceptions) — plus s. 98, which gave Chapter VI (special provisions relating to State Transport Undertakings) effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent therewith contained in Chapter V or any other law (the old counterpart of s. 68B of the Motor Vehicles Act, 1939).

Private transport operators (Vijay Kr Sharma and others) challenged ss. 14 and 20 of the Karnataka Act before the Supreme Court: (i) as repugnant to ss. 73, 74 and 80 of the MVA 1988 and therefore void under Art 254(1); and/or (ii) as impliedly repealed by the later Central enactment.

Issues
  1. Are ss. 14 and 20 of the Karnataka Act repugnant to ss. 74/80(2) (and 73/74/80) of the MVA 1988 — do the two statutes occupy the same field so that one cannot stand with the other (Art 254(1); implied repeal)?
  2. Method: can the doctrine of pith and substance be applied to determine repugnancy where the two laws are under different entries of the Concurrent List (Entry 42 — acquisition — vs Entry 35 — mechanically propelled vehicles/their regulation)?
Arguments — petitioners (operators), as met by the Court
  • The MVA 1988 confers a right to apply for, and duty on the RTA to ordinarily grant, contract-carriage permits (ss. 74 r/w 80(2)); the Karnataka Act negates and freezes that very right — an absolute embargo. The two sets of provisions, applied to the same applicants and the same permits, produce conflicting results in the same occupied field → irreconcilable → State law void (the dissent accepts this reasoning).
  • Parliament’s later, comprehensive legislation on the same field (permits for contract carriages) impliedly repeals the earlier State embargo.
Arguments — State of Karnataka (upholding the 1976 Act)
  • Different dominant purposes: the Karnataka Act is acquisition/nationalisation legislation (Entry 42, Concurrent List + Art 31, implementing Arts 39(b) & (c) — ending abuse of contract services, better public transport, preventing concentration of wealth); the MVA is regulation of motor-vehicle operation (Entry 35, Concurrent List). Different subject-matters → Art 254 never engaged.
  • Ancillary: ss. 14/20 are ancillary to acquisition (who may hold the acquired field); ss. 74/80 are general permit provisions — both stand together.
  • Internal MVA answer: s. 98 (like s. 68B of the 1939 Act) lets Chapter VI’s State-undertaking scheme provisions override Chapter V — so where a scheme notifies nationalisation, ss. 74/80 do not apply; the MVA itself envisages denial of permits to private operators → no genuine conflict. (Also: the old MVA ss. 51/57 corresponded to ss. 74/80, yet the President still assented to the 1976 Act.)
Decision
  1. Per Sawant J. (majority): first test — same subject-matter? “Whenever repugnancy … is alleged, what has to be first examined is whether the two legislations cover or relate to the same subject-matter. The test … is the usual one, namely, to find out the dominant intention” (pith and substance). If dominant intentions differ → different subject-matters; mere reference to allied/cognate matters — even partial coverage of the same area in a different context — does not produce Art 254 repugnancy; the legislation must be “substantially on the same subject” and “cover the entire field covered by the other”.
  2. Pith and substance is not displaced by Art 254: “Not to apply the theory of pith and substance when the repugnancy … is to be considered under Art. 254 … would be illogical” — the doctrine first validates competence (encroachment review), then answers whether the State law, “though otherwise constitutionally valid, has lost its validity because Parliament has made a legislation with a conflicting provision on allegedly the same matter”; if it resolves Union-State list conflicts by dominant purpose, “there is no reason why the repugnancy between … two legislations under different entries in the same List, viz. the Concurrent List should not be resolved by … the same touchstone”.
  3. Application: Karnataka Act = Entry 42 (acquisition) read with Art 31 for Arts 39(b),(c); MVA = Entry 35 (regulation of motor vehicles). Objects “materially different” → “the provisions of Article 254 do not come into play”. The permit embargo in ss. 14/20 is ancillary to acquisition/nationalisation; ss. 74/80 are general regulation; “both these statutes can stand together”. Presumption against implied repeal: Parliament enacted the 1988 Act “knowing fully well the existence of the earlier statute and yet it did not expressly repeal it → … no need to repeal”. And s. 98/Chapter VI proves the MVA contemplates nationalised exclusion — no irreconcilable clash.
  4. Per R. Misra J. (concurring): the MVA “does not purport to make any provision in regard to acquisition of contract-carriage permits which formed the dominant theme … of the State Act”; ss. 73/74 do not say who may apply; s. 80 is liberalised, but s. 20’s ancillary exclusion “does not directly run counter”; excluding a class of applicants is not covered by the 1988 provisions → no direct inconsistency → no invitation to Art 254(1).
  5. Per K. Ramaswamy J. (dissenting): entries “merely demarcate” fields; the entry used is not decisive — Arts 246(2)/254 give Parliament paramountcy in the concurrent field. The MVA liberalises and confers the permit-application right while the Karnataka Act freezes and negates it — “the existence of two sets of provisions … is sufficient to produce conflicting results … in the same occupied field”; “operational incompatibility and irreconcilability” → ss. 14(1) & 20(3) void as against ss. 73, 74, 80. Special-prevails-over-general cannot save a void law (voidity obliterates ab initio); the State’s route is re-enactment with Presidential assent under Art 254(2).
  6. Result: majority upheld ss. 14 & 20 of the Karnataka Act — no repugnancy, no implied repeal; petitions rejected.
Principle

(1) Repugnancy has a pith-and-substance threshold: before Art 254 is invoked, ask whether the two laws are substantially on the same subject, judged by dominant intention — across different Concurrent entries too; partial, incidental or cognate overlap is not enough; the State law must occupy the entire field of the Central law. (2) Different dominant purposes = no conflict (acquisition/nationalisation vs regulation here). (3) Ancillary provisions ride with their head (s. 20 scheme = acquisition machinery; permit provisions = regulation). (4) Express non-repeal by a knowing Parliament strengthens the presumption against implied repeal; check the Central Act’s own override clauses (MVA s. 98) before finding clash. (5) Dissent’s counter-rule: in the concurrent field the entry chosen is not decisive; genuine operational incompatibility voids the State law — with Art 254(2) re-enactment as the cure.

Case 65 · State of Kerala v. Mar Appraem Kuri Company Ltd. — (2012) 7 SCC 106 RESEARCHED · DU has no excerpt

C.A. Nos. 6660 with 6661-6681 of 2005 & connected matters, decided 8-5-2012 · Constitution Bench · [Art 254 — repugnancy: making vs commencement; implied repeal]
Facts

Chits (chitties) — informal savings-credit devices — are special contracts: both the Kerala Chitties Act, 1975 (Kerala Act 23 of 1975) and the Chit Funds Act, 1982 (Central Act 40 of 1982) are enacted under Entry 7, List III (Contracts). The Central Act received the President’s assent on 19-8-1982 (law-making process complete); s. 1(2) extended it to the whole of India (except J&K), while s. 1(3) left commencement to notification — never notified in Kerala. Its architecture showed occupation of the field: s. 3 — provisions have effect notwithstanding anything to the contrary in any other law; s. 90 — repeal of State chit legislation (s. 90(1)); intent to be a complete code.

On 29-7-2002, the Kerala Finance Act (Act 7 of 2002) inserted s. 4(1A) into the Kerala Chitties Act, 1975, extending it to chitties registered outside Kerala where ≥ 20% of subscribers usually resided in Kerala. Private chitty firms (Mar Appraem Kuri Co. Ltd. and others) challenged s. 4(1A) as void under Art 254(1) for repugnancy with the 1982 Central Act. The Single Judge held that, absent a s. 1(3) notification bringing the Central Act into force in Kerala and absent s. 89 rules, the Kerala Chitties Act had not been repealed; the High Court on comparison found “several … inconsistent and hostile provisions” and held the State law void from 19-8-1982 with s. 4(1A) ineffective — the State of Kerala appealed.

Provision-comparison (recorded by the Court): “discount” defined differently (s. 2(g) Central vs s. 2(4) Kerala); s. 8 Central minimum capital & reserve-fund requirements have no Kerala counterpart; dual sanction/registration (State sanction + registration under the Central Act) — a foreman registered under the Central Act would be subjected to inconsistent Kerala provisions.

Issues (as framed)
  1. (i) Does “making” of the law or its “commencement” bring about repugnancy under Art 254(1)? (Must repugnancy “exist in fact”, not by mere possibility — Tika Ramji?)
  2. (ii) Effect in law of the repeal: does non-enforcement of the Central Act in Kerala create a legislative vacuum; was the State legislature competent to enact s. 4(1A) in 2002; and what survives of the Kerala Act?
Arguments — State of Kerala / supporting chit firms (appellants)
  • “Made” in Art 254 identifies which legislature made the law; the declaration of repugnancy is conditional on operation; the question of repugnancy arises only after enforcement/commencement — and the Central Act has never been extended to Kerala (s. 1(3) notification absent; s. 89 rules unframed).
  • Repugnancy “must exist in fact and not depend on mere possibility” (Tika Ramji); mere enactment of a Central law on a List III subject cannot immediately void all State enactments — else a total legislative vacuum; “if any provision …” and “to the extent of the repugnancy” in Art 254(1) militate against wholesale voiding.
Arguments — private chitty firms (respondents)
  • Commencement is irrelevant: “law made” (used seven times in Art 254) refers to enactment; there is no mention of commencement anywhere in the Article; repugnancy arose on 19-8-1982 when the President assented.
  • Parliament intended a complete code occupying Entry 7 entirely (s. 3 non obstante + s. 90 repeal manifest the intent); conditional-commencement reading would let a State bypass Parliament’s will; both laws being under the same entry, 254(1) squarely applies; and actual inconsistent/hostile provisions exist (so Tika Ramji’s “in fact” test is satisfied).
Decision (headnote A–H; conclusions in para 28)
  1. Thresholds restated (para 16): legislative power is conferred by Art 245, not by the entries — “entries only demarcate legislative fields”; federal supremacy under Art 246(1) operates only on an “irreconcilable”, “real” conflict — the non obstante “operates only if reconciliation is impossible”; “law with respect to” ≠ “law affecting a subject-matter”.
  2. Making, not commencement (paras 17–19, 28): the verb “made/make” in Arts 245, 246, 250, 257 is deliberate — supremacy attaches to the making of law. “Broadly speaking, law making is exclusively the function of the Legislatures”; the legislative process is complete when the Bill passes both Houses and receives the President’s/Governor’s assent (Arts 111/200); postponement of enforcement = conditional legislation — “no part of the legislative function is left unexercised”. The enforcement argument “stands demolished” by the definitions of “laws in force” (Arts 13(3)(b), 372(3) Expl.) and “existing law” (Art 366(10)) — laws “in force” include laws never in operation. Throughout Art 254 the emphasis is on making, not commencement. → Repugnancy arises on the making of the law, not its commencement (approving the rule in Pt. Rishikesh).
  3. Occupation of the field (para 19): Parliament’s intention to make the 1982 Act “a complete code” and occupy Entry 7 entirely is manifested by s. 3 (notwithstanding clause) and s. 90 (repeal of State legislation). Consequently, though unenforced in Kerala, the State legislature stood divested of power to enact s. 4(1A) on 29-7-2002 — save and except under Art 254(2); without the President’s assent, s. 4(1A) is void. The State “was bound to keep in mind” the existing Central law when amending in 2002.
  4. Proviso to 254(2) (para 28): even an assented State law cannot tie Parliament — it may “add to, amend, vary or repeal” it as soon as it is made — Parliament need not wait for the State law to commence.
  5. Voiding + implied repeal (headnote F–G): two independent tests both satisfied — (a) actual irreconcilable inconsistencies (the provision-comparison) and (b) intent to cover the entire field → the Acts “cannot stand together”; therefore the Kerala Chitties Act, 1975 became void under Art 254(1) on 19-8-1982 and “stood impliedly repealed” (reinforced by express s. 90(1) of the Central Act).
  6. No legislative vacuum (paras 25–26, 28): by Art 367, the General Clauses Act s. 6 applies to the repeal — s. 6(b) preserves previous operation, s. 6(c) preserves rights, privileges, obligations and liabilities acquired/incurred under the Kerala Act; and ss. 85(a) and 90(2) of the Central Act continue the Kerala Act’s application to chits in operation on the date of commencement (once notified) — transactions between 19-8-1982 and commencement stand protected. Hence “there would be no legislative vacuum” — the State’s reductio rejected.
  7. Two routes of repugnancy (headnote H, para 17): (1) legislations in their allotted spheres overlap and conflict → Parliament predominates by virtue of the non obstante clause in Art 246(1); (2) both laws on Concurrent-List matters in conflict → Art 254(1). In both situations the Parliamentary legislation predominates.
  8. Result: appeal of the State dismissed — s. 4(1A) void; Kerala Chitties Act, 1975 void/impliedly repealed from 19-8-1982 (subject to GCA s. 6 and ss. 85(a)/90(2) savings).
Principle

(1) Timing: Art 254 repugnancy is tested at the making of the Central law (assent + gazette), never at its commencement — conditional/deferred commencement leaves no legislative function unexercised; “laws in force” definitions foreclose the enforcement argument. (2) Occupation: a Central Act that is a complete code with a notwithstanding clause (s. 3) and a repeal clause (s. 90) manifests intent to cover the whole field — the State legislature is then denuded, and any later State law on the matter is void unless saved by Art 254(2) assent. (3) Implied repeal + no vacuum: void State law ⇒ implied repeal, but GCA s. 6 (via Art 367) + ss. 85(a)/90(2) preserve accrued rights and pending chits — existence of savings defeats the “legislative vacuum” objection. (4) Structure: the two predomination routes — allotted-sphere conflicts via Art 246(1); concurrent-field conflicts via Art 254(1); and entries at all times only demarcate (Art 245 confers).

📜 Dec-2024 · Q3(a) · 10 marks
“‘It does not appear to be a sound proposition to confine Article 254(1) only to the situation where the Central-State laws fall in the Concurrent List rather than when they fall in different Lists and yet are inconsistent to some extent.’ Do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer with help of relevant legal provisions and judgments.”

Model Answer (≈ 800–900 words · balanced “partially agree”)

1. Decode the statement (2 lines). The proposition attacks the Hoechst confinement of Art 254(1) (paras 69–70): repugnancy under 254(1) arises only where both laws occupy the same field of a Concurrent-List subject (A.S. Krishna’s two conditions); List-II-vs-List-I/III overlaps are ultra vires under Art 246(1) (“subject to” in 246(3)) — failure of competence, not repugnancy. The statement says this confinement is unsound; so should you — partially agree.

2. Why confinement is attacked — the case FOR the statement (4 points, cite):

  • Text: “a law made by Parliament which Parliament is competent to enact” naturally includes Parliament’s List I and Arts 249–253 laws — competence runs wider than List III. M.P. Jain (Indian Constitutional Law — the DU reference) contends the phrase was chosen precisely to cover Union-list and special-power laws; and the words “with respect to … the Concurrent List” may grammatically attach only to the second limb (“existing law”).
  • Exception vs main provision (P.K. Tripathi, “The Text is Explicit”, AIR 1986): cl (2) is an exception to cl (1); an exception is always narrower — there is nothing objectionable in cl (1) having larger content than cl (2). Using cl (2) as the “guide” (Hoechst’s method) therefore proves too much.
  • Lacuna — the treaty-law problem: a law made under Art 253 (implementing conventions) on a State-subject matter conflicts with a State law: Art 246(1)’s non obstante cannot be invoked (253 is its own override), and on Hoechst’s reading 254(1) also fails → the State law survives though the Union law must prevail. Only the broad reading closes this gap (M.P. Jain).
  • Same-entry rigour: requiring both laws to be on the same Concurrent entry was criticised in Vijay Kr Sharma — Sawant J. applied pith & substance/dominant purpose across different Concurrent entries; K. Ramaswamy J. (dissent) went further: entries “merely demarcate” fields and “the concerned entry or entries are not the source of power”.

3. Why confinement nevertheless has force — the case AGAINST (3 points):

  • Systemic design: if 254(1) swallowed all “inconsistencies”, Art 246(1) (List I supremacy), Art 246(3) (“subject to”) and Art 251 (Arts 249/250 laws) would be rendered largely redundant — each overlap has its own allotted resolver; repugnancy is the intra-concurrent-field rule.
  • Grammar settled: “with respect to … the Concurrent List” qualifies both limbs of 254(1) — Hoechst para 69 (“it is now settled”); A.S. Krishna two conditions; Mar Appraem Kuri (65) headnote H codifies the two routes: allotted-sphere overlap → Art 246(1); concurrent-field conflict → Art 254(1).
  • Competence, not repugnancy, explains the different-List case: a State law clashing irreconcilably with a Union-list law has no legislative field left (“subject to”) — declaring it void “to the extent of repugnancy” would mis-describe a total competence failure (Hoechst para 69).

4. Your verdict (the mark-fetcher). “I partially agree.” (i) It is not sound to confine 254(1) absolutely — the clause must cover laws Parliament makes under Arts 252/253 and other special heads (lacuna otherwise), and must not be read to require identity of entries (Vijay Kr Sharma: pith and substance governs even repugnancy). (ii) But for genuine List-II-vs-List-I inconsistencies the confinement is right — Art 246(1) is the true resolver, and Hoechst’s competence-route, Mar Appraem’s two-route scheme and Zaverbhai’s same-matter test together form the coherent architecture. (iii) Best synthesis: 254(1) = same-field conflicts (any Concurrent entries, incl. existing laws) + Parliament’s special-power laws; 246(1)/251 = allotted-sphere overlaps.

Authorities kit: Arts 246(1),(3), 249–253, 254(1),(2), 251 · Hoechst paras 66–70 · A.S. Krishna · Mar Appraem Kuri (H) · Vijay Kr Sharma (Sawant J + Ramaswamy J dissent) · Zaverbhai · M.P. Jain; P.K. Tripathi (AIR 1986) · Bait phrases: “clause (2) as guide cuts down clause (1)”, “treaty-law lacuna”, “two routes of predomination”.

📜 Dec-2024 · Q3(b) · 10 marks — “Write a short note on the doctrine of colourable legislation.”

Model Answer (≈ 600–700 words)

1. Definition + maxim (open with these): A legislature commits a colourable exercise when, having apparently acted within its powers, it in substance and reality transgresses them — the transgression being “veiled by what appears … to be a mere pretence or disguise” (Duff J. in AG for Ontario v. Reciprocal Insurers, [1924 AC 328, 337], quoted in K.C. Gajapati (60)). The doctrine rests on the maxim “you cannot do indirectly what you cannot do directly.”

2. Essentials (the four exculpatory/incipient rules — from Gajapati paras 9–18): (i) it is a question of competence, not bona fides — no motive inquiry (“Whether a statute is constitutional or not is … always a question of power”, Cooley); (ii) it applies only to limited/qualified jurisdictions, and only where the real object lies outside them — patent, manifest, direct or “disguised, covert and indirect”; (iii) form is no defence — “the substance of the Act that is material, not merely the form”; a legislature cannot violate prohibitions “by employing an indirect method”; (iv) conversely, motive cannot invalidate: if the object is within power, however “unjust or inequitable” the choice, the law stands — and even an avowed trespass saves nothing if the enacting clauses are truly intra vires (Lefroy).

3. Illustrations (at least three): Gajapati itself — the Orissa Agricultural Income Tax (Amendment) Act, 1950 was challenged as a device to deflate zamindari compensation (Entry 42); held not colourable: Entry 46 taxing power genuinely exercised; rates may be unjust, but “that does not affect the competency”; pursuing the ministers’ motives was “a wrong line of enquiry” — appeal dismissed. Contrast Maharaja Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar [1952 SCR 889] — Bihar Land Reforms Act ss. 4(b), 23(f) rested on fictitious, fact-unrelated deductions dressed as a “principle of compensation” → colourable use of Entry 42. Comparative: Union Colliery v. Bryden (mining regulation used to exclude Chinese aliens); AG Alberta v. AG Canada (Lord Maugham: no acting “under the guise, or the pretence, or in the form” of own powers to trespass).

4. Colourable vs pith and substance (the discriminator examiners love): P&S asks “which list does the law really belong to?” (attribution; incidental trespass forgiven — Prafulla). Colourable asks “is the real object beyond this legislature’s power altogether?” (nothing is forgiven — pretence voids). A law with a bad-faith motive but a competent object fails neither (Gajapati).

5. Close: The doctrine polices the limits of legislative jurisdictions and fundamental-rights prohibitions while leaving legislative policy alone — “it would be colourable only if the real object is not attainable … by reason of any constitutional limitation or … lies within the exclusive field of another legislature” (Gajapati para 9).

Kit: Gajapati paras 9–18 (Cooley p. 379; Reciprocal Insurers; Lefroy p. 75; Maxim; Kameshwar Singh; Lord Maugham) · contrast Prafulla/Drive-in (pith & substance) · bait phrases: “guise or pretence”, “competence, not mala fides”, “the substance, not the form”.

  • Pith & substance: name ≠ nature; true character decides the list; incidental trespass forgiven; degree = evidence only (Prafulla; G. Chawla; Drive-in).
  • Colourable: pretence for a forbidden object; competence not mala fides; “cannot do indirectly” (Gajapati; contrast Kameshwar Singh).
  • Harmonious/non obstante: read together → reconcile by construing each by the other → only then supremacy (C.P. & Berar; Hoechst para 41).
  • Residuary: whole-entry reading; exclusions don’t shrink Art 248; tax power is distinct (Dhillon; Hoechst 74–76).
  • Repugnancy: pith&S first; same matter + irreconcilable; degree-not-kind supersedes (Zaverbhai); 254(1) confined to concurrent field (Hoechst); making not commencement (Mar Appraem); assent route + proviso (254(2)).

🧪 Trap-driller — 10 one-liners

  1. Nexus needs both elements — no nexus → extraterritorial → void (not Art 245(2)).
  2. Art 245(2) = old Parts A/B/C only; “for” ≠ “in” (G.V.K.).
  3. UT ≠ State: 246(4), not 246(3).
  4. Entries confer nothing — Art 246 does (Dhillon; Mar Appraem (A)).
  5. Non obstante is the last resource, not the first (C.P. & Berar).
  6. Incidental trenching = valid; degree only evidences character.
  7. Colourable ≠ bad motive (Gajapati); motive ≠ salvation (Kameshwar Singh).
  8. Exclusion in a List I entry ≠ exclusion from Art 248/Entry 97.
  9. Taxing power never ancillary; Lists II 45–63 segregated (Hoechst).
  10. Repugnancy tested at making; void “to the extent”, saved by 254(2) assent.
