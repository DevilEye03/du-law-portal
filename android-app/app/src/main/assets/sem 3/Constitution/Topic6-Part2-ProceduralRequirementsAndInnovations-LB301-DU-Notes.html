§1 · Judicial Activism, Overreach & Self-Restraint

Item (a) opens with a pairing: “Judicial Activism and overreach/self-restraint”. The syllabus wants you to hold three concepts together — activism as the engine, self-restraint as the brakes, and “overreach” as the accusation made when the brakes fail (or when the driver leaves the road).

⚖️ Judicial activism

Where the court energises procedure or doctrine to reach a substantive injustice: enlarging locus standi, treating a letter as a petition, appointing its own commissioners, awarding compensation, writing guidelines, monitoring compliance. Its engine is Art 32/Art 226 read with Art 142 (“complete justice”).

DU exemplar: Bhagwati J.'s “new jurisprudence” demanding “judicial statesmanship and high creative ability” (S.P. Gupta, para 24).

🛑 Overreach (the critique)

The charge that the court has crossed from enforcing law into making policy — occupying space the Constitution assigns to the elected branches. The DU material itself states the limit from the inside:

“…the Court must not overstep the limits of its judicial function and trespass into areas reserved to the Executive and the Legislature.” S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., para 24 — DU case material (book p. 183)

🧘 Self-restraint

The court's voluntary disciplines: comity with High Courts, insistence on ordinary remedies first, laches, res judicata, the “sufficient interest” filter, and refusing to act on political or pressure-group motives.

DU exemplar: Trilokchand — “This Court puts itself in restraint in the matter of petitions under Article 32 and this practice has now become inveterate.” (Hidayatullah C.J.)

§2 · Locus Standi DU CASE MATERIAL — PRIMARY SOURCE

“Locus standi” = the place (right) of standing — who may address the court. The DU case book's Part-II core is the dedicated “Locus Standi” passage of Bhagwati J. in S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, 1981 Supp (1) SCC 87 (paras 13–26, book pp. 177–185); it is reproduced as a full case-note in §9 and digested here article-by-article.

2.1 The traditional rule — and why it existed

“The traditional rule of locus standi is that only a person whose personal legal right or interest is infringed may maintain an action for redress… It is a rule of ancient vintage [from] the time when private law dominated the legal system…” S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., para 14 — DU case material (book p. 178)

Under the classical rule, the plaintiff must plead a legal injury to himself — not the public's. A taxpayer could not sue over public expenditure; a bystander could not sue over a polluted river. The injury had to be special, not general. That worked in a private-law world; in a constitutional democracy governed by Part III and Part IV it produced an absurdity: the larger the wrong, the fewer the plaintiffs.

2.2 Exceptions the court had already recognised (para 15)

ExceptionWhat it allowsDU-cited authority

2.3 The disability rule — another person litigates for you (para 16)

  • Minor / unsound mind — the next friend sues: Order XXXII CPC.
  • Detenu — habeas corpus may be moved by another: the prisoner often cannot reach the court (hence the “letter” jurisprudence).
  • Prisoner — the court has entertained prisoners' letter-petitions directly.
  • Minority shareholder — may sue for wrongs to the company where majority is complicit.

2.4 The breakthrough — public injury & the relaxed rule (paras 17–19)

“[A] member of the public acting bona fide and having sufficient interest may move the High Court under Art 226 or the Supreme Court under Art 32 complaining of a wrong to an indeterminate class… [the courts] would readily respond even to a letter addressed to them, acting pro bono public[o]… procedure is but a handmaiden of justice… the petition must be bona fide — not for personal gain or private profit or political motivation or other oblique motives…” S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., para 17 — DU case material (book pp. 179–180)

Public injury vs private injury (paras 18–19)

  • Private injury: hurts me — traditional standing suffices.
  • Public injury: hurts an indefinite class or the community — pollution of air/water,noxious-gas emissions, cruelty to prisoners, bonded labour. The public at large cannot assemble as plaintiffs; if the court insists on the classical rule, the wrong goes unredressed because no single victim is identifiable.
  • The law must be “a device of organised social action” — standing rules are handmaidens, not masters, of that aim.

Deterrence & prudence (para 17 tail)

  • Bona fide filter: no personal gain, private profit, political motivation or oblique purpose.
  • Prudential preference: courts prefer cases where the affected class can be determinate (even while permitting indeterminate-class petitions) — a note of restraint embedded in the relaxation itself.

2.5 Who protects the public? The AG problem & the PIL definition (para 20)

Traditionally the Attorney General (Union) / Advocate General (State) sues for public wrongs under ss. 91–92, CPC — but both are chosen by the political branches, so public remedies could stall at the pleasure of government. Bhagwati J.: standing rules must therefore be liberalised — courts themselves must let citizens step into that role. Hence the definition carried in the DU notes:

PIL = “litigation undertaken for the purpose of redressing a public injury, enforcing a public duty, protecting social, collective, ‘diffused’ rights and interests.” The trigger is “sufficient interest”, which the court keeps deliberately discretionary — “no strait-jacket formula.” S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., para 20 — DU case material (book p. 181)

2.6 The internal limits — overreach guard (paras 24–25)

“…the Court must not overstep the limits of its judicial function and trespass into areas reserved to the Executive and the Legislature.” There is a “vital distinction between locus standi and justiciability” — relaxing who may sue does not create subject-matter jurisdiction over political questions. Pressure groups must not use the courts as instruments of conflict, and PIL must not become the vehicle of a “secondary public injury” — a private grievance cosmetised as public. The passage concedes the danger: courts must be alert to motive (para 24) and confine themselves to bona fide, rights-based claims (para 25). S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., paras 24–25 — DU case material (book pp. 183–184)

2.7 The application — lawyers may sue, “priests in the temple of justice” (para 26)

A group of Bombay lawyers (led by Iqbal Chagla) moved to enforce duties owed to the public: their concern was “deeper than that of a busybody” — such petitioners “cannot be told off at the gates” (Krishna Iyer J. in Fertiliser Corp. Kamgar Union AIR 1981 SC 344). Lawyers are “priests in the temple of justice”; their locus standi is the widest among ordinary citizens. S.P. Gupta, Bhagwati J., para 26 — DU case material (book p. 185)

§3 · Laches (unreasonable, unexplained delay)

Laches = such delay, unexplained and unexcused, that the claim has become stale and it would be inequitable to entertain it. Unlike limitation it is not a statute — it is the court's equitable refusal, policed by the maxim vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt (“the law aids the vigilant, not those who sleep”). Syllabus case 47 (Trilokchand) is the fountainhead.

3.1 The puzzle: no Limitation Act, yet delay kills

Settled (all five judges in Trilokchand agreed)

  • The Limitation Act, 1963 does not directly apply to Art 32/226 proceedings — there is no prescribed period.
  • A legislative attempt to impose a short period would itself be questionable under Art 13(2) (it could extinguish a fundamental right).
  • Time spent pursuing representations/appeals to authorities is ordinarily a good explanation for delay.

But the Court's own rules do the work

  • Analogy: where the writ remedy mirrors a suit remedy limited by statute, the court “adopts the statute as its own rule of procedure” and measures delay by that period (Bachawat J.).
  • Public policy: stale claims are refused even absent a statutory bar; only an extinguished right (prescription, s.27 Limitation Act) leaves nothing to enforce; an extinguished remedy still permits a public-policy refusal.

3.3 Interaction with res judicata & exhaustion (the Daryao filter)

Delay and bars interlock: a High Court that refuses on laches or alternative-remedy grounds has not decided the merits — so its order generally cannot be pleaded as res judicata against a later Art 32 petition (unless the facts it found are themselves relevant under Art 32). Conversely, delay that is explained by pursuing remedies is excused (Trilokchand headnote). Daryao draws this line; Trilokchand applies it.

3.4 The modern restatement (2026): a flexible rule of practice

In Mizo Chief Council v. Union of India (2026; Pardiwala & Mahadevan JJ.), reviewing the law from Trilokchand onwards, the Supreme Court held:

  • Laches in Art 32 proceedings is “a flexible rule of practice anchored in judicial discretion, not a rigid rule of law”;
  • “The operative test is not one of 'unreasonable delay' but of 'unexplained delay'” — the adequacy of the explanation is decisive;
  • Two filters: would entertaining the petition disturb settled matters/third-party rights, and has the delay been satisfactorily explained;
  • The State “cannot be permitted to benefit from its own lethargy by weaponising the doctrine of laches” where the delay is partly its own doing (indecision, false assurances);
  • Still threshold-strict: the petitioner must prove a fundamental right and its actual or threatened breach.

PETITION FAILED — not on laches, but because no FR breach was proved (land compensation/privy-purse claim, notification of 1955, petition filed 2014). RESEARCHED

§4 · Res Judicata (claim-preclusion across the writ court)

Res judicata — a thing adjudicated is treated as the truth (res iudicata pro veritate habitur): no person shall be vexed twice for the same cause (nemo debet bis vexari pro una eademque causa). Statutorily it is s. 11, CPC; constitutionally the question is whether it fences in the “heartbeat” remedy of Art 32. Syllabus case 46 (Daryao) answers: yes — with conditions.

📐 Section 11 CPC — the elements (inline)

No court shall try any suit or issue in which the matter directly and substantially in issue has been directly and substantially in issue—(i) in a former suit between the same parties (or their litigating under the same title); (ii) in a court competent to try the subsequent suit; (iii) where the matter was finally decided in the former suit. “Suit” is read widely (any litigation, incl. writs) once the twin pillars — same parties + same title + same matter finally decided on merits — exist. Two maxims travel with it: public interest in finality of litigation, and a person should not be tried twice for the same cause (Daryao's rationale).

4.2 Daryao's reasoning — why Art 32 is not immune

Arguments of the petitioner (as recorded)

  • Res judicata is a technical rule akin to estoppel — inapplicable to fundamental-rights enforcement.
  • Art 32(1) itself is a fundamental right; whittling it down with a procedural bar is unconstitutional.
  • A High Court trying under Art 226 cannot be the “same court” as the Supreme Court under Art 32 — the s.11 competence test fails; the reliefs/forums differ.
  • English analogy: habeas corpus petitions may be filed one after the other — successive petitions never precluded.

Arguments of the respondents

  • Art 32 guarantees an “appropriate proceeding”, not an automatic original petition; the proper channel after a 226 verdict was Art 136 (SLP).
  • Finality rests on public policy; repeated litigation vexes the same person twice — the twin maxims apply to FR petitions too.
  • Raj Lakshmi Dasi v. Banamali Sen: res judicata can operate even where the first court could not have tried the second matter — competence need not be identical.
  • The HC's jurisdiction under 226 is substantially the same as the SC's under 32 (same parties, same cause of action, same character of relief).

Decision — Daryao v. State of U.P., AIR 1961 SC 1457 (27-03-1961; Gajendragadkar C.J.) RESEARCHED

  • Held: the Art 32 petition is barred — the Allahabad HC had dismissed the identical 226 petition on merits by a speaking order. The four-proposition table above is the operative test.
  • Rejected: “res judicata = technical estoppel” (it “rests on maxims… taken from Roman law”, not on technical estoppel); “the HC cannot bind an Art 32 petition” (competence is substantially satisfied); the English habeas-corpus analogy (“no analogy between the petition for habeas corpus and petitions under Art 226 or 32”); and the notion that Art 32 is merely a discretionary power like Art 226 — “citizens have ordinarily the right to invoke Art 32” once an illegal FR violation is shown.
  • Confirmed: the rule “can be invoked even against a petition filed under Art 32” (also on the authority of an earlier SC Art 32 round).

4.3 Trilokchand's contrast — the in-limine case

In Trilokchand the earlier Bombay HC petition had been dismissed in limine — so, applying Daryao (point 4), the Full Court held res judicata did not bar the Art 32 petition; the petition nevertheless failed on laches (§3). The headnote also records the Court's famous double thought: the principle of res judicata is applied to Art 32 “although the expression is somewhat inapt and unfortunate” — the motivating factor being “another parallel jurisdiction in another Court” (comity + finality). Trilokchand (1969) 1 SCC 110 — headnote/observations RESEARCHED

§5 · Exhaustion of Alternative Remedies

The doctrine of self-help first: where an efficacious alternative remedy exists — suit, appeal, revision, statutory tribunal, departmental channel — the writ court will ordinarily decline to interfere. It is a doctrine of comity and complementary jurisdiction, not of extinguishment (its refusal normally carries no res judicata effect — §4).

5.1 The rule and its constitutional flavouring

Core statements (Trilokchand)

  • “In rare cases, where the ordinary process of law appears inefficacious, does this Court interfere even where other remedies are available.”
  • “Extraordinary remedies should not take the place of ordinary remedies.”
  • The SC refrains where the party has already moved the HC for the same relief, and where a better remedy lies in the High Court, “which can go into the controversy more comprehensively” (affidavits, evidence, cross-examination) — “comity between the Supreme Court and the High Court.”
  • This self-restraint “has now become inveterate” (Hidayatullah C.J.). RESEARCHED

Why the rule exists

  • Division of labour: the writ remedy is residual — “court of first resort” only where no other door opens.
  • Factual advantages: ordinary forums can record evidence; writ courts act on affidavits.
  • Delay-explanation link: time spent pursuing the alternative remedy is ordinarily a good explanation for delay in filing the writ (Trilokchand) — exhaustion helps your laches profile.
  • No forfeiture: refusal on this ground is not a merits decision → no res judicata (Daryao pt.2; Nawab Hussain). RESEARCHED

§6 · Public Interest Litigation & the Concept of Pro Bono Publico

PIL is the institutional form of relaxed locus standi: litigation “undertaken for redressing a public injury, enforcing a public duty, protecting social, collective, 'diffused' rights” (DU, para 20). Its operating motto is the DU phrase — the courts act pro bono publico: for the public good, not for any party's private gain.

📌 Concept

A suit or petition where the cause is the public's, the remedy is class-wide or community-wide, and standing rests on sufficient interest rather than personal injury. The litigant may be an NGO, a lawyer, a journalist, an activist — or no litigant at all (suo motu).

🔑 Pro bono publico

Latin: “for the public good”. In the DU passage it is the label for the court's willingness to act on a mere letter disclosing public injury — justice untethered from fee, formality and filing-fee logic. Closely allied: “procedure is but a handmaiden of justice”.

⚖️ Constitutional anchors

Art 32/226 (forum), Art 21 (life/dignity content), Art 39A (equal justice & free legal aid), Art 41–43A+48A/51A(g) (welfare & environment duties), Art 142 (complete justice), Art 141 (binding declaration).

§7 · Power to Issue Appropriate Orders & Directions

Item (b) is the remedial jurisdiction: what the court may actually do after the access doctrines of item (a) admit the case. The DU case book carries no separate excerpt for item (b), so this section is built on the verbatim constitutional text VERBATIM plus the syllabus cases' application RESEARCHED. Remember the DU sentence: “procedure is but a handmaiden of justice” — and the countervailing one from Bandhua: the power, though of “widest amplitude”, “is limited by its very nature, that it is judicial power.”

📜 Article 32 — verbatim (Constitution of India)

32. Remedies for enforcement of rights conferred by this Part.—(1) The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed.

(2) The Supreme Court shall have power to issue directions or orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by this Part.

(3) Without prejudice to the powers conferred on the Supreme Court by clauses (1) and (2), Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise within the local limits of its jurisdiction all or any of the powers exercisable by the Supreme Court under clause (2).

(4) The right guaranteed by this article shall not be suspended except as otherwise provided for by this Constitution.

Reading 32(1)–(4)

  • 32(1) — the right to move is itself a fundamental right (this is why Daryao says citizens are “ordinarily entitled” to relief; and why Trilokchand's Hegde J. called it non-discretionary).
  • 32(2) — the engine of item (b): three species — directions, orders, writs — “whichever may be appropriate”. “Appropriate” is the self-restraint word: relief must fit the breach.
  • 32(3) — residual empowerment of other courts by Parliament; note also Art 139: Parliament may confer on the SC power to issue writs for other purposes beyond 32(2).
  • 32(4) — suspension only as the Constitution itself provides (e.g., Arts 352–360 mechanics; after the 44th Amendment, Arts 20 & 21 can never be suspended).

Why “directions or orders or writs” matters

  • The list is expansive, not exhaustive — the Court has issued continuing directions, monitoring regimes, guidelines, commissions, consent terms and money awards under it.
  • Writs are named “in the nature of” the five classical forms — Indian courts issue them with adapted, flexible scope (e.g., habeas for bonded labour detention).
  • 32(2) is jurisdictional; 32(1) is enforceable against the State machinery itself — K.S. Puttaswamy-style reading of the remedy as substantive.

§8 · Power to Award Compensation

Can a court enforcing a right award money? Until Rudul Sah (1983) the orthodox answer was no — release was the writ's terminal point. Rudul Sah made compensation the second limb of an effective Art 32 remedy; Oleum (1987) then extended the compensatory logic to private hazardous enterprises through absolute liability. These are syllabus cases 49 and 50.

📜 Rudul Sah — the founding formula (verbatim)

“Article 21 would be denuded of its significant content if the power of this Court were limited to passing orders of release from illegal detention. One of the telling ways in which the violation of that right can reasonably be prevented and due compliance with the mandate of Article 21 secured, is to mulct its violators in the payment of monetary compensation.”

— Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar, AIR 1983 SC 1086 (Chandrachud C.J.) RESEARCHED

8.1 The constitutional logic of the award

Right ↔ Remedy symmetry

Art 32(2) says “directions or orders” without excluding money; if the “heart and soul” article cannot award a repair, it would be a right without a remedy for the poorest victims.

Deterrence function

Compensation is the only language the violator understands: it makes violation costly for the State/enterprise and future-proof for citizens — “palliative” justice (Rudul Sah).

Reparation, not bounty

Public-law compensation = reparation for breach of a constitutional duty; distinct from tort damages (which remain claimable in a civil suit — Rudul Sah expressly kept that door open).

DU anchor for Part-II · (the “Locus Standi” extract)

S.P. Gupta v. Union of India — Locus Standi & Public Interest Litigation

1981 Supp (1) SCC 87 (also AIR 1981 SC 1276; “First Judges Case”) · Bhagwati J.'s “Locus Standi” discussion, paras 13–26 · DU case material, book pp. 177–185 DU CASE MATERIAL

Facts (as they bear on Part-II)

The wider litigation concerned the constitutional validity of government action regarding the transfer/posting of High Court judges and the use of presidential communications — a Part-I matter (Topic 6 syllabus item on constitution of courts). What makes it the DU backbone of Part-II is Bhagwati J.'s sweeping discussion of locus standi — who may invoke Art 32/226 — prompted by the question whether ordinary citizens and associations could challenge executive action at all. Within the passage, a group of Bombay lawyers led by Iqbal Chagla figures as the concrete applicants whose standing had to be validated (para 26). The court traversed: the classical private-injury rule; its statutory and public-law exceptions; the disability rule; the public-injury doctrine; the dependence of the Attorney General route on political branches (ss. 91–92 CPC); the PIL definition; and the safeguards against misuse.

Issues

1. Whether the traditional rule — standing only for personal legal injury — must be relaxed in a Constitution dedicated to rule of law and socio-economic justice. 2. Whether a member of the public may move Art 226/Art 32 for an indeterminate class affected by a public injury, and whether a mere letter can be treated as a petition. 3. What limits bind that relaxation (bona fides, justiciability, separation of organs).

Arguments

For liberalisation (the forward case, developed in the opinion): private-law standing “cannot be the measure” of a constitutional court; the rule of law is hollow if only the rich can litigate; the AG/Advocates-General answer to the political branches, so citizens must be allowed to enforce public duties; public wrongs (pollution, prison conditions, bonded labour) have no single “injured owner”; PIL = litigation for redressing public injury, enforcing public duty, protecting social/collective/diffused rights; “sufficient interest” should be discretionary; procedure is but a handmaiden of justice.
The cautionary case (also inside the DU passage): para 17 — petitions must be bona fide, never for personal gain, private profit, political motivation or oblique motives, and courts prudentially prefer determinate-class cases; para 24 — courts must not “overstep the limits of the judicial function and trespass into areas reserved to the Executive and the Legislature”; pressure groups must not weaponise the court; there is a “vital distinction between locus standi and justiciability”; para 25 — a secondary public injury (private grievance in public clothing) is impermissible.

Decision (the four-step doctrine the court laid down)

Step 1 — Traditional rule recognised (para 14): redress only for personal legal injury — a rule “of ancient vintage” from the age “when private law dominated”. Step 2 — Exceptions preserved (paras 15–16): ratepayers (K. Ramadas Shenoy AIR 1974 SC 2177; Varadarajan AIR 1973 Mad 55), decision-making participants (Queen v. Bowman [1898 1 QB 663]), statutory special interest (Jasbhai AIR 1976 SC 578); disability rule — minors (Order XXXII CPC), detenus, prisoners' letter-petitions, minority shareholders. Step 3 — Public-injury relaxation declared (paras 17–20): a member of the public, bona fide and sufficiently interested, may sue for an indeterminate class; courts respond even to a letter, acting pro bono publico; public wrongs must be redressable; PIL defined; “sufficient interest” left to judicial discretion. Step 4 — Limits fenced (paras 24–25): no trespass into executive/legislature domains; no pressure-group litigation; no secondary public injury. Application (para 26): Iqbal Chagla's lawyers have standing — their concern is “deeper than that of a busybody”; they “cannot be told off at the gates”.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“Locus standi in Indian constitutional law is neither classical nor unlimited: it is classical by default, relaxed for public injury, conditioned by sufficient interest and bona fides, and bounded by justiciability and separation of powers.” The court's liberalised standing is the doorway; paras 24–25 are the doorframe. This passage — with Trilokchand's restraint observations — is the DU-cited balance-sheet of judicial activism.

Syllabus Case 46 · Item (a) — Res Judicata

Daryao v. State of U.P.

AIR 1961 SC 1457; (1962) 1 SCR 574 · decided 27-03-1961 · Gajendragadkar C.J. · no DU excerpt — built from standard sources RESEARCHED

Facts

The petitioners (in a dispute over land/property against the State of U.P. and others) first moved the Allahabad High Court under Art 226. The High Court heard the matter and dismissed the petition on merits — by a speaking order — holding that no fundamental right of the petitioners had been proved to be infringed, or that the alleged contravention was constitutionally justified. Undeterred, on 14 March 1956 the petitioners filed a petition in the Supreme Court under Art 32, raising precisely the same grounds and seeking the same or similar writs. The respondents raised a preliminary objection: the claim was barred by res judicata. The Court noted that the question of res judicata had earlier been left open (Fazl Ali J. had “reserved opinion” whether a 32 petition lies after a 226 dismissal) — Daryao was the occasion to settle it.

Issues

1. Whether a petition under Art 32 is barred when the same party has already obtained an adverse on-merits decision of the High Court under Art 226 on the same facts and reliefs. 2. Whether the doctrine of res judicata (s.11 CPC / public-policy maxims) is at all applicable to fundamental-rights petitions — i.e., whether Art 32 immunity defeats it. 3. Whether Art 32 confers only a discretionary power (as Art 226 does), such that the earlier forum's competence objection has force.

Arguments

Petitioner: (i) Res judicata is a technical rule akin to estoppel and irrelevant to enforcement of fundamental rights; (ii) Art 32(1) is itself a fundamental right — applying a procedural bar shrinks it unconstitutionally; (iii) the competence test of s.11 fails because the High Court trying under Art 226 is not the court that would try under Art 32, and the reliefs differ; (iv) in England successive habeas corpus petitions are entertained — dismissal of one never bars the next; (v) the High Court judgment is not the “same” adjudication for an Art 32 purpose.
Respondents: (i) Art 32 guarantees only an “appropriate proceeding”, not an original hearing; after a 226 verdict the proper route was Art 136; (ii) finality of litigation rests on public policy — a person shall not be vexed twice for the same cause; (iii) Raj Lakshmi Dasi v. Banamali Sen shows res judicata can operate even where the first court could not have tried the second matter — identity of jurisdiction is not essential; (iv) the High Court's jurisdiction under 226 is substantially the same as the Supreme Court's under 32 — same parties, same cause of action, same character of relief.

Decision

  • Dismissed. The Art 32 petition is barred by res judicata because the Allahabad High Court had decided the identical matter on merits.
  • Four-point test adopted (as in §4.1): merits dismissal → barred; laches/alternative-remedy dismissal → generally not barred (except where facts found are relevant under Art 32); in limine with order → depends on the order's nature; in limine without a speaking order or withdrawn → no bar.
  • Rejected the petitioner's doctrinal case: res judicata “rests on maxims… taken from Roman law” and is not technical estoppel; the s.11 competence test is substantially satisfied; the habeas-corpus analogy was distinguished (“no analogy”); Art 32 is not a mere discretionary power like Art 226 — “citizens have ordinarily the right to invoke Art 32”.
  • Confirmed reach: the rule “can be invoked even against a petition filed under Art 32” — including where the earlier decision was of the Supreme Court itself in a prior Art 32 round.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“A final on-merits decision of the High Court under Art 226 is res judicata against a subsequent Art 32 petition by the same party on the same facts and reliefs — the public interest in finality and the maxim against twice-vexation are not suspended by the fundamental-rights character of the remedy. But only a speaking merits decision binds: dismissals for laches, want of prosecution, alternative remedy, or in limine without reasons leave the Art 32 door open.” → connects: §4 (res judicata), §3 (laches no-bar), §5 (exhaustion no-bar).

Syllabus Case 47 · Item (a) — Laches · Self-Restraint · Exhaustion

Trilokchand Motichand v. H.B. Munshi

(1969) 1 SCC 110; AIR 1970 SC 898; [1969] 2 SCR 824 · decided 22-11-1968 · Constitution Bench (5 Judges), Hidayatullah C.J. · no DU excerpt RESEARCHED

Facts

The petitioners were Bombay traders from whom sales tax was collected under the Bombay Sales Tax Act, 1953; a refund of ₹26,563.50 was withheld/forfeited because they did not satisfy the statutory condition of passing the tax on to their customers (the “passed-on” condition). In 1958 they challenged the levy/condition in the Bombay High Court under Art 226, arguing ultra vires and infringement of fundamental rights — the petition was dismissed in limine without adverting to those contentions. Years later, the Supreme Court's decision in Kantilal Babulal (discovered 29-09-1967) appeared to vindicate their position. On 9 February 1968 — roughly a decade after the payments and five years after the 226 dismissal — they filed an Art 32 petition claiming refund as money paid “under a mistake of law or coercion” under s. 72, Indian Contract Act, 1872.

Issues

1. Whether the petition was barred by res judicata given the earlier High Court disposal. 2. Whether an Art 32 petition — for which no period of limitation is prescribed — can be refused on grounds of delay/laches, and whether the Limitation Act or its analogies apply. 3. Whether the power under Art 32 is discretionary (as under Art 226) or a matter of right.

Arguments

For the petitioners: Art 32 is a guaranteed right; no limitation statute applies directly or indirectly; the Court's power under Art 32 is not discretionary; Hegde J. — “a relief asked for under Article 32 cannot be refused on the ground of laches”; law reports show no instance of refusal on delay; importing laches would “pull down from the high pedestal now occupied by the fundamental rights”; the earlier dismissal was in limine (no merits) so no res judicata; Sikri J. — even a stale-claim doctrine needs extreme delay: yardstick ≈ one year from discovery of the right, and they moved within six months of Kantilal Babulal; time pursuing remedies explains delay.
For the State/respondent (and the majority): the writ under Art 32 issues as a matter of course on breach — but “this does not mean that in giving relief the Court must ignore and trample under foot all laws of procedure — evidence, limitation, res judicata and the like”; where the writ remedy corresponds to a suit remedy limited by statute, the Court “adopts the statute as its own rule of procedure”; statutes of limitation rest on public policy (vigilantibus non dormientibus jura subveniunt); the extraordinary remedies of Arts 32/226 “are not intended to enable a claimant to recover monies the recovery of which by suit is barred by limitation”; a petition will not be entertained containing “misleading and inaccurate statements”; comity — “extraordinary remedies should not take the place of ordinary remedies”.

Decision

  • Res judicata — NO (Full Court). The High Court had dismissed in limine without merits; applying Daryao (points 3–4), there was no bar — “the expression [res judicata] is somewhat inapt and unfortunate”, but the principle applies on public-policy grounds driven by the parallel jurisdiction of the two courts (comity).
  • Laches — YES (majority; Sikri and Hegde JJ. dissenting). The Limitation Act does not directly apply, but by analogy/public policy the Court treats the analogous period as the measure of a permissible claim; a ~10-year-old claim for money recoverable by suit (now time-barred) is stale. Petition dismissed.
  • Discretion/character of Art 32: majority treated the jurisdiction as accompanied by discretionary self-restraints — hence the famous headnote: instances of self-restraint = existence of other ordinary remedy, delay, comity with the High Court, refusal of fresh grounds after an adverse 226 round.
  • Delay-explanation rule: time spent in appeals/representations to authorities “may ordinarily be taken as a good explanation for the delay”.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“The right to move under Art 32 is guaranteed, but the remedy is not immune from equitable self-discipline: the Court may refuse relief for unexplained delay measured by the analogy of limitation, for the existence of an efficacious alternative remedy, or out of comity to a parallel forum — while an in-limine, non-speaking High Court order never bars a fresh Art 32 petition.” This single case teaches laches (§3), exhaustion (§5), the non-merits rule of res judicata (§4), and judicial self-restraint (§1).

Syllabus Case 48 · Item (a) — Locus Standi & PIL in action

Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India

(1984) 3 SCC 161; AIR 1984 SC 802 · decided 16-12-1983 · Bhagwati J., R.S. Pathak & A.N. Sen JJ. · no DU excerpt RESEARCHED

Facts

Bandhua Mukti Morcha — an organisation campaigning against bonded labour — addressed a letter dated 25-02-1982 to Justice P.N. Bhagwati alleging that hundreds of migrant labourers were working in the stone quarries and stone-crushers of Faridabad district, Haryana, under “inhuman and intolerable” conditions: hazardous dust, non-payment of wages, no drinking water, no medical care, rampant accidents, and workers not allowed to leave until “advances” were settled. The Supreme Court treated the letter as a writ petition under Art 32 and (on 26-02-1982) appointed two advocates — Ashok Srivastava and Ashok Panda — as Commissioners to investigate. Their reports (later reinforced by two further Commissioners) and worker affidavits (24-08-1982) confirmed forced labour: e.g., of ~250 persons in straw jhuggies at Lakarpur, 100 from Bilaspur and 150 from Allahabad, many “forcibly kept by the contractor”. The State of Haryana and mine-lessees denied everything.

Issues

1. Whether a letter can be entertained as a writ petition, and whether an NGO like BMM has locus standi to litigate for workers who cannot approach the court (PIL). 2. Whether the Supreme Court may appoint commissions to ascertain facts in a PIL (inherent power?). 3. Whether, on a showing of forced labour, the labourer must be presumed a bonded labourer within the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976, and who must rebut. 4. What relief Art 32/21/23 permits — release alone, or rehabilitation and statutory welfare enforcement.

Arguments

Petitioner (BMM): the letter discloses violation of workers' fundamental rights; indigent, migrant, possibly detained workers cannot themselves move the Court — exactly the public-injury situation of S.P. Gupta paras 17–20; forced labour is prohibited by Art 23; life with dignity (Art 21) requires water, health, wages; the State's constitutional obligation extends to ensuring lessees/contractors observe labour-welfare laws.
Respondents — ASG (for the Union/State) & lessee's counsel: (i) even if the letter's averments were true, no fundamental right of the petitioners or of the workers was infringed — no locus; (ii) worker affidavits are untested — no cross-examination of the deponents, so no reliable proof; (iii) definitionally, a bonded labourer must have incurred a bonded debt — nothing shows an “advance or economic consideration”; there may be forced labourers, but not “bonded” labourers under the Act; (iv) State of Haryana: there is no bonded labour in these quarries (an assertion the material contradicted).

Decision

  • Letter entertained; preliminary objections overruled. Art 32 permits “appropriate proceedings”; Art 32/226 lay down no prescribed procedure; where victims are indigent and in bondage, PIL enlarges locus standi — refusal would defeat the constitutional mandate (“procedure is a handmaiden”).
  • Commission power inherent in Art 32. The traditional adversarial model is unsuited to vulnerable groups; commissioners' reports supply prima facie material the court may act on.
  • Burden shifted: “Whenever it is shown that a labourer is made to provide forced labour, the Court would raise a presumption that he is required to do so in consideration of an advance or other economic consideration… and he is therefore a bonded labourer.” Rebuttable only by satisfactory material from the employer/State — which was absent.
  • Relief package (mandatory directions) to the Union, State of Haryana and quarry owners: identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labourers; wages, medical facilities, drinking water, sanitation, safety (dust control, protective gear), schooling; strict enforcement of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976, Mines Act 1952, Workmen's Compensation Act 1923 and allied welfare laws — with continuing supervision (later follow-up orders in 1991 on implementation failure).
  • Art 21 read with Arts 39(c), 41, 42 (Directive Principles) requires more than release — rehabilitation is part of the right to life; non-implementation itself is a constitutional failure.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“In PIL the court may reshape procedure to reach substance: a letter = petition; standing = public interest; facts = through its own commissioners; burden on the custodian of power to disprove bondage; and relief that rehabilitates, not merely releases. Bandhua is S.P. Gupta's doctrine operationalised — locus standi relaxed (paras 17–20), limits respected (it decided rights, not policy — para 24), remedy of ‘widest amplitude’ yet ‘judicial’ in nature.”

Syllabus Case 49 · Item (b) — Power to award Compensation

Rudul Sah v. State of Bihar

AIR 1983 SC 1086; (1983) 4 SCC 141 · decided 01-08-1983 · Chandrachud C.J., A.N. Sen & R.B. Misra JJ. · no DU excerpt RESEARCHED

Facts

Rudul Sah was arrested on the charge of murdering his wife. On 3 June 1968 the Sessions Court at Muzaffarpur acquitted him — yet he remained in jail for over fourteen years, finally released on 16 October 1982. The State's explanations were shifting and evidence-free: a jailer's affidavit said the Additional Sessions Judge had ordered that he be detained “till further orders” as a person of “unsound mind”, but there was no medical record, no diagnosis, no treatment proof, and no order authorising post-acquittal detention; eventually the State conceded administrative lapses. His family moved the Supreme Court under Art 32 (habeas corpus), seeking release (soon infructuous), State-funded medical treatment, rehabilitation, and compensation for 14 years of unlawful imprisonment.

Issues

1. Whether continued detention after acquittal violated Art 21 (procedure established by law). 2. Whether, in a habeas/Art 32 proceeding, the Court can go beyond ordering release and award monetary compensation for the FR violation — and if so, on what footing (palliative? reparation? deterrent?). 3. Whether the plea of insanity (unsupported) could justify the detention.

Arguments

Petitioner: 14 years' post-acquittal imprisonment is an absolute violation of Arts 21 and 32; mere release cannot undo the harm — lost liberty, mental agony, stigma, destitution; the Court must have power to compensate, otherwise Art 32 delivers liberty without justice; sought medical costs and rehabilitation funds.
State of Bihar: the detention was pursuant to the Additional Sessions Judge's order (produce only on government/IG-Prisons orders); Rudul Sah was of “unsound mind” and was kept for treatment in the jail hospital; the Court's jurisdiction under Art 21/32 is limited to ordering release from unlawful detention — compensation is outside the writ's province (and belongs, if anywhere, to a civil claim).

Decision

  • Detention wholly unjustified. “The State's response was ruthless and had no factual basis”; even a genuinely mentally ill person retains legal rights in trial — insanity cannot licence post-acquittal imprisonment without procedure.
  • Compensation awarded for the first time under Art 32: the Court directed the State of Bihar to pay ₹30,000 as interim compensation within two weeks, in addition to ₹5,000 already paid (₹35,000 total).
  • The formula: “Article 21 would be denuded of its significant content if the power of this Court were limited to passing orders of release from illegal detention. One of the telling ways in which the violation of that right can reasonably be prevented and due compliance with the mandate of Article 21 secured, is to mulct its violators in the payment of monetary compensation.”
  • Scope preserved: the award is interim/palliative; it does not bar Rudul Sah from filing a separate civil suit against the State and its officers for full damages.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“Constitutional compensation: where the State breaches Art 21, the Art 32 court's remedy includes money — release without reparation is an incomplete remedy. Compensation here performs three functions at once: palliative (rebuild the victim's life), deterrent (price future violations), and declaratory (State liability for agents' lawlessness). It is a public-law award, distinct from — and not a bar to — private-law damages; quantum may be enhanced on fuller material.”

Syllabus Case 50 · Item (b) — Appropriate orders, directions & compensation

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India — the Oleum Gas Leak Case

AIR 1987 SC 1086 · judgment 20-12-1986 (reported 1987) · Constitution Bench, Bhagwati C.J. · no DU excerpt RESEARCHED

Facts

Shriram Foods and Fertilizer Industries (SFFI) — a unit of Delhi Cloth Mills Ltd. — sat in Kirti Nagar, Delhi, amid a population of lakhs, manufacturing caustic soda, chlorine, sulphuric acid and oleum (fuming sulphuric acid). Environmental lawyer M.C. Mehta had filed a PIL under Arts 21 and 32 seeking closing/relocation of the caustic-chlorine and sulphuric-acid plants to safer sites. While the petition was pending, on 4 December 1985 oleum gas leaked — an advocate practicing at Tis Hazari courts died, many workers and residents were hospitalised; a second, smaller leak followed on 6 December. The District Magistrate acted under s. 133 CrPC (order dated 6-12-1985) directing the unit to cease handling dangerous substances. The Supreme Court, managing the crisis through interim consent conditions (eleven conditions on CPCB monitoring, safety board, protective gear, alarm systems, personal undertakings of the DCM Chairman, a ₹20 lakh deposit + ₹15 lakh bank guarantee for compensation claims, on reports of expert committees including Manmohan Singh and Nilay Choudhary), referred the constitutional questions to a larger bench. Applications for compensation were made by the Delhi Legal Aid & Advice Board and the Delhi Bar Association on behalf of victims.

Issues

1. Whether compensation claims are maintainable under Art 32 (the original petition had not pleaded compensation; new applicants were not parties). 2. Whether Art 21 can be enforced against a private enterprise — including the Art 12 “other authority” question and the effect of governmental consents/licences/grants. 3. Whether the English rule in Rylands v. Fletcher (strict liability with exceptions) suffices for hazardous industries in India, or a new principle is required. 4. Whether permanent closure should be ordered, or regulated operation with conditions — balancing life, livelihood and development.

Arguments

Petitioner / applicants: the leaks infringed the right to life (Art 21 includes right to a pollution-free environment) of an unidentifiable class — PIL standing and letter-style flexibility apply; victims are poor and cannot litigate individually — compensation applications must be entertained; expert committee opinion: risk can be reduced by safety measures but not eliminated — relocation is the only long-term solution; enterprises in inherently dangerous trades must answer for harm regardless of care taken; the Court should direct closure/relocation and award compensation.
Respondents: Shriram: (i) no compensation prayer in the original petition, and the new applicants (Delhi Legal Aid Board, Bar Association) were not parties; (ii) Shriram is a private company outside Art 12 — Art 21 cannot run against it; (iii) permanent closure would idly displace ~4,000 workers and cripple an essential-goods plant; liability, if any, must follow Rylands with its exceptions. State: interim safety conditions (the eleven-condition regime) can mitigate the risk, so blanket closure is unnecessary.

Decision

  • Compensation maintainable. Applications by the Delhi Legal Aid & Advice Board and Delhi Bar Association “cannot be thrown out” — they enforce the Art 21 right of persons who “by reason of poverty or disability… cannot approach a Court of law”; Art 32 procedure flexes for them.
  • Art 21 reaches the hazardous enterprise: the Court examined Art 12 (“other authority”) dimensions and the State-regulated, licensed, consented and subsidised character of the enterprise; an undertaking engaged in inherently dangerous activity owes “an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community” — highest safety standards, and liability for harm.
  • New rule — absolute liability (formulation): “Where an enterprise is engaged in a hazardous or dangerous activity and harm results… the enterprise is strictly and absolutely liable… not subject to any of the exceptions… under the rule in Rylands v. Fletcher.” No act of God, stranger, victim's fault, consent or statutory authority. Quantum: “larger and more prosperous the enterprise, greater the compensation” (deterrence/deep pockets); “compensation is the social cost” of the activity.
  • Orders and directions: industry not permanently closed (balance with livelihoods/national production) but regulated by the consent-condition regime, continuous CPCB inspection, safety boards, personal undertakings, deposit and bank guarantee for victims; the Court considered and favoured setting up environmental courts (expert, time-bound remedy) as a systemic direction.
  • Right content: Art 21 includes the right to live in a clean, healthy, pollution-free environment — anchoring environmental PIL.

Principle

Rule to write in the exam

“Three propositions: (1) Forum — Art 32 compensation applications on behalf of an unidentifiable, indigent class are maintainable as PIL; (2) Right — Art 21 embraces a pollution-free environment and binds even private hazardous enterprises through the State's regulatory weave; (3) Remedy — absolute, exceptionless, size-scaled liability, enforced not by shutting industry down but by appropriate orders and directions — conditions, monitoring, security, undertaking — under continuing judicial supervision. This is item (b) in one case: orders, directions, and compensation.”

December 2024 Examination · Q6(c) · 10 marks — “Write short notes on any two of the following: … (c) Doctrine of locus standi”
Write a short note on the Doctrine of locus standi. (10 marks — DU LL.B. III Semester, Dec 2024)
Model answer (≈ 450 words — structured for full marks)

Meaning. Locus standi (“place to stand”) denotes the right of a person to be heard by a court — whether the litigant has a sufficient connection to the grievance to sustain the proceeding.

1. The traditional rule. At common law and in classical Indian practice, only a person whose personal legal right or interest is infringed may sue; general public grievances were nobody's cause of action. As Bhagwati J. observed in S.P. Gupta (1981 Supp (1) SCC 87), this is “a rule of ancient vintage” from the age “when private law dominated”.

2. Recognised exceptions. (i) Ratepayer/taxpayer (K. Ramadas Shenoy AIR 1974 SC 2177; Varadarajan AIR 1973 Mad 55); (ii) participant in the decision-making process (Queen v. Bowman [1898 1 QB 663]); (iii) statutory special interest (Jasbhai Motibhai Desai AIR 1976 SC 578); and (iv) the disability rule — minors sue through a next friend (Order XXXII CPC), detenus through others (habeas corpus), prisoners through letter-petitions, minorities through shareholders.

3. The public-injury relaxation (PIL). For wrongs to an indeterminate class — pollution, prison cruelty, bonded labour — the court holds that “a member of the public acting bona fide and having sufficient interest” may move the High Court under Art 226 and the Supreme Court under Art 32; courts will “readily respond even to a letter… acting pro bono publico”, because “procedure is but a handmaiden of justice”. PIL = litigation for redressing public injury, enforcing public duty, and protecting social, collective and diffused rights; “sufficient interest” is deliberately left to judicial discretion. The Attorney-General route (ss. 91–92 CPC) depends on the political branches, so standing had to be liberalised. Applied: the letter in Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984) 3 SCC 161 became a writ petition.

4. Limits. The relaxation is conditioned: petitions must be bona fide — not for personal gain, political motivation or oblique ends; pressure groups must not weaponise the court; there must be no “secondary public injury”; and courts “must not overstep… and trespass into areas reserved to the Executive and the Legislature” — a “vital distinction between locus standi and justiciability” (S.P. Gupta, paras 24–25). Stale claims remain defeatable by laches (Trilokchand).

Conclusion. The modern doctrine is thus: classical by default, relaxed for public injury on sufficient interest, disciplined by bona fides and justiciability — a doorway wide, a doorframe firm.

December 2024 Examination · Q6(b) · 10 marks — companion question to item (a)'s “overreach/self-restraint”
Write a short note on the Theory of Separation of Powers. (10 marks — DU LL.B. III Semester, Dec 2024 — adjacent to “Judicial Activism and overreach/self-restraint”)
Model answer (≈ 400 words — tailored to this topic)

Core. Montesquieu's theory (L'Esprit des Lois, 1748) — legislative makes, executive executes, judicial decides — assigns each function to a distinct organ to prevent tyranny through concentration. Its genius is functional: liberty survives when power checks power.

Indian position. The Constitution does not adopt a rigid mechanical separation (as the USA does) but a system of checks and balances with functional independence: Art 50 directs the State to secure judicial independence; the judiciary alone adjudicates (Arts 32/226/132–136); legislatures legislate (Arts 107–122/211–212); the executive governs (Arts 52–78/155–167). Separation is thus a basic-structure principle (Indira Nehru Gandhi; L. Chandra Kumar's discussion of the institutional integrity of courts), not a bar on cooperation — the “deliberate mix” of ordinance powers, delegated legislation and judicial review shows a designed interlock.

Link to this topic — activism, overreach, restraint. Judicial review is the judiciary's check on the other organs (Art 13; basic structure; Arts 32/226). Activism = courts energising remedies (letters as petitions, commissions, compensation — Bandhua, Rudul Sah, Oleum); overreach = the court stepping into policy domains reserved to the elected branches; self-restraint is the corrective: comity with High Courts, insistence on ordinary remedies, laches, res judicata and exhaustion (Trilokchand: “extraordinary remedies should not take the place of ordinary remedies”), and the S.P. Gupta limit — courts “must not… trespass into areas reserved to the Executive and the Legislature” (para 24). The health of the separation scheme is measured precisely by that balance: courts that decide rights enforce the Constitution; courts that decide policy unsettle it.

Conclusion. In India the theory survives as functional separation with calibrated overlap — each organ checking the others, the judiciary enforcing the Constitution's own limits on its own jurisdiction.
