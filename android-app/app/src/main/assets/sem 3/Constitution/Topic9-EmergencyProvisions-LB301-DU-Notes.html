Orientation & Exam Map

Topics 7 and 8 divided legislative and economic power between Union and States. Topic 9 asks the darkest question in federalism: what happens when the normal machinery breaks down — or when the Union says it has? The Constitution's answer is a graded set of "emergency valves" (Part XVIII) plus a standing Union direction-powers machinery (Arts 256–257, 365). Read together, they form one architecture: Art 355 declares the Union's duty; Arts 256–257 give it standing directions; Art 365 converts non-compliance into "failure of constitutional machinery"; Art 356 supplies the sanction; Art 352 supplies the national-level override; Arts 358–359 suspend rights; Art 360 is the rarely-thought-of financial valve.

Syllabus (verbatim). "(a) Proclamation of Emergency on grounds of war, external aggression and armed rebellion (Articles 352, 358, 359); (b) Power of Union Executive to issue directions (e.g. Articles 256, 257) and the effect of non-compliance (Article 365); Duty of the Union to protect the States against external aggression and internal disturbance (Article 355); (c) Imposition of President's Rule in States – Grounds, Limitations, Parliamentary Control, Judicial Review (Articles 356-357); (d) Financial Emergency (Article 360)" — Topic 9, 6 classes.

The constitutional bargain, in one breath. Emergency provisions are deliberately "the most stringent parts of the Constitution", yet they were made self-extinguishing: every power here is conditioned — written Cabinet advice (Art 352(3)), time limits (one month / two months / six months), special majorities, an Electoral-Certificate gate after one year (Art 356(5)), parliamentary disapproval resolutions (Art 352(7)–(8)), and — the great post-1977 innovation — judicial review of the material behind the President's "satisfaction" (S.R. §1 The Emergency Architecture — Part XVIII & its satellites

🔴 National Emergency

Art 352 — war / external aggression / armed rebellion (earlier "internal disturbance")
Effect engines: Art 353 (Union executive + legislative expansion) · 358 (Art 19 automatically, only war/external aggression) · 359 (other rights by Presidential order; never Arts 20, 21) · 357 (laws for States) · 250 (Parliament legislates on State List).

🟡 State Emergency (President's Rule)

Arts 356–357 — failure of constitutional machinery in a State.
Triggered by: Governor's report or otherwise; Art 365 (non-compliance with Union directions = deemed failure); Art 355 backdrop.
Effects: assume State functions · Parliament exercises State legislature · suspension/“suspended animation” · 357 saving for laws.

🟠 Financial Emergency

Art 360 — financial stability or credit of India threatened.
Effects: directions on canons of financial propriety · reduce salaries (State staff; all Union staff including SC/HC Judges) · reserve Money Bills · two-month approval.
Never invoked in India.

🧵 How the pieces link (the sentence to memorise)

  1. Art 355: it is the duty of the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure constitutional government in every State.
  2. Arts 256–257: standing executive power of the Union to give directions (compliance with Union laws; no impediment to Union executive power; national/military communications; protection of railways — extra costs reimbursed).
  3. Art 365: if a State fails to comply with any such direction, "it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution" — i.e. the deemed gateway to Art 356.
  4. Art 356: the proclamation — assume functions, declare State legislative powers exercisable by Parliament, make incidental provisions (never touching High Courts), subject to two-month/six-month/three-year clocks.
  5. Art 352: the national-level override, which then expands Union power further (353), can lift Art 19 (358) and suspend court-enforcement of other rights (359).
  6. Art 360: the financial valve — parallel machinery, never used.

🕰 Why the text reads the way it does — amendment history in brief

  • 38th Amdt (1975) — Emergency-era tightening: retrospective insertions into Arts 352, 356 (old cl. (5)) and 359 (new cl. (1A)) and 360 (old cl. (5)) — ins. and omitted within four years.
  • 42nd Amdt (1976, w.e.f. 3-1-1977) — "The Emergency Constitution": partial-proclamation powers in 352(1), Art 353 proviso, 356 one-year clauses, 357(2), 358 proviso, 359 provisos.
  • 44th Amdt (1978, w.e.f. 20-6-1979) — the great repair: "internal disturbances" → "armed rebellion"; Art 352(3) Cabinet-in-writing; approval/disapproval machinery; Arts 20–21 carved out of Art 359(1); Art 358 confined to war/external-aggression emergencies and made recital-based; Art 360(2) approval; Art 356's one-year gate with Electoral Commission certificate.
  • Punjab cluster: 59th (359A, 1988) · 63rd (omits 359A & the Punjab proviso, 1989) · 64th (re-inserts Punjab proviso, 1990) · 67th & 68th (11-5-1987 Punjab Proclamation: three years → five years).

§2 Article 352 — Proclamation of Emergency (war · external aggression · armed rebellion)

Art 352(1) (as amended — key words bracketed). "If the President is satisfied that a grave emergency exists whereby the security of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened, whether by war or external aggression or [armed rebellion], he may, by Proclamation, make a declaration to that effect [in respect of the whole of India or of such part of the territory thereof as may be specified in the Proclamation]." Explanation: a Proclamation "may be made before the actual occurrence of war or of any such aggression or rebellion, if the President is satisfied that there is imminent danger thereof."bracketed words: 42nd Amdt s.48 (whole/part) & 44th Amdt s.37 (armed rebellion)

⚙️ Procedure clauses (all post-1978)

  • (2) may be varied/revoked by subsequent Proclamation.
  • (3) — the 44th Amdt's master-key: no Proclamation (or variation) unless the decision of the Union Cabinet "that such a Proclamation may be issued has been communicated to him in writing".
  • (4) laid before each House; ceases to operate at one month unless approved by resolutions of both Houses (with the Lok-Sabha-dissolved proviso → 30 days after reconstitution).
  • (5) once approved, ceases after six months; renewable six months at a time by both Houses' resolutions — never beyond six months if Lok Sabha dissolved (same 30-day rule).
  • (6) approving/resolving House needs majority of total membership + ⅔ of members present and voting (double special majority).
  • (7) President shall revoke if the Lok Sabha passes a disapproving resolution.
  • (8) notice by one-tenth members → special sitting within 14 days to consider disapproval.
  • (9) power to issue different Proclamations on different grounds (e.g. war in one region, rebellion in another), even simultaneously.

📜 Grounds: the three words and how they got there

  • Original 1950: "war or external aggression or internal disturbance".
  • 26-10-1962: first Proclamation — Chinese attack on the NEFA; Art 19 suspended via Art 358; Art 359 order covering Arts 14, 21, 22 (Defence of India Ordinance link). 1971: 3-12-1971 (Indo-Pak war); 1975: 25-6-1975 ("internal disturbance") — both revoked on 21-3-1977.
  • Controversy → cure: the 1975 declaration on the vague ground of "internal disturbance" (used alongside preventive detentions, censorship, and the Art 359 order) directly produced the 44th Amdt: "internal disturbances" substituted by "armed rebellion" (s.37, w.e.f. 20-6-1979). Today's text thus demands an objective, externalisable ground; an internal-law-and-order collapse per se is the subject-matter of Arts 355–356, not Art 352.
  • Partial emergencies: valid only since the 42nd Amdt — a Proclamation may cover a specified part of India (the original text allowed only a whole-of-India declaration).
  • Explanation (44th Amdt): prospective declarations permitted — imminent danger.

📌 Exam-grade details

  • Art 366(18) defines "Proclamation of Emergency" as one under 352(1) — so Arts 358/359/250/253(?) keys are read distributively: 358's trigger is narrower than the definition.
  • 1975 → 1978 causation: the 38th Amdt (retrospective cl. (5)-type insertions, protected orders) was swept away by the 44th Amdt's procedural gates; the most-cited constitutional turning point is the substitution of "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion".
  • The double lock after one year (Art 356(5)-style gate for States) has no Art 352 counterpart — instead Art 352's continuance is open-ended as long as Parliament keeps approving every six months, which is why the political off-ramps (352(7)–(8)) were added.
  • Art 352 vs Art 356: 352 = whole/part of India, security-of-India threshold, rights-suspension engines; 356 = one State, constitutional-machinery threshold, no rights suspension (358/359 do not apply to State Emergencies — a classic trap).

§3 Articles 358 & 359 — Suspending rights during an Emergency

Art 358 — suspension of Art 19 (automatic, external-only)

358(1): "While a Proclamation of Emergency [declaring that the security of India … is threatened by war or by external aggression] is in operation, nothing in Article 19 shall restrict the power of the State … to make any law or to take any executive action which the State would but for Part III be competent to make or to take", but such law ceases to have effect when the Proclamation ceases, except as to things already done. Proviso (42nd Amdt): for a part-only Proclamation, extended to unaffected States if their security is threatened by activities in the affected part. 358(2) (44th Amdt): protection only for laws containing a recital that they relate to the Proclamation — otherwise Art 19 remains a shield.
  • Scope: only Art 19; trigger: only war or external-aggression Proclamations — not armed-rebellion emergencies (44th Amdt, s.39: words substituted for "while a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation"; cl. (1) renumbering).
  • Operation: automatic — no separate order; suspension runs with the Proclamation; both right and remedy gone; legislature/executive may act freely on declared emergencies (e.g., censorship laws in 1962/1971/1975).
  • 1975 instance: Art 19 was switched off for the entire emergency (1975–77), enabling the MISA detentions and press censorship that the 44th Amdt thereafter disciplined.

Art 359 — suspension of enforcement of other rights (order-based)

359(1): "Where a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, the President may by order declare that the right to move any court for the enforcement of such of the rights conferred by Part III (except Articles 20 and 21) as may be mentioned in the order and all proceedings pending in any court … shall remain suspended for the period during which the Proclamation is in force or for such shorter period as may be specified in the order." (1A) the named rights do not restrict State law-making/executive action (recital-based laws protected; lapse on order's end). (1B) recital requirement. (2) whole or part of India (extension rules). (3) laid before each House.
  • Scope: any Part III right named in the order — but never Arts 20 and 21 (the 44th Amdt's immutable core: protection in respect of conviction; life & personal liberty); trigger: any Proclamation (internal or external); operation: needs a Presidential order; what is suspended: only the remedy (right to move court) — the right itself continues, and violations remain actionable after the order lapses.
  • Art 359A (Application of Part XVIII to Punjab) was inserted by the 59th Amdt, 1988 (with a two-year sunset), omitted by the 63rd Amdt, 1989 (w.e.f. 6-1-1990) — a reminder that the emergency machinery itself has been made state-specific in the past.

⚖️ Case-law on the suspension machinery

  • Makhan Singh Tuli v. State of Punjab, AIR 1964 SC 381 — cleaned the lines: Art 358 suspends Art 19 wholesale when the emergency is in force; Art 359 suspends only the enforcement of the rights named in the order; courts must look to the substance of a detention order, not its form, so that a detention cannot survive merely because another statute's route was used.
  • ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976) 2 SCC 521 — during the 1975 Emergency, the majority (five Judges, overruling the wider protection) held that after an Art 359 order there was no right to move any court — even to contest detention under Art 21; H.R. Khanna J dissented (life cannot be taken without process), a dissent later vindicated. The Union's 1975 order under Art 359 named Arts 14, 21 and 22.
  • 44th Amdt (1978) — the structural reply: Art 359(1) rewritten to exclude Arts 20 and 21; Art 21 given "due process" footing by cl. (2); 358 narrowed to war/external aggression + recital discipline.
  • Union of India v. K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) 10 SCC 1 — reads the post-44th Art 21 as a substantive guarantee; during the pandemic-era discussions the settled position is that no Art 359 order can ever touch Arts 20–21.

🧠 Common exam traps

  • Trap 1: "Art 358 applies to armed-rebellion emergencies." — No. Since the 44th Amdt it applies only to war/external-aggression Proclamations (Art 358(1) as substituted).
  • Trap 2: "During President's Rule under Art 356, Art 19 is automatically suspended." — No. Arts 358/359 are engines of Art 352 only.
  • Trap 3: "A 359 order abolishes the right." — No: only the right to move court + pending proceedings; the right revives and old violations become justiciable again.
  • Trap 4: "Arts 20–21 can be suspended in a genuine war." — No: the 44th Amdt made them untouchable, full stop.
  • Trap 5: recital: a law without the emergency-recital gets no 358/359(1A) protection — it is tested on ordinary grounds.

§4 Articles 355, 256, 257 & 365 — The Union's directions machinery

Art 355 — Duty of the Union to protect States. "It shall be the duty of the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure that the Government of every State is carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution."

Art 256 — Obligation of States and the Union. "The executive power of every State shall be so exercised as to ensure compliance with the laws made by Parliament and any existing laws which apply in that State, and the executive power of the Union shall extend to the giving of such directions to a State as may appear to the Government of India to be necessary for that purpose."

Art 257 — Control of the Union over States in certain cases. (1) State executive power shall not "impede or prejudice" the Union's executive power — with reciprocal direction power. (2) Directions as to construction/maintenance of means of communication "declared … to be of national or military importance" (Parliament may declare highways/waterways; Union retains its naval/military/air-force works functions). (3) Directions as to measures for the protection of the railways within the State. (4) Extra costs reimbursed: excess expenditure caused by (2)/(3) directions paid by Government of India — agreed sum or, failing agreement, an arbitrator appointed by the Chief Justice of India.

Art 365 — Effect of failure to comply with directions. "Where any State has failed to comply with, or to give effect to, any directions given in the exercise of the executive power of the Union under any of the provisions of this Constitution, it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution."

🔍 Art 355 — two limbs

Jeevan Reddy J in S.R. Bommai DU excerpt ✓: Art 355 is "both a responsibility and a power" — (i) protect against external aggression and internal disturbance; (ii) ensure constitutional government in every State. It is the constitutional hinge on which Arts 256–257 directions and Art 356 action hang, and it runs to the Union's protection of States, not their punishment — a point exploited for the Kailash PYQ (§9).

📜 Arts 256–257 — what the directions cover

Compliance with Union laws (256) · non-impediment of Union executive functions (257(1)) · national/military communications (257(2)) · protection of railways (257(3)) · cost-sharing with CJI-arbitration backstop (257(4)). Related: Art 255 — recommendation/sanction requirements treated as procedural; Art 258 — Union may entrust its functions to a State with consent; Art 258A — State may entrust functions to the Union; Art 257A — armed-forces deployment to assist a State, omitted by the 44th Amdt (w.e.f. 20-6-1979), now handled by consent/statute (e.g., AFSPA, 1958 in notified areas).

⚡ Art 365 — the hinge

Non-compliance with any direction (constitutional or statutory Union law) lets the President hold — i.e., formally conclude — that Art 356's trigger exists. It converts an executive default into "failure of constitutional machinery" without needing a new fact-finding exercise; but as Rameshwar Prasad insists, whatever route supplies the satisfaction, the material must still be objective and reviewable (case 71).

🏛 Real-world directions in operation

  • Art 256: Union directions for implementation of central schemes/laws — e.g., directions under the Essential Commodities framework, election-law compliance, census enumeration, or Calcutta-style all-India services coordination; failure of a State to implement a valid Union law is the classic 365 fact-pattern.
  • Art 257(2): national highways/“means of communication of national or military importance” — the NHAI/border-roads pattern; costs beyond normal State duties reimbursed under 257(4), CJI-appointed arbitrator if no agreement.
  • Art 257(3): railway protection measures — e.g., deployment of State police for rail-property security during agitations.
  • Art 355 in action: Union armed assistance to States has historically moved on request/consent (257A now omitted) or under statute (Armed Forces (Special Powers) Act, 1958 in notified areas; deployment during communal crises upon State invitation).
  • Converse protection: Art 355 also underpins Union intervention when a State cannot maintain order — but post-Bommai, protective duty ≠ licence to dislodge an elected government without material (case 70).

🧭 Non-compliance → what exactly happens?

  1. Direction issued in exercise of Union executive power under any provision of the Constitution (256, 257, 258, 353 during an Art 352 emergency, statutory powers etc.).
  2. State "fails to comply with, or to give effect to" it — omission suffices, no contumacy required.
  3. President may hold (discretionary formulation but advice-bound post-74(1)) that Art 356's situation has arisen → proclamation path opens.
  4. But: Rameshwar para 96/122 — the burden of proving the existence of the situation rests on the Union (s. 106 of the Evidence Act); a bare holding with no objective material is "ipse dixit" and is struck down.
  5. Safeguard interlocks: two-month approval clock (356(3)); no dissolution-as-of-right; review under Bommai parameters (§8).

§5 Articles 356–357 — President's Rule in the States

Art 356(1). "If the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor of a State or otherwise, is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution, the President may by Proclamation—
(a) assume to himself all or any of the functions of the Government of the State and all or any of the powers vested in or exercisable by the Governor or any body or authority in the State other than the Legislature of the State;
(b) declare that the powers of the Legislature of the State shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament; and
(c) make such incidental and consequential provisions … including provisions for suspending in whole or in part the operation of any provisions of this Constitution relating to any body or authority in the State:
Provided that nothing in this clause authorises the President to assume any powers vested in or exercisable by a High Court, or to suspend its constitutional protections.

(2) revocable/variable by subsequent Proclamation. (3) laid before both Houses; ceases in two months unless approved by both Houses (Lok-Sabha-dissolved proviso → 30 days after reconstitution). (4) once approved, ceases after six months; renewable by continuance resolutions — never beyond three years (Punjab proviso: the 11-5-1987 Proclamation's ceiling was written as five years by the 67th/68th Amdts). (5) Beyond one year, continuance resolution cannot be passed unless (a) an Art 352 Proclamation is in operation for the whole/part of that State, and (b) the Election Commission certifies that continuing is necessary because of difficulties in holding general elections to that Assembly (Punjab proviso via 64th Amdt). No-dissolution-before-approval (Bommai holding, on the scheme of (3)): irreversible steps — notably dissolution of the Assembly — only after both Houses approve; until then the House may be suspended (Sarkaria & Punchhi recommend codifying this by amendment).

🎯 Grounds — what "cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution" covers

  • Art 365 route: non-compliance with Union directions (any constitutional provision) — deemed failure.
  • Governor's report of loss of majority: defections/horse-trading — but only if tested or untestable (floor test first: 70, 71).
  • Breakdown of law & order rendering the State government unable to function per the Constitution (classic instances: PM's assassination riots 1984?, Punjab 1980s, UP 1991-2?) — must be evidenced.
  • Policies violating the basic structure: Bommai — a State government pursuing anti-secular/anti-socialist policies can be a valid 356 ground (researched part of the disposition; DU excerpt supplies the federalism frame).
  • NOT grounds: mal-administration of a majority government ("remedy … lies elsewhere and not in Article 356(1)" — Rameshwar ¶158) · political disagreement with policies · mere "instability" without evidence · preventing a party from staking a claim (Rameshwar ¶140: extraneous) · anticipatory dissolutions on assumed future illegality (¶141-143).
  • Sarkaria Commission's touchstone (quoted in DU Rameshwar): use Art 356 as a last resort, for the purpose contemplated by the Constitution, and resume normalcy as soon as possible; explore all alternatives — including a minority government enjoying confidence — before reporting failure (Rameshwar ¶120; also the Five-member Committee of Governors appointed after the November 1970 Conference of Governors, DU-quoted).

🏛 Art 357 — exercising legislative power during President's Rule

  • 357(1)(a): Parliament may confer on the President the State Legislature's law-making power (and permit delegation by the President).
  • 357(1)(b): such laws may confer powers/impose duties on the Union or its officers.
  • 357(1)(c): President may authorise expenditure from the State Consolidated Fund when the Lok Sabha is not in session, pending parliamentary sanction.
  • 357(2) (42nd Amdt drafting): laws made under 357 that Parliament/President could not otherwise make continue in force after the Proclamation ceases, until altered/repealed by the competent Legislature — the saving that protected hundreds of Ordinance-era State laws (compare Krishna Kumar Singh on lapse of Ordinances).
  • Contrast with Art 356(1)(b): the Proclamation itself makes State legislative powers "exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament"; Art 357 is the machinery clause for actually legislating/delegating/spending.

🔍 Grounds — linked provisions worth quoting in answers

  • Art 164(2) — collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Assembly: why the floor is the constitutionally ordained test (Bommai; Nabam Rabia).
  • Art 174/163 — Governor's summoning/proroguing/dissolving powers are aid-and-advice-bound when a government enjoys confidence (Nabam Rabia, DU excerpt ✓).
  • Art 159 — Governor's oath: preserve, protect and defend the Constitution — invoked in Rameshwar ¶156 against Raj Bhavan overreach.
  • Art 361 — immunity of President/Governor: does not bar review of the material on which the report rests; suits against the Government remain open (proviso to 361(1)) — framed as Point 4 in Rameshwar.
  • Art 74(2) — bar on inquiring into the aid-and-advice of Ministers: held in Bommai not to bar examination of the factual material the advice was based on (Sawant J, quoted in DU Rameshwar ¶95): "the courts can always probe into the factual existence of material … but not the wisdom or reasonableness of the advice".
  • 10th Schedule — Speaker's turf, not the Governor's: disqualification questions are for the Speaker (judicial power), and were an impermissible ground for recommending dissolution (Rameshwar ¶¶155–159).

§6 Article 360 — Financial Emergency (never invoked)

Art 360(1). "If the President is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or of any part of the territory thereof is threatened, he may by Proclamation make a declaration to that effect." (2) may be revoked/varied; laid before both Houses; ceases at two months unless approved by both Houses (Lok-Sabha-dissolved proviso → 30 days after reconstitution). (3) Executive authority of the Union extends to directions to any State "to observe such canons of financial propriety as may be specified" and other directions the President deems necessary/adequate. (4) Notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, directions may include (i) reducing salaries/allowances of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a State; (ii) requiring all Money Bills and Art 207 Bills to be reserved for the President's consideration after State Legislature passage; and (b) the President may direct reduction of salaries/allowances of persons serving the Union — including Judges of the Supreme Court and the High Courts. history: old cl. (5) inserted retrospectively by 38th Amdt 1975 and omitted by 44th Amdt 1978; cl. (2) as printed substituted by 44th Amdt

⚙️ Procedure

No separate "Cabinet in writing" clause (unlike 352(3)) but Art 74(1) aid-and-advice applies; two-month approval by both Houses like Art 356(3); no six-month continuance machinery is written into 360 — the text relies on the approval + ordinary politics; revocation by subsequent Proclamation. (Contrast: no three-year cap text, no ECI-certificate gate — one reason scholars call 360 the "unfinished" emergency clause.)

💥 Effect toolkit

① Directions to States on financial propriety. ② Cut State-employee salaries. ③ Reservation of State Money Bills/207 Bills for President. ④ Cut all Union salaries — including SC & HC Judges' salaries (the most-discussed clause; the 44th Amdt retained it while removing the old retrospective cl. (5)). ⑤ By implication, intensified Centre-State fiscal coordination and IMF/parliamentary management of the crisis.

🌏 Never used — but keep ready

No Art 360 Proclamation has ever been issued in India. Near-misses to cite as context: the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis (forex reserves for ~2 weeks of imports; solved by pledging gold, liberalisation and IMF assistance — not by emergency powers); repeated fiscal-deficit warnings (FRBM framework, 2003+). Candidates often conflate it with Art 356 — mark the difference: 360 attacks fiscal stability of India, 356 attacks constitutional governance of a State.

Structural reading for exams. Arts 352 (national), 356 (State) and 360 (financial) form a ladder of severity with shrinking thresholds: security of India → constitutional machinery of a State → financial stability/credit of India. Each has its own approval clock (one month / two months / two months), but only 352 has disapproval-resolutions (352(7)–(8)); only 356 has the three-year cap + ECI gate; only 360 can touch judicial salaries' quantum — and only 352/359 can touch rights. The examiner's favourite one-liner: "Financial Emergency has remained a dead letter — drafted, detailed, dormant."

Case 69 · State of Rajasthan v. Union of India — (1977) 3 SCC 592 · AIR 1977 SC 1361

Bench (7 Judges): M. Hameedullah Beg C.J., Y.V. Chandrachud, S. Murtaza Fazl Ali, P.N. Bhagwati, P.K. Goswami, N.L. Untwalia and A.C. Gupta JJ. · Order 29-4-1977, reasons 6-5-1977 · Constitutional Bench, Original Suits 1–6 of 1977 + Writ Petitions 67–69 of 1977.

Facts. The Janata Party swept the March 1977 Lok Sabha elections, defeating the Congress across the country. On 18-4-1977, the Union Home Minister, Shri Charan Singh, addressed letters to the Chief Ministers of the six Congress-ruled States — Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Punjab, Bihar, Himachal Pradesh and Orissa (Odisha) — advising them to dissolve their Legislative Assemblies and seek a fresh mandate; the Union indicated that if the advice was not followed, action under Art 356 (and, implicitly, Art 365 — non-compliance with directions) might follow. The six States filed suits under Art 131 (plus connected writ petitions) seeking declarations that such an invocation would be unconstitutional and mala fide and injunctions restraining recourse to Art 356 on that sole ground.
Issues. (1) Is the President's satisfaction under Art 356 justiciable — and if so, how far (the then Art 356(5), inserted retrospectively by the 38th Amdt, 1975, was invoked in the debate)? (2) Does mere defeat of the ruling party in a Lok Sabha election constitute a "situation" warranting Art 356? (3) Is dissolution permissible without prior ratification by both Houses under Art 356(3)? (4) Was the letter "mala fide" — used to threaten? (5) Are the Art 131 suits (and the prayers for anticipatory injunctions) maintainable?
Arguments — Petitioner States. Art 356(3) makes parliamentary approval integral; dissolving assemblies on the strength of a single electoral defeat is political vendetta, not constitutional failure; the threat was "deceptive" and exercised for a purpose foreign to the power (mala fide); the Court should grant an injunction against a threatened illegal act; Art 131 disputes about the composition/tenure of State governments lie within the original jurisdiction.

Arguments — Union of India. The letter is mere "advice" — no Proclamation exists, hence no cause of action; whether a situation warrants Art 356 is subjective and non-justiciable (Art 365 non-compliance → Art 356 is the statutory route); Art 74(2) shields the advice; Art 131 requires a legal right of the State as an entity, not of a transient Ministry; in any case the exercise of Art 356 cannot be condemned unless it breaches constitutional conditions.
Decision. All suits and petitions dismissed; no injunction. The Court held: (i) the satisfaction is subjective but not absolutely immune — it may be challenged on the grounds that it is mala fide, or based on wholly extraneous and irrelevant grounds (or with no material at all) — the "narrow, minimal area" of review later so described in DU Rameshwar Prasad ¶149; (ii) Art 356 carries a preventive and curative purpose — restoring constitutional government, not merely reacting to collapse; (iii) a President's decision taken on advice cannot be condemned merely because the choice between continuing a government and going to the electorate is politically motivated — the letter's grounds had a "clear nexus" with Art 356; (iv) Art 356(3) approval is not a condition precedent to exercise of Art 356(1) power (Beg C.J.: clause (3) "does not touch actions taken, proceedings completed, consequences ensued and orders executed"), and 356(1)(b) itself necessarily implies power to dissolve; (v) the Art 131/anticipatory-injunction route was unavailable.
Principle. "The President's satisfaction is subjective, reviewable only on the narrow grounds of mala fide/extraneous/no material — and the Federal Court of India will not sit in appeal over political satisfaction." This minimal-review doctrine governed for sixteen years until S.R. Bommai extended it to material-scrutiny, floor-test and burden-of-proof standards (DU Rameshwar ¶¶98, 149).
DU-sourced quotations from this judgment — as printed in the DU case-book inside the S.R. Bommai excerpt (¶20), Beg C.J.:

"A conspectus of the provisions of our Constitution will indicate that, whatever appearance of a federal structure our Constitution may have, its operations are certainly, judged both by the contents of power which a number of its provisions carry with them and the use that has been made of them, more unitary than federal."

"In a sense, therefore, the Indian Union is federal. But, the extent of federalism in it is largely watered down by the needs of progress and development of a country which has to be nationally integrated, politically and economically coordinated, and socially, intellectually and spiritually uplifted…"

"If then our Constitution creates a Central Government which is 'amphibian', in the sense that it can move either on the federal or unitary plane, according to the needs of the situation and circumstances of a case, the question which we are driven back to consider is whether an assessment of the 'situation' … are matters for the Union Government itself or for this Court to consider and determine."

These passages are the classic citation for "federalism under strain" and reappear in DU's Bommai excerpt; the DU Rameshwar excerpt adds the doctrinal epitaph: the "narrow minimal area of judicial review … advocated in State of Rajasthan case is no longer the law of the land in view of its extension in Bommai case" (¶149).

Case 70 · S.R. Bommai v. Union of India — (1994) 3 SCC 1

Bench (9 Judges) — full composition in DU: S. Ratnavel Pandian, A.M. Ahmadi, Kuldip Singh, J.S. Verma, P.B. Sawant, K. Ramaswamy, S.C. Agrawal, Yogeshwar Dayal and B.P. Jeevan Reddy JJ. · judgment pronounced 11-3-1994 (petition filed 1989 — five years) · six separate opinions (DU Rameshwar ¶104) · scope as bracketed in DU: "President's Rule in States under Article 356 – grounds and scope of judicial review".

Facts — Karnataka (the lead case, DU). At the March 1983 elections, the Janata Dal won 139 of 225 seats (Congress 66); Shri R.K. Hegde became Chief Minister and resigned on 12-8-1988; Shri S.R. Bommai was then sworn in as CM (August 1988). On 17-4-1989 the Congress legislature-party leader wrote withdrawing support (alleging dissensions — "17 MLAs"); the next day 19 letters reached the Governor (17 Janata Dal MLAs + 1 independent associate + 1 BJP member). The Governor verified signatures through the Legislature Secretariat's Secretary (19-4-1989 report) and recommended President's action; seven of the nineteen wrote on 20-4-1989 that their signatures had been obtained by misrepresentation and reaffirmed support for Bommai. The Cabinet convened the House for 27-4-1989; the CM and Law Minister informed the Governor and Bommai offered a floor test (even preponing the session). The Governor's second report the same day called the retractions coerced and spoke of horse-trading; on 21-4-1989 the Proclamation under Art 356 issued, dismissing the Ministry and dissolving the Assembly — before the floor test — later approved by Parliament under Art 356(3) (DU Rameshwar ¶105). The Karnataka H.C. (3-Judge) dismissed Bommai's writ: the report's facts could not be held irrelevant and a floor test was "neither compulsory nor obligatory nor a prerequisite".

Facts — the other five States (DU + ¶104). Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh (BJP governments dismissed after the 6-12-1992 Babri Masjid demolition — failure to prevent destruction of a structure revered by a religious community = basic-structure/secularism charge), Meghalaya (Proclamation of 11-10-1991 — after the CM's resignation; breakaway group's claim) and Nagaland — six in all. The Court examined all six proclamations together.
Issues (as DU frames them, plus the reported framing). (I) Whether Art 356 power can be exercised on the materials here and what the constitutional limits are; (II) the meaning of the expression "a situation has arisen in which the Government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution"; (III) what is the effect of Article 355 on Arts 356/256/257 (DU's bracketed scope: grounds and scope of judicial review); (IV) is the President's satisfaction — and the Governor's report behind it — amenable to judicial review; (V) can a government be dismissed/dissolved without a floor test; (VI) do violations of the basic structure (e.g., governmental policies offending secularism) justify Art 356?
Arguments — Union (as recorded in DU facts and the reported judgment). The Governor's reports were the authentic material; the Legislature Secretariat had verified the 19 signatures; horse-trading and dissensions had broken the majority; the "or otherwise" limb gives the President independent sourcing power; Art 355 casts the Union a duty to ensure constitutional government, so intervention is sanctioned; in the Babri-batch States, the administrations failed in their duty to preserve communal harmony and law and order (basic-structure breach); questions of "situation" are political and beyond judicially manageable standards.

Arguments — Bommai and the other States. Bommai repeatedly offered a floor test; the letters were unverified as to source (seven retracted); the report was the Governor's "ipse dixit"; dissensions ≠ loss of majority until tested; federalism and democracy are basic features and a hostile Centre cannot use Art 356 to dislodge inconvenient governments; Art 74(2) cannot cloak the material; Parliament's later approval under Art 356(3) cannot cure a proclamation void at inception; the Court must place the burden on the Union (s. 106, Evidence Act).
Decision (disposition researched — DU excerpt stops before the operative portions). On 11-3-1994 the nine Judges delivered six opinions. Unanimously: a proclamation under Art 356 is subject to judicial review; the constitutional validity of the Governor's report and the satisfaction built on it can be examined. By the majority: the proclamations in Karnataka, Meghalaya and Nagaland were unconstitutional; the proclamations in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh were valid — DU Rameshwar confirms both limbs: "the majority has declared invalid the dissolution of Assemblies of Karnataka and Nagaland and held as valid the dissolution of the Assemblies of Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh" (¶139; compare ¶104 listing six States with Karnataka, Nagaland and Meghalaya among the invalidations, and ¶154 summarising "Karnataka and Nagaland … bad and not … Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh"). Relief (researched): the Karnataka Proclamation was declared void, but by judgment-date elections had already installed a fresh House (same for Meghalaya/Nagaland), so relief was largely declaratory — the Court nevertheless laid down the restitution principle: if a proclamation is invalidated, a dismissed ministry revives and a dissolved assembly is restored where feasible (reported at ¶377 of Jeevan Reddy J's opinion, discussed in DU Rameshwar ¶147).
Principles (the nine-judge package). (1) Federalism, democracy, secularism and socialism are basic features; Art 356 power is de jure the President's but de facto the Council of Ministers' — conditioned, not absolute. (2) Art 355 is "both a responsibility and a power" (Jeevan Reddy J, DU ✓) — it protects States; it does not license their punishment. (3) The floor of the House is the constitutionally ordained forum to test majority — "the assessment of the strength of the Ministry is not a matter of private opinion of any individual, be he the Governor or the President" (DU Rameshwar ¶119 quoting the majority). (4) Review extends to the material: courts may ask whether the report is based on relevant material, is bona fide, and was duly verified (DU Rameshwar ¶135); Art 74(2) bars probing advice's wisdom — not the factual existence of material (DU ¶95). (5) Burden on the Union (s. 106 Evidence Act) — DU ¶96/¶122. (6) Satisfaction ≠ ipse dixit: "The drastic and extreme action under Article 356 cannot be justified on mere ipse dixit, suspicion, whims and fancies of the Governor. This Court cannot remain a silent spectator watching the subversion of the Constitution" (DU Rameshwar ¶136). (7) A proclamation may be struck down even after parliamentary approval; invalidity is ab initio. (8) Minority governments are permissible; defection law is the Speaker's, not the Governor's; mal-administration is not a 356 ground.

Case 71 · Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India — (2006) 2 SCC 1

Bench (5 Judges) — DU: Y.K. Sabharwal C.J. and K.G. Balakrishnan, B.N. Agrawal, Ashok Bhan and Arijit Pasayat JJ. · scope as bracketed in DU: "Can dissolution of Assembly under Article 356(1) of the Constitution of India be ordered to prevent the staking of claim by a political party on the ground that the majority was obtained by illegal means." · interim brief order 7-10-2005; judgment reported (2006) 2 SCC 1.

Facts (DU). The Bihar Assembly (243 members; majority = 122) completed its term on 6-3-2005. February 2005 elections produced a hung House: NDA (BJP+JD(U)) 92 · RJD 75 · LJP 29 · Congress 10 · CPI(ML) 7 · SP 4 · NCP 3 · BSP 2 · Independents 17 · Others 9. Smt. Rabri Devi resigned (28-2-2005); letters followed: LJP (Paswan) — supporting neither RJD nor BJP; BJP claimed 92 + 10–12 independents "not to allow the RJD to form a Government"; SP, CPI(ML) neutral; RJD staked a claim on 5-3-2005 — but even RJD + all 17 independents = 108 < 122; NDA + 10 independents = 102 < 122; six independents' claim lacked authorisation. The Governor (in his report of 6-3-2005) recorded that he had "explored all possibilities" and no party/group could claim majority → Notification GSR 162(E) dated 7-3-2005: Art 356 Proclamation, Assembly kept in suspended animation (approved by the Lok Sabha on 19-3-2005 and the Rajya Sabha on 21-3-2005 — DU).

Post-November-2005 elections again returned no combination at 122. On 21-5-2005 the Governor reported that 17–18 LJP MLAs were "moving towards" the JD(U), that allurements were being offered and that a claim would "distort the verdict of the people"; a joint letter of the RJD–LJP leadership (22-5-2005, late evening) followed. The Union forwarded the material to the President — who was in Moscow: papers despatched at 11 p.m. on 22-5-2005, presidential action in the early IST hours of 23-5-2005 (01:52 / 03:50), and the Proclamation/notification dissolving the Assembly at 14:30 IST on 23-5-2005 — before the newly elected House had met even once. Parliament approved it (LS/RS, March 2005 sittings had approved the first Proclamation; the second approval followed under Art 356(3)). Writ petitions before the Patna H.C. failed; the Supreme Court passed an interim order on 7-10-2005 declaring the 23-5-2005 Proclamation unconstitutional while declining status quo ante, with reasons and final relief in the judgment.
Issues (Sabharwal C.J.'s four Points, DU). Point 1 — [question not reproduced in the DU excerpt]. Point 2 — "Whether the proclamation dated 23rd May, 2005 dissolving the Assembly of Bihar is illegal and unconstitutional?" (¶40: "This point is the heart of the matter"). Point 3 — if so, "is it necessary to direct status quo ante as on 7th March, 2005 or 4th March, 2005?" Point 4 — "What is the scope of Article 361 granting immunity to the Governor?" (the DU excerpt reproduces the question; the discussion of this Point does not appear in the excerpt — settled law outside the excerpt: the immunity is personal to the Governor; the material and acts remain reviewable, and suits against the Government stay open under the proviso to Art 361(1)) researched note.
Arguments — Union (AG, Solicitor General, ASG — DU ¶¶136–148, 155). (i) The Governor's report itself is the material; it is not permissible in judicial review to go into the material behind the report or whether it was verified. (ii) The situation is full of "imponderables, nuances, implications and intricacies" — there are no judicially manageable standards; courts must keep hands off (¶137). (iii) Installation ≠ removal: Bommai concerned removal after a floor test, so its parameters do not apply (¶144). (iv) Art 356 power is preventive and/or curative — denying government to a group that would cobble a majority by illegality is within it (¶145). (v) The power is legislative in character; mala fides/extraneous grounds cannot be attributed to legislative acts (¶146). (vi) Jeevan Reddy J.'s ¶377 in Bommai "requires re-look" in light of earlier decisions (¶148). (vii) Anticipating the Tenth Schedule problem: even a two-thirds LJP-JD(U) merger would be defection-tainted — dissolution prevents the violation (¶155).

Arguments — petitioners (and the Court's own catalogue of Bommai ratio). The case is "squarely covered against the Government" by Bommai's ratio (¶125); there can be no presumption of allurement merely because MLAs change political alignment (¶125); minority governments are normal (¶125); the report was unverified opinion — no interviews, conflicting retraction letters, unknown sources of "horse-trading" information (¶124); the proper course was a floor test (CM offered it) or, failing that, written reasons for not holding one (¶119); the Governor had to explore whether any other Ministry could be formed, even a minority one enjoying confidence (¶120); the burden of proving the situation is on the Union (s. 106 Evidence Act, ¶¶96, 122).
Decision (DU). ¶161: "For all the aforesaid reasons, the Proclamation dated 23rd May, 2005 is held to be unconstitutional." The Court held: the "satisfaction" rested solely on the Governor's reports — his own opinion on unascertained material, not objective material (¶¶120, 123); the Governor "threw all canons of propriety to the wind", acted with "undue haste" that "clearly smacked of mala fides", and the Proclamation "equally suffered from mala fides" (¶118); "The drastic and extreme action under Article 356 cannot be justified on mere ipse dixit, suspicion, whims and fancies of the Governor … This Court … is the sentinel on the qui vive" — the Governor "may be [the] main player, but [the] Council of Ministers should have verified facts" before treating the report "as a gospel truth"; indeed "the Governor … misled the Council of Ministers" (¶136). Preventing a party from staking a claim was the extraneous main ground (¶149); assuming Tenth Schedule disqualifications was impermissible — defection power belongs to the Speaker (judicial power), not the Governor (¶¶155, 158–159); "the Governor is not an autocratic political Ombudsman" and "the ground of mal administration by a State Government enjoying majority is not available for invoking power under Article 356 … the remedy for corruption or similar ills lies elsewhere" (¶158). Point 3 / relief (¶162): status quo ante declined — "larger public interest, … ground realities and taking a pragmatic view": the Election Commission had completed four-phase preparations, nominations/scrutiny/withdrawal for Phase 1 were over, polling was fixed for 18-10-2005, huge expenditure had been incurred, and it was "fit not to put the State in another spell of uncertainty" — relief moulded to let the elections run, "with the fond hope that the electorate may again not give fractured verdict … the Indian electorate possessing utmost intelligence".
Principles. (1) A 356 proclamation must rest on objective, verified, germane material — a Governor's unverified report is "ipse dixit" and void. (2) Floor test first: where majority is doubted, the House alone may decide; refusing/omitting it without written reasons is fatal (¶119). (3) Exploration duty: before reporting failure, the Governor must explore all alternatives — including a minority government commanding confidence (¶120; Five-member Committee of Governors, 1970; Sarkaria). (4) Extraneous-purpose doctrine: dissolving to prevent a party from staking a claim (or to pre-empt "improper" realignments) is outside Art 356 (¶¶140–143) — "even if preventive, [the] power cannot be abused" (¶145). (5) Mal-administration, defection-speculation and Tenth Schedule fears are not 356 grounds; Speaker/defence-of-constitution remedies lie elsewhere (¶¶156, 158). (6) Parliamentary approval does not cure an invalid proclamation; review extends to the material even after Art 356(3) ratification (¶147-148, following Bommai). (7) Relief is moulded pragmatically: declaration of unconstitutionality can coexist with refusal of status quo ante once elections are mid-stream (¶162). (8) Bommai parameters are binding — nine-judge law, not to be re-opened (¶148).
Three DU lines worth reproducing verbatim in an exam.

"…in all cases where the support to the Ministry is claimed to have been withdrawn by some Legislators, the proper course for testing the strength of the Ministry is holding the test on the floor of the House. That alone is the constitutionally ordained forum for seeking openly and objectively the claims and counter-claims…" (¶119)

"Thus, it is open to the Court, in exercise of judicial review, to examine the question whether the Governor's report is based upon relevant material or not; whether it is made bona fide or not; and whether the facts have been duly verified or not. The absence of these factors resulted in the majority declaring the dissolution of State Legislatures of Karnataka and Nagaland as invalid." (¶135)

"The narrow minimal area of judicial review as advocated in State of Rajasthan case is no longer the law of the land in view of its extension in Bommai case…" (¶149)

⚖️ Grounds on which a proclamation falls (settled list)

  1. Mala fide — improper purpose or personal bias (Rajasthan; Rameshwar ¶118: "canons of propriety … undue haste … smacked of mala fides").
  2. Wholly extraneous / irrelevant grounds — e.g., punishing realignment, preventing a claim, mal-administration, Tenth-Schedule speculation (Rameshwar ¶¶140–159).
  3. Absence of material / ipse dixit — "mere ipse dixit, suspicion, whims and fancies" (¶136).
  4. Unverified facts / unascertained sources — no interviews, conflicting letters, unknown inputs (¶¶123–124).
  5. Refusal of a feasible floor test without written reasons (¶119; Bommai).
  6. Failure to explore alternatives — minority government available (¶120; Sarkaria/1970 Governors' Committee).
  7. Breach of Art 356(1) proviso or procedure — touching High Courts; non-approval within clocks; dissolving before Art 356(3) approval — irreversible-acts rule of Bommai.
  8. Basic-structure violation by the Union's own use — e.g., using Art 356 to dismantle federalism (Kailash-type argument, §9).

Limits of review (so the answer is balanced): no appeal against the choice between dismissal and fresh elections (Rajasthan); sufficiency of material not questioned — only its legitimacy (quoted from Sawant J's Bommai opinion in DU Rameshwar ¶121-122: "sufficiency of material cannot be questioned, the legitimacy of inference … is certainly open to judicial review"); no probing of ministerial advice under Art 74(2) — but the factual bedrock is examinable (¶95).

🏛 Sarkaria Commission (1983)

Centre–State relations reference. On Art 356 (quoted in DU Rameshwar): use as last resort; apply only where constitutional machinery genuinely cannot be carried on; the Governor should give the incumbent CM an opportunity and probe alternatives; resume normalcy ASAP; never dissolve the Assembly merely to suspend it — keep it in suspended animation where elections are avoidable. DU ¶120 also quotes the Five-member Committee of Governors appointed after the New Delhi Conference of Governors, November 1970, recommending installation of any government that can enjoy confidence — even a minority one.

🗳 Vohra Committee (1993) & anti-defection

Report on criminalisation of politics — background to why "horse-trading" fears are real, yet Rameshwar insists they must be evidenced, not assumed. Tenth Schedule machinery: Speaker decides disqualification (Kihoto Hollohan, DU ¶86); the Governor may not pre-judge it (¶155).

📜 Punchhi Commission (2007–10)

Successor commission: reiterated a proviso-based approach — Art 356 invocation should ideally require Assembly suspension (not dissolution), gubernatorial reasons recorded, and possibly an amendment so that the Governor's report be sent to the President through the Council of Ministers. Useful "reform" paragraph for Centenary Q7 (§9).

DECEMBER 2024 · Q2 · 20 marks
Question (verbatim). The Republic of Dakshin (Dakshin), is a federal polity. The State of Kailash (Kailash) is one of the 23 provinces of the Republic of Dakshin. For the longest time political party 'A' formed both the Union government in Dakshin and the State Government in Kailash. However, in 2023, political party 'B' won the provincial elections in Kailash and formed the state government therein. The principle of asymmetric federalism imbues the Constitution of the Republic of Dakshin. In light of this principle, the State of Kailash has been given special privileges under the Constitution of the Republic of Dakshin. In January 2024, the political party A decided to revoke the special privilege conferred on Kailash through a constitutional amendment. This led to non-violent civil protests in Kailash. On February 1, 2024, the President of Dakshin issued a proclamation under Article 356 of the Constitution of the Republic of Dakshin on the ground that there was a failure of constitutional machinery in Kailash. This action of the Union government was challenged before the Supreme Court of India by the State of Kailash. The laws of Dakshin are in pari materia with the Constitution of India. In light of the aforementioned facts, argue on behalf of the State of Kailash. Your arguments must discuss the jurisprudence of federalism, Article 356, as well as the extent and limits of judicial review. (20)

Opening frame. "Laws in pari materia" invites straight application of Indian constitutionalism: Kesavananda–Bommai federalism, Art 356 as conditioned power, and the Rajasthan→Bommai→Rameshwar line on review. Three heads of argument, exactly as the question asks.

I. JURISPRUDENCE OF FEDERALISM (the constitutional atmosphere). (a) Federalism is a basic feature — S.R. Bommai: democracy, federalism and secularism are beyond amendment; the Centre's power over States is a trust, not ownership. (b) DU-sourced colour: Beg C.J. in State of Rajasthan (AIR 1977 SC 1361, quoted at DU p. 12): whatever federal appearance the Constitution has, its operations are "more unitary than federal", and the Union is an "amphibian" that moves on either plane — but the choice of plane is justiciable (that very passage ends by asking whether assessment of the "situation" belongs to the Union Government "or for this Court"). (c) Sawant J. in Bommai (DU): States are "neither satellites nor agents"; Jeevan Reddy J.: Art 355 is a duty to protect States, not a weapon against them. (d) Asymmetric federalism is India's own technique of unity-through-difference — Art 244A (Assam's autonomous councils), Arts 371A/371G (Naga/Mizo customary law), 371C (Manipur tribes), 371F (Sikkim), 371D/J (Andhra/Telangana field-level reservations), and (until 2019) the Art 370 template: asymmetry strengthens the Union by securing consent. Ambedkar's defence of the federal compromise (flexibility without fissiparity) frames the reply: a privilege granted for federal peace cannot be revoked fiscally-causally in a manner that destroys the federal bargain itself — the amendment power (Art 368, in pari materia) is plenary but bounded by the basic structure (Kesavananda; Minerva Mills's "essential features" logic applied to the federal distribution). Argument: Kailash should first challenge the revocation amendment itself (procedure + basic structure), because the illegitimacy of the cause infects everything the Union does in response.

II. ARTICLE 356 — the proclamation fails on every limb. (1) No "situation" of failure: party B formed and presumably continues a government; the reported trigger is non-violent civil protest against the Union's own amendment. Protests — even unrest — are Art 355-protection territory (internal disturbance), not automatic machinery-failure; mal-administration/dissent can never be the ground (DU Rameshwar ¶158: "remedy for corruption or similar ills lies elsewhere and not in Article 356(1)"). (2) No Art 365 gateway: the facts disclose no Union direction to Kailash that went uncomplied with — the 365→356 hinge is missing; "or otherwise" material must still be objective (Bommai). (3) Extraneous purpose: using Art 356 to disable a State that resents (or lawfully litigates) a Union amendment is precisely the "prevent a party/State from acting" extraneousness condemned in DU Rameshwar ¶¶140–143 — the power cannot be used as punishment for political dissent; "even if preventive, power cannot be abused" (¶145). (4) Mala fide indicia: party A's loss of Kailash in 2023 + revocation in January + proclamation on 1 February suggests pique; the Governor's report (if any) must be tested for relevant, bona fide, verified material (¶135); if majority of party B is doubted, only a floor test may decide (¶119 — "constitutionally ordained forum"). (5) Art 355 inverted: the Union's duty is to protect Kailash and to keep its government running per the Constitution; intervention to dislodge a confidence enjoying ministry flips the clause. (6) Clocks and gates: approval within two months (356(3)); six-month continuances; and — decisive drafting point — after one year continuance is impossible without an Art 352 emergency in operation plus the Election Commission's certificate (356(5)): absent a national emergency, President's Rule in Kailash is legally time-boxed to one year maximum, and the Bommai no-dissolution-before-approval rule bars dissolving the House to manufacture a fait accompli. (7) Art 357 saving and the High-Court proviso protect institutional continuity.

III. EXTENT AND LIMITS OF JUDICIAL REVIEW. Extent: State of Rajasthan (1977) opened the narrow gate — satisfaction challengeable if mala fide, wholly extraneous, or without material; S.R. Bommai (1994, 9 Judges) extended review to the material itself (relevant? bona fide? verified?), imposed the s.106 burden on the Union, held Art 74(2) is no bar to probing factual material, and affirmed review even after parliamentary approval; Rameshwar Prasad (2006) walked the whole stack and struck the Bihar Proclamation while refusing status quo ante as a matter of relief-discretion. So Kailash may ask the Court to: strike the Proclamation; restore the dissolved assembly/status quo ante if elections have not been ordered (DU ¶162 logic); direct a floor test; and sever/restrain any coercive consequences. Limits (state them for balance): no appeal against the political choice between dismissal and fresh elections; sufficiency of material is not justiciable — only its legitimacy (Sawant J., DU ¶¶121–122); the Court will not substitute its opinion on stability; relief may be moulded (Rameshwar) rather than restorative; and the Court does not decide the merits of the amendment in a 356 proceeding — that needs the separate Art 368 challenge. Closing: the proclamation is unsustainable: protests are not failure; the purpose is extraneous; the material (if unverified) is ipse dixit; and even a procedurally perfect proclamation collapses on Bommai grounds — while the amendment itself must answer the basic-structure/federalism test.

CENTENARY CHANCE EXAMINATION · Q7 (item (e)) · 20 marks
Question (as preserved from the Centenary paper). Article 356 — safeguards against misuse and judicial pronouncements.

1. Why the clause needs armour. Art 356 vests de jure power in the President, de facto in the Council of Ministers, with only a subjective satisfaction and no fact-finding body — temptations: dismissing opposition-ruled States, using Governor's reports as political instruments, dissolving assemblies mid-crisis. DU Rameshwar ¶95: the power is "an emergency power but not absolute"; it exists to protect the Constitution (Art 355 duty), never to reverse verdicts.

2. Textual/parliamentary safeguards (as amended, 1978 onward). (i) Art 356(1) proviso: High Courts untouched; (ii) 356(3): two-month approval by both Houses or lapse — and, per the Bommai majority, no irreversible act (dissolution) before approval; (iii) 356(4): six-month continuances, hard three-year cap; (iv) 356(5): the one-year gate — beyond a year requires an Art 352 emergency in force + Election Commission certificate of election difficulty (the sharpest anti-misuse lever); (v) 356(2) revocation; (vi) Art 357 confines legislative exercise and saves (but also limits) collateral laws; (vii) Art 74(1) — President acts only on ministerial advice, so the political ownership of every proclamation is traceable to the ruling party.

3. Institutional safeguards. Governor's duties distilled from DU material: seek a floor test before reporting loss of majority (¶119); explore all alternatives, including minority governments (¶120; Five-member Committee of Governors, 1970); record written reasons if a floor test is impossible; no Ombudsman/defection-speculation role (¶¶155–159). Sarkaria Commission: Art 356 as last resort, suspension-not-dissolution where possible, prompt restoration. Punchhi Commission: reasoned reports, possibly routed through the Council of Ministers; guidelines for gubernatorial conduct. Anti-defection law (Tenth Schedule) channels political flux to the Speaker, not Raj Bhavan.

4. Judicial pronouncements — the chronological spine. (a) State of Rajasthan (1977) 3 SCC 592 (7 JJ): suits against the 1977 post-election dissolution threat dismissed; but the Court conceded the minimal review gate — satisfaction challengeable if mala fide / wholly extraneous / without material; preventive-corrective reading; Art 356(3) not a pre-condition. (b) S.R. Bommai (1994) 3 SCC 1 (9 JJ, six opinions): the watershed — federalism/democracy/secularism = basic structure; proclamation justiciable; review reaches the material (relevant, bona fide, verified); floor test constitutionally ordained; burden on the Union (s.106); Art 74(2) not a shield for facts; approval no cure → invalidity ab initio, dismissed ministries/dissolved assemblies restorable; Karnataka, Meghalaya, Nagaland invalid; M.P., Rajasthan, H.P. valid (DU ¶¶104, 139). (c) Rameshwar Prasad (2006) 2 SCC 1 (5 JJ): Bihar's 23-5-2005 dissolution struck — unverified Governor reports = ipse dixit; "sentinel on the qui vive"; extraneous purpose (preventing a claim); mal-administration not a ground; Speaker—not Governor—owns defection questions; relief moulded (no status quo ante; elections completed). (d) Nabam Rabia (2016) & Rawat (2016) [DU bonus]: Art 174 discretion aid-&-advice-bound; floor-test observers; "sentinel" logic extended. (e) Applications: Goa 2017, Karnataka 2018, Maharashtra 2019, MP 2020 — court-ordered floor tests.

5. Emergency-era backstory (for depth). 38th Amdt (1975) shielded orders retrospectively; 42nd Amdt (1976) widened clocks; 44th Amdt (1978) rebuilt the citadel: written Cabinet decision for 352, approval machinery, and the Art 356(5) gate; plus the broader rights package (Arts 20–21 untouchable). The clause today is usable but expensive: approval, time-limits, certificate gates, and — above all — evidence-testing in court.

6. Evaluation/reform paragraph. Despite safeguards, misuse anxieties persist (reports of "permanent President's Rule" tendencies; gubernarial activism). The way forward lies in codifying Bommai/Rameshwar duties: mandatory floor-test protocols, reasoned Governor reports with verified annexures, Punchhi-style routing through the Council, and legislated timelines — so that Art 356 remains, as DU Rameshwar puts it, a scalpel for constitutional failure rather than a hammer for political inconvenience.
