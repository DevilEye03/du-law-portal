What is Drafting? — Meaning, Need & Attributes of a Good Draft

Opening discussion of the DU unit, enriched with definitions and skill-parameters from companion sources

1.1 Meaning of Drafting DU Case Material pp. 1

“Drafting in general means, putting one’s own ideas in writing. Drafting of any matter is an art. Drafting of legal matters requires greater skills and efficiencies. It requires thorough knowledge of law, procedure, settled judicial principles, besides proficiency in English language.”

A perfect drafting of matters relating to suits, applications, complaints, writ petitions, appeals, revisions, reviews and connected matters “shall obviously lead to good result in terms of money, time, energy and expectation of not only the learned members of the Bench, but also the Bar as well as the parties to the litigation. It creates a congenial atmosphere where the glory of the judiciary and the Law grows to sky-heights. So is the case with regard to the drafting of conveyance/deeds.”

🎓 Academic definition ICSI

“Drafting may be defined as the synthesis of law and fact in a language form.” — Stanley Robinson, Drafting: Its Application to Conveyancing and Commercial Documents (1980), Ch. 1, p. 3, quoted in the ICSI study material. Legal drafting is “the crystallisation and expression in definitive form of a legal right, privilege, function, duty or status.”

The process operates on two planes — the conceptual and the verbal: the draftsman first seeks the right concepts, then the right words. “Drafting is first thinking and second composing.”

⭐ The Golden Maxim of Drafting (remember for life)

“The first rule on which a draftsman must act is this — that before his draft is commenced, the whole design of it should be conceived; for if he proceeds without any settled design, his draft will be confused and incoherent… he will be puzzled at every step… because he does not know what his own object is.”

— Davidson, Conveyancing, 4th Edn., Vol. I, p. 20, quoted in ICSI Study Material (Lesson 2).

📌 Real-life example — why the “art” matters

“Many dead-sure-win cases drag on for years in the courts only because of faulty drafting. Irrelevant matters, unnecessary details are often included and the facts placed before the lawyer by his client are not marshalled. The result is that the material facts are often mixed up with inessential matter.” KAMKUS — A plaintiff with a stamped, registered sale deed may still lose years if the plaint fails to plead title, possession, limitation-saving facts and jurisdiction in clear paragraphs.

History of Pleadings — From Oral Altercation to Written Statement

DU Case Material pp. 1–2 · why pleadings moved from the mouth to the parchment to paper

“The method of arriving at an issue by alternate allegations has been practised in the civilized countries from earliest times. The art of pleadings apparently is as ancient as any portion of our procedural law.” The DU material traces two parallel streams — India and England:

1

Ancient India — Oral pleadings before the King

In ancient India pleadings existed “but not in the present form.” Parties appeared in person in open court and oral altercation took place before the judge. Pleadings were conducted either by the party himself or by an eloquent person well-versed in Dharma Śāstras and Quran — called Pandit (Hindu law) and Maulvi (Muslim law) in ancient and medieval India. They helped kings and judges in the administration of justice.

2

England — Henry II & the birth of the “issue”

The art of pleading is traceable “in substantially the same form in England in the days of Henry II.” The word “issue” is found in the first year of the reign of Edward II — showing the art of arriving at an issue was practised even earlier, “for an issue had not been only the constant effect, but the professed aim and the object of pleading.”

3

The Judge as moderator — one cause, one issue

The duty of the King/judge was to ‘superindent’ i.e. ‘moderate’ the oral contentions. His aim: to arrive at a specific point affirmed on one side and denied on the other; when both agreed it was the question requiring decision, the parties were said to be “at issue” and pleadings ended. Judges were strict — never more than one issue per cause of action; a defendant with multiple defences had to elect one.

4

Queen Victoria’s reign — multiple issues allowed

Since the reign of Queen Victoria, parties were allowed to raise more than a single issue, either of law or fact — the origin of alternative pleas & multi-issue trials which today’s Order VIII and Order XIV (framing of issues) reflect.

5

Parchment record → paper pleadings (Edward IV)

During viva voce altercation, a court officer wrote on a parchment roll an official report of the parties’ allegations along with the acts of court — together called the “record”, preserved as perpetual judicial record. When each pleader began borrowing the roll and entering his own statement, oral pleading fell into disuse. With the development of printing and paper, pleadings were drawn on plain paper and interchanged between the parties — probably in the reign of Edward IV.

6

Judicature Act 1873 (England) — procedural reforms

The Judicature Act 1873 “brought in many reforms in the realm of pleading” which, with frequent changes, are still in force — fusion of law and equity, simplified forms of action.

7

Modern Indian law of pleading — the CPC, 1908

“The modern Indian law of pleading like any other law is based on the English system” and civil pleading is governed by the Code of Civil Procedure, which a lawyer must master for the practice and procedure of civil litigation. Indian pleading rules were given statutory form in Orders VI–VIII, CPC (the 1908 Code; substantially amended/redrafted up to 1976 and later).

🔍 Why this history matters for drafting

  • Pleading was born to crystallise an issue — every modern rule (facts only, material facts, conciseness) serves that single end.
  • The move from oral to writing created the danger of surprise & prolixity — hence rules of notice, particulars, and verification.
  • The one-issue-per-suit rigour evolved into alternative pleading — permitted today, but only if expressly pleaded (see Section 7).
  • India borrowed the English system → the four fundamental rules of English pleading are the very skeleton of Order VI CPC.

Meaning of Pleadings — Definition, Components & Classification

DU Case Material pp. 2–3 · Order VI Rule 1 CPC · subsequent & additional pleadings

📘 Definition (DU Case Material, p. 2)

“Pleadings are the statement of facts in writing drawn up and filed in a court by each party to a case stating therein what his contention shall be at the trial and giving all such details as his opponent will need to know in order to prepare his case in answer.”

KAMKUS adds Mogha’s classic formulation: “Pleadings are statements, written, drawn up and filed by each party to a case, stating what his contentions will be at the trial and giving all such details as his opponent needs to know in order to prepare his case in answer.”

Statutory definition — Order VI Rule 1 CPC

O. 6, R. 1: “‘Pleading’ shall mean plaint or written statement.”

The DU material candidly notes: “This definition is not very clear in itself” — hence the Code defines the two components separately (via Orders VII and VIII):

🧾

(a) Plaint

“A statement of claims, called the ‘plaint’, in which the plaintiff sets out his cause of action with all necessary particulars.” Governed by Order VII R.1 (nine particulars).

🛡️

(b) Written Statement

“A statement of defences… in which the defendant deals with every material fact alleged by the plaintiff in the plaint and also sets up any new facts which tell in his favour, adding such objections as he wishes to take to the claim.” Governed by Order VIII.

🌍 English-law definition (as reproduced in DU material)

“Pleading includes any petition or summons and also includes the statement in writing of the claim or demand of any plaintiff, and of the defence of any defendant thereto, and of the reply of the plaintiff to any counter-claim of a defendant.”

📌 Modern wide usage (DU material, syllabus note)

In broad usage, ‘pleading’ includes every legal document filed in a lawsuit, petition, motion and/or hearing — including complaint, petition, answer/reply, rejoinder, motion, declaration, and memorandum of points and authorities (written argument citing precedents and statutes). Since India follows an adversary system, each party stakes its claim in writing; “it is only after the completion of the pleadings that a matter is argued and subsequently the dispute is adjudicated.”

Classification of pleadings — original, subsequent & additional

Fig. 3.1 — Taxonomy of pleadings under the CPC (DU Case Material, pp. 2–3)

(i) Subsequent pleadings — detail

  • The only subsequent pleading filed as a matter of right, without leave of court, is the written statement of the plaintiff by way of defence to a plea of set-off set up by the defendant.
  • Any other pleading subsequent to the defendant’s WS (other than defence to set-off) can be presented only with the leave of the court and on such terms as the court thinks fit — Order VIII Rule 9.
  • The court may at any time require a written statement or an additional written statement from any party and fix a time for presenting the same (≤ 30 days) — O.8 R.9.
  • Any ground of defence arising after institution of the suit or presentation of the WS may be raised in the WS — Order VIII Rule 8.
  • The subsequent pleading — i.e., plaintiff’s WS — is in some States termed “replication”; the term formerly used in England, where the plaintiff’s written statement is now called “reply”.

(ii) Additional pleadings — detail

  • Court may at any time require a WS or additional WS from plaintiff, defendant or both (O.8 R.8/R.9).
  • Additional pleadings are not subsequent pleadings in the true sense: they are pleadings by way of further and better statement of the nature of the claim or defence, or further and better particulars of any matter stated in the pleadings — these may be ordered under Order VI Rule 5 CPC.

Pleadings vs Conveyancing — the great divide of this paper DU syllabus note

⚖️ PLEADINGS

Dictionary meaning of ‘plead’ = “to state and argue a case.”

  • Contentions of parties in a dispute reduced into writing.
  • Applies to court proceedings — civil & criminal.
  • Begins the lawsuit: plaintiff’s plaint (cause of action) + defendant’s answer (defences & denials).
  • Includes complaint, petition, answer, rejoinder, motion, declaration, memorandum of points & authorities.
  • Goal: determine what the parties are currently fighting about → narrow to definite issues.

📜 CONVEYANCING

Dictionary meaning of ‘conveyance’ = “an act by which property is conveyed or voluntarily transferred from one person to another by means of a written instrument and other formalities.” Also means the ‘instrument’ itself.

  • Applies to documents executed outside court — not for court proceedings.
  • Purpose: creating evidence of a transaction (sale, lease, gift, will…), which may be used before a court only if a dispute later arises.
  • Governed by Transfer of Property Act, Registration Act, Indian Stamp Act, etc.

One-line distinction to quote in exams: “Pleadings are applicable to court proceedings; conveyancing is applicable to documentation done outside the court and not meant for court proceedings particularly — though they may be used in court proceedings to substantiate a particular contention, claim or submission.”

🧭 Backdrop: Substantive vs Procedural law (why a drafting student needs both)

Substantive law decides rights, liabilities and duties (e.g., Indian Penal Code/BNS, Sale of Goods Act, Transfer of Property Act, rent control laws). Procedural law lays down how those rights are decided and enforced — “procedural law lubricates substantive law… it puts life into substantive law by providing remedy” (CPC, CrPC/BNSS, Indian Evidence Act/BSA). Some laws are hybrid: e.g., under the Registration Act, 1908, failure to register a compulsorily-registerable document takes away the substantive right to prove it. A draftsman must see this nexus — law + facts + language. DU material (syllabus note)

Function & Object of Pleadings

DU Case Material pp. 3–4 · Pinson v. Lloyds Bank · advantages that flow from pleadings

“The object of pleadings is to assist the court and the parties to the dispute in its adjudication. Its function is of multi-dimension.”

“The function of a pleading is not simply for the benefit of the parties but also and perhaps primarily for the assistance of a Court by defining with precision the area beyond which, without the leave of the court and consequential amendment of pleading, conflict must not be allowed to extend.” — Stable J., in Pinson v. Lloyds Bank Ltd., (1941) 2 K.B. 72 (quoted in DU Case Material, p. 3; full case note at § 9.1)
“Wherever a system of pleading may exist, the sole object of it is that each side may be fully alive to the questions that are about to be argued, in order that they may have an opportunity of bringing forward such evidence as may be appropriate to the issue.” — Lord Halsbury, quoted in KAMKUS, General Principles of Drafting

4.1 The multi-dimensional functions of pleadings DU material pp. 3–4

📢

1. Fair notice

To give a fair notice to each party of what the opponent’s case is — nobody is taken by surprise at trial.

🎯

2. Precise issue-framing

To ascertain with precision the points on which parties agree and those on which they differ, and thus bring them to a definite issue.

🗑️

3. Eradicating irrelevancy

Purpose of pleading is also eradicating irrelevancy — parties know exactly what facts they must prove.

💰

4. Saving expense of proof

Parties are saved the expense/trouble of calling evidence which may be unnecessary in view of admissions of the opposite party.

🛡️

5. No surprise at trial

By knowing beforehand what points the opponent will raise, parties prepare to meet them — “not taken by surprise.”

🔒

6. Limits the conflict

Conflict cannot extend beyond the pleaded area without leave of court & amendment — pleadings fix the boundary of adjudication.

⚖️ The court is bound by the pleadings

“Truly speaking, the object of the pleading is to narrow down the controversy of the parties to a definite issue… The court has no power to disregard the pleading and reach conclusions that it thinks are just and proper.”

Corollary (KAMKUS): “Where a claim has never been made in the defence, no amount of evidence can be looked into upon a plea which was never put forward.” Courts have repeatedly reversed judgments deciding issues neither raised nor argued — see Gita Rani Paul (§ 9.5).

Fig. 4.1 — How pleadings funnel an unbounded dispute into a decidable issue (O. 14, Rule 1 CPC frames the issues from the pleadings)

4.2 Advantages that flow when issues are fairly & properly ascertained DU material p. 4

  1. Knows what is left in dispute: “They may discover that they are fighting about nothing at all; e.g., when a plaintiff in an action of libel finds that the defendant does not assert that the words are true, he is often willing to accept an apology and costs and so put an end to the action.”
  2. Knows exactly what facts must be proved: otherwise parties may go to great trouble and expense in procuring evidence of facts the opponent does not dispute — and if they assume the opponent will not raise a point, “they may be taken suddenly by surprise at the trial.”
  3. Determines the appropriate mode of trial: ascertaining the nature of the controversy may show it is “a pure point of law, which should be decided by the judge.”
  4. Res judicata safety-net: it is desirable to place on record the precise question raised “so that the parties or their successors may not fight the same battle over and again.”

📢 Duty on the advocate

The DU notes record that a learned Judge of the Calcutta High Court “strongly emphasised the need of careful study of the art of pleading and condemned the obscure pleadings which were shocking — even as filed in the Calcutta High Court.” Therefore “it is the duty of every advocate to take extreme care in drafting his pleadings.” DU Pleadings Notes — also: Orders 6, 7 & 8 CPC are very important for drafting pleadings; Appendix A to the CPC contains model forms which are useful but seldom consulted by mofussil pleadings drafted by untrained clerks.

The Four Fundamental Rules of Pleadings — The Heart of the Unit

DU Case Material pp. 4–9 · English common-law rules on which Order VI CPC is based

“The English law of pleading has got four fundamental rules of pleading upon which Order VI of the Code of Civil Procedure is based”:

1Facts, not law

“Every pleading must state facts and not law.”

2Material facts

“It must state all material facts and material facts only.”

3Facts, not evidence

“It must state only the facts on which the party relies and not the evidence by which they are to be proved.”

4Concise + precise

“It must state such facts concisely, but with precision and certainty.”

MNEMONIC — “F·M·E·C” (Facta, Materialia, Evidentia, Concise)
FFacts — not abstract law MMaterial facts — all & only EExcluding evidence CConcise, precise, certain

1Rule 1 — Every pleading must state FACTS and not LAW DU pp. 4–6

“The first fundamental rule is that neither provisions of law nor conclusions of mixed law and facts should be alleged in a pleading. The pleading should be confined to facts only and it is for the judge to draw such inferences from those facts as are permissible under the law of which he is bound to take judicial notice.”

Why? “It is the duty of the court to find out and examine all pleas of law that may be applicable to the facts of the case.” Parties may, of course, make submissions on law any time — e.g., non-maintainability of the suit, a pure point of law, can be urged even though no specific plea has been raised in the pleading.

✍️ Illustrations — how NOT to plead / how TO plead

❌ BAD (pleading law / conclusion)✅ GOOD (plead the FACTS)💡 Why
“Abu Mohammad made a gift of his property to the plaintiff.” Plead how the gift was made, how it was accepted, and how possession was delivered — the facts constituting a valid gift under Muslim law. “Made a gift” = conclusion of law from unpleaded facts.
“The defendant has been guilty of negligence.” State how and in what respect he was negligent and how he became bound to use due care to prevent injury to others. Duty + breach must be pleaded as facts.
Defence: “The defendant does not owe the plaintiff.” “He never borrowed; goods were never ordered / never delivered; or they were not equal to sample.” Denial of indebtedness without facts is a plea of law.
“The contract is rescinded.” Plead in what manner and by what means it was rescinded. Rescission is a legal conclusion; mode is fact.
“The defendant defamed the plaintiff.” KAMKUS Plead the public utterances/publications, the exact words, their meaning, and innuendo where ambiguous. Words + publication = facts; ‘defamed’ = inference.
“The respondent is guilty of cruelty; petitioner entitled to divorce.” KAMKUS Plead that husband came home drunk and beat her on specific dates, abused her before relations, denied visits to parents, demanded dowry with threats, never cohabited, etc. Only such facts establish physical/mental cruelty.
“Plaintiff is the legal heir of the deceased.” Show how he was related, the relationship of other claimants, and facts showing he was nearer in relation. ‘Legal heir’ is an inference of law. KAMKUS

(a) Foreign laws — no judicial notice, so plead as FACT

“The courts do not take any judicial notice of foreign laws and hence they must be pleaded as facts. The status of the foreign country intended to be relied upon should be set forth as substantially as any other facts.”
Related provisions: Indian Evidence Act, 1872 — proof of foreign law generally by experts/opinion (ss. 45–47); Indian courts take judicial notice only of Indian law. Likewise custom governing parties (e.g., matrimonial custom) must be pleaded unless it has repeatedly received judicial recognition and acquired force of law.

(b) Mixed questions of law and fact — plead both

“Where a question is one of mixed law and fact, it is permissible and proper to plead both the facts and the legal conclusion. For instance, the defendant may say the suit is barred by limitation, or that he is entitled to set-off, after narrating the facts on which he bases his conclusion.”

Also permissible: legal pleas denying the other side’s right — res judicata, limitation, estoppel u/s 116 Evidence Act (landlord vs. tenant) — but these must be taken at the first instance; if not pleaded, no evidence can be led on them, and even amendment may be refused because the defect takes away an accrued right (KAMKUS).

(c) Condition precedent — O. 6, Rule 6 CPC

“Any condition precedent, the performance of which is intended to be contested, shall be distinctly specified in the pleading of the plaintiff or defendant” — O.6 R.6. Instance: legality of the notice under Section 80 CPC (notice to Government before suit). Conversely, performance/occurrence of all conditions precedent is implied unless contested.
Related: s. 80 CPC (two-month notice to Government/Govt. officer); s. 106 TPA (notice to determine tenancy); conditions under specific statutes must be pleaded where contested.

(d) Custom and usage of trade — plead like facts

“Custom and usage of any trade and business shall be pleaded like any other facts if a party wants to rely on them. But a custom repeatedly brought before courts and recognised by them regularly is deemed to have acquired the force of law and need not be pleaded.”

  • Example 1: An occupancy tenant entitled by local custom to cut trees on his holding need not plead that custom to rely on the right.
  • Example 2: A party relying on a usage of a particular trade which is at variance with a provision of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 must plead the usage with its detailed incidents; “if it is not pleaded, no evidence to prove it shall be admitted.” (Related: Contract Act recognition of usage of trade; s. 11 Evidence Act usages.)
  • Where the plea is based on custom, it must be stated in precise form — e.g., a childless Mohammedan widow claiming ¼ share under Shia law must allege her husband was a Shia.
(e) Negligence, right, liability, unlawful/wrongful act — must be specifically pleaded

“The facts of negligence, right or liability, unlawful or wrongful act should be specifically pleaded. Every plea of fact should be specifically raised and proved.” A court cannot permit a party to lead evidence inconsistent with his plea; allowing the other side to lead rebuttal evidence does not cure the legal defect. 2Rule 2 — All MATERIAL FACTS and material facts only DU pp. 6–7

“The second fundamental rule is that every pleading shall contain only a statement of material facts on which the party pleading relies for his claim or defence — enunciated in Order VI Rule 2 CPC.” Omission “may increase the difficulty in the court’s task of ascertaining the rights of the parties.”

What is a ‘material fact’? — the twin test

“The facts which are essential to the plaintiff’s cause of action or to the defendant’s defence. A fact is material if the party is bound to prove it at the trial (unless admitted) before he can succeed in his claim or defence.”

Golden consequence: “Unless a fact is pleaded, it shall NOT be allowed to be proved at the hearing of the suit.”

Rule of caution: a material fact can be inserted later by amendment (O. 6, R. 17) — the right of plaintiff/defendant — but the party amending “is likely to be saddled with the costs of the other side.” Where a suit is brought under a particular statute, all facts necessary to bring the suit within the statute must be alleged; and where a rule of law has an exception, all facts taking the case out of the rule/exception are material.

Instances of ‘material facts’ from the DU material

  1. A childless Mohammedan widow claiming ¼ share under Shia law must allege her husband was a Shia.
  2. A plaintiff claiming alternative relief must plead the facts entitling him to that relief.
  3. Where the question of age or time affects the rights of parties, facts must be specifically pleaded.
  4. A plaintiff suing on the basis of a title must state the nature of the deed from which he derived title.
  5. A plea that a woman claiming maintenance lost her right by continuous desertion / living in adultery must be specifically raised.
  6. Where the plea is based on custom, state it in precise form (Shia-law example again).
  7. DU notes A plaintiff claiming right of pre-emption u/s 15(2)(b) Punjab Pre-emption Act must plead the necessary facts of the claim.
  8. DU notes Every plea of fact must be specifically pleaded and proved.

📗 KAMKUS extension — more instances where materiality is clear

  • Judicial divorce (desertion): respondent left without consent and without justifiable excuse; declarations to neighbours showing animus deserendi.
  • Ejectment of trespasser: defendant “threatens and intends to repeat the illegal act”; for stay of demolition — ownership + sanctioned plans; for government land — undisturbed possession > 12 years.
  • Defamation: words intended to defame / understood so by men at large; innuendo where ambiguous; special damages (e.g., services terminated because of the article) with particulars.
  • Special rule/custom (sapinda marriage): plead that no custom permitted it — or the long-settled family/caste custom that did permit it.
  • Money suit & limitation: facts of part-payment or anything giving the debt a fresh three-year lease.
  • Claim on a document: state the effect of the document (sale-deed dated …, duly registered).
  • Specific performance: plaintiff has always been and is willing to perform his part of the contract.

🚫 Examples of facts NOT material (KAMKUS)

  • Suit on a promissory note: need not plead that plaintiff requested payment and defendant refused — no demand is necessary when the note is due and payable immediately.
  • Suit for goods sold: not material that goods belonged to plaintiff or were sold in belief defendant would honestly pay.
  • General damages are presumed as natural/probable consequences — need not be pleaded; but special damages must be alleged with facts, else the action fails. Likewise, for public nuisance, plead the special injury over and above what is common to others.

3Rule 3 — FACTS, not EVIDENCE DU p. 7

“Every pleading must contain a statement of material facts but not the evidence by which they are to be proved” (O. 6, R. 2). The DU material deploys the classic Latin apparatus:

FACTA PROBANDA — “facts to be proved”

The material facts on which a party relies — these ARE stated in the pleading.

  • They are not facts in issue, but relevant facts which will be proved at trial in order to establish facts in issue.
  • Eg: “Defendant borrowed ₹10 lakh on 1-1-2026; repayable on 1-7-2026; failed despite demand dated 10-7-2026.”

FACTA PROBANTIA — “facts by which proved”

The evidence/facts by which facta probanda are to be proved — these are NOT stated in the pleading.

  • These are the evidence (documents, witnesses, conversations) proving the material facts.
  • Eg: “X witnessed the execution”; “bank statement at Annexure”; “witnesses saw the assault.”

Two classic illustrations from the DU material

1️⃣ Suit for damages for malicious prosecution

PLEAD ONLY: “Defendant was actuated by malice in prosecuting him.”
DO NOT PLEAD: that plaintiff had previously given evidence against defendant and defendant “vowed to take revenge” — that is evidence; plaintiff is free to tender it at the trial.

2️⃣ Life-insurance policy: ‘dies by his own hand’

PLEAD ONLY (defence): “The assured died of his own hand.”
DO NOT PLEAD: that the assured bought the pistol days before death and made preparations to kill himself — evidentiary details.

⚠️ Exception — when facts and evidence are inseparably mixed

“In some cases where the facts in issue and relevant facts are so mixed up that it is very difficult to separate them, the relevant facts may be stated.” Eg: where a custom is based on a village administration paper which is both the basis of claim and its sole proof — the record itself must be pleaded. Conversely, where customs are contained in a Manual of Customary Law (Punjab Rewāja Ām), the manual is only evidence and it is not necessary to refer to it in the plaint. DU notes

⚖️ Bad drafting vs Good drafting — practical demonstrations DPC Skill Notes

❌ BAD DRAFTING

1. Ambiguous pronouns: “He told him that he would pay him when he received it.” (Who is who?)

2. Changing descriptions: ‘Rajesh Kumar Sharma’ → ‘Rajesh’ → ‘Mr Sharma’ → ‘the petitioner’.

3. Vague fraud: “B fraudulently took my property.” — which property? what representation? when? what reliance?

4. Repetition: Para 4, 8 & 12 all say “defendant borrowed ₹10 lakh.”

5. Hedging: “If the defendant had perhaps received the notice, he might have…”

✅ GOOD DRAFTING

1. “The defendant informed the plaintiff that the defendant would pay the plaintiff after receiving the goods.”

2. Consistently: “the plaintiff” … “the defendant” (or ‘plaintiff Ramashankar’ where several).

3. “On 5 June 2026, defendant represented he was absolute owner of property No. X, knowing it had already been sold to C under registered sale-deed dated 1-4-2026; relying thereon, plaintiff paid ₹30 lakh.”

4. Plead the loan once, then state its consequences.

5. “Notice sent by registered post to defendant’s recorded address and delivered on 12 June 2026.”

Signature & Verification of Pleadings

DU Case Material p. 8 (points v–vii) · Orders VI Rules 14, 15, 15A · IPC/BNS liability

✍️ Signature — Order VI Rule 14 CPC

Every pleading shall be signed by the party and his pleader (advocate), if any. If the party is unable to sign “by reason of absence or other good cause”, it may be signed by any person duly authorised by him to sue or defend on his behalf (the authorisation must be produced before the court).

Purpose: to prevent any possible denial by a party that he did not authorise the proceedings; a pleader’s signature alone is not enough even if a vakalatnama is filed — the pleading must bear the signature/thumb impression of the party (KAMKUS).

DU material (point v): “Every pleading shall be signed by the party and his advocate, and if the party is unable to sign, it may be signed by his agent.”

📜 Verification — Order VI Rule 15 (+ Rule 15A)

Every pleading shall be verified at the foot by the party/parties or by some other person proved to the satisfaction of the court to be acquainted with the facts. The verifier must specify, by reference to numbered paragraphs, what he verifies of his own knowledge and what upon information received and believed to be true. The verification shall be signed and state the date and place of signing; Rule 15(4) also requires an affidavit in support.

Rule 15A (inserted by the Commercial Courts Act, 2015) prescribes a commercial-dispute-specific form of verification.

Aim of verification (KAMKUS): “to fix responsibility of the statements made in the pleading upon someone before the court proceeds to adjudicate upon them.”

🏢 Where a CORPORATION is a party DU p. 8 (point vii)

“In cases where a corporation is a party, pleading may be verified by the Secretary, or by a Director, or by any other principal officer of the corporation who is able to depose to the facts of the case.” In the verification clause one should denote, according to the paragraph numbers, what is verified of own knowledge and what upon information received and believed to be true.

Four drafting questions when the client is a company (DPC Skill Notes):

  1. Who is authorised? 2) What is the source of authority (board resolution/POA)? 3) Can that person depose to the relevant facts? 4) Is the authority document being placed on record?

Real example — DU model Order 37 suit: “Mr. P, Executive Director of the Plaintiff, is a duly constituted attorney… authorised and competent to sign and verify the plaint, vakalatnama etc. and to institute this suit on behalf of the Plaintiff.”

🚨 False verification = criminal liability DU p. 8 (point vi)

“False verification is an offence punishable by the Indian Penal Code, as making a false statement is by itself an offence. Therefore the responsibility of verification is very great.”

Old law (IPC, 1860)

s. 191 — giving false evidence;
s. 193 — punishment: if in a stage of a judicial proceeding, imprisonment up to 7 years + fine; other cases up to 3 years. False affidavits fall within “false evidence”.

New law (BNS, 2023)

s. 227 — giving false evidence (corresponds to IPC 191);
s. 229 — punishment (corresponds to IPC 193) — same quantum: up to 7 years in judicial proceedings. (Effective 1-7-2024.)

Model verification clause (as in the prescribed material)

“I, ………… (name), son of Shri …………… (father’s name), do hereby verify that the contents of paragraphs 1 to …… of the above plaint are true to my personal knowledge, and that the contents of paragraphs …… are based on information received and believed to be true.” Verified at Delhi on this …… day of …… 20…   |   Sd/- (Party)
DU models add: “Last para is the humble prayer to the Hon’ble Court.” (so verification must also cover the prayer.)

Note (KAMKUS): a defective verification is not fatal to the suit — the court cannot dismiss on that ground alone; the defect can be cured by amendment (plaint then relates back to original date). But defective verification causes delay — so draft it with the same care as the body.

Alternative Pleas — Permission, Illustrations & Limits

DU Case Material pp. 8–9 (closing topic of the unit)

The rule

“Law does not prohibit a plaintiff from relying on several distinct and different rights in the alternative, or a defendant from raising as many distinct and separate defences as he likes.”

DU illustrations

ScenarioPermissible alternative pleas
Plaintiff suing for possession of A’s house(i) As the adopted son of A; and in the alternative, under a will executed by A in his favour.
Claim to landProprietary right; or in the alternative, easementary right (over a disputed passage).
Pre-emption actionDefendant may set up estoppel in addition to a denial of the custom of pre-emption.
Hindu purchaser from a Hindu widow(i) Widow separated during husband’s lifetime → she was owner when she sold; or alternatively, widow was in possession over 12 years → owner by adverse possession.
Defendant sued on a promissory note(i) “I never executed the promissory note”; and in the alternative, “plaintiff’s claim is barred by limitation.”

⚠️ The three drafting disciplines of alternative pleas

  1. They must be SEPARATELY & EXPRESSLY stated. “The court will not allow any such pleas on grounds covered by implication unless specifically set out.”
  2. Plead the facts of each alternative. DU example: in a suit by a son to set apart transfers by his mother on the ground of her unsoundness of mind, he further averred the donee was residing with his mother and completely under her dominion and control, and knew her mental condition — such facts must be pleaded for the alternative to bite.
  3. Alternative ≠ mutually destructive. “I never entered the contract” + “I fully performed the contract”, pleaded simultaneously without alternative structure, destroys both. Courts allow alternative pleas but inconsistent/mutually destructive pleadings are not permitted (also the limit on amendments under O.6 R.17). DPC Skill Notes

✅ VALID ALTERNATIVE STRUCTURE

Primary: “Defendant never executed the promissory note dated 1-1-2024.”

Alternative (without prejudice): “Even assuming execution, plaintiff’s claim is barred by limitation, the note having become time-barred on ….”

Positions can coexist logically: if fact A fails, consider legal position B.

❌ MUTUALLY DESTRUCTIVE PLEAS

“There was no contract at all” and “the contract was fully performed by me” — without pleadings in the alternative, each destroys the other; the court cannot act on a self-contradictory case.

Draftsman must structure, label and separate them (“in the alternative”, “without prejudice to the above”).

Statutory anchors: Order VI Rule 12 expressly permits pleading alternative implied contracts/relations; Order VIII Rules 6 & 6A allow set-off and counter-claim alongside denials; Order VII permits claiming alternative reliefs. The general right flows from Order VI Rule 2 read with settled case-law — alternative claims/defences allowed, mutually destructive ones not. Nalsar/Justice Anand Venkatesh on structuring pleadings

1 · Pinson v. Lloyds and National Provincial Foreign Bank Ltd.
(1941) 2 K.B. 72 : [1941] 2 All E.R. 636 : 57 T.L.R. 558 (English Court of Appeal) — as cited in DU Case Material, p. 3
📕 DU Case Material — Function & Object of Pleadings⚖️ English pleading law📌 Parent of Order VI doctrine
Facts

The litigation arose out of a banking/financial transaction in which the claimants sued the bank group; the contest turned substantially on the sufficiency of the pleadings — the statement of claim and the particulars delivered with it. The defendants contended that the statement of claim failed to disclose material facts, and that the particulars could not be used to patch the deficiencies of a pleading. The matter travelled to the English Court of Appeal, and the observations reproduced in the DU material are attributed to Stable J. (the observations on particulars are of Scott L.J. at pp. 75, 79).

DU material reproduces only the principal pronouncement on the function of a pleading; the facts above are drawn from the reported judgment’s context as relied upon in pleading jurisprudence worldwide (cited in the Supreme Court of Nigeria, NSW Supreme Court, Australian HCA etc.).

Issues
  1. What is the function of a pleading in a civil action?
  2. Can particulars supply material facts omitted from the statement of claim — i.e., can they cure an inherently bad pleading?
  3. Must a defendant plead to the particulars delivered by the other side?
Arguments — Plaintiff (claimant)

The pleadings along with the delivered particulars gave the defendants full notice of the case; the documents should be read together, and any supposed gap in the statement of claim was sufficiently filled by the particulars, so the action should proceed to trial.

Arguments — Defendant (Bank)

The statement of claim itself must contain the material facts; particulars are not a substitute for averments in a pleading. A party “need not — and indeed ought not — to plead to particulars”, because where particulars are properly limited to making pleaded facts more specific, an answer to the facts as pleaded answers the facts as particularised.

Decision

The Court accepted the orthodox doctrine and laid down two canons that are still quoted across the common-law world:

  1. “The proper function of particulars is not to state the material facts omitted from the statement of claim in order, by filling the gaps, to make good an inherently bad pleading — however common that pernicious practice may have become.” (per Scott L.J., p. 75)
  2. A defendant need not, and ought not to, plead to particulars (p. 75); particulars do not amount to amendments of the pleading.

And Stable J.’s formulation — the one the DU material reproduces — was adopted as the very definition of the function of pleadings: a pleading defines with precision the area beyond which, without leave of court and consequential amendment, conflict must not be allowed to extend.

Principle evolved: (1) Pleadings — not particulars — are the battleground: they define with precision the area of conflict, primarily for the assistance of the court and only secondarily for the parties; (2) conflict cannot spread beyond the pleaded area without leave of court + amendment; (3) particulars merely particularise pleaded material facts — they can never manufacture a cause of action missing from the pleadings; (4) fairness of trial demands each side know the other’s case beforehand — surprise is anathema to pleading law. This single case is the intellectual root of Order VI CPC’s entire scheme (material facts, no evidence, amendment discipline) and of the DU material’s insistence that “the court has no power to disregard the pleading.”
2 · Deepak Bajaj v. State of Maharashtra
(2008) 16 SCC 14, reported also as 2010 (4) SCC (Cri) 122 — Supreme Court of India (judgment: 12 November 2008)
📕 DU Case Material — inside model Writ Petition (Crl), pp. 64–67🛡️ Preventive detention · Art. 226/227📌 COFEPOSA s. 3(1)
Facts

The appellant Deepak Bajaj was under a preventive detention order dated 22-5-2008 passed under s. 3(1) of the Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act, 1974 (COFEPOSA). The grounds of detention (running into 76 pages) alleged that he imported 29 duty-free consignments meant as raw material for goods meant to be exported, but sold them in the local market, and misused duty-free replenishment certificates (DFR), among other allegations. Critically, the detaining authority had relied on confessional statements of witnesses, but the retractions of those confessions — and other relevant material — were not placed before the detaining authority by the sponsoring authority. The challenge was mounted at the pre-execution (pre-arrest) stage by way of writ petition, argued by Shri Soli Sorabjee (for petitioner).

How the DU material uses it: the DU model Writ Petition (Crl) challenging a detention order under the NDPS Act quotes this judgment at para 3 to summarise the law on pre-execution challenge — demonstrating how a draftsman marshals precedent inside the grounds of a pleading.

Issues
  1. Is a petition to quash a preventive-detention order maintainable at the pre-arrest/pre-execution stage?
  2. Are the five grounds laid down in Alka Subhash Gadia illustrative or exhaustive?
  3. Does non-placement of retractions of confessional statements (and other relevant material) before the detaining authority vitiates the detention order?
Arguments — Petitioner (Deepak Bajaj)
  • Challenge at pre-execution stage is maintainable where the order is clearly illegal; liberty is a precious fundamental right under Art. 21 and should not be lightly transgressed.
  • The detention order is non est because material — retractions of the very confessions relied upon — was never placed before the detaining authority; had it been placed, the authority might not have passed the order (cf. Ashadevi v. K. Shivraj, 1979 (1) SCC 222).
  • Detaining authority must apply its mind to all material; non-consideration of vital material vitiates satisfaction.
  • Alternatively, continued detention is futility — activities were of 2002 vintage; no live nexus.
Arguments — State / Respondent
  • As a general rule, an unexecuted detention order cannot be quashed at pre-execution stage unless one of the exceptional grounds is made out; detention orders must be executed, not litigated in advance.
  • Relevant material was considered; duty is not to collect every document from every ministry.
  • The petitioner was an absconder wilfully evading execution (publication/citation under s. 82 CrPC), which itself weighed against discretionary relief.
Decision
  • Writ petition ALLOWED; detention order dated 22-5-2008 quashed, no costs.
  • The Court rested on one dispositive ground: “the relevant material was not placed before the Detaining Authority… failure to place the retractions… would certainly vitiate the impugned detention order.”
  • It held the five grounds in Alka Subhash Gadia are illustrative, not exhaustive — entertaining a pre-execution petition is an exception, not the general rule.
  • Reputation is a facet of Art. 21: “If a person is sent to jail, then even if he is subsequently released, his reputation may be irreparably tarnished.”
What the DU material extracts for drafting (verbatim summary in the model writ)
  1. (a) The five grounds of Alka Subhash Gadia are illustrative and not exhaustive; a judgment is not to be read “mechanically as a Euclid’s theorem, nor as if it were a statute”.
  2. (b) Pre-execution challenge is exception, not general rule — but if the person satisfies the court the order is clearly illegal, the court should not compel him to go to jail for an illegality later vindicated; Art. 21 liberty not lightly transgressed.
  3. (c) Non-placement of retractions and other relevant material before the detaining authority vitiates the detention order even at pre-execution stage.
Principle evolved: (1) Pre-execution judicial review of preventive-detention orders lies in the rare, exceptional zone — illustrative five heads + any ground making the order clearly illegal; (2) the detaining authority’s subjective satisfaction must be based on complete and fair material — suppression or non-placement of exculpatory material (here, retractions) is itself a vitiation; (3) if a confession is considered, its retraction must also be placed and considered; (4) reputation = facet of Art. 21. Drafting lesson from the DU usage: a well-drafted writ quotes the controlling precedent’s proposition in numbered propositions and ties each to pleaded facts (delay, stale incident, non-placement) — the model of “law + facts + citation” pleading.
3 · Addl. Secretary to the Government of India v. Smt. Alka Subhash Gadia
1992 Supp (1) SCC 496 : 1992 SCC (Cri) 301 — Supreme Court of India
📕 Cited in DU Case Material (inside Writ Petition draft + Deepak Bajaj summary)🛡️ Preventive detention
Facts

Smt. Alka Subhash Gadia challenged a preventive-detention order before it was executed, by writ petitions under Arts. 226/32. The question of when — and on what grounds — a writ court may interfere with a detention order at the pre-execution stage was authoritatively answered by the Supreme Court.

Issue

Can a detention order be set aside by a writ court before execution/arrest — and if so, in which exceptional situations?

Arguments

Detenu: court may interfere whenever illegality is shown; the categories need not be closed.
State: as a rule, detention must be executed; challenge ordinarily lies post-execution; interference at pre-stage is impermissible.

Decision — the FIVE GROUNDS for pre-execution interference
  1. The impugned order is not passed under the Act under which it is purported to have been passed;
  2. It is sought to be executed against a wrong person;
  3. It is passed for a wrong purpose;
  4. It is passed on vague, extraneous and irrelevant grounds;
  5. The authority which passed it had no authority to do so.

General rule: no interference pre-execution; these are the exceptional instances (later held illustrative by Deepak Bajaj, and elaborated in Sayed Taher Bawamiya, (2000) 8 SCC 630).

Principle evolved: Preventive detention — the “exceptional circumstances” doctrine: writ courts keep their hands off unexecuted detention orders as a rule, except in the five stated classes (and thereafter, any case of clear illegality). Drafting lesson: the DU model writ organises its grounds around exactly these heads — wrong purpose / vague grounds / stale incident / procedural infirmity — teaching the student to plead facts under legal heads (“ground-wise” drafting).
4 · Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra & Anr.
(2002) 4 SCC 388 : W.P. (C) No. 509/97 (with connected matters) — Supreme Court of India (Constitution Bench)
📗 DU Drafting & Pleadings Notes — Curative Petition certificate📜 Arts. 129, 136, 141, 142
Facts

The petitioner challenged a final judgment of the Supreme Court itself, after the ordinary remedies had been exhausted — review petition was dismissed (by circulation). With no further appeal available, she sought reopening of the judgment on the footing that she had been denied a hearing and that a real party in interest had not been impleaded. The Court examined whether its jurisdiction could be invoked after final disposal in the absence of any statutory right of appeal.

How the DU notes use it: the DU material’s model “Curative Petition” contains a certificate keyed to “the requirements laid down in the judgment… reported as 2002 (4) SCC 388” — students must plead compliance with this judgment’s conditions.

Issue

Whether, in the absence of a right of appeal, a person aggrieved by the Supreme Court’s own final judgment can invoke its jurisdiction under Art. 132/136 read with Arts. 141 and 142 to set it aside, and if so, on what conditions?

Arguments — Petitioner
  • Natural justice violated (no personal hearing in review); a person cannot be left without any remedy — ubi jus ibi remedium.
  • Power to do complete justice under Art. 142 includes power to recall its own judgment where no remedy exists.
  • Affidavit also alleged the daughter of the original respondent was a necessary party who was never heard.
Arguments — Respondents / concerns of the Court
  • Finality of judgments is sacrosanct: res judicata and constructive res judicata bar re-agitation; Art. 136 is a discretionary remedy to be used sparingly.
  • Allowing a “third” stage would undermine the appellate hierarchy and open floodgates.
  • Review (O. XLVII R. 1) is the last resort ordinarily; a curative route must be extraordinary, rare and structured.
Decision
  • The Court acknowledged a residual power to cure a patent defect of jurisdiction / denial of natural justice where no other remedy is available — the “curative petition” — as a rare invocation of Arts. 129/136 read with 141/142.
  • Laid down the guidelines: petition filed within 30 days of the judgment; must be supported by a certificate by a Senior Advocate of there being a “knock at the doors of justice” (strong, prima facie case + disclosure of shortening of limitations); full record of earlier proceedings; review petition must have been by circulation; parties to be heard only in open court (court may, in the first instance, call for explanations in chamber); no merits re-hearing as of right.
  • The petition in the case itself was allowed on the procedural limb (notice/impleadment issues) — the Court protected finality while keeping a “rare remedy” window.
Principle evolved: (1) No person can be left remedied against a final judgment obtained without opportunity of hearing — the curative petition is the “last judicial resort”; (2) it is an exception, not an appeal — the grounds are rigid (violation of natural justice, fraud, no alternative remedy); (3) structure and formalities (senior-counsel certificate, 30 days, open-court hearing) themselves protect finality. Drafting lesson: read the DU model certificate — a pleading can pre-empt maintainability objections by pleading compliance with the governing judgment’s checklist (exactly what Rupa Ashok Hurra requires).
5 · Gita Rani Paul v. Dibyendra Kumar
AIR 1991 SC 393 — Supreme Court of India (on appeal from Calcutta High Court)
📙 KAMKUS — “Absence of plea in written statement”⚖️ No decision on unpleaded issue
Facts

A property dispute travelled from the Calcutta High Court to the Supreme Court. The appellate court (High Court) had accepted an appeal on an issue which was neither raised nor argued before the court below — i.e., it decided the matter on a point outside the pleadings/issues that had framed the litigation at the trial stage.

Issue

Can an appellate court sustain a judgment on a ground/issue that was never raised, framed or argued in the court below?

Arguments

Appellant (successful party in SC): the High Court travelled beyond the pleadings; parties were never afforded an opportunity to meet that issue — decision violates fairness and the rules of pleading.
Respondent: the issue arose on the record and could be treated as covered.

Decision

The Supreme Court reversed the Calcutta High Court, holding that the High Court fell into error in accepting the appeal on an issue neither raised nor argued before the court below.

Principle evolved: No case can be decided on an issue not raised by the pleadings and framed by the court; “where a claim has never been made… no amount of evidence can be looked into upon a plea which was never put forward” (KAMKUS’s statement of the same doctrine). This is the flip-side of Pinson: pleadings protect both sides — you cannot spring unpleaded issues, and you cannot succeed on facts you never pleaded. Link: also supports Rule 2 (material facts) — plead everything you must prove.
6 · Chunni Lal Chawdhary v. Bank of Baroda
1981 Sri L.J. 411 (as cited in KAMKUS)
📙 KAMKUS — Effect of non-filing of Written Statement⚖️ O. 8 CPC consequences
Facts

The defendant-bank did not file a written statement in the suit. The trial court and the parties proceeded with evidence; the question was the extent to which a defendant without a WS can participate — cross-examine the plaintiff’s witnesses and lead evidence.

Issue

(a) Does non-filing of WS amount to admission of all plaint allegations? (b) Can such a defendant cross-examine on facts he never pleaded?

Arguments

Defendant: no prejudice; it may contest through cross-examination anyway. Plaintiff/Bank: without a WS there is nothing to meet; admissions by non-denial should hold; any cross-examination must be confined to pleaded matters.

Decision

Held: (i) non-filing of WS does not mean the defendant admitted all facts; he may still take part in the hearing and cross-examine the plaintiff’s witnesses to demolish their version-in-chief; BUT (ii) he cannot cross-examine on questions of fact which he himself has not pleaded, nor adduce evidence on facts not pleaded by filing any written statement.

Principle evolved: Pleading is the gateway of evidence — “no pleading → no evidence on new facts”, yet silence in WS is not a universal admission. Balanced discipline: participation allowed, expansion of issues not. Drafting lesson: this is why the written statement must plead every defence (newly arisen grounds too, O.8 R.8) — the client’s evidentiary rights are capped by his pleadings.

DU · 2017 · Q1(a)“What are the fundamental rules of pleadings in respect of drafting a plaint?”
MODEL ANSWER

Introduction: Pleadings are statements of facts in writing drawn up and filed by each party stating what his contention shall be at trial and giving his opponent all details needed to prepare his answer (DU definition). In India, “pleading” means plaint or written statement (O. 6, R. 1 CPC). The English law of pleading has four fundamental rules on which Order VI CPC is based — and every plaint must satisfy all four.

Rule 1 — Pleading must state FACTS and not LAW
  • Neither provisions of law nor conclusions of mixed law and fact may be alleged; the judge draws legal inferences from pleaded facts (judicial notice of Indian law).
  • Illustrations: pleading only “Abu Mohammad made a gift” is bad — plead how the gift was made, accepted and possession delivered; “defendant is negligent” is bad — plead how and in what respect; “contract is rescinded” bad — plead the manner of rescission.
  • Exceptions: (a) foreign law (no judicial notice — plead as fact); (b) mixed questions of law and fact (limitation, set-off — plead facts + conclusion); (c) condition precedent intended to be contested must be distinctly specified (O. 6, R. 6 — e.g., legality of s. 80 notice); (d) custom and trade usage pleaded like facts (unless judicially recognised as law); (e) negligence, unlawful act etc. must be specifically pleaded.
Rule 2 — All MATERIAL FACTS and material facts only (O. 6, R. 2)
  • Material facts = facts essential to cause of action/defence which the party must prove to succeed. If in doubt, plead it — an unpleaded fact cannot be proved at trial.
  • Instances: Shia-law widow must plead husband was a Shia; alternative relief needs its facts; age/time facts; nature of title-deed; loss of maintenance by desertion/adultery; custom in precise form.
  • Exceptions: (a) contents of documents — state the effect briefly (sale-deed dated…) unless precise words material; (b) matters of inducement — introductory facts, kept to minimum; (c) performance of conditions precedent implied unless contested; (d) matters law presumes / burden on other side (consideration in note-suit, s. 118 NI Act); (e) evidence — never.
Rule 3 — FACTS, not EVIDENCE
  • Plead facta probanda (facts to be proved), not facta probantia (facts by which proved).
  • Illustrations: malicious-prosecution plaintiff pleads “actuated by malice” only — not the revenge-vow evidence; insurance defence pleads “died of his own hand” — not pistol-purchase details. Exception: where fact-in-issue and evidence mix (custom proved by village administration paper), the record itself is pleaded.
Rule 4 — Concise, with precision and certainty
  • Summary form, strict chronological order, active voice, consistent names, consecutively numbered paragraphs (one fact per para), dates/sums in figures (as well as words — O.6 R.2(3)); concise but never obscure; parties cannot change their case.
  • Signature by party + advocate (O. 6, R. 14) and verification distinguishing own knowledge vs information (O. 6, R. 15); corporation pleads through secretary/director/principal officer; false verification is punishable (IPC 191/193 → BNS 227/229).

Conclusion: A plaint obeying these four rules narrows the controversy to definite issues, prevents surprise, saves proof-expense and enables the court to frame issues (O. 14) — “the court has no power to disregard the pleading.”

DU · 2017 · Q5(b)“How is ‘conveyancing’ different from ‘pleadings’?”
MODEL ANSWER

Pleadings (dictionary ‘plead’ = to state and argue a case) are the parties’ contentions in a dispute reduced to writing — beginning-stage documents of a lawsuit (plaint + written statement + related petitions/replies). They apply to court proceedings, civil and criminal, and aim to determine what the parties are fighting about; they are filed to invoke the court’s adjudicatory process.

Conveyancing (‘conveyance’ = act by which property is voluntarily transferred by written instrument) is the drafting of documents executed outside court — wills, sale deeds, leases, mortgages, gifts — whose purpose is to create evidence of a transaction, not to argue a dispute. They may later be used in court only if a dispute arises.

One-line answer: “Pleadings are applicable to court proceedings; conveyancing is applicable to documentation done outside the court — though such documents may be used in proceedings to substantiate a contention.” (DU material) Both, however, are products of the same skill: synthesis of law and fact in language form.

PRESCRIBED QB · KAMKUS Q1“What do you mean by pleading? Discuss its fundamental rules.”
MODEL ANSWER (structure for a 10-mark question)

Meaning: (i) O. 6, R. 1: pleading = plaint or written statement; (ii) working definition: written statements, drawn up and filed by each party, stating his trial-contentions with details the opponent needs to prepare his answer; (iii) plaint = statement of claim with cause of action and particulars (O. 7, R. 1); WS = statement of defences dealing with every material fact + new facts + objections; (iv) classification: original / subsequent (reply to set-off as of right; replication/reply) / additional (O. 6 R. 5; O. 8 Rr. 8–9).

Fundamental rules (state the four in bold, then 2–3 lines each with provision + one illustration + one exception as in the 2017 answer above):

  1. Facts, not law (+ 5 exceptions); 2. All material facts and only them (O. 6 R. 2; test of materiality; documents’ effect / inducement exceptions); 3. Facts, not evidence (facta probanda vs facta probantia); 4. Concise, precise, certain (chronology, numbered paras, signature & verification).

Object-link (value addition): These rules operationalise the object of pleadings — fair notice, precise issues, eradication of irrelevancy — as Stable J. said in Pinson v. Lloyds Bank, a pleading defines with precision the area beyond which conflict must not extend.

Conclusion: Rules are the skeleton; skill (style, marshalling of facts) is the flesh — both needed for the “perfect pleading” the DU material demands.

EXPECTED · UNIT I“Explain the objects and functions of pleadings. What advantages flow from them?”
MODEL ANSWER

Objects: assist the court and parties in adjudication; fair notice of opponent’s case; ascertain points of agreement/difference → definite issue; eradicate irrelevancy; know what facts to prove; avoid surprise; save expense of unnecessary evidence; confine conflict (no power to disregard pleadings).

Advantages (DU’s four): (i) parties know exactly what is left in dispute (libel example — may settle for apology+costs); (ii) know precisely what facts to prove — no waste, no surprise; (iii) determine the appropriate mode of trial (may be a pure point of law); (iv) record the precise question so successors don’t fight the same battle again.

Quote: Stable J. in Pinson (function of pleading) + Lord Halsbury (each side fully alive to the questions to be argued). Converse: unpleaded issues cannot be decided (Gita Rani Paul); unpleaded facts cannot be proved (Chunni Lal Chawdhary).

EXPECTED · RULE 1“Pleadings must state facts and not law — discuss exceptions.”
MODEL ANSWER

Rule: no legal provisions or legal conclusions in pleadings; judge applies law to facts (judicial notice). Reason: court’s duty to examine every applicable law; parties may argue law any time (non-maintainability may be urged even if not pleaded). Illustrations: gift (plead making/acceptance/delivery of possession); negligence (plead duty + breach facts); money-suit defence (plead never borrowed / goods never ordered or delivered / not equal to sample); rescission (plead manner); declaratory suit (plead how related, not “legal heir”).

Exceptions (five, DU): (a) foreign law — pleaded as fact (courts don’t take judicial notice; prove by expert opinion, Evidence Act ss. 45–47); (b) mixed law & fact — plead both (limitation, set-off, res judicata, estoppel u/s 116 Evidence Act — but at first instance); (c) condition precedent contested — O. 6 R. 6 (s. 80 notice); (d) custom & trade usage — like facts unless judicially recognised force of law; unpleaded usage → no evidence; (e) negligence/unlawful/wrongful acts — specific facts required. Close with: pleadings are tolerated legal inferences only where the facts justifying them are themselves pleaded.

EXPECTED · SIGNATURE & VERIFICATION“Discuss the law relating to signature and verification of pleadings.”
MODEL ANSWER

Signature — O. 6, R. 14: party + pleader; if party cannot sign (absence/good cause), duly authorised person signs; authorisation produced in court; purpose — prevent denial of authorisation. DU point v: signed by party and advocate; agent if party unable.

Verification — O. 6, R. 15: at the foot, by party or person acquainted with facts to court’s satisfaction; specify, by paragraph numbers, what is verified of own knowledge vs information received and believed true; sign with date & place; affidavit in support (R. 15(4)); R. 15A for commercial disputes. Model clause (quote the specimen in § 6). Liability: false verification = false evidence — IPC ss. 191/193 → BNS ss. 227/229 (up to 7 years in judicial proceedings). EXPECTED · ALTERNATIVE PLEAS“Can a party plead in the alternative? Illustrate with DU examples.”
MODEL ANSWER

Yes — law does not prohibit a plaintiff relying on several distinct rights in the alternative, or a defendant raising as many defences as he likes. DU illustrations (all five): adopted-son vs will; proprietary vs easementary right; estoppel + denial of pre-emption custom; widow-separated vs 12-year adverse possession; promissory note not-executed vs limitation-barred.

Disciplines: (1) plead each alternative separately and expressly — courts will not act on implication; (2) plead the facts of each alternative (son-vs-mother’s-donee illustration: dominion, control, knowledge of unsound mind); (3) alternatives must not be mutually destructive; (4) statutory anchors — O. 6 R. 12 (alternative implied contracts), O. 8 Rr. 6/6A (set-off/counter-claim), O. 7 (alternative relief).

KAMKUS Q11“How can a plaint be amended?”
MODEL ANSWER

Provision: O. 6, R. 17 CPC — court may at any stage allow either party to alter/amend pleadings on such terms as may be just; all amendments necessary for determining the real questions in controversy shall be made (mandatory limb). Proviso (2002): no application after commencement of trial unless, despite due diligence, the matter could not have been raised earlier. Procedure: application (IA) stating proposed amendment + reasons; court considers delay, bad faith, prejudice compensable by costs.

Principles: amendment is of wide ameliorative purpose — prevent multiplicity; justice on merits; parties not refused relief for mistake/negligence/inadvertence unless mala fide or incurable injury (Ganga Bai; B.K. Narayana Pillai; Revajeetu Builders). Limits: no new/inconsistent case (O. 6 R. 7); no constructive res judicata; amendment relates back to original date (so limitation is saved) — cf. also defective verification cured by amendment. Material facts omitted at peril — hence amend before trial, not after.

(b) s. 138 NI complaint — mirror DU Form 29: complainant details; accused details; cheque details (No./date/amount/bank); cheque in discharge of legally enforceable debt/ liability (plead the underlying debt — material fact); presentation & return memo; notice u/s 138(b) dated … served on …; 15-day period expired on … without payment → cause of action arose on …; complaint filed within one month (s. 142(b)); jurisdiction (place of drawee bank branch — ss. 142/142A NI); relief: punishment u/s 138 — imprisonment up to two years, or fine up to twice the amount of the cheque, or both (plus compensation u/s 357(3) CrPC where appropriate); verification; list of documents (cheque, dishonour memo, notice, annexures).

Cross-application of Unit I rules: same facts, two fora — plead facts once in each draft with forum-specific statutory particulars; keep evidence out; chronological numbering.

QB · KAMKUS Q15“What do you mean by written statement? What are the two parts of a W.S.?”
MODEL ANSWER

Meaning: the defendant’s statement of defence dealing with every material fact alleged in the plaint, setting up new facts in his favour, and taking objections to the claim — filed under O. 8 CPC (within 30 days, extendable to 90). Non-filing does not admit everything (Chunni Lal Chawdhary), but bars evidence on unpleaded facts.

Part I — Statement on merits (traverse): para-wise admission/denial of each material allegation of the plaint, as required by O. 8 R. 3 — specific denial, specifying which parts are admitted; evasive denials prohibited; new facts of defence pleaded affirmatively.

Part II — Preliminary objections / legal defences: jurisdiction, limitation, res judicata, estoppel, non-joinder, discharge, set-off (O. 8 R. 6) and counter-claim (O. 8 R. 6A, to be expressly stated “by way of counter-claim”) — each raised separately and distinctly (O. 8 R. 7), with alternative pleas expressly labelled.

Close: verification + signature (O. 6 Rr. 14–15) as in plaint.

📌 Definition & object in 60 seconds

  • Pleading = plaint or WS (O. 6 R. 1); statement of facts filed to declare trial-contentions + give opponent notice.
  • Objects: notice · precise issue · eradicate irrelevancy · no surprise · save proof-cost · limit conflict · record for successors.
  • Quotes: Pinson (area of conflict) + Halsbury (fully alive to questions).

📌 Four rules in four lines

  • 1 Facts not law — judge applies law (5 exceptions: foreign, mixed, condition precedent, custom, specific wrongs).
  • 2 Material facts only — plead-all + only-these (exceptions: document-effect, inducement, presumptions).
  • 3 Not evidence — facta probanda yes, facta probantia no (mixed-fact exception).
  • 4 Concise + precise — chronology, numbered paras, active voice, consistent names, figures, verification.

🚩 Top 8 examinee-errors in this unit (avoid!)

  1. Calling the 4 rules “Order 7 rules” — they are English rules on which Order VI is based.
  2. Forgetting the exceptions to Rule 1 (five) — half marks lost.
  3. Not distinguishing subsequent vs additional pleadings (replication; O.8 Rr. 8–9; O.6 R.5).
  4. Pleading document contents in full instead of the effect.
  5. Forgetting that dates are now in figures as well as words.
  6. Skipping verification paragraph-numbering (own knowledge vs information).
  7. Leaving alternative pleas implicit.
  8. Citing cases without principle — always end with “Held/principle: …”.
