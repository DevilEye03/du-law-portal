What this unit is

After the theory of Unit I (“Drafting Rules & Skills”, pp. 1–9), the DU case material moves to practice: twelve ready-to-use model forms covering the entire life-cycle of a civil case — institution (suit, plaints), defence (written statement), interim stage (injunctions, affidavit), access to court (indigent person), post-judgment (execution), and special applications (caveat, transfer). Each form is a specimen of the four fundamental rules of pleading operating in real drafting: material facts in chronological order, no evidence, precise dates/sums, verification + supporting affidavit. These notes explain every form paragraph-by-paragraph, add the governing provisions (CPC Orders XXXVII, XXXIX, XXXIII, XXI; ss. 151, 148A, 25; Specific Relief Act; Transfer of Property Act; Limitation Act…), the case law, and the DU/PYQ questions on each form.

Study method: (1) read the master map below; (2) for each form — first the “provision box” (the law), then the “anatomy/walkthrough” (the DU model paragraph-wise), then the boxed exam points; (3) finish with § 14 (cases) and § 15 (PYQs). Everything marked DU is verbatim/near-verbatim from the prescribed case material.

1.1 The eight components (with the provision or rule behind each)

🧩 The one-line story method (skill notes, applied to every form)

“A good pleading tells the Court a legally relevant story in logical sequence.” For any DU form, ask in order: Who? → What transaction? → What performance? → How much? → What default/breach? → What demand/notice? → What cause of action? → Why this Court? → What provision? → What relief? → Who verifies? pleaded by reference (material fact) → annexed (notice to the other side) → proved at trial (evidence). Never quote the whole document; state its effect (O. VI R. 9).

✍️ Drafting mechanics repeated across all twelve DU forms

  • Paragraph discipline: every allegation “That…” in its own numbered paragraph; connected facts may share a paragraph only if inseparable (O. VI R. 2(2)).
  • Dates & sums in figures and words (O. VI R. 2(3)); the DU forms leave blanks with “Rs. …../- (Amount in words)” — never omit the words.
  • Brevity clause: applications incorporate the parent pleading by reference — “contents of the accompanying suit may be read as part and parcel of this application which are not repeated here for the sake of brevity” (DU Forms 4, 6 — KAMKUS IA practice).
  • Affidavit off-loading: where facts are long, the application says “For the reasons above in the annexed affidavit, the applicant prays…” (KAMKUS IA drafting rule).
  • “Note” at the end of DU forms: the material’s own exam pointers — e.g. Form 1: “correct paragraphs in verification · territorial & pecuniary jurisdiction”; Form 9: “Counter-claim, Set-off can be joined… verified and supported by affidavit”.

2.1 The whole of Order XXXVII — rule by rule

RuleWhat it says (exam-ready)
R. 1 — ScopeOrder applies to suits for recovery of money or other property by the plaintiff against a defendant, where the claim: (a) is founded upon a bill of exchange, hundi or promissory note; or (b) is for a liquidated demand in money payable by the defendant with or without interest — arising upon a written contract, or upon an enactment (fixed sum / debt other than a penalty), or upon a guarantee (principal claim being debt or liquidated demand). Not for unliquidated damages, declaratory or equitable reliefs.
R. 2 — InstitutionPlaint states expressly that it is filed under O. XXXVII and claims no relief outside the Order. Court issues summons; defendant must appear within 10 days of service and give address for service. Then plaintiff serves “summons for judgment” supported by affidavit verifying the cause of action and amount.
R. 3 — Appearance & leave to defendR. 3(5): within 10 days of service of summons for judgment, defendant may apply by affidavit or otherwise, disclosing facts sufficient to entitle him to defend — leave granted unconditionally or on terms (deposit/security). Proviso: leave shall not be refused unless court satisfied the facts disclosed do not indicate a substantial defence or that the intended defence is frivolous/vexatious. 2nd Proviso: part admitted → that part must be deposited first. R. 3(6): no appearance / no application / refusal of leave / breach of conditions → plaintiff entitled to judgment forthwith (deemed admissions). R. 3(7): delay excusable for sufficient cause.
R. 4 — Setting asideAfter decree, court may, under special circumstances, set aside the decree, stay/set aside execution and give leave to defend — on such terms as it thinks fit (also s. 151 residual power).
R. 5Power to order the bill/note to be deposited with an officer of the court.
R. 6Recovery of costs of noting non-acceptance of a dishonoured bill or note.

🔗 Prayer & verification (DU verbatim structure)

PRAYER (a) decree for Rs. … ; (b) interest @ …% from (date) to filing — pendente lite and future interest @ …% ; (c) costs of suit ; (d) “such other and further order(s) as may be deemed fit and proper”. — The three interest heads (pre-suit → pendente lite → future) are standard; CPC s. 34 governs interest up to decree and post-decree interest rate (s. 35/35A).

VERIFICATION (verbatim model): “Verified at Delhi on this … day of ….. 20… that the contents of paras 1 to … of the plaint are true to my knowledge derived from the records of the Plaintiff maintained in the ordinary course of its business, those of paras …. to … are true on information received and believed to be true and last para is the humble prayer to this Hon’ble Court.” — Note the company-specific twist: knowledge = business records (O. VI R. 15; Part I § 6).

How Unit I’s four rules appear inside this single plaint DPC skill notes

  • Rule 1 (facts not law): the plaint never says “defendant is legally liable” — it explains why.
  • Rule 2 (material facts): agreement · construction · bill · cheque · dishonour · demand · non-payment — nothing missing, nothing extra.
  • Rule 3 (not evidence): para 9 mentions the bank memo but never becomes an examination-in-chief of the bank manager.
  • Rule 4 (concise & precise): short paragraphs, strict chronology, dates in figures + words.

3.1 The law of affidavits (study for any “What is an affidavit?” question)

📜 Provisions KAMKUS (IV) Affidavit

  • Definition: a written declaration on oath — a written statement sworn before a person having authority to administer oaths; confined to facts the deponent can prove.
  • Order XIX CPC governs affidavits in civil courts: R. 1 — court may order any particular fact to be proved by affidavit; R. 2 — court may order attendance of the deponent for cross-examination; R. 3 — affidavits shall be confined to relevant facts (no argument, no law).
  • s. 139 CPC — who may administer oaths: High Court (affidavits for judicial proceedings), State Govt (others); Oath Commissioners appointed from court staff; advocates/notaries under the Notaries Act also attest.
  • Stamp: Stamp Act 1899, Sch. I Art. 4 — affidavits chargeable, but no stamp on affidavits filed/used in courts; court-fee as prescribed.
  • Attachment to pleadings: O. VI R. 15(4) — every pleading to be supported by an affidavit (KAMKUS); DU puts the note “must be supported by an Affidavit” under Forms 1, 3, 4, 7, 8.

🚩 Compliance rules & liabilities

  • Must “strictly conform to O. XIX R. 3”; if sources of information are not disclosed, the affidavit is not in accordance with law and is inadmissible.
  • Verification is essential — otherwise it cannot be treated as an affidavit.
  • False affidavit = criminal: IPC s. 199 (false statement in a declaration receivable as evidence) & s. 200 (using such declaration knowing it false) → BNS ss. 236 & 237 IPC→BNS comparative table (official); if it becomes “false evidence” in a judicial proceeding: IPC 191/193 → BNS 227/229 (Part I § 6).
  • No amendment of an affidavit — only a supplementary affidavit with leave of court (KAMKUS rule i); clerical corrections initialled by the attesting authority (rule h).
  • Interlocutory exception: where a fact is not within deponent’s knowledge — “I am informed… and verify/believe it to be true”, stating the name & address of the informant (O. XIX R. 3 practice).

“I swear / solemnly affirm that my declaration is true, that the contents of this affidavit are true, that it conceals nothing material, and that no part of it is false.”

4.1 The Specific Relief Act, 1963 — preventive-relief map (attach to this form)

ProvisionSubstance (and how the DU plaint uses it)
s. 36Preventive relief is granted at the discretion of the court by injunction — temporary or perpetual. (Background of every injunction prayer.)
s. 37Temporary injunctions — continue until a specific time/further order; may be granted at any stage of the suit; regulated by the CPC (= Form 4, O. XXXIX). Perpetual injunction only by decree at hearing on the merits (= this Form 3).
s. 38Perpetual injunction when granted — to prevent breach of an obligation existing in the plaintiff’s favour where damages would be inadequate.
s. 39Mandatory injunction — to compel performance (court must be able to enforce). Contrast: DU Form 3 is prohibitory (restrain dispossession/interference).
s. 40Damages in lieu of / in addition to injunction — must be claimed in the plaint (proviso lets court allow later on terms). Drafting point: if you want damages, add the prayer now.
s. 41 (a)–(j)When injunction is REFUSED: (a) restraining a pending judicial proceeding (unless multiplicity); (b) proceedings in a superior court; (c) application to legislature; (d) criminal proceedings; (e) breach of a contract not specifically enforceable; (f) nuisance not clearly nuisance; (g) continuing breach plaintiff acquiesced in; (h) equally efficacious relief available otherwise; (i) plaintiff’s conduct disentitles him to court’s aid (“clean hands”); (j) no personal interest. → The model written statement (Form 9) attacks this very plaint through 41(h) & 41(i) — see § 10.
s. 42Negative agreement — injunction to perform the negative part of a contract even if its positive part can’t be specifically enforced (proved performance & no failure on his part).

Cross-refs: s. 9 CPC (civil courts’ jurisdiction) · O. VII R. 1 (plaint contents) · O. 39 (interim stage — Form 4) · SRA s. 34 (declaration + consequential relief — pleaded by the defendant in Form 9) · Delhi Rent Control Act (tenant-suits context — § 8).

📘 DU model at a glance DU pp. 17–18

Caption: “IA NO. ____ OF 20… IN SUIT NO. ____ OF 20…” — an interlocutory application always carries the parent suit’s cause title. Parties (real-style names in DU’s model): Sh. Om Veer Singh (Plaintiff/Applicant) v. Dr. U. Basu & Sh. Tapan Kumar (Defendants/Respondents) · Title: “APPLICATION FOR TEMPORARY INJUNCTION UNDER ORDER XXXIX, RULE 1 & 2 READ WITH SECTION 151 OF THE CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE, 1908” · Five paragraphs + prayer (ex-parte ad-interim) + affidavit note.

The five paragraphs are the exam gold:

  1. Suit for permanent injunction pending before this Court.
  2. Incorporation: “contents of the accompanying suit… may kindly be read as part and parcel of this application which are not repeated here for the sake of brevity”.
  3. Prima-facie case + likelihood of success.
  4. If not restrained ad-interim, plaintiff shall suffer irreparable loss and injury and the suit shall become anfractuous (winding/pointless) and lead to multiplicity of cases.
  5. Balance of convenience lies in favour of plaintiff and against defendants.

PRAYER: (a) ex-parte ad-interim injunction restraining defendants/associates/servants/agents from interfering in peaceful physical possession and from dispossessing; (b) omnibus. — “This Application has to be supported by an affidavit.”

5.1 Rule text — what you must reproduce in an exam answer

Order XXXIX Rule 1 — when temporary injunction may be granted

“Where in any suit it is proved by affidavit or otherwise —

  • (a) that any property in dispute in a suit is in danger of being wasted, damaged or alienated by any party, or wrongfully sold in execution of a decree;
  • (b) that the defendant threatens, or intends, to remove or dispose of his property with a view to defrauding his creditors;
  • (c) that the defendant threatens to dispossess the plaintiff or otherwise cause injury to the plaintiff in relation to any property in dispute in the suit,

the Court may by order grant a temporary injunction to restrain such act, or make such other order… until the disposal of the suit or until further orders.” — DU para (the tenant suit) fits clause (c) precisely.

Order XXXIX Rule 2 — injunction against breach of contract / injury

In a suit for restraining the defendant from committing a breach of contract or other injury of any kind (whether compensation is claimed or not), the plaintiff may, at any time after commencement of the suit and before or after judgment, apply for a temporary injunction to restrain the breach complained of, or any breach of like kind arising out of the same contract or relating to the same property.

Rule 2 also reaches non-parties who are agents/servants of the defendant with knowledge of the order (practice: implead/notify them — DU prayer names “associates, servants, agents and representatives”).

Rule 4: any injunction order may be discharged, varied or set aside on application; proviso — if a party knowingly made a false/misleading statement of a material particular and the injunction was ex parte, the Court SHALL vacate it unless reasons are recorded (anti-fraud guard — pair with DU’s affidavit support).

s. 151 CPC — inherent power to secure ends of justice/prevent abuse (the omnibus engine behind every “and for such other orders” prayer).

6.1 Rule 2A in full + the remedy architecture

📜 Order XXXIX Rule 2A (consequences of disobedience/breach)

  • (1) On disobedience of any injunction granted or other order made under R. 1 or R. 2, or breach of any term on which it was granted — the Court granting it (or the court to which the suit stands transferred) may order attachment of the property of the person guilty, and may also order his detention in the civil prison for a term not exceeding three months, unless the Court sooner directs his release.
  • (2) Attachment shall remain in force not more than one year; if disobedience continues at the end of that year, the attached property may be sold; out of the proceeds the court may award compensation to the injured party and pay the balance to the party entitled.

DU’s “list of properties annexed” = the attachment-target schedule; “maximum term” in the prayer = the 3-month ceiling.

🔗 Related provisions at this place

  • s. 151 CPC — inherent powers (DU title): restoration of possession is usually grounded here (R. 2A itself gives attachment/prison, not restoration).
  • O. XXXIX R. 4 proviso — the other side’s shield: an ex-parte injunction obtained by false statement must be vacated; genuine order, however, is enforced even if later set aside (cases below).
  • s. 144 CPC — restitution after an order is reversed (mirror remedy when the R. 2A-punished order eventually falls — balance of the system).
  • Undertakings: courts often grant injunctions on undertakings; breach of undertaking = contempt independently of R. 2A (DU RWA model used the Contempt Act on exactly this footing).

⚖️ The two cases that define this form (full notes in § 14.3–14.4)

1. Samee Khan v. Bindu Khan (1998) 7 SCC 622 — disobedience of an injunction is not erased even if the injunction order is subsequently set aside; the contemptuous act cannot be retroactively legitimised (severity of punishment may be mitigated).
2. Lavanya C v. Vittal Gurudas Pai (2025) — SC (Mithal & Karol JJ.) reaffirmed it: subsequent setting-aside/dismissal “does not absolve liability for its disobedience during its pendency”; contempt powers exist to maintain “the dignity and majesty of law”; on facts, 3-month jail deleted on age-mitigation and compensation raised ₹10 L → ₹13 L + 6% interest — showing the flexibility of quantum within the rule’s framework.

Drafting lesson for the exam: in a R. 2A application, always plead (i) existence of the order (date), (ii) service/knowledge, (iii) the precise act of disobedience with dates, (iv) wilfulness, (v) assets for attachment — DU’s five paragraphs are exactly this checklist.

⚖️ Case note — A.A. Haja Muniuddin v. Indian Railways via prescribed-style commentary

Held: O. (Also confirms “sufficient means” = means beyond bare sustenance for the person and family — a benevolent, extended meaning; even a public limited company can sue as pauper if it truly has no means.)

  1. Admit: “contents of para 1 … correct and is admitted.”
  2. Deny for want of knowledge (defendant can’t know plaintiff’s internal facts): “denied for want of knowledge. The Plaintiff be put to the strict proof of each and every allegation.”
  3. Flat denial + positive counter-fact: “absolutely incorrect and are denied. It is specifically denied that the Plaintiff is the owner… As a matter of fact, Mr. N is the owner.”
  4. Split admission: “correct that the Defendant is in possession… remaining contents … are absolutely incorrect and are denied.” + placeholder: “5–10 (each and every allegation must be replied specifically … only illustrative)”.

Then contest jurisdiction (para 12), valuation (para 13 — “correct valuation is Rs. …”).

The rules these moves implement

  • O. 8 R. 3(1): answer every material allegation specifically — evasive denial = deemed admitted.
  • O. 8 R. 1: WS within 30 days (extendable, max 90) — late WS risks judgment (R. 10).
  • O. 8 R. 7: distinct grounds separately stated — the 16 POs are model compliance.
  • O. 8 R. 8: new grounds arising after suit can be raised (leave practice).
  • “Without prejudice to one another”: alternative/consistent defences preserved (O. 6 R. 7 allows consistency issue only for plaintiff; defendant may plead in alternative — Unit I § 7).
  • Evidence limit: “put to strict proof” is permissible formula; narrating cross-examination would breach Rule 3 (facts ≠ evidence — Chunni Lal: no pleading → no evidence, Part I § 9.6).
  • Set-off (O. 8 R. 6) & counter-claim (R. 6A — treated as a plaint; must say “by way of counter-claim”) — DU’s closing Note; verify like a plaint + affidavit.

🚩 Scope — where a caveat will NOT travel

  • Civil proceedings only — not maintainable against criminal petitions or Art. 226 writs (Deepak Khosla v. UOI — § 14.6). (Criminal side has its own notice regimes; don’t import s. 148A.)
  • Probate/letters of administration: the historic home of “caveat” — caution against grant of probate (Indian Succession Act practice; caveat originated there).
  • A caveat buys notice, not victory — it never grants stay by itself; the merits are fought in the reply.
  • Must be lodged in the court where the application is expected; affidavit + vakalatnama + proof of dispatch accompany filing.

⚡ Drafting points & real-life uses

  • Draft the “order under challenge” precisely (DU para 1: court, judge, suit no., date, nature of order) — ambiguity defeats the caveat’s matching function.
  • Name the expected remedy (DU: Art. 227) so the registry can match the incoming petition.
  • Uses: post-judgment Art. 227/revision fear; stay applications in appeals; property-transfer matters; anticipatory litigation strategy in civil money cases.
  • DU’s sample order is a dismissed O. 6 R. 17 amendment — the same rule that Part I § 8 explains: refusal of amendment is a classic trigger for High Court revision.
  • 90 days → calendar reminder; if the case drags, re-lodge.

Case-Law Compendium — Facts · Issues · Arguments · Decision · Principles

Every case attached to these twelve forms

Sourcing note

The DU model forms themselves are proformas with blanks and carry no citations inside them. The cases below are therefore drawn — as directed — from the project’s uploaded material (DU companion notes, KAMKUS prescribed-style book, skill notes) plus bare-Act/leading-judgment verification for each form’s governing provision. Each note is in full exam format.

1 · Milkhiram (India) Private Ltd. & Ors. v. Chamanlal Bros.
AIR 1965 SC 1698 : (1965) — Supreme Court of India, 4-Judge Bench (J.R. Mudholkar J.), 23 April 1965 — governing case of Form 1
📕 Order XXXVII — leave to defend📌 “substantial defence / triable issue” test
Facts

The plaintiff-firm sued on promissory notes (and connected bills/guarantee transactions) under the summary procedure of Order XXXVII. The defendants contested on the footing that the real transactions were governed by an agreement involving unliquidated amounts and that the blank cheques delivered as security had been misused. The trial court examined whether the affidavit-defence raised a triable issue — it found the defence “vague” and “not bona fide” (defendant had produced no documentary evidence) — but still granted leave to defend on condition of security for the entire claim and costs. The defendants, aggrieved that leave was not unconditional, carried the matter through the Letters Patent (summarily dismissed) to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues
  1. Did the suit fall within O. XXXVII (appellants’ contention: unliquidated amounts under an agreement are outside the Order; the notes-based cause of action was merely ancillary)?
  2. Did the affidavit-disclosure raise a triable issue entitling the defendant to unconditional leave? Was the security-condition justified for want of documentary evidence?
Arguments — Defendants/Appellants

The defence disclosed a triable issue; therefore it was incumbent on the trial judge to grant unconditional leave; conditioning leave on security for the full claim was punitive where the merits had not been tried.

Arguments — Plaintiff/Respondent

The alleged defence was vague, sham and unsupported; the cause of action rested independently on the promissory notes; the conditional leave was a legitimate safeguard for a claim otherwise liable to summary decree.

Decision
  • The Court upheld that the cause of action on the promissory notes was prima facie independent and fell within O. XXXVII — the Order applies notwithstanding connected unliquidated issues.
  • Laid down the test: ask whether the defence raises a real issue and not a sham one — i.e., if the facts alleged by the defendant are established, would they constitute a good or even a plausible defence? If satisfied on that, leave must be given.
  • The trial court was wrong to impose the condition of security for the entire claim merely because documentary evidence had not been produced with the affidavit.
Principle evolved (the four-point restatement used across High Courts): (1) substantial defence likely to succeed → plaintiff not entitled to sign judgment; defendant gets unconditional leave; (2) triable issues showing a fair or reasonable defence (not necessarily a positively good one) → ordinarily unconditional leave; (3) no substantial defence / frivolous & vexatious → leave refused → summary decree (O. 37 R. 3(6)); (4) part admitted → deposit that part first (2nd proviso). Drafting lesson: the defendant’s leave-application must plead facts constituting the defence in affidavit form — exactly the Unit I discipline (material facts, no evidence).
2 · Rajni Kumar v. Suresh Kumar Malhotra
2003 (3) SCALE 434 — Supreme Court of India — O. XXXVII R. 4 (setting aside)
📕 Summary-suit decrees📌 “special circumstances” standard
Facts & issue

After a decree under the summary procedure, the defendant moved to set it aside under O. XXXVII R. 4. The question: what standard governs setting aside a summary-suit decree and restoring leave to defend?

Decision & principle

The Supreme Court held that the court must assess whether the circumstances are extraordinary or unusual — R. 4 requires “special circumstances”; the power is exercised to prevent grave injustice, not to reward delay. Along with setting aside, the court may stay execution and give leave to appear and defend, on such terms as it thinks fit. Drafting lesson: an application under R. 4 must plead the special circumstances with dates (non-service, illness, force majeure) and disclose the defence on merits — both limbs, in affidavit form.

3 · Samee Khan v. Bindu Khan
(1998) 7 SCC 622 — Supreme Court of India — governing case of Form 5 (O. XXXIX R. 2A)
📕 Disobedience of injunction📌 survival of liability after setting aside
Facts

An injunction order had been granted in the litigation; the respondent violated it during its pendency; later, the injunction order itself was set aside / the suit concluded against the grantee. The contemnor’s plea: once the order fell, the disobedience could not survive.

Issue

Is liability for disobedience of an injunction erased by the subsequent setting aside of that injunction?

Arguments

Contemnor: the underlying order ceased to exist → no live command → no punishable breach. Opposite party: the dignity of the court requires that a flagrant breach during the order’s life be punishable regardless of later events; otherwise injunctions become optional.

Decision

The Supreme Court held: “Even if the injunction order was subsequently set aside, the disobedience does not get erased.” The contemptuous act cannot be retroactively legitimised; at most, the severity of punishment may be reduced in the changed circumstances.

Principle evolved: O. XXXIX R. 2A liability attaches to the act of disobedience while the order subsisted — subsequent vacation of the order is a mitigating, not an extinguishing, factor. Drafting lesson (Form 5): plead the order-date, knowledge/service and the breach-date so the liability is anchored inside the order’s life — which is precisely DU paras 3–4.
4 · Smt. Lavanya C & Anr. v. Vittal Gurudas Pai (since deceased) by Lrs. & Ors.
(2025) — Supreme Court of India (Mithal & Karol JJ.) — reaffirming & applying O. XXXIX R. 2A
📕 Form 5 current law📌 quantum: jail vs compensation
Facts

The appellants were guilty of disobeying an injunction/undertaking granted before the trial court. The trial court punished them with attachment of property, three months’ civil imprisonment and ₹10 lakh compensation; the High Court upheld liability, citing Samee Khan, observing that even if the injunction was later set aside, disobedience during pendency is not erased, and that the appellants were guilty of breach of their own undertaking. The matter reached the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Does subsequent setting-aside of the injunction absolve the disobedience?
  2. Was the punishment (3 months’ detention + compensation) appropriate on the facts?
Arguments

Appellants: order invalid/aside; advanced age — jail should be excused. Respondents: express violation of a court order; contempt powers exist to maintain the court’s authority; compensation justified by loss.

Decision
  • SC confirmed liability — “when there has been an express violation of a Court order… the exercise of contempt jurisdiction cannot be faulted”; disobedience “is not erased” even if the order is subsequently set aside or the suit dismissed (following Samee Khan).
  • On quantum: deleted the three-month imprisonment considering the appellant’s advanced age; enhanced compensation from ₹10 lakh to ₹13 lakh with 6% interest from the date of the lower court’s judgment.
  • Reiterated that the powers of contempt exist “to ensure that the dignity and majesty of law” are maintained.
Principle evolved: (1) breach of an injunction = contempt, surviving later vacation; (2) remedies under R. 2A are discretionary and severable — detention can be substituted by enhanced compensation when mitigation exists; (3) undertakings before courts are enforced like orders. Drafting lesson: Form 5’s prayer for “maximum term” must be balanced with mitigation facts — plead the wilfulness (for liability) precisely; expect the court to calibrate punishment.
5 · Dr. Subramaniam Swamy v. Radhakrishna Hegde
(1990) 1 SCC 4 — Supreme Court of India — governing case of Form 11 (s. 25 CPC)
📕 Transfer of proceedings📌 “justice over convenience”
Facts & issue

An application sought transfer under s. 25 CPC. The Court examined what standard should guide transfer where the forum-choice of the dominus litis conflicts with the respondent’s need for a fair trial.

Arguments

Resisting party: suit/petition duly filed in the chosen forum; convenience favours retention. Applicant: trial in that forum would be unfair / justice denied.

Decision & principle

Held: the paramount consideration under s. 25 is the requirement of justice. Mere convenience of the parties (or one of them) is not enough — it must be shown that trial in the chosen forum will result in denial of justice; but if the ends of justice demand transfer, the Court should not hesitate. The plaintiff’s/petitioner’s position as dominus litis “cannot eclipse the requirement of justice — justice must be done at all costs, if necessary by transfer.”

Principle evolved: s. 25 is a justice-driven, not convenience-driven, jurisdiction; forum-choice is a factor, never a trump. Drafting lesson (both DU variants): convert every hardship into a justice-impairment — DU’s divorce variant pleads health, minor child, no shelter, dignity; the B.P. Mishra variant pleads safety, penury, proximity — never merely “far away”.
6 · The caveat cases — Deepak Khosla v. UOI · Moideen v. Kadeesa Umma · Employees Association v. RBI
Delhi High Court / Kerala High Court / (practice) — scope & duties under s. 148A CPC — governing cases of Form 10
📕 Caveat limits
(a) Deepak Khosla v. Union of India & Ors. Delhi HC

Facts: the petitioner lodged a caveat under s. 148A r/w s. 151 CPC praying that a petition (by an accused/other side) not be listed without advance notice and that no ex-parte order be passed without hearing him; the Registry objected that a CPC caveat cannot operate in criminal/quasi proceedings. Held: s. 148A applies exclusively to civil proceedings — a caveat under the CPC is not maintainable against criminal petitions (or Art. 226 matters). Principle: know your forum — for criminal-side notice protection use the specific CrPC/BNSS provisions, not s. 148A.

(b) Moideen v. Kadeesa Umma Kerala HC

Facts: partition suit reached final decree; the respondent lodged a caveat under s. 148A seeking notice before any further order. Held/Hearing: the court treated the caveat as a legitimate notice-claim in the civil proceeding — illustrating that caveats are used beyond the plaint stage (execution/final-decree stages too). Principle: caveat protects across the life of a civil matter, not only at institution.

(c) Employees Association v. RBI practice ruling

Held: by virtue of s. 148A(4), the applicant (on receiving caveat notice) is duty-bound to furnish the caveator copies of his application and of all documents referred to, at the caveator’s expense. Principle: the section imposes three duty-holders — caveator (serve AD notice), court (notify caveator), applicant (furnish papers) — non-compliance by any breaks the mechanism; orders without s. 148A(3) notice are void.

Definition to quote: “A caveat is a caution or warning given to the court not to issue any grant or take any step without notice to the party lodging it” (AIR 1978 Cal 492) — a precautionary measure (originally against hurried grants of probate), now statutorily regularised by s. 148A (inserted 1976) with a 90-day life.
7 · Access & interim-standards cases — A.A. Haja Muniuddin v. Indian Railways · Paidsetti Bhanknarayna v. Paidsetti Rajeshwar Rao
Supreme Court / AIR 1999 Ori 92 — governing cases of Forms 6 & 4
📕 Pauper suits · Temporary injunctions
(a) A.A. Haja Muniuddin v. Indian Railways via uploaded commentary

Held: O. XXXIII is an enabling provision — it lets a person sue without prepaying court fee; if the suit is dismissed, the State recovers the fee and it is a first charge on the subject-matter. “Sufficient means” receives a benevolent, extended meaning (bare sustenance for person + family; exempt property excluded). Principle: access-to-justice provision to be construed liberally; but screening genuine indigence remains the court’s duty (fraudulent disposal → rejection).

(b) Paidsetti Bhanknarayna v. Paidsetti Rajeshwar Rao, AIR 1999 Ori 92 source text

Held (temporary injunction standard): the plaintiff need not fully establish his title at the interim stage — it is enough to show a fair question to raise as to the existence of the right claimed, and that the property should be preserved in its present condition until that question is decided. Principle: the three-prong test (prima facie case · irreparable injury · balance of convenience) operates on probability, not proof — which is why Form 4’s paragraphs are assertions of prongs, not evidence.

How to write these in the exam: citation → court/year → facts → issue → both sides → decision → one-line principle → one line tying it to the form you are drafting. The last line converts a “case” into a “Forms of Pleadings” answer.

QB · KAMKUS Q.6 & Q.9“What do you mean by interlocutory orders?” · “What is ex parte decree?”
MODEL ANSWERS

Interlocutory applications/orders: filed during the pendency/course of a litigation; decided en route, not finally; must be drafted with a pleading’s care — precise, brief, irrelevant-free; grounds in the words of the enabling provision; facts may live in the accompanying affidavit (“for the reasons in the annexed affidavit…”). CPC examples (KAMKUS list): O. 6 R. 17 amendment · s. 95 compensation (arrest/attachment on insufficient grounds) · s. 144 restitution · s. 151 · s. 152 correction of judgments/decrees · O. 9 Rr. 4 & 9 (set aside dismissal in default) · O. XI R. 1 — interrogatories with leave · objections under s. 47/O. 21 Rr. 58, 89–91, 98 · O. 22 substitution · O. 28 commissions · O. 39 interim injunction · O. 40 receiver · O. 47 review. This unit’s Forms 4, 5, 6, 12 are all interlocutory.

Ex parte decree: a decree passed without the defendant appearing/responding. In ordinary suits → O. 9 R. 3 (defence heard, defendant refused to cross-examine/witness → decree; or after summons + no appearance). In summary suits (Form 1): O. 37 R. 3(6) — no appearance, no leave application, refusal of leave, or breach of conditions → plaintiff is entitled to judgment forthwith (allegations deemed admitted). Setting aside: O. 9 R. 13 (ordinary) / O. 37 R. 4 (summary — “special circumstances”, Rajni Kumar). Protection against unfair surprise is the caveat (s. 148A — Form 10).

QB · KAMKUS Q.13 & Q.14“What do you understand by an Affidavit?” · “What is pauper suit?”
MODEL ANSWERS

Affidavit: written statement sworn/affirmed before an authority competent to administer oaths. Framework: O. XIX CPC (R. 1 — proof of facts by affidavit; R. 2 — cross-examination of deponent; R. 3 — confined to relevant facts) · s. 139 CPC who administers (HC for judicial proceedings; State Govt otherwise; Oath Commissioners) · parts: cause title → deponent’s description → oath/affirmation → recitals (capacity + numbered facts) → verification (knowledge vs information; “nothing material concealed”) → identification → attesting authority’s certificate · discipline: no law/argument/opinion; disclose sources (“I am informed…” + informant’s name/address in interlocutory matters) · verification mandatory or it is not an affidavit; no amendment — supplementary affidavit only · false affidavit = IPC 199/200 → BNS 236/237 (and IPC 191/193 → BNS 227/229 in judicial proceedings) · supported-plaint requirement: O. VI R. 15(4). DU model: Form 2’s three paras (authority & knowledge from business records · suit true on instructions · annexures true copies) + verification. [Details § 3.]

Pauper suit (forma pauperis): suit by an indigent person without prepaying court fee — O. XXXIII (R. 1 definition & prior-rejection bar; R. 1A clerk’s inquiry; R. 2 contents: short title, relief-matter, value, property particulars, previous rejection; verified like plaint; rejection grounds include fraudulent disposal within 2 months; on grant → treated as plaint; court may assign pleader; fees recovered if he wins — first charge on subject-matter; if he loses — State recovers from him; appeal against rejection: O. 43 R. 1; pauper appeals: O. 44) + Art. 39A & LSA Act 1987 context. DU model: Form 6’s five paragraphs + undertaking to pay fee if decreed. [Details § 7.]

QB · KAMKUS Q.15 & Q.11“Written statement — its two parts?” · “How can a plaint be amended?”
MODEL ANSWERS (condensed)

WS — two parts: (I) Statement on merits/traverse — para-wise specific admission/denial of every material allegation (O. 8 R. 3; evasive denial = deemed admitted) + affirmative new facts of defence; (II) Preliminary objections/legal defences — limitation, jurisdiction, valuation, non/mis-joinder, res judicata (s. 11), stay (s. 10), verification defects, statutory bars (SRA 41(h)/(i)/34/14; s. 80; s. 69 Partnership Act; Benami s. 4) — stated separately (O. 8 R. 7), without prejudice to each other, plus set-off/counter-claim if any (O. 8 Rr. 6/6A — “by way of counter-claim”), verification + affidavit. DU Form 9 is the perfect specimen: 16 POs + merits reply. Timing: 30 days (max 90); silence → judgment (R. 10); no evidence on unpleaded facts (Chunni Lal).

Amendment of plaint: O. 6 R. 17 — court may allow alteration/amendment at any stage on just terms; mandatory limb: all amendments necessary for determining the real controversy shall be made; proviso (2002): after trial begins — only if due diligence + no prejudice; procedure = application stating proposed amendment + reasons; wide ameliorative purpose (Ganga Bai, B.K. Narayana Pillai, Revajeetu Builders); limits: no new/inconsistent case (O. 6 R. 7), relates back for limitation, cures verification defects. [Full answer: Part I § 10.]

QB · KAMKUS Q.17 & Q.18“Draft a plaint and WS for specific performance of a contract for purchase of a car?” · “Draft a plaint for recovery of money.”
MODEL ANSWER — Q.17 specific performance (movable property!)

Plaint (apply Form 8’s skeleton): heading/title “SUIT FOR SPECIFIC PERFORMANCE OF CONTRACT” → parties → ① plaintiff’s need (car for business/family) → ② defendant absolute owner of car (describe: make, reg. no., chassis/engine no. — movable specificity!) → ③ negotiations on dates → ④ agreement to sell in writing (Annexure A; price; date of delivery) → ⑤ advance/earnest paid (receipt annexed) → ⑥ terms: balance + transfer of possession/RC endorsement → ⑦ demand on date → refusal → ⑧ repeated requests (dates) → ⑨ legal notice → no compliance → ⑩ ready & willing (SRA s. 16(c)) → ⑪ cause of action dates → ⑫ limitation (Art. 54 — 3 years from refusal) → ⑬ territorial jurisdiction (agreement/refusal here) → ⑭ court fee → prayer: decree directing sale + delivery + costs + omnibus → verification + affidavit. Note: for movables, also plead defendant’s continued ownership & non-transfer (or implead transferee — SRA s. 19) and mention remedy-alternative (compensation, SRA s. 14 read with Contract Act s. 73).

WS skeleton (apply Form 9): Preliminary objections — no written agreement (denial) / agreement void-unregistered / purchase for immoral/illegal purpose / limitation / no jurisdiction / benami, etc. (pick facts) + merits reply para-wise (admit/deny/specific counter-facts; deny readiness if true — O. 8 R. 3) + prayer + verification + affidavit; counter-claim for advance refund if entitled (O. 8 R. 6A).

Q.18 recovery of money

Use Form 1’s A–T flow: if the claim is a liquidated sum on a written contract/cheque → add para “suit under O. XXXVII” + affidavit (summary); if unliquidated (goods sold at fair price, services) → ordinary plaint under O. 7 R. 1 with same paragraphs minus the O. 37 gate. Never forget: figures + words, cause-of-action dates, limitation, jurisdiction, valuation, prayer (principal + interest heads + costs), verification with correct paragraph numbers.

QB · KAMKUS Q.3“What do you understand by cause of action?”
MODEL ANSWER

Meaning: the factual ground(s) which, if proved, entitle the plaintiff to a judgment — “every fact which would be necessary to be proved, and without which the plaintiff would not be entitled to a decree.” Procedural role: must appear in the plaint — O. 7 R. 1(iii) (facts constituting cause of action and when it arose) + O. 6 R. 2 (material facts only); jurisdiction depends on where it arises (ss. 16–17); limitation runs from it (Arts. 54/137…); no cause of action = rejection/dismissal (O. 7 R. 11; and a standard WS objection — DU Form 9 para 4). Illustrations: money suit — loan/advance + refusal; ejectment — tenancy + notice + expiry + refusal (Form 7); specific performance — agreement + readiness + refusal (Form 8). A single application may involve several causes; they must all be pleaded — unpleaded facts cannot be proved at trial (Unit I Rule 2 + Gita Rani Paul).

✅ Final takeaway

These twelve forms are the same form twelve times: heading with provision → cause title → numbered material facts in story-sequence → jurisdiction/limitation/valuation → prayer matching the provision → signature → verification → affidavit. Master that spine once (§ 1), add each form’s governing provisions (§§ 2–13) and its case law (§ 14), and any DU drafting question — today’s or tomorrow’s — becomes an exercise in filling a known structure with given facts.
