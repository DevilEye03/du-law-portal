0Orientation — What this Unit is Really About

The one-sentence statement of the topic

Once an industrial dispute is pending before a conciliation officer, arbitrator or Tribunal, the employer’s ordinary managerial prerogative — to change conditions of service and to punish or dismiss workmen — is restrained by statute, so that the employer cannot destroy or prejudice the very subject-matter that the adjudicating authority is about to decide. Section 33 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (now §90 of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020) creates that restraint, and §33-A (now §91) supplies the workman’s own remedy where the restraint is breached. Around those two sections has grown a distinct body of law on suspension and subsistence allowance — the subject of all three prescribed cases.

Citation caution — do not repeat the common error

Some commercially circulated notes (including one widely used “Master Notes” set) map §33/§33-A of the IDA to §82 of the IRC, and §11-A to “§83”. Those numbers are wrong. In the Industrial Relations Code, 2020: §40 = notice of change of conditions of service (the old §9-A); §90 = conditions of service to remain unchanged during pendency (the old §33); §91 = special provision for adjudication of a complaint of contravention (the old §33-A); §53 = reference of disputes; §50 = Tribunal’s power on discharge/dismissal (the old §11-A); §56 = full wages pending appeal (the old §17-B). Always quote the language of the provision — “express permission in writing”, “one month”, “protected worker”, “complaint in writing” — so that the examiner can see you know the text.

0.3 Why this unit matters in practice — three real-life illustrations

1. The “just settled the dispute” trap

A factory and its union are before the Industrial Tribunal over a wage-revision demand. The management, annoyed at the union’s general secretary (a protected workman), dismisses him for an old charge of misconduct. Without prior permission under §33(1)/(3), that dismissal is a contravention. The union secretary files a §33-A complaint before the very Tribunal; the Tribunal quashes the dismissal without the parties waiting for a fresh Government reference — and the employer may also face prosecution under §31.

2. The “unrelated misconduct” route

The same Tribunal is hearing a dispute about canteen facilities when a driver is found drunk on duty — a matter wholly unconnected with the canteen dispute. The employer may dismiss him under §33(2)(b), but only after paying one month’s wages and applying for approval; if it forgets the application, the dismissal is void and the workman is deemed to have continued in service.

3. The six-year limbo

A workman is suspended after a domestic enquiry while the Tribunal takes six years to decide the employer’s permission application. He is paid nothing. Fakirbhai answers this: denial of all subsistence makes it impossible for him to defend himself, violates natural justice, and vitiates the permission; Ram Lakhan adds that if the standing orders are silent, he is entitled to full salary for the whole period.

AThe Statutory Framework — §33 & §33-A IDA (now §90 & §91 IRC, 2020)

A.1 Policy and purpose — why Parliament tied the employer’s hands

The mischief

An industrial dispute is referred to adjudication precisely because the parties cannot agree. If, while the reference is pending, the employer were free to dismiss the very workmen who are the subject-matter of the dispute, or to alter the service conditions that the Tribunal is about to examine, the employer could render the adjudication illusory — it could “create facts” and defeat the workman’s case before the award was written. Conversely, if the employer were absolutely forbidden to act at all during pendency, it would be unable to maintain discipline or to deal with misconduct unconnected with the dispute.

Section 33 strikes a graded balance: a prior permission requirement for everything connected with the pending dispute (so that the subject-matter cannot be destroyed); a post-action approval requirement for matters not connected (so that ordinary discipline is not paralysed); and an absolute prior permission requirement for protected workmen, who are the natural targets of victimisation.

The Supreme Court’s classic formulation of the legislative purpose is in Air India v. V.A. Rebellow: §33 is “enacted to protect the workmen against victimisation or unfair labour practices, consistently with the preservation of the employer’s bona fide right to maintain discipline and efficiency in the industry”.

A.2 Section 33, Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 — clause by clause (verbatim, with commentary)

§33(1) — the connected-matters rule: prior permission

“During the pendency of any proceeding in respect of an industrial dispute before a conciliation officer or a Board of Conciliation or an arbitrator or a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, no employer shall— (a) in regard to any matter connected with the dispute, alter, to the prejudice of the workmen concerned in such dispute, the conditions of service applicable to them immediately before the commencement of such proceeding; or (b) for any misconduct connected with the dispute, discharge or punish, whether by dismissal or otherwise, any workmen concerned in such dispute, save with the express permission in writing of the authority before which the proceeding is pending.”

  • “During the pendency” — the restraint operates from the commencement of the proceeding until its conclusion; today, in the IRC, also during the reference before an arbitrator/Tribunal.
  • “In respect of an industrial dispute” — the proceeding must be one in respect of an industrial dispute. A §33 application cannot be made where no such proceeding is pending.
  • “Connected with the dispute” — the touchstone of §33(1). “Connected” is wider than “identical”: a dispute about victimisation, or about the dismissal of the workmen concerned, makes disciplinary action arising out of the same facts “connected”.
  • “Alter, to the prejudice” — the alteration must be prejudicial; a beneficial or neutral change is outside the clause. Illustration: a pending reference on wage revision — the employer cannot, without permission, reduce the dearness allowance or withdraw a facility; but it may raise wages (no prejudice).
  • “Discharge or punish, whether by dismissal or otherwise” — the net is deliberately wide and catches not only dismissal and discharge but any punishment: withholding of increments, demotion, suspension as a punishment, stoppage of promotion, fine, or the entry of an adverse remark.
  • “Save with the express permission in writing” — express means expressed, not implied; in writing means precisely that. The permission must come before the action. Action taken without such permission is a contravention of §33 — remediable by a §33-A complaint and punishable under §31.
§33(2) — the unconnected-matters rule: action + subsequent approval

“During the pendency of any such proceeding in respect of an industrial dispute, the employer may, in accordance with the standing orders applicable to a workman concerned in such dispute [or, where there are no such standing orders, in accordance with the terms of the contract, whether express or implied, between him and the workman]— (a) alter, in regard to any matter not connected with the dispute, the conditions of service applicable to that workman immediately before the commencement of such proceeding; or (b) for any misconduct not connected with the dispute, discharge or punish, whether by dismissal or otherwise, that workman:

Provided that no such workman shall be discharged or dismissed, unless he has been paid wages for one month and an application has been made by the employer to the authority before which the proceeding is pending for approval of the action taken by the employer.”

  • “May … in accordance with the standing orders … or the terms of the contract” — the source of the employer’s power is the standing order/contract, not §33(2). The sub-section is permissive only in the sense that the pendency does not take away the employer’s ordinary power; all other requirements of the standing orders and of natural justice remain.
  • “Not connected with the dispute” — see the decision tree in Figure 3. The employer bears the risk of misjudging this: if the misconduct is in fact connected, action under §33(2)(b) without prior permission is a contravention.
  • No permission, only approval — the action is taken first and then placed before the authority. Hence the standard is a prima facie one (Lord Krishna Textile Mills v. Its Workmen): the authority sees whether a prima facie case for approval is made out; it may approve or refuse, but it cannot impose conditions or pass a conditional order.
  • The three requisites of the proviso — (Lalla Ram v. Management of D.C.M. Chemical Works Ltd.): (i) the employer must have paid or tendered one month’s wages; (ii) it must have made the application for approval; and (iii) the action must rest on a proper enquiry or a prima facie case free from mala fides, victimisation or unfair labour practice. The “one month’s wages” have been read to include the wages due — if the amount is short, approval may be refused.
  • Consequence of non-approval or of no application — the action “falls”; the workman is deemed never to have been dismissed and continues in service: Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank Ltd. v. Ram Gopal Sharma, (2002) 2 SCC 244, which dissented from Punjab Beverages (P) Ltd. v. Suresh Chand, (1978) 2 SCC 144 (which had held the dismissal merely illegal, not void). The workman may also complain under §33-A even where approval has been granted, to challenge it on the ground of victimisation.
§33(3) — the protected workman: prior permission, come what may

“Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (2), no employer shall, during the pendency of any such proceeding in respect of an industrial dispute, take any action against any protected workman concerned in such dispute— (a) by altering, to the prejudice of such protected workman, the conditions of service applicable to him immediately before the commencement of such proceeding; or (b) by discharging or punishing, whether by dismissal or otherwise, such protected workman, save with the express permission in writing of the authority before which the proceeding is pending.

Explanation.—For the purposes of this sub-section, a ‘protected workman’, in relation to an establishment, means a workman who, being a member of the executive or other office-bearer of a registered trade union connected with the establishment, is recognised as such in accordance with rules made in this behalf.”

  • “Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (2)” — a protected workman gets prior-permission protection even for unconnected misconduct. The approval route of §33(2) is closed to the employer in his case.
  • Why extra protection? Because a union office-bearer is the most likely victim of an employer’s desire to remove a troublesome leader; the object is to preserve the leadership of the union while the dispute is being fought.
  • It is protection, not licence — the workman does not acquire immunity from disciplinary action; he is merely entitled to have the Tribunal scrutinise it before the penalty takes effect.
  • Words substituted — the phrase “a member of the executive or other office-bearer” was substituted for the narrower “an officer” by Act 45 of 1971, widening the class.
§33(4) — the numerical cap

“In every establishment, the number of workmen to be recognised as protected workmen … shall be one per cent of the total number of workmen employed therein subject to a minimum of five protected workmen and a maximum of one hundred protected workmen, and for the aforesaid purpose, the appropriate Government may make rules providing for the distribution of such protected workmen among various trade unions, if any, connected with the establishment and the manner in which the workmen may be chosen and recognised as protected workmen.”

§33(5) — the three-month limit: Parliament’s answer to Fakirbhai-type delays

“Where an employer makes an application to a conciliation officer, Board, an arbitrator, a Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal under the proviso to sub-section (2) for approval of the action taken by him, the authority concerned shall, without delay, hear such application and pass, within a period of three months from the date of receipt of such application, such order in relation thereto as it deems fit: Provided that where any such authority considers it necessary or expedient so to do, it may, for reasons to be recorded in writing, extend such period by such further periods as it may think fit: Provided further that no proceedings before any such authority shall lapse merely on the ground that any period specified in this sub-section has expired without such proceedings being completed.”

The legislative history is itself an examinable link: the phrase “as expeditiously as possible” was substituted by the three-month time limit by Act 46 of 1982 (w.e.f. 21-8-1984) — the very decade in which Fakirbhai was decided, where the §33(3) application had pended for nearly six years. Parliament’s three-month rule and the Court’s subsistence-allowance rule are two responses to one disease: delay.

A.3 Section 33-A — the workman’s own remedy

§33-A, IDA, 1947 — verbatim

“Where an employer contravenes the provisions of section 33 during the pendency of proceedings before a conciliation officer, Board, arbitrator, Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, any employee aggrieved by such contravention may make a complaint in writing, in the prescribed manner— (a) to such conciliation officer or Board, and the conciliation officer or Board shall take such complaint into account in mediating in, and promoting the settlement of, such industrial dispute; and (b) to such arbitrator, Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal and on receipt of such complaint, the arbitrator, Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, as the case may be, shall adjudicate upon the complaint as if it were a dispute referred to or pending before it, in accordance with the provisions of this Act, and shall submit its award to the appropriate Government and the provisions of this Act shall apply accordingly.”

Essential elements of a §33-A complaint

  1. A contravention of §33 — an alteration of service conditions to the prejudice of a workman concerned, or a punishment/dismissal, without the permission or approval required by the applicable sub-section (including the three-month-limitation-free proviso to §33(2)).
  2. An “aggrieved employee” — the person affected; the language of §33-A speaks of “any employee aggrieved by such contravention” (and in the IRC, correspondingly, “any employee”).
  3. Pendency — the complaint lies only while the proceeding in respect of the industrial dispute is pending before the named authority.
  4. Writing and prescribed manner — the complaint must be in writing in the form prescribed by the rules.
  5. Forum — the very authority before which the dispute is pending (or the conciliation officer/Board, who must use it in mediation).

Why §33-A is doctrinally significant

  • Direct access to adjudication: the workman does not have to persuade the appropriate Government to make a fresh reference under §10. It is one of the very few provisions of the IDA that by-passes the reference machinery — and the IRC preserves it in §91.
  • Full adjudicatory power: once the complaint is received, it is deemed to be a referred dispute; the authority has all its §11/§11-A powers and must make an award, which is published under §17 and enforceable like any other award.
  • Its limits: mere contravention does not automatically entitle the workman to reinstatement; the Tribunal must go on to decide the merits — whether the misconduct was proved and whether the punishment was justified (i.e., it applies the Unit-5 §11-A test).
  • Relationship with the approval route: a §33-A complaint is independent of the employer’s §33(2)(b) approval application and the two are ordinarily heard together; a workman may complain under §33-A even after approval has been granted, to allege victimisation (Jaipur Zila).

A.7 Allied machinery that a Unit-6 problem question may involve

§9-A (IRC §40) — notice of change

An employer proposing to change any condition of service in the Fourth Schedule (IDA) / Third Schedule (IRC) — wages, allowances, hours, leave, shift working, withdrawal of concessions, etc. — must give 21 days’ notice to the workmen likely to be affected. A change effected in breach of §9-A is no change at all, and a dispute may be raised. Link to §33: §9-A regulates the proposal to change; §33 regulates a change or punishment during a pending proceeding. Together they are the twin restraints on managerial prerogative.

§10(4) and interim relief (IRC §50(2))

Where a reference specifies the points of dispute, the Tribunal must confine itself to them “and matters incidental thereto”. Hotel Imperial held that interim relief is such an incidental matter: where reinstatement/wages are in issue, interim relief may be granted without any express reference of that question. Under the IRC, §50(2) now expressly empowers the Tribunal to grant interim relief in the interest of justice.

§33-C(2) and enforcement

The Labour Court may compute and recover money due to a workman under a settlement, award or the provisions of Chapter V-A/V-B. In Jaipur Zila the workman successfully invoked §33-C(2) on the footing that a dismissal without approval was void, so that wages continued to accrue. §33-C is also the tool for recovering arrears of subsistence allowance directed by an award.

㉑ The Management of Hotel Imperial v. Hotel Workers’ Union CASE No. 21 · Unit 6

(1960) 1 SCR 476 : AIR 1959 SC 1342 · Three connected Civil Appeals by special leave from the Labour Appellate Tribunal of India · Bench: K.N. Wanchoo, J. (three-Judge Bench) · The foundational case on suspension pending §33 and on a Tribunal’s power to grant interim relief
Context — the problem the case solves

An employer completes a proper domestic enquiry, concludes that a workman deserves dismissal, and then discovers that it cannot dismiss him at once: an industrial dispute is pending and §33 requires the permission of the authority. What is to happen in the interval? Two sub-questions arise: (i) may the employer suspend him without pay in the meantime; and (ii) may the Tribunal order interim relief to keep the workman alive while the main dispute is decided? Hotel Imperial answers both, and its answer to the first — the implied term doctrine — is the anchor of this whole unit.

Facts — the hotel strike of 1955 and the three references
  1. The parties: the three appellants were the managements of (1) Imperial Hotel, New Delhi, (2) Maiden’s Hotel, Delhi, and (3) Swiss Hotel, Delhi; the respondents were their respective workmen represented by the Hotel Workers’ Union, Katra Shahanshahi, Chandni Chowk, Delhi.
  2. The background: disputes between the hotels and their workmen over conditions of labour had been running for some time. Matters came to a head at the end of September 1955, and a strike of all the workmen in all three hotels took place on 5 October 1955.
  3. The Imperial Hotel spark (August 1955): before the general strike there had been trouble in Imperial Hotel only. In that connection charge-sheets were served on 22 workmen and an enquiry was held by the management, which concluded that the workmen were guilty of misconduct and decided to dismiss them. On 4 October 1955 notices were served on those workmen informing them that the management had decided to dismiss them, subject to obtaining permission under §33. This action set off the general strike in all three hotels on 5 October 1955.
  4. The 5 October notices: each of the three managements issued notices directing the workmen to re-join duty within three hours, failing which action would be taken. The workmen did not re-join within that time; fresh notices were issued the same day asking them to show cause why disciplinary action should not be taken, and they were informed that they would be under suspension in the meantime.
  5. The 7 October notices: the three managements issued notices informing the workmen that it had been decided to dismiss them, and that they were being suspended pending the obtaining of permission under §33.
  6. References and numbers: a reference was made on 12 October 1955 in respect of Imperial Hotel, covering a large number of matters, including the case of the 22 workmen whom the management had decided to dismiss on 4 October — but not the workmen dismissed on 7 October. After further enquiries the action of 7 October was confirmed against 19 workmen. Those 19 had in the meantime applied under §33-A, complaining that they had been suspended without pay for an indefinite period and thus punished in breach of §33. So the Imperial Hotel dispute concerned 44 workmen in all — 25 covered by the reference of 12 October and 19 who had filed §33-A applications. No §33 application was made at all for those 19: the §33 application filed on 22 October 1955 related only to the original 22 workmen.
  7. Maiden’s Hotel: the case related to 26 workmen whose dismissal was finally considered necessary after 7 October; a reference was made on 23 November 1955 covering those 26. Later, 12 of them were re-employed on 10 December 1955, so that the real dispute concerned 14 workmen.
  8. Swiss Hotel: after the notices of 7 October, an application was made by the Union under §33-A to the conciliation officer; eventually, on 10 November 1955, a reference was made in respect of 14 workmen.
  9. The interim-relief applications and orders: the workmen applied for interim relief — on 22 October (Imperial) and 26 November 1955 (Maiden’s and Swiss). Replies were filed on 5 December 1955, the same day on which the Industrial Tribunal passed its orders: in Imperial Hotel, that 43 of the 44 workmen be paid their wages plus ₹25 per month per head in lieu of food until the final decision on their dismissal; in Maiden’s Hotel, the 12 workmen whom the management was prepared to take back were ordered to report by 10 December, and the remaining 13 were to be paid their wages from 1 October 1955 plus ₹25 per month in lieu of food (no order was passed for the 26th workman, a sweeper); Swiss Hotel was treated on the same terms, with six workmen taken back.
  10. The appeals and the interim stay: the three hotels appealed; the Labour Appellate Tribunal dismissed the appeals on 28 May 1956. The hotels obtained special leave from the Supreme Court, which on 5 June 1956 granted a stay on the condition that the employers pay half of the amount adjudged payable by the orders of 5 December 1955 in respect of arrears accrued till then, and continue to pay in the same proportion until the dispute was determined.
  11. The sequel: after that order, even the workmen who had not been re-employed on 5 December 1955 were taken back — on 15 July 1956, 2 workmen in Swiss Hotel, 13 in Maiden’s Hotel and 43 in Imperial Hotel were taken back in service. (The Union argued that this re-employment amounted to a waiver of the dismissal and suspension; the Court expressly declined to decide that question at that interlocutory stage.)
Issues
  1. Are any wages payable to workmen who are suspended pending the obtaining of permission under §33 for their dismissal?
  2. In particular, does the employer have the power to suspend the contract of employment at all, or must it continue to pay wages during the so-called period of suspension?
  3. Is an Industrial Tribunal competent to grant interim relief — and can it do so without making an “interim award” that is submitted to Government and published under §17?
Arguments
For the hotel managements — Mr. B. Sen and others (appellants)
  1. Suspension pending §33 permission is a bar to the payment of any wages. Their proposition, in the words of the judgment, was that “suspension of the workmen pending permission under Section 33 of the Act imposes an absolute bar to the payment of any wages to the suspended workmen”.
  2. §33 creates an exceptional statutory situation: but for the statutory ban, the employer who had completed a proper enquiry and decided to dismiss could have brought the contract of service to an end immediately, and the wage obligation with it. Section 33 step in and compels the employer to seek permission — a proceeding over whose duration the employer has no control at all.
  3. It is therefore “just and fair” to imply a term in the contract of employment permitting suspension pending permission — the contract of employment being temporarily suspended so that the workman is not bound to render service and the employer is not bound to pay wages. This, they said, works no hardship: if permission is granted the workman gets nothing for the period; if it is refused, he gets all his back wages from the date of suspension.
  4. Precedent: the decisions in Lakshmi Devi Sugar Mills Ltd. v. Pt. Ram Sarup, (1956) SCR 916 (suspension beyond the four days provided by the standing orders held not to be “punishment”, payment depending upon the outcome of the permission application) and Management of Ranipur Colliery v. Bhuban Singh, AIR 1959 SC 833 (suspension without pay pending permission justified), together with Sasa Musa Sugar Works (P) Ltd. (where there were no standing orders at all), showed that the employer’s entitlement did not depend upon the presence of a standing order.
  5. On interim relief: the Tribunal could only adjudicate upon the points referred; interim relief had not been referred; and in any event any determination — interim or final — had to be submitted to Government and published as an award under §15 read with §§17 and 17-A, which had not been done.
For the workmen — Hotel Workers’ Union (respondents)
  1. Under the ordinary law of master and servant, suspension involving non-payment of wages is not contemplated at all in the absence of an express term in the contract of employment.
  2. Here there were admittedly no standing orders providing for suspension without wages. The orders of suspension in these cases therefore “merely amounted to this that the employers were not prepared to take work from the workmen”; the right of the workmen to receive wages remained, and the employer was bound to pay them during the period of so-called suspension.
  3. There is nothing in the language of §33 to warrant the conclusion that an employer who must apply for permission may also suspend the workman — the Act neither authorises nor implies any such power; the employer must therefore bear the burden of its own statutory disability.
  4. On interim relief: the Tribunal’s power is ample — reinstatement and/or compensation having been referred, interim relief is a matter “incidental thereto” under §10(4) and need not be separately referred; and an order of interim relief is not an “interim award” of the kind that must be published.
  5. On the facts: all the workmen were ultimately taken back in service, which shows that the decisions to dismiss and to suspend were waived, so full wages are payable for the interim period.
Decision (Holding)
  • (1) The common-law starting point (undisputed): “It is now well settled that the power to suspend, in the sense of a right to forbid a servant to work, is not an implied term in an ordinary contract between master and servant, and that such a power can only be the creature either of a statute governing the contract, or of an express term in the contract itself. Ordinarily, therefore, the absence of such power … would mean that the master would have no power to suspend a workman and even if he does so in the sense that he forbids the employee to work, he will have to pay wages during the so-called period of suspension.” Where, however, a power to suspend exists in the contract, statute or rules, “the suspension has the effect of temporarily suspending the relation of master and servant with the consequence that the servant is not bound to render service and the master is not bound to pay”.
  • (2) Industrial adjudication is not bound by that common law: following the Federal Court in Western India Automobile Association v. Industrial Tribunal, Bombay, (1949) FCR 321 — “Adjudication does not … mean adjudication according to the strict law of master and servant … the tribunal is not fettered in any way by these limitations” — and Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Brijnandan Pandey, (1956) SCR 800 — an Industrial Tribunal “may create new obligations or modify contracts in the interests of industrial peace, to protect legitimate trade union activities and to prevent unfair practice or victimisation” — the Court held that Industrial Tribunals “have the power to go beyond the ordinary law of master and servant, if circumstances justify it”.
  • (3) §33 has introduced a “fundamental change” in the law of master and servant so far as cases falling within the Act are concerned, and therefore “in the peculiar circumstances which have arisen on account of the enactment of Section 33, it is but just and fair that Industrial Tribunals should imply such a term in the contract of employment”.
  • (4) The implied term — precisely stated: “a term should be implied by Industrial Tribunals in the contract of employment that if the master has held a proper enquiry and come to the conclusion that the servant should be dismissed and in consequence suspends him pending the permission required under Section 33, he has the power to order such suspension, thus suspending the contract of employment temporarily, so that there is no obligation on him to pay wages and no obligation on the servant to work.”
  • (5) The two possible outcomes: “It follows therefore that if the Tribunal grants permission, the suspended contract would come to an end and there will be no further obligation to pay any wages after the date of suspension. If, on the other hand, the permission is refused, the suspension would be wrong and the workman would be entitled to all his wages from the date of suspension.”
  • (6) The implied term does not oust the Tribunal’s power of interim relief: “such a power of suspension in the employer would not … take away the power of the tribunal to grant interim relief if such power exists under the Act.”
  • (7) Interim relief is an “incidental” matter: §10(4) requires the Tribunal to confine itself to the points referred “and matters incidental thereto”; and “there can be no doubt that if, for example, question of reinstatement and/or compensation is referred to a tribunal for adjudication, the question of granting interim relief till the decision of the tribunal with respect to the same matter would be a matter incidental thereto under Section 10(4) and need not be specifically referred in terms to the tribunal”.
  • (8) Interim relief is not the same as an “interim award”: “an award” is defined in §2(b) as “an interim or final determination … of any industrial dispute or of any question relating thereto”, and an interim award determining some of the matters referred must be published under §17. “Such awards are however not in the nature of interim relief for they decide the industrial dispute or some question relating thereto. Interim relief, on the other hand, is granted under the power conferred on the tribunal under Section 10(4) with respect to matters incidental to the points of dispute for adjudication.” The Court nevertheless assumed, without deciding, that the order of 5 December 1955 suffered from a technical defect for want of publication, and proceeded to consider what relief it should itself grant.
  • (9) The relief actually granted: “Ordinarily, interim relief should not be the whole relief that the workmen would get if they succeeded finally.” Since the Tribunal and the Appellate Tribunal had awarded the entire wages plus ₹25 per head on the footing that no suspension was possible at all, and their orders “might have been different if they had held otherwise”, the Supreme Court ordered that the employers pay half the amount adjudged payable by the order of 5 December 1955 for the entire period — from 1 October 1955 to 10 December 1955 or 15 July 1956, as the case may be. Appeals partly allowed; the order of 5 December 1955 modified accordingly; parties to bear their own costs. The Court also recorded its expectation that, more than three years having gone by in these preliminaries, “the Industrial Tribunal will now dispose of the matter as expeditiously as possible”.
  • (10) The waiver point left open: “as all the workmen concerned were taken back in service they should be paid full wages for the interim period as their re-employment means that the decision to dismiss them and the consequent order of suspension were waived” — “This is a matter on which we do not propose to express any opinion … the effect of re-employment … on the question of waiver cannot be determined at this stage”; and the order passed was “an interim relief and it will be liable to be modified one way or the other” when the Tribunal finally determines the dispute.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. At common law, a power to suspend without pay must be found in a statute, the rules or an express contractual term; absent it, a “suspension” merely means that the employer does not take work, and wages remain payable.
  2. §33 alters that position for industrial establishments: because the employer is statutorily barred from giving effect to a decision to dismiss arrived at after a proper enquiry, an Industrial Tribunal may and should imply a term entitling the employer to suspend the workman without pay pending the decision on permission — the contract of employment being temporarily suspended on both sides.
  3. The implied power is conditional: it arises only where (a) a proper enquiry has been held, (b) the employer has concluded that dismissal is warranted, and (c) the employer is compelled by §33 to seek permission before acting. It does not authorise arbitrary, unenquired suspension.
  4. The wages follow the permission: permission granted → no wages for the suspension period; permission refused → the suspension is wrongful and the workman gets all his wages from the date of suspension.
  5. The Tribunal has power to grant interim relief as a matter incidental to the reference under §10(4), without the question being referred, and such relief is not an “interim award” requiring publication — though it must be less than the whole relief claimed.
Principles and observations (obiter and contextual)
  • Industrial adjudication is creative, not merely declaratory: the Federal Court’s and Rohtas Industries’ statements that an Industrial Tribunal is not fettered by the strict law of master and servant and may create new obligations “in the interests of industrial peace” are the jurisprudential foundation of the whole §33 jurisprudence (and, later, of §11-A itself).
  • Standing orders are not decisive of the principle: the Court held that there is “very little difference in principle” between (i) a case where the standing orders permit a few days’ suspension without pay and the suspension continues much longer, and (ii) a case where there are no standing orders at all — the ratio of the earlier cases must therefore have rested on an implied term (as in Lakshmi Devi Sugar Mills, Ranipur Colliery and Sasa Musa Sugar Works).
  • The employer’s delay is outside its control: the argument accepted from Ranipur Colliery was that “the time taken by the tribunal to accord permission … was beyond the control of the employer”, so that the employer “having done all that he could do to bring the contract of service to an end should not be expected to continue paying the employee thereafter”. (Note how Fakirbhai later turns this reasoning around: if the employer cannot control the delay, neither can the workman — hence the subsistence allowance.)
  • Interim relief must be interim in substance: it “should not be the whole relief that the workmen would get if they succeeded finally” — a principle that explains the 50% award and remains the working standard for §50(2) IRC interim relief.
How to use Hotel Imperial
  • Any question on suspension during pendency begins here: state the common-law rule, then the “fundamental change” introduced by §33, then the implied term, then the two outcomes.
  • Interim relief questions: the §10(4) “incidental” reasoning is the authority; link it forward to §50(2) IRC, which now codifies interim relief, and backward to §§2(b), 15, 17 and 17-A.
  • Never cite Hotel Imperial alone on wages during suspension. It answers “may the employer suspend?” and “need he pay full wages?” — it does not answer “may he pay nothing for years?”. That question belongs to Fakirbhai (Part B.2) and its reconciliation to Ram Lakhan.
  • Predicted question: “Explain the implied-suspension doctrine. Is it still good law?” — answer: yes, but qualified by the subsistence-allowance line.
Precision guards
  • Cite it as (1960) 1 SCR 476 : AIR 1959 SC 1342 (decided 1959, reported 1960). The Bench was of three Judges — a fact of some importance, since it was the reason the Supreme Court referred Ram Lakhan to a three-Judge Bench in 1996.
  • All three cases in this unit were heard together and decided by one judgment (three connected appeals).
  • The interim relief granted was 50% of the amount awarded below, not full wages — do not misstate the relief.
  • The Court expressly declined to decide the waiver point arising from the workmen’s re-employment; do not attribute any holding on waiver to this case.
  • The §33-A applications by the 19 Imperial Hotel workmen are recorded in the facts but the case does not decide §33-A — that provision was the procedural vehicle, not the issue.

㉒ Fakirbhai Fulabhai Solanki v. Presiding Officer CASE No. 22 · Unit 6

(1986) 3 SCC 131 : AIR 1986 SC 1168 · Civil Appeals by special leave from the Industrial Tribunal, Gujarat (Application (IT) No. 88 of 1979 and Complaint (IT) No. 124 of 1979 in Reference (IT) No. 434 of 1978) · Bench: E.S. Venkataramiah, J. · The case that put natural justice into the suspension rule
Context — the qualification of Hotel Imperial

Hotel Imperial permitted implied suspension without pay. But its reasoning assumed a Tribunal that decides permission applications quickly — “those were perhaps halcyon days”, as the Court was later to say. Fakirbhai presents the modern reality: a §33(3) application against a protected workman that took nearly six years to decide, during which the workman received nothing at all. The Court held that this denies him the means to defend himself and therefore violates the principles of natural justice, vitiating the permission granted.

Facts
  1. The appellant was a workman in the factory of the management of Alembic Chemical Works Co. Ltd., Baroda. An industrial dispute was pending before the Industrial Tribunal, Gujarat in Reference (IT) No. 434 of 1978.
  2. During that pendency the management served a charge-sheet on him asking him to show cause why disciplinary action should not be taken for an alleged act of misconduct committed on 12 July 1979 — namely, that he had been playing cards along with two other workmen during the working hours of the factory. It was alleged that he had given a letter addressed to Shri R.A. Desai, Manager (Industrial Relations), admitting his guilt and tendering an apology.
  3. A disciplinary enquiry was held against all three workmen, including the appellant. At its conclusion the Inquiry Officer, Shri J.N. Patel, Director (Manufacturing), found him guilty, and the management decided to dismiss him.
  4. But he was a “protected workman” within the Explanation to §33(3); the management therefore could not dismiss him without permission and made Application (IT) No. 88 of 1979 under §33(3) for that permission.
  5. The suspension and the wage denial: the appellant was suspended from service with effect from 13 August 1979 — after he had been found guilty at the domestic enquiry but without any wages or allowances. The standing orders governing him contained no provision for payment of any subsistence allowance (whether the whole or part of what he was entitled to draw) during the pendency of a §33(3) application.
  6. The parallel complaint: he also filed an application under §33-A complaining of violation of §33 by the management; it was registered as Complaint (IT) No. 124 of 1979.
  7. The delay: both the §33(3) application and the §33-A complaint were filed in 1979 but were disposed of only on 5 August 1985 — after almost six years. During the whole of that period (13 August 1979 to 5 August 1985) he was paid not even a subsistence allowance.
  8. The Tribunal granted permission to dismiss and rejected the §33-A complaint. He appealed, confining his argument before the Supreme Court to the effect of the non-payment of any subsistence allowance on the Tribunal’s decision under §33(3).
Issues
  1. Whether the non-payment of subsistence allowance during the pendency of proceedings under §33(3) amounts to the denial of a reasonable opportunity to defend himself, and therefore to a violation of the principles of natural justice.
  2. Whether, on that account, the Tribunal’s permission to dismiss the protected workman is vitiated.
Arguments
For the workman (appellant) — Mr. … (counsel contended)
  1. Denial of subsistence made effective defence impossible: he could not maintain himself or his family, let alone incur the expenses of the litigation before the Tribunal for nearly six years; the §33(3) proceeding was therefore decided while he was disabled from defending himself.
  2. This amounts to a violation of the principles of natural justice, and the permission accorded by the Tribunal was accordingly vitiated.
  3. Reliance on service-law authority: State of Maharashtra v. Chandrabhan Tale, (1983) 3 SCC 387 — which had struck down a rule reducing a suspended civil servant’s subsistence allowance to a nominal Re. 1 per month as unreasonable and void, holding that a civil servant under suspension is entitled to the normal subsistence allowance even after conviction by the trial court and pending his appeal, whether he is on bail or in prison.
For the management (respondent)
  1. Reliance on Hotel Imperial: that decision had recognised an implied power in the management to suspend a workman without pay where a decision to dismiss had been taken and §33 permission had to be sought; and Ranipur Colliery v. Bhuban Singh, AIR 1959 SC 833, had reasoned that the employer cannot be expected to keep paying when the delay is outside its control.
  2. The standing orders are silent — hence, it was submitted, no obligation to pay any subsistence allowance arises; the workman continued to be a member of the establishment merely nominally.
  3. In any event, the Tribunal granted permission after considering the material, and the appellant had not shown that he lacked the means to defend himself.
Decision (Holding)
  • The workman remains an employee during suspension: “It follows from the provisions of sub-section (3) of Section 33 … that the workman does not cease to be a workman until the Tribunal grants permission to dismiss the workman and the management dismisses the workman pursuant to such permission. An order of suspension by itself does not put an end to the employment. The workman continues to be an employee during the period of suspension and it is for this reason ordinarily the various standing orders in force in several factories and industrial establishments provide for payment of subsistence allowance which is normally less than the usual salary and allowances….”
  • He must be enabled to defend the §33(3) proceeding: “When an application is made under Section 33(3) of the Act the workman is entitled to defend himself before the Tribunal. In these proceedings it is open to him to show that the domestic enquiry held against him was not in accordance with law and principles of natural justice and the action proposed to be taken against him by the management is unjust and should not be permitted. Sometimes it may be necessary to either of the parties to lead evidence even before the Tribunal.”
  • The delay makes the denial fatal: “The proceedings before the Tribunal very often take a long time to come to an end. In this very case the proceedings were pending before the Tribunal for nearly six years. Most of the workmen are not in a position to maintain themselves and the members of their families during the pendency of such proceedings. In addition to the cost of maintenance of his family the workman has to find money to meet the expenses that he has to incur in connection with the proceedings pending before the Tribunal.”
  • The core holding on natural justice: “And if no amount is paid during the pendency of such an application it has to be held that the workman concerned has been denied a reasonable opportunity to defend himself in the proceedings before the Tribunal. Such denial leads to violation of principles of natural justice and consequently vitiates the proceedings before the Tribunal under sub-section (3) of Section 33 of the Act and any decision given in those proceedings against the workman concerned.”
  • Distinguishing the earlier cases: “But in neither of the above two decisions the court considered the question from the angle from which we have approached the problem. In neither of them the court had the occasion to consider whether the denial of payment of subsistence allowance during the pendency of the proceedings under Section 33(3) of the Act would amount to violation of principles of natural justice.” Those decisions “approached the question from the angle of the common law right of a master to keep a workman under suspension … Those were perhaps halcyon days when such applications were being disposed of quickly.”
  • The mischief of delay named: “An unscrupulous management may by all possible means delay the proceedings so that the workman may be driven to accept its terms instead of defending himself in the proceedings under Section 33(3) of the Act. … Denial of payment of at least a small amount by way of subsistence allowance would amount to gross unfairness.”
  • Suspension does not end the service relationship — Khem Chand applied: “An order of suspension of a government servant does not put an end to his service under the government. He continues to be a member of the service in spite of the order of suspension…. The real effect of the order of suspension is that though he continued to be a member of the government service he was not permitted to work, and further, during the period of his suspension he was paid only some allowance — generally called ‘subsistence allowance’ — which is normally less than his salary….”
  • The implied term extended — a reasonable amount must be paid: “In such a case the right of the workman to receive some reasonable amount which may be fixed either by the standing orders or, in the absence of any standing order, by the authority before which the application is pending, by way of subsistence allowance during the pendency of the application under Section 33(3) of the Act with effect from the date of suspension should be implied as a term of the contract of employment.”
  • Result: the proceedings before the Tribunal under §33(3) — and its decision granting permission to dismiss — were vitiated by the denial of a reasonable opportunity to defend.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. Total denial of any subsistence allowance during a prolonged §33(3) proceeding violates the principles of natural justice, because it deprives the workman of a reasonable opportunity to defend himself in that proceeding.
  2. That is a different question from the one Hotel Imperial answered. Hotel Imperial decided whether the employer may suspend (yes — by an implied term); Fakirbhai decides what must be paid while that suspension lasts (at least a reasonable subsistence allowance).
  3. The workman continues in service during suspension; the relationship of master and servant subsists, and a subsistence allowance is a necessary incident of that subsisting relationship.
  4. A reasonable subsistence allowance is implied as a term of the contract of employment, payable from the date of suspension — its rate to be fixed by the standing orders, or (if they are silent) by the authority before which the application is pending.
  5. Consequently, a decision granting permission to dismiss under §33(3) can be set aside where the workman was denied that allowance.
Principles and observations (obiter and contextual)
  • Cross-fertilisation of service law and industrial law: the Court imported the civil-service jurisprudence of Khem Chand and Chandrabhan Tale into industrial adjudication — the two bodies of law share a common concern with dignity and livelihood.
  • A sea-change in the master-servant relationship: “the very concept of the relationship of master and servant has undergone a sea-change since the date on which Hotel Imperial case was decided” — a signal of a broader, welfare-oriented reading of the IDA.
  • §33(1) and §33(3) stand together: “The case falling under Section 33(1) of the Act is not in any way different from a case falling under sub-section (3) of Section 33 and in both these cases previous permission of the authority concerned should be obtained before any action is taken … unlike a case falling under Section 33(2)(b) … where only its approval to an action already taken is required.” (This sentence is the seed of the §33(1)/§33(2) distinction tested every year.)
  • Natural justice is the reason, not the contract: the doctrine does not rest on the presence of a standing order provision — it rests on the workman’s right to be heard with the means of being heard (audi alteram partem in its material sense).
How to use Fakirbhai
  • Whenever the facts disclose a long suspension without wages — especially of a protected workman — this is the operative authority: the denial goes to natural justice and vititates the permission.
  • Frame it as a qualification, not a contradiction of Hotel Imperial: “Hotel Imperial said the employer need not pay full wages; Fakirbhai says he must pay some.”
  • Pair it immediately with Ram Lakhan for the rate — Fakirbhai leaves the rate to the standing orders or the authority; Ram Lakhan supplies the default (full salary) when both are silent.
  • Predicted question: “A protected workman was suspended pending a §33(3) application and paid nothing for five years. Advise him.” — Answer: suspension lawful (Hotel Imperial), denial of allowance unlawful (Fakirbhai), entitlement to full salary absent standing orders (Ram Lakhan).
Precision guards
  • Cite it as (1986) 3 SCC 131 : AIR 1986 SC 1168. The Bench was of two Judges (Venkataramiah, J.) — relevant because the 1996 reference order in Ram Lakhan noted that Hotel Imperial was a three-Judge decision.
  • The misconduct alleged was playing cards during working hours with an admission-cum-apology letter — the gravity of the charge is not the reason for the decision; the reason is the denial of subsistence.
  • Do not say the Court awarded him reinstatement or a specific sum: it held the proceedings vitiated and directed the implication of a term for payment of a reasonable allowance — the quantification was left to the standing orders or the authority.
  • The §33-A complaint was filed and rejected; the Supreme Court’s holding proceeds on the §33(3) proceeding. Mention both, but attribute the vitiation to the §33(3) permission.

㉓ Ram Lakhan v. Presiding Officer CASE No. 23 · Unit 6

(2000) 10 SCC 201 · Civil Appeals by special leave from the High Court of Delhi (W.P. against the order of the Presiding Officer, Industrial Tribunal, Tis Hazari, Delhi) · Bench: S. Saghir Ahmad, J. (three-Judge Bench, constituted by an order of 2 January 1996) · The capstone: reconciliation and the default rule
Context — resolving a conflict of authority

Tribunals and High Courts had split: some applied Hotel Imperial and refused any subsistence allowance where the standing orders were silent (the very view taken by the Industrial Tribunal and the Delhi High Court in this case, despite Fakirbhai being cited to them); others applied Fakirbhai and granted a reasonable allowance. Because Hotel Imperial was a decision of a three-Judge Bench, the Supreme Court directed that the question be placed before a three-Judge Bench — and that Bench held that there is no conflict at all.

Facts
  1. The appellants were employees of Swatantra Bharat Mill, against whom charge-sheets were issued in 1986 and who were subsequently suspended.
  2. An industrial dispute was already pending before the Industrial Tribunal by virtue of Delhi Administration Notification No. F-24(798)/94-Lab dated 1 April 1986. Accordingly, the management filed an application under §33(1) for permission to dismiss the employees on completion of the enquiry.
  3. The workmen opposed the application and claimed that they were entitled to be paid subsistence allowance during the pendency of the disciplinary proceedings for the period of suspension. On that, the Tribunal framed the following preliminary issue: “At what rate, if any, the Management is to pay the subsistence allowance to the workman.”
  4. The Presiding Officer, Industrial Tribunal, Tis Hazari, Delhi, relying on Hotel Imperial, dismissed the objections and held that the workmen were not entitled to any subsistence allowance.
  5. The Delhi High Court dismissed their writ petitions in a one-line order: “In view of the decision of the larger Bench of the Supreme Court in the case of Hotel Imperial v. Hotel Workers’ Union, we are not inclined to interfere in this petition. Dismissed.” Fakirbhai had been cited before the High Court, “but it did not follow the decision and preferred to follow the judgment in Hotel Imperial case”.
  6. The Supreme Court, while entertaining the appeals, passed this order on 2 January 1996: “In view of the fact that the judgment in Hotel Imperial case was rendered by a three-Judge Bench, we consider it appropriate that these petitions be listed before a three-Judge Bench.”
Issues
  1. Whether the management has the right to suspend an employee pending disposal of an application under §33(1) made after completion of a domestic enquiry.
  2. Whether the employee is entitled to subsistence allowance during such suspension, and at what rate.
  3. Whether Hotel Imperial and Fakirbhai are in conflict.
Arguments
For the workmen (appellants)
  1. Reliance on Fakirbhai: total denial of subsistence allowance for a prolonged period violates natural justice; a reasonable subsistence allowance must be implied as a term of the contract; the Tribunal and the High Court erred in refusing to follow it.
  2. The management has no control over the disposal of its own §33(1) application — but neither has the workman, who cannot be left without the means of subsistence for an indefinite period.
  3. The preliminary issue framed by the Tribunal itself (“at what rate, if any”) presupposed that some rate might be payable; the Tribunal answered it by holding that nothing was payable, which is contrary to law.
For the management (respondent)
  1. Reliance on Hotel Imperial (and on Ranipur Colliery): where a proper enquiry has been held and a decision to dismiss taken, the employer may suspend and is under no obligation to pay wages for the period during which the permission application remains pending.
  2. The standing orders/service rules do not provide for a subsistence allowance in these circumstances; the Court should not write a financial obligation into the contract.
  3. If permission is ultimately refused, the workman will be paid all arrears — so no prejudice is caused in the meantime.
Decision (Holding)
  • The case-law chain reviewed: Hotel Imperial (1959) → T. Cajee v. U. Jormanik Siem, AIR 1961 SC 276 → R.P. Kapur v. Union of India, AIR 1964 SC 787 → Balvantrai Ratilal Patel v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 1968 SC 800 → V.P. Gidroniya v. State of M.P., AIR 1970 SC 1494 → Vice-Chancellor, Jammu University v. Dushiant Kumar Rampal → State of M.P. v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 1977 SC 1466 → Khem Chand v. Union of India, AIR 1963 SC 687 → State of Maharashtra v. Chandrabhan Tale, (1983) 3 SCC 387 → Fakirbhai (1986) → O.P. Gupta v. Union of India, (1987) 4 SCC 328 → Capt. M. Paul Anthony v. Bharat Gold Mines Ltd., (1999) 3 SCC 679.
  • What Hotel Imperial actually decided: it was “concerned with the preliminary question whether the Management during the pendency of its application under Section 33(1) … can legally suspend the employees after holding a proper departmental enquiry. The question whether an employee would be entitled to subsistence allowance during the period of suspension was not directly involved in that case.” The observation that there would be “no obligation on him to pay wages and no obligation on the servant to work” reflects only the rule that a suspended employee does not get full wages — “He gets only reduced salary (subsistence allowance), prescribed by the rules.”
  • THE RECONCILING HOLDING: “Read in the light of the above discussion, there will be found no conflict of opinion between the decisions … in Hotel Imperial case and in Fakirbhai case. While the right to place an employee under suspension pending disposal of the application under Section 33(1) is to be conceded to the Management on the basis of the decision in Hotel Imperial case, the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance during the period of such suspension has to be conceded to the employee on the basis of the decision in Fakirbhai case and other decisions of this Court….”
  • THE UNIFYING PRINCIPLE: “Just as the employer has no control over the disposal of the application under Section 33(1) …, so also the employee has no control over the disposal of that application. … While the Management can afford to wait for the disposal of that application, it would be impossible for an employee who survives only on his salary to wait for the disposal of that application for an indefinite period. It would not be possible for him to sustain himself. … Thus, the right of the Management to suspend and the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance are intertwined and both must survive together.”
  • THE DEFAULT RULE: “the subsistence allowance shall be paid to the appellants for the whole of the period of suspension at such rates as is provided under the Standing Orders or the Service Rules and if there is no such provision, they would be entitled to be paid full salary even during the period of suspension.”
  • Result: appeals allowed; the judgments of the Delhi High Court and of the Industrial Tribunal were set aside; the arrears of subsistence allowance were directed to be paid within three months from the date on which a certified copy of the order was produced before the officer concerned.
Ratio Decidendi
  1. No conflict: Hotel Imperial and Fakirbhai answer different, complementary questions — the employer’s right to suspend (and to withhold full wages) and the employee’s right to a subsistence allowance.
  2. Intertwined rights: the employer’s suspension power is not unconditional; it is conditioned upon payment of a reasonable subsistence allowance. Both rights “must survive together”.
  3. The default rule: where the standing orders/service rules prescribe a rate, that rate governs; where they are silent, the workman is entitled to full salary during suspension.
  4. The employer cannot rely on its own delay: the delay in deciding the approval/permission application is as much outside the workman’s control as the employer’s, and cannot be used to justify leaving him without means.
Principles and observations (obiter and contextual)
  • Method of harmonious construction: the Court refused to treat Fakirbhai as having impliedly overruled (or diluted) a three-Judge Bench decision; it read the two together. This is a model of precedent-handling worth citing in any answer on the doctrine of precedent.
  • Practical consequence of the default rule: because most standing orders provide only a fraction of wages as subsistence allowance (commonly half), the default of “full salary” gives employers a strong incentive to legislate the rate into their standing orders — a neat illustration of how a judge-made default shapes industrial practice.
  • Structural echo of Hotel Imperial: the same methodology — implying a contractual term to fit a statutory scheme — is used in both cases; the difference is that in Ram Lakhan the implied term operates in the workman’s favour.
  • Link to §33(5): the three-month limit for deciding approval applications (inserted by Act 46 of 1982) is the statutory companion to Ram Lakhan: the Court supplies the money; the statute supplies the speed.
How to use Ram Lakhan
  • Always cite it LAST in the trilogy and quote the sentence: “the right of the Management to suspend and the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance are intertwined and both must survive together.”
  • The default rule is the single most examinable detail: standing orders silent → full salary (not nothing, not 50%).
  • Use it to answer any “reconcile the authorities” question, and as authority that the employer’s delay cannot defeat the workman’s claim.
  • Link forward to the IRC: neither §90 nor §91 codifies this rule; the Court’s rationale (natural justice and Article 21) survives the re-enactment.
Precision guards
  • Cite as (2000) 10 SCC 201; the Bench was of three Judges (Saghir Ahmad, J.), which is why it is the authoritative reconciliation.
  • Do not say Fakirbhai was overruled — it was explained and preserved; equally, do not say Hotel Imperial was overruled. Both survive.
  • The application in this case was under §33(1); the ruling applies to §33(1) and §33(3) alike (the Court in Fakirbhai had already equated them), and to approval cases under §33(2)(b) in principle.
  • The direction was to pay at the standing-order/service-rule rate, and full salary only in default — do not state the relief as “full salary in every case”.

The policy tension in one paragraph

Both provisions rest on the same proposition: the pendency of an industrial dispute alters the legal relationship between employer and workman. The employer is not deprived of its right to manage, nor of its right to punish; but the exercise of those rights is subjected to the supervision of the very authority whose proceeding is pending, so that it can neither pre-empt the adjudication nor punish the litigant for litigating. Section 33 is therefore best described not as a “suspension of managerial rights” but as their procedural channelling — a classic instance of Indian labour legislation preferring industrial peace secured by procedure to freedom of contract.

C.3 Suspension — the concept, its four types, and whether it is “punishment”

What suspension is — and is not

Suspension in industrial law is the temporary withholding of work and wages from a workman while some proceeding — a domestic enquiry, a criminal trial, or an application under §33 — is pending, or as a penalty imposed at the end of such a proceeding. It is not a termination: Khem Chand, applied in Fakirbhai, is emphatic — “An order of suspension … does not put an end to his service … He continues to be a member of the service in spite of the order of suspension.”

The four species

  1. Interim suspension pending enquiry — the ordinary case; wages governed by the standing orders by way of a subsistence allowance.
  2. Suspension pending permission/approval under §33 — the species dealt with in Hotel Imperial; the implied-term controversy arose here.
  3. Suspension as a punishment — imposed by way of a penalty under the standing orders (e.g., “suspension without wages for a specified period”); this is “punishment” within §33(1)(b)/(2)(b) and requires permission/approval accordingly.
  4. Deemed suspension — where the workman is in custody for a period exceeding a specified number of hours, the standing orders/service rules often provide for automatic suspension; note that a workman in custody is still entitled to a fair enquiry before dismissal (Unit 5 — Raghubir Singh, quoting Jaffar Imam).
Is suspension “punishment”? — the test that decides which sub-section applies

The question is not one of form but of character and effect. Lakshmi Devi Sugar Mills v. Pt. Ram Sarup held that suspension pending a §33 permission application, even if it exceeds the number of days allowed by the standing orders, is not “punishment” — it is an interim measure, so its wages consequences depend on the outcome of the permission application. But where the employer’s order imposes suspension as the penalty for misconduct — i.e., it is the order-in-award, not a holding operation — it is punishment, and must satisfy §33(1)(b)/(2)(b)/§33(3) according to the connection with the dispute and the status of the workman.

C.4 Subsistence allowance — concept, quantum and constitutional anchor

AspectAnalysis
Meaning“Subsistence” means a means of supporting life, especially a minimum livelihood (O.P. Gupta v. Union of India, quoting the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary). A subsistence allowance is therefore not a wage for work but a sum to keep body and soul together during suspension.
Source of the rightOriginally the standing orders or service rules; in their absence, an implied term of the contract of employment (Fakirbhai) — and, under the Codes, no express provision at all, so the implied term continues to do the work.
Constitutional anchorArticle 21. State of Maharashtra v. Chandrabhan Tale, (1983) 3 SCC 387, read the right to life into the service rules on suspension and struck down a rule that reduced the allowance to a nominal Re. 1 per month. The same rationale — livelihood as a condition of the ability to defend oneself — supplies the natural-justice holding in Fakirbhai.
Usual quantumIndustrial standing orders and service rules commonly provide for a fraction of the last drawn wages — a very frequent figure being half, sometimes increased after a long period of suspension (for example, to three-quarters after six months, so that the workman is not driven to destitution). The rate is, in each case, a question of the standing order or rule; the residuary rule is Ram Lakhan’s full salary.
EnforcementArrears may be recovered through the award in the §33 proceeding or the §33-A complaint, or by a claim under §33-C(2) where the sum is “due”; in Ram Lakhan the Court itself directed payment within three months of production of a certified copy of the order.
Deductions and the CodesThe allowance is wages for most statutory purposes — it counts for the Payment of Wages Act, 1936 / Code on Wages, 2019 (deductions, timely payment), for provident fund and for gratuity computations in appropriate cases; and the Code on Social Security, 2020 continues the contributory framework. A suspended workman is therefore not outside the social-security net.

C.5 The tiers of protection during pendency

Ordinary workman, unconnected misconduct

Weakest tier: dismissal permitted after one month’s wages and an approval application; the authority’s review is prima facie and it can neither impose conditions nor pass a conditional order. But non-approval makes the dismissal void (Jaipur Zila).

Ordinary workman, connected misconduct

Stronger tier: prior permission is a condition precedent; the wrong labelling of the misconduct as “unconnected” converts a valid action into a contravention — and the employer carries that risk.

Protected workman / union office-bearer

Strongest tier: prior permission whatever the connection, a cap of 1% (min 5, max 100) of the establishment’s workmen, recognition under the Rules (Rule 61 of the Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules, 1957), and the protection of the union’s negotiating leadership from decapitation while the dispute is on foot.

Two practical points about protected status

(i) It is annual and procedural: the union nominates its office-bearers, ordinarily by 30 April each year, and the employer must recognise them within the statutory cap; the protection is not a permanent badge. An office-bearer who is himself facing disciplinary proceedings is not entitled to be nominated so as to pre-empt an enquiry.

(ii) It is not immunity: the employer may still proceed against a protected workman — it must merely obtain permission first; and the authority will grant permission where the domestic enquiry is fair and the proposed action is not mala fide or victimising.

C.6 Principles distilled — the eight rules of Unit 6

  1. Pendency changes the default: during a pending proceeding, the employer’s disciplinary power becomes supervised — permission for connected matters and for protected workmen; approval for unconnected dismissals.
  2. Connection decides the route; and misjudging connection is the employer’s risk, not the workman’s.
  3. Permission is wider than approval: the authority asked for permission decides whether the employer may act; the authority asked for approval only asks whether there is a prima facie case, and cannot impose conditions (Lord Krishna Textile Mills).
  4. Non-compliance is fatal in different ways: void dismissal for want of approval (Jaipur Zila); a §33-A remedy and §31 penalty for any contravention.
  5. Suspension is lawful, silence is not: the employer may suspend after a proper enquiry and a decision to dismiss (Hotel Imperial), but must pay a subsistence allowance throughout (Fakirbhai; Ram Lakhan).
  6. Denial of subsistence is a denial of hearing: it violates natural justice and vititates the permission granted in that proceeding.
  7. The default rate is full salary where the standing orders are silent — the high-water mark of the trilogy.
  8. The doctrine survives the Codes: §90 and §91 reproduce §33 and §33-A in substance, and neither codifies the allowance; the constitutional rationale continues.

C.7 Critical appraisal — the unfinished business of §33

What the scheme does well

  • It preserves the adjudication. Without §33, a determined employer could dispose of the disputants and render the reference academic.
  • It is graded, not absolute. The connected/unconnected distinction, and the permission/approval pairing, allow ordinary discipline to continue — a genuine balance.
  • It gives the workman standing. §33-A/§91 supplies a remedy without the usual bottleneck of a Government reference.
  • It has adapted judicially. The subsistence-allowance trilogy responded to what Parliament had not foreseen — six-year delays.

What remains weak

  • The “connection” test is undefined. It generates the bulk of §33 litigation; the employer must guess, and a wrong guess is a contravention.
  • The allowance is uncodified. The IRC reproduces §33 but says nothing about subsistence — leaving the workman to depend on judge-made law.
  • Delay remains the central pathology. §33(5) prescribes three months, but the provisos allow indefinite extension and non-lapse; Fakirbhai’s six years could still happen.
  • Remedial overlap. §33-A, §33-C(2), §31 and the reference under §10 can all be invoked on one set of facts, producing parallel proceedings and inconsistent interim orders.
  • The approval route is procedurally thin: a prima facie review cannot catch subtle victimisation — which is why the workman retains a full merits remedy later.
Reform suggestions (for a “critically examine” answer)
  • Define “connected with the dispute” in the statute, or at least require the authority to decide that question as a preliminary issue within a fixed time.
  • Codify the subsistence allowance — a floor of not less than half the last drawn wages, rising after six months, payable within seven days of suspension — so that no workman has to relitigate Fakirbhai.
  • Make §33(5)’s three months a true outer limit by confining extensions to recorded reasons and capping them.
  • Consolidate the remedies: the §33-A complaint should be the single forum, with the §33-C recovery and the §31 prosecution following upon its award rather than running alongside it.
  • Route minor grievances to the Grievance Redressal Committee under §4 IRC, so that the restraint provisions are reserved for genuine disputes.
Model paragraph — “Discuss the nature and scope of the restraints imposed by §33 of the IDA, 1947 (now §90 IRC, 2020)” (6 marks)

Section 33 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 — re-enacted as §90 of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — restrains the employer’s managerial prerogative during the pendency of proceedings before a conciliation officer, arbitrator, Labour Court, Tribunal or National Industrial Tribunal, so that the subject-matter of the pending adjudication is not destroyed and workmen, particularly union office-bearers, are not victimised. The restraint is graded. In matters connected with the dispute, §33(1) forbids any prejudicial alteration of service conditions and any punishment “by dismissal or otherwise” without the express permission in writing of the authority. In matters not connected, §33(2) permits the employer to act in accordance with the standing orders or the contract, but a dismissal or discharge requires the payment of one month’s wages and an application for approval; the distinction between permission and approval is crucial, the former being a condition precedent and the scope of the latter’s enquiry being confined to a prima facie case (Lord Krishna Textile Mills; Lalla Ram). For a protected workman, §33(3) requires prior permission regardless of connection, the class being limited by §33(4) to one per cent of the workmen, with a minimum of five and a maximum of one hundred. Contravention is remedied by a §33-A complaint — adjudicated as if it were a referred dispute — and is punishable under §31; and where the approval required by the proviso to §33(2)(b) is not sought or is refused, the dismissal is void and inoperative (Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank Ltd. v. Ram Gopal Sharma, (2002) 2 SCC 244). Finally, the pendency also governs the employer’s power of suspension: the employer may suspend after a proper enquiry (Hotel Imperial), but the workman continues in service and must be paid a subsistence allowance (Fakirbhai), the default being full salary where the standing orders are silent (Ram Lakhan). The provision thus converts the employer’s unilateral prerogative into a supervised one, without depriving the employer of the power to maintain discipline.

D.1 Section 90, Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — the full text

§90 — Conditions of service, etc., to remain unchanged under certain circumstances during pendency of proceedings

“(1) Where an industrial dispute pertaining to an establishment or undertaking is already pending before a conciliation officer or an Arbitrator or a Tribunal or a National Industrial Tribunal, as the case may be, with regard to matters not covered by the notice of change issued by an employer under section 40, no employer shall— (a) in regard to any matter connected with such dispute, alter to the prejudice of the workers concerned in such dispute the conditions of service applicable to them immediately before the commencement of such proceedings; or (b) for any misconduct connected with the dispute, discharge or punish, whether by dismissal or otherwise any worker concerned in such dispute, save with the express permission in writing of the authority before which the proceeding is pending.

(2) During the pendency of any such proceeding in respect of an industrial dispute referred in sub-section (1), the employer may, in accordance with standing orders applicable to a worker concerned in such dispute or, where there are no such standing orders, in accordance with the terms of the contract, whether express or implied, between him and the worker— (a) alter, in regard to any matter not connected with the dispute, the conditions of service applicable to that worker immediately before the commencement of such proceeding; or (b) for any misconduct not connected with the dispute, discharge or punish, whether by dismissal or otherwise, that worker: Provided that no such worker shall be discharged or dismissed, unless he has been paid wages for one month and an application has been made by the employer to the authority before which the proceeding is pending for approval of the action taken by the employer.

D.3 The subordinate legislation — how “protected workman” status is actually obtained

InstrumentContent relevant to §33(3)–(4) / §90(3)–(4)
Rule 61, Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules, 1957Lays down the machinery of recognition: every registered trade union connected with the establishment must communicate to the employer the names and addresses of its office-bearers who are to be recognised as protected workmen — ordinarily on or before 30 April every year — within the statutory cap of one per cent (minimum five, maximum one hundred). The employer must recognise the union’s nominees within the cap; Rule 61(2) is regarded as mandatory in this behalf, the employer’s function being limited to keeping the number within the cap rather than choosing favourites. The rule also provides for substitution where a recognised protected workman ceases to hold office, and an office-bearer who is himself facing disciplinary proceedings cannot be nominated so as to defeat an enquiry already commenced. Particulars, not conclusions, are what the Tribunal needs: the identity of the pending reference, the impugned action, and the manner in which §33/§90 was contravened.

§11 IDAProcedure and powers of the authorities: they follow such procedure as they think fit, subject to the rules, but are bound by the principles of natural justice — the duty which Fakirbhai enforced through the subsistence allowance.
§§15, 17, 17-A IDADuty to submit the award; publication; commencement. The distinction between an “interim award” (which determines a referred question and must be published) and “interim relief” (an incidental order) comes from §2(b) read with these sections — Hotel Imperial.
§17-B IDA / §56 IRCFull wages last drawn pending the employer’s appeal against a reinstatement award — the later statutory guarantee of subsistence at the appellate stage (Unit 5, Part D.3).
§31, §32 and §34 IDA§31 makes a contravention of §33 an offence punishable with fine; §32 deals with offences by companies and the liability of those in charge; §34 restricts the courts competent to take cognizance.
§33-C IDARecovery of money due from an employer under a settlement, award or the provisions of Chapters V-A/V-B; it is the enforcement arm through which §33-A awards, subsistence-allowance arrears and back wages are realised (see Jaipur Zila, where the workman proceeded under §33-C(2)).
Chapter V-C IDA / Chapter IX IRC (Unfair Labour Practices)Victimisation for trade-union activity is an unfair labour practice; the §33 protection is the procedural shield for the same value. An unfair labour practice may be the subject of a reference or a complaint, and vitiates disciplinary action.

D.5 Constitutional and service-law anchors

Constitution of India

  • Article 12 — a statutory corporation or government company is “State”: its disciplinary action is subject to Part III (U.P. Warehousing Corporation, applied in Raghubir Singh).
  • Articles 14 and 16 — non-arbitrariness in disciplinary action; discrimination between similarly placed delinquents; the proportionality standard (Unit 5).
  • Article 19(1)(g) — the employer’s freedom of trade is subject to reasonable restriction in the interest of industrial peace (Art 19(6)) — the constitutional basis of §33 itself.
  • Article 21 — livelihood: the source of Chandrabhan Tale and of the natural-justice holding in Fakirbhai. An allowance of Re 1 per month is unconstitutional; so is the total denial of maintenance for years.
  • Articles 226/227 and 136 — the routes by which §33 orders reach the High Court and Supreme Court (as in Ram Lakhan, where the High Court’s one-line dismissal was reversed).
  • Article 311 — applies only to civil servants of the Union or a State, not to industrial “workmen”; invoking Art 311(2)(b) against a workman is a serious error (Unit 5 — Raghubir Singh).

Wages and social security statutes

  • Payment of Wages Act, 1936 / Code on Wages, 2019 — the subsistence allowance is “wages” for the purposes of payment and permissible deductions; wages must be paid within the prescribed time, and unauthorised deductions are recoverable. Under the Code on Wages, §17–18 govern deductions and §45 provides for claims.
  • Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 / Code on Social Security, 2020 — contributions continue on amounts that constitute “basic wages”; continuity of service (if the suspension ends in reinstatement) affects provident fund and pension.
  • Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 / Code on Social Security, 2020 — gratuity accrues on continuous service; a suspension does not break continuity, and on reinstatement the period counts (subject to the employer’s right to treat the suspension period as not on duty, which is precisely why the allowance matters).
  • Employees’ Compensation Act, 1923 / Code on Social Security, 2020 — relevant if the misconduct caused injury or if a workman is disabled during the dispute.

D.7 Comparative and collateral points

United Kingdom and ILO

The English law of suspension derives from the contract of employment and the implied duty of mutual trust and confidence; there is no statutory “permission” requirement. The ILO jurisprudence (Recommendation No. 119 of 1963 on termination of employment; Convention No. 158 of 1982 on termination at the initiative of the employer) requires a neutral body to examine the justification of a termination — the same value that §33 and §11-A protect in the Indian scheme. Convention 158 remains unratified by India.

Parallel proceedings — criminal, departmental and §33

  • A criminal prosecution and a domestic enquiry may proceed simultaneously; the employer need not await the trial’s outcome where the misconduct is simple and the enquiry fair (Delhi Cloth & General Mills Ltd. v. Kushal Bhan, AIR 1960 SC 806).
  • But where the workman is in custody, the enquiry must still be fairly conducted — a detention order is no substitute for an enquiry (Calcutta Dock Labour Board v. Jaffar Imam, applied in Raghubir Singh).
  • The §33 permission/approval proceeding is not a trial of the misconduct; it is a check on the employer’s bona fides and procedure. Merits are decided later, under §11-A/§50.

F.2 Model answer — 15 marks (the dedicated Unit-6 answer)

Question

“Discuss the restraints imposed by §33 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (now §90 of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020) on the employer’s managerial prerogative during the pendency of industrial proceedings, and explain the law relating to the suspension of a workman and the payment of subsistence allowance during such pendency, with reference to the prescribed case law.”

Answer

1. Introduction — the mischief. Industrial adjudication takes time. If, during that time, an employer were free to dismiss the disputants or to alter the very conditions of service under adjudication, the reference would be decided in a vacuum created by the employer: the workman would be dismissed, the union decapitated and the dispute rendered academic. Section 33 of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 — re-enacted as §90 of the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 — prevents this by making the employer’s disciplinary and managerial powers subject to the supervision of the authority before which the proceeding is pending. Its object, in the words of the Supreme Court in Air India v. V.A. Rebellow, is “to protect the workmen against victimisation or unfair labour practices, consistently with the preservation of the employer’s bona fide right to maintain discipline and efficiency in the industry”.

2. The statutory scheme — a graded restraint. The restraint is not uniform; it is calibrated to the connection between the employer’s proposed action and the pending dispute:

  • §33(1) — connected matters: during the pendency of a proceeding before a conciliation officer, Board of Conciliation, arbitrator, Labour Court, Tribunal or National Tribunal, no employer shall (a) alter, to the prejudice of the workmen concerned, the conditions of service applicable immediately before the commencement of the proceeding, or (b) punish or discharge them “for any misconduct connected with the dispute”, save with the express permission in writing of the authority. Permission is therefore a condition precedent.
  • §33(2) — unconnected matters: the employer may act in accordance with the standing orders or the contract of employment; but no workman “shall be discharged or dismissed, unless he has been paid wages for one month and an application has been made … for approval of the action taken”. Here the action is taken first and validated afterwards.
  • §33(3) — protected workman: “Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (2)”, no action by way of prejudicial alteration of service conditions or punishment/dismissal may be taken against a protected workman without the express permission in writing of the authority. The class is defined by the Explanation (member of the executive or other office-bearer of a registered trade union connected with the establishment, recognised under the Rules) and capped by §33(4) at one per cent of the workmen, with a minimum of five and a maximum of one hundred.
  • §33(5) — speed: an approval application must be heard and decided within three months, though the period may be extended for reasons recorded in writing and the proceedings do not lapse on expiry — a limitation inserted by Act 46 of 1982 precisely because of the delays that had come before the Court.
  • §33-A — the workman’s remedy: where the employer contravenes §33, “any employee aggrieved by such contravention may make a complaint in writing” to the authority before which the proceeding is pending (or to the conciliation officer/Board in the first instance), and that authority “shall adjudicate upon the complaint as if it were a dispute referred to or pending before it”, and submit its award in the ordinary way. Section 33-A thus by-passes the reference machinery and gives the workman direct access to the Tribunal already seised of the dispute. Under the IRC it survives as §91.

3. Permission and approval are not the same thing. The distinction, most clearly drawn in Lord Krishna Textile Mills v. Its Workmen, is that the requirement of approval means that “the ban imposed by §33(2) is not as rigid or rigorous as that imposed by §33(1)”, and “the jurisdiction to give or withhold permission is prima facie wider than the jurisdiction to give or withhold approval”. In an approval enquiry, the authority considers “whether a prima facie case for according approval is made out”; it may neither impose conditions nor pass a conditional order, and it does not sit in appeal over the domestic enquiry. The three requisites of the proviso to §33(2)(b) — one month’s wages, the application for approval, and a proper enquiry free from victimisation — were formulated in Lalla Ram v. Management of D.C.M. Chemical Works Ltd. And the consequence of non-compliance is drastic: in Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank Ltd. v. Ram Gopal Sharma, (2002) 2 SCC 244, a four-Judge Bench held that failure to make, or the withdrawal of, an application for approval is itself a contravention of the proviso, that the order of dismissal is void and inoperative — the workman being deemed never to have been dismissed — and that a §33-A complaint lies even after approval has been granted, to challenge it as victimisation; Punjab Beverages (P) Ltd. v. Suresh Chand, (1978) 2 SCC 144, was not followed on the point.

4. The pendency question — may the employer suspend? Having decided to dismiss but being barred by §33 from doing so at once, may the employer suspend the workman in the meantime? In The Management of Hotel Imperial v. Hotel Workers’ Union, (1960) 1 SCR 476 : AIR 1959 SC 1342, Wanchoo J. held, first, that at common law the power to suspend without pay “is not an implied term in an ordinary contract between master and servant” and must be found in a statute or an express contract — failing which the employer must pay wages. But, secondly, industrial adjudication is not bound by that strict law: following the Federal Court in Western India Automobile Association, (1949) FCR 321, and Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. Brijnandan Pandey, (1956) SCR 800, an Industrial Tribunal may “create new obligations or modify contracts in the interests of industrial peace”. Thirdly, and decisively, “Section 33 has introduced a fundamental change in the law of master and servant”, so that “in the peculiar circumstances which have arisen on account of the enactment of Section 33, it is but just and fair that Industrial Tribunals should imply such a term in the contract of employment” — namely, that where the employer has held a proper enquiry, concluded that the workman should be dismissed and is compelled by §33 to seek permission, it may suspend him, thereby temporarily suspending the contract of employment with “no obligation on him to pay wages and no obligation on the servant to work”. The wages follow the permission: if permission is granted, no wages are payable for the period; if it is refused, “the suspension would be wrong and the workman would be entitled to all his wages from the date of suspension”. The Court also upheld the Tribunal’s power to grant interim relief as a matter “incidental” to the reference under §10(4) — though interim relief “should not be the whole relief” claimed finally — and ordered the interim payment to be reduced to one half.

5. The qualification — total denial of subsistence offends natural justice. Hotel Imperial was decided in “halcyon days”, as the Court later put it, when permission applications were disposed of quickly. In Fakirbhai Fulabhai Solanki v. Presiding Officer, (1986) 3 SCC 131 : AIR 1986 SC 1168, a protected workman of Alembic Chemical Works (charge-sheeted for playing cards during working hours) was suspended from 13 August 1979 without any allowance, and the §33(3) application was decided only on 5 August 1985 — nearly six years later. Venkataramiah J. held that the workman “does not cease to be a workman until the Tribunal grants permission”, that suspension “does not put an end to the employment”, and that in the §33(3) proceeding the workman is entitled to defend himself — to show that the enquiry was contrary to law and natural justice and that the proposed action is unjust. “This very case” showed that such proceedings take years, and that “most of the workmen are not in a position to maintain themselves and the members of their families” or to meet litigation costs. Accordingly: “if no amount is paid during the pendency of such an application … the workman concerned has been denied a reasonable opportunity to defend himself … Such denial leads to violation of principles of natural justice and consequently vitiates the proceedings.” The Court distinguished Hotel Imperial and Ranipur Colliery (which had considered only the common-law right of the master to suspend) and held that the right to receive “some reasonable amount … by way of subsistence allowance … with effect from the date of suspension should be implied as a term of the contract of employment”, the rate being fixed by the standing orders or, in their absence, by the authority. The permission granted was therefore vitiated. It also relied on the service-law principle in Khem Chand v. Union of India, AIR 1963 SC 687, and on State of Maharashtra v. Chandrabhan Tale, (1983) 3 SCC 387, which had struck down as unconstitutional a rule reducing a suspended civil servant’s allowance to Re. 1 per month, reading the right to life (Article 21) into the suspension rules.

6. The reconciliation and the default rule. In Ram Lakhan v. Presiding Officer, (2000) 10 SCC 201 — a three-Judge Bench constituted because Hotel Imperial itself was a three-Judge decision, and because the Industrial Tribunal and the Delhi High Court had refused any allowance relying on it while ignoring Fakirbhai — the Supreme Court held that “there will be found no conflict of opinion” between the two decisions: “While the right to place an employee under suspension pending disposal of the application under Section 33(1) is to be conceded to the Management on the basis of the decision in Hotel Imperial case, the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance during the period of such suspension has to be conceded to the employee on the basis of the decision in Fakirbhai case….” The reasoning is memorable: “Just as the employer has no control over the disposal of the application …, so also the employee has no control over the disposal of that application … it would be impossible for an employee who survives only on his salary to wait … for an indefinite period.” Hence: “the right of the Management to suspend and the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance are intertwined and both must survive together.” And then the default rule: subsistence allowance is payable “at such rates as is provided under the Standing Orders or the Service Rules and if there is no such provision, they would be entitled to be paid full salary even during the period of suspension” — arrears to be paid within three months.

7. Synthesis — the law in five propositions.

  1. Connection decides the route: connected matters and protected workmen → prior permission; unconnected dismissals → one month’s wages plus approval; and the employer bears the risk of misjudging connection.
  2. Non-compliance has hard consequences: void dismissal under §33(2)(b) (Jaipur Zila); a §33-A complaint and §31 penalty for any contravention; and no bar to a later merits adjudication under §11-A/§50.
  3. Suspension is permitted, but not for free: the implied power in Hotel Imperial is real, yet it is now inseparable from the duty to pay a subsistence allowance.
  4. Denial of subsistence is a denial of hearing: it violates natural justice, vitiates the permission granted, and is unconstitutional when the allowance is nominal (Article 21 — Chandrabhan Tale).
  5. The default is full salary: employers who leave their standing orders silent bear the cost (Ram Lakhan).

8. Conclusion. Section 33 (now §90 IRC) does not abolish the employer’s managerial prerogative; it proceduralises it. The employer may still discipline, dismiss and alter conditions — but, while a dispute is pending, it must first ask the authority (or afterwards justify the action to it), must pay a month’s wages where the dismissal is unconnected, and must maintain the workman during any suspension it imposes. Read with §33-A (now §91), which gives the aggrieved employee direct access to the pending Tribunal, the scheme converts a unilateral power into a supervised one. The trilogy of Hotel Imperial, Fakirbhai and Ram Lakhan completes the picture by answering the human question the statute left open: the workman may not have a right to full wages during suspension, but he has a right to live — and to be able to defend himself — while the law decides his fate.

F.3 Model answer — Dec 2024 Q.7 (suspension allowance during §33(1) pendency)

Question

“The management has applied for permission under §33(1) of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 to dismiss a workman, and has suspended him pending the decision. Is the workman entitled to any payment during this period? Discuss.”

Answer — structure

(a) The starting point is that suspension does not end the employment. Cite Khem Chand (AIR 1963 SC 687): “An order of suspension … does not put an end to his service … He continues to be a member of the service in spite of the order of suspension.” The workman therefore remains entitled to the incidents of a subsisting relationship — in particular to a subsistence allowance.

(b) The employer’s right to suspend is judicially implied, not statutory. Hotel Imperial: at common law there is no implied power of suspension without pay; but §33 works a “fundamental change” in the master-servant law, and it is “just and fair” that Tribunals imply a term permitting suspension where a proper enquiry was held, dismissal decided upon, and permission is compelled. If permission is granted, no wages for the period; if refused, all wages from the date of suspension.

(c) But the implied term does not permit payment of nothing. Fakirbhai: the question whether total denial of subsistence violates natural justice was not considered in Hotel Imperial; in a §33 proceeding — which in that case lasted nearly six years — the workman must be enabled to defend himself, and a reasonable allowance must be implied from the date of suspension, its rate fixed by the standing orders or, failing them, by the authority. Denial “vitiates the proceedings”.

(d) The rate — and the default. Ram Lakhan: standing order/service rule rate if one exists; full salary if the rules are silent; arrears within three months. The rights of suspension and of subsistence “are intertwined and both must survive together”. Constitutional support: Chandrabhan Tale (Re. 1 rule void; Article 21).

(e) Additional statutory protection: if the workman is a protected workman, the employer needed §33(3) permission, not an application under §33(1) simpliciter — a defect that itself vitiates the proceeding; and once an award of reinstatement is made and the employer appeals, §17-B IDA/§56 IRC requires the payment of full wages last drawn.

(f) Conclusion. The workman is entitled to a subsistence allowance from the date of suspension — at the rate in the standing orders, or to full salary if they are silent — and if the permission is refused, to all his wages for the period, with continuity of service.

F.4 Model answer — Nov–Dec 2025 Q.4 (protected workman; five years without allowance)

Question (adapted from the 2025 paper)

“A protected workman was suspended for alleged misconduct after a domestic enquiry found him guilty. The management filed an application under §33(3) for permission to dismiss him. The standing orders are silent on subsistence allowance, and he received no wages for five years while the Tribunal proceedings were pending. Advise him.”

Answer

1. Frame the two questions separately. (i) Was the suspension lawful? (ii) Was the total denial of any allowance for five years lawful? The trilogy answers them in that order — and the second question is the live one.

2. The statutory position. The workman being a protected workman (Explanation to §33(3), recognised under Rule 61 of the Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules, 1957, within the §33(4) cap of 1%, minimum five, maximum one hundred), the employer could not dismiss or punish him during the pendency of the proceeding except with the express permission in writing of the authority. The management rightly applied under §33(3) — but note that §33(5) requires the application to be decided within three months, and a five-year pendency therefore indicates a failure of the statutory scheme to which Parliament itself responded.

3. The suspension itself is lawful. Hotel Imperial: an implied term permits suspension where a proper enquiry has been held and dismissal decided upon; the enquiry here was held and the finding was adverse, so the suspension is not per se vulnerable — provided the enquiry was fair and the suspension was not itself a disguised punishment.

4. The denial of the allowance is not lawful. Fakirbhai is on all fours — a protected workman, a §33(3) proceeding, a prolonged delay, no payment at all. The Supreme Court held that such denial deprives the workman of a reasonable opportunity to defend himself, violates the principles of natural justice, and vitiates the proceedings and the permission granted. Five years is longer than the six-year period in Fakirbhai’s own proceeding in intensity of effect and squarely within its rationale. Add the constitutional dimension: Chandrabhan Tale, (1983) 3 SCC 387, read Article 21 into the suspension rules and struck down a nominal Re. 1 allowance; total denial is a fortiori unconstitutional against a State employer.

5. The rate — the default rule. Ram Lakhan: “the right of the Management to suspend and the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance are intertwined and both must survive together”; where the standing orders/service rules are silent — as here — the workman is entitled to full salary for the whole period of suspension, and not to a reduced or nominal amount. The Court directed arrears within three months of production of a certified copy of the order.

6. Remedy and strategy. (i) Challenge the permission order (and any award upholding it) on the ground that the proceeding was vitiated for want of the allowance — a ground of natural justice, available in the writ jurisdiction under Article 226 and in appeal; (ii) claim the arrears of the allowance/full salary through the §33 proceeding or by a separate application for recovery (a claim of “money due” under §33-C(2) where the amount is ascertained); (iii) if the Tribunal has not yet decided the §33(3) application, apply at once for an interim direction for payment of the allowance — the power is expressly recognised in §33(5)-type proceedings and, under the IRC, is codified in §50(2); and (iv) if a dismissal ultimately follows, resist it, for the permission was obtained in a vitiated proceeding.

7. Conclusion. Advise the workman that (a) his suspension may stand, (b) the five-year denial of any subsistence allowance is unsustainable and vitiates the permission proceedings, (c) he is entitled to full salary for the entire period because the standing orders are silent, and (d) he should pursue the arrears together with the challenge to the permission order.

F.5 Model answer — the recurring 10-marker: §33(1) versus §33(2)

Question

“Distinguish between §33(1) and §33(2) of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947. What is the consequence of non-compliance in each case?”

Answer — skeleton in full

1. Common field, divided control. Both sub-sections operate only “during the pendency of a proceeding in respect of an industrial dispute”. They divide the employer’s acts into those connected with the dispute and those not connected, and attach different controls to each.

2. §33(1) — connected matters require prior permission. Two prohibitions: (a) altering, to the prejudice of the workmen concerned, the conditions of service applicable immediately before the commencement of the proceeding; (b) discharging or punishing them for misconduct connected with the dispute — unless the authority grants express permission in writing. Permission is a condition precedent; the authority’s jurisdiction is wider, since it is asked to decide whether the employer should be allowed to act at all.

3. §33(2) — unconnected matters require subsequent approval. The employer may act in accordance with the standing orders (or the contract, where there are none), but for a dismissal or discharge the proviso requires one month’s wages to be paid and an application for approval to be made; and the approval application must be decided within three months (§33(5)). The enquiry is limited to a prima facie case, and the authority cannot impose conditions (Lord Krishna Textile Mills). The three requisites were stated in Lalla Ram.

4. Consequence of non-compliance — §33(1). The action is a contravention: it grounds a §33-A complaint by the aggrieved employee (adjudicated as if it were a referred dispute), exposes the employer to the penalty in §31, and on the merits is likely to be set aside. The workman may also rely on the contravention to show victimisation in the main reference.

5. Consequence of non-compliance — §33(2). More drastic and self-executing: since the proviso is mandatory, a dismissal without the required application for approval (or after withdrawal of the application) is void and inoperative, and the workman is “deemed never to have been dismissed”: Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank Ltd. v. Ram Gopal Sharma, (2002) 2 SCC 244, not following Punjab Beverages, (1978) 2 SCC 144. Even where approval is granted, a §33-A complaint lies to challenge it on the ground of victimisation; and where the dismissal was unconnected, the workman keeps his full remedy later under §11-A/§50 on the merits.

6. Where §33(3) fits. A protected workman is excluded from the §33(2) route altogether: “Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (2)”, prior permission is required for any action against him, connected or not. This is the strongest tier of protection, and it explains why the three prescribed cases concern protected workmen or §33(1) suspensions.

7. Under the IRC. Sections 33(1), (2), (3) and (5) are re-enacted in §90(1)–(5), and §33-A in §91; the distinction and the consequences survive intact, with the addition of the conciliation-mediation layer in §91.

F.6 Short notes — the five-markers that recur

(a) Complaint under §33-A / §91

Where an employer contravenes §33 during the pendency of a proceeding, “any employee aggrieved by such contravention may make a complaint in writing” — to the conciliation officer/Board (who must take it into account in mediation), or to the arbitrator/Labour Court/Tribunal/National Tribunal, which “shall adjudicate upon the complaint as if it were a dispute referred to or pending before it” and submit its award. It is a direct remedy, bypassing the §10 reference; the authority must nevertheless go into the merits, since contravention alone does not entitle the workman to reinstatement as of right. Under the IRC, §91 preserves it and adds the mediation route.

(b) Protected workman under §33(3)–(4)

A workman who, being “a member of the executive or other office-bearer of a registered trade union connected with the establishment”, is recognised as such under the rules (Rule 61 of the Industrial Disputes (Central) Rules, 1957: nomination by the union, ordinarily by 30 April; recognition mandatory within the cap), and whose number may not exceed 1% of the workmen, subject to a minimum of five and a maximum of one hundred. Against him, no prejudicial alteration of service conditions and no punishment or dismissal is permissible during pendency without the authority’s express permission in writing. It is protection against victimisation, not immunity from discipline.

(c) The implied-suspension doctrine

Hotel Imperial: at common law suspension without pay needs a statutory or contractual basis; but because §33 bars immediate dismissal after a proper enquiry, an Industrial Tribunal may imply a term permitting suspension pending permission, so that the contract of employment is temporarily suspended on both sides. Conditions: (i) proper enquiry; (ii) decision to dismiss; (iii) §33 compulsion. Consequences: permission granted → no wages for the period; permission refused → all wages from the date of suspension. It is qualified by the duty to pay a subsistence allowance (Fakirbhai; Ram Lakhan).

(d) The default rule on subsistence allowance

Ram Lakhan: the allowance is payable at the rate prescribed by the standing orders or service rules; in the absence of any such provision, the workman is entitled to full salary during the period of suspension. The rule is worker-protective in the extreme because most standing orders fix only a fraction of wages — and it gives employers a clear incentive to prescribe a rate. The rationale is that the delay in deciding the §33 application is as much outside the workman’s control as the employer’s, and he “survives only on his salary”.

(e) Interim relief — §10(4) and §50(2) IRC

The Tribunal must confine itself to the points referred “and matters incidental thereto”; interim relief relating to the same matter (e.g., wages pending a reinstatement reference) is such an incidental matter and requires no separate reference (Hotel Imperial). It is not an “interim award” and need not be published under §17 — but it should not be the whole of the final relief. The IRC now codifies the power in §50(2): the Tribunal “may, in the interest of justice, grant such interim relief as it may consider necessary”.

(f) “One month’s wages” — the small clause with large consequences

The proviso to §33(2)(b) requires that the workman be paid wages for one month before discharge/dismissal, in addition to the application for approval. The object is to give the workman immediate means pending the approval decision — a legislative recognition of the same need that Fakirbhai addressed for suspensions. Failure to pay (or a short payment, e.g. omitting due increments) can lead to refusal of approval; and, with Jaipur Zila, a failure to comply with the proviso makes the dismissal vulnerable to being treated as void and inoperative.

F.8 Answer-writing technique for this unit

Do

  • Classify first: connected or unconnected? protected or not? That classification decides the answer.
  • State the requirement precisely: “express permission in writing” for §33(1)/(3); “wages for one month and an application for approval” for §33(2)(b); “three months” for §33(5).
  • Use the trilogy in order: Hotel Imperial (may suspend) → Fakirbhai (must pay something) → Ram Lakhan (must pay everything, if the rules are silent).
  • Quote the two sentences that carry the unit: “the right of the Management to suspend and the right of the employee to receive subsistence allowance are intertwined and both must survive together”; and from Fakirbhai, “such denial leads to violation of principles of natural justice and consequently vitiates the proceedings”.
  • Bring in Article 21 and Chandrabhan Tale — examiners reward the constitutional dimension.
  • End with the IRC: §90/§91, and the fact that the allowance doctrine remains uncodified.

Do not

  • Do not say “no wages are payable during suspension”. The correct statement is: full wages are not payable, but a subsistence allowance is — and full salary if the rules are silent.
  • Do not cite Hotel Imperial alone. That is the commonest Unit-6 error and gives only half the answer.
  • Do not reverse §33(1) and §33(2). Connected = prior permission; unconnected = subsequent approval.
  • Do not say a protected workman cannot be dismissed at all. He can — with prior permission; the protection is procedural.
  • Do not forget §33(5)’s three months, or the first proviso’s power of extension.
  • Do not misnumber the IRC provisions: it is §90 and §91 (not “§82/§83”).

G.4 Glossary — the vocabulary of this unit

Permission

Approval in advance of the employer’s action; required by §33(1) (connected matters) and §33(3) (protected workmen). Its absence is a contravention. The authority’s jurisdiction is wider than in an approval enquiry (Lord Krishna Textile Mills).

Approval

Post facto sanction for a dismissal/discharge already effected under §33(2)(b); requires one month’s wages and an application, decided prima facie and within three months. Non-approval (or no application) → the dismissal is void (Jaipur Zila).

Protected workman

A union executive member or office-bearer recognised under the rules, capped at 1% of the establishment’s workmen (min. 5, max. 100), who may not be punished or prejudicially altered during pendency without prior permission. In the IRC, “protected worker”.

Subsistence allowance

The reduced payment made to a suspended employee to keep body and soul together; “subsistence” means “means of supporting life, especially a minimum livelihood” (O.P. Gupta). Payable from the date of suspension; full salary where the rules are silent.

Implied term

The contractual term read in by Industrial Tribunals to make the statutory scheme workable — first to permit suspension without full wages (Hotel Imperial), then to compel a reasonable allowance (Fakirbhai). Note the same technique used twice, in opposite directions.

Interim relief

Payment ordered during the pendency of the reference as a matter “incidental” to it under §10(4); not an “interim award” requiring publication under §17. It “should not be the whole relief” claimed finally (Hotel Imperial). Under the IRC, §50(2).

Contravention

Any breach of §33 — acting without permission, dismissing without the proviso’s compliance, or acting against a protected workman without permission. It grounds a §33-A complaint and a §31 prosecution.

“Connected with the dispute”

The dividing line between §33(1) and §33(2) — nowhere defined in the Act. Judicially it means that the action would touch the subject-matter of the pending reference: the same workmen, the same incidents, or the matter under adjudication.

G.5 Examiner’s traps — the eight mistakes that cost marks

Traps
  1. Reversing the sub-sections. §33(1) = connected = prior permission; §33(2) = unconnected = subsequent approval. Getting this backwards destroys the answer.
  2. “No wages during suspension.” Wrong. No full wages — but a subsistence allowance is mandatory; and if the standing orders are silent, the entitlement is to full salary.
  3. Citing Hotel Imperial alone. It answers the suspension question, not the allowance question. Always complete the trilogy.
  4. Forgetting that a protected workman needs prior permission even for unconnected misconduct — the §33(2) approval route is not available against him.
  5. Overlooking the “one month’s wages” requirement — a small clause that decides many approval cases.
  6. Treating a contravention as automatically entitling the workman to reinstatement. No: the §33-A complaint is adjudicated on the merits; §11-A/§50 then governs justification and punishment.
  7. Missing §33(5)’s three months (and the deliberate provisos that prevent the proceeding from lapsing).
  8. Mislabelling the IRC provisions as “§82/§83”. They are §90 (restraint) and §91 (complaint).

A closing note for the reader

Unit 6 is a small section with an unusually large footprint. Section 33 is only five sub-sections long, yet it decided the fate of every dismissal during a pending dispute for seventy years, and it produced the one doctrine of Indian industrial law that every service-lawyer knows by heart — the right of a suspended employee to a subsistence allowance. The unit teaches a single lesson in three movements. Hotel Imperial told the employer that the old common-law position had been replaced, but generously read the statute to imply a power of suspension — while insisting that the moment the permission was refused, the wages for the whole period followed. Fakirbhai then held that the same implied term could not be used to starve a workman out of his defence, since a reasonable allowance is inseparable from a reasonable opportunity. And Ram Lakhan settled the apparent clash by refusing to see one at all: the employer’s right to suspend and the employee’s right to be paid while suspended are “intertwined and both must survive together”. Read together with §33-A — the workman’s own complaint, adjudicated as though it were a referred dispute — the section emerges not as a technical restraint on management, but as the statutory guarantee that a pending dispute will be decided on its merits and not by attrition.
