Meaning & Scope of Victimology [Syllabus limb (i)]

Definitions · history · contents · theories · classifications — the conceptual foundation

1.1 Definitions — What is "Victimology"? Who is a "Victim"?

Core definition (Karmen, adopted by Paranjape — LEARN VERBATIM):
"Victimology is the scientific study of victimisation, including the relationships between victims and offenders, the interactions between victims and the criminal justice system (police, courts, correctional officials), and the connections between victims and other social groups and institutions — such as the media, businesses and social movements."

📖 Separovic's synthesis (quoted by Raina in DU material)

"Victims are persons threatened, injured or destroyed by an act or omission of another man (man, structure, organisation or institution)." → Separovic interprets crime = violation of basic human rights (life, health, security, well-being); victimisation must be evaluated as human-rights violation of the individual.

🌐 UN Declaration 40/34, Arts. 1–2 (1985) — the international definition

Art. 1: "Victims" = persons who, individually or collectively, have suffered harm — physical or mental injury, emotional suffering, economic loss or substantial impairment of fundamental rights — through acts/omissions violating criminal laws (including laws on criminal abuse of power).
Art. 2: A person is a victim regardless of whether the offender is identified, apprehended, prosecuted or convicted, and regardless of family relationship; includes immediate family/dependents and persons harmed while intervening to help.

⚖️ Indian statutory definition — BNSS s. 2(1)(y) [old CrPC s. 2(wa), inserted 2008, w.e.f. 31-12-2009]

"Victim means a person who has suffered any loss or injury caused by reason of the act or omission for which the accused person has been charged, and the expression includes his or her guardian or legal heir." Exam note India's definition is narrower than the UN's (it ties victimhood to a charge against an accused; UN covers unidentified offenders too — the gap is partly filled by BNSS s. 396(4), compensation even where offender is not traced).

🧭 Elias Neumann (Latin America, 1984 — in Raina)

Drew attention to victims of the legal system itself — victims of police brutality, torture, non-existence of legal assistance. This expands victimology beyond conventional crime to abuse-of-power victimisation — the foundation of India's public-law compensation cases (Rudul Shah → Nilabati → D.K. Basu).

1.2 Historical Evolution — From Private Vengeance to Victim Justice

Raina ("Early history of victimology", DU material pp. 245–246) + Paranjape trace five phases:

Phase 1 · Pre-organised society — Private vengeance
People took law into their own hands; unrestricted retaliation. Victim (or clan) was the sole avenger — no State, no procedure.
Phase 2 · Clan/tribal society — Collective responsibility + Composition
Victimisation became a collective (clan) concern; to reduce feuding, offenders with surplus wealth offered payment to victims as compensation — formalised in the Code of Hammurabi; parallels: Greek death-fines, early Hebrew/Hindu/Turkish fines, Roman Twelve Tables, Law of Moses. Victim was central and compensated.
Phase 3 · Rise of the State — The victim is "eclipsed"
The State took over the exclusive right to punish; crime became an injury to the State, not to the person. The victim shrank to a mere witness/informant — "the forgotten person in criminal justice administration". Retribution/deterrence theories spoke only of the offender.
Phase 4 · 1940s–1960s — Birth of victimology as science
Mendelsohn, Von Hentig, Wolfgang founded victimology (initially "victim precipitation" studies). By the late 1960s the emerging science "significantly boosted the victims' status" (Raina). Criminal-injuries compensation boards appeared (UK 1964; many US states).
Phase 5 · 1980s–today — Rights-based victimology
Women's rights movement + human-rights jurisprudence widened the field; feminists attacked "victim-blaming"; UN Declaration 40/34 (1985) globalised victim rights; India: compensation jurisprudence (1983→), CrPC amendments 2008 (2(wa), 357A, 372 proviso) & 2013 (357B, 357C, 326A/B IPC, 166B), NALSA Scheme 2018, and now BNSS 395–398 (2024).

1.3 Scope & Contents of Victimology

The 7 broad contents (Paranjape) — draw as a wheel in exams

2️⃣ Victim–offender relationship
3️⃣ Victim–criminal justice interface

5️⃣ Victims vs societal relations
6️⃣ Victims & costs of crime
7️⃣ Compensatory redress for victims ⭐ (core of syllabus limb iv)
  • Modern scope is multi-disciplinary — law + sociology + psychology + medicine + economics + media studies; professionals include judges, lawyers, policymakers, doctors, social workers (Paranjape).
  • Not confined to crime-victims: covers victims of traffic hazards, natural disasters, war crimes, abuse of power, corruption (Paranjape); accident/flood/famine/tsunami/blast victims share the common element of suffering from forces beyond control.
  • Raina's Indian caution: in India victimology is still understood "more as a method of sentencing the perpetrator than of restituting the victim" — it must grow into (a) compensating victims of conventional crime and (b) compensating victims of abuse of power.

Classifications of victims (quote any one + examples)

Von Hentig (1948) — 5 types

  1. Innocent — wrong place, wrong time.
  2. Depressive — careless, unsuspecting, easy target.
  3. Greedy — duped due to craze for money/easy gain (e.g., Ponzi-scam investors).
  4. Wanton — vulnerable to life-stresses: juveniles, pregnant women, prostitutes.
  5. Tormentor — attacked by the target of his own abuse (e.g., batterer beaten by battered partner).

Abdel Fattah (Canadian) — 5 categories

  1. Non-participating — wholly innocent: foeticide (BNS equivalent of IPC 315/316), kidnapping, infanticide, geronticide.
  2. Latent — victimised but unaware (blackmail networks).
  3. Provocative — provoked into self-harm (abetment of suicide, dowry death).
  4. Participating — sati, devadasi, prostitution, some cyber-crimes.
  5. Defiant/retaliating — resists but fails: rape, robbery, dacoity, domestic violence.

Role of the Victim in Criminal Justice Administration [Syllabus limb (ii)]

Forgotten person → participant · stage-wise role · UN Declaration 40/34 · restorative justice · media

2.1 The journey: from "forgotten person" to rights-holder

😶 Traditional position: victim = only informant + witness for the State; no say in investigation, withdrawal, sentencing; conviction gives only "mental satisfaction", no relief (Paranjape; Schafer 1968).
▼ reform levers ▼
📜 UN Declaration 40/34 (1985)
⚖️ SC compensation jurisprudence (1983→)
✏️ CrPC amendments 2008 + 2013
🆕 BNSS 2023 (Ss. 395–398)
▼
🧍‍♀️ Emerging position: victim = participant with rights — to report, be informed, be protected, be heard (counsel), claim compensation, appeal, and be treated with dignity.

Why the victim stays marginal — 8 reasons for non-reporting (K.D. Gaur, in Paranjape)

  1. Public apathy/indifference
  2. Petty/insignificant effect
  3. Offender unknown
  4. Fear of threat/harassment by culprit
  5. Social stigma — especially rape, abortion, sexual offences
  6. Cost/time of prolonged litigation
  7. Witness reluctance/hostility
  8. No faith in police action

Consequence: victim apathy encourages criminality → victimologists must fix the victim's access barriers to justice agencies.

2.3 UN Declaration of Basic Principles of Justice for Victims of Crime & Abuse of Power (GA Res. 40/34, 29 Nov 1985)

Reading 1 Adopted on the report of the 7th UN Congress (Milan, 1985); India's 2008/2013 amendments and BNSS 395–398 are its domestic transplants. Structure it in answers as A → B:

A. Victims of Crime (paras 4–17)

  1. Access to justice & fair treatment (4–7): treat victims with compassion & respect for dignity; access to justice + prompt redress; create/strengthen judicial & administrative mechanisms; inform victims of rights, role, scope, timing of proceedings; allow views & concerns at appropriate stages; provide proper (legal) assistance; minimise inconvenience, protect privacy & safety (incl. family/witnesses) from intimidation/retaliation; avoid unnecessary delay; use informal mechanisms (mediation, arbitration, customary justice) where appropriate.
  2. Restitution (8–11): offenders/third parties should make fair restitution — return of property, payment for harm/loss, reimbursement of expenses, provision of services, restoration of rights; restitution as a sentencing option; environmental restitution (restore environment, infrastructure, relocation costs); where public officials violated criminal law, the State pays restitution (successor State liable if regime gone).
  3. Compensation (12–13): where not fully available from offender, States shall provide financial compensation for (a) significant bodily injury/impairment of physical or mental health; (b) family/dependents of deceased or incapacitated victims; establish/strengthen national funds.
  4. Assistance (14–17): material, medical, psychological & social assistance; inform victims of services; train police, justice, health, social-service personnel; special attention to victims with special needs (children, women, disabled).

B. Victims of Abuse of Power (paras 18–21)

Victims = those harmed by acts/omissions not yet criminal under national law but violating international human-rights norms; States should enact norms proscribing abuses of power with remedies of restitution/compensation + material, medical, psychological, social support; negotiate multilateral treaties; report periodically. → India's counterpart: PHR Act 1993 + constitutional-tort compensation (Rudul Shah line).

2.4 Impact of victimisation & secondary victimisation

💔 Physical

Hyper-tension, numbness, grief; "fight or flight" response; immediate or delayed injuries (knives/firearms fatal); some permanent, some healable; delayed shock/trauma on memory return.

💸 Financial

Medical costs; property damage; litigation costs; loss of earnings/employment; funeral expenses; relocation costs; destroyed family/marital life (esp. rape survivors).

🧠 Psychological

Fear, anxiety, shame, self-blame, anger → PTSD (Ferraro 1995); alcoholism, drug abuse, social withdrawal; worse when victim is disbelieved or blamed ("walked alone", "dressed attractively"); repeat victimisation traps (esp. rape).

🔁 Secondary victimisation — victimisation by the system (Paranjape; Raina on media)

  • Primary = the crime itself; secondary = fresh trauma from police (hostile questioning, delay), courts (tiresome procedure, manipulated evidence, acquittal shock), hospitals (treatment withheld till police arrive), society (blame, ostracism), media (sensational reporting, identity exposure).
  • Worst when offender = protector-turned-perpetrator (custodial torture, police atrocities, jail/orphanage abuse) — shatters trust, hard to prove.
  • Collective-violence victims (riots, blasts, terrorism) suffer prolonged trauma + displacement till resettlement.
  • Raina: media must wield power "judiciously and responsibly" and, like the State, preserve social order — directly tested via Nipun Saxena (identity bans).

2.5 Restorative justice & the victim-assistance programme

⚖️ Restorative justice — "paradigm shift" (Paranjape)

Beyond retributive/preventive models: involves offender + victim + community to balance punishment with victim-rights protection. Compensatory relief = key feature. Four themes:

🌱 Restoration
victim restored to normal life whether or not offender is caught; offender acknowledges harm & redresses it
🤝 Accountability
victim–offender mediation (France 1993, Germany 1994); guilt acceptance soothes victim
🛡️ Community protection
community surveillance reforms offender; community support returns victim to normal life
🧰 Skill development
vocational training for offender and victim; competency programmes
UN Handbook on Justice for Victims (1999) — Assistance Programme: objectives, Advisory Group, services
  • Objectives: govts + NGOs assist restoration/rehabilitation; help beyond immediate injury, through the aftermath; expand victim participation in justice process; build community support; serve under-served victims.
  • Advisory Group (per area): police, prosecution, health, psychologists, academics, local representatives — representative (gender, age, region, religion, class); functions: collect victimisation data, surveys, trigger relief on police/medical/media reports, liaise with NGOs, represent victims in process — with confidentiality, no identity disclosure.
  • 8 services: crisis intervention (medical aid, food, shelter, safety, money) → counselling → advocacy → support during investigation → during trial → after disposal → violence prevention → offender–victim mediation.
Comparative snapshots — France · USA · others (Paranjape)
  • France (partie civile): injured party impleaded from investigation stage; can move court against delay/distortion; supplements evidence; resists unjustified withdrawal/closure; welfare bodies may implead for rape/child victims.
  • USA: victim-opportunity to ventilate cause before trial court; National Crime Victimization Survey guides prevention; Payne v. Tennessee (1991) — victim rights at sentencing; Victim Impact Panels (victim meets convicted offender for restoration).
  • Continental consensus: two basic rights — (i) participate (know, be heard, help find truth) + (ii) compensation + interim relief. (Belgium, Canada, Germany, UK, Japan, NZ, SA, Mexico — Mexico even constitutionalised victim rights.)
  • SASCV (South Asian Society of Criminology & Victimology, est. Feb 2011): 8 SAARC-region states share best practices; restorative justice; training; victim-sensitive policy.

2.6 Prescribed-reading capsules — Zender & Makkar/Friday (how to cite them)

📚 Reading 2 — Lucia Zender, "Victims", Oxford Handbook of Criminology (2nd ed. 1997)

  • Thesis: the late-20th-century "rediscovery" of the victim — victim moved from margin to centre of criminology, policy and politics.
  • Drivers she analyses: (i) victimisation surveys (UK British Crime Survey from 1982 — exposed the "dark figure" of unreported crime and unequal risks); (ii) feminist campaigns on rape/domestic violence (redefined private harms as public crimes; attacked victim-blaming); (iii) left realism (crime hits the poor hardest — take victims seriously without abandoning social justice); (iv) victim-support movement & criminal-injuries compensation (UK 1964).
  • Critical edge (use for evaluation marks): warns against the political use of "the victim" — idealised victims invoked to justify punitiveness; victims' interests do not automatically equal harsher punishment (many want recognition, repair, prevention); risks of responsibilisation (making citizens bear crime-prevention burdens) and of zero-sum thinking (victim rights vs offenders' rights).
  • Exam line: "As Zender cautions, victim-centred reform must empower victims through rights and redress — not merely instrumentalise them to legitimise punitiveness."

📚 Reading 3 — Makkar & Friday (eds.), Global Perspectives in Victimology (1993, pp. 147–163 portion)

  • Thesis: comparative/global victimology — victimisation patterns, victim movements and redress models differ across societies but converge on the UN Declaration's core (access, restitution, compensation, assistance).
  • Themes in the prescribed pages: cross-national victim surveys and risk distribution; growth of victim-assistance and compensation schemes worldwide; victims of abuse of power in developing/transitional societies; need to adapt global standards to local justice institutions (customary, community-based mechanisms alongside courts).
  • Exam line: "The global-perspectives literature (Makkar & Friday) shows victimology is no longer a Western import but a universal template — India's trajectory from Hammurabi-style composition to BNSS victim-compensation schemes mirrors that global arc."

Victims' Rights & Remedies in India [Syllabus limb (iii) · Raina's "Indian experience" i–vi]

Constitution · statutes · public-law compensation · judicial trend — the remedial architecture

3.1 Constitutional mandate (Raina §i)

🏛️ Fundamental Rights + DPSP

  • Art. 21 — life & personal liberty "except according to procedure established by law" → SC read into it: right to live with dignity, rape as Art-21 violation (Bodhisattwa), right to compensation for State violations (Rudul Shah → Nilabati → D.K. Basu).
  • Art. 14 — equality before law / equal protection → available to "persons" including foreigners (Chandrima Das).
  • Art. 32 / 226 — writ remedy; compensation as public-law relief ("monetary amends").
  • Art. 38 — State to promote welfare of the people; Art. 39A — free legal aid; Art. 41 — assistance in cases of disablement/undeserved want.

📌 Raina's rule

"When an individual's fundamental or legal rights are violated because of the callous attitude of the custodians of law, the State shall be held responsible and courts should not hesitate in granting compensation in appropriate cases."

Exam use Open any "abuse of power" answer with Art. 21 + this rule, then march through Rudul Shah → PUDR → Nilabati → D.K. Basu.

⚠️ Raina's critique — why statutes alone fail (quote in answers)

  • Victim/dependents have no legal right to claim compensation from offender or State under these provisions — courts act only within the framework.
  • Courts rarely use fine/compensation powers because (i) offender seen as unable to pay; (ii) judges won't keep files open amid workload (41st Law Commission Report flagged this rigidity).
  • Under old s. 357, no fine → no compensation (gap cured by 357(3)/395(3) direct-compensation power + 357A/396 State fund).

3.3 Compensatory jurisprudence — two tracks (Raina §v)

🟢 Track A — Victims of (conventional) crime

  • Hari Kishan v. Sukhbir Singh, AIR 1988 SC 2127 — all criminal courts must exercise s. 357 liberally; compensation reassures the victim she is "not forgotten"; reconciles victim with offender; amount must be reasonable.
  • Bodhisattwa Gautam v. Subhra Chakraborty, AIR 1996 SC 922 — rape on false promise of marriage + forced abortions → SC ordered interim maintenance/compensation during trial, even before conviction; rape court can award interim + final compensation.
  • Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum v. UoI, (1995) 1 SCC 14 — rape = offence against basic human rights + Art. 21; compensation jurisdiction is part of the overall jurisdiction of the rape-trial court; guidelines (legal representation, anonymity, Criminal Injuries Board).
  • State of Gujarat v. Hon'ble High Court of Gujarat, (1998) 7 SCC 392 — victims/families compensated from wages earned by prisoners (via fund, respecting sensibilities).

🔴 Track B — Victims of abuse of power (constitutional tort)

  • Rudul Shah v. State of Bihar, AIR 1983 SC 1086 — ₹35,000 for 14-year illegal detention; Art. 21 "denuded of content" if courts could only order release; State must repair damage, may recover from officers.
  • PUDR v. State of Bihar, AIR 1987 SC 355 — unwarranted police firing (21 dead incl. children) → ₹20,000 per death as working principle (not absolution).
  • Nilabati Behara v. State of Orissa, AIR 1993 SC 1960 — custodial death → ₹1 lakh "monetary amends"; sovereign immunity inapplicable; Art. 32 obliges SC to forge new tools; cites ICCPR Art. 9(5).
  • D.K. Basu v. State of W.B., AIR 1997 SC 610 — compensation is "sometimes perhaps the only suitable remedy"; State vicariously liable, indemnified by wrongdoer; award is compensatory not punitive; without prejudice to civil suit/criminal prosecution; quantum per facts.
  • Also: Bhim Singh (₹50,000, malicious arrest of MLA); SAHELI (₹75,000, 9-yr-old beaten to death in custody); Inder Singh v. Punjab (1995) 3 SCC 702 (torture by Punjab police — State pays for failure to protect).

4.1 BNSS s. 395 — Order to pay compensation [CrPC 357]

Bare-text essence: "When a Court imposes a sentence of fine, or a sentence (including death) of which fine forms a part, the Court may, when passing judgment, order the whole or any part of the fine recovered to be applied for the four statutory purposes below; and where no fine is imposed, it may still order the accused to pay compensation directly [395(3)]."

4.1.1 The four application-heads of s. 395(1)

💼 (a) Prosecution expenses
defraying expenses properly incurred in prosecution
🩹 (b) Loss/injury compensation
to any person for loss/injury by the offence, where recoverable in civil court ⭐ most-used head
🏺 (c) Fatal-Accidents dependents
where offence caused death (or abetment thereof) — compensation to persons entitled under the Fatal Accidents Act, 1855
🍚 (d) Bona-fide purchaser refund
where offence involves theft / misappropriation / breach of trust / cheating — refund to innocent buyer

4.1.2 Sub-sections (2)–(5): the operating rules

  • 395(2): if fine imposed in a case appealable, no compensation order until appeal period over / appeal decided (protects accused from irreversible payout).
  • 395(3) ⭐: where Court imposes a sentence of which fine does NOT form part, it may still order the accused to pay compensation to the victim of loss/injury. → Rachhpal Singh turns on this: High Court gave only life imprisonment (no fine) → compensation was validly awarded under 357(3)/395(3).
  • 395(4): Appellate / Sessions / High Court (revision) may equally make 395(1)/(3) orders — compensation jurisdiction travels upward.
  • 395(5): sums paid/recovered under 395 are taken into account in any later civil suit for the same matter (no double recovery).

4.1.3 Judicially-evolved principles for s. 395 (cite these!)

PrincipleAuthority
Liberal exercise — every criminal court should use s. 395 to meet the ends of justice; reassures victim she is "not forgotten"; reconciles victim & offender; constructive step forward.Hari Kishan v. Sukhbir Singh, AIR 1988 SC 2127 (Shetty, J.) — quoted in Jacob George
Quantum must be reasonable — depends on facts of each case; consider capacity of accused to pay (object is collection + payment, not default imprisonment).Hari Kishan; Palaniappa Gounder v. State of T.N. (1977) 2 SCC 634; Sarwan Singh v. Punjab (1978) 4 SCC 111 (both cited in Rachhpal)

4.2 BNSS s. 396 — Victim Compensation Scheme [CrPC 357A + 357B]

Legislative history (one line for exams): Recommended by the 154th Law Commission + Malimath Committee → inserted as CrPC 357A by Act 5 of 2009 (w.e.f. 31-12-2009) → carried into BNSS s. 396; CrPC 357B (Criminal Law Amendment 2013, Verma Committee) → BNSS s. 396(7).

4.2.1 Sub-section walkthrough

Sub-sectionContentExam significance
396(1)Every State Govt, in coordination with Centre, shall prepare a scheme providing funds for victims/dependents who suffered loss/injury and require rehabilitation.Mandatory ("shall"); each State notifies its own VCS with slabs (e.g., Delhi VCS; NALSA 2018 model for sexual offences).
396(2)On court recommendation, DLSA/SLSA decides the quantum under the scheme.Court recommends → Legal Services Authority quantifies & disburses.

4.3 BNSS s. 397 — Treatment of victims [CrPC 357C]

Bare-text essence: "All hospitals, public or private — Central/State/local-body or any other person — shall immediately provide first-aid or medical treatment, free of cost, to victims of offences under BNS ss. 64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 70, 71, 124(1) (rape & aggravated sexual offences + acid attack) and POCSO ss. 4, 6, 8, 10, and shall immediately inform the police."
  • Origin: Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 (Verma Committee) → CrPC 357C → BNSS 397 (now expressly extended to POCSO victims).
  • No-refusal rule (Laxmi): no hospital/clinic may refuse pleading lack of speciality — stabilise + first-aid first, then refer; private hospitals bound equally (Laxmi: State to arrange if private hospital defaults).
  • Teeth: refusal/contravention → BNS s. 200 (IPC 166B) — punishment for non-treatment of victim by person in charge of hospital.
  • Companion duties: examination with consent, within 24 hrs for rape (BNSS 184); two-finger test banned (Rajesh v. Haryana).

4.4 The surrounding sections — 398, 399, 400 + BNS fine provisions

🛡️ BNSS 398 — Witness Protection (new)

Every State shall prepare & notify a Witness Protection Scheme — threat categorisation, protection measures, identity protection, relocation, special deposition. Gives statute-book status to Mahender Chawla v. UoI (2018). Victim-witnesses in rape, terror, organised-crime trials are prime beneficiaries.

💵 BNS fine-to-victim provisions (feeds 396(7))

  • BNS 124(1) (acid attack): fine shall be just & reasonable to meet medical expenses, paid to victim.
  • BNS 65(2)/70(2) (rape/gang-rape of minor): fine with victim-payment direction.
  • BNS 72 (identity disclosure) + BNS 73 (court-proceeding publication): punish media/breach — the Nipun Saxena shield.
🧮 395 vs 396 — the classic distinction (learn as a table): (1) Source: offender's fine (395) vs State fund (396) · (2) Trigger: conviction needed (395) vs acquittal/discharge/untraced also covered (396) · (3) Decider: trial/appellate court itself (395) vs court recommends + DLSA/SLSA quantifies (396) · (4) Purpose: recompense for proved loss (395) vs rehabilitation (396) · (5) Cumulative: 396(7) expressly adds State money over BNS fines — no set-off.

DU Case 19 · DU material pp. 253–256

State of Punjab v. Ajaib Singh — AIR 1995 SC 975 : (1995) 2 SCC 486

Bench: R.M. Sahai, J. · Appeal against acquittal (Art. 136) · Ss. 302 IPC, 100 IPC (private defence), compensation despite acquittal

Facts (as found by Courts below)

  • Night of 16-12-1976, ~11 PM, G.T. Road before Sat Kartar Cold Storage, Phagwara: two Punjab Traffic Police groups clashed over authority to check trucks. Sub-Inspector Ajaib Singh (respondent) fired 3 shots from his service revolver — killing ASI Gurnam Singh and Constable Paramjit Singh; third shot hit a cycle-shop shutter. Time/date/place and the firing itself were undisputed.
  • Prosecution version: deliberate, unjustified, cold-blooded murder (deceased caught/held, shot point-blank).
  • Defence version: Ajaib Singh, tipped by a truck driver that men in police uniform were extorting truck drivers, reached the spot, challenged ASI Gurnam Singh who did not disclose identity and moved suspiciously; grappling followed; Ajaib Singh suffered 9 danda-blow injuries (held not self-inflicted) from 3 companions → fired in private defence under s. 100 IPC, clauses I & II (reasonable apprehension of death/grievous hurt).
  • Trial Court: disbelieved prosecution's origin-story (accepted defence version of origin; unexplained FIR delay — constables allegedly hiding in fields on a winter night; no rojnamacha entry for "nakabandi"), but convicted u/s 302 (life + s. 87 Arms Act 2 yrs) holding right of private defence exceeded (dandas vs firearm). Co-accused Balbir Kumar → probation (s. 383); Jit Singh acquitted.
  • High Court: dismissed State revision for death sentence; acquitted Ajaib Singh — deceased party was illegally checking trucks/extorting; forensic evidence showed shots from deceased's revolver too; apprehension of death was reasonable; firing within s. 100 IPC.
  • Before SC: counsel for respondent (R.K. Garg) offered ₹5 lakh as genuine remorse (not cover/excuse) given 18-year delay; State counsel refused money, pressed for conviction as cold-blooded murder.

Issues

  1. Whether the High Court's acquittal was palpably erroneous/perverse warranting Art. 136 interference?
  2. Whether the respondent exceeded the right of private defence (dandas vs firearm)?
  3. Whether monetary compensation to victims' families was appropriate despite acquittal?

Arguments

State (appellant)

  • Cold-blooded murder; private defence exceeded — danda attack cannot justify firearm use.
  • Deceased's revolver found holstered inside woollen overcoat — clinching proof he never fired; forensic opinion be ignored.
  • Refused compensation — wanted conviction, not money.

Respondent (Ajaib Singh)

  • No previous enmity (found by trial court) — mistaken-identity clash the deceased provoked.
  • Even trial court accepted right of private defence existed; 9 real injuries → reasonable apprehension of death; ss. 100(I)–(II) don't mandate weapon-parity.
  • Delay of ~18 years; offered ₹5 lakh from genuine remorse to avoid further litigation.

Decision

  • Appeal dismissed. Acquittal-view was a possible view — appellate court against acquittal interferes only if view impossible; even accepting the holster fact and ignoring forensic opinion, the finding was neither perverse nor infirm.
  • s. 100 IPC test: weapon-parity is not the law; question is whether assault could cause reasonable apprehension that death would otherwise ensue — 3 men with dandas + 9 injuries satisfied it.
  • Compensation: respondent to deposit ₹5 lakh in one month with HC Registrar — ₹3.5L to ASI Gurnam Singh's dependents + ₹1.5L to Constable Paramjit Singh's dependents.
⭐ Ratio / principle evolved: (1) Victim compensation is decoupled from conviction — the Court can direct substantial compensation to victims' families even while upholding acquittal, where the incident is admitted and justice to victims demands it. (2) Speedy-trial concern: 18-year time-lag deprecated — speedy trial/disposal is the sine qua non of criminal jurisprudence (2017 PYQ answer quotes this). (3) Private-defence proportionality = reasonable apprehension, not weapon-matching.
📝 Use in: "compensation without conviction" (with BNSS 396(3)); delay/trauma of victims; Raina's "broader interpretation of victim". Pair with Jacob George (rehabilitation over incarceration).
DU Case 20 · DU material pp. 257–262

Dr. Jacob George v. State of Kerala — (1994) 3 SCC 430

Bench: B.L. Hansaria, J. · Ss. 312/314 IPC, MTP Act 1971, s. 357 CrPC · Sentence vs compensation

Facts

  • Deceased Thankamani (deserted-then-reconciled wife; mother of one son) became pregnant again and sought abortion. Through PW1 (relative) she reached appellant's clinic at Nilambur (appellant = homeopath, no surgical training).
  • 14–15 Jan 1987: admitted; ₹600 agreed (₹500 + balance); taken to theatre ~10 PM on 15.1.1987; declared "successful" at midnight though unconscious; brief rally at 5 AM (16th), then sinking, froth, death. Post-mortem: uterus perforated by crude use of scientific gadgets — "daring, crude and criminal" (HC).
  • Trial (Asst. Sessions Judge): acquitted — charges not proved beyond reasonable doubt (PWs 3–4 nurses hostile).
  • High Court: State appeal + suo motu revision → convicted under s. 314 IPC (death caused by act done with intent to cause miscarriage, with woman's consent — max 10 yrs); RI 4 years + ₹5,000 fine (₹4,000 to children); refused probation.

Issues

  1. Whether conviction u/s 314 IPC was sustainable (PW1 sole reliance, hostile witnesses, defence of crude-stick abortion by PW1)?
  2. Whether benefit of Probation of Offenders Act (Madras HC Manickam Pillai cited) should be granted?
  3. What is the proper sentence — purposes of punishment vs compensation/rehabilitation of the victim's minor son (s. 357)?

Arguments

Appellant (Dr. Jacob George — Sh. Jain)

  • Conviction rests only on PW1; PWs 3–4 hostile → no corroboration.
  • Defence: PW1 himself caused abortion crudely (stick/rod); deceased brought for emergency care.
  • Sentence: ~2 months already undergone — reduce to period undergone; alternatively probation (to save practice via POA Act s. 12).

State

  • PW1 (near relative, central actor) fully reliable; PW5 (clinic employee) admitted 14th admission; autopsy corroborates (perforation, ~36-hr timeline).
  • Defence demolished: a clinic-head facing medico-legal emergency would inform police — failure "speaks volumes".
  • Quack surgery killing a consenting woman deserves custodial + compensatory response; probation inappropriate.

Decision

  • Conviction upheld — case not covered by any MTP Act 1971 exception (health/humanitarian/eugenic grounds); Court expressly left open whether a right to abortion inheres in Art. 21.
  • Probation refused: homeopath perforating uterus ≠ trained surgeon's error of judgment; practice must suffer for the "unwanted act".
  • Sentence remodelled — the famous four-purpose analysis: retribution (served by conviction's blow to practice; s. 314 carries no death penalty) + prevention (2 months = eye-opener) + deterrence (conviction + jail stigma deters quacks) + reformation (2 months behind bars taught trauma) → substantive sentence reduced to period already undergone (~2 months).
  • Fine enhanced ₹5,000 → ₹1,00,000 (6 months to deposit; on default, HC sentence revives): invoking s. 357 + Hari Kishan ("reassure the victim she is not forgotten"); ₹1,000/month nursing needs of destitute minor son; amount to nationalised-bank deposit in son's name — guardian withdraws interest till majority.
⭐ Ratio: (1) Rehabilitation of the victim's dependants can outweigh long incarceration — compensation is a constructive, reconciling sentencing tool. (2) All criminal courts should exercise s. 357/395 liberally; "reasonable" quantum = fact-sensitive (child's needs × offender's roaring practice). (3) Probation is character-sensitive — quackery disentitles; s. 12 protection rightly denied. (4) Four purposes of punishment (R-P-D-R) must each be consciously tested at sentencing.
📝 Use in: s. 395/BNSS-395 answers; "rehabilitation of victim vs punishment of offender"; sentencing philosophy; Bodhisattwa comparison (interim relief). Quote: fine-structure with bank-deposit machinery = model of restorative sentencing.
DU Case 21 · DU material pp. 263–268

The Chairman, Railway Board v. Mrs. Chandrima Das — AIR 2000 SC 988 : (2000) 2 SCC 465

Bench: S. Saghir Ahmad, J. · Art. 226 PIL · Gang rape of Bangladeshi national by railway staff · ₹10 lakh compensation

Facts

  • Victim: Smt. Hanufa Khatoon, Bangladeshi national (elected Union Board representative), arrived Howrah Station 26-2-1998 for Jodhpur Express to Ajmer Sharif; waitlisted → TTE sent her to Ladies Waiting Room.
  • Touts + railway staff trap: Ashoke Singh (tout) & Siya Ram Singh (ticket broker) "confirmed" berth; boy Kashi took her for food (vomiting after meal); ~9 PM Ashoke + Rafi Ahmed (Parcel Supervisor) lured her to Yatri Niwas (Room 102, booked on railway pass) — credentials vouched by lady attendants; Sitaram Singh (Khalasi) joined en route; inside waited Lalan Singh (Parcel Clerk) & Awdesh Singh.
  • Gang rape: forced into room, bolted from outside (Awdesh guarding), forced liquor, brutal gang rape by 4; victim escaped in shock to platform; missed train; Siya Ram posed as saviour → took her to a Jorabagan flat → raped again, gagged till profuse bleeding → rescued on landlord's alarm by Jorabagan police (GRPS Case 19/98).
  • PIL: Advocate Mrs. Chandrima Das filed Art. 226 petition (compensation + clean-up of Howrah station crime). Calcutta HC awarded ₹10 lakh — rape at railway building by railway employees. Railways appealed.

Issues

  1. Maintainability/locus: Art. 226 PIL by an unconnected advocate? Public law vs private-law (civil suit) remedy?
  2. Can a foreigner (Bangladeshi) claim Fundamental-Rights relief in India?
  3. Is the Railway/Union vicariously liable for rape by employees — or was it purely individual acts + sovereign function (Kasturilal)?

Arguments

Railways / Union (appellants)

  • Private-law matter: victim should sue for tort damages with evidence; writ court can't decide disputed facts.
  • No locus: Chandrima Das personally unconnected — PIL incompetent.
  • Foreigner: Fundamental Rights (Part III) are for citizens only — no Art. 21 claim by a Bangladeshi.
  • No vicarious liability: rape is not an official act; only individual culprits liable; running railways = sovereign function (Kasturilal).

Respondent (supported by HC findings)

  • Public-law violation: rape by State employees at State premises = Art. 21 violation; writ remedy lies alongside tort suit (Common Cause — public functionaries + FR breach → Art. 226/32).
  • PIL locus: petition espouses public interest (victim + station crime) — maintainable.
  • "Life" belongs to "persons": Art. 14 & Art. 21 use "person", not "citizen" (unlike Arts. 15, 16, 19); UDHR Art. 3 + ICCPR (ratified by India; Kubic Darusz) give "life" the same expansive meaning.
  • Vicarious liability: railways + Yatri Niwas = commercial activity, not sovereign power; employees acting under cover of official position (pass-booked room) → State liable.

Decision

  • Appeal dismissed; ₹10 lakh compensation upheld — payable through the Bangladesh High Commissioner within 3 months.
  • Public law covers it: where public functionaries + FR violation/enforcement of public duties are involved, Art. 226 lies though a civil suit also lies (Common Cause, Regd. Society v. UoI (1999) 6 SCC 667).
  • Rape = Art. 21 violation (Bodhisattwa: rape destroys psychology, pushes into deep emotional crisis; violates right to life with dignity).
  • Foreigners hold Art. 21: Arts. 14 & 21 say "person" (Constitution-text + Art. 19-citizen contrast cases: State Trading Corp., Hans Muller, Anwar); UDHR-consonant reading — "life" cannot be narrowed.
  • Vicarious liability affirmed: running railways/Yatri Niwas is commercial; Kasturilal (sovereign immunity) inapplicable; tort-by-employee + public-law domain (room secured via official position) → Union liable.
⭐ Ratio (4 holdings — memorise): (1) Rape violates Art. 21 (dignity-autonomy). (2) Art. 21 protects every person on Indian soil, citizen or foreigner (text + UDHR harmonisation). (3) Writ compensation lies for State-actor sexual violence despite availability of tort suit. (4) State is vicariously liable where employees exploit official position/facilities; commercial functions attract no sovereign immunity.

Facts

  • Civil land dispute (plot abutting houses, Srawan Bodla, Malout): deceased Virsa Singh obtained interim stay against Kuljinder Singh on 11-10-1996; appellant No. 2's family backed Kuljinder Singh. Afternoon verbal + grappling fight defused by womenfolk.
  • ~8 PM same day: appellants + 3 others arrived in white Maruti (CHK 8320), raised 'lalkara' (challenge) to "teach a lesson for the stay"; Rachhpal Singh (.12-bore DBBL gun) and Gurmit Singh (.44-40 rifle) + 3 dang-wielding men attacked Virsa Singh & young son Kulwant Singh on their roof — each suffered 2 bullet injuries, died on spot. Eyewitnesses: PW3 Ravinder Singh (son/brother) + PW4 Darbara Singh (visiting relative returning a trolley).
  • Investigation: FIR 11 PM (9 km away; Magistrate by 2:45 AM); blood-earth, 2 empty .12 + 3 empty .44-40 casings seized; arrests 25.10.1996 (rifle + live cartridges on Gurmit; car seized; dangs recovered on disclosure); Rachhpal arrested 27.10.1996 (DBBL + belt of 9 cartridges). PW2 Dr. Randhawa: ante-mortem firearm lacerations of lung.
  • Sessions: appellants → death (s. 302) + fine ₹5,000; 3 others → life. High Court: conviction confirmed; death → life (not rarest-of-rare); 3 others acquitted of 302 (449 only, time undergone); on victim's Crl. Revision 443/99, awarded ₹2 lakh per appellant (₹4L total) u/s 357 + 5 yrs RI default (consecutive).

Issues

  1. Reliability of PWs 3–4 (could they see? why didn't PW3 intervene? trolley story unproved?).
  2. s. 357 locus: where Sessions imposed ₹5,000 fine, could the HC award ₹2L compensation without first enhancing fine — must compensation come only from fine u/s 357(1)(b)?
  3. Was ₹2L each arbitrary/excessive without evidence of capacity to pay / victim needs and without hearing the accused (Palaniappa; Sarwan Singh)?

Arguments

Appellants (K.B. Sinha)

  • Eyewitnesses planted: couldn't have seen from their spot; PW3's non-intervention unnatural; PW4's late visit + missing trolley = improbable.
  • s. 357(1) only: compensation must flow from fine imposed; HC should have enhanced the ₹5,000 fine and paid from it — no standalone compensation atop the fine.
  • ₹2L arbitrary: no material on capacity/needs; no opportunity to lead evidence; violates Palaniappa/Sarwan Singh proportionality.

State / complainant

  • Prompt FIR, on-spot empties matching weapons, medical corroboration — conviction concurrent.
  • s. 357(3) route: HC altered sentence to life only for s. 302, without recording/confirming any fine → deemed no-fine sentence → s. 357(3) direct compensation squarely available.
  • Object of s. 357 + brutal double murder of breadwinners justified invoking compensation; capacity inferable (land, tractor-trolley, Maruti).

Decision

  • Conviction + life sentence confirmed (evidence believed).
  • s. 357(3) upheld: since HC awarded only substantive life imprisonment u/s 302 (no fine recorded), s. 357(3) "comes into play" — Court may award compensation to victim/family even absent fine; HC was well within jurisdiction.
  • Quantum moderated: HC lacked full capacity-material, but object of s. 357 justified invocation; assessing from record + judicial notice (agri-land, tractor-trolley, Maruti = reasonably affluent) → reduced ₹2L → ₹1L per appellant (₹2L total).
⭐ Ratio: (1) 357(1) vs 357(3) test: compensation from fine needs a fine; where sentence carries no fine, 357(3) permits direct compensation orders — appellate courts need not route through fine-enhancement. (2) Quantum = capacity × gravity × victim-needs (Palaniappa; Sarwan Singh affirmed) — but scanty material doesn't defeat jurisdiction; courts may assess via record + judicial notice, moderating to a safe figure. (3) Compensation jurisdiction of courts is settled law.
📝 Use in: BNSS 395(1) vs 395(3) distinction problems; "capacity to pay" principle; default sentence consecutive-run point. Always pair with Jacob George for "reasonable quantum".
DU Case 23 ★ (starred — online) · PIL series

Laxmi v. Union of India — (2014) 4 SCC 427; (2016) 3 SCC 669

PIL by acid-attack survivor Laxmi (attacked 2005, aged 15) · Regulating acid sale + minimum compensation + free treatment · Led to IPC 326A/B, CrPC 357B/C

Facts

  • Laxmi, attacked with acid in Delhi (2005) by a spurned suitor's associates — grievous disfigurement, multiple surgeries, immense physical/mental trauma; trial-court conviction followed, but compensation hopelessly inadequate for surgeries/rehabilitation.
  • PIL (2006): prayed for (i) amendments to IPC/CrPC creating dedicated acid-attack offences; (ii) ban/regulation of over-the-counter acid sale; (iii) prosecution guidelines + rehabilitation (treatment + compensation + aftercare).
  • Context: free availability of concentrated acid; no dedicated penal provision (prosecuted as ordinary hurt/grievous hurt/attempt-murder); victims bankrupted by surgeries.

Issues

  1. How to curb easy availability of acid/corrosives?
  2. What minimum compensation + medical-treatment guarantees must States/UTs provide acid-attack victims?
  3. What legislative reform is needed for acid offences?

Arguments (PIL — petitioner vs Union/States)

Petitioner (Laxmi)

  • Acid = weapon of patriarchal control; existing law treats it as ordinary hurt — needs separate offence + stringent punishment.
  • Complete ban / strict licensing of retail acid; acids as scheduled poisons.
  • Free treatment + substantial compensation + rehabilitation as victim's right; wide publicity of schemes.

Union / States (response)

  • Centre framed Model Rules under the Poisons Act, 1919 for sale/storage/licensing — circulated to States/UTs.
  • States (e.g., T.N.) undertook strict rules within 2 months; acid attacks made cognizable + non-bailable.
  • Supported compensation via s. 357A schemes + free treatment linkage.

Decision — series of directions (2013 → 2015)

  • Acid-sale regulation (2013 order): no over-the-counter sale to minors (below 18); seller to keep register (buyer ID + address + quantity + purpose); buyer must show govt. photo-ID + stated purpose; dealers to declare stocks to SDM within 15 days; undeclared stock → confiscation + fine up to ₹50,000; States to notify acid as poison.
  • Minimum compensation: ₹3 lakh per acid-attack victim in every State/UT — ₹1L within 15 days of incident/notice (immediate medical expenses) + ₹2L within 2 months.
  • Free treatment: full medical assistance free — private hospitals equally bound; no refusal for lack of speciality (stabilise + first-aid, then refer); SLSAs to give wide publicity to VCS.
  • Legislative fruit: Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 — IPC 326A (acid attack, min 10 yrs + fine-for-medical-costs) & 326B (attempt), CrPC 357B (State money additional to fine) & 357C (free treatment) — now BNS 124, BNSS 396(7), 397.
⭐ Ratio: (1) Preventive + restorative justice fused — Court regulated the weapon (acid market) alongside the wound (treatment + money). (2) Floor-level compensation as a right (₹3L, time-bound tranches) — model for all VCS slabs. (3) Private hospitals are State-agents for emergency victim-care — no commercial refusal. (4) PIL can midwife substantive penal reform (326A/B template).
📝 Use in: acid-attack problems; BNSS 396/397 answers; "compensation + regulation" essays; gender-justice dimensions. Real-example add-on: post-Laxmi, Delhi/UP VCSs list acid-attack slabs up to ₹5–7L; NALSA 2018 scheme dedicates an acid-attack head.
DU Case 24 ★ (starred — online) · Victim-identity + compensation architecture

Nipun Saxena v. Union of India — 2017 SCC OnLine SC 1776; (2019) 2 SCC 703

PIL on media disclosure of rape-victim identity + inadequacy of VCS · Ss. 228A IPC (BNS 72), 327 CrPC (BNSS 366), 372 proviso · NALSA Scheme 2018 for sexual-assault survivors & POCSO children

Facts

  • Trigger: media repeatedly identified rape survivors despite s. 228A IPC — e.g., naming a survivor as a State-board topper (identity instantly deducible); blurring the face but naming village + relatives. Investigation itself re-traumatised victims through repetitive questioning.
  • PIL sought enforcement of identity-protection + uniform, adequate compensation machinery for sexual-offence victims (women and POCSO children).
  • Parallel track: post-10-8-2018 order, a Committee finalised "NALSA's Compensation Scheme for Women Victims/Survivors of Sexual Assault/Other Crimes — 2018"; Court found no comparable scheme for POCSO child victims (Rules under POCSO amendment awaited) — a "hiatus".

Issues

  1. Scope of s. 228A IPC — what counts as "identity disclosure"? Who (if anyone) can authorise it?
  2. What procedural shields (FIR publicity, in-camera trial, court records) must apply?
  3. What compensation framework should govern women survivors and POCSO children?

Arguments

Petitioner

  • s. 228A + s. 327(2) (Amendment Act 43 of 1983) mandate absolute non-disclosure — media circumvents via indirect identifiers.
  • Victims need anonymity at every stage (FIR → trial → appeal → records) + one-stop support + assured compensation.

State / ASG

  • Accepted protection goals; noted practical necessities (name in FIR file, police records — s. 228A(2) exception for police).
  • POCSO amendment + dedicated compensation Rules forthcoming; interim guidance acceptable.

Decision — two judgments' holdings combined

  • Identity ban (2019) 2 SCC 703: no person may print/publish in print, electronic or social media the name or any matter by which the victim's identity may be known (ban covers indirect identifiers too); breach = offence u/s 228A (now BNS 72) — 2 yrs + fine. Police must keep FIRs under rape/POCSO provisions off the public domain (media included); authorities/courts privy to identity must seal/mask records.
  • Authorised disclosure — narrow gates: adult victim may herself authorise in writing [228A(2)(b)]; if victim dead/unsound → next of kin applies to Sessions Judge/Magistrate; if victim is a minor → only with Special Court permission, child's interest paramount.
  • Appeal anonymity: victim appealing u/s 372 proviso (BNSS 413) may use pseudonyms (X/Y).
  • Trial shields: in-camera proceedings u/s 327(2) (BNSS 366); publication of court proceedings without prior permission punishable (s. 228 IPC → BNS 73).
  • Compensation architecture: NALSA Scheme 2018 adopted nationally (graded slabs + interim relief for sexual-assault survivors); extended as interim guideline for POCSO Special Courts under Rule 7 (2012 Rules) till Centre frames Rules — gender-neutral, w.e.f. 2-10-2018; funds to be protected in interest-bearing accounts for children; copies to every HC Registrar General → District/Special Judges + Legal Services Committees + Judicial Academies.
  • One-Stop Centres: States/UTs urged to run one-stop centres in every district (medical + legal + counselling under one roof).
⭐ Ratio: (1) Victim anonymity is absolute-by-default — "identity" means any identifying matter, not just the name; exceptions need victim's writing or court sanction. (2) Rehabilitation is parallel to punishment — NALSA 2018 + POCSO interim awards institutionalise money-during-trial. (3) Courts must engineer administrative compliance (circulation chains, OSCs) — victim justice is implementation-heavy.
📝 Use in: media/identity problems; POCSO compensation; "drawbacks of 357/357A → NALSA Scheme" narrative (2022 PYQ answer cites exactly this). Pair with Chandrima (anonymity was also ordered there) + Delhi Domestic Working Women.

DU LL.B. · Criminology (2022) · Q.6 — Problem question

"Raghav, a police officer, was sentenced to life imprisonment u/s 376 IPC for the rape of an illegally arrested woman in his police station. From commission till conclusion of trial, the victim and her family suffered immense physical, mental and financial agony. Substantiate with relevant case law and legal provisions dealing with the rights of victims."

MODEL ANSWER — structure: (A) Identify issues → (B) Victim definition → (C) UN framework → (D) Rights catalogue applied → (E) Compensation (395/396) → (F) Case-law wall → (G) Conclusion applying to Raghav's victim.

(A) Issues: (i) What rights does a rape victim (also an abuse-of-power victim — illegal arrest + custodial rape by a police officer) hold in Indian criminal justice? (ii) What compensation/rehabilitation can she claim, from whom — offender, State, or both?

(B) Who is a victim? Open with Justice Krishna Iyer ("law not victim oriented…") + UN Declaration Arts. 1–2 (harm incl. emotional suffering + fundamental-rights impairment; family/dependents included; offender's identification irrelevant) + BNSS s. 2(1)(y) (loss/injury by the charged act; includes guardian/legal heir). This victim qualifies triply: sexual-offence victim, custodial/abuse-of-power victim (Neumann's category), and her family as secondary victims.

(C) UN Declaration 40/34 framework: (1) Access to justice & fair treatment (compassion, dignity, information, participation, protection, no delay); (2) Restitution (offender/third parties; State where officials offended — para 11 squarely covers a policeman-rapist); (3) Compensation from State funds where offender cannot fully pay; (4) Assistance (medical, psychological, social). India transplants these via BNSS 395–398 + constitutional tort.

(D) Rights applied to this victim:

  1. Right to register FIR & be investigated fairly — BNSS 173 (mandatory registration, Lalita Kumari); complaint to Magistrate (BNSS 210); illegal arrest itself actionable (BNSS 399 compensation; constitutional tort).
  2. Right to private counsel — BNSS 360 (counsel under PP's directions; written arguments) / 361 (conduct prosecution in Magistrate court with permission); Shiv Kumar v. Hukam Chand distinction; note legislative-judicial curbs on private counsel (DU 2022 solved answer emphasises this).
  3. Right to appeal — BNSS 413 proviso (acquittal / lesser offence / inadequate compensation); pseudonym appeal per Nipun Saxena.
  4. Right to restitution — BNSS 395: fine-based (395(1)(b)) or direct (395(3)); life sentence without fine still permits Rachhpal-route compensation.
  5. Right to State compensation — BNSS 396: court recommendation → DLSA/SLSA quantum; interim + free medical benefits (396(6)); applicable even without conviction; 396(7) cumulation with BNS fine (BNS 64/65/70).
  6. Right to assistance & dignity — medical exam within 24 hrs with consent (BNSS 184); free treatment (BNSS 397); Delhi Domestic Working Women guidelines (legal aid at police station, anonymity); no two-finger test (Rajesh); in-camera trial (BNSS 366); identity shield (BNS 72; Nipun Saxena bans incl. FIR off public domain).

(E) Compensation — dual-track liability of Raghav + State: (i) Offender track: BNSS 395 — fine/compensation from Raghav (life sentence → 395(3) direct order; Rachhpal; Jacob George "liberal exercise"). (ii) State track: BNSS 396 scheme + constitutional tort — custodial rape by police = Art. 21 violation; State vicariously liable (Chandrima Das — ₹10L for gang rape by railway staff; Nilabati; D.K. Basu — compensatory, interim/final, no sovereign immunity). Also NALSA Scheme 2018 slabs for sexual-assault survivors (Nipun Saxena). (iii) Medical track: BNSS 397 free treatment; refusal → BNS 200.

(F) Case-law wall (cite 5–6): Chandrima Das (closest — State-employee rape → ₹10L) · Nipun Saxena (identity + NALSA scheme) · Jacob George (rehabilitation over jail; liberal 395) · Bodhisattwa (interim relief pre-conviction; rape = Art. 21) · D.K. Basu/Nilabati (State pays for custodial abuse) · State of Gujarat v. HC Gujarat (wages-to-victims; restorative imagination).

(G) Conclusion: Conviction of Raghav satisfies retribution, but victim justice demands restoration: she can claim (a) BNSS 395 compensation from Raghav, (b) BNSS 396 State rehabilitation money via DLSA/SLSA (+ NALSA 2018 slabs), (c) writ compensation for custodial Art-21 violation, (d) free medical/psychological care, anonymity and counsel rights through trial and appeal. Courts must act suo motu-sensitively — victim is no longer the "forgotten person".

DU LL.B. · Criminology (2017) · Q.7(a) — Essay

"It has now been felt that we have cared too much for the criminal and his rehabilitation but very little for the victim." Comment in the light of various judicial decisions.

MODEL ANSWER — structure: (A) Thesis & theoretical basis → (B) Dual obligations → (C) Judicial proof (Track A + Track B) → (D) Statutory catching-up → (E) Remaining gaps → (F) Action plan + conclusion.

(A) Thesis: Agree — modern penology (reformative theory, probation, parole, Arts. 20–22 shields, D.K. Basu arrestee-safeguards) built an elaborate offender-care architecture while the victim remained, in Paranjape's phrase, the "forgotten person" — a mere witness whose vindication ended at conviction. Quote Krishna Iyer + Raina ("victimology in India = method of sentencing, not restitution").

(B) The dual-obligation theory (DU 2017 solved answer): restitution rests on (i) the criminal's obligation (he inflicted harm) + (ii) the State's obligation (it monopolised punishment yet failed to protect). The State having taken the exclusive right to punish must accept the correlative duty to repair.

(C) Judicial proof — Track A (conventional crime): Hari Kishan (liberal 357; "not forgotten") → Jacob George (jail cut, ₹1L rehabilitation fund for minor son — victim-rehabilitation as ultimate aim) → Ajaib Singh (₹5L despite acquittal; 18-year delay deprecated; speedy disposal = sine qua non) → Rachhpal (357(3) without fine; capacity-calibrated quantum) → Bodhisattwa (interim relief pre-conviction) → Delhi Domestic Working Women (rape-court's inherent compensation jurisdiction) → State of Gujarat v. HC Gujarat (prison wages to victims) → Laxmi (₹3L floor + free treatment) → Nipun Saxena (NALSA 2018 slabs; POCSO interim).

Track B (abuse of power): Rudul Shah (₹35K; Art. 21 needs compensation teeth) → PUDR (₹20K working principle) → Nilabati (₹1L monetary amends; no sovereign immunity; ICCPR 9(5)) → D.K. Basu (compensatory-not-punitive; State pays + recovers; civil/criminal remedies preserved) → Chandrima Das (₹10L; rape = Art. 21; foreigners covered; commercial-function liability) → Bhim Singh/SAHELI/Inder Singh.

(D) Statutory catching-up: CrPC 2008 (2(wa) victim definition; 357A scheme; 372 proviso appeal) + 2013 (326A/B, 357B/C, 166B) → BNSS 395–398 (offender-pays + State-pays + free treatment + witness protection); POA Act s. 5; MV Act no-fault; POCSO interim awards; SC/ST slabs; NALSA 2018.

(E) Gaps admitted: Raina's critique — no claimable right, rare judicial use (capacity doubts, docket pressure — 41st Law Commission); uneven State VCS slabs; secondary victimisation by police/courts/media persists; private-counsel role still curbed (Shiv Kumar).

(F) Action plan + conclusion (Raina's 6 points): State responsibility → separate boards/tribunals → special rape/child provisions → abuse-of-power vigilance → compassionate access → restitution + assistance. Conclude: the statement was true of classical penology; since the 1980s the Court has built a compensatory jurisprudence and Parliament a rehabilitation architecture — but victimology must still grow from sentencing technique into a rights-regime, ending with Zender's caution (empower victims; don't instrumentalise them for punitiveness).

7.4 Ten golden lines (if you remember nothing else)

  1. Victimology = scientific study of victimisation (Karmen); victim = lynch-pin; founded by Mendelsohn & Von Hentig.
  2. UN Arts. 1–2 define victim by harm (incl. family + rescuers), not by offender's fate; India (BNSS 2(1)(y)) is narrower — 396(4) fills the gap.
  3. Precipitation/penal-couple = rejected (victim-blaming); facilitation + routine-activity (target + offender − guardian) = accepted.
  4. Victim's ladder: 173 → 184 → 397 → 360/361 → 366 → 395 → 413 → 396 (FIR to rehabilitation).
  5. 395 = offender pays (with/without fine); 396 = State pays (even acquittal/untraced + interim); 397 = hospitals treat free.
