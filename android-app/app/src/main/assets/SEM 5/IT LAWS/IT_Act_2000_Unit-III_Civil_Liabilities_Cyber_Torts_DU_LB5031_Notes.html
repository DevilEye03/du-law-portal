🎯 THE ONE IDEA THAT CARRIES THIS ENTIRE UNIT

The IT Act creates a specialist, low‑cost, summary compensation forum for wrongs done to computers and data. Two heads of liability, with two different standards:

  • s.43 — act‑based, broadly strict, applies to anyone and to any computer resource. Ten enumerated acts of unauthorised interference. But the breach alone does not produce damages — the claimant must plead and prove the loss (Vinod Kaushik).
  • s.43A — negligence‑based, applies only to a body corporate and only to sensitive personal data or information. Three cumulative elements, laid down with precision by TDSAT in SBI v. Chander Kalani.

If you remember one sentence for the exam: s.43 asks "what did you do to the computer?"; s.43A asks "were you negligent about the data you were holding, and did that cause loss?" — and the entire adjudication runs through the Adjudicating Officer (claims up to ₹5 crore), on appeal to TDSAT, and then to the High Court.

📍 Three things to keep straight before you start
  • Compensation vs. penalty. The AO awards compensation to the victim under ss.43 and 43A, and may impose a penalty for other contraventions (s.45 residuary penalty). Section 46 uses "damage", "injury" and "compensation" interchangeably, ignoring the classical distinction between compensation (indemnity, guided by equity) and damages (which can be punitive). Flag this in a critical answer.
  • Civil and criminal run in parallel. The same conduct can attract compensation under s.43 and punishment under s.66 (and s.65/s.66C for source‑code tampering and identity theft). The AO has no criminal jurisdiction — where a Chapter XI offence is made out, the matter goes to the magistrate.
  • Currency. All sections are cited in their post‑2008‑amendment form. The 2008 Amendment inserted s.43A and raised the AO's pecuniary ceiling from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore (s.46(1A)). The Cyber Appellate Tribunal was abolished in 2017; its functions are now discharged by TDSAT.

1The mischief: cyber torts as a sui generis statutory tort regime

Foundational concept  Read this before any section. It explains why the IT Act needed its own private‑law machinery at all.

1.1 Why ordinary tort law was not enough

Before 2000, a victim of digital interference had to sue in an ordinary civil court for trespass to goods, conversion, breach of confidence or negligence. Four problems made that route practically useless:

ProblemWhy ordinary tort law failedThe IT Act's answer
Cost and delayA civil suit in India takes years and costs a fortune. A ₹50,000 data‑theft loss could never economically be litigated.s.46 creates a specialist, low‑cost, summary adjudicatory forum with a fee‑based complaint procedure. In Sanjay Dhande the application fee alone was ₹2,03,260, but the process took months, not years.
ProofProving that a specific person accessed a server from a specific IP address requires technical evidence that ordinary civil courts were ill‑equipped to evaluate.The Act creates its own evidentiary architecture (Ch. II, ss.3/3A) and, through s.79A, an Examiner of Electronic Evidence; the AO can obtain expert reports under s.46(5).
Remedy mismatchTrespass to goods damages are measured by diminution in value of the chattel — a concept that fits a hacked server badly. Breach of confidence required a pre‑existing relationship.s.43 enumerates ten specific acts and attaches a flat statutory liability "to pay damages by way of compensation to the person so affected" — no need to fit the wrong into a common‑law tort.
No forum of first resortJurisdictional questions in cross‑border cyber cases are nightmarish; s.61 of the Act bars civil courts where the AO has jurisdiction.s.61: "No court shall have jurisdiction to entertain any suit or proceeding in respect of any matter which an adjudicating officer appointed under sub‑section (1) of section 46 or the Appellate Tribunal… is empowered by or under this Act to determine." A dedicated, exclusive ladder: AO → Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) → High Court.

2Dispute resolution machinery: the Adjudicating Officer & Appellate Tribunal

Syllabus item 1  The syllabus expressly names s.46 (Adjudicating Officer) and s.57 (Appellate Tribunal). This is the procedural spine of the whole Unit — and the source of several "gotcha" marks.

s.46 · POWER TO ADJUDICATE · VERBATIM (FIVE SUB-SECTIONS)

Power to adjudicate

(1) For the purpose of adjudging under this Chapter whether any person has committed a contravention of any of the provisions of this Act or of any rule, regulation, direction or order made thereunder which renders him liable to pay penalty or compensation, the Central Government shall, subject to the provisions of sub‑section (3), appoint any officer not below the rank of a Director to the Government of India or an equivalent officer of a State Government to be an adjudicating officer for holding an inquiry in the manner prescribed by the Central Government.
(1A) The adjudicating officer appointed under sub‑section (1) shall exercise jurisdiction to adjudicate matters in which the claim for injury or damage does not exceed rupees five crore: Provided that the jurisdiction in respect of the claim for injury or damage exceeding rupees five crore shall vest with the competent court.
(2) The adjudicating officer shall, after giving the person referred to in sub‑section (1) a reasonable opportunity for making representation in the matter and if, on such inquiry, he is satisfied that the person has committed the contravention, he may impose such penalty or award such compensation as he thinks fit in accordance with the provisions of that section.
(3) No person shall be appointed as an adjudicating officer unless he possesses such experience in the field of Information Technology and legal or judicial experience as may be prescribed by the Central Government.
(4) Where more than one adjudicating officers are appointed, the Central Government shall specify by order the matters and places with respect to which such officers shall exercise their jurisdiction.
(5) Every adjudicating officer shall have the powers of a civil court which are conferred on the Appellate Tribunal under sub‑section (2) of section 58, and—
  (a) all proceedings before it shall be deemed to be judicial proceedings within the meaning of sections 193 and 228 of the Indian Penal Code;
  (b) the adjudicating officer shall be deemed to be a civil court for the purposes of section 195 and Chapter XXVI of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973;
  (c) the adjudicating officer shall have the power of a civil court for the purposes of summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person and examining him on oath, and for the discovery and production of documents or electronic records.
The three points that carry marks: (i) the ₹5 crore ceiling and its proviso sending larger claims to the competent court; (ii) the "reasonable opportunity for making representation" in sub‑s.(2) — the statutory source of natural justice; (iii) the civil‑court powers in sub‑s.(5) — which are enabling powers, not a mandate to conduct a full civil trial (the exact holding in SBI v. Chander Kalani).

2.3 The procedure — and the "summary inquiry" holding that decides half the marks

⚖️ The procedural holding in SBI v. Chander Kalani — quote this in any AO-jurisdiction question

The bank argued that because the AO is deemed a civil court for certain purposes and has civil‑court powers, it ought to have followed the detailed procedure of holding a fair trial — leading evidence and allowing cross‑examination on disputed facts. TDSAT rejected this: "Considering the specific provision of giving reasonable opportunity to make representation and on such inquiry, a finding or satisfaction can be recorded, there is no difficulty in holding that the procedure does not warrant an elaborate trial as is required to be held by a civil court in a suit. No doubt some powers of civil court have been vested in the Adjudicating Officer but these are only to enable the officer to summon or discover relevant materials, whenever necessary for coming to a proper finding in such inquiry. From the powers, it cannot be concluded that the inquiry should be replaced by an elaborate trial like that of a suit in civil court. Such interpretation would defeat the very purpose of providing a summary procedure of inquiry for adjudicating claims upto Rs.5 crore by the Tribunal under the IT Act."

✅ Real example — what the AO's summary procedure looks like in practice

In Sanjay Dhande the AO: (i) received the complaint dated 26 September 2013; (ii) called for documents from both sides (bank statements, the FIR, correspondence with the bank's deputy branch manager and its MD & CEO, the duplicate SIM replacement form, the forged passport); (iii) examined the police investigation papers; (iv) reviewed the RBI Master Circular on KYC/AML/CFT dated 2 July 2012 and the RBI Guidelines on Information Security, Electronic Banking, Technology Risk Management and Cyber Frauds dated 29 April 2011; (v) heard argument; and (vi) delivered a reasoned order on 16 January 2014 — under four months from complaint to award. No formal trial, no witness box, no cross‑examination. That is what "summary procedure" means.

⚠️ Common mistake

Do not write "the Adjudicating Officer is a civil court" without qualification. The AO is deemed a civil court for the limited purposes of s.195 and Chapter XXVI CrPC and for the specific powers in s.46(5); it is not a civil court and does not conduct a civil trial. And do not forget the ₹5 crore ceiling — a question that gives a ₹10 crore loss requires you to say the claim must go to the competent civil court, not the AO.

3Damage to computer & computer system — s.43's ten heads

Syllabus item 2  The syllabus names the specific heads: "access/facilitates access, data theft, virus attacks, email bombings, denial of service attack, damage to computer source code". Learn all ten clauses — the section is quoted in full below.

s.43 · PENALTY AND COMPENSATION FOR DAMAGE TO COMPUTER, COMPUTER SYSTEM, ETC. · VERBATIM

Penalty and compensation for damage to computer, computer system, etc.

If any person without permission of the owner or any other person who is in charge of a computer, computer system or computer network,—
(a) accesses or secures access to such computer, computer system or computer network [or computer resource];
(b) downloads, copies or extracts any data, computer data base or information from such computer, computer system or computer network including information or data held or stored in any removable storage medium;
(c) introduces or causes to be introduced any computer contaminant or computer virus into any computer, computer system or computer network;
(d) damages or causes to be damaged any computer, computer system or computer network, data, computer data base or any other programmes residing in such computer, computer system or computer network;
(e) disrupts or causes disruption of any computer, computer system or computer network;
(f) denies or causes the denial of access to any person authorised to access any computer, computer system or computer network by any means;
(g) provides any assistance to any person to facilitate access to a computer, computer system or computer network in contravention of the provisions of this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder;
(h) charges the services availed of by person to the account of another person by tampering with or manipulating any computer, computer system, or computer network;
(i) destroys, deletes or alters any information residing in a computer resource or diminishes its value or utility or affects it injuriously by any means;
(j) steals, conceals, destroys or alters or causes any person to steal, conceal, destroy or alter any computer source code used for a computer resource with an intention to cause damage;
he shall be liable to pay damages by way of compensation to the person so affected.
Note: the words in square brackets ("or computer resource" in clause (a)) were inserted by the 2008 Amendment, which also substituted "Penalty and compensation" in the marginal heading. The original heading read "Penalty for damage to computer, computer system, etc."
📜 The Explanation to s.43 — the four defined terms

In this section, unless the context otherwise requires—
(i) "computer contaminant" means any set of computer instructions that is designed to modify, destroy, record or transmit information within a computer, computer system or computer network without the intent or permission of the owner of the information;
(ii) "computer database" means a representation of information, knowledge, facts, concepts or instructions in text, image, audio, video that is being prepared or has been prepared in a formalised manner or has been produced by a computer, computer system or computer network and is intended for use in a computer, computer system or computer network;
(iii) "computer virus" means any computer instruction, information, data or programme that destroys, damages, degrades or adversely affects the performance of a computer resource or attaches itself to another computer resource and operates when a programme, data or instruction is executed or some other event takes place in that computer resource;
(iv) "damage" means to destroy, alter, delete, add, modify or rearrange any computer resource by any means.

⚠️ Two traps in s.43
  • Clause (j) requires intention. Clauses (a)–(i) contain no mental element; clause (j) expressly requires "an intention to cause damage". If a question involves source code, you must address intention. This is the single most overlooked distinction in s.43.
  • Clauses (d), (e), (f) and (g) are drafted "or causes to be". So the person who causes another to damage, disrupt, deny or facilitate is as liable as the person who does the act — this reaches the principal behind the act, not merely the finger on the keyboard.

⚖️ The Vinod Kaushik rule — the third element, and the most commonly tested trap

"The claim for compensation would not arise merely on account of the breach of Section 43. The claim would have to be established like in Civil Court, by making requisite averments with regard to the damage suffered, and thereafter by leading evidence in support thereof, to show that one or the other prohibited activities enumerated in Section 43 of the IT Act have resulted in the sufferance of damages by the person concerned." — Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi, Delhi HC, 27.01.2012, quoting and approving the AO's reasoning

✅ How the three elements actually play out — a worked example

Facts: R, a dismissed employee, still has his old login credentials (never deactivated). He logs into the company's CRM at 11 pm, downloads 4,000 customer records, and e‑mails them to a competitor.

  • Element 1: without permission — his employment, and with it his authority, ended on dismissal. Satisfied.
  • Element 2: clause (a) (secures access) and clause (b) (downloads/extracts data). No intention element required for either. Satisfied.
  • Element 3: the company must plead and prove its loss — the value of the customer relationships lost to the competitor, the cost of forensic investigation, the cost of re‑securing the system. If the company pleads only "our data was taken" and leads no evidence of loss, the claim fails on quantum — exactly as in Vinod Kaushik.
  • Plus: the company is itself exposed under s.43A if it failed to deactivate the credentials — see §5.
  • Plus: the same conduct attracts s.66 (dishonestly or fraudulently doing any act referred to in ss.43–44) and, if personal data was involved, s.72A and s.66C.

🔍 Four doctrinal points on the overlap
  1. Different fora, different standards. The AO works on a summary inquiry with a preponderance‑style satisfaction under s.46(2); the magistrate applies the criminal standard. An acquittal under s.66 does not bar a s.43 claim, and vice versa.
  2. The AO has no criminal jurisdiction. Where a Chapter XI offence is made out, the matter must be transferred to the magistrate having jurisdiction. (Yet note the anomaly in Vinod Kaushik, where the AO imposed a ₹100 token fine under s.66C and the Delhi HC declined to interfere — the petitioner, not the respondent, was the aggrieved party on that point.)
  3. Extra‑territoriality. s.75: the Act applies to an offence or contravention committed outside India by any person irrespective of his nationality. This is the answer to the Vinod Kaushik self‑learning exercise on whether the AO has jurisdiction where the unauthorised access originated outside India.
  4. s.61 bars the civil court, not the criminal court. Where the AO has jurisdiction, no civil court may entertain the suit; but the criminal remedy under Chapter XI lies to the magistrate, unaffected by s.61.

5Section 43A — negligence‑based corporate data‑protection liability

Syllabus item 3  Inserted by the 2008 Amendment. This is the single most examined provision in Unit III — and the one that generates the PYQs on duplicate SIMs and unauthorised transfers.

s.43A · COMPENSATION FOR FAILURE TO PROTECT DATA · VERBATIM

Compensation for failure to protect data

Where a body corporate, possessing, dealing or handling any sensitive personal data or information in a computer resource which it owns, controls or operates, is negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures and thereby causes wrongful loss or wrongful gain to any person, such body corporate shall be liable to pay damages by way of compensation, to the person so affected, not exceeding five crore rupees.
Note the cap: s.43A itself contains a ₹5 crore ceiling on compensation. Section 43 has no statutory cap — the quantum is fixed under s.47's factors.

5.1 The Explanation — three defined terms, widely drawn

📜 Explanation to s.43A — the three definitions TDSAT analysed in Chander Kalani

(a) "body corporate" means any company and includes a firm, sole proprietorship or other association of individuals engaged in commercial or professional activities;
(b) "reasonable security practices and procedures" means the security practices and procedures designed to protect such information from unauthorised access, damage, use, modification, disclosure or impairment, as may be specified in an agreement between the parties or in any law in force and, in the absence of such agreement or law, such reasonable security practices and procedures, as may be prescribed by the Central Government in the prescribed manner;
(c) "sensitive personal data or information" means such personal information, as may be prescribed by the Central Government in consultation with such competent authorities or associations as it may deem fit.

⚖️ TDSAT's reading of the three definitions (SBI v. Chander Kalani, 2018) — quote this

"A careful reading of the Explanation reveals that 'body corporate' has been given a wide and inclusive definition so as not to be confined only to a company incorporated under the Company Law. 'Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures' are all such practices and procedures which are designed to protect particular kind of information not only from unauthorised access, damage but also from its use, modification, disclosure or impairment as may be specified in an agreement between the parties or in any law in force and in absence of both, such reasonable security practices and procedures, as may be prescribed by the Central Government in the prescribed manner. Clearly, the definition is again very widely worded and includes unauthorised use, disclosure etc. 'Sensitive Personal Data or Information' has been assigned a meaning as may be prescribed by the Central Government in consultation with competent bodies or associations."

5.2 The three‑element test — the exact holding

⚖️ The authoritative statement of the test — SBI v. Chander Kalani, TDSAT, 31 July 2018

"On a careful reading of Section 43A, it is absolutely clear that negligence in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures alone creates a liability to pay damages or compensation under Section 43A, if such negligence has caused wrongful loss or wrongful gain to the person affected. Negligence by a banker in not properly comparing specimen signature with a signature on a cheque may also lead to wrongful loss or wrongful gain but such negligence will not be amenable to the scope of Section 43A. For this Section to apply, firstly, the body corporate should be possessing, dealing or handling sensitive personal data or information in a computer resource under its ownership, control or operation. Secondly, it should be found negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures and thirdly, by such negligence it caused wrongful loss or wrongful gain to any person."

Chander Kalani: "The Bank through its computer resource was clearly possessing, dealing and handling sensitive personal data and information of the complainants in its computer resource" — FD details, account numbers, financial information within Rule 3.A person who is not a body corporate (an individual) can never be liable under s.43A — only under s.43. Data that is not "sensitive" (e.g. information freely available in the public domain) is outside Rule 3.
2 · Negligence in security practicesThe body corporate was negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures — and that negligence must relate to security practices and procedures, not to the underlying transaction generally.Dhande: Vodafone issued a duplicate SIM without verifying the photograph/signature against original KYC documents, took no payment receipt, let the franchisee backdate the form, and made no confirmatory call to the original number — "the minimum due diligence one would expect". ICICI violated RBI KYC norms (bogus accounts), had no Fraud Investigation Unit, and had no real‑time fraud analytics flagging foreign‑IP/rapid‑withdrawal anomalies. Chander Kalani: the Bank acted on unverified e‑mail instructions and a scanned (not original) signature on the A‑2 form, with no confirmation through the registered mobile — a failure within Rule 8 of the SPDI Rules.Chander Kalani's express carve‑out: "Negligence by a banker in not properly comparing specimen signature with a signature on a cheque may also lead to wrongful loss or wrongful gain but such negligence will not be amenable to the scope of Section 43A." Signature‑verification negligence, standing alone, is ordinary tort/contract liability, not s.43A.
3 · CausationThe negligence caused wrongful loss or wrongful gain to any person.Dhande: the duplicate SIM was the "proximate enabling cause" of the ₹19 lakh loss. Chander Kalani: the Bank's "negligent acts did cause wrongful loss to the complainant".Loss caused by the claimant's own conduct, or by a third party whose act the negligence did not enable. (Note, however, that in Chander Kalani TDSAT rejected the argument that the complainant's own lack of diligence defeated the claim — the AO's finding that "both the Complainant and the Respondent Bank have to share the blame" did not reduce the ₹40 lakh award, because the complainant had claimed ₹1 crore.)

5.3 The burden‑shifting that TDSAT has developed in practice

🔍 The doctrinal debate the DU material flags as "the single most important open question in this area"

"Section 43A occupies an awkward middle ground between strict liability and ordinary negligence. Unlike s.43 (which imposes liability essentially on proof of the enumerated unauthorised act, subject to Vinod Kaushik's requirement that damages still be independently proved), s.43A requires the claimant to establish a genuine negligence case against the body corporate — a significantly higher evidentiary bar for individual data‑breach victims, who rarely have the technical capacity to demonstrate what 'reasonable security practices' were not followed."

"TDSAT's jurisprudence (Chander Kalani, Sanjay Dhande, and the later Bank of India v. Sandeep line) has, in practice, mitigated this by treating conspicuous procedural failures (no confirmatory call, unverified KYC, no fraud‑analytics flags) as prima facie evidence of the absence of reasonable security practices — effectively shifting the practical burden onto the body corporate to demonstrate compliance with the SPDI Rules / Rule 8 framework, once the claimant shows the security lapse."

In Chander Kalani TDSAT did exactly this: "nothing has been brought on record by the Bank to show that it had implemented and maintained reasonable security practices to protect such information from unauthorised use"; "the Bank has failed to bring on record any material to show that it had in place a policy as required by Rule 4"; "the appellant Bank did not bring on record any material to show that it had complied with the provisions for 'reasonable security practices and procedures' in terms of Rule 8."

⚠️ Common mistake — the most expensive error in this Unit

Treating s.43 and s.43A as interchangeable. A problem question that names a bank and a telecom operator is a s.43A question; one that names a disgruntled ex‑employee is a s.43 question. A question that names both requires you to address both.

6The SPDI Rules, 2011 — the operational framework

Syllabus item 3  The syllabus's "ss.43A r/w sensitive personal information rules" is a reference to the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011 — the "SPDI Rules", notified on 11 April 2011 and effective 13 April 2011, framed under s.43A read with s.87(2)(ob) and (ze) of the IT Act.

6.1 Rule 3 — the definition that decides every s.43A case

📜 Rule 2(i) and Rule 3, SPDI Rules 2011 — verbatim substance

Rule 2(i) — "personal information" means any information that relates to a natural person, which, either directly or indirectly, in combination with other information available or likely to be available with a body corporate, is capable of identifying such person.

Rule 3 — "sensitive personal data or information" of a person means such personal information which consists of information relating to:

  1. Password;
  2. Financial information such as bank account or credit card or debit card or other payment instrument details;
  3. Physical, physiological and mental health condition;
  4. Sexual orientation;
  5. Medical records and history;
  6. Biometric information;
  7. Any detail relating to the above clauses provided to a body corporate for providing a service; and
  8. Any of the information received under the above clauses by a body corporate for processing, stored or processed under lawful contract or otherwise.

Expressly NOT sensitive personal data or information: such information as is freely available or accessible in the public domain or furnished under the Right to Information Act, 2005 or any other law for the time being in force.

⚖️ Sanjay Dhande — the metadata holding, and why it matters so much

Vodafone argued that it "does not deal with, handle or store the Contents of Voice or Text messages, and hence is not dealing with Personal Sensitive data". The AO rejected this outright, in a passage that is the most quotable in the whole case:

"They also state that they do not have any access to the content of the messages (voice or text) being transmitted over its network and it neither stores or possesses nor handles or deals with the content of the messages (voice or text) being transmitted over its network. This is amusing, given that they store SMSes and MMSes, albeit in transit, and provide interception facilities to Police and others. In fact, even the Metadata, i.e. caller and called number logs, locations, duration and time of call etc. are highly sensitive personal data. Who called suicide helpline, or AIDS helpline, who is calling whom frequently at night, which two phones were in close vicinity for how long, all this is undoubtedly highly sensitive, personal data. The content of voice call or SMS or MMS is obviously still more sensitive. Hence, a Telecommunication company saying that they do not 'handle' sensitive, personal data, is an argument which has no merit at all."

🔍 The exam‑critical nuance — how to phrase the DPDP/s.43A relationship

7.2 Doctrinal continuity — from Rule 3 to the DPDP definition

✅ The continuity point that earns currency marks

The Sanjay Dhande holding that metadata is sensitive personal data foreshadows the DPDP Act's definition of "personal data", which is drafted in terms of data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to that data. The doctrinal move is the same: the law protects identifiability, not merely the content of a communication. Cite Sanjay Dhande when you explain why the DPDP definition is not a radical break but a codification of where the case law had already arrived.

7.3 What the DPDP regime adds that s.43A never had

s.43A / SPDI — what it gave
  • Compensation to the victim — up to ₹5 crore.
  • A specialist forum (AO → TDSAT).
  • A definition of sensitive personal data.
  • A standard (IS/ISO/IEC 27001) and a deemed‑compliance route.
  • An audit obligation for high‑volume entities.
DPDP Act 2023 + Rules 2025 — what it adds
  • Statutory rights for the Data Principal — access, correction, erasure, grievance redressal, nomination.
  • Breach notification — to the affected Data Principal and to the Board, "without undue delay".
  • Purpose limitation and data minimisation — data may be processed only for a specified purpose.
  • Consent management — notice managers for significant data fiduciaries.
  • A dedicated regulator — the Data Protection Board of India.
  • Cross‑border transfer conditions.
⚠️ Common mistake

Ignoring the DPDP Act and Rules when answering post‑2023 problem questions and relying solely on the 2011 SPDI Rules. Examiners are testing both. The correct move is to answer the liability question under s.43A (because that is what the DU cases decide) and then add a currency paragraph on the DPDP regime and its phased commencement.

8Privacy, disclosure & breach of contract — ss.45, 72, 72A, 67C, 79A

Syllabus item 3  The syllabus names ss.45, 72 and 72A expressly, and s.67C for preservation of information by intermediaries. These are the provisions that close the gaps s.43A leaves.

s.72 · PENALTY FOR BREACH OF CONFIDENTIALITY AND PRIVACY · VERBATIM

Penalty for breach of confidentiality and privacy

Any person who, in pursuance of any of the powers conferred under this Act, rules or regulations made thereunder, has secured access to any electronic record, book, register, correspondence, information, document or other material without the consent of the person concerned, discloses such material to any other person, shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, or with both.
Who it binds: a person who has secured access in pursuance of powers conferred under the Act — i.e. an intermediary, a controller, an adjudicating officer's staff, an authorised officer under s.69, or a person exercising a statutory power. It does not reach a private individual who simply hacks an account (that is s.43/s.66).
s.72A · PUNISHMENT FOR DISCLOSURE OF INFORMATION IN BREACH OF LAWFUL CONTRACT · VERBATIM

Punishment for disclosure of information in breach of lawful contract

Save as otherwise provided in this Act or any other law for the time being in force, any person including an intermediary who, while providing services under the terms of lawful contract, has secured access to any material containing personal information about another person, with the intent to cause or knowing that he is likely to cause wrongful loss or wrongful gain, discloses such information to any other person, shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years, or with fine which may extend to five lakh rupees, or with both.
Note the difference from s.72: s.72A (i) applies to a person including an intermediary providing services under a lawful contract; (ii) requires intent to cause or knowledge of likelihood of causing wrongful loss or gain; and (iii) carries the higher penalty of 3 years / ₹5 lakh. Section 72A is the provision that catches the dishonest employee, the collusive insider and the intermediary who sells customer data.
📜 s.45 · RESIDUARY PENALTY

"Where no penalty is otherwise provided for any contravention of the provisions of this Act or of any rule, regulation, direction or order made thereunder, such contravention shall be liable to a penalty which shall not exceed twenty‑five thousand rupees, by way of compensation to the person affected by such contravention, or by way of penalty to the Central Government, as the adjudicating officer may think fit." s.45 is the catch‑all. If a contravention has no specific penalty anywhere in the Act, the AO may award up to ₹25,000 — either as compensation to the victim or as a penalty to the Central Government.

📜 s.67C · PRESERVATION AND RETENTION OF INFORMATION BY INTERMEDIARIES

(1) An intermediary shall preserve and retain such information as may be specified for such duration and in such manner and format as the Central Government may prescribe. (2) The Central Government may prescribe the procedure and the duration for which an intermediary shall retain any information for the purposes of the Act or any other law for the time being in force. Read with the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2026, which intersect with s.79 and s.72A where intermediaries are alleged to have failed data‑retention or preservation duties. Relevant to civil claims against platforms — cross‑reference Unit V.

📜 s.79A · EXAMINER OF ELECTRONIC EVIDENCE

For the purposes of providing expert opinion on electronic‑form evidence before any court or other authority, the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, specify any department, body or agency as an Examiner of Electronic Evidence. Cross‑refers to s.45A of the Evidence Act, 1872 (carried forward as s.45A of the BSA, 2023), which makes the Examiner's opinion a relevant fact and treats the Examiner as an expert. This is the evidentiary bridge between Unit II and Unit III: every s.43/s.43A claim depends on admissible electronic evidence — server logs, CCTV footage, transaction records — and the s.63(4) BSA dual‑certificate/hash‑value requirement now governs the evidentiary threshold before Adjudicating Officers and TDSAT, not merely in criminal trials.

9Case brief 1: Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi

DU CASE‑MAT 1 · s.43  The leading authority that a breach of s.43 does not automatically produce damages. Printed in full in the DU case material at page 11 et seq.

CASE‑MAT 1 · TIER 1 · LEADING AUTHORITY ON s.43 QUANTUM

Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi

W.P.(C) No. 160/2012 Delhi High Court Order dated 27 January 2012 Vipin Sanghi, J. Article 226

1Citation & Bench

Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi, W.P.(C) No. 160/2012, Delhi High Court, order dated 27 January 2012, Vipin Sanghi, J. A petition under Article 226 of the Constitution assailing the order of Sh. Rajesh Aggarwal, Adjudicating Officer under the Information Technology Act, 2000, Government of Maharashtra, in Complaint No. 2/2010. Available at https://it.maharashtra.gov.in/Site/Upload/ACT/Madhvika%20Vs%20Kaushik-highcourt.PDF

2Admission of Facts

The petition was filed under Article 226 to assail an order passed by the Adjudicating Officer, Government of Maharashtra, in Complaint No. 2/2010 preferred by the petitioners. The reason for approaching the High Court was that the Cyber Appellate Tribunal, New Delhi — to which an appeal lay against the impugned order — was bereft of its Chairman and was not in a position to function. Consequently the jurisdiction of the High Court was invoked.

The petitioners had preferred a complaint against, inter alia, Ms. Madhvika Joshi, Respondent No. 1. Petitioner No. 2 was married to Ms. Madhvika Joshi, whereas petitioner No. 1 was her father‑in‑law. Ms. Joshi accessed the e‑mail accounts of both the petitioners and printed chat sessions and e‑mails from those accounts, which she then used to pursue her case filed against the petitioners under s.498A of the Indian Penal Code.

Procedural history (from the CIS analysis of the Maharashtra s.46 cases): the complaint was filed in 2010 for privacy violations committed between 2008 and 2009. The first Adjudicating Officer dismissed the complaint on 9 August 2010, reasoning that, owing to the marriage, there was a relation of mutual trust which resulted in the complainant and his son consensually sharing their passwords. That ruling was appealed to the Cyber Appellate Tribunal, which in a decision of 29 June 2011 found irregularities in the complainant's son's privity to the proceedings and remanded the complaint for re‑adjudication. The re‑adjudication before Shri Rajesh Aggarwal produced the final order of 10 October 2011, which is the subject of the Delhi HC's decision.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the respondent's access to the e‑mail accounts, without permission, constituted a breach of s.43 of the IT Act.
  2. Whether a breach of s.43 automatically entitles the claimants to damages, or whether damage must be independently pleaded and proved.
  3. Whether the Adjudicating Officer — exercising civil‑court‑like powers — had jurisdiction to impose a token fine under s.66C.
  4. Whether the respondent's use of the retrieved material to pursue a s.498A IPC case could give rise to a cause of action for damages against her.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments of the petitioners (husband and father‑in‑law)
  • The respondent had hacked their e‑mail accounts and thereby committed an enumerated act under s.43 — accessing, and downloading, copying or extracting data without permission of the owner or the person in charge.
  • The claim for compensation was framed at ₹50 lakhs: (a) ₹1.03 lakh towards court fee / application money; (b) ₹25,000 towards expenses on lodging and travel to Pune and Mumbai for pursuing the case before the Cyber Crime Cell Pune and the Adjudicating Officer at Mantralaya Mumbai and attending hearing dates; (c) ₹50,000 towards litigation charges paid to counsel; (d) ₹5,000 towards documentation expenses.
  • Heads of loss alleged: being dragged into unwanted litigation by a false s.498A case; the complainant's son kept in eight days' police custody by waving copies of "illegally hacked, fabricated and created" chat sessions; delay of an overseas business assignment to Hong Kong; blocking a client‑site posting to the USA by unauthorised seizure of a passport through the police; blocking business opportunities by deleting business e‑mails regularly for more than 15 months; and damaging reputation in society.
  • An exemplary penalty and costs with rigorous imprisonment were sought, so that "economically well placed employees and companies" would not repeat such practices.
  • The Adjudicating Officer had no jurisdiction to levy a fine under s.66C, since he functions like a civil court, whereas a fine could be imposed only by a criminal court dealing with offences under the Act.
Arguments of the respondent (wife)
  • The right to pursue legal remedies — including under s.498A IPC — cannot be questioned. The invocation of a right available to a person to ventilate grievances does not give a cause of action to claim damages to those against whom action may be initiated.
  • The information retrieved was used only for the purpose of pursuing her case, and for no other purpose. It was not used to malign the petitioners or to cause them any loss; it was given only to the police authorities or the court.
  • The consequences flowing from the registration of the s.498A case are remote and indirect.
  • The petitioners had led no evidence of any damage suffered on account of the retrieval of their e‑mails and chat sessions.
  • Her motive — self‑defence in litigation, not malice — was relevant to the quantum, not the fact, of any breach.

5Matrix of Holding

  • The Adjudicating Officer was right in holding the respondent technically guilty of breaching s.43 — she accessed the e‑mail accounts without permission and downloaded, copied and extracted data from them, squarely within the enumerated acts of s.43.
  • But the claim for compensation would not arise merely on account of the breach of s.43. "The claim would have to be established like in Civil Court, by making requisite averments with regard to the damage suffered, and thereafter by leading evidence in support thereof, to show that one or the other prohibited activities enumerated in Section 43 of the IT Act have resulted in the sufferance of damages by the person concerned."
  • The petitioners had evidently not led any evidence to show what damage they had suffered.
  • The information was used only in the s.498A IPC case and was not made public to malign the petitioners or to hurt their business or reputation; it was provided only to the police authorities or the court.
  • The right to pursue legal remedies under s.498A IPC cannot be questioned. The invocation of such a right does not give a cause of action to claim damages, unless it is eventually found that such complaint or action was filed maliciously.
  • All consequences flowing from the registration of the s.498A case are remote and indirect; in any event it was too premature to conclude that the respondent was accountable for the so‑called damages.
  • The token fine of ₹100 under s.66C was upheld. The Court found no merit in the submission that the AO could not levy it: "The fine has been imposed under Section 66C against the respondent No.1. In case respondent No.1 is aggrieved by the said token fine on the ground that the adjudicating authority did not have jurisdiction to levy the same under Section 66C, it shall be open to her to take appropriate action."
  • Petition dismissed. The Court left the door open: "In case it is held by the concerned Court that the proceedings under Section 498A have been maliciously instituted, it shall be open to the petitioners, at that stage, to take appropriate steps to claim damages against the respondent No.1 for loses or damages directly arising from such conduct of respondent No.1."
Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Section 43 liability is not automatic and not strict in its remedial consequence. A breach establishes the wrong; compensation requires independent proof of loss — pleaded with particularity and proved by evidence, exactly as in a civil court. s.43 is not a strict‑liability damages provision in practice.
  2. Remoteness and indirectness defeat the claim. Consequences flowing from litigation the wrongdoer was entitled to initiate are remote and indirect; liability is confined to directly arising damage.
  3. Exercise of a legal right is not a wrong. Using material in judicial proceedings, where the material is not published to malign or injure, does not give rise to a damages claim, unless the proceedings are ultimately found to be malicious.
  4. The AO's inquiry is summary and its remedial reach is broad. The AO may find a contravention and impose a token penalty; the claimant's remedy is a separate question requiring proof.
  5. Where the appellate forum is non‑functional, Article 226 is available. The petitioner approached the Delhi HC under Article 226 precisely because the Cyber Appellate Tribunal had no Chairman — an institutional failure the Court noted without comment on the merits.

6Application & Significance

Vinod Kaushik is the leading authority on s.43 quantum and is the answer to every problem question that presents unauthorised access without clearly quantified loss. It is also the doctrinal bridge to Unit II: the respondent's access to the accounts turned on the credentials she held, which is the same point made by s.42 (control of private key) and s.3A(2)(b) (exclusive control of authentication data at the time of signing). The integrity of an electronic record and the attribution of an electronic record are both, ultimately, credential‑dependent.

🎓 Exam takeaway — the two‑line answer

"Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi (Delhi HC, 27.01.2012) establishes that a breach of s.43 does not, by itself, entitle the claimant to damages: the claim must be established as in a civil court, by averring and proving the damage suffered. The breach was established, but because the petitioners led no evidence of loss — and because the material was used only in judicial proceedings and not published to malign them — no compensation was awarded."

The answer: (i) yes — the AO's jurisdiction under s.46 is engaged because s.43 applies to access "without permission of the owner", whoever performs it, and the hired person is as liable as the principal; (ii) yes — s.75 provides that the Act applies to an offence or contravention committed outside India by any person irrespective of his nationality; and (iii) yes, subject to s.61's bar and the ₹5 crore ceiling, though a claim about a copyrighted database would also engage the Copyright Act, 1957 (compare Syed Asifuddin, where s.63 of the Copyright Act was invoked alongside s.65 of the IT Act).

10Case brief 2: Amit Dilip Patwardhan v. Rud India Chains Pvt. Ltd.

DU CASE‑MAT · s.43(b) + clean hands  Complaint No. 1 of 2013, decided 15 April 2013 by Shri Rajesh Aggarwal, Adjudicating Officer, Government of Maharashtra. Listed in the DU case material at page 1 et seq.

CASE‑MAT · TIER 2 · S.43(b) & THE CLEAN‑HANDS DOCTRINE

Amit Dilip Patwardhan v. Rud India Chains Pvt. Ltd.

Complaint No. 1 of 2013 Adjudicating Officer, Government of Maharashtra Decided 15 April 2013 Sh. Rajesh Aggarwal

1Citation & Bench

Rajesh Aggarwal (the same Adjudicating Officer who decided Vinod Kaushik and Sanjay Dhande). 2Admission of Facts

The complainant, Amit Dilip Patwardhan, filed a complaint against his ex‑employer, Rud India Chains Pvt. Ltd., for illegally obtaining copies of the complainant's bank account statement. The complainant had left the employ of the respondent to work with a competing business company — but not before colluding with the competing business company and diverting the respondent's customers to them.

For redress, the respondent filed suit for a decree of compensation and led the complainant's bank statements in evidence to prove unlawful gratification. Since the bank statements were obtained electronically by the respondent without the complainant's consent, the jurisdiction of the Adjudicating Officer was invoked.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the respondent's electronic obtaining of the complainant's bank account statements, without his consent, amounted to an enumerated act under s.43 — in particular clause (b) (downloading, copying or extracting data or information).
  2. Whether bank account statements constitute sensitive personal data or information within Rule 3 of the SPDI Rules, 2011.
  3. Whether the complainant — himself at fault for diverting the respondent's customers — was entitled to compensation.
  4. Whether the bank's own failure to protect its depositor's data could be examined when the bank was not a party to the complaint.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments of the complainant
  • His bank account statements were obtained electronically without his consent — an enumerated act under s.43(b).
  • Bank account statements are sensitive personal data within Rule 3(2) (financial information) and Rule 3(7)–(8) (details relating thereto, and information received for processing under lawful contract or otherwise).
  • The respondent misused the statements — leading them in evidence in a suit against him — and thereby invaded his privacy.
  • Compensation was sought for the invasion of privacy and the prejudice caused in the civil suit.
Arguments of the respondent (ex‑employer)
  • The complainant had himself acted dishonestly — colluding with a competing business and diverting the respondent's customers to them.
  • The statements were led in evidence to prove unlawful gratification in a bona fide civil suit for a decree of compensation.
  • The complainant came to the forum with unclean hands; a person who has himself committed a wrong cannot invoke the Act's protective machinery.
  • In any event, no quantifiable loss was established.

5Matrix of Holding

  • The Adjudicating Officer found that the respondent had, by unlawfully obtaining the complainant's bank account statements which constitute sensitive personal data, violated the complainant's privacy — bringing the conduct within s.43.
  • The Adjudicating Officer astutely applied the equitable doctrine of clean hands to deny compensation to the complainant.
  • Because the complainant's bank was not a party to the complaint, the Adjudicating Officer was unable to make a ruling on the lack of action by the bank to protect the sensitive personal data of its depositors — a notable limitation, expressly noted.
  • The net effect was a finding of contravention without a compensatory award — a striking parallel to the outcome in Vinod Kaushik.
Principle evolved
  1. Bank account statements are sensitive personal data or information within Rule 3 of the SPDI Rules, 2011 — financial information, and details relating thereto provided for the purpose of providing a service.
  2. Obtaining them electronically without consent is an enumerated act under s.43(b), irrespective of the purpose for which they were obtained.
  3. The clean‑hands doctrine applies in s.46 adjudication. A complainant who has himself committed a dishonesty against the respondent cannot obtain compensation, even where a contravention is established. The AO's jurisdiction is equitable in character and the remedy is discretionary.
  4. Non‑joinder has real consequences. Where the entity whose security lapse enabled the disclosure (here, the bank) is not made a party, the AO cannot adjudicate its conduct — a practical lesson in drafting the complaint.

6Application & Significance

Amit Patwardhan is the leading illustration of s.43(b) in an employer‑employee context, and of the equitable limits on the s.46 remedy. It is the companion to Vinod Kaushik: both show that a finding of contravention is only half the battle. It is also a direct authority for the proposition that financial information is the paradigm case of sensitive personal data — the head that later decided SBI v. Chander Kalani.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Cite Amit Patwardhan for two propositions: (i) "Bank account statements are sensitive personal data or information under Rule 3 of the SPDI Rules, 2011, and obtaining them electronically without consent attracts s.43(b)"; and (ii) "The Adjudicating Officer applied the equitable doctrine of clean hands to deny compensation to a complainant who had himself diverted the respondent's customers — a contravention may be found without a compensatory award." Pair it with Vinod Kaushik whenever the question asks whether a breach of s.43 automatically yields damages.

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Multiple body corporates in a single transactional chain can each independently attract s.43A liability, apportioned by their relative causal contribution — here ₹6 lakh (ICICI) and ₹12 lakh (Vodafone) against an assessed total of ₹18 lakh.
  2. The onus of securing customer data lies on the body corporate, not on the customer. Where the customer is an experienced user, the case for apportionment against him collapses entirely: "the liability of the loss cannot be passed on to him."
  3. Metadata is sensitive personal data or information. A telecom company cannot escape s.43A by claiming to deal only with carriage and not content; subscriber identity data, call logs, locations, durations and times are sensitive personal data. The SIM is "a veritable key to person's sensitive financial and personal information".
  4. Subscriber verification IS within the scope of "reasonable security practices and procedures" — the AO and, on appeal, TDSAT both rejected the submission that it is not.
  5. A "mere phone call" to the original number is the minimum due diligence expected. A conspicuous, costless omission of that kind is itself proof of the absence of reasonable security practices.
  6. Franchisee failures are the operator's failures. Where the franchisee issued the duplicate SIM and violated the operator's own rules, the operator cannot shelter behind the franchisee's independence.
  7. Regulatory circulars supply the standard of care. RBI KYC/AML/PMLA circulars and RBI's 2011 electronic‑banking guidelines, and DOT SIM‑issuance guidelines, are the benchmarks against which "reasonable security practices and procedures" are measured.

6Application & Significance

Sanjay Dhande is the authority for multi‑defendant s.43A problem questions. Both the Nov–Dec 2024 and May–June 2025 problem questions on s.43A were closely modelled on this fact pattern. It is also the leading Indian authority that metadata is personal data — the proposition that anticipates the DPDP Act's identifiability‑based definition.

TIER 2 · APPEAL BEFORE TDSAT · THE APPELLATE AUTHORITY

Vodafone Idea Ltd v. Sanjay Govind Dhande

2020 SCC OnLine TDSAT 124 Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT) Shiva Kirti Singh, Chairperson s.43(g) and s.43A

1Citation & Bench

Vodafone Idea Ltd v. Sanjay Govind Dhande and others, 2020 SCC OnLine TDSAT 124, decided by the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal under s.57 of the IT Act, Shiva Kirti Singh, Chairperson — the appellate decision affirming the Adjudicating Officer's order of 16 January 2014. The DU case material cites this decision in its table of authorities but does not reproduce it in full; the summary below follows the reported judgment and is flagged accordingly.

2The issues on appeal

  1. Whether Vodafone Idea Ltd. is liable under ss.43(g) and 43A for negligence and failure to maintain reasonable security practices in issuing a duplicate SIM card that facilitated fraudulent transactions.
  2. Whether ICICI Bank Ltd. is liable under s.43A for deficient security measures and failure to detect or prevent unauthorised transactions from the complainants' account.

3Arguments of the parties

Vodafone's arguments on appeal
  • It is merely a service provider under the Department of Telecom licence and does not possess or handle sensitive personal data; hence s.43A does not apply to a telecom service provider.
  • It had a Customer Agreement Form only from one complainant in an individual capacity, not as representative of the other two complainants.
  • Subscriber verification was not part of reasonable security practices and procedures under s.43A.
  • The identity and nature of any sensitive personal data possessed or handled by it had not been established.
  • There was no intentional act and no assistance given to the fraudsters, so no contravention of s.43(g).
ICICI's arguments on appeal
  • The pleas were similar to those raised before the AO — that it had followed the required procedures and had not been negligent in security practices.

4Matrix of Holding

  • The Tribunal noted the AO's detailed record of Vodafone's arguments and found the AO's findings to suffer from no infirmity.
  • The Tribunal reiterated the law as laid down in its earlier judgment dated 20 December 2019 (Bank of India v. Sandeep, Cyber Appeal No. 3 of 2018) and applied it.
  • "In the present case also, on the basis of acts noticed above and the law reiterated by this Tribunal in the aforesaid judgment dated 20.12.2019, Vodafone is found liable under Sections 43(g) and 43 A of the IT Act whereas the ICICI Bank is found deficient in security measures and hence liable under Section 43 A of the IT Act. The appeals are found to be without merits and are dismissed accordingly."
  • The Tribunal also noted the lack of cooperation from the bank during the investigation and the absence of any internal inquiry, and relied on the RBI guidelines on KYC and electronic banking security, concluding that these were violated by the bank.
Principle evolved
  1. A telecom service provider is a body corporate handling sensitive personal data. The "carriage not content" defence fails — the subscriber identity data, KYC documents, call records and location data held by the operator are sensitive personal data or information.
  2. Subscriber verification is part of "reasonable security practices and procedures" within s.43A. Issuing a duplicate SIM without verifying the claimant's identity and authority is a security‑practices failure, not a mere administrative lapse.
  3. Section 43(g) is available against an intermediary whose negligence assisted unauthorised access. Vodafone was held liable not only under s.43A (negligence) but also under s.43(g) — "assisting any person to facilitate access to a computer resource without permission" — an important extension, since the AO had proceeded only under s.43A.
  4. Apportionment by causal contribution is affirmed, with the telecom operator bearing the larger share where the duplicate SIM was the proximate enabling cause.
🎓 Exam takeaway — the s.43A model answer skeleton

For any duplicate‑SIM / OTP‑fraud problem question, run this sequence: (1) identify each body corporate in the chain — telecom operator, bank, platform; (2) apply the Chander Kalani three‑element test to each; (3) plead both s.43A and s.43(g) against the operator; (4) plead the regulatory circulars (RBI KYC, RBI electronic banking guidelines 2011, DOT SIM guidelines) as the standard of care; (5) reject any attempt to pass the loss to the customer where he is not a novice; (6) apportion by causal contribution, giving the operator the larger share where the duplicate SIM was the critical enabler; (7) add interest at 12% compound monthly for delayed payment, as both AOs did; (8) add a currency paragraph on the DPDP Act/Rules 2025.

🎯 The two self‑learning exercises printed with the case — model answers

Exercise 1: "Section 43A makes corporates liable to pay compensation for failure to protect sensitive personal data and information in any computer resource which it owns, controls or operates. Can a telecommunication company part with mobile phone bills with a third party? If so, under what circumstances can they legally do it?"
Hint printed: the SPDI Rules, 2011 and the IT (Intermediaries Guidelines) Rules, 2011 read with ss.67C, 72 and 72A of the IT Act, 2000.

Model answer: Not at all, save in the narrow circumstances where disclosure is lawful. A mobile phone bill is sensitive personal data or information — it contains the subscriber's name and address, the numbers called and the duration, and hence falls within Rule 3(2) and (7) of the SPDI Rules, 2011. Disclosure to a third party engages Rule 6 (publication of information), which requires prior permission of the information provider; Rule 5, which requires written consent for collection and disclosure; and s.72A, which makes it an offence for a person including an intermediary, while providing services under a lawful contract, to disclose material containing personal information about another person with intent to cause, or knowledge of likelihood of causing, wrongful loss or wrongful gain. Lawful disclosure is possible only where (i) the subscriber has given prior written consent; (ii) disclosure is compelled by law — a court order, a statutory demand, or a lawful interception order; (iii) disclosure is to a government agency for the purpose of verification of identity or for investigation under a lawful authority; or (iv) disclosure is required under s.67C and the Intermediary Guidelines, 2021 for preservation and production of information. In every other case, parting with the bill exposes the operator to s.43A compensation and to prosecution under s.72A.

Exercise 2: "Does negligence by a banker in not properly comparing specimen signature on a cheque which leads to wrongful loss to customer make such negligence amenable to the scope of section 43A."
Hint printed: Sanjay Dhande (TDSAT); Bank of India v. Sandeep, Cyber Appeal No. 3 of 2018, TDSAT, decided 20/12/2019.

Model answer: No. TDSAT in SBI v. Chander Kalani (31.07.2018) held expressly that "negligence by a banker in not properly comparing specimen signature with a signature on a cheque may also lead to wrongful loss or wrongful gain but such negligence will not be amenable to the scope of Section 43A." Section 43A reaches only negligence in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures in relation to sensitive personal data or information. Signature verification is an ordinary banking function, and its negligent performance gives rise to a civil claim for breach of contract or negligence — not to s.43A compensation. The position would be different only if the failure to compare the signature were itself the product of a failure of the security practices and procedures required by Rules 4–8 of the SPDI Rules, 2011 — e.g. where the bank's systems permitted an unauthorised person to access the specimen signature record.

Exercise 3 (printed elsewhere in the DU material): "Whether the cyber contraventions/civil liabilities, mentioned in various clauses of Section 43 require intention to cause damage or intention does not matter at all." — answered at §3.4 above: nine of the ten clauses impose liability without any mental element; only clause (j) requires that the act be done "with intent to cause damage or destruction".

CASE‑MAT 3 · TIER 1 · THE DEFINITIVE s.43A JUDGMENT

State Bank of India v. Shri Chander Kalani

Cyber Appeal No. 13 of 2015 TDSAT Decided 31 July 2018 Shiva Kirti Singh, Chairperson s.57 read with ss.43A, 46, 47, 61, 62

1Citation & Bench

State Bank of India v. Shri Chander Kalani, Cyber Appeal No. 13 of 2015, Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal, decided 31 July 2018, Shiva Kirti Singh, Chairperson. An appeal under s.57 of the IT Act against the order dated 12 January 2015 of the Adjudicating Officer, Government of Maharashtra (Principal Secretary, IT) in Complaint No. 1 of 2014. Available at https://it.maharashtra.gov.in/Site/Upload/ACT/SBI-Vs-Chander-Kalani-TDSAT-31July2018_201809061010495837.pdf — printed in full in the DU case material at pages 15–19. The DU syllabus separately lists a later decision in the same dispute, State Bank of India v. Chander Kalani, 2019 SCC OnLine Del 7031 (Delhi High Court), under the same data‑protection heading; the TDSAT judgment reproduced in the case material is the substantive authority and is the one to study in full.

2Admission of Facts

The complainants, Shri Chander Kalani and his wife Smt. Romi Kalani, were senior citizens and Non‑Resident Indians (NRIs), residents of Mumbai with business dealings in Lagos, Nigeria and West Africa. They had an NRI Account with the Bank together with fixed deposits, and six joint fixed deposits with different maturity dates and amounts.

  • Mr. Kalani had, in the past, used his Email identity for directing the Bank to create fixed deposits out of funds available in his savings bank account — but he had confirmed the same through talks from his registered mobile number. The complainants had never sought facilities of internet banking or phone banking, nor permitted in writing or orally any transactions using Email.
  • Towards the end of October 2013, his phone had got defunct and he did not supply any alternative phone/mobile number through which the Bank could recheck or confirm instructions.
  • On 28.10.2013, 06.11.2013, 12.11.2013 and 25.11.2013, fraudulent Emails originating in his name from his Email ID directed the Bank to transfer funds. Three fixed deposits were fraudulently terminated and the money transferred to unknown beneficiaries — approximately ₹63 lakhs. In all, 60,000 GBP was transferred to two other banks.
  • On 13 December 2013, when Mr. Kalani visited the Bank to update his passbook and collect one original FDR, he discovered that the FDs had been fraudulently broken and the money transferred without any authorisation.
  • He complained the same day — 13.12.2013. The Bank then itself took steps to inform the Police as well as the two other banks where, in total, 60,000 GBP had been transferred, and this led to recovery of 16,710 GBP (approx.) from one of the accounts, which was made available to the complainant. The net loss was approximately 43,300 GBP.
  • Complaint No. 1 of 2014 was filed on 30 December 2013, in which two officials of the Bank and unknown persons were also shown as respondents.
  • The Adjudicating Officer examined police investigation reports dated 10.03.2014 and 26.08.2014, which confirmed that the Email ID used by the fraudsters was hacked.

The claim: the complainants claimed ₹1 crore in all. Para 11 of the claim petition showed the first claim as ₹63 lakhs with interest @ 18% per annum from the date of the fraudulent transfer, and the second claim as ₹37 lakhs under heads such as Legal Fees and Expenses, Contraventions, Negligence and Failure to protect Complainants' data.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the Bank, a body corporate, was negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures in respect of sensitive personal data or information, so as to attract s.43A.
  2. Whether the jurisdictional facts of s.43A — possession, negligence and causation — were proved by the claimant, or whether the claim failed for want of proof of the hacking of the Email ID.
  3. Whether the Adjudicating Officer's summary inquiry — without an elaborate trial, oral evidence and cross‑examination — satisfied s.46 and the rules of natural justice.
  4. Whether ss.43(a) and (b) were attracted against the Bank at all, or only against the unknown persons who were beneficiaries of the fraud.
  5. Whether the compensation of ₹40 lakhs was excessive, given the AO's finding that both the complainant and the Bank had to share the blame.
  6. Whether the complainant's own lack of diligence — using Email for banking, failing to use alternate email or SMS alerts, and not informing the Bank of his defunct mobile number — defeated or reduced the claim.

4Examination of Reasoning — the arguments of both sides

Arguments of the appellant Bank (Mr. Ramji Srinivasan, learned senior counsel)
  • Lack of diligence by the complainant: being an NRI he had used his Email identity in the past to direct the Bank to create FDs; at the relevant time his phone was defunct and he supplied no alternative number through which the Bank could recheck or confirm.
  • Belated complaint of hacking: the complaint of hacking of the Email ID was made only on 13.12.2013.
  • Quantum is disproportionate: the loss of approximately 43,300 GBP was not on account of any negligence on the part of the Bank; the award of ₹40 lakhs — equivalent to almost the entire loss — is not just and proper, more so when the AO himself held that both the complainants and the Bank have to share the blame.
  • No reasons: how the Bank is liable to share the blame has not been established by supply of reasons; the compensation should have been less.
  • Expert evidence: the AO should have exercised his wide powers to obtain reports from experts as to whether the Email ID was actually hacked and, if so, by whom.
  • ss.43(a) and (b) not attracted: the complainant referred to those provisions but they are not attracted; s.43A does not provide for compensation unless the facts lead to a conclusion that the body corporate is negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures and that such negligence caused the wrongful loss.
  • No proof of hacking: the claimant filed no proof of hacking of his Email ID, which was necessary because the Bank had not admitted hacking in its pleadings; the jurisdictional facts for the applicability of s.43A were not proved.
  • Procedure: the AO ought to have asked the parties to lead evidence and allowed the other parties to cross‑examine such evidence before giving findings on disputed facts; since the AO has some powers of a civil court and is deemed to be a civil court for certain purposes, he ought to have followed the detailed procedure of a civil trial.
Arguments of the respondents / complainants
  • The Bank's defence has not been substantiated by any material. There is no correspondence or document raising any doubt about the complainant's grievances; the Bank never doubted it in its complaint to the Police, and the police investigation found nothing wrong with the case lodged.
  • The prior use of Email was only for creating fixed deposits, and that too after confirmation through the registered mobile number — never for taking out funds.
  • There was no delay in lodging the complaint: the entire fraudulent transaction came to light only on 13.12.2013 when Mr. Kalani visited the Bank. Even thereafter the Bank acted and recovered 16,710 GBP; had there been any wrong intention, no amount could have been traced or recovered after his complaint.
  • The appellant has nowhere denied the essential material facts — that the Bank transferred the amount; that the complainants never opted for transaction through Email, phone or internet; and that the Bank should have been diligent to cross‑check before acting on the fraudulent Emails.
  • Scanned signature is no signature. For a transfer in foreign exchange, the signature of the account holder on the A‑2 Form is required; the joint account was in the names of husband and wife, yet the Bank accepted the Form with the scanned signature of only one of them. Admittedly, no digital signature of the complainants was used for the fraudulent transaction.
  • The Bank itself supplied the FD details and numbers in reply to the fake Emails, which facilitated the fraud; that supply of information from the Bank's computer system was unauthorised and clearly a negligent act.
  • ss.43(a) and (b) were mentioned because unknown persons who were beneficiaries of the fraud were made parties as unknown; the liability of the Bank is under s.43A, and that is the finding of the AO as well.

5Matrix of Holding — the six holdings that matter

Holding 1 — The summary‑procedure holding (para 11). The Bank's contention that the AO ought to have held an elaborate trial was rejected:

⚖️ Paragraph 11 — quote this on the nature of the AO's inquiry

"In view of last submission it is necessary to notice that under Section 46, the jurisdiction of the Adjudicating Officer is limited to claim not exceeding Rs.5 crore. The rules of natural justice are clearly applicable because subsection (2) of Section 46 requires the Adjudicating Officer to provide a reasonable opportunity to the person alleged to have caused injury or damage, for making representation in the matter and by holding inquiry through this process, if the Adjudicating Officer is satisfied that such person has committed the contravention, he may impose penalty or award such compensation as may be appropriate and in accordance with the provisions of the IT Act. Considering the specific provision of giving reasonable opportunity to make representation and on such inquiry, a finding or satisfaction can be recorded, there is no difficulty in holding that the procedure does not warrant an elaborate trial as is required to be held by a civil court in a suit. No doubt some powers of civil court have been vested in the Adjudicating Officer but these are only to enable the officer to summon or discover relevant materials, whenever necessary for coming to a proper finding in such inquiry. From the powers, it cannot be concluded that the inquiry should be replaced by an elaborate trial like that of a suit in civil court. Such interpretation would defeat the very purpose of providing a summary procedure of inquiry for adjudicating claims upto Rs.5 crore by the Tribunal under the IT Act."

Holding 2 — Proof and pleadings; the burden effectively discharged by the police reports (paras 13, 14).

⚖️ Paragraphs 13–14

"So far as pleadings and requirement of proof of hacking is concerned, it has already been noted that admittedly police report supported the case of the claimant that his Email ID was hacked and no material was produced by the appellant to create any doubt on this issue. The submissions advanced on behalf of the respondent/complainant deserve acceptance. The appellant failed to bring on record any material to rebut the case of the complainant that they had never authorised the Bank to transfer their money to other bank or entities through internet or Emails. Although a submission was advanced that the Bank, as a policy, had extended the facility of banking through Emails to NRI account holders but nothing has been brought on record to support this plea."

"The Emails disclose that the Bank itself leaked‑out certain vital information relating to details and numbers of the Fixed Deposits of the complainants that were becoming due for maturity. The transfer of funds on instructions through Emails and on a scanned signature on A‑2 Form leave no manner of doubt that the Bank was negligent in sharing financial information of the complainants which is covered by Rule 3 which defines sensitive personal data or information under 'The Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules 2011'. Hence it can safely be held that the Bank, a body corporate, while processing, dealing and handling sensitive personal data or information in its computer resource, owned, controlled and operated by it, was negligent in not verifying from the available records that the complainants had never authorised transfer of money or instructions through Email and the act of the Bank in divulging informations relating to complainants' fixed deposits was also a negligent act without implementing 'reasonable security practices and procedures'. Such negligent acts did cause wrongful loss to the complainant. We find no good reasons or materials to reverse the satisfaction arrived at the inquiry as recorded by the learned Adjudicating Officer in the impugned order."

Holding 3 — The three‑element test (paras 15–17) — reproduced verbatim at §5.2 above. The three elements are: (i) the body corporate is possessing, dealing or handling sensitive personal data or information in a computer resource under its ownership, control or operation; (ii) it is negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures; and (iii) such negligence caused wrongful loss or wrongful gain to any person. The signature‑verification carve‑out is part of this holding: "Negligence by a banker in not properly comparing specimen signature with a signature on a cheque may also lead to wrongful loss or wrongful gain but such negligence will not be amenable to the scope of Section 43A."

Holding 4 — The SPDI Rules analysis (paras 18–20) — the most examinable part of the judgment:

⚖️ Paragraphs 18–20 — the Rules analysis

Para 18: "There is no dispute that much before the cause of action arose in the present case, the Central Government had, on 11.04.2011 published the Rules in the Gazette of India in exercise of powers conferred under provisions of the IT Act including Section 43A thereof. Since the Rules came into force from 13.04.2011, the definition of sensitive data or personal information came to include, as per Rule 3, financial information such as Bank Account or Credit Card or Debit Card or other payment instrument details. It also includes any details relating to this clause provided to the Bank for providing service or for processing, whether such information is stored or processed under lawful contract or otherwise. For the case at hand, clauses (ii), (vii) and (viii) of Rule 3 are relevant. The Bank through its computer resource was clearly possessing, dealing and handling sensitive personal data and information of the complainants in its computer resource."

Para 19: "The reasonable security practices and procedures required to be implemented and maintained had to be designed to protect such information from various acts such as unauthorised access and uses. In the present case nothing has been brought on record by the Bank to show that it had implemented and maintained reasonable security practices to protect such information from unauthorised use. Rather the Bank itself used Email received on its computer resource unauthorisedly (without authority from the complainants for such use) for transfer of complainants' money to another bank account. Had there been reasonable security practices and procedures in operation, the concerned personnel could have found out that transfer through Email had not been authorised by the complainants. In fact, no safety or security measure appears to be in place because the transfer was made even without confirmation through registered mobile. The fact that complainant's registered mobile number was not functioning should have worked in his favour because by way of reasonable security practices, the Bank if it had the practice of confirmation through mobile phone of the account holder, ought to have rejected the request for transfer of funds through Email on the valid ground that there was no confirmation available through registered mobile of the account holder. Use of scanned signature on A‑2 Form received through Email was another unauthorised and illegal use of a so called signature which cannot have any sanctity either under the general law or under the IT Act."

Para 20: "Rule 4 requires a body corporate to have in place a policy for privacy and disclosure of information. In the present case in spite of serious allegations of unauthorised use of data and information relating to the complainants, the Bank has failed to bring on record any material to show that it had in place a policy as required by Rule 4. Rule 8 contains provisions for the purpose of finding out as to when a body corporate shall be considered to have complied with reasonable security practices and procedures. The appellant Bank did not bring on record any material to show that it had complied with the provisions for 'reasonable security practices and procedures' in terms of Rule 8."

And the conclusion at para 21: "it is found that the Bank was negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures at the relevant time at least and thereby it caused wrongful loss to the complainants and hence it is liable to pay damages by way of compensation, as held by the learned Adjudicating Officer. All the ingredients for attracting and applying Section 43A of the Act are made out."

Holding 5 — Quantum not reduced by contributory blame (para 22).

⚖️ Paragraph 22 — the apportionment point

"The complainants have claimed damages of Rs.1 crore. Para 11 of the claim petition shows that the first claim is for Rs.63 lakhs with interest @ 18% per annum from the date of fraudulent transfer and the second claim is also for compensation for Rs.37 lakhs under various heads such as Legal Fees and Expenses, Contraventions, Negligence and Failure to protect Complainants' data. In the light of such large claims the learned Adjudicating Officer has not committed any mistake in awarding a compensation of only the actual loss of approximately of Rs.40 lakhs suffered by the complainants. Hence, this plea also is not found acceptable."

The critical nuance: although the AO recorded that "both the Complainant and the Respondent Bank have to share the blame", TDSAT did not reduce the ₹40 lakh award, because the claimant had claimed ₹1 crore and the award covered only the actual loss. Contributory blame is a factor in quantum, not a defence to liability — and it does not automatically halve the award.

Holding 6 — Disposal (para 23). "In the result, we find no merit in the Appeal. It is dismissed accordingly. The amount of compensation awarded to the respondents should be made available to them, if not already made available, within one month from today failing which it shall become payable with interest @ 8% per annum with annual rest, from the date of the order of Adjudicating Officer till the date of realisation. The respondents are also held entitled to Rs.25,000/‑ as consolidated cost of the Appeal. This shall also be payable along with the compensation awarded."

Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Section 43A imposes liability on proof of a three‑element test: (i) possession, dealing or handling of sensitive personal data or information in a computer resource owned, controlled or operated by the body corporate; (ii) negligence in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures; and (iii) causation of wrongful loss or wrongful gain.
  2. The Explanation's three definitions are to be read widely — "body corporate" is not confined to a company incorporated under company law; "reasonable security practices and procedures" covers not merely unauthorised access and damage but also use, modification, disclosure or impairment; "sensitive personal data or information" is that prescribed by the Central Government.
  3. The AO's inquiry is a summary procedure. Section 46 does not warrant an elaborate trial; the civil‑court powers vested in the AO are only to enable him to summon or discover relevant materials. Rules of natural justice are nonetheless applicable, because s.46(2) requires a reasonable opportunity to make representation.
  4. A body corporate that fails to place any material on record showing compliance with Rules 4 and 8 is negligent. The practical burden shifts to the body corporate to demonstrate compliance once the claimant shows the security lapse and the resulting loss.
  5. A non‑functioning registered mobile number works in the claimant's favour. Where the bank's own practice was to confirm through the registered mobile, it ought to have rejected the transfer request in the absence of confirmation.
  6. A scanned signature on an A‑2 Form received through Email is an "unauthorised and illegal use of a so called signature" which has no sanctity either under the general law or under the IT Act. This links Unit II and Unit III: authentication failures are civil‑liability failures.
  7. Financial information — bank account, credit card, debit card or other payment instrument details — is sensitive personal data or information under Rule 3(ii), read with clauses (vii) and (viii).
  8. Contributory blame does not automatically reduce compensation, and does not defeat the claim where the award is confined to the actual loss out of a much larger claim.

6Application & Significance

Chander Kalani is the touchstone for every s.43A problem question. It is the case to cite for the elements of the section, for the wide reading of the Explanation, for the Rule 3/4/8 analysis, for the summary nature of the AO's procedure, and for the proposition that the onus of proving compliance rests practically on the body corporate. Read with Sanjay Dhande it gives the complete s.43A architecture.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the Bank was negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures in respect of the complainants' sensitive personal data or information.
  2. Whether the complainants' own conduct — conducting transactions only through emails, which is "an insecure way of doing things", not using alternate email or SMS alerts, and not informing the Bank of the defunct mobile number — made them share the blame.
  3. What compensation was appropriate, and on what heads.

4Matrix of Holding — the operative paragraph 6

📜 The AO's final conclusions, verbatim (as reproduced in the TDSAT judgment)

1. "Complainant has been doing transactions with the banks only through emails, which is insecure way of doing things. Mechanisms like alternate email, SMS alerts etc. were not used. Complainant had not informed the bank about his defunct mobile number. Hence, both the Complainant and the Respondent Bank have to share the blame."

2. "I hold the Respondent No. 1 (State Bank of India) in violation of Section 43A of the IT Act, and order them to a compensation of Rupees Forty Lakhs to the Complainant to partly cover his loss, within a month of this order, failing which compound interest of 12 percent compounded monthly will also be chargeable."

Two features of the AO's order are important and are often missed. First, the award was framed as compensation "to partly cover his loss" — the AO was consciously not awarding the whole ₹63 lakh. Second, the finding of shared blame was recorded, but the quantum was set at approximately the actual loss of ₹40 lakhs; TDSAT later held that this was unexceptionable given the ₹1 crore claim. Note also that the AO examined the police investigation reports dated 10.03.2014 and 26.08.2014 before arriving at his findings.

Principle evolved
  1. A bank that acts on unverified email instructions, with a scanned signature on the A‑2 Form of only one joint account holder, and without confirmation through the registered mobile, is negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures.
  2. The customer's own insecure practices (transacting only by email; not using alternate email or SMS alerts; not informing the bank of a defunct mobile number) may result in shared blame — but this operates on quantum and does not exonerate the body corporate.
  3. Compensation may be awarded on multiple heads — the claim here included legal fees and expenses, contraventions, negligence and failure to protect the complainant's data — and is not confined to the pecuniary loss alone.
🎓 Exam takeaway — the answer skeleton for any s.43A problem question

Step 1: identify the body corporate. Step 2: apply the three‑element test from Chander Kalani para 16. Step 3: identify the sensitive personal data under Rule 3 — financial information is the usual answer. Step 4: test compliance with Rules 4, 5, 7 and 8; where nothing is on record, negligence follows. Step 5: establish causation. Step 6: address contributory blame, and remember it operates on quantum only. Step 7: fix compensation within the ₹5 crore ceiling, considering s.47's factors, and add interest. Step 8: add a currency paragraph on the DPDP Act 2023 and DPDP Rules 2025.

13Online defamation

Syllabus item 5  Defamation is not a creature of the IT Act. It is a common‑law tort (and, in its criminal limb, a codified offence) that has acquired a new medium. The IT Act's contribution is procedural and evidential — it makes the publication of a defamatory imputation through a computer resource traceable, and it makes the platform that carries it potentially answerable.

🔍 The framing sentence to open your answer with

"Online defamation is the publication of a defamatory imputation concerning an identifiable person through a computer resource — an e‑mail, a website, a social‑media post, a comment, a review, a video, or a message on a messaging platform. The substance of the wrong is governed by the ordinary law of defamation; the medium alters the analysis in four specific ways: (i) the number of publications; (ii) the identification of the publisher; (iii) the choice of forum; and (iv) the availability of an injunction against an intermediary."

13.1 The essentials — unchanged, but applied differently online

EssentialThe ordinary requirementWhat changes online
1 · Defamatory imputationThe words must tend to lower the claimant in the estimation of right‑thinking members of society generally, or to expose him to hatred, contempt or ridicule, or to cause him to be shunned or avoided. Innuendo and secondary meaning count.The imputation is judged by how an ordinary, reasonable user of the platform would understand it. Memes, hashtags, emojis, screenshots and "quote tweets" can all carry defamatory meaning; the test remains the ordinary reader, not the specialist.
2 · PublicationCommunication to at least one person other than the claimant.Every download, view, share or re‑tweet is potentially a fresh publication. Each re‑publication by a third party may create a new cause of action, subject to the rules on innocent dissemination and the safe harbour.
3 · IdentificationThe imputation must refer to the claimant, expressly or by innuendo; a reasonable person must understand it as referring to him.Anonymous publication is the norm online. The claimant must then either use a John Doe / Ashok Kumar order (the Indian equivalent of a Norwich Pharmacal order) or obtain subscriber details from the intermediary or from the platform.
4 · Damage to reputationIn India, damage is presumed in libel; in slander, damage must generally be proved (save in the slander‑actionable‑per‑se categories, which now include imputations of unchastity to a woman under s.499 Explanation 2 IPC).Online publication is generally treated as libel (permanent form), so damage is presumed — but the claimant must still prove the quantum of loss if he seeks damages, and must show the imputation reached the relevant audience.
5 · Absence of a defenceTruth (justification), fair comment, privilege (absolute and qualified), consent, apology, accord and satisfaction.Fair comment is the most litigated online: the line between criticism of a public figure and a defamatory imputation is drawn by asking whether the statement is one of fact or of opinion. Truth is a complete defence but must be substantially proved.
✅ Real examples — how the analysis runs
  • A one‑star Google review alleging that a restaurant served contaminated food: libel; damage presumed; the defence would be fair comment or truth; the platform may be joined for takedown.
  • A WhatsApp message forwarded to 200 contacts alleging that a colleague embezzled funds: each recipient is a publication; the sender is liable; the platform's safe harbour depends on whether it acted expeditiously on a takedown request.
  • An anonymous Twitter/X account alleging corruption by a company director: identification may be by innuendo; a John Doe order will be sought against the platform and search engine to identify the account holder.
  • A YouTube video alleging that a doctor caused a patient's death through negligence: both defamation and, potentially, a claim under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019; the intermediary must take down or restrict on a court order or government notification under Rule 3(1)(b) of the IT Rules, 2021.
  • A blog post alleging that a company's product is unsafe: the company must show the statement is false and not fair comment on a matter of public interest; the honest‑opinion defence is strong where the underlying facts are stated.

13.2 The four ways the medium changes the law

(a) Multiple and repeated publication

Every re‑tweet, share, re‑post or forward is a fresh publication, potentially generating a fresh cause of action and restarting limitation. This is a double‑edged sword: it extends liability to re‑publishers, but it also defeats the "once published" argument for takedown — content that is taken down may already have been mirrored across the internet.

(b) Anonymous publication

Indian courts have developed the practice of granting John Doe / Ashok Kumar orders and dynamic injunctions, and of directing intermediaries and search engines to de‑index or disclose subscriber information. The claimant's first move is therefore procedural: identify the publisher, then sue.

(c) Jurisdiction

Because publication is worldwide, the claimant may sue where the imputation was published, where it was read, or where the claimant's reputation was damaged. Section 46 adjudication is territorially limited to claims up to ₹5 crore; s.75 gives extra‑territorial reach to contraventions committed outside India by any person irrespective of nationality. Cross‑refers to Unit IX (Jurisdiction).

(d) Injunction against an intermediary

Section 79 of the IT Act exempts an intermediary from liability for third‑party information hosted on its platform, subject to the conditions of the Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code Rules, 2021 (as amended in 2026) — including the duty to act expeditiously (within 36 hours) on receiving actual knowledge in the form of a court order or a government notification, and to remove or disable access to the specified content. Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) 5 SCC 1 struck down s.66A and read down s.69A and s.79(3)(b); Swami Ramdev v. Facebook Inc. (Delhi HC, 2019) ordered global takedown of defamatory content once it was removed from Indian servers. Cross‑refers to Unit V.

Saying that online defamation is "an offence under s.66A of the IT Act". Section 66A was struck down as unconstitutional in Shreya Singhal v. CASE‑MAT 4 · TIER 2 · INDIA'S FIRST CYBER‑DEFAMATION CASE

SMC Pneumatics (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Jogesh Kwatra

Suit No. 1201/2001 Renumbered as New Suit No. 65/14 Delhi District Court Decided 12 February 2014 Ex parte ad interim injunction

1Citation & Bench

SMC Pneumatics (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Jogesh Kwatra, Suit No. 1201/2001, renumbered as New Suit No. 65/14, decided by the Delhi District Court on 12 February 2014. The order is reported at http://www.indiankanoon.org/doc/149167816/ and is cited in the DU case material's table of authorities. The DU case material does not reproduce the order in full; the brief below follows the reported order and the DU case‑material summary, and that provenance is flagged.

2Admission of Facts

The plaintiff, SMC Pneumatics (India) Pvt. Ltd., was a company carrying on business in pneumatics, with subsidiaries and offices across the world. The defendant, Jogesh Kwatra, was an ex‑employee of the plaintiff company. After leaving the plaintiff's employment, the defendant allegedly began sending defamatory, vulgar and derogatory e‑mails to the plaintiff's management and, more seriously, to the plaintiff's subsidiaries and associates worldwide, with the effect of damaging the plaintiff's business reputation. The plaintiff accordingly sued for a permanent injunction restraining the defendant from sending such e‑mails, and for damages.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the plaintiff had made out a prima facie case of defamation through electronic communication, so as to warrant interlocutory relief.
  2. Whether the balance of convenience lay in favour of restraining the defendant pending trial.
  3. Whether the plaintiff would suffer irreparable injury that could not be compensated in damages, given that the reputational harm was spreading to subsidiaries across the world.
  4. Whether equitable relief is available against ongoing cyber‑defamatory conduct before a full trial on the merits.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments of the plaintiff company
  • The defendant was a former employee who was using e‑mail — a company‑independent, instantaneous and cheap medium — to send defamatory, vulgar and derogatory communications.
  • The communications were addressed not merely to the plaintiff's management but to its subsidiaries and business associates worldwide, magnifying the reputational injury far beyond a conventional workplace grievance.
  • The conduct was continuing: unless restrained, the defendant would send further e‑mails, and the injury would multiply.
  • The injury was irreparable — once defamatory statements have been transmitted to subsidiaries across the world, they cannot be recalled and the damage to commercial reputation cannot be adequately compensated in money.
  • The balance of convenience lay overwhelmingly with the plaintiff: restraining the defendant from sending further e‑mails caused him no legitimate loss, whereas non‑restraint exposed the plaintiff to continuing harm.
Arguments of the defendant
  • The e‑mails were communications by a former employee about matters within his knowledge and were either true or fair comment on the plaintiff's conduct.
  • Any grievance he had was legitimate — arising out of his employment — and the plaintiff's claim was an attempt to silence him.
  • An ex parte order restraining him was oppressive; at the interim stage the court should not pre‑judge the truth or falsity of the statements.
  • The plaintiff had not shown irreparable injury that money could not compensate.

5Matrix of Holding

The Delhi District Court granted an ex parte ad interim injunction, restraining the defendant from sending defamatory, derogatory or abusive e‑mails to the plaintiff or to its subsidiaries. The Court found:

  • a prima facie case of defamation through electronic communication;
  • that the balance of convenience lay with the plaintiff, since restraint caused the defendant no legitimate prejudice while non‑restraint exposed the plaintiff to continuing worldwide reputational harm; and
  • that the injury was irreparable and incapable of adequate monetary compensation, given the continuing nature of the conduct and the global reach of the e‑mails.
Principle evolved
  1. Equitable relief is available against cyber‑defamatory conduct even before full trial. The ordinary principles governing interlocutory injunctions — prima facie case, balance of convenience, irreparable injury — apply without modification to defamation committed through e‑mail.
  2. The global reach of electronic publication aggravates the injury and strengthens the case for an injunction. Because the e‑mails went to subsidiaries worldwide, the injury was not confined to a single workplace and could not be undone.
  3. Continuing wrongs warrant continuing restraint. Where the wrong is ongoing rather than a single completed act, the injunction is the natural remedy.
  4. Injunctive relief is a remedy distinct from, and additional to, monetary compensation under ss.43 / 43A — the claimant does not have to elect between them.

6Application & Significance

SMC Pneumatics is India's first reported cyber‑defamation case. It is important to note, with the DU case material, that the case did not proceed to a reasoned final judgment on the merits — it is cited chiefly for the ex parte ad interim injunction. Its enduring significance is that it established, at the very outset of Indian cyber‑law, that equitable relief is available against cyber‑defamatory conduct, independent of the criminal remedies then available under s.499 IPC and of any monetary compensation under the IT Act. It is also the doctrinal ancestor of the modern practice of John Doe / Ashok Kumar orders, dynamic injunctions and de‑indexing directions against platforms and search engines.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Use SMC Pneumatics whenever the question asks about remedies for online defamation or about civil remedies outside the IT Act. The correct formulation: "SMC Pneumatics (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Jogesh Kwatra (Delhi District Court, 12.02.2014) is India's first reported cyber‑defamation case. The Court granted an ex parte ad interim injunction restraining an ex‑employee from sending defamatory and derogatory e‑mails to the plaintiff company and its subsidiaries worldwide, holding that the ordinary principles governing interlocutory injunctions apply to electronic publication, and that the global and continuing nature of the wrong made the injury irreparable and not adequately compensable in damages."

14.2 Critical analysis and doctrinal debates

🔍 Debate 1 — s.43A's "awkward middle ground"

"Section 43A occupies an awkward middle ground between strict liability and ordinary negligence. Unlike s.43 (which imposes liability essentially on proof of the enumerated unauthorised act, subject to Vinod Kaushik's requirement that damages still be independently proved), s.43A requires the claimant to establish a genuine negligence case against the body corporate — a significantly higher evidentiary bar for individual data‑breach victims, who rarely have the technical capacity to demonstrate what 'reasonable security practices' were not followed."

"TDSAT's jurisprudence (Chander Kalani, Sanjay Dhande, and the later Bank of India v. Sandeep line) has, in practice, mitigated this by treating conspicuous procedural failures (no confirmatory call, unverified KYC, no fraud‑analytics flags) as prima facie evidence of the absence of reasonable security practices — effectively shifting the practical burden onto the body corporate to demonstrate compliance with the SPDI Rules / Rule 8 framework, once the claimant shows the security lapse."

"Whether the DPDP Act, 2023's more explicitly rights‑based, obligation‑forward architecture (with statutory breach‑notification duties and a dedicated Data Protection Board) will displace this judicially developed burden‑shifting approach, or simply run in parallel with residual s.43A litigation for pre‑DPDP conduct, is the single most important open question in this area as of 2026."

🔍 Debate 2 — Apportionment has no statutory formula

Sanjay Dhande apportions liability between two body corporates (bank and telecom operator) in a single fraud chain by relative causal contribution — but the IT Act contains no explicit statutory apportionment formula, leaving Adjudicating Officers and TDSAT to develop apportionment ad hoc. This is an area ripe for legislative codification. In an essay, note the parallel with the common law's approach to joint and several tortfeasors and the Law Reform (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1937 idea of contribution between concurrent wrongdoers — the AO in Dhande arrived at a proportionate result without any statutory warrant for it.

🔍 Debate 3 — The AO's institutional competence

Chander Kalani confirms a summary procedure, but critics — including Amlan Mohanty, whose work appears in the syllabus's own e‑Reading list — query whether Adjudicating Officers, typically state IT Secretaries without dedicated judicial training, are institutionally equipped to adjudicate technically complex, high‑value claims up to ₹5 crore. The point is sharpened by the prolonged vacancy of the Cyber Appellate Tribunal Chairmanship — a live problem noted in Sanjay Dhande itself ("the post of Chairman, Cyber Appellate Tribunal is vacant for more than three years") and in Vinod Kaushik, where the petitioner had to approach the Delhi High Court under Article 226 precisely because the Tribunal was non‑functional. The institutional answer arrived at in 2017 was the abolition of the Cyber Appellate Tribunal and the transfer of its functions to TDSAT under the TRAI Act, 1997 — which at least secured a functioning, technically staffed appellate forum, but did not address the composition of the first‑tier adjudicatory office.

🔍 Debate 4 — Compensation, damages and the "sui generis" label

The AO's power under s.47 is to award compensation, not damages. That distinction matters: damages are awarded on proof of loss under established principles; compensation is a statutory, discretionary remedy designed to be accessible and summary. The CIS analysis of the Maharashtra s.48/s.46 cases records the doctrinal excursus: Indian courts have drawn on Shantilal Mangaldas & Co. v. Shantilal Sheth, AIR 1969 SC 634 (compensation is not limited to the precise loss proved), Ranbir Kumar Arora v. Union of India, AIR 1983 P&H 431, and the House of Lords in The Owners of the Steamship "Mediana" v. Owners of Cargo lately laden on board the "Halley" [1900] AC 113 (the inquiry is directed to compensation for the loss actually suffered, not to punishing the wrongdoer). In Sanjay Dhande the AO's ₹18 lakh figure expressly included "loss and legal fees etc.", which shows the remedial reach extending beyond pure pecuniary loss.

🔍 Debate 5 — Privacy as a constitutional right versus a statutory remedy

The privacy provisions in Unit III sit on top of a constitutional foundation. Kharak Singh v. State of U.P., AIR 1963 SC 1295 and Gobind v. State of M.P., AIR 1975 SC 1378 recognised a right to privacy under Article 21; K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1 (nine‑Judge Bench, 24 August 2017) held privacy to be a fundamental right intrinsic to life and liberty, expressly acknowledging that informational privacy is a species of it. The background to Amit Patwardhan is People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India, (1997) 1 SCC 301 (tapping of telephones) and Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, (1978) 1 SCC 248. The point for an essay: ss.43A, 72 and 72A give statutory, compensatory effect to a constitutional value, and the constitutional right supplies the interpretive lens where the statute is ambiguous.

PYQ 1 "A's account is debited after her SIM is duplicated due to Y's (telecom) negligence; X (bank) claims it sent SMS alerts; Y claims it does not handle 'content' and hence not personal data. Decide." May–June 2025, Q.2 · 12–14 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — Identify the legal basis. This is a s.43A question, not a s.43 question. Both X (the bank) and Y (the telecom operator) are body corporates within the Explanation to s.43A — "any company" and, inclusively, a firm, sole proprietorship or other association of individuals engaged in commercial or professional activities. Section 43, which applies to any person and to any computer resource, is not the primary provision, though the fraudster's own conduct would attract s.43(a) and (b) if he could be identified and joined.

Step 2 — Apply the three‑element test of State Bank of India v. Chander Kalani, 2020 (TDSAT, 31.07.2018) to each respondent.

(i) Possession / dealing / handling of sensitive personal data in its own computer resource. As to X: A's account details, account numbers and financial information are sensitive personal data or information within Rule 3(ii) of the SPDI Rules, 2011 — "financial information such as bank account or credit card or debit card or other payment instrument details" — read with clauses (vii) and (viii), which extend to any detail relating thereto provided for providing a service, and to information received for processing under a lawful contract. The bank was "clearly possessing, dealing and handling" that data in its own computer resource. As to Y: the subscriber's identity data, KYC documents, call records, and the fact that the registered mobile number is used by the bank to identify the customer and to generate One Time Passwords. In Sanjay Dhande v. ICICI Bank; Vodafone (AO, 16.01.2014) the Adjudicating Officer held that a SIM card "is a veritable key to person's sensitive financial and personal information", and that "even the Metadata, i.e. caller and called number logs, locations, duration and time of call etc. are highly sensitive personal data".

(ii) Negligence in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures. As to Y: the duplication of A's SIM on forged documents, without verifying the photograph and signature against the original KYC documents, without taking a receipt for the duplicate‑SIM fee, and — the decisive point — without a mere phone call to A's original number, which the AO in Dhande described as "the minimum due diligence one would expect". That single omission, had it been performed, would have averted the entire loss. Y's liability is aggravated because the duplicate SIM was the proximate enabling cause of the fraud: the OTPs and transaction alerts were diverted to it. As to X: the bank's conduct must be tested against the same standard. Sending SMS alerts is not a security practice that discharges the duty, because the alerts were sent to a number the fraudster controlled. The relevant questions are whether the bank had in place the practices required by Rule 4 (a published privacy policy) and Rule 8 (IS/ISO/IEC 27001 or another notified standard, or an approved self‑regulatory code, with an independent audit for high‑volume entities); whether it had real‑time fraud analytics capable of flagging anomalous transactions; whether it had a Fraud Investigation Unit; and whether it complied with the RBI Master Circular on KYC/AML/CFT/PMLA dated 2 July 2012 (para 2.8 on Money Mule Accounts) and the RBI Guidelines on Information Security, Electronic Banking, Technology Risk Management and Cyber Frauds dated 29 April 2011. In Dhande the bank was found deficient on every one of these counts.

(iii) Causation. There is an "undeniable direct nexus" — in the AO's words — between the blocking of A's SIM, the issuance and use of the duplicate SIM by the fraudster, and the unauthorised debits. The bank transactions happened after the duplicate SIM was procured and activated.

Step 3 — Dispose of Y's "content" argument. It is without merit. In Sanjay Dhande the Adjudicating Officer held, of the identical argument, that "a Telecommunication company saying that they do not 'handle' sensitive, personal data, is an argument which has no merit at all", for two reasons: (a) the operator does deal with the content — it stores SMSes and MMSes, albeit in transit, and provides interception facilities to the police; and (b) even if it did not, metadata — caller and called number logs, locations, duration and time of call — is itself highly sensitive personal data. On appeal in Vodafone Idea Ltd v. Sanjay Govind Dhande, 2020 SCC OnLine TDSAT 124, TDSAT went further and held the operator liable under both s.43A and s.43(g) (assisting any person to facilitate access to a computer resource without permission), rejecting the submission that subscriber verification is not part of "reasonable security practices and procedures".

Step 4 — Apportionment. Section 43A contains no apportionment formula. Following Sanjay Dhande, liability is apportioned by relative causal contribution. In Dhande, out of an assessed total compensation of ₹18 lakhs, the AO directed the bank to pay ₹6,00,000 and the telecom operator ₹12,00,000, because "Vodafone must share bigger blame because duplicate SIM card played most critical role in this crime". On these facts, Y bears the larger share and X a smaller one, because the duplicate SIM was the enabling cause; the bank's failure is one of security practices and analytics, not of the primary enabler.

Step 5 — Contributory conduct of A. There is nothing to suggest A was at fault; in any event, the loss cannot be passed to a customer who did not divulge her details to the criminals. Contributory blame in any event operates on quantum, not liability — see Chander Kalani, where the AO's finding that "both the Complainant and the Respondent Bank have to share the blame" did not reduce the ₹40 lakh award.

Step 6 — Procedure and remedy. A may file a complaint under s.46 before the Adjudicating Officer, whose jurisdiction extends to claims not exceeding ₹5 crore (s.46(1A)); the inquiry is a summary procedure, requiring only a reasonable opportunity of representation under s.46(2), not an elaborate civil trial (Chander Kalani, para 11). An appeal lies to TDSAT under s.57 within 45 days, and thereafter to the High Court under s.62 within 60 days. The AO should direct payment within a month, failing which compound interest at 12% compounded monthly is chargeable — the standard direction in both Dhande and Chander Kalani.

Step 7 — Currency paragraph. A should also be advised of her rights as a Data Principal under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — access, correction and erasure, grievance redressal and nomination — and of the breach‑notification obligations imposed on the bank and the operator as Data Fiduciaries by the DPDP Rules, 2025 (notified 13 November 2025; phased 12–18‑month compliance; full commencement targeted 13 May 2027). Section 43A continues to govern the claim itself, since the conduct preceded full DPDP commencement.

Conclusion: Both X and Y are liable under s.43A. Y bears the larger share of the compensation, apportioned by causal contribution, because the duplicate SIM was the proximate enabling cause of the fraud; X is liable for the failure of its security practices, real‑time analytics and fraud‑investigation machinery. Y's "content" defence fails because metadata is sensitive personal data. A is not to be blamed.
PYQ 2 "X's SIM is stopped for two days due to public holidays; fraudsters obtain a duplicate SIM and transact ₹80 lakh. Discuss liability of Bank and telecom Service Provider." Nov–Dec 2024, Q.3(b) · 10–12 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — The governing provision is s.43A, read with the Explanation and the SPDI Rules, 2011. Both the bank and the telecom service provider are body corporates.

Step 2 — The Chander Kalani three‑element test is applied to each. The pattern is directly parallel to Sanjay Dhande, and the examiner expects you to say so expressly and to follow the same apportionment analysis.

Step 3 — The telecom service provider. The failure is the issuance of a duplicate SIM on forged documents. The relevant omissions, drawn from Dhande: failure to check the photograph on the forged licence against the database; failure to match the signature; the online File Net system being down; the applicant returning in ten minutes with forged details and a photograph printed on ordinary scanned paper; no payment receipt for the duplicate‑SIM fee; the store manager backdating his signature; and — the decisive point — failure to make a single phone call to the original number to confirm. The registered mobile number is the identifier the bank uses for alerts and OTPs; a duplicate SIM therefore hands the fraudster the keys to the account. In Dhande the AO held that "a mere phone call on the Complainant's mobile number, which is the minimum due diligence one would expect, could have averted the difficulties and agony suffered by the Complainant".

Step 4 — The bank. Its liability is for the failure of security practices and procedures, measured against the RBI instruments relied on in Dhande: the KYC/AML/CFT/PMLA Master Circular dated 2 July 2012 (para 2.8 on Money Mule Accounts), and the Guidelines on Information Security, Electronic Banking, Technology Risk Management and Cyber Frauds dated 29 April 2011 (fraud risk management; transaction monitoring; a dedicated e‑mail ID and phone number for reporting suspected frauds; mystery shopping; ready sharing of data with the police; customer awareness). The specific failures typically present in this fact pattern are: no real‑time fraud analytics flagging the anomalous transactions; a non‑existent Fraud Investigation Unit; non‑cooperation with the police; and the absence of any material on record to show compliance with Rules 4 and 8 of the SPDI Rules.

Step 5 — The "public holidays" point is a red herring, but a useful one. The fact that the SIM was stopped "for two days due to public holidays" does not reduce the provider's liability; if anything it aggravates it, because the provider knew the number was inactive and nonetheless issued a duplicate SIM to a third party. Note the converse in Chander Kalani: where the complainant's registered mobile was defunct, TDSAT held that the fact "should have worked in his favour", because a bank that had a practice of confirming through the registered mobile ought to have rejected the transfer request in the absence of confirmation.

Step 6 — Apportionment and quantum. Following Dhande, the telecom service provider bears the larger share, because the duplicate SIM was the critical enabler; the bank's share is smaller. In Dhande the split was ₹12 lakh (operator) and ₹6 lakh (bank) out of an assessed total of ₹18 lakhs, reflecting a loss of ₹19,01,073.16 reduced by ₹3.26 lakh recovered. Here, on a loss of ₹80 lakh, a proportionate apportionment on the same 2:1 ratio would be approximately ₹53.3 lakh against the telecom service provider and ₹26.7 lakh against the bank — well within the ₹5 crore ceiling in s.46(1A) and s.43A. Add interest at 12% compound monthly for delayed payment.

Step 7 — Parallel criminal liability. The same facts may attract s.66 (computer‑related offences committed dishonestly or fraudulently) and, where the fraudster assumed X's identity by using the duplicated SIM, s.66C (identity theft). Section 43A is civil and compensatory; the criminal provisions are separate and cumulative. Cross‑refers to Unit IV.

Conclusion: Both the bank and the telecom service provider are liable under s.43A, apportioned by causal contribution with the larger share falling on the telecom service provider. The public‑holiday stoppage of the SIM does not excuse the provider; it aggravates the position. The bank's liability rests on the failure of its security practices, analytics and fraud‑investigation machinery.
PYQ 3 "Ex‑employee uses undeactivated login credentials to access and leak client data. Discuss liability of the company and the ex‑employee." Nov–Dec 2025, Q.2 · 12–15 marks · Problem question
Model answer

Step 1 — Two separate defendants, two separate provisions. This question deliberately presents one s.43 defendant (the ex‑employee, an individual) and one s.43A defendant (the company, a body corporate). Do not conflate them.

Step 2 — The ex‑employee: s.43, and specifically clause (a) read with clause (b).

  • Clause (a): "accesses or secures access to such computer, computer system or computer network or resource without permission of the owner or any other person who is in charge of a computer, computer system or computer network or resource". The ex‑employee's employment having ended, he no longer has permission. The fact that the credentials are technically valid but undeactivated is irrelevant: permission is the criterion, and permission has ceased. This is the point the question is testing.
  • Clause (b): "downloads, copies or extracts any data, data base or information from such computer, computer system or computer network or resource" — the client data he removes.
  • Clause (e) may also be engaged if he "disrupts or causes disruption of" the system; clause (g) if he "assists any person to facilitate access"; clause (j) if he "steals, conceals, destroys or alters or causes any person to steal, conceal, destroy or alter any information residing in a computer resource ... intending thereby to cause damage" — note that clause (j) alone requires intention to cause damage or destruction, which is plainly present here.
  • Quantum: apply Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi (Delhi HC, 27.01.2012): a breach of s.43 does not automatically produce damages. The claim "would have to be established like in Civil Court, by making requisite averments with regard to the damage suffered, and thereafter by leading evidence in support thereof". The company must therefore plead and prove its loss — the value of the client data, the business lost, the cost of investigation and remediation, and legal expenses.

Step 3 — The company: s.43A, applying the Chander Kalani three‑element test.

  • Element (i): the company, a body corporate, possesses, deals or handles sensitive personal data or information — the client data — in a computer resource it owns, controls or operates. Client data will typically include financial information, passwords and biometric data within Rule 3.
  • Element (ii): the company was negligent in implementing and maintaining reasonable security practices and procedures. The failure to deactivate the ex‑employee's credentials is itself a failure of reasonable security practices and procedures. This is the crucial move: the question asks you to recognise that access‑control hygiene is a security practice, not merely an HR function. In Chander Kalani the absence of any material showing compliance with Rule 8 was fatal to the bank; in Sanjay Dhande the absence of real‑time fraud analytics and a functioning Fraud Investigation Unit was fatal to the bank. A company that leaves dormant credentials live has no Rule 8 standard to point to.
  • Element (iii): the negligence caused wrongful loss — the leak of client data and the resulting commercial injury.
  • Result: the company is liable to pay compensation to its clients (and, in an appropriate case, for its own loss) up to the ₹5 crore ceiling. Note that in Sanjay Dhande the AO's reasoning expressly treated the onus as resting on the body corporate: "while the customers are expected to use their discretion to secure their net banking / mobile banking IDs and passwords, the onus of securing customer's data is on the banks".

Step 4 — The criminal‑adjacent and contractual limbs. The ex‑employee's conduct additionally attracts: s.66 (dishonest or fraudulent commission of the s.43 acts); s.66C (identity theft) if he used another's electronic signature, password or unique identification feature; s.43A is not available against him because he is an individual, not a body corporate; s.72A is available if, while providing services under the terms of a lawful contract (his employment contract, and any confidentiality or non‑disclosure covenant), he secured access to material containing personal information about another person and disclosed it with intent to cause, or knowing that he was likely to cause, wrongful loss or wrongful gain — punishable with imprisonment up to three years or fine up to ₹5 lakh, or both. Contractual remedies (breach of the confidentiality covenant; breach of the duty of fidelity owed by a former employee) and, in an appropriate case, the law of breach of confidence run in parallel.

Step 5 — The evidentiary point that decides the case in practice. The company's claim will stand or fall on admissible electronic evidence: server access logs, authentication records, the leak's transmission trail. Under the Bhartiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, s.63(4), the certificate requirement is now a dual certificate — from the device custodian and the expert — accompanied by a hash value, which materially raises the bar beyond the old single‑certificate regime. See Unit II §8 and Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer, (2014) 10 SCC 473.

Conclusion: The ex‑employee is liable under s.43(a) and (b) — permission having ceased on the termination of employment, the use of live credentials is unauthorised access — and, having leaked the data, under s.43(j) (which requires, and here admits of, intention) and potentially s.66C and s.72A. The company is liable to its clients under s.43A, because the failure to deactivate dormant credentials is itself a failure of reasonable security practices and procedures. Damages against the ex‑employee must be pleaded and proved in accordance with Vinod Kaushik.
PYQ 4 "Discuss the elements of s.43A liability and illustrate with Sanjay Dhande and Chander Kalani." Likely long question · 15 marks · Doctrinal
Model answer — structure
  1. Introduction. s.43A was inserted by the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008 (in force 27 October 2009). It creates a negligence‑based, compensation‑only liability on a body corporate for failure to protect sensitive personal data or information, with a statutory ceiling of ₹5 crore. Quote the section.
  2. The Explanation. Set out the three definitions — body corporate (any company, and including a firm, sole proprietorship or other association of individuals engaged in commercial or professional activities); reasonable security practices and procedures (practices designed to protect information from unauthorised access, damage, use, modification, disclosure or impairment, as specified in an agreement or in any law in force and, in their absence, as prescribed); and sensitive personal data or information (as prescribed). Note TDSAT's statement in Chander Kalani that all three are widely worded.
  3. The three elements, quoting Chander Kalani para 16 verbatim, and elaborating each: (i) possession, dealing or handling; (ii) negligence in security practices and procedures — with the signature‑verification carve‑out; (iii) causation of wrongful loss or wrongful gain.
  4. The SPDI Rules. Rule 3's list and the public‑domain exclusions; Rule 4's privacy policy; Rule 8's IS/ISO/IEC 27001 standard and the deemed‑compliance route; the audit obligation for high‑volume entities.
  5. Illustration — Sanjay Dhande. The duplicate‑SIM facts; the ICICI failures; the Vodafone failures; the metadata holding; the multi‑defendant apportionment (₹6 lakh / ₹12 lakh); the 12% compound interest direction; the systemic recommendations to DeitY, DoT, TRAI and the Banking Secretary; and the TDSAT affirmation in 2020 SCC OnLine TDSAT 124, which added s.43(g) liability.
  6. Illustration — Chander Kalani. The fraudulent‑e‑mail facts; the A‑2 Form scanned signature; the FD details leaked by the bank itself; the Rule 3(ii), (vii) and (viii) analysis; the Rule 4 and Rule 8 failures; the summary‑procedure holding; and the quantum holding that contributory blame does not reduce compensation where the award is confined to actual loss out of a ₹1 crore claim.
  7. Contrast with s.43 — different defendants, different subject matter, different liability standards, different caps.
  8. Critical evaluation. The "awkward middle ground" between strict liability and negligence; the judicially developed burden‑shifting; the absence of an apportionment formula; the institutional competence of the AO.
  9. Currency. The DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 (notified 13 November 2025; phased 12–18‑month compliance; full commencement targeted 13 May 2027); the Data Protection Board of India; breach‑notification duties; the point that s.43A continues to govern pre‑commencement conduct.
  10. Conclusion. Use the model conclusion at §16.3.
PYQ 5 "Critically analyse whether s.43A remains relevant or necessary after the DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025." Likely long question · 15 marks · Critical essay
Model answer — the arguments on both sides
s.43A is still relevant and necessary
  • Transitional necessity. s.43A liability for conduct occurring before full DPDP commencement remains litigated under the old framework — as the continuing Buradkar (TDSAT, September 2024) and Bank of India v. Sandeep line demonstrates.
  • A live cause of action. Claims accruing before commencement are not extinguished by the DPDP Act; they continue to be adjudicated under s.43A through the AO → TDSAT → High Court ladder.
  • Accessibility. The s.46 forum is summary, specialist and inexpensive — a victim can file a complaint without counsel and without court fee. The DPDP regime's remedies run through the Data Protection Board, whose accessibility is as yet untested.
  • A proven remedial record. Dhande and Chander Kalani produced actual, substantial awards — ₹18 lakhs and ₹40 lakhs respectively — within months, without any need for a full civil suit.
  • No express repeal. The DPDP Act does not repeal s.43A; it supersedes the SPDI Rules regime only as it comes into force.
s.43A is being progressively displaced
  • Rights‑based architecture. The DPDP Act confers statutory rights on the Data Principal — access, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, nomination — which s.43A never did. s.43A only ever provided compensation after loss.
  • Prospective duties. Purpose limitation, data minimisation, consent management and breach notification are obligations that operate before any loss occurs. s.43A is entirely post‑loss.
  • A dedicated regulator. The Data Protection Board of India replaces the ad hoc reliance on state IT Secretaries as Adjudicating Officers — directly answering the Amlan Mohanty critique of institutional competence.
  • Broader definition of personal data. The DPDP Act's identifiability‑based definition is wider than Rule 3's closed list of sensitive categories — a point Sanjay Dhande's metadata holding had already anticipated.
  • Practical redundancy for new conduct. For post‑commencement conduct, a claimant will ordinarily have a richer set of remedies under the DPDP Act than under s.43A.
Conclusion: The correct answer is neither "obsolete" nor "unchanged". s.43A is functionally redundant for post‑commencement conduct but juridically and practically indispensable for pre‑commencement conduct, where it continues to supply the only tested compensatory remedy and the only body of appellate authority. The two regimes will run in parallel for a transitional period, and the single most important open question as of 2026 is whether the DPDP regime's rights‑based architecture will displace the judicially developed burden‑shifting under s.43A, or simply run alongside it.

16Revision summary, high‑value points and sources

16.1 The revision card

📌 KEY RULE

s.43 creates liability for ten enumerated categories of unauthorised computer‑resource interference — access without permission; downloading, copying or extracting data; introducing virus or contaminant; damaging; disrupting; denying access; assisting unauthorised access; charging services to another's account; destroying, deleting or altering source code; and stealing, concealing or destroying computer source code — but damages must be independently proved (Vinod Kaushik). s.43A creates negligence‑based liability on body corporates for failure to maintain reasonable security practices and procedures in handling sensitive personal data, causing wrongful loss or wrongful gain, capped at ₹5 crore.

📌 STATUTORY BASIS

ss.43, 43A, 45, 46, 47, 48, 57, 59, 61, 62, 67C, 72, 72A, 75, 79A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 · Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011 · Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and DPDP Rules, 2025 · Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 (as amended 2026) · Bhartiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, ss.45A and 63(4) · IPC ss.499–502 (now BNS ss.356–358) for online defamation · RBI KYC/AML/CFT/PMLA Master Circular dated 2 July 2012 and Guidelines on Information Security, Electronic Banking, Technology Risk Management and Cyber Frauds dated 29 April 2011 · DoT/TRAI SIM‑issuance guidelines.

📌 TOP FIVE CASES
  1. Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi, W.P.(C) 160/2012 (Delhi HC, 27.01.2012) — s.43 breach ≠ automatic damages; loss must be pleaded and proved.
  2. Sanjay Govind Dhande v. ICICI Bank; Vodafone (AO, 16.01.2014), affirmed in Vodafone Idea Ltd v. Sanjay Govind Dhande, 2020 SCC OnLine TDSAT 124 — multi‑defendant s.43A liability apportioned by causal contribution; metadata is sensitive personal data.
  3. State Bank of India v. Chander Kalani, Cyber Appeal No. 13/2015 (TDSAT, 31.07.2018) — the three‑element s.43A test; the Rule 3/4/8 analysis; the summary‑procedure holding.
  4. SMC Pneumatics (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Jogesh Kwatra (Delhi District Court, 12.02.2014) — India's first cyber‑defamation case; injunctive relief available against ongoing cyber‑defamatory conduct.
  5. Syed Asifuddin v. State of A.P., 2006 (1) ALD (Cri) 96; 2005 CriLJ 4314 (AP HC, 29.07.2005) — the same conduct (source‑code tampering) can attract parallel civil (s.43(i)) and criminal (ss.65/66) liability.
📌 THE DEBATE IN ONE LINE

Whether s.43A's judicially developed burden‑shifting — conspicuous security lapse ⇒ inference of negligence — will survive, be codified, or be superseded once the DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 fully commence (targeted 13 May 2027).

📌 RECENT DEVELOPMENT

The DPDP Rules, 2025, notified 13 November 2025, operationalise India's first omnibus data‑protection statute, with an 18‑month phased compliance window and the newly constituted Data Protection Board of India — the most significant civil‑liability development in Indian IT law since the 2008 Amendment introduced s.43A itself.

16.2 Common exam mistakes — avoid every one of these

MistakeCorrection
Treating s.43 and s.43A as interchangeableThey have different liability standards (broadly act‑based for s.43; negligence‑based for s.43A) and different subject matter (s.43 = any computer resource; s.43A = sensitive personal data handled by a body corporate).
Forgetting to independently address quantum under s.43Vinod Kaushik: a breach of s.43 does not yield damages automatically. Damage must be averred and proved as in a civil suit. This is the single most commonly tested trap in this Unit.
Ignoring the DPDP Act and Rules when answering post‑2023 problem questionsExaminers are testing both. Answer the liability question under s.43A and add a currency paragraph on the DPDP regime and its phased commencement.
Conflating the AO's civil‑court powers with a full civil trialChander Kalani, para 11: the AO's procedure is summary; the civil‑court powers exist only to enable him to summon or discover relevant materials.
Saying the Cyber Appellate Tribunal hears s.57 appealsIt was abolished; the functions were transferred to TDSAT under the TRAI Act, 1997 (as amended 2017).
Describing s.43's clauses as requiring intentionOnly clause (j) requires that the act be done "with intent to cause damage or destruction". The other nine impose liability without any mental element.
Arguing that a telecom operator does not handle personal dataSanjay Dhande: metadata is sensitive personal data; the argument "has no merit at all".
Forgetting the ₹5 crore ceilingBoth s.46(1A) (the AO's jurisdictional limit) and s.43A (the statutory cap on compensation) fix the ceiling at ₹5 crore.
Missing s.43(g) in duplicate‑SIM problemsTDSAT in Vodafone Idea v. Dhande held the operator liable under both s.43(g) and s.43A. Always plead both against the operator.
Treating online defamation as an IT Act offenceDefamation is not an offence under the IT Act. It is under IPC ss.499–502 (now BNS ss.356–358). s.66A was struck down in Shreya Singhal.

16.3 Model exam conclusion — one paragraph you can reproduce verbatim

🎓 Model conclusion (verbatim)

"Civil liability under the IT Act operates on a bifurcated model: s.43 imposes liability for enumerated acts of unauthorised access or damage to a computer resource, but as Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi makes clear, this does not translate into automatic compensation — damage must be pleaded and proved as in an ordinary civil suit. Section 43A, by contrast, is expressly negligence‑based, requiring proof that a body corporate handling sensitive personal data failed to implement reasonable security practices and procedures, a standard clarified with precision by TDSAT in State Bank of India v. Chander Kalani and applied to multi‑party fraud chains in Sanjay Dhande v. ICICI Bank; Vodafone. This adjudicatory framework, administered through a summary process before Adjudicating Officers with appeal to TDSAT, has functioned as a specialised, accessible forum for cyber‑tort victims, but it is now being progressively overlaid — and will eventually be substantially displaced — by the more comprehensive, rights‑based Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and its 2025 Rules, marking the most significant civil‑liability development in Indian information technology law since the 2008 Amendment introduced s.43A itself."
