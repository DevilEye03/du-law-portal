00.2 What the three excerpts actually are

1. Austin — the opening move

The excerpt is the first lecture's core: "The matter of jurisprudence is positive law: law, simply and strictly so called." Austin clears the field — divine law, positive morality, metaphorical law — then conducts his two famous analyses: the essentials of a command and the marks of sovereignty. It ends where Lecture I ends, with the shapes of supreme government (monarchy; aristocracy, generic and specific; oligarchy; democracy). Everything the examiner asks about Austin's "three elements" and "two marks" is here.

2. Hart — two full chapters

Chapter V of The Concept of Law ("Law as the Union of Primary and Secondary Rules") is reprinted whole, and Chapter VI ("Rule of Recognition and Legal Validity") follows — the casebook stitches them at the chapter boundary ("a rule of recognition…" ends V; the chapter on validity opens VI). You therefore have Hart's most important pages: the four failures, the gunman, the internal point of view, the three defects and three remedies, and the whole doctrine of the rule of recognition as ultimate rule.

3. Kelsen — the first chapter (with a gap)

The excerpt is the opening chapter ("Law and Nature") of the Max Knight translation, plus later pages: the pure theory; act and legal meaning; subjective v. objective meaning; the norm as scheme of interpretation; "ought"; validity and effectiveness; the four spheres of validity; positive and negative regulation; the social order (retribution, blood revenge); the legal order as order of human behaviour and as coercive order (monopoly of force, collective security, minimum of liberty); the robber-gang test and St. Augustine; basic-norm chain; the two objections to coercion in the definition; dependent legal norms. The famous Stufenbau (hierarchy of norms) detail falls in the omitted pages and in later chapters — the stepping-stone to it is here (the chain statute → constitution → basic norm).

01Conceptual Frame — What Positivism Is, and the Three Answers

01.1 The shared project: three theses (grey — standard framing; the readings supply the evidence)

Thesis 1 · Social-fact thesis

Law is a human artefact: its existence is a matter of fact about social practice — commands issued (Austin), rules accepted (Hart), norms posited (Kelsen). Not the pronouncement of a higher order. Austin: law "set by political superiors"; Hart: the rule of recognition "exists only as… practice"; Kelsen: positive law, "set" by human acts, whose validity rests on a presupposed basic norm.

Thesis 2 · Separation thesis

The existence of law is one question; its goodness another. Kelsen is the loudest in the excerpt: justice "cannot be made an element of the concept of law"; an unjust but effective coercive order is still law — the Communist order is law despite the capitalist's judgement, and vice versa. Austin: the "ends or final causes for which governments ought to exist" are no concern of the analytical jurist.

Thesis 3 · The analytical method

The job of jurisprudence is to describe and analyse, not to evaluate, advise or reform: Austin isolates the "matter" of jurisprudence and dissects its terms; Kelsen's theory "attempts to answer the question what and how the law is, not how it ought to be. It is a science of law (jurisprudence) not legal politics"; Hart spends his two chapters dissolving the amalgam of "obliged" and "obligation," and of validity and efficacy.

01.2 The one problem all three answer: why does a norm bind?

AUSTIN'S ANSWER · because it is the command of a habitually-obeyed sovereign — the duty is correlative to the sanction; "command and duty are correlative terms"→
HART'S ANSWER · because it satisfies the rule of recognition — a secondary rule accepted by officials as the test of what counts as law ("its existence is a matter of fact")→
KELSEN'S ANSWER · because of the basic norm — "Coercion of man against man ought to be exercised in the manner and under the conditions determined by the historically first constitution"; presupposed, not enacted; conditional on effectiveness

Read the three as a chain of corrections. Austin locates validity in the will of the sovereign (but the parliament binds itself; the "sovereign" cannot be identified in a modern state — Hart's four failures). Hart locates it in a rule about rules (but the rule of recognition itself cannot be valid — it "can neither be valid nor invalid"; it exists as fact — Kelsen would say "presupposed"). Kelsen locates it in a presupposition whose content is given by the historically first constitution, whose only condition is effectiveness. Each answer dissolves the previous one's puzzle and leaves a puzzle of its own — that is the exam-friendly story.

02(a) John Austin — Law as the Command of the Sovereign

02.1 The province of jurisprudence — the clearing of the field

Open with Austin's own opening sentence — it is the whole lecture compressed: "The matter of jurisprudence is positive law: law, simply and strictly so called: or law set by political superiors to political inferiors." Positive law is "often confounded with objects to which it is related by resemblance, and with objects to which it is related in the way of analogy" — all covered by the "large and vague expression law." The province is determined by separation:

LAWS BY GOD TO MEN · the Divine law ("the law of nature" is an ambiguous name for it — the only natural law possible to speak of "without a metaphor")·
LAWS SET BY MEN TO MEN — class 1: by political superiors (sovereign and subject) = positive law, the appropriate matter of jurisprudence·
LAWS SET BY MEN TO MEN — class 2: not by political superiors (as such) = positive morality: father to child, master to servant, club rules; plus opinion-rules "set and enforced by mere opinion" — "the law of honour," "the law set by fashion," much of "international law"·
METAPHORICAL LAWS · laws of the lower animals, of vegetable decay, of inanimate motion — "where intelligence is not… there is not the will which law can work on" — "through these misapplications of a name… has the field of jurisprudence and morals been deluged with muddy speculation"

Why the name "positive"? As against the law of God, the rules of political superiors are "positive law, or law existing by position"; as against positive morality they are marked "commodiously with the name of positive law." Note Austin's own caution: rules not made by political superiors "are also positive, or exist by position, if they be rules or laws, in the proper signification of the term" — so "positive" alone is not the test; the test is the source.

02.2 Law in its largest literal sense — the genus definition

"A law, in the most general and comprehensive acceptation in which the term, in its literal meaning, is employed, may be said to be a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him." Under this definition fall, "without impropriety, several species" — and the "line of demarcation" between them must be drawn precisely, "as much mistiness and intricacy has been infused into the science of jurisprudence by their being confounded."

02.3 Command — the "key to the sciences of jurisprudence and morals"

Every law or rule, properly so called, "is a command. Or, rather, laws or rules, properly so called, are a species of commands." Command is "the simpler as well as the larger of the two" — and "since it is the key to the sciences of jurisprudence and morals, its meaning should be analysed with precision." The analysis:

"If you express or intimate a wish that I shall do or forbear from some act, and if you will visit me with an evil in case I comply not with your wish, the expression or intimation of your wish is a command. A command is distinguished from other significations of desire, not by the style in which the desire is signified, but by the power and the purpose of the party commanding to inflict an evil or pain in case the desire be disregarded."

If you cannot or will not harm me, an imperative phrase is not a command; if you can and will, "the expression of your wish amounts to a command, although you are prompted by a spirit of courtesy to utter it in the shape of a request." — Tacitus: "Preces erant, sed quibus contradici non posset" ("they were prayers, but such as could not be refused") — the soldiery's petition to the son of Vespasian. (grey: casebook prints "nan posset" — OCR slip for "non posset")

Consequence: the essence is power plus purpose — not phrasing, not formality, not ceremony. The same words are a command or not depending on the standing of the speaker. This is why Austin's theory makes the sovereign relation (power) the foundation of everything.

02.4 Command, duty and sanction — one notion, three aspects

"Being liable to evil from you if I comply not with a wish which you signify, I am bound or obliged by your command, or I lie under a duty to obey it… Command and duty are, therefore, correlative terms: the meaning denoted by each being implied or supposed by the other. Or… wherever a duty lies, a command has been signified; and whenever a command is signified, a duty is imposed."

The three constituents of a command: (1) a wish conceived by a rational being that another shall do or forbear; (2) an evil to proceed from the former and be incurred by the latter in case of non-compliance; (3) an expression or intimation of the wish.

TermWhat is put foremostWhat is left implicit
CommandThe expression/intimation of the wishThe evil; the chance of incurring it ("kept in the background of my picture")

"Each of the three terms signifies the same notion; but each denotes a different part of that notion, and connotes the residue." — the logicians' distinction (denotation v. connotation).

Rewards are not sanctions. "It is only by the chance of incurring evil, that I am bound or obliged to compliance. It is only by conditional evil, that duties are sanctioned or enforced. It is the power and the purpose of inflicting eventual evil, and not the power and the purpose of imparting eventual good, which gives to the expression of a wish the name of a command." A law holding out a reward "confers an eventual right, and not an obligation," on those who comply; the imperative part is addressed to the party who must render the reward. (Putting reward into "sanction" would be a "toilsome struggle with the current of ordinary speech.")

02.5 Two species of commands: laws/rules v. occasional or particular commands

"Where it obliges generally to acts or forbearances of a class, a command is a law or rule. But where it obliges to a specific act or forbearance… a command is occasional or particular." The distinction is in the object, not the source — a particular command is not elevated to a law by the rank of the commander.

02.6 Blackstone's line rejected — and the two refinements

Blackstone (and others) distinguished law from particular command by the persons addressed: a law obliges generally the community or a class; a particular command obliges an individual or named persons. Austin's two answers:

  1. Commands obliging the whole community may still be particular: the corn-export detention order binds everyone but is not a law; so too a sovereign order, enforced by penalties, for "a general mourning, on occasion of a public calamity" — "it obliges to acts which it assigns specifically"; "if the sovereign commanded that black should be the dress of his subjects, his command would amount to a law. But if he commanded them to wear it on a specified occasion… merely particular."
  2. Commands obliging individually-determined persons may be laws: "A father may set a rule to his child… a guardian, to his ward: a master, to his slave or servant. And certain of God's laws were as binding on the first man, as they are binding at this hour on the millions who have sprung from his loins." So too Parliament creating an office and binding the grantee to services of a given description — such laws, "exclusively binding specified or determinate persons," were styled by Roman jurists privilegia (a name, Austin notes, that "will hardly denote them distinctly" — it names "a heap of heterogeneous objects").

The resulting definition: "A law is a command which obliges a person or persons. But, as contradistinguished or opposed to an occasional or particular command, a law is a command which obliges a person or persons, and obliges generally to acts or forbearances of a class." In plainer words: "a command which obliges a person or persons to a course of conduct." And: most laws of political superiors are "general in a twofold manner: as enjoining or forbidding generally acts of kinds or sorts; and as binding the whole community, or, at least, whole classes of its members" — because "to frame a system of duties for every individual of the community, were simply impossible: and if it were possible, it were utterly useless."

02.7 Superiority = might — and the tautology exposed

"Taken with the meaning wherein I here understand it, the term superiority signifies might: the power of affecting others with evil or pain, and of forcing them, through fear of that evil, to fashion their conduct to one's wishes." Not rank, wealth or virtue — those are other senses of "superior."

  • God is "emphatically, the superior of Man" — his power is "unbounded and resistless."
  • "To a limited extent, the sovereign One or Number is the superior of the subject… the master, of the slave or servant: the father, of the child."
  • Reciprocal superiority: "the party who is the superior as viewed from one aspect, is the inferior as viewed from another." The monarch is the superior of the governed — but "the governed, collectively or in mass, is also the superior of the monarch: who is checked in the abuse of his might by his fear of exciting their anger." A member of a sovereign assembly is the superior of the judge (whom the law binds) — but "in his character of citizen or subject, he is the inferior of the judge: the judge being the minister of the law."
  • The payoff: "It appears then, that the term superiority… is implied by the term command… 'That laws emanate from superiors' is, therefore, an identical proposition. For the meaning which it affects to impart is contained in its subject… to affirm of laws universally 'that they flow from superiors'… is the merest tautology and trifling."

02.9 Custom and judge-made law — the two false opinions, and the true one

Two opinions rejected: (1) the admirers of custom (especially German admirers): customary law "obliges legally (independently of the sovereign or state)" because subjects have observed it — "they are positive law… inasmuch as they are enforced by the courts of justice" but "exist as positive law by the spontaneous adoption of the governed"; (2) their opponents: all judge-made law "is purely the creature of the judges" — to impute it to the sovereign legislature is "one of the foolish or knavish fictions with which lawyers… have perplexed and darkened the simplest and clearest truths."

Austin's solution — both are wrong: "At its origin, a custom is a rule of conduct which the governed observes spontaneously… The custom is transmuted into positive law, when it is adopted as such by the courts of justice, and when the judicial decisions fashioned upon it are enforced by the power of the state. But before it is adopted by the courts, and clothed with the legal sanction, it is merely a rule of positive morality: a rule generally observed… but deriving the only force, which it can be said to possess, from the general disapprobation falling on those who transgress it."

"When judges transmute a custom into a legal rule… the legal rule which they establish is established by the sovereign legislature. A subordinate or subject judge is merely a minister. The portion of the sovereign power which lies at his disposition is merely delegated. The rules, which he makes, derive their legal force from authority given by the state: an authority which the state may confer expressly, but which it commonly imparts in the way of acquiescence."

  • Customs become law "established by the state directly, when the customs are promulged in its statutes; established by the state circuitously, when the customs are adopted by its tribunals."
  • Commands may be express or tacit — "if the desire be signified by conduct (or by any signs of desire which are not words), the command is tacit." Judicial rulings on customs are thus tacit commands of the sovereign legislature: "The state, which is able to abolish, permits its ministers to enforce them: and it therefore signifies its pleasure, by that its voluntary acquiescence, 'that they shall serve as a law to the governed.'"
  • Admitted difference: law made judicially ("improper legislation") and law made by statute ("properly legislative manner") are "distinguished by weighty differences" — to be taken up in later lectures.
  • The sovereign's title: borrowing Hobbes — "the legislator is he, not by whose authority the law was first made, but by whose authority it continues to be a law."

02.10 Sovereignty and independent political society — the two marks

The "essential difference of a positive law" (the difference severing it from every non-positive law): "every positive law… is set by a sovereign person, or a sovereign body of persons, to a member or members of the independent political society wherein that person or body is sovereign." Even if it springs from another fountain, it is positive law "by the institution of that present sovereign in the character of political superior." Hence the two marks, positive and negative:

CONCISE FORMULA · "If a determinate human superior, not in a habit of obedience to a like superior, receive habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, that determinate superior is sovereign in that society, and the society (including the superior) is a society political and independent."→
POSITIVE MARK · the generality/bulk of the society are in a habit of obedience to a determinate and common superior (one and the same person or body)→
NEGATIVE MARK · that determinate person or body is not in a habit of obedience to a determinate human superior — "It is the union of that positive, with this negative mark, which renders that certain superior sovereign"

Determinacy matters: "no indeterminate party can command expressly or tacitly, or can receive obedience or submission: no indeterminate body is capable of corporate conduct." (Point 3 of the analysis — the "common" and "determinate" requirement.)

Vagueness is due, not accidental. "The definition of the abstract term independent political society… cannot be rendered in expression of perfectly precise import, and is therefore a fallible test." The positive mark is fallible: "in order that the bulk of its members may render obedience to a common superior, how many… and how often… and how long… in order that that obedience may be habitual?" The negative mark is likewise uncertain (the Saxon case could cross the line). "The definition or general notion of independent political society, is therefore vague or uncertain."

The same difficulty everywhere: "What can be more indefinite, for instance, than the expressions, reasonable time, reasonable notice, reasonable diligence?… the line of demarcation which distinguishes libel and fair criticism… that degree of mental aberration which constitutes idiocy or lunacy?" These are "the very absurd enquiry whether questions of this kind are questions of law or of fact… The truth is that they are questions neither of law nor of fact. The fact be perfectly ascertained, and so may the law… the difficulty is in bringing the species under the rule; in determining not what the law is, or what the fact is, but whether the given law is applicable to the given fact."

International law is not positive law. "The law obtaining between nations regards the conduct of sovereigns considered as related to one another… every positive law is set by a given sovereign to a person or persons in a state of subjection to its author… the law obtaining between nations is law (improperly so called) set by general opinion. The duties which it imposes are enforced by moral sanctions: by fear… of provoking general hostility." (Independent societies, as communities, live in a "state of nature" — so there is no sovereign over them.)

02.11 The shapes of supreme government

  • Every sovereign portion is less than the whole community: "In most actual societies, the sovereign powers are engrossed by a single member of the whole, or are shared exclusively by a very few… even in the actual societies whose governments are esteemed popular, the sovereign number is a slender portion of the entire political community."
  • Monarchy = sovereign portion of one; aristocracy (generic sense) = sovereign portion of a number. The "essentially and broadly distinguished" difference: "In the case of a monarchy… the sovereign portion… is simply or purely sovereign. In the case of an aristocracy… that sovereign portion is sovereign as viewed from one aspect, but is also subject as viewed from another" — the individual members are subject to the body of which they form part.

These "can hardly be distinguished with precision, or even with a distant approach to it" — one man's oligarchy is another's "liberal aristocracy"; the proportion "may stand… at any point in a long series of minute degrees."

03(b) H.L.A. Hart — Law as the Union of Primary and Secondary Rules

03.1 A fresh start — the four failures of the command model

Chapter V opens where Chapter IV closed: "the simple model of law as the sovereign's coercive orders failed to reproduce some of the salient features of a legal system." Note the method — Hart deliberately does not rely on international law or primitive law ("which some may regard as disputable or borderline examples of law"); he points to "certain familiar features of municipal law in a modern state." The four ways in which the theory failed:

1. Self-binding legislation

Of all varieties of law, a criminal statute "most resembles orders backed by threats" — yet it "commonly applies to those who enact it and not merely to others." A sovereign who must be outside the law-obeying circle cannot accommodate statutes that bind the legislature itself.

2. Power-conferring rules

"There are other varieties of law, notably those conferring legal powers to adjudicate or legislate (public powers) or to create or vary legal relations (private powers) which cannot, without absurdity, be construed as orders backed by threats." (Austin's answer — that they are "fragments" of duty-imposing rules — distorts how they are "spoken of, thought of, and actually used in social life"; it is "as absurd as the theory that all the rules of a game are 'really' directions to the umpire and the scorer.")

3. Mode of origin

"There are legal rules which differ from orders in their mode of origin, because they are not brought into being by anything analogous to explicit prescription" — custom being the standard case. The ancillary device of the tacit order "seemed to have no application to the complex actualities of a modern legal system, but only to very much simpler situations like that of a general who deliberately refrains from interfering with orders given by his subordinates."

4. Continuity of legislative authority

The analysis "in terms of the sovereign, habitually obeyed and necessarily exempt from all legal limitations, failed to account for the continuity of legislative authority characteristic of a modern legal system, and the sovereign person or persons could not be identified with either the electorate or the legislature of a modern state." And the last rescue device — distinguishing legislators "in their official capacity" from themselves "in their private capacities" — "impeccable in itself," yet it "involved supplementing the theory with something it does not contain: this is the notion of a rule defining what must be done to legislate."

The diagnosis: "The last three chapters are therefore the record of a failure and there is plainly need for a fresh start… The root cause of failure is that the elements out of which the theory was constructed, viz. the ideas of orders, obedience, habits, and threats, do not include, and cannot by their combination yield, the idea of a rule, without which we cannot hope to elucidate even the most elementary forms of law."

03.3 The idea of obligation — the gunman, and the two points of view

The gunman situation

A orders B to hand over his money and threatens to shoot him. For the command theory, this is "the notion of obligation or duty in general… writ large"; for Hart, it is "a perfectly correct appreciation of the fact that where there is law, there human conduct is made in some sense non-optional" — but the theory's errors are most instructive at this very first step.

"Obliged" v. "had an obligation"

"It is certainly one in which we would say that B, if he obeyed, was 'obliged'… It is, however, equally certain that we should misdescribe the situation if we said… that B 'had an obligation' or a 'duty.'" The first "is, in the main, a psychological one referring to the beliefs and motives with which an action was done"; two complications: a trivial threat (a pinch) leaves B not even "obliged"; nor where there are no reasonable grounds for thinking the threat will be implemented.

What "had an obligation" means. The statement that someone had an obligation is not a psychological prediction: it is a statement that a social rule exists, under which the conduct is required and deviation "is generally regarded as a lapse" — an occasion for criticism, demands for conformity, and social pressure. The rule is used "as a standard for the appraisal of their own and other persons' conduct." This is the internal point of view: "the view of those who do not merely record and predict behaviour conforming to rules, but use the rules as standards for the appraisal of their own and others' behaviour."

The external point of view — that of an observer, which "limits itself to the observable regularities of behaviour" — can express only "I was obliged to do it," "I am likely to suffer for it if…" It "cannot reproduce… the way in which the rules function as rules in the lives of those who normally are the majority of society" — for whom "the violation of a rule is not merely a basis for the prediction that a hostile reaction will follow but a reason for hostility."

The tension: "the life of any society which lives by rules… is likely to consist in a tension between those who… accept and voluntarily co-operate in maintaining the rules… and those who… reject the rules and attend to them only from the external point of view as a sign of possible punishment." The theorist's duty: "to remember the presence of both these points of view and not to define one of them out of existence. Perhaps all our criticisms of the predictive theory of obligation may be best summarized as the accusation that this is what it does to the internal aspect of obligatory rules."

Two conditions for survival: rules restricting "the free use of violence, theft, and deception"; and the internal point of view must be held by the majority — "the latter [rejecters] cannot be more than a minority, if so loosely organized a society… is to endure."

03.5 The three remedies — recognition, change, adjudication

Rule of recognition

"The simplest form of remedy for the uncertainty… is the introduction of what we shall call a 'rule of recognition.' This will specify some feature or features possession of which by a suggested rule is taken as a conclusive affirmative indication that it is a rule of the group to be supported by the social pressure it exerts."

Simple form: "an authoritative list or text… in a written document or carved on some public monument" — the crucial step is "the acknowledgement of reference to the writing or inscription as authoritative." Complex form (developed systems): identification "by reference to some general characteristic" — enactment by a specific body, long customary practice, relation to judicial decisions; and where several characteristics are criteria, "provision may be made for their possible conflict by their arrangement in an order of superiority, as by the common subordination of custom or precedent to statute, the latter being a 'superior source' of law."

Even the simplest form "introduces… the idea of a legal system" and contains "the germ of the idea of legal validity."

Rules of change

"The remedy for the static quality… is the introduction of what we shall call 'rules of change'": empowering "an individual or body of persons to introduce new primary rules… and to eliminate old rules." "It is in terms of such a rule, and not in terms of orders backed by threats, that the ideas of legislative enactment and repeal are to be understood."

They may be simple or complex; powers "unrestricted or limited"; and they "define… the procedure to be followed in legislation." Close connection with recognition: "the latter will necessarily incorporate a reference to legislation as an identifying feature." If a system's only "source of law" is legislation, the rule of recognition "will simply specify enactment as the unique identifying mark" — as in "the imaginary kingdom of Rex I": "the rule of recognition would simply be that whatever Rex I enacts is law."

Private powers, same family: the rules conferring on individuals power to vary their positions "are the making of wills, contracts, transfers of property… though of course an elementary form of power-conferring rule also underlies the moral institution of a promise." And: "as recent theory such as Kelsen's has shown, many of the features which puzzle us in the institutions of contract or property are clarified by thinking of the operations of making a contract or transferring property as the exercise of limited legislative powers by individuals."

Rules of adjudication

"The third supplement… consists of secondary rules empowering individuals to make authoritative determinations of the question whether, on a particular occasion, a primary rule has been broken. The minimal form of adjudication consists in such determinations." These rules also "define the procedure"; they "define a group of important legal concepts: in this case the concepts of judge or court, jurisdiction and judgment."

Intimate connection: "a system which has rules of adjudication is necessarily also committed to a rule of recognition of an elementary and imperfect sort" — courts' determinations "cannot avoid being taken as authoritative determinations of what the rules are," and judgments "will become a 'source' of law." Imperfect: "judgments may not be couched in general terms and their use… depends on a somewhat shaky inference from particular decisions," fluctuating "with the skill of the interpreter and the consistency of the judges."

Most systems go further: "instead they have supplemented the primary rules of obligation by further secondary rules, specifying… the penalties for violation, and… conferred upon judges… the exclusive power to direct the application of penalties." These are "the centralized official 'sanctions' of the system."

03.6 The union — "the heart of a legal system"

"If we stand back and consider the structure which has resulted from the combination of primary rules of obligation with the secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication, it is plain that we have here not only the heart of a legal system, but a most powerful tool for the analysis of much that has puzzled both the jurist and the political theorist."

  • Legal concepts "with which the lawyer is professionally concerned" — "obligation and rights, validity and source of law, legislation and jurisdiction, and sanction" — "are best elucidated in terms of this combination of elements."
  • Political concepts — "the state, of authority, and of an official" — "require a similar analysis if the obscurity which still lingers about them is to be dissipated."
  • Why the power: "Most of the obscurities and distortions surrounding legal and political concepts arise from the fact that these essentially involve reference to what we have called the internal point of view." Under the simple regime of primary rules it appears "in its simplest form, in the use of those rules as the basis of criticism, and as the justification of demands for conformity, social pressure, and punishment."

03.7 Chapter VI — the rule of recognition and legal validity: criteria, and validity v. efficacy

Chapter VI refines the rule of recognition into a full doctrine. The key moves:

Validity and efficacy are distinct. The validity of a particular rule "may or may not" be affected by its inefficacy "unless the rule of recognition of the system includes among its criteria, as some do, the provision (sometimes referred to as a rule of obsolescence) that no rule is to count as a rule of the system if it has long ceased to be efficacious."

But total disregard is fatal. "From the inefficacy of a particular rule… we must distinguish a general disregard of the rules of the system" — so complete and protracted that the system "had never established itself" or "had ceased to be the legal system of the group." Then "the normal context or background for making any internal statement in terms of the rules of the system is absent" — to "insist on applying a system of rules which had either never actually been effective or had been discarded would… be as futile as to assess the progress of a game by reference to a scoring rule which had never been accepted."

One who makes an internal statement of validity "may be said to presuppose the truth of the external statement of fact that the system is generally efficacious" — yet validity does not mean efficacy: teaching Roman law "as if the system were efficacious still" is meaningful; so too is "the White Russian who still claims property under some rule of descent which was a valid rule of Tsarist Russia."

The predictive theory of validity rejected (mirror of the rejected predictive theory of obligation): the common theory "that to assert the validity of a rule is to predict that it will be enforced by courts" — "the mistake… is the same: it consists in neglecting the special character of the internal statement and treating it as an external statement about official action." The verdict is decisive: the judge's "statement that a rule is valid is an internal statement recognizing that the rule satisfies the tests for identifying what is to count as law in his court, and constitutes not a prophecy of but part of the reason for his decision." Even for a private person, withdrawal of a statement before a contrary court ruling may rest on many grounds; "there are more reasons for withdrawing statements than the fact that they are wrong, and also more ways of being wrong."

03.8 The ultimate rule and the supreme criterion — and the use of the chain of validation

The Oxfordshire chain (learn this example by heart): "Is this purported by-law of the Oxfordshire County Council valid? Yes: because it was made in exercise of the powers conferred… by a statutory order made by the Minister of Health. At this stage the statutory order provides the criteria… We may query the validity of the statutory order and assess its validity in terms of the statute empowering the minister… Finally, when the validity of the statute has been queried and assessed by reference to the rule that what the Queen in Parliament enacts is law, we are brought to a stop in inquiries concerning validity: for we have reached a rule which… provides criteria for the assessment of the validity of other rules; but it is also unlike them in that there is no rule providing criteria for the assessment of its own legal validity."

  • Supreme criterion defined: "a criterion of legal validity or source of law is supreme if rules identified by reference to it are still recognized as rules of the system, even if they conflict with rules identified by reference to the other criteria, whereas rules identified by reference to the latter are not so recognized if they conflict with the rules identified by reference to the supreme criterion." "Superior" and "subordinate" are "merely a relative place on a scale" — no notion of legally unlimited power is imported.
  • Two ideas disentangled: "It is important to disentangle them from the theory, which we have rejected, that somewhere in every legal system… there must be a sovereign legislative power which is legally unlimited." In the UK "there is a legislature subject to no Constitutional limitations" — "according to Constitutional theory," enactment by it is "the supreme criterion of validity." But the US "in which there is no such legally unlimited legislature may perfectly well contain an ultimate rule of recognition which provides a set of criteria of validity, one of which is supreme": where the legislature is limited by "a Constitution which contains no amending power, or places some clauses outside the scope of that power." (grey, cross-feed: the Indian basic-structure doctrine is precisely a "clauses outside the scope of the amending power" case — Unit-4's pyramid.)
  • What questions remain: about the ultimate rule we can ask whether courts, legislatures, officials, citizens "actually… use this rule"; whether the system is satisfactory; "are there prudential reasons for supporting it? Is there a moral obligation to do so?" — "but equally plainly, when we ask them… we are no longer attempting to answer the same kind of question." From "valid because it satisfies the rule" to "this rule is in fact used as the ultimate rule" is "a move… from an internal statement of law… to an external statement of fact"; and to "a system worthy of support" is "a statement of value."

03.9 "Assumed but cannot be demonstrated"? — the standard metre bar

Some writers say the rule of recognition's own validity "cannot be demonstrated but is 'assumed' or 'postulated' or is a 'hypothesis.' This may, however, be seriously misleading." Two things truly are presupposed by a serious assertion of validity: (1) the speaker "himself makes use of a rule of recognition which he accepts as appropriate for identifying the law"; (2) "this rule of recognition… is not only accepted by him but is the rule of recognition actually accepted and employed in the general operation of the system" — and "if the truth of this presupposition were doubted, it could be established by reference to actual practice: to the way in which courts identify what is to count as law."

"We only need the word 'validity'… to answer questions which arise within a system of rules where the status of a rule as a member of the system depends on its satisfying certain criteria… No such question can arise as to the validity of the very rule of recognition which provides the criteria; it can neither be valid nor invalid but is simply accepted as appropriate for use in this way. To express this simple fact by saying darkly that its validity is 'assumed but cannot be demonstrated,' is like saying that we assume, but can never demonstrate, that the standard metre bar in Paris which is the ultimate test of the correctness of all measurement in metres, is itself correct."

Two more distinctions: (i) distinguish "assuming the validity" from "presupposing the existence" — the latter "obscures what is meant by the assertion that such a rule exists"; (ii) in a mature system "the statement that a rule exists may now no longer be what it was in the simple case of customary rules — an external statement of the fact that a certain mode of behaviour was generally accepted as a standard"; it may be "an internal assessment applying an accepted but unstated rule of recognition." But "a rule of recognition is unlike other rules of the system. The assertion that it exists can only be an external statement of fact. For whereas a subordinate rule of a system may be valid and in that sense 'exist' even if it is generally disregarded, the rule of recognition exists only as a complex, but normally concordant, practice of the courts, officials, and private persons in identifying the law by reference to certain criteria. Its existence is a matter of fact."

04(c) Hans Kelsen — The Pure Theory of Law

04.1 The pure theory — what it is, and what it is "pure" of

The excerpt opens with the self-description — quote it first in any answer: "The Pure Theory of Law is a theory of positive law. It is a theory of positive law in general, not of a specific legal order… As a theory, its exclusive purpose is to know and to describe its object. The theory attempts to answer the question what and how the law is, not how it ought to be. It is a science of law (jurisprudence) not legal politics."

  • "Pure": "it only describes the law and attempts to eliminate from the object of this description everything that is not strictly law. Its aim is to free the science of law from alien elements."
  • The adulteration: traditional 19th–20th century legal science "uncritically… has been mixed with elements of psychology, sociology, ethics, and political theory" — understandable, since those disciplines deal with closely connected subject-matters.
  • Methodological syncretism: the theory "undertakes to delimit the cognition of law against these disciplines, not because it ignores or denies the connection, but because it wishes to avoid the uncritical mixture of methodologically different disciplines… which obscures the essence of the science of law and obliterates the limits imposed upon it by the nature of its subject matter."

04.2 The act and its legal meaning

Is law a natural or a social phenomenon? The clean delimitation is not easy: society "may be thought of as part of life in general and hence of nature," and what is customarily called law "seems at least partly to be rooted in nature." Analyse any body of facts interpreted as "legal" — a parliamentary decision, an administrative act, a judgment, a contract, a crime — and two elements are distinguishable:

1. The act

"An act or series of acts — a happening occurring at a certain time and in a certain place, perceived by our senses: an external manifestation of human conduct."

Examples: people assemble in a large room, make speeches, some raise their hands; a man in a robe speaks from a dais; a merchant writes a letter and receives an answer; somebody causes the death of somebody else.

2. The legal meaning

"The meaning conferred upon the act by Law": a statute is passed, law is created; a judicial decision; a legally binding contract; murder. This is the jurist's familiar distinction "between the process of legislation and its product, the statute."

04.3 Subjective and objective meaning — acts that interpret themselves

  • The legal meaning "is not immediately perceptible by the senses." The actor connects his act with a meaning — the subjective meaning — which "may, but need not necessarily, coincide with its objective meaning, that is, the meaning the act has according to the law."
  • Invalid testament: someone makes dispositions about his belongings, "stating in writing what is to happen… The subjective meaning of this act is a testament. Objectively, however, it is not, because some legal formalities were not observed."
  • The secret organisation: it condemns to death a man thought to be a traitor and has a member execute what it "subjectively believes to be and calls 'a death penalty'; objectively and legally however, not a death penalty but a Feme murder was carried out" — though "the external circumstances… are no different from the execution of a legal death penalty." (grey: "Feme" is Kelsen's own term — the medieval Vehmgericht's clandestine killing; the casebook prints "Feme.")
  • Self-interpretation: "A plant is unable to tell the classifying botanist anything about itself… But an act of human conduct can indeed carry a legal self-interpretation": parliament expressly declares it is enacting a statute; a man calls his disposition "last will and testament"; two men declare they are making a contract. "The scientist investigating the law, sometimes finds a legal self-interpretation which anticipates his own interpretation."

04.4 The norm — scheme of interpretation, and the "ought"

(a) The norm as scheme of interpretation

The external fact "as an element of nature, is not an object of legal cognition." The specifically legal meaning "is derived from a 'norm' whose content refers to the act; this norm confers legal meaning to the act, so that it may be interpreted according to this norm. The norm functions as a scheme of interpretation." Even the view that the act is a natural phenomenon "is only a specific interpretation, different from the normative, namely a causal interpretation."

And the chain: "The norm which confers upon an act the meaning of legality or illegality is itself created by an act, which, in turn receives its legal character from yet another norm." Death penalty not murder: "results from a thinking process: from the confrontation of this act with the criminal code and the code of criminal procedure." Letters = contract: conformity with the civil code. An assembly is a parliament, its meaning a statute: "from the conformity of all these facts with the norms laid down in the constitution."

(b) Norm and norm creation

The legal order is "a normative order of human behavior — a system of norms." "By 'norm' we mean that something ought to be or ought to happen, especially that a human being ought to behave in a specific way." Norms command, permit — and "particularly… authorize" — "authorize" meaning "to confer upon someone else a certain power, specifically the power to enact norms himself." The acts whose meaning is a norm "are acts of will."

The broad "ought": customary usage restricts "ought" to commands, "may" to permissions, "can" to authorizations; "in the present work the word 'ought' is used to express the normative meaning of an act directed toward the behavior of others; this 'ought' includes 'may' and 'can.'"

The norm is not the will-act: "A norm can be valid even if the act of will whose meaning the norm is, no longer exists… It is incorrect… to characterize norms… as the 'will' or the 'command' of the legislator or state, if by 'will' or 'command' a psychological act of will is meant. The norm is the meaning of an act of will, not the act of will."

04.5 Validity and effectiveness — the "is" and the "ought" of law

"Since the validity of a norm is an ought and not an is, it is necessary to distinguish the validity of a norm from its effectiveness. Effectiveness is an 'is-fact' — the fact that the norm is actually applied and the fact that people actually behave according the norm."

But there is a connection: "A general legal norm is regarded as valid only if the human behavior that is regulated by it actually conforms with it, at least to some degree. A norm that is not obeyed by anybody anywhere… is not regarded as a valid legal norm. A minimum of effectiveness is a condition of validity." Validity also "presupposes… that it is possible to behave in a way contrary to it" — a norm prescribing the necessarily-occurring is as senseless as one prescribing the impossible.

Time: "A legal norm becomes valid before it becomes effective"; a court applying a statute "immediately after promulgation" applies "a valid legal norm." But "a legal norm is no longer considered to be valid if it is permanently ineffective. Effectiveness is a condition of validity in the sense that effectiveness has to join the positing of a legal norm if the norm is not to lose its validity."

Two facts under "effectiveness": (1) the norm "is applied by the legal organs (particularly the law courts)… the sanction in a concrete case is ordered and executed"; (2) "this norm is obeyed by the individuals subjected to the legal order." The ideal case: "if the stipulation of sanctions intends to prevent the commission of delicts, we are faced with the ideal case of the validity of a legal norm if this norm is never applied, because the awareness… of the sanction… has become the motive to refrain." Effectiveness is then confined to obedience. And obedience may rest on other motives: where the delict is also a religious delict, "the law is effective… because religion is effective."

04.7 Positive and negative regulation — commanding, authorizing, permitting

  • Behaviour is positively regulated when a definite action or omission is commanded (the omission commanded = the action forbidden). "To say that the behavior of an individual is commanded… amounts to the same as saying the individual is obliged."
  • Positively regulated also when the individual is authorized to bring about certain consequences — "particularly… to create norms"; or, in a system with sanctions, authorized to perform coercive acts; or permitted to perform an act otherwise forbidden — "a norm which limits the sphere of validity of a general norm that forbids the act." Self-defence is the example: a general norm forbids force; a special norm permits it in self-defence.
  • He who complies with an authorization or permission "applies" the norm: the judge applies the statute to a concrete case "by a decision which constitutes an individual norm"; the enforcement officer applies the individual norm of the judgment; the self-defender applies the permitting norm.
  • Negative regulation: behaviour "not forbidden… without being positively permitted… is permitted only in a negative sense." Negative permitting is "fundamentally connected with the function of commanding — a definite human behavior can be permitted only within a normative order that commands different kinds of behavior."
  • "Permit" = "entitle": "If A is commanded to endure that B behaves in a certain way, it is said that B is permitted (that is, entitled) to behave in this way… the quality of B's behavior 'to be permitted' is merely the reflex of the quality of A's behavior 'to be commanded.'"

04.8 The social order — retribution, blood revenge, and the religious mirror

  • Social order: a normative order regulating human behaviour "in its direct or indirect relations to other human beings." "Morals and law are such social orders." (Logic is not: its norms regulate thought, "one does not think 'toward' another man.")
  • Motivation: the function of every social order is to motivate "refrain from certain acts deemed detrimental 'socially'… and to perform certain acts deemed socially useful" — through "the idea men have of norms."
  • Ideal types of order: (1) commanding without any consequence attached; (2) command + reward for compliance or punishment for the opposite — "the principle of retribution"; (3) the legal order's type: command "just attaching a disadvantage to the opposite behavior." Reward and punishment may both be called "sanctions," but usually "only punishment, not reward."
  • Socially organized sanctions: acts "to be performed by certain individuals designated by the social order in a procedure regulated by this order." "The oldest sanction of this kind is blood revenge" in primitive society — executed by the victim's kin group against the killer's; it "originally worked only in the relation between groups," developing within one group only when the community grew beyond the family. Non-fulfilment of the revenge obligation is left to "the transcendental sanction" of the murdered man's spirit.
  • The religious development: "centralization of the superhuman authority… the many spirits of the dead were reduced to a few gods and finally to one all-powerful God"; the world beyond "split into a heaven for the good and a hell for the evil." Punishment dominates: "the legally correct behavior of primitive men… is determined primarily by the fear of misfortunes… (the so-called tabus)"; "the image of hell… is much more vivid than the usually vague idea of a life in heaven." The most important social order, the legal, "essentially makes use only of punishment."

04.9 The legal order (a) — an order of human behaviour

"A theory of law must begin by defining its object matter… to start from the usage of language" — "law" as equivalent to Recht, droit, diritto. Comparing what different peoples at different times have called "law": "all these objects turn out to be orders of human behavior." And the key definition: "An 'order' is a system of norms whose unity is constituted by the fact that they all have the same reason for their validity; and the reason for the validity of a normative order is a basic norm — from which the validity of all norms of the order are derived. A single norm is a valid legal norm, if it corresponds to the concept of 'law' and is part of a legal order; and it is part of a legal order, if its validity is based on the basic norm of that order."

  • Animals and things as subjects: primitive legal orders regulate non-humans — "we read in the Bible that an ox that has killed a man ought to be killed"; "in ancient Athens, there was a special Court, in which a stone or spear or any other object could be tried"; "in the Middle Ages it was possible to sue an animal… The accused animal was condemned and executed in formal legal procedure, exactly like a human criminal." Explanation: "animistic ideas, according to which not only men, but also animals, and inanimate objects have a 'soul.'" Modern orders regulate only human behaviour — but may prescribe man's behaviour towards animals, plants and things (killing game at certain times; damaging rare plants or historic buildings).
  • Positive or negative: behaviour may be a positive action or an omission; the order regulates "only so far as it refers, directly or indirectly, to other individuals." Relations may be individual (refrain from killing; debtor pays creditor) or collective (military service; a punishable suicide attempt).
  • The decisive point: "the legal norm obliges the debtor not only and, perhaps, not so much in order to protect the creditor, but in order to maintain a certain economic system" — "in the last analysis, it is this relation to the legal community which is decisive."

04.10 The legal order (b) — a coercive order

"The first characteristic… common to all social orders designated by the word 'law' is that they are orders of human behavior. A second characteristic is that they are coercive orders… they react… with a coercive act; that is to say, by inflicting on the responsible individual an evil — such as deprivation of life, health, liberty, or economic values — which, if necessary, is imposed upon the affected individual even against his will by the employment of physical force." (The criminal who "wishes to suffer the punishment" or commits a crime "in order to go to jail where he can be sure of food and shelter" shows "ordinarily the affected individual regards the coercive act as an evil" — such cases are exceptions.)

04.11 The legal order (c) — normative meaning, the robber gang, and the basic norm

"Under threat of" is the wrong description. "The meaning of a threat is that an evil will be inflicted under certain conditions; the meaning of a legal order is that certain evils ought to be inflicted." This "is not only the subjective meaning of the acts by which the law is established but also their objective meaning." The highwayman's command "also has the subjective meaning of an 'ought'… In this respect… there is no difference between describing the command of a robber and the command of a legal organ. The difference appears only when the objective meaning of the command is described": we attribute to the one, not the other, "the objective meaning of a norm binding the addressed individual."

The decisive question: "Why do we suppose that of the two acts, which both have the subjective meaning of an 'ought,' only one established a valid, that is, binding, norm?… What is the reason for the validity of the norm?" The answer lies in the chain of interpretation: the killing is the execution of a court decision; the court's act is the execution of a statute; the statute is the execution of the constitution; "for the historically first constitution such an interpretation is possible only, if we presuppose that one ought to behave according to the subjective meaning of the act… This norm is the basic norm of the national legal order. It is not established by a positive legal act, but is presupposed… To make manifest this presupposition is an essential function of legal science. This presupposition is the ultimate (but in its character conditional and therefore hypothetical) reason for the validity of the legal order."

The basic norm, formulated: "If the law is conceived of as a coercive order, then the formula by which the basic norm of a national legal order is expressed runs as follows: 'Coercion of man against man ought to be exercised in the manner and under the conditions determined by the historically first constitution.'" It "refers only to a constitution which is the basis of an effective coercive order: only if the actual behavior of the individuals conforms, by and large, with the subjective meaning of the acts… are the acts interpreted as legal acts."

Why not the robber gang? An isolated act cannot be legal — "law… is not a single norm, but a system of norms." An organized gang has an internal order (robbery and murder forbidden between members — "if robbery and murder were not forbidden in the relations between the robbers, no community, no robber gang would exist") and an external one. Why is its coercive order not a legal order? "Because no basic norm is presupposed… But why is no such basic norm presupposed? Because this order does not have the lasting effectiveness without which no basic norm is presupposed" — if the gang's activity is treated as illegal and terminated by the legal order of the territory; "in short, if the coercive order regarded as the legal order is more effective than the coercive order constituting the gang."

Pirate states: the "pirate states" of the northwest African coast (Algiers, Tunis, Tripolis), preying on Mediterranean navigation "in defiance of international law," were nonetheless "states": their internal order "presumably prohibited mutual employment of force… so that the minimum of collective security existed." A state may be illegal externally yet a state.

Justice is not the criterion — St. Augustine's challenge. Collective security/peace "is an objectively determinable fact… not a value judgment… this value, therefore, cannot be made an element of the concept of law and can therefore not serve as a criterion for the distinction between a legal community and a robber gang." Augustine's Civitas Dei is the contrary position: "Set justice aside then, and what are kingdoms but thievish purchases? because what are thieves' purchases but little kingdoms?"; "Where true justice is wanting, there can be no law." But Augustine's justice — "a virtue distributing onto everyone his due" — turns out to mean giving "the true God… (the Judeo-Christian God, not the gods of the Romans) what is his due": "with this, Roman Law is denied the character of law." The consequence is unacceptable: if justice is the criterion, "the capitalistic coercive order of the West is not law from the point of view of the Communist ideal of justice, nor the Communist coercive order of the Soviet Union from the point of view of the capitalist ideal of justice. A concept of law with such consequences is unacceptable by a positivist legal science."

The revolution test: after the French (18th century) and Russian (20th century) revolutions, other states hesitated to treat the new orders as law — the one had "violated the monarchic principle of legitimacy," the other "had abolished private property of the means of production"; "for the last-named reason, even American courts refused to acknowledge acts of the revolutionary Russian government as legal acts; the courts declared that these were not acts of a state, but of a robber gang. However, as soon as the revolution-born coercive orders turned out to be effective, they were recognized as legal orders, the governments as state governments, and their acts as state acts, that is, legal acts."

04.12 Legal obligations without sanctions? — the two objections answered

  1. A coercive act may be merely authorized, not commanded. "The definition of law as a coercive order can be maintained even if the norm that stipulates a coercive act is not itself essentially connected with a norm that attaches… a sanction to the nonordering or nonexecuting of the coercive act — if, therefore, the coercive act… is to be interpreted objectively not as commanded but only as authorized or positively permitted."
  2. Norms that do not stipulate coercive acts (permitting, authorizing, and even commanding without a sanction). They can be maintained "insofar as they are dependent norms, because they are essentially connected with norms that stipulate the coercive acts." The typical argument against coercion in the definition cites "the norms of constitutional law" — the precise point the dependent-norm doctrine answers (§04.13).

The overall positivism of the method: "From the definition of law as a coercive order follows that a behavior may be regarded as legally commanded (i.e., as the content of a legal obligation) only if the contrary behavior is made the condition of a coercive act directed against the individual thus behaving."

04.13 Dependent legal norms — and the "rule of law" of legal science

The superfluous first norm. If one norm commands behaviour and a second attaches a sanction to the opposite behaviour, "the first norm is not an independent norm, but fundamentally tied to the second"; the first "merely designates — negatively — the condition under which the second stipulates the sanction; and if the second one positively designates the condition under which it stipulates the sanction, then the first one is superfluous from the point of view of legislative technique."

Modern criminal codes "do not contain norms that prohibit, like the Ten Commandments, murder, adultery and other crimes": "a norm: 'You shall not murder' is superfluous, if a norm is valid: 'He who murders ought to be punished.'" The code prohibits "by attaching to it a sanction."

05.2 Hart's indictment of Austin — the four failures, restated as exam bullets

  1. Self-bindingness: criminal statutes bind the enactors; a sovereign outside the obedience circle cannot explain it. Rescue device (official v. private capacity) needs — and therefore smuggles in — a rule defining what must be done to legislate, the very thing the command theory says is lawless.
  2. Power-conferring rules: powers to legislate, adjudicate, marry, make wills, contract — "cannot, without absurdity, be construed as orders backed by threats." Austin's "fragments of duty-imposing rules" reading "distorts the ways in which these are spoken of, thought of, and actually used in social life," like saying all rules of a game "are 'really' directions to the umpire."
  3. Mode of origin: rules from custom do not arise by "anything analogous to explicit prescription"; the "tacit order" device fits "a general who deliberately refrains from interfering," not a modern system.
  4. Continuity and identification: the "sovereign, habitually obeyed and necessarily exempt" cannot account for continuity of legislative authority; and "the sovereign person or persons could not be identified with either the electorate or the legislature of a modern state."

And the deepest point: the failure is "instructive" — at each point "it was possible to see… why it was bound to fail and what is required for a better account. The root cause of failure is that the elements out of which the theory was constructed, viz. the ideas of orders, obedience, habits, and threats, do not include, and cannot by their combination yield, the idea of a rule."

05.5 What each leaves behind — the standard criticisms (grey — standard doctrine; the readings supply most, the rest is exam-side)

Austin's loose ends

  1. The sovereign cannot be "identified with the electorate or the legislature" (Hart).
  2. No account of constitutional limits (Hart's US/UK contrast) or of rules that bind law-makers.
  3. The "habit" analysis misses the normative dimension: obedience out of habit ≠ acceptance of a standard (Hart's internal point of view).
  4. International law and constitutional law handled by fiat.

Hart's loose ends

  1. The rule of recognition's existence is a practice — but what if the practice is contested (revolution)? Hart: then the system is not established — but the description "it can neither be valid nor invalid" leaves the revolutionary legal order described sociologically, not legally.
  2. The content of the rule of recognition is uncertain in hard cases — "questions as to the precise content and scope… may not admit of a clear or determinate answer."
  3. Officials' acceptance is the pivot — the ordinary citizen's viewpoint gets a thinner treatment.

Kelsen's loose ends

  1. The basic norm is presupposed — by whom, and why that one? Hart: it is "seriously misleading" to call it an assumption; it hides the factual character of the practice.
  2. "A minimum of effectiveness" — how much? "by and large" is as imprecise as Austin's "habitual."
  3. The exclusion of justice makes law and power dangerously close — the natural-law counter (Unit 7 arrives with the attack).

05.7 The three one-paragraph verdicts

Austin

The founder's power — and his limits. He gave jurisprudence a province and a method: isolate positive law, analyse its terms, stop asking what law ought to be. His command–duty–sanction analysis remains the cleanest introduction to the imperative element of law; his sovereignty analysis explains why law needs a determinate source. But the model is built for a simple state: it cannot bind the law-maker, cannot house power-conferring rules, cannot account for continuity, and cannot see rules as standards rather than threats.

Hart

The repair — and its price. By moving validity into secondary rules and obligation into the internal point of view, Hart saved positivism from Austin's dead ends: the self-binding statute, the constitution, custom all find their place. The price is that the ultimate rule is no longer a legal rule at all — "there is no rule providing criteria for the assessment of its own legal validity"; and its existence "is a matter of fact." Hart's positivism is therefore a social-fact positivism: law rests on practice, and the practice runs out where the revolution begins.

Kelsen

The most rigorous — and most austere. Kelsen's purity buys a science of law: norms described as norms, validity as an ought, effectiveness as a conditioning fact, justice excluded by the relativity of value-judgments. The basic norm supplies a reason for validity without a legislator ("the law is the meaning of an act of will, not the act of will"). The cost is that the ultimate foundation is a presupposition — "conditional and therefore hypothetical" — and the law's content is morally indifferent by design. Austin gave law a sovereign; Hart gave it officials; Kelsen gave it a theory.

Q1 · "Austin's theory of law as the command of the sovereign. State it, then show how far Hart's criticisms destroy it."

Part I — the theory. (1) The province: "the matter of jurisprudence is positive law: law set by political superiors to political inferiors" — severed from the law of God, positive morality (opinion-rules: honour, fashion, international law) and metaphorical law. (2) The genus: "a rule laid down for the guidance of an intelligent being by an intelligent being having power over him"; "every law… properly so called is a command." (3) The analysis: command = wish + "power and purpose… to inflict an evil" (form is irrelevant — Tacitus's prayers "quibus contradici non posset"); command, duty and sanction are "one complex notion" in three aspects ("command and duty are correlative terms"); rewards give no duty — "it is only by conditional evil, that duties are sanctioned." (4) Generality: a law obliges "generally to acts or forbearances of a class" — the judge's sentence is an occasional command. (5) Superiority = might — "that laws emanate from superiors is… an identical proposition." (6) Sovereignty: a determinate superior, habitually obeyed by the bulk, not habitually obedient to a like superior — the union of positive and negative "marks." Every positive law is set by such a sovereign: "the legislator is he, not by whose authority the law was first made, but by whose authority it continues to be a law" (Hobbes). (7) Consequences: custom becomes law when "adopted as such by the courts" = a tacit command; international law is "law… set by general opinion," with moral sanctions; declaratory, repealing and imperfect laws are exceptions within the province.

Part II — Hart's four failures. (1) A criminal statute "commonly applies to those who enact it" — the gunman-style analysis cannot make a sovereign self-binding; the "official v. private capacity" rescue "involved supplementing the theory with something it does not contain: this is the notion of a rule defining what must be done to legislate." (2) Power-conferring rules (to legislate, adjudicate, marry, contract) "cannot, without absurdity, be construed as orders backed by threats." (3) Mode of origin: rules from custom are not born of "explicit prescription"; the "tacit order" device fits a general, not a state. (4) Continuity and identification: the sovereign "habitually obeyed and necessarily exempt" cannot explain continuity of legislative authority "and the sovereign person or persons could not be identified with either the electorate or the legislature." Root cause: "the ideas of orders, obedience, habits, and threats… cannot by their combination yield the idea of a rule."

Part III — verdict. Hart's criticisms destroy the command model as a general theory; they do not destroy the insight that law has an imperative core and a determinate source. Austin fails because he built law from obedience rather than from rules; the remedy is the union of primary and secondary rules (Q2). Austin's own lasting contributions remain: the analytical method, the command–duty–sanction triad, and the sovereignty analysis which explains why law, unlike morality, has a determinate origin.

Q2 · "Law is the union of primary and secondary rules." Elucidate, showing why the union is needed.

(1) The two types. Primary rules "require human beings to do or abstain from certain actions, whether they wish to or not" — they impose duties, concern "physical movement or changes." Secondary rules are "parasitic upon or secondary": they "provide that human beings may… introduce new rules of the primary type, extinguish or modify old ones, or in various ways determine their incidence," conferring powers — public or private — and leading "to the creation or variation of duties or obligations." (2) The pre-legal world. A society of primary rules alone (Hart calls it such a structure, not "custom") can exist: it needs rules against violence, theft and deception, and a majority living by the rules from the internal point of view. (3) The three defects. Uncertainty — "a set of separate standards, without any identifying or common mark" (like etiquette); no procedure to settle what the rules are. Static character — change only by slow growth and decay; no deliberate adaptation; in the extreme case no release, no transfer of benefits. Inefficiency — diffuse social pressure, no final authoritative determination of violation, self-help and vendetta; the lack of official determination being "the much more serious defect." (4) The three remedies. For uncertainty, the rule of recognition: "some feature or features possession of which by a suggested rule is taken as a conclusive affirmative indication that it is a rule of the group" — from an authoritative text to general characteristics (enactment, long practice, relation to judicial decisions) ranked "in an order of superiority, as by the common subordination of custom or precedent to statute." For the static character, rules of change — empowering legislation and repeal, connected to recognition (which incorporates "a reference to legislation"), and their private counterpart (wills, contracts, transfers — "the exercise of limited legislative powers by individuals," as Kelsen's theory shows). For inefficiency, rules of adjudication — empowering authoritative determinations of breach, defining judge/court, jurisdiction and judgment; "a system which has rules of adjudication is necessarily also committed to a rule of recognition of an elementary and imperfect sort"; plus centralized sanctions. (5) The union. "All three remedies together are enough to convert the regime of primary rules into what is indisputably a legal system"; the secondary rules are "all about such rules," on a different level. "We have here not only the heart of a legal system, but a most powerful tool for the analysis" — of obligation and rights, validity and source, legislation and jurisdiction, sanction — and beyond law, of "the state, of authority, and of an official," because these "essentially involve reference to… the internal point of view." (6) Caveat (Hart's own). He does not claim the union is present wherever "law" is used; the range of cases is linked "by less direct relations — often of analogy… to a central case." The union is the central case, given a central place for "their explanatory power."

Q3 · "The rule of recognition: nature, ultimacy, supremacy — and validity v. efficacy."

Nature. A secondary rule for the conclusive identification of primary rules; in developed systems it works "by reference to some general characteristic" — enactment, custom, judicial decision — with criteria "arranged… in an order of superiority." Even its simplest form "introduces… the idea of a legal system" and contains "the germ of the idea of legal validity." Ultimacy. Follow the chain: Oxfordshire by-law → Minister of Health's statutory order → enabling statute → "what the Queen in Parliament enacts is law" — "we are brought to a stop in inquiries concerning validity." The ultimate rule "provides criteria for the assessment of the validity of other rules; but… there is no rule providing criteria for the assessment of its own legal validity." Hence "it can neither be valid nor invalid but is simply accepted as appropriate" — saying its validity is "assumed but cannot be demonstrated" is "like saying that we assume, but can never demonstrate, that the standard metre bar in Paris… is itself correct." Supremacy. One criterion is supreme where rules identified by it prevail over those identified by the others. "Supreme" must be disentangled from "legally unlimited": the UK has an unlimited legislature (supreme criterion = enactment); the US has no legally unlimited legislature, yet has "an ultimate rule of recognition" and a supreme criterion in its Constitution (clauses outside the amending power). Existence. "The assertion that it exists can only be an external statement of fact"; a subordinate rule "may be valid and in that sense 'exist' even if it is generally disregarded," but "the rule of recognition exists only as a complex, but normally concordant, practice of the courts, officials, and private persons in identifying the law by reference to certain criteria. Its existence is a matter of fact." Validity v. efficacy. Valid ≠ enforced; but a general and protracted disregard destroys the system (the "normal context… for making any internal statement is absent"); validity statements "presuppose the truth of the external statement of fact that the system is generally efficacious" — without meaning it (Roman law taught as living; the White Russian's claim). The predictive theory of validity commits "the same mistake" as the predictive theory of obligation: the judge's statement "constitutes not a prophecy of but part of the reason for his decision."

Q4 · "What is the Pure Theory of Law? Explain the basic norm and its function."

Purity. "The Pure Theory of Law is a theory of positive law… in general"; "its exclusive purpose is to know and to describe its object… what and how the law is, not how it ought to be. It is a science of law (jurisprudence) not legal politics." "Pure" because it "attempts to eliminate from the object of this description everything that is not strictly law" — psychology, sociology, ethics, political theory: the "methodological syncretism" of 19th–20th century legal science "obscures the essence of the science of law." The two elements. Every legal fact is an act (a happening in time and space) plus its legal meaning — "the meaning conferred upon the act by Law." The meaning is not perceptible: it is conferred by a norm — "the norm functions as a scheme of interpretation" — and the norm itself is created by an act which "receives its legal character from yet another norm": murder v. execution of sentence (criminal code), contract (civil code), statute (constitution). Norm. "By 'norm' we mean that something ought to be or ought to happen"; the "ought" is broad — it "includes 'may' and 'can'"; the acts whose meaning is a norm "are acts of will," but "the norm is the meaning of an act of will, not the act of will" (legislators die; statutes remain valid). Validity. Validity is an ought; effectiveness an is-fact; "a minimum of effectiveness is a condition of validity"; a norm "no longer considered to be valid if it is permanently ineffective"; validity precedes effectiveness in time. The basic norm. The validity of every norm is traced back: court decision ← statute ← constitution. "For the historically first constitution such an interpretation is possible only, if we presuppose that one ought to behave according to the subjective meaning of the act" — i.e., the basic norm: "Coercion of man against man ought to be exercised in the manner and under the conditions determined by the historically first constitution." It "is not established by a positive legal act, but is presupposed"; it "is the ultimate (but… conditional and therefore hypothetical) reason for the validity of the legal order"; and it "refers only to a constitution which is the basis of an effective coercive order." Function. It answers the decisive question — why one act's subjective "ought" is also objectively valid and another's is not (the highwayman's command and the legal organ's command) — and thereby distinguishes law from the robber gang: no basic norm is presupposed for the gang, "because this order does not have the lasting effectiveness." It is also the instrument by which legal science makes "manifest" its presupposition. (5) Critical note: Hart replies that "assumed or postulated" is "seriously misleading" — the ultimate rule "can neither be valid nor invalid"; its existence is a matter of fact. Kelsen's answer would be that the presupposition is a condition of interpretation, not a statement about practice — the great positivist divide of the 20th century.

Q5 · "Austin on sovereignty: the two marks, their illustrations, and their fallibility."

Concise formula: "If a determinate human superior, not in a habit of obedience to a like superior, receive habitual obedience from the bulk of a given society, that determinate superior is sovereign in that society, and the society (including the superior) is a society political and independent." The positive mark: the generality of the society is in a habit of obedience to a determinate and common superior. The negative mark: that superior is not habitually obedient to a determinate human superior. "It is the union of that positive, with this negative mark, which renders that certain superior sovereign." Corollaries: (i) determinacy — "no indeterminate party can command expressly or tacitly, or can receive obedience"; (ii) generality of class — one and the same superior; (iii) a "considerable" number (Montesquieu: political power "necessarily" comprises several families). Illustrations: the allied armies in France (1815) — transient obedience, no sovereignty; Saxony and the Holy Alliance — permanent inferiority without habitual submission = independence "in fact or practice"; intestine war — either two societies or anarchy; the viceroy — a society political but subordinate; savage tribes — only family chiefs, hence their "laws" are customary rules and mere positive morality. Fallibility: "the positive mark… is a fallible test" — "how many… what proportion… how often… how long… in order that that obedience may be habitual?"; the negative mark is "also an uncertain measure" (the Saxon boundary case); the definition is "vague or uncertain," and the same vagueness infects "reasonable time… libel and fair criticism… idiocy or lunacy" — whence "the very absurd enquiry whether questions of this kind are questions of law or of fact. The truth is that they are questions neither of law nor of fact" — the problem is applying a known rule to a known fact. Consequences: international law is not positive law ("law… set by general opinion," moral sanctions); the definitions of Grotius (perfect independence — no government has it) and Von Martens ("ought not to receive commands") both fail — the one by omitting the positive essential and misstating the negative, the other by four counted objections. Forms: monarchy (one member) v. aristocracy in the generic sense (a number); within the latter, oligarchy/aristocracy/democracy by proportion — "can hardly be distinguished with precision"; and in a monarchy the sovereign portion is "simply or purely sovereign," whereas in a governing body the members are "sovereign as viewed from one aspect, but… also subject as viewed from another."

Q6 · "The gunman situation and the internal point of view: Hart's account of obligation."

The situation. A orders B to hand over his money, threatening to shoot him. For the command theory this displays "the notion of obligation or duty in general… writ large": legal obligation is the gunman situation plus sovereignty plus generality. B was obliged — B had no obligation. "We would say that B, if he obeyed, was 'obliged'… We should misdescribe the situation if we said… that B 'had an obligation' or a 'duty.'" "Obliged" is "in the main, a psychological" statement — beliefs and motives; it fails where the threatened harm is trivial (a pinch) or where there are no reasonable grounds for believing the threat will be carried out. Obligation proper. The statement that someone had an obligation is a statement that a social rule exists requiring the conduct, deviation from which is "generally regarded as a lapse" — an occasion for criticism, demands and pressure. Rules exist, in the relevant sense, only where there is the internal point of view: the view of those "who do not merely record and predict behaviour conforming to rules, but use the rules as standards for the appraisal of their own and others' behaviour" — for whom "the violation of a rule is… not merely a basis for the prediction that a hostile reaction will follow but a reason for hostility." The external point of view — the observer's, confined "to the observable regularities of behaviour" — can say only "I was obliged," "I am likely to suffer," "they will do that to you"; it "cannot reproduce… the way in which the rules function as rules in the lives of those who normally are the majority of society." The tension. Social life "is likely to consist in a tension between those who… accept and voluntarily co-operate in maintaining the rules, and those who… reject the rules and attend to them only from the external point of view as a sign of possible punishment"; the theorist must "remember the presence of both these points of view and not to define one of them out of existence." Conclusion: "all our criticisms of the predictive theory of obligation may be best summarized as the accusation that this is what it does to the internal aspect of obligatory rules." The gunman case is therefore the pivot on which Hart turns from Austin's sanction-based duty to a rule-based obligation — which is why the internal point of view is "the reason… an analysis in terms of primary and secondary rules has this explanatory power."

Q7 · "Custom and judge-made law in Austin — and how Hart and Kelsen re-describe them." (cross-feed with Unit-5)

Austin. Two opinions are groundless: that custom is law independently of the state (its German admirers), and that judge-made law is "purely the creature of the judges" (their opponents) — the latter calls the imputation to the sovereign "one of the foolish or knavish fictions with which lawyers… have perplexed and darkened the simplest and clearest truths." Austin's own: "At its origin, a custom is a rule of conduct which the governed observes spontaneously… The custom is transmuted into positive law, when it is adopted as such by the courts of justice, and when the judicial decisions fashioned upon it are enforced by the power of the state. But before it is adopted… it is merely a rule of positive morality." The judge "is merely a minister"; "the portion of the sovereign power which lies at his disposition is merely delegated" — expressly or "in the way of acquiescence." Commands are express or tacit; judicial rulings on custom are "tacit commands of the sovereign legislature." Custom is established by the state "directly, when… promulged in its statutes;… circuitously, when… adopted by its tribunals." Hart. Custom figures not as command but as one of the criteria "in an order of superiority" — "the common subordination of custom or precedent to statute, the latter being a 'superior source' of law"; and the rule of recognition may identify rules "by reference to… their long customary practice, or their relation to judicial decisions." Kelsen. The constitution may "institute custom as a law-creating fact"; the norms regulating custom as a source are authorizing, and hence dependent norms — conditions of the sanctions; a single norm of the protection of custom isn't needed. The comparison (for the Unit-5 answer): all three agree with Jain's case-law that judicial adoption is the moment of law — but locate it differently: in the sovereign's will (Austin), in the criteria of recognition (Hart), in the constitution's authorization (Kelsen). Austin's "tacit command" is exactly the "analytical view" of custom Jain reported in Unit-5; Hart's "order of superiority" is the Unit-5 hierarchy restated; Kelsen gives the "sources" of law a normative grammar.

Q8 · "Compare Austin's command theory, Hart's rule theory and Kelsen's pure theory of law."

First movement — the common project. All three are positivists: they describe law as it is; they separate its existence from its goodness (Austin has "no concern" with the ends of government; Hart keeps value questions out of validity-testing — "a statement of value" is a different move; Kelsen: "a concept of law with such consequences is unacceptable by a positivist legal science"); and all three make the source of law central. Second movement — the differences. (1) What law is: a command imposed by a superior (Austin); a union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules (Hart); a coercive order of norms, the objective meaning of legal acts (Kelsen). (2) Obligation: liability to evil, correlative with command (Austin); a social rule seen from the internal point of view — "obliged" v. "obligation" (Hart); an "ought" whose opposite is the condition of a coercive act (Kelsen). (3) The source of validity: the sovereign's might (habitual obedience, negative mark); the rule of recognition (a social practice — "its existence is a matter of fact"); the basic norm (presupposed — "the ultimate… and therefore hypothetical reason"). (4) Sanctions: central to definition (the evil that makes a wish a command); centralized by secondary rules but not definitional; the "decisive criterion" — but normatively: "evils ought to be inflicted," not "will be." (5) Custom and constitution: tacit command and ordinary law; criteria in order of superiority and supreme criterion; authorized law-creating fact and penultimate link to the basic norm. (6) The line of attack: Hart shows Austin's model cannot bind the law-maker, accommodate powers, explain origin or identify the sovereign ("the root cause… [the ideas] cannot… yield the idea of a rule"); against Kelsen, Hart shows the "presupposition" conceals a fact — "we only need the word 'validity'… within a system"; Kelsen's own criticism of Austin is implicit and radical: the command is a threat unless it has objective normative meaning — which only the basic-norm presupposition supplies. Verdict. The three are a ladder: Austin gives law a determinate author, Hart a rule-governed practice, Kelsen a foundation in cognition. For an Indian-law answer add the resonance (grey): the Constitution is the supreme criterion (Hart) and the reference of our basic norm (Kelsen), while Art. 13's invalidating power shows that even the unlimited UK model is not the only shape a system can take.

Short notes (each = one paragraph, with the source)

1. Positive law, positive morality, divine law

Austin's threefold division of the field: laws set by God (divine law — the only "natural law" speakable of without metaphor); laws set by men to men as political superiors (positive law — "the appropriate matter of jurisprudence"); laws set by men not as political superiors, plus opinion-rules (positive morality — honour, fashion, much of international law). Metaphorical laws (animal, vegetable, inanimate) are excluded altogether.

2. The three improprieties

Declaratory laws (authentic interpretation — but often "imperative in effect"); repealing or permissive laws (revocations of commands, remotely imperative — rights and correlative duties revived); imperfect laws in the Roman sense (wanting a sanction — "not so properly a law, as counsel, or exhortation"). Plus the companion rule: "there are no laws merely creating rights."

3. "Command and duty are correlative"

Wherever a duty lies a command has been signified, and vice versa; and with sanction the three are "the same complex notion" — each term denotes one part and connotes the residue. The logicians' language: "each denotes a different part of that notion, and connotes the residue."

4. Laws v. occasional commands

Generality of the act-class, not of the persons: "acts or forbearances of a class are enjoined generally by the former; acts determined specifically… by the latter." The corn-export order, the general mourning order, the sovereign's punishment of the legally innocent, and the judge's sentence are all particular — the last being "the best illustration" of the distinction.

5. Superiority is might

Not rank, wealth or virtue: "the power of affecting others with evil or pain, and of forcing them, through fear of that evil, to fashion their conduct to one's wishes." God's is "unbounded and resistless"; human superiority is reciprocal (monarch/multitude; assembly-man/judge). Hence "that laws emanate from superiors is… an identical proposition… the merest tautology."

6. The negative mark of sovereignty

The sovereign "must not be habitually obedient to a determinate human superior" — laws set by opinion may affect the sovereign; occasional obedience may be yielded; but no habitual obedience. This is what distinguishes the independent political society from the subordinate one (the viceroy; Saxony's boundary case) — and makes sovereignty a factual, fallible test.

7. International law is positive morality

Independent political societies live "in a state of nature" toward each other; there is no sovereign over them; hence "the law obtaining between nations is law (improperly so called) set by general opinion," enforced by moral sanctions — "fear… of provoking general hostility." (Hart deliberately avoids international law in his argument; Kelsen's international order is outside the excerpt.)

8. The gunman situation

B was "obliged" but did not "have an obligation": the first is a psychological statement (beliefs and motives; and only where the threat is serious and credible); the second asserts a social rule from which deviation is a lapse. The command theory takes the gunman as the paradigm and so "misdescribes" obligation.

9. Internal v. external point of view

Internal: participants using rules "as standards for the appraisal of their own and others' behaviour" — the forms "I had an obligation," "You have an obligation." External: the observer recording regularities — "I was obliged," "I am likely to suffer if…" — which "cannot reproduce… the way in which the rules function as rules in the lives of those who normally are the majority." Both must be remembered; the predictive theory's error is to define the internal aspect "out of existence."

10. The three defects of a primary-rule society

Uncertainty (no identifying mark, no authoritative text or official); static character (only slow growth and decay; no release or transfer in the extreme case); inefficiency (diffuse pressure, no final determination, self-help and vendetta — the absence of authoritative determination being the historically more serious defect).

11. Rules of change and private powers

Rules empowering legislation and repeal — "it is in terms of such a rule… that the ideas of legislative enactment and repeal are to be understood"; and their private counterparts: wills, contracts, transfers — with Kelsen's insight that these are "the exercise of limited legislative powers by individuals." Rex I's kingdom shows the simplest case: "whatever Rex I enacts is law."

12. Rules of adjudication

Secondary rules "empowering individuals to make authoritative determinations" of breach — the "minimal form of adjudication"; they define judge/court, jurisdiction and judgment, carry the concepts, and commit the system to an "elementary and imperfect sort" of rule of recognition — judgments become "a 'source' of law" via "a somewhat shaky inference from particular decisions."

13. The rule of recognition

The secondary rule specifying "some feature or features possession of which by a suggested rule is taken as a conclusive affirmative indication that it is a rule of the group" — from the authoritative text (the "crucial step" is acknowledgment of the writing as authoritative) to general criteria ranked in order of superiority; it "introduces… the idea of a legal system" and holds "the germ of the idea of legal validity."

14. Validity v. efficacy (Hart)

A rule may be valid though generally disregarded (subject to a possible rule of obsolescence); but total and protracted disregard destroys the system — the "normal context" for internal statements is absent (the discarded scoring rule). Internal validity statements presuppose, without meaning, general efficacy (Roman law taught as living; the White Russian).

15. The standard metre bar

Hart's reply to the "assumed/postulated" account of the ultimate rule: its validity cannot be "assumed but not demonstrated" any more than we "assume, but can never demonstrate, that the standard metre bar in Paris… is itself correct." The rule of recognition is neither valid nor invalid — "simply accepted as appropriate."

16. The basic norm

"Coercion of man against man ought to be exercised in the manner and under the conditions determined by the historically first constitution." Not enacted but presupposed; "the ultimate (but in its character conditional and therefore hypothetical) reason for the validity of the legal order"; refers only to a constitution underlying an effective coercive order; "to make manifest this presupposition is an essential function of legal science."

17. The robber gang and pirate states

The gang has an internal order and an external terror; it is not law because no basic norm is presupposed — "because this order does not have the lasting effectiveness"; the territorial legal order prevails as more effective. The pirate states (Algiers, Tunis, Tripolis) show the converse: internal order + territorial effectiveness = a state — "even if its external activity is illegal according to positive international law."

18. Dependent legal norms

Norms that do not stipulate coercive acts are tied to those that do: "You shall not murder" is superfluous if "He who murders ought to be punished"; permissions (self-defence; the UN Charter's Arts. 2(4)/39/51 unit; licences), derogating norms, authorizing norms (constitution; procedure codes) and definitional norms ("By murder is to be understood…") are dependent — so the law "does not have exclusively a commanding or imperative character," and everything fits the Rechtssatz: "under specific conditions… specific coercive acts ought to be performed."

Key
  1. "The matter of jurisprudence is positive law: law, simply and strictly so called: or law set by political superiors to political inferiors."
  2. Wish; evil to follow non-compliance; expression of the wish — the distinguishing marks being the power and the purpose to inflict the evil.
  3. Each term names the same complex notion, denoting one part and connoting the residue: command = the expression/intimation of the wish; duty = the obnoxiousness to the evil; sanction = the evil itself.
  4. Only conditional evil binds ("it is only by conditional evil, that duties are sanctioned or enforced"); the imperative part of a reward law is addressed to the party required to render the reward.
  5. (1) Commands obligatory on the whole community may be particular (corn-export; general mourning); (2) commands binding determinate persons may be laws (father's rule; God's laws to Adam; privilegia).
  6. Positive: the bulk habitually obedient to a determinate and common superior. Negative: that superior not habitually obedient to a determinate human superior. Fallible because "how many… how often… how long" cannot be fixed — "reasonable time" questions are "neither of law nor of fact" but of applicability.
  7. No sovereign exists above independent states — they live in a "state of nature" toward one another; the law between nations "is set by general opinion," enforced by moral sanctions.
  8. "The custom is transmuted into positive law, when it is adopted as such by the courts of justice" — before that it is positive morality; the judicial rules are tacit commands, the sovereign's delegated authority exercised "in the way of acquiescence."
  9. Self-bindingness; power-conferring rules; mode of origin (custom); continuity/identification of the sovereign. Root cause: the ideas of orders, obedience, habits and threats "cannot by their combination yield the idea of a rule."
  10. "Obliged" = beliefs and motives (the gunman's B); "had an obligation" = a social rule, deviation from which is a lapse — the internal point of view. Case: the gunman situation.
  11. Primary rules impose duties on physical conduct; secondary rules confer powers and "in various ways determine their incidence or control their operations" — they are "on a different level… all about such rules."
  12. Uncertainty → rule of recognition; static character → rules of change (with private power-conferring rules); inefficiency → rules of adjudication (plus centralized sanctions). "All three remedies together… indisputably a legal system."
  13. Authoritative determinations of breach "cannot avoid being taken as authoritative determinations of what the rules are" — so judgments become a "source" of law, though "somewhat shaky" (not general; depends on interpreter and judges).
  14. Not only the heart of a legal system but "a most powerful tool for the analysis" — of legal concepts (obligation, rights, validity, source, legislation, jurisdiction, sanction) and of state, authority and official, because they "essentially involve reference to… the internal point of view."
  15. By-law (Oxfordshire CC) → Minister of Health's statutory order → enabling statute → "what the Queen in Parliament enacts is law" — the rule providing criteria for other rules but with no rule providing criteria for its own validity.
  16. Supreme = rules identified by it prevail over rules identified by others; "supreme" ≠ "unlimited" (US: limited legislature, yet supreme criterion in the Constitution's un-amendable clauses; UK: unlimited, so the criterion converges with it).
  17. The claim that the ultimate rule's validity is "assumed but cannot be demonstrated": the rule "can neither be valid nor invalid but is simply accepted as appropriate"; the correct description: its existence is an external statement of fact — "a complex, but normally concordant, practice."
  18. A norm never effective is not regarded as valid, and permanently ineffective norms lose validity ("effectiveness has to join the positing"). The robber gang's order has no lasting effectiveness, so no basic norm is presupposed for it.
  19. "Coercion of man against man ought to be exercised in the manner and under the conditions determined by the historically first constitution" — presupposed, not enacted, and conditioned on effectiveness, hence "conditional and therefore hypothetical." Justice cannot be the criterion: it is a relative value-judgment (Augustine's god is not the Romans' — "Roman Law is denied the character of law"); revolutions are recognized as law once effective.
  20. Norms essentially connected to sanction-stipulating norms: the superfluous "You shall not murder"; positive permissions (self-defence; UN Charter unit; licences); derogating norms; authorizing norms (constitution, procedure codes, definitions). Conclusion: the law is described by the science of law as: under specific conditions, specific coercive acts ought to be performed.

09.6 Last word

Unit 6 is the story of one question asked three times: what makes an act of will into law? Austin answered with a sovereign: the command, the habit of obedience, the sanction — clean, rigorous, and, in the end, unable to bind its own author, unable to house powers, unable to explain continuity, unable to see that law is not merely obeyed but used. Hart repaired the theory by moving from orders to rules: primary rules carry the burdens, secondary rules carry the machinery (recognition, change, adjudication), and the internal point of view carries the meaning of obligation — but the repair costs the theory its own keystone, since the ultimate rule can neither be valid nor invalid, and "its existence is a matter of fact." Kelsen then changed the grammar: not commands but norms, whose validity is an "ought," whose foundation is a presupposition — the basic norm behind the historically first constitution — and whose test is effectiveness, not justice; the robber gang fails the test, the pirate state passes it, and the unjust state is law all the same. The three towers are three answers, three objections, and one lesson — that positivism's strength (method, purity, separation) and its vulnerability (the foundation that is willed, practiced or presupposed rather than justified) are the same fact. When Unit 7 arrives with natural law, Savigny and Pound, it will be attacking exactly this: the place where law stops asking for a reason. Keep that in mind, and no Unit-7 question will surprise you.
