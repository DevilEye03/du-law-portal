01.2 The three theses, side by side

Natural law (Fuller)

Law's morality is internal. Law is a purposive, reciprocal, ongoing enterprise — "subjecting human conduct to the governance of rules" — and the enterprise carries its own moral requirements: generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility, constancy, congruence. A system that wholly fails them does not make law at all "except in the Pickwickian sense." This is a procedural natural law: not "unjust law is not law" in the substantive sense (Fuller concedes a law may be internal-morally perfect and substantively unjust), but "lawless law is not law."

Historical (Savigny)

Law's source is national. "The nature of any particular system of law… was a reflection of the spirit of the people" — the Volksgeist (Puchta's name). Law grows "with the growth, and strengthens with the strength of the people, and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality." Hence custom is not only prior to but superior to legislation; codification is at best an end-point, not an instrument; and the jurist's task is historical understanding, not rational reconstruction.

Sociological (Pound)

Law's function is engineering. The aim is "to build as efficient a structure of society as possible" — "the satisfaction of the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction and waste." Law must recognise interests (individual, public, social), define the limits of their recognition, and secure them within those limits; the jurist's and judge's task is to classify, balance and value. Hence law must be studied (and made) with social facts in view — the eight-point programme.

02(a) Natural Law School — Lon L. Fuller and the Hart–Fuller Debate

02.1 The battle-line — Hart's positivism and the two American challengers

Ratnapala opens with the state of play: "Jurisprudence was enlivened in the second half of the 20th century by new debates about law and morality. Two of these involved Herbert Hart, the major figure in British legal positivism." Hart's position — the one Fuller attacked: the connection between law and morality is "not necessary but contingent."

  • Law "often gives effect to morality, as it prohibits crimes and torts and demands the performance of contracts." But "a law, however immoral, will be law if it is recognized as law according to the established rules of recognition."
  • The sensible response to such laws: "not to deny that they are law but to correct their effects by other laws passed where necessary with retrospective effect."

The challengers: "Two American professors of law questioned this general theory and argued that law cannot be separated from morality in the sense proposed by Hart and his positivist predecessors. The first was Lon Fuller, whose theory was inspired by the German legal philosopher Gustav Radbruch. The second was Ronald Dworkin, who was a student of Fuller at Harvard Law School." Their arguments "are related in some ways to the classical ideas of natural law… but they also introduced new dimensions." Ratnapala's own conclusion: "Fuller and Dworkin approached the question from different directions, but I conclude that their theories are fundamentally similar." (grey: Dworkin's own theory is outside these excerpts — note the reference, don't expand.)

Fuller's identity: "Lon Louvois Fuller (1902–78) is best known for the debate with Hart on the connection between law and morality. Fuller was not a natural law theorist in the traditional sense, but in some respects his theory is more far reaching." His theory "is best understood against the background of the post-war debate about the legality of crimes against humanity committed under positive law."

02.2 The historical roots — Nuremberg, the Nazi state, and the question Fuller asked

The tribunals. After the Second World War the Allied powers (US, UK, France, USSR) established international military tribunals at Nuremberg (Germany) and Tokyo (Japan) "to bring to justice officials of Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan accused of war crimes and crimes against humanity."

The common defence. "The accused persons… were acting under lawful orders and hence their actions were lawful. Any retrospective punishment of these acts, they argued, would contravene the basic rule of justice that a person should only be punished for crimes against the law (nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege)." The debate "was precipitated by the conviction of Nazi officials by the Nuremberg Tribunal."

How the Nazis came (and what they did). The Nazi Party came to power "through democratic elections under Germany's Weimar Constitution" — which "had certain fatal defects, including the president's power to suspend civil liberties in case of emergencies and the legislature's power to amend the constitution by two thirds majority. These defects allowed the Nazi Party to transform the Constitution from within." At the height of Hitler's powers the system displayed:

The seven features (from the Nuremberg judgments)

  1. Frequent retroactive laws punishing the guiltless or excusing atrocities;
  2. Enforcement of secret (unpublished) laws that denied citizens the guidance of the law;
  3. Uncontrolled discretions that identified the law with the momentary wishes of officials;
  4. A verbal order by Hitler regarded as sufficient authority to exterminate thousands;
  5. Frequent lawlessness — extra-judicial punishment by the state through "the Party in the streets" (party thuggery);
  6. Unification of legislative, executive and judicial power in Hitler (the Fuehrer principle);
  7. Total intimidation of courts — "Judges were required to decide cases as the Fuehrer would."

The question

"The question that Fuller raised was whether in such conditions there can be a legal system capable of producing laws in a meaningful sense." Note the shape of it — not "were the Nazi laws immoral?" but "were they laws at all?" Fuller's answer would be that the system had so far departed from the morality of order that it had ceased to make law "except in the Pickwickian sense."

The reading's footnote: these facts are taken from the Nuremberg judgments, particularly USA v. Altstötter (The Justices Case) 3 TWC 1 (1948); 6 LRTWC 1 (1948); 14 Ann Dig 278 (1948); and from Fuller's 1958 Harvard Law Review essay, 648–57.

02.3 The Radbruch doctrine — and its pre-history

Radbruch (1878–1949): professor at Heidelberg; leading German legal philosopher before the war; Social Democrat; Reichstag member; German Minister of Justice 1921–24; returned to Heidelberg 1926; removed by the Nazis 1933; resumed after the war, where he proposed the "Radbruch doctrine."

Pre-war position (Rechtsphilosophie 1932)

He sought "to combine elements of German legal positivism with natural law thinking, but the finished theory placed him closer to the positivists." The idea of law has three aspects, of equal value: (1) expediency — the various purposes of human co-existence; (2) justice; (3) legal certainty. "The legal certainty that positive law affords may justify even the validity of unjust or inexpedient law" — but certainty "has no absolute precedence over the demands of justice and expedience." Conflicts are left "to the resolution of individual conscience" — conscience "will usually choose legal certainty… 'but there may be "shameful laws" which conscience refuses to obey.'" And the famous line: for a person who disobeys an unjust law on conscience, "the law may prove its power but can never demonstrate its validity." Meaning: power ≠ validity; validity of an unjust law is "a matter of subjective judgment according to one's conscience"; officials may "in good conscience enforce even the most unjust laws."

Post-war revision ("Statutory Lawlessness and Supra-Statutory Law," 1946)

The horror of the Nazi reign of terror led to "much soul searching by German legal positivists." Using the grudge informer cases — persons who betrayed critics of the Nazi regime "with knowledge that the betrayed persons faced certain execution," whose defence was that they violated no law — Radbruch argued "that post-war German courts were right to convict the informers in disregard of the Nazi law." He could not do this without revising his pre-war theory, and reformulated it:

THE DOCTRINE (learn it verbatim): "The conflict between justice and legal certainty may well be resolved in this way: The positive law, secured by legislation and power, takes precedence even when its content is unjust and fails to benefit the people, unless the conflict between statute and justice reaches such an intolerable degree that the statute, as 'flawed law,' must yield to justice.… One line of distinction… can be drawn with utmost clarity: Where there is not even an attempt at justice, where equality, the core of justice, is deliberately betrayed in the issuance of positive law, then the statute is not merely 'flawed law,' it lacks completely the very nature of law. For law, including positive law, cannot be otherwise defined than as a system and an institution whose very meaning is to serve justice. Measured by this standard, whole portions of National Socialist law never attained the dignity of valid law."

Assessment (Ratnapala's): "Radbruch thus proposed a moral test that applies to all positive laws. His test is not a high hurdle to clear. It does not invalidate every unjust law, for what is unjust is a matter of legitimate debate. It nullifies only laws whose injustice is beyond rational doubt." It "proved influential in many post-war trials in German courts and in the Nuremberg trials."

02.4 Hart's criticism (1958) — "Positivism and the Separation of Law and Morals"

In 1958 the Harvard Law Review carried the two opposing articles. Hart's two criticisms of the Radbruch doctrine:

  1. Confusion of legal and moral duty. "Radbruch had confused the distinction between legal duty and moral duty. According to Hart, one may be under a legal duty to obey an inhumane law but have an overriding moral duty to disobey it. (This was, in substance, Radbruch's pre-war position.) The grudge informers and Nazi officials should not have been punished, because they acted in breach of morality and not law."
  2. The doctrine does more harm than good. Hart accused Radbruch of "extraordinary naiveté" and of having "only half-digested the spiritual message of liberalism."

The wife-catcher case (Hart's example). A woman was convicted by the post-war West German court of appeal for illegally depriving her husband of his freedom. She wanted to be rid of him and reported him for criticising the Reich, "knowing that he would be sentenced to death. He was sentenced but sent to the battlefront, where he survived against great odds." Her defence: she did no wrong under the law. Hart's point: "in convicting her, the post-war German court violated a different cherished value — the fidelity to law."

The two choices. From the positivist standpoint there were two choices: "to let her go free or punish her under a new retrospective statute." The latter "would have made plain that in punishing the woman a choice had to be made between two evils, that of leaving her unpunished and that of sacrificing a very precious principle of morality endorsed by most legal systems."

The clarity argument. "What difference does it make if the woman is punished by the court under the Radbruch doctrine or by the legislature under retrospective law?… the latter course has the value of clarity. Everyone can understand the idea that 'laws may be law but too immoral to obey,' whereas the contrary opinion that immoral law is not law is not widely held and 'raises a whole host of philosophical issues before it can be accepted.'" Ratnapala's verdict: "This is a utilitarian argument. Why engage in difficult debates when a simple solution is at hand?"

02.5 Ratnapala's four answers to Hart (the casebook's own counter-attack — use it)

  1. Radbruch was narrower than Hart supposed. "Radbruch was not saying that all immoral laws are invalid, but only those of the most inhumane kind." Hart's confidence that the public will regard even the most heinous statutes as law "may be misplaced" — lawyers know the disbelief of clients told that a statute bars their claim: "'How can this be law?' they ask, and they are not referring to genocidal laws but to laws such as those taking property without compensation." Hart's claim is "untested." And: "the Radbruch doctrine is now implemented in substance by the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court."
  2. The retrospective-statute choice is available only when the evil regime is ousted. "The Allied powers and the West German state had this choice because the Nazi regime was defeated… If the odious regime continues in power, its inhumane laws will remain in force nationally. What happens when a foreign court is asked to apply these laws?" — Oppenheimer v. Cattermole [1976] AC 249: the House of Lords considered the 1941 Nazi decree taking away the citizenship of Jews who left Germany and confiscating their property. Lord Cross of Chelsea: "To my mind a law of this sort constitutes so grave an infringement of human rights that the courts of this country ought to refuse to recognise it as a law at all" (at 278). Lord Salmon: no authority compels recognition; "the barbarity of much of the Nazi legislation… is happily unique… on the grounds of public policy they should refuse to do so" (at 281–2). Ratnapala: "from time to time a court is confronted with a statute whose injustice cannot, in a practical sense, be rectified by retrospective statute."
  3. Utilitarianism cuts both ways. "The utilitarian reasoning that Hart adopted is no more conclusive than the natural law reasoning he rejected. There is reason to think that officials may be less willing to enforce evil law if they know that in the event of a regime change a court will not entertain the defence of lawful orders. The Radbruch doctrine, by utilitarian calculation, may do more good than harm."
  4. Hart's argument conflicts with his own concept of law. In The Concept of Law Hart criticised the command theory for misunderstanding the idea of a rule. "A rule of law has an external and an internal aspect. The external aspect is objective and factual (Parliament has enacted the law). The internal aspect is mental and subjective (I ought to obey the Act of Parliament for reasons A, B and C). It is the internal aspect that distinguishes a law from the demand of a gunman… In Hart's own words, 'Law surely is not the gunman situation writ large, and legal order is surely not to be thus simply identified with compulsion' (1958, 603). This was precisely Radbruch's argument."

02.6 Fuller's response (1958) — "Positivism and Fidelity to Law"

Radbruch had died in 1949; "Harvard professor Lon Fuller… developed Radbruch's central ideas into a theory of the morality of law." Hart's proposal — invalidate the inhumane laws by retrospective statutes (rather than judicial annulment) — "does not advance the value of fidelity to the law. Whether the laws are set aside by court or legislature, the effect is the same — what was once law is declared not to have been law. The only question then is 'who should do the dirty work, the courts or the legislature'" (Fuller 1958, 649).

Fuller defended the post-war decisions: the German legal system under Nazi rule "had degenerated to the point that it ceased to make law 'except in the Pickwickian sense in which a void contract can be said to be one kind of contract'" (Fuller 1969, 39). And the famous declaration: "'To me there is nothing shocking in saying that a dictatorship which clothes itself with a tinsel of legal form can so far depart from the morality of order, from the inner morality of law itself, that it ceases to be a legal system'" (Fuller 1958, 660).

The thesis, summarised (Ratnapala's eight bullets — deploy them):

  1. Law's authority cannot be based on law, but on the moral attitudes of the community.
  2. Law is an ongoing purposive enterprise. We cannot fully understand the nature of law without considering its purpose.
  3. The purpose of law is to allow individuals to communicate and coordinate with each other.
  4. The purpose of law requires reciprocity between the ruler and the citizens. "Law is not a one-way projection of authority but a system of cooperation."
  5. Law's purpose cannot be achieved unless it displays certain qualities — the internal morality of the law.
  6. A legal system that wholly lacks one or more of these qualities fails to make law, except in the Pickwickian sense.
  7. The internal morality involves both morality of duty and morality of aspiration (basic rules of social possibility; aspiration beyond them).
  8. The external and internal moralities have reciprocal influence — "The decline of one leads inevitably to the decline of the other."

02.7 External and internal morality of law

External morality — the content

"It is a ridiculous tautology to say: The law is valid because the law says it is. Law's authority must ultimately rest on the moral attitudes of the people. The law earns fidelity by the general moral quality of its rules." This is the external morality: "the substantive content of legal rules. If this content is pervasively unjust the legal system will fail to command the respect of the community and must maintain itself by force. This is common sense." Hart agreed — in The Concept of Law he argued that "a legal system must have a minimum content of natural law to be effective" (1997, 193–200).

Internal morality — the form

"Fuller argued that law also has an internal morality that arises from its very nature as a purposive human activity. The internal morality is not principally about the content of the law but concerns the qualities that enactments must possess to become law at all." Fuller's project: begun in the 1958 reply, "continued in his Storrs Lectures at the Yale Law School and was completed in the two editions of his book The Morality of Law." (1964; revised 1969.)

02.8 Law is purposive, reciprocal and an ongoing enterprise

Purposive. "He began with the insight that law cannot be understood without considering its purpose. If we describe to a child the various parts of a computer without explaining what it does or what purposes it serves, we give them a very limited idea of what a computer is. Similarly, if we describe the law, say to a Martian, only by its formal features (that it is made by parliament, is applied by courts, etc) without saying what purpose the law serves, we give an incomplete account of it." Not the specific purposes of particular laws (revenue, social security, narcotics) "but the purpose of having law at all. The abstract purpose of law is to make it possible for individuals to communicate, to coordinate and to reach understanding with each other (Fuller 1969, 185–6). Law does this by subjecting human beings to the governance of rules (1969, 106)."

Reciprocal. "The law cannot be conceived as whatever the ruler wills if its purpose is to facilitate human coordination." Positivists wrongly conceive law "not as the product of an interplay of purposive orientations between the citizen and the government but as a one-way projection of authority, originating with government and imposing itself upon the citizen" (1969, 204). Common knowledge: laws of democratic legislatures "do not always advance human coordination, and often impede it. Yet history shows that legal systems that consistently fail to serve law's human purpose degenerate into dictatorial command systems."

Ongoing. "Law by nature is an ongoing enterprise… an aspirational activity, in the sense that it aspires to an ideal of the law that can never be fully achieved." The motor car analogy: a Rolls Royce near the ideal; at the other end "a shell of what was once a car"; "it would be silly to call this a car"; between them "cars in various conditions of excellence and dilapidation"; "at some lower point on this spectrum it is no longer sensible to call the object a motor car… if the motor car is beyond repair and ceases to serve its purpose, most rational persons will agree that it is no longer a motor car, except in the special sense that a wrecked car is a kind of car." Same with legal systems: "they achieve law to varying degrees, from excellence to abomination. At some point it is no longer sensible to call a law a law, for it fails law's purpose, which is facilitating social life. The question at this point is not whether the law is good or bad but whether there is a law at all. If the law, for example, is not publicised or requires the impossible, it cannot do its job — that of guiding people's conduct. Even a well-meant law can fail for these reasons." Just as a car needs maintenance, "the legal system needs constant attention to what makes law possible."

02.9 King Rex and the "eight ways to fail to make law"

The allegory. Assume a country ruled by King Rex, with unlimited law-making power — "utterly selfish, uncaring and incompetent." He issues no rules, only commands punishing the disobedient and rewarding the obedient — but "because of his incompetence he makes no attempt to find out who has been obedient and who has been disobedient. Consequently, he frequently punishes obedience and rewards disobedience… He fails to produce law." At other times his commands "are so confused, ambiguous and inaudible that his subjects do not know what Rex wants them to do or not do. Sometimes he commands today that something must have been done yesterday. Again, he fails to make law capable of guiding conduct." Fuller used the analogy "to demonstrate how the Nazi regime debased and destroyed the legal system through ad hoc commands, retroactive laws, ad hominem laws, secret enactments, punishment without trial, indemnities for state condoned crimes, official disregard for the law and the practice of thuggery."

02.10 The internal morality is a morality of duty and of aspiration

  • Fuller (following Kant, Adam Smith and others) distinguished the two moralities. "If we imagine a vertical moral scale, the lower half will occupy the morality of duty and the upper half the morality of aspiration."
  • Morality of duty: "the fundamental and essential moral duties… mainly of forbearances or negative injunctions — 'Do not murder,' 'Do not steal,' 'Do not break your contracts.'"
  • Morality of aspiration: "the morality of striving towards the highest achievements open to human beings."
  • The pointer: "Somewhere along this scale there is an invisible pointer where the pressure of duty leaves off and the challenge of excellence begins" — "a fluctuating pointer, hard to locate but vitally important." Social attitudes to reward and punishment indicate where it rests.
  • Asymmetry of praise and blame: a person "is usually condemned for violating morality of duty but not praised for observing it" (theft: condemned; not stealing: no praise). "In contrast, a person is usually praised for displaying morality of aspiration but not condemned for the lack of it" — the cat in the raging torrents: praised for the rescue, not condemned for thinking better of it.
  • The legal system measured on the scale: a system failing to provide "a basic framework of rules that enables peaceful social life fails the morality of duty." No legal system in history has fully met the external and internal moralities — none ever will. Ratnapala's Australian illustration: arbitrary discretions; retrospective tax and property-taking laws; laws "so complex that ordinary citizens… have no idea what they are"; obscure rules, regulations and precedents "undiscovered even by the best judges and lawyers"; unresolved contradictions — "yet most rational observers will place the Australian legal system at the upper end of Fuller's moral scale… because the constitutional features of the system make it open to self-correction." The Nazi system "fell far below the pointer."
  • The overall position: "law may fail by some standard of aspirational morality but will still be law. A law that fails the morality of duty is not law at all except in the Pickwickian sense."

02.11 Hart's rejoinder (1965) — and Fuller's two counters

Hart's 1965 review of The Morality of Law

A major part "criticised Fuller's misunderstandings of positivist positions and identified technical deficiencies." One criticism "went to the heart": Hart had earlier noted that the inner morality of law is "compatible with very great iniquity" (1961, 202) — a legislature may comply with all its requirements and still produce unjust law; hence the requirements "are ethically neutral. They can be put to good or bad use." In the review: the eight requirements "are not principles of morality but principles of good legal craftsmanship"; Fuller was "confusing morality with efficiency" (1965, 1286).

The poisoner (Hart's reductio)

"Fuller's logic would mean that principles of efficacy in any purposive activity would represent the inner morality of that activity… even despicable activities have an internal morality." The poisoner: "Common sense tells the poisoner to choose a poison that is hard to detect and administer the poison without being observed. 'But to call the principles of the poisoner's art "the morality of poisoning" would simply blur the distinction between the notion of efficiency for a purpose and those final judgments about activities and purposes with which morality in its various forms is concerned' (1965, 1286)." Ratnapala: "This is a grotesque misreading of Fuller's theory."

Fuller's counter 1 — knowledge is a precondition of moral judgment. The demand that rules be known, general and observed "seems at first sight ethically neutral, but Fuller argued that without these qualities one cannot judge the morality of the law at all because one only sees patternless ad hoc commands." An unlimited power "expressing itself solely in unpredictable and patternless interventions could be said to be unjust only in the sense that it does not act by a known rule. It is hard to call it unjust in any more specific sense until one has discovered what hidden principle, if any, guided its interventions" (1969, 157–8). And generality "also has ethical merit in mitigating discriminatory laws, such as those used in apartheid and segregation" (1969, 160).

Fuller's counter 2 — responsible agency. "Fuller's most important argument was that internal morality arises from a particular view of human beings as responsible agents. Every departure from the principles of inner morality… is an affront to people's dignity as responsible agents. To judge a person's actions by unpublished or retroactive law, or to order a person to do an act that is impossible, is to convey to them your indifference to their powers of self-determination" (1969, 162).

Fuller's counter 3 — managerial direction v. law (the revised edition's answer). Five of the principles apply to management: managers announce directions clearly, without contradictions, avoid the impossible, don't change instructions frequently. But generality, prospectivity and congruence have a special place in law that they do not have in management. "Generality of directions is only a matter of convenience in management" — subordinates cannot complain if the manager departs from general orders. "As for the principle of prospectivity, Fuller stated that the issue does not arise: no manager with a semblance of sanity would direct a subordinate today to do something yesterday" (1969, 208–9). The upshot: "the inapplicability of these three requirements brings out the essential difference between managerial direction and law. Management represents a one-way projection of authority, and hence only requires principles of efficiency. On the contrary, a relatively stable reciprocity of expectations between the lawgiver and the subject is part of the very idea of a functioning legal order. Law, unlike management, is not simply about directing persons to perform tasks set by a superior but is a matter of providing citizens with a sound and stable framework for their interactions with one another, the role of government being that of a guardian of the integrity of this framework." And the diagnosis: "his positivist critics confused his principles of inner morality with requirements of efficacy because they regarded law essentially as a one-way projection of authority."

02.12 The connection of internal and external moralities — and the two regimes

  • Clear concession: "Fuller conceded that a law that complies with internal morality may yet be unjust." The People's Republic of China's one-child policy: "general, prospective, promulgated clearly, and so forth. Yet it has been widely condemned as unjust. Inner morality is logically consistent with external injustice. This does not mean that the inner morality is not true morality."
  • The two regimes: "Regime A operates by clearly declared prospective and general rules that are consistently and scrupulously enforced, while regime B operates through ad hoc commands, retrospective laws, secret laws and extra-judicial punishment without charges or trial. Is there any moral difference between the regimes? Clearly there is. Regime A provides a degree of order that allows citizens to avoid official retribution and go about their lives even within the confines of harsh laws. In regime B nothing is certain and people are in constant jeopardy."
  • Symbiosis: "History also demonstrates repeatedly that the internal and external moralities of law are symbiotic. One does not live long without the other. This is why the internal morality of law, under its other name, 'the rule of law,' is widely regarded as a moral imperative."

03(b) The Historical School — Savigny

03.1 What the "historical outlook" comprises

"It comprises, on the one hand, inquiries into the past and evolution generally with the object of elucidating the position today. The question to be answered is to what extent the 'oughts' of contemporary laws have been fashioned by the past… On the other hand, there are inquiries into the past, especially into primitive and undeveloped communities, which are conducted for their own sake in order to discover what 'law' might appropriately be taken to mean in them. Such inquiries are distinguishable as the Anthropological approach and will be touched on at the end of the chapter."

Origin: "The Historical School arose more or less contemporaneously with Analytical positivism at the beginning of the nineteenth century, and should be regarded as another manifestation of the reaction against natural law theories. It did not emerge as something novel… it had been germinating long before then."

03.2 The prelude — Roman law, the Glossators, and the loss of historical sense

The historical-sense point: people regarded the Corpus Juris "more or less as a simultaneous product" though "more than one hundred years spanned the jurists of the Classical period, whose writings comprise the Digest, while between them and Justinian another three centuries elapsed." The lack of historical sense "led to elaborate and fanciful explanations of differences which were easily explicable on historical grounds."

03.3 The spark — Thibaut's 1814 proposal and Savigny's reply

Germany's position at the century's start: Roman law functioned as the common law, subject to canon law, imperial enactments and customary law "so far as this was still extant"; Roman law was assumed accepted as a whole "in the form of Justinian's codification as found in the works of the Glossators and Commentators"; methods of applying the law differed little from the Italian courts of the Glossators' time; "the panorama of the law as a whole was confusing, for local variations were innumerable."

Thibaut (of Heidelberg), 1814

Proposed a code on the lines of the Code Napoleon. (Reading's footnote: for the events of 1814 see Stammler, "Fundamental Tendencies in Modern Jurisprudence" (1923) 21 Mich L.R. 623.)

Savigny (1779–1861) — On the Vocation of our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence

Immediately answered Thibaut. "with which, in the words of Ihering, a new jurisprudence was born. So powerful was his influence that the move towards codification was effectively halted and it was not until 1900, after many years of sustained agitation, that Germany ultimately acquired her code, the Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch."

Five further factors combined to prepare the way: (1) the reaction against "the unhistorical assumptions of the natural law theorists… as these were exposed as hollow and false, so the need was felt for a realistic investigation into historical truths"; (2) the attempt to found legal systems on reason "without reference to past or existing circumstances had proved to be revolutionary, culminating in the French Revolution, with all its brutalities" — a reaction set in against rationalism; this weighed heavily with Savigny, "a conservative nobleman, who acquired a deep and lasting hatred for the revolution"; (3) the French conquests under Napoleon aroused European nationalism; (4) the French spread the idea of codified law, and the reaction against anything French carried hostility to codification; (5) the pioneers — Montesquieu (law shaped by social, geographical and historical considerations) and Burke (tradition as "a guide to social change"). "These factors, boosted by the genius of Savigny, started European thought along a new road."

03.4 Savigny the scholar — and his mission

  • Born Frankfurt 1779; nurtured in the natural law discipline; historical interest kindled at Marburg and Göttingen, encouraged by Niebuhr at Berlin; "a lasting veneration for Roman law."
  • 1803 — Das Recht des Besitzes (The Law of Possession): traced how the original Roman doctrines of possession had developed into the doctrines prevailing in contemporary Europe.
  • The History of Roman Law in the Middle Ages (6 volumes, 1815–31): analysed the Roman element to its roots; thesis — Roman law had been received into Germany so long ago that "her legal soul had become a mixture of Roman and local laws."
  • The System of Modern Roman Law: analysed Roman and local laws. Together, "an imperishable monument to his learning and industry." He was also Prussian Minister of Legislation.
  • The mission (not opposed to reform): to preach "the warning that reforms, which went against the stream of a nation's continuity, were doomed." A muddled, outmoded legal system "was usually due to a failure to comprehend its history and evolution." The prerequisite to reforming German law: "a deep knowledge of its history. Historical research was therefore the indispensable means to the understanding and reform of the present" — and he said: "The existing matter will be injurious to us so long as we ignorantly submit to it; but beneficial if we oppose to it a vivid creative energy — obtain the mastery over it by a thorough grounding in history and thus appropriate to ourselves the whole intellectual wealth of preceding generations." "His warning, then, was that legislators should look before they leap into reform, but he spoilt this advice by over-generalisation."

03.5 The core thesis — the Volksgeist

"The core of Savigny's thesis is to be found in his essay On the Vocation. The nature of any particular system of law, he said, was a reflection of the spirit of the people, who evolved it. This was later characterised as the Volksgeist by Puchta, Savigny's most devoted disciple. All law, according to him, is the manifestation of this common consciousness." He wrote (Puchta, Outlines of the Science of Jurisprudence, trans. Hastie): "Law grows with the growth, and strengthens with the strength of the people, and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality."

  • The nation: "only a community of people linked together by historical, geographical and cultural ties." Some boundaries clearly defined, others not — "reflected in the unity or variety of their respective laws." Even where unity is clear there may lie within it "inner circles" of variations — cities and guilds.
  • Where the Geist shows: "it is the broad principles of the system that are to be found in the spirit of the people and which become manifest in customary rules."
  • Unconscious growth: "law is a matter of unconscious growth. Any law-making should therefore follow the course of historical development. Custom not only precedes legislation, but is superior to it, and legislation should always conform to the popular consciousness."
  • Not universal: "Law is thus not of universal application; it varies with peoples and ages."
  • The standard: "The Volksgeist cannot be criticised for being what it is. It is the standard by which laws, which are the conscious product of the will as distinct from popular conviction, are to be judged. An individual jurist may misapprehend the popular conviction, but that is another matter."
  • The bridge sentence: "In place of the moral authority, which the natural lawyers of the preceding age had sought to posit behind law, the Historical School substituted social pressure, which provided the bridge between the work of the Historical School and that of the Sociological School."
  • Method: the view of law "dovetailed with Savigny's historical method of work, for if law is a reflection of people's spirit, then it can only be understood by tracing their history." But "in his revolt against the lack of historical sense, which characterised natural law theory, he swung the pendulum of legal thought too much the other way."

03.6 Lawyers as the representatives of the Geist — and Allen's objection

"The Volksgeist, according to Savigny, only formulates the rudimentary principles of a legal system. He saw clearly enough that it could not provide all the detail that is necessary. He accordingly maintained that as society, and consequently law, becomes more complex, a special body of persons is called into being whose business is to give technical, detailed expression to the Volksgeist… These are the lawyers, whose task is to reflect accurately the prevailing Geist."

Dias's verdict on this move: "This is nothing but a fictitious assumption, in no way related to reality, to cover up an obvious weakness in his principal contention. As Sir Carleton Allen said, it is not possible to pretend that the rule in Shelley's case, for instance, is rooted in the instincts of the British." And a worse implication: "The only persons who talked of the Volksgeist were academic jurists, unversed in the practical problems of legal administration. Therefore, the Volksgeist resolved itself into what these theorists imagined it to be."

The limited sense in which it is acceptable: the Geist manifests "if at all, only in a few branches of the law and, even in these, by way of modifying and adapting any innovations." So the proposition may be taken to mean "simply that in these spheres of the law it would be helpful if legislators took account of tradition when framing new laws."

03.7 Legislation subordinate to custom — the five grounds against immediate codification

"Consistently with this theory, Savigny further maintained that legislation was subordinate to custom. It should at all times conform to the Volksgeist." He did not oppose legislation or codification "at some appropriate time in the future," but his attitude "was generally one of pessimism." The five grounds:

03.8 The eleven objections to the Volksgeist (Dias's numbered list — learn the shape and three examples)

  1. No single spirit exists. There is a stream of continuity and tradition, "the difficulty lies in fixing it with precision"; "even in a small group (a fortiori in a nation) people hold different views on different issues. 'The' spirit does not exist." The thesis is "the juristic contribution to Romanticism" — his historical sense deserted him; it amounts "to the adoption of an a priori preconception" (as with his Possession: "to draw an inference from limited data, and then to use it as an a priori talisman").
  2. The reception of Roman law contradicts it. Transplanting Roman law "in the alien climate of Europe nearly a thousand years later… postulates, if anything, some quality in law other than popular consciousness." And the modern record: the German Civil Code adopted in Japan, the Swiss Code in Turkey, the French Code in Egypt — "without any apparent violence to popular susceptibilities"; the French Code displaced Roman-Dutch law in Holland and the Dutch never went back; Roman-Dutch law "flourishes today in two such dissimilar national climates as southern Africa and Sri Lanka" though dead in its homeland; the world-wide reception of English law is "evidence of supra-national adaptability and resilience."
  3. It minimises individual genius. "Every man is a product of his time, but occasionally there are men who by their genius are able to give legal development new directions" — the Classical Roman jurists, Littleton, Coke; modern policy-makers. Ehrlich: "customs are norms of conduct, juristic laws are norms for decision. They are always the creation of jurists."
  4. Its influence is limited in scope and function. National character shows "in family law rather than in commercial or criminal law" — the general reception of Roman law "did not include Roman family law"; alien systems introduced into India and Turkey "affected the indigenous family laws least of all." Distinguish the creative influence from the adaptative and abrogative: in modern times the Geist's function "is that of modifying and adapting rather than creating." Turkey's deliberate marriage-law reform produced "a fascinating process of action and reaction" — arguably confirming Savigny's warning, but showing also that the Geist works on, not above, policy. "The Volksgeist is discernible only in retrospect, but he sought to make it a test of validity, i.e. to use it in the present time frame where it is irrelevant. This confusion in his frames of reference left his theory open to attack."
  5. Law is sometimes deliberately used to change ideas and to further inter-state co-operation — Bismarck's Railway and Factories Accident Law 1871, "well before social conditions were ripe," to cut the ground from under the socialist movement.
  6. Ruling oligarchies manufacture institutions — e.g. slavery, born of convenience, not of a Volksgeist.
  7. Imitation, not conviction: "Many customs owe their origin to the force of imitation rather than to any innate conviction of their righteousness."
  8. Whose spirit? Local customs may not reflect the whole population (Savigny allowed "inner circles" — but then "how is it that only some people and not all have evolved a special rule?"); the Law Merchant was "cosmopolitan in origin." "It is not at all clear who the Volk are whose Geist determines the law."
  9. Conflict, not growth: important rules "sometimes develop as the result of conscious and violent struggle between conflicting interests within the nation" — trade-union and industrial law. "Evolution does not follow an inexorably determined path."
  10. The political objection (from opponents): taken literally, the thesis "would thwart the unification of Germany permanently by emphasising the individuality of each separate state and by fostering a parochial sense of nationalism."
  11. The internal inconsistency — Roman law as German law. While protagonist of the Volksgeist, Savigny worked "for the acceptance of a purified Roman law as the law of Germany." The Germanic school (led by Eichorn, his fellow professor at Berlin) wanted ancient Germanic laws restored; "Savigny never opposed the work of Eichorn, but he opposed the expulsion of Roman law." Explanations: personal devotion to Roman law; the belief (from Possession) that "the Romans were better craftsmen than incompetent moderns" — but post-Roman interpretations were often "adaptations… to meet contemporary needs," arguably themselves "expressions of the Volksgeist of each different country." Better reason: given long-established reception, "to dispense with it would have unsettled three centuries of development." His ultimate argument — that at reception Germanic law was "incapable of expressing the Volksgeist" — fails: "it fails to show how an alien system was better able to express it than the indigenous law. Far from the law being a reflection of the Volksgeist, it would seem that the Volksgeist had been shaped by the law."

03.9 Savigny's other excess — negotiable instruments as "logically impossible"

His veneration for Roman law "led him to advance certain dubious propositions." Roman law adhered strictly to privity of contract "with few exceptions" — no one other than parties can be entitled or obliged under a contract. "The law of negotiable instruments, of course, is a contradiction of this. Savigny accordingly condemned negotiable instruments as 'logically impossible.'" The lesson: "one of the more unfortunate results of devotion to a postulate as well as the limitations of the Volksgeist idea" — the populace had no feeling in the matter, while "the feelings of the commercially minded were strongly in favour." "Indeed, the crucial weakness of Savigny's approach was that he venerated past institutions without regard to their suitability to the present."

03.10 Gierke (1841–1921) — associations, and the Genossenschaft v. Herrschaft struggle

  • Profoundly interested in "associations" — "which has always exercised a peculiar fascination for German thinkers." Associations are sometimes treated as persons; Gierke denied that recognition of an association as a person depended on the state — "the reality of social control lies in the way in which autonomous groups within society organise themselves."
  • Classification: (a) groups organised on a territorial basis (the state) v. family/extraterritorial groups; (b) associations founded on fraternal collaboration (Genossenschaften) v. founded on domination (Herrschaften).
  • The historical reading: "legal and social history is most accurately portrayed as a perpetual struggle between the Genossenschaft and the Herrschaft." Feudal society: tight hierarchical groups on property-holding; opposed in the Middle Ages by collaborative groups (guilds, cities); these degenerated; "with the Renaissance and Reformation the state appeared as the significant factor"; in his own day "the collaborative principle had prevailed and… the state encouraged the growth of independent collaborative organisations." "The pivot of social control, then, lay not in state organisation but rather in these collective bodies within the state."
  • Assessment: a collectivist (not individualist) approach — touching the sociologists, but ranked among the historians for the historical line; his "mass psychology" doctrines "though largely fanciful, anticipated modern inquiries." He never reconciled "the independence of autonomous bodies with the supreme power of the state": he devised a pyramidal structure (hierarchy of corporate bodies culminating in the state) and tried to limit absolute state power "by arguing that the state was the expression of reason and the sense of right" — "easily brushed aside by those who wished to use Gierke's doctrines as a justification for absolute totalitarian power."

03.11 The Anthropological approach — Maine (1822–1888)

"Anthropological investigations into the nature of primitive and undeveloped systems of law are of modern origin and might be regarded as a product of the Historical School. Pride of place will here be accorded to Sir Henry Maine… the first and still remains the greatest representative of the historical movement in England."

  • Method: built on the German School's Roman-law material, "but also to bring to bear a more balanced view of history than is found in Savigny"; learned in English, Roman, Hindu and Celtic law. "Instead of stressing the uniqueness of national institutions, he brought to bear a scientific urge to unify, classify and generalise the evolution of different legal orders. Thus he inaugurated both the comparative and anthropological approaches."
  • Static and progressive societies; the four stages (roughly the same in early development of both): (1) law-making by personal command believed divinely inspired — the Themistes of ancient Greece, the dooms of the Anglo-Saxon kings; (2) commands crystallise into customs; (3) the ruler superseded by a minority controlling the law — the pontiffs in ancient Rome; (4) the revolt of the majority against "this oligarchic monopoly, and the publication of the law in the form of a code — e.g. the XII Tables in Rome." Static societies do not progress beyond this point.
  • Progressive societies develop the law by three methods — fiction, equity and legislation (ample examples in Roman and early English law).
  • Maine's generalisations: "no human institution was permanent, and… change was not necessarily for the better." Unlike Savigny, "he favoured legislation and codification" — the advance of civilisation demands an increasing use of legislation; the confused state of English law "was due to its preeminently judge-made character"; codification is "an advanced form of legislative development… the stage at which all the preceding phases… are woven into a coherent whole." He "did not share Savigny's mystique of the Volksgeist."
  • Status to Contract: in early societies (static and progressive) the individual's legal condition is determined by status; the march of progressive societies "witnesses the disintegration of status and the determination of the legal condition of the individual by free negotiation." The famous generalisation: "The movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract."

04.3 The tasks of the legal order — and the five things to be done

"The common law, he said, still bears the impress of individual rights." To achieve the purposes of the legal order there must be — (a) recognition of certain interests, individual, public and social; (b) definition of the limits within which such interests will be legally recognised and given effect to; (c) the securing of those interests within the limits as defined.

When determining the scope and subject-matter of the system, five things must be done: (i) preparation of an inventory of interests, classifying them; (ii) selection of the interests which should be legally recognised; (iii) demarcation of the limits of securing the selected interests; (iv) consideration of the means whereby laws might secure them; (v) evolution of the principles of valuation of the interests.

04.4 Social engineering — the master analogy

"Pound likened the task of the lawyer to engineering — an analogy which he used repeatedly. The aim of social engineering is to build as efficient a structure of society as possible, which requires satisfaction of the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction and waste. It involves the balancing of competing interests."

Interests defined: "claims or wants or desires (or, I would like to say, expressions) which men assert de facto, about which the law must do something if organised societies are to endure. It is the task of the jurist to assist the courts by classifying and expatiating on the interests protected by law."

04.5 The inventory — Pound's taxonomy of interests

A · Individual interests

"Claims or demands or desires involved in and looked at from the standpoint of individual life."

  1. Personality: (a) the physical person, (b) freedom of will, (c) honour and reputation, (d) privacy, (e) belief and opinion.
  2. Domestic relations (distinguish from society's interest in the institution): (a) parents, (b) children, (c) husbands, (d) wives.
  3. Substance: (a) property, (b) freedom of industry and contract, (c) promised advantages, (d) advantageous relations with others, (e) freedom of association, (f) continuity of employment.

B · Public interests

"Claims or demands or desires asserted by individuals involved in or looked at from the standpoint of political life" — "the claims asserted in title of a politically organised society; as one might say for convenience, the claims of the state, the political organization of society."

  1. Interests of the state as a juristic person: (a) the integrity, freedom of action and honour of the state's personality; (b) claims of the politically organised society as a corporation to property acquired and held for corporate purposes. (Dias: "not applicable in this country where the position of the Crown has obviated the need for the personification of the state" — the British/Commonwealth point.)
  2. Interests of the state as guardian of social interests — "This seems to overlap with the next major category."

C · Social interests most important

"Claims or demands or desires, even some of the foregoing in other aspects, thought of in terms of social life and generalised as claims of the social group. This is much the most important category, since most, if not all, the interests in category A would be statable here from a social, rather than an individual, point of view."

  1. General security — security against "forms of action and courses of conduct which threaten its existence": (a) general safety, (b) general health, (c) peace and order, (d) security of acquisitions, (e) security of transactions.
  2. Security of social institutions — (a) domestic, (b) religious, (c) political, (d) economic. Conflict: divorce legislation (institution of marriage v. the spouses' interests); disabilities on children of illegitimate and adulterous unions "with the object of preserving the sanctity of marriage" — "not altogether fortunate, since the extent to which such vicarious suffering has deterred would-be offenders is minimal"; tension between individual religious freedom and a state church's dominance.
  3. General morals — security against acts "offensive to the moral sentiments of the general body of individuals therein for the time being": prostitution, drunkenness, gambling laws.
  4. Conservation of social resources — "that the goods of existence shall not be wasted… the latter may be made to go as far as possible": (a) natural resources, (b) protection and training of dependants and defectives (human resources). Clashes with the individual interest in dealing with one's property as one pleases.
  5. General progress — (a) economic: freedom of use and sale of property, free trade, free industry, encouragement of invention by patents — "(i) and (ii) are less marked today… progress has been achieved by a reversal of them"; restrictive-practice legislation checks private monopolies while demand grows for state or state-controlled monopolies; patents "open the possibility of acquiring patents in order to suppress inventions — on the whole, therefore, item (a) is the least happy." (b) political: free speech, free associations; (c) cultural: free science, free letters, free arts, promotion of education and learning, aesthetics.
  6. Individual life — "that each individual be able to live a human life therein according to the standards of society": (a) self-assertion, (b) opportunity, (c) conditions of life.

Note the reading's own misprint: the "general progress" definition is printed twice in the casebook — the second occurrence (under item 5) is a repetition of the "conservation of social resources" formula; the correct general-progress idea is the one given under 5(a)–(c) in the text. Use the substance, not the duplicated sentence. (grey flag: Dias's editors' slip.)

04.6 The means of securing — and the "same plane" rule

Means: "the device of legal person and the attribution of claims, duties, liberties, powers and immunities. There is also the remedial machinery behind them, which aims sometimes at punishment, sometimes at redress and sometimes at prevention."

Balancing "on the same plane." "Interests, he insisted, should be weighed 'on the same plane,' as it were. One cannot balance an individual interest against a social interest, since that very way of stating them may reflect a decision already made. One should transfer the interests involved on to the same 'plane,' preferably in most cases to that of the social plane, which is the most general. Thus, freedom of the person might be regarded as an individual interest, but it is translatable as an interest of the society that its members should be free." Whether a choice can be given effect "depends on the texture of the legal institutions that are involved. Some are more flexible than others and permit freer play for the balancing process."

04.8 Pound's contribution — as Dias allows

"That his contribution is considerable goes without saying. He more than anyone helped to bring home the vital connection between laws, their administration and the life of society. His work also set the seal on prior demonstrations of the responsible and creative task of lawyers, especially the judges. In so far as his theory laid such heavy emphasis on the existence of varied and competing interests and the need for adjustment between them, it will have enduring value."

04.9 Dias's critique — the seven blows (the reading's own assessment)

  1. The engineering analogy misleads. "What, for instance, is the 'waste and friction' in relation to the conflict of interests?" A bridge is built to a plan of the finished product — "the stresses and strains to be allowed to each part are worked out with a view to producing the best bridge of that kind in that place. With laws there can be no plan, worked out in detail, of any finished product, for society is constantly developing and changing, and the pressures behind interests are changing too. Therefore, the value or importance to be allotted to each interest cannot be predetermined."
  2. Claims do not all pre-exist law. "Pound assumed that de facto claims pre-exist laws, which are required to 'do something' about them. Some claims, however, are consequent on law, e.g. those that have resulted from welfare legislation." And "'do something'… is not enough… 'Recognition' has many gradations… the cult of Scientology is not outlawed, but it has been officially condemned, which makes it difficult to say in what sense the law recognises or does not recognise it."
  3. It is the yardstick, not the interest, that matters. "It is not interests as such, but the yardsticks with reference to which they are measured that matter." An interest may be treated as an ideal in itself: the slave-owner's proprietary right — upheld or not, "depends upon whether sanctity of property or sanctity of the person is adopted as the ideal." "The choice of an ideal, or even a choice between competing ideals, is a matter of decision, not of balancing."
  4. The balancing metaphor presupposes a scale that never exists. "If two interests are to be balanced, that presupposes some 'scale' or 'yardstick'… The 'weight' to be attached to an interest will vary according to the ideal that is used" — with the ideal of individual freedom, self-assertion interests weigh more; with the welfare ideal, the reverse. "The point is that the whole idea of balancing is subordinate to the ideal that is in view. The march of society is gauged by changes in its ideals and standards for measuring interests."
  5. Interests arise in cases, not in lists. "All questions of interests and ideals should be considered in the context of particular issues as and when they come up for decision. An interest is not presented to a judge preclassified… but in relation to one or more other interests in a given situation. Each situation has a pattern of its own." "It is for the judge to translate the activity involved in the case before him in terms of an interest and to select the ideal… therefore, the listing of interests is not as important as the views which particular judges take of given activities and the criteria by which they evaluate them."
  6. How do we know interests exist? When they are litigated. "The answer is: when presented in litigation. Lists of interests can be drawn up, not in advance of, but after the various interests have been contended for in successive cases. The recognition of a new interest is a matter of policy."
  7. The lists are personal opinion and the categories doubtful. "Lists of interests are only the products of personal opinion… his distinction between categories B and C, Public and Social interests, is doubtful. Even the distinction between A and C… is of minor significance" — Pound himself says most cases are better considered by transferring individual interests to the social plane. "None of these remarks is intended to detract from the value of Pound's analysis of the interests themselves. All that is urged is that as a guide to the administration of laws the listing of interests is unhelpful."

The final verdicts. (1) "It is difficult to see how the balancing of interests will produce a cohesive society where there are minorities whose interests are irreconcilable with those of the majority… Whichever interest is favoured, the decision will be resented by those espousing the other, a compromise will most likely be resented by both." (2) Where a substantial proportion of the populace is "parochially minded" with "little or no sense of nationhood," "the prime task in such countries is the creation of interests and the emphasis is, once more, on the need for ideals. Pound's theory cannot be accepted too generally." (3) "The criticism here is not that Pound ignored ideals of guidance, but that he seems to have devoted too little attention to them" — witness his own distinction between 'natural law' (grey paraphrase: "a rationally conceived picture of justice as an ideal relation among men…") and 'positive natural law' ("a system of logically derived universal legal precepts shaped to the experience of the past…"). (4) "It is submitted with respect that it would have been preferable had he enlarged on the criteria of evaluating interests instead of developing particular interests. It is possible that his work has not had the practical impact that it ought to have had because of this somewhat sterile preoccupation with interests and too little attention to the criteria of evaluation."

05.4 Pound v. Kelsen and Austin — the sociological school's two quarrels

  • Against Kelsen's purity: the purity doctrine "undertakes to delimit the cognition of law" from sociology (Unit 6 §04.1); Pound's whole programme is the opposite — "the making, interpretation and application of laws [must] take account of social facts"; (a) factual study of social effects; (b) social investigations as preliminaries to legislation. Where Kelsen studies the ought (validity), Pound studies the is (effectiveness and effect).
  • Against Austin's command: law is not a "one-way projection of authority" — that is Fuller's phrase, but it captures the common positivist shape Pound rejected too. The common law "still bears the impress of individual rights," and the judge is a participant in securing interests, not a minister applying commands.
  • Both schools agree law must be judged by what it does — Pound by its social effects, Kelsen by its validity-conditioned-on-effectiveness. But Pound's test is evaluative (better/worse engineering), Kelsen's is descriptive (effective/ineffective). Dias's criticism of Pound — that his work "has not had the practical impact that it ought to have had" — is the sociological school admitting its own unfinished business: the criteria of evaluation.

05.5 The bridge: social pressure — from Savigny to Pound (Dias's sentence unpacked)

NATURAL LAW: behind law stood moral authority — the law's legitimacy is its conformity with a moral order (Fuller: "the moral attitudes of the community"; Radbruch: the "meaning [of law] is to serve justice")→
HISTORICAL: in place of moral authority, social pressure — the Volksgeist's customary rules are sustained by the community's convictions, not by a legislator's fiat→
SOCIOLOGICAL: social pressure becomes the subject-matter — interests asserted de facto, about which "the law must do something if organised societies are to endure"; and Dias's critique adds the missing piece: what guides the law is the ideal by which interests are measured

05.7 The three one-paragraph verdicts

Fuller

The most rigorous modern form of natural law — and the most honest. He does not claim that unjust law is not law in the substantive sense; he claims that lawless law is not law — a system that fails the exigencies of its own enterprise (generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility, constancy, congruence) cannot guide conduct, and a normative order that cannot guide conduct is not a legal system but a command system. The Nazi illustration does the work; the Rex allegory and the managerial-direction contrast do the argument. The price: Fuller's own concession that internal morality is compatible with external injustice (the one-child policy) shows the theory is a floor, not a ceiling — necessary conditions of legality, not sufficient conditions of justice.

Savigny

The valuable truth, ruined by overemphasis. The truth: law is not a timeless rational construct; it is embedded in a community's life, history, language and habits, and reforms that ignore this are fragile. The overemphasis: positing a single discoverable "spirit of the people" — which Dias dismantles in eleven steps (no single spirit; reception contradicts it; individuals create; scope limited to the "personal" branches; conflict, imitation and oligarchy play the larger part; and “far from the law being a reflection of the Volksgeist… the Volksgeist had been shaped by the law"). His practical legacy — make legislation historically informed, respect custom — survives; his metaphysics does not; and his own practice (purified Roman law for Germany; "logically impossible" negotiable instruments) betrayed his own theory.

Pound

The great mover of jurisprudence into the social world — and the great unfinished project. "He more than anyone helped to bring home the vital connection between laws, their administration and the life of society" — the interest inventory and the engineering analogy turned law into a means and society into its test. But the reading's own critique lands with force: an engineering analogy needs a plan, and society has none; a balance needs a scale, and the scale is an ideal — "the whole idea of balancing is subordinate to the ideal that is in view"; and the judge, not the list, does the valuing. Dias's diagnosis is the school's epitaph: "a somewhat sterile preoccupation with interests and too little attention to the criteria of evaluation." Pound made jurisprudence sociological; the criteria of evaluation remain the unfinished business.

Q1 · "Fuller's internal morality of law. What are its elements, and what is the consequence of their total failure?"

Skeleton. (1) Foundations: law is not a "one-way projection of authority" but a purposive, reciprocal and ongoing enterprise, whose "abstract purpose… is to make it possible for individuals to communicate, to coordinate and to reach understanding with each other" — law "does this by subjecting human beings to the governance of rules." (2) Two moralities. External morality = the substantive content of rules ("law's authority must ultimately rest on the moral attitudes of the people"); internal morality = "not principally about the content of the law but concerns the qualities that enactments must possess to become law at all." (3) The eight virtues (each paired with its failing): generality (ad hoc commands); publicity (secret laws); prospectivity (retroactivity); clarity (unintelligible rules); consistency (contradictions); possibility of compliance (impossible demands); constancy (frequent change); congruence (announced v. administered law). (4) The consequence: "the total failure in any one of these eight directions does not simply result in a bad system of law; it results in something that is not properly called a legal system at all" — "except in the Pickwickian sense in which a void contract can be said to be one kind of contract" — and "there can be no rational ground for asserting that a man can have a moral obligation to obey a legal rule that does not exist, or is kept secret from him, or that came into existence only after he has acted…" (5) Rex and the Nazis: the allegory (punishing obedience; confused, inaudible, retroactive commands) models "how the Nazi regime debased and destroyed the legal system through ad hoc commands, retroactive laws… secret enactments… thuggery." (6) The duty/aspiration dimension: the internal morality occupies the lower half (duty — basic rules making social life possible) and aspires to the upper (excellence); the "invisible pointer"; "a law that fails the morality of duty is not law at all except in the Pickwickian sense." (7) Conclusion: this is a procedural natural law — it does not say unjust law is no law; it says lawless law is no law — and Fuller concedes internal compliance with external injustice (the one-child policy is "general, prospective, promulgated clearly… yet widely condemned as unjust" — "inner morality is logically consistent with external injustice. This does not mean that the inner morality is not true morality" — regime A v. regime B shows the moral difference; "the internal morality of law, under its other name, 'the rule of law,' is widely regarded as a moral imperative."

Q2 · "The Hart–Fuller debate. State the Radbruch doctrine, Hart's criticisms, Fuller's reply — and who has the better argument?"

Skeleton. (1) The context: Nuremberg/Tokyo; the defence of lawful orders; nullum crimen sine lege; the Nazi state's features (retroactive and secret laws; Fuehrer principle; intimidated courts). (2) Radbruch's pre-war position: three equal values (expediency, justice, legal certainty); conscience as resolver; "the law may prove its power but can never demonstrate its validity." (3) The 1946 doctrine (quote): positive law yields to justice where "the conflict… reaches such an intolerable degree"; where "equality, the core of justice, is deliberately betrayed… the statute… lacks completely the very nature of law. For law, including positive law, cannot be otherwise defined than as a system and an institution whose very meaning is to serve justice." A low hurdle: only injustice "beyond rational doubt." (4) Hart's 1958 criticisms: confusion of legal and moral duty (the informers breached morality, not law); and the doctrine "does more harm than good" — "extraordinary naiveté"; the wife-catcher case (the state should have chosen "between two evils" and punished by retrospective statute, preserving "fidelity to law"); the clarity argument: "'laws may be law but too immoral to obey'… is not widely held and 'raises a whole host of philosophical issues.'" (5) Fuller's 1958 reply: retrospective statute or judicial annulment — "the effect is the same"; "the only question… 'who should do the dirty work, the courts or the legislature'"; the Nazi system had "degenerated to the point that it ceased to make law 'except in the Pickwickian sense'"; "a dictatorship which clothes itself with a tinsel of legal form can so far depart from the morality of order, from the inner morality of law itself, that it ceases to be a legal system." (6) Hart's 1965 rejoinder: inner morality "compatible with very great iniquity"; principles of "good legal craftsmanship"; "confusing morality with efficiency"; the poisoner's art. (7) Fuller's counters: without generality/publicity/prospectivity "one cannot judge the morality of the law at all"; each departure is "an affront to people's dignity as responsible agents"; the managerial-direction contrast — generality, prospectivity and congruence have a special place in law because law rests on "a relatively stable reciprocity of expectations," government being "a guardian of the integrity of this framework." (8) Ratnapala's four answers to Hart (narrower doctrine; Oppenheimer — where a retrospective statute is impossible courts refuse recognition on public policy; utilitarianism cuts both ways — officials' deterrence; and the deepest: Hart's own internal aspect — "Law surely is not the gunman situation writ large… This was precisely Radbruch's argument."). (9) Assessment: Hart wins the argument about method (clarity); Radbruch–Fuller win the argument about reality. The debate's legacy is not a victor but a doctrine: Fuller's eight conditions are the modern "rule of law" — and the Rome Statute "implements in substance" the Radbruch formula. A balanced answer ends there.

Q3 · "Is the internal morality of law true morality, or only efficiency? Answer with the poisoner, and Fuller's reply."

Skeleton. (1) The charge (Hart 1965): "compatible with very great iniquity" (1961, 202); the eight requirements "are not principles of morality but principles of good legal craftsmanship"; "confusing morality with efficiency"; the poisoner's art — "to call the principles of the poisoner's art 'the morality of poisoning' would simply blur the distinction between the notion of efficiency for a purpose and those final judgments about activities and purposes with which morality in its various forms is concerned." (2) Counter 1 — epistemic: without the inner qualities "one cannot judge the morality of the law at all because one only sees patternless ad hoc commands"; a power acting by "unpredictable and patternless interventions could be said to be unjust only in the sense that it does not act by a known rule"; generality mitigates discriminatory laws (apartheid, segregation). (3) Counter 2 — agency: "every departure from the principles of inner morality… is an affront to people's dignity as responsible agents… to convey to them your indifference to their powers of self-determination." (4) Counter 3 — the decisive one (managerial direction v. law): five principles apply to management; but "generality of directions is only a matter of convenience in management"; prospectivity "does not arise" for management; the three inapplicable requirements expose the difference: management is "a one-way projection of authority… only requires principles of efficiency," whereas "a relatively stable reciprocity of expectations between the lawgiver and the subject is part of the very idea of a functioning legal order" — law provides "citizens with a sound and stable framework for their interactions," government being "guardian of the integrity of this framework." The positivist critics could not see this "because they regarded law essentially as a one-way projection of authority." (5) The rebuttal to the poisoner: the analogy fails because poisoning has no reciprocity — its "purpose" is the destruction of the other, and its "principles" are constitutive of nothing; the inner morality is constitutive of law itself, and law is the only human practice whose point is coordination by rules. (6) The moral payoff: regime A v. regime B — clear prospective general laws consistently enforced v. ad hoc, retroactive, secret, extra-judicial terror — "is there any moral difference? Clearly there is"; and the two moralities are "symbiotic… one does not live long without the other." Ratnapala's verdict on Hart's reductio: "a grotesque misreading of Fuller's theory." (7) Conclusion: Hart's objection would be right if law were a one-way projection; since it is not, it fails — though Fuller's honest concession (inner compliance with external injustice) means the inner morality is a necessary, not sufficient, condition of good law.

Q4 · "Savigny's historical school: law as Volksgeist. State the thesis and its practical consequences."

Skeleton. (1) Background: the school as "another manifestation of the reaction against natural law theories"; the prelude (Glossators, Accursius, Cujas — the recovery of historical sense); Germany 1800 — Roman law as received common law, "local variations… innumerable"; Thibaut's 1814 code proposal answered by Savigny's On the Vocation ("a new jurisprudence was born"); codification halted till the BGB 1900; the five background factors (anti-natural-law; anti-revolution — Savigny's "deep and lasting hatred"; nationalism; anti-French; Montesquieu and Burke). (2) The thesis: "the nature of any particular system of law… was a reflection of the spirit of the people" — the Volksgeist (Puchta's term): "Law grows with the growth, and strengthens with the strength of the people, and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality." Nation = community linked by historical, geographical, cultural ties; "inner circles" (cities, guilds) explain internal variation; the broad principles of the system "become manifest in customary rules"; law is "a matter of unconscious growth"; "custom not only precedes legislation, but is superior to it, and legislation should always conform to the popular consciousness"; law "varies with peoples and ages"; the Geist is "the standard by which laws, which are the conscious product of the will… are to be judged." (3) The lawyers: as society grows complex, "a special body of persons" — the jurists — give "technical, detailed expression to the Volksgeist"; the method is historical — "thorough grounding in history" is how we master ("not ignorantly submit to") the existing law. (4) Legislation and codification: subordinate; five grounds against immediate codification — defects of contemporary codes; no Geist on some matters; no exhaustive future coverage; the imperfect-codification dilemma; evasion; codification must await "an organic, progressive, scientific study of the laws." (5) The consequences drawn: reform "against the stream of a nation's continuity" is "doomed"; Allen: doctrines tended "to hang traditions like fetters upon the hands of reformative enterprises"; Germany had to legislate drastically in 1900 anyway. (6) Assessment (the reading's own): "a salutary corrective to the methods of the natural lawyers. He did grasp a valuable truth about the nature of law, but ruined it by overemphasis"; the limited acceptable sense — in the few "personal" branches, legislators should "take account of tradition when framing new laws."

Q5 · "Critically examine the idea of the Volksgeist." (or: "Discuss the objections to Savigny's theory.")

Skeleton (the eleven objections, grouped). (i) No single spirit: people "hold different views on different issues. 'The' spirit does not exist" — "the juristic contribution to Romanticism," an "a priori preconception"; (ii) reception contradicts it: Roman law transplanted into "the alien climate of Europe"; modern transplantations (German Code in Japan, Swiss in Turkey, French in Egypt and Holland; Roman-Dutch law flourishing in South Africa and Sri Lanka; English law world-wide) — "supra-national adaptability and resilience"; (iii) genius and the individual: Classical Roman jurists, Littleton, Coke; Ehrlich — "customs are norms of conduct, juristic laws are norms for decision. They are always the creation of jurists"; (iv) limited scope and function: family law more "personal" than commercial/criminal law; the alien systems "affected the indigenous family laws least of all" (India, Turkey); modern function is adaptation, not creation; and "the Volksgeist is discernible only in retrospect, but he sought to make it a test of validity" — confusion of frames of reference; (v) law changes ideas: Bismarck's 1871 Accident Law; (vi) oligarchy: slavery; (vii) imitation: many customs rest on imitation "rather than… innate conviction"; (viii) whose spirit?: local customs; the Law Merchant — "cosmopolitan in origin"; (ix) conflict, not growth: trade-union law from "conscious and violent struggle"; (x) the political objection: the thesis would "thwart the unification of Germany"; (xi) self-contradiction: he worked for a purified Roman law; "far from the law being a reflection of the Volksgeist, it would seem that the Volksgeist had been shaped by the law." Plus the two subsidiary flaws: the lawyers-as-voice device is "a fictitious assumption" (the rule in Shelley's case); and the decadent case — negotiable instruments condemned as "logically impossible" because of devotion to Roman privity. Assessment: the objections show the theory's use as a warning (legislators should not ignore tradition) not as a test (validity cannot be measured by a retrospective spirit).

Q6 · "Pound's sociological jurisprudence: law as social engineering. Explain the programme, the interests, and the balance."

Skeleton. (1) The school and the idea: American sociological jurisprudence, Pound (1870–1964); "the making, interpretation and application of laws [must] take account of social facts"; the eight-point programme (social effects; social investigations before legislation; effectiveness; judicial method; sociological legal history; just and reasonable individual solutions; a ministry of justice; achievement of the purposes of the laws). (2) The tasks: recognition of interests (individual, public, social); definition of limits; securing within limits; five operations (inventory; selection; demarcation; means; principles of valuation). (3) The master analogy: social engineering — "to build as efficient a structure of society as possible… the satisfaction of the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction and waste"; interests defined as "claims or wants or desires… which men assert de facto, about which the law must do something if organised societies are to endure." (4) The inventory: individual (personality — person, will, honour, privacy, belief; domestic relations; substance — property, industry and contract, promised advantages, advantageous relations, association, continuity of employment); public (state as juristic person; as guardian of social interests); social — the most important (general security; security of institutions; general morals; conservation of resources; general progress — economic, political, cultural; individual life). (5) The means: legal person; claims, duties, liberties, powers, immunities; remedies (punishment, redress, prevention); and the five institutions — rules, principles, conceptions, doctrines, standards. (6) The balance: interests weighed "on the same plane" — "one cannot balance an individual interest against a social interest, since that very way of stating them may reflect a decision already made"; transfer to the social plane (freedom of the person = society's interest that its members be free); the effect depends on "the texture of the legal institutions." (7) The contribution: "he more than anyone helped to bring home the vital connection between laws, their administration and the life of society"; the judges' creative task; the emphasis on competing interests "will have enduring value." (8) Conclusion: engineering without a plan; lists without a scale — "it would have been preferable had he enlarged on the criteria of evaluating interests…" (Q7 takes up the criticism fully.)

Q7 · "Dias's criticisms of Pound. Evaluate the sociological theory through them."

Skeleton (seven blows + two verdicts). (1) The plan problem: a bridge is built to a plan; "with laws there can be no plan… for society is constantly developing and changing… the value or importance to be allotted to each interest cannot be predetermined" (and what is "waste and friction"?). (2) Claims do not all pre-exist law: some "are consequent on law" (welfare legislation); "recognition has many gradations" — Scientology is not outlawed but officially condemned — so "do something" is empty. (3) The yardstick problem: "it is not interests as such, but the yardsticks with reference to which they are measured that matter"; the slave-owner's right — sanctity of property or of the person? "The choice of an ideal… is a matter of decision, not of balancing." (4) The balance problem: balancing presupposes a scale; the "weight" varies with the ideal — "the whole idea of balancing is subordinate to the ideal that is in view. The march of society is gauged by changes in its ideals and standards for measuring interests." (5) The case problem: "an interest is not presented to a judge preclassified… but in relation to one or more other interests in a given situation"; the judge translates the activity into an interest and selects the ideal — so "the listing of interests is not as important as the views which particular judges take." (6) The epistemology problem: interests are known "when presented in litigation"; lists are drawn up "after," not in advance; "the recognition of a new interest is a matter of policy." (7) The category problem: lists are "products of personal opinion"; B/C distinction "doubtful"; A/C "of minor significance" (Pound himself transfers individual to social); "as a guide to the administration of laws the listing of interests is unhelpful." (8) The two verdicts: balancing cannot produce cohesion where minority interests are "irreconcilable" — "whichever interest is favoured, the decision will be resented… a compromise will most likely be resented by both"; and where publics are parochial, "the prime task… is the creation of interests… the emphasis is… on the need for ideals. Pound's theory cannot be accepted too generally." (9) The epitaph: not that Pound ignored ideals — his 'natural law'/'positive natural law' distinction proves awareness — but "he seems to have devoted too little attention to them… his work has not had the practical impact that it ought to have had because of this somewhat sterile preoccupation with interests and too little attention to the criteria of evaluation." (10) Assessment: the criticisms are a blueprint, not a demolition — they say what the sociological school must supply (criteria of evaluation, ideals, case-focused method); Pound supplied the map, the criteria are the unfinished business. (grey: modern successors — Rawlsian balancing, proportionality analysis in constitutional law — are attempts to supply exactly what Dias missed.)

Q8 · "Compare the Natural Law (Fuller), Historical (Savigny) and Sociological (Pound) schools as reactions to positivism."

Skeleton. (1) The positivist target (Unit 6): Austin's command-sovereign; Hart's criteria-validity ("a law, however immoral, will be law if it is recognized as law according to the established rules of recognition"); Kelsen's purity. (2) Fuller's reaction — the moral: law cannot be a "one-way projection of authority"; its purpose (coordination by rules) entails the eight virtues; total failure = not law ("Pickwickian"); the debate with Hart (Radbruch; the dirty work; the poisoner; managerial direction). (3) Savigny's reaction — the historical: law is not willed but grown; the Volksgeist; custom superior to legislation; codification premature (Thibaut); correctives (Maine's generalisations) and the eleven objections. (4) Pound's reaction — the social: law is engineering; interests (A/B/C) recognised, delimited, secured; social facts in the making, interpretation and application; the criticism (ideals, not lists). (5) The movement (the casebook's own sentence): natural law posited "moral authority" behind law; the Historical School "substituted social pressure, which provided the bridge… to the Sociological School." (6) Divergences: natural law judges law by its internal morality; historical by its conformity with the national spirit; sociological by its satisfaction of wants — a spectrum from the moral, to the cultural, to the instrumental. (7) Agreements against positivism: all three deny that law is exhausted by its form — law's existence is not separable from purpose (Fuller), history (Savigny), or effects (Pound); and all three relocate validity in a social fact: moral attitudes, popular consciousness, de facto claims. (8) Their weakness in turn: Fuller's floor is no ceiling (one-child policy); Savigny's spirit is "a priori" and retrospective; Pound's balance needs a scale. (9) Conclusion: read together, the three schools are the modern curriculum of "law beyond rules" — and India's constitutional practice (basic structure; Directive Principles; PIL) runs on all three at once. (grey: the constitutional illustrations are outside the readings.)

Short notes (each = one paragraph, with the source)

1. The Radbruch doctrine

1946 revision: positive law takes precedence "unless the conflict between statute and justice reaches such an intolerable degree that the statute, as 'flawed law,' must yield to justice"; where "equality, the core of justice, is deliberately betrayed… the statute… lacks completely the very nature of law… its very meaning is to serve justice." A low hurdle: only injustice "beyond rational doubt" — later influential in German and Nuremberg trials and (substance) in the Rome Statute. Pre-war Radbruch: three equal values; conscience; "the law may prove its power but can never demonstrate its validity."

2. The grudge informer cases

Persons who betrayed critics of the Nazi regime knowing they faced execution; defence: no law violated. Post-war German courts convicted them in disregard of the Nazi law; Radbruch argued the conviction was right, revising his pre-war conscience-based theory into the 1946 doctrine.

3. The wife-catcher case

Hart's illustration: a woman reported her husband for criticising the Reich; sentenced to death but sent to the front and survived. The post-war court's conviction of her, Hart argued, sacrificed "fidelity to law"; proper positivist course — punish by retrospective statute, "a choice… between two evils," with the "value of clarity": "laws may be law but too immoral to obey."

4. "Who should do the dirty work"

Fuller 1958: whether inhumane laws are set aside by court or legislature "the effect is the same — what was once law is declared not to have been law"; the only question is who does it. Defended the post-war decisions: the Nazi system made law only "in the Pickwickian sense"; unshocking that a "tinsel of legal form" regime "ceases to be a legal system."

5. The Pickwickian sense

"A void contract can be said to be one kind of contract" — law in the same attenuated sense. Fuller's via media: a system wholly failing the inner morality "is not properly called a legal system at all except in the Pickwickian sense"; and a law failing the morality of duty "is not law at all" so.

6. The eight ways to fail / the eight virtues

Ad hoc non-generality; non-publication; retroactivity; obscurity; contradiction; impossibility; instability; incongruence — against generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, consistency, possibility of compliance, constancy, faithful administration. Total failure in any one = no legal system; obedience to such rules is "as futile… as casting a vote that will never be counted."

7. Morality of duty and aspiration

The vertical scale: lower half duty (forbearances: do not murder, do not steal), upper half aspiration (striving for excellence); the "invisible pointer"; condemnation without praise for duty (theft), praise without condemnation for aspiration (the cat from the torrents). The legal system is measured on the same scale — above or below the pointer (Australia v. Nazi Germany).

8. Managerial direction v. law

Five principles fit both; generality, prospectivity and congruence do not — for management generality is "convenience" and prospectivity "does not arise." The inapplicabilities reveal law's essence: reciprocity of expectations, the citizen's framework, government as "guardian of the integrity of this framework" — against the "one-way projection" picture; hence the inner morality is not efficiency.

9. The Volksgeist

Puchta's name for the people's common consciousness; "the nature of any particular system of law… a reflection of the spirit of the people"; "law grows with the growth… and finally dies away as the nation loses its nationality"; manifest in customary rules; law is unconscious growth; custom precedes and outranks legislation; law varies with peoples and ages; the Geist is the standard for the "conscious product of the will."

10. The 1814 debate — Thibaut v. Savigny

Thibaut of Heidelberg proposed a code on Code Napoleon lines; Savigny's On the Vocation of our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence answered — "a new jurisprudence was born" (Ihering); German codification halted till the BGB 1900. Background: anti-natural-law, anti-revolution, nationalism, anti-French, Montesquieu/Burke.

11. The five grounds against immediate codification

Contemporary codes preserve adventitious Roman rules while rejecting its principles; codes may introduce unadaptable provisions; no exhaustive future coverage; the imperfect-code dilemma (when lawyers could make a perfect code, none would be needed); codes advertise loopholes and encourage evasion. Answers: amendment is possible; subsequent experience disposed of the second; codification should await "an organic, progressive, scientific study of the laws" — and in fact Germany legislated in 1900.

12. Gierke and the associations

Associations' recognition as persons does not depend on the state; autonomous groups organise social control; the Genossenschaft (fraternal collaboration) v. Herrschaft (domination) struggle as the key of legal-social history (feudalism → guilds and cities → the state → the collaborative principle prevailing). Collectivist; pyramidal hierarchy culminating in the state; the state as "expression of reason and the sense of right" — brushed aside by totalitarian uses.

13. Maine and the four stages

Themistes/dooms (divinely inspired commands) → crystallisation into customs → minority monopoly (pontiffs) → revolt and publication in codes (XII Tables). Static societies stop there; progressive societies proceed by fiction, equity, legislation. Maine favoured codification; and the generalisation: "The movement of progressive societies has hitherto been a movement from Status to Contract." He inaugurated the comparative and anthropological approaches.

14. Social engineering

"The aim of social engineering is to build as efficient a structure of society as possible, which requires satisfaction of the maximum of wants with the minimum of friction and waste"; balancing competing interests; the lawyer as engineer. Dias's objections: no plan exists for a changing society; "what is the 'waste and friction' in relation to the conflict of interests?" — and "the value or importance to be allotted to each interest cannot be predetermined."

15. Pound's three tasks / five operations

Recognition of interests (individual, public, social); definition of limits; securing within limits. When fixing scope and subject-matter: inventory and classification; selection; demarcation of limits; means; principles of valuation. The common law "still bears the impress of individual rights" — the tasks correct this.

16. The "same plane" rule

One cannot balance an individual interest against a social interest — "that very way of stating them may reflect a decision already made"; transfer to the same plane (freedom of the person = society's interest that members be free); the extent of effect depends on "the texture of the legal institutions" — some "permit freer play for the balancing process."

17. Rules, principles, conceptions, doctrines, standards

Pound's institutions of the law: rules attach definite consequences to definite factual situations; principles are authoritative starting-points where rules fail; conceptions are categories of transactions (trust, bailment); doctrines are unions of rules/principles/conceptions in logical schemes; standards prescribe limits of permissible conduct per the circumstances (due care, reasonable notice). Standards are the sociological element par excellence.

18. "The listing of interests is unhelpful"

Dias's central criticism: ideals, not lists, decide — "it is the ideal with reference to which any interest is considered that matters"; lists are "products of personal opinion"; categories doubtful; interests are known only when litigated; the judge's views and criteria matter more than the inventory. Epitaph: "a somewhat sterile preoccupation with interests and too little attention to the criteria of evaluation."

09.6 Last word

Unit 7 is the casebook's answer to Unit 6, and the answer is threefold. If positivism said the law is what is posited, Fuller answers that positing has internal requirements — a system that cannot guide conduct makes no law, so the morality of law is not an add-on but the condition of law's possibility; and the Hart–Fuller debate, from Nuremberg to the one-child policy, is the cleanest demonstration that the "is" and the "ought" of law are, at the very least, mutually dependent. If positivism said the law is what is willed, Savigny answers that it is what is grown — the people's spirit, its customs, its history; and though Dias dismantles the Volksgeist in eleven steps, the residue survives as the permanent warning that reform without history is fragile. And if positivism said the law is what is valid, Pound answers that it is what works — an engineering of interests; and Dias's own critique supplies the missing half of the school, that what finally tells is not the list of interests but the ideal by which they are measured. Read whole, the unit is a single movement — "moral authority" (natural law) → "social pressure" (historical) → "interests" (sociological) — and the casebook hands you the sentence that joins them. Keep that sentence, the signature quotations, and the attacks that come with each doctrine, and every Unit-7 question — including the ones Unit 8 may throw at you — will find you ready.
