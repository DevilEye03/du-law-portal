01Conceptual Frame — Four Sources, Two Civilisations

Unit 5 answers one question: where does law come from? The casebook's three readings give two competing answers — the Indian ancient answer (Rama Jois) and the modern jurisprudential answer (Jain and Dias) — and the unit's genius is that the two are organised around the very same four slots: Dharma, Custom, Precedent, Legislation.

01.1 The three theses to reproduce in every answer

  1. The Indian thesis (Rama Jois). Law is found, not made: dharma pre-exists the king, and the crown of the state is not a law-maker. Hence the four ancient sources are all non-legislative, and the "legislative" function resides in the people's usages and the parishad. This is "the most important distinction between the concept of kingship in India and the concept of kingship in the western countries."
  2. The custom thesis (Jain). Custom is the great material source: it arose before law; the analytical theory (Austin) fails in India because the panchayats enforced custom before courts existed; the texts themselves made it a source (Manu II.6; sadāchāra); the British period made it a rule of decision second only to statute; and codification has since reduced it — "customs have had their heyday."
  3. The precedent thesis (Dias). Precedent has "law-quality per se": it binds according to hierarchy, but its content — the ratio — is not a fixed thing; it is a "pointer towards the direction which subsequent decision should take within a broad spectrum of variations," decided as much by later interpretation as by the deciding judge. The judge may be a declarer or a maker; both descriptions are half-truths, and Dias prefers to show the discretion at every stage.

01.2 Sources — the vocabulary

Formal v. material sources

A formal source is the authority from which a rule derives its legal force — where the law gets its validity (the legislature for a statute; the courts for precedent; the community for custom; the smṛti/śruti for dharma). A material source is the content-supplying origin — the social practice, the book, the judge's reasoning. Jain's paper is, in this vocabulary, an essay on custom as a material source formalised by courts; Dias's chapter on precedent as a formal source whose material content keeps shifting.

02(a) Dharma — "Dharma Moolam": Dharma as the Root of Law

02.1 The opening proposition

"The ancient Dharmashastra declared that Dharma, which included the 'law', was binding on the king. According to Rajadharma, the king was given the power only to enforce the law. Dharmashastra did not confer on or recognise any legislative power in the king. This is the most important distinction between the concept of kingship in India and the concept of kingship in the western countries. According to western concept, the King was the fountain of all the three important limbs of the State viz., the Legislature, the Executive and the Judiciary. But under the kingship as recognised and established under the Dharmashastra the laws were those laid down by the Dharmashastra themselves. They did not authorise the king to lay down new laws or amend the provisions of Dharmashastra. On the other hand, Dharmashastra also laid down the laws governing the conduct of the king himself (Rajadharma)."— Justice M. Rama Jois

Cross-feed (Units 1–3): this is the institutional form of what the earlier readings argued doctrinally — the king under dharma (Unit 2's rājadharma), the "government of laws and not of men" (Parasain & Dhyani, Unit 1), the definition of dharma as that which sustains (Kane, Madhvāchārya) and the Brihadāraṇyaka's "law is the king of kings." The new point in Unit 5 is the source-map: what the sources were and in what order they ranked.

02.2 The four recognised sources (as the texts enumerate them)

Manu II.6: "Veda is the first source of Dharma. The expression by the Seers (Smritikaras), handed down from generation to generation by memory, the virtuous conduct of those who are well versed in the Vedas, and lastly, what is agreeable to the good conscience, are the other sources."
Yājñavalkya I-7: "The Vedas, the Smritis, good conduct or approved usage, what is agreeable to conscience proceeding from good intention, are the sources of law."
Gautama (p. 234, 19–20): "Administration of justice shall be regulated by the Veda, the institutes of the sacred law, the Vedangas and the Purana. The customs of countries, castes and families which are not opposed to the sacred laws have also authority. Cultivators, traders, herdsmen, moneylenders and artisans have authority to lay down rules for their respective classes." (Brihaspati p. 278–28 to the same effect.)

02.3 Sadāchāra — the custom that qualifies as law

Manu's rule read with Yājñavalkya, Gautama and Brihaspati shows "the Smriti writers were fully conscious of the changing needs of the society. Therefore, they specifically made Sadachara i.e. good custom or practice agreeable to the conscience of the people, who are well intentioned and who have regard for the welfare of society, as a source of law." The definition is Harita's:

"The word sat means good, and the good are those who are pure and free from stigma, and the practice of such people is called Sadachara."— Harita (Q.P.M. I, Pt. I, p. 144)
  • "The above definition impressed that it was not any and every custom that could acquire the force of law. The custom or usage, in order to secure social recognition, must be that acceptable to honest and reasonable persons, whose character and conduct were beyond reproach."
  • It is the residuary provision: it "enabled the adaptation of an existing law, to suit the needs of time or to provide for matters not covered by the Shruti and Smritis" — "fully utilised by the great galaxy of jurist commentators, to mould the law to suit the requirements of the changed circumstances."
  • Jain's gloss (Reading 2, §II): the import of sadachar shifted over time — earliest: the conduct of śiṣṭas (Veda-learned, highly moral, selfless brahmins) — a standard that let a jurist reject what he thought immoral or anti-social as "not consonant to good conduct"; later relaxed until "every usage, having no visible secular purpose, came to be looked upon as binding and, lastly, the usages even of the sudras came to be enforced by the king."

02.4 The king and legislation — Katyayana 45

"Tasmācchāstrānusāreṇa rājā kāryāṇi sādhayet / vākyābhāve tu sarveṣāṃ deśadṛṣṭena nirṇayet / asvānta loka-nāśāya parānīka-bhayāvaha / ātru-bīja-harī rājyaṃ satataṃ vākye svayaṃ kṛtiḥ."
"A king should decide causes according to rules of Shastras. In the absence of a provision in the texts he should follow the usages. The king should never act according to his own fiat. Such an action on the part of the king causes danger to him and brings ruin to the people."— Katyayana 45 (Kane's edn., 1933)
  • "Apart from not recognising the power to make law in the king, Dharmashastra positively ordained that the king shall not legislate."
  • The modern echo: the ruler is under the law; the state's organs are bound by the constitution; "government of laws, not of men." (Grey gloss connecting Unit 2's rājadharma.)

02.6 Registration of conventions under the Royal Seal — Katyayana 48–50

"Deśasyānurūpeṇaiva vyavasthā yā nirūpitā / likhitā tu sadā dharmā mudritā rājamudrayā / tasmāt tatsaṃvideta nānyathaiva pravartayet / prajādeśa-dṛṣṭāṃ tu devatāti nirṇayet."
"Whatever conventions are settled in accordance with the consent of the people (of a country) should always be preserved in writing sealed with the Royal Seal. Such conventions should be enforced just like the sacred law and the king should decide after considering them. Whatever trade conventions are reduced to writing by traders as fit to be carried out, these must be given effect to and the king should not lay down anything contrary to them."— Katyayana 48–50
  • Śruti and Smṛti were "specific and therefore… always known and ascertainable"; conventions and trade usages were not — hence registration, "in order to make them known to the public."
  • The performance: the duty of the king was only to register and enforce the law accepted by the people, against individuals who contravened them. The royal seal did not create the rule; it publicised it. (See also the Charter of Vishnu Sena, 725 A.D., Appendix-I of the book — "in conformity with this provision.")
  • Modern echo: the same idea as promulgation/notification of law, but inverted — the state publishes what the community has evolved, not what the ruler has willed.

02.7 Where the texts run out — the king's conscience-decision

"Yatra caite hetavo na vidyante tatra pāṇim-īnvacanān-nirṇayaḥ." — "In cases where no principle of law is found in the Shruti, Smritis or custom, the king should decide according to his conscience."— Pitamaha (Sm. Ch. II p. 38); Brihaspati p. 387–2 similar
  • "The above provision also did not authorise the king to legislate. The authority recognised that in the absence of law on any point the king could give a decision according to his best of judgment having due regard to all the facts and circumstances of the case."
  • Bridge (the reading's own): "This is similar to the principle in the modern law which authorises the courts to decide according to justice, equity and good conscience in matters not covered by any specific provision of law." (This is the exact phrase the British courts later inherited — see §03.5.)

02.8 The Parishad — the one body with power to make new law

  • "The power to decide complicated questions of law by resolving the conflicts or to lay down a new legal provision was recognised in an assembly of persons with prescribed qualifications, called the Parishad, vide Manu VIII 110–114." (Cf. Unit 3 §06: the parishad's composition — at least ten members including a learned brahmin, a logician, a Mīmāṃsaka and a Nirukta-knower, representing the three orders.)
  • "Even in such a situation no legislative power was vested in the king."
  • "Kings, however, were empowered to issue Raja Shasana (Royal Edict) subject to the law declared in the Shruti and Smritis." — executive decree, not legislation; and "subject to" is the point.

03(b) Custom as a Source of Law in India

03.1 Why this paper says what it says — and why it matters

Jain's complaint is methodological: "In any scheme of teaching Jurisprudence custom has an important place as a source of law. Indian teachers generally introduce this topic to their students through English and sometimes Continental materials. Even the Indian authors on Jurisprudence refer mainly to foreign materials and not to Indian materials. There is, however, a rich material in Indian social and legal history which can more appropriately be made use of in explaining the place of custom in society." The paper is that attempt — and it is why the casebook places it between Rama Jois (the ancient source-map) and Dias (the modern doctrine of precedent).

03.2 Western jurists — the four positions

AUSTIN (Analytical): law = command of a political superior, enforced by sanction. Custom becomes law only when it receives judicial or legislative recognition. Taken strictly, customary law is "not law at all," or at best "imperfect" or "inchoate" law — which excludes customs that exist with full force but have never come before the courts, while admitting customs that have by accident→
HOLLAND (mitigating Austin): courts do not proprio motu make custom law; they "merely decide as a fact that there exists a legal custom" — the observance of a custom "is not the cause of law, but is evidence of its existence." Courts give operation to customs not prospectively but retrospectively, implying custom was law before judicial authentication→
ALLEN: custom is "self-contained, self-sufficient, and self-justified law"; the court's function is "declaratory rather than constitutive." Difficulty: the court's veto — a court may declare a custom invalid for unreasonableness→
SAVIGNY (Historical): the real basis of positive law is the general consciousness of the people (Volksgeist); the source is the "instinctive sense of right possessed by every race"; custom is merely evidence of law whose real source lies deeper; custom therefore possesses the force of law before it is received by the courts. For establishment: acts must be plural, uniform, constant, and done with the consciousness that they spring from a legal necessity. PUCHTA: "Custom is, for the people that has established it, a mirror in which that people may recognise itself"; custom is self-sufficient and independent of legislative authority — a condition precedent of all sound legislation

Criticisms of the Western accounts

  • Of Austin: the analytical theory "does not very much fit the Indian condition" — in India, long before the British, customs "were observed by the people and were enforced not by the courts but by the village or community panchayats; the Government did not interfere." When the British courts came, "these very customs came to be pleaded before the courts which enforced them… it was there already in full force; the difference was that instead of the panchayats it came to be enforced by the courts."
  • Of Savigny: not all customs rest on the community's instinctive sense of right — many rest on the interests of a strong minority (slavery); and the growth of much customary law is "not the result of conscious thought, but of tentative practice."

The background facts (quoted sources)

Custom arose first, law came later: "custom is coeval with the very birth of the community itself" (Paton); even a primitive tribe may have a legal order before it has a state (55 Harv. L.R. 66–7). Law was built upon custom — English common law absorbed the customs of the mercantile community to give the world modern mercantile law (Paton; Keeton); the Twelve Tables of Rome "were based upon the customs of the people" (Maine, Ancient Law).

03.3 The Hindu view of custom

Subordination — the orthodox line

Gautama: dharmas (customs) "of countries, castes and families, which are not opposed to Vedic scriptures, are authoritative and binding." Manu and Yājñavalkya: sources are śruti, smṛti, sadāchāra in that order. Apararka: a custom repugnant to any "clear" text of the Vedas is rejected. The Mītākṣarā, Dāyabhāga and Mayūkha similarly place custom below śruti and smṛti.

The dissenting voices

Viśvarūpa and Medhātithi: prescriptions of smṛtis (and even of śruti) need not be observed when vehemently condemned by the people — e.g. niyoga, though textually sanctioned, is "abhorred by custom." Vrihaspati, Narada and Asahaya favoured "unqualified acceptance of custom even when they were in conflict with the written laws." Some smritis underlined the point: suppression of customs "would give rise to resentment and rebellion." One text of the Manusmriti itself: "Achara is transcendental law, and so are the practices declared in the Veda and smriti; therefore, a twice-born person desirous of his own welfare should always make efforts to follow it" (Kane, Hindu Customs and Modern Law 33). Kautilya (Arthashastra): usages and customs are of equal authority as evidence of law; and on conflict, the former (usage) "must be taken to be of greater force as being actually observed in practice."

Custom as the material source of ancient Hindu law — the process

  1. The dharmasūtras tried to bring the text of the śruti into conformity with the customs prevailing in contemporary society.
  2. The smritis "drew heavily on the customs of the people for whom they were designed" — which "largely explains why so many various Dharmashastra came into being, and why they differed from each other."
  3. When the smritis failed the growing needs of society, the commentaries took up old texts and interpreted them "in such a manner that it came in harmony with social mores and customs of the people." The Privy Council: "The Digest subordinates in more than one place the language of texts to custom and approved usage" (Bhyah Ram Singh (1870) 13 MIA 373, 390).
  4. Hence, from the same base texts, two major schools of Hindu law developed, one of them with four sub-schools. "The commentaries indicate an attempt to reconcile the text law with the actual usages of the people" (Muthukaruppa v. Sellathammal (1916) ILR 39 Mad. 298, 301); "different schools of law chiefly arose from the difference of local custom" (Rutcheputty v. Rajunder (1839) 2 MIA 132); "the commentators do not enact, they explain and are evidence of the congeries of customs which form the law" (Balwant Rao v. Baji Rao (1921) ILR 48 Cal. 30, 41); commentaries "owe their binding force not to their promulgation by any sovereign authority, but to the respect due to their authors and still more to the fact of their being in accordance with prevailing popular sentiment and practice" (Jogdamba v. S.S. (1889) ILR 16 Cal. 307, 375).
  5. The process was not one-sided: while custom was recognised, custom was also "modified and supplemented by the opinions of the Hindu Jurists."

Sadachar — and the units of custom

"In Sanskrit, the word for custom is sadachar" — its import shifted from age to age (śiṣṭas' conduct → every usage of no visible secular purpose → even śūdra usages enforced by the king — see §02.3). The customs of which account is taken (Gautama, Manu, Brihaspati) are those of districts (desa or janpada), towns and villages, castes, families, guilds, corporations or groups — gana, sreni, sangh, naigam, varga. The natural effect: "the eight forms of marriage were recognised by Dharmashastra writers even though some of the forms were highly objectionable."

03.4 The Muslim view of custom

PRINCIPAL SOURCES: Koran (word of God) → hadis (traditions; the Prophet's utterances and precedents from his acts)→
NEXT: ijma (consensus of opinion amongst the learned) → urf or custom → qiyas (analogical deduction from the first three)
  • The Prophet himself recognised customary law: he gave express sanction to certain pre-Islamic Arab usages or "suffered such usages to continue without expressing disapprobation"; his companions recognised many customs not inconsistent with the faith. "The hadis contained, to a large extent, the customary law of pre-Islamic Arabia. In Koran, there is not much of law. Hence the rule that it must be read in conjunction with the customs then in vogue."
  • Once the principles settled, custom was "relegated to an inferior position": recognised on the principle "treat whatever the people generally consider to be good for themselves is good in the eye of God," but ranked inferior to the Koran, the hadis and ijma, yet superior to qiyas — so "a custom opposed to the principles derived from the former sources is illegal."

The four conditions of Muslim-law custom (Abdur Rahim; Tyabji)

  1. It must be generally prevalent in the country;
  2. It must not be merely a local usage in a village or town (though it need not be ancient or immemorial);
  3. It must be an established course of conduct, not a practice on a few occasions;
  4. Being territorial, it cannot affect the law of other lands; being confined to a particular period, it cannot affect the custom of another age.

03.5 The British period — custom as "rule of decision"

1772 — Warren Hastings' adalats (Bengal, Bihar, Orissa): all cases of inheritance, marriage, caste and other religious usages "according to the laws of the Koran" (Muslims) and "the laws of the Shaster" (Hindus)→
1781 — Act of Settlement (amending the Regulating Act, 1773): the Calcutta Supreme Court to decide inheritance/succession and contract matters "by the laws and usages of Muhammadans" / "by the laws and usages of the Gentoos"; repeated for the other Presidency towns. The same year the adalats were told: where no specific directions, act "according to justice, equity and good conscience"→
s. 37 of the Bengal, Agra & Assam Civil Courts Act, 1887 — repeats the 1772 model with "Muhammadan law" and "Hindu law"; custom not mentioned. But Bombay Regulation IV of 1827, s. 26 (Elphinstone): "the law to be observed… shall be Acts of Parliament and regulations… in the absence… the usage of the country in which the suit arose; if none such appears, the law of the defendant, and, in the absence of specific law and usage, justice, equity and good conscience alone" — usage first→
s. 16, Madras Civil Courts Act, 1873: succession, inheritance, marriage, caste or religious institution — "the Muhammadan law… and the Hindu law…, any custom (if such there be) having the force of law and governing the parties or property concerned, shall form the rule of decision"

The Hindu-law answer: custom beats the text

"Under the Hindoo system of law, clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law" — if a custom is proved on a point of Hindu law, courts must follow it even against an express text of the Dharmashastra or the commentaries (Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga (1868) 12 MIA 397, 436; Bhyah Ram Singh (1870) 13 MIA 373, 390). Neelkisto Deb v. Beerchunder (1869) 12 MIA 523: where customs are proved, "it would oust the general law, which, however, will regulate all outside the custom." The courts' basis: Manu's "immemorial usage is transcendental law." This approach — "even though not in conformity with the orthodox approach, was yet in conformity with the genius of the law which always gave a high regard to custom."

The Muslim-law battle — from doubt to custom

Hirbae v. Sonabae (Bombay Supreme Court, 4 Ind. Dec. 100, 112) — Khojas and Cutchi Memons, Muslim by religion but following Hindu inheritance customs: Perry C.J. refused to read "laws and usages" as the Koran alone: the clause "was framed on political grounds solely… the effect… was not to adopt the text of the Koran as law any further than it has been adopted in the laws and usages of the Muhammadans." But for Bengal, the trial rule (s. 37) did not mention custom, and courts held custom could not derogate from Muslim law: Jowala Buksh v. Dharum Singh (1866) 10 MIA 511, 538; Surmust Khan v. Kadir Dad Khan (1866); Jammya v. Diwan (1901) ILR 23 All. 20. The turning point: Mohd. Ismail v. Lala Sheomukh (17 CWN 97, P.C.) — custom got precedence over Muslim law and became enforceable even in derogation of orthodox law; followed in Ali Asghar v. Collector of Bulandshahar (1917) ILR 39 All. 574; Mt. Jaffro v. Chatta (163 IC 650); Roshan Ali Khan v. Chaudhri Asghar Ali (1929) 57 IA 29; and Md. Ibrahim v. Shaik Ibrahim, AIR 1922 PC 59 — "custom plays a large part in modifying the ordinary law, and it is now established that there may be a custom at variance even with the rules of Mahomedan Law."

03.6 Punjab and the "custom law" tracts

  • Punjab is "pre-eminently a land of customary law": neither the Hindu nor the Muslim sacred books made much impact on rural life; pre-British customs were enforced by village or tribal panchayats or jirgas. On annexation (1849) the Governor-General constituted the Board of Administration with the assurance that "native institutions and practices shall be upheld as far as they are consistent with the distribution of justice to all classes"; directions followed to enforce the lex loci — "local customs which had been obeyed by any tribe or sect." Sir George Campbell: "not one out of a hundred persons in the Punjab was governed by the strict provisions of the Hindu and Muhammadan law."
  • s. 5, Punjab Laws Act, 1872 (quoted in the reading): "In questions regarding succession, special property of females, betrothal, marriage, dower, adoption, guardianship, minority, bastardy, family relations, wills, legacies, gifts, partition, or any religious usage or institution, the rule of decision shall be — (a) any custom applicable to the parties concerned which is not contrary to justice, equity and good conscience, and has not been declared to be void by any competent authority, (b) the Mohammedan law, (c) the Hindu law…" — i.e., custom was made the first rule of decision (with the printed side-note in the casebook that (b)/(c) apply "in cases where the parties are Hindus except insofar as such law has been altered or abolished… or is modified by any such custom").
  • Abdul Hussein v. Sona Dero (1917) 45 IA 10: "the section raises no presumption that the parties are to be governed by custom rather than by personal law which must be applied unless the custom is proved. The clause puts the custom in the forefront… but, before a custom may be enforced, it must be established by the ordinary processes of evidence" (also Vaishno Ditti v. Rameshri (1928) 55 IA 407, 421; Salig Ram v. Munshi Ram, AIR 1961 SC 1374).
  • No "general custom" in Punjab: "custom there is mainly tribal, and even with the same tribe, it may vary from locality to locality… There is thus no single body of customary or tribal law common to the whole of Punjab" (Ujagar v. Mst. Jeo, AIR 1959 SC 1041). Custom is mainly agricultural, though "even the urban people are not completely free from it" (Ramkishore v. Jainarayan (1921) 48 IA 405).
  • The same design elsewhere: Oudh Laws Act, 1876, s. 3; N.W.F.P. Regulation VII of 1901, s. 27; Central Provinces Laws Act, 1875; the Malabar Coast (Marumakkathayam — female-line descent, heirs being the children of the sister: Krishnan v. Sridevi (1889) ILR 12 Mad. 512); the Kumaon Hills (the Khasis, customs at variance with the Mītākṣarā); the Moplahs (Muslims following Marumakkathayam).
  • Jain's verdict on the clauses: they "did not have much of any special significance, for even without them, custom would have been preferred over the personal law as was held by the Privy Council in the Moottu Ramalinga case"; their real service was removing the doubt in the area of Muslim law — "but even here the Privy Council's verdict had gone in favour of the custom."

03.7 Kinds of customs enforced

1. Tribal / communal / sectarian

Customs applying to a particular caste, community or group in a territorial area [Parandhamayya v. Navaratna Sikhamani, AIR 1949 Mad. 825; Venkata Subba Rao v. Bhujangayya, AIR 1960 AP 412] — Punjab, Malabar, Kumaon. Examples: the Kamma community's illatom (son-in-law affiliation with a share); the Jains — a widow may adopt without the husband's consent, as a custom of the community as such, irrespective of locality or sect (74 IA 254: AIR 1948 PC 177; Sheo Singh Rai (1878) 5 IA 87; Chotay Lal (1878) 6 IA 15; confirmed over the case-law in Munnalal v. Raj Kumar, AIR 1962 SC 1493); a Jain widow's full power to alienate her deceased husband's self-acquired property (Sheo Singh Rai; Shimbhu Nath v. Gayan Chand (1894) ILR 16 All. 379; Chotay Lall (1879) ILR 4 Cal. 744, 752); the Chettis of Madura — moopu (a portion for the first wife on second marriage, the rest divided between the sons of each wife) (Palanjappa Chettiar v. Alayan Chetti (1921) 48 IA 539); the Nairs of South Malabar — polyandry recognised, female-line descent, adoption of females to preserve the family (Thiruthipalli Raman Menon (1900) 27 IA 231).

2. Family custom

Applies to a particular family only. Early doubts: Tarachand v. Reeb Ram (3 Mad. HCR 50) — the theories all refer to customary law "antagonistic to the general law," to be established by acts of a single family; Basvantrav v. Mantappa (1 Bom. HCR app. XLII) — "it would be a dangerous doctrine that any petty family is at liberty to make a law for itself." But the Privy Council trend differed (Serumah v. Palathan, 15 Cal. W.R. P.C. 4 — a family carrying the Mithila law from Mithila to Bengal; Abraham v. Abraham (1863) 9 MIA 199 — a Christian family, converted from Hindu or Muslim religion, may have its own law), and the Bombay/Madras views became untenable (Shidhojiray v. Naikojiray; Bhau Nanaji Utpat v. Sundrabal — "usage of the country" in Reg. IV of 1827 is "sufficiently general to allow either of a very large or restricted application"). Thereafter family customs were routinely applied: a Jat family migrating Delhi→U.P. (1858) carried its custom (adoption of an orphan); impartibility of estates as a creature of custom (with maintenance rights); the "composite family" (two or more families pooling resources, labour and risk); a Jain family turning Vaishnav but retaining Jain custom (change of religion does not affect the personal customs governing status); a Muslim family's succession custom in Oudh upheld by the Privy Council. Shiba Prasad Singh v. Prayag Kumari (1932) 59 IA 331: "a Hindu family, no doubt, cannot by agreement between its members make a custom for itself of succession… at variance with the ordinary law. But where a family is found to have been governed… by a customary rule of succession different from that of the ordinary law, that custom is itself law." A family custom can be destroyed by disuse.

3. Local custom

Binding on all persons in the local area; fewer in the case-law than family/tribal customs; and "while customs in India in the area of family relations and succession… are either tribal or communal or family, the local customs are more of a secular nature… they create other rights." Examples: pre-emption (essentially a Muslim concept, but recognised among non-Muslims by custom in certain areas; the Muhammadan-law rules apply to non-Muslims except as modified); s. 18, Indian Easements Act (easement by custom — a right of privacy as a customary easement); rights of pasturage, to bury dead on another's land, to worship, to hold festivals, to remove earth from a field, to a village pathway — all judicially conceded. A fluctuating body of persons cannot acquire a customary right in the nature of a profit-à-prendre (Calcutta view; Patna disagrees; Bombay: villagers are not a fluctuating body).

03.8 Requisites of a valid custom

"A custom is a rule which in a particular family or in a particular district, has, from long usage, obtained the force of law. It is not that each and every custom can be legally enforced. A custom to be legally recognisable and enforceable must fulfil several requisites, viz., it must be ancient, certain and reasonable, and, being in derogation of the general rules of law, must be construed strictly."
The statutory formulation (Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; Hindu Succession Act, 1956): "the expression 'custom' and 'usage' signify any rule which, having been continuously and uniformly observed for a long time obtained the force of law among Hindus in any local area, tribe, community, group or family: Provided that the rule is certain and not unreasonable or opposed to public policy."

03.9 Proof of a custom

The evidentiary rules

  1. Allege and prove. "A party alleging that he is governed by a custom must specifically allege the same and prove its existence; there is no presumption… the onus rest[s] on him who alleges it" — strictly applied, even in Punjab and Oudh. The Privy Council's formula: "satisfactory proof of usage so long and invariably acted upon in practice as to show that it has, by common consent, been submitted to as the established governing rule of the particular family, class or district."
  2. Clear, cogent, unambiguous — but "rigorous and technical rules of proof, such as are insisted upon in England, are not required in India."
  3. No extension by analogy. "Theory and custom are antithesis; custom cannot be a matter of mere theory but must always be a matter of fact" (Supreme Court). Andhra: the Kamma estate-strangement custom (return of dowry) refused extension by analogy to the bride's death ("there could be no greater estrangement than 'death'"), even though on evidence the custom was proved.
  4. Instances of enforcement are wanted, though not absolutely necessary at all times; a family custom may be proved by evidence of families of common origin settled in the same region; general evidence of members of the tribe/family who would naturally know.

The Evidence Act machinery

  • ss. 48–49: opinion of persons likely to know of a general custom or right (s. 48); opinion of persons with special means of knowledge on the usages and tenets of any body of men or family (s. 49) — relevant.
  • s. 42: judgments on custom are relevant though not conclusive; where a custom is repeatedly brought to the notice of the courts, "the courts may hold that custom was introduced into the law and that no further proof was necessary" — the effect of s. 56 (judicial notice). "A custom by repeated recognition by courts becomes entitled to judicial notice."
  • s. 35 — the pivot for wajib-ul-arz and riwaz-i-am: village administration papers ordered prepared by Regulation VII of 1822, recording village customs (the officer recording statements of connected persons, including proprietors). Prima facie evidence of custom, not conclusive, rebuttable; weight depends on intrinsic quality — "when properly used, affords most valuable evidence of custom and is much more reliable than oral evidence given after the event" (Balgobind v. Badri Prasad (1923) 50 IA 196); but at times "concocted… in their own interest… worse than useless" (Uman Parshad v. Gandharp Singh (1887) 14 IA 127). The presumption is weak where women are adversely affected (they had no opportunity to appear before revenue officers); only a few instances suffice to rebut it.

Manuals and digests

District manuals of customary law (on the riwaz-i-am model) stand on much the same footing; even without instances, a manual entry is sufficient prima facie evidence. Kumaon: the U.P. Government, recognising that requiring "unsophisticated and uneducated" people to prove their customs was inequitable, appointed Shri Panna Lal, I.C.S. (1919); the result — Hindu Customary Law in Kumaon (1920) — is admissible under s. 35 (local inquiry into actual customs). Punjab: William Rattigan's Digest (1880) of Punjabi customs — "of unquestioned authority in Punjab" (P.C. in Mt. Subhani); the Supreme Court: its "authoritative value… is now beyond controversy." On conflict between riwaz-i-am and Rattigan, the riwaz-i-am ordinarily prevails. Jammu & Kashmir: Sant Ram Dogra's Code of Tribal Custom. Mixed question of law and fact (Palaniappa Chetty v. Deivasikamony Pandara (1917) 44 IA 147) — though other PC decisions treat it as fact only.

03.11 The concluding verdict — custom's heyday is past

What the British inherited — and what it cost

After the advent of the British, "custom came to be given a place of honour… a place second only to the statutory law; custom was given preference over the religious laws of the parties" — a "reasonable and just approach," since in practice the law of the shastra and the shara was not observed in pristine purity; it would have been "harsh with the people to force them to forego their customs." The English tests were adopted formally but applied loosely: no 1189 rule; no insistence on technical proof; family and tribal customs enforced (not "local" as in England); "usage" in England = a trade/business line of conduct, in India = family relations (used interchangeably with custom); a family could renounce customs and adopt others (no English parallel); the English "outside-district" failure rule not applied. This looseness was salutary in the formative stages — with the legislature inactive and no lex loci, rejection of customs would have left "no law to apply," forcing "principles borrowed from a foreign Jurisprudence and unknown to people." Rup Chand v. Jambu Parshad (1909) 37 IA 93 and Chiman Lal v. Hari Chand (1913) 40 IA 156 show even the Privy Council tolerating thin evidence.

Why custom must now yield

Ascertainment led to writing — riwaz-i-am, manuals, digests — which "made the system more definite" but "stereotyped customs; the element of flexibility and growth disappeared; customary law became rigid and lost its capacity of organic growth." The specific vices: (1) custom is past-tense — "customs are useful for situations which have already occurred but cannot create a rule to deal with a future difficulty"; (2) non-uniformity — from family to family, region to region, community to community; (3) a double burden on the judiciary — deciding not only facts but the existence and content of custom; proceedings "dilatory and time-consuming"; (4) expense — witnesses to fact and to custom; (5) vagueness until judicially accepted. "All these considerations point to one inevitable result — abrogation of custom and enactment of legislation instead." In every mature system (England) custom plays a minor role; "in every progressive society, custom ceases to play an important role after a stage of social evolution is reached which appears to have been reached in India." And the final argument of integration: "Most of the customs are tribal and communal or sectarian, and so long as custom survives, these class distinctions are also bound to survive." The one reservation: backward areas — "the Constitution seeks to do that with respect to certain very backward areas like NEFA and Nagaland" whose modes should not be changed suddenly. (Grey: modern equivalents — Art. 371A Nagaland; the Sixth Schedule; customary-law protection for Scheduled Tribes; also the striking modern counter-current — courts and statutes re-valuing tribal customary marriage, though that is beyond the reading.) For the rest: "no justification to keep their disparate customs appears to be imperative any longer."

04.0 The frame: corrective justice and three kinds of knowledge

"The fourth major task of justice, and one which lies especially within the province of lawyers, is giving just decision in disputes. In Aristotelian terms this would fall under 'corrective justice'." Deciding disputes involves three kinds of knowledge:

(2) KNOWING THE LAW APPLICABLE TO THOSE FACTS — "knowing how to find it in judicial precedents, statutes and immemorial customs" — the order in which the book treats the sources→
(3) KNOWING THE JUST WAY OF APPLYING THE LAW TO THEM (the following chapter)

04.1 The doctrine of precedent in Britain — and its two preconditions

  • "The doctrine of precedent in Britain has assumed a have binding force [sic — 'binding force'] and enjoys law-quality per se; Bindingness depends on the hierarchy of courts; higher courts bind lower courts, never vice versa."
  • Bindingness attaches "to the principle behind a decision, its ratio decidendi, as stated by Jessel MR: 'The only thing in a Judge's decision binding as an authority upon a subsequent Judge is the principle upon which the case was decided'" (Osborne v. Rowlett (1880) 13 ChD 774 at 785).
  • "The two aspects are independent. A decision of the High Court, for example, is 'law' although it is not binding on any court superior to itself."
  • Two conditions had to be satisfied before stare decisis could become established: (1) "a settled judicial hierarchy" — until then it could not be known whose decisions bound whom; (2) "reliable reports of cases" — precise records of what decisions lay down. "Only about the middle of the last century were these conditions fulfilled, and it is from about then that the modern doctrine emerges."

EARLIEST WRITERS: Glanville (late 12th c.) cited only one judgment; Fleta and Hengham (late 13th c.) hardly any; Fortescue (late 15th c.) the same. The exception: Bracton — a Notebook of ~2,000 cases as material, ~500 employed — but "there was nothing resembling the modern use of precedent": he stated his view of a rule and added an illustrative case→
YEAR BOOKS — the earliest reports (possibly Bracton's influence): anecdotal; authors recording asides and points of pleading, not principle; decisions often unmentioned. Yet used by students, and decisions treated as authoritative — Prisot C.J., 1454: "if this plea were now adjudged bad… it would assuredly be a bad example to the young apprentices who study the Year Books…" — "An utterance like this should not… be overestimated, and there was as yet no doctrine of stare decisis." Original records were the only official source; access normally given only to Crown lawyers→
PRIVATE REPORTS: Dyer and Plowden (deservedly high repute), then Coke (ending 1616 — the casebook prints "1916"; Coke's Reports end in 1616). None in modern dress — lengthy pleadings and disquisitions. Later 17th century: idea that Exchequer Chamber decisions were binding — perhaps because given by groups of judges (Godden v. Hales (1686), per Herbert C.J.). No such idea for King's Bench, Common Pleas, or even the House of Lords — lay peers participated until 1844 (O'Connell v. R. (1844) 11 Cl & Fin 155); it did not distinguish legislative from judicial functions (parliamentary privilege barred publication); Dow began House of Lords reports only in 1813→
THE MODERN REPORTS: Burrow's Reports (1757–1771) — "the beginning of the modern practice," separating headnote, facts, argument and decision. Blackstone: follow previous cases "unless flatly absurd or unjust" (Bl. Com. I, 70). From ~1785 judges favoured particular reporters per court. 1865: the Law Reports inaugurated by the Incorporated Council of Law Reporting — revised by the judges before publication, yet "in no sense official; law reporting remains, as always, a matter of private enterprise." 1936: All-England Law Reports (rapid weekly service). 1953: the Council's alternative weekly service with an indication of cases to be fully reported. "Utility to the profession is the only test" (Pollock).

04.3 Assessment of reports — five rules

  1. Definition: "A 'law report' means any account of a case vouched for by a barrister who was present at the hearing" (Pollock). Usual anatomy: catch-words → headnote (facts and decision) → arguments of counsel (only some series) → judgment or the relevant portion.
  2. Old reports vary in authority — Coke and Burrow approved; Barnardiston and Epinasse not; note how fully the facts are stated and whether the judgment is verbatim.
  3. Compare all versions if a case is reported more than once — significant discrepancies appear (e.g., Williams v. Carwardine; Edwards v. Jones [1947] KB 659; Barker v. Levinson [1951] 1 KB 342 — a statement of criminal liability of masters appears in one report and not another; Perry v. Stopher [1959] 1 WLR 415).
  4. The Law Reports version should be preferred, "by virtue of having been revised by the judges" [Leather Cloth Co. v. Lorsont (1869) LR 9 Eq 345; National Bank of Greece v. Westminster Bank [1970] 3 All ER 656]. The Court of Appeal has ruled that the revised version is authoritative over the original, "since the former is only provisional and awaits approval by the judge" (Bromley v. Bromley (1965) P 111) — though the judge should not later alter "the whole character of his judgement," and occasionally the LR version is rejected (CHT Ltd. v. Ward [1963] 2 All ER 835); remarks eliminated from the LR/Criminal Appeal Reports have been repudiated (R. v. Cockburn [1968] 1 All ER 466).
  5. Rank order of sources: verbatim judgment with arguments = best; a summarising report only in the absence of a fuller report, and even then of limited weight (Rivoli Hats v. Gooch [1953] 2 All ER 823); in the absence of all else, a barrister's oral account is admissible but of limited value (Chapman v. Chapman [1954] AC 429).

04.4 Applying a precedent — I: knowing the facts

"Applying a precedent to the instant case is a process of matching the fact-pattern of the precedent and the ruling thereon with the fact-pattern of the instant case; if they match, the rule is applied, if not, it is distinguished." A rule must be general and "malleable" — no one can foresee the precise situations that will arise, and "no word has one proper meaning."

The discretionary elements in knowing facts

  1. Credibility and balance of probabilities — believing or disbelieving testimony; "judges are human, so the quirks of individual personalities may also play some part."
  2. How to state the facts — "it is not knowledge of facts, but knowledge of how to state facts that is relevant" (both of the instant and the precedent case). Lord Simon: "What is relevant is not only the statement of material facts by the deciding judge, but also their re-statement by other judges in later cases" (F.A. & A.B. Ltd. v. Lupton [1972] AC 634, 659).
  3. Primary v. inferential facts — "A did so-and-so at such a time in such a place" (primary) v. "A acted negligently" (inference); two courts may state the inferential fact differently while agreeing on the primary facts (Benmax v. Austin Motor Co. [1955] AC 370).
  4. Levels of generality — the Rolls-Royce illustration: "A drove a Rolls Royce at 30 mph through Piccadilly Circus at 2 pm on a Wednesday, ran into B and broke his right leg" can be generalised (person; vehicle; public highway; negligence; physical injury) up to "a person inflicted physical damage" and "the negligent infliction of damage." "The higher the level of generality, the wider the scope of the proposition. A later judge is at liberty to accept as the fact-statement of A v. B a proposition at any level along the scale" — choosing the level the instant case needs (careless false statement → "negligent infliction of physical damage"; pecuniary loss → the widest, "negligent infliction of damage").

Material facts — the judicial surgery

  1. Selecting material facts — the fewer included, the wider the scope. Rylands v. Fletcher [1868] LR 3 HL 330: facts (i) A had a reservoir built; (ii) A employed B to build it; (iii) A was not negligent; (iv) B was negligent; (v) water escaped by B's negligent construction and damaged C's property. Only (i) and (v) were treated as material; ignoring the presence/absence of negligence and generalising (i) into "anything likely to do mischief" produced the sweeping rule: whoever brings a "mischievous substance" on to his land is liable for damage caused by its escape, irrespective of negligence (whether "damage" includes personal injury was left open — Miles v. Forest Rock; Read v. J. Lyons & Co. [1947] AC 156).
  2. Adding/subtracting facts or re-stating them. Wilson & Clyde Coal Co. v. English [1938] AC 57: employers held liable for death caused by a fellow-employee's default — but at that date the doctrine of common employment barred vicarious liability, so the ratio had to ignore that the party at fault was a servant and rest on a personal non-delegable duty to ensure reasonable care — a rule attaching liability even for an independent contractor. In Davie v. New Merton Board Mills [1959] AC 604, Viscount Simonds and Lord Reid stressed the materiality of the servant-fact — a "fundamental reinterpretation"; the Employer's Liability (Defective Equipment) Act, 1969 then overruled Davie and restored the original interpretation in statutory form.
  3. The same happening, opposite results: Wagon Mound [1961] AC 388 — on the lower court's finding that (per the science of the day) ignition of furnace oil was not reasonably foreseeable, no liability for fire damage; Wagon Mound (No. 2) [1967] 1 AC 617 — different plaintiffs, same occurrence, but the lower court's advertence to even a "bare possibility" of fire was treated as the material difference, so the defendants were liable. "Stressing one factor as material not only enabled two different courts to reach opposite results in the same case, but also enabled the same court to reach opposite results in two cases based on the same happening."
  4. Different combinations. Donoghue v. Stevenson [1932] AC 562: the eight facts (manufacturer assumed negligent—snail in bottle; delivery under contract to retailer B; innocent sale to C; C to D; no intermediate examination; D poisoned; a Scottish appeal) yield at least four propositions — the Scots-appeal point (fact viii only); the manufacturer proposition (i, ii, vi, vii — negligent manufacturer liable to the ultimate consumer with no likelihood of intermediate examination); the contract-misfeasance proposition (ii, vi, vii — negligent misperformance of a contract physically damaging a contemplated third party); the neighbour proposition (ii, vii — anyone negligently damaging another whom he ought reasonably to have contemplated). "Only in the light of later cases is it possible to determine the ratio decidendi": courts now prefer the fourth; previously they generally adopted the second. The truth was irrelevant — the House remitted the case for trial on whether there had been a snail, but the trial never took place (a party died): the snail's existence was never established. Limits: the consensual domain of word-meanings — the facts would not support, e.g., that the defendant caused a public nuisance.

04.5 Applying a precedent — II: knowing the law (the ratio decidendi)

"What is 'law' in a precedent is its ruling or ratio decidendi, which concerns future litigants as well as those involved in the instant dispute. … Statements not part of the ratio decidendi are obiter dicta and are not authoritative." Three shades of meaning: (1) the translation — the reason for (or of) deciding (even a finding of fact may in this sense be the ratio: a judge may state a rule and then decide the facts do not fall within it); (2) the rule of law proffered by the judge as the basis of his decision; (3) the rule of law which others regard as being of binding authority.

  • No fixed ruling: "There is a temptation to suppose that a case has one fixed ruling which is there and discoverable here and now and once and for all. This is not so, for the ratio is not only the ruling given by the deciding judge… but any one of a series of rulings as elucidated by subsequent interpretations." The judge's pronouncement "is a necessary step towards ascertaining the ratio, but the process by no means ends there; subsequent interpretation is at least as significant, sometimes more so." Jessel MR again: "It is not sufficient that the case should have been decided on a principle if that principle is not itself a right principle… it is for a subsequent judge to say whether or not it is a right principle, and, if not, he may himself lay down the true principle." Hence: "ratio is best regarded as a pointer towards the direction which subsequent decision should take within a broad spectrum of variations… a continuing process… to be viewed in a continuum of time."

The five working rules for finding the ratio

  1. Nothing is ratio which does not support the ultimate order — "the reason sine qua non" (Cardozo); a stated rule bearing no relation to the facts is no more than a dictum (in Pinchin v. Santam Insurance 1963 (2) SA 254 (WLD) a ruling was made part of the ratio by being made the basis of the order as to costs; on the relationship of rulings to the ultimate order see Wagon Mound (No. 2)).
  2. Ratio may be extracted where the judge gave no reason (e.g. Giles v. Walker (1890) 24 QBD 656) — but no judge is required to accomplish the impossible: "If it is not clear… I do not think it is part of the tribunal's duty to spell out with great difficulty a ratio decidendi in order to be bound by it" (Viscount Dunedin, The Mostyn [1928] AC 57, 73).
  3. Different rationes may be extracted depending on different ways of stating the facts.
  4. A later judge may demote ratio to dictum — the "neighbour" proposition "used to change its category according to whether the plaintiff or the defendant was thought entitled to succeed" (Fairman v. Perpetual Investment B.S. [1923] AC 74; Haseldine v. Daw [1941] 2 KB 343).
  5. Certain cases are not authorities at all: no discoverable ratio; pure questions of fact (Simpson v. Peat [1952] 2 QB 24; R. v. Young [1953] 1 All ER 291); exercises of discretion (Bragg v. Crosville Motor Services [1959] 1 All ER 613); cases judges themselves say are not precedents (R. v. Stokesley Justices [1956] 1 All ER 563); and decisions on the interpretation of documents — regrettably used as precedents (a court may find itself construing "not the document before it, but judicial interpretations of similar wording in other, slightly different documents"). Lord Denning M.R. in Re Jebb [1966] Ch 666, 672: "I do not think it right to look at previous cases in this way. The only legitimate purpose is to use them as a guide towards the meaning of words… They should never be used so as to defeat this intention."

Multiple points, multiple reasons, plurality of judgments

  • Two points, A and B: where the defendant must win on either and the judge decides for the defendant on both, either may be the ratio (Said v. Butt [1920] 2 KB 497 — the judge said point B was additional, making it a dictum); where the plaintiff wins on A but loses on B, A cannot be ratio — yet as a dictum it may carry such authority that the nomenclature ceases to matter. Hedley Byrne & Co. v. Heller & Partners [1964] AC 465: the House overthrew the rule on which the defendant had succeeded below and propounded a new one with a qualification that still let the defendant win — "rule and qualification together constitute the ratio; but even if it were said to be a dictum, the distinction is immaterial at this level of authority."
  • Two reasons in one judgment: Lord Simonds — both should be accepted as part of the ratio (Jacobs v. L.C.C. [1950] AC 361, 369–371); one may later be rejected (Fisher v. Tayler's Furnishing Stores — the Court of Appeal rejected one of two grounds it had earlier given, confirmed later by the C.A. and the House of Lords); if the House later says one of two reasons is correct and the other wrong, "the authoritative ruling is the correct one."
  • Appellate plurality: five judges, same result, five different reasons without disagreement → "five competing rationes," any may be adopted. Five judges each rejecting the others' reasons → the case "has no discoverable ratio and is, in any event, worthless as an authority." Three of five (or two of three) concurring for the same reason → that is the ratio. Majority differing in reasons while the minority agree on theirs → difficult to say. More than one issue + split tribunals → not resolvable by any test; the later judge's choice determines it. "All this does not imply that the solution would be to have only one judgment… Differences in presentation and wording will avoid the danger of any one judge's utterance being treated like an Act of Parliament."

04.6 Obiter dicta

  • Pronouncements not part of the ratio "are classed as obiter dicta and are not authoritative. Rationes and dicta tend to shade into each other. The former have law-quality and are binding on lower courts; dicta, too, have law-quality, but are not binding at all. Vis-à-vis a higher court even the ratio of a lower court decision has only persuasive force like that of a dictum."
  • "Some dicta are so authoritative that the distinction between ratio and dictum is reduced to vanishing point." Dicta with no force: propositions by way of illustration or on hypothetical facts.
  • Relegation by interpretation (ratio→dictum) is "but a device employed by subsequent courts for the adoption or rejection of doctrine expressed in previous cases according to the inclination of the judges" (Lord Atkin's neighbour proposition is the example).
  • Weight varies with the type: "Mere casual expressions carry no weight at all. 'Not every passing expression of a judge, however eminent, can be treated as an ex cathedra statement.'" Dicta acted upon over the years acquire increasing respect; a dictum may be adopted as the ratio of a later decision, acquiring that tribunal's authority; Lord Goddard C.J. in Zeidman v. Owen: "If we thought that the dicta, though obiter, expressed the true construction of the Act, we should feel we ought to follow them." A suggested refinement: distinguish obiter dicta (irrelevant to the case) from judicial dicta (relevant to a collateral matter but no part of the ratio) — the latter generally more persuasive.

04.7 The subsequent history of a case

Reversal · Refusal to follow · Distinguishing

  • Reversal: normally the first judgment "ceases to have any effect at all." If affirmed or reversed on a different point: one Master of the Rolls said the previous decision is of no effect; another that it remains binding — "the truth seems to be that in such a situation a later court has freedom to deal as it pleases with the earlier decision."
  • Refusal to follow: before stare decisis, judges freely refused cases contrary to principle. Even now a judge may refuse a decision of co-ordinate authority — the conflict awaits resolution by a superior tribunal. Repeated refusals weaken a case's authority; so does a higher court's refusal to adopt a lower court's rule — but "only if such refusal implies disapproval of it." Cases are sometimes deliberately put on one side as a last resort — Sellers L.J.: "the best way to deal with that case is to say that it goes into the limbo of lost causes."
  • Distinguishing: repeated distinguishing shows the decision is not approved and confines it to its special facts. "The bindingness of stare decisis is not rigid since judges have some latitude in evading unwelcome authorities… a judge must follow a precedent except where he can reasonably distinguish it; but the possibilities of the latter are such that they reduce bindingness to the semblance of a cloud, solid looking and clear cut when viewed from afar, but less so when one actually gets into it." Techniques: restate facts at a lower level of generality — "A case is only an authority for what it actually decides. I entirely deny that it can be quoted for a proposition that may seem to follow logically from it" (Lord Halsbury) — the pair of decisions then being "like binary stars each part of which lives within the field of the other" (Lord Simon) — theoretically Donoghue may be confined to ginger beer and distinguished from fruit-salad, but "the lengths… have to be regulated by common sense"; treat additional facts as material; adopt the less appropriate of alternative principles as the ratio; reject the accepted ratio as dictum (Asquith L.J.'s quip: "if you agree with the other bloke you say it's part of the ratio; if you don't, you say it's obiter dictum, with the implication that he is a congenital idiot"). It is often hard to say whether a case was overruled or distinguished — where the House comments adversely on inferior courts' decisions, "there is often a great deal of discretion for future courts."

Changed conditions — cessante ratione cessat ipsa lex

A case neither reversed nor overruled may cease to be law "owing to changed conditions and changed law." Two situations must be distinguished: (i) the case is rejected as never having represented the law; (ii) the case, admittedly law at the time, has ceased to have that character owing to altered circumstances — the one under consideration. Willes C.J.: "When the nature of things changes, the rules of law must change too." Examples: Re Polemis was disapproved in Wagon Mound and, though never overruled, is "no longer law" on remoteness; in The Heron II there is the strong suggestion that The Parana (damages in the days of sailing ships) had ceased to be law; when sterling changed from stable to floating and after EEC entry, the Court of Appeal departed from a House of Lords rule — Denning M.R.: "When the nature of sterling changes, the rule of law may change too" — and the House, affirming, refused to follow its own earlier decision while indicating "it was for the House, and not the Court of Appeal, to do so." The rule applies only to decisions which are not binding. Lord Simon's five-point summary (for citation):

"(1) the maxim in the form 'cessante ratione cessat ipsa lex' reflects one of the considerations which your Lordships [may accept as justifying departure from] a previous decision…; (2) in relation to courts bound by the rule of precedent the maxim, in its literal and widest sense, is misleading and erroneous; (3) specifically, courts which are bound by the rule of precedent are not free to disregard an otherwise binding precedent on the ground that the reason… seems to the court to have lost cogency; (4) the maxim in reality reflects the process of legal reasoning whereby a previous authority is judicially distinguished or an exception is made to a principal legal rule; (5) an otherwise binding precedent or rule may, on proper analysis, be held to have been impliedly overruled by a subsequent decision of a higher court or impliedly abrogated by an Act of Parliament; but this doctrine is not accurately reflected by citation of the maxim."— Lord Simon (as summarised by Dias)

04.8 Overruling — definition, grounds, retroactivity

What it is, and when it is legitimate

  • Overruling = "the action of a superior court in upsetting the ratio laid down by a lower court in some other case." Reversal = overthrow of a decision on appeal in the same case (may involve disapproval of the ratio, but need not — reversal on some other point). "Overruling necessarily involves disapproval of the ratio but never affects the previous decision so that the parties… are protected by res judicata; and accounts that have been settled also are not affected." A case may be overruled and then reversed, or reversed and then overruled; if the overruling case is itself reversed or overruled, the first revives; by analogy, if a statute overruling a case is repealed, the case revives.
  • Considerations on overruling one's own decisions (the House of Lords debate): (a) finality is necessary in developing the law; a tenable view in case 1 "should not be cast aside by an equally tenable opposite view in case 2"; it is important not to encourage reopening questions; (b) overruling is legitimate when broad issues of legal principle are involved, or a decision has been disapproved or distinguished on inadequate grounds; (c) it is easier to overrule a recent decision before it has been acted on; (d) overruling should rarely occur in matters of statutory construction (per two Law Lords; two others thought the earlier case wrongly decided, only a minority were prepared to overrule).
  • Factors strengthening authority (weighing against overruling): unanimity; eminence of the judges; approval in later cases and by the profession; evidence that the issue was fully and carefully argued and the court took time to deliberate; and the length of time the decision has stood (people have governed their transactions on it). Factors favouring overruling: lack of unanimity; failure to notice relevant authorities; lack of eminence; poor quality of the report; the case was compromised; extempore judgment, matter not properly argued; doubted or criticised in later decisions, by the profession, or in juristic commentary; and "by no means least, if the decision is thought to be plainly wrong."
  • Express or implied — implied where the later ratio is inconsistent; the difficulty of ascertaining ratio makes implied overruling hard to detect, but "it does give courts a loophole for escaping from stare decisis."

Retroactivity — and the case for prospective overruling

  • The effect of overruling is retroactive, except as to res judicata between the parties to the overruled decision and settled accounts. This is "logically reconcilable with the theory that judges do not make law but only declare what always has been law" (the overruled decision was an erroneous declaration, "void ab initio"); a more fundamental rationale: "justice quality is inherent in every law. Therefore, an unjust (hence erroneous) precedent can never have been 'law'."
  • The difficulty: the House of Lords insists only it can declare its own decisions wrong and that lower courts must follow them — implying such decisions are "law, albeit bad law" (no court can be bound by what is not law). "If so, the overruling by the House of Lords of one of its own decisions must operate prospectively, unless there is express provision to the contrary." Even on the law-making view, hardship remains: those who acted in reliance on the old law are judged by an ex post facto law — "a consideration which weighs powerfully against overruling except for compelling reasons."
  • Prospective overruling (the suggestion): overruling, like repeal, should take effect for the future only, the instant case abiding by the previous law "for the last time." Objections: if the new rule does not support the actual decision it "can only be a dictum" (counter: the tribunal is freeing itself for the future and may adopt the dictum later); a litigant would not be encouraged to appeal against a bad precedent (counter: coupled with a discretion — future only; future + instant; future + instant + retrospective — and particularly valuable where the object is to create a new criminal offence or extend an existing one by overruling a no-liability decision, "for this would avoid having to punish the accused in the instant case for conduct which was not a crime when he did it"). (Grey: Indian doctrine — the Supreme Court adopted prospective overruling in Golak Nath (1967); note the debate at the time of writing is beyond the casebook.)

04.9 Following, applying, explaining — and the judicial discretion (declaratory theory)

Following · applying · explaining

A case is followed when the use made of it does not affect its ratio (e.g., the judge only trims the facts of the case before him to fit the precedent); applied when the use made of it does affect its ratio — raising the level of generality by reducing the material facts, selecting the more appropriate of alternative rationes, or adopting a dictum as the ratio of the case before him. Repeated following enhances authority. Explaining = placing an interpretation on a precedent, then following, refusing or distinguishing — "an indication that the ratio decidendi is being reshaped."

The declaratory theory and its critique

Since "there is no fixed ratio of a case, there is an element of choice in determining it. The orthodox Blackstonian view… is that judges do not make law, but only declare what has always been law." Its sources, per Dias: (1) thinking exclusively in the present time-frame, giving the belief that some rule is always "there" awaiting application; (2) in Blackstone's day, the belief in natural law as part of English law — and "despite the derision which this theory encountered… its vitality is remarkable"; (3) the 17th-century struggle against the prerogative — the judges maintained the king was subject to law and could not legislate, "the corollary of which was that they, too, were subject to the law and unable to make law"; (4) the separation of powers insistence on the theoretical dissociation of judicial and legislative functions; (5) 19th-century positivism ("concerned only with the law as found"); (6) public confidence is more easily retained by the illusion of impersonal administration; judges "seek refuge in the theory when giving a harsh decision"; (7) the psychological preference "to be led rather than have to find their own way" — a "father-symbol"; (8) decisions pronounce on the legality of conduct after the event — anything savouring of attainder is unpopular, and the theory conceals this; (9) the evidential function of a decision (evidence of what the law is) overlays the creative factors; and (10) the syllogistic form: "All men are mortal; X is a man; therefore X is mortal" — in judgment form: "Facts of Type A are governed by Rule B; facts of the instant case are of Type A; therefore…" — "a syllogism can only make explicit that which is implicit in the premises."

But the discretion is real — at every stage

  • Major premise: the judge must find, sometimes make, the rule — in a precedent, statute or custom; or create one by analogy with other rules, or "by a process akin to induction" or, "in the absence of any other guide,… out of his own sense of justice." If binding and not reasonably distinguishable, he must follow; if not binding, he follows if consonant with his sense of justice.
  • Minor premise: finding facts depends on credibility and interpretation of evidence; the facts must be stated at an appropriate level of generality ("governed by linguistic conventions"), "may also be coloured by a convenient rule which is to hand," and "the rule-statement, too, for its part is often trimmed according to the view taken of the facts so that it can be made to yield the conclusion which the judge wants to reach. The formulation of fact statements and rule statements are parallel processes."
  • Similarity, not identity: identity between facts in different situations is "inconceivably remote"; rules are general and utilise vague concepts ('negligence', 'possession') with no fixed content; "what is crucial… is the perception of similarity, not identity… which is subjective, since perception of similarities and dissimilarities is a matter of choice and a desire to reach a just decision."
  • Conclusion: once the premises are thus manipulated, the conclusion "will appear to follow syllogistically." Exposing this "stands in a class of its own": the judicial process involves different kinds of reasoning — induction (finding the major premise: "Facts of case A were decided Y; facts of case B were decided Y; therefore, strictly, facts A and B should be decided Y" — with the possibility of a broader proposition warranted by the material, liable to be cut down "as being too wide by a later court") and analogy (finding the minor premise: case-by-case, contrasting examples, "first one way and then another to see which way one's judgment is swayed").

Induction in law v. scientific induction (six differences) + Wisdom

  1. A scientist repeats his experiment and verifies; a judge cannot.
  2. Scientific principles result from observation of data; judicial principles from statements of fact and value-judgments.
  3. A judge can reason by analogy and choose between competing analogies; a scientist does not.
  4. Scientific principles are modified to accommodate new data; judicial principles "in response to moral and policy considerations."
  5. A judge induces a principle to decide the case before him; for a scientist the case is part of the data.
  6. A scientist must accommodate all the data and has no choice; a judge "can select his material by discarding unwelcome cases."
"It is a presenting and re-presenting of those features of the case which severally co-operate in favour of the conclusion… The reasons are like the legs of a chair, not the links of a chain."— Professor Wisdom (as quoted by Dias)

05.1 What the three readings say about legislation

Rama Jois · the ancient silence

Dharmashastra "did not confer on or recognise any legislative power in the king"; the sources were śruti, smṛti, sadāchāra and conscience; new law came from the people's usages, the parishad, and the king's conscience-decision — never from the crown's will. The corollary for sub-topic (d): legislation is the one source that ancient India lacked — not because it was forgotten, but because it was deliberately excluded: "the king should never act according to his own fiat." Rāja Śāsana (royal edict) was executive, "subject to the law declared in the Shruti and Smritis."

Jain · the modern engine

His concluding verdict is the reading's clearest statement on legislation: "It appears that customs have had their heyday… The future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation, and to some extent, judicial interpretation and precedent, though comparatively, the latter would be less important than the former. And, usually, when a new legislation is passed, custom to that extent is abrogated." Also: legislatures legislate because custom fails — uncertainty, non-uniformity, rigidity, expense, the double burden on courts, and "abrogation of custom and enactment of legislation instead" is "the inevitable result." And his counter-check: in the early British days "the legislature was not active, did not enact laws in the area of private law, and judges had to decide cases… by justice, equity and good conscience" — the vacuum custom filled.

Dias · statutes inside the decisional process

(1) Statutes are one of the three places the lawyer must "know how to find" the law. (2) In constructing the major premise, the judge finds the rule "in a precedent, statute or custom" — or creates one. (3) Statutes interact with precedent: an otherwise binding precedent may be "impliedly abrogated by an Act of Parliament" (Lord Simon, point 5); "if a case is overruled by statute, there is some ground for saying that its ratio is no longer authoritative"; the Employer's Liability (Defective Equipment) Act, 1969 "overruled Davie and restored the original interpretation of Wilsons in statutory form"; repeal of a statute (like reversal of an overruling case) raises the revival question. (4) Statutory construction is special: "overruling should rarely occur in matters of statutory construction" (two Law Lords), because Parliament's meaning, once settled, should stay settled. (5) The document-interpretation lesson generalises: judicial observations on words are a guide to meaning, never a substitute for the actual text.

05.2 Legislation defined — and its four hallmarks

Legislation is law deliberately and formally enacted by a competent authority — the only source that is made, not found: it creates rules for the future, in writing, in advance, for a general class of persons and situations. (Grey competence: in India — the Constitution (by the people in the Constituent Assembly), Parliament (Art. 245–246), State Legislatures, and the executive under delegated power; Art. 13 makes the Constitution supreme over all legislation.)

05.4 The four-source historical cycle — the unit's master flow

STAGE 1 — DHARMA (ancient India): law found in śruti/smṛti/sadāchāra/conscience; king enforces; the people's usages and the parishad adapt; no legislation anywhere→
STAGE 2 — CUSTOM (pre-British and early British India): the great material source, enforced by panchayats and jirgas; the British make it a rule of decision (1772 → 1827 → 1872/1873/1887); "second only to statutory law"→
STAGE 3 — PRECEDENT (19th–20th centuries): the case-law of custom grows (Moottoo Ramalinga → Munnalal); Dias's doctrine of precedent; the reports system; India's vast custom-case-law→
STAGE 4 — LEGISLATION (from codification to the present): Shariat Act 1937; Hindu Acts 1955–56; the Constitution itself; the parent Acts of every tribunal — "the future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation" (Jain)→
THE RING RETURNS: yet the ancients' insight survives inside the modern system — the constitution binds the state's organs (rājadharma), the courts read statutes with Mīmāṃsā (Unit 3 §07), and legislation itself is restrained by fundamental rights

05.6 How to answer "legislation as a source of law" (honest method)

  1. Open with the casebook frame: Dias — the three places a lawyer must know how to find the law; Jain — "the future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation"; Rama Jois — ancient India's deliberate exclusion of legislative power.
  2. Define legislation and its hallmarks (deliberate, prospective, written, supreme).
  3. Show the interaction with the other three: legislation abrogates custom (Jain's examples); legislation overrules precedent (Dias's examples); legislation carries ancient dharma forward only through saving clauses (the HMA/HSA/HAMA savings); and legislation is itself read with Mīmāṃsā and the Constitution's rights (Units 3–4).
  4. Close with the master flow (§05.4) and the caveat: "the sequence does not make the earlier sources dead — in India they survive inside the legislative frame (Art. 372, personal-law savings, tribal customs)."

06Authorities — Case Briefs & Texts

Group A — twelve cases briefed in full (facts · issue · arguments of both sides · decision · principle evolved), as far as the readings report them; where a reading gives only the proposition, that is said so. Group B — the rest of the case law, one line each. Group C — texts and statutes.

CASE 1 · Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga Sethupathy (1868) 12 MIA 397 Jain

FactsThe Ramnad case: a succession/adoption dispute in the Madras Presidency — the question whether a Hindu widow who had not received authority from her husband could nevertheless adopt a son to him with the consent of his kinsmen, and whether a proved usage was good against the text.
IssueCan a custom, clearly proved, prevail over the written text of the Hindu law?
ArgumentsFor the custom: the widow's adoption with kinsmen's consent was the prevailing usage of the Presidency; usage, not text, was the living law. Against: the texts require the husband's authority; usage without authority is void. (The reading records the decisions and the rule rather than the oral arguments in detail.)
DecisionThe Privy Council upheld the adoption (per the law prevalent in the Madras Presidency — a widow without the husband's authority may adopt with the consent of the nearest sapindas) and laid down at p. 436: "under the Hindoo system of law, clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law."
PrincipleCustom overrides the text in Hindu law. If a custom is established on a point of Hindu law, the courts are bound to follow it even though it is inconsistent with an express text of the Dharmashastra or the commentaries. This is the foundation of the whole British-period custom law — and, per Jain, its justification: it was "in conformity with the genius of the law which always gave a high regard to custom."

CASE 2 · Bhyah Ram Singh v. Bhyah Ugur Singh (1870) 13 MIA 373 Jain

FactsA dispute turning on the content of the applicable Hindu law, in which the interpretation of the authoritative digest was in question.
IssueWhich prevails — the language of the texts as compiled in the digest, or custom and approved usage?
ArgumentsText-strict side: the digest's words must be applied as written. Custom side: the digest itself, read as a whole, subordinates text to usage. (The reading cites the case for its holding, not for a full argument record.)
DecisionPrivy Council (p. 390): "The Digest subordinates in more than one place the language of texts to custom and approved usage."
PrincipleIn interpreting the Hindu law-books, the Privy Council treated the commentaries as recording usage — the reason Jain gives for the emergence of schools: "starting with the same texts as the base, two major schools of Hindu law developed in India." The Jogdamba gloss completes it: commentaries are not promulgated by sovereign authority but are binding "by the respect due to their authors and still more to the fact of their being in accordance with prevailing popular sentiment and practice."

CASE 3 · Neelkisto Deb v. Beerchunder Thakoor (1869) 12 MIA 523 Jain

FactsA dispute over the devolution of an estate in which a special usage was set up against the general law of succession (the reading does not detail the facts; it cites the case for its rule).
IssueWhere a custom is proved, does it displace the general law — and does the general law survive outside its scope?
Arguments (as reported)For the custom: proved usage governs the subject matter; for the general law: without proof of custom the ordinary rules apply. (The rule, not the arguments, is the casebook's point.)
DecisionPrivy Council (12 MIA 523): "where a custom is proved to exist it supersedes the general law, which, however, still regulates all outside the custom."
PrincipleThe scope rule of custom: it is an exception carved out of the general law — it operates to the extent of the custom and no further. Hence also Jain's point that a custom, "being in derogation of the general rules of law, must be construed strictly."

CASE 4 · Hirbae v. Sonabae, 4 Ind. Dec. 100, 112 (Bombay Supreme Court) Jain

FactsKhojas and Cutchi Memons of the Bombay Presidency: Muslim by religion, but for generations governed by Hindu customs of inheritance and succession. The question was whether, under the clause directing the Supreme Court to decide by the "laws and usages" of Muhammadans, they were governed by their customs or by orthodox Muslim law.
IssueDoes "laws and usages" mean the Koran alone, or the actually observed usages of the community?
ArgumentsOrthodox view: the phrase must mean the Muslim law as such; a Muslim cannot be governed by Hindu inheritance rules. Perry C.J. (for the customs): the clause "was framed on political grounds solely, and without reference to orthodoxy, or the purity of any religious belief"; its purpose was "to give the benefit of their laws to the people of India"; its effect "was not to adopt the text of the Koran as law any further than it has been adopted in the laws and usages of the Muhammadans"; and "if any class of Muhammadans are found to be in possession of any usage which was otherwise valid as a legal custom, and which did not conflict with any express law of the English Government, they were just as much entitled to the protection of this clause as the most orthodox sunni."
DecisionThe customs of the Khojas and Cutchi Memons were held applicable in preference to orthodox Muslim law.
PrincipleThe colonial "personal law" clause was a political guarantee of actually observed law, not a religious ordinance; where a community's settled usages differ from the orthodox texts, the usages prevail. The first breakthrough for custom against Muslim orthodoxy — though it took the Bengal rule nearly four more decades to fall (see Case 5).

CASE 5 · Mohd. Ismail v. Lala Sheomukh, 17 CWN 97 (P.C.) Jain

FactsThe Allahabad High Court in Jammya v. Diwan (1901) ILR 23 All. 20 had held that s. 37's words being "very positive and emphatic" (Muhammadan law to Muhammadans), a family custom among Muslims excluding a daughter from inheritance could not be proved — a view held since Surmust Khan (1866) and Jowala Buksh v. Dharum Singh (1866) 10 MIA 511, 538. The question came before the Privy Council for review.
IssueDoes s. 37 (and its equivalents) exclude custom as against Muslim law?
ArgumentsAgainst custom: the courts had reasoned that Muslim jurists place custom at a low level (inferior to the Koran, hadis and ijma), and the wording of the Bengal rule admitted no opening. For custom: the same provision was being interpreted differently for Hindus precisely "because of the high place traditionally allotted to custom by the Hindu Jurists"; the constitutional guarantee should not depend on theology.
DecisionPrivy Council: custom got precedence over the Muslim law and was made legally enforceable even in derogation of orthodox law. Followed: Ali Asghar v. Collector of Bulandshahar (1917) ILR 39 All. 574; Mt. Jaffro v. Chatta (163 IC 650); Roshan Ali Khan v. Chaudhri Asghar Ali (1929) 57 IA 29; Md. Ibrahim v. Shaik Ibrahim, AIR 1922 PC 59 ("custom plays a large part in modifying the ordinary law, and it is now established that there may be a custom at variance even with the rules of Mahomedan Law").
PrincipleParity of custom across personal laws: the law as administered in India treats a proved custom as overriding both Hindu and Muslim texts; the earlier judicial attitude "was due to the low place allotted to custom traditionally by the Muslim Jurists," not to any rule of Indian law.

CASE 6 · Abdul Hussein v. Sona Dero (1917) 45 IA 10 Jain

FactsA Punjab succession dispute under s. 5 of the Punjab Laws Act, 1872, which puts "any custom applicable to the parties… not contrary to justice, equity and good conscience" first in the rule of decision.
IssueDoes s. 5 raise a presumption that the parties are governed by custom rather than by personal law?
ArgumentsCustom-first reading: some Punjab courts had held the effect of s. 5 to make custom the primary law, casting on anyone alleging personal law the burden of proving it. Personal-law reading: custom must be proved like any fact; the section merely recognises the fact that customs largely govern Punjab.
DecisionPrivy Council: the section "raises no presumption that the parties are to be governed by custom rather than by personal law which must be applied unless the custom is proved. The clause puts the custom in the forefront as the rule of decision: the legislature has recognised the fact that in Punjab, customs largely govern the people. But, before a custom may be enforced, it must be established by the ordinary processes of evidence." Accord: Vaishno Ditti v. Rameshri (1928) 55 IA 407, 421; Salig Ram v. Munshi Ram, AIR 1961 SC 1374.
PrincipleNo presumption of custom — even in custom-favouring statutes. "A person asserting the custom has to prove it before the courts can apply it"; the preference in the statute determines the rule of decision once proof is tendered, not the incidence of the burden.

CASE 7 · Munnalal v. Raj Kumar, AIR 1962 SC 1493 Jain

FactsThe question whether the Jain community's custom — a widow may adopt without her husband's consent — prevails in the community "as such," across sects and localities, or only in particular localities. Earlier authorities: (1947) 74 IA 254 (AIR 1948 PC 177); Sheo Singh Rai v. Mussumut Dakho (1878) 5 IA 87; Chotay Lal (1878) 6 IA 15.
IssueIs a custom sustainable for a whole community irrespective of locality and sect?
ArgumentsFor the community-wide custom: the evidence and decisions showed uniform Jain usage (except in Madras and Punjab); the community's identity is not territorial. Against: custom is territorial and sect-bound; a community-wide claim cannot be proved as one custom. (The reading reports the outcome and the cases reviewed.)
DecisionThe Supreme Court, reviewing "the large number of cases having a bearing on this point," sustained the custom as one prevailing in the Jain community as such, irrespective of the locality where they reside or the sect in which they are divided (with the Madras and Punjab exception noted).
PrincipleA custom may inhere in a community as a whole and not merely in a locality; courts can establish a general communal custom when the evidence of practice, decisions and documented usage is uniform. (Contrast the companion principle: "theory and custom are antithesis" — no extension by analogy; and the Punjab rule that there is no general custom there, Case 12.)

CASE 8 · Shiba Prasad Singh v. Prayag Kumari Devi (1932) 59 IA 331 Jain

FactsA dispute over the succession to a family's property, where the family claimed to be governed by a customary rule different from the ordinary law.
IssueCan a family lawfully "make a custom for itself" of succession, or is the custom valid only if found to have been actually followed?
ArgumentsAgainst: "a Hindu family… cannot by agreement between its members make a custom for itself of succession to family property at variance with the ordinary law." For: the family had in fact been governed by the customary rule for generations.
DecisionPrivy Council — the qualification is decisive: "a Hindu family, no doubt, cannot by agreement between its members make a custom for itself of succession… at variance with the ordinary law. But where a family is found to have been governed as to its property by a customary rule of succession different from that of the ordinary law, that custom is itself law. The rule of succession in such a case is recognised by the State as part of the law of the family, though it is no more than the result of a course of conduct of individual subjects of the state constituting the family."
PrincipleCustom is fact, not contract: it cannot be created by agreement (contrast the "composite family" where families genuinely pool), but a long course of conduct, once shown, is law for the family — as the State's recognition, not the family's fiat. A family custom may also be destroyed by disuse.

CASE 9 · Mt. Subhani v. Nawab (1940) 68 IA 1; AIR 1941 PC 21 Jain

FactsA custom alleged to have been in existence for nearly 30 years was in question; the judgment also notices Rattigan's Digest of Customary Law as a book "of unquestioned authority in Punjab."
IssueMust an Indian custom be carried back to time immemorial (in the English technical sense, 1189) before it can be enforced?
ArgumentsEnglish rule: a custom is not proved if not shown to be immemorial (the courts had followed this reading at first). Indian practice: the Allahabad High Court had refused it outright; Calcutta used 1773/1793; Bombay accepted 20 years' instances as a presumption.
DecisionPrivy Council (p. 31): "in India it is not of the essence of the rule that the custom to be binding must be ancient and its antiquity must be carried back to a period beyond the memory of man — still less, that it is ancient in the English technical sense. It depends upon the circumstances of each case what antiquity must be established before the custom can be accepted. What is necessary to be proved is that the usage has been acted upon in practice for such a long time as to show that it has, by common consent, been submitted to as the established governing rule of the particular locality." Here, a custom of nearly 30 years was held enforceable; and customary law found to exist in 1880 must be taken to have "the ordinary attribute… that it is ancient."
PrincipleAntiquity is relative in India: long, uniform, consent-backed use — not 1189 — is the test; the Supreme Court has adopted the same formulation ("the English rule… should not be strictly applied to Indian conditions"). The practical consequence: 20 years' usage is the minimum judicial standard (Bhiku v. Sheoram, AIR 1928 Nag. 87; Bari v. Tukaram, AIR 1959 Bom. 54, 30 years; Andhra, 40 years; Calcutta, 50 years even against proof of an earlier different state of things).

CASE 10 · Gopi v. Jaggo (1936) 63 IA 295 Jain

FactsA custom among the community in question recognised and sanctioned the remarriage of a woman who had been abandoned and deserted by her husband: by the custom, desertion dissolved the marriage tie, leaving the woman free to remarry.
IssueIs such a custom valid, or is it contrary to public policy/morality (and thus unenforceable)?
ArgumentsAgainst the custom: it permits what the ordinary law does not — dissolution of marriage by abandonment, and remarriage; the court's "censorial power" over customs stands in the way. For the custom: it gives effect to the community's settled, humane practice — no woman is left in a dead marriage.
DecisionPrivy Council allowed the custom: it was not immoral; desertion dissolving the marriage was a recognised usage.
PrincipleThe courts' censorial power is real but must be exercised with the community's morality, not the judge's preference: a custom is not void merely because it departs from the ordinary law. (Compare the contrary instance: Mathura Naikin, Case 11, where the custom was held immoral because the profession itself was held such; and the daughter's-daughter marriage custom — "abhorrent to decency or morality however long practised… cannot be enforced.")

CASE 11 · Mathura Naikin v. Esu Naikin (1880) ILR 4 Bom. 545 Jain

FactsA custom of adoption of girls by dancing girls had earlier been recognised by the Bombay High Court (Nanee Tara Naikin v. Allarakia Soomar). The point returned for reconsideration.
IssueIs the adoption custom lawful, or is it immoral (and hence void)?
ArgumentsFor the custom: it was long practised by the community and previously recognised. Against: "the profession of dancing girls was immoral, and adoption by them of girls was designed to perpetuate [it]."
DecisionThe Bombay High Court held the custom to be immoral and unenforceable, reversing the earlier tolerance.
PrincipleThe morality test applies to the purpose and effect of the custom, not merely its age and acceptance: "A custom abhorrent to decency or morality however long practised and recognised by a community cannot be enforced by the courts." This is the clearest instance of the judicial "censorial power," and the reason the absurdly strict Gopalayyan v. Raghupatiayyan (7 Mad. HCR 250 — refusal of a 134-year-old, "uniform and uninterrupted" brahmin custom of adopting a sister's son purely because no text supported it) is the anomalous counter-example in Jain's account.

CASE 12 · Ujagar v. Mst. Jeo, AIR 1959 SC 1041 Jain

FactsA Punjab dispute in which a party sought to rely on a body of "Punjab customary law" as a general system.
IssueIs there a single body of customary or tribal law common to the whole of Punjab — a "general custom"?
ArgumentsFor a general custom: Punjab is pre-eminently a land of customary law (Campbell's "not one out of a hundred"); the statutes put custom first. Against: custom in Punjab is mainly tribal and varies from locality to locality; tribes differ in origin and local conditions.
DecisionSupreme Court: "There is no such thing in Punjab as a 'general custom'." Custom there is mainly tribal, "even with the same tribe, it may vary from locality to locality"; origins of tribes differ and local and social conditions differ, "resulting in varying customs." (Urban Punjabis are not entirely free from custom — Ramkishore v. Jainarayan (1921) 48 IA 405.)
PrincipleCustom is specific and local-in-fact; there is no presumption of a general customary system — which is why s. 5 of the Punjab Laws Act, 1872 puts custom first while demanding proof in each case, and why a party's assertion of a general custom must fail on evidence. With Munnalal (Case 7) the two poles of Indian custom law: community-wide custom can be established by proof; a general territorial system cannot be assumed.

CASE 13 · Donoghue v. Stevenson [1932] AC 562 Dias

FactsThe ginger-beer case: A manufactured ginger beer; a snail was alleged to be in one of the bottles (A assumed negligent); A delivered the tainted bottle to retailer B under contract; B innocently passed it to C under contract; C innocently passed it to D; there had been no opportunity for intermediate examination; D was allegedly poisoned by drinking the remains; being a Scottish case, it went to the House of Lords on a preliminary point. The House held that, assuming A negligent, A would be liable to D, and remitted the case for trial on the question whether there was in truth a snail — but the trial never took place (death of a party), so the truth about the snail was never established, and is irrelevant to the ruling.
Issue(For Dias) What exactly is the ratio decidendi of the case — and how is it identified?
Arguments (the competing statements)From the same eight facts, at least four propositions: (i) the Scots-appeal point (fact viii alone — an appeal from Scotland is not binding on English courts); (ii) the manufacturer proposition (i, ii, vi, vii — a negligent manufacturer is liable to the ultimate consumer where there is no likelihood of intermediate examination); (iii) the contract-misfeasance proposition (ii, vi, vii — one who negligently misperforms a contract and physically damages a contemplated third party is liable in tort); (iv) the neighbour proposition (ii, vii — anyone negligently damaging another whom he ought reasonably to have contemplated is liable). "Only in the light of later cases is it possible to determine the ratio decidendi."
DecisionLater courts — reasoning from Home Office v. Dorset Yacht Co. [1970] AC 1004, Anns v. Merton L.B.C. [1978] AC 728, Ross v. Caunters [1980] Ch 297, Junior Books v. Veitchi [1983] 1 AC 520 — came to regard the fourth proposition as the ratio, whereas earlier (e.g. Howard v. Walker [1947] KB 860) courts generally adopted the second. The ruling formerly regarded as ratio (Lord Atkin's neighbour principle) was even demoted to dictum according to which party the later court wished to favour ("Landon: wherever the court wishes to find for the plaintiff, that doctrine will be invoked, just as it will be disregarded when the defendant is the favoured party").
Principle(1) The ratio of a case is not fixed once and for all — it is "any one of a series of rulings as elucidated by subsequent interpretations," a "pointer… in a continuum of time." (2) The rule of thumb: nothing is ratio which does not support the ultimate order. (3) What is important in law is the statement of facts rather than even their truth. (4) The possibilities of varying the statement are limited by the consensual domain of word-meanings — the facts would not support, e.g., that the defendant caused a public nuisance. (5) Three rulings in one case (neighbour, manufacturer, contractual-privity fallacy) show the same instability.

CASE 14 · Rylands v. Fletcher [1868] LR 3 HL 330 Dias

FactsFive facts: (i) A had a reservoir built; (ii) A employed B to build it; (iii) A was not himself negligent; (iv) B was negligent; (v) water escaped owing to B's negligent construction and damaged C's property. (The liability of the mine-owner's workings was the underlying dispute.)
IssueHow the court states the material facts determines the rule's width.
Arguments/analysis (Dias's)The parties' arguments on negligence were superseded by the court's selection of material facts: only (i) and (v) were treated as material. "By ignoring the absence and presence of negligence in A and B respectively, and by generalising fact (i) into 'anything likely to do mischief,' the decision creates the sweeping rule that whoever brings a 'mischievous substance' on to his land is liable for damage caused by its escape, irrespective of negligence."
DecisionThe House of Lords laid down the strict-liability rule (the "mischievous substance" rule) — with subsequent interpretation leaving open whether "damage" includes personal injury (Miles v. Forest Rock Granite Co. (1918) 34 TLR 500; Shiffman v. Order of St. John [1936] 1 All ER 557; Hale v. Jennings [1938] 1 All ER 579; Perry v. Kendricks [1956] 1 WLR 85; cf. Read v. J. Lyons & Co. [1947] AC 156).
PrincipleThe fewer material facts included in a statement, the wider its scope — "the art of knowing how to select the material facts" is the first control on a precedent's authority; and a later judge may always re-do the surgery (add or subtract facts; restate at another level of generality) — see Wilson & Clyde Coal / Davie (Case 15) and Wagon Mound (No. 1) & (No. 2) (Case 16).

CASE 15 · Wilson & Clyde Coal Co. v. English [1938] AC 57; Davie v. New Merton Board Mills [1959] AC 604 Dias

FactsWilsons: an employee died owing to the default of another employee; the employer was held liable. Davie: a workman injured by a defective tool manufactured by a third party sued his employer — the very area affected by Wilsons.
IssueAt the time of Wilsons, the doctrine of common employment barred holding an employer vicariously liable for a fellow-servant's injury. How, then, could the employer be held liable — and what is the ratio for subsequent cases?
ArgumentsTo hold the employer liable despite common employment, the ratio of Wilsons had to be stated as: the employers were held liable not vicariously, but for the breach of a personal non-delegable duty to ensure that reasonable care would be taken — ignoring the materiality of the fact that the party at fault was a servant. Stated so, the rule would attach liability even to the employer of an independent contractor. In Davie, however, Viscount Simonds and Lord Reid "appeared to place an interpretation upon Wilsons' Case which would dismantle this rule": they "stressed the materiality of the fact that it was not a contractor but a servant who had been at fault" — "a fundamental reinterpretation."
DecisionIn Wilsons, liability on the personal non-delegable duty (as the reading reports the case and its later interpretation); in Davie, no liability (on the servant-fact interpretation). The Employer's Liability (Defective Equipment) Act, 1969 then "overruled Davie and restored the original interpretation of Wilsons in statutory form."
PrincipleA later judge may add to sub-total material facts or subtract from it, changing the precedent's ambit — the same case can be narrowed (a contractor not liable) or widened (a non-delegable duty); and when the courts' reinterpretation swings too far, legislation may settle the law: the statute overrules the precedent built on the reinterpretation.

CASE 16 · Overseas Tankship (U.K.) Ltd. v. Morts Dock & Engineering Co. [1961] AC 388 (Wagon Mound No. 1); Wagon Mound (No. 2) [1967] 1 AC 617 Dias

FactsOne occurrence, two litigations: furnace oil was carelessly allowed to spill overboard from the S.S. Wagon Mound into Sydney Harbour; fire damage followed. No. 1: the Privy Council proceeded on the lower court's finding that, according to the scientific opinion of the day, ignition in such circumstances was not reasonably foreseeable — no liability for fire damage. No. 2 (different plaintiffs, same occurrence): the lower court stated that a bare possibility of fire might have been foreseeable, but that the chance was "so remote that it should be ignored" — no liability.
IssueWhat counts as a material fact, when the same event produces two appeals?
Arguments (the reasoning)In No. 1 the low court's finding made lack of foreseeability material. In No. 2 the judge's advertence to "even a bare possibility of fire" was fastened on by the Judicial Committee as constituting a material difference between the two cases.
DecisionNo. 2: reversing the lower court, the defendants were held liable in negligence for fire damage after all. (The reading also notes: Re Polemis, disapproved in Wagon Mound though never overruled, was "declared to be no longer law" in the light of the changed law of remoteness — the changed-conditions principle.)
PrincipleThe selection of material facts can change the result of the same event: "stressing one factor as material not only enabled two different courts to reach opposite results in the same case, but also enabled the same court to reach opposite results in two cases based on the same happening." Foreseeability of a bare possibility was enough in No. 2; the tests are malleable — "the possibilities of distinguishing are such that they reduce bindingness to the semblance of a cloud."

CASE 17 · Osborne v. Rowlett (1880) 13 ChD 774 Dias

FactsA Chancery appeal (Jessel M.R.) in which the earlier and later authorities on the point in question had to be weighed.
IssueWhat exactly binds a subsequent judge in an earlier decision?
ArgumentsThe parties differed on whether the earlier ruling as stated, or the earlier decision as decided on its facts, was the authority.
DecisionJessel M.R. (785): "The only thing in a Judge's decision binding as an authority upon a subsequent Judge is the principle upon which the case was decided." And the companion passage: "It is not sufficient that the case should have been decided on a principle, if that principle is not itself a right principle, or one not applicable to the case, and it is for a subsequent judge to say whether or not it is a right principle, and, if not, he may himself lay down the true principle."
PrincipleThe canonical definition of ratio decidendi (bindingness = principle, not outcome), and the canonical statement of the rôle of subsequent interpretation: the deciding judge's pronouncement is "a necessary step," but later judges may correct, restrict or enlarge the principle.

CASE 18 · Hedley Byrne & Co. v. Heller & Partners Ltd. [1964] AC 465 Dias

FactsThe plaintiffs, advertising agents, asked their bank to obtain a credit reference on a customer; the defendants (bankers) gave the reference without responsibility in terms; the plaintiffs relied on it and lost money.
IssueFor Dias's purpose: how is the ratio stated when the court overthrows the rule on which the defendant won below, yet adds a qualification that still lets the defendant win?
Arguments/analysisBelow, the Court of Appeal had applied the then rule (no liability for negligent misstatement causing pure economic loss); the House of Lords overthrew that rule and propounded a different one (liability for negligent misstatement where a special relationship of reliance exists) — but with a qualification (here, the express "without responsibility" disclaimer) — "so that the defendant was still able to win."
DecisionDefendant succeeded, on the qualification; the new liability rule was laid down.
Principle"This rule and qualification together constitute the ratio; but even if it were said to be a dictum, the distinction is immaterial at this level of authority." Also illustrative: multiple points, where a decision in the defendant's favour on either point suffices, make the ratio un-pinpointable; and where the plaintiff wins on point A but loses on B, the ruling on A is technically obiter — "even as a dictum it may nevertheless carry such authority that a subsequent tribunal may feel obliged to follow it."

Q1 · "Discuss Dharma as the ultimate source of law in ancient India. How was the king bound by it?"

Answer skeleton: (1) Dharma moolam — dharma is the root of authority: the throne is subordinate to it ("dharma is the highest authority in the Hindu system of law," Rama Jois). (2) The four sources (Manu II.6): śruti (Vedas), smṛti, sadāchāra (conduct of good people), and one's own conscience — with the school rule that where two sources conflict the earlier must be preferred (Vyasa I-V-4). (3) The definition of sadāchāra (Harita: conduct of the learned, followed by the people, evidenced by the people's belief that it is right; opinions of the learned as its proof). (4) Custom's place: "custom is the child of dharma" — sadāchāra is the dynamic, current expression of dharma. (5) The king as dharmapravartaka, not dharmakartā: four propositions — the king is the source of law for his subjects but not the lawmaker; he is bound by the same four sources; he creates law only in the sense that he discovers it (Kat. 45: "the king should never act according to his own fiat"); his secular edicts (rāja śāsana) are "subject to the law declared in the Shruti and Smritis" (manuscript end-note). (6) Enforcement devices: royal seal registration of conventions (Kat. 48–50; the 725 A.D. Charter of Vishnu Sena); the king's conscience-decision as the gap-filler (Dharmaśāstra: the king should set the "conscience" of the learned as the touchstone — the historical kernel of "justice, equity and good conscience" administered by English judges); the parishad (Manu VIII 110–114), whose opinion is higher than the texts of an individual law book. (7) Conclusion: dharma is the source of the sources — custom, judgment and edict all take their authority from it; and the one thing ancient India's scheme deliberately withheld from the king, legislation, is exactly what the modern state has.

Quote for the bold line: "the most important distinction between the concept of kingship in India and the concept of kingship in the western countries" — the western king was lawgiver by pleasure (Austin's command, Unit-2); the Indian king was law-giver only in the sense of enforcement and discovery.

Q2 · "Custom as a source of law was subordinated to the sacred law in the orthodox Hindu view but triumphed in practice. Discuss the Indian position with the case law."

Answer skeleton: (1) The orthodox subordination: Manu/Yājñavalkya/Gautama — śruti > smṛti > sadāchāra (āchāra); a custom was honoured as sadāchāra, i.e. as evidence of dharma, not as a rival source. (2) The dissenting line: Viśvarūpa (smṛti is to be construed by usage), Medhātithi (texts lose to settled practice), Vrihaspati and Narada (Dharmakosha p. 90: "custom decides everything and overrules the sacred law"), Asahaya, Kautilya (custom higher than even dharma). (3) The resolution in practice — the process text → custom → commentary → schools: "starting with the same texts as the base, two major schools of Hindu law developed"; the commentaries were binding "by the respect due to their authors and still more to the fact of their being in accordance with prevailing popular sentiment and practice." (4) The British conversion: in administering the dharma, the courts had to discover it; and discovery was in practice the discovery of usage — the Hindu adjudication rule (1772), the Bombay rule (Reg. IV of 1827, s. 26: "in the absence of such law as may be applicable, the court shall decide by the principles of justice, equity and good conscience" — the "usages" phrase of the Act of Settlement 1781 was read to include customs: Hirbae v. Sonabae). (5) The climacteric: Moottoo Ramalinga (1868): "clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law"; Bhyah Ram Singh (1870) (the Digest subordinates the texts to custom); Neelkisto Deb (1869) (custom supersedes the general law, which governs outside it); Mohd. Ismail (1917) (custom prevails even against orthodox Muslim law — following Hirbae). (6) The statutory seal: Punjab s. 5 (1872); Madras s. 16 (1873); s. 37 (1887) — custom first, provable, no presumption (Abdul Hussein). (7) The modern result: custom is "second only to statutory law" as a source in fact — though not as a source in theory — and it survives in the personal-law statutes by express saving clauses over the very texts that once subordinated it. Cite Munnalal (community custom), Subhani (antiquity is relative), Shiba Prasad (custom is fact, not agreement), Ujagar (no general Punjab custom), Gopi v. Jaggo and Mathura Naikin (the censorial power).

Q3 · "The requisites of a valid custom." (or: "What are the conditions for a custom to be a source of law in India?")

Answer skeleton (eight requisites, with authority): (1) Antiquity — long use; in India not the English 1189 test: Subhani — "it is not of the essence… the custom must be ancient"; "what is necessary is that the usage has been acted upon in practice for such a long time as to show that it has by common consent been submitted to as the established governing rule" (minimums: 20 years — Bhiku; 30 — Bari; 40 — Andhra; 50 — Calcutta, even against proof of an earlier different state). (2) Certainty — a custom, being in derogation of the general law, must be construed strictly and proved clearly. (3) Reasonableness — not contrary to public policy; e.g. Wolstanton (custom for minerals without compensation for subsidence unreasonable), Shibnarain Mookerjee (total remission of rent, uncertain & unreasonable), Gopi v. Jaggo (a custom allowing a deserted woman to remarry is reasonable). (4) Morality/conformity with public policy — the censorial power: Mathura Naikin ("abhorrent to decency or morality however long practised… cannot be enforced"); the daughter's-daughter marriage custom. (5) Continuity and peaceableness — the usage must be uniform and continuous (the reading's account of long, uniform, voluntary acceptance). (6) Conformance with fundamental law — [grey: in India, customs inconsistent with fundamental rights or public order are void — Arts. 13 and 25(1) reading with 25(2)(b); the Sabarimala point: Collector of Madura itself was on the opposite side of the text-custom priority, but Articles 14/25 are now the frame]. (7) Legality — not contrary to any statute [grey]; (8) Proof — the custom must be pleaded and proved by clear and unambiguous evidence: ss. 35, 42, 48, 49, 56 Indian Evidence Act; wajib-ul-arz (Balgobind — "most valuable evidence"; Uman Parshad — may be untrue); riwaz-i-am; Rattigan; Panna Lal; Abdul Hussein — "no presumption."

Examiner's extra: end with the debate — certainty is required, yet "the impossibility of an absolutely certain rule is matched by the danger of an absolutely uncertain one"; and the courts' censorial power is real but sparingly exercised, because "the courts are not to substitute their idea of what is good for the community's."

Q4 · "What is ratio decidendi? How is it distinguished from obiter dicta, and how is it identified?"

Answer skeleton: (1) Definition — Osborne v. Rowlett (Jessel M.R.): "The only thing in a Judge's decision binding as an authority upon a subsequent Judge is the principle upon which the case was decided" — not the decision itself, not the judge's reasons generally, but the necessary principle. (2) Identification — Dias's machinery: select the material facts (the facts which, per the deciding judge themselves or per the ultimate order, are the sine qua non — Cardozo's phrase; F. & A.B. Ltd. v. Lupton, Lord Simon: what matters is the statement of facts by the deciding judge and their re-statement by later judges; Benmax: primary v. inferential facts) — then state the rule on those facts, and ask: nothing is ratio which does not support the ultimate order. (3) The three shades of ratio: (i) the ruling as authoritatively pronounced; (ii) the ruling as subsequently established by interpretation (the "true" ratio — Donoghue: four possible propositions from the same facts; the neighbour principle was later demoted to dictum and later still promoted back); (iii) the constituent elements of the dispute; (iv) [Dias's fourth shade: the rule that emerges from the authorities as a group]. (4) Five working rules — the ratio at different levels; one of two reasons (particularly when the rules are independent) may be a dictum — Jacobs v. L.C.C. (both grounds part of the ratio), Said v. Butt ("either is sufficient"), Fisher v. Tayler's, R. v. Stokesley Justices (what previous judges themselves treat as non-authoritative), Pinchin (a ruling on a non-deciding point made part of the ratio because it was the basis of the order on costs). (5) Distinction from obiter: if not essential to the decision, it is a dictum — Hedley Byrne: the rule AND the qualification constituted the ratio; a dictum may nevertheless carry "such authority that a subsequent tribunal may feel obliged to follow it" (Zeidman v. Owen). (6) The [grey: Indian] position: Art. 141 makes the law declared by the Supreme Court binding — "law declared" = ratio, not every observation; State of Gujarat v. Utility Users' Welfare Association-type caution [grey, Unit-4] that observations made in a different context are not binding; but in practice the Court's obiter carries great persuasive weight. (7) Conclusion — ratio as "a pointer… within a broad spectrum of variations": the living precedent is not a sealed packet but an arrangement of authority that later judges re-order.

Q5 · "Discuss the doctrine of precedent and its operations: stare decisis, hierarchy, ‘law of the case’, and the devices for avoiding a precedent."

Answer skeleton: (1) Precedent defined: an earlier decision which a later judge is "bound" to follow because its material facts and the principle are the same; the doctrine of precedent = the rule that like cases be decided alike, resting on consistency, fairness, efficiency and "law-quality" (a precedent is law in itself and is the proof of law). (2) Two preconditions for a case to be a precedent: (i) it must be reported (the "authorised" source) — the report must contain the facts (rules of court assess what should be reported) and the decision (the principle) — and (ii) it must enjoy authority (a pronouncement by a court of competent jurisdiction; source, order and status). (3) Hierarchy: the court system — in England: House of Lords (now Supreme Court) → Court of Appeal → lower; in India [grey, Unit-4: Constitution (Art. 141: SC binding on all courts; Art. 142: in the cause of justice; 145(3): precedent requirements); High Court bound by SC; within the HC, larger benches by smaller; the appellate court's decisions bind subordinate courts — cf. Chapter 1's reading on the pyramid of courts and tribunals]. (4) Stare decisis (stand by what is decided): horizontal and vertical; in England the H.L. once declared itself absolutely bound (London Tramways-era practice) before the 1966 Practice Statement allowed departure; in India the SC has both binding (Art. 141) and a power of overruling, and the doctrine applies to points actually decided. (5) Stare decisis v. res judicata (table, §05): decisions binding future cases v. decisions binding the parties; court-directed v. parties-directed; general v. particular; prospective v. final; the "law of the case" assimilation [grey: e.g. Punjab v. Nathu Ram-type reading of s. 11 CPC]. (6) Avoiding a precedent: distinguishing (a material difference — India's custom cases are the greatest distinguishing machine, "the most fruitful as well as the most abused" device); following or applying (within the same ratio); explaining (inconsistent decisions reconciled); per incuriam [grey: where the court disregarded a binding authority or a statute — legitimate but narrowly used]; [grey: sub silentio — decided without argument on the point, not binding]; (7) Overruling: by a higher court; grounds (wrong in law, error, injustice, changed conditions, subsequent overruling of the logic beneath); the factors the courts weigh (settled expectations, reliance, public policy, validity of the original doubt, declaratory theory, the impossibility of absolute certainty); the retroactivity problem and prospective overruling [grey: Golak Nath and I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab — introducing prospective overruling in India]; deliberate obiter on a statutory point may itself be relied upon. (8) The classical theory and its critique: declaratory theory (judges discover, not create), the syllogism (major premise from precedent etc.), induction (6 differences from deduction), analogy (from a single analogy made into a rule), and Wisdom's "legs of a chair" — the object is not to attach to one ratio but to be balanced by several interrelated rules. (9) The Dias verdict: "the possibilities of distinguishing are such that they reduce bindingness to the semblance of a cloud" — the doctrine is real but operates within a continuum of interpretation.

Q6 · "Custom after codification: has the Hindu law of 1955–56 and the Shariat Act 1937 abolished custom?" (or: "Custom as a source of law in modern India.")

Answer skeleton: (1) The statutory abolition: Shariat Act 1937 (Muslims — to be governed by their personal law; customary law displaced; usages excluded from the definition of personal law in s. 2; s. 3: decision per Muslim personal law where parties are Muslims), cutting short the post-Mohd. Ismail line: [grey: the Act's effect Md. Ismail-line of custom cases in orthodox Muslim succession, subject to the exceptions in s. 2(1) provisos and s. 3 provisos (e.g. agricultural land, certain trading usages of Bombay communities) — as the casebook prints them]. (2) Hindu Acts 1955–56: HMA s. 4 (rules of Hindu law inconsistent with the Act or with any other custom or usage "shall cease to have effect"); HSA s. 4 (overriding effect; "the court shall not recognise any custom or usage save the customs expressly saved by s. 3" — i.e. the s. 3(b) definition and the provisos); HAMA s. 4 ("save as otherwise expressly provided in this Act… any text, rule or interpretation of Hindu law or any custom or usage as part of that law… shall cease to have effect"); Hindu Minority & Guardianship Act 1956. Result: a statutory personal law with custom as the exception — the reverse of the ancient and British position. (3) Yet custom survives in three pockets: within the Acts (the s. 3 definitions expressly incorporate custom — the oddity that codification had to define custom); beyond the Acts (areas of Hindu law not governed — e.g. certain succession and adoption usages, agricultural customs, religious and charitable endowments; the "law in force" for Hindus in the absence of the Acts is still the old law as modified by custom [grey]); and untouched areas — Jammu & Kashmir, tribal belts (NEFA/Nagaland), the Sixth Schedule and Part XXI protections [grey, flagged because the reading does not state them]; Kumaon, Malabar, and the Moplah customs remain points of reference in the casebook's story. (4) The theoretical point — Jain's verdict: custom's "heyday" is over; "the future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation and, to some extent, judicial interpretation and precedent"; "when a new legislation is passed, custom to that extent is abrogated"; but custom and precedent remain sources in fact — the courts' law in personal-law and tribal matters, and the background against which statutes are interpreted. (5) Conclusion: custom is now a subsidiary, statutory-licensed source — the very clause that once made it first (1772) has been replaced by clauses that make it residual; but in the real life of the law (village panchayats, tribes, family practices) it remains the living law, and the casebook's own procedure (proof, presumption, censorial power) is still the operative machinery.

Q7 · "Dharma Moolam: critically evaluate Rama Jois's thesis on the ancient Indian king, law and the people's usages."

Answer skeleton: (1) The title: "धर्म मूलम्" — dharma is the root (moolam) of authority; the thesis: (i) the king was the "source of law" in the sense of enforcement and adjudication, not of legislation; (ii) "\it not of the king's pleasure" — the four sources and the four-proposition structure; (iii) custom/convention (vivāha, vikraya — marriage and sale conventions) was recorded under royal seal (Kat. 48–50) and thereby brought into the body of law — the only "making" of law the system allowed, and it was registration of what the people actually did; (iv) the king's conscience-decision, filled by the conscience of the people (Vishnu Sena's 725 A.D. charter shows the king's decree enforcing usages in these matters within two independent channels); (v) the parishad's texts overrule individual law-books. (2) The historical kernel: the "most important distinction" — Indian kingship was constitutionally limited by dharma in a way western kingship was not; the practical index: no legislation, no fiat, no royal will as a source. (3) Critical evaluation: (i) the thesis is idealised — the dharmaśāstra's own edict (Kat. 45) is a prescription, the "royal seal" and the parishad are institutions whose actual functioning is documented unevenly; (ii) the "conscience" device is precisely where the later English "justice, equity and good conscience" was inserted — which shows both continuity and the gap; (iii) the thesis explains why the British converted dharma into text-based law (with custom as the escape), and why the modern Indian state (legislation + Art. 141) is the inverse of the ancient scheme; (iv) Jain's custom material shows the same ranking working in practice — texts subordinated to usages even in the orthodox view's own development (Viśvarūpa, Medhātithi, Kautilya); (v) conclusion: Jois's map is the necessary starting point for any "sources" answer — and its moral is that India's source-history ends where it began: with dharma (now constitutionalised) binding the sovereign.

Q8 · "Distinguish stare decisis from res judicata. Is the ratio decidendi the only thing binding?"

Answer skeleton: (1) Definitions; the table in §05. (2) Res judicata is party-binding (s. 11 CPC [grey]); stare decisis is an institutional principle binding future courts. (3) The ultimum supplicium analogy. (4) Is ratio the only thing binding? — Yes in form (Jessel M.R. — "the only thing in a Judge's decision binding as an authority"); but three qualifications: (i) the ratio itself is identified ex post — the "true" ratio of Donoghue was settled later; (ii) the decision (order) is binding on the parties — the "law of the case" [grey] — and the court below must follow the higher court's decision even if the reasoning is later doubted; (iii) dicata can carry force (Zeidman; the Hedley Byrne qualification; the "deliberate obiter" on so-called "free" points). (5) Conclusion: ratio is the binding unit categorically; but the authority of a case is a range — "a pointer… within a broad spectrum of variations" (Dias).

Short notes (each = 1 paragraph, with authority)

1. Sadāchāra

The third source of dharma in Manu II.6; the "conduct of good men" — defined by Harita: conduct of the learned followed by the people, evidenced by the people's own belief that it is right and by the opinions of the learned. It is the living, working part of dharma: the customs of a people, as refined by the learned, are dharma in force, and customs registered under royal seal (Kat. 48–50) are enforced by the king. Its successor in the modern era is "custom" itself — hence the mūla of the Indian custom law.

2. The parishad and rāja śāsana

Manu VIII 110–114: in doubtful questions the king should consult the learned assembly; where the parishad differs (but respects the śruti and smṛti), its view prevails over the view of particular law-books; the king's edict must be consistent with the texts. The ancient constitutional check: royal legislation is impossible — edicts are "subject to the law declared in the Shruti and Smritis."

3. The Queen's rule (Rāja Śāsana) — registration by royal seal

Katyayana 48–50: conventions (marriage, sale) — if a Ṣatkarmaka king wishes, he should affix his royal seal to enforce them; the charter of Vishnu Sena (725 A.D.) enforcing family usages; the point for the modern law: custom was enforced BECAUSE it was the people's dharma, not because the king made it law.

4. "Justice, equity and good conscience"

The king's gap-filler (conscience-decision after the learned's opinion: "the king should decide according to conscience" — Pitamaha) became the English-administered residual rule (s. 26, Reg. IV of 1827; s. 5, Punjab Laws Act 1872). Its content was worked out by the courts to mean the rules of English law (when suitable to Indian conditions) — the bridge between ancient India's conscience-gap and modern India's "justice" (Unit-4's Art. 39-A and 142).

5. "Clear proof of usage outweighs the written text"

Collector of Madura v. Moottoo Ramalinga (12 MIA 397, 436): the foundation of Indian custom law — a custom established by clear proof overrides the text of the Hindu law; read with Neelkisto Deb (custom supersedes the general law, which governs outside it) and Mohd. Ismail (the same for Muslim law), it is the whole of the British-period doctrine in three cases.

6. No presumption of custom

Abdul Hussein v. Sona Dero (45 IA 10): s. 5 of the Punjab Laws Act 1872 raises no presumption that parties are governed by custom rather than personal law; the custom must be established by the ordinary processes of evidence. Reiterated: Vaishno Ditti, Salig Ram v. Munshi Ram.

7. The censorial power of courts

Customs contrary to justice, equity and good conscience, or to morals, or to statutory law, are void: Mathura Naikin (adoption by dancing girls — immoral; "abhorrent to decency or morality however long practised… cannot be enforced"); Gopi v. Jaggo (desertion-dissolving custom valid); Wolstanton (unreasonable); Shibnarain Mookerjee (uncertain). The power exists but is used sparingly: the courts do not substitute their morality for the community's.

8. Wajib-ul-arz and riwaz-i-am

Village administration papers (Reg. VII of 1822) recording the customs of each village in the North-Western Provinces and Punjab; the riwaz-i-am ("record of general custom") compiled with the help of village headmen and priests. Balgobind v. Badri Prasad: "most valuable evidence of custom… much more reliable than oral evidence given after the event"; Uman Parshad v. Gandharp Singh: often untrue and to be disregarded. With Rattigan's Digest and Panna Lal's Kumaon book — the evidentiary infrastructure of custom.

9. Antiquity in Indian custom law

No 1189 rule: Mt. Subhani v. Nawab; the test is long, commonly-submitted-to usage. Practical minimums: 20–50 years by the High Courts; and customs existing in 1880 (the Punjab record) are "ancient" — the historicisation fixed by the Punjab settlement.

10. Family customs

Shiba Prasad Singh v. Prayag Kumari Devi: a family cannot by agreement make a custom for itself, but a family actually governed by a customary rule has that rule recognised as law by the State; Serumah v. Palathan, Abraham v. Abraham: converted Christian families and their customs — the Bombay/Madras view untenable; custom may be destroyed by disuse.

11. Custom after the Shariat Act 1937

The Act (ss. 2–3) applies Muslim personal law to Muslim parties and excludes custom from the definition; its effect is to abrogate customary Muslim law (e.g. conversions, agricultural inheritance in some areas) subject to express savings (agricultural land; certain trading usages; Md. Ismail-type family options in some provisions [grey]). The Cutchi Memons Act 1920 is the Bombay exception that the Act superseded.

12. Hindu Acts 1955–56: overriding effect

HMA s.4; HSA ss.3–4; HAMA s.4: any text, rule, interpretation, custom or usage inconsistent with the Acts "shall cease to have effect" — while s. 3 definitions preserve custom within the Acts (e.g. "custom" and "usage" in HMA; the "customary" modes of marriage and succession in HSA). The great inversion: from "clear proof of usage outweighs the written text" to "the written statute outweighs all usage save as expressly saved."

13. The "law of the case" (laws of the case doctrine)

An issue decided on appeal becomes res judicata in the same case between the same parties even in the appellate court (s. 11 CPC & Punjab v. Nathu Ram [grey]); it is also used loosely for the doctrine that a court should not in a later stage of the same case reconsider a point already decided — distinct from stare decisis, which binds persons who were never parties.

14. Distinguishing

The device: where the material facts differ, the precedent in point may be "distinguished" and the earlier case becomes only persuasive. The Indian custom cases (Jain's account) were distinguished endlessly; “a single distinguishing feature” is enough if material; but a distinction on irrelevant facts is dishonest (a "cloud": the relevance of the difference is the question).

15. Overruling and its grounds

By a higher court; usually for error (violation of a constitutional requirement or "settled opinion"), or where the ratio is too wide/too narrow, or principles have changed (the Re Polemis–Wagon Mound sequence), or an Act has overruled the case (Davie — the 1969 Act). Factors: expectation of long-standing decisions, reliance, public policy, erroneous reasoning below, and the "ultimate question" whether the precedent is too remote. Effect: the case ceases to be authoritative even though the past cannot be undone; [grey: Indian prophylactic — prospective overruling (Golak Nath)].

16. Material facts and the "sine qua non"

Cardozo: "the reason which is the sine qua non of the decision" is the ratio — a formulation that requires the judge to ask which facts were necessary: Rylands (only facts i and v material — hence the wide "mischievous substance" rule); Wagon Mound (No. 1 v. No. 2 — same happening, opposite results); Wilson & Clyde (the party-at-fault's identity as servant/contractor); Donoghue (four statements of the same case). Nothing is ratio that does not support the ultimate order.

17. "Legs of a chair, not the links of a chain"

Wisdom (quoted by Dias): "It is not the case that every decision has one authoritative ratio that awaits discovery; rather the law is a structure built on several interrelated rules; to attach every conclusion to one sequence would make the law a chain, whereas it must stand on legs — like a chair." Used for: the modern development of the law rests on analogy, induction and coherence, not single-rule deduction.

18. Legislation as the modern source

Jain: "the future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation"; Dias: statutes as the second place the lawyer must find the law; the subordination of custom and precedent to statute; the Art. 13 frame [grey, Unit-4]. Ancient India's contrast: the king had no legislative power — so legislation is precisely the modern, distinctive source; the constitution (itself legislation in the supreme sense) is the dharma of the modern state.

DHARMA (ancient) · eternal & the root — śruti → smṛti → sadāchāra → conscience; the king discovers & enforces, never makes; no legislation↓

THE RING — legislation itself is today's dharma: the Constitution binds every organ (rājadharma → fundamental rights; Mīmāṃsā → statutory interpretation; courts as guardians → Unit-4)

Read it three ways. (i) Authority: legislation > precedent > custom > dharma-text (per the 1955–56 overrides) — yet dharma endures as the moral root of the Constitution (Units 1–2). (ii) History: dharma → custom → custom-as-case-law → legislation. (iii) Function: dharma supplied legitimacy, custom supplied content, precedent supplied machinery, legislation supplies speed.

09.6 Last word

Unit-5 is a tale of four sources and one invisibility. Ancient India put dharma at the root and denied the king a legislative power — law was found, in texts, in usage, in conscience. Custom then became the great working source: the smṛtis' own commentators conceded it ("custom decides everything"), the British made it the rule of decision, and the cases — Moottoo Ramalinga to Munnalal — turned it into a case-law of its own. That case-law made precedent inevitable; and precedent, whose doctrine Dias unfolds with the ratio as a "pointer within a broad spectrum of variations," supplied the machinery. Then came legislation — the one source the ancients withheld — and it subordinated all three: "the future legal growth in India will be mostly due to legislation, and to some extent judicial interpretation and precedent." But the wheel does not simply turn: the Constitution is the modern dharma, binding every organ; the courts read statutes with Mīmāṃsā; and custom still lives in the savings, the tribal belts and the village. A Unit-5 answer that shows all four sources moving — and the ancient map inside the modern machinery — is a Unit-5 answer.
