Menon's Unit-3 definition is the frame for the whole of Unit 4: a legal system is "part of its social system", and it has four components — (1) principles and values (the Constitution), (2) operational norms (statutes), (3) institutions of enforcement, and (4) a cadre of personnel administering it. 01.1 The two movements inside the unit

Movement 1 — Top-down: promise → structure

The Preamble's "Justice, social, economic and political" is the end; the hierarchy of courts with its jurisdictions is the machinery. Jha's diagrams therefore do two things at once: they describe where each forum sits, and they show how every dispute — from a consumer complaint to a GST matter to an election dispute — is carried by an appeal path that ends at the Supreme Court of India, which itself is created by the Constitution (Arts. 124 ff.) and whose writ jurisdiction (Arts. 32, 226) is the enforcement arm of the Preamble's Liberty and Equality.

Movement 2 — Bottom-up: access → promise

Swaroop inverts the perspective: a formal pyramid is worthless to a litigant who "cannot unlock the golden key." The legal-aid movement (and its strategic arm — legal literacy, legal-aid camps, PIL, Lok Adalats) exists to carry the promise of the Preamble down to the 70% illiterate rural poor. Hence his key sentence: "Legal aid is not a charity or a chance… it is constitutional mandate to the State and right of public."

02(a) Preamble of the Indian Constitution

02.1 Why the syllabus asks it here — the casebook's own lead

The Preamble enters Unit 4 through Swaroop, whose entire argument runs: legal aid is not charity but a constitutional mandate, and the mandate begins in the Preamble. His exact words (pp. 116–117):

"The Founding Fathers of the Constitution of India have right from the Preamble, taken a positive approach of doctrine of philosophy of Equal Justice which becomes apparent on the plain perusal of the preamble of the Constitution. The preamble promise is further strengthened by the constitutional provisions in Articles 14, 19, 21, 22(1), 32, 39-A, 51-A and 226 of the Constitution of India."— R. Swaroop

And later (p. 122): the reforms must "translate Constitutional mandate and obligation propounded right from Preamble Promise in their fighting faith by its Founding Fathers, a reality." The Unit-3 reading by Menon supplies the Preamble's goal-text; the two together are the casebook's entire treatment of sub-topic (a).

02.2 The Preamble — text, words, ideas (as quoted/relied on by the readings)

"WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its citizens:

JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;

IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION."

The four ideals (Menon's phrasing — Unit-3 reading)

  • Justice — "social, economic and political": the three-fold justice that the whole legal system exists to deliver; "social justice is the signature tune of our Constitution" (Swaroop, p. 123).
  • Liberty — "of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship": the cluster of Arts. 19 and 25, and the Art. 21 "procedure established by law" which Maneka Gandhi turned into a "reasonable, fair and just" standard.
  • Equality — "of status and of opportunity": Art. 14 (equality before law and equal protection) and Art. 39-A's "equal opportunity" to "secure justice."
  • Fraternity — "assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of Nation": the dignity limb that generates the obligation to reach justice to the "low visibility area of humanity."

The two operations the casebook performs on it

  1. It is the value-source of equal justice. "Anyone who is priced out of the judicial system… loses faith in democracy and rule of law" — so the Preamble's promise is not rhetoric; it is the premise from which Art. 14, Art. 21 and Art. 39-A obligations are read.
  2. It is a standard of review of the system. Swaroop's test for the whole justice system is the Preamble promise: "The litigant… is the heart of judicial anatomy… he is the consumer of justice, and he should be respected" — and the 0.2% of GDP spent on law and justice is called "grossly inadequate… in a democratic set up" precisely because it fails the promise.

02.4 Article 39-A — the Preamble's operational commandment

"39-A. Equal justice and free legal aid. — The State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice, on the basis of equal opportunity and shall, in particular, provide free legal aid, by suitable legislation or schemes or in any other way, to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities." [Enforced w.e.f. 3.1.1977]

  • What the reading stresses: (i) inserted by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976; (ii) it is a Directive Principle, but "also emphasises that free legal service is an inalienable element of 'reasonable, fair and just' procedure" — hence judicially enforceable through Art. 21; (iii) its triple function: equal opportunity in the operation of the legal system; free legal aid by legislation or schemes; removal of bar of "economic or other disabilities."
  • The reading's blunt summary: "Legal aid is not a charity or a chance, but… constitutional mandate to the State and right of public, which is not now an opinion, but a constitutional obligation and compulsion. As such it is not a pledge or a plan of a government, but has assumed, the status of people's movement."
  • Its statutory offspring (p. 117 and p. 126): s. 304 CrPC (court-assigned counsel), the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Act 39 of 1987 — authorities at national, State and district levels; statutory status to Lok Adalats; in force only from 9-11-1995, "almost eight years after its enactment").

02.6 Six exam-ready points on the Preamble

  1. It is the promise, not a grant. The Preamble "secures to all its citizens" — the subject is the citizen, the object is the promise; hence legal aid is a citizen's right, not State largesse ("social entitlement of the people… not charity or bounty" — Centre of Legal Research).
  2. Its words are the vocabulary of the unit. Every later section uses them: "equal justice" (39-A), "equal opportunity" (39-A and Art. 16), "dignity of the individual" (Arts. 21, 23), "unity and integrity" (writ jurisdiction and the single integrated judiciary), "social justice" (PIL and Lok Adalat).
  3. It is the standard by which the reading condemns the status quo: 0.2% of GDP on law and justice; ~9 judges per million (115+ in the USA); 8–12 years' average life of a civil suit; 30 million pending cases; 91% of suits going to trial (as against 90%+ settling pre-trial in the USA).
  4. Its promise is enforced, not merely declared — through Art. 32/226 (writs), through statutory schemes, and through the court's own directions (e.g., the Khatri direction to magistrates and Sessions Judges to inform every unrepresented accused of his right).
  5. It grounds two doctrines used across the casebook: the "Rule of Law" (Menon: "The Rule of Law is supreme") and "equal justice in the administration of law" (Swaroop: "we should inject equal justice into legality").
  6. Do not overclaim: DPSPs (Art. 39-A included) are not directly enforceable; the casebook's method — and yours — is to show the reading converting the directive into a right through Art. 21. State exactly that chain; it is what the examiner wants.

03.6 The design principles the diagrams teach

1. Convergence on the Supreme Court

Every one of the twenty pathways (8 regulators + 12 tribunal systems) terminates at the Supreme Court, either by statute, by Art. 136 (SLP — service, income-tax), or by Art. 32/226 writ route. The hierarchy is thus a single integrated pyramid — the same design the Unit-3 reading found in ancient India (Kula → Śreṇī → Gaṇa → Adhikṛta → Nripa/Shaasita).

2. Tribunalisation — specialisation without fragmentation

Each subject (consumer, tax, telecom, competition, insolvency, railways, rent…) has its own expert first-instance/adjudicatory body, and the appellate tribunals themselves are specialised (NCLAT, APTEL, TDSAT, SAT, ITAT, DRAT). But because the final say is always with the constitutional courts, the system keeps a uniform law — the check the Unit-3 material called "the Rule of Law is supreme."

3. Writs as the supervisory spine

Art. 32 (SC) and Arts. 226/227 (HC) run vertically through every tribunal system — that is why the casebook's service-matters diagram expressly lists Art. 136, Art. 32, and Arts. 226/227 as the three routes from CAT to the constitutional courts. Legal remedies are never exhausted by the statutory ladder.

4. Jurisdiction vocabulary is the marking scheme

Original (first instance), Appellate (error-correcting), Advisory (reference — SC only), Writ, Supervisory (227), Revisional, Review (Art. 137), Curative, SLP (Art. 136). A 15-mark answer that simply lists these with one example each, and ends with the pyramid, is complete. Add: "jurisdiction = the authority of a court to hear and determine a matter, fixed by the Constitution and statutes (territorially, pecuniarily, and by subject-matter and hierarchy)."

04(c) Legal Services — Legal Aid & Lok Adalat

04.1 The need — Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar, AIR 1979 SC 1369 (1375)

The reading opens with the judgment that launched the modern legal-aid movement in India. One writ petition under Art. 32 (habeas corpus, by advocate Pushpa Kapila Hingorani, on the basis of a 1977 report on undertrials in Bihar jails) exposed the condition of undertrial prisoners — one of them, Hussainara Khatoon, in jail for four-five years though the maximum term for the offence was far less. Bhagwati J. observed that without a nationwide legal service programme the benefits of the legal process could not reach the poor, and (the passage to memorise):

"Today, unfortunately, in our country the poor are priced out of the judicial system with the result that they are losing faith in the capacity of our legal system to bring about changes in their life conditions and to deliver justice to them. The poor in their contact with the legal system have always been on the wrong side of the line. They have always come across 'Law for the poor' rather than 'Law of the poor'. The law is regarded by them as something mysterious and forbidding — always taking something away from them and not as a positive and constructive social device for changing the social economic order and improving their life conditions by conferring rights and benefits on them. The result is that the legal system has lost its credibility for the weaker sections of the community. It is, therefore, necessary that we should inject equal justice into legality and that can be done only by the dynamic and activist scheme of legal services.
… it is high time that a comprehensive legal service programme is introduced in the country. That is not only a mandate of equal justice implicit in Article 14 and right to life and liberty conferred by Article 21, but also the compulsion of the Constitutional Directive embodied in Article 39-A."— Bhagwati J.

Three propositions to lift: (1) the legal system has lost credibility for the poor — "Law for the poor, not Law of the poor"; (2) the cure is to inject equal justice into legality; (3) legal service is grounded in Art. 14 + Art. 21 + Art. 39-A — the exact constitutional chain of §02.

04.2 The constitutional mandate — "a constitutional obligation and compulsion"

(i) The State cannot plead poverty

"The State cannot, therefore, avoid its constitutional obligation to provide free legal aid to the accused by pleading financial or administrative inability." It is also "the constitutional obligation of the Court, as the guardian of the fundamental rights of the people, as a sentinel on the qui vive, to enforce the fundamental right of the accused to fair trial and his right to free legal aid."
American authority quoted by the reading (Khatri v. State of Bihar, AIR 1981 SC 928, 930): Rhem v. Malcolm (377 F. Supp. 995) — "the law does not permit any Government to deprive its citizens of constitutional rights on a plan of poverty"; Jackson v. Bishop — "human considerations and constitutional requirements are not in this day to be measured by dollar considerations."

(ii) Art. 22(1) — counsel of choice

"No person, who is arrested, shall be detained in custody, without being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds for such arrest, nor shall he be denied the right to consult and to be defended by a legal practitioner of his choice." Two consequences the reading draws: the right exists at the consultation stage, and no counsel can be thrust upon the accused without ascertaining his wishes. Also noted: long before the Constitution, s. 340(1) of the old CrPC gave an accused a right to be defended by a pleader — now s. 303 of the 1973 Code, "with the addition of the words 'of his choice' at the end."

(iii) Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, AIR 1978 SC 597 — the pivot case

When Art. 21 says no deprivation "except according to procedure established by law," it is not enough that there be "some resemblance of procedure"; the procedure must be "reasonable, fair and just." "Now, a procedure which does not make legal services available to an accused person who is too poor to afford a lawyer and who would, therefore, have to go through the trial without legal assistance, cannot possibly be regarded as 'reasonable, fair and just.' It is an essential ingredient of reasonable, fair and just procedure to a prisoner who is to seek his liberation through the court's process that he should have legal services available to him."

(iv) Why India needs it — the reading's socio-legal statistics

India is a "developing democratic republic" with the Welfare-State doctrine adopted; less than 33% of the people can read and write sufficiently and usefully; the majority are poor or indigent, "most of them below the poverty line, even though the poverty line is drawn liberally." Hence "equal access to justice" is "a constitutional commandment and statutory imperative" — and the formal statistics of §04.9 show why the system failed on its own terms.

04.3 The case law that built the right — three judgments in sequence

Hussainara (1979): free legal aid implicit in Art. 21; State must act→
Khatri v. State of Bihar, AIR 1981 SC 928: the obligation extends to the first production before the Magistrate; the Magistrate/Sessions Judge must inform the accused of the right→
Suk Das v. UT of Arunachal Pradesh, AIR 1986 SC 991: the right does not depend on the accused applying — failure to provide legal assistance vitiates the trial; subject to an exception for economic offences etc.

04.4 Khatri v. State of Bihar (AIR 1981 SC 928) — the "blinded prisoners" case: what exactly was held

  • Stage: the obligation extends "not only at the stage of trial but also at the stage when he is first produced before the Magistrate."
  • The informing duty: "Even this right to free legal services would be illusory for an indigent accused unless the magistrate or the Sessions Judge before whom he is produced informs him of such right. It is the common knowledge that about 70 per cent of the people in the rural areas are illiterate… It would make a mockery of legal aid if it were to be left to a poor, ignorant and illiterate accused to ask for free legal services. Legal aid would become merely a paper promise…" In Khatri itself the judicial Magistrate "failed to discharge this obligation in the case of the blinded prisoners, and they merely stated that no legal representation was asked for by the blinded prisoners and hence none was provided."
  • The direction: "We would, therefore, direct the magistrates and Sessions judges in the country to inform every accused who appears before them and who is not represented by a lawyer on account of his poverty or indigence that he is entitled to free legal services at the cost of the State. Unless he is not willing to take advantage of the free legal services provided by the State, he must be provided legal representation at the cost of the State."
  • The State's duty: every State must "make provision for grant of free legal services to an accused who is unable to engage a lawyer on account of reasons such as poverty, indigence or incommunicado situation. The only qualification would be that the offence charged against the accused is such that, on conviction, it would result in a sentence of imprisonment and is of such a nature that the circumstances of the case and the needs of social justice require that he should be given free legal representation."

04.5 Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh (AIR 1986 SC 991) — no application needed

  • Question: "is the exercise of this fundamental right conditional upon the accused applying for free legal assistance so that if he does not make an application for free legal assistance the trial may lawfully proceed without adequate legal representation being afforded to him?"
  • The reading's answer — the celebrated passage: "It is common knowledge that about 70% of the people living in rural areas are illiterate… It is this absence of legal awareness which is responsible for the deception, exploitation and deprivation of rights and benefits from which the poor suffer… Their legal needs always stand to become crisis oriented because their ignorance prevents them from anticipating legal troubles and approaching a lawyer for consultation and advice in time… The law ceases to be their protector because they do not know that they are entitled to the protection of law… poverty becomes with them a condition of total helplessness… It would in these circumstances make a mockery of legal aid if it were to be left to a poor, ignorant and illiterate accused to ask for free legal service."
  • Held: the right is not conditional on application; the trial of an unrepresented indigent accused without legal aid is vitiated. (In Suk Das itself the conviction for criminal intimidation, s. 506/34 IPC, against an appellant who was "unable to afford legal representation due to poverty," was set aside — the Court recognising that the accused could have been retried after free legal assistance was provided.)
  • The exception: "there may be cases involving offences, such as, economic offences or offences against law prohibiting prostitution or child abuse and the like, where social justice may require that legal service may not be provided by the State."

Also cited: State of Kerala v. Kuttan, 1988 Cri LJ 453 (referring to the same line of cases); Raj Kishore v. State, AIR 1969 Cal 321 (1969 Cri LJ 860) — the assigned counsel must be competent, must be given the complete brief and time to prepare; failure "vitiates the trial"; and no counsel may be thrust on the accused (Art. 22(1)).

04.6 Statute and reports — from persuasion to obligation

Law Commission of India

  1. 14th Report (1958), Vol. I, "Reform of Judicial Administration": the first recommendation of State legal aid and assignment of counsel at government expense — "Unless some provision is made for assisting the poor man for the payment of court-fees and lawyer's fees and other incidental costs of litigation, he/she is denied equality in the opportunity to seek justice." (p. 487)
  2. 41st Report (1969): representation by a lawyer should be available at government expense in all cases tried by a Court of Sessions (Vol. 1, paras 24, 34–38).
  3. 48th Report: free legal assistance by the State for all undefended accused for want of means.

The Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973

S. 303 (successor to old s. 340(1)): "Any person accused of an offence before a Criminal Court… may of right, be defended by a pleader" — with the words "of his choice" (per the reading) — a right, but it did not oblige the court to assign a pleader at State expense (many High Courts nevertheless issued circulars and rules for assignment in Sessions trials).
S. 304(1) (the 41st Report "codified," with the Joint Committee's qualification that State aid is available only where the accused "has not sufficient means to engage a pleader"): "Where, in a trial before the Court of Session, the accused is not represented by a pleader, and where it appears to the Court that the accused has not sufficient means to engage a pleader, the Court shall assign a pleader for his defence at the expense of the State." Sub-s. (2): High Court rules (selection of pleaders; facilities; fees). Sub-s. (3): State may extend the provision to "any class of trials before other Courts."

Connect: the reading's sequence is 1958 (report) → 1969 (report) → 1973 (s. 304) → 1976/77 (Art. 39-A) → 1980 (CILAS) → 1987/1995 (LSA Act) → present (NALSA). An answer on "the constitutional mandate of legal aid" should walk this ladder.

04.7 The institutional history — CILAS and the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987

SEPT. 1980 — CILAS (Committee for Implementing Legal Aid Schemes): resolution of 26-9-1980; P.N. Bhagwati J. (as he then was) Chairman; monitor and implement legal-aid programmes "on a uniform basis in all the States and union territories"; evolved a model scheme (incl. organisation of legal-aid camps) and set up legal aid and advice boards→
Review found deficiencies → felt need for statutory authorities at national, State and district levels → Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Act 39 of 1987) enacted; the CILAS term was extended (e.g., from 14-5-1990 for one year or till NALSA was constituted)→
BROUGHT INTO FORCE w.e.f. 9-11-1995 — "almost eight years after its enactment" — NALSA at the national level, State Authorities, District Authorities (and Taluka/Sub-Divisional Committees) under the Act
  • Earlier, "many states evolved their own programmes and even enacted State legislation or promulgated certain schemes or Rules"; State Boards, District Legal Aid Committees and Taluka Committees were constituted — but a central legislation was felt necessary to co-ordinate and promote on a uniform basis.
  • The Act performs two functions the essay highlights: it creates the three-tier authorities, and it gives statutory status to Lok Adalats and their awards (see 04.9).

04.8 Strategic legal aid — the second, "frontally attacking poverty" programme

From Centre of Legal Research v. State of Kerala, AIR 1986 SC 2195, quoting Bhagwati J.: "the legal aid programme which is needed for the purpose of reaching social justice to the people cannot afford to remain confined to the traditional or litigation-oriented legal aid programme but it must… adopt a more dynamic posture and take within its sweep what we may call strategic legal aid programme consisting of promotion of legal literacy, organization of legal aid camps, encouragement of public interest litigation and holding of Lok Adalats or Niti melas for bringing about settlements of disputes whether pending in Courts or outside."

What "strategic" means — the long definition

"What is necessary is to supplement the traditional legal service programme with strategic legal service programme. The strategic legal service programme aims at prevention and elimination of various kinds of injustices which the poor as a class suffer because of poverty and endeavours to launch a frontal attack on the poverty itself… It does not involve merely quantitative extension of traditional legal services… It involves novel, radical, more dynamic and multi-dimensional uses of law… seeks to provide representation to groups of social and economic protest. It does not regard litigation as playing an important or even significant role in the life of the poor… it is concerned with the problems of the poor as a class rather than with the individual problems of the poor… directed towards group-oriented approach to the problem of poverty rather than individual-oriented treatment, and basically it is calculated to make the poor as class-conscious and powerful, self-reliant and capable of using law as a potent weapon."

Legal literacy — the first item

"Legal literacy is a pre-condition to maintain the 'rule of law'" (Bhagwati). Knowledge of rights gives the poor "strength and confidence to fight" and avoids needless difficulties from ignorance. The CILAS model scheme included "programme for promotion of legal literacy and spread of legal awareness among the weaker sections… by way of organizing legal aid camps, especially in rural areas, slums or labour colonies." Voluntary organizations, social action groups, journalists and advocates have rendered significant service — and "the assistance of voluntary agencies and social action groups must therefore be taken by the State… an obligation which flows directly from Article 39-A."

The five problems (the reading's list): to manage the unmanageable; to break the unbreakable; to beat the unbeatable; to hit the unhittable; to defend the indefensible — hence "we are obliged to create and constitute a Neo-Jurisprudence, a public-oriented… progressive, professional and pervasive programme," in which "the litigant, who is the heart of judicial anatomy," is treated as "the consumer of justice, and he should be respected."

04.9 Public Interest Litigation — the strategic arm of the movement

Definition (as the reading states it)

"Public Interest Litigation has been devised as a tool to secure benefit to a class or group of persons, either victims of exploitation, or oppression or who are denied the constitutional rights but cannot come to court personally for relief by reason of ignorance, poverty, destitution, helplessness, disability or social or economic disadvantage. It is a form of litigation where jurisdiction of the Court is invoked on behalf of such persons… by a third person or a social action group… regardless of its personal injury. Cases of this kind involve the rights of thousands of people at a time, unlike traditional litigation, which are basically of adversary character…" It is "essentially a co-operative or collaborative effort on the part of the petitioner, the State or public authority and the court to secure observance of the constitutional or legal rights, benefits and privileges conferred upon the vulnerable sections of the community."

The four loci standi / procedure cases

  1. S.P. Gupta v. President of India, AIR 1982 SC 149 (Judges' Appointment and Transfer case) — the locus standi watershed (quoted at p. 188): "where a legal wrong or legal injury is caused to a person or to a determinate class of persons… and such person or determinate class of persons is by reason of poverty, helplessness, or disability or socially or economically disadvantaged position, unable to approach the Court for relief, any member of the public can maintain an application for an appropriate direction, order or writ in the High Court under Article 226 and… in this Court under Article 32." The reading adds: "It is only the moneyed who have so far had the golden key to unlock the doors of justice. But, now for the first time the portals of the court are being thrown open to the poor and the downtrodden…"
  2. People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, AIR 1982 SC 1473 — the Rule of Law argument: "The Rule of Law does not mean that the protection of the law must be available only to a fortunate few… The poor too have civil and political rights, and the Rule of Law meant for them also, though today it exists only on paper and not in reality."
  3. M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, AIR 1987 SC 1086 — "the law should keep pace with changing socio-economic norms; where a law of the past does not fit in the present context, the Court should evolve new law in a public interest litigation"; the Court has "incidental and ancillary powers" to devise new methods and strategies for enforcing fundamental rights in PIL/social action cases (compensation for victims of hazardous activities).
  4. Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, AIR 1984 SC 802 (815) — the procedure answer (see next box).

Bandhua Mukti Morcha — "abandon the laissez-faire approach"

"When the poor come before the Court, particularly for enforcement of their fundamental rights, it is necessary to depart from the adversarial procedure and to evolve a new procedure… The problems of the poor… are qualitatively different… they need a different kind of lawyering skill and different kind of judicial approach. If we blindly follow the adversarial procedure in their case, they would never be able to enforce their fundamental rights, and the result would be nothing but a mockery of the Constitution. We therefore [must] abandon the laissez-faire [let things be] approach in the judicial process… and forge new tools, devise new methods and adopt new strategies… clearly made permissible by the language of clause (2) of Article 32 because the Constitution-makers while enacting that clause have deliberately and advisedly not used any words restricting the power of the Court to adopt any procedure which it considers appropriate… if we want the fundamental rights to become a living reality and the Supreme Court to become a real sentinel on the qui vive, we must free ourselves from the shackles of outdated and outmoded assumptions…"

The reading's conclusion on PIL: "the entire theatre of the law is changing… social justice is the signature tune of our Constitution… the Courts must become the courts for the poor and struggling masses of the country… they must shed their character as upholders of established order and the status quo."

04.10 The Lok Adalat — "the jurisprudence of peace"

Nature and working (as the reading describes it)

  • The institution: an "alternative dispute settlement mechanism, evolved as a part of the CILAS programme with the object of taking justice to the doorsteps of the poor" — Lok Adalats (People's Courts) and Niti melas, organised under the supervision of State Legal Aid and Advice Boards.
  • Philosophy: Lok Adalats "do not treat the issues before it as disputes under contest and decide cases but treat them as differences and resolve them by conciliatory and persuasive efforts… a species of peace making"; parties are told the pros and cons — conciliation and compromise versus "the traditional dilatory procedure of adversarial litigation." "Participatory justice" is the unique feature; settlement is by "mutual and free consent"; "there is no winner and no loser… the intention being to help warring parties to work things out, shake hands, and become friends."
  • Process: date and place fixed about a month ahead; publicity through press; presiding officers identify conciliable pending cases; cases classified and substance recorded; pre-Lok Adalat conferences; parties motivated by "legal aid teams, which included, law students, social workers and volunteers"; at the campsite the mediation continues; settlement reduced to writing, signed by parties and countersigned by the panel, passed on to the Court concerned for final decree or order — the Court verifying that all parties consented and that "the fairness and legality of the settlement" hold.
  • Panels: two or three persons — "one of them could be a retired judge or a senior retired civil servant or an advocate, a law teacher, and others, social workers and eminent persons of the locality," chosen for record of public service, honesty and respectability.

Why it matters — the reading's statistics

  • "roughly 91 percent of cases instituted in the Courts go for trial and only 9 percent of cases are settled without judicial agitation, in U.S.A. more than 90 percent of cases… are settled before they go for trial."
  • "34 percent of the total world's poor populace is in India"; rural poor in more than 5,18,000 villages are "either illiterate, indigent or ignorant of their rights."
  • Maladies of the traditional system: "huge and heavy expenses, unexpected and unpredictable delay in disposal and, cumbersome and complex process of Court" — the "life span of a civil case… ranging average, between 8 to 12 years," plus a second round at the execution stage.
  • Pending workload "has rapidly crossed the 30 million" (as at the reading's date); "The ratio of Judges per million in India is almost 9, whereas it is more than 115 in U.S.A."; only "about 4,50,000 and odd number of Advocates in India" (cf. Menon's Unit-3 figure of ~2,30,000 advocates — different vintages; the casebook is internally inconsistent, state the figure you cite with its source).
  • Expenditure on law and justice is "reported 0.2 percent of the Gross Domestic Product… grossly inadequate and insufficient in a democratic set up" — hence "Judicial Cardiogram" for "urgent and useful, effective and ebullient reforms."

Legal status — from voluntary agency to statutory forum

Lok Adalats were "functioning as a voluntary agency without any statutory backing for its decisions"; they resolved "compoundable criminal complaints, civil and revenue disputes, MACT cases, and even institutional cases." Growing popularity → demand for statutory backing: the Government, exercising its duty under Art. 39-A, drafted the Legal Services Authorities Bill, 1987 → Act No. 39 of 1987 "acknowledging the institution of Lok Adalat and giving statutory status to Lok Adalats" — brought into force w.e.f. 9-11-1995.

04.11 Public participation — and the reading's closing note

Centre of Legal Research v. State of Kerala, AIR 1986 SC 2195

"The State Government undoubtedly has an obligation under Article 39A… to set up a comprehensive and effective legal aid programme… But we have no doubt that… it is absolutely essential that people should be involved in the legal aid programme because the legal aid programme is not charity or bounty, but it is a social entitlement of the people and those in need of legal assistance cannot be looked upon as mere beneficiaries… but they should be regarded as participants in it." And: "the best way of securing people's participation… is to operate through voluntary organizations and social action groups… they have their finger on the pulse of the people… they know… what are the unmet legal needs of the people, what are the sources of exploitation and injustice…" — and therefore "voluntary organizations and social action groups must be encouraged and supported by the State in operating the legal aid programme."

Bench & Bar — and the verdict

In the USA and other Western countries "the contribution of the Bar in rendering free and competent legal aid is praiseworthy and it must be emulated." The legal-aid fraternity "must respond with juristic sensitivity to the voice from the silence zone (a class of litigants) and mass voice of weak, meek, poor, suppressed and exploited women and destitute children." The Bar is "really, a backbone of the legal services to compliment and complete the Constitutional obligation"; NALSA has undertaken important programmes; "members of Bench and Bar, N.G.Os. and Government Agencies must render voluntary helping hand" [Dineshbhai Dhemenrai v. State of Gujarat, 2001 (1) GLR 603].

The reading's own verdict on the system — the sentence to close any answer: "What is the use of the system, which does not help lowly and lost, poor and downtrodden and which creates distance between law and justice."

05(d) Legal Profession & the Advocates Act, 1961

05.1 The opening claim

"Law, as a profession, appears to have been in vogue in ancient Indian thought. Its concept was quite different from that it is today pursuant to the Regulating Act of 1773…"— A.N. Veera Raghavan (1972)

Two things to note: (1) this is the cross-unit hook — India's ancient legal professionals (the Unit-3 material: royal judges, sabhyas, the parishad's experts, the judges' qualifications in the Arthashastra tradition) formed a different conception of the profession; (2) the modern profession is a creation of the colonial state — the Regulating Act, 1773 → Charter of 1774 → post-independence unification by the Advocates Act, 1961.

05.3 The three main features of the 1961 Act (as the reading states them)

  1. One class of legal practitioners. "to have, in course of time only one class of legal practitioners viz. advocates" — ending the barrister/vakil/pleader/mukhtar/attorney multiplicity.
  2. Transfer of powers from courts to Bar Councils. "to take away the powers till then vested in the Courts, in the matter of admission of advocates and the maintenance of the rolls, and their disciplinary conduct (subject to an ultimate appeal to the Supreme Court)".
  3. A central Bar Council with powers, inter alia, "to recognise the degree in law for admission as advocates." Every Bar Council is a body corporate with a common seal, able to sue and be sued.

Connect to §04: it is the same Act whose professional-conduct rules make "legal services to the poor… one of the social obligations of every lawyer."

05.4 State Bar Councils — composition and functions

Composition

  • Advocate-General of the State as ex-officio member (Delhi: no Advocate-General for a Union Territory — the Additional Solicitor-General, and later the Solicitor-General of India, was made the ex-officio member).
  • Elected members: 15 for Assam & Nagaland, Orissa, Delhi and Himachal Pradesh; 20 for all other State Bar Councils — elected by proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote from amongst advocates on the State roll.
  • Term (as amended): four years from publication of election result (originally six years with rotation). Each Council has a Chairman and Vice-Chairman, a Secretary, and "an accountant, if necessary."

Committees and functions

  • Committees: one or more Disciplinary Committees (2 members elected by the Council + 1 co-opted person who has practised at least 10 years); an Executive Committee (5 members); an Enrolment Committee; and others as necessary.
  • Functions (s. 6, as summarised): admit persons as advocates on its roll and prepare/maintain the roll; entertain and determine cases of misconduct; safeguard the rights, privileges and interests of advocates; promote and support law reform; provide for election of its members; perform other assigned functions.

05.5 Bar Council of India — composition and functions

Composition & committees

  • Attorney-General of India and the Solicitor-General of India as ex-officio members + one member elected by each State Bar Council from amongst its members.
  • Committees: one or more Disciplinary Committees — 3 members: two elected from amongst advocates of at least 10 years' standing, the senior-most being chairman; a Legal Education Committee of 10 (5 members of the Council + 5 non-members); an Executive Committee; others as needed.
  • Income: 40% of the Rs. 250 enrolment fee paid to each State Bar Council is its main source.

Functions (s. 7, as summarised)

  • Prepare and maintain the common roll of advocates.
  • Lay down standards of professional conduct and etiquette for advocates; the procedure for the disciplinary committees of State Bar Councils.
  • Safeguard the rights, privileges and interests of advocates; promote and support law reform.
  • Deal with matters referred by a State Bar Council; exercise general supervision and control over State Bar Councils.
  • Promote legal education and lay down standards "in consultation with the Universities whose degree in law shall be a qualification for enrolment"; visit and inspect Universities; manage and invest funds; provide for election of members.

Special powers of the Bar Council of India (s. 49 and related, as the reading lists them): (1) removal from the rolls — where enrolment was obtained by misrepresentation (after hearing), on death, on request, or as punishment for misconduct; (2) revision — calling for records of any disposal by a State Bar Council from which no appeal lies, to satisfy itself of legality/propriety (no prejudicial order without hearing); (3) directions (s. 48-B) to State Bar Councils or their committees for proper discharge of functions; if a State Bar Council cannot perform its functions, directions to its ex-officio member "shall have effect, notwithstanding anything contained in the rules made by the State Bar Council"; (4) rule-making and approval — State Bar Council rules under ss. 15 and 28 have effect only if approved by the Bar Council of India (contrast: under the 1926 Act the High Court's sanction was needed); s. 49 gives the BCI a general rule-making power (professional conduct and etiquette; principles for guidance of State Bar Councils; enforcement of its directions; "any other matter which may be prescribed").

05.6 Senior advocates (s. 16)

  • Persons who were "senior advocates immediately before the appointed day, i.e. 1-12-1961" are deemed senior advocates.
  • The Supreme Court and the High Courts may designate any advocate as a senior advocate "if in its opinion by virtue of his ability, experience and standing at the Bar, he is deserving of such distinction."
  • Senior advocates are governed by the Supreme Court rules applicable to them and are subject to the restrictions laid down by the Bar Council of India in the interest of the legal profession (they do not draft pleadings, do not appear without a junior, etc.).

05.7 Qualifications for admission (s. 24) — the gateway

The four qualifying categories

  1. A person who has obtained a degree in law from any University in India, or from any University outside India recognised by the Bar Council of India;
  2. A barrister;
  3. A vakil or pleader who is a law graduate, if his application for enrolment was made within two years from the appointed day (1-12-1961);
  4. A person who has for at least three years been a vakil/pleader/mukhtar, or was at any time entitled to be enrolled as an advocate of a High Court or Court of a Judicial Commissioner or in a Union Territory.

Plus: citizen of India; completed 21 years; fee of Rs. 250 to the State Bar Council; other requirements of the State Bar Council rules (university certificate of the law degree; character). A national of any other country may be admitted if a citizen of India duly qualified and permitted to practise law in that country.

Training and examination — s. 24(1)(d), and its erosion

Original rule: a course of training (one year, later reduced to six months — apprenticeship with an advocate of standing, attendance at courts and chambers, maintenance of diaries, attendance at lectures, and passing an examination) prescribed by the State Bar Council. The proviso exempted: (1) holders of pre-31-3-1964 law degrees or barristers called before that date (or later-called with recognised practical training); (2) persons who held a judicial office for 2 years in pre-15-8-1947 India, or were High Court advocates there; (3) persons who discontinued practice to take government/local-authority/other employment; (4) classes declared exempt by the Bar Council of India. The reading's verdict: amendments (1964) and later notifications "narrowed down the scope of the requirement as to training and examination practically making it a nullity" — no training/examination needed for a 3-year-course law degree or Bar call on or before 31-12-1968, or for a master's degree in law.

Conditions for enrolment (s. 28 rules): State Bar Councils, with BCI approval, may prescribe conditions — "generally prohibit the enrolment of a person who… is in full or part time service or employment or is engaged in any trade or profession," with proviso exceptions: law officers (fulfilling conditions), articled clerk or attorney, a person in part-time service as professor or lecturer/teacher in law, a member of a Hindu Joint Family interested in its joint management, and other classes approved by the Bar Council of India. Application is in the prescribed form to the State Bar Council of the jurisdiction where the applicant proposes to practise; refusal by the Enrolment Committee must be referred, with grounds, to the Bar Council of India and disposed of finally in conformity with its opinion.

05.8 Legal education — the Bar Council of India's standards (as of the article)

The framework

  • Eligibility to join law: a university graduate (or equivalent recognised by the BCI); three-year course of instruction (from the first term of 1967-68; for fourteen universities 1968-69).
  • Curriculum: ten compulsory subjects and six optional; in the last year, six months of instruction and practical training in the rules of courts and in drafting of pleadings and documents.

05.10 The Supreme Court's own regulation of the profession

From the Federal Court to the Supreme Court Rules

  • Federal Court: s. 214, Government of India Act, 1935 — rules with the Governor-General's approval for practice and procedure, including persons practising; Federal Court Rules, 1942: qualifications for enrolment; 10 years' standing for enrolment as a senior advocate; no advocate could appear unless instructed by an agent.
  • Art. 145(1) of the Constitution: the Supreme Court makes rules, with the President's approval, inter alia "as to persons practicing before it."
  • Supreme Court Rules, 1950 (under Art. 145(1)): the roll in two parts — seniors and others; more than 10 years' standing for senior advocates.
  • Order IV: Rule 2(b) — restrictions on senior advocates; Rule 3 — robes and costumes as directed; the Chief Justice of India directed black coat, robe and band (as worn by barristers before High Courts); Rule 5 — Advocate-on-Record (AOR): 1 year's training with an approved AOR and tests notified in the Official Gazette; an office in Delhi within ten miles of the Court House; undertaking to employ a registered clerk within a month; registration fee of Rs. 25; attorneys exempt from the training/test.

Aswini Kumar Ghosh v. Arabinda Ghosh — the 1951 Act interpreted

The Supreme Court Advocates (Practice in High Court) Act, 1951 gave every advocate of the Supreme Court "the right to practise in any High Court whether or not he is an advocate of that High Court." In Aswini Kumar Ghosh the Supreme Court held by majority (Patanjali Sastri C.J. and majority) that the Supreme Court advocate became entitled as of right to appear and plead as well as act in all the High Courts in India, including the original sides of the Calcutta and Bombay High Courts, without being instructed by an attorney and free from the restrictions in the High Court rules. (The non-obstante clause of s. 2 overrides conflicting provisions of the Bar Councils Act, 1926 and High Court rules.) Grey gloss: the judgment is AIR 1952 SC 369; the minority (S.R. Das J.) restricted "practise" to plead; the reading follows the majority. After 1-12-1961 (Chapter III of the Advocates Act) there has been no further admission of persons as advocates of the Supreme Court; enrolment is on State rolls, and the 1951 Act lapsed with the working of Chapter IV.

05.11 The author's conclusion — and the unsettled items

  • "The Advocates Act, 1961 has marked the beginning of a new era in the history of the legal profession by vesting largely in the Bar Councils the power and the jurisdiction which the Courts till then exercised, by fulfilling the aspirations of those who had been demanding an all-India Bar and effecting a unification of the Bar in India, power to practise in all the Courts and bound by rules made and code of conduct laid down by their own bodies… The Act has enabled representatives from the several States to come together to a common forum and has brought about integration."
  • Legal education: the conferment of power over legal education, recognition of degrees and inspection of universities has "resulted in a uniform pattern of legal education, while at the same time preserving intact the powers and responsibilities of the universities." Caution: "The problem of language in so far at any rate as regards legal education might present difficulties and would seem to be of utmost magnitude."
  • Autonomy preserved: an attempt to make the Attorney-General of India ex-officio Chairman of the Bar Council of India (and Advocate-Generals chairmen of State Bar Councils) was "stoutly resisted by the Bar Councils, on the ground that it would encroach upon the autonomy given to the Bar Councils" — the Government dropped it.
  • Left over: s. 49-A provisions; agitations to end the dual system (advocates and attorneys — the Calcutta/Bombay original side).

CASE 1 · Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar, AIR 1979 SC 1369 Reading 2

FactsA writ petition under Art. 32 in the nature of habeas corpus (by advocate Pushpa Kapila Hingorani) exposed the plight of undertrial prisoners in Bihar's jails, many of whom had been in prison longer than the maximum term for the offences charged — one of them, Hussainara Khatoon, for four to five years. The material came from a 1977 report on Bihar jails and a newspaper article; the State of Bihar remained absent despite service.
IssueCan persons be kept in jail as undertrials for periods beyond the maximum punishment for the offence charged, without the legal process reaching them — and is free legal aid implicit in the Constitution?
ArgumentsFor the petitioners: detention so long is illegal and violates Art. 21; the poor cannot pay for a lawyer, and magistrates mechanically pass remand orders, so the court must treat the inability to secure counsel as a denial of fair procedure. For the State: (as reported) the State did not effectively contest the factual assertions; the matter was argued to be one of administrative incapacity and scarce resources. (The casebook reports the petitioners' side in detail; the State's response was, in substance, absent.)
DecisionBhagwati J. (with Desai J.): the undertrial prisoners named were ordered released forthwith — continued detention was illegal and violative of Art. 21; bail was granted on personal bonds without sureties. The Court strongly recommended to the Government of India and the State Governments that a comprehensive legal service programme be introduced, holding that this follows from "the mandate of equal justice implicit in Article 14 and right to life and liberty conferred by Article 21, but also the compulsion of the Constitutional Directive embodied in Article 39-A." The State "cannot avoid its constitutional obligation… by pleading financial or administrative inability."
PrincipleLegal aid is a constitutional requirement, not charity. The poor "are priced out of the judicial system"; the answer is to "inject equal justice into legality." Also: speedy trial is part of Art. 21 and the legal process must actually reach those it is meant to serve. The judgment is the foundation of the Indian legal-aid movement and one of the first PIL-based directions of the modern Court.

CASE 2 · Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, AIR 1978 SC 597 Reading 2

FactsThe petitioner's passport was impounded under the Passports Act, 1967 without a statement of reasons; she challenged the procedure adopted.
IssueIs "procedure established by law" under Art. 21 any procedure that a legislature may provide, or must it satisfy a standard of fairness?
ArgumentsPetitioner: impounding without reasons violates natural justice and Art. 21; the procedure authorising it must be fair. Union of India: the Act authorises impounding; a legislative procedure suffices; reasons could be withheld in the public interest.
DecisionBhagwati J. (leading) with the Court: "procedure established by law" means a procedure that is "right and just and fair" — a procedure must be adjudged "reasonable, fair and just" before a person may be deprived of life or personal liberty under it; reasons, or at least a reasoned opportunity, must be given.
PrincipleThe Maneka test: procedural due process under Art. 21. The legal-aid reading applies it directly — "a procedure which does not make legal services available to an accused person who is too poor to afford a lawyer… cannot possibly be regarded as 'reasonable, fair and just.' It is an essential ingredient of reasonable, fair and just procedure… that he should have legal services available to him."

CASE 3 · Khatri v. State of Bihar, AIR 1981 SC 928 Reading 2

FactsThe "blinded prisoners" case: undertrial prisoners in Bhagalpur jail had been blinded by police, allegedly for resistance, and were later produced before magistrates — in many cases without any legal representation, and the magistrates merely recorded that no representation had been asked for.
IssueDoes the State's obligation to provide free legal services extend to the stage of first production before the Magistrate? Can the right be denied because the accused did not ask for it?
ArgumentsPetitioners: the constitutional obligation is continuous and begins with the first production; a blind, illiterate accused cannot be expected to demand counsel — the court must inform him. State of Bihar: no legal representation was asked for; hence none was provided (the very answer the Court condemned).
DecisionBhagwati J.: the obligation extends "not only at the stage of trial but also at the stage when he is first produced before the Magistrate." The Court directed all magistrates and Sessions Judges in the country to inform every unrepresented accused that he is entitled to free legal services at State cost, and directed every State to make provision for free legal services (with the qualification that the offence be punishable by imprisonment and that the needs of social justice so require). Citing Rhem v. Malcolm: "the law does not permit any Government to deprive its citizens of constitutional rights on a plan of poverty"; and Jackson v. Bishop: "human considerations and constitutional requirements are not in this day to be measured by dollar considerations."
PrincipleThe duty to inform — "it would make a mockery of legal aid if it were to be left to a poor, ignorant and illiterate accused to ask for free legal service. Legal aid would become merely a paper promise." Legal aid is an enforceable constitutional obligation of the State and of the court, starting at the first production; the accused's silence is never a waiver. The one legitimate limit (from Suk Das, traceable to this direction): economic offences, prostitution, child abuse etc., where social justice may weigh the other way.

CASE 4 · Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh, AIR 1986 SC 991 Reading 2

FactsThe appellant and five others were charged with criminal intimidation (s. 506 read with s. 34 IPC) for allegedly threatening an assistant engineer. The appellant was indigent and remained unrepresented throughout; he never applied for free legal aid. Four accused were acquitted; the appellant was convicted and sentenced to two years' imprisonment, and was dismissed from service on that basis.
IssueCan the fundamental right to free legal assistance be denied because the accused did not apply for it? Is a trial conducted without such assistance vitiated?
ArgumentsAppellant: the trial was vitiated for denial of free legal aid; he was too poor to engage counsel and too ignorant to ask. Union Territory/State: no application for legal aid was made, so failure to provide it did not vitiate the trial (the view taken by the High Court).
DecisionBhagwati, Madon and Oza JJ. — the appeal was allowed; conviction and sentence set aside; the connected dismissal from service quashed. "It may therefore now be taken as settled law that free legal assistance at State cost is a fundamental right of a person accused of an offence which may involve jeopardy to his life or personal liberty and this fundamental right is implicit in the requirement of reasonable, fair and just procedure prescribed by Article 21." The trial "must accordingly be held to be vitiated on account of a fatal constitutional infirmity."
PrincipleNo application, no waiver. "It would… make a mockery of legal aid if it were to be left to a poor, ignorant and illiterate accused to ask for free legal service" — because ~70% of rural people are illiterate and "the law ceases to be their protector because they do not know that they are entitled to the protection of law." Hence the court (magistrate/Sessions Judge) must inform; the State's obligation is not conditional. Exception: economic offences, anti-prostitution and child-abuse laws etc., where social justice may require that State legal service not be provided.

CASE 5 · Centre of Legal Research v. State of Kerala, AIR 1986 SC 2195 Reading 2

FactsThe Court considered the proper design of the State's legal-aid programme — whether it could remain a litigation-oriented service, and whether the people themselves must participate in it.
IssueIs legal aid confined to representing the poor in court, or is the State obliged to run a strategic programme? Must the programme involve the people and voluntary organisations?
ArgumentsFor expansion (as adopted by the Court): the traditional, litigation-oriented programme cannot reach social justice; what is needed is promotion of legal literacy, legal-aid camps, encouragement of PIL, and Lok Adalats/niti melas. Legal aid "is not charity or bounty, but it is a social entitlement of the people"; the needy are "participants," not "mere beneficiaries." For the narrower view: administration through officers of the State would suffice. (The casebook reports the Court's reasoning rather than discrete submissions.)
DecisionThe State's obligation under Art. 39-A includes a comprehensive and effective legal-aid programme; people's participation is essential, and "the best way of securing it is to operate through voluntary organizations and social action groups… operating amongst the deprived and vulnerable sections… at the grass-root level… they have their finger on the pulse of the people." Such organisations "must be encouraged and supported by the State in operating the legal aid programme… an obligation which flows directly from Article 39A."
PrincipleLegal aid as social entitlement, not State welfare; the two-tier division of the programme — traditional (litigation-oriented) and strategic (legal literacy, camps, PIL, Lok Adalats) — and the duty to enlist voluntary organisations. "The legal aid programme… must involve public participation."

CASE 6 · S.P. Gupta v. President of India, AIR 1982 SC 149 Reading 2

FactsThe Judges' Appointment and Transfer case — a challenge (via letters, treated as writ petitions) to the transfer of High Court judges and the non-appointment of certain judges, brought by judges, lawyers and journalists — including persons with no personal injury.
Issue(i) The validity of transfers and the justiciability of judicial appointments; (ii) the standing of a stranger to the dispute — the locus standi question; (iii) the maintainability of letters/representation as petitions.
ArgumentsPetitioners: judicial independence is undermined by transfers without consent and by the method of appointments; the Court's public interest jurisdiction should be available to any member of the public; even a letter/telegram constitutes a writ petition. Respondents (Union of India / President): the transfers are executive acts; the petitioners have no locus standi to challenge the administration of judicial appointments; letters are not petitions.
DecisionBhagwati J. (for the majority on standing): the traditional rule of locus standi stands considerably relaxed — quoted (p. 188): "where a legal wrong or legal injury is caused to a person or to a determinate class of persons… and such person or determinate class of persons is by reason of poverty, helplessness, or disability or socially or economically disadvantaged position, unable to approach the Court for relief, any member of the public can maintain an application for an appropriate direction, order or writ in the High Court under Article 226 and in case of breach of any fundamental rights… in this Court under Article 32." Letters addressed to the Court are treated as writ petitions.
PrincipleThe liberalisation of standing that made PIL workable: the "golden key" passage — "It is only the moneyed who have so far had the golden key to unlock the doors of justice. But, now for the first time the portals of the court are being thrown open to the poor and the downtrodden, the ignorant and the illiterate." Plus the associated holdings on judicial independence, and the conception of democratic judicial review with the court as audit alteram partem of public administration.

CASE 7 · People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India, AIR 1982 SC 1473 Reading 2

FactsA public interest petition concerning the working conditions of workmen engaged in construction of the Asian Games facilities in Delhi — alleged violations of labour laws and of Art. 24 (child labour) and the Equal Remuneration Act; the petitioner organisation had itself no personal injury.
IssueIs the Rule of Law available to the poor? Is a petition by a social action group maintainable where the victims cannot approach the court?
ArgumentsPetitioner: the violations affect a large class too poor and ignorant to sue; the Court must enforce constitutional and statutory rights on their behalf. Union of India: the State had taken some measures; (the casebook reports the petitioner's and the Court's position, including the State's partial compliance submissions).
DecisionThe Court examined the facts (appointing commissions), found violations of labour and equality norms, and held directions appropriate; crucially it held that the Rule of Law means the law's protection for the poor too: "The Rule of Law does not mean that the protection of the law must be available only to a fortunate few… The poor too have civil and political rights, and the Rule of Law meant for them also, though today it exists only on paper and not in reality."
PrinciplePIL as the enforcement of the Rule of Law for the excluded; the court's fact-finding commissions and the "co-operative effort" character of PIL (petitioner + State + court). Casebook sentence to quote: the legal system "would be destructive of the Rule of Law" if violations of the rights of large numbers of poor, ignorant or socially disadvantaged persons "go unnoticed and unredressed."

CASE 8 · M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, AIR 1987 SC 1086 Reading 2

FactsA Constitution Bench considered, in the wake of the oleum gas leak (1985) litigation, the scope of PIL and the liability of enterprises engaged in hazardous activities for deaths and injuries caused by accidents during their operation.
IssueCan the Supreme Court award compensation in a PIL under Art. 32? Can it devise new procedures and new law where the old law does not fit present conditions?
ArgumentsPetitioner: victims of hazardous activities must be compensated; the Court must evolve new remedies. Respondents: compensation is beyond the writ jurisdiction; tortious claims belong to ordinary courts; the existing law of negligence may not apply to enterprise risk. (The casebook reports the Court's holding and the competing positions in general form.)
DecisionThe Court held that the law should keep pace with changing socio-economic norms; where a law of the past does not fit the present context, the Court should evolve new law in a public interest litigation; the Court has "incidental and ancillary powers" to devise new methods and strategies for securing enforcement of fundamental rights, particularly in PIL/social action cases — including the power to award compensation to victims of hazardous or dangerous activities.
PrinciplePIL is a law-making and remedy-making jurisdiction; "moulding the remedies to suit the needs of the situation… regardless of pickled precedents in remedial methodology, is part of judicial dynamics" (Krishna Iyer J., quoted in the reading). The strict law of locus standi, procedure and remedies bends where fundamental rights of the poor and victims are at stake.

CASE 9 · Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India, AIR 1984 SC 802 Reading 2

FactsA letter from a social action organisation (Bandhua Mukti Morcha) reported bonded labour in stone quarries in Haryana; it was treated as a writ petition under Art. 32. The Court appointed a commission to investigate; the report disclosed bonded labour and violations of the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 and allied Acts.
IssueMay the Supreme Court depart from the adversarial procedure (and act on commission reports and letters) in order to enforce the fundamental rights of the poor? What is the source of that power?
ArgumentsPetitioner: the poor cannot produce adversarial proof; their cases need new procedures; Art. 32(2) is the vehicle. Union of India/respondents: disputes of fact must be proved by the party invoking the court; the Court cannot conduct investigations through commissions; a letter cannot be a petition. (The State's contentions were essentially procedural.)
DecisionBhagwati J.: "When the poor come before the Court, particularly for enforcement of their fundamental rights, it is necessary to depart from the adversarial procedure and to evolve a new procedure… The problems of the poor… are qualitatively different… they need a different kind of lawyering skill and different kind of judicial approach. If we blindly follow the adversarial procedure… it would be nothing but a mockery of the Constitution." The Court must "abandon the laissez-faire approach… and forge new tools, devise new methods and adopt new strategies" — "clearly made permissible by the language of clause (2) of Article 32." The Court examined the commission report, found bonded labour, and issued directions (release, rehabilitation, prosecution).
PrincipleProcedural flexibility in constitutional adjudication. Art. 32(2) deliberately uses no words restricting the Court's power to choose appropriate procedure; the Supreme Court must remain "a real sentinel on the qui vive"; therefore letters as petitions, commission-based fact-finding, and non-adversarial methods are legitimate where fundamental rights of the poor are in issue. Also the reading's coda: lawyers' minds are conditioned by the existing system — beware the "unconscious predilection" for it.

CASE 10 · Raj Kishore v. State, AIR 1969 Cal 321 (1969 Cri LJ 860) Reading 2

FactsA Sessions trial in which counsel was assigned to an accused but (as the casebook records) the assignment was not accompanied by a proper brief and adequate time to prepare.
IssueWhat is the content of the right to assigned counsel — is mere appointment enough?
Arguments (as reported)For the accused: assignment of counsel is illusory unless the advocate is competent, is given the complete brief, and has time to prepare; failure denies effective representation. For the State/prosecution: the appointment satisfied s. 340(1) of the old Code / the High Court rules. (The casebook reports the rule laid down; it is taken as the settled proposition.)
DecisionThe Calcutta High Court held that such counsel "must be given the complete brief of the case and time to prepare the case"; failure is "a denial of proper representation of the accused and vitiates the trial."
PrincipleThe right is to effective representation, not nominal: competent counsel + complete brief + preparation time. Combined with Art. 22(1) (counsel of choice), it means counsel can neither be inadequate nor imposed: "no counsel can be thrust upon the accused without ascertaining the wishes of the accused and without giving him any choice in selecting his lawyer."

CASE 11 · Aswini Kumar Ghosh v. Arabinda Ghosh, AIR 1952 SC 369 Reading 3

FactsTwo advocates of the Supreme Court (enrolled under the Supreme Court Advocates (Practice in High Court) Act, 1951) sought to appear and act in the original side of the Calcutta High Court — and, as attorneys' monopoly had it, under the High Court's rules and Letters Patent they could not act (or plead on the original side) unless instructed by an attorney.
IssueDoes s. 2 of the 1951 Act — "every advocate of the Supreme Court shall be entitled as of right to practise in any High Court whether or not he is an advocate of that High Court" — confer the right to act as well as plead, overriding the High Court's original-side rules requiring an attorney, and the Bar Councils Act, 1926 and the barristers' conventions?
Arguments (both sides)Petitioners (advocates): "practise" includes acting and pleading; the non-obstante clause of s. 2 overrides conflicting provisions (ss. 9(4), 14(3) of the Bar Councils Act, 1926 and High Court rules); a statutory right cannot be trimmed by court-made rules; reliance also on Art. 19(1)(g). Respondents (and the Bar/attorneys' side): "practise" is contextual — on the original side it has always meant pleading under attorney's instructions; s. 2 gives no right greater than advocates of that High Court possess (the "ambulatory interpretation"); no new right is conferred in the High Court where the advocate is already enrolled.
DecisionMajority (Patanjali Sastri C.J. and majority of the judges): an advocate of the Supreme Court is entitled as of right to appear and plead as well as act in all the High Courts in India, including the original sides of the Calcutta and Bombay High Courts, without being instructed by an attorney and free from the restrictions of the High Court rules. The Court declined the "ambulatory interpretation" (co-extension with local advocates' rights at any given time), and the non-obstante clause was given full effect over conflicting provisions. (S.R. Das J. dissented/had reservations, reading "practise" as appear and plead only; the casebook records the majority result.)
PrincipleStatutory words ("practise," "as of right") are read in their full breadth; a non-obstante clause overrides conflicting rules; and the Supreme Court bar is a national bar — the 1951 Act's policy (carried into the Advocates Act, 1961, with the all-India roll) is that an advocate's right to practise is not confined to the court of enrolment. Follow-up in the reading: after 1-12-1961 no further advocates of the Supreme Court were admitted; enrolment is on State rolls maintained under the 1961 Act.

07.2 Model Answer 1 · 20 marks

Q.1 "Legal aid is a constitutional mandate, not charity." Discuss with reference to the constitutional provisions and the decisions of the Supreme Court.

Opening. The proposition is the thesis of R. Swaroop's essay "Legal Service/Aid and Lok Adalat," built on Hussainara Khatoon: because the poor are "priced out of the judicial system," the Constitution itself requires a legal-service programme; and the Court in Centre of Legal Research added that "the legal aid programme is not charity or bounty, but it is a social entitlement of the people."

(1) The Preamble. "The Founding Fathers… right from the Preamble, taken a positive approach of doctrine of philosophy of Equal Justice" — the promise of Justice, social economic and political, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity is "strengthened by the constitutional provisions in Articles 14, 19, 21, 22(1), 32, 39-A, 51-A and 226."

(2) The mandate. Maneka Gandhi (AIR 1978 SC 597): the Art. 21 procedure must be "reasonable, fair and just" — and "a procedure which does not make legal services available to an accused person who is too poor to afford a lawyer… cannot possibly be regarded as reasonable, fair and just." Hence legal service is "an essential ingredient" of fair procedure. Art. 39-A (inserted 1976; enforced 3-1-1977) commands: "The State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice, on the basis of equal opportunity and shall, in particular, provide free legal aid… to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities." Art. 22(1) secures counsel of choice — so counsel can never be thrust on an accused.

(3) The trilogy of cases. Hussainara (AIR 1979 SC 1369): legal aid implicit in Art. 21; the State "cannot avoid its constitutional obligation… by pleading financial or administrative inability" — Rhem v. Malcolm: "the law does not permit any Government to deprive its citizens of constitutional rights on a plan of poverty." Khatri (AIR 1981 SC 928): the obligation begins at the first production before the Magistrate; magistrates and Sessions Judges were directed to inform every unrepresented accused of his entitlement — "it would make a mockery of legal aid if it were to be left to a poor, ignorant and illiterate accused to ask for free legal service." Suk Das (AIR 1986 SC 991): the right is a fundamental right implicit in Art. 21 and not conditional on application; its denial "vitiates" the trial; the only qualification is the reading's exception (economic offences, prostitution, child abuse). Raj Kishore (1969 Cal): the assigned counsel must be competent, given the complete brief and time — otherwise the trial is vitiated.

(4) The institutional translation. Law Commission's 14th (1958, p. 487: without help with court-fees and lawyer's fees "he/she is denied equality in the opportunity to seek justice"), 41st (1969) and 48th Reports → s. 304 CrPC (court shall assign a pleader at State expense where the accused has not sufficient means; s. 304(3) permits extension to other courts) and s. 303 (right to be defended "of his choice") → CILAS, 26-9-1980 (Bhagwati J.; model scheme; legal-aid camps) → Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (national, State and district authorities; in force 9-11-1995) → NALSA.

(5) Critique and conclusion. The reading's statistics (0.2% of GDP on law and justice; ~9 judges per million; 8–12 years for a civil suit; 30 million pending) show the distance between mandate and reality; its answer is the strategic programme (legal literacy, legal-aid camps, PIL, Lok Adalats). Close: "we should inject equal justice into legality" — a mandate, not charity; a "constitutional obligation and compulsion," not a pledge of government.

07.3 Model Answer 2 · 20 marks

Q.2 Describe the hierarchy of courts in India, explaining the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and the High Court, and the place of tribunals and regulatory authorities in the system.

Opening box (draw the pyramid): Supreme Court → High Court → District Courts → Civil Judges/Judicial Magistrates. This is Anupam Jha's master diagram, and its constant is that every pathway of appeal ends at the Supreme Court.

(1) The Supreme Court. Original: writ jurisdiction (Art. 32), original suits, transfer of cases, election disputes of the President and Vice-President; Appellate: appeals under the Constitution (Arts. 132–134), statutory appeals, and SLPs under Art. 136 — the discretionary extraordinary jurisdiction; Advisory: Art. 143(1) (President's reference — the casebook diagram's "143(1) & 317" prints a slip; 317 concerns PSC members); Review: Art. 137 and the curative petition; and international commercial arbitration appears beside it. (Unit-3 link: Art. 136's discretionary apex is the descendant of the ancient king's sole power as final court.)

(2) The High Court. Appellate (from subordinate courts and tribunals); Original (original suits above ₹2 crores — Delhi figure); Writ (Art. 226); Revisional; Supervisory (Art. 227); and election disputes of MPs, MLAs and MLCs.

(3) District and subordinate courts. District Courts — civil side: appellate (against civil judges) and original (₹20 lakh–₹2 crores; Delhi); criminal side: appellate (against magistrates) and original (minimum sentence above 7 years/special-law jurisdiction). Below: Civil Judges (up to ₹20 lakhs), Chief Judicial Magistrate (up to 7 years), Judicial Magistrates (up to 3 years).

(4) Tribunals and regulators — the parallel ladders. Regulators: CCI→NCLAT; ERCs & PNGRB→APTEL; TRAI→TDSAT; IBBI→NCLAT; SEBI, IRDA, PFRDA→SAT; each then to the Supreme Court. Tribunals/commissions: service matters (CAT→HC Arts. 226/227→SC Art. 136/Art. 32); consumer (DCDRC up to ₹50 lakhs → SCDRC ₹50 lakh–₹2 crores → NCDRC above ₹2 crores, appellate over SCDRC and CCPA → SC); labour (Labour Courts/Industrial Tribunals→HC→SC); MACT→HC→SC; income tax (AO→JCIT(A)→CIT(A)→ITAT→HC→SC); rent (Rent Controller→Rent Control Tribunal→HC→SC); debt recovery (DRT→DRAT→HC→SC); railways (RCT→HC→SC); companies (CLB/Company Court–HC); information (PIO→AA→CIC→HC→SC); GST (Authority→Appellate Authority→GST Appellate Tribunal, National Appellate Authority→HC→SC); commercial (Commercial Court→HC Commercial Division→Commercial Appellate Division→SC).

(5) Design principles. (i) Specialisation without fragmentation — expert bodies, but uniform final law through the constitutional courts; (ii) writs as the supervisory spine (Arts. 32, 226/227 run vertically through every tribunal system); (iii) merit in the diagram memory: markets→SAT, competition & insolvency→NCLAT, energy→APTEL, telecom→TDSAT. Conclusion: one integrated pyramid whose base serves the citizen and whose apex guards the Constitution — "the Rule of Law is supreme" (Menon), and Art. 136 is its emergency brake.

07.4 Model Answer 3 · 20 marks

Q.3 Trace the evolution of the legal profession in India from 1774 to the Advocates Act, 1961, and evaluate the Act.

Opening. "Law, as a profession, appears to have been in vogue in ancient Indian thought. Its concept was quite different from that it is today" (Raghavan). The modern profession is the creation of the colonial and post-colonial state, in four movements.

(1) King's Courts (1773–1861). The Regulating Act, 1773 → the Supreme Court at Fort William (Charter of 26-3-1774); Clause 11: the Court may enrol "advocates and attorneys at law… and no other person or persons whatsoever… shall be allowed to appear and plead, or act" — a British monopoly (English and Irish barristers, Scottish faculty advocates; the attorneys were Irish attorneys and solicitors). Bombay and Madras followed. An Indian lawyer had no right of appearance.

(2) Company's Courts (1793–1861). Before British power, the Moghul courts had Vakils; they appeared in the Company's courts too, until Bengal Regulation VII of 1793 created the first regular profession for the Company's courts — enrolment by the Sadar Dewani Adalat, fees by percentage of the value of property, and only Hindus and Muslims; Regulation XXVII of 1814 (pleaders as arbitrators and legal advisers); Regulation XII of 1833 (any nationality or religion); Bengal Practitioners Act, 1846 (all persons of good character; attorneys and barristers in the Sadar courts; fee agreements); Legal Practitioners Act, 1855 (barristers and attorneys of the Supreme Courts into the Company's courts — while Indian practitioners still could not appear before the Supreme Courts).

(3) The Crown and the High Courts (1858–1926). Government of India Act, 1858; Indian High Courts Act, 1862 (Letters Patent courts replace the Supreme Courts and Sadar Adalats); Letters Patent, 1865, Cl. 9: enrolment of advocates, Vakils and attorneys. The Legal Practitioners Act, 1879 consolidated the law (s. 4 all-court practice; discipline with the High Court, the Letters Patent for attorneys, and the revenue authority for revenue agents; dismissal only after opportunity to defend; s. 6 High Court rules; s. 27 fees; s. 36 touts — still in force). Qualifications varied: Madras (degree + one year's training), Calcutta (graduate + law degree + articled clerk to a vakil of five years' standing). Women were excluded under the rules (Regina Ghua, Calcutta; Sudarshan Sabala Hazara, Patna) until the Legal Practitioners (Women) Act, 1923: "no woman shall, by reason only of her sex, be disqualified…" Dissatisfaction with the barrister/vakil distinction → Chamber Committee (Nov. 1923; divided on the dual system; recommended Bar Councils) → Indian Bar Councils Act, 1926 (assent 9-9-1926; 15-member Councils: Advocate-General ex-officio, 4 nominated by the High Court, 10 elected; s. 9 rules with High Court sanction; s. 14 practice) — but enrolment power remained with the High Courts, the Councils were advisory, and the original sides and attorneys were untouched: "regarded as unsatisfactory."

(4) To a unified Bar (1948–61). The Constitution, 1950 and the new Supreme Court made an all-India Bar imperative → All-India Bar Committee (Justice S.R. Das) → Law Commission's 14th Report (1955) (M.C. Setalvad) endorsed it → Legal Practitioners Bill, 1959 (Lok Sabha, 19-11-1959) → passed as the Advocates Act, 1961 (assent 19-5-1961).

(5) Evaluation. (i) One class — s. 29: from the appointed day, only advocates; s. 33: advocates alone may practise; unauthorised practice punishable (six months). (ii) Powers shifted from courts to self-governing bodies — admission, rolls and discipline with the State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India (bodies corporate; BCI's functions include the common roll, standards of professional conduct and etiquette, supervision of State Councils, and legal education — degrees it recognises, universities it inspects; income: 40% of the Rs. 250 enrolment fee), subject to ultimate appeal to the Supreme Court. (iii) Enrolment (s. 24: Indian or BCI-recognised law degree; barrister; transitional categories; 21 years; citizen; Rs. 250), with s. 24(1)(d) training/examination later "practically made… a nullity" by notification, and s. 28 conditions (no full-time service or trade; exceptions for law officers, articled clerks/attorneys, part-time law teachers, Hindu joint family). (iv) Senior advocates (s. 16) and the Supreme Court's own regulation (Art. 145(1); Order IV: two-part roll, robes, Advocate-on-Record — one year's training, tests, Delhi office, registered clerk). (v) The 1951 Act's policy vindicated in Aswini Kumar Ghosh (AIR 1952 SC 369), majority: an SC advocate may appear, plead and act in all High Courts as of right.

Verdict (the author's own). "The Advocates Act, 1961 has marked the beginning of a new era… unification of the Bar in India… integration." Cautions: the language problem in legal education; resistance to any executive chairmanship preserving Bar autonomy; and the unresolved dual system agitation. Grey update: s. 30 was notified only on 15-6-2011; the Act now extends to J&K post-2019; BCI's 5-year integrated course is now the mainstream.

07.5 Model Answer 4 · 15 marks

Q.4 What is public interest litigation? How has the Supreme Court relaxed the rule of locus standi, and what procedures has it devised for the poor?

Definition. PIL "has been devised as a tool to secure benefit to a class or group of persons… who are denied the constitutional rights but cannot come to court personally for relief by reason of ignorance, poverty, destitution, helplessness, disability or social or economic disadvantage." It is invoked "on behalf of such persons… by a third person or a social action group… regardless of its personal injury"; it "involves the rights of thousands of people at a time," unlike adversary litigation; and it is "essentially a co-operative or collaborative effort on the part of the petitioner, the State or public authority and the court."

(1) Locus standi. The traditional rule (relief only to the person legally injured) was "considerably relaxed" in S.P. Gupta (AIR 1982 SC 149, 188): where legal wrong or injury is caused to a person or determinate class, and they are "by reason of poverty, helplessness, or disability or socially or economically disadvantaged position, unable to approach the Court for relief, any member of the public can maintain an application" under Art. 226 or Art. 32. The "golden key" passage makes the point: "It is only the moneyed who have so far had the golden key to unlock the doors of justice… now for the first time the portals of the court are being thrown open to the poor and the downtrodden." PUDR (AIR 1982 SC 1473) anchors it in the Rule of Law: "The poor too have civil and political rights, and the Rule of Law meant for them also, though today it exists only on paper and not in reality."

(2) Procedure. Bandhua Mukti Morcha (AIR 1984 SC 802): "it is necessary to depart from the adversarial procedure and to evolve a new procedure" — letters are writ petitions, commissions investigate facts, and this is "clearly made permissible by the language of clause (2) of Article 32… the Constitution-makers… deliberately and advisedly [did] not use any words restricting the power of the Court to adopt any procedure which it considers appropriate." We must "abandon the laissez-faire approach… and forge new tools, devise new methods and adopt new strategies," so that "the Supreme Court [becomes] a real sentinel on the qui vive." M.C. Mehta (AIR 1987 SC 1086) adds the remedial dimension — "the law should keep pace with changing socio-economic norms"; the Court can "evolve new law" and award compensation, for "moulding the remedies to suit the needs of the situation… regardless of pickled precedents in remedial methodology, is part of judicial dynamics."

(3) Verdict. PIL is "a strategic arm of the legal aid movement," which "holds out great possibilities for the future"; its historic service is to bring the "forgotten specimens of humanity… the people of India who gave to themselves this magnificent Constitution" into the courtroom. Connect to the art of the answer: social justice is "the signature tune of our Constitution"; the courts "must become the courts for the poor and struggling masses."

07.6 Model Answer 5 · 15 marks

Q.5 Examine the institution of Lok Adalat: its nature, working, importance and legal status.

Nature. The Lok Adalat (People's Court — "niti melas" in the CILAS programme) emerged as "an alternative dispute settlement mechanism… with the object of taking justice to the doorsteps of the poor." It is "an institution or an agency, for handling disputes by conciliation and counseling, a species of peace making": Lok Adalats "do not treat the issues before it as disputes under contest and decide cases but treat them as differences and resolve them by conciliatory and persuasive efforts." Its unique feature is participatory justice — settlement by discussion in an informal atmosphere with mutual and free consent; "there is no winner and no loser," the object being "to help warring parties to work things out, shake hands, and become friends."

Working. The date and place are fixed about a month in advance and publicised; presiding officers identify conciliable pending cases; cases are classified and substance recorded; pre-Lok Adalat conferences are held and parties motivated by legal-aid teams (law students, social workers, volunteers); at the campsite the mediation team — members of the local Legal Aid Committee, advocates, elders, social activists ("conciliators") — continues the process; on compromise, the settlement is reduced to writing, signed and countersigned, and passed to the court concerned for a final decree or order, the court verifying that all parties consented and that the settlement was fair and legal. Panels: two or three members — a retired judge, senior retired civil servant, advocate, law teacher, social workers and eminent local persons, chosen for public service, honesty and respectability.

Importance. The statistics: roughly 91% of Indian cases go to trial and only 9% settle, whereas in the USA more than 90% settle before trial; civil cases average 8–12 years plus an execution second round; pending work has crossed 30 million; ~9 judges per million against 115+ in the USA; 34% of the world's poor live in India; only ~4.5 lakh advocates then; and the state spends just 0.2% of GDP on law and justice. Against this, Lok Adalats are quicker, cheaper, free of dilatory tactics, "concerned more with narrowing the differences and finding settlement in accordance with natural justice… than with expatiating upon procedural complexities"; they are "based on jurisprudence of peace," a "rendezvous for social amity and affinity and social justice."

Legal status. Begun as a voluntary agency "without any statutory backing for its decisions" (handling compoundable criminal complaints, civil and revenue disputes, MACT cases, institutional cases), it acquired statutory status in the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 — "acknowledging the institution of Lok Adalat and giving statutory status to Lok Adalats" — brought into force w.e.f. 9-11-1995; the Act also constituted the National, State and District Authorities. Conclusion. The Lok Adalat is the reading's answer to "what is the use of the system, which does not help lowly and lost…": a culture of compromise, not of contest — "it creates not only peace but also a culture of compromise."

07.7 Model Answer 6 · 15 marks

Q.6 Compare the composition and functions of the State Bar Councils and the Bar Council of India under the Advocates Act, 1961.

Frame. The 1961 Act's structural achievement is to vest in self-governing bodies corporate the powers the courts had exercised — admission, rolls and discipline — "subject to an ultimate appeal to the Supreme Court." It creates one all-India body and State bodies, with the States subordinate but autonomous.

Composition. State Bar Council: the Advocate-General ex-officio (Delhi: the Additional Solicitor-General, later the Solicitor-General, since a Union Territory has no Advocate-General), plus 15 elected members for Assam & Nagaland, Orissa, Delhi and Himachal Pradesh, and 20 for every other State — elected by proportional representation through the single transferable vote; term now four years (originally six with rotation); a Chairman, Vice-Chairman, Secretary, and accountant. Bar Council of India: the Attorney-General and the Solicitor-General ex-officio + one member elected by each State Bar Council from among its members — so the federal composition mirrors the structure of the Indian Union.

Committees. State: one or more Disciplinary Committees (two elected members + one co-opted person of at least ten years' practice); an Executive Committee (five); an Enrolment Committee; and others. BCI: a Disciplinary Committee of three (two elected from advocates of ten years' standing; the senior-most chairman); a Legal Education Committee of ten (five members, five non-members); an Executive Committee; others.

Functions. State: admit persons as advocates and maintain the State roll; entertain and decide cases of misconduct; safeguard the rights, privileges and interests of advocates; promote and support law reform; elections; other assigned functions. BCI: the common roll; standards of professional conduct and etiquette; procedure for the State disciplinary committees; safeguard rights and law reform; decide matters referred by a State Council; general supervision and control over State Councils; legal education — standards, recognition of law degrees in consultation with universities, visit and inspect universities; manage funds; elections; plus special powers: removal from the roll for misrepresentation, death, request or misconduct; revision of State Council orders (with hearing); directions under s. 48-B (including directions to the ex-officio member if a State Council cannot function, effective notwithstanding State rules); and rule-making — State rules under ss. 15/28 take effect only on BCI approval (contrast the 1926 Act's High Court sanction), and s. 49 is the BCI's general rule-making power.

Finance and symmetry. BCI's main income is 40% of the Rs. 250 enrolment fee paid to each State Council. Conclusion: a federation of the Bar — State Councils for admission and discipline, the BCI for standards, supervision and education — and the deliberate rejection of executive chairmanship, because the Bar "stoutly resisted" any encroachment "upon the autonomy given to the Bar Councils."

07.8 Model Answer 7 · 15 marks

Q.7 Discuss the right to practise of advocates under the Advocates Act, 1961 and the rules of the Supreme Court. What is the position of senior advocates and advocates-on-record?

The Act. s. 29: "subject to the provisions of this Act, as from the appointed day (1-6-1969), there shall be only one class of persons entitled to practise" — advocates — throughout India, "in all courts including the Supreme Court," before any tribunal or person legally authorised to take evidence, and before any other authority where entitled. s. 33: advocates alone are entitled to practise in any court. s. 30: the right to practise as of right (in the wider courts/tribunals) — the article records it as not yet fully operational in 1972; grey: notified on 15-6-2011. Unauthorised practice: imprisonment up to six months.

Content of the right. It includes acting as well as pleading: Aswini Kumar Ghosh v. Arabinda Ghosh (AIR 1952 SC 369), majority (Patanjali Sastri C.J.) — under the Supreme Court Advocates (Practice in High Court) Act, 1951, s. 2 (non-obstante), a Supreme Court advocate is entitled as of right to appear, plead and act in all High Courts, including the original sides of the Calcutta and Bombay High Courts, without instruction by an attorney and free from the High Court rules. (The minority read "practise" narrowly; the reading follows the majority.) After 1-12-1961 there is no separate admission as advocates of the Supreme Court — enrolment is on the State rolls, and the common roll is with the Bar Council of India.

Senior advocates (s. 16). Those who were senior advocates immediately before 1-12-1961 are deemed so; and the Supreme Court and High Courts may "designate any advocate as senior advocate if in its opinion by virtue of his ability, experience and standing at the Bar, he is deserving of such distinction." Seniors are governed by the Supreme Court rules applicable to them and subject to the restrictions laid down by the Bar Council of India "in the interest of the legal profession."

Advocates-on-record (Order IV, Supreme Court Rules, 1950, made under Art. 145(1)). The roll of the Supreme Court is in two parts — seniors and others (senior enrolment at more than ten years' standing, as under the Federal Court Rules, 1942). Rule 5: an AOR must have undergone one year's training with an approved AOR and passed the Court's notified tests; he must keep an office in Delhi within ten miles of the Court House and undertake to employ a registered clerk within a month; registration fee Rs. 25; attorneys are exempt. Rule 2(b) places restrictions on seniors; Rule 3 prescribes costume — by the Chief Justice's direction, black coat, robe and band as worn by barristers.

Conclusion. The design: a single national profession (one class, common roll, all-courts right), internally differentiated by merit (senior advocates), and administered by the profession's own bodies — with the Supreme Court retaining the last word over those who practise before it.

07.9 Model Answer 8 · 10 marks

Q.8 "The Preamble promise is strengthened by Articles 14, 19, 21, 22(1), 32, 39-A, 51-A and 226." Explain the promise and its working.

(i) The promise. The Preamble constitutes India a "Sovereign Socialist Secular Democratic Republic" and secures to all citizens Justice, social, economic and political; Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Equality of status and of opportunity; and promotes Fraternity assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation. The casebook's reading of it: the Founding Fathers "right from the Preamble, taken a positive approach of doctrine of philosophy of Equal Justice."

(ii) The strengthening articles, in their jobs. Art. 14 (equal justice); Art. 19 (liberty of expression, belief, etc.); Art. 21 (life and personal liberty — after Maneka, "reasonable, fair and just" procedure, and therefore legal service); Art. 22(1) (counsel of choice); Arts. 32 and 226 (the writ engines — 32 for fundamental rights, 226 for the High Courts; both are the PIL portals per S.P. Gupta); Art. 39-A ("the State shall secure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice, on the basis of equal opportunity" — free legal aid for those disabled by "economic or other disabilities," inserted 1976, enforced 3-1-1977); Art. 51-A (fundamental duties — the citizen's side of the promise).

(iii) How it works. The promise is not self-executing: it converts into (a) the right side — Art. 21 + Art. 39-A, enforced by the courts as in Hussainara, Khatri, Suk Das; (b) the institutional side — CILAS (1980), the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (in force 1995), NALSA and the Lok Adalats; and (c) the professional side — the Bar's obligation under the rules of professional conduct, "legal services to the poor is one of the social obligations of every lawyer." Verdict: the promise supplies the standard; the articles supply the machinery; and the reading's verdict — "we should inject equal justice into legality" — is what the answer must reproduce with the Preamble's own words.

07.10 Model Answer 9 · 10 marks

Q.9 Write a short answer on the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 and the work of the Committee for Implementing Legal Aid Schemes (CILAS).

(i) CILAS. Concerned that legal aid is "the implementation of a constitutional mandate," the Government of India by resolution of 26-9-1980 appointed the Committee for Implementing Legal Aid Schemes with P.N. Bhagwati J. (as he then was) as Chairman, "to monitor and implement legal aid programs on a uniform basis in all the States and union territories." It evolved a model scheme for legal aid — including the organisation of legal aid camps, promotion of legal literacy and spread of legal awareness among weaker sections (rural areas, slums, labour colonies) — and set up several legal aid and advice boards; its programme included Lok Adalats and niti melas, whence the institution grew.

(ii) Deficiencies and statute. Review showed deficiencies; "it was felt that it would be desirable to constitute statutory legal service authorities at the national, State and District levels." Hence the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Act 39 of 1987): National (NALSA), State and District Authorities, with the Act "acknowledging the institution of Lok Adalat and giving statutory status to Lok Adalats." The Act was brought into force only from 9-11-1995 — "almost eight years after its enactment" — and the CILAS term was extended meanwhile (e.g., for one year from 14-5-1990 or until NALSA was constituted).

(iii) Significance. Legal aid thereby moved from committee charity to statutory entitlement — "not a pledge or a plan of a government, but… the status of people's movement"; and with NALSA's programmes, "members of Bench and Bar, N.G.Os. and Government Agencies must render voluntary helping hand." Connect: this is Art. 39-A's "suitable legislation or schemes" made flesh.

07.11 Model Answer 10 · 10 marks

Q.10 "Legal literacy is a pre-condition to maintain the rule of law." Explain the strategic legal aid programme.

The traditional (litigation-oriented) legal aid programme is insufficient: the reading, on the authority of Centre of Legal Research v. State of Kerala (AIR 1986 SC 2195), requires supplementing it with a strategic legal service programme consisting of four elements: (1) promotion of legal literacy — "knowledge of their rights and entitlement would give to the poor strength and confidence to fight" and would help them "avoid needless difficulties which arise from ignorance"; (2) organisation of legal aid camps — "carrying legal services to the doorsteps of the rural poor"; (3) encouragement of public interest litigation; and (4) holding of Lok Adalats or niti melas. Its character: it "aims at prevention and elimination of various kinds of injustices which the poor as a class suffer because of poverty and endeavours to launch a frontal attack on the poverty itself… a qualitative and radical change in the whole emphasis, aims and functioning of the legal service programme… seeks to provide representation to groups of social and economic protest… directed towards group-oriented approach to the problem of poverty." The reading's justification of the legal-literacy limb is the empirical one: about 70% of rural people are illiterate; "even literate people do not know what their rights and entitlements under the law are… it is this absence of legal awareness which is responsible for the deception, exploitation and deprivation of rights and benefits from which the poor suffer"; "the law ceases to be their protector because they do not know that they are entitled to the protection of law." Hence "legal literacy is a pre-condition to maintain the 'rule of law'" (Bhagwati J., quoted from V.R. Krishna Iyer's Equal Justice and Forensic Process). Voluntary organisations and social action groups must be enlisted — "an obligation which flows directly from Article 39A." The reading's closing strategy list (manage the unmanageable; break the unbreakable; beat the unbeatable; hit the unhittable; defend the indefensible) and the "Neo-Jurisprudence" of "public-oriented, participation performing, progressive, professional and pervasive programmes" complete the answer.

09Exam Strategy, Master Table & Source Map

09.1 How to write Unit-4 answers — the four "P"s

The two that win marks

  1. Pyramid + ladders. Every hierarchy answer must open with the drawn pyramid (SC→HC→District→Civil Judges/Judicial Magistrates) and close with the converging fact: every regulator/tribunal ladder ends at the Supreme Court. Draw it; examiners reward it.
  2. Promises → provisions → precedents → programmes. The unit's argument is a chain, not a list: Preamble promise (equal justice) → Articles (14, 19, 21, 22(1), 32, 39-A, 51-A, 226) → precedents (Maneka, Hussainara, Khatri, Suk Das) → programmes (CILAS, 1987 Act, NALSA, Lok Adalat, PIL). Write it forward for legal-aid questions and backward for Preamble questions.

The two that guard against error

  1. Pagination honesty. The casebook's pecuniary/criminal limits are "with reference to Delhi Courts"; Raghavan is a 1972 article (s. 30 notified 15-6-2011; 5-year LL.B. now the norm; J&K exception gone after 2019); Swaroop's statistics (30 million pending, ~9 judges per million, 91% v. 9%, 0.2% GDP) are dated. Unit 4 is the institutional photograph of everything the first three units argued. Its four sub-topics are the four components of a legal system: the Preamble gives the principles — "Justice, social, economic and political," and the philosophy of equal justice that right-from-the-start colours the whole reading; the hierarchy gives the institutions — one pyramid, specialised tribunals and regulators, and every appeal path converging on the Supreme Court, whose Art. 136 is the modern echo of the ancient king's final court; legal services give the access — a constitutional mandate that is "not charity… but a constitutional obligation and compulsion," enforced by a trilogy of judgments from Hussainara to Suk Das, and institutionalised in CILAS, the 1987 Act, NALSA, PIL and the Lok Adalat, "the jurisprudence of peace"; and the profession gives the personnel — a unified, self-governing Bar, born of a colonial monopoly in 1774 and completed in 1961, whose own rules make "legal services to the poor… one of the social obligations of every lawyer." The casebook's own test of the system is the question it puts in its pages: "What is the use of the system, which does not help lowly and lost, poor and downtrodden and which creates distance between law and justice?" — and its answer is the unit you have just studied: promise, structure, access and profession, designed so that the distance between law and justice need not exist. Write it as one system, not four topics — that is the difference between a pass and a distinction.
