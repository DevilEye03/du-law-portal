01Conceptual Framework — How to Think About "Legal Systems"

1.1 The starting proposition

"There would appear to be three at least which occupy an uncontested place of prominence: the Romano-Germanic family, the Common law family and the family of Socialist law. These three groups, whatever their value and extension throughout the world, do not however take into account all contemporary legal phenomena. There are other systems, situated outside these three traditions or sharing only part of their conception of things, which prevail in a large number of contemporary societies."— David & Brierley (opening)

Note that the extract begins with the West's own map — three principal families — and then immediately destabilises it: three families "do not take into account all contemporary legal phenomena." The whole reading is a controlled argument against a "naive sense of superiority" (its own words) that expects every other system to disappear.

1.2 The two questions a comparative asks of any legal system

Question 1 — What is law?

Western/positivist answer (the reading's paraphrase): "law is… the body of rules that are really observed, the application of which is entrusted to the courts"; or in the sociological version, "only those rules which are really observed as a matter of practice." The ideal-law answer: "law may also be seen as a model of ideal behaviour, one not to be confused with the actual rules by which individuals act which courts apply" — the pre-nineteenth-century European university tradition "taught, almost exclusively, an ideal law constructed on the basis of Roman law." Muslim, Jewish and Hindu communities take the second view for their own law; Far Eastern societies take a third view — the very value of law is questioned.

Question 2 — What is law's place in society?

In the West (and, with qualifications, in Islamic and Hindu communities) "law is held to be a necessary part of, indeed a basis for, society. Good social order implies the primacy of law: men must live according to law and, where necessary, be prepared to fight for the supremacy of law; administrative authorities, no less than any other part of society, must act legally, courts must ensure that law is respected. Law, a mirror of justice, is in this conception superior even to equity itself; outside the law, there can only be anarchy, or arbitrariness, chaos or the rule of force. Law is therefore venerated, the courts are temples of justice, the judges its oracles." — In the Far East, the opposite: "for the Chinese, law is an instrument of arbitrary action rather than the symbol of Justice; it is a factor contributing to social disorder rather than to social order."

1.3 The two postulates of non-Western societies

"In non-western societies the governing social principles to which reference is made are of two types. On the one hand law is fully recognised as being of great value but the law itself is framed in a different concept than it is in the West; on the other, the very notion of law is rejected, and social relations are governed by other extra-legal means. The first view is that of Muslim and Hindu societies, while the latter is that adopted in countries of the Far East and large parts of Africa and Malagasy."— David & Brierley

This single distinction is the spine of sub-topics (a)–(d) and the most quotable line of the reading: it divides the non-Western world into law framed differently (Islamic/Hindu) and law rejected in favour of extra-legal ordering (Far East, Black Africa, Malagasy). Learn it verbatim.

1.4 Reception, colonisation and the survival of the old

  • Two routes of expansion — identical for both Western families: "colonization or reception." Colonisation annexed territories outright; voluntary "reception" occurred in countries not colonised but driven by "the need for modernization, or the desire to westernize," leading to "the penetration of European ideas."
  • Reception does not erase. European laws were often "received" even by countries "which possessed their own civilization, had their own ways of thinking and acting and their own indigenous institutions, all of which antedate such reception." Sometimes "reception has left some of these original institutions in place"; nowhere is this clearer than in Muslim countries, where reception was "only partial, leaving some legal relations subject to the principles of the traditional, local law."
  • The application differs. "The old ways of thinking and acting peculiar to these countries may also mean that the application of the new has been quite different from what it is in Europe" — critical for the Far East; and for Africa and America one must ask whether geography, population distribution and local conditions "have not led to the development of laws substantially different from their European models."
  • India's own case (per David): the Common law in India was "only partially received"; "attention must be given to its transformation or adoption by reason of its co-existence with the tradition of previous civilizations." This is the sentence that connects David to Menon and Sudame.
  • Cross-check (Menon): "the legal system we now have is largely the gift of the British rulers… the system is still alien to the majority of Indians whose legal culture is more indigenous and whose contact with the formal legal system (the imported British model) is marginal if not altogether non-existent." The entire Unit 3 is an argument about the meaning of that fact.

Connecting thread with Units 1–2 (same casebook): Parasain's plea to "relocate the Indian mind in Indian thought"; Dhyani's demonstration that the ancient order was "a government of laws and not of men"; Sudame's own challenge that legal education in India is "broadly based upon the Anglo-Saxon law" whose foundations, "born out of their indigenous customs… do not find a natural blending with the Indian societal fabric." Unit 3 gives you the world map on which to locate that argument — and the ancient system is one of the "other systems" David & Brierley refuse to dismiss.

02(a) Common Law Legal System

2.1 Definition and composition of the family

"A second family is that of the Common law, including the law of England and those laws modelled on English law. The Common Law, altogether different in its characteristics from the Romano-Germanic family, was formed primarily by judges who had to resolve specific disputes. Today it still bears striking traces of its origins."— David & Brierley

Membership: England and Ireland (Europe), and the extra-European common law world — India, the United States, Canada, and the many states modelled on English law. The family is defined not by geography but by genealogy: membership = descent from the English law. The reading is emphatic that the family's peculiarities are originary — "traces of its origins" still mark it today.

Three origin-trait(s) that explain everything

  1. Judge-made. "Formed primarily by judges who had to resolve specific disputes" — the law was built case by case, out of adjudication, not out of doctrine.
  2. Dispute-specific rules. "The Common law legal rule is one which seeks to provide the solution to a trial rather than to formulate a general rule of conduct for the future. It is then much less abstract than the characteristic legal rule of the Romano-Germanic family."
  3. Procedure-first. "Matters relating to the administration of justice, procedure, evidence and execution of judgments have, for Common law lawyers, an importance equal, or even superior, to substantive legal rules because, historically, their immediate pre-occupation has been to re-establish peace rather than articulate a moral basis for the social order."

2.2 The link with royal power — Common law as a public law

"The origins of the Common law are linked to royal power. It was developed as a system in those cases where the peace of the English kingdom was threatened, or when some other important consideration required, or justified, the intervention of royal power. It seems, essentially, to be a public law, for contestations between private individuals did not fall within the purview of the Common law courts save to the extent that they involved the interest of the crown or kingdom."— David & Brierley

"The divisions of the Common law, its concepts and vocabulary, and the methods of the Common law lawyer, are entirely different from those of the Romano-Germanic family."

2.3 Expansion of the family — and its fragmentation

The two engines — colonisation and reception

"As with the Romano-Germanic family, so too the Common law has experienced a considerable expansion throughout the world — and for the same reasons: colonization or reception. The observations made with respect to the Romano-Germanic family apply with equal value."

Europe v. outside Europe — a crucial division

"A distinction between the Common law in Europe (England and Ireland) and that outside Europe must be made. In certain extra-European countries, the Common law may have been only partially received — as, for example, in certain Muslim countries or India — and where it was received, attention must be given to its transformation or adoption by reason of its co-existence with the tradition of previous civilizations."

The American example — autonomy within the family

"A different environment has, in any event, created differences between the Common law of the countries where it originated and that of those into which it was imported. This observation is particularly true with respect to the Common law family because it groups some countries such as the United States and Canada where a civilization different in many respects from that of England has developed. The laws of these countries enjoy a largely autonomous place within the family."

Three lessons for Indian answers: (1) India is a case of partial reception + co-existence with previous civilisations — exactly the framework Menon uses for the "alien system" critique; (2) a legal family is not a monolith — the US/Canada enjoy autonomous places even inside it; (3) the same must be asked of India: has a different environment created an Indian common law "substantially different from its English model"?

03(b) Civil Law Legal System — the Romano-Germanic Family

3.1 Definition, membership and the scholars' science

"A first family may be called the Romano-Germanic family. This group includes those countries in which legal science has developed on the basis of Roman jus civile. Here the rules of law are conceived as rules of conduct intimately linked to ideas of justice and morality. To ascertain and formulate these rules falls principally to legal scholars who, absorbed by this task of enunciating the 'doctrine' on an aspect of the law, are somewhat less interested in its actual administration and practical application. These matters are the responsibility of the administration and legal practitioners."— David & Brierley

Membership: the countries "in which legal science has developed on the basis of Roman jus civile" — most of continental Europe, and (by colonisation or reception) vast territories across Latin America, parts of Africa and Asia. The name itself "is selected to acknowledge the joint effort of the universities of both Latin and Germanic countries."

3.2 Four distinctive features (the exam checklist)

1. Rules of conduct, not trial-solutions

The legal rule is a general rule of conduct — "intimately linked to ideas of justice and morality." Contrast the Common law's rule which "seeks to provide the solution to a trial." The civil-law judge's reasoning runs from the general rule to the case; the common lawyer's from case to rule. (The reading's word for the civil-law rule: much more abstract.)

2. Doctrine leads; practice follows

"To ascertain and formulate these rules falls principally to legal scholars… absorbed by this task of enunciating the 'doctrine'." Application is "the responsibility of the administration and legal practitioners" — a division of labour unknown to the Common law, where the judges themselves made the law.

3. Private law first

"The law has evolved, primarily for historical reasons, as an essentially private law, as a means of regulating the private relationships between individual citizens; other branches of law were developed later, but less perfectly, according to the principles of the 'civil law' which today still remains the main branch of legal science." (Compare Common law's public-law origin; the two families are mirror-images.)

4. Codes since the nineteenth century

"Since the nineteenth century, a distinctive feature of the family has been the fact that its various member countries have attached special importance to enacted legislation in the form of 'codes'." The code is the scholar's science made statute — the concrete link between university doctrine and state law.

3.3 Reception in the non-European world — the three scenarios

"Through colonization by European nations, the Romano-Germanic family has conquered vast territories where the legal systems either belong or are related to this family. The phenomenon of voluntary 'reception' has produced the same result in other countries which were not colonized, but where the need for modernization, or the desire to westernize, has led to the penetration of European ideas." But outside Europe the laws, "although retaining membership in the Romano-Germanic family, nonetheless have their own characteristics which, from a sociological point of view, make it necessary to place them in distinct groups."

3.4 Test yourself — the four mini-answers

  1. Why "Romano-Germanic"? Because the family was built by the joint scholarly effort of the universities of both Latin and Germanic countries from the twelfth century, on the basis of Justinian's compilations.
  2. Why is doctrine central? Because ascertaining and formulating the rules "falls principally to legal scholars" — the code is the product of a science, not of litigation.
  3. Why is civil law "private law" first? For "primarily historical reasons" it evolved as regulation of private relationships; public branches came later "but less perfectly."
  4. Why codes? Since the nineteenth century member countries "attached special importance to enacted legislation in the form of codes" — the systematised, accessible, general form of the scholar's law.

04(c) Hybrid Legal Systems — the Mixed Systems and the Convergence of the West

4.1 The hybrid idea

"The inclination to speak of a family of western law is all the stronger when one considers that the laws of some states cannot be annexed to either family, because they embody both Romano-Germanic and Common law elements. The laws of Scotland, Israel, the Union of South Africa, the Province of Quebec and the Philippines would fall into this group."— David & Brierley

Definition for the exam: a hybrid (or mixed) legal system is one which "cannot be annexed to either family" because it "embodies both Romano-Germanic and Common law elements." Hybridity is thus not a third family but a compositional fact: the system combines civil-law (codified, doctrinal, Romanist) and common-law (judge-made, procedural, English) elements within one legal order.

4.2 The convergence — "one great family of western law"

"Over the centuries there have been numerous contacts between countries of the Romano-Germanic family and those of the Common law, and the two families have tended, particularly in recent years, to draw closer together. In both, the law has undergone the influence of Christian morality and, since the Renaissance, philosophical teachings have given prominence to individualism, liberalism and personal rights. Henceforth, at least for certain purposes, this reconciliation enables us to speak of one great family of western law."— David & Brierley

The five forces of convergence

  1. Contacts between the two families' countries over the centuries — "numerous."
  2. Christian morality — a shared moral substrate in both.
  3. Renaissance philosophy — "individualism, liberalism and personal rights" gained prominence in both.
  4. Method, not structure — "the Common law retains, to be sure, its own particular structure, very different from that of the Romano-Germanic system, but the methods employed in each are not wholly dissimilar."
  5. Convergent rule-formation — "Above all, the formulation of the legal rule tends more and more to be conceived in Common law countries as it is in the countries of the Romano-Germanic family" (i.e., statutory-general formulation grows in the common-law world).

And its limit (the reading's discipline)

  • Convergence is "at least for certain purposes" — not the dissolution of the families.
  • "The Common law retains, to be sure, its own particular structure, very different from that of the Romano-Germanic system."
  • What converges is substance and method: "As to the substance of the law, a shared vision of justice has often produced very similar answers to common problems in both sets of countries."
  • The word "western" is doing quiet work: the "great family" is a Western coalition; what the hybrid states and the non-Western systems show is that the map does not end here.

4.3 The second hybridity — and the socialist inversion

The reading notes a second grouping from "another point of view": "the Romano-Germanic and Common law families are included in the same deliberately ignominious term of 'capitalist' or 'bourgeois laws' by jurists of the socialist camp, made up of the Soviet Union and those countries that have used its law as a model or which, like the U.S.S.R., profess an adherence to Marxist-Leninist teachings."

Use this in any "hybrid" or "classification" answer as the closing move: hybridity is not only compositional (both families' elements in one system) but also perspectival — the socialist jurist sees the two Western families as one object (bourgeois law). Classification itself depends on the classifier's standpoint. This sentence is also the doorway into Section 05: the Socialist family is the third principal family, and it is defined precisely by its opposition to the other two.

5.2 Islamic law (with Hindu and Jewish law) — the "model law" tradition

Why a Western jurist can understand it

"The attitude of the Muslim, Hindu and Jewish communities about the law is easily understood by a western jurist, even though the definition of law itself in western jurisprudence has always given rise to difficulties and no single definition has so far elicited any general acceptance. One of the fundamental reasons for this lack of agreement is the debate between the proponents and adversaries of the notion of 'natural law'. But it is because the idea of 'natural law' exists that we are able to understand the starting premise of these other systems."

The two-faced debate (and where each side stands)

Positivist face: "law is held by some to be no more than the body of rules that are really observed, the application of which is entrusted to the courts. This is the view today in our western universities in which our national laws are taught."

Ideal-law face: "law may also be seen as a model of ideal behaviour, one not to be confused with the actual rules by which individuals act which courts apply. European universities, in their pre-nineteenth-century tradition, paid very little attention to national or customary laws of the time and taught, almost exclusively, an ideal law constructed on the basis of Roman law."

"In Muslim countries, in the same way, more attention is given to the model law linked to the Islamic religion than to local custom (treated as a phenomenon of fact) or the laws and decrees of the sovereign (treated as merely administrative measures) and neither of these is thought to possess the full dignity of law. The same can be said of Jewish law and, in a very different context, Hindu law."— David & Brierley

The consequences (learn these five)

  1. Ideal law ≠ observed law. "Law, then, whether linked to a religion or corresponding to a particular way of thinking about the social order, is not in either case always necessarily observed by private persons or applied by courts. It may nonetheless exert considerable influence on both" — "righteous men may endeavour to rule their own lives according to what they consider to be truly the law."
  2. The Western equivalence does not hold. "In western societies there is a large degree of equivalence between justice, positive law and social manners. The same cannot however be said of non-western societies where 'rules of law' (in the western sense) remain unorganized, fragmentary and unstable, and where there is generally feeling that true law is to be found elsewhere than in legislation, custom or judicial decisions."
  3. And yet they are legal systems. "Without taking sides in the debate between positivists and advocates of natural law, Muslim and Hindu law, therefore, must be included within the major contemporary legal systems."
  4. Jewish law is excluded — on reach, not worth. "Jewish law, despite its historical and philosophical interest, must be omitted because its sphere of influence is incomparably less than that of the other two."
  5. Partial reception confirms the distinction. In Muslim countries the reception of European law "has been only partial, leaving some legal relations subject to the principles of the traditional, local law" (§03.3) — the model law refused to yield entirely.

Connect to Unit-2 (same casebook): this is the comparative-legal-systems face of the Ṛta/Dharma thesis — Hindu law's "true law found elsewhere than in legislation" is exactly Sudame's dharma (a command, not a statute), Seema Singh's Ṛta (the Grundnorm behind all enacted law), and Kautilya's hierarchy (Dharma > Vyavahāra > Charitra > Rājaśāsana). You can use this reading to answer the examiner's favourite follow-up: "is Hindu law a legal system?" — David & Brierley: yes, it must be included among the major contemporary legal systems.

5.3 The Socialist legal family

Its status and ancestry

"The Socialist legal system makes up a third family, distinct from the first two. To date the members of the socialist camp are those countries which formerly belonged to the Romano-Germanic family, and they have preserved some of the characteristics of Romano-Germanic law. Thus, the legal rule is still conceived in the form of a general rule of conduct; and the divisions of law and legal terminology have also remained, to a very large extent, the product of the legal science constructed on the basis of the Roman law by European universities."— David & Brierley

The family is therefore derived but detached: "apart from points of similarity, there do exist such differences that it seems proper to consider the socialist laws as detached from the Romano-Germanic family — the socialist jurist most decidedly do — and as constituting a distinct legal family, at least at the present time."

Six marks of originality

  1. Revolutionary character — "in opposition to the somewhat static character of Romano-Germanic laws, the proclaimed ambition of socialist jurists is to overturn society and create the conditions of a new social order in which the very concepts of state and law will disappear."
  2. Sole source: the legislature — "The sole source of Socialist rules of law resides therefore within the revolutionary work of the legislature, which expresses popular will, narrowly guided by the Communist Party."
  3. Law subordinate to economics — "legal science as such is not principally counted upon to create the new order: law according to Marxism-Leninism — a scientific truth — is strictly subordinate to the task of creating a new economic structure."
  4. Collectivised production — "all means of production have been collectivised. As a result the field of possible private law relationships between citizens is extraordinarily limited compared to the pre-Marxist period."
  5. Private law loses pre-eminence — "all law has now become public law." (The mirror-image of the Romano-Germanic family's private-law-first history.)
  6. A narrower realm of law — "This new concept removes from the very realm of law a whole series of rules which jurists of bourgeois countries would consider legal rules."

Its geography — three tiers

  • USSR — "The family of Socialist laws originated in the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics where these ideas prevailed and a new law has developed since the 1917 Revolution."
  • Socialist republics of Europe — "in those of the first group a greater persistence of characteristics properly Romano-Germanic is detected."
  • Socialist republics of Asia — "it is useful to enquire how these new concepts are reconciled in practice with the principles of Far Eastern civilization which governed such societies before the Socialist era" (the same question the Far East section poses of China's communism).

Exam note: two families have "the same" label from opposite directions — the socialist jurist uses the single contemptuous term "capitalist" or "bourgeois laws" for the two Western families together (§04.3). Always pair: (i) the socialist family is derived from the Romano-Germanic (general rules, divisions, terminology) and (ii) it is revolutionary and anti-private-law (all law = public law; state and law destined to disappear).

5.4 "Other systems" — the world beyond the three families

The claim of the three families' universality — and the answer

"The three families just described, each of which has numerous variants, are undoubtedly the three principal families of law existing in the contemporary world. Strictly speaking there is no law in the world today which has not drawn certain of its elements from one or other of these families. Some even hold the view that all other systems, no more than survivors from the past, will ultimately disappear with the passing of time and the progress of civilization."

"This attitude however proceeds from a rather naive sense of superiority and is really no more than an hypothesis, it does not acknowledge an observable reality in the modern world. All contemporary states have, it is true, taken over a number of western ideas either because it was necessary to preserve their independence or because it was useful in their internal development. It does not follow that the older ways of thinking which, not so very long ago, were widely accepted in these different societies have been totally abandoned. Everyone will recognise the superiority of western technology; opinions differ however on the superiority of western civilization taken as a whole. The Muslim world, India, the Far East and Africa are far from having adhered to it without reservation. These countries remain very largely faithful to [their own philosophies]."— David & Brierley

This is the reading's moral centre and the single best quotation for any "critically examine" question. Note the names in the list — India is among the civilisations "far from having adhered" to western civilisation "without reservation" — the hook on which Menon's and Sudame's readings hang.

The Far East — law put into question

"Here there is no question of studying an ideal law distant from rules laid down by legislators or simply followed in practice: here the very value of law itself has traditionally been put into question."

  • The Chinese view: "law is an instrument of arbitrary action rather than the symbol of Justice; it is a factor contributing to social disorder rather than to social order. The good citizen must not concern himself with law; he should live in a way which excludes any revindication of his rights or any recourse to the justice of courts. The conduct of individuals must, unfailingly, be animated by the search for harmony and peace through methods other than the law. Man's first concern should not be to respect the law. Reconciliation is of greater value than justice; mediation must be used to remove conflicts rather than invoking law to resolve them. Laws may exist to serve as a method of intimidation or as a model; but law is not made with a view to being really applied, as in the West. Scorn is reserved for those who aspire to regulate matters according to law…"
  • The traditional formula: "Countries of the Far East have, traditionally, held the view that law is only for barbarians."
  • Modern continuity: "The Chinese communist regime and the westernization of Japan have not fundamentally changed this conception rooted as it is in their ancient civilizations." China: "rejected the legal codes drawn up after the fall of imperial rule along western lines and, after some brief hesitation, then repudiated the Soviet method of building communism. The techniques finally adopted… have given up to the present time a very narrow place to law." Japan: "Codes on the European model have been instituted… but, generally speaking, the population makes little use of them; people abstain from using the courts and the courts themselves encourage litigants to resort to reconciliation; and new techniques have been developed for applying or removing the need of applying the law."

Black Africa and Malagasy — cohesion over law

"The preceding observations regarding the Far East apply as well to the black African countries and the Malagasy Republic (Madagascar). There too, in milieux in which the community's cohesion prevails over any developed sense of individualism; the principal objective is the maintenance or restoration of harmony rather than respect for law. The Western laws adopted in Africa are often hardly more than a veneer; the vast majority of the population still lives according to traditional ways which do not comprise what we in the West call law and without heed to what is very often nothing more than an artificially implanted body of rules."

06(e) Legal System in Ancient India — Dharmashaastra, the Constitutional Frame and the Courts

6.1 Why this reading, and what "shaastra" means

Legal education in India, Sudame opens, "is broadly based upon the Anglo-Saxon law. The foundations of western or Anglo-Saxon schools of jurisprudence are secure with the profundity of their philosophies. However, since they are born out of their indigenous customs; these do not find a natural blending with the Indian societal fabric." The "unparalleled dynamism of heterogenous Indian societal foundations" produces the daily challenges of the profession — and prompts the need "to get back to our roots – our indigenous knowledge systems flowing from the shaastras."

Shaastra = science

"The word 'shaastra' needs to be recognised as being synonymous with 'science' — an organised body of knowledge on a given subject. It is generally used as a suffix to the name of a specialised area of knowledge." Legal Methods itself, "as a shaastra, is an organised body of knowledge on the subject of law" — hence Dharmashaastra, Nyaayashaastra, Arthashaastra, Meemaamsa, the shaastras of law.

Anyamatapariksha — the forgotten Indian practice

"In this modern world, in the context of what was our age-old traditional practice anyamatapariksha (examination of parallel contemporaneous doctrines/knowledge systems/theories/opinions etc.), our indigenous knowledge systems have become the 'anyamata' and its study has now become a demand of globalization." Indian scholars should complement foreign-knowledge research by "exploring the parallel knowledge systems relying and banking on its own intellectual heritage… and borrowing from such other rich systems by interfacing them with our own system" — for the threefold purpose: (i) to revive our own identity; (ii) to find novel and indigenous solutions to contemporary problems; (iii) to bring our uniqueness to the global village.

6.2 Understanding Dharma — laying "dharmoprudential" foundations

"Dharma is one of those Sanskrit words that defy all attempts at an exact rendering in English or any other tongue. That word has passed through several vicissitudes."— Prof. P.V. Kane (as quoted by Sudame)
Purushārtha — the four ends
Right conduct (dharma), economic interests (artha), satisfaction of desires (kāma), liberation of spirit (moksha). The first three = Trivarga, meant for "the welfare and happiness of people"; this doctrine "sets humans apart from animals." Dharma is superior to all other objects; artha and kāma must always conform to dharma (law).
Dharma ≠ religion
"The precise import of dharma is not conveyed by the word 'religion' which is generally taken to denote a particular faith, dogma or doctrine, rites and rituals." "Dharma, in the case of Dharmashaastra, includes everything that a person ought to do in life… a code of conduct: it teaches both religious and secular matters."
Śrauta & Smārta
Dharma was divided into śrauta (based on the Vedas — rites and ceremonies from the Vedic Samhitas and Brahmanas, the "religious" aspect) and smārta (based on the Smritis — "the various classes and stages of life," from which "the basic code of conduct flows"). "In the context of law and legal methods, the term dharma is concerned and stays limited to the smārta aspect i.e. the law or code of conduct."

Two definitions you must quote

1. Madhvāchārya (Minister in the Vijayanagar Empire; commentary on Parāshara Smriti): "Dharma is that which sustains and ensures the progress and welfare of all in this world and eternal bliss in the other world." — "This definition of dharma was also adopted by the Supreme Court and was further briefly put as dharma is that 'which regulates the mutual obligations of individual and the society'." [A.S. Narayana Deekshitulu v. State of A.P., (1996) 9 SCC 548 — full brief in §09.]

2. Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (as quoted): "mere creation of kingship was not enough, the most excellent dharma (law) — a power superior to that of the king, was created to enable the king to protect the people… Law is the king of kings; nothing is superior to law." — "Dharma is promulgated in the form of commands."

Origin: dharma "originated in the form of a solution to the eternal problems confronted by human race owing to the natural human instincts." Manusmriti (quoted through Rama Jois): "There is no act of man which is free from desire; whatever a man does is the result of impulse or desire" — and fulfilment of desires and wealth is appreciated "only when these are in consonance with the law or dharma." Oxford dictionary (as quoted): dharma = "truth or law that affects the whole universe."

6.3 Indigenous sources of law — and their "recommendatory" character

The organisation of Sanskrit literature (the ladder of authority)

→

The hierarchy of sources (learn the priority)

"The Vedas and Smritis along with customs and usage are considered to be the primary source of dharma." Then the rules of conflict:

  1. Vedas > Smritis: "Amongst these, the Vedas are considered to be superior and are to prevail over the smritis in case of conflict."
  2. Equity/popular usage > conflicting Smritis: "in cases of conflict between two smritis, the principles of equity as determined by popular usages are to have an overriding effect."
  3. No royal discretion: "Thus, even in cases of conflicts between the sources of law, the king was not given a discretionary power but was required to apply the law as approved by custom and usage by people themselves."

The Supreme Court's caution (Abhiraj Kuer, AIR 1962 SC 351)

"The Supreme Court of India has dwelt upon the importance of these texts while recognising the recommendatory nature of dharmasutras and grihyasutras. It observed that while interpreting the books of morality, religion, law, etc., a great deal of caution is required to be exercised. The court also observed that the commentaries thereon are many-a-times mingled with statements of law but actually are intended to be recommendatory statements regarding the mandatory laws." [Full brief in §09.]

The Bombay High Court and Narada's rule of conflict (Dnyaneshwar B. Ganwat, 2010)

"The Full Bench preferred to accept the latter view… this approach was in consonance with what ancient Jurist Narada declared — Dharmashastra Virodhe Tu Yuktiyukta Vidhe Smrita — that is, when the Dharmashaastra or law codes of equal authority conflict with one another, the one appearing to be reasonable, or more reasonable, is to be preferred and followed. The views of modern Jurist Seervai, has also advocated a similar principle." — "It can be gathered how the judiciary has been taking conscious efforts to resort to indigenous sources of law for finding conformity in cases of conflict." [Full brief in §09.]

6.4 The sophistication of the ancient system — the frame

"The ancient Indian society had a highly evolved unique and sophisticated legal system. The study of this ancient system is important not just as a matter of history but to understand the intricacies of the Indian society, the circumstances that existed then, the nature of problems which were resolved, its stages of evolution, the levels that the judicial system has achieved in the past and the manner in which the problems were sought to be resolved. After all, the impact of all this evolution is the present day's growth and development."

"The importance of the study of Dharmashastra lies in the fact that the entire range of basic legal concepts of rights, duties, liabilities, powers, offences, punishments, constitutional, civil and criminal jurisprudence is embedded in it. The jurisprudence that has evolved today and is being followed by sovereign states in the form of various enactments finds its roots in the different facets of rajadharma and vyavahaaradharma."

6.5 Classification of Dharma — Rājadharma and Vyavahāradharma

Rājadharma — the king's constitutional law

  • "Rajadharma is the law laying down the powers, duties and responsibilities of the king. This is the basis of the modern-day constitutional law. It is supposed to be the fundamental law of the State, containing the principles upon which the government is founded, which provides for division of sovereign powers and specifies the powers of each of the division as also the manner in which the sovereign power is exercised."
  • "Rajadharma was the Constitution for all the States in the whole of India in the real sense of that expression."
  • "It was the firm view of ancient Indian thinkers that dharma (law) without Rajadharma for its enforcement (through instrumentality of the State) would only be an illusion."
  • Subjects of Rajadharma: legislative and executive powers, administration of justice, duties of the king (State), protection of property, treasury/finance, freedom and liberty, equality, etc.

Vyavahāradharma — the law of proceedings

  • "The system of civil and criminal legal proceedings is known as vyavahaaradharma. The king was to decide cases in accordance with the vyavaharadharma. The modern day civil and criminal laws have evolved from vyavahaaradharma."
  • "Vyavahaara means a law-suit or a legal proceeding. It is defined as proceedings in a court of law between two parties in which the violation of dharma is established by effort."
  • Origin: "When people were dharma abiding and truthful there existed neither hatred nor envy nor any legal disputes. Practice of dharma having declined in mankind, law suits (vyavahaara) were invented and the king was entrusted with the power to decide law-suits as he had the sanction of dharma to enforce obedience to, and to order punishment for disobedience of, dharma."

The 18 Vyavahārapadas — the ancient heads of litigation (quote at least ten)

"Earlier, eighteen topics of disputes or laws were identified and collectively known as 'vyavahaarapada'": 1. payment of debts (runadaan); 2. deposits (nikshepa); 3. sale without ownership (aswamy vikraya); 4. joint undertakings/partnerships (sambhuya samuthana); 5. resumption of gift (dattasyanapakarma); 6. payment of wages (vetanadaana); 7. violation of convention of guilds and corporations (samvidvyatikrama); 8. duties of husband and wife (stripumdharma); 9. partition (vibhaaga); 10. sale and purchase (krayavirayanusaya); 11. betting and gambling (dyutasamahavaya); 12. dispute between master and servant (swamipala vivaada); 13. boundary dispute (simavivaada); 14. defamation (vakparushya); 15. assault (dandaparushya); 16. theft (steya); 17. offence by violence (saahasa); 18. adultery (strisangrahana).

The classification within the classification (a favourite examiner's probe): "Out of these, the first fourteen topics are said to be arising out of wealth (arthasamutthaani) and the last four, out of violence (himsodbhavaani)." Matters omitted from the eighteen titles or doubtful were categorised as prakirnaka.

Also note the procedural division: "Out of these eighteen topics of litigation, thirteen were classified as civil disputes while five were categorised as crimes" (§06.7).

The founding quotation (Rama Jois, as quoted)

"Dharma in the context of legal and constitutional history only means Vyavaharadharma and Rajadharma evolved by the society through ages which is binding both on the king (the ruler) and the people (the ruled)."— Justice M. Rama Jois

Evolution in one line: "In the stateless society, people were acting in accordance with dharma and protected each other. It was a duty-based society. As time progressed and natural human instincts of desires and material wealth began to overwhelm the populace, the authority of king was created to protect the weak and punish the wrongdoers" — and at that point Rajadharma and Vyavaharadharma came to be established, "mark[ing] the commencement of India's legal and constitutional history."

6.6 Rule of law and separation of powers — the Parishad and the King

The Parishad and its exceptional competence — the ancient legislature

"The principal sources of law… were the shrutis, smritis, puranas, customs or usages. But in situations where the law was silent and required legislation, the power to lay down new legal provisions was vested in an assembly of persons known as parishad." The parishad consisted of at least ten members, competent in: (i) knowledge of one of the three Vedas; (ii) a logician; (iii) a Meemaamsaka; (iv) one who knows Niruktah (etymology); (v) one who recites the sacred law; (vi) three men belonging to the first three orders — a student-bachelor (Bramhachari), a householder (Grihastha), a hermit (Vanaprastha). "Further, in some exceptional cases, the power to decide complicated questions of law by resolving conflicts was also recognised in the parishad."

The King — the highest office under the law

"Rajadharma had given the king the highest position in the State. But dharma or law was placed even above the king. Therefore, the king had no power to legislate. Only in cases of absence of laws in the Shrutis, Smritis or customs, the king was empowered to decide according to his conscience." The king was entrusted with dual prime responsibilities: the highest executive (shaasaka) and the highest judiciary (nyaayadhishah). "Welfare of the people was to be the king's chief concern and their protection, his highest duty. Administration of justice was considered to be his personal responsibility."

The five fundamental duties of the king (quote as a list)

  1. To punish the wicked;
  2. To honour and protect the good;
  3. To enrich the treasury by just methods;
  4. To be impartial towards litigants; and
  5. Protection of the country.

The conclusion the reading draws: "This separation of powers between the parishad and the king ensured the existence of rule of law." Two modern doctrines in one breath: rule of law "flows from rajadharma which also made the king its subject," and separation of powers "was founded in rajadharma where the king had the power only to enforce the laws and not to legislate."

The People's Courts

"The first three courts from the bottom of the legal system were the Kula…, the Shreni… and the Gana… These three were known as the 'people's courts' and had the jurisdiction to decide all civil and criminal matters except those involving offences committed with violence."

Why this design matters (quote it): "This is a very peculiar way of gradation of courts wherein the various categories of impartial peers had the original jurisdiction to decide matters. This kind of a gradation encouraged cultural and professional diversity in courts which is one of the major requisites of judicial appointments even today. This diversity ensured the correct understanding of prevalent social and professional situations for every matter."

Hierarchy, appeals and the apex

"These courts had inter-se appellate jurisdictions. The Shreni could review the decision of Kula and the Gana had the power to review the decision of Shreni. Superior to the Gana was the court of Adhikrita or the court appointed by the king… [which] had the power to decide cases relating to commission of violent offences also. The highest court in hierarchy was the court of Nripa or the king himself. This court was known as Shaasita. Only the king had the authority to inflict fines and corporal punishments. Administration of justice was a matter solely looked after by this court and this function could not be delegated. The king was vested with the power to hear appeals from the decisions of any authority including Kula, Shreni and Gana."

Article 136 and the Law Commission — the ancient system's modern echo

"Article 136 of the Indian Constitution finds its roots in this provision" (the king's power to hear appeals from any authority). The Law Commission of India (in its report on "Delay and Arrears in Trial Courts") examined the indigenous judicial system and remarked: "With the progressing society, the panchayats and assemblies of brotherhood or of local respectables, however, did not cease functioning altogether because of their useful role of settling minor disputes in rural areas through compromise and that is why the British retained them through necessary legislation." (Cross-link: the same Law Commission passage supports the panchayat jurisprudence in the Unit-1 notes; and Vytla Sitanna, the Privy Council's Panchayat award case, is briefed there.)

Other courts — the classification

"Apart from these courts (known as Pratishthita) that were established in some village/town, there were mobile courts (known as Apratishthita) also. Mudrita were the courts that were appointed by the king and authorised to use the royal seal."

The last word of the Commission

"We have just noticed that the present judicial system is the result of a gradual process which has been going on incessantly, and that it is not the product of one day. Changes, modifications and amendments have been made both in the hierarchy of courts as well as in the procedures followed by them, as the society gradually became more and more developed."— Law Commission (quoted via Kerala Solvent Extractions v. A. Unnikrishnan)

All were appointed by the king himself.

6.9 The proceedings — the four parts, and the four kinds of decision

Dharmādhikaraṇa — the court hall

"The court hall was known as Dharmadhikarana. The king could appoint Judges or Sabhyas as the members of Courts of Justice. In the absence of the king, the Chief Justice could preside over the court." A singular detail — proof of the separation of functions: "while acting as a Judge, the king was to be in a simple dress, without ornaments and with a pleasant demeanour. This was to create distinction between judicial and executive functions."

The four sequential parts of a proceeding

  1. Purvapaksha — case of the first party (vaadin);
  2. Uttara — reply of the defendant (prativaadin);
  3. Kriya — trial of the case;
  4. Nirnaya — the judicial decision.

(The mirror of the modern structure: plaint → written statement → trial → judgment.)

The four types of judicial decision (remedies)

  1. Dharma — where the wrongdoer admitted or confessed his guilt;
  2. Vyavahara — after adducing evidence, where parties made conflicting statements;
  3. Charitra — following the precedent of an earlier decision or a usage squarely applying;
  4. Rajashasana — the king's judgment where there was no legal principle applicable or no evidence adduced.

"The guiding principles for application of mind in choosing one of the above ways of deciding a matter were laid, thereby securing the system from the menace of arbitrariness."

7.2 Tantrayukti — the Indic model of research methodology & scientific writing

What it is

"Tantrayukti… is a combination of two words: 'tantra' and 'yukti'. 'Tantra' means a scientific work (root 'tan' = to compose, to write; tantra "holds the diffused elements or the expanse of a subject"). 'Yukti' connotes a device or a means (root 'yuj' = to arrange). Tantrayukti can thus be said to literally mean 'the arrangement of a scientific work'. These are the devices that need to be employed while composing or reading a scientific work. It is not possible to compose or construe any scientific or organised work without these devices."

The thirty-two devices (yuktis)

subject (adhikarana), arrangement (vidhaana), union of words (yoga), category (padaartha), implication (hetvaartha), enunciation (uddesha), declaration (nirdesha), instruction (upadesha), specification (apadesha), extended application (atidesha), determination from a statement to be made (pradesha), analogy (upamaanam), presumption (arthaapattih), doubt (samshaya), connected argument (prasanga), reversion (viparyaya), context (vaakya-shesha), assent (anumata), description (vyaakhyaana), etymological explanation (nirvachana), example (nidarshana), exception (apavarga), special term (sva-samjna), question/prior-view (purva-paksha), reply (uttara-paksha), certain (ekaanta), anticipation (anaagatavekshana), retrospection (atikraantavekshana), injunction (niyoga), alternative (vikalpa), aggregation (samuchchaya), ellipsis (uhya).

Customisation — the number depends on the work

  • Brief work / limited-scope paper: subject (adhikarana), etymological explanation (nirvachana), question/prior-view (purva-paksha), doubt (samshaya), presumption (arthaapattih), example (nidarshana), and assent (anumata)/reply (uttara-paksha).
  • Detailed work: add anticipation (anaagatavekshana), retrospection (atikraantavekshana), extended application (atidesha), special term (sva-samjna).
  • The basic steps of any work: subject matter (jijnasavishaya/adhikarana) → doubt (samshaya) → questions/objections/prior-view (purva-paksha) → thesis (siddhaantah) → review (samanvaya).

"This model of research methodology is highly adaptable to the nature of a text… the yuktis should be studied systematically and employed cautiously, judiciously and discriminatingly."

The two functions

1. Arrangement of sentences (Vaakya-yojana) — a yukti bringing about "a coherent or meaningful connection between/among the apparently unconnected sentences." It relates to the form of the treatise: yoga, uddesha, nirdesha etc.

2. Organisation of meanings (Artha-yojana) — the yukti which "reveals the concealed meaning and which shows that it is consistent with the context." It relates to the matter of the text: adhikarana, padaartha, uhya. The meanings revealed are of four types: (i) that which cannot be explicitly expressed; (ii) that which appears contradictory; (iii) that which is only slightly expressed; (iv) that which is expressed through peculiar words.

Utility — and the quotation to remember: the yuktis are "like a substantial academic index to the work"; they guide the writer's vidhaana (arrangement) and the reader's decipherment. "Just as the sun causes a bed of lotuses to blossom or just as a lamp lights up a house, so also the tantrayuktis shed light on the meanings of the scientific subjects." Why now: the Supreme Court has repeatedly observed problems of legal drafting — "lack of precision in expression, legal language and the complications that it entails" — and has remarked that "writing lengthy judgments particularly while deciding interim matters is the main cause of delay in disposal of cases" (citing Rajesh Kumar v. Rajendra Kumar Saxena); hence the need to "look into parallel knowledge systems."

7.3 Epistemology and appreciation of evidence — the Prāmaṇa theory

The problem

"The object of any legal proceeding is the determination of rights and liabilities which depend upon determination of facts. It is the law of evidence which consolidates methods and techniques of classification of facts and appreciation of evidence… Epistemology is a branch of knowledge that theorizes knowledge itself. When principles of epistemology are applied to the field of law, the branch is known as legal epistemology… it defines and classifies knowledge and goes ahead to give parameters for its validity and invalidity." Every Indic philosophical system — "Nyaaya-Vaisheshika, Saankhya-Yoga, Puurva-Uttar Meemaamsa" — has its own recognised set of pramaanas (sources of knowledge).

KNOWLEDGE
→
VALID SOURCES (prāma): Pratyaksham (perception) · Anumaanam (inference) · Upamaanam (comparison) · Shabdah (verbal testimony)
[N.S. 1.1.3: प्रत्यक्षमनुमानमुपमानं शब्दः प्रमाणानि]
→
INVALID SOURCES (aprāma): Samshayah (doubt) · Viparyaya (error) · Tarkah (hypothetical assumption)

Why it matters in court

  1. "In matters of evidence, it is only upon the Court's belief in the existence of a fact that it can be said to be 'proved'" (Section 3, Indian Evidence Act, 1872) — hence the question of evaluation is really a question of validly sourcing knowledge.
  2. The Supreme Court has enforced the same discipline for affidavits: Order 19, Rule 3 CPC "requires the deponent to disclose the nature and source of his knowledge with sufficient particularity" (Sukhwinder Pal Bipan Kumar v. State of Punjab, (1982) 1 SCC 31 — full brief in §09).
  3. The critique: legal epistemology is "a lesser explored area in modern Indian legal education. Legal methods without epistemic foundations will keep a legal researcher distant from understanding the validity, invalidity, classification of knowledge and thereby result in increasing subjectivity while placing reliance upon a particular source of knowledge."

7.4 Logic and legal reasoning — Nyāya's five-member syllogism

First, the distinction (say it, don't blur it)

"Logic is concerned with the validity of the conclusion based on the proposition and reason employed. It is a technical and formal process of arriving at a conclusion according to principles of validity. Logic ensures the stability of an argument or a judicial-decision. Legal reasoning can be said to be a part of the logical process… consists of reasons employed from the legal framework in view of the facts and circumstances of a case. This step also consists of the less technical touchstones of humanitarian approaches which require the exercise of judicial discretion."

Svaartha- and Paraartha-anumaana

Inferences may be drawn "for one's own self (svaartha-anumaanam)" or "for other's sake (paraartha-anumaanam)." The latter involves "a much more formal procedure": demonstrate the proposition, the reasoning, cite examples of similar nature, prove their applicability, and thereby conclude — "all these steps are required to be organised in specific logical method to have a correct sthaapana of the argument… developed through a syllogism."

The Pancha-avayava — Nyāya's five-member syllogism (learn the five in order)

1. PROPOSITION (Pratijna)
the declaration of what is to be established
→
2. REASON (Hetuh)
the statement of the reason/means of establishing what is to be established
→
3. EXAMPLE (Drshtaantah)
illustration of the proposition; provides a supporting reason
→
4. APPLICATION (Upanayah)
states the validity of the example so cited
→
5. CONCLUSION (Nigamanam)
re-stating/confirmation of the proposition after the reason has been mentioned

Worked illumination of the difference (the reading's own point): a syllogism for arguing and one for decision-making differ in structure depending on their purpose — but "for the purpose of legal reasoning in argumentation or decision-making, one needs to use all the elements of a syllogism."

The controlling authority: "Reliefs granted by courts must be seen to be logical and tenable within the framework of the law… It is essential to maintain the integrity of legal reasoning and the legitimacy of the conclusions. They must emanate logically from the legal findings and the judicial results must be seen to be principled and supportable on those findings" (Kerala Solvent Extractions Ltd. v. A. Unnikrishnan, (2006) 13 SCC 619). — "Reasons are the lifelines of legal arguments and judgments."

7.5 The five fallacies (Hetvābhāsā) — critical thinking's Indic toolkit

"Critical thinking skills in law can be enhanced not just by acquiring the knowledge of components of legal reasoning. It needs to be supplemented with the sensitization regarding the various fallacies of reasoning… [which] equip a researcher of law with sharpened rational faculties… to [1] steer clear of them in legal reasoning… and [2] identify them in the course of legal research."

1. Savyabhichaarah
erratic reason
the reason that wanders — does not firmly prove
2. Viruddhah
the contradictory reason
proves the opposite of what is asserted
3. Prakaranasamah
reason equal to the question
assumes what it must prove
4. Saadhyasamah
the unproved reason

5. Kaalaatiitah
the mistimed reason
valid only when the time has passed

7.6 Mīmāṃsā — the indigenous parallel to Maxwell

The premise

"In India and globally, Maxwell's Principles of Interpretation are popularly resorted to… What needs to be put in perspective is the fact that these principles of interpretation are one of the systems of interpretation… What came to be ignored in the course of its appreciation was another system of interpretation which was awaiting contextualization in the field of law and, which was indigenous to us." — the Meemaamsa principles of interpretation developed by Jaimini in his sutras (though they existed prior to Jaimini). Created "for religious purposes like enabling correct performance of yagya," they were "adopted by various disciplines like philosophy, law, grammar etc." Jaimini defined duty as "that which is to be fulfilled as the object of a command" — and since "law and duty are inseparably connected terms that also reverberate with the concept of dharma, Jaimini is said to have also defined the term law by way of implication."

The Supreme Court's two rulings (quote both)

Surjit Singh v. MTNL, (2009) 16 SCC 722: principles of interpretation are "not principles of law but a methodology of explaining the meaning of words used in a text" — and "Any system of interpretation can be used which helps in solving a difficulty. In certain situations, Maxwell's principles would be more appropriate, while in other situations the Meemaamsa principles may be more suitable."

B. Premchand v. Mohan Koikal, (2011) 4 SCC 266: "Meemaamsa rules of interpretation were our traditional principles of interpretation used for thousands of years by our jurists. It is deeply regrettable that in our law courts today these principles are not cited. Today, our so-called educated people are largely ignorant about the great intellectual achievements of our ancestors, and the intellectual treasury which they bequeathed to us. The Meemaamsa rules of interpretation are one of these great achievements, but regrettably they are hardly ever used in our law courts. There is no reason why Meemaamsa principles of interpretation should not be used in appropriate occasions. It is not stated anywhere in the Constitution of India that only Maxwell's principles of interpretation can be utilised."

7.7 The Mīmāṃsā apparatus — classification of provisions

Obligatory texts (the commands)

  • Pradhan chodanas — primary commands, "in the form of one wide statement in the shape of a general injunction which expresses the whole of its object and purpose."
  • Vidhi — positive command: "You shall do it."
  • Nishedha — negative command: "You shall not do it." "The force of command of both is the same in principle."

Non-obligatory texts (attached to the commands)

  • Arthavaada — explanatory/illustrative text "connected with a vidhi… may explain the reason of a vidhi or illustrate it."
  • Namadheya — "descriptions of names… the same as the definition clauses in modern laws."
  • Niyama — directory rules: "subsidiary or incidental statements."
  • Paryudasa — provisos/qualified exceptions: "The rule of modern laws that a case falling under the proviso cannot come under the main clause and vice versa resonates with the concept of paryudasa."
  • Vidhivaishamya — inconsistency between provisions: "if the construction of one is accepted, then the other cannot stand. Such constructions are not permitted even under other parallel systems of interpretations."
  • Nyaaya — maxims: "devices that can be used to interpret a provision or explain the given case forcefully… correspond to the term 'maxims'… of immense importance just like the Latin maxims." (Some govern interpretation; some govern general experience.)
  • Pratyaksha / Kalpya — express provisions (pratyaksha) and implied rules (kalpya).

7.9 The six axioms of interpretation (Jaimini) — with their Maxwell correspondences

AxiomMeaningThe reading's modern correspondence
1. Sarthakya"Every word and sentence must have some meaning and purpose. If there are more words, there will be more meanings. Any interpretation rendering a part of the provision otiose is a faulty interpretation known as anarthakya-dosha."Maxwell's literal rule of interpretation.
2. Laghava"Where one rule or proposition would suffice, more must not be assumed… the construction which makes the meaning simpler and shorter is to be preferred.""The rule that construction should not be burdensome."
3. 4. The Supreme Court's application — Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. v. Essar Power Ltd., (2008) 4 SCC 755

Interpreting the Electricity Act, 2003, the Court "explained and applied the axioms of samanjasya, vikalpa and gunapradhan. It held that Sections 174 and 175 of the Electricity Act, 2003 can be read harmoniously by applying samanjasya rule. Further, it held that Section 174 is the pradhan whereas Section 175 is the guna or subordinate. Therefore, Section 175 (the guna or subordinate) has to be adjusted to make it in consonance with Section 174 (the pradhan or principal)." [Full brief in §09.]

7.10 The conclusion of the reading — akhanda-vidya

"The Indian systems of knowledge have not just comprehensively but exhaustively dealt with the subjects of law, logic and their interpretative techniques. The need of the hour is to revive and put the Shaastric parameters embedded in Dharmashaastra, Nyayashaastra, Meemaamsa etc. to practical utility in the field of law. Introducing Shaastric legal education shall promote 'akhanda-vidya' i.e. analysing the object or issue as a whole as opposed to segregating it into subjects that are allied to it… This leads to the creation or dissemination of an all-encompassing profound body of knowledge related to that subject and enables one to 'think like a lawyer' in the truest sense. In fact, the present-day trend of a 'multi-disciplinary' research finds its roots in this concept of akhanda-vidya. The revival of our indigenous knowledge system in the field of law and thereby aiding India's sustenance in the globalised world is promised by this revolution of Shaastric legal education."— Ila Sudame (conclusion)

Exam summary of §07 in three lines: Tantrayukti = Indic research methodology (32 devices; arrangement of sentences + organisation of meanings); Prāmaṇa = Indic epistemology for evidence (4 valid, 3 invalid sources of knowledge); Pañcāvayava = Nyāya's five-member syllogism for legal reasoning (+ 5 hetvābhāsā fallacies); Mīmāṃsā = the indigenous interpretation system (adhikaraṇa procedure; Śruti–Liṅga–Vākya–Prakaraṇa; six axioms) — recognised, and applauded, by the Supreme Court itself.

08(e, contd.) Our Legal System — N.R. Madhava Menon

8.1 The opening proposition — and its two-edged consequence

"The legal system of a country is part of its social system and reflects the social, political, and cultural characteristics of that society. It is, therefore, difficult to understand the legal system outside the socio-cultural milieu in which it operates. It is true in the case of India also even though the legal system we now have is largely the gift of the British rulers. There is a view that the system is still alien to the majority of Indians whose legal culture is more indigenous and whose contact with the formal legal system (the imported British model) is marginal if not altogether non-existent. The language, technicality and procedure of the inherited legal system are indeed factors which limit access to justice for the illiterate, impoverished masses of our country. Nevertheless, the rights and benefits conferred by the laws and the Constitution offer the opportunity for those very people to enjoy the fruits of a welfare democracy which the people of India have given unto themselves on the 26th January 1950. It is in this context familiarity with law and its processes becomes essential to every Indian, rich or poor, man or woman, young or old."— N.R. Madhava Menon

Frame it as the reading's own resolution: the inherited system is alien in form (language, technicality, procedure) but owned in substance (the Constitution is self-given — "which the people of India have given unto themselves"). That is why "familiarity with law" is essential for every Indian. Also note the direct echo of David & Brierley's India remark (§02.3: partial reception + co-existence with previous civilisations) and of Sudame's Anglo-Saxon-vs-Indigenous diagnosis (§06.1).

8.3 The Constitution: the fundamental law of the land

What it is

  • "The Constitution of a country is variously described depending upon the nature of the policy and the aspirations of the people"; "generally a written document"; federal (several independent units joined together) or unitary. India: Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic — quasi-federal structure.
  • "The Constitution of India represents the collective will of 700 million Indians and, as such, the reservoir of enormous power. It describes the methods by which this power conferred on the State is to be exercised for the benefit of the people."
  • "It is a political document which distributes State power amongst different organs (Central and State Governments; Legislative, Executive and Judicial wings of each Government) and regulates its exercise in its incidence on the people."
  • Form of government: democratic and republican; method: parliamentary through adult franchise.

The goals: Preamble + Fundamental Rights + DPSP

Preamble: "Justice, social, economic and political; Liberty of thought, expression, faith and worship; Equality of status and of opportunity, and to promote among them all"; "Fraternity assuring dignity of the individual and the 'unity and integrity of Nation'."

Fundamental Rights (the reading's own list): (a) equality before law; (b) equality of opportunity in matters of public employment; (c) prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, sex etc.; (d) protection of life and personal liberty; (e) protection of the right to freedom of speech, assembly, association, movement, profession or occupation; (f) prohibition of forced labour; (g) right to freedom of religion; (h) protection of interests of minorities; (i) right to constitutional remedies for enforcement of the above.

DPSP: "the Constitution gives certain Directives to the State to follow in its policies and programmes. Principles of State Policy have been recognised to be as sacrosanct as Fundamental Rights… they together constitute a reference for State action in every sphere."

Enforcement: FRs enforceable through the High Courts and the Supreme Court.

The judiciary's unique place — and the reading's key sentence

"The Constitution envisages a unique place for the judiciary. Apart from overseeing the exercise of State power by the Executive and the Legislatures of the State and the Central Governments, the Supreme Court and the High Courts are charged with the responsibility of effectively protecting citizens' rights through its writ jurisdiction. This offers a cheap and expeditious remedy to the citizen to enforce the guaranteed rights. The Supreme Court liberalised the rules so as to enable poor and illiterate citizens to have easy access to courts for enforcing their basic rights."

"The Rule of Law is supreme, and the independence of judiciary is reality in our country. This forms the bulwark of democracy and compels everyone to abide the law… must be understood and subscribed to by every Indian if we are to succeed in our declared goals."— Menon

8.4 Laws, civil and criminal

"The laws of the country are too numerous, varied and complex; they are bound to be so because law is as large as life itself… In a Welfare State like ours, laws are the more so because they are expected to regulate a variety of social and economic activities so as to subserve the common good." Parliament, State legislatures and local councils "make and unmake the laws day in and day out"; "Courts interpret them in specific fact situations and, in the process, extend the scope and application of the laws"; the common man adds to the maze "by creating his own laws through contracts and agreements."

Civil law — the six or seven major headings

  • Family law — source both statute and religion: marriage, divorce, maintenance, custody of children, adoption, inheritance and succession. "Though the Constitution envisages a Uniform Civil Code, each religious group at present follows largely its own norms in matrimonial and family relations."
  • Law of property — rights of ownership, transfer, mortgages, trusts, intestacy.
  • Law of contract — enforcement of obligations arising from agreements and promises: sale of goods, loans of money, partnerships, insurance, guarantees, negotiable instruments, agency.
  • Law of torts — "propriety of actions and infraction of duties"; injuries to person or property "caused by failure to take reasonable care and caution" → actionable wrongs, "which usually compensates the victim."
  • Laws of commerce and business — economic operations of individuals, partnerships, companies and governmental regulation of them (taxation included).
  • Labour law — the relationship between employer and employees in the production and distribution of wealth.

Criminal law — its structure

  • Nature: public wrongs — "wrongs against the order and well being of the society in general"; "the persons guilty of such wrongs are prosecuted and punished by the State"; "specific and… defined in the Penal Code and a few other special and local laws."
  • Mens rea: "criminal laws insist (apart from a few exceptional offences) on a particular intent or state of mind as a necessary ingredient of a criminal offence. It also recognises degrees of criminality and gradations of crime."
  • Two principles: "Ignorance of law is never taken as an excuse. Certain situations where guilty intention could not have been entertained — such as infancy, insanity, mistake of fact, etc. — are recognised as defences to criminal responsibility."
  • Classification by object: crimes against the human body, property, reputation of the individual, against the State, against public rights.
  • Procedural classification: cognizable (police can investigate or arrest without judicial warrant) and non-cognizable; bailable and non-bailable; compoundable or otherwise.

8.5 Procedural law, civil and criminal

Civil procedure — the course of a suit

  1. Initiation: a suit before a civil court; procedure for trial, appeals, execution of decrees and orders laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure; jurisdiction variations under the Suits Valuation Act; court fees under the Court Fees Act; periods of limitation under the Limitation Act; relevancy, admissibility and probative value of evidence under the Evidence Act (civil and criminal).
  2. Nature: "adversary proceedings where two parties oppose each other."
  3. Pleadings: set out "the precise question in dispute or the cause of action"; the defendant may file a written statement admitting or denying the allegations; pleadings may be supplemented by admissions of fact, answers and interrogatories, oral statements, and admissions and denials of documents.
  4. Hearing: commences with service of the plaint copy; a party may appear personally, through an agent or a pleader — but "according to the Advocates Act, right to practise law before courts is given to Advocates only"; parties summon their witnesses; evidence recorded day-to-day; judgment pronounced in open court.
  5. Ending: civil proceedings being private, "they can at any time be abandoned or compromised"; judgments enforceable through the authority of the court; refusal to obey may lead to penal consequences; decrees appealable within the specified period.

8.7 The personnel of the law

"Administration of justice requires the co-operation not only of the parties and the judges but also of officers of court who include the Advocates, the court staff and the para-legal personnel who assist the lawyers and judges."

Judges

  • "All judicial officers from the Supreme Court Judge to the Munsiff in a small taluka are independent of both the legislature and the executive. They are free to administer law without fear or favour and they cannot be interfered with by anyone including the top functionary of the Government. They have the power to punish those who commit contempt of court or disobey their legitimate orders."
  • Appointment: President, acting on the advice of the Cabinet and the Chief Justice of India, appoints SC and HC judges; the Governor appoints State judicial officers "similarly on the advice of the State High Court/Government."
  • Security: "Their salaries and service conditions are determined by law and cannot be changed to their disadvantage. Their removal from service requires a special procedure and the control of their judicial functions vests on the higher judiciary."

Lawyers and the Bar

  • "Lawyers are the key functionaries assisting the judges in the administration of justice. They are officers of court and are constituted into an independent profession under an Act of Parliament — the Advocates Act, 1961. No others may practise before the courts."
  • Why they matter: "Without the expert assistance of lawyers on either side of a dispute, judges will find it difficult to find the truth on disputed facts in issue and interpret the law applicable to varied situations. CASE 1A.S. Narayana Deekshitulu v. State of Andhra Pradesh(1996) 9 SCC 548 · Supreme Court · K. Ramaswamy J.
Facts

Chapter IV of the Andhra Pradesh Charitable and Hindu Religious Institutions and Endowments Act, 1987 abolished the hereditary rights of religious office-holders (Archakas, Mirasidars — temple priests and servants), converting their perquisites into salary/honoraria and vesting administration in statutory authorities. A batch of petitions challenged the chapter's constitutional validity.

Issues

(1) Did the abolition of hereditary offices violate the freedom of religion under Articles 25 and 26? (2) Where lies the line between religion and its secular administration? (3) What is the meaning of "dharma" in constitutional discourse?

Arguments of the petitioners

Performance of pūjā and temple services is an essential religious practice integral to the Hindu faith; hereditary office carries religious dignity and customary honours which cannot be reduced to a salary; the Act touched the right of the denomination to manage its own religious affairs (Art. 26) and the individual's right to profess and practise religion (Art. 25).

Arguments of the State

Administration and management of temples and their properties is a secular activity which the State may regulate by legislation in the public interest; abolition of hereditary perquisites does not touch the essentials of belief or worship but only modernises remuneration, prevents exploitation and mismanagement, and protects the interests of devotees and the institution as a whole.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of Chapter IV, drawing the classic distinction between the religious and secular aspects of a temple. The judgment is famous for the Court's excursion into Hindu jurisprudence: it quoted Madhvāchārya (the Minister in the Vijayanagar Empire, in his commentary on the Parāshara Smriti) — "Dharma is that which sustains and ensures the progress and welfare of all in this world and eternal bliss in the other world" — and summarised dharma as that "which regulates the mutual obligations of individual and the society."

Principle evolved: Dharma is a live constitutional concept, not a museum piece: it "regulates the mutual obligations of individual and the society," connects the individual's duty to social welfare, and is invoked by the Supreme Court itself while adjudicating modern statutory disputes. (Also the anchor of Unit-1's eco-dharma discussion and Unit-2's Kartavya thesis — the same definition returns in every unit.)
CASE 2Abhiraj Kuer v. Debendra SinghAIR 1962 SC 351 · Supreme Court
Facts

A dispute over the validity of an adoption under the Hindu law of the Banaras school: the adopted person stood in a relationship akin to the prohibited degrees of marriage, and the adoption was challenged on that ground. Deciding the question required interpreting the texts of the Smritis and the commentaries, and distinguishing binding law from moral-religious exhortation.

Issue

Is the Smriti/commentary prohibition mandatory law or only a recommendatory precept — and how should "books of morality, religion and law" be interpreted?

Arguments of the appellant (challenging the adoption)

The śāstric text is prohibitory on its face; the rule against such affinity applies by assimilation to adoption; the commentary must be read as declaring mandatory law; hence the adoption is void.

Arguments of the respondent (upholding the adoption)

The statement is a moral or religious precept of a recommendatory nature, not a legal prohibition; long-standing usage of the school shows such adoptions were treated as valid; the true law is gathered from usage and from the authoritative commentaries read as a whole.

Decision and reasoning

The Court held the rule recommendatory, not mandatory, and upheld the adoption. The governing hermeneutic it laid down: "while interpreting the books of morality, religion, law, etc., a great deal of caution is required to be exercised"; the commentaries "are many-a-times mingled with statements of law but actually are intended to be recommendatory statements regarding the mandatory laws" — the commentator may be describing an ideal, not prescribing a command.

Principle evolved: Recommendatory v. mandatory — the master-key for reading the Dharmasāstra as a source of law. A statement in a Smriti or commentary is not automatically law; the court must distinguish what prescribes (mandatory) from what praises or idealises (recommendatory). This is the modern judiciary's method of handling the "model law" character of Hindu law that David & Brierley describe (§05.2) — and exactly what Sudame calls recognising the "recommendatory nature of dharmasutras and grihyasutras."
CASE 3Dnyaneshwar B. Ganwat v. Karyakari Abhiyanta, Chaskaman Prakalp Vibhag2010 SCC OnLine Bom 766 · Bombay High Court (Full Bench)
Facts

Before the Full Bench of the Bombay High Court arose a question — arising out of proceedings connected with the Chaskaman irrigation project — on which earlier decisions of the Court had taken two conflicting views (what Sudame describes as "law codes of equal authority"). The Bench had to choose between them.

Issue

When two equally authoritative statements of law conflict, which is to be followed — and by what criterion?

Arguments

For the view the Full Bench eventually adopted: it was in consonance with "what ancient Jurist Narada declared — Dharmashastra Virodhe Tu Yuktiyukta Vidhe Smrita — that is, when the Dharmashaastra or law codes of equal authority conflict with one another, the one appearing to be reasonable, or more reasonable, is to be preferred and followed." The same principle was "also advocated" by modern jurist H.M. Seervai, and was reinforced by the various other judgments considered by the Full Bench.

Decision

The Full Bench "preferred to accept the latter view" — the one harmonious with the Narada maxim and Seervai's principle — after considering the conflicting authorities.

Principle evolved: Reasonableness as the tie-breaker between conflicting authorities — a criterion stated by Narada, rediscovered by Seervai, and applied by a modern Full Bench. The significance for the unit: "It can be gathered how the judiciary has been taking conscious efforts to resort to indigenous sources of law for finding conformity in cases of conflict."
CASE 4Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. v. Essar Power Ltd.(2008) 4 SCC 755 · Supreme Court · Markandey Katju J.
Facts

Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. (a State utility) and Essar Power Ltd. were parties under the Electricity Act, 2003 regime and a power-purchase arrangement. The question was whether Section 174 of the Act (giving it an overriding effect over other laws) ousted the operation of other law — in particular the arbitration machinery under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — notwithstanding Section 175, which provides that the Act is "in addition to and not in derogation of" any other law.

Issues

(1) How is the apparent conflict between s.174 and s.175 to be construed — which is principal (pradhāna) and which subordinate (guṇa)? (2) Does the Arbitration Act's machinery survive the overriding clause?

Arguments of the appellant (GUVNL)

Section 174 makes the Electricity Act paramount over every other law; the arbitration provisions of the 1996 Act stand displaced; the matter must proceed exclusively under the Electricity Act regime.

Arguments of the respondent (Essar Power)

Section 175 expressly preserves other laws — the Act is only "in addition to" them; the 1996 Act squarely applies; the two sections are capable of harmony, and s.174 must not be read to swallow every incidental remedy.

Decision and reasoning

Katju J., for the Court, held that the Mīmāṃsā principles of interpretation are "of great utility" and applied them: (i) sāmāñjasya (harmony) — ss.174 and 175 "can be read harmoniously"; (ii) guṇapradhāna — "Section 174 is the pradhan whereas Section 175 is the guna (or subordinate)… Hence Section 174 will prevail over Section 175 in matters where there is any conflict (but no further)"; (iii) vikalpa — where truly irreconcilable, the option is exercised with reference to context. So the sections did not oust the Arbitration Act generally — s.174 prevails only to the extent of actual conflict.

Principle evolved: Mīmāṃsā is a working system of statutory interpretation in India's highest court. The axioms (śruti–liṅga–vākya–prakaraṇa as construction rules; sārthakya, lāghava, arthaikatva, guṇapradhāna, sāmāñjasya, vikalpa as axioms) are applied as living doctrine to a 2003 statute — the most concrete proof of Sudame's thesis that "the Indian knowledge system supplies method to modern law."
CASE 5B. Premchand v. Mohan Koikal(2011) 4 SCC 266 · Supreme Court · Markandey Katju J.
Facts

Dispute over seniority and promotion in a government service governed by Rule 27(c) of the relevant rules, which fixed seniority by reference to the date of the candidate's first effective advice by the Public Service Commission. The appellants, who were so advised earlier but with a later entry into service, claimed seniority ahead of respondents who had been selected earlier.

Issue

Is Rule 27(c) plain enough for the literal rule to decide the dispute, or must equitable considerations (the respondents' earlier selection/appointment) prevail?

Arguments of the appellants

Rule 27(c) is plain and clear; seniority is governed by the date of first effective advice; equity favouring the respondents cannot override the clear text — "if there is a conflict between equity and the law, it is the law which must prevail."

Arguments of the respondents

They were selected earlier by the PSC and, on that basis, claim a better equity; a construction favourable to them should be preferred.

Decision and reasoning

The Court applied the literal rule: "In our opinion, Rule 27(c) of the Rules is plain and clear. Hence, the literal rule of interpretation will apply to it." The judgment then expounds the philosophy of the literal rule ("we mean what we say and we say what we mean… if we do not follow the literal rule of interpretation, social life will become impossible") and — critically for this unit — links it to Mīmāṃsā: "In Mimansa, the literal rule of interpretation is known as the 'Shruti' or 'Abhida' Principle," illustrated by the Gārhapatya nyāya (where the word "aindrya" is a liṅga and "gārhapatyam upatiṣṭhate" is the śruti, the śruti prevails). The Court added its famous lament: "Meemaamsa rules of interpretation were our traditional principles of interpretation used for thousands of years by our jurists. It is deeply regrettable that in our law courts today these principles are not cited… There is no reason why Meemaamsa principles of interpretation should not be used in appropriate occasions. It is not stated anywhere in the Constitution of India that only Maxwell's principles of interpretation can be utilised."

Principle evolved: (1) Literal construction = Śruti rule — the Mīmāṃsā apparatus is not a competing exotic doctrine but the indigenous name for the canons courts already apply; (2) equity yields to clear law; (3) the Constitution imposes no monopoly of Maxwell — Mīmāṃsā is equally available, and should be used.
CASE 6Surjit Singh v. Mahanagar Telephone Nigam Ltd.(2009) 16 SCC 722 · Supreme Court (2008) · Markandey Katju J.
Facts

The appellant and his wife lived together at Rajouri Garden, Delhi. The premises had a telephone line in the appellant's name (No. 5121187) and another in his wife's name (No. 5416493); a third line (No. 3265301) stood in the appellant's name at his business premises. On default in payment of dues for the wife's line, MTNL disconnected both of the appellant's lines under Rule 443 of the Indian Telegraph Rules.

Issue

May a subscriber's telephone lines be disconnected for non-payment of dues on a line registered in the name of a relative living in the same house — and does it matter whether that relative is economically dependent?

Arguments of the appellant

He and his wife are two separate legal entities; Rule 443 permits disconnection only for the subscriber's own default; the definition of "subscriber" in Rule 2(pp) supports him — and a literal reading of Rule 443 confirms that he cannot be penalised for his wife's fault (relying on Dr. B.V. Manek AIR 1996 Bom 53 and Y. Pridhvi Kumar AIR 1993 AP 131).

Arguments of the respondent (MTNL)

The wife was a housewife with no independent source of income — economically dependent on the appellant, who in substance paid her bills; the Delhi High Court's consistent view (Madan Tayal; Rajiv Gosain; Sukh Dayal Narula) and the Gujarat High Court's view (Indravadan Pranlal Shah) support disconnection where the defaulting relative lives in the same premises; and the intention of Rule 443 — prompt payment — would be defeated by a strictly literal reading.

Decision and reasoning

The Court drew the distinction: where the defaulting relative is economically independent and paying his or her own bills, the other subscriber's line cannot be disconnected; where the relative is economically dependent (the wife here), the bills are in substance paid by the breadwinner, and disconnection of his lines is lawful. "In this case, the literal rule has not to be adopted, because we have also to see the intention of the rule… we have to adopt a purposive construction." Then the indigenous turn: "We can also utilise the Mimansa Rules of Interpretation in interpreting Rule 443" — the words of Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa's Param Laghu Manjūṣā give three meanings (abhidhā = literal; lakṣaṇā = indicative/suggestive; vyañjanā = figurative); "in the present case, we have to adopt the Lakshana (or Linga) rule of interpretation rather than the Shruti or Abidha (the literal) rule." The Court also invoked the Sphaḍi (wooden sword) nyāya — "what is prescribed as a means to an action is to be taken in a sense suited to the performance of the action" (Jaimini 3:1:2) — and the substitution principle (Jaimini 6:3:9).

Principle evolved: (1) Economic dependence is the test for treating one person as the "real" subscriber; (2) the literal rule is not always the only rule — purposive construction serves the intention of the rule; (3) Mīmāṃsā supplies the vocabulary for that purposive turn: liṅga/lakṣaṇā over śruti/abhidhā, and the nyāyas (maxims) interpret provisions "forcefully." The Court's general dictum is the one to quote: "Any system of interpretation can be used which helps in solving a difficulty. In certain situations, Maxwell's principles would be more appropriate, while in other situations the Meemaamsa principles may be more suitable."
CASE 7Kerala Solvent Extractions Ltd. v. A. Unnikrishnan(2006) 13 SCC 619 · Supreme Court (decided 1993)
Facts

On complaints that the respondent employee had secured employment by suppression of truth and false representation of his qualifications, the appellant issued a show-cause notice and, on the employee's admission that he had only completed the 10th standard, terminated his services for fraudulent misrepresentation. The single judge of the High Court, finding no hardship or prejudice, set aside the termination "as a special case and not by way of precedent," directing the worker's continuance; the Division Bench dismissed the writ appeal; the employer appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issue

May a writ court, on misplaced sympathy, grant relief which the law does not justify — and what does that do to the legitimacy of judicial reasoning?

Arguments of the appellant (employer)

The termination was lawful and squarely founded on the worker's own admission of fraudulent misrepresentation; the High Court's interference was justified by no legal principle — only by compassion; "misplaced sympathy" is not a ground for relief.

Arguments of the respondent (worker)

Leniency was deserved: no actual prejudice was shown; the worker had served for years; equitable considerations favour non-interference with the single judge's compassion.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court deprecated the tendency — "well-meant but wholly unsustainable" — towards "a denudation of the legitimacy of judicial reasoning and process": "The reliefs granted by the courts must be seen to be logical and tenable within the framework of the law and should not incur and justify the criticism that the jurisdiction of the courts tends to degenerate into misplaced sympathy, generosity and private benevolence. It is essential to maintain the integrity of legal reasoning and the legitimacy of the conclusions. They must emanate logically from the legal findings and the judicial results must be seen to be principled and supportable on those findings."

Principle evolved: Logical integrity is the precondition of judicial legitimacy. Relief must "emanate logically from the legal findings"; sympathy is not a legal ground. This is the modern positive-law face of the same discipline Sudame finds in the Nyāya syllogism: reasons are "the lifelines of legal arguments and judgments," and conclusions that are not justified are errors in judgment — the practical bridge between §07.4 (pañcāvayava) and the modern law of remedies.
CASE 8Sukhwinder Pal Bipan Kumar v. State of Punjab(1982) 1 SCC 31 · Supreme Court (three-Judge Bench)
Facts

Petitions under Article 32 of the Constitution supported by affidavits in which allegations were not properly verified — the deponents failing to state what was within their knowledge, what was on information, and the source of the information.

Issue

What must an affidavit contain to have probative value — and what is the consequence of an affidavit that does not disclose the nature and source of the deponent's knowledge?

Arguments

For strictness: an affidavit is a mode of placing evidence before the court; verification exists "to enable the Court to find out which facts can be said to be proved on the affidavit evidence of rival parties," to test genuineness and authenticity, and to make the deponent responsible; allegations must be affirmed as true to knowledge, or as "informed and verily believed," with the source disclosed so that the other side may "verify it and make an effective answer." Against: the petition's allegations were said to rest on information and belief honestly held; strict form should not defeat the substance.

Decision and reasoning

The Court held that under Order XIX, Rule 3 of the Civil Procedure Code it is incumbent upon the deponent to disclose the nature and source of his knowledge with sufficient particulars; where allegations are not so affirmed, the petition "cannot be treated as supported by an affidavit as required by law"; an affidavit which does not comply has no probative value and is liable to be rejected.

Principle evolved — and its epistemological kernel: a fact is proved only through a properly sourced claim to knowledge. Exactly as Sudame uses it: the law of evidence and the Indic prāmaṇa theory converge, because both demand that the claim-maker distinguish perception, information and belief, and disclose the source — otherwise the knowledge is suspect (doubt, error or mere tarka). The identification of the ancient "sources of valid knowledge" with the modern rules of proof is this unit's most elegant link between sub-topic (e) of Unit 3 and the syllabus's appellate/evidence modules.
CASE 9Rajesh Kumar v. Rajendra Kumar Saxena2012 SCC OnLine All 3966 (as cited in the casebook)
Facts

Cited by Sudame for a systemic observation rather than a doctrinal holding: the courts and the Supreme Court have repeatedly remarked on problems of legal drafting — "lack of precision in expression, legal language and the complications that it entails" — and on the judicial habit of writing lengthy judgments particularly while deciding interim matters.

Issue

What role does the craft of writing play in the administration of justice — and what follows from the fact that "the present-day trend of a 'multi-disciplinary' research finds its roots in akhanda-vidya" (§07.10)?

Decision and the reading's point

The observation attributed (through this citation) to the courts: writing lengthy judgments, especially on interim matters, is the main cause of delay in disposal of cases.

Principle evolved (for the exam): if the problem persists "despite the rich history to the subject of modern research methodology and scientific writing," then "it is certainly necessitated that we look into parallel knowledge systems and examine the best practices for effective content development and its expression" — i.e., the Tantrayukti discipline of arrangement (vākya-yojana) and meaning (artha-yojana) is a practical, administrative answer to judicial delay, and not merely an antiquarian curiosity.

Q.1  "Distinguish between the Common Law and the Romano-Germanic (Civil Law) families of law." 20 marks · 850 words

Introduction. David & Brierley open their comparative map with three "families" that "occupy an uncontested place of prominence" — Romano-Germanic, Common law and Socialist. The first two are the two great Western families, and the whole of their chapter is a controlled contrast between them. The point of departure is simply stated: the Common law was "formed primarily by judges who had to resolve specific disputes," while the Romano-Germanic family was built by the scholars of the universities on the basis of Roman jus civile.

1. The source of law — judges v. scholars. In the Common law, the rule "seeks to provide the solution to a trial rather than to formulate a general rule of conduct for the future"; it is "much less abstract" than the Romano-Germanic rule. In the civil-law family, "the rules of law are conceived as rules of conduct intimately linked to ideas of justice and morality," and their formulation "falls principally to legal scholars" absorbed in enunciating the "doctrine" — with administration and practical application left to "the administration and legal practitioners."

2. Substance v. procedure. For Common lawyers, "procedure, evidence and execution of judgments" have "an importance equal, or even superior, to substantive legal rules," because their historical "pre-occupation has been to re-establish peace rather than articulate a moral basis for the social order." The civil-law tradition reverses the ranking: doctrine first; and its "main branch" remains private law — the civil law — governing "private relationships between individual citizens."

3. Public v. private origin. "The origins of the Common law are linked to royal power" — it grew in cases "where the peace of the English kingdom was threatened"; it "seems, essentially, to be a public law," a "public law issuing from procedure." The Romano-Germanic family, by contrast, "has evolved, primarily for historical reasons, as an essentially private law," with other branches "developed later, but less perfectly."

4. Intellectual parentage. In the Common law, "the learning of the Romanists founded on the jus civile played only a very minor role"; "its divisions, its concepts and vocabulary, and the methods of the Common law lawyer, are entirely different." The civil-law family is defined by that Roman base: it was "found[ed] by the scholarly efforts of the European universities which, from the twelfth century and on the basis of the compilations of the Emperor Justinian (A.D. 483-565), evolved and developed a juridical science common to all." The very name "Romano-Germanic" acknowledges the joint effort of the universities of both Latin and Germanic countries.

5. Modern form — codes. "Since the nineteenth century, a distinctive feature of the family has been the fact that its various member countries have attached special importance to enacted legislation in the form of 'codes'" — the scholar's science systematised into statute. The Common law retains its case-law structure.

6. Expansion — the same two engines. Both families "have experienced a considerable expansion throughout the world — and for the same reasons: colonization or reception." In both, the observations about partial reception apply: Muslim countries, India and the Far East received European law only in part or transformed its application in the light of "old ways of thinking and acting." The United States and Canada "enjoy a largely autonomous place within the family."

Conclusion. The two families are mirror-images: the one, a public law issuing from procedure, made by judges, concrete and case-bound; the other, a private-law science of general rules, made by scholars, and codified. Yet — and this is the examiner's favourite second half — "the two families have tended, particularly in recent years, to draw closer together": shared Christian morality, Renaissance individualism and liberalism, similar methods, a convergent formulation of the legal rule, and "a shared vision of justice" producing "very similar answers to common problems." Hence the hybrid systems (§04) and the phrase "one great family of western law."

Q.2  "The Socialist legal family is a third family, distinct from the first two. Discuss, and show how it differs from both." 15 marks

Introduction. "The Socialist legal system makes up a third family, distinct from the first two" — and its distinctness is the unusual case of a family whose members "formerly belonged to the Romano-Germanic family" yet which stands detached, "at least at the present time."

1. What it retains. The socialist camp "preserved some of the characteristics of Romano-Germanic law": the legal rule "is still conceived in the form of a general rule of conduct; and the divisions of law and legal terminology have also remained, to a very large extent, the product of the legal science constructed on the basis of the Roman law by European universities."

2. What makes it original — and revolutionary. First, its ambition: "in opposition to the somewhat static character of Romano-Germanic laws, the proclaimed ambition of socialist jurists is to overturn society and create the conditions of a new social order in which the very concepts of state and law will disappear." Second, its source of law: "The sole source of Socialist rules of law resides… within the revolutionary work of the legislature, which expresses popular will, narrowly guided by the Communist Party." Third, law's subordination: "law according to Marxism-Leninism — a scientific truth — is strictly subordinate to the task of creating a new economic structure." Fourth, collectivisation: "all means of production have been collectivised," so "the field of possible private law relationships between citizens is extraordinarily limited." Fifth, the eclipse of private law: "private law has lost its pre-eminence — all law has now become public law" — exactly the inverse of the civil-law family's private-law-first history. Sixth, a narrower realm of law: the conception "removes from the very realm of law a whole series of rules which jurists of bourgeois countries would consider legal rules."

3. Geography. The family "originated in the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics… since the 1917 Revolution." Its European people's republics show "a greater persistence of characteristics properly Romano-Germanic"; in its Asian members, the interesting question is "how these new concepts are reconciled in practice with the principles of Far Eastern civilization which governed such societies before the Socialist era."

4. The perspective point. From the socialist standpoint, the two Western families are lumped together as "capitalist" or "bourgeois laws" — classification "from another point of view," which shows that legal families are not neutral facts but also standpoints.

Conclusion. Socialist law is derived yet detached: Romano-Germanic in form (general rules, divisions, terminology), revolutionary in content (legislature as sole source, economics over law, all law public). It is the third family, and its definition is inseparable from its rejection of the other two.

Q.3  "In Muslim and Hindu societies law is recognised as of great value but framed in a different concept; in the Far East the very notion of law is rejected. Elucidate with reference to David & Brierley." 20 marks · 900 words

Introduction. David & Brierley's master distinction for the non-Western world: "On the one hand law is fully recognised as being of great value but the law itself is framed in a different concept than it is in the West; on the other, the very notion of law is rejected, and social relations are governed by other extra-legal means. The first view is that of Muslim and Hindu societies, while the latter is that adopted in countries of the Far East and large parts of Africa and Malagasy."

1. The Western baseline. Behind both postulates lies the Western position: "in the West… law is held to be a necessary part of, indeed a basis for, society… Law, a mirror of justice, is in this conception superior even to equity itself; outside the law, there can only be anarchy, or arbitrariness, chaos or the rule of force. Law is therefore venerated, the courts are temples of justice, the judges its oracles."

2. Islamic (and Hindu and Jewish) law — the "model law" tradition. The attitude is "easily understood by a western jurist" because it turns on the natural-law debate with which the West is familiar. The positive-law view — "the body of rules that are really observed, the application of which is entrusted to the courts" — is "the view today in our western universities." But "law may also be seen as a model of ideal behaviour, one not to be confused with the actual rules by which individuals act which courts apply" — the pre-nineteenth-century European university tradition, which "taught, almost exclusively, an ideal law constructed on the basis of Roman law." "In Muslim countries, in the same way, more attention is given to the model law linked to the Islamic religion than to local custom (treated as a phenomenon of fact) or the laws and decrees of the sovereign (treated as merely administrative measures) and neither of these is thought to possess the full dignity of law. The same can be said of Jewish law and, in a very different context, Hindu law."

3. The consequences. Such law "is not in either case always necessarily observed by private persons or applied by courts," yet it "may nonetheless exert considerable influence" — "righteous men may endeavour to rule their own lives according to what they consider to be truly the law." In the West "there is a large degree of equivalence between justice, positive law and social manners"; not so in these societies, where "rules of law" in the Western sense "remain unorganized, fragmentary and unstable" and "true law is to be found elsewhere than in legislation, custom or judicial decisions." Nevertheless — the decisive sentence — "without taking sides in the debate between positivists and advocates of natural law, Muslim and Hindu law, therefore, must be included within the major contemporary legal systems." Jewish law "must be omitted because its sphere of influence is incomparably less."

4. The Far East — law put into question. "Here… the very value of law itself has traditionally been put into question." For the Chinese, "law is an instrument of arbitrary action rather than the symbol of Justice… a factor contributing to social disorder rather than to social order." The good citizen "should live in a way which excludes any revindication of his rights or any recourse to the justice of courts… Reconciliation is of greater value than justice; mediation must be used to remove conflicts rather than invoking law to resolve them." "Countries of the Far East have, traditionally, held the view that law is only for barbarians." Neither Chinese communism nor Japan's westernisation "fundamentally changed this conception": China repudiated both the Western codes and "the Soviet method of building communism," giving "a very narrow place to law"; Japan has European codes but "the population makes little use of them," courts "encourage litigants to resort to reconciliation."

5. Black Africa and Malagasy. The same applies in milieux where "the community's cohesion prevails over any developed sense of individualism; the principal objective is the maintenance or restoration of harmony rather than respect for law"; Western laws are "often hardly more than a veneer," the population living by "traditional ways which do not comprise what we in the West call law."

Conclusion. The reading is a warning against the "naive sense of superiority" that expects all other systems to disappear. "Everyone will recognise the superiority of western technology; opinions differ however on the superiority of western civilization taken as a whole. The Muslim world, India, the Far East and Africa are far from having adhered to it without reservation" — and for the Indian student, the culminating lesson is that Hindu law is a major contemporary legal system, whose "true law" lies in dharma — the subject of the next half of this unit.

Q.4  "Trace the judicial system of ancient India — the gradation of courts, their jurisdiction, the qualifications of judges, and the kinds of decision." 20 marks · 900 words

Introduction. "The ancient Indian society had a highly evolved unique and sophisticated legal system," and its study is "important not just as a matter of history" — "the impact of all this evolution is the present day's growth and development." The key to it: "the entire range of basic legal concepts of rights, duties, liabilities, powers, offences, punishments, constitutional, civil and criminal jurisprudence is embedded in" the Dharmashastra, in the twin branches of Rājadharma and Vyavahāradharma.

1. The constitutional frame. Rājadharma — "the law laying down the powers, duties and responsibilities of the king" — is "the basis of the modern-day constitutional law"; "it was the Constitution for all the States in the whole of India in the real sense of that expression." It contained "division of sovereign powers" and specified the powers of each division. It imposed on the king five fundamental duties: to punish the wicked; to honour and protect the good; to enrich the treasury by just methods; to be impartial towards litigants; and protection of the country. "But dharma or law was placed even above the king," so "the king had no power to legislate" — only where the Śrutis, Smritis and customs were silent could he decide according to his conscience. Where the law was silent and legislation was needed, the parishad — an assembly of at least ten members, including a knower of one of the three Vedas, a logician, a Mīmāṃsaka, one who knows Nirukta, one who recites the sacred law, and three men of the first three orders (Brahmachari, Grihastha, Vanaprastha) — could lay down new provisions and resolve complicated conflicts. "This separation of powers between the parishad and the king ensured the existence of rule of law."

2. The five-tier gradation. The people's courts — Kula (impartial persons of the litigants' family/caste, functioning as panchayatdars), Śreṇī (a corporation of the same craft, profession or trade) and Gaṇa (an assembly of one place but different castes/avocations) — had jurisdiction over "all civil and criminal matters except those involving offences committed with violence." Inter se appeals ran upward: Śreṇī could review Kula; Gaṇa could review Śreṇī. Above them was the Adhikṛta, the court appointed by the king, which could also decide violent offences; and at the apex the Nripa or Shaasita, the king's own court — "only the king had the authority to inflict fines and corporal punishments," and this administration of justice could not be delegated. The king heard appeals from every authority. "Article 136 of the Indian Constitution finds its roots in this provision." Besides these Pratishthita (established) courts there were Apratishthita (mobile) courts and Mudrita courts (appointed by the king and authorised to use the royal seal). The Law Commission (77th Report) noted that the panchayats survived "because of their useful role of settling minor disputes in rural areas through compromise — and that is why the British retained them through necessary legislation."

3. The judges and their qualifications. The court hall was the Dharmādhikaraṇa; the king passed final decrees; the Chief Justice gave the final opinions; other judges or sabhyas investigated each case on merits. The Chief Justice had to be: well versed in all eighteen titles of law (vyavahārapada); proficient in logic (nyāya); proficient in interpretation (Mīmāṃsā); master of the sources of law (Vedas and Smritis); and possessed of "capacity to extract truth by application of law." The sabhyas had to be "noble, honourable men of tried integrity," able to administer justice, "well versed in laws, rules of prudence," and "fearless, independent and impartial towards friends and foes." While sitting as judge, the king wore "a simple dress, without ornaments and with a pleasant demeanour" — to create "distinction between judicial and executive functions." "The concept of independence of judiciary existed even then."

4. Procedure and decision. Litigation ran through four sequential parts: purvapaksha (the first party's case), uttara (the defendant's reply), kriyā (trial), nirṇaya (the decision). Of the eighteen vyavahārapadas (from runādāna to strīsaṅgrahaṇa), fourteen arise out of wealth (arthasamutthāni) and four out of violence (hiṃsodbhāvāni); thirteen are civil and five criminal. Four types of decision were recognised: dharma (on admission or confession); vyavahāra (on evidence, where statements conflict); charitra (on precedent or applicable usage); rājaśāsana (the king's judgment where no principle applies and no evidence is led) — with guiding principles fixing the choice and "securing the system from the menace of arbitrariness."

Conclusion. The ancient system was not "the product of one day" (Law Commission): it combined constitutional limitation of the king, popular courts of peers, appellate hierarchy culminating in the king, expressly qualified and independent judges, a codified procedure and a closed list of decision-types. Its legacy is living: Article 136, panchayats, judicial independence, and the rule of law itself, which "flows from rajadharma which also made the king its subject."

Q.5  "Examine the sources of law in ancient India and the 'recommendatory' character of the Dharmasutras. How has the judiciary used these indigenous sources?" 20 marks · 850 words

Introduction. The sources of ancient Indian law are organised in a hierarchy with explicit conflict rules — and their authority before modern courts rests on a hermeneutic distinction the Supreme Court itself drew: much of what the śāstras and the commentaries state is recommendatory, not mandatory.

1. The organisation of the literature. Sanskrit literature is classified into three groups: the Vedic Samhitas (Rig, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva), the Brāhmaṇas (including the early Upanishads) and the Sūtras. The first two together are Śruti; the Sūtras are "not Vedas but… systematic formal treatises connected with the Vedas." Among the six Vedāṅgas, Kalpa contains the Kalpasūtras, divided into Śrautasūtras (sacrifices), Gṛhyasūtras (domestic ceremonies) and Dharmasūtras (politics, government, civil and criminal law). "The Vedas and Smritis along with customs and usage are considered to be the primary source of dharma."

2. The conflict rules. (i) "the Vedas are considered to be superior and are to prevail over the smritis in case of conflict"; (ii) "in cases of conflict between two smritis, the principles of equity as determined by popular usages are to have an overriding effect"; (iii) "even in cases of conflicts between the sources of law, the king was not given a discretionary power but was required to apply the law as approved by custom and usage by people themselves." Where the law was entirely silent, the parishad legislated, and only then might the king decide per his conscience.

3. Dharma is the substance of these sources. "Dharma is that which sustains and ensures the progress and welfare of all in this world and eternal bliss in the other world" (Madhvāchārya, adopted by the Supreme Court); it is that "which regulates the mutual obligations of individual and the society"; "it is the king of kings; nothing is superior to law" (Brihadāraṇyaka Upanishad). It is not "religion" in the narrow sense but "everything that a person ought to do in life… a code of conduct: it teaches both religious and secular matters."

4. The recommendatory distinction — the Supreme Court's caution. In Abhiraj Kuer v. Debendra Singh (AIR 1962 SC 351) the Court recognised "the recommendatory nature of dharmasutras and grihyasutras": "while interpreting the books of morality, religion, law, etc., a great deal of caution is required to be exercised"; the commentaries "are many-a-times mingled with statements of law but actually are intended to be recommendatory statements regarding the mandatory laws" — ideal statements, not commands.

5. The rule of conflict — Narada's maxim in the Bombay High Court. In Dnyaneshwar B. Ganwat (2010 SCC OnLine Bom 766), the Full Bench adopted the view consonant with Narada's "Dharmashastra Virodhe Tu Yuktiyukta Vidhe Smrita": "when the Dharmashaastra or law codes of equal authority conflict with one another, the one appearing to be reasonable, or more reasonable, is to be preferred and followed" — a principle "also advocated" by Seervai. "It can be gathered how the judiciary has been taking conscious efforts to resort to indigenous sources of law for finding conformity in cases of conflict."

Conclusion. The indigenous sources function in modern law through three disciplines: hierarchy (Vedas over Smritis), equity (popular usage over conflicting Smritis), and hermeneutic caution (recommendatory v. mandatory). The judiciary has not merely preserved these sources — it has actively used them, and the lesson of this unit is that they remain workable law.

Q.6  "Mīmāṃsā as a parallel system of interpretation of statutes. Examine its classification of provisions, its procedure, its rules of construction and its axioms — and the judicial recognition it has received." 20 marks · 950 words

Introduction. "In India and globally, Maxwell's Principles of Interpretation are popularly resorted to… What came to be ignored in the course of its appreciation was another system of interpretation… which was indigenous to us" — the Mīmāṃsā principles "developed by Jaimini in his sutras." Created "for religious purposes like enabling correct performance of yagya," they were "so rational and logical that subsequently they began to be used in law, grammar, logic, philosophy etc." Jaimini defined duty as "that which is to be fulfilled as the object of a command" — and since law and duty are inseparable, "law by way of implication."

1. Classification of provisions. Texts are obligatory or non-obligatory. Obligatory: the pradhāna chodanās (primary commands, "one wide statement in the shape of a general injunction"), which are vidhis ("You shall do it") or nishedhas ("You shall not do it") — "the force of command of both is the same in principle." Non-obligatory: arthavādas (explanatory/illustrative — explaining the reason of a vidhi or illustrating it), nāmadheyas (names/descriptions — "the same as the definition clauses in modern laws"), and niyamas (subsidiary or incidental statements — directory rules). Paryudāsa designates provisos or qualified exceptions: "a case falling under the proviso cannot come under the main clause and vice versa." Vidhivaishamya is inconsistency — constructions which cannot stand together "are not permitted even under other parallel systems." Nyāyas are maxims, corresponding to Latin maxims, some governing interpretation and some general experience. Provisions are pratyakṣa (express) or kalpya (implied).

2. The procedure — adhikaraṇa. To ascertain meaning, the Mīmāṃsakas used a five-staged theme: (i) the subject matter requiring interpretation is set out; (ii) the doubt is explained; (iii) the purva-pakṣa (prima facie argument) is set out; (iv) the answer — the other side — is set out; (v) the conclusion (final interpretation) is arrived at.

3. The four rules of construction, in strict order. Śruti (direct assertion): a sentence complete and explicit in sense and grammar must not be strained. Liṅga (indicative power): a word with more than one meaning takes the meaning given by its context. Vākya (syntactical connection): disconnected words are joined grammatically so as to make a sensible proposition. Prakaraṇa (context): a clause clear but incomplete in meaning is read with the passage with which it coalesces. The order of preference is fixed: "Irrespective of the consequence of the meaning, the shruti rule of construction is to be preferred over every other rule"; then liṅga; then vākya; prakaraṇa is the last resort. "The Meemaamsa system has abundantly insisted on the literal interpretation of any provision."

4. The six axioms. Sārthakya — every word and sentence must have meaning and purpose; rendering a part otiose is the fault of anarthakya-doṣa (corresponds to Maxwell's literal rule). Lāghava — where one rule suffices, more must not be assumed; the simpler, shorter construction is preferred (construction should not be burdensome). Arthaikatva — no double meaning to the same word or sentence in the same place; consistency unless the context requires otherwise (Maxwell: the same expression presumably carries the same meaning in every part of the Act). Guṇapradhāna — a subordinate expression clashing with the principal idea must be adjusted to or discarded; the main clause carries the principal idea. Sāmāñjasya — harmony: contradiction is not to be presumed where reconciliation is possible; the rule of harmonious construction. Vikalpa — option: where reconciliation is impossible, one may be chosen, with reference to the context, facts and circumstances.

5. Judicial recognition — three cases. Surjit Singh v. MTNL, (2009) 16 SCC 722: "Any system of interpretation can be used which helps in solving a difficulty. In certain situations, Maxwell's principles would be more appropriate, while in other situations the Meemaamsa principles may be more suitable" — and the Court actually applied the liṅga/lakṣaṇā rule in preference to the literal śruti rule, invoking Nāgeśa Bhaṭṭa's three meanings (abhidhā, lakṣaṇā, vyañjanā) and the Sphaḍi (wooden sword) nyāya. B. Premchand v. Mohan Koikal, (2011) 4 SCC 266: the literal rule is "the 'Shruti' or 'Abhida' principle"; it is "deeply regrettable" that these principles are not cited in our courts, and "It is not stated anywhere in the Constitution of India that only Maxwell's principles of interpretation can be utilised." Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam v. Essar Power, (2008) 4 SCC 755: the Court applied sāmāñjasya (ss. 174–175 of the Electricity Act, 2003 read harmoniously), guṇapradhāna (s.174 pradhāna; s.175 guṇa) and vikalpa.

Conclusion. Mīmāṃsā is not an exotic rival to Maxwell but the indigenous science of the same craft — classification, procedure, rules and axioms, with a settled order of preference — and the Supreme Court has expressly held it usable in our courts. "Principles of interpretation are not principles of law but a methodology" — and we have two methodologies, not one.

Q.7  "Examine the Nyāya contribution to legal reasoning and appreciation of evidence — the sources of knowledge, the five-member syllogism and the fallacies." 15 marks · 800 words

Introduction. "Any argument or judicial decision without the application of logic or legal reasoning is incapable of standing the test of reasonability and legitimacy." Sudame's reading supplies the Indic apparatus for both: the prāmaṇa theory (knowledge and evidence) and the pancāvayava syllogism with its fallacies (reasoning).

1. Logic and legal reasoning are distinct. "Logic is concerned with the validity of the conclusion based on the proposition and reason employed… a technical and formal process… Logic ensures the stability of an argument or a judicial-decision." "Legal reasoning… is a part of the logical process… consists of reasons employed from the legal framework in view of the facts and circumstances of a case," including "the less technical touchstones of humanitarian approaches which require the exercise of judicial discretion."

2. The epistemology — evidence as valid knowledge. "The object of any legal proceeding is the determination of rights and liabilities which depend upon determination of facts." Every Indic philosophical system — Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, Sāṅkhya-Yoga, Pūrva-Uttara Mīmāṃsā — has its own set of prāmaṇas. Nyāya recognises four sources of valid knowledge (pramā): perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna) and verbal testimony (śabda) [N.S. 1.1.3] — and three sources of invalid knowledge (apramā): doubt (saṃśaya), error (viparyaya) and hypothetical assumption (tarka). The modern echo: "it is only upon the Court's belief in the existence of a fact that it can be said to be 'proved'" (s.3, Evidence Act); and Order 19, Rule 3 CPC, as enforced in Sukhwinder Pal Bipan Kumar v. State of Punjab (1982) 1 SCC 31, requires the deponent "to disclose the nature and source of his knowledge with sufficient particularity" — an affidavit failing this "has no probative value and is liable to be rejected." Legal epistemology "defines and classifies knowledge and goes ahead to give parameters for its validity and invalidity"; without it, legal methods "increase subjectivity."

3. The five-member syllogism (pancāvayava). Inference is drawn for oneself (svārthānumāna) or for others (parārthānumāna); the latter is formal, and its structure is a syllogism: pratijñā (the proposition to be established); hetu (the reason establishing it); dṛṣṭānta (the example — an illustration and supporting reason); upanaya (the application — stating the validity of the example); nigamana (the conclusion — restating the proposition after the reason). "Going by their purpose — arguing or decision-making — how different these syllogisms are required to be is dependent upon how the nature of their structure is," but one needs all the elements.

4. The fallacies (hetvābhāsā). Nyāya identifies five: savyabhicāra (erratic reason), viruddha (contradictory reason), prakaraṇasama (reason equal to the question), sādhyasama (unproved reason) and kālātīta (mistimed reason). Knowledge of them serves a twofold purpose: steering clear of them, and identifying them in legal research. "These fallacies can produce conclusions that are not logically justified and ultimately cause errors in judgment."

5. The judicial endorsement. "Reliefs granted by courts must be seen to be logical and tenable within the framework of the law… It is essential to maintain the integrity of legal reasoning and the legitimacy of the conclusions. They must emanate logically from the legal findings…" (Kerala Solvent Extractions v. A. Unnikrishnan, (2006) 13 SCC 619). "Reasons are the lifelines of legal arguments and judgments."

Conclusion. The Nyāya apparatus supplies what modern legal education lacks: an explicit theory of valid knowledge for evidence, a complete argument-structure for reasoning, and a catalogue of logical errors. Employing the pancāvayava "can very effectively aid in maintaining the integrity of legal reasoning in the process" — and, in the reading's final word, the goal is "akhanda-vidya": analysing the issue as a whole, "to 'think like a lawyer' in the truest sense."

Q.8  "Examine the components of the Indian legal system as analysed by N.R. Madhava Menon. Is the Indian legal system still alien to Indians?" 20 marks · 900 words

Introduction. "The legal system of a country is part of its social system and reflects the social, political, and cultural characteristics of that society." Menon's essay opens with the Indian paradox: the legal system "is largely the gift of the British rulers," and yet it is self-owned — "the rights and benefits conferred by the laws and the Constitution offer the opportunity for those very people to enjoy the fruits of a welfare democracy which the people of India have given unto themselves on the 26th January 1950."

1. The four components. A legal system consists of: basic principles and values (largely outlined by the Constitution); operational norms — rights and duties of citizens in Central, State and local laws; institutional structures for enforcement; and a cadre of legal personnel endowed with the responsibility of administering the system.

2. The Constitution. "India is declared to be a Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic… quasi-federal structure." It is a "political document which distributes State power amongst different organs"; the form is "democratic and republican," the method "parliamentary through adult franchise." The Preamble secures "Justice, social, economic and political; Liberty of thought, expression, faith and worship; Equality of status and of opportunity," and promotes "Fraternity assuring dignity of the individual and the 'unity and integrity of Nation'." Toward it, the Constitution guarantees the classic Fundamental Rights (equality before law; equality of opportunity; no discrimination on grounds of religion, sex etc.; life and personal liberty; freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement and profession; no forced labour; freedom of religion; minority protection; constitutional remedies), and gives the State Directives in the DPSP — "recognised to be as sacrosanct as Fundamental Rights… a reference for State action in every sphere." The judiciary holds "a unique place": it oversees the exercise of State power, protects citizens through writ jurisdiction — "a cheap and expeditious remedy" — and the Supreme Court has "liberalised the rules" for the poor and illiterate. "The Rule of Law is supreme, and the independence of judiciary is reality in our country."

3. Laws — civil and criminal. "Law is as large as life itself." By the remedies sought and the procedure followed, all laws fall into two groups: civil (rights, duties and obligations of members inter se — family law, with its sources "both statute and religion"; property; contract; tort; commerce and business; labour; taxation) and criminal (wrongs against the order and well-being of society, defined in the Penal Code and special laws; insisting on mens rea; recognising degrees and gradations; "ignorance of law is never taken as an excuse"; defences of infancy, insanity, mistake of fact; classified by object — body, property, reputation, State, public rights — and procedurally as cognizable/non-cognizable, bailable/non-bailable, compoundable/otherwise).

4. Procedure — civil and criminal. The CPC governs the suit: pleadings (plaint and written statement), admissions, interrogatories, oral statements, documents; adversary trial; evidence recorded day-to-day; judgment in open court; decrees appealable within the period; private proceedings may be abandoned or compromised. The CrPC governs the criminal process — a 'fair trial' consistent with "the constitutional commitment to individual liberty and freedom" — through four stages: investigation, prosecution, trial, disposition. The police must produce the arrested person before a Magistrate within 24 hours; no 'third degree methods'; confessions to police inadmissible; bail in bailable cases; counsel of choice; no self-incrimination; "every accused is presumed innocent"; proof beyond reasonable doubt; benefit of doubt; cross-examination; legal aid at State expense; right to be heard on sentence; and the emphasis in modern criminal justice on reformation and rehabilitation. Alongside the codes run the informal tracks — tribunals, quasi-judicial agencies, arbitration councils, Nyāya panchayats — "cheap, expeditious and less cumbersome"; and legal aid — counsel to the poor, pauper suits, Legal Aid Schemes.

5. Courts and personnel. Courts are "created by Statutes"; the Constitution provides the Supreme Court and the High Courts (the Union Judiciary) with original and appellate jurisdiction; beneath them the State Judiciary: Sessions Courts and Magistrates' Courts (criminal); District, Sub-Divisional and Munsiff Courts (civil); plus special courts and tribunals. Personnel: judges — "independent of both the legislature and the executive," free to administer law "without fear or favour," salaried by law, removable only by special procedure; and lawyers — "officers of court," an independent profession under the Advocates Act, 1961, enrolled by Bar Councils (elected members, disciplinary powers), bound by the "social obligation… of providing legal services to the poor."

Conclusion. Is the system alien? Menon's answer is double-tiered: in form (language, technicality, procedure) the inherited system does "limit access to justice for the illiterate, impoverished masses"; in substance it is the people's own — the Constitution was given "unto themselves," and its rights, remedies and legal-aid machinery are precisely the instruments by which the alien form is being made accessible. "Familiarity with law and its processes becomes essential to every Indian, rich or poor" — the answer to alienation is not abandonment of the system but access to it — a conclusion that also echoes David & Brierley's India (partial reception, co-existence) and Sudame's call to root legal education in indigenous knowledge systems.

Q.9  "What are hybrid legal systems? Illustrate, and discuss the tendency of the two Western families to draw closer." 15 marks

Introduction. "The laws of some states cannot be annexed to either family, because they embody both Romano-Germanic and Common law elements" — these are the hybrid (mixed) systems. The observation arises in the course of a larger one: the two Western families have been drawing closer.

1. The mixed systems. The reading names Scotland, Israel, the Union of South Africa, the Province of Quebec and the Philippines. Scotland and Quebec combine civilian foundations with common-law surroundings; South Africa's Roman-Dutch base carries strong English influence; Israel and the Philippines combine continental codes with common-law institutions. Each is a living fusion — and proof that the "two families" are not sealed compartments.

2. The convergence. "Over the centuries there have been numerous contacts… and the two families have tended, particularly in recent years, to draw closer together." The causes: in both, "the law has undergone the influence of Christian morality," and "since the Renaissance, philosophical teachings have given prominence to individualism, liberalism and personal rights" — "Henceforth, at least for certain purposes, this reconciliation enables us to speak of one great family of western law." The convergence is of method and substance, not structure: "the Common law retains… its own particular structure, very different," but "the methods employed in each are not wholly dissimilar"; above all, "the formulation of the legal rule tends more and more to be conceived in Common law countries as it is in the countries of the Romano-Germanic family"; and "as to the substance of the law, a shared vision of justice has often produced very similar answers to common problems."

3. The qualification. The reading is careful: convergence is "at least for certain purposes"; the structures remain different; and the two families together are, from the socialist standpoint, one object — "capitalist" or "bourgeois laws." Classification depends on the classifier's standpoint.

4. Relevance. Hybridity shows that "legal family" is an analytical tool, not a cage; India itself (partially received Common law co-existing with indigenous tradition — §02.3; a constitutional system that is, in Menon's words, "quasi-federal" with civil-law-style codified statutes and common-law-judge-made law) is often described in exactly these terms — which is why the syllabus asks the question.

Conclusion. Hybrid systems are the practical evidence of convergence: mixed in composition, they belong to neither family and both, and they justify — with the reading's own careful caveats — speaking of one great family of Western law.

E.g., the Sphaḍi (wooden sword) nyāya: "what is prescribed as a means to an action is to be taken in a sense suited to the performance of the action" (Jaimini 3:1:2).

Cognizable / non-cognizableProcedural classification of offences: cognizable are those "in which the police can investigate or arrest persons without judicial warrant"; the others require a Magistrate's warrant. Companion classifications: bailable/non-bailable; compoundable/otherwise.

Writ jurisdictionThe unique remedy under Articles 32 and 226 (with the High Courts) for quick enforcement of Fundamental Rights against the State or its agencies: "citizens can approach these courts even through a letter sent by post as the Supreme Court has declared that procedure should not be allowed to come in the way of dispensation of justice." 🏁 The last word

Unit 3 completes the syllabus's movement from the cosmos (Ṛta) through the State (Kautilya, the Mahabharata, Rāmarājya) to the world map of legal systems — and places India on it. David & Brierley's three families are the West's map; their own honesty admits it is incomplete and that "the Muslim world, India, the Far East and Africa are far from having adhered to it without reservation." Sudame shows what sits outside that map: a legal system with its own constitution (Rājadharma), its own code of procedure (Vyavahāradharma), its own courts, judges and appellate hierarchy — and, most strikingly, its own sciences of research, evidence, reasoning and interpretation, which the Supreme Court of India has expressly held available to Indian courts today. Menon then grounds the whole argument in the working present: legal system as part of the social system, the Constitution self-given, the courts and the profession that administer it. The three readings are one argument: the Indian legal system is not an outpost of the Common law — it is a major legal system in its own right, and the Common law is only its received layer. Learn the map, learn the ancient system's architecture, quote the Mīmāṃsā and the two postulates — and write with the confidence of a student who knows both the borrowed and the native.
