01Conceptual Framework — "Is there an Indian Jurisprudence?"

1.1 The problem of the colonised mind (Parasain's starting point)

India is a "knowledge civilization" and the only surviving civilization of antiquity with continuity to the modern period. Yet a deliberate misconception was created — that there is no analytical/philosophical tradition outside the West; that Indian philosophy is "primitive speculation", tied to occult cults and "an odd assortment of spirituality, mysticism and imprecise thinking" (Matilal). This misconception, coupled with the complacency of independent India in continuing the British administrative and legal framework, has deprived India of genuine critical inquiry into its own philosophical literature.

"At the touch of the Judge of the Supreme Court, who had been trained in the English school of special pleading… the rule of native law dissolved and, with or without his intention, was to a great extent replaced by rules having their origin in English law-books. Under the hand of the judges of the Sudder Courts… the native rules hardened and contracted a rigidity which they never had in real native practice." — Sir Henry Maine, Village Communities (1895), quoted by Parasain
"Contemporary Indian law is, for the most part, palpably foreign in origin or inspiration and it is notoriously incongruent with the attitudes and concerns of much of the population which lives under it." — Marc Galanter, "The Displacement of Traditional Law in Modern India" (1968), quoted by Parasain

Krishnachandra Bhattacharyya's Swaraj in Ideas names this: "cultural subjection" — an unconscious intellectual slavery in which one's traditional cast of ideas is superseded "without comparison or competition by a new cast representing an alien culture which possesses one like a ghost". He asks for intellectual decolonisation while assimilating foreign ideas with awareness — not becoming a "mere material supplier" to a framework built to subjugate our knowledge system. Parasain's answer to critics who call this "revivalism": those same critics praise Europe's 13th-century relocation of the European mind in its classical Greek roots as a "Renaissance", but condemn India's parallel move as "obscurantism". Hence: what is needed is to relocate the Indian mind in Indian thought.

Following A.K. Ramanujan ("Is there an Indian way of thinking?" — affirmative, but without parroting Western methods), Parasain asks: is there an Indian philosophy of law? His answer is affirmative and is built on four moves: (i) a critique of colonial legal historiography; (ii) the identification of the Indian foundational concept — dharma; (iii) the search for a metaphysical foundation; (iv) a systematic East–West comparison.

Watch the trajectory of the West: Aquinas built natural law as a rational foundation for canon law; Kant ("true politics cannot take a single step without paying homage to morals") separated jurisprudence from theology using practical reason — the categorical imperative, universalisability, man as end-in-himself; Bentham freed it further by appealing to the greatest happiness of the greatest number. Indian jurisprudence, by contrast, is built on the realisation that happiness itself is ephemeral — the Indian ideal is ānanda (sat-cit-ānanda), not transient pleasure; and the American "pursuit of happiness" jurisprudence (rights-based, sometimes sacrificing collective well-being) is contrasted with the dharmic model.

"Many commentators opine that such a thorough metaphysics of morality is not to be found in Indian philosophy… because the Dharmashastras (Manu and Yajñavalkya) did not push their inquiries into their metaphysical foundations, nor did the metaphysicians develop the social implications of their thought. The principal direction of Indian thought has never been to social and material sciences, but to a deeper level of being." — Parasain, summarising the objection (Raju) and its explanation

Parasain's rejoinder: the metaphysics of Indian moral ideals must be sought in the Epics and the Bhagavad-Gītā — "the cream of the Upanishadic philosophy". The Gītā teaches two dharmas, pravṛtti (activity) and nivṛtti (renunciation): "Twofold dharma is the cause of stability of the world order and also the means by which men attain prosperity and the highest good" (Gītā XVIII.78). The Upanishads do not deny the world — they allow it as empirical reality (vyāvahārika sattā); the Smritis recognise that immutable truths must be adapted to time and place. Krishna's rebuke to the retiring Arjuna — "lowness of spirit, unbecoming of his svadharma, dishonourable, unmanly" (Gītā II.2-3) — shows that spiritual enlightenment and social duty are not separate.

1.3 Five threads that run through Indian ethical-legal thought (Parasain)

1. Karma

Every act produces an inescapable result — metaphysical causality (Matilal). It answers three problems: (i) the world is not arbitrary; (ii) free will exists — against fatalism/determinism; (iii) worldly inequality is explained without a capricious God (Śaṅkara: God creates depending on beings' dharma/adharma, "just as rain is the common cause of vegetation but the difference of species is due to the seeds").

2. Nature of the self

Many schools see the self as interconnected with ultimate reality (Advaita: Ātman = Brahman). Harming others is harming oneself — a metaphysical basis for ethics.

3. Dharma

Righteous order and obligations — with three foundational elements: scriptural authority, tradition, "exemplary conduct", and only marginally custom. It is the ecology of well-being, emerging from devotional relationships with all elements and creatures.

4. Consciousness

Ethical development is cultivation of self-awareness (meditation, yoga) — ethics as purification of mind.

5. Ahiṃsā

Non-violence in action, thought AND language; extends to animals and all living beings.

Plus: Contextuality

Ritusamayata (appropriateness) applied to poetry, music, ritual, medicine — even to punishment: "Punishment should be inflicted after examining the circumstances, place and time as well as the worth and offence of the culprit" (Manu 8.126). The Brihadāraṇyaka "DA DA DA" — gods hear dāmyata (control), demons dayādhvam (compassion), humans datta (give).

Note the movement from Rāmāyaṇa to Mahābhārata: the Rāmāyaṇa's ethics are "formalistic" (truth-telling and promise-keeping are unconditional); the Mahābhārata is pragmatic — formal obligations need not override all other considerations (Krishna's attitude compared by Matilal to a poet who "accepts the constraints of metre, verses and metaphors… but has absolute control over them"). The Mahābhārata also says tradition is not Dharma if it fails to accommodate emerging situations (Parasain's reading of the epic, with Habermas's Legitimation Crisis in the background) — hence maitribhāva (benefit of others) may even require breaking a merely personal vow.

One paragraph answer-builder for "what is Bharatiya jurisprudence?": It is the philosophy of law rooted in the Indian knowledge tradition, whose foundational concept is dharma (sustaining order — dhāraṇāt dharma ityāhuḥ), whose law is understood as Ṛta/cosmic order revealed as Śruti, whose king is under law, whose ethic is duty (kartavya) and obligation (ṛṇa) rather than rights, and whose metaphysics is supplied by Karma, the self, consciousness, ahiṃsā and the puruṣārthas.
Its characteristic method: unitary and integrated vision of man, life and world (vs the Western fragmentation of reason/faith/emotion); contextuality (ritusamayata); and a reasoned, dialogical engagement (anyamataparīkṣā — Sudame) with other traditions — Tagore's "no absolute barriers of communication, because their foundation is the same".

02(a) Environmental Consciousness in the Vedic Age

2.1 The thesis in one line

The Vedic world-view treats the cosmos as a single moral-legal order (Ṛta) in which man is not the master but a friend and participant of nature; hence ecological restraint is not merely prudence but dharma — a duty with jurisprudential (not just religious) force. This is the oldest stratum of "environmental law" in India, and the DU case material treats it as the foundation of environmental jurisprudence in India.

"Dharma is the ecology of well-being which emerges organically from devotional relationships with all elements and creatures. Most of the central texts in the Indic knowledge and tradition describe the mutual relationship between nature and humans not as that of domination, but of friendship and harmony. Even people who do not believe in divine order should adhere to dharma for the sake of shared well-being. Observance of dharma secures two objectives — happiness on earth as well as salvation."— Mohan Parasain, "Indian Jurisprudence: A Philosophical Overview" (DU case material)

2.5 The institutional dimension — the state's environmental role

Dhyani (II & VIII): the Vedas and Upaniṣads enjoin both king and individuals to follow established dharma. The ancient Indian state was never a "necessary evil" — it was necessary for the advancement of dharma, and had positive welfare duties (the seeds of the modern welfare state):

  • State control of the economy — "traces of state socialism can be found in the Arthashastra in which a positive role is envisaged for the State"; Kautilya "shows great fear of big corporations and recommends strict control over them" (Arthashastra IV.2).
  • Duty to support learning and the unemployed (Manu; Kane): "though there were no Acts of Parliament guaranteeing services to the people, the public opinion, the view of the eminent writers and the practice of the best Kings created an atmosphere in which it was thought imperative for the King representing the State to encourage learning, to give employment to those who were unemployed."
  • Matsya-nyāya excluded: the big fish swallowing the small was never allowed by the State; "it was believed that economic development ought to be the concern of the State and an equitable division of the available wealth was a duty of the ruler. When the government was good there occurred a Rama Rajya."
  • Heavy taxation to support the poor: "Manu's Code and Kautilya's Arthashastra suggested heavy taxation to support the impoverished masses"; poverty is treated as a state of sinfulness (Mahabharata) and wealth's true purpose is discharge of social duties.

Dhavan (Administrative Code): the Mauryan state ran a huge public sector — a state mercantile marine (superintendent-general of navigation, navādhyakṣa), a state textile industry (sūtrādhyakṣa), state mining (ākarādhyakṣa) and a state trading department. The rules were compiled in the fourth part of the Arthashastra — an Administrative Code — including:

  • Maritime/riparian navigation rules: state ships on large rivers with captain (śāsaka), pilot (niyāmaka) and crew; fixed passage money (yātrā-vetanam) for passengers on royal ships; proof that Indians were a seafaring, trading people.
  • Labour-protective rules for women workers in the weaving department: an official who "stares at the face" of a woman worker or speaks of matters other than her work "will be punished as if guilty of first assault"; delay in payment of wages is punishable; paying wages "for no work done" is an offence.
  • Customs/tax code: unsealed merchandise — duty doubled; counterfeit seal — penalty of eight times the duty; torn seal — detention.

Exam linkage: these rules show that ancient India regulated resource use, labour and trade by written, state-enforced norms — i.e., environmental/economic governance was not merely moral exhortation.

2.6 Contextuality, happiness, and the modern environmental debate

Critique of the "pursuit of happiness" model

Parasain: Indian jurisprudence is built on the realisation that happiness itself is ephemeral ("the Indian concept of Ānanda is not ephemeral happiness but an aspect of the highest being: sat-cit-ānanda"); modern psychology has exposed the "happiness industry" and the paradoxical nature of our pursuit of happiness. American constitutional jurisprudence (Jeffersonian "pursuit of happiness" + the State's duty to remove obstacles) generated right-based constitutional jurisprudence which may even sacrifice collective well-being for individual rights. The dharmic frame, by contrast, derives individual well-being from collective well-being: "Kāma which is in the individual dimension reaches the societal dimension of artha and dharma and ultimately the highest well-being of the individual or Moksha."

Modern environmental jurisprudence (Dhyani's links)

Dhyani lists among the "new linkages" built by the Supreme Court: "conservation of humane environment" as a basic feature of the emerging legal theory. The cases he cites in this chain (all in DU case material):

  • Bangalore Medical Trust v. B.S. Muddappa, AIR 1991 SC 1902 — public park / civic amenity land; environment and public health prevail over private and commercial interests; M.J. Sivani v. State of Karnataka, AIR 1995 SC 1770; D. Vyas v. Ghaziabad Development Authority, AIR 1993 All 57.
  • State of Maharashtra v. Manubhai Pragaji Vashu, AIR 1996 SC 1 and Consumer Education Research Centre v. Union of India, AIR 1995 SC 992 — right to life/environment.
  • Open government, S.P. Gupta v. Union of India, AIR 1982 SC 149; right to information, Secretary, Ministry of I&B v. Cricket Association of W.B., AIR 1995 SC 1236; L.I.C. v. Manubhai Shah, AIR 1993 SC 171.

You may add (supplementary, not from case material): Article 48A and Article 51A(g) of the Constitution, the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, and the public-trust doctrine — but always anchor the answer in the dharmic principle ("history of environmental law in India begins in the Vedas", as the DU material asserts).

03(b) Codification of Law — Dharmashastras & Smritis

3.1 What "codification" means here

Codification = the reduction of customary, oral and revealed norms into written, ordered, rule-like texts of general application. In the Bharatiya tradition this happened in stages, and the DU case material is clear that the Dharmashastras are both more and less than a modern code:

"Dharmashastra means 'the teaching (or science) of righteousness' and includes the modern understanding of the concept 'law' and much more (Derrett). Because Dharma, as righteousness, has greater importance than the norms required for day-to-day administration of justice… the Dharmashastras are not only the repository of ancient Indian legal thought, but the Indian concept of Dharma is the 'institutional a-priori' of even its modern legal system."— Parasain
"The period of Vedic development was succeeded by the period of Dharmashastra… The era of Dharmashastra is described as the Golden Age of Hindu legal theory wherein there was a blending of religion and morality with law. They deal with the religious and moral law, the duties of the castes and Kings as well as civil and criminal law."— Dhyani

Note the key distinction (Parasain): "The injunctions in the Dharmashastras may be compared with the modern Bare Acts, which in itself may be proved to be outdated for modern jurisprudence. Therefore, the argument is not in favour of reviving the Dharmashastras and vigorously infusing them into the modern legal system, but to enquire how the spirit of the Dharma can be useful for the legal order of our times. The Dharmashastras as texts could be static but Dharma is dynamic and has the seeds of discursive practice in it."

3.6 Sources of dharma and the rules of conflict (the "hierarchy of norms")

Manu's four sources of the sacred law (Dhyani — "the observation in the Code of Manu that Sruti, the Smriti, customs and sayings of virtuous men and one's own conscience (self-approval) are the four sources of the sacred law is sufficient to show that Dharmashastras were the inter-mixtures of law, religion and morality"):

ŚRUTI Vedas — revealed; supreme
SMṚTI remembered codes
SADĀCHĀRA customs/practices of good men
ĀTMATUṢṬI self-approval / conscience

The Smṛti-writers' own rules of interpretation (Dhyani)

  • "A principle of law, established in one instance should be extended to other cases also, provided there is no impediment." (analogy/extension)
  • "Between rules general and special the special is to prevail." (specialia generalibus derogant)
  • "If there be a contradiction between a Sruti and a Smriti the former is to be followed, but if there be no such contradiction, the Smriti should be acted upon."
  • "If the two texts differ, reason must prevail in practice."

Sudame's order (Vedas > Smritis > equity):

"The Vedas and Smritis along with customs and usage are considered to be the primary source of dharma. Amongst these, the Vedas are considered to be superior and are to prevail over the smritis in case of conflict. Further, in cases of conflict between two smritis, the principles of equity as determined by popular usages are to have an overriding effect. Thus, even in cases of conflicts between the sources of law, the king was not given a discretionary power but was required to apply the law as approved by custom and usage by people themselves."

Dhavan's conflict-rules (law-court practice — the most exam-usable)

Courts recognised three systems of substantive law: (i) dharma-śāstra (laws deriving ultimate sanction from the Smṛtis — not always secular); (ii) artha-śāstra (principles of government — "uniformly secular", so much so that some writers argue it had an independent origin); (iii) custom (sadāchāra or charitra). Bṛhaspati on the letter vs. justice: "The court should not give its decision by merely following the letter of the shastra, for if the decision is completely devoid of reasoning, the result is injustice (dharma-hani)." And Bṛhaspati on custom vs. letter: "the court should decide according to the customs and usages of the country even if they are in conflict with the letter of the law" — with his famous regional illustrations: the maternal uncle's daughter is accepted in marriage by Brāhmaṇas of the south; in Madhyadeśa Brāhmaṇas become hired labourers and craftsmen and eat cow's flesh; eastern Brāhmaṇas eat fish and their women are addicted to drinking — "on account of the acts specified, these communities, in their respective countries, should not be liable to undergo penance or incur judicial punishment."

Nārada's four bases of judicial decision — in REVERSE order of authority: (i) Dharma-śāstra; (ii) previous judicial decisions (vyavahāra) or custom (charitra); (iii) decrees of the sovereign. "The authority of these four is in the reverse order, each preceding one being superseded by the one following it" — i.e., the king's decree overrides custom, custom overrides precedent, precedent overrides the śāstra text. The Arthashastra contains an identical provision. (Dhavan calls this "highly developed".)

Evolutionary law: "Both Manu and Parashara say: the laws of kritayuga are different from those of treta and dwapara, and the laws of kali yuga are different from those of all the previous ages — the laws of each age being according to the distinctive character of each age (yuga rūpānusārataḥ)."
Changing customs under state supervision: Kātyāyana — "whatever custom is proved to be followed in any particular region, it should be duly recorded as established (dhārya) in a record stamped with the seal of the Sovereign"; and the same authority — "when the Sovereign is satisfied that a particular custom is contrary to equity, in the same way — that is, in the way it was established — it should be annulled by a formal decision of the Sovereign." (Dhavan: "This remarkable provision indicates how highly developed was the judicial and legal system of ancient India.")

3.7 Custom within the codified system — the "two views" (M.P. Jain, DU case material)

View 1 — custom SUBORDINATE to Śruti/SmṛtiView 2 — custom SUPERIOR
Gautama: dharmas (customs) of countries, castes and families "which are not opposed to Vedic scriptures" are authoritative and binding. Manu and Yājñavalkya declare the sources as śruti, smṛti and sadāchāra in that order. Āparārka: a custom repugnant to a clear Vedic text is to be rejected. Mitākṣarā, Dāyabhāga, Mayūkha place custom below śruti/smṛti.Viśvarūpa and Medhātithi: prescriptions of Smṛtis (even of Śruti) need not be observed when "vehemently condemned by the people" (e.g., niyoga). Vṛhaspati, Nārada, Asāhāya favoured unqualified acceptance of custom even in conflict with written laws. Some Smṛtis warn that suppression of customs breeds resentment and rebellion. One Manusmṛti text itself: "Achara is transcendental law… therefore a twice-born person desirous of his own welfare should always make efforts to follow it." Kautilya (Arthashastra): usages and customs are of equal authority as evidence of law; in conflict, custom prevails as actually observed in practice.

The reconciliation (Jain's conclusion): customs played a very important role as a material source of ancient Hindu law. Dharma-sūtras brought Śruti texts in conformity with contemporary custom; Smṛtis drew heavily on the customs of the people for whom they were designed — "that largely explains why so many various Dharmashastras came into being and why they differed from each other"; when Smṛtis failed the growing needs, commentaries adapted smṛti-law by interpretation to customs that had taken root — "what the commentaries did was to take up an old text of the Dharmashastra and interpret it in such a manner that it came in harmony with social mores and customs of the people." It is how, "starting with the same texts as the base, two major schools of Hindu law developed in India (Mitākṣarā and Dāyabhāga), and one of them came to have four sub-schools."

3.9 Royal patronage of codification — the Nibandhas (Dhyani)

  • "In the face of the multitude of smritis, the Kings under their royal patronage prepared digests of Dharmashastra for resolving seeming contradictions in smritis and presenting a coherent picture of the Dharma which the people might follow and the King may enforce."
  • "When such digests or commentaries (like Mitakshara) were composed under royal direction and were upheld in the States in which they were first composed and afterwards in others can they be denied the title of laws?" — Dhyani's rhetorical answer: no.
  • Kings who composed Nibandhas: Bhoja, Aparārka, Pratāparudradeva. Writers commissioned by kings: Vijñāneśvara, Mādhavācārya, Hemādri, Kandeśvara, Vācaspati.
  • Legitimacy: "The need to follow the old law was its willing acceptance by the people as their Dharma" — and even under foreign rule (16th–17th centuries) people followed it; Nibandhas composed in the name of Todarmal, and under orders of Warren Hastings and T.E. Colebrooke, attest to the continued validity of the Smṛtis even for rulers who did not adopt Indian religion.
  • Kings "claimed descent from Manu himself and delighted to bear the title 'the King who does not swerve from the Laws of Manu'"; Kālidāsa's Raghuvaṃśa describes Dilīpa as one who "did not swerve even by a line from the path blazed out by Manu."

3.10 "Was the Dharmashastra a code?" — the balanced exam answer

Yes — it functioned as a codeNo — it was more (and different) than a code
Written, authoritative, general rules covering all life (birth to death); internal taxonomy (three Kāṇḍas; 18 titles); substantive + procedural rules (vyavahāra in "both substantive and procedural forms" — Dhyani); conflicts resolved by fixed rules of preference; state-enforced (danda); royal digests; commentaries "attained the rank of a law book in ancient and medieval India" — even in the Austinian sense; binding on king and subject alike.Source is not a sovereign command but śruti + smṛti + sadāchāra + conscience — a "four-sources" model; sanction partly inherent (public opinion, dharma itself — Bryce via Dhyani: "we have instances of 'laws' behind which there is no State as originator or sustainer…"); covers morality, religion and penance, not just law; and was dynamic, contextual and plural — different Smṛtis for different regions and ages; interpreted by reason and equity, not mechanical literalism.

Conclusion for the exam: The Dharmashastras were codification without a code's positivism — an ordered, written, state-supported DHARMA-order. Parasain's formula: the texts may be static, Dharma is dynamic; the Śruti is "the spirit of the law"; and, as the Mahābhārata insists, tradition ceases to be Dharma when it fails to solve emerging problems — hence "problem-solving approaches along with consideration of the benefit of others at large (maitribhāva) even at the cost of breaking a vow, which is merely a personal virtue." That is the living principle our courts still apply (see the Mīmāṃsā cases, §05).

04(c) Dispute Resolution System in Ancient India

4.1 The foundational philosophy (Seema Singh & Madhav Yadav)

"Bharat's approach to resolving conflicts has long been shaped by its rich and traditional knowledge system, rooted in philosophical traditions that emphasise principles of Dharma, Ahimsa, Samvada and community harmony. Rule of Dharma was the Grundnorm, and justiciable behaviour was the duty of every citizen. Amicable solution of the dispute was the first objective of the Justice System and punishment was the last resort."— Seema Singh & Madhav Yadav, "Customary Dispute Resolution Mechanism in Bharat" (DU case material)

Depth of the tradition

"The rich tradition of community-based dispute resolution in Bharat expands nearly three millennia, encompassing nearly fifteen centuries before and after the dawn of the Common Era. Amartya Sen attributes Bharat's democratic aspirations for peaceful existence to collaborative traditions and public reasoning inherent in Bharatiya ethos." The system "had continuously evolved to accommodate specific local values, customs and social structures", reflecting the pluralistic nature of Bharatiya society.

4.2 Vedic foundations — Sabha and Samiti

Roots go back to Vedic times (c. 4000–1000 BCE): the Aryans "primarily used to invoke the unwritten law of divine wisdom, reason, communal harmony and prudence, which they held was valid for heaven and earth as well". The ideal of justice (nyāya) was central to the maintenance of cosmic order (ṛta) and social order (dharma); conflict resolution looked beyond punishment to "restoration of social order and moral justice".

Sabha & Samiti

The earliest references to dispute resolution are in the Ṛgveda, "where the ideal of the concept of the sabha (assembly) and the samiti (council) emerged as democratic institutions for collective decision-making and conflict resolution. The assemblies functioned as chambers where the dispute was deliberated upon by learned members of the community including the elders, scholars and eminent citizens." The Atharvaveda "further elaborated on the role of these institutions, describing how they served as platforms for both legislative and judicial functions."

Parties, scope & the mediator

  • Parties: Praśnin (plaintiff) and Abhipraśnin (defendant) brought disputes before the Sabha.
  • Scope: civil disputes only — "these court authorities were limited to resolve civil disputes only and had no authority over criminal cases."
  • The Madhamasi was "more like a mediator or arbitrator rather than a judge who attempted to resolve conflicts rather than imposing punishments."

4.3 The Epic period — dialogue, mediation and the king under law

Mediators of the epics

Rāmāyaṇa: Vibhīṣaṇa, Aṅgada, Hanumān; Mahābhārata: Lord Krishna — "these figures depict how community leaders were able to bring the conflict parties to the dialogue table and direct them to mutually acceptable settlements", "having an instrumental role as a mediator." The Mahābhārata, through Krishna, Bhīṣma and Yudhiṣṭhira, "provides deep understanding that dispute resolution should prioritise morality and moral duty." The Rāmāyaṇa, through Lord Rāma's commitment to dharma, "exemplifies the history of justice while illustrating the ideal ethical approach to dispute resolution."

Restorative justice

"Bharatiya traditions have emphasised restorative justice that recognises disputes as a destruction within the society, unlike the adversarial legal system that focuses on victories of individuals. In ancient Bharat restorative justice was not just about punishing the wrongdoers but about making things right for the victim and the whole community."

Dhavan's rule-of-law episodes

Justice S.S. Dhavan (Allahabad HC) opens his survey with the proposition that India has the oldest judiciary in the world — "no other judicial system has a more ancient or exalted pedigree" — and proves it with the texts:

  • Mahabharata: "A King who after having sworn that he shall protect his subjects fails to protect them should be executed like a mad dog"; "The people should execute a king who does not protect them, but deprives them of their property and assets and who takes no advice or guidance from any one. Such a king is not a king but misfortune." → sovereignty based on an implied social compact; breach = forfeiture of kingship.
  • Kautilya (Arthashastra): "In the happiness of his subjects lies the King's happiness; in their welfare his welfare; whatever pleases him he shall not consider as good, but whatever pleases his people he shall consider as good."
  • Rāmāyaṇa: Rāma was compelled to banish his beloved queen Sītā because his subjects disapproved of his taking back a wife who had spent a year in her abductor's house — "the king submitted to the will of the people though it broke his heart."
  • Mahābhārata: a common fisherman refused his daughter to the King of Hastināpura unless his condition (that her son inherit) was accepted — the ancestor of the popular-sovereignty idea (Bhīṣma's renunciation).

Social contract & danda (Dhyani): In the Mahābhārata "there is a reference to social contract theory wherein king is appointed by God with the consent of the people and a dandaneeti (sanction or punishment) was created to punish those who violated Dharma. Manu too envisages the importance of punishment which is the principal Raj Dharma of the King to protect the subject… The existence of Sabha and Samitis was basic to give validity to the decisions of the King." And: "without a sovereign authority to establish law and order and maintain peace, the stronger will swallow the weaker in the manner of fish" (matsya-nyāya) — hence the State is not a "necessary evil" but necessary for the advancement of dharma.

4.4 Kautilya's Arthashastra — formalisation (4th century BCE)

"In the 4th century BCE Kautilya's Arthashastra formalized customary dispute resolution and institutionalised community-based arbitration as the central conflict resolution mode. This ancient work institutionalised not just customary norms but procedural norms as well that highlighted equality, justice, and community participation while resolving conflict. The Arthashastra acknowledged the efficacy of indigenous wisdom and customary law in conflict resolution and thus afforded scholarly legitimation to grassroots justice processes that had taken hold." (Kangle, The Kautiliya Arthashastra 7.1.35.)

Dharmastha courts

Kautilya enjoined that suits should be heard by three judges (dharmasthāstrayaḥ) — a bench, not a single judge (Dhavan: "Our present judicial system… does not follow this excellent safeguard. Today every suit is heard by a single Munsif or Civil Judge or District Judge for reasons of economy. But the state in ancient India was more interested in the quality of justice than economy.")

Kantaśodhana courts

Special criminal/administrative courts of three commissioners (pradeṣṭāraḥ) — "three commissioners or three ministers shall deal with measures to suppress disturbance to peace (kantakasodhanam kuryuh)". They took cognizance of offences against the State (false weights, adulterated goods, excessive prices, unfair wages, official misconduct, theft, dacoity, sex offences) — "all the characteristics of administrative courts".

Judicial service

"Under the Maurya Empire a regular judicial service existed"; each district had its hierarchy of judicial officers and the king remained the highest court of appeal. (Dhavan also notes the magnificent Administrative Code in the 4th part of the Arthashastra — state shipping, textiles, mining, commerce — see §2.5.)

4.5 Dharmashastra-era tribunals (Seema Singh & Yadav)

  • Yājñavalkya's tribunal: "Yajnavalkya Smriti is divided into 4 types of courts: Shreni (for artisans), Puga (for traders), and Kula (for family), which only tried issues related to civil cases, and appeals from these were heard by Judges (Highest Court), who were appointed by the King."
  • Parāśara: certain issues should be determined by parishad — an association/assembly of the learned, deciding "on the principles of justice, equity and good conscience". "The modern legislative theory of resolving disputes by domestic forum can find its origin in an ancient customary law in Bharat."
  • Patañjali: "Progress comes swiftly, in mediation for those who try hardest, instead of deciding who was right and who was wrong."
  • Evidence already "modern": witness testimony (sākṣī), documentary evidence (lekha), circumstantial evidence (kāryapramāṇa) — "while also recognising the limitations of human perception and the possibility of error in judgment."
  • Prāyaścitta (atonement): fasting, chanting mantras, donations, rituals — "focused on rehabilitation rather than retribution"; different disputes got different fora: commercial disputes → merchant-guild arbitration; matrimonial disputes → extended family with respected elders.
  • Satyāgraha's roots: "though later popularised by Mahatma Gandhi, [it] had its roots in ancient Bharat philosophy where truth (satya) was considered the ultimate arbiter of disputes."
  • Nārada Smṛti elaborately described the procedures to deal with disputes; vyavahāra is "the term used in ancient legal literature to denote law and law-suits."

4.6 The institutional architecture — hierarchy of courts

Sudame's five-tier hierarchy (Rama Jois chart, as in DU material)

NṚPA / ŚĀSITĀ King's court — highest; only authority to inflict fines & corporal punishment; hears appeals from all below
ADHIKṚTA court appointed by the king — can try violent offences too
GAṆA assembly of persons of one place, different castes/avocations
ŚREṆĪ corporation of same craft/profession/trade
KULA assembly of impartial persons of the family/caste — functioning as panchayatdars

The first three are the "People's Courts" — jurisdiction over all civil and criminal matters except offences involving violence. "This kind of gradation encouraged cultural and professional diversity in courts — one of the major requisites of judicial appointments even today… ensure[d] the correct understanding of prevalent social and professional situations for every matter."
Appeals: inter-se — Śreṇī reviewed Kula; Gaṇa reviewed Śreṇī; and direct appeal to the King from any authority (Kula, Śreṇī, Gaṇa). "Article 136 of the Indian Constitution finds its roots in this provision."

Dhavan's hierarchy (Bṛhaspati Smṛti) & the modern mirror

Vācaspati Miśra: "The binding effect of the decisions of these tribunals, ending with that of the king, is in the ascending order, and each following decision shall prevail against the preceding one because of the higher degree of learning and knowledge."
The modern mirror (Dhavan): "the Indian judiciary today also consists of a hierarchy of courts organised on a similar principle — the village courts, the Munsif, the Civil Judge, the District Judge, the High Court, and finally the Supreme Court which takes the place of the King's Court. We are following an ancient tradition without being conscious of it."
Why family courts mattered: the joint family could be very large; disputes needed "firmness combined with sympathy and tact"; "the judicial system had its roots in the social system, which explains its success" (modern Japan has a similar family-court system).

Nature of the courts (Sudame)

Pratiṣṭhita courts were established in some village/town; Apratiṣṭhita were mobile courts; Mudrita were courts appointed by the king and authorised to use the royal seal. The court hall was the Dharmādhikaraṇa. Art. 136 root-note: "The king was vested with the power to hear appeals from the decisions of any authority including Kula, Sreni and Gana." The Law Commission of India (77th Report, "Delay and Arrears in Trial Courts") remarked that "with the progressing society, the panchayats and assemblies of brotherhood or of local respectables… did not cease functioning altogether because of their useful role of settling minor disputes in rural areas through compromise and that is why the British retained them through necessary legislation."

4.7 The King's court — personnel, oaths and the code of conduct

The KingFountain source of justice — "dispensing justice and awarding punishment was one of the primary attributes of sovereignty"; expected to administer justice in person, "but strictly according to law, and under the guidance of judges learned in law." Kātyāyana: "The king should enter the court-room modestly dressed (vineeta-vesha), take his seat facing east, and with an attentive mind hear the suits of his litigants. He should act under the guidance of his Chief Justice (Praadvivaka), judges, ministers and the Brahmana members of his council. When the judge realises that the king has deviated from equity and justice, his duty is not to please the king — for this is no occasion for soft speech (vaktavyam tat priyam natra); if the judge fails in his duty, he is guilty." His duty was to give the final opinion; the king passed the final decree.

Sabhāsada (jurors/assessors)"The most noteworthy feature of the judicial system." Counselors who sat with the sovereign as assessors — equivalent of the modern jury, with one important difference: they were learned in law (the modern jury is "twelve shopkeepers"). Yājñavalkya: "The Sovereign should appoint as assessors of his court persons who are well versed in the literature of the law, truthful, and by temperament capable of complete impartiality between friend and foe." They had to speak without fear — Kātyāyana: "The assessors should not look on when they perceive the Sovereign inclined to decide a dispute in violation of the law; if they keep silent they will go to hell accompanied by the King" (repeated in Śukra-nītisāra). The Sovereign was to pass the decree (jaya-patra) in accordance with their verdict — compare the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council ("humbly advise", but binding) and Soviet people's assessors. Exception: if the assessors could not decide a "difficult or doubtful" dispute (sandigdha-rūpīṇaḥ), the Sovereign might decide in exercise of his sovereign privilege.

⚠️ Distinction preserved (do not confuse with modern practice)

In criminal trials the question of guilt/innocence was decided by the judges/jurors, but the quantum of punishment was left to the King — as in the trial scene of the Mṛcchakaṭikā ("The Little Clay Cart"): after pronouncing Charudatta guilty of Vasantasenā's murder, the judge remarks — "The decision with regard to Charudatta's guilt or innocence lies with us and our decision is binding (Pramanam), but the rest lies with the King."

"Five causes destroy impartiality and lead to judges taking sides in disputes." Judges were forbidden to talk to parties privately while the suit was pending — a private hearing was recognised as leading to pakṣapāta. Integrity and the punishment of corruption

  • Bṛhaspati: "A judge should decide cases without any consideration of personal gain or any kind of personal bias; and his decision should be in accordance with the procedure prescribed by the texts. A judge who performs his judicial duties in this manner achieves the same spiritual merit as a person performing a Yajna."
  • "Corruption was regarded as a heinous offence and all the authorities are unanimous in prescribing the severest punishment on a dishonest judge": Bṛhaspati — "A judge should be banished from the realm if he takes bribes and thereby perpetrates injustice and betrays the confidence reposed in him by a trusting public. A corrupt judge, a false witness, and the murderer of a Brahmin are in the same class of criminals." Viṣṇu: "The state should confiscate the entire property of a judge who is corrupt."
  • Śukra-nītisāra's full catalogue: judges should be "well versed in procedure, wise, of good character and temperament, soft in speech, impartial to friend or foe, truthful, learned in law, active (not lazy), free from anger, greed, or desire (for personal gain)."

4.9 Evidence, ordeals and the pursuit of truth

  • No ordeals where proof exists: "Our judicial system prohibited resort to supernatural devices [trial by ordeal], if oral or documentary evidence was available" — whereas "in England it prevailed till the very close of the middle ages." (Dhavan: "the law of evidence is an index of the quality of a judicial system. In this respect the Indian judicial system was in advance of any other system of antiquity.")
  • The real test: "In disputes the Court has to ascertain what is true and what is false from the witnesses" (Gautama).
  • Perjury abhorred: "bearing false witness was viewed with great abhorrence"; "all the smritis were unanimous in holding that perjury before a law court was a heinous sin as well as a serious crime" (Bṛhaspati V.34; Manu VIII.80-87; Yājñavalkya II.73-74; Nārada I.220-228; Baudhāyana I.13-19).
  • External testimony to a thousand-year tradition of truthfulness: Megasthenes (3rd c. BCE): "Truth they hold in high esteem"; Fa Hien and Huan Tsiang (7th c. CE, reign of Harsha) recorded similar observations. "A virtue practised for a thousand years became a tradition."
  • Oath administered by the judge himself, "not by a peon as today": Bṛhaspati — "Judges who are well-versed in the dharmashastra should address the witness in words praising truth and driving away falsehood (from his mind)"; Nārada — "The judges should inspire awe in the witness by citing moral precepts which should uphold the majesty of truth and condemn falsehood." The address was "not a set of words but a moral exhortation intended to put the fear of God in him."
  • No delay in examining witnesses: Kātyāyana — "The Sovereign should not grant any delay in the deposition of witnesses; for delay leads to great evil and results in witnesses turning away from the law" — "obviously because delay dims the memory and stimulates imagination."
  • Types of evidence recognised: sākṣī (witnesses), lekha (documents), kāryapramāṇa (circumstantial) — with acknowledged limits of human perception (Seema Singh & Yadav).
  • Dhavan's summary: "The fundamental duty of the Court was to do justice 'without favour or fear'"; the four kinds of decision — Dharma (wrongdoer confesses), Vyavahāra (evidence adduced on conflicting statements), Charitra (precedent/usage squarely applying), Rājaśāsana (king's judgment where no principle or evidence) — "thereby securing the system from the menace of arbitrariness" (Sudame).

4.10 Summary — Dhavan's fundamentals of the ancient judicial process

✔ Trial always in public, always by several judges collectively; cases in serial order except urgency.
✔ Delay condemned — dilatory judges punished; the Sovereign was not to interfere with the judiciary; rather, the judiciary was under a duty to interfere with a wrong decision of the king.
✔ Judges impartial; no private relations with parties; partiality, harassment or deliberate breach of procedure punishable; corruption = banishment + forfeiture.
✔ Supernatural proof discouraged; accused could not be punished unless guilt proved according to law; perjury crime + sin.
✔ Res judicata (prāṅ-nyāya) known; decrees of all courts except the King's subject to appeal/review on fixed principles.
✔ The verdict of the modern English writer J.W. Spellman: "In some respects the judicial system of ancient India was theoretically in advance of our own today."

And the verdict on the colonial misrepresentation (Dhavan): H. Mayne called the ancient system "an apparatus of cruel absurdities"; Alan Gledhill wrote there was "a dearth of legal principles"; an Anglo-Indian jurist spoke of "oriental habits of life". These statements "are untrue… the effect of this misrepresentation, which has few parallels in history, was to create a false picture of the Indian judicial system both in India and outside." (Fair-minded British Indologists — Havell, Basham, Spellman — do not share the prejudice.)

4.11 Medieval India — continuity, then Islam (Dhavan)

Post-Harsha continuity

After Harsha's empire disintegrated, "a veil of obscurity descends" — but the judicial system was not uprooted: "the standards and ideals of justice were maintained in each kingdom, in spite of political divisions, the unity of civilisation was preserved, and the fundamental principles of law and procedure were applied throughout the country" — evidenced by all-India authority of the great commentaries (Mitākṣarā, Śukranītisāra) written in this period.

The Sultanate

Islam brought "a new religion, a new civilisation, and a new social system". The ideal of justice was among the highest of the Middle Ages: Quran — "Justice is the balance of God upon earth…"; and "to God a moment spent in the dispensation of justice is better than the devotion of the man who keeps fast every day and says prayer every night for 60 years." The first Qadi was appointed by Caliph Umar, who "enunciated the principle that the law was supreme and that the judge must never be subservient to the ruler" — he even dismissed a Qadi who rose out of deference when Umar appeared in his own suit. Under Balban (per Barani), justice was "the keystone of sovereignty". BUT: "the outstanding feature of the entire Sultanate period was confusion and chaos… everything was temporary, personal, and had no basic strength… if the King was drunk, his magistrates were seen drunk in public. Justice is not possible without security, and the Sultans of India never felt secure." (Contrast: Badaoni records that under Muhammad Tughlaq the Qadi dismissed a libel suit filed by the King himself against Shaikhzada Jami — though the Sultan then executed the defendant without trial.)

The Mughal system

  • Unit of judicial administration was the Qazi (borrowed from the Caliphate); every provincial capital had one; at the head, the Qāzī-ul-quzāt; every town/qasba had its Qazi. In theory he had to be "a Muslim scholar of blameless life, thoroughly conversant with the prescriptions of the sacred law."
  • The Imperial Diwan's charge to a new Qazi: "Be Just, be honest, be impartial. Hold trials in the presence of the parties and at the court-house… Do not accept presents from the people of the place where you serve… Write your decrees, sale-deeds, mortgage bonds and other legal documents very carefully, so that learned men may not pick holes in them and bring you to shame. Know poverty (faqr) to be your glory (fakhr)." — "But due to lack of supervision and absence of good tradition, these noble ideals were not observed… all the Qazis of the Mughal period, with a few honourable exceptions, were notorious for taking bribes" (Jadunath Sarkar).
  • The Emperor was the fountain source of justice; he held court every Wednesday, hearing selected cases as a court of highest appeal, not original court; all Emperors from Akbar to Aurangzeb took the judicial function seriously; Jahangir's Golden Chain is famous.
  • The structural weakness (Sarkar): all three sources of Indo-Muhammadan law were outside India; no Indian Emperor's or Qazi's decision was ever accepted as laying down a legal principle; Muslim law in India "was therefore incapable of growth and change, except so far as it reflected changes of juristic thought in Arabia or Egypt." (Contrast with the Indian Smṛti-Nibandha tradition, which grew locally.)
  • Legacy: after Aurangzeb, provincial governors/faujdars arrogated sovereign powers — "a relic of this usurpation of the Emperors' power is the name Faujdari given to criminal trials even today"; the Sadre Diwani Adalat continued till replaced by the High Courts; our terminology is Mughal: Munsif, Mudda'i, Mudda'liya.

4.12 Colonial disruption and the persistence of custom (Seema Singh & Yadav + Dhyani)

18th c.Company sets up parallel systems of laws and courts; courts "entirely staffed by officials" — public participation in justice administration prohibited.
1772Warren Hastings' adalat system (Bengal, Bihar, Orissa): inheritance, marriage, caste and religious usages decided "according to the laws of the Shaster" for Hindus and the Koran for Muslims; 1781 — for unprovided cases, "justice, equity and good conscience" (Act of Settlement; later s.37 Bengal, Agra & Assam Civil Courts Act 1887 which used "Muhammadan law and Hindu law").
1860IPC, CrPC, Contract Act + Baden-Powell's land-revenue framework "replaced customary law"; Collectorates and courts assumed authority from caste/village panchayats. "The influence of the IPC and CrPC on the adaptation of European concepts of equality in Bharat is hardly considered. British laws sidelined the customs and usages of the common people. The focus shifted from community feeling to individual rights."
1879Ravinder Kumar's Western India in the Nineteenth Century: the panchayat system protected the Kunbis (agriculturists), who knew the traditional authority and its social equity; the Vanis (money-lenders' agents) gained under the doctrine of freedom of contract; Kunbis regained a right to survive only through the conciliatory provisions of the Deccan Agricultural Debt Relief Act, 1879. "The British legal system codified the laws that resulted in procedures that were unfamiliar to the ordinary people and were not in conformity with the codes and rules of local traditions."
1920sNumerous legislations enacted on state recommendations to revive Nyaya Panchayats — but "there was an absence of systematic oversight over the administration of justice in villages." "Despite colonial attempts to undermine indigenous mechanisms, customary forms of adjudication persisted in numerous regions, demonstrating their enduring tenacity and profound cultural foundations."
Gandhi's verdictGandhiji "consistently advocated for a boycott of law courts and endorsed village panchayats" — to save poor peasants the journey, the time, the money and the exploitation by lawyers. India of My Dreams: "In Panchayat Raj only the Panchayat will be obeyed, and the Panchayat can only work through the law of their making." Prof. N.G. Ranga: "Our masses would not be able to play their rightful part in our democracy without this foundation stone of village Panchayats."

Dhyani's wider critique (the "soul" of this sub-topic): the English law received by India "was imposed by colonial rulers by a stroke of pen which systematically destroyed the Indian social traditions, laws and moral values," and independent India made "no systematic effort… to study, analyse and evaluate the basic fundamentals of indigenous law and legal theory." Krishna Iyer: "Transplantation of English system cannot succeed except in a limited way in India… the colonial heritage is now a hindrance to socialist progress and development of our essentially Indian people-oriented legal system. The all-too large gap, even conflict between law and life is possible due to this failure of Indian creativity"; and "unfortunately even after liberation many former colonies including India did not shake off this neo-colonial jurisprudence." The imperatives of independence demanded "justice, equity and good conscience" be severed from its anglophilic bias: "conscience is the finer texture of norms woven from ethos and lifestyle of a community… Free India has to find its conscience in our rugged realities and no more in alien thought." (Rattan Lal v. Vardesh Chander, AIR 1976 SC 589, per Krishna Iyer J.)
Balance: "it must be admitted that the benefits of British jurisprudence in the form of rule of law, prerogative writs and independence of judiciary were certainly shared and enjoyed by the Indians" — hence the need for an objective assessment of both streams, and the answer to "can we succeed by ignoring our ancient philosophy?" is: no — but not by blind imitation either: "only blind imitation of western legal ideas is a kind of national humiliation… a mere carbon copy of others" (Dhyani; Panikkar's trilogy of assimilation, tolerance, synthesis).

GROUP A — Texts, codification and interpretation

CASE 1A.S. Narayana Deekshitulu v. State of Andhra Pradesh(1996) 9 SCC 548 · Supreme Court · K. Ramaswamy J. (Hansaria J. concurring)
Cited in: Ila Sudame, "The Shaastra of Law" (DU case material, Unit 3(e)) — for the definition of dharma adopted from Madhvācārya; also cited in Parasain's eco-philosophy frame. | Why it is the single most important modern case of this unit: the Supreme Court consciously imported the ancient definition of dharma into constitutional adjudication.
Facts

Chapter IV of the Andhra Pradesh Charitable and Hindu Religious Institutions and Endowments Act, 1987 abolished the hereditary rights of religious office-holders (Archakas, Mirasidars — priests and temple servants, e.g., of the Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams), converting their remuneration into salaries/honoraria and vesting administration in statutory authorities. A batch of petitions challenged the chapter's constitutional validity.

Issues

(1) Did the abolition of hereditary offices and the substitution of secular pay scales violate the freedom of religion guaranteed by Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution? (2) Where is the line between religion and its secular administration? (3) What is the meaning of dharma in constitutional discourse?

Arguments of the petitioners (office-holders/Mirasidars)

Performing the pūjā and temple services is an essential religious practice, an integral part of the Hindu faith; hereditary office carries religious dignity and customary honours, which cannot be converted into a mere salary; the Act interfered with the right of the denomination to manage its own religious affairs (Art 26) and with the individual's right to profess and practise religion (Art 25).

Arguments of the State

The administration and management of a temple and its properties is a secular activity, which the State may regulate by legislation in the public interest; the abolition of hereditary perquisites (a share of offerings/income) does not touch the essentials of religious belief or worship — it merely modernises remuneration and prevents exploitation and mismanagement; the interests of the devotees and the institution as a whole prevail over the pecuniary privileges of a class of office-holders.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of Chapter IV. It drew the classic distinction (echoing earlier Art 25 jurisprudence) between religious faith, doctrines and essential practices (protected) and the secular administration of religious endowments (regulable by the State). K. Ramaswamy J., examining the ancient sources, expressly adopted the definition of dharma given by Madhvācārya in his commentary on the Parāśara Smṛti: "Dharma is that which sustains and ensures the progress and welfare of all in this world and eternal bliss in the other world" — further condensed by the Court as: dharma is that "which regulates the mutual obligations of individual and the society". Hansaria J. (concurring): "Dharma is for the stability of the society, the maintenance of social order and the general well-being and progress of humankind." The Court also directed the constitution of a committee to determine appropriate salaries/honoraria for the abolished hereditary offices (later implemented for the TTD by the sequel order of 1997).

Principle evolved: Dharma ≠ religion in the Article 25 sense. Dharma is the secular-moral law of social order ("that which sustains… regulates the mutual obligations of individual and society"); it is the ancient constitutional substratum of the modern Indian legal order, and a court may use Dharmashastric definitions as persuasive, living sources of constitutional principle without reviving the shastras as law. The religious service and the person who performs it are different; the latter is regulable.
Exam linkage

Any 20-mark answer on "Does ancient Indian jurisprudence survive in modern law?" should open or close with this case: it proves the institutional a-priori claim (Parasain) in action. Pair it with Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam (Mīmāṃsā applied) and Vytla Sitanna (panchayat award upheld).

CASE 2Abhiraj Kuer v. Debendra SinghAIR 1962 SC 351 · Supreme Court
Cited in: Ila Sudame, "The Shaastra of Law" — for the caution required in interpreting "books of morality, religion, law, etc." and the recommendatory vs mandatory distinction. Also the standard authority on treating Smṛti/commentary statements as recommendatory.
Facts

A dispute over the validity of an adoption under the Hindu law of the Banaras school — the adopted person being one whom the adopter "could not have married" (the viruddha sambandha / affinity rule applied by analogy from the law of marriage to adoption), and the adoption challenged as violating that prohibition. To decide the question the Court had to interpret the texts of the Smṛtis and the commentaries and to determine which statements are binding law and which are only moral-religious exhortation.

Issues

(1) Is the smṛti/commentary rule prohibiting adoption of a person within the prohibited marriage relationship mandatory law or only recommendatory? (2) How are "books of morality, religion and law" to be interpreted — and is a commentary's statement always a statement of law?

Arguments of the appellant (challenging the adoption)

The text of the shastra is unambiguous and prohibitory; the rule against such affinity applies with equal force to adoption (which is assimilated to marriage for prohibited degrees), hence the adoption is void; the commentary must be read as declaring mandatory law.

Arguments of the respondent (upholding the adoption)

The statement in the texts/commentaries is a moral or religious precept of a recommendatory nature, not a legal prohibition; long-standing usage and acceptance in the school of law show that such adoptions were regarded as valid; the true law is to be gathered from usage and the authoritative commentaries read as a whole.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court held that the rule in question is recommendatory and not mandatory, and upheld the validity of the adoption. In doing so the Court enunciated the governing hermeneutic: while interpreting books of morality, religion and law, a great deal of caution is required; the commentaries "are many-a-times mingled with statements of law but actually are intended to be recommendatory statements regarding the mandatory laws" — i.e., the commentator may be describing an ideal, not prescribing a legal rule.

Principle evolved: (1) The dual character of Dharmashastric statement — mandatory legal rule vs recommendatory moral precept — is a real and judicially enforceable distinction; only the former binds. (2) Courts must read ancient texts within their own genre ("caution"), not as modern statutes. (3) This is the doctrinal bridge between the "codification" of the Smṛtis and the separation of law from morality — which is exactly Dhyani's point that Smṛti-writers gave law "a coherent and logical treatment… distinguishable from other non-legal values".
Exam linkage

Use for "Was Dharmashastra law or morality?" and for "How did the courts treat the texts?" — pair with the Bombay Full Bench's Narada maxim (Case 3).

CASE 3Dnyaneshwar B. Ganwat v. Karyakari Abhiyanta, Chaskaman Prakalp Vibhag2010 SCC OnLine Bom 766 · Full Bench, Bombay High Court
Cited in: Ila Sudame — for the authority of the ancient jurist Nārada's maxim in resolving conflicts between law codes of equal authority.
Facts

Before a Full Bench of the Bombay High Court arose a question on which two lines of authorities of equal authority (a statutory provision and its interpretation in conflicting decisions/juristic opinion) differed. The Full Bench had to settle which view to follow in the matter (in the Chaskaman irrigation-project acquisition context, per the case material).

Issues

How should a court choose between two conflicting texts/views of equal authority — by mere seniority of the precedent, or by the reasonableness of the competing interpretations?

Arguments (as summarised by the Full Bench)

One view: the later/other authority having equal force, the court is bound to follow the settled line — predictability over reasonableness. The other view: where codes of equal authority conflict, the court should adopt the construction which is more reasonable and consonant with equity — the approach supported by modern jurists (Seervai) and by the ancient Indian rule itself.

Decision and reasoning

The Full Bench preferred the reasonable view, holding that the approach was "in consonance with what ancient Jurist Narada declared — Dharmashastra Virodhe Tu Yuktiykta Vidhe Smrita — that is, when the Dharmashastra or law codes of equal authority conflict with one another, the one appearing to be reasonable, or more reasonable, is to be preferred and followed." The modern view of jurist H.M. Seervai advocating a similar principle was relied upon.

Principle evolved: The Mīmāṃsā/Nārada canon — reason (yukti) prevails where authoritative texts of equal standing conflict — is not antiquarian lore but a live rule of interpretation, expressly applied by a modern Full Bench and consistent with the best modern practice. (It also matches the Smṛti rule quoted by Dhyani: "If the two texts differ, reason must prevail in practice.")
CASE 4Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. v. Essar Power Ltd.(2008) 4 SCC 755 · Supreme Court · Markandey Katju J.
Cited in: Ila Sudame — for the Supreme Court's express application of the Mīmāṃsā axioms (sāmāñjasya, vikalpa, guṇapradhāna) to ss. 174–175 of the Electricity Act, 2003.
Facts

Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam Ltd. (a State utility) and Essar Power Ltd. were parties under the Electricity Act regime and a power purchase arrangement. A question arose whether Section 174 of the Electricity Act, 2003 (which gives the Act an overriding effect over other laws) ousted the operation of Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (appointment of arbitrators), given that Section 175 provides that the Electricity Act is "in addition to and not in derogation of" any other law.

Issues

(1) How to construe the apparent conflict between s.174 and s.175 of the Electricity Act, 2003 — which is the principal (pradhāna) and which the subordinate (guṇa) provision? (2) Whether the Arbitration Act machinery survives the Electricity Act's overriding clause.

Arguments of the appellant (GUVNL)

Section 174 makes the Electricity Act paramount over every other law; therefore the arbitration provisions of the 1996 Act stand displaced, and the appointment/applications must proceed exclusively under the Electricity Act regime.

Arguments of the respondent (Essar Power)

Section 175 expressly preserves other laws — the Act is only "in addition to" them; the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 squarely applies, including s.11; the two sections can be harmonised, s.174 not being meant to swallow every incidental remedy.

Decision and reasoning

Katju J., for the Court, held that the Mīmāṃsā principles of interpretation are "of great utility" in resolving such conflicts. Applying them: (i) sāmāñjasya (harmony) — the two provisions can be read harmoniously; (ii) guṇapradhāna — "Section 174 is the pradhan whereas Section 175 is the guna (or subordinate)… the principle laid down in Section 174 is the principal or primary whereas the principle laid down in Section 175 is the accessory or subordinate to the principal. Hence Section 174 will prevail over Section 175 in matters where there is any conflict (but no further)"; (iii) vikalpa/bādha — where really irreconcilable, the principal prevails. Read thus, the sections do not oust the Arbitration Act generally — s.174 prevails only to the extent of actual conflict.

Principle evolved: Mīmāṃsā is a working system of statutory interpretation for Indian courts. Its axioms — śruti, linga, vākya, prakāraṇa (construction rules) and sārthakya, lāghava, arthaikatva, guṇapradhāna, sāmāñjasya, vikalpa (axioms) — correspond to, and in the Court's words anticipate, the canons of modern interpretation (literal rule, contextual reading, harmonious construction, avoidance of surplusage). The judgment is the most concrete proof that the Indian knowledge system supplies method to modern law — the very thesis of this unit.
Exam linkage

Use in (b) — "Codification of Law": the codes had an inbuilt science of interpretation; the proof is the Supreme Court applying it in 2008. Also use in the Sources of Law unit (interpretation of statutes).

GROUP B — Dispute resolution, legitimacy and judicial integrity

CASE 5Vytla Sitanna v. Marivada Viranna(1934) ILR 57 Mad 749 = 36 Bom LR 563 · Privy Council
Cited in: Seema Singh & Madhav Yadav — the decisive authority that panchayat awards are enforceable; "indigenous solutions for indigenous problems". (The DU reading's footnote cites it as "(1934) ILR 57 Mad 749"; the same decision is reported at 36 Bom LR 563 (PC) — the award was upheld by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council, which the reading refers to as "the Supreme Court".)
Facts

One Tirupayya died, leaving property in the Godavari district. Disputes arose over the estate: it was alleged that Ammanna had been adopted by Tirupayya's father Dorayya as an illatom son-in-law and had acquired a share, while the widow (Venkamma) and others claimed for themselves. The family referred the disputes to a panchayat of respected members (panchayatdars). The panchayat gave an award partitioning the estate between the widow and Ammanna (leaving certain jewels with Venkamma without deciding her title). Without stating the parties' contentions or reasons, the panchayatdars' award satisfied everyone: the widow and Ammanna both signed a muchilika addressed to the Sub-Collector of Godavari, informing him of the award and binding themselves to abide by it.

Issues

(1) Was the panchayat award a bona fide settlement of a genuine dispute, binding on the estate, or an arrangement operating to defeat other claimants (the plaintiff)? (2) Could the award — 41 years later, after the widow's death — be reopened? (3) Was the panchayat's failure to record reasons/determine the illatom adoption fatal?

Arguments of the appellant (who challenged the award's effects)

The so-called adoption was not proved and was never set up at the time of the award; the widow could not have conveyed a good title beyond her own interest; the panchayat's award, being unreasoned and made without deciding the adoption claim, was not a bona fide settlement and cannot bind the estate; the increase of the estate from 8 to 44 acres was the widow's own work — she was thus entitled to press her claims.

Arguments of the respondent (relying on the award)

Ammanna had given up his rights in his own family and had managed Dorayya's family affairs — he was treated as one of the family; the award was a genuine compromise of a real, bitter family dispute; both parties accepted it in writing, submitted it to the Collector and lived by it for four decades; the estate, having been enjoyed under the award, cannot now be redistributed.

Decision and reasoning

Their Lordships (the majority agreeing with Phillips J.'s view in the lower court) held that the award was a bona fide settlement of disputes which was binding on the estate, which had been represented by the widow. The panchayatdars' omission to record reasons or contentions did not vitiate the award; their mode of dealing satisfied everyone at the time, and the award stood unquestioned for 41 years until the widow's death. The panchayat's award was upheld and given full effect.

Principle evolved: Award by a panchayat (village elders' tribunal) is a legally effective disposition — a bona fide settlement binding on the parties and their estates, recognised and enforceable by the highest courts; the ancient consensual forum is not a "pre-legal" curiosity but a valid institution of dispute resolution. The Privy Council (and later Indian courts) treat such awards as arbitration settlements, thereby giving customary ADR formal recognition and enforceability. (This is the doctrinal anchor for Lok Adalats, mediation and the Mediation Act, 2023.)
Exam linkage

Use in (c) for every "revival of panchayat justice" question; pair with the Law Commission's 77th Report observation and the Constitutional provisions (Art 40, 73rd Amendment, Schedules 5 & 6).

CASE 6Rattan Lal v. Vardesh ChanderAIR 1976 SC 589 · Supreme Court · Krishna Iyer J.
Cited in: Dhyani (fn. 6) — for Justice Krishna Iyer's philosophy of "justice, equity and good conscience" as an Indian, not Anglican, conscience.
Facts

A criminal proceeding arising in the context of the Indian-cum-Jammu & Kashmir law where the Court had to apply the concepts of justice, equity and good conscience inherited from the colonial legal order, and to assess a beneficial sentencing/probation scheme across legal systems.

Issues

Can the colonial formula "justice, equity and good conscience" be treated as a neutral, culture-free import of English law? Or must "conscience" mean the conscience of the Indian community?

Arguments of the appellant/respondent sides (per the material)

One side invoked the received English rule and its precedents (the classic "justice, equity and good conscience" = English law as it stood in 1772 or 1887). The other side urged that the formula must be given a content drawn from the Indian ethos, not from English law-books.

Decision and reasoning

Krishna Iyer J., in his characteristic reformist vein, held that transplanted concepts cannot be mechanically applied; the "conscience" of the formula is "the finer texture of norms woven from ethos and lifestyle of a community" — and since "British and Indian ways of life vary so much… the validity of an anglophilic bias in Bharat's justice, equity and good conscience is questionable today. Free India has to find its conscience in our rugged realities and no more in alien thought." (The judgment also carries his wider warning that the gap between law and life results from "the failure of Indian creativity" — the colonial heritage as "a hindrance to socialist progress.")

Principle evolved: Judicial creativity anchored in indigenous values. The general clauses used by the colonial system are to be filled with Indian social content; a "decolonised" conscience is part of a genuinely Indian legal theory. This is the modern judicial voice of Dhyani's thesis.
CASE 7Ashok Kumar v. Union of IndiaAIR 1991 SC 1792 · Supreme Court · Ahmadi J.
Cited in: Dhyani — "Liberty is the life line of every human being." Dhyani presents it as the Apex Court's echo of the ancient concern for personal liberty (the king's Dharma included freedom and liberty as a subject of Rājadharma — Sudame).
Facts

Challenge/consideration in the context of a detention matter concerning the liberty of an individual (a preventive-detention-related proceeding), where the Court refused to countenance State action infringing personal liberty beyond what the law permits.

Issues

The scope of judicial protection of personal liberty against executive action under the law of preventive detention.

Arguments

The State: statutory power to detain and the presumption of regularity; the detenu: detention not supported by the statutory conditions, and liberty being a fundamental right, its deprivation must strictly satisfy the statute.

Decision and reasoning

Striking the balance, Ahmadi J. declared: "Liberty is the life line of every human being. Life without liberty is 'lasting' but not 'living'. Liberty is, therefore, considered one of the most precious and cherished possessions of a human being. Any attempt to take liberties with the liberty of a human being is visited with resistance."

Principle evolved: Personal liberty is the most precious possession; State action infringing it must be strictly justified by law. Dhyani uses the case to show that the emerging human-rights jurisprudence — which he traces to India's ancient valuation of the individual within the social order — is now the "signature tune of new legal theory".
CASE 8Ramon Services Pvt. Ltd. v. Subhash KapoorAIR 2001 SC 207 · Supreme Court
Cited in: Dhyani (fn. 36) — for the postulate of the role of law and the lawyer in the new economic order.
Facts

Proceedings arising out of a complaint (defamation-type) involving a corporate employer and an individual; while disposing of the matter the Court addressed the larger question of the profession's public role in a changing economy.

Issues

Is the practice of law a mere trade? What are the Bar's obligations in a legal-order where legal aid, PIL and rule of law responsibilities are expanding?

Arguments

Narrow view: law is a business of briefs and fees. Wider view (accepted): law is a public trust instrument.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court observed: "Law is no trade or briefs of the litigants nor merchandise. In the days ahead legal aid to the poor and the weak, public interest litigation and other rule of law responsibilities will demand a whole range of responsibilities from the Bar… the hope of democracy is the dynamism of the new frontiersmen of the law in this developing area… [and cater to] such free service to the community in jural sector as part of the profession's tryst with people of India."

Principle evolved: The lawyer is a officer of justice, not a trader; access to justice for the poor is the profession's constitutional tryst. Dhyani cites this as the imperative of the welfare-state stage — the modern echo of the ancient idea that the king's dharma included the protection of the weak and impartial justice for litigants.

GROUP C — Modern rights jurisprudence linked to the ancient frame (Dhyani's chain)

CASE 9Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan(1997) 6 SCC 241 · Supreme Court
Cited in: Dhyani — non-discrimination on grounds of sex; gender justice as part of the new egalitarian legal theory.
Facts

Petition after the gang-rape of social worker Bhanwari Devi; the absence of effective law against sexual harassment of women at the workplace; international instruments (CEDAW) invoked in the absence of domestic legislation.

Issues

Is there a justiciable right of women to be free from sexual harassment at the workplace? How is the vacuum to be filled?

Arguments

Petitioners: the State had failed to protect a constitutional right under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21; CEDAW (ratified by India) and the Beijing platform should guide the court; guidelines needed as interim law. Respondents: absence of legislation; court cannot legislate.

Decision and reasoning

The Court laid down the Vishaka Guidelines (drawn from CEDAW), binding until a statute is enacted — the foundation of the later Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013. The right to a workplace free of sexual harassment was read into Article 21.

Principle evolved: Where the legislature is silent, the judiciary may draw on international standards to give effect to fundamental rights — judicial creativity for the weak. Dhyani uses such cases to show "a powerful movement in legal theory towards… human rights jurisprudence"— "weaving a common moral law by tilting the beam of the scale of justice in favour of the common man denied so long… their legitimate due — liberty, dignity, equality, fairness."
CASE 10A.D.M. Jabalpur v. Shiv Kant ShuklaAIR 1976 SC 1207 · Supreme Court (Emergency)
Cited in: Dhyani — as part of the pre-Emergency and Emergency-era record of judicial restraint in safeguarding liberty.
Facts

During the 1975–77 Emergency, writ petitions challenged detentions under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA) and the denial of Article 21 protection by executive order.

Issues

Can a person move Article 32 for enforcement of Article 21 when the President's order under Article 359 suspends the right to move courts for enforcement of fundamental rights?

Arguments

Petitioners: Article 21 is the natural right of life and liberty — "the right to life does not depend on the Constitution" — courts cannot be shut out. Union: the suspension order is absolute; no writ lies.

Decision and reasoning

A majority (4:1) held that during the suspension, no petition for Article 21 could be maintained; the majority also rejected the "natural rights" argument. Justice H.R. Khanna's powerful dissent insisted that life and liberty are not the gift of the Constitution and even the Constitution's framers could not permit their complete effacement by a mere executive order.

Principle evolved (post-Emergence): The majority's position was effectively overruled in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) and the "basic structure"/natural-rights reading of Article 21 now prevails. Dhyani cites this case precisely to show that "for about three decades the judicial process could not respond in equal measure in safeguarding human liberty" — and that the post-Emergency judiciary (Krishna Iyer, Bhagwati, Desai, Chinnappa Reddy, Ranganath Misra, A.S. Anand, Kuldip Singh, K.T. Thomas) "interpreted law more radically and progressively with a slant towards protection of the interest of the weak and the poor."
CASE 11A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras  ·  R.C. Cooper v. Union of India  ·  I.C. Golak Nath v. State of PunjabAIR 1950 SC 27 · AIR 1970 SC 564 · AIR 1967 SC 1643
Cited in: Dhyani's narrative of liberty jurisprudence [the casebook's text gives the years 1950, 1978 and 1979 for these lines of authority; the correct reports are 1950, 1970 and 1967 respectively].
Facts & issue

Gopalan — preventive detention under the Preventive Detention Act; whether Article 21 requires "due process" or only "procedure established by law" (majority: the latter — no American due process). R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation case) — whether Article 31(2) compensation and the "direct impact" of expropriation on individual rights could be tested; the Court abandoned the Gopalan compartmentalisation of rights. Golak Nath — whether fundamental rights can be amended by Parliament (bare majority held: no; Art 368 cannot destroy Art 13 rights).

Arguments

State: express constitutional text confines review; structural arguments. Petitioners: substantive limits (due process; basic rights) are implicit in the constitutional scheme — the Indian analogue of the ancient limit of dharma over power.

Decision and principle

Gopalan (restraint) → Cooper (cumulative effect; rights can be tested) → Golak Nath (amendment limit) → and, after the Emergency, Kesavananda Bharati's basic-structure doctrine and Maneka Gandhi's reading of Article 21 with Articles 14 & 19 (just, fair, reasonable procedure). Principle: constitutional (and judicial) review of power is the modern continuation of the ancient maxim — law is the king of kings; nothing is superior to law; Dhyani notes that after decades of hesitation the Apex Court now guards "the supremacy of law — 'Be you ever so high, the law is above you'".

CASE 12Bangalore Medical Trust v. B.S. MuddappaAIR 1991 SC 1902 · Supreme Court
Cited in: Dhyani — "conservation of humane environment" among the new legal values.
Facts

A civic amenity/parks site in a residential layout of Bangalore was sought to be converted for a private nursing home by a public trust; residents challenged the change of user.

Issues

Can public recreational space (a lung of the city) be diverted to private commercial use? What weight do environmental and public-health interests carry?

Arguments

Trust/developer: the change was sanctioned by the authority and would serve medical needs. Residents: the sanction was illegal and contrary to the approved layout; the park was a pre-existing amenity of the residents whose right to it the authority could not defeat.

Decision and reasoning

The Supreme Court held the conversion invalid: the reservation of the site as a park/civic amenity is for the public; its diversion to a private nursing home was impermissible and arbitrary; the Court stressed that open spaces, parks and environmental quality are essential for the health and well-being of urban communities, and public authorities act as trustees of such amenities.

Principle evolved: Environmental/public-health interests of the community prevail over private commercial interest; public authorities hold land and amenities in trust for the people — the trusteeship idea of Manu and the Īśopaniṣad (teṣu tyaktena bhuñjīthāḥ) in modern constitutional dress.

Official PYQ · Dec 2015 · Q5 · 20 marks"A custom to be legally recognisable and enforceable must fulfil several requisites such as certainty, reasonableness, historical origin, strict construction etc." Comment on the statement by exploring the importance of customs in different personal laws of India.
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

Custom is the oldest source of law — "the mirror in which a people may recognise itself" (Puchta). In India custom occupies a double position: as a formal source of Hindu/Muslim personal law (ancient doctrine) and as a judicially proven fact (colonial doctrine). The quoted statement captures the colonial-courts' test of a valid custom; the deeper question is whether that test does justice to the Indian theory of custom, which — as the DU case material shows — exalted usage above the written text (Collector of Madura: "clear proof of usage will outweigh the written text of the law").

I. The requisites (English/colonial test)

For a custom to be recognised by Indian courts it must be: (1) ancient and certain — not vague; (2) reasonable — not contrary to justice, morality or public policy; (3) continuous and peaceably enjoyed — not by force; (4) obligatory — observed as of right, not mere practice (opinio necessitatis); (5) not contrary to statute; and (6) strictly construed — customs derogating from the general law are construed narrowly.

II. Custom in Hindu (Dharmashastric) law — the two views

M.P. Jain's essay (DU material) shows a deliberate debate: Gautama, Manu, Yājñavalkya, Āparārka, and the Mitākṣarā–Dāyabhāga–Mayūkha placed custom below śruti/smṛti (a custom repugnant to a clear Vedic text is rejected). But Viśvarūpa, Medhātithi, Vṛhaspati, Nārada, Asāhāya and Kautilya gave custom supremacy — the Arthashastra: usages and customs are of equal authority as evidence of law and prevail as actually observed. Even the Manusmṛti: "Achara is transcendental law." The reconciliation: custom was the material source — the dharmasūtras brought śruti texts into conformity with custom; the Smṛtis drew on customs of the people (hence many Smṛtis, hence regional schools: Rutcheputty; Muthukaruppa); commentaries "do not enact, they explain" (Balwant Rao) and owe their force to popular sentiment (Jogdamba). Collector of Madura and Bhyah Ram Singh made usage override text. Kautilya's customs of districts/towns/families/guilds (deśa, jana-pada, kula, gaṇa, śreṇī, saṅgha, naigama, varga) were enforced by the king — the very units of the people's courts.

III. Custom in Muslim law

The Qur'an contains little law; the Prophet expressly sanctioned pre-Islamic Arab usages; the ḥadīth "contained, to a large extent, the customary law of pre-Islamic Arabia." After settlement, custom was relegated below the Qur'an, ḥadīth and ijmā but above qiyās. Validity conditions: general prevalence (not merely local); established course of conduct (not a few occasions); territorial and temporal limits (Abdur Rahim; Tyabji). A custom opposed to the primary sources is illegal.

IV. Custom in modern Indian law — the colonial legacy

The 1772 Warren Hastings adalats applied Hindu/Muslim personal law; the 1781 Act of Settlement (and later s.37 of the Bengal, Agra & Assam Civil Courts Act, 1887) added "justice, equity and good conscience". Since 1860, the great codes (IPC, Contract Act, CrPC) and the Constitution abolished or displaced much custom — yet the Constitution protects custom (Art 13 save personal law; Art 371A Nagaland; Schedules 5 & 6) and modern legislation partially codifies it (Hindu Marriage Act 1955 s.29(2) preserves custom; the 1956 Succession Act; the 1943–45 Madhya Pradesh laws). The Supreme Court still enforces proved customs and panchayat settlements (Vytla Sitanna), and Gram Nyayalayas/Lok Adalats administer village justice on customary lines.

Conclusion

The requisites of certainty, reasonableness and strict construction are judicial tools for the identification of custom in a heterogeneous society — but the Indian jurisprudential tradition went further: it made custom the living test of the law itself, with the state even recording customs under the royal seal (Kātyāyana) and annulling those contrary to equity. A balanced answer therefore treats the English requisites as "gatekeepers" and the Dharmashastric doctrine as the "soul": custom is law because the people accept it as dharma — and dharma, as the texts say, protects when protected and destroys when violated.

UNIT-1(a) · Q-M1 · 20 marks"Environmental consciousness in the Vedic Age was not mere religious sentiment but a jurisprudential position." Discuss with reference to Ṛta, the sources of Vedic literature and the relevance of the Vedic world-view for modern environmental law.
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

The question asks whether the Vedic relation to nature amounts to law or only to piety. The DU material's answer: the Vedic age conceived a single normative order — Ṛta — which is simultaneously a physical, moral and ethical law, so that ecological restraint is a duty (dharma), not a preference. Parasain's formula: "Dharma is the ecology of well-being which emerges organically from devotional relationships with all elements and creatures… the mutual relationship between nature and humans is not one of domination, but of friendship and harmony. Even people who do not believe in divine order should adhere to dharma for the sake of shared well-being."

I. The textual sources

The four Saṃhitās (Ṛg, Yajur, Sāma, Atharva) with the Brāhmaṇas/Upaniṣads form Śruti; the six Vedāṅgas (with Kalpa-sūtras — Śrauta, Gṛhya, Dharma) form the interpretive apparatus; then the Dharma-sūtras (Āpastamba, Gautama, Baudhāyana, Vāsiṣṭha) and Smṛtis. Dhyani stresses that the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda describe this world as a place to live in, not to escape — the "other-worldly India" image is a colonial canard: "the ideals of Atharva Vedic sages have been positive and looking forward to live happily for a hundred years… in some hymns a happy, healthy and full life of a hundred years is described as immortality (Amaratva)."

II. Ṛta and dharma — the jurisprudential core

Parasain: law as Ṛta is "inherent in the universe" and "revealed to mankind as Śruti" — unlike the Western view of law as human construct. Dhyani: "the nature of law in the Vedic age was of a theological character which was described as rita for universal order and harmony. This corresponds to the natural law of the Western world." The ideal of the Vedic order: a socio-legal order free from conflict, exploitation and misery — Ṛgveda: "may our intellect and wealth be for the good of humanity and universal peace."

III. The values — a catalogue

(i) Ahiṃsā — non-injury in act, thought and speech, extending to all life-forms (the "unity of sādhana" across schools — Dasgupta); (ii) Trusteeship — Manu: "take whatever is essential for bodily sustenance; to take more is an act of theft and deserves to be punished"; the Īśopaniṣad: "enjoy by way of sacrifice, without greed"; (iii) Earth as mother and field of action (karmabhūmi, mātṛbhūmi); (iv) Life-affirming prosperity — "sharadah śatam", "Charaiveti", "revel in wealth respected", "may we live a hundred years without poverty" (Yajurveda), "poverty is a state of sinfulness" (Mahabharata) — wealth (artha) is legitimate because it is the vehicle of dharma; lobha (limitless accumulation) alone is sin; (v) Social unity in diversity — "meet together, speak together…" — called "law or Dharma" for directing the diversity of men's actions; (vi) Contextuality — ritusamayata; even punishment is to be inflicted "after examining the circumstances, place and time as well as the worth and offence of the culprit" (Manu 8.126); (vii) Universal welfare — "sarvetra sukhinah santu."

IV. Institutionalisation

Dhyani: the state was never a "necessary evil" but the instrument of dharma — Manu and Kautilya even prescribed heavy taxation to support the impoverished masses; Kautilya feared great corporations and recommended state control (Arthashastra IV.2) — the "traces of state socialism" and the welfare state in ancient India. Dhavan's Arthashastra Administrative Code shows the state regulating shipping, textiles and commerce, protecting women workers (wage-delay and harassment punishable) — ancient "consumer/labour" law, and the archetype of environment-and-economy regulation.

V. Modern relevance

Three contemporary bridges: (1) Constitutional — Art 48A, Art 51A(g) and the public-trust doctrine continue the trusteeship idea; (2) Judicial — the Supreme Court has held public parks and open spaces to be held in trust for the community (Bāngalore Medical Trust v. B.S. Muddappa, AIR 1991 SC 1902) and recognised "conservation of humane environment" as a basic feature of the emerging legal theory (Dhyani's catalogue, with D. Vyas v. Ghaziabad Development Authority, AIR 1993 All 57); (3) Jurisprudential — Parasain's critique of the "pursuit of happiness" model: Indian jurisprudence is built on the realisation that happiness is ephemeral (ānanda as sat-cit-ānanda), so that well-being is collective before it is individual — the exact logic of sustainable development.

Conclusion

The Vedic world-view was a law of the cosmos before it was a law of the state: Ṛta made restraint normative, dharma made it obligatory, and the state enforced it as part of Rājadharma. Modern environmental law is the heir of that insight — which is why the DU material treats environmental consciousness as continuous from the Vedas to the Supreme Court, and why a "development vs environment" binary is foreign to the dharmic frame.

UNIT-1(b) · Q-M2 · 20 marks"Dharmashastras were the inter-mixture of law, religion and morality." Critically examine this statement. Did the Smriti writers develop law as a distinct entity?
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

The statement is Dhyani's reading of Manu's own four sources of the sacred law — śruti, smṛti, the customs of virtuous men and self-approval (conscience) — which shows "that Dharmashastras were the inter-mixtures of law, religion and morality." The critical question is whether that inter-mixture exhausted the tradition, or whether the Smṛtis also carried a separate, positive legal core. The answer is both — and the tension between the two is the very engine of the unit.

I. The case for inter-mixture

(1) Content: the three Kāṇḍas — Āchāra (rites and general duties), Vyavahāra (civil law: protection of life and property) and Prāyaścitta (atonement) — pass under one term, Dharmashastra; (2) Sanction: breach is sin as much as crime — perjury, for example, was "a heinous sin as well as a serious crime" (Manu VIII.80-87; Nārada I.220-228; Bṛhaspati V.34); (3) Verse-form and lawgiver-genre: the texts are śāstra (teaching), not statute — "Dharmashastra means the teaching (or science) of righteousness and includes 'law' and much more" (Derrett via Parasain); (4) Morality ranked higher: "moral and religious obligations were regarded by the exponents of Dharmashastra as of greater importance than mere legal obligations" (Dhyani); (5) Interpreted by the moral canon too: conscience is the fourth source.

II. The case for a distinct legal core — "codification"

(1) Yājñavalkya's code: "a perusal of Yajnavalkya Smriti indicates the existence of a highly developed legal theory with a system of rules of law (Vyavahara) in its strict sense in both substantive and procedural forms"; (2) 18 titles of law (vyavahārapadas) — debts, deposits, sale without ownership, partnership, gifts, wages, guild conventions, husband-wife, partition, sale/purchase, gambling, master-servant, boundaries, defamation, assault, theft, violence, adultery — 14 "arising out of wealth" and 4 "arising out of violence", 13 civil and 5 criminal: a subject-matter index worthy of any code; (3) Rājadharma and Vyavahāradharma are, in Rama Jois's words, the commencement of India's legal and constitutional history — public law and private law, with separation of powers (the king enforced, did not legislate) and rule of law (dharma above the king); (4) Procedural law — plaint, reply, trial, decree (pūrva-pakṣa, uttara, kriyā, nirṇaya); evidence rules; res judicata; appeals; (5) Law "distinguishable from other non-legal values" — Dhyani: "in the Smritis one can also see the emergence of the concept of law as a distinct and separate entity from ethics and morality. Some of the Smriti writers provide a coherent and logical treatment to law in a positive way distinguishable from other non-legal values."

III. The critical resolution

The distinction is not between law mixed with morality and law separate from it, but between mandatory and recommendatory statements — the very distinction the Supreme Court drew in Abhiraj Kuer v. Debendra Singh (AIR 1962 SC 351): interpreting "books of morality, religion, law", great caution is required, for the commentaries "are many-a-times mingled with statements of law but actually are intended to be recommendatory statements regarding the mandatory laws." Where a statement is mandatory, it is law in the fullest sense and is enforced by danda; where recommendatory, it remains moral exhortation. The hierarchy of sources (śruti → smṛti → sadāchāra → conscience) with its conflict rules (śruti prevails; between smṛtis, reason/equity prevails — Nārada's "Dharmashastra Virodhe Tu Yuktyukta Vidhe Smrita", applied by the Bombay Full Bench in Dnyaneshwar B. Ganwat) shows an ordered legal system embedded in a moral order. And the tradition itself dissolved the fixity of codification: the texts are static, but Dharma is dynamic — "the Mahabharata repeatedly claims that tradition is not Dharma if it fails to accommodate emerging situations" (Parasain).

Conclusion

The Dharmashastras are therefore best described as an inter-mixture with a legal core — codification of dharma, not of sovereign command. Authority lay not in promulgation but in "the respect due to their authors and still more to the fact of their being in accordance with prevailing popular sentiment and practice" (Jogdamba) — which is why the Privy Council could say the Digest subordinates the language of texts to custom and approved usage (Bhyah Ram Singh). For the modern lawyer, the lesson is Parasain's: don't revive the texts as Bare Acts; recover the spirit of Dharma for the legal order of our times.

UNIT-1(b) · Q-M3 · 20 marksExplain the hierarchy of courts in ancient India and their jurisdiction. How far is the modern Indian judicial system a continuation of that hierarchy? (Or: "We are following an ancient tradition without being conscious of it" — Dhavan. Discuss.)
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

Justice S.S. Dhavan opens his survey with the claim that India has the oldest judiciary in the world — "no other judicial system has a more ancient or exalted pedigree." Two textual hierarchies are preserved in the DU material: Bṛhaspati Smṛti's four-tier ladder (family arbitrator → judge → Chief Justice (Prādivivāka/adhyakṣa) → King's court) and the Arthashastric five-tier system (Kula → Śreṇī → Gaṇa → Adhikṛta → Nṛpa/Śāsitā). Both share one principle: jurisdiction by importance of dispute, and decision superseding the court below (Vācaspati Miśra: each following decision prevails "because of the higher degree of learning and knowledge").

I. The five-tier system (Rama Jois, in the DU material)

Kula — assembly of impartial persons of the family/caste, functioning as panchayatdars; Śreṇī — the corporation of persons of the same craft, profession or trade; Gaṇa — assembly of persons of one place but different castes/avocations. These three are the "People's Courts": original jurisdiction over all civil and criminal matters except offences involving violence. Their appellate structure is inter-se — Śreṇī reviews Kula, Gaṇa reviews Śreṇī — and a direct appeal lies to the King from any of them (the root of Article 136). Above them: the Adhikṛta (court appointed by the king, competent even over violent offences) and the Nṛpa/Śāsitā (the king's court — the only court with power to inflict fines and corporal punishment; the highest appellate court). Courts were pratiṣṭhita (stationary), apratiṣṭhita (mobile) or mudrita (royal-seal courts). The court hall was the Dharmādhikaraṇa.

II. Yājñavalkya's people's courts and the Dhavan ladder

Yājñavalkya's tribunal of Kula, Śreṇī and Puga (family, artisans, traders) tried civil causes; appeals went to judges appointed by the King (Seema Singh & Yadav). Dhavan's four-tier ladder is the same logic in family-centred form: the joint family (up to four generations) needed a first forum of family arbitrators — "the judicial system had its roots in the social system, which explains its success."

III. The modern mirror

Dhavan: "the Indian judiciary today also consists of a hierarchy of courts organised on a similar principle — the village courts, the Munsif, the Civil Judge, the District Judge, the High Court, and finally the Supreme Court which takes the place of the King's Court. We are following an ancient tradition without being conscious of it." The parallels are exact: village/Gram Nyayalaya ↔ Kula-Gaṇa (people's courts); Munsif–Civil Judge–District Judge ↔ Adhikṛta tier; High Court and Supreme Court ↔ the King's court as court of appeal and record; Article 136 ↔ the direct appeal to the King. The 77th Report of the Law Commission (also in the DU material) confirms the continuity of the process and the persistence of panchayats because of "their useful role of settling minor disputes in rural areas through compromise."

IV. Two differences worth marking (the critical part)

(1) Single judge vs bench: ancient justice was always by benches — "persons entrusted with judicial duties… should function in groups of three, five, or seven" (Śukra-nītisāra); Kautilya: suits by three judges (dharmasthāstrayaḥ). "Our present judicial system, created by the British, does not follow this excellent safeguard. Today every suit is heard by a single Munsif or Civil Judge or District Judge for reasons of economy. But the state in ancient India was more interested in the quality of justice than economy" (Dhavan); (2) Lay vs learned juries: ancient assessors (sabhāsada) were learned in law — "the modern jury of today consists of laymen — 'twelve shopkeepers'" (Dhavan); (3) Separation of the judicial from the executive: the king, when judging, wore simple dress (vineeta-vesha) to distinguish judicial from executive functions; in the modern district system, executive and judicial magistracies were separated only in 1973.

Conclusion

The hierarchy is ancient; the guarantees of that hierarchy (independence, integrity, publicity, appeal) were also ancient — Dhavan's verdict, quoting Spellman: "In some respects the judicial system of ancient India was theoretically in advance of our own today." The modern system should be understood as the continuation, in British constitutional dress, of a Bharatiya institutional idea — an excellent conclusion for any answer on the Indian legal system.

UNIT-1(c) · Q-M4 · 20 marks"Rule of Dharma was the Grundnorm of the ancient Indian legal order — the King was never above law." Discuss. How was this guarantee institutionalised?
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

Seema Singh & Yadav state the thesis bluntly: "Rule of Dharma was the Grundnorm, and justiciable behaviour was the duty of every citizen." Dhyani refines it: the ancient lawgivers "enunciated the doctrine of partial or limited sovereignty assigned to the king to administer the country without legislative powers or rule-making power whatsoever"; Hindu thinkers "always held law or Dharma superior to the State or Government — the State was by no means a source of law as propounded by European pundits like Hobbes and Hegel."

I. The texts of supremacy

Mahabharata: "A King who after having sworn that he shall protect his subjects fails to protect them should be executed like a mad dog"; "the people should execute a king who does not protect them… such a king is not a king but misfortune" — sovereignty rests on an implied social compact; breach forfeits kingship. Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad: law is the king of kings — nothing is superior to law; with the aid of law even a weakling overcomes the strong. Kautilya: "In the happiness of his subjects lies the King's happiness… whatever pleases his people he shall consider as good." Rāma banished Sītā because his subjects disapproved; the fishermen's condition to the King of Hastināpura was accepted; Manu: "Dharma when violated verily destroys; Dharma when preserved preserves." The Mahabharata sanctions rebellion against a worthless king; and duties were codified — Rājadharma (the king), āśrama-dharma, varṇa-dharma, strī-dharma, dāmpatya-dharma, āpad-dharma (Manu).

II. How it was institutionalised (four guarantees)

(1) No legislative power: the king enforced law; interpretation belonged to the learned (the parishad, the rishis) — "the interpretation of the law of Dharma did not rest with the King but with the rishis, and the former was enjoined to abide by the expert advice of his ministers in administration and from judges in adjudication and from the purohita in penances. On doubtful points the King was to be guided by the opinion of a parishad of learned men." Only where śruti, smṛti and custom were silent could the king decide according to his conscience.

(2) Judicial conduct codes: Kātyāyana — the king enters the court "modestly dressed (vineeta-vesha)… seated facing east… under the guidance of his Chief Justice, judges, ministers and the Brahmana members of his council"; a king who dispenses justice thus "resides in heaven." Nārada — the king on the dharmāsanam must take the oath of Vivasvan's son (Yama, the god of death, impartial to all beings); the seven virtues meet in him "like seven flames in the fire."

(3) Independent judges and the duty to warn: Kātyāyana — "if the king wants to inflict an illegal or unrighteous decision, it is the duty of the judge to warn the king and prevent him… this is no occasion for soft speech (vaktavyam tat priyam natra); if the judge fails in his duty, he is guilty." Judicial independence existed in the full modern sense: judges were "fearless, independent and impartial towards friends and foes" and "well versed in laws, rules of prudence" (Sudame).

(4) Assessors (sabhāsada) whose verdict binds: learned assessors; Kātyāyana — "if they keep silent [when the Sovereign inclines to violate the law] they will go to hell accompanied by the King"; the king passes the jaya-patra according to their verdict; only in sandigdha (difficult/doubtful) cases could the Sovereign decide by his own privilege. Sovereign immunity from judicial control did not exist: "the Sovereign was not to interfere with the judiciary, but on the contrary the latter was under a duty to interfere in case of a wrong (judicial) decision by the king."

III. From ancient to modern

The same idea is the modern rule of law: Dicey's exclusion of arbitrariness and the paramountcy of law; constitutional supremacy (Art 13, Art 14/21); judicial review of executive and legislative action; the basic-structure doctrine. Dhyani's conclusion: "the government of the ancient Hindus was a government of laws and not of men"; Kautilya and Vācaspati's "dharma is superior to the king" is the ancestor of our constitutionalism; and the modern message, in the Apex Court's words (Dhyani's citation of the post-Emergency jurisprudence): "Be you ever so high, the law is above you."

Conclusion

The ancient Indian guarantee of "law above king" was deeper than the West's seventeenth-century achievement: it was unwritten, religious, customary and political at once — the king as trustee, the rishis as guardians, the people as the ultimate sanction. That is why the DU material calls Dharma the Grundnorm: it is the validity-conferring ultimate rule of the whole order, anticipating by millennia the modern concept of constitutional supremacy.

UNIT-1(c) · Q-M5 · 20 marksExamine the customary dispute resolution mechanisms of Bharat — their philosophical foundations, the colonial disruption, and their contemporary revival.
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

Bharat's customary dispute resolution tradition spans "nearly three millennia, encompassing nearly fifteen centuries before and after the dawn of the Common Era" (Seema Singh & Yadav), and its philosophy in one line is: amicable settlement first, punishment last; dharma as Grundnorm; restorative justice, not retribution.

I. Foundations

Vedic: Nyāya (justice) was central to ṛta and dharma; the Ṛgveda's Sabha and Samiti — assemblies of elders, scholars and eminent citizens — were "democratic institutions for collective decision-making and conflict resolution", elaborated by the Atharvaveda as platforms for legislative and judicial functions; parties were Praśnin (plaintiff) and Abhipraśnin (defendant); the Madhamasi was a mediator-arbitrator; jurisdiction was civil only. Epic: Rāmāyaṇa's Vibhīṣaṇa, Aṅgada, Hanumān; Mahābhārata's Krishna, Bhīṣma, Yudhiṣṭhira — mediators bringing parties "to the dialogue table" for mutually acceptable settlements; Rāma's own conduct exemplifies justice under dharma. Dharmashastric: Nārada Smṛti's elaborate procedure; Yājñavalkya's Kula–Śreṇī–Puga tribunals (family, artisans, traders) with appeals to king-appointed judges; Parāśara's parishad deciding by justice, equity and good conscience; Patañjali — "progress comes swiftly in mediation for those who try hardest, instead of deciding who was right and who was wrong." Kautilya (4th c. BCE): formalised and institutionalised community-based arbitration "as the central conflict resolution mode", legitimating grassroots justice, with procedural norms of equality, justice and community participation. Satyāgraha's roots lie here — truth (satya) as the ultimate arbiter. Evidence was already refined: sākṣī (witnesses), lekha (documents), kāryapramāṇa (circumstantial evidence) — with acknowledgement of the limits of human perception. Panchayat (assembly of five) was the enduring institution — adjudicatory and mediatory, reconciliation before adjudication; sanctions: monetary penalties, social ostracism, banishment.

II. Colonial disruption

The Company set up courts "entirely staffed by officials" — public participation in justice administration ended. The 1860 codes (IPC, CrPC, Contract Act) with Baden-Powell's land-revenue framework "replaced customary law"; Collectorates displaced caste/village panchayats. Ravinder Kumar's study of Maharashtra shows the Kunbis (agriculturists) losing their familiar panchayat protection while the Vanis (money-lenders' agents) gained under the doctrine of freedom of contract — the Kunbis' remedy came only through the Deccan Agricultural Debt Relief Act, 1879. Codified procedures were "unfamiliar to the ordinary people and not in conformity with the codes and rules of local traditions"; the focus "shifted from community feeling to individual rights." Gandhi advocated boycotting courts and restoring village panchayats — "In Panchayat Raj only the Panchayat will be obeyed, and the Panchayat can only work through the law of their making" (India of My Dreams) — and warned against the exploitation of poor litigants by lawyers and long journeys to town. Despite this, "customary forms of adjudication persisted in numerous regions, demonstrating their enduring tenacity and profound cultural foundations."

III. Regional plurality (one or two examples to cite)

Gond (Central/Eastern India, 15m+): village councils using Jatra (storytelling/ritual), Ghotul (youth dormitory — harmonious living, gender-inclusiveness), Khapri (mixed-gender consensus) plus oaths, ordeals and social sanctions. Santhal: Manji (village; Manjhi Hadam) → Desh Manjhi (5–8 villages) → Pargana (15–20 villages; Parganait) → Dighri Parishad (highest council) — handles civil and criminal matters, prioritising reconciliation over punishment. Khasi-Jaintia: Dorbar Shnong (Rangbah Shnong) → Dorbar Raid (Sirdar) → Dorbar Hima (Syiem). Garo: A'king Nokma with Sardars; Bobildinga/Gro-dinga public discussions; Melaa/Bichal councils. Bhil: Nyaya Panchayat under Patel/Gameti; Chapai gatherings with rituals. Naga: village courts, protected by Art 371A.

IV. Contemporary revival

Constitutional: Art 40 (village panchayats as self-governing bodies); 73rd Amendment (Arts 243–243O; 11th Schedule — social justice and grassroots dispute resolution); Fifth and Sixth Schedules (Art 244(1),(2)) preserving tribal governance; Art 371A shielding Naga customary law. Statutory: Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (Lok Adalats — "settlement encouraged and guided by respected community members, judges and lawyers functioning in roles similar to traditional Elders Panchayat"); Mediation Act, 2023 (community members as mediators; enforceable mediated agreements; accommodates local customs); Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2025 (consent-based resolution; institutional arbitration); Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008 (village-level courts). Judicial: Vytla Sitanna v. Marivada Viranna — the panchayat award upheld as a bona fide settlement binding on the estate; "the judicial process itself values indigenous solutions for indigenous problems."

Conclusion

The tradition is not a museum piece: "the essence of samvada (dialogue) and collaborative approach towards the settlement persisted… these traditional practices provide practical, accessible, and cost-effective justice at the grassroots level and also embrace the principles of restorative justice that aligns with international standards." The ADRs we now celebrate are the modern names of ancient institutions — the case material's concluding claim, and a strong closing line for this answer.

UNIT-1 · Q-M6 · 20 marksIs there an Indian philosophy of law? Discuss its metaphysical foundations and the major distinctions between the Eastern and the Western perspectives.
MODEL ANSWER
Introduction

Parasain begins with the misconception foisted on India — that there is no analytical tradition outside the West, that Indian thought is "an odd assortment of spirituality, mysticism and imprecise thinking" (Matilal). Following A.K. Ramanujan's affirmative answer to "Is there an Indian way of thinking?", and K.C. Bhattacharyya's project of swaraj in ideas (freedom from "cultural subjection"), the answer is: yes — an Indian philosophy of law exists, grounded in dharma and revealed in the Ṛta-Śruti conception of law.

I. Two tests

A philosophy of law needs (a) pramāṇaśāstra — a theory of proof/axiomatic principles to resolve conflicting claims (Nyāya, Buddhist and Jain logic; Mīmāṃsā's rules; even Caraka's yukti) and (b) a metaphysical foundation — validity anchored in something beyond human decree. The West travelled Aquinas → Kant (practical reason; sapere aude — "have courage to use your own understanding") → Bentham (greatest happiness); the Indian tradition's metaphysics lies in the Upaniṣads, the Epics and the Bhagavad-Gītā (pravṛtti and nivṛtti dharma: "twofold dharma is the cause of stability of the world order" — Gītā XVIII.78), with Karma (moral causation; answers, without a capricious God, the problems of arbitrariness, free will and inequality — Śaṅkara's rain-and-seeds analogy), the nature of the self (Advaita's Ātman-Brahman identity), consciousness, ahiṃsā and the puruṣārthas (Dharma–Artha–Kāma–Mokṣa) as its pillars.

II. Four distinctions (East vs West)

(1) Foundation: Western theories are individualistic and contractarian (Hobbes → Rawls: self-interest central; law as rational strategy); dharmic theories are communitarian and idealistic, centred on collective well-being, law inseparable from morality and culture-specific ethics. (2) Sovereignty: Austinian law emanates from the sovereign — "honour the State, the root of law and wealth"; in the Dharmashastras the king is subject to law — "honour the law (Dharma), the root of state and wealth" (Chatrapati Singh); Dhyani adds that no ancient ruler could say with Alauddin Khilji or Louis XIV "I am the State"; the king enjoyed limited sovereignty — "the highest medium of enforcement of temporal law." (3) Rights vs duties: the West is right-based; India is duty-based (kartavya) and obligation-based (ṛṇa) — with the individual's wellbeing derived from the collective wellbeing (Kāma → Artha → Dharma → Mokṣa). (4) Source: Western law is a human construct; dharmic law is Ṛta — cosmic order inherent in the universe, revealed as Śruti.

III. The critical caution (for full marks)

Two opposite errors must be avoided: uncritical contempt (colonial Indology; Mayne's "apparatus of cruel absurdities"; Gledhill's "dearth of legal principles" — Dhavan: "the effect of this misrepresentation… was to create a false picture… both in India and outside") and uncritical revivalism (the texts are not modern Bare Acts; the goal is "not reviving the Dharmashastras… but to enquire how the spirit of the Dharma can be useful for the legal order of our times"). Nor should one fabricate a single Indian metaphysics or deny the diversity (Nyāya's logical realism, Vaiśeṣika's atomism, Sāṃkhya's dualism, Mīmāṃsā's pluralism, Vedānta's monism — all within the āstika fold). Modern India is, as K.M. Panikkar says, a synthesis: "the inheritance that India has stepped into is only partly Hindu and Indian… an effective, even if imperfect, synthesis between East and West."

Conclusion

Tagore's "surplus in man" — the human capacity to question and transcend one's own values — and his image of cultures as mountain peaks "having different altitudes, temperature, flora and fauna, and yet belonging to the same chain of hills" supply the method: dialogue (anyamataparīkṣā), not domination. The integration of Indian Knowledge Systems with liberal democratic tradition is the project of this course — and the reason Unit 1 exists.

UNIT-1 · Q-M7 · 20 marksWrite short notes on any TWO of the following: (a) Parishad (b) Vyavahārapada (c) Kantaśodhana Courts (d) Sabhāsada (e) Prāyaścitta (f) Matsya-nyāya.
MODEL ANSWERS (six notes — write any two)
(a) Parishad

An assembly of learned persons with the power to supply the law where the sources are silent and, in exceptional cases, to resolve complicated questions of law. Composition (at least ten members): one learned in one of the three Vedas; a logician; a Mīmāṃsaka; one who knows Nirukta (etymology); one who recites the sacred law; and three men of the first three āśramas — brahmacārin, gṛhastha, vānaprastha. Why it matters: law-making was not the king's prerogative — the power to lay down new legal provisions where śruti/smṛti/custom were silent belonged to the parishad; this separation of powers between parishad and king "ensured the existence of rule of law". Parāśara would have certain issues decided by a parishad "on the principles of justice, equity and good conscience" — the ancestor of the modern domestic forum and of the conscience-based equity jurisdiction.

(b) Vyavahārapada — the 18 titles of law

The subject-matter index of the Dharmashastric codes: (1) ṛṇadāna (debts), (2) nikṣepa (deposits), (3) asvāmivikraya (sale without ownership), (4) sambhūyasamutthāna (partnership), (5) dattasyānapakarma (resumption of gifts), (6) vetanadāna (wages), (7) samvidvyatikrama (violation of guild conventions), (8) strīpuṃdharma (husband–wife), (9) vibhāga (partition), (10) krayavikrayānusaya (sale/purchase), (11) dyūtasamāhwaya (gambling), (12) svāmipālavivāda (master–servant), (13) sīmāvivāda (boundaries), (14) vākpāruṣya (defamation), (15) daṇḍapāruṣya (assault), (16) steya (theft), (17) sāhasa (violence), (18) strīsaṃgrahaṇa (adultery). First fourteen: artha-samutthāni (from wealth); last four: hiṃsodbhāvani (from violence); 13 civil, 5 criminal; leftovers = prakīrṇaka. A Chief Justice had to be "well versed in all eighteen titles of law" — the code was the test of judicial learning.

(c) Kantaśodhana Courts

Special courts of criminal jurisdiction under the Arthashastra: "Three commissioners (pradeṣṭāraḥ) or three ministers shall deal with measures to suppress disturbance to peace (kantaśodhanam kuryuḥ)." They tried offences against the State and violations of law by officials in discharge of duty: false weights, adulterated goods, excessive prices, unfair withholding of wages, idle work, official misconduct, theft, dacoity, sex offences. "These Courts had all the characteristics of administrative courts"; the 4th part of the Arthashastra is a complete Administrative Code (state shipping — navādhyakṣa; textiles — sūtrādhyakṣa with women workers' protective rules; mining — ākarādhyakṣa; customs — śulkādhyakṣa; counterfeit seal = eight times the duty). They are the earliest Indian example of regulatory/administrative adjudication — the ancestor of tribunals.

(d) Sabhāsada

The assessors/jurors of the king's court — "the most noteworthy feature of the judicial system… the equivalent of the modern jury, with one important difference: the jury of today consists of laymen — 'twelve shopkeepers' — whereas the councillors who sat with the Sovereign were to be learned in law" (Yājñavalkya: well versed in the literature of the law, truthful, completely impartial between friend and foe). Their duty was to disagree with the Sovereign if he erred — Kātyāyana: "if they keep silent they will go to hell accompanied by the King." The king's decree (jaya-patra) followed their verdict (compare the Privy Council's binding "advice"; compare Soviet people's assessors). Exception: in sandigdha (doubtful) cases the Sovereign could decide by his own privilege. Exam point: the sabhāsada show that ancient practice combined expert judging with lay participation — and that the bench was never to be overawed.

(e) Prāyaścitta

The third Kāṇḍa of the Dharmashastra — "regulations relating to the atonement for various sins committed." As ADR: "ancient Bharat legal thought also recognised various forms of alternative dispute resolution, including the practice of prayaschitta (atonement) for certain offences, which focused on rehabilitation rather than retribution" — fasting, chanting mantras, donations, rituals. It is the clearest ancient statement of the restorative model: the wrongdoer's reintegration into the community instead of exclusion; and it mirrors the social sanction of expulsion/ostracism used by the panchayats. Exam point: prāyaścitta is the ancestor of probation, compensation and restorative-justice mechanisms.

(f) Matsya-nyāya

The "law of the fish" — the big fish swallowing the small — the anarchic state of nature that ancient Indian political theory used as the justification of the State ("without a sovereign authority… the stronger will swallow the weaker in the manner of fish" — Dhyani). It appears in the Mahābhārata (social contract: the king appointed with the consent of the people; dandaneeti created to punish violations of dharma) and in Kautilya. Key points: (i) the State is thus not a necessary evil but necessary for dharma; (ii) the ruler's duty is to prevent matsya-nyāya — "it was believed that economic development ought to be the concern of the State and an equitable division of the available wealth was a duty of the ruler"; (iii) "when the government was good there occurred a Rama Rajya." Exam point: matsya-nyāya is India's own state-of-nature theory — use it to answer "compare the Indian social contract with Hobbes."
