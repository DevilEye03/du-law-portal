1.1 The unit's three recurring theses

  • Thesis A — Law is cosmic before it is political: law's root is not the sovereign's command but the self-imposed regulations of the universe (Ṛta); human law is valid only when aligned with it — "in case of conflict between man-made law and Cosmic/Natural Law, the Cosmic Law must prevail" (Seema Singh).
  • Thesis B — Duty precedes right: the Bharatiya order is Kartavya-based; rights (Adhikār) are natural outcomes of fulfilled duties, "totally opposite to the modern day right-duty corollary" (Seema Singh). The modern rights-first model is a colonial-era philosophical shift, not the Indian baseline.
  • Thesis C — The state is a divine-human necessity under Dharma, not a Leviathan: the state arises because matsya-nyāya would otherwise devour society (Bhīṣma; Valmīki; Kautilya), but the king is under Dharma — his orders are void if they violate it (Rawat; Sharma & Rawat).

02(a) Concept of Ṛta and Kartavya

2.1 The opening image (use it as your introduction in the exam)

"Just imagine what would happen if the sun failed to rise, rivers ceased their flow, or the very cells in our bodies suddenly declared themselves exhausted? The result would be a swift and absolute collapse of our existence… the universe is sustained not by chance, but by the relentless and harmonious fulfilment of its Kartavya. Every celestial body, every natural phenomenon, and every living cell has a natural obligation to perform its Kartavya, to maintain the cosmic order.

Ṛta is a Cosmic Law which governs nature and Kartavya sustains the same. If a breakdown in cosmic law, in nature's duty, brings instant devastation, what then, if in society a majority of individuals abandon their obligations for a relentless pursuit of their own rights?"— Seema Singh, "Ṛta and Kartavya: The Interlinking Jurisprudence" (opening)

The author's central question: "Is Kartavya the true epicentre of both universal existence and human civilization?" A rights-only framework is like "a body where each cell demands its own autonomy without fulfilling its function" — it "undermines the very interdependence that is the foundation of both natural and social order." Hence: shift from a rights-centric to a Kartavya-rooted perspective.

2.2 Kartavya vs Duty — the philosophical distinction

Duty (modern sense)

  • External — "a burden imposed from the outside", like statutory duties.
  • Created by human authority (statute, contract, command).
  • Compliance is a legal relation — breach = legal sanction.

Kartavya (Bharatiya sense)

  • Internalised natural obligation, "intrinsically linked to the concept of Dharma, morality and the Cosmic Order."
  • Not imposed from outside; it is one's own nature. Dharma is the positive inherent nature of a thing.
  • Examples: "providing fruits, shelter and shadow are the Dharma and Kartavya both for a tree; quenching thirst and flowing is the Dharma and Kartavya both for her [the river]; similarly Sun, Mother Earth and so on."
  • A "spiritual imperative, a conscious choice to live in alignment with one's true nature and contribute to the cosmic order."

Consequence: "Traditionally, Bharatiya society was structured around the fulfilment of one's Dharma… individuals were guided by an inner moral compass rather than external legal compulsion. The adherence to Kartavya created a self-regulating society where individuals' actions were not just for personal gain, but for the maintenance of a harmonious whole." (citing Justice M. Rama Jois, Legal and Constitutional History of India 575-76.)

DHARMA = inherent nature of a thing
→
KARTAVYA = living that nature as natural obligation

SELF-REGULATING SOCIETY = order without external compulsion

2.3 The shift from Dharma-Kartavya to rights-based governance

Bharat's "prolonged history of invasions and social disruption" created the ground for systematic exploitation, and the modern form of government imposed by the British marked a profound philosophical shift. The British model, "rooted in the European struggles between the king and the Church," was fundamentally rights-centric, establishing the ruler — king, queen or government — as the supreme authority and the 'provider' and 'protector' of "a dependent populace". This system, "driven by the assertion of man-made law over cosmic or customary law", centralised power and rights in the ruler.

Before
Kartavya-based society: internalised Dharma; the king was himself bound; society self-regulating; collective well-being the bedrock.
The colonial break
Codified modern judicial system "supplanted the traditional, duty-based framework"; man-made law displaced cosmic/customary law; citizens became dependents of the state rather than duty-bearers.
After
Rights-centric independent India: "the law focuses on individual claims often overshadowing the collective responsibilities that were once the bedrock of social harmony."

The author's critical question: must we accept the global right-duty jural co-relate model (Hohfeldian), which "while providing a framework for individual liberties, often fosters an adversarial relationship between the citizens and the state, and between individuals themselves"?

The alternative: Kartavya-Adhikar

"In contrast to the western right-duty jural co-relate, we have the time-tested ancient concept of the Kartavya-Adhikar. Here, Adhikar (rights) are seen not as inherent entitlements but as natural outcomes of fulfilled Kartavya (duties). The right to be protected, for instance, is a natural consequence of the ruler's duty to protect. This model fosters a sense of mutual responsibility and shared destiny, where the fulfilment of one's duty ensures the rights of all."

Bharat's unique vantage: "Bharat, as a civilisation, stands unique in its historical journey, having experienced the full spectrum of societal structures — the profound harmony of a Kartavya-based society, and now the complexities of a rights-based society which took root during a period of foreign influence. This dual experience presents a rare opportunity for philosophical and introspective inquiry."

2.4 Cosmic Law, Ṛta and the Purusha Sūkta — the metaphysical foundation

Step 1 — law's root is cosmic: "the inherent, self-imposed regulations that govern the universe itself… so foundational that they are often cited in human legal systems as Natural Law. In essence, they act as the ultimate standard, or Grundnorm, against which all other laws are measured. When human-made laws fail to deliver justice, courts often turn to the principles of Natural Law to interpret the rules." — Note the explicit use of the Kelsenian term "Grundnorm" for Ṛta; this is the bridge to Unit 6.

Step 2 — science and the Vedas converge: the Big Bang (a singularity ~13.8 billion years ago, "God particles", atoms, stars, galaxies) and Darwin (evolution of species, not origin of life) are described; then the parallel: "the Rigveda, one of the most ancient human texts, offers a complex and compelling account of the universe's origin that, in some ways, resonates with contemporary scientific thought."

The Purusha Sūkta (Ṛgveda X.90) — verses & reading

Verse 1: "The Purusha (universe) has a thousand heads, a thousand eyes and a thousand feet. Thousand is a symbol of innumerable… He pervades the world from all sides… and also extends in the ten directions." — omnipresence.

Verse 2: "In reality, Purusha is all this (creation); all that was in the past and all that will be in the future… the entire creation is interwoven with the immortal principle of that great Lord; by becoming His food, man rises above the gross world and becomes immortal." — time-transcendence; the cyclic journey of beings: energy ↔ mass (the reading links it to Einstein and to Krishna's immortal-soul teaching in the Gītā).

Verse 3: "The Purusha is greater than all the greatness that can be expressed in words. One part of Him has become all these (visible) worlds, and three parts of Him are situated in the immortal world of the Supreme Brahman." — the reading parallels this with modern cosmology: ¾ of the universe is dark energy, ¼ baryonic (ordinary) matter.

Verse 4: "The three parts of the Purusha are above and one part repeatedly becomes creation, pervading all living and non-living beings."

The jurisprudential conclusion

"Purusha is a manifestation carrying the infinite Universe, all the solar system and all of us as a part of its body… In Sankhya Darshan the same is known as Purush (Consciousness) and Prakriti (Nature). Being the part of the manifestation of the same Purusha, we are bound to follow the same set of rules as the multiverse and nature follows. This Cosmic law is known as Ṛta."

The Austinian mirror: "As in the modern man-made law, law is the command of the sovereign, being the small part of the infinite multiverse, the Ṛta is the command of the Supreme Consciousness, the Purusha. If we violate man-made law the sovereign punishes us. Similarly, if we violate the Ṛta, Purusha will punish us. In crux, man-made laws must be in alignment with the cosmic laws known as Ṛta. In other words, in case of conflict between man-made law and Cosmic/Natural Law, the Cosmic Law must prevail."

Etymological hint (the reading's own): words bearing the syllable Ri (ऋ) — Ṛta (ऋत), Rishi (ऋषि), Prakriti (प्रकृति), Ṛtu (ऋतु), Trigun (त्रिगुण), Ṛṇa (ऋण), Stree (स्त्री), Purush (पुरुष), Kartavya (कर्तव्य), Adhikar (अधिकार) — "all are and are supposed to be aligned with cosmic law."

Ṛta — its full definition (quote this)

According to the Nyāya Sūtras: "कर्तव्यानिष्ठं सम्यक् पालनं राज्यं वर्धते" — "a nation flourishes when its citizens steadfastly uphold their duties. This enduring principle underscores that rights can only exist within a strong culture of duty."

  • Ṛta = "the fundamental principle that governs and sustains the universe, encompassing all that exists… the principles of divine wisdom that regulate all phenomena, both in the heavens and on Earth. Importantly, the concept of Ṛta also signifies the ultimate source of justice, a guiding principle for human conduct."
  • It ensures "the continuity of natural order… the regularity of phenomena such as rainfall, plant growth, daylight, and the cyclicality of the seasons."
  • "Nature is neither self-centric nor right-centric. It follows the path of Kartavya where it performs its duty towards us irrespective of our visible reluctance to perform our duty towards it."
  • Ṛta is the supreme truth and sustaining force; its antithesis, 'an-Ṛta', signifies destruction. Everything has "an inherent tendency to exist in harmony with this universal order." "Kartavya is aligned with Ṛta, while demanding only Rights — omitting performance of Kartavya is an-Ṛta, which is against the supreme cosmic rule."

2.5 Ṛta–Kartavya–Dharma knitted together: the law of the monarch and of the prajā

For the King (Rājadharma under Ṛta)

  • Dharmashastra "prescribes Kartavya for all"; its central feature for the legal system is "the detailed exposition of the principles governing the Monarch's role", rooted in the belief that "Dharma underpins all world events; in its absence, the strong would subjugate the weak."
  • "Dharma prescribes special duties for human beings to protect those who are weak, who cannot raise their voice; to protect the rights of every inanimate and animate object created by the supreme divine — from mountain to river, from plants to animals, from sky to earth." — the environmental/animal-welfare bridge (Unit 1(a)).
  • The ruler's authority is of divine origin in accordance with the cosmic order, but "this divine origin does not imply that the Ruler is absolute or infallible." His duty: "administration of the kingdom with a primary focus on the welfare of the people" — "Praja-sukham (happiness of citizens under good governance) is measured by the degree to which the governed prospers" (Kautilya, Arthashastra 1.19.34).
  • Protection of the kingdom through the four Upāyas: sama (conciliation), dāna (gifts), daṇḍa (punishment), bheda (division); ministries as institutions.

For the Prajā (Prajādharma)

  • "Kartavya of Praja… represents the ethical bedrock upon which a harmonious society is built. The Dharmashastra asserts that these duties are not mere social conventions but are universal, foundational principles of righteous living that transcend the individual's Varna and Ashrama."
  • "Kartavya of citizens towards their Nation, Society and Family; to be a Dharma-abiding citizen; contributing to the welfare of the community through volunteering and paying taxes."
  • Citizens should be virtuous, ahiṃsak (non-violent) and sadāchārī (truthful); their spiritual progress aligned with cosmic order is also Dharma.
  • Inner disciplines have public effects: "indriyanigrahah (control of the senses)… leads to a clear mind capable of discerning right from wrong, benefiting both the self and the community"; dayā (mercy) and dānam (charity) express the interconnectedness of all beings; collectively a path to moksha.

03(b) Kautilya's Concept of State and Law

3.1 The treatise and its character

The Arthashastra (4th century BCE) is "one of the oldest surviving manuals on governance and public administration in the world"; its name means "science of wealth" (artha). Though usually read as economics/political science, its detailed treatment of legal concepts "makes it an invaluable legal document of ancient India": criminal law, civil disputes, commercial regulations and administrative procedures are treated "with remarkable intricacies." It has 15 books and 150 chapters; Book 3 ("Concerning Law", 20 chapters) and Book 4 (13 chapters) are devoted to the administration of justice; the remaining parts deal with economic development and foreign relations. A.K. Sen called it "the first book on economics."

"Kautilya's philosophies exhibit a pragmatic approach to justice that reconciles moral principles with practical governance needs, emphasising social stability and state security. The treatise also elucidates the relationship between Dharma (moral law) and Rajadharma (royal duty), illustrating how ancient Indian legal thought integrated ethical principles with statecraft."— Radhakrishnan Pillai

Legal philosophy in one line: "Law, according to him, is an instrument of social order that supports the goals of the state: preservation of peace, economic prosperity, and protection of the people. While Vedic literature emphasised Dharma (righteousness), Kautilya emphasised Vyavahara (legal process), linking the two with Niti (practical policy). This tripartite lens reflects a unique Indian jurisprudential framework."

3.2 The four sources of law — Kautilya's hierarchy

RĀJAŚĀSANA King's edict — royal decrees having the force of law; SUPREME in Kautilya's hierarchy
CHARITRA History — social conventions & community norms
VYAVAHĀRA Evidence — based on witnesses
DHARMA Sacred law — based on Vedas and Smritis

"These four pillars of law operate as: Dharma being the governance of eternal truth governs the world; Vyavahara establishes the facts through witness testimony; Charitra draws from the wisdom of the people following historical traditions; and Rajashasana embodies sovereign authority through royal decrees and commands having the force of law."

The conflict rule (essential for the exam): "The King who administers justice according to Dharma, Vyavahara, Charitra and Rajashasana will be able to conquer the entire world by the four quarters (Chaturantāmmahīm). Whenever there is conflict between history (Charitra) and the sacred law, or between evidence (Vyavahara) and the sacred law, the issue must be resolved in accordance with the sacred law (Dharmashastras). But whenever sacred law (Dharma) is in conflict with rational law (Dharmanyāya = king's law), the latter shall be held authoritative, for the reason that we no longer have access to the justification for how a particular shastra was derived from dharma — the original text (on which the sacred law has been based) is not available."

Compare with Dhavan/Sudame (Unit 1): the Dharmashastric order preferred dharma-śāstra over artha-śāstra and made reason prevail between two smṛtis. Kautilya's order is pragmatic: the living king's law prevails where the sacred text's derivation is no longer traceable — but only within the boundary of Dharma.

3.3 Structure of courts and legal officials

KING — SUPREME JUDGE final court of appeal; source, enforcer and protector of law
PRADVIVAKA — CHIEF JUDGE presides over important disputes
KANTAKAŚODHANA — CRIMINAL/ADMINISTRATIVE COURTS economic crimes, offences against the state, official misconduct; "comprehensive regulatory tribunals"
DHARMASHTIYA — CIVIL COURTS marriage, inheritance, trespass, labour, contract — presided over by Amatyas; power to impose nominal fines

Dharmasthiya Courts

Civil courts presided over by Amatyas (high officials). Their main function: "to dispose of the disputes arising out of violation of traditional rules and regulations"; authority to impose nominal fines. They handled marriage, inheritance, trespass, labour, contract and other civil aspects of life.

Kantakaśodhana Courts

"Kantakaśodhana literally means removal of thorns" — criminal courts, particularly for economic crimes and serious offences causing disturbance to peace. They took cognizance "not only of offences against the State but also violations of the law by officials in the discharge of their official duties" — from fraudulent trading and unfair labour conditions to theft, robbery and sexual offences. "They served as comprehensive regulatory tribunals." (See Unit 1 §04.4 for Dhavan's account — three pradeṣṭāraḥ commissioners.)

Also: other officials — Amatyas (ministers) and Antapala (border magistrates) — handled judicial functions. "Kautilya stressed accountability, record-keeping, and swift justice."

3.4 Saptāṅga Rājya — the seven-limbed organic state

"Kautilya described the structure of the state in his Saptang theory or Saptangrajya… Kautilya believed in the organic theory of state: as per him there cannot be a country without people and there is no kingdom without a country. These seven elements combine to form a state." (Sequence in the text: Svāmin — Book 1; Amātya, Janapada, Durga, Kosha — Books 2–5; Daṇḍa and Mitra — Books 6–14.)

1. SVĀMIN
The King — created by the blessings of Indra, Yama, Fire, Sun, Water, Air; "symbol of justice"; must be just, intelligent, decisive; his character decides the state's prosperity
2. AMĀTYA
Ministers — 7 or 8 carefully examined; learned, brave, skilled in weapons; consulted on peace & war, wealth, territory; selected on merit, loyalty, competence (Manu's advice)
3. JANAPADA
People & territory — fertile land, abundant resources, hardworking populace, social harmony; "no kingdom without a country"
4. DURGA
Fort — capital fortified by desert, water, trees, armed men or mountains; safety from external threats, internal stability
5. KOSHA
Treasury — trustworthy men; income & expenditure, revenue, gold mines; funds army, public works, welfare
6. DAṆḌA
Army / coercive power — well-trained, disciplined, loyal; deters aggression, maintains internal order
7. MITRA
Ally — alliances only where mutual interests align; "those who are weak today may be strong tomorrow"; isolation is weakness; support, trade, influence, security

Exam point: This is the ancient answer to "what is a state?" — five of the seven limbs are institutions, one is the people, and one is the king; the state is organic, not contractual-mechanical. Note the striking echo in Rāmarājya's Saptavarga (king, ministers, nation/territory, fort, treasury, army, allies — Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda 100/68) and in the modern list of state essentials (population, territory, government, sovereignty).

3.6 Procedure, evidence and the burden of proof

Evidence first

"Kautilya's Arthashastra has given prime importance to the evidence in judicial proceedings. He says: 'For whether a complaint is lodged first or last, it is the evidence of witness that must be depended upon. In the absence of witness the circumstances connected with the quarrel in question shall be taken as evidence.' Conclusive proof was essential before sentencing a person. This doctrine is followed in Indian jurisprudence as 'innocent until proved guilty'." Remarkably, "the testimony of witnesses was considered to be more reliable than documentary evidence" in the Arthashastra.

Registration & scrutiny

Before trial: the year, season, month, fortnight (paksha), date, nature and place of the deed, amount of the debt, and "the country, residence, caste, gotra, name and occupation of both the plaintiff and the defendant, both of whom must be fit to sue and defend (kṛtasamarthāvasthayoḥ), having been registered first, the statements of the parties shall be taken down… These statements should then be thoroughly scrutinised."

Perjury — the moderate stance

"For perjury, Kautilya takes a notably moderate stance… disagreeing with other schools, Kautilya limits punishment to recommend only a fine of 24 paṇas for giving false evidence and 12 paṇas for refusing to give evidence (3.11.48-49). This relative leniency may reflect his concern that imposing overly harsh penalties could discourage individuals from coming forward as witnesses." He does not approve the rule (3.11.88) making a witness liable to pay the decretal amount for refusing evidence.

Suo moto jurisdiction for the vulnerable

"In normal circumstances, a judge cannot take up a case unless a formal complaint is brought by the aggrieved party. However, in matters concerning temples, Brahmins, ascetics, women, minors, the elderly, the sick, or orphans, the dharmastha (judge) is empowered to initiate proceedings suo moto, if these individuals do not themselves approach the court. In such cases, disputes involving these groups must not be dismissed by citing lack of jurisdiction (deśacchala), by deferring them (kālacchala), or by rejecting them on the basis of adverse possession (bhogacchala)." Then: put questions to parties and witnesses; "pronounce judgment after listening to both parties and weighing all the evidence produced." — The ancient ancestor of protective jurisdiction, PIL's logic, and class-action protection for the vulnerable.

3.7 Punishment and penal theory (Daṇḍanīti)

Daṇḍanīti ("the science of law enforcement") is an important part of the Arthashastra; Daṇḍa is one of the seven essential elements of the state. The king must balance Daṇḍa with the other three upāyas — sama (conciliation/diplomacy), dāma (gifts), bheda (internal discord amongst enemies).

Three prerequisites of effective law enforcement

  1. Honesty of the law enforcers — "the law enforcers themselves, including the king, must be honest and law-abiding." Kautilya: "Thus, the king shall first reform the administration, by punishing appropriately those officers who deal in wealth; they, duly corrected, shall use the right punishments to ensure the good conduct of the people of the towns and the countryside" (4.9). He lists thirteen types of "undesirable people" who amass wealth secretly by injuring the population — corrupt judges and magistrates, heads of villages/departments who extort, perjurers and procurers of perjury, sorcerers/witchcraft, poisoners, narcotic dealers, counterfeiters and adulterators of precious metals — to be exiled or made to pay adequate compensation when exposed by secret agents (4.4). "Interestingly, corrupt judges were in the list of the 'undesirable persons'… Kautilya considers economic crimes — bribery, hoarding, cheating — more dangerous than violent crimes."
  2. Judicial fairness — standard of proof (conclusive proof before sentencing), prompt trials, minimisation of Type I error (convicting the innocent), and implicitly Type II error ("since the king was required to compensate the victim if the crime was not solved").
  3. Impartiality, proportionality and certainty of punishment — ati-daṇḍa, sama-daṇḍa, nyūna-daṇḍa (excessive, proportionate, deficient); "Kautilya preferred monetary fines to non-monetary punishment" and ensured fines were actually paid.

Purposes of punishment

"Deterrence was one of the primary goals… swift and certain application of justice"; rehabilitation — "minor offenders were sometimes given a chance to reform." The classical warning (Shamasastry's translation, p.13): "punishment when awarded with due consideration makes people devoted to righteousness and works productively of wealth and enjoyment; while punishment, when ill-awarded or under the influence of greed and anger or owing to ignorance, excites fury even among hermits and ascetics dwelling in forests, not to speak of householders. When the law of punishment is not in place, the proverb of fishes (matsyanyayamudbhāvayati) comes into play — in the absence of a magistrate (daṇḍadharābhāve) the strong will swallow the weak, but under his protection the weak resist the strong."

3.8 Contract law and civil disputes (Book 3 — an early commercial code)

"Kautilya's Arthashastra, particularly Book 3 'Concerning Law', presents one of the earliest and most systematic legal frameworks governing commerce, contracts, and financial responsibility in the ancient world… anticipating certain modern commercial law ideas such as fiduciary duty, misuse of trust, and force majeure."

3.9 Property, inheritance and corporate law

Inheritance (Book 3)

  • Distinction between joint family property and self-acquired property: the self-acquired property of a son is not divisible unless acquired by parental resources.
  • Property acquired using joint ancestral property: sons and grandsons up to the fourth generation get specific shares (aṃśabhāgaḥ), because the family line (piṇḍa) is continuous and uninterrupted (avicchinnah) up to four generations; when the line is broken (vicchinna-piṇḍa), all such heirs take equal shares.
  • No male heir: brothers or co-residents take movables (dravyam); daughters (of marriages other than the first four) take immovables (riktham); daughters of the four approved forms of marriage inherit; then father; then brothers and their sons.
  • A living father dividing property must show no bias; "the father shall not deprive any of his sons of his share without sufficient reason"; the eldest son must support younger brothers unless of bad character.
  • Adoption as a legal method to preserve lineage: where a person is "fallen" (from caste, born of outcastes, eunuchs) and his line is at the verge of extinction, relatives may beget sons for him, who receive proportional shares.
  • Land disputes, encroachments, boundaries: resolved by inspection (pariksha), witness testimony and documentary evidence — careful examination of facts plus physical inspection of the land.

Corporate & commercial law

  • Śreṇis (guilds) recognised as legal entities with specific rights and responsibilities — internal governance, manufacturing standards, ethical codes, price and quality control; they "acted as assemblies for resolving disputes internally, and their verdicts could not be questioned or challenged in royal courts"; guild leaders represented members before the king and managed taxation and market conduct — "effectively functioning as early corporate bodies with legal standing."
  • Superintendent of Accounts (Book 2, ch. 7 "The Business of Keeping up Accounts in the Office of Accountants"): precise records of every department — profits, losses, wages, taxes, prices, domestic and foreign trade; monthly and annual audits mandatory, verified by inspection, precedent, arithmetic checks and espionage; rewards for officers increasing net revenue; severe penalties for delay, falsification, double entries, omissions, manipulation — fines from equal to 20 times the loss; sealed books; annual verification on the Vyuṣṭa day with reference to place, time, source and officer responsible; "the king is urged to punish fraud but reward capable superintendents."
  • Anti-corruption: the thirteen "anti-social elements"; honesty of enforcers as a prerequisite; checks and balances; economic crimes treated as more dangerous than violent crimes.

3.10 The king in legal administration

"The king is the source, enforcer, and protector of law… If the king is energetic, diligent and active then his people will also be the same as him. But if he is careless or negligent then his people will also mirror that energy and will end up wasting and undermining the king's achievements. Hence, the king shall always be wakeful and mindful. When in court, he shall never cause his petitioners to wait at the door, for when a king makes himself inaccessible to his people and entrusts his work to his immediate officers, he may be sure to engender confusion in business, and to cause thereby public disaffection, and himself prey to his enemies."— Arthashastra (Shamasastry trans.), quoted by Pillai

His responsibilities: (i) sitting in court daily to hear cases; (ii) appointing and supervising judges; (iii) ensuring justice without bias; (iv) protecting the weak. "The Arthashastra warns that a king who fails to uphold justice will lose legitimacy. The concept of 'Dharmaraja' — a just ruler — was central to Kautilya's vision."

04(c) State & Law in the Mahabharata period — Rājyotpatti

4.1 The text and its setting

The Śāntiparva of the Mahabharata, "particularly the Rājadharmānuśāsana Parva, stands as a foundational text on ancient Indian statecraft, where Pitamaha Bhishma, in dialogue with Yudhishthira in the aftermath of the Kurukshetra war, centrally addresses the core questions regarding the origins and nature of the state." The epic "describes the words Raja (King) and the Rajya (State) as so closely linked that the state is considered to come into existence only with the advent of the king. It depicts the institution of the state as a divine necessity to ensure security and justice for humanity in response to Matsyanyaya — a condition marked by lawlessness and anarchy, which led to the extinction of Dharma and the gradual deterioration of social order as it took hold among people at the end of Satya Yuga."

Two distinct theories of the origin of the state appear in Chapters 59 and 67 of the Śāntiparva: first, the Divine Origin Theory; second, Dharma-Samvida (Dharma-based Contract theory) — "which aligns with the modern Social Contract Theory, though with important contextual and philosophical differences." Both "extensively explore the entire process of social evolution, starting from its roots in the original state of nature, incorporating both divine creation and the concepts of a coronation oath or popular agreement."

Set the cosmology first: Śāntiparva ch. 231 describes the Yuga cycle — Krita/Satya (Golden), Treta (Silver), Dvāpara (Bronze), Kali (Iron) — each with its own characteristics and progressively declining duration; and the Triguna: Sattva (wisdom, purity, tranquility, higher consciousness), Rajas (energy, drive, desire — excess leads to restlessness, aggression), Tamas (darkness, ignorance, inertia — lethargy, apathy).

4.2 Theory I — The Divine Origin Theory (Śāntiparva ch. 59)

Step 1 — Yudhishthira's two questions (59:5, 59:6-8)

"How did the title of king (Rajan) come into existence?" (59:5)
"Why does one person rule over others who are equally intelligent and courageous, even though all are subject to the same life cycles and share similar physical and mental attributes?" (59:6-8) — "equal in hands, arms, necks and intellect… equal in birth and death, equal in all qualities of men — why do the superior-in-brilliance-and-courage bow to one?"— Śāntiparva 59:5-8 (as quoted in the DU reading)

Step 2 — the pre-political Golden Age (59:14-16)

"In the beginning, there was neither any kingdom (Rajya) nor a king (Raja), no punishment (Danda) nor anyone to impose it. All the people governed themselves by following Dharma (their duty), and in doing so, they protected each other's rights. All the people were nurtured and nourished by Dharma. However, after some time, they began to experience great hardship in carrying out this mutual protection. Gradually, a sense of delusion clouded their minds. When all individuals were overtaken by delusion, they lost the ability to judge right from wrong, leading to the downfall of their Dharma."— Śāntiparva 59:14-16 (DU reading's translation)

Step 3 — the decline

"Society gradually deteriorated. Men became increasingly envious, desiring what was not theirs. This desire grew into lust, which brought forth anger and greed, eventually unravelling order and plunging society into chaos." With religious practices and Vedic study lost, noble deeds diminished; fear overtook the gods; they sought refuge in Brahma, lamenting that greed (lobha), attachment (moha) and other vices had reduced them to mortals (59:21-25).

Step 4 — the creation of Daṇḍanīti

"Brahma saw that society could not thrive without laws enforced by a ruler. Therefore, at the request of the Gods, he created the system of Dandaniti, the science of punishment/chastisement, to establish law and order in the world. To codify this system, Brahma composed a scripture comprising one hundred thousand chapters, infused with divine wisdom, detailing the principles of Dharma (virtue), Artha (profit), and Kama (pleasure) in great depth, collectively referred to as Trivarga — the fourth principle, Moksha (salvation), differing in purpose and character, transcending the worldly concerns of the other three."

"दण्डेन सहितो ह्येष लोकरक्षणकारकः॥ निग्रहानुग्रहरतो लोकाननुचरिष्यति॥७७॥ — The law of chastisement that accompanies this Niti (policy) is the protector of the whole universe. It will be enforced everywhere, diligently punishing the wicked and extending grace to the righteous."— Śāntiparva 59:77

Shiva (lord of Uma) first studied and mastered it, then Indra, Vrihaspati and Shukracharya. The gods then approached Vishnu to choose a king: Vishnu gave birth to Virajas, who refused, seeking Moksha; his descendants (Krittimat, Kardama, Ananga) upheld Dharma; Ananga's son Ativala married Sunita, daughter of Mrityu, and their son Vena became king.

Step 5 — Vena's fall and Prithu's coronation

"Driven by his intense desires, Vena abandoned Dharma and acted unrighteously towards all forms of life. The rishis expelled him and killed him through the power of their mantras. From Vena's thigh came the Nishadas, the tribal inhabitants of the mountains. But from his right arm emerged Prithu, a noble figure who mirrored Indra in his strength and wisdom — well-versed in the Vedas, warfare and the principles of governance. Prithu pledged to the Rishis that he would uphold Dharma and the Dandaniti as a ruler of the mortal realm. Prithu is revered for levelling the uneven land of the earth with his bow and is celebrated as the eighth incarnation of Vishnu. The gods, sages and celestial beings gathered to crown him king of the world. Because of his protection of the Brahmanas from harm (Kshati) he earned the title of Kshatriya. Prithu made the Earth prosperous through righteousness and elevated its glory; as a result it came to be known as 'Prithvi'." (59:81-126)

Divine Origin theory vs the Western "Divine Right of Kings" — the crucial contrast

Divine Right of Kings (Western)

The monarch derives authority directly from God, and is hence accountable only to God — not to the people, parliament, or even the church. James I of England (1609): "The state of monarchy is the supremist thing upon earth: for kings are not only God's lieutenants upon earth, and sit upon God's throne, but even by God himself they are called gods." Subjects must obey unconditionally; resistance is defiance of the divine order; a tyrannical king is a punishment for the people's own sins; "loyalty to a wicked monarch becomes the ultimate religious duty, effectively removing any accountability of the ruler to the populace."

Bhishma's divine origin (Bharatiya)

"The king is not a representative or agent of God on earth. He is indeed divine, but his divinity is based on his virtuous character. He is accountable for his actions, and his orders hold authority only when they align with Dharma (the law). Orders that violate Dharma are invalid, and the king himself is confined by the boundaries of Rajadharma, with no authority to exceed these limits." — A Devansh (divine being) in human form who rules in accordance with Dharma: "a distinct place in the history of ancient Bharatiya political traditions."

Cross-support from Unit-1 material (Dhyani): "No Indian ruler could claim with impunity and so brazenly as Alauddin Khilzi of medieval India and Louis XIV of France did that they were the State"; the king was a trustee (Ashoka, Harsha); "fear of popular revolt was another deterrent"; "the Mahabharata sanctions rebellion against a worthless king"; and Manu — "Dharma when violated verily destroys; Dharma when preserved preserves." Dhavan adds the Mahabharata's rule: "A King who after having sworn that he shall protect his subjects fails to protect them should be executed like a mad dog" and "the people should execute a king who does not protect them… Such a king is not a king but misfortune" — "sovereignty was based on an implied social compact, and if the King violated the traditional pact, he forfeited his kingship."

4.3 Theory II — Dharma-Samvida (Dharma-based Contract) (Śāntiparva ch. 67)

"Ancient Indian texts on polity scarcely enunciated a theory directly akin to the Western concept of the Social Contract… Nevertheless, certain parallels can be observed, particularly in the Dharma-Samvida and the notions of political obligation expressed by ancient Indian scholars like Bhishma and Kautilya. What is most crucial to emphasise here is the evidence of a governmental contract between the people and the ruler." — and "the roots of such ideas can actually be found much earlier in the Mahabharata, which was written long before Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau."

The first, failed, self-rule agreement (67:18-19)

05(d) Idea of State and Law in Ramarajya

5.1 The thesis — an indigenous model of the ideal state

The chapter opens with the crisis of our age — unchecked state power, erosion of civil liberties, disintegration of community values — and the "resurgence of debates within contemporary social science concerning the nature of an Ideal State." The dominant (Western) narrative credits Plato with the first systematic conception of the Ideal State in The Republic — "justice, education and communism at the centre"; "for him, that state is ideal where philosophers are the rulers." The chapter's charge against Plato: fundamentally utopian, born of disillusionment with Athenian democracy after Socrates' execution; and per Karl Popper (The Open Society and its Enemies), the philosopher-king was "his philosophical critique of democracy that was politically unrealistic and famously accused of laying the intellectual foundation for totalitarianism."

"Challenging these long-standing biases within the social sciences, this chapter seeks to present the ancient Indian conception of the ideal state as exemplified by the model of Ramarajya — a vision that is neither apolitical nor irrational but fundamentally state-centric and representative of a highly sophisticated body of political thought, arguably more advanced than that of the West."— Sharma & Rawat

The definition

"Ramarajya can be defined as the just and welfare-oriented rule of Prabhu Shri Rama as depicted in the Valmiki Ramayana… the seventh avatar of Lord Vishnu. If one defines a welfare state as one fundamentally committed to the well-being of all citizens, where the ruler is morally obligated to serve the public good, remains open to criticism, and is accountable to the people, then Ramarajya fully embodies those ideals."

Swami Karpatri Maharaj (Marxwaad Aur Ramrajya): "Ramarajya is a form of governance rooted in the principles of Dharma as prescribed in the scriptures (Sastra). In such a rule, the will, interests, and consent of the people are given utmost importance." The system is also known as Ishwarrajya (God's kingdom), Dharmarajya (kingdom of righteousness), impartial Dharma-based state, spiritual governance, and Dharma-controlled administration.

5.2 Valmiki's description of Ramarajya — the "constitutional text"

The concept appears at three places: (i) Bala Kanda — Narada's six verses on Rama's future reign (concise); (ii) Yuddha Kanda, Canto 128 — the most detailed description of the actual state of the kingdom under Rama's rule, "rooted in Dharma and cosmic order"; (iii) Uttara Kanda — Bharata recounts the reign's characteristics as a "philosophical archetype of righteous governance." "Across all three accounts, the depiction of Ramarajya remains largely consistent."

5.3 The Ayodhya polity — continuity, consent and a "constitutional" monarchy

Continuity of the ideal (not an innovation of Rama)

"A closer and more critical reading of Valmiki Ramayana suggests that the foundations of this ideal governance were laid much earlier — King Dasharatha's reign was already based on Dharma, morality and public welfare… inherited from a well-established tradition within the Iksvaku Dynasty, sustained and transmitted across generations." (This is the answer to "Ramarajya = only Rama's reign?" — no; the model is dynastic-constitutional.)

Political fragmentation with political consciousness

"India was culturally unified but politically fragmented into numerous small kingdoms… Yet the political consciousness of the time was developed enough for the people of Ayodhya to recognise the dangers of a stateless society." Valmiki, like the Dharmashastra and the Mahabharata, invokes Matsyanyaya: "in an anarchic state… larger fish swallowing the smaller ones… people exploit and destroy one another and society descends into chaos" (Ayodhya Kanda 67/31). This fear explains why, immediately after Dasharatha's demise, the citizens urgently appealed to Vashistha for a new monarch (67/38).

Kingship's own origin-story (Uttara Kanda, Agastya's narration): in Satyuga humanity lived ruler-less; with sin and disorder, people prayed to Brahma, who appointed Ksupa (Kaśyapa), "endowed with the radiance and powers of the guardian deities", as ruler — "the institutions of the state came into being with the emergence of the king."

Limited / constitutional monarchy — the evidence

  • Succession not automatic: "The outward structure of Valmiki's kingship appeared hereditary, but a closer examination of the Iksvaku lineage reveals that dynastic succession was not absolute… subject to a complex and nuanced system of political consent, involving not only ministers and royal elders but also feudal lords, whose endorsement was crucial."
  • Ratification by the assembly: "Even when Dasharatha nominated Rama as Yuvaraja, this decision was only formalised after it was ratified by the royal assembly (Ayodhya Kanda 1–2; and 2/10 — Dasharatha obtained the consent of all the eminent Brahmanas in his court). The monarch's will, therefore, was not final; it had to be negotiated within a framework of collective authority… signifies the presence of democratic tendencies within the monarchical framework of Ayodhya."
  • Rules of succession: the eldest son customarily inherited (79/7), but "if deemed unfit, he could be bypassed in favour of a more capable younger son"; in the absence of a direct heir, the king's brother could assume the throne; the Yuvaraja typically succeeded on death/abdication — semi-elective kingship, "challenging the notion of a purely autocratic or divine-right monarchy."
  • King as representative of the divine, but under Dharma: Rama's dialogue with Vali on the ruler–subject relation as father–son (Kishkinda Kanda 18/41-42): "rulers are givers of the most essential elements of life: Dharma (moral order), Artha (material prosperity), and Jivana (life itself)… do not harm them, do not slander them… for they are truly deities who roam this Earth in human form" — an ideal of rule "not by power alone, but through moral integrity and virtue."
  • The reciprocal relationship: "Even though the people considered the king to be supreme, they expressed their responses according to the king's conduct… The people are not portrayed as passive recipients of rule. They observe, evaluate, and react to royal conduct… a ruler who leads by example, governs with Nyaya, and continuously earns the trust of his people."

Rama's character — the "qualification clause" (Ayodhya Kanda 2/28-48)

Valmiki's catalogue: truthful, heroic, endowed with divine qualities like Indra, upholder of Dharma and Artha, pleasant like the moon, patient like the Earth, wise like Brihaspati, powerful like Indra, modest, "one who does not covet others' wealth", calm, comforter of the distressed, soft-spoken, grateful, self-restrained, gentle, steady-minded, always welfare-oriented, devotee of scholars/elders/Brahmins, master of all weapons, knowledgeable in the Veda, well-versed in all branches of learning, master of Gandharvaveda (music), generous and large-hearted, protector of villages and towns, skilled in warfare, victorious, affectionate toward his people (praja-vatsala), keeper of all four varnas engaged in their duties, a great archer, cheerful, Dharma-abiding, radiant like Vishnu, free from vices like attachment and anger, just, radiant as the sun (2/28-47) — so ideal that "the people of the earth wished to make him their ruler" (2/48).

5.4 The science of governance — Rama's lessons to Bharata (Ayodhya Kanda 100)

What good governance (Sushasan) requires

  • The king should protect and care for all the people in accordance with Dharma (100/48); through the righteous exercise of Danda (punishing wrongdoers justly), "the wise ruler ensures the welfare of his subjects, secures order over the entire earth, and ultimately attains heaven" (100/76).
  • "By adhering to righteous policies (Niti), the king ensures the safety and prosperity of his subjects; but if he fails in his duties, they face destruction" (Uttara Kanda, Praksipta Sarga 2/5).
  • Governance of the kingdom = the king's Sanatan Dharma (Bala Kanda 25/19½); he must be prepared to act "severe or mild, culpable or harmless, in order to safeguard his subjects" — that is why he is Praja-palak (25/18); he "does not act out of self-interest but places public welfare above his own comfort."
  • One-sixth share of merit: "A righteous king shares in one-sixth of the spiritual merit earned by his subjects through study of the Veda, austerities and virtuous deeds, as he protects and upholds the dharmic order" (Uttara Kanda 74/31).

Rama's checklist of vices, virtues and strategies

  • 14 flaws to avoid (100/65-67): atheism, falsehood, anger, carelessness, procrastination, ignoring wise counsel, laziness, narrow-mindedness in planning, pointless strategies, failure to execute plans, failure to safeguard secrets, and others — the "king's defects".
  • Dasvarga — ten vices born of desire (100/68; Manu 7.47 lists them as hunting, dice, sleeping by day, censoriousness, overindulgence in sensuality, intoxication, the musical triad, and listless wandering).
  • Ashtvarga — eight vices born of anger: slander, reckless daring, treachery, jealousy, fault-finding, misuse of wealth, harsh speech, excessive punishment.
  • Saptavarga — the seven limbs of the state: king, ministers, nation (territory), fort, treasury, army, allies (exactly Kautilya's saptāṅga).
  • Chaturvarga — the four Upāyas: Sam (conciliation), Dam (gifts), Danda (punishment), Bheda (dissension).
  • Shadgunya — the six measures of statecraft: Sandhi (alliance), Vigraha (war), Yana (strategic movement / seeking shelter), Asana (neutrality), Dwaidhibhav (dual policy), Samshraya (taking refuge under a stronger power) — chosen as the situation favours the state (100/69).

5.5 The Mantriparishad and the Sabha — institutions of accountability

Council of Ministers — two tiers

(1) Ritvija / Purohita / Gurus — the advisory council: consulted before important tasks; generally Brahmins; performed duties similar to ministers (Bala Kanda 7/4, 7/6). "The Rajpurohita, who was in fact a minister, played a significant role in all affairs of the state — even the king could not disregard his counsel. He served as the Prime Minister, and his position was second only to that of the king." (2) Amatya — the cabinet (Mantri Mandal): each minister the head of a department; consultation vital for good governance. Rama's own test at Chitrakoot (100/15-16): "the main reason for the king's victory rests on good advice, effective only when ministers, well-versed in ethics and scriptures, keep it completely secret." Through a network of spies, they knew "what was being done, what had been done, and what was intended" (7/9); as vigilant guardians they observed every judgement "through the eyes of Dharma" (7/16). After Dasharatha's death, in the absence of Rama and Bharata, the Gurus handed the decision on the succession to Sage Vashishtha.

The Sabha — assembly with real (if limited) power

The king was Sabhapati (president) of the Sabha; in his absence the Purohita presided (typically only in emergencies, 3/24-35). Amatyas and Purohitas/Gurus were ex-officio members. The term Sabha appears in two senses: (i) a public gathering for discussion (Ayodhya 6/20 — after Rama's declaration as Yuvaraja); (ii) the Royal Assembly hall (67/2). "The approval of the Sabha was mandatory for all important state decisions" — the coronation of Rama, of Bharata, general administration, war. The Sabha's primary duty: accept the king's proposal or suggest a better alternative; the king generally consulted ministers before presenting proposals.

Modern parallel given by the reading itself: "The final power was with the king, like even today, if any law has to be implemented, then the help of ordinance is taken for implementing it and when the circumstances are favourable, it is passed by the parliament. Though the independence of the Sabha was limited, its functioning reveals that governance was, to some extent, accountable to society, and the social order was shaped with the collective welfare in mind. If any system proved detrimental to society at large, the people in the Sabha did not merely oppose it; they actively provided alternatives and often implemented change."

5.6 Nyāya-Daṇḍa Vyavasthā — the judicial system of Ramarajya

"The Idea of Ramarajya was not merely a mechanism for maintaining political order, but a pragmatic constitutional framework grounded in Dharma, where justice was not treated as an abstract legal category, but as the concrete realisation of righteousness (Dharma), rooted in the Sastras and sustained by the traditions. Rama himself administered justice according to the established tradition of Rajadharma and thus became the fountain of both Dharma and Nyaya for all alike."

EPIC CASEThe Dog's Justice — Ramarajya's most famous judicial precedentUttara Kanda (Praksipta) Sarga 1-2 · Valmiki Ramayana
Facts

Rama sat in his court every day, where every grievance was heard. One day, after all cases were disposed of, he asked Lakshmana to check outside for any remaining petitioner. Lakshmana found a dog, injured on the head. The dog would not speak to Lakshmana: "I want justice from Rama. I will only tell him my problem." Brought inside, the dog complained that a Brahmin ascetic named Sarvathasiddha had struck him on the head without any reason, though he had done nothing wrong.

Issue

What is the just punishment where the offender is a Brahmin (who, under the Śāstras, cannot suffer corporeal punishment) and the victim is an animal — with no ordinary legal provision squarely applicable?

Arguments (as recorded)

For the complainant (the dog): an unprovoked assault; compensation/justice due regardless of the offender's status. For the offender (Sarvathasiddha): he admitted his crime candidly — "In a moment of Krodha, I hit him on the head with a stick. I am guilty of this. You can give me whatever Danda (punishment) you want." The ministers and courtiers (the Sabha's view): "This case is highly intricate, for the offender is a Brahmana, and according to the Sastric injunctions, no corporeal punishment may be inflicted upon a Brahmana. Hence the ordinary provisions of law are inapplicable here. Therefore, being a king, it is within your prerogative to give the judgment."

Decision

"Then, in keeping with Dharma, Rama asked the dog itself what punishment should be given." The dog replied: "My lord! Make him the chief Mahant of the Kalinjar Math." Rama did so; the ascetic was happy; the court was astonished — "the punishment seemed more like a reward than a penalty." Rama then instructed the dog to explain.

Reasoning (the dog's explanation)

"In his previous birth, [the dog] was the head of the same Math. Due to pride, arrogance, and the misuse of his authority, he had deviated from the path of Dharma and, as a result, was reborn as a dog. If this priest, who is already marked by Krodha and cruelty, is elevated to the same position, he too will sink into misery and ultimately degrade himself."

Principle evolved: (1) Justice is for all beings — "so impartial and inclusive that not only human beings but even animals had their appeals heard and duly redressed"; (2) accessibility — the court is open daily, without professionals' fees, the petitioner (Karyarthi) has immediate access; (3) the victim participates in the sentence (restorative-consensual justice — the ancestor of victim-impact and restorative justice); (4) punishment may be invisible — the highest penalty here is trust: elevation to a seat of authority, which becomes the corrupt man's ruin; a punishment that is "the most relevant to the offender's karma", not merely a retributive tariff; (5) no crime goes unaddressed because of the status of the offender — where the śāstra's ordinary rule fails, the king, guided by Dharma, still must do justice.

The judicial architecture

  • Court = Sabha / Rajsabha — "Judiciary, in that period was centred in the Sabha or Rajasabha, which functioned as the court of law."
  • King as Chief Justice: "Unlike modern times, where the judiciary operates independently, in Ramarajya the king himself acted as the chief justice, personally presiding over the royal court; that is why the king was often referred to as the Dharmapala (guardian of Dharma) (Aranya 1/18) and Dandadhara (executor of punishment) (Aranya 1/19)."
  • Dharmasana: the king sat daily upon the seat of Dharma (Uttara 1/1), "wherein Dharma denoted Nyaya or justice."
  • Sabhasads — the ancient jury: the Sabha included "ministers knowledgeable in state affairs, scholars well-versed in the Dharmashastra, experts in ethics and governance, and kings called Sabhasads (1/3)… their duty to assist the king in the Court of law. They may be termed as the jury of that ancient period."
  • No lawyers, no costs: "Since there were neither professional lawyers as we see today nor the burden of court expenses, justice remained impartial and accessible. A petitioner (Karyarthi) has the right to immediate access to court (1/5.5)."
  • Delay is sin: Rama told Lakshmana that "a king who fails to dispense justice each day, or who delays and neglects its administration, incurs grave sin and becomes destined for hell" (Uttara 53/6).
  • Few cases = sign of strength: "Litigation was rare… the judiciary was strong, as society preferred to abide by Dharma and avoid transgression… neither children, nor youth, nor those of middle age faced death, nor did anyone encounter obstacles in seeking justice" (1/9).
  • Final court of appeal: "Rama's Court of Justice probably also discharged the functions of the final Court of Appeal in the state" (P.C. Dharma).
  • The king as agent, not author, of law: "The king acted merely as an agent of the state, administering justice impartially to enforce the Dharma which was neither of his own making nor necessarily of his own choice but based upon the Sastras and traditions in society… Rama himself was the living embodiment of Dharma, always committed to the welfare of all beings (1/22)."

Principles

  • No punishment without crime: "The Valmiki Ramayana makes it clear that imposing Danda without the commission of a crime is unjust" (Uttara 79/8).
  • Punishment only on deviation from Dharma: "Rama's decision to punish Vali only upon his deviation from the righteous path" (Kishkinda 18/20-21) — "those who act against the social order can be brought back to their righteous path only through Danda."
  • Just punishment → heaven; unjust → downfall: "A king who inflicted punishment justly and lawfully was led to heaven (Uttara 79/9), but if he punished unjustly, whether with bias or deceit, he paved the way to his own downfall" (Ayodhya 100/59).

  • Equality before the law (Dharma): "The king would punish even his own son if he committed a crime; all were equal before the rule of law (Dharma), even the king was also bound by it and must work for the welfare of society at large. In Rama's Kingdom, Dharma was upheld above all else… his actions exhibit respect for the established laws and traditions of society" — and "members of the Nyaya Sabha i.e., the king's council, would not hesitate to give Danda to their own sons if the situation demanded, ensuring that the Dharma remained supreme and impartial" (Bala 7/10).

5.8 Conclusion of the reading

"Ramarajya is not merely a metaphor for an ideal state; it represents a lived civilizational experience of Dharma in India's historical consciousness… His vision of political leadership was ideal as it fell under the purview of Dharma, which set the benchmarks for statecraft and administration, with the primary objective being the welfare and happiness of all beings… The Dharmarajya of Rama has been described as the 'विग्रहवान् धर्मः' (Dharma embodied) or the epitome of morality, where the ruler himself worshipped people (आराधनाय लोकस्य) and the kingdom (राज्यम् उपासित्वा)… [and] consequently emerged as a well-established metaphor for the welfare state… Several rulers in ancient India sought to associate their reigns with this ideal… Such a Dharmic polity served as a constant reminder to those in authority of their ethical obligations, resonating with the modern notions of constitutional morality, where the legitimacy of authority rests not merely on the power of the state, but on adherence to rule of law and the welfare of the citizenry."— Sharma & Rawat (conclusion)

GROUP A — The Epic "Cases" (full briefs)

EPIC CASE 1The Origin of Kingship — Yudhishthira's Enquiry and Bhishma's Answer (Divine Origin Theory)Mahabharata, Śāntiparva ch. 59
Facts / Setting

After the Kurukshetra war, Yudhishthira, burdened with kingship, sits with Bhishma in the Rājadharmānuśāsana Parva. He poses the foundational question of political philosophy: "How did the title of king (Rajan) come into existence?" — and why does one person rule over others equal to him in intelligence, courage, birth, death and all qualities?

Issue

What is the origin and justification of kingship, and what follows from it for the limits of royal power?

Arguments / Account of both sides

The pre-political condition (Bhishma's opening proposition): initially, "there was neither any kingdom nor a king, no punishment nor anyone to impose it. All the people governed themselves by following Dharma… they protected each other's rights." — life governed by Dharma, not by force. The counter-movement: mutual protection became burdensome; delusion obscured right from wrong; envy, desire, lust, anger and greed "unravelled order and plunged society into chaos"; Vedic study and noble deeds disappeared. The gods' position (their plea to Brahma): the loss of Vedic wisdom through human greed (lobha) and attachment (moha) had diminished even the gods, making them "akin to mortals" — hence some corrective institution was necessary.

Decision

Brahma "saw that society could not thrive without laws enforced by a ruler" and created Daṇḍanīti — "the science of punishment/chastisement" — as a scripture of one hundred thousand chapters detailing Dharma, Artha and Kāma (Trivarga), with Moksha as the transcendent fourth. The law of chastisement "is the protector of the whole universe… diligently punishing the wicked and extending grace to the righteous" (59:77). It was studied by Shiva, then Indra, Vrihaspati and Shukracharya. At the gods' request, Vishnu produced the line of kings — Virajas (who refused, seeking Moksha), Krittimat, Kardama, Ananga, Ativala, and Vena.

Principle evolved: Kingship is of divine origin — but as a trust for the preservation of Dharma, not as a delegation of divine authority. The king is Devansh: divine by virtue of character and dharmic conduct, not by blood or office alone; his orders are valid only when they conform to Dharma (explicitly the opposite of the Western Divine Right of Kings). The state comes into being with the king and exists because Dharma, left unenforced, collapses into matsya-nyāya.
EPIC CASE 2Vena's Fall and Prithu's Coronation — the unrighteous king is extinguishedŚāntiparva 59:81-126
Facts

Vena, descendant of the pious kings, becomes king. "Driven by his intense desires, Vena abandoned Dharma and acted unrighteously towards all forms of life."

Issue

What may be done with a king who abandons Dharma? Can divine origin protect him?

Arguments

Vena's position (implicit): royal power is his by descent and divine appointment — hence beyond question. The rishis' position: kingship is conditional on Dharma; the guardians of Dharma have the duty — and power — to remove the unrighteous ruler who harms "all forms of life."

Decision

The rishis expelled Vena and killed him through the power of their mantras. From his thigh came the Nishadas (mountain tribes) — an emblem of the degraded issue of an unrighteous line; from his right arm emerged Prithu, "a noble figure who mirrored Indra in strength and wisdom", learned in the Vedas, warfare and governance. Prithu pledged to the Rishis that he would uphold Dharma and the Daṇḍanīti. On his pledge — a coronation contract — the gods, sages and celestial beings crowned him king of the world; "because of his protection of the Brahmanas from harm (Kshati) he earned the title of Kshatriya"; his righteous rule made the earth prosperous, so it came to be called Prithvi.

Principle evolved: (1) Divine origin is no immunity — an unrighteous king forfeits his kingship and may be removed and even executed by the guardians of Dharma (cf. Dhavan's Mahabharata rule: a king who fails to protect his subjects "should be executed like a mad dog"; "the people should execute a king who does not protect them"). (2) Legitimacy is created by a pledge (coronation oath/agreement) to uphold Dharma and Daṇḍanīti — the earliest form of a coronation oath limiting the ruler. (3) Royal titles are earned by protective conduct (Kshatriya; Prithvi).
EPIC CASE 3The People v. Anarchy — the failed self-help agreement and the Contract with Manu (Dharma-Samvida)Śāntiparva ch. 67
Facts

In the condition of arajakata (kinglessness) described at 67:16-17 — "if there were no kings on earth wielding the rod of chastisement, then just as in water large fish devour the smaller ones, so too would the strong exploit the weak" — the community first tried self-regulation: they agreed to cast out those who spoke harshly, used violence, imposed severe punishment, engaged with others' wives or took others' wealth, "in order to build confidence among people of all castes" (67:18-19). The agreement failed, and the people appealed to Brahma: "We are perishing without a king. Please appoint a ruler… whom all of us will honour together and who will look after us" (67:21).

Issue

Can order be established by mere agreement among equals, or is a ruler necessary? And on what terms may rulership be accepted?

Arguments of the people

Brahma proposed Manu; Manu declined — "ruling over men is extremely challenging, as they are naturally deceitful and full of greed" (67:22). The people then bargained, promising: 1/50th of their animals, 1/50th of their gold, and 1/10th of their crops for the treasury (67:23-24); that the most skilled in weapons would follow him as the gods follow Indra (67:25); and — in return for his protection — a quarter of the merit earned by his protected subjects would accrue to the king (67:27).

Decision

On these terms, Manu accepted. The king's authority was thereafter grounded in the people's acceptance, and the people "granted the king only those powers that were necessary and appropriate for governing the state."

Principle evolved (Dharma-Samvida): The state originates in a dharma-based contract between people and ruler — and the contract itself is under Dharma. Tax is consideration for protection (praja-sukham); the king is a benevolent, not autocratic, ruler; his powers are enumerated (limited); the people's duties (taxes, weapons-service) are specified — and in the ancient scheme the duties imposed on the people are greater than those imposed on the king. This is the closest Indian analogue of (and historical predecessor to) the social contract — aligned with Locke (limited authority; right to resist abuses: note Bhishma's other texts sanctioning rebellion against a worthless king), but opposite to Hobbes (no irrevocable surrender; no absolute sovereign). The reading calls Dharma-Samvida "the most thorough explanation for the emergence of the state".
EPIC CASE 4The Dog v. Sarvathasiddha (the Brahmin ascetic) — "I want justice from Rama"Valmiki Ramayana, Uttara Kanda (Praksipta) Sarga 1-2
Facts

Rama held court daily; after all cases were heard he sent Lakshmana to see if anyone remained. A dog — injured on the head — refused to speak to Lakshmana, insisting on Rama alone. In court, the dog alleged that the Brahmin ascetic Sarvathasiddha had struck it on the head without cause. The ascetic, summoned, admitted the assault: "In a moment of Krodha, I hit him on the head with a stick. I am guilty of this. You can give me whatever Danda you want."

Issues

(1) Does the court's protection extend to a non-human complainant? (2) What punishment fits where the offender is a Brahmin (exempt from corporeal punishment under the Śāstras) and the ordinary provisions are "inapplicable"?

Arguments

The dog: unprovoked injury; wants justice, not sympathy — and only from Rama. Sarvathasiddha: candid admission; accepts any punishment. The ministers/courtiers (Sabha): the case is "highly intricate" — no corporeal punishment for a Brahmana; the ordinary law cannot yield an answer; "being a king, it is within your prerogative to give the judgment."

Decision

"In keeping with Dharma, Rama asked the dog itself what punishment should be given." The dog demanded: "Make him the chief Mahant of the Kalinjar Math." Rama decreed accordingly; the ascetic rejoiced; the court was astonished that a penalty looked like a reward. On Rama's instruction, the dog explained: in a previous birth he himself had been the head of that very Math; pride, arrogance and misuse of authority had turned him from Dharma and reduced him to a dog; elevating the already wrathful, cruel priest to the same seat would lead him to the same ruin. "In his previous birth… he will sink into misery and ultimately degrade himself."

Principles evolved: (1) Justice is universal — for all beings, human or animal; the weakest complainant has direct access to the highest court (the ancestor of animal-welfare jurisprudence — see Unit 1(a), and the reading's argument that Dharma protects "every inanimate and animate object… from mountain to river, from plants to animals"); (2) accessible, simple, costless justice — no lawyers, no fees, daily court, no delay (delay "incurs grave sin" — Uttara 53/6); (3) the victim participates in sentencing — restorative justice; (4) the fitting penalty may be non-corporeal and even disguised as a favour — what the offender "deserves" is measured by dharma and consequence, not by a tariff: the punishment is precisely the office that bred his karma; (5) where the textual law is silent, the king, guided by Dharma and advised by the Sabha, must still do justice without leaving a wrong unredressed.
EPIC CASE 5Rama and Vali — the ruler as giver of Dharma, Artha and Jivana; punishment only upon deviationKishkinda Kanda 18/20-21, 18/41-42
Facts

Rama kills Vali (the monkey king) in the Kishkinda episode — a killing that needs justification, since Vali was not present to Rama's cause. Rama's dialogue with the dying Vali supplies the jurisprudence of the ruler's position and of legitimate punishment.

Issues

(1) On what basis may the king punish? (2) What does the ruler owe his subjects — and who is the ruler himself?

Arguments

Vali (the grievance): punishment visited upon me without my having harmed you — is this just? Rama (the justification): Vali was punished only "upon his deviation from the righteous path" (18/20: "this punishment is meted out because of your transgression") — and "those who act against the social order can be brought back to their righteous path only through Danda" (18/21). In the same discourse (18/41-42), about the ruler–subject relationship (framed as father–son), Rama declares: rulers are the givers of the most essential elements of life — Dharma (moral order), Artha (material prosperity) and Jivana (life itself) — therefore subjects must not harm, slander, accuse or speak harshly to them, "for they are truly deities who roam this Earth in human form."

Decision

Vali's punishment is upheld as consequential on his own deviation from Dharma — not arbitrary royal violence; and the ruling fixes the reciprocal duties: the ruler is the giver of dharma, prosperity and life (justifying the subject's reverence), while the subject's reverence is conditional on the ruler's own dharma.

Principles evolved: (1) Daṇḍa without transgression is unjust (explicitly stated at Uttara 79/8: "imposing Danda without the commission of a crime is unjust"); (2) the ruler's authority is a function of the goods he delivers (dharma, artha, jivana) — welfare legitimacy, not mere power; (3) the same discourse yields the ideal of rule "not by power alone, but through moral integrity and virtue."
EPIC CASE 6Rama's Lessons on Sushasan to Bharata — the 14 flaws, the seven limbs, the six policiesAyodhya Kanda 100 (also 25/18-19½; Uttara 74/31)
Facts

In exile at Chitrakoot, Rama meets Bharata (who refuses the throne) and delivers a systematic exposition of Rājadharma/Rājanīti — what it "genuinely means for a king to govern justly."

Issue

What is the content of good governance (Sushasan)?

Arguments / Positions

The king's obligations: protect and care for all the people in accordance with Dharma (100/48); exercise Danda righteously to ensure welfare, secure order and attain heaven (100/76); follow Niti — "the king ensures the safety and prosperity of his subjects; but if he fails in his duties, they face destruction" (Uttara, Praksipta 2/5); governance is the king's Sanatan Dharma (Bala 25/19½); he may be "severe or mild… to safeguard his subjects" (25/18); he shares one-sixth of his subjects' merit as he protects the dharmic order (Uttara 74/31); and he must avoid the 14 flaws (atheism, falsehood, anger, carelessness, procrastination, ignoring wise counsel, laziness, narrow-mindedness, pointless strategies, failure to execute or to keep secrets, etc. — 100/65-67). For personal discipline: renounce the Dasvarga (ten vices of desire: hunting, dice, sleeping by day, censoriousness, overindulgence in sensuality, intoxication, musical triad, listless wandering — Manu 7.47) and the Ashtvarga (eight vices of anger: slander, reckless daring, treachery, jealousy, fault-finding, misuse of wealth, harsh speech, excessive punishment). For the state: the Saptavarga (king, ministers, nation, fort, treasury, army, allies); for diplomacy the Chaturvarga (Sam, Dam, Danda, Bheda) and the Shadgunya (Sandhi, Vigraha, Yana, Asana, Dwaidhibhav, Samshraya).

Decision and principle
Principles evolved: (1) Good governance is a catalogue of virtues, vices and institutions — a complete "science of administration" mirroring Kautilya's saptāṅga, upāyas and ṣāḍguṇya; (2) the king's success or ruin tracks his moral discipline — vices of desire and anger are not private matters but governance failures; (3) welfare is the measure — the ruler's acts are judged by their effect on the subjects (sarvajana hita).
EPIC CASE 7Dasharatha's Nomination of Rama — ratification by the Assembly (semi-elective kingship)Ayodhya Kanda 1-2, 2/10, 79/7
Facts

King Dasharatha, wishing to retire, announces his decision to appoint Rama as Yuvaraja (crown prince) "for the welfare of the people."

Issue

Is the monarch's will alone sufficient to settle the succession?

Arguments

Royal prerogative view: succession is hereditary; the eldest son takes the throne "by custom" (79/7); the king's word suffices. The constitutional view (prevailing in fact): the nomination was "only formalised after it was ratified by the royal assembly" — Dasharatha obtained "the consent of all the eminent Brahmanas present in his court" (2/10); succession was "subject to a complex and nuanced system of political consent, involving not only ministers and royal elders but also feudal lords, whose endorsement was crucial"; an unfit heir could be bypassed for a more capable younger son; in the absence of a direct heir, the king's brother could succeed.

Decision and principle
Principles evolved: "The monarch's will was not final; it had to be negotiated within a framework of collective authority" — kingship in the Ramayana was limited/constitutional and semi-elective; "it signifies the presence of democratic tendencies within the monarchical framework of Ayodhya." This is the textual basis for the reading's bold claim that Ramarajya was "a practised and sustained model of governance," not utopia.
EPIC CASE 8The Citizens of Ayodhya and the Succession after Dasharatha — "a kingdom without a king is a wilderness"Ayodhya Kanda 67/2, 67/31, 67/38; Uttara Kanda 77/37-43 (Agastya's narration)
Facts

Dasharatha dies while Rama is in exile and Bharata is away. The citizens assemble (67/2) and, terrified of kinglessness, urge Vashistha to crown a successor immediately — "the citizens of Ayodhya urgently appealed to Sage Vashistha for the appointment of a new monarch" (67/38). In the Uttara Kanda, Agastya narrates to Rama the original emergence of kingship: in the Satyuga humanity lived ruler-less; with sin and disorder the people prayed to Brahma, who appointed Ksupa (Kaśyapa), endowed with the radiance and powers of the guardian deities, as ruler of the earth.

Issue

Why must a ruler be appointed at once, and what is the origin of the institution?

Arguments

The citizens: "नाराजके जनपदे स्वकं भवति कस्यचित् — in a kingdom without a king nothing remains one's own; मत्स्या इव जना नित्यं भक्षयन्ति परस्परम् — as fish, people devour each other" (67/31) — the fear of matsya-nyāya makes immediate appointment imperative. Vashistha (as the institution's guardian): "स नः समीक्ष्य विज्ञाय वृत्तं नृपं विना राष्ट्रमरण्यभूमिम् — a realm without a king is a wilderness" (67/38).

Decision and principle
Principles evolved: (1) The state (rājya) and the king (rāja) are co-extensive — "the state is considered to come into existence only with the advent of the king"; from the people's own perception of anarchy flows the demand for authority ("the institutions of the state came into being with the emergence of the king"); (2) kingship's origin is both divine (Brahma's appointment) and popular (the people's petition) — the Ramayana's own blend of the two theories Bhishma expounds; (3) the state's function is protection — "the king was ordained to protect the people, for without a king, the state could not survive."

Q.1  "The concept of Ṛta and Kartavya is the foundational jurisprudence of ancient India." Discuss. 20 marks · 900 words

Introduction. Ṛta and Kartavya are the twin pillars of Bharatiya jurisprudence. Ṛta is the cosmic order that governs the universe — the sum of the laws of nature, truth and justice — while Kartavya (duty) is the human response to that order. The classical Indian jurist Seema Singh describes Ṛta as "the command of the Supreme Consciousness, the Purusha," and thus "law in the true legal sense," whose violation is termed an-Ṛta — a condition equivalent to destruction. Together, the two concepts build a jurisprudence in which rights are consequences of duties, not the reverse.

1. Ṛta as the Grundnorm. The Ṛgveda (X.90) begins with Purusha, the Supreme Being, whom the verses (X.90.1-4) describe as "all this — whatever has been and whatever is to be" — and from whose sacrificial dismemberment the cosmos, the varṇas and Dharma itself arise. Because law flows from this source, cosmic laws act "as the ultimate standard, or Grundnorm, against which all other laws are measured" — exactly the function Kelsen assigns to the basic norm of a legal order, but with substantive content: truth, order, harmony, justice. In case of conflict between man-made law and cosmic/natural law, the cosmic law must prevail.

2. Kartavya — duty as a natural obligation. The concept of Kartavya must be distinguished from the modern concept of duty. Duty is statutory; Kartavya is a natural obligation. Duty attaches to an office or contract; Kartavya is intrinsic to one's place in the cosmic order. Duty's breach is remedied by court; the failure of Kartavya is a violation of cosmic law, punishable by an-Ṛta — the very destruction of the social and natural order. Fundamental Duties under Article 51A are a statutory echo; but "while Fundamental Duties represent specific obligations, Kartavya transcends them, embodying a more profound, cosmic and indeed divine sense of purpose."

3. The duty-before-right model (Kartavya-Adhikar). Seema Singh, following Dr. P.S. Paranjape, explains that the Indian approach is "totally opposite to the modern day right-duty corollary": a right is not the premise but the outcome of a duty performed. Rights are consequences of Kartavya. He cites the classical obligations: indriyanigraha (control of senses), daya (kindness to all beings), danam (charity) — culminating in moksha (salvation) as the highest goal of Purushartha. This inverted structure means that the modern "rights-based governance" — which treats rights as primary and duties as parasitic — is the reverse of the Bharatiya order; the colonial shift to rights-based governance is precisely what displaced Kartavya from legal discourse.

4. Duties of the ruler. The model is completed by the ruler's duty. The king's sole Kartavya is praja-sukham — "the happiness of the people" — which Kautilya makes the king's own reward (Arthashastra 1.19.34). The Nyāya Sūtra's maxim — "कर्तव्यनिष्टं सम्यक् पालनं राज्यं वर्धते" (the righteous discharge of duty means the proper protection of the state, and it prospers thereby) — supplies the theory in one line. Rājadharma (the king's duty) sits above Rājaśāsana (the king's command): the state is legitimate only while it performs its duty.

5. The sanction theory: Ṛṇa and Daṇḍanīti. Every being is born with three debts (Ṛṇa): to the Devas, to the Rishis, and to the Pitris (ancestors) — and the Dharmashastra texts add the ṛṇa to humanity. The performance of Kartavya discharges these debts. Where Kartavya fails, Daṇḍa (punishment) operates: the Mahabharata's Daṇḍanīti (Śāntiparva 59) is the enforcement arm of the same cosmic order — "the protector of the whole universe… diligently punishing the wicked and extending grace to the righteous."

6. The living legacy. The Supreme Court of India's motto, "यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः" (Yato Dharmastato Jayah — where there is Dharma, there is Victory), is drawn from the Mahabharata (13.153.39) and stands above the courtroom door precisely because the Court, like Kautilya's king, is the guarantor of Dharma. The powers under Articles 32, 136 and 142 are intended to uphold that ultimate standard. And the Preamble's collective resolve — "We, the People of India" — is best read as a community of duties: "the aims and objectives enshrined in the Preamble reflect our collective responsibility; this is the true essence of 'We, the People of India'" (Seema Singh).

Conclusion. Ṛta supplies the substantive Grundnorm; Kartavya supplies the human obligation; Daṇḍanīti supplies the sanction; and Rājadharma subjects the state itself to the law. This is a complete jurisprudence — and it is a jurisprudence of duties, in which rights are but the flowers of obligations fulfilled.

Q.2  "Explain Kautilya's concept of the State and Law as expounded in the Arthashastra, and assess its relevance in the contemporary world." 20 marks · 950 words

Introduction. The Arthashastra — "the science of political economy and governance," attributed to Kautilya (Chanakya), the mentor of Chandragupta Maurya — is structured in 15 Books and about 150 chapters, and stands with the Dharmashastras as the classical Indian text of Rājadharma. Its jurisprudential heart is the claim that law is dharma in action: the king is the instrument of the law, not its author.

1. The dharma-based framework. Kautilya's legal system is grounded in the Saptāṅga (seven limbs of the state): Svāmin (king), Amātya (ministers), Janapada (territory/population), Durga (fort), Kosha (treasury), Daṇḍa (army), Mitra (ally) — "the king is just one of the limbs," and the state's health is the health of the whole. He explicitly recognises (in Book 3) the primacy of Dharma as the supreme source of law, and endorses the four classic sources of law: Dharma (sacred law), Vyavahāra (evidence and legal procedure), Charitra (custom of traders and guilds), and Rājaśāsana (royal decree) — resolving conflicts upwards, with the royal decree prevailing when the sacred text's derivation cannot be traced. This is a complete theory of sources, one that anticipates modern hierarchy-of-norms doctrine.

2. The state's organs and the administration of justice. The judicial machinery is a hierarchy: (i) the king, the apex and the final court; (ii) Dharmasthiya — courts of the learned (Amātyas well-versed in sacred law), with nominal fines; (iii) Kaṇṭakaśodhana — "removal of thorns" courts for economic offences, official misconduct and public welfare; (iv) Pradvivāka — appellate judges. Kautilya establishes principles modern law would rediscover: "evidence of witness must be depended upon"; "a person is deemed innocent until proved guilty"; witnesses' testimony is preferred to documents; punishment only after conclusive proof; no dismissal of cases on technical pleas (deśa-, kāla-, bhoga-cchala); and a rigorously codified law of perjury (3.11.48-49: fines of 24 and 12 paṇas — modest, to protect the institution of evidence from destruction).

3. Substantive law. Book 3 is a commercial and family code: contracts void for fraud, coercion or secrecy; suretyship; rescission with restitution; fiduciary obligations; force majeure as an excuse; the Superintendent of Commerce and fixed price margins against exploitation; and inheritance through the four-generation piṇḍa (offering) rule, with daughters, adoption and pārkṣā (testing of documents). The Śreṇis (merchant guilds) were legal entities with internal dispute settlement — "their verdicts could not be questioned or challenged in royal courts."

4. Penal theory. Daṇḍanīti must satisfy three requisites — and Kautilya's enumeration of thirteen "anti-social elements", led by corrupt judges ("those who have the power to judge but abuse it"), is a startlingly modern insight. Punishment is graduated (ati-, sama-, nyūna-daṇḍa), subject to the king's discretion, proportionate to the intent of the offender, and calibrated to the offender's status; the four purposes of daṇḍanīti are the maintenance of order, the protection of the weak, the deterrence of crime, and the upholding of justice. Fines are generally preferred to corporal punishment; from the "wealth-destroying" type to the "death" type — but always as the last resort, and always resting on proof.

5. Contemporary relevance. The reading's own verdict is that the Arthashastra's relevance "has not increased but has remained the same": (i) governance — the threefold triad of Dharma (ethics), Nyāya (justice) and Nīti (policy) maps onto constitutional morality, rule of law and public policy; (ii) anti-corruption — audits by the Superintendent of Accounts, penalties up to twenty times the amount misappropriated, rewards to honest officers, and the philosophy of checks and balances anticipate the Lokpal and modern accountability regimes; (iii) law and economics — contract, fiduciary, consumer protection and price regulation sit directly on Kautilya's Book 3; (iv) criminal justice — "innocent until proved guilty," reformative rather than purely retributive punishment, victim compensation where the crime remains unsolved, and restorative sentencing are all there; (v) institutional design — the triadic structure of state, judiciary and economic regulators anticipated the separation of powers; (vi) leadership — the four precepts (vigraha-healing, protecting, supporting, welcoming) and the king's priority to public welfare resonate directly with modern theories of servant leadership. In Book 4 the king is warned: never keep petitioners waiting at the palace gate — nothing could be closer to the modern demand of citizen-centric governance.

Conclusion. Kautilya offers the world's first comprehensive "dharmic legal framework" — a theory of sources, courts, evidence, substantive obligations and proportionate punishment organically united under the supremacy of Dharma. It proves that the Indian tradition is not merely spiritual but rigorously institutional, and that its relevance is not a matter of revival but of continuity.

Q.3  "Trace the origin of the State and the concept of law in the Mahabharata period with reference to the Śāntiparva's two theories of Rājyotpatti." 20 marks · 950 words

Introduction. The Śāntiparva's Rājadharmānuśāsana Parva — Bhishma's discourse to Yudhishthira after the Kurukshetra war — is "a foundational text on ancient Indian statecraft." It answers the question of Rājyotpatti (origin of the state) through two theories: the Divine Origin Theory (ch. 59) and the Dharma-Samvida, the Dharma-based Contract theory (ch. 67). The epic treats Rāja and Rājya as so closely linked that "the state is considered to come into existence only with the advent of the king."

1. The problem — Matsyanyāya. Both theories begin from the same diagnosis: in the absence of authority, "just as in water large fish devour the smaller ones, so too would the strong exploit the weak" (67:16). This is the classic state of nature — arajakatā (anarchy) — which, in the epic's cosmology, occurs at the transition from the Krita (Satya) Yuga of Sattva into the Treta of Rajas and Tamas.

2. Theory I — Divine Origin (ch. 59). Yudhishthira asks how the title of king arose and why one man rules over equals (59:5-8). Bhishma answers: "in the beginning there was neither any kingdom nor a king, no punishment nor anyone to impose it… all the people governed themselves by following Dharma" (59:14-16). But delusion, envy, lust, anger and greed destroyed that order; the gods, fearing for the loss of Vedic wisdom, appealed to Brahma, who "created the system of Daṇḍanīti" — a scripture of one hundred thousand chapters elaborating Dharma, Artha and Kāma (Trivarga, with Moksha as the transcendent fourth). Study of it passed from Shiva to Indra, Vrihaspati and Shukracharya; Vishnu's line produced Vena, who "abandoned Dharma," was expelled and killed by the rishis, and from whose right arm emerged Prithu — who pledged to the rishis to uphold Dharma and Daṇḍanīti, and on that pledge was crowned. The principle: kingship is divine in origin but conditional on virtue — the king is accountable, his orders are valid only when they conform to Dharma, and an unrighteous king may be removed. This is the exact antithesis of the Western Divine Right of Kings (James I), for whom the monarch is accountable to God alone and resistance is sin.

3. Theory II — Dharma-Samvida (ch. 67). Bhishma traces three stages: the ideal pre-political state; the state of nature under matsya-nyāya; and the institution of kingship. The failure of the people's first self-regulating agreement — casting out the violent and the adulterous, to live in mutual confidence — is the crucial narrative point: agreement alone cannot found order. The people then approach Brahma: "We are perishing without a king" (67:21); Manu declines; and the people bargain: one-fiftieth of animals, one-fiftieth of gold, one-tenth of crops, skilled warriors to follow him, and a quarter of the people's merit to the king (67:23-27). On these terms Manu accepts. The theory: the state originates in a contract made under Dharma; the people grant the king only necessary and appropriate powers; taxation is consideration for protection; the ruler must be benevolent, not autocratic.

4. The comparison. Bhishma's state of nature strikingly resembles Hobbes's — the reading acknowledges "much in common" — but differs radically in prescription: for Bhishma the foundation of civilisation lies not in self-interest but in the "mastery and transcendence of internal vices," and the sovereign is not absolute ("obedience to the king's command is only justified as long as it aligns with Dharma"). With Locke the parallel is direct: both reject absolute authority and both recognise a right of resistance against the abuser — the Mahabharata even sanctions the execution of a king who fails to protect his subjects. With Rousseau, the difference is subtler: consent is present, but it is consent under Dharma, and Dharma "transcends both the king and the state."

5. Which prevails? The reading's conclusion: the two theories serve different objectives — ch. 59 restricts royal power; ch. 67 secures royal power. But "from the perspective of ancient Bharatiya political philosophy, the theory of Dharma-based Contract (Dharma-Samvida) should be regarded as the most thorough explanation for the emergence of the state," because it incorporates both the sovereign and the Praja, and locates the state's legitimacy in an agreement that is itself governed by a higher law.

Conclusion. The Mahabharata does not merely describe power; it justifies it. Rājyotpatti is the story of law's victory over the fish's law: the state is born of anarchy, constituted by contract, and bound by Dharma — a theory that anticipates the social contract by two millennia while correcting its absolutist variant.

Q.4  "Elucidate the idea of the State and the Law in Ramarajya as depicted in the Valmiki Ramayana, and show how it answers both Plato and the Divine Right tradition." 20 marks · 950 words

Introduction. Social science conventionally credits Plato's Republic with the first systematic conception of the Ideal State — the philosopher-king ruling by reason. Karl Popper's critique made the same tradition suspect: Plato's utopia was politically unrealistic and has been accused of preparing the ground for totalitarianism. The model of Ramarajya — "the just and welfare-oriented rule of Prabhu Shri Rama" in the Valmiki Ramayana — challenges this monopoly: "neither apolitical nor irrational but fundamentally state-centric," it is a practised, constitutional and duty-bound ideal of governance, "arguably more advanced than that of the West."

1. The textual foundation. Ramarajya appears at three places — Bala Kanda (Narada's six verses), the Yuddha Kanda Canto 128 (the fullest account), and Uttara Kanda (Bharata's recollection). Its features: no widow's sorrow, no fear of wild creatures, no illness; no theft, violence or immorality; all classes devoted to Dharma; long, healthy and prosperous lives; trees ever fruitful, rains timely, breezes gentle — environmental harmony as a test of governance; the four varṇas content and at their duties; a people truthful, moral and dharmic. It is a welfare ideal: sarvajana hita, the happiness of all beings.

2. The state: a limited, semi-elective monarchy. A close reading shows Ayodhya's kingship was not a divine-right autocracy: (i) succession was hereditary in form but subject to political consent — ministers, royal elders and feudal lords had to endorse; (ii) Dasharatha's nomination of Rama as Yuvaraja took effect only after ratification by the royal assembly (Ayodhya 1-2, 2/10) — "the monarch's will… was not final"; (iii) an unfit heir could be bypassed; in default of an heir the brother could succeed — hence semi-elective kingship; (iv) the king was Sabhapati of the Sabha, whose approval "was mandatory for all important state decisions"; (v) the institution of the state itself is told (Uttara Kanda, Agastya) as a response to statelessness — people who "devour each other like fish" (Ayodhya 67/31), a kingdom without a king being "a wilderness" (67/38). The state thus arises from popular demand and popular consent, not from divine delegation.

3. The law: Nyāya-Daṇḍa Vyavasthā. Rama "administered justice according to the established tradition of Rājadharma," sitting daily on the Dharmasana (seat of Dharma); the Rājasabhā functioned as the court of law; sabhasads — ministers, Śāstra-scholars, experts in ethics — assisted the king and served as "the jury of that ancient period." There were no professional lawyers and no court fees: "justice remained impartial and accessible"; the petitioner had the right of immediate access; delay of even a day was a sin. Crucially, "the king acted merely as an agent of the state," administering a Dharma "neither of his own making nor necessarily of his own choice but based upon the Sastras and traditions" — the king as instrument, not author, of law. Punishment followed four forms (Dhik, Vak, Artha, Badh-Daṇḍa) on strict principles: no Danda without a crime; punishment only on deviation from Dharma (Rama–Vali); unjust punishment destroys the king; failure to punish shares the sinner's sin; and before the law all were equal — Rama would punish his own son, and the Nyāya Sabha would not hesitate to give Danda to their own sons.

4. The famous case. The dog's complaint against the Brahmin ascetic Sarvathasiddha shows the system's reach: justice for animals; daily accessible hearing; and when the Śāstra's ordinary rule (no corporeal punishment for a Brahmin) failed, Rama asked the victim for the sentence, and silently consigned the cruel priest to the very abbotship that had ruined the dog in a prior birth — punishment tailored to the offender's karma, in the form of trust. Justice universal, restorative, and invisible if need be.

5. Answering Plato and the Divine Right. Plato's ideal is ruled by a philosopher-king who need not answer to the people; Ramarajya's ruler is answerable to Dharma, to the Sabha and to the people's own evaluation of his conduct ("The people are not portrayed as passive recipients of rule. They observe, evaluate, and react to royal conduct"). The Western Divine Right demands obedience as religious duty; the Ramayana makes any royal order subservient to Dharma, so that the king's divinity — "deities who roam this Earth in human form" (Kishkinda 18/41-42) — is a description of his virtues, not an immunity. Karpatri Maharaj accordingly defines Ramarajya as governance "rooted in the principles of Dharma as prescribed in the scriptures… the will, interests, and consent of the people are given utmost importance."

Conclusion. Ramarajya is "not merely a metaphor for an ideal state; it represents a lived civilizational experience of Dharma" — a constitutional, duty-bound welfare state whose legitimacy rests on Dharma, Nyāya and the people's happiness. Its dictum — that the people worshipped Dharma embodied in the ruler and the ruler worshipped the people — is the exact inversion of despotism, and it "resonates with the modern notion of constitutional morality."

Q.5  "Compare the Bharatiya concept of the origin of the State (Mahabharata & Ramayana) with the Western Social Contract theory." 20 marks · 900 words

Introduction. The social contract tradition — Hobbes (1651), Locke (1689), Rousseau (1762) — explains the state as the product of a covenant among individuals in a state of nature. The readings show that "the roots of such ideas can actually be found much earlier in the Mahabharata," in the theory of Dharma-Samvida (Śāntiparva 67) and the practice of Ramarajya's semi-elective kingship. The comparison must proceed point by point.

1. The starting condition. Both traditions begin in chaos. Hobbes: "war of every one against every one"; life "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Bhishma: matsya-nyāya — "just as in water large fish devour the smaller ones, so too would the strong exploit the weak" (67:16); arajakatā. The symmetry is striking — but the diagnoses differ: for Hobbes the trouble is fear among equal men; for Bhishma it is also a spiritual diagnosis — the loss of Dharma through greed (lobha), attachment (moha) and anger — so that "the foundation of human civilisation… lies not solely in the pursuit of self-interest, but in the mastery and transcendence of internal vices."

2. The mechanism: covenant v. Dharma-Samvida. In Hobbes, men covenant with an absolute sovereign and surrender all rights irrevocably. In Bhishma, the people's first agreement — the self-regulating covenant to expel the violent and the adulterous — fails, and they then contract with a king on explicit terms: one-fiftieth of animals, one-fiftieth of gold, one-tenth of crops, military followership, and a quarter of their merit (67:23-27). The ancient bargain is therefore a two-sided, priced contract: taxes in exchange for protection; the king's authority is limited to "powers that were necessary and appropriate for governing the state." (Compare Locke's limited government — and note the Ramayana's version: the citizens' choice through Vashistha, the Sabha's ratification of the succession, the people's power to evaluate and react to royal conduct.)

3. What the contract is made under. The decisive difference: the ancient contract is made under Dharma, and Dharma "transcends both the king and the state." No Western theorist subjects the contract itself to a higher (legal-moral) law that binds the sovereign: Hobbes's sovereign is above the law; Locke's right of revolution is extra-legal; only Rousseau's general will, ambiguously, stands above the prince. In the Bharatiya frame, the king's orders are invalid if they violate Dharma; the king is accountable; the Mahabharata sanctions rebellion against — even execution of — a king who fails to protect his subjects (an idea Dhyani calls the "implied social compact" of ancient polity).

4. The content of legitimacy: vices and virtues. Western contract theory is a theory of interests (security, property, rights). Bharatiya theory is a theory of obligations and character: the king's legitimacy is his dharmic conduct (Prithu's pledge; Rama's virtues at Ayodhya 2/28-48; the King as Dharmapala, Dandadhara, Praja-palak); the people's counterpart is their Kartavya — taxes, military service, and dharma itself. Ramarajya adds the "one-sixth share of merit" and the Mahabharata's quarter-share to the king: the contract is not merely economic but spiritual.

5. The verdict of the reading. "Ancient Indian texts on polity scarcely enunciated a theory directly akin to the Western concept of the Social Contract… Nevertheless… the evidence of a governmental contract between the people and the ruler" is unmistakable — and it is "a more rational framework than many Western political philosophies, as it prioritises the supremacy of Dharma over the authority of the sovereign." Its superiority is not nostalgia: the modern rule of law, constitutional morality, the accountability of the executive and the welfare state are precisely the institutional content of Dharma-Samvida.

Conclusion. The comparison yields three theses: (i) India had a contract theory before Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau; (ii) it is a qualified contract — limited, priced, and revocable in case of abuse; (iii) it is a dharmic contract — consent does not create the norm, Dharma does. "Where there is Dharma, there is Victory" is its motto: the state wins only by obeying the law it enforces.

Q.6  "Examine Kautilya's courts and procedure with reference to Dharmasthiya, Kaṇṭakaśodhana and the law of evidence, and show how they reflect a dharma-based legal order." 15 marks

Introduction. The Arthashastra contains "one of the earliest and most systematic expositions of legal theory, jurisprudence, and legal procedure" in the world (Pillai). Its order of courts and its rules of proof convert the supremacy of Dharma into an enforceable, judge-administered law.

1. The courts. (i) Dharmasthiya — courts presided over by Amātyas "well-versed in sacred law" for ordinary civil and criminal disputes, distinguished by their nominal fines; (ii) Kaṇṭakaśodhana — literally "removal of thorns," courts for economic offences, corruption and official misconduct, protecting the public from the state's own servants and from market exploitation; (iii) Pradvivāka — appellate judges; (iv) the king as the highest court. Their fidelity to Dharma is the source of their authority: the Dharma–Nyāya–Nīti triad (ethics, justice, policy) is the reading's own summary of the system's architecture.

2. Sources of law and hierarchy. Dharma, Vyavahāra, Charitra and Rājaśāsana — with the rule that where the sacred law's derivation is untraceable, the royal decree prevails. The judge's first duty is therefore to locate the correct source — a "law-finding," not law-making, function: no judge can invent a rule contrary to Dharma. This corresponds precisely to the dharmic theory of §02(a): the ruler commands within, never above, the law.

3. Procedure and evidence. Kautilya's rules are strikingly modern: "evidence of witness must be depended upon"; circumstances in the absence of trustworthy witnesses; "a person is deemed innocent until proved guilty"; testimony preferred to documentary evidence; and moderate perjury fines (24/12 paṇas, 3.11.48-49) — designed to protect witness institutions from destruction, a sophisticated answer to the law-and-economics of evidence. Punishment awaited conclusive proof — the reading itself records the principle "punishment depends upon [conclusive] proof," anticipating the evidentiary threshold doctrine of modern criminal law.

4. Protective justice. The dharma-based character appears most clearly in the suo-moto jurisdiction of the dharmasthiya: cases may be taken up for temples, Brahmins, ascetics, women, minors, the elderly, the sick and orphans; and the judge may not reject a case on procedural pleas. The earliest ancestor of protective standing belongs to Kautilya, not to 20th-century PIL.

Conclusion. Kautilya's courts do not merely implement royal will: they are the institutional face of Dharma — hierarchy of sources, priority to truth in evidence, protection of the vulnerable, and punishment as a calibrated instrument of social order. The system, the reading insists, remains relevant "not only for governance but also for law, justice and public policy."

Q.7  "Critically examine the Divine Origin Theory of the State in the Mahabharata. Is it a theory of divine right or of divine duty?" 15 marks

Introduction. Śāntiparva 59 answers Yudhishthira's question — how does one man rule over his equals? — by tracing kingship to Brahma's creation of Daṇḍanīti and Vishnu's line of kings. Whether this is "divine right" or "divine duty" is the critical question.

1. The theory's structure. Brahma created Daṇḍanīti (a science of 100,000 chapters on Dharma, Artha and Kāma) because "society could not thrive without laws enforced by a ruler"; it was studied by Shiva, Indra, Vrihaspati and Shukracharya; Vishnu's dynasty produced the kings of the earth. This is genuinely a divine-origin account: kingship descends from the gods.

2. But: divinity as a function of virtue. Three textual signals transform it into a theory of divine duty: (a) the lineage itself deposes offenders — Vena, the god-descended king, is expelled and killed by the rishis "through the power of their mantras," and Prithu is crowned only after pledging to uphold Dharma and Daṇḍanīti; (b) the title Kshatriya is earned, not inherited (Kshati = protection from harm); (c) the king is a Devansh — a divine being in human form whose divinity is "based on his virtuous character," who is accountable, and whose orders are invalid against Dharma. Contrast James I's theory: kings are "God's lieutenants," accountable to God alone, and resistance is religious sin.

3. The internal critique of the same chapter. The theory also carries its own limit: Virajas, the first king offered the throne, refused it "seeking moksha" — kingship is a burden assumed for the world, not a privilege enjoyed of the world. Daṇḍanīti's own verse (59:77) makes the king "diligently punish the wicked and extend grace to the righteous" — a mandate, not a licence.

4. The theoretical consequence. Because the divine origin is a divine mandate, the two Śāntiparva theories are complementary: ch. 59 (divine origin) restricts royal power, ch. 67 (contract) justifies it — and on the reading's own ranking, Dharma-Samvida is the more thorough explanation of the state. The divine-origin account thus functions as the moral foundation; the contract theory supplies the political justification; and Dharma supplies the boundary of both.

Conclusion. The Mahabharata's theory is best described as divine duty in the form of divine origin: the king is divine only insofar as he is a dharmic ruler, and he is bound by the very Daṇḍanīti that he wields. It is the emphatic contradiction of the West's Divine Right — kingship by mandate, not by right.

Q.8  "Evaluate the relevance of the ancient Bharatiya jurisprudence of the State (Ṛta, Dharma-Samvida and Rāmarājya) for the modern Indian legal system." 15 marks

Introduction. The four readings converge on a single claim: the ancient Bharatiya theory of state and law is not archaeology but a living constitutional resource. Three sites of relevance can be demonstrated.

1. The constitutional site. The Preamble's "We, the People of India" — read through Seema Singh — is "the true essence" of collective responsibility: the Kartavya model inverts the rights-first paradigm and makes rights the consequences of duties. Fundamental Duties (Art. 51A) are the codified fragment of Kartavya, though Kartavya "transcends them." The Supreme Court's motto, "यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः," and its powers under Articles 32, 136 and 142 institutionalise Ṛta as the Grundnorm of the legal order: "the powers granted under Articles 32, 136 and 142 are intended to uphold it." The ancient judicial code (public trial, bench of judges, no private hearing, no punishment without proof) is the substantive rule of law — the answer to Dicey is older than Dicey.

2. The institutional site. Kautilya supplies the design blueprints: śreṇis as legal entities with unchallengeable internal awards = modern arbitration and the legal personality of associations; suo-moto jurisdiction for the vulnerable = protective standing and PIL; the Superintendent of Accounts' audits and severe anti-corruption penalties = Lokpal-era accountability; regulated commerce, price ceilings and anti-adulteration powers = consumer and competition law; "innocent until proved guilty," victim compensation and reformative punishment = the Code of Criminal Procedure and the reformative theory of sentencing. The Dharma–Nyāya–Nīti triad is constitutional morality, rule of law and public policy in one sequence.

3. The political-ideal site. Rāmarājya supplies the normative horizon: accountability of the ruler to an assembly, consent-based succession, justice accessible without cost or delay, the welfare measure of sarvajana hita — "resonating with the modern notion of constitutional morality, where the legitimacy of authority rests not merely on the power of the state, but on adherence to rule of law and the welfare of the citizenry." It is the Indian corrective to both Plato's utopia and the Divine Right.

Conclusion. The readings' verdict — that the relevance of Kautilya's legal framework "has not increased but has remained the same" — applies to the whole Unit. The jurisprudence of Ṛta-Kartavya, Dharma-Samvida and Rāmarājya is not an antique to be revived by sentiment; it is the original description of what modern Indian constitutionalism is trying to be.

They are the earliest corporate bodies with legal capacity and the ancestor of institutional arbitration — "the very idea of arbitration, right from the very inception… in Kautilya's Arthashastra."
SabhasadThe members of the ancient court — ministers, Śāstra-scholars, experts in ethics and governance — assisting the king in the court of law; "the jury of that ancient period" (Ramayana, Uttara Kanda 1/3). They were not ornamental: the members of the Nyāya Sabha would give Daṇḍa to their own sons where Dharma required.
KāryārthīThe petitioner in Ramarajya's court: entitled to immediate access (Uttara 1/5.5), with no lawyers' fees and no delay. The most touching example: the dog, who refused to speak to anyone but Rama — "I want justice from Rama."
SaptāṅgaKautilya's seven limbs of the state: Svāmin, Amātya, Janapada, Durga, Kosha, Daṇḍa, Mitra (king, ministers, territory-population, fort, treasury, army, ally). The king is only one limb — the state's health is systemic. Ramayana's Saptavarga (Ayodhya 100) is identical: proof of a common canon of statecraft.
ṚṇaThe debt-doctrine of Hindu jurisprudence: duties as debts — to the Devas, the Rishis, the Pitris (and, in some texts, to humanity). Kartavya is the repayment of ṛṇa; hence the refusal of duty is not merely a legal wrong but a cosmic default, and the sanction for default is an-Ṛta (destruction). The conceptual link between Ṛta and the moral economy of obligation.
TrivargaDharma, Artha and Kāma — the three aims elaborated by Brahma's Daṇḍanīti, with Moksha as the transcendent fourth. In Kautilya: dharma (ethics), artha (political economy) and kāma (pleasure) — the triad whose reconciliation is the content of law and the test of good governance ("keeping the balance between these three aims of life").
Rāma's four DaṇḍasDhik (condemnation — Vibhishana), Vak (words — Maricha, Malyavaan), Artha (fine — thieves), Badh (capital — Vali) — plus the other forms of imprisonment, exile and mutilation. Principles: no Danda without crime; no punishment without deviation from Dharma; just punishment elevates the king, unjust destroys him; failure to punish shares the sinner's sin.
Kartavya v. DutyDuty (modern): statutory, office-based, court-enforced, breach remedied. Kartavya (ancient): a natural obligation intrinsic to the cosmic order; breach = violation of Ṛta itself, and its punishment is not a remedy but the collapse of order. Fundamental Duties are the statutory remainder of a duty-based legal culture; Kartavya alone "embodies a more profound, cosmic and indeed divine sense of purpose."
Dasvarga & AshtvargaRama's classical catalogue of kingly vices (Ayodhya 100): ten born of desire (hunting, dice, sleeping by day, censoriousness, overindulgence in sensuality, intoxication, the musical triad, listless wandering — cf. Manu 7.47) and eight born of anger (slander, reckless daring, treachery, jealousy, fault-finding, misuse of wealth, harsh speech, excessive punishment). Private vices, public consequences: a king's discipline is governance.
RājadharmaDuty-of-the-king in the dharmic scheme: protection of the people (praja-sukham), obedience to the Śāstras, impartial justice, and the welfare of all beings as the king's own reward (Arthashastra 1.19.34). Rājadharma is the constant: it binds the king of the Mahabharata, Kautilya's administrator-king, and the Rama of Valmiki alike.

Kaṇṭakaśodhana — "removal of thorns." 11. The king's divinity is a function of virtue; he is accountable and bound by Dharma; James I's king is accountable only to God and the subject must obey. 12. Because order by self-regulation alone cannot survive private vice; the point is that enforceable public authority is a necessary completion of the moral covenant — the state supplies the sanction Dharma needs. 13. To preserve the institution of witnesses from ruin — evidence is the foundation of justice, and punishing perjury excessively destroys the very proof-system (anticipating the modern law-and-economics of litigation). 14. 9.1 How Unit 2 is examined — and how to write it

The three "P"s of a 20-mark answer

  1. Propositions over narration. Examiners reward assertions with text-attribution. Every paragraph should carry at least one quoted principle — "राजा चेन्न भवेल्लोके…" (67:16), "praja-sukham" (1.19.34), "where there is Dharma, there is Victory" (13.153.39), "no Danda without a crime" (Uttara 79/8).
  2. Pairing (the compare question is guaranteed). Unit 2 is written as a conversation with the West: Ṛta–Grundnorm, Dharma–Samvida–Hobbes/Locke, Devansh–Divine Right, Ramarajya–Plato. Learn the four pairs cold; they convert any question into a structure.
  3. Presence. End every answer with the modern relevance (Preamble, Art 51A, Arts 32/136/142, rule of law, PIL, anti-corruption, restorative justice). The DU material insists on it — so does the examiner.

The four must-know quotes (write them exactly)

  1. "यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः" — Mahabharata 13.153.39; Supreme Court motto. (Reading 1)
  2. "जले त्स्याविव भक्ष्यन् दुर्बलं बलित्तरः" — Śāntiparva 67:16: as fish in water devour the weak, so the strong would exploit the weak without a king. (Reading 3)
  3. "कर्तव्यनिष्टं सम्यक् पालनं राज्यं वर्धते" — the righteous discharge of duty is the state's prosperity. (Reading 1, Nyāya Sūtra)
  4. "Praja-sukhe sukham rajnah…" — the happiness of the people is the happiness of the king; the people's welfare is his own. (Arthashastra 1.19.34)

Also ready: "the king acted merely as an agent of the state" (Reading 4); "obedience to the king's command is only justified as long as it aligns with Dharma" (Reading 3); "evidence of witness must be depended upon" (Reading 2).

🏁 The last word

Unit 2 completes the arc that Unit 1 began. You have now followed the Bharatiya idea of law from its cosmic ground (Ṛta) through its human obligation (Kartavya), its institutional form (Kautilya's state and courts), its political origin (Rājyotpatti — from matsya-nyāya to the Dharma-based contract), and its perfected ideal (Rāmarājya, the welfare state of Dharma embodied). The four readings are a single argument: law in the Indian tradition is not the will of the sovereign but the order of the cosmos, and the sovereign is its servant. That is why the modern Indian state's own motto is "यतो धर्मस्ततो जयः" — and why, in any comparison you are asked to write, the Indian thesis is not memory but theory: a Grundnorm with content, a contract under a higher law, a king who is divine only while he is dharmic, and a justice that even a dog can demand. Master the texts, quote the verses, name the comparisons — and write, as the DU material does, with pride and precision.
