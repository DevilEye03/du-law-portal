Core conceptual distinctions

Misappropriation

The accused first obtains possession innocently or neutrally—by finding, mistake, delivery not arising from a trust or another non-theft route—and later converts or uses the property dishonestly. Entrustment is not required.

Criminal breach of trust

The accused initially receives property or dominion because the owner/possessor entrusted it or placed it in a fiduciary/legal relationship. The later dishonest use, disposal or conversion violates a direction of law or express/implied contract.

Cheating

The dishonest or fraudulent intention exists at the inception of the transaction. Deception induces delivery, retention, or an act/omission causing or likely to cause harm. A mere later breach of a genuine contract is ordinarily civil, not cheating.

Ownership versus possession

Misappropriation and CBT focus on dishonest control/conversion. Cheating may involve delivery of property or harm without a traditional custody dispute. Always identify who had possession, dominion, title and the duty to account.

Criminal misappropriation — BNS §§314–315 / IPC §§403–404

BNS §314: dishonest misappropriation or conversion

§314 = another’s movable property + neutral possession + dishonest misappropriation/conversion
  1. Property of another. The property must belong to someone other than the accused in the relevant legal sense. A joint owner’s use is not automatically criminal where the person has a right to use; dishonest appropriation of the entire proceeds or exclusion of the co-owner may change the result.
  2. Movable property. BNS §314 is confined to movable property. Immovable property is outside the section, though other offences or civil remedies may apply.
  3. Possession obtained in a neutral manner. The property may come through finding, mistake, delivery without fiduciary entrustment or another non-theft route. It is not necessary that the accused stole it.
  4. Misappropriation or conversion to own use. Treating another’s property as one’s own—selling, pledging, consuming, using proceeds, or retaining it contrary to the owner’s rights—can suffice.
  5. Dishonesty. There must be intention of wrongful gain/loss. Honest use, protection or reasonable steps to locate the owner do not suffice.

Temporary misappropriation

Explanation 1 expressly provides that dishonest misappropriation for a time only is misappropriation. Temporary return or an intention to restore later is not a complete defence where the intervening conversion was dishonest.

Finder’s property and the moment mens rea arises

Explanation 2 protects a person who finds property not in another’s possession and takes it to protect or restore it. The finder becomes liable when, knowing or having means of discovering the owner, he appropriates it to his own use before making reasonable efforts or allowing a reasonable time for claim. What is reasonable is a question of fact. It is not necessary that the finder know the exact owner if he does not honestly believe it is his own or cannot be found.

Not theft, later misappropriation

A honestly takes property believing it to be his own. After discovering the mistake, he dishonestly keeps it. The initial taking is not theft; the later conduct attracts §314.

Friendly use

A takes a book from a friend’s library believing he has implied permission to read it. No theft. If he later sells it for himself, he may commit misappropriation.

Joint property

One joint owner takes a horse intending only to use it where he has a right to use. No dishonest misappropriation. Selling and appropriating all proceeds may be different.

Government note

A finds a Government promissory note with blank endorsement, knows it belongs to Z, and pledges it for a loan while intending eventual restoration. Temporary dishonest appropriation is still an offence.

§315: property possessed by deceased at death

This connected provision protects the interval after death but before property reaches a legally entitled person. The accused must know that the property was in the deceased’s possession at death; it must not have passed to a legally entitled possessor; and the accused must dishonestly misappropriate or convert it. Punishment is up to three years and fine, extended up to seven years where the offender was the deceased’s clerk or servant.

Punishment and procedure

Under BNS §314, punishment is imprisonment of either description for not less than six months and up to two years, with fine. Under the BNSS Schedule it is non-cognizable, bailable and triable by Any Magistrate. BNS §315 is also non-cognizable and bailable, but triable by a Magistrate First Class.

Criminal breach of trust — BNS §316 / IPC §§405–409

The essence of CBT is not merely that property was lost. It is that a person who received property or dominion in a relationship of trust dishonestly dealt with it contrary to the law or the contract governing that trust.

EntrustmentProperty or dominion placed with accused
Legal relationshipDirection of law or express/implied contract
DishonestyMisappropriate, convert, use, dispose or wilfully suffer
ViolationConduct contrary to mode of discharge
CBTApply status-based punishment

§316(1): statutory ingredients

  1. Entrustment or dominion. The prosecution must show that the accused was entrusted with property or had dominion/control over it. Entrustment can be direct or arise from office, agency, employment, carrier/warehouse custody or contract. Mere ownership or physical access is not enough.
  2. Property or dominion must be linked to the accused. It is not necessary that the accused personally receive physical custody if he exercises legally relevant dominion over the property.
  3. Dishonest misappropriation/conversion/use/disposal. The accused may convert it to his own use, use or dispose of it dishonestly, or wilfully allow another to do so.
  4. Violation of law or legal contract. The use/disposal must breach a direction of law prescribing how the trust is to be discharged, or an express/implied legal contract touching discharge of the trust. Good-faith deviation causing loss may be civil but not criminal CBT.
  5. Mens rea. “Dishonestly” under BNS §2(7) requires intention of wrongful gain or wrongful loss.

Statutory deeming rules: provident fund and ESI

Provident Fund/Family Pension Fund. An employer of an establishment—whether or not exempted under §17 of the Employees’ Provident Funds and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952—who deducts the employee’s contribution from wages for credit to a statutory Provident Fund or Family Pension Fund is deemed to have been entrusted with the deducted amount. Default in paying it into the Fund in violation of law is deemed dishonest use in violation of a legal direction.

Employees’ State Insurance Fund. An employer who deducts the employee’s contribution from wages for credit to the ESI Fund administered by the Employees’ State Insurance Corporation under the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948 is similarly deemed entrusted. Default in payment in violation of the Act is deemed dishonest use contrary to a direction of law. These are statutory deeming rules: the prosecution still has to connect the accused with the employer role, deduction and default.

Entrustment versus dominion

Entrustment

Property is delivered for a particular purpose—storage, carriage, investment, dyeing, collection, payment, safekeeping or management—and the recipient must deal with it according to law/contract.

Dominion

The person may not physically hold the property but has control over its disposition or accounting, as directors had dominion over Government cloth in Jaikrishnadas.

What is not enough

A mere debtor-creditor relationship is not automatically entrustment of money; an ordinary breach of contract or failure to pay is not automatically CBT or cheating.

Failure to account

Failure alone is not conclusive. But failure to account plus false explanation, surrounding circumstances, admissions, absence of property and control may justify an inference of dishonest conversion.

Evidence point from DU case material. The prosecution need not prove the precise mechanics of conversion in every case. It must prove entrustment/dominion and circumstances from which dishonest misappropriation/conversion may legitimately be inferred. But conviction cannot rest on failure to account in a vacuum. The legal burden remains on the prosecution to prove ingredients beyond reasonable doubt; the accused’s silence or inability to explain is not an automatic reverse burden. A false explanation is only one circumstance in the evidentiary chain and must be assessed with entrustment, control, admissions, records, demands and other proved facts.

§317 as the connecting stolen-property provision

BNS §317(1) designates as “stolen property” property whose possession was transferred by theft, extortion, robbery or cheating, and property that has been criminally misappropriated or in respect of which CBT has been committed. It therefore connects the Unit 10 offences: the primary wrong may be §314, §316 or §318, while a later person who dishonestly receives or retains the property knowing or having reason to believe it is stolen may attract §317(2). Section §317 is not a substitute for proving the primary offence and is kept here as a limited bridge, not as an expansion of the syllabus.

Cheating — BNS §§318–319 / IPC §§415–420 and 416/419

§318(1): two alternative branches

Property-delivery branch
Deception + fraudulent/dishonest inducement to deliver property to any person or consent that any person retain property.
Harmful-act branch
Deception + intentional inducement to do/omit an act the person otherwise would not do/omit + damage/likely damage to body, mind, reputation or property.

Ingredients of cheating

  1. Deception of a person. The person may be deceived by words, conduct, false representation or dishonest concealment of facts. The victim must be deceived in the relevant transaction.
  2. Inducement. In the property branch it must be fraudulent or dishonest; in the second branch it must be intentional. There must be a causal connection between deception and delivery/act/omission.
  3. Delivery/retention or harmful conduct. The person deceived must deliver property, consent to retention, or do/omit something causing or likely to cause legally recognised damage/harm.
  4. Damage is not always actual monetary loss. The harmful-act branch includes body, mind, reputation or property and permits likelihood of harm.
  5. Dishonest intention at inception. A promise honestly made but later broken is ordinarily a civil breach. If the accused never intended to perform when he made the promise and used it to obtain property, cheating may be established.

Dishonest concealment

The Explanation makes dishonest concealment of facts a deception. Concealment must be material to the transaction and dishonest; mere silence is not automatically cheating where there is no legal duty or misleading conduct.

Cheating by personation — §319 / IPC §§416, 419

  1. The accused must cheat by pretending to be some other person, knowingly substituting one person for another, or representing himself/another as someone other than the true person.
  2. The personated individual may be real or imaginary; existence of a real victim is not necessary.
  3. Personation is the mode of deception; the underlying cheating must still be proved. A mere false name without inducement or harm is not automatically §319.
  4. Punishment under §319(2) is up to five years, or fine, or both; BNSS classifies it as cognizable and bailable, triable by Any Magistrate.

Detailed DU case law

Jaikrishnadas Manohardas Desai v. State of Bombay

AIR 1960 SC 889 · Supreme Court of India · IPC §409 read with §34 · BNS §316(5) / §3(5) mapping, subject to status proof

Facts

The Textile Commissioner invited tenders in 1948 for dyeing Pugree cloth. Parikh Dyeing and Printing Mills Ltd., of which the first appellant was Managing Director and the second appellant a Director and technical expert, obtained the contract. Approximately 2,51,05¾ yards of Government cloth were supplied for dyeing. About 1,11,000 yards were dyed and returned. The contract was cancelled for the undelivered balance, and the company admitted that approximately 1,29,748 yards remained with it on Government account and had to be delivered.

The company later faced financial difficulties. The Textile Commissioner repeatedly demanded delivery/accounting. The factory and residences were raided; the remaining cloth could not be traced. The appellants suggested that old cloth had been eaten by white ants/moths and thrown away as rubbish, but gave no contemporaneous notice or supporting records. The first appellant continued to visit the factory; the second handled correspondence. The High Court found that the appellants had dominion over the entrusted cloth.

Procedural history

A jury trial resulted in convictions under IPC §409 read with §34. The High Court found a misdirection affecting the jury verdict, reviewed the evidence and nevertheless upheld the conviction, reducing the first appellant’s sentence from five to three years and the second’s from four to one year. The appellants appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues
  1. Whether entrustment/dominion and dishonest misappropriation were proved though the precise mode of conversion was not shown by direct evidence.
  2. Whether failure to return/account for Government cloth could support an inference of dishonest conversion.
  3. Whether the Managing Director could be liable under joint liability although he was not physically present at the moment of removal.
Arguments
  • Appellants: failure to return could create civil liability but not criminal breach of trust; the first appellant had moved to Ahmedabad, was occupied with insolvency proceedings and was not physically present in Bombay when the goods allegedly disappeared. The disappearance could be due to white ants/moths, disposal as rubbish or mortgagees’ possession. The prosecution had no direct proof of the precise conversion.
  • Prosecution/State: the cloth was Government property entrusted for a defined dyeing purpose; the appellants had dominion and admitted the balance. Repeated demands were ignored, the cloth was not found, the white-ant story was unsupported and false, records were not produced, and the course of conduct supported dishonest conversion. Directors participated in the common criminal act; physical presence was not necessary for this non-violent property offence.
Decision

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeals. It held that direct proof of the precise mode of conversion is not indispensable. Entrustment and failure to account, viewed with other circumstances, may justify an inference of dishonest misappropriation/conversion. Mere failure to account is not automatically enough, but an untrue explanation and surrounding circumstances can make the inference compelling. The Court also held that §34 IPC did not require physical presence in every offence: participation in the criminal act may be through acts done at different times and places; the necessary elements were common intention and participation in the action, not mere planning.

Principles evolved: (1) Entrustment/dominion is the foundation of CBT. (2) The prosecution need not prove the exact mechanics of misappropriation where circumstantial evidence establishes dishonest conversion. (3) Failure to account plus a false explanation and corroborating circumstances may support conviction, but failure alone is not conclusive. (4) Joint liability does not invariably require physical presence; it requires participation in a criminal act in furtherance of common intention.

Hypothetical memory diagram. It does not replace proof of entrustment and dishonest circumstances.

BNS application: Begin with §316(1). Apply §316(5) only if the person’s specified status—public servant, banker, merchant, factor, broker, attorney or agent—fits the statutory language. BNS §3(5) is the common-intention bridge.

Mahadeo Prasad v. State of West Bengal

AIR 1954 SC 724 · Supreme Court of India · IPC §420 · BNS §318(4)

Facts

Mahadeo Prasad agreed to buy 25 ingots of tin from Dulichand Kheria at a price payable cash against delivery. The complainant had 14 ingots and purchased 11 more so that the entire quantity could be supplied. The goods were delivered at the appellant’s guddi. Mahadeo took delivery, kept the Jamadar waiting and left without paying. The complainant had parted with the ingots because of the promise of cash payment.

Evidence showed that Mahadeo’s bank overdraft was already close to its limit; on the date of delivery he had only a small margin and no evidence of expected funds sufficient to pay. He had also hypothecated 70 ingots to the bank. The defence claimed that the transaction was on credit, that the promise of cash was invented and that Mahadeo later went to negotiate a settlement.

Procedural history

The Additional Chief Presidency Magistrate convicted him under IPC §420 and sentenced him to one year’s rigorous imprisonment. The Calcutta High Court affirmed. He appealed by special leave.

Issue

Whether failure to pay after receiving delivery was merely a civil breach of a sale contract, or whether Mahadeo had no intention to pay at the time of the promise and used that false promise to induce delivery—thereby committing cheating and dishonest inducement of delivery.

Arguments
  • Appellant: non-payment ordinarily creates civil liability; he may have miscalculated his capacity or intended to pay later; the declining tin market made the seller anxious to sell; the bill mentioned interest for non-payment, showing a credit arrangement; his later attempt to settle showed absence of fraudulent intention.
  • Prosecution/State: the seller was a stranger and agreed only to cash against delivery; Mahadeo knew his financial position and had no realistic funds; his conduct in taking goods and leaving without payment, bank hypothecation and surrounding circumstances showed that he never intended to pay and made the promise solely to obtain delivery.
Decision

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and upheld the conviction. If Mahadeo intended to pay when he promised cash and later failed, the case would be civil. But the evidence established that he had no intention to pay at the time of the promise; the promise was the deception/inducement that caused delivery. His later settlement effort did not erase the initial fraudulent intention.

Principle evolved: The decisive test for cheating is intention at the time of inducement. Subsequent failure to perform a genuine promise is not, by itself, cheating; a promise made without intention to perform to obtain property is cheating under IPC §420/BNS §318(4). Civil liability and criminal cheating can coexist where the inception facts establish deception.

Hypothetical exam visual: examine the mind at the time of promise, not merely the later non-payment.

BNS application: The serious property-delivery provision is §318(4): cheating plus dishonest inducement to deliver property. Ordinary §318(2) is not the correct maximum-punishment provision when delivery is proved.

Akhil Kishore Ram v. Emperor

AIR 1938 Pat 185 · High Court of Judicature at Patna · IPC §§415/420 · BNS §§318(1)/(4)

Facts

Akhil Kishore Ram operated a business selling charms and incantations, using thirteen aliases and advertising across several provinces. Six transactions were charged. An advertisement for “Gupta Mantra” promised that objects otherwise requiring lakhs could be achieved by repeating the mantra seven times; it stated that there was no hardship or preparation and offered a reward if the mantra proved fallible. Purchasers paid approximately ₹270 by value-payable post and received printed instructions: repeat the mantra, stare at the moon for fifteen minutes without blinking, maintain a special diet, bathe and keep the process secret.

The prosecution case was not that the Court had to decide whether magic could work. It was that the advertisement positively represented no hardship or preparation was needed, while the undisclosed instructions imposed a difficult condition. The victims said that if they had known the condition they would not have ordered the material. The trial and Sessions Courts convicted him on all charges.

Procedural history

The petitioner was convicted on six charges, tried in two batches, and sentenced to eighteen months’ rigorous imprisonment in each trial, concurrently, with fines. Appeals to the Sessions Judge failed. He applied to the Patna High Court, challenging both the existence of cheating and the severity of sentence.

Issue
  1. Whether the accused committed cheating merely because purchasers were disappointed and the Court did not share belief in incantations.
  2. Whether the false assurance about no hardship/preparation and concealment of the condition constituted deception and dishonest inducement.
  3. Whether the sentences were excessive.
Arguments
  • Petitioner/defence: charms and incantations were accepted by many in India and could be genuinely believed in; the prosecution did not prove that the mantras were ineffective or that he knew them to be useless. The Court could not convict merely because it did not share the religious/material belief. Readers should have expected instructions; moon-gazing was not necessarily impossible. At most this was a technical or civil dispute, and sentence was excessive.
  • Prosecution/State: the specific assurance that no hardship or preparation was needed was false or materially misleading; the actual instructions imposed a difficult condition which purchasers said would have changed their decision. The wide advertising, aliases, secrecy condition, reward promise and scale of business showed a calculated scheme to induce payment.
Decision

The Patna High Court dismissed the applications and upheld conviction. It held that the issue was not whether the mantra was genuinely effective but whether the advertisement deceived purchasers about a material condition and induced them to send money. The undisclosed moon-gazing/preparation requirement contradicted the assurance on which the victims acted. The scale, aliases and design of the business supported the inference of dishonest intention. The sentences were not excessive in the circumstances.

Principle evolved: Cheating may be established by a materially false representation or dishonest concealment about the conditions of a transaction. The prosecution need not prove the metaphysical inefficacy of a product when it proves that a specific representation about how it could be used was deceptive and induced payment. Good-faith belief is a factual defence only if it genuinely negatives deception/dishonesty.

Hypothetical visual of the Court’s reasoning: the issue was a false material assurance, not whether the Court believed in magic.

BNS application: Use §318(1) for the ingredients and §318(4) where the deception dishonestly induced delivery of money/property.

Q4(b)/2019 — Distinguish criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust

Question
Explain the difference between criminal misappropriation and criminal breach of trust.

Model answer

Introduction. Both offences involve dishonest dealing with another’s property. The decisive distinction is the manner in which the accused originally obtained possession. Criminal misappropriation under BNS §314 (IPC §403) does not require entrustment. Criminal breach of trust under BNS §316 (IPC §405) requires entrustment of property or dominion over it and a dishonest violation of the law or contract governing the trust.

PointCriminal misappropriation — BNS §314 / IPC §403Criminal breach of trust — BNS §316 / IPC §405
Original possessionProperty comes into accused’s hands neutrally—finding, mistake, innocent receipt or another non-fiduciary route.Property or dominion is entrusted or placed under accused’s control for a purpose.
EntrustmentNot necessary; its absence is what separates the offence.Essential ingredient.
Subject matterMovable property under §314.“Property” or dominion over property; not restricted by the same express movable-property wording.
Prohibited conductDishonestly misappropriates or converts to own use.Dishonestly misappropriates/converts, uses/disposes, or wilfully suffers another to do so.
Norm violatedDishonest conversion itself.Direction of law or express/implied legal contract governing discharge of trust.
Mens reaDishonesty—intention to cause wrongful gain/loss.Same dishonesty, but attached to the trust relationship and its violation.
IllustrationFinder honestly takes a ring to protect it, then sells it after discovering the owner can be found.Warehouse keeper sells goods entrusted for storage; agent uses remitted money contrary to investment direction.
Punishment/procedure§314: 6 months–2 years + fine; non-cognizable, bailable.§316(2): up to 5 years/fine/both; cognizable, non-bailable; enhanced punishments for special statuses.
IPC§403.Definition §405; punishment §§406–409.

Conclusion. A dishonest conversion after neutral acquisition is criminal misappropriation. The same kind of dishonest conversion after entrustment/dominion and in violation of the governing law/contract is criminal breach of trust. Thus, entrustment is the dividing line. In an answer, mention that the uploaded exam compilation’s “IPC §413” reference is a typo; the correct IPC equivalent is §403.

Cross-unit authority

Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar — cheating and attempt

The DU exam material discusses this case in the attempt unit. It is useful when a Unit 10 problem asks whether cheating was completed or merely attempted.

Abhayanand applied to sit for the M.A. English examination as a private candidate, falsely claiming to be a graduate and teacher and submitting forged supporting certificates. The University issued permission/admission card, but information reached it and the card was withdrawn before he could sit. He argued that his acts were only preparation and that he had not obtained the final property/benefit. The Supreme Court held that he had crossed preparation: he intended cheating, completed preparation and performed an act towards commission; the admission card was property and the deception was aimed at causing the University to deliver it/permit him to sit. He was convicted for attempt to cheat under IPC §420 read with §511 (BNS mapping in the uploaded material: §318(4) read with §62).

Exam principle. Attempt to cheat may be complete before actual delivery when the accused has the required intention and performs an act towards commission beyond mere preparation. Always identify the exact point at which the law intervened.

Answer method

Exam-ready issue prompts for unseen Unit 10 problems

If property was found

Ask: Was it in another’s possession? Did accused initially take to protect/restore? When did he learn/should he discover the owner? Was there dishonest conversion before reasonable efforts/time?

If property was entrusted

Ask: Who entrusted? For what purpose? Did accused have possession or dominion? What legal direction/contract governed? What act shows dishonest use/disposal/conversion?

If a promise was broken

Ask: What was accused’s intention when promise was made? Was there deception at inception? Was property delivered because of it? If no, classify as civil breach.

If identity was false

Prove ordinary cheating first, then ask whether the deception was by personation. A false name alone is not enough without inducement or harm.

Ready-made answer frameworks

Criminal misappropriation checklist

  1. State BNS §314/IPC §403.
  2. Identify property as movable and belonging to another.
  3. Explain neutral/innocent acquisition; no entrustment.
  4. Locate later dishonest conversion or misappropriation.
  5. Test wrongful gain/loss under dishonesty.
  6. Apply temporary misappropriation/finder’s property.
  7. Consider §315 if property belonged to a deceased person at death.
  8. State punishment and procedural classification.

CBT checklist

  1. State §316(1)/IPC §405 definition.
  2. Prove entrustment or dominion.
  3. Identify the law/contract prescribing mode of discharge.
  4. Identify dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use, disposal or wilful suffering.
  5. Separate failure to account from dishonest conversion; use surrounding circumstances.
  6. Test special status under §316(3), (4) or (5).
  7. Apply joint liability only where participation/common intention is proved.

Cheating checklist

  1. State §318(1) and identify the correct branch.
  2. Prove deception, including dishonest concealment.
  3. Prove fraudulent/dishonest or intentional inducement.
  4. Show delivery/retention or harmful act/omission.
  5. Place dishonest intention at inception.
  6. Classify §318(2), (3) or serious property-delivery §318(4).
  7. If identity is false, add §319 and prove personation.
  8. If stopped before completion, analyse attempt under §62 with Abhayanand Mishra.

Last-minute revision sheet

§314 MISAPPROPRIATION

Neutral possession + later dishonest conversion. No entrustment. Movable property. Temporary conversion counts.

§316 CBT

Entrustment/dominion + dishonest breach. Violate legal direction or contract. Special status increases punishment.

§318 CHEATING

Deception at inception + inducement + delivery/harm. Later breach alone is not cheating.

JAikrishnadas

Failure to account is not alone conclusive, but false explanation and circumstances can prove conversion; physical presence not always required for common intention.

Mahadeo Prasad

Cash promise made without intention to pay = cheating; genuine promise followed by failure = civil breach.

Akhil Kishore Ram

Material false assurance/concealed condition can deceive even if seller claims belief in the product.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Using IPC §413 as the equivalent of BNS §314. The correct equivalent is IPC §403.
  • Assuming every failure to return property is CBT; entrustment and dishonest breach must be proved.
  • Assuming every broken promise is cheating; intention at inception is the central test.
  • Failing to distinguish possession, dominion, ownership and title.
  • Ignoring BNS §315’s special deceased-property provision within the statutory range.
  • Writing only “deception” for cheating without explaining inducement and delivery/harm.
  • Invoking §318(4) without proving delivery of property/valuable security or the signed/sealed instrument.
  • Assuming personation is complete merely because a false name was used; prove cheating by the false identity.
  • Ignoring special status-based CBT provisions under §316(3)–(5).
