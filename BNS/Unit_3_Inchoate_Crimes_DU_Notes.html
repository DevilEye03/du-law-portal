0.1  What is an "inchoate" crime?

The DU course material itself (General Introduction) defines the idea :

"Then again, in some cases a crime is constituted, although the actus reus has not consummated and no injury has resulted to any person. Such cases are known as inchoate crimes, like attempt, abetment or conspiracy." — General Introduction, DU Course Material, Law of Crimes–I

Inchoate (Latin incohare, "to begin") crimes are offences of anticipation — the law intervenes before the intended harm occurs. A person who instigates another to commit an offence, agrees with others to commit it, or makes an attempt to commit it, is punishable even though the offence is never completed and no injury results. The criminality lies not in the result but in the dangerous resolve expressed in outward conduct. This single idea threads through the whole of BNS Chapter IV (§§ 45–62) and is the conceptual key to this unit.

The Inchoate-Crimes Framework — Stages of Crime & the Logic of Early Intervention

Why the law punishes what has not yet happened; the four stages of a crime; and how abetment, conspiracy and attempt each interrupt the causal story at a different point.

1.1  The doctrinal problem: punishing the incomplete

Classical criminal liability is built on the marriage of mens rea (the guilty mind) and actus reus (the guilty act), producing the conduct-result harm that the offence definition demands — death for murder, dishonest taking for theft. But criminal law would be a poor guardian of public peace if it could act only after the corpse falls. Three doctrines therefore extend liability backwards along the timeline of crime:

  • Abetment (BNS §§ 45–60 / IPC §§ 107–120) — liability for participation in another person's crime through instigation, conspiracy or aid. The abettor's guilt is his own; the principal's guilt is not a condition of it.
  • Criminal conspiracy (BNS § 61 / IPC §§ 120-A, 120-B) — liability for the agreement itself. Where the object is a serious offence, the crime is complete the moment the minds meet — consensus facit legem of the darkest kind. Nothing further need be done.
  • Attempt (BNS §§ 62, 109–110, 226 / IPC §§ 511, 307–309) — liability for one's own incomplete execution: the offender has crossed the Rubicon between preparing and perpetrating.

The DU course material explains why the law reaches this far back, in language you can quote in the exam :

"There are four stages in the commission of crime — (i) intention to commit an offence, (ii) preparation, (iii) attempt and (iv) forbidden consequence ensuing from the act of the accused after the stage of preparation is over. An attempt is direct movement towards the commission of an offence after the preparation is made. An accused is liable for attempting to commit an offence even if the forbidden consequence does not ensue for reasons beyond his control and he is to be punished for creating alarm and scare in the society." — Unit 9 (Attempt), DU Course Material, Law of Crimes–I (2023 edn.)

That last clause is the classic social-danger rationale: the attempter (and a fortiori the instigator and the conspirator) has demonstrated a settled criminal resolve; the harm he threatens is the harm he intended; the only reason it did not mature is luck or police intervention. Law that waited for the "result" would be law that gambles with the public's safety.

Abetment — BNS §§ 45–60 ↔ IPC §§ 107–120

The definition and the three modes; who is an abettor; extraterritorial abetment; the liability rules when the abetted person deviates; and the graded punishments. With the full web of Explanations and illustrations from the statute.

2.1  Definition of abetment — BNS § 45 / IPC § 107

BNS § 45 / IPC § 107 — Abetment of a thing

A person abets the doing of a thing, who —

(First) — Instigates any person to do that thing; or

(Secondly) — Engages with one or more other person or persons in any conspiracy for the doing of that thing, if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy, and in order to the doing of that thing; or

(Thirdly) — Intentionally aids, by any act or illegal omission, the doing of that thing.

Explanation 1. A person who, by wilful misrepresentation, or by wilful concealment of a material fact which he is bound to disclose, voluntarily causes or procures, or attempts to cause or procure, a thing to be done, is said to instigate the doing of that thing.

Illustration. A, a public officer, is authorised by a warrant from a Court to apprehend Z. B, knowing that fact and also that C is not Z, wilfully represents to A that C is Z, and thereby intentionally causes A to apprehend C. Here B abets by instigation the apprehension of C.

Explanation 2. Whoever, either prior to or at the time of the commission of an act, does anything in order to facilitate the commission of that act, and thereby facilitates the commission thereof, is said to aid the doing of that act.

So abetment has exactly three doors — I·C·A: Instigation, Conspiracy, intentional Aid. Note the asymmetry of the three limbs:

  • Instigation needs no result at all — the very act of goading (even an attempt to cause the thing by misrepresentation, per Expl. 1) is the abetment.
  • Conspiracy-abetment (the second limb) needs an act or illegal omission in pursuance of the conspiracy — a smaller requirement than the stand-alone offence of s. 61/120-A, where only agreements to do a legal act by illegal means need an overt act. An agreement to commit an offence is criminal conspiracy without more, but it still needs that pursuant act to amount to abetment by conspiracy of the thing conspired.
  • Aiding requires intentional facilitation — the aid may be given before or at the time of the act (Expl. 2), and presence at the scene is not itself aiding (but see s. 54/114 for constructive presence below).

2.1.1  Abetment by instigation — the heart of s. 306/108 cases

"Instigation" is not defined in the section beyond Expl. 1; the courts have filled it in. To instigate is to goad, urge forward, provoke, incite or encourage the doing of an act — active suggestion, not passive knowledge or even silent consent. The Supreme Court's working tests:

"In order to bring out the offence of abetment [under s. 306 IPC], it is necessary to show that the accused had instigated the deceased… goading or suggestion… the word 'instigate'… means to goad or urge forward or to provoke, incite, encourage or stir up." — distilled from Gian Kaur and applied in Satvir Singh, AIR 2001 SC 2828
  • A retort is not instigation. If the deceased threatens "I will kill myself if you refuse my demand", and the accused answers "go ahead" in annoyance, that is no instigation — otherwise "the parents would be guilty of abetment of suicide if… most unreasonable demand is made by their child… on the threat of committing suicide and they were to retort that the child could do so" (Satvir Singh).
  • Harassment alone is not instigation. Mere cruelty, quarrels or dowry demands that do not proximately and positively push the victim towards suicide will not do — there must be a positive act proximate to the death (the Supreme Court's repeated post-Gian Kaur position, applied to the facts in Satvir Singh).
  • Continuing instigation. A course of conduct can amount to instigation if it is calculated to and does in fact create the determination to die; a single helpless suggestion by a spouse/parent may still be instigation if it is the operative goad (Gian Kaur facts, § 3.4 below).
  • Instigation by misrepresentation/omission. Expl. 1 makes even an attempt to procure the act by wilful misrepresentation or wilful non-disclosure a complete instigation — the classic example being one who falsely tells a creditor "X has no court protection; attach his goods" and sets the law in motion against him.

2.1.2  Abetment by conspiracy (second limb)

Where two or more agree that an offence shall be committed and an act or illegal omission occurs in pursuance of that agreement, each is an abettor of the offence — even of parts he did not personally execute. Illustration: A and B conspire that B shall poison Z; in pursuance of the conspiracy A procures and delivers the poison; B administers it. B is guilty of murder; A is guilty of abetting it by conspiracy and suffers murder's punishment (s. 49 illus. (b)/IPC 109 illus.). The abettor need not even know the principal: Expl. 5 to s. 46/108 — it is enough that he engages in the conspiracy in pursuance of which the offence is committed.

2.1.3  Abetment by intentional aid (third limb)

Aiding = intentionally facilitating. The classic illustrations: keeping watch while a theft is done; supplying the ladder; delivering the keys; wilfully standing aside so that the offence succeeds (illegal omission — e.g. a gate-keeper whose duty was to shut the gate). Two limits deserve emphasis:

  • The aid must be intentional — facilitation must be prior to or at the time of the act (Expl. 2). Help rendered after the offence (harbouring, disposal) is not abetment of the offence; it may be a separate offence (e.g. ss. 58–60 concealment; harbouring provisions).
  • Innocent or unknowing aid is not abetment — one who unknowingly (or without the guilty intention) facilitates is not an abettor; conversely, one may aid an act believing it criminal when it is not (see s. 56/116 illus. (a)).

2.2  Who is an "abettor" — BNS § 46 / IPC § 108

BNS § 46 / IPC § 108 — Abettor

A person abets an offence, who abets either the commission of an offence, or the commission of an act which would be an offence, if committed by a person capable by law of committing an offence with the same intention or knowledge as that of the abettor.

Explanation 1. The abetment of the illegal omission of an act may amount to an offence although the abettor may not himself be bound to do that act.

Explanation 2. To constitute the offence of abetment it is not necessary that the act abetted should be committed, or that the effect requisite to constitute the offence should be caused. Illustrations: (a) A instigates B to murder C. B refuses. A is guilty of abetting B to commit murder. (b) A instigates B to murder D. B, in pursuance of the instigation, stabs D. D recovers. A is guilty of instigating B to commit murder.

Explanation 3. It is not necessary that the person abetted should be capable by law of committing an offence, or that he should have the same guilty intention or knowledge as that of the abettor, or any guilty intention or knowledge. Illustrations: (a) A, with a guilty intention, abets a child or a person of unsound mind… (b) A, with the intention of murdering Z, instigates B, a child under seven… B does the act causing Z's death — A is punishable as for murder; (c) A instigates B, of unsound mind, to set fire to a house — B commits no offence, A is guilty of abetting; (d) A induces B in good faith to believe property is A's — B takes it without dishonesty; A is guilty of abetting theft though B commits none.

Explanation 4. The abetment of an offence being an offence, the abetment of such abetment is also an offence. Illustration: A instigates B to instigate C to murder Z. B does so; C murders Z. Both A and B are liable as for murder.

Explanation 5. It is not necessary to the commission of the offence of abetment by conspiracy that the abettor should concert the offence with the person who commits it; it is sufficient if he engages in the conspiracy in pursuance of which the offence is committed.

Read together, the five Explanations contain the entire sociology of the accomplice, and exam questions live in them:

2.3  Extraterritorial abetment — BNS §§ 47–48 / IPC § 108-A

BNS § 47 / IPC § 108-A — Abetment in India of offences outside India

A person abets an offence within the meaning of this Sanhita who, in India, abets the commission of any act without and beyond India which would constitute an offence if committed in India.

Illustration. A, in India, instigates B, a foreigner in country X, to commit a murder in that country. A is guilty of abetting murder.

BNS § 48 — Abetment outside India for offence in India NEW IN BNS

A person abets an offence within the meaning of this Sanhita who, without and beyond India, abets the commission of any act in India which would constitute an offence if committed in India.

Illustration. A, in country X, instigates B to commit a murder in India. A is guilty of abetting murder.

The IPC punished only the outbound direction — instigating from India an offence abroad (s. 108-A). The BNS adds the inbound direction — the handler abroad who runs an offence in India through instigation, conspiracy or aid is now an abettor under Indian law. This pairs with BNS s. 1(5) (extended territoriality: acts targeting computer resources in India etc.) and modern cross-border crime — terror networks, online radicalisation, romance-scam call centres.

2.4  The liability rules — when the person abetted deviates: BNS §§ 49–54 / IPC §§ 109–114

These six sections are a statutory study in causation and fault-allocation between abettor and principal. Learn them as a story.

BNS § 49 / IPC § 109 — Punishment of abetment if act abetted is committed in consequence

Whoever abets any offence shall, if the act abetted is committed in consequence of the abetment, and no express provision is made for its punishment, be punished with the punishment provided for the offence.

Explanation. An act or offence is committed in consequence of abetment when it is committed in consequence of the instigation, or in pursuance of the conspiracy, or with the aid which constitutes the abetment.

BNS § 50 / IPC § 110 — Person abetted does the act with a different intention

Whoever abets the commission of an offence shall, if the person abetted does the act with a different intention or knowledge from that of the abettor, be punished with the punishment provided for the offence which would have been committed if the act had been done with the intention or knowledge of the abettor and with no other.

Point: the abettor is judged by his own mens rea, not the principal's. Instigate a theft; the thief commits a rape — the abettor answers for abetment of theft, not rape.

BNS § 51 / IPC § 111 — When one act abetted and a different act done

When an act is abetted and a different act is done, the abettor is liable for the act done, in the same manner and to the same extent as if he had directly abetted it: Provided the act done was a probable consequence of the abetment, and was committed under the influence of the instigation, or with the aid or in pursuance of the conspiracy.

Illustrations. (a) A instigates a child to poison Z's food; the child mistakenly poisons Y's food — A liable as if he had instigated the poisoning of Y. (b) A instigates B to burn Z's house; B sets fire and commits theft of property — A is not guilty of abetting the theft (distinct act, not a probable consequence). (c) A instigates B and C to break into a house for robbery and arms them; B and C, resisted by Z, murder Z — if the murder was a probable consequence, A is liable for murder.

BNS § 52 / IPC § 112 — Cumulative punishment

If the act for which the abettor is liable under s. 51 is committed in addition to the act abetted and constitutes a distinct offence, the abettor is liable to punishment for each offence. Illustration: A instigates B to resist a distress; B resists and causes grievous hurt — B liable for both; A liable for both if he knew grievous hurt was likely.

BNS § 53 / IPC § 113 — Different effect caused

When an act is abetted with the intention of causing a particular effect, and an act for which the abettor is liable causes a different effect, he is liable for the effect caused, provided he knew the act abetted was likely to cause that effect. Illustration: A instigates B to cause grievous hurt to Z; Z dies — if A knew the hurt was likely to cause death, A is punished as for murder.

BNS § 54 / IPC § 114 — Abettor present when offence is committed

Whenever any person, who if absent would be liable to be punished as an abettor, is present when the act or offence is committed, he shall be deemed to have committed such act or offence.

Constructive presence: presence converts accessory liability into deemed principal liability — the comrade who stands by while the murder he procured is done is treated as its author, not merely its abettor.

2.5  Punishment when the offence is not committed — BNS §§ 55–57 / IPC §§ 115–117

BNS § 55 / IPC § 115 — Abetment of offence punishable with death or life (offence not committed)

Whoever abets the commission of an offence punishable with death or imprisonment for life, shall, if that offence be not committed in consequence of the abetment, be punished with imprisonment of either description up to 7 years + fine; and if any hurt is done in consequence, up to 14 years + fine.

Illustration. A instigates B to murder Z; B refuses — A liable up to 7 years; if hurt be done to Z, up to 14 years.

BNS § 56 / IPC § 116 — Abetment of offence punishable with imprisonment (offence not committed)

Whoever abets an offence punishable with imprisonment shall, if that offence be not committed, be punished with imprisonment up to one-fourth of the longest term for that offence, or fine, or both; if the abettor or the person abetted is a public servant whose duty is to prevent the offence — up to one-half of the longest term.

Illustrations. (a) A instigates B to give false evidence; B does not — A is nevertheless punishable. (b) A, a police officer whose duty is to prevent robbery, abets a robbery — one-half of the longest term. (c) B abets a robbery by A, such a police officer — same enhancement.

BNS § 57 / IPC § 117 — Abetting the commission of an offence by the public or more than ten persons

Whoever abets the commission of an offence by the public generally or by any number or class of persons exceeding ten, shall be punished with imprisonment up to 7 years and fine. Illustration: A affixes a placard instigating a sect of more than ten members to assemble and attack a rival procession.

Sentencing logic : the graver the offence abetted, the heavier the punishment even on failure — 7/14 years for a death-penalty offence's abetment (ss. 55/115), a fraction of the maximum for lesser offences (ss. 56/116), and an absolute figure where mass instigation endangers public peace (ss. 57/117). Public servants who betray their preventive duty are punished double (½ instead of ¼).

2.6  Concealment offences — BNS §§ 58–60 / IPC §§ 118–120

BNS § 58 / IPC § 118 — Concealing design to commit offence punishable with death or life

Whoever intending to facilitate, or knowing it to be likely that he will thereby facilitate, the commission of an offence punishable with death or life, voluntarily conceals, by any act or omission or by the use of encryption or any other information hiding tool, the existence of a design to commit such offence, or makes a representation he knows to be false respecting such design — (a) if the offence be committed: up to 7 years; (b) if not: up to 3 years; + fine.

BNS § 59 / IPC § 119 — Public servant concealing a design he is bound to prevent

Same conduct by a public servant, for an offence it is his duty to prevent — (a) if committed: up to one-half of the offence's maximum; (b) if punishable with death/life: up to 10 years; (c) if not committed: up to one-fourth + fine.

BNS § 60 / IPC § 120 — Concealing design to commit offence punishable with imprisonment

Concealment (by act or illegal omission) of a design to commit an offence punishable with imprisonment — (a) if committed: up to one-fourth; (b) if not: up to one-eighth, of the longest term + fine.

BNS modernisation : §§ 58–59 add "or by the use of encryption or any other information hiding tool" — the first time the penal code names cryptography. Designed for terror financing, dark-web coordination and steganography, where the concealor hides the design (not the evidence) in encrypted channels before the strike. Quote this in any answer touching cyber-enabled inchoate liability.

3.1  The two sections

BNS § 108 / IPC § 306 — Abetment of suicide

If any person commits suicide, whoever abets the commission of such suicide, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

BNS § 107 / IPC § 305 — Abetment of suicide of child or person of unsound mind

If any child, any person of unsound mind, any delirious person or any person in a state of intoxication, commits suicide, whoever abets the commission of such suicide, shall be punished with death or imprisonment for life, or imprisonment for a term not exceeding ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

The Right to Die & the Fate of IPC § 309 — From Rathinam to BNS § 226

The constitutional journey of attempted suicide — the one "attempt" offence the BNS has abolished — through the lens of Gian Kaur (which is DU-prescribed precisely because it decided this question).

4.1  The two sections that travelled together

IPC § 309 (reproduced in Gian Kaur, DU CM) — Attempt to commit suicide

Whoever attempts to commit suicide and does any act towards the commission of such offence, shall be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to one year, or with fine, or with both.

Notice the wording "does any act towards the commission" — the same attempt-language as s. 511/62. The paradox the courts wrestled with: § 306 punishes him who abets a suicide; § 309 punishes him who fails at one; the successful suicide is beyond punishment. If the "right to die" were a fundamental right, § 306 would punish the assistant to a constitutional right, and § 309 would punish the assertion of it. That was exactly the syllogism of P. Rathinam — and exactly what the Constitution Bench demolished in Gian Kaur.

4.3  The dignified-death passage — Gian Kaur's seed for the euthanasia debate

While rejecting a general right to die, the Constitution Bench planted the seed of a qualified right to a dignified death — the passage every later euthanasia judgment begins from:

"The 'right to life' including the right to live with human dignity would mean the existence of such a right up to the end of natural life. This also includes the right to a dignified life up to the point of death including a dignified procedure of death… the 'right to die' with dignity at the end of life is not to be confused or equated with the right to die an unnatural death curtailing the natural span of life. … This category of cases [terminally ill / persistent vegetative state, where the process of natural death has commenced] may fall within the ambit of the 'right to die' with dignity as a part of right to live with dignity… These are not cases of extinguishing life but only of accelerating conclusion of the process of natural death which has already commenced." — Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab (1996) 2 SCC 648, ¶¶ 24–25 (DU CM)

From this grew Common Cause (A Regd. Society) v. Active euthanasia remains an offence (murder / abetment of suicide). The comparative authorities canvassed in Gian Kaur are worth a line each in a long-answer: the UK Suicide Act 1961 s. 2 (suicide decriminalised but complicity punishable up to 14 years); Rodriguez v. B.C. (A.G.) (Canadian SC — sanctity of life as excluding choice in self-inflicted death); Airedale N.H.S. Trust v. Bland (withdrawal of artificial feeding from a PVS patient — "the Rubicon which runs between the care of the living patient and euthanasia"); Compassion in Dying (US 9th Circuit on bans on physician-assisted suicide).

Criminal Conspiracy — BNS § 61 ↔ IPC §§ 120-A & 120-B

The crime that is committed the moment minds meet; the agreement-plus-overt-act proviso; proof by inference under IEA § 10 / BSA § 8; and the conspiracy-abetment-common intention-common object grid — the terrain of Nalini.

5.1  Text and history

BNS § 61 / IPC §§ 120-A & 120-B — Criminal conspiracy and its punishment

61(1) When two or more persons agree with the common object to do, or cause to be done —

(a) an illegal act; or

(b) an act which is not illegal by illegal means,

such an agreement is designated a criminal conspiracy:

Provided that no agreement except an agreement to commit an offence shall amount to a criminal conspiracy unless some act besides the agreement is done by one or more parties to such agreement in pursuance thereof.

Explanation. It is immaterial whether the illegal act is the ultimate object of such agreement, or is merely incidental to that object.

61(2) Whoever is a party to a criminal conspiracy —

(a) to commit an offence punishable with death, imprisonment for life or rigorous imprisonment for a term of two years or upwards, shall, where no express provision is made for its punishment, be punished in the same manner as if he had abetted such offence;

(b) other than a criminal conspiracy to commit an offence punishable as aforesaid, shall be punished with imprisonment up to six months, or fine, or both.

History in one paragraph. At common law, conspiracy was a misdemeanor of agreeing to do an unlawful act or a lawful act by unlawful means; the IPC as enacted (1833 draft/Macaulay; 1860) needed no separate conspiracy offence because abetment's second limb covered it. Experience — political murders, dacoity — showed a gap: agreements that never matured into any overt act escaped. The Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1913 inserted §§ 120-A/120-B, borrowing the common-law idea and the proviso (overt act requirement only where the object is not an offence). The BNS merges the two sections into one, adds "with the common object", and (via s. 111/113 organised crime and s. 61(2)(a)) keeps the abetment-linked punishment scale for grave conspiracies.

5.2  Essentials — the six-point checklist

EssentialExplanation
1. Two or more personsA man cannot conspire alone; husband-and-wife (formerly one legal person) and corporation-officer scenarios have their own case law. If all but one are acquitted or fake identities intervene, the conspiracy collapses for that accused.
2. AgreementThe gist of the offence. Need not be formal or in writing; may be inferred from conduct; may be rolling ("wheel" and "chain" conspiracies) — each conspirator need not know all the others (Expl. 5 to s. 46/108; Nalini).
3. Common object (BNS wording)To do (a) an illegal act (any offence or civil wrong), or (b) a legal act by illegal means. The new words "with the common object" re-emphasise the shared aim — aligning conspiracy language with ss. 3(5)/190(1) (common intention / common object).
4. Overt act provisoWhere the agreed object is an offence, the conspiracy is complete on agreement alone. Where the object is a non-offence illegal act (e.g. civil wrong) or a legal act by illegal means, one overt act in pursuance is needed.
5. Meeting of mindsMere knowledge, presence or parallel conduct is not agreement; but agreement "may be proved by necessary implication" — from utterances, writings, acts, omissions and conduct (Nalini).
6. No need of completion"The parties to such an agreement will be guilty of criminal conspiracy, though the illegal act agreed to be done has not been done" — the crime is consummated by the agreement itself (Nalini, quoting the classic rule).

5.3  The law of proof — how a secret is judicially established

State of Tamil Nadu v. Nalini (the DU-prescribed conspiracy case) states the working rules, each of them quotable:

"The gist of the offence [of criminal conspiracy under s. 120-A IPC] is an agreement to break the law. The parties to such an agreement will be guilty of criminal conspiracy, though the illegal act agreed to be done has not been done. So too, it is not an ingredient of the offence that all the parties should agree to do a single illegal act." — State of Tamil Nadu v. Nalini, AIR 1999 SC 2649 (¶ on the essence of conspiracy)
"[A] conspiracy is always hatched in secrecy and it is impossible to adduce direct evidence of the same. The offence can be only proved largely from the inference drawn from acts or illegal omission committed by the conspirators in pursuance of a common design… The agreement, sine qua non of conspiracy, may be proved either by direct evidence which is rarely available in such cases or it may be inferred from utterances, writings, acts, omissions and conduct of the parties to the conspiracy which is usually done… [T]here is no difference between the mode of proof of the offence of conspiracy and that of any other offence: it can be established by direct evidence or by circumstantial evidence." — State of Tamil Nadu v. Nalini, AIR 1999 SC 2649
BSA § 8 / IEA § 10 — Things said or done by conspirator in reference to common design

Where there is reasonable ground to believe that two or more persons have conspired together to commit an offence or an actionable wrong, anything said, done or written by any one of such persons in reference to their common intention, after the time when such intention was first entertained by any one of them, is a relevant fact against each of the persons believed to be so conspiring — as well for proving the existence of the conspiracy as for showing that any such person was a party to it.

Two features of this rule deserve emphasis: (i) it is an exception to the hearsay rule — the declaration of one conspirator becomes evidence against all; (ii) it operates only after the common intention is first entertained, and only for acts/sayings in reference to the common design (the "onward and contemporaneous" rule). But there is a doctrinal caution: before the prosecution may use s. 10, there must be independent evidence showing reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy existed — s. 10 is a lock on an already-half-opened door, not a crowbar.

Attempt — BNS §§ 62, 109–110, 226 ↔ IPC §§ 511, 307, 308, 309

The preparation–attempt Rubicon; the general attempt provision and its impossible-attempt illustrations; the specific attempt offences; the English tests as adopted in Mohd. Yakub; and the impossibility doctrine.

6.1  Why punish the unsuccessful?

Four rationales, each supported by authority you can cite:

  • Social-danger / alarm rationale (the DU course material's own language): the attempter is punished "for creating alarm and scare in the society" — the community has suffered the fright of the near-miss even where the harm missed.
  • Subjective dangerousness: the attempter has shown the same mens rea as the completed criminal; luck alone divides them ("for reasons beyond his control" — the CM's words). Punishing only success would make punishment a lottery.
  • Prevention: the police may intervene at the last overt act without waiting for the corpse; attempt liability is what makes interception lawful.
  • Reform/deterrence symmetry: the marginal deterrence of attempt liability pulls potential offenders back before the point of no return.

But the criminal law has deliberately refused to punish every step towards every crime — intention and preparation are generally free. The line-drawing is the whole difficulty, and it is the burden of the tests in § 6.3.

6.2  The statutory scheme — general and specific attempts

BNS § 62 / IPC § 511 — Punishment for attempting to commit offences punishable with imprisonment for life or imprisonment

Whoever attempts to commit an offence punishable by this Sanhita with imprisonment for life or imprisonment, or to cause such an offence to be committed, and in such attempt does any act towards the commission of the offence, shall, where no express provision is made by this Sanhita for the punishment of such attempt, be punished with imprisonment of any description provided for the offence, for a term which may extend to one-half of the imprisonment for life or… one-half of the longest term of imprisonment provided for that offence, or with such fine as is provided for the offence, or with both.

Illustration (a). A makes an attempt to steal some jewels by breaking open a box, and finds after so opening the box, that there is no jewel in it. He has done an act towards the commission of theft, and therefore is guilty under this section.

Illustration (b). A makes an attempt to pick the pocket of Z by thrusting his hand into Z's pocket. A fails in the attempt in consequence of Z's having nothing in his pocket. A is guilty under this section.

Notice three things. First, the BNS adds "or to cause such an offence to be committed" — the section now expressly reaches the person who tries to bring about the offence through another (attempted innocent-agent Instigators).

Second, this is a residual provision — "where no express provision is made" — so the specific attempts below displace it; and it does not apply to offences punishable with fine only or death (hence the need for s. 55/115 on the abetment side and for express capital limbs like s. 109(2)).

Third, the two illustrations are the impossible attempts: the empty box and the empty pocket — theft that could not succeed — and the statute still says "guilty". Hold that thought for § 6.5.

THE YAKUB SYNTHESIS (Reddy J., ¶ 32)(1) intention to commit the particular offence; (2) some act done which would necessarily have to be done towards its commission; (3) the act must be proximate to the intended result — "the measure of proximity is not in relation to time and action but in relation to intention": the act must reveal, with reasonable certainty (in conjunction with other facts, not necessarily in isolation), an intention, as distinguished from a mere desire, to commit the offence
"Had the truck been stopped and searched at the very commencement of the journey or even at Shirsat Naka, the discovery of silver ingots might at the worst lead to the inference that the accused had prepared or were preparing… It could be said that the accused were transporting or attempting to transport silver somewhere but it would not necessarily suggest… that the intention was to export the silver. The fact that the truck was driven up to a lonely creek from where the silver could be transferred into a sea-faring vessel was suggestive or indicative, though not conclusive, that the accused wanted to export the silver. But [the] circumstance that all this was done in a clandestine fashion, at dead of night, revealed, with reasonable certainty, the intention of the accused that the silver was to be exported." — Chinnappa Reddy, J., State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub (DU CM) — proximity measured against intention

6.4  Attempt to murder — BNS § 109 / IPC § 307

BNS § 109 / IPC § 307 — Attempt to murder

Whoever does any act with such intention or knowledge, and under such circumstances that, if he by that act caused death, he would be guilty of murder, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description up to 10 years + fine; and if hurt is caused to any person by such act, the offender shall be liable to imprisonment for life or such punishment as is hereinbefore mentioned.

§ 109(2) When any person offending under this section is under sentence of imprisonment for life, he may, if hurt is caused, be punished with death.

Ingredients: (i) an act done with the intention or knowledge such that, had death ensued, the doer would be guilty of murder (the murder-definition mens rea: intention within s. 101/300 cls. first and secondly, or knowledge that death was likely, within cl. thirdly); (ii) death does not ensue; (iii) graded sentence — hurt aggravates to life; the life-convict's attempt with hurt can be capital. The proof of intention is circumstantial: weapon used, part of the body targeted, repetition of blows, prior enmity, absence of provocation — and non-fatal result by pure chance does not dilute the attempt (Om Parkash: series of acts doctrine — even slow starvation is an attempt when death is intended; the act need not be the "last act"). Contrast s. 110/308: the act, if death resulted, would be culpable homicide not amounting to murder (e.g. sudden fight, provocation) — hence the milder 3/7-year scale. The Dec 2015 DU problem (shooting-learner in a crowded street) is answered on this knife-edge: intention → 307/109; only knowledge of death's likelihood → murder "secondly" analysis on the completed death; gross advertent negligence → 304-A/106(1).

6.5  Impossible attempts — the DU short-note favourite

December 2014, Q8(b) — "Impossible attempts". Build the note in four moves:

MoveContent
1. The puzzleThe hand goes into the pocket — the pocket is empty; the box is broken — the box is empty. Can there be an attempt to steal what is not there? Two classical lines.
2. The "objective" lineR. v. McPherson (1857): stealing chattels already stolen — "an attempt must be to do that which, if successful, would amount to the felony charged, but here the attempt never could have succeeded"; witchcraft/effigy example (hurting by spell, burning in effigy) — no attempt, for the means are intrinsically useless. Indian adoption: Asgarali Pradhania v. Emperor, AIR 1933 Cal 893 (DU's 2023 attempt unit) — the "red liquid and powder" that could not cause miscarriage: since failure was due to the accused's own defective means (not to intervention), what he did was mere preparation, no attempt to cause miscarriage.
3. The "subjective" lineStephen (and Kenny): everyone who, believing a state of facts, does an act which would be an attempt if those facts existed, attempts the offence, "although its commission… was impossible on account of… a fact unknown to him". Glanville Williams' pickpocket and the empty safe.
4. The Indian statutory answerBNS § 62 illustrations (a) and (b) / IPC § 511 illustrations (a) and (b) — the empty box and the empty pocket — both expressly "guilty". Factual impossibility is therefore no defence: the act done with the guilty mind is itself the danger the law punishes. (English law reached the same terminus in the era of DPP v. Stonehouse, as noticed in Mohd. Yakub.) What still exculpates is pure legal impossibility (the "act" intended is not in law an offence, e.g. the effigy-spell) — the means are such that no crime was ever on the cards.
Margin line : "The law punishes the danger, not the harvest." An attempt that could not succeed still shows the complete criminal will; only when the will itself aims at a legal nothing does attempt doctrine let go.

7.1  State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub

(1980) 3 SCC 57 : AIR 1980 SC 1111 — Supreme Court (Sarkaria & Chinnappa Reddy JJ.) DU CASE MATERIAL

Facts

On secret information that silver would be transported from Bombay for illegal export, officers of the Central Excise (Superintendent Wagh, Inspector Dharap and staff) kept watch on the night of 14–15 September 1968 at Shirsat Naka on National Highway No. 8. Past midnight they saw Jeep No. MRC-9930 (driven by accused 1, Mohd. Yakub) coming from Bombay followed by Truck No. BMS-796 (driven by accused 2, Shaikh Jamadar Mithubhai, with accused 3, Issak Hasanali Shaikh, as cleaner). Instead of the Bassein road, the vehicles took the fork towards Kaman village and the Ghodbunder creek, and halted near a bridge. The accused began unloading small, heavy bundles from the truck. The officers pounced. Simultaneously, "the sound of the engine of a mechanised sea-craft" was heard from the creek — and the vessel slipped away as the officers rounded up the party. From the jeep, concealed in a shawl, were recovered 15 silver ingots; from under the sawdust in the truck, 24 more (43 ingots in all, 1,312.410 kg, worth about ₹ 8 lakh); 4 ingots had already been unloaded and lay by the footpath leading to the creek. Yakub gave a false name and address, and carried a pistol (unlicensed — a separate Arms Act case), a knife and ₹ 2,133. None of the accused was a silver dealer. They were charged under the Customs Act, 1962 (ss. 135(1)(a), 7, 8, 33, 34), the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947 (ss. 12(1), 23), and the Imports & Exports (Control) Act, 1947 (s. 5) read with the Exports (Control) Order, 1968 — in essence, attempted smuggling (export) of silver out of India.

Course of litigation (the issue in its pure form)

The accused pleaded bare denial: they knew nothing of the silver, had merely been engaged to drive the vehicles, and were driven to the creek by the police. The Trial Magistrate convicted (A-1: 2 years RI + ₹ 2,000; A-2 & A-3: 6 months RI + ₹ 500 each). The Sessions Judge allowed their appeals and acquitted: the facts showed "no more than… 'preparation' for bringing this silver to the creek", and until the silver was actually put on a boat there was no "direct movement towards the commission of the offence"; since preparation to export was not punishable under the Customs Act, the accused went free. The Bombay High Court (1 November 1973) dismissed the State's appeal and upheld the acquittal. Hence the State's appeal to the Supreme Court — on a single, clean question: whether the proved circumstances established, beyond reasonable doubt, an ATTEMPT to export the silver in contravention of law?

Arguments

For the accused (supporting the acquittal): the overt acts stopped at transporting and unloading the ingots near a creek; no ingot had been placed on any vessel; nothing proved a vessel was even waiting; inter-coastal transport was a lawful possibility; therefore at most preparation, which the Customs Act did not punish; acquittal on facts could not be disturbed.

For the State: intention to export was the only rational inference from concealment, the midnight journey to a lonely creek, the unloading at the water's edge, the waiting sea-craft and the false identity; the overt acts had gone far beyond preparation and were "reasonably proximate" to completion; penal anti-smuggling provisions must be construed to suppress the mischief, not to nurse it.

Held — Sarkaria J. (leading judgment)

(i) Standard of proof: the Evidence Act's definition of "proved" (s. 3) demands no absolute certainty — "perfect proof in this imperfect world is seldom to be found"; a prudent-man probability suffices, and circumstantial evidence is as good as any. (ii) What is an attempt: Kenny's "last proximate act… hindered by circumstances beyond his control" is too narrow; attempt defies exact definition and is "a mixed question of law and fact"; there are three stages (intention → preparation → deliberate overt steps); the overt act need not be the penultimate one if it "manifest[s] a clear intention… being reasonably proximate to the consummation"; citing Abhayanand Mishra: "attempt begins where preparation ends"; the two-limb test — intention + an act done towards commission (in the course of committing, though not the penultimate act). (iii) Application: the accused had taken "most of the steps necessary in the course of export by sea" — concealment, night journey, arrival at the shore, unloading begun at the creek with the vessel's engine audible: "beyond the stage of preparation… the only step that remained… was to load it on a sea-craft". (iv) Mischief-rule construction: since the provisions combat smuggling — "an anti-social activity which adversely affects the public revenues… foreign exchange… the financial stability and economy of the country" — a narrow reading of "attempt" "has to be eschewed": the word is "wide enough to take in its fold any one or series of acts committed, beyond the stage of preparation, in moving the contraband goods deliberately to the place of embarkation, such act or acts being reasonably proximate to the completion of the unlawful export". (v) Result: appeal allowed, acquittal set aside; convicted under s. 135(1)(a) Customs Act read with s. 5, Imports & Exports (Control) Act, 1947; sentences: A-1 — 1 year RI + ₹ 2,000 (default 6 months); A-2 & A-3 — 6 months RI + ₹ 500 (default 2 months).

Chinnappa Reddy J. (concurring) — the doctrinal treatise

The concurrence is a guided tour of attempt theory: Eagleton (proximity, acts immediately connected); Gardner v. Akeroyd (the difficulty of immediate-vs-remote); rejection of the last-possible-act principle (the cocked-gun-and-trigger example); Stephen's Digest ("series of acts… if not interrupted"); Davey v. Lee and the Archbold/Turner formulation, with Turner's own retreat in Russell on Crime (steps "sufficient to show, prima facie, the offender's intention"); Reddy J.'s objection to excluding aliunde mens rea evidence from the actus reus inquiry; Haughton v. Smith (Hailsham L.C.'s three propositions); DPP v. Stonehouse; the Indian scheme (ss. 307, 308, 511; "attempt" undefined); Abhayanand Mishra (disapproval of the last-act test); and Malkiat Singh — the paddy truck stopped at Samalkha, 15 miles from the Delhi-Punjab boundary: no attempt, because the acts were "completely harmless" if the accused changed his mind; but that test is fact-specific, else "in every case where an accused is interrupted at the last minute… he may always say that when he was interrupted he was about to change his mind". Then the synthesis (¶ 32, quoted in § 6.3 above): intention + an act that would necessarily have to be done + proximity measured "in relation to intention" — the truck stopped early would have shown transport, not export; the truck at the lonely creek, in clandestine darkness, revealed export "with reasonable certainty".

Principles evolved

  • Attempt is a mixed question of law and fact; no single verbal formula (Kenny's included) suffices.
  • Overt act need NOT be the penultimate act; attempt begins where preparation ends.
  • Proximity is measured against intention revealed, not against time or distance remaining.
  • Anti-smuggling and other mischief-suppressing statutes get a generous, mischief-rule construction of "attempt".
  • Circumstantial evidence proves attempt as well as any other fact (prudent-man standard).

DU-exam hooks

"Attempt begins where preparation ends" (Sarkaria J., quoting Abhayanand Mishra) · "the measure of proximity is not in relation to time and action but in relation to intention" (Reddy J.) · the cocked-gun rejection of the last-act test · the paddy truck vs the silver at the creek — the perfect contrast pair.

7.2  Smt. Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab

(1996) 2 SCC 648 — Constitution Bench (J.S. Verma J.) DU CASE MATERIAL

Facts

The appellants, Gian Kaur and her husband Harbans Singh, were convicted by the Trial Court under s. 306 IPC for abetting the suicide of Kulwant Kaur, and sentenced each to 6 years RI and ₹ 2,000 fine (default: 9 months RI). On appeal, the High Court maintained both convictions but reduced Gian Kaur's sentence alone to 3 years RI. The convicts appealed to the Supreme Court, attacking the conviction on the ground that s. 306 IPC was unconstitutional.

The constitutional reference

The argument ran: in P. Rathinam v. Union of India (1994) 3 SCC 394 a two-Judge Bench had struck down s. 309 IPC (attempt to commit suicide) as violative of Art. 21, holding that the "right to die" is part of the right to live; if so, a person abetting another's suicide is merely "assisting in the enforcement of the fundamental right under Article 21" — so s. 306 must fall with s. 309. The Division Bench, finding this a substantial question of interpretation of Art. 21, referred the matter to a Constitution Bench; the Court appointed the Attorney General and senior counsel Fali S. Nariman and Soli J. Sorabjee as amici curiae — "to decide this ticklish and sensitive issue".

Arguments (in the rich detail the judgment records)

For the appellants (Ujagar Singh; B.S. Malik): both ss. 306 and 309 unconstitutional. Ujagar Singh supported Rathinam's invalidation of s. 309 but only on the Art. 14 ground; Malik urged both Arts. 14 and 21 — the right to die within Art. 21 makes assisted suicide non-punishable, so s. 306 "is unconstitutional for this reason alone".

Attorney General: s. 306 is a distinct offence and can exist independently of s. 309; Rathinam's construction of Art. 21 (right to die) is wrong.

Nariman (amicus): ss. 306 and 309 are independent substantive offences; the desirability of deleting s. 309 is a different thing from its unconstitutionality; the euthanasia debate is irrelevant to validity; Art. 21 "guarantees protection of life and liberty and not its extinction"; s. 309 does not even violate Art. 14 — the sentencing discretion allows compassionate application.

Sorabjee (amicus): s. 306 survives (violates neither Art. 14 nor 21); but s. 309 is unconstitutional — not on Rathinam's Art. 21 reasoning, but under Art. 14, as a "monstrous and barbaric" provision punishing an "unworthy" class — a provision "universally acknowledged" to be punitive cruelty upon a victim of circumstances.

Precedents surveyed

Maruti Shripati Dubal (Bom HC — s. 309 violates Arts. 14 & 21; "right to die" read into Art. 21); State v. Sanjay Kumar Bhatia (Del HC — continuance of s. 309 "an anachronism unworthy of human society", but no constitutional ruling); Chenna Jagadeeswar (AP HC — s. 309 valid; courts can prevent harshness); P. Rathinam itself (right "not to live a forced life"; s. 309 "deserves to be effaced… to humanize our penal laws… attuning… to the global wavelength"); the 42nd Law Commission Report (1971) recommending repeal (with its Manu/mahaprasthana excursus and Fedden's "monstrous procedure" passage); the lapsed 1972 Amendment Bill.

Held (the five holdings)

1. "Right to die" is not in Art. 21. The Rathinam analogy (freedom not to speak / not to associate / not to do business ⟹ right not to live) is "misplaced": those are rights whose negative aspect needs no overt act; suicide "requires positive overt acts" whose genesis cannot lie in the right to life. "Article 21 is a provision guaranteeing protection of life and personal liberty and by no stretch of imagination can extinction of life be read to be included in 'protection of life'." "Right to life is a natural right embodied in Article 21 but suicide is an unnatural termination or extinction of life and, therefore, incompatible and inconsistent with the concept of right to life." "The 'right to die', if any, is inherently inconsistent with the 'right to life' as is 'death' with 'life'." Rathinam overruled on this point.

2. The dignified-death qualification (¶¶ 24–25, quoted in § 4.3 above): right to live with dignity includes a dignified procedure of death; for the terminally ill in whom "the process of natural death has commenced", accelerating that process is not the "unnatural death curtailing the natural span of life" — the seed of passive-euthanasia law later grown in Common Cause (2018).

3. s. 309 does not violate Art. 14 either. Suicide is definable ("intentional taking of one's life"); uneven gravity is met by tailoring sentence — no minimum, imprisonment not compulsory, fine-only possible; courts in practice apply the Probation of Offenders Act / s. 360 CrPC with compassion (the listed cases from Barkat (1934) to Rukhmina Devi (1988)). Desirability of repeal ≠ unconstitutionality (¶ 17: the 42nd LCR's logic "is a different matter and non-sequitur in the context of constitutional validity").

4. s. 306 is valid and independent. "Section 306 enacts a distinct offence which is capable of existence independent of Section 309." Abetment of an attempt to commit suicide is outside s. 306 (punishable, if at all, under s. 309 read with s. 107); other jurisdictions decriminalise the attempt but punish complicity (UK Suicide Act 1961 s. 2(1): aiding/abetting/counselling/procuring — up to 14 years); Rodriguez (Canada), Airedale v. Bland (HL), Compassion in Dying (9th Cir.) — the abettor "abets the extinguishment of life of another person"; the sympathy due to the failed suicide-attempter "do[es] not avail for the benefit of another person assisting in the commission of suicide". Bombay HC's validation of s. 306 in Naresh Marotrao Sakbre noted with approval.

5. Disposal: both ss. 306 and 309 IPC constitutionally valid; Dubal and Rathinam disapproved; Chenna Jagadeeswar approved; appeals remitted to the Division Bench for decision on merits "in accordance with law treating Sections 306 and 309 IPC to be constitutionally valid".

Principles evolved

  • Art. 21 = right to live with human dignity to the end of natural life; it does not include a right to die / to end life.
  • Rathinam overruled: s. 309 IPC was constitutionally valid (until Parliament repealed it in the BNS — the legislative mercy the Court said was a matter for Parliament).
  • s. 306/108 is an autonomous offence: the abettor of suicide is not excused by any consideration of the victim's own (former) criminality.
  • "Instigation" for s. 306 = active suggestion/encouragement — the conception later applied in Satvir Singh and the abetment-of-suicide jurisprudence.
  • Dignified death at the end of natural life read into Art. 21 — foundation of passive euthanasia (Common Cause, 2018).

DU-exam hooks

"Protection of life ≠ extinction of life" · "suicide is an unnatural termination… incompatible… with the concept of right to life" · the Rathinam analogy's flaw (negative aspect of Art. 19 rights vs positive overt acts) · s. 306 independent of s. 309 (validated in fact by the BNS design: 108 kept, 309 repealed) · ¶¶ 24–25 dignified-death passage.

7.3  Satvir Singh & Ors. v. State of Punjab

AIR 2001 SC 2828 : (2001) 8 SCC 633 — Supreme Court (K.T. Thomas & S.N. Variava JJ., 27-9-2001) RESEARCH · judgment reconstructed from the report (listed in DU case material; judgment reconstructed from the report)

Facts

Tejinder Pal Kaur (PW-5), "a young mother of two kids… a double graduate", was married on 15-11-1992 to Satvir Singh (A-1, a businessman); his parents Devinder Singh (A-2) and Paramjit Kaur (A-3) lived in the same house. Though dowry was given, within four-five months of the wedding the family began harassing the bride for not bringing a car and a house — taunting her that she had "brought rags instead of wedding costumes". In November 1995 her father paid ₹ 20,000 to the husband to buy peace; the harassment did not stop. On the night of 16-6-1996, a salad served to Satvir Singh had excessive salt; he "unleashed abuses" on his wife, was "profusely supported by his mother", then "reinforced by his father" — and, on the prosecution's case, "they went to the extent of suggesting to her why not end your life in front of one of the trains as many such trains are running nearby". At about 4 a.m. on 17-6-1996, Tejinder Pal Kaur walked alone to the railway line and lay before an incoming train from Jallandhar. She was devastatedly maimed — left arm lost from the shoulder, spinal cord ruptured, a paraplegic — but she survived, describing herself as "a living corpse". Her testimony, accepted by both courts, was the mainstay of the prosecution.

Course of litigation

The Sessions Court convicted all three appellants under s. 116 read with s. 306 IPC (2½ years RI + ₹ 10,000 each) — reasoning that although the offence of abetment of suicide required a completed suicide, abetting the attempt could be punished through s. 116 (abetment of an offence punishable with imprisonment, if the offence be not committed) — besides s. 498-A (2 years + ₹ 5,000 each). The victim herself cross-appealed for enhancement. The High Court (single Judge) concurred with the findings but substituted s. 304-B (dowry death) for s. 306, still read with s. 116, and raised the sentence to 5 years RI each. The accused appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues

(i) Can s. 306 IPC apply where the woman survives her suicide attempt? (ii) Can s. 116 IPC be tagged to s. 306 to punish "abetment of an attempt" to commit suicide? (iii) Can s. 304-B (dowry death) attach to a case of (attempted) suicide? (iv) What is the role of the s. 113-A Evidence Act presumption, and does harassment amount to "instigation"?

Arguments

For the appellants: s. 306's plain words — "If any person commits suicide" — make a completed suicide the jurisdictional fact; with the wife alive there could be no s. 306, and a fortiori no s. 116 read with 306 (abetment of an abetment); s. 304-B requires death of a woman within seven years of marriage in the stated circumstances — a living "victim" takes it out of the section altogether; the s. 113-A presumption ("may presume") cannot fill the foundational facts, and there was a dearth of evidence of dowry-demand cruelty soon before the attempt.

For the State: the cruelty was of a piece with the suicide attempt; the s. 113-A presumption applied (suicide attempt within seven years of marriage + cruelty shown); if s. 306 fails only because death did not ensue, s. 116 supplies the gap; alternatively s. 304-B read with s. 116 punishes the attempt at a dowry death.

Held (K.T. Thomas J.)

1. s. 306 presupposes a completed suicide. "Section 306 renders the person who abets the commission of suicide punishable for which the condition precedent is that suicide should necessarily have been committed. It is possible to abet the commission of suicide. But nobody would abet a mere attempt to commit suicide. It would be preposterous if law could afford to penalise an abetment to the offence of mere attempt to commit suicide." (The Court noted the "unique legal phenomenon" that attempt to suicide is the only attempt that is itself an offence — s. 309 — while the completed suicide is beyond the law's reach.)

2. No "abetment of abetment". "The learned Sessions Judge went wrong in convicting the appellants under section 116 linked with Section 306 IPC… the crux of the offence under Section 306 itself is abetment… It is inconceivable to have abetment of an abetment. Hence there cannot be an offence under section 116 read with section 306 IPC." (Abetment of an attempt to commit suicide would fall, if at all, under s. 309 read with s. 107.)

3. s. 304-B cannot apply to suicide at all. First, "Section 304-B cannot apply to a case of suicide at all, whether it is sequel to cruelty or harassment with the demand for dowry or not" — dowry death is a homicide-like deemed causation, not a suicide section; secondly, "the concept of abetment of an offence under Section 304-B is inconceivable in the absence of death of a woman within the statutory period".

4. The presumption and "instigation". s. 113-A is discretionary ("the Court may presume"); "it is not proper for the court to make an incriminating presumption against the accused on a very crucial ingredient of the offence… more so when it is quite possible to draw a presumption the other way around as well". On the evidence there was "dearth of evidence" of dowry-linked cruelty soon before the attempt. And on the nature of instigation, the judgment insists on a positive act of goading — a retort to a threat of suicide is not instigation ("if such a retort is accepted to be constituting instigation, then… the parents would be guilty of abetment of suicide if, even when the most unreasonable demand is made by their child… on the threat of committing suicide… they were to retort that the child could do so").

5. Disposal: the conviction and sentence under s. 116 IPC were set aside — whether linked to s. 306 or to s. 304-B. (The 498-A conviction for cruelty was not disturbed by this holding.)

Principles evolved

  • s. 306 IPC (now s. 108 BNS) requires, as a fact precedent, a completed suicide; abetment of attempted suicide is not s. 306.
  • s. 116/56 cannot ride on s. 306 — there is no abetment of an abetment; the correct vehicle, if any, is the attempt-section read with the abetment-definition.
  • s. 304-B/80 (dowry death) is not a suicide provision — it requires death and operates by deeming the husband/relative to have caused it.
  • Harassment, cruel taunts, even a retort to a suicide threat ≠ instigation; the presumption under BSA § 117 / IEA § 113-A is discretionary and will not be drawn on doubtful foundational facts.
  • Where the victim survives, the cruelty provision (s. 498-A / BNS ss. 85–86) — not the suicide provisions — is the operative charge.

DU-exam hooks

"Condition precedent" language · "inconceivable to have abetment of an abetment" · the salt-in-the-salad / "end your life before a train" facts as the perfect problem-hypothesis · the retort-illustration (parents & unreasonable child) · pairing with Gian Kaur: instigation defined (Gian Kaur) + completed suicide required (Satvir).

7.4  State of Tamil Nadu v. Nalini & 25 Others

AIR 1999 SC 2649 : (1999) 5 SCC 253 — Supreme Court (D.P. Wadhwa J., speaking with S.S.M. Quadri & K.T. Thomas JJ.; judgment 11-5-1999) RESEARCH · judgment reconstructed from the report (listed in DU case material; judgment reconstructed from the report)

Facts (the Rajiv Gandhi assassination case)

On 21 May 1991 at Sriperumbudur (Tamil Nadu), a human bomb — Thenmozhi Rajaratnam ("Dhanu"), an LTTE Black Tiger — assassinated former Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi and 17 others at an election rally. The conspiracy, master-minded by LTTE leader V. Prabhakaran and executed through the one-eyed operative Sivarasan ("one-eyed Jack"), had its roots in LTTE resentment at the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord and the deployment of the IPKF (1987–90). The trail — infiltration of the team into India, safe houses in Madras/Pondicherry, video reconnaissance of the venue by Haribabu (who died in the blast and whose camera recorded the final frame), the belt-bomb, and the pre- and post-blast harbouring — was unravelled by the SIT of the CBI. 26 accused, including Nalini (who had befriended the team and accompanied Dhanu to the venue, and in whose flat Sivarasan had sheltered), were tried by the Designated Court (TADA) at Poonamallee. The prosecution alleged five facets of the conspiracy: (1) clandestine infiltration into India; (2) hiring of safe accommodation for the conspirators; (3) unauthorised wireless operation; (4) the assassination itself; (5) harbouring the offenders and destroying evidence. The Designated Court convicted all 26 on a mix of charges — ss. 120-B, 302 IPC, TADA and the Arms/Explosives Acts — and awarded death to four: Nalini, Santhan, Murugan and Perarivalan; life or long terms to the rest. All convictions and the death reference came up together before the Supreme Court.

Issues

(i) What is the gist of criminal conspiracy, and how is a secret agreement to be proved? (ii) Did the evidence establish a conspiracy to commit a terrorist act under TADA ss. 3(1)/4 — i.e. to overawe the Government or strike terror — or only to murder Rajiv Gandhi? (iii) Which of the 26 were parties to the agreement, given differing degrees of association with the LTTE? (iv) Were the death sentences proper — especially for Nalini?

Arguments

For the appellants: conspiracy must be proved against each accused individually; mere association with, or sympathy for, the LTTE, or knowledge of its methods, is not agreement; several accused were involved only in unconnected LTTE activity; for some, the confession under TADA s. 15 was the sole basis and it was retracted/unreliable; no evidence showed an object to overawe the Government or strike terror as TADA required — the charge-sheet itself never alleged it; for Nalini, her role was peripheral and her death sentence disproportionate.

For the State (CBI/SIT): the agreement was proved circumstantially — infiltration, hideouts, wireless traffic, reconnaissance, the assassination and the shelter-and-flight phase were all acts in pursuance of one common design; s. 10 Evidence Act (now BSA s. 8) made each conspirator's acts and words evidence against all; the killing of a former Prime Minister for what he did in the interest of the country was itself an act of exceptional depravity warranting the sentence of death.

Held

1. The law of conspiracy (the passages quoted in § 5.3): the gist is "an agreement to break the law", punishable though the illegal act is never done, and without requiring all parties to agree to a single illegal act; agreement is the sine qua non, provable by direct evidence (rarely available) or inferred from "utterances, writings, acts, omissions and conduct"; "there is no difference between the mode of proof of the offence of conspiracy and that of any other offence"; a conspiracy "is always hatched in secrecy" and is "proved largely from the inference drawn from acts or illegal omission committed by the conspirators in pursuance of a common design".

2. Individual membership scrutiny: only those who knowingly agreed to participate in the conspiracy to assassinate Rajiv Gandhi could be convicted under 120-B read with 302; "mere association with LTTE or knowledge of LTTE activities was insufficient; agreement and participation in the conspiracy were essential". The Court sifted the 26 individually, acquitting some on the TADA counts (and in a few cases entirely), while upholding the core conspiracy-murder convictions against the active team.

3. Object of the conspiracy: the proof showed a conspiracy to murder Rajiv Gandhi — not a conspiracy to commit a terrorist act or disruptive activity under TADA: overawing the Government appears "nowhere in the charge"; under s. 3(1) TADA overawing must be the primary object, not a consequence; "even if… terror was struck by the assassination… the question is if striking of terror was intended and for that again there is no evidence". Result: TADA convictions set aside across the board; convictions under 120-B/302 IPC and allied statutes maintained for the core conspirators.

4. Sentence: "Murder of a former Prime Minister for what he did in the interest of the country was an act of exceptional depravity… an unparalleled act in the annals of crimes committed in this country." Death sentences of Santhan, Murugan and Perarivalan confirmed; Nalini's death sentence commuted to life (her youth, her motherhood of a child born in prison, her lesser role at the venue, good conduct and the possibility of reformation weighing with the Court). (Later history: the Tamil Nadu Governor commuted Nalini's sentence in 2000; the remaining three were granted de facto remission by the Court's 2014 order after 11 years' delay in deciding mercy petitions, and Perarivalan was released in 2022 — a story for the death-sentence unit, but a memorable footnote here.)

Principles evolved

  • Gist of s. 120-A/61 conspiracy = agreement; agreement may be proved by necessary implication from acts and conduct; no direct proof needed.
  • Conspiracy is complete without any act in execution (for offence-object agreements); each conspirator need not know or agree to every object.
  • Mode of proof of conspiracy = mode of proof of any offence (direct or circumstantial); s. 10 Evidence Act / BSA s. 8 operates after the common intention first arises.
  • Mere association/sympathy/knowledge ≠ conspiracy; the prosecution must place each accused inside the agreement.
  • Charge-court theory: the court decides on the charge framed and evidence led; the prosecution cannot upgrade the object of the conspiracy at the appellate stage (TADA slant rejected).
  • The court looks to the object of the agreement, not its consequential terror, in classifying special-statute conspiracies.

DU-exam hooks

"Agreement to break the law" · "hatched in secrecy… proved largely from inference… in pursuance of a common design" · "no difference between the mode of proof of conspiracy and that of any other offence" · mere LTTE association insufficient · "exceptional depravity… unparalleled act" · IEA s. 10 / BSA s. 8 in action.

From the 2023 DU course material, Unit 9 (Attempt — ss. 511, 307, 309 IPC) — names listed in the material; holdings verified from the reports RESEARCH · judgment reconstructed from the report

A · Abhayanand Mishra v. State of Bihar, AIR 1961 SC 1698 (Raghubar Dayal & K. Subba Rao JJ.)

Facts: the accused applied for admission to the M.A. class of Patna University, submitting an application form supported by a certificate he knew was not a genuine B.A. degree certificate. Before admission could be granted the fraud was detected. Charge: attempt to cheat (s. 511 r/w s. 420 IPC). Held: the offence of attempt to cheat begins when, with intent to cheat, the accused does some act towards its commission — the submission of the form with the forged certificate was such an act; it was not mere preparation. The Court disapproved the "last act" test and gave the classic two-limb formulation adopted verbatim in Mohd. Yakub: intention + (having made preparations) an act towards commission, "need not be the penultimate act… but must be an act during the course of committing that offence". Principle: "attempt begins where preparation ends".

B · Om Parkash v. State of Punjab, AIR 1961 SC 1782 : (1962) 2 SCR 254 (Raghubar Dayal & K. Subba Rao JJ.)

Facts: Om Parkash's relations with his wife became strained; she left for her brother's house; on assurances of good treatment she returned; the husband then subjected her to deliberate starvation over a period, but she managed to escape and survive. Convicted under s. 307 IPC. Held (appeal dismissed): a person commits attempt to murder "when he has intention to commit murder and in pursuance of that intention does an act towards its commission, irrespective of the fact whether that act is the penultimate act or not". Starvation was a series of acts each of which was towards killing (the judgment adverts to the slow-poisoning analogy — "completion or attempted completion of one of a series of acts intended… to result in killing is an attempt"); that the wife had a chance to escape did not neutralise the attempt. Principle: the series-of-acts doctrine for s. 307/109; even a "slow" method is an attempt.

C · Asgarali Pradhania v. Emperor, AIR 1933 Cal 893 (Calcutta High Court)

Facts: the accused supplied to a pregnant woman a "red liquid" and a packet of powder, telling her they would produce miscarriage; she tasted the mixture, found it unbearably strong, spat it out, and screamed when he tried to force her; he fled. Prosecution: attempt to cause miscarriage (s. 511 r/w s. 312). Neither substance was capable of causing miscarriage. Held (acquittal): following R. v. McPherson (1857) — "an attempt must be to do that which, if successful, would amount to the felony charged" — where the means adopted are "intrinsically useless", the failure is due to the accused's own ignorance, not to circumstances independent of his volition; what he did was only preparation, no attempt. (Contrast the statutory illustrations to s. 511/62 — see the impossibility analysis at § 6.5.) Principle: the objective/impossibility line of attempt, and the preparation-attempt boundary on means.

D · Malkiat Singh v. State of Punjab, (1969) 2 SCR 663 (extracted within Mohd. Yakub in the DU CM)

Facts: a truck carrying paddy was stopped at Samalkha, 32 miles from Delhi and about 15 miles short of the Delhi-Punjab boundary; prosecution for attempted illegal export of paddy. Held: no attempt — the accused "may have changed… mind at any place between Samalkha Barrier and the Delhi-Punjab boundary"; the test: whether "if the offender changes his mind and does not proceed further… the acts already done would be completely harmless". Principle: the harmless-acts test, as qualified (fact-specific, not a licence for "I was about to repent") by Chinnappa Reddy J. in Yakub.

Q1
Write a short note on "Impossible attempts".
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.8(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
Concept definition

An impossible attempt is an attempt to commit an offence which, unknown to the accused, could not have succeeded on the facts as they actually were — the pocket picked is empty, the chattels stolen already, the "poison" harmless. The question is whether criminal guilt can rest on an act directed at a result that was never attainable.

The two doctrinal lines
  • Objective line — impossibility exculpates: R. v. McPherson (1857): "an attempt must be to do that which, if successful, would amount to the felony charged" — the prisoner had tried to steal chattels already stolen; conviction wrong. Its twin is the witchcraft example — one who believes in spells and "burns his enemy in effigy" with intent to hurt is guilty of nothing. Indian adoption: Asgarali Pradhania v. Emperor, AIR 1933 Cal 893 (in DU's 2023 attempt unit) — the accused gave a pregnant woman a "red liquid" and powder to cause miscarriage; neither could do so; she spat it out. Held: the means were "intrinsically useless"; failure flowed from the accused's own ignorance, not from circumstances independent of his volition — mere preparation, no attempt; acquittal.
  • Subjective line — guilty mind suffices: Stephen's formulation: one who, "believing that a certain state of facts exists, does an act… which would, if that state of facts existed, be an attempt to commit an offence", attempts that offence "although its commission was… impossible". The criminality is the complete wicked will expressed in an outward act.
The Indian statutory answer conclusion

The legislature has adopted the subjective line: the illustrations to BNS § 62 / IPC § 511 themselves decide the two classic cases — (a) the box broken open proves empty ("he has done an act towards the commission of theft, and therefore is guilty"); (b) the hand thrust into an empty pocket ("A fails… in consequence of Z's having nothing in his pocket. A is guilty"). Factual impossibility is thus no defence. What still exculpates is pure legal impossibility — where what the accused willed would not have been an offence even on the facts as he believed them (the effigy-spell line: no crime was ever possible in law). The Indian cases, including Mohd. Yakub (where the officers' interception was the only reason export failed — attempt plainly made out), apply the act-towards-commission test without asking whether success was objectively on the cards.

Q2
Define abetment. Explain its three modes with illustrations, stating clearly whether the offence abetted must actually be committed.
DU pattern (core long answer) — covers BNS §§ 45–46 / IPC §§ 107–108
Definition framework

BNS § 45 / IPC § 107: a person abets the doing of a thing who (First) instigates any person to do it; (Secondly) engages in conspiracy for doing it, if an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of it and in order to its doing; (Thirdly) intentionally aids by act or illegal omission its doing. Mnemonic: I–C–A.

Mode 1 — Instigation
  • To goad, urge, provoke, incite, encourage — active suggestion. Explanation 1 includes instigation by wilful misrepresentation or wilful concealment of a material fact one is bound to disclose — even an attempt so to procure the act is instigation (illustration: representing C to be Z to a warrant officer).
  • Judicial gloss: a retort to a suicide threat is not instigation; harassment simpliciter is not (Satvir Singh); instigation must be a positive act proximate to the result (Gian Kaur line).
Mode 2 — Conspiracy
  • Agreement + an act or illegal omission in pursuance and in order to the doing of the thing. Illustration: A and B conspire to poison Z; A procures and delivers the poison, B administers it — A abets murder by conspiracy (s. 49 illus. (b) / IPC 109).
  • The abettor need not have conspired with the actual perpetrator (Expl. 5 to s. 46/108).
Mode 3 — Intentional aid
  • Explanation 2: aid rendered "prior to or at the time of" the act which facilitates it — keeping watch, supplying the ladder, unlocking the gate, and equally an illegal omission (the servant whose duty was to guard, who wilfully looks away).
  • Innocent or unknowing facilitation is not abetment; nor is after-the-event help (that is harbouring, if anything).
Must the offence be committed? the inchoate point

No. Explanation 2 to s. 46/108: "To constitute the offence of abetment it is not necessary that the act abetted should be committed, or that the effect requisite to constitute the offence should be caused." A instigates B to murder C; B refuses — A is guilty all the same. If the offence is committed in consequence, s. 49/109 gives the abettor the offence's own punishment; if not, ss. 55–56/115–116 give the graded substitute (7/14 years for death-or-life offences; one-fourth — one-half for public servants — for the rest). Abetment is thus a true inchoate offence: the guilty participation is itself the crime.

Q3
K, a young working wife, was repeatedly taunted by her husband and in-laws for not bringing a scooter and a house in dowry. Her father paid them ₹ 50,000 to buy peace, but the harassment continued. One night, after an abusive episode over the food served to the husband, the three taunted K to "go and end your life before one of the trains that run nearby". The next dawn K lay on the track before an incoming train; she was maimed, but survived. Discuss the criminal liability of the husband and the in-laws.
DU pattern (modeled on the Satvir Singh facts) — BNS §§ 108, 85–86, 56 / IPC §§ 306, 498-A, 116; BSA § 117 / IEA § 113-A
Issue

Whether the husband and in-laws can be convicted of abetment of suicide (or anything else) when the wife survived her suicide attempt; and what difference the alleged taunt "end your life before a train" makes.

Rule IRAC
  • BNS § 108 / IPC § 306 requires as a fact precedent that the person committed suicide — "the condition precedent is that suicide should necessarily have been committed" (Satvir Singh, AIR 2001 SC 2828).
  • No "abetment of abetment": s. 306's own gist is abetment, so s. 116/56 cannot ride on s. 306/108 — "it is inconceivable to have abetment of an abetment".
  • Abetment of an attempt to commit suicide would in principle fall under the attempt-to-suicide section read with s. 45/107 — but IPC s. 309 is repealed and BNS s. 226 covers only attempts to compel or restrain a public servant — not this fact-pattern.
  • What remains operative for the surviving victim: s. 85–86 BNS / s. 498-A IPC (cruelty — wilful conduct likely to drive her to suicide, or dowry harassment), the exact conviction that survived in Satvir Singh.
  • BSA § 117 / IEA § 113-A presumption (suicide within seven years + cruelty → court may presume abetment) is irrelevant unless the wife dies; and even then it is discretionary.
Application

R's survival empties s. 108/306 of its fact precedent; the Sessions-style conviction under "s. 116 r/w 306" (as in Satvir Singh) must fail on that short ground. However, months of dowry-linked taunts (scooter and house), the ₹ 50,000 extraction and the final abusive episode are squarely "cruelty" within BNS § 86(b) / s. 498-A — harassment with a view to meeting an unlawful demand for property. The "end your life before a train" taunt, while morally proximate to the tragedy, cannot be converted into instigation of suicide while the woman lives; had she died, the same taunt, coupled with the final episode, could ground s. 108/306 (per the instigation standard in Gian Kaur: active suggestion calculated to and resulting in the fatal resolve).

Conclusion

K, K's father and mother cannot be convicted of abetment of suicide or of any attempted abetment of it; they are liable for cruelty under BNS §§ 85–86 / IPC s. 498-A (up to 3 years + fine) — precisely the outcome the Supreme Court reached on the s. 116 count in Satvir Singh.

Q4
"Abetment is complete although the act abetted is not committed, though the punishment varies with the event." Discuss with reference to the scheme of BNS §§ 45–60 / IPC §§ 107–120.
DU pattern (long answer) — abetment punishment scheme
The proposition and its sources

Explanation 2 to s. 46/108 makes abetment an inchoate offence: the crime is the instigation/conspiracy/aid, not the result. But the sentencing tracks the event in four graded steps:

  1. Offence committed in consequence — s. 49/109: abettor gets the offence's own punishment (e.g. instigator of a murder that happens → punishment of murder). Explanation: "in consequence of" = in consequence of the instigation / in pursuance of the conspiracy / with the aid.
  2. Grave offence not committed — s. 55/115 (death/life offences): up to 7 years + fine; and if hurt results in consequence of the abetment, up to 14 years.
  3. Minor offence not committed — s. 56/116: up to one-fourth of the longest term (or fine, or both); one-half where the abettor or the person abetted is a public servant bound to prevent the offence — the law's special displeasure at treacherous guardians.
  4. Deviation scenarios — where the doer's act or result departs from the abetment: s. 50/110 (different intention: abettor judged by his own mens rea); s. 51/111 (different act: liable only if probable consequence; s. 52/112 adds cumulative punishment); s. 53/113 (different effect: liable if he knew it likely); s. 54/114 (abettor present: deemed principal).
Illustrations that carry the answer
  • A instigates B to murder Z; B refuses → s. 55/115 (7 years).
  • A instigates B to burn Z's house; B also steals → A not liable for the theft (distinct act) — s. 51 illus. (b).
  • A instigates B to cause grievous hurt; Z dies → A guilty of murder's punishment if he knew death likely — s. 53 illus.
  • A, instigator of a robbery-resistance, present when B causes grievous hurt → A deemed to have committed it — ss. 52/54.
Conclusion

The scheme is a calibrated ladder of culpability: the abettor's guilt is fixed by his own act and mind at the moment of abetment; the quantum rises with the harm his criminal enterprise in fact produced. This is inchoate liability with a consequential tail — not strict liability for another's crime.

Q5
A, B, C, D and E, all members of an unlawful assembly, decided to attack X, who was a member of a rival political party. Each was told the attack should not go so far as to cause death. All five went at night to attack X; but D and E, who had an old score to settle with X, secretly carried pistols and shot X dead while A, B, C attacked with hockey sticks. Can A, B, C, D and E be held guilty under s. 149 IPC? Explain.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.7(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (answered here with the Unit 3 overlay)
Provisions framework

s. 149 IPC / s. 190(1) BNS: every member of an unlawful assembly (five or more, common object — s. 141/189) is guilty of offences committed in prosecution of the common object, or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object. To be contrasted with criminal conspiracy, s. 120-B/61 (agreement) and common intention, s. 34/3(5) (participation in the act).

Analysis
  • The assembly and its object: five persons with the common object of attacking X — an unlawful assembly (s. 141/189). The object, as communicated to all, was an assault that should not exceed causing death — i.e. hurt-level violence.
  • D and E: they shared a further, secret object — revenge by death — concealed from the rest and pursued with hidden pistols. They are guilty of murder (s. 302/103); and since they deliberately hid their design, the others cannot be made liable for it through s. 149 — the murder was neither in prosecution of the communicated common object nor known to the others as likely.
  • A, B and C: liable for the offence the assembly actually and commonly committed — voluntarily causing hurt (ss. 323/115(1) area) read with s. 149/190(1); and (depending on the weapons and injuries) grievous hurt if that was within the contemplation of an attack with hockey sticks — the "knew likely" limb lets the court lift liability to what armed night-attacks of that kind predictably produce, but not to a secret homicidal design they were kept out of.
  • Conspiracy overlay (the Unit 3 point): D and E's prior, secret agreement to kill X is a criminal conspiracy (s. 120-B/61(2)(a)) — an antecedent agreement with a murderous object — distinct from the assembly's common object; had the prosecution charged it, D and E would answer for conspiracy as well as murder. The three with sticks, having no agreement with D and E's secret design, are outside that conspiracy: common object is proved by membership and the object's manifestation; conspiracy must be proved as an agreement (Nalini: mere association/knowledge insufficient).
  • Common intention (s. 34/3(5)) would help convict D and E of the murder jointly (shared homicidal intention in furtherance of which the fatal act was done); it cannot stretch to A, B, C whose intention was confined to the assault.
Conclusion

Yes — all five are liable under s. 149/190(1) for the offences within the assembly's common object (the assault — and its probable extensions); but the murder is D and E's alone (murder + conspiracy), since their homicidal object was a secret departure from, not a development of, the common object. The problem is a textbook demonstration that assembly liability is object-based, conspiracy liability agreement-based, and common intention act-based.

Q6
"The gist of the offence of criminal conspiracy is an agreement to break the law." Discuss the essentials of criminal conspiracy (BNS § 61 / IPC § 120-A) and the special rule of evidence that governs its proof, with reference to State of Tamil Nadu v. Nalini.
DU pattern (long answer) — conspiracy essentials + proof
Essentials framework
  1. Two or more persons — no conspiracy of one.
  2. Agreement — formal or informal, express or inferred; the gist (Nalini). "It is not an ingredient of the offence that all the parties should agree to do a single illegal act."
  3. Object — (a) an illegal act (offence or civil injury), or (b) a legal act by illegal means; immaterial whether the illegal act is the ultimate object or merely incidental (Explanation).
  4. Overt act proviso — for agreements whose object is an offence, the conspiracy is complete on agreement; for other illegal objects, some act besides the agreement in pursuance is required.
  5. Completion unnecessary — punishable "though the illegal act agreed to be done has not been done."
  6. Punishment (s. 61(2)/120-B) — grave objects (death/life/RI ≥ 2 years): as if the conspirator had abetted the object offence; others: up to 6 months/fine/both.
Proof — BSA § 8 / IEA § 10
  • "[A] conspiracy is always hatched in secrecy and it is impossible to adduce direct evidence… the offence can be only proved largely from the inference drawn from acts or illegal omission committed by the conspirators in pursuance of a common design" (Nalini).
  • The agreement "may be proved by necessary implication" — from utterances, writings, acts, omissions and conduct; "no difference between the mode of proof of the offence of conspiracy and that of any other offence" — direct or circumstantial.
  • BSA § 8 / IEA § 10: once there is reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy exists, anything said, done or written by any conspirator in reference to the common intention (after it is first entertained) is relevant against each — an exception to the hearsay rule; but some independent evidence of the conspiracy must precede its use.
Nalini applied
  • Five facets proved circumstantially (infiltration, safe houses, wireless, the assassination, harbouring) → the agreement inferred; s. 10 let each conspirator's acts speak against all 26.
  • But membership was scrutinised individually: "mere association with LTTE or knowledge of LTTE activities was insufficient; agreement and participation in the conspiracy were essential" — several TADA convictions fell.
  • Object re-characterisation rejected: proof showed a conspiracy to murder, not to overawe Government (TADA) — the court decides on the charge and evidence, not on appellate re-labelling.
Conclusion

Conspiracy punishes the meeting of guilty minds; the Evidence Act compensates for its secrecy by converting each conspirator's pursuant conduct into mutual evidence; and the courts police both gates — the agreement must be proved, and proved against each accused.

Q7
A, B and C agree together to poison Z to death. In pursuance of the agreement B procures the poison and delivers it to A, and A administers it to Z, who dies. On a later occasion, by a slip of the hand, a dose intended for Z finds its way into Y’s plate; Y falls gravely ill but recovers. C, after the first meeting, had second thoughts, told A and B that he wanted out, and attended nothing further before the poison was procured. Discuss the liability of A, B and C.
DU pattern (conspiracy + abetment composite) — BNS §§ 45, 46, 49, 51, 61 / IPC §§ 107, 108, 109, 111, 120-A/B
Issues

Liability of (i) A, who agreed and executed; (ii) B, who agreed and procured the instrumentality; (iii) C, who agreed but later had second thoughts; for the offence of poisoning and, separately, for the attempted poisoning of Y.

Rule & application IRAC
  • Conspiracy (s. 61/120-A, B): A, B and C agreed to an illegal act — murder — so the conspiracy is complete at the agreement itself (offence-object; proviso inapplicable); all three are guilty of criminal conspiracy to murder, punishable under s. 61(2)(a) "in the same manner as if he had abetted" murder. Note for C: for a grave-object conspiracy the agreement itself is the crime — unilateral remorse does not undo it; at most genuine communication of withdrawal before any overt act in pursuance would relieve the abetment limbs (below) and mitigate on sentence; courts have nonetheless recognised that withdrawal communicated in time to prevent the act can exclude liability for the principal offence.
  • Z's death (consummated): A — murderer (s. 103/302). B — abettor by conspiracy and by aid (procuring the poison), the act done in pursuance of the conspiracy → s. 49/109: B suffers murder's punishment. C — abettor by conspiracy for whatever acts occurred in pursuance while he was party; if the substantial acts (procuring, delivering, poisoning) occurred after C's effective withdrawal and communication, C escapes s. 49/109 for the completed murder but remains punishable for the conspiracy itself.
  • Y's poisoning (the mistaken object): this is the different act done scenario — s. 51/111 illus. (a) is on all fours (the child poisoning Y's plate by mistake): A liable for Y's poisoning as if abetted directly, B likewise if the mistake was a probable consequence of the abetment committed under its influence — a mix-up between two adjacent plates is the classic probable-consequence case; C, if withdrawn, is outside.
  • No defence of impossibility is needed anywhere here; but note for completeness the illustration-based answer (s. 62 illus.) had the poison proved harmless.
Conclusion

A: murder of Z + poisoning of Y. B: same, as abettor (ss. 49, 51/109, 111) + conspiracy. C: conspiracy to murder (s. 61(2)(a)) — with sentence relief for timely withdrawal; no liability for the completed acts effected after his disassociation. The problem shows the three doctrines nested: agreement (conspiracy), participation (abetment), and deviation (s. 51/111).

Q8
"An attempt begins where preparation ends." Discuss the tests evolved to distinguish preparation from attempt, and state the test finally adopted by the Supreme Court in State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Yakub.
DU pattern (long answer) — the doctrinal core of attempt
Why the line matters framing

Preparation is generally lawful (the actor may still repent and his acts are "completely harmless" — Malkiat Singh); attempt is punishable. The course-material rationale for punishing the attempter: he is liable "even if the forbidden consequence does not ensue for reasons beyond his control", for he has created "alarm and scare in the society". The line is where the offender ceases to arrange the crime and starts executing it.

The tests (per the twin judgments in Mohd. Yakub)
  • Kenny's last proximate act — rejected as "too narrow" (Sarkaria J.); "attempt defies a precise and exact definition"; it is "a mixed question of law and fact".
  • Eagleton proximity (Parke B., 1855, the locus classicus) — "acts immediately connected" vs "acts remotely leading towards" the offence; accepted, with the caveat (Goddard C.J.) that the immediate/remote divide is easier said than applied; Parke B.'s own last-act aside is "entirely unacceptable" as a general rule — else a man with a cocked gun stopped at the trigger would escape.
  • Stephen's series-of-acts — "an act done with intent… forming part of a series of acts which would constitute its actual commission if it were not interrupted"; Reddy J.: a "confession of inability to define" precisely where definition is needed.
  • Turner/Archbold equivocality (approved in Davey v. Lee) — an act "immediately and not merely remotely connected", which "cannot reasonably be regarded as having any other purpose" than the crime; Turner himself later relaxed it to a prima-facie indication of intention; Reddy J. refused to bar aliunde evidence of mens rea from the actus reus inquiry.
  • Haughton v. Smith — Hailsham L.C.'s three propositions: intention ≠ attempt; an overt act forming part of the interrupted series; the act must be proximate, not merely preparatory. Applied in DPP v. Stonehouse.
  • Abhayanand Mishra (SC) — disapproved the last-act test; the two-limb rule: intention + an act done towards commission "need not be the penultimate act… but must be an act during the course of committing".
  • Malkiat Singh harmless-acts test (Ramaswami J.) — attempt requires overt acts which, if the offender stops, would not be "completely harmless"; qualified by Reddy J. as fact-specific, lest every interrupted offender claim he "was about to change his mind".
The Yakub synthesis (Chinnappa Reddy J., ¶ 32)
  1. an intention to commit the particular offence;
  2. some act done which would necessarily have to be done towards its commission; and
  3. the act must be proximate to the intended result — where "the measure of proximity is not in relation to time and action but in relation to intention": the act, with other circumstances, must reveal with reasonable certainty an intention (not a mere desire) to commit that offence.
Application in Yakub

Silver concealed, driven at midnight to a lonely creek, four ingots unloaded at the water's edge, a mechanised craft's engine audible — export intended and attempted; the only remaining step was loading. Contrast: the same truck stopped at Shirsat Naka would have shown, at most, preparation ("transporting or attempting to transport silver somewhere" — not export). Sarkaria J. added the mischief-rule gloss for anti-smuggling statutes: "attempt" there is "wide enough to take in its fold any one or series of acts committed, beyond the stage of preparation, in moving the contraband goods deliberately to the place of embarkation".

Q9
On intelligence that foreign currency is being smuggled out of India, officers stop T’s truck 30 km short of the Attari border; sewn into the upholstery they find ₹ 90 lakh in foreign currency. T says he was only moving the money to a warehouse, and that he has done nothing towards crossing the border. The prosecution charges him with attempting to export the currency in contravention of the law. Decide.
DU pattern (modeled on Mohd. Yakub / Malkiat Singh) — BNS § 62 / IPC § 511 and special-statute attempts
Issue

Whether T's conduct, stopped 30 km short of the border with the contraband hidden, amounts to attempted smuggling — or only preparation.

Rule & application IRAC
  • Yakub test: intention + act necessarily done towards commission + proximity measured against intention revealed. In Yakub, the accused had reached the place of embarkation and begun unloading with the vessel's engine audible — "most of the steps necessary in the course of export by sea had been taken"; only loading remained.
  • Malkiat Singh test: where the vehicle is intercepted far from the boundary, the acts remain equivocal — the offender may still change course and his acts remain "completely harmless"; the paddy truck stopped 15 miles from the border was only preparation.
  • Applied: T at 30 km from the border, goods merely concealed in transit, has not done any act that "would necessarily have to be done" in the export itself beyond carriage — the evidence is still consistent with storage, domestic movement, or abandonment; the intention to export is not yet revealed "with reasonable certainty" by the overt acts. This is preparation, and preparation is not punishable under the export-control statute (unlike offences where the law punishes preparation itself — e.g. preparation to commit dacoity, s. 399 IPC / s. 310(4) BNS).
What would change the answer
  • Intelligence showing a rendezvous at the border creek + T's route converging on it at night — closer to Yakub.
  • T stopped while actually loading the truck onto a country boat at the creek — attempt made out (loading = Yakub's remaining step).
  • If the statute punishes "attempt to export" expressly (as the Customs Act does), the Yakub mischief-rule construction applies generously — but generosity cannot manufacture proximity that the facts lack.
Conclusion

On the given facts T is at the preparation stage — no attempt. The contrast pair to quote: Malkiat Singh (15 miles = preparation) vs Mohd. Yakub (at the creek, unloading = attempt); the difference is the certainty of intention the overt acts reveal.

Q10
"An accused is liable for attempting to commit an offence even if the forbidden consequence does not ensue for reasons beyond his control." Why is attempt punishable although no harm results? Are there acts short of attempt that the law nevertheless punishes? Explain.
DU pattern (long answer) — rationale + the exceptional preparatory offences
Rationales framework
  • Alarm-and-scare rationale (DU course material's own words): the attempter has already created public alarm — the community's peace, not only the target, is the protected interest.
  • Equal culpability: the attempter possesses the complete mens rea of the offence; only fortune ("reasons beyond his control" — the watchman's arrival, the pistol's misfire, the empty pocket) divides him from the completed offender. Law does not make liability a lottery.
  • Preventive necessity: attempt liability is what licenses the police to intervene at the last overt act — the silver in Yakub was intercepted precisely because interception at the attempt stage is lawful.
  • Reformative/deterrent symmetry: the threatened half-maximum (s. 62/511) calibrates punishment to the unfinished harm while preserving the deterrent message.
Acts short of attempt that are punishable anyway
  • Preparation offences: preparation to commit dacoity (s. 399 IPC / s. 310(4) BNS), preparation for waging war (s. 122 IPC / BNS s. 147), possession-type offences (arms, counterfeit material) — where the preparation itself signals exceptional danger.
  • The inchoate family below the act: abetment (ss. 45–60/107–120) and conspiracy (s. 61/120-A, B) — punishable with no act of execution at all (agreement/instigation suffice); s. 55/115 & 56/116 punish the abettor though the offence is never attempted by anyone.
  • Impossible attempts: the s. 62/511 illustrations punish acts whose success was factually impossible — the law punishes the danger shown, not the harvest reaped.
Conclusion

Attempt liability is forward-looking protection: the criminal will, once externalised in a proximate overt act, is the law's trigger. But the line is held — intention and preparation remain free except where Parliament has expressly said that the danger of the preparation itself (dacoity, war) or of the bare agreement (conspiracy, instigation) justifies earlier intervention.

Q11
Trace the judicial and legislative journey of the law on attempt to commit suicide in India (IPC § 309 to BNS § 226), with reference to Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab. Is abetment of suicide still an offence?
DU pattern (long answer) — right-to-die journey
The journey structure = the six stations of § 4.2
  • 1987 — Dubal (Bom HC): s. 309 invalid under Arts. 14 & 21 ("right to die" in Art. 21).
  • 1994 — P. Rathinam (SC, 2 Judges): "right to live… bring in its trail the right not to live a forced life"; s. 309 "deserves to be effaced… to humanize our penal laws"; violative of Art. 21.
  • 1996 — Gian Kaur (Constitution Bench, DU CM): Rathinam overruled. Art. 21 guarantees protection of life; "by no stretch of imagination can extinction of life be read to be included in 'protection of life'"; suicide is "an unnatural termination… incompatible and inconsistent with the concept of right to life". s. 309 violates neither Art. 21 nor Art. 14 (no minimum sentence; fine alone possible; compassionate application in practice). But the dignified-death seed: right to live with dignity includes "a dignified procedure of death"; for the terminally ill in whom "the process of natural death has commenced", accelerating that process is not "an unnatural death curtailing the natural span of life" — the foundation of Common Cause (2018) (passive euthanasia, living wills).
  • 2017 — Mental Healthcare Act s. 115: a person attempting suicide presumed to have severe stress and not to be tried under the Code — decriminalisation by presumption.
  • 2023/2024 — BNS: s. 309 not re-enacted; simple attempt to suicide is decriminalised; only s. 226 BNS survives — attempt to commit suicide "with the intent to compel or restrain any public servant from discharging his official duty" (SI ≤ 1 year / fine / both / community service).
Abetment of suicide

Untouched and unambiguous: s. 108 BNS / s. 306 IPC (10 years + fine); aggravated form for child/unsound-mind/delirious/intoxicated victims — s. 107 BNS / s. 305 IPC (death or life, or up to 10 years). Gian Kaur held s. 306 "a distinct offence which is capable of existence independent of s. 309" — the abettor "abets the extinguishment of life of another person", and the compassion due to the victim does not extend to his instigator (UK Suicide Act 1961 s. 2: suicide decriminalised, complicity punishable up to 14 years — the same design). The BNS is Gian Kaur codified: victim decriminalised, instigator punished; and Satvir Singh polices the boundary: a completed suicide is the fact precedent of s. 108/306, and harassment of the surviving victim is answered by s. 85/498-A.

Conclusion

The arc is: constitutional validation (1996) → compassionate non-enforcement (2017) → legislative decriminalisation (2024) — with the abettor's liability standing at every station. A mercy-driven statute is still allowed to hate the manipulator of another's despair.

Q12
"Establishment of an overt act is not a requirement of law to allow s. 34 [IPC] to operate, inasmuch as the section gets attracted when a criminal act is done by common intention of all." Explain. How does common intention differ from criminal conspiracy?
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.7(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (answered with the Unit 3 overlay)
Common intention — s. 34 IPC / s. 3(5) BNS framework

When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each is liable for it as if it were done by him alone. The provision is a rule of liability, not an offence: it substitutes joint for individual responsibility for the done act.

Why no separate overt act by each is needed
  • The section speaks of "a criminal act done… in furtherance of the common intention of all" — what must be shared is the intention, not the performance. One may hold, one may watch, one may strike: each is liable for the act because each intended it and it was done in furtherance of that shared design.
  • Participation may be inferred from conduct — presence at the scene, arms carried, the roles played; the "overt act" debate concerns proof, not an ingredient: what the prosecution must prove is the shared intention plus the act done in its furtherance by someone of the group, not a discrete act by every member.
  • Hence the classic formulations: common intention may develop "on the spot" (sudden, contemporaneous meeting of minds); once it exists at the time of the act, constructive liability attaches to all.

Conclusion

s. 34 needs no individual overt act because its premise is the done act shared by intention; conspiracy needs no done act at all because its crime is the agreement. One looks forward from a completed act to implicate the confederates; the other looks backward from an agreement to punish the plot before it ripens.

Q13
Write short notes on any two: (a) Abetment of suicide of a child or person of unsound mind; (b) 'Instigation' distinguished from 'aiding'; (c) The last-act test and the cocked-gun objection; (d) Abetment outside India for an offence in India.
DU pattern (short notes) — pick any two in the exam; all four modelled
(a) Abetment of suicide of child / unsound mind — BNS § 107 / IPC § 305

If any child, person of unsound mind, delirious person or intoxicated person commits suicide, whoever abets it is punishable with death or life, or up to 10 years + fine. The aggravated gravity rests on the victim's impaired capacity: like the innocent agent (Expl. 3, s. 46/108), his will is legally or factually overborne, so the abettor's authorship approaches murder. BNS modernises IPC's language ("child"; "person of unsound mind"; "idiot" dropped). Proof still needs the § 108/306 anatomy: suicide committed + abetment (instigation) + causal consequence — with the capacity element elevating the offence.

(b) Instigation vs aiding

Both are modes of abetment (s. 45/107), but they differ in direction and materiality: instigation operates on the will of the principal — goading, urging, provoking, active suggestion (wilful misrepresentation/concealment suffice, even as an attempt); it is complete when the instigation is given, whatever the principal then does. Aiding operates on the means or opportunity — facilitation prior to or at the time of the act (keeping watch, supplying implements, illegal omission of a duty); it presupposes the principal's own act and requires intentional facilitation. One can aid without instigating (the silent lookout) and instigate without aiding (the remote inciter). Presence at the scene aids neither — but if the abettor is present when the offence occurs, s. 54/114 deems him to have committed it.

(c) The last-act test and the cocked-gun objection

Kenny (and Parke B.'s aside in Eagleton) would confine attempt to the "last proximate act" — the final act depending on the offender himself, hindrance beyond his control. In Mohd. Yakub, Chinnappa Reddy J. pronounced the general principle "entirely unacceptable": "if that principle be correct, a person who has cocked his gun at another and is about to pull the trigger but is prevented… cannot be convicted of attempt to murder." The law does not wait for the trigger: Abhayanand Mishra and Om Parkash settle that the act need not be penultimate — any act in the course of committing, proximate by its revelation of intention, suffices ("the measure of proximity is not in relation to time and action but in relation to intention").

(d) Abetment outside India for an offence in India — BNS § 48

The mirror-image of s. 47/108-A (abetment in India of offences abroad): a person who, without and beyond India, abets the commission in India of an act that would be an offence if committed in India, is an abettor — "A, in country X, instigates B to commit a murder in India. A is guilty of abetting murder." New in the BNS (no IPC counterpart), it closes the extraterritorial gap for handlers abroad — terror controllers, diaspora fraud architects, cross-border instigators — and works with BNS s. 1(5)'s extended territoriality. Punishment follows the ordinary ss. 49–56/109–116 scheme.
