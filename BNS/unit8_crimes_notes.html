Exam alert — the age revolution IPC § 361 protected a male minor under 16 and a female minor under 18. PART IIIKidnapping — BNS § 137 ↔ IPC §§ 359, 360, 361, 363

3.1 Etymology, object and the two kinds

“Kidnapping” compounds kid (child) + napping (to steal) — literally child-stealing; but in law it “is not confined to child stealing. It has been given a wider connotation as meaning carrying away of a human being against his/her consent, or the consent of some person legally authorised to accord consent on behalf of such person” (DU exam-format material; Manupatra commentary, citing Kenny). The object is twofold: “to secure the personal liberty of citizens, to give legal protection to children of tender age from being abducted or seduced for improper purposes and to preserve the rights of parents and guardians over their wards” (Manupatra); and, per Gajendragadkar J. in State v. Harbansing Kisansing AIR 1954 Bom 399 (quoted in Varadarajan and Manupatra): “the more important object of these provisions undoubtedly is to afford security and protection to the wards themselves.”

Statute — BNS § 137 (as per course gazette)
137. (1) Kidnapping is of two kinds: kidnapping from India, and kidnapping from lawful guardianship––
(a) whoever conveys any person beyond the limits of India without the consent of that person, or of some person legally authorised to consent on behalf of that person, is said to kidnap that person from India;
(b) whoever takes or entices any child or any person of unsound mind, out of the keeping of the lawful guardian of such child or person of unsound mind, without the consent of such guardian, is said to kidnap such child or person from lawful guardianship.
Explanation.––The words “lawful guardian” in this clause include any person lawfully entrusted with the care or custody of such child or other person.
Exception.—This clause does not extend to the act of any person who in good faith believes himself to be the father of an illegitimate child, or who in good faith believes himself to be entitled to the lawful custody of such child, unless such act is committed for an immoral or unlawful purpose.
(2) Whoever kidnaps any person from India or from lawful guardianship shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine.

IPC predecessors: § 359 (two kinds), § 360 (from India), § 361 (from lawful guardianship — “any minor under sixteen years of age if a male, or under eighteen years of age if a female, or any person of unsound mind”, same Explanation and Exception), § 363 (punishment: up to seven years + fine).

3.2 Kind I — Kidnapping from India (§137(1)(a) / IPC §360): ingredients

  1. Conveying of any person — “convey” means to take, carry or transport; a physical act; the person may be major, minor or of unsound mind; conveyance may be by force or by fraud-induced consent (DU exam-format notes; Manupatra).
  2. Beyond the limits of India — the territorial threshold; the offence is complete on crossing it.
  3. Without consent — of that person, or of someone legally authorised to consent on his/her behalf (for a child or person of unsound mind); consent vitiated by fear or misconception (BNS §28 / IPC §90) is no consent.

3.3 Kind II — Kidnapping from lawful guardianship (§137(1)(b) / IPC §361): ingredients

  1. Taking or enticing — the operative act (analysed below).
  2. The person must be a “child” (BNS: below 18, §2(3); IPC: male u/16, female u/18) or a person of unsound mind.
  3. Out of the “keeping” of the lawful guardian — “keeping” “connotes the idea of charge, protection, maintenance and control” (State of Haryana v. Raja Ram, quoted in Vadgama); the minor need not be in the guardian’s physical possession — continuous control suffices (in Varadarajan, Savitri left at a relative’s house remained in her father’s keeping).
  4. Without the consent of the guardian — “it is only the guardian’s consent which takes the case out of its purview” (Raja Ram).
  5. Consent of the child is wholly immaterial — and so is the kidnapper’s motive, save within the Exception (Raja Ram; State v. Sulekh Chand; Manupatra list).
“Lawful guardian” vs “legal guardian” “Lawful” ≠ “legal”: a lawful guardian is “a guardian whose custody is sanctioned by law” (Fatti (Mst.) v. Emperor); the Explanation extends it to “any person lawfully entrusted with the care or custody” — e.g., a schoolmaster or employer is a lawful guardian though not a legal guardian; a person obtaining custody by illegal means is not one (Manupatra commentary).

“Takes” and “entices” — the contested verbs (the heart of all three DU cases)

  • “Takes” — “does not necessarily connote taking by force… This word merely means ‘to cause to go’, ‘to escort’ or ‘to get into possession’” (Vadgama, adopting Raja Ram); the minor’s mental attitude during the taking is irrelevant (In re Khalandar Saheb).
  • “Entices” — “involves the idea of inducement or allurement by giving rise to hope or desire in the other… This may work immediately or it may create continuous and gradual but imperceptible impression culminating after some time, in achieving its ultimate purpose” (Vadgama).
  • The Varadarajan line: if the minor leaves home “completely uninfluenced by any promise, offer or inducement emanating from the guilty party”, there is no taking; but “if the guilty party has laid a foundation by inducement, allurement or threat… and if this can be considered to have influenced or weighed with her in leaving her guardian’s custody… then prima facie it would be difficult for him to plead innocence on the ground that the minor had voluntarily come to him” (Vadgama, reconciling Varadarajan).
  • Strict-liability flavour: R. v. Prince (1875) LR 2 CCR 154 (in DU exam material): bona fide belief that the girl was over the protected age is no defence — the section dispenses with mens rea as to age; “if the kidnapped girl turns out to be under 18 years of age, the kidnapper must take the consequences” (Manupatra).
  • Continuing offence: the offence is complete when the minor is actually taken and “is a continuing one until the minor’s return to his guardian” (Jeevan v. Rex, AIR 1949 Ori 22, Manupatra); “taking” may be a series of acts — a question of fact.

PART IVDU Case Law I — S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras

S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras
AIR 1965 SC 942 · Coram: J.R. Mudholkar, K. Subba Rao & M. Hidayatullah, JJ. · Appeal by special leave from High Court of Madras (full text in DU Casebook, Unit 8, pp. 243–246)
Facts
  • Savitri (PW4), born 13.11.1942 (hence a minor, “on the verge of attaining majority”, on 01.10.1960), was the third daughter of S. Natarajan (PW1), an Assistant Secretary to the Government of Madras, living at Nungambakkam with his wife and two daughters — Rama (PW2, medical college) and Savitri (a second-year B.Sc. student of Ethiraj College).
  • A few months before 30.09.1960 Savitri became friendly with the appellant Varadarajan, a next-door neighbour; they conversed across houses. On 30.09.1960 Rama found them talking; Savitri said she wanted to marry the appellant. The father questioned her; she wept but said nothing. The father took her the same day to Kodambakkam and left her at the house of his relative K. Natarajan (PW6) to keep her away from the appellant.
  • On 01.10.1960 at about 10 a.m. Savitri herself left K. Natarajan’s house, telephoned the appellant asking him to meet her on a certain road, went there, and got into his car of her own accord. They picked up P.T. Sami, went to a shop where the appellant bought two gundus and a tirumangalyam selected by Savitri, and had an agreement to marry registered at the Registrar’s office (attested by Sami and co-accused P.K. Mar, who was acquitted). They stayed a day at Ajanta Hotel, then travelled to Sattur, Sirukulam, Coimbatore and Tanjore, where police tracing the father’s kidnapping complaint found them on 03.11.1960.
Procedural history

Convicted by the Fifth Presidency Magistrate, Egmore, under §363 IPC and sentenced to one year’s rigorous imprisonment; conviction affirmed by the High Court of Madras; appeal by special leave.

Issues
  1. Whether a minor can abandon the guardianship of her guardian, and whether Savitri did so.
  2. Whether the appellant’s conduct amounted to a “taking” of Savitri out of the keeping of her lawful guardian within §361 IPC (the essential ingredient of kidnapping).
Arguments — Appellant
  • Savitri had abandoned her father’s guardianship before the appellant entered the picture.
  • In the alternative, the appellant did not take her out of the keeping of her lawful guardian: she telephoned him, joined him voluntarily, and the insistence on marriage came from her side; he merely complied with her wishes.
Arguments — Respondent (State) / courts below
  • Savitri, though at K. Natarajan’s house, remained in the lawful keeping of her father; the appellant’s active role — driving her about, purchasing jewellery, registering the marriage agreement, keeping her with him from place to place — constituted taking/enticing.
  • His facilitating conduct and continued harbouring of a minor girl who had left home completed the offence under §361/363.
Decision

Appeal allowed; conviction and sentence set aside; appellant acquitted. The Court found it unnecessary to decide the “abandonment of guardianship” question, because the ingredient of “taking” itself failed:

  • “Taking or enticing away a minor out of the keeping of a lawful guardian is an essential ingredient of the offence of kidnapping… what we have to find out is whether the part played by the appellant amounts to ‘taking’.”
  • “There is not a word in the deposition of Savitri from which an inference could be drawn that she left the house of K. Natarajan at the instance or even a suggestion of the appellant… she got into that car of her own accord… the insistence of marriage came from her side. The appellant, by complying with her wishes can by no stretch of imagination be said to have taken her out of the keeping of her lawful guardian.”
  • She “was not a child of tender years… was on the verge of attaining majority… not an uneducated or unsophisticated village girl but a senior college student”.
  • The governing distinction: “there is a distinction between ‘taking’ and allowing a minor to accompany a person… where the minor alleged to have been taken by the accused person left her father’s protection knowing and having capacity to know the full import of what she was doing voluntarily joins the accused person… the accused can[not] be said to have taken her away… Something more has to be shown… some kind of inducement held out by the accused person or an active participation by him in the formation of the intention of the minor to leave the house of the guardian.”
  • It would suffice if the prosecution proved that “though immediately prior to the minor leaving the father’s protection no active part was played by the accused, he had at some earlier stage solicited or persuaded the minor to do so”; absent that, merely helping her design not to return “falls short of an inducement… and is, therefore, not tantamount to ‘taking’.”
  • English support: Reg v. Christian Olifir (Baron Bramwell) and Rex v. James Jarvi (Jelf J.) — if the girl leaves of her own accord and the accused takes no active part, “his not doing so [returning her] is no infringement of this Act… for the Act does not say he shall restore her, but only that he shall not take her away”; and Halsbury: previous solicitation + subsequent harbouring can convict, but mere harbouring of a self-leaver cannot.
  • Protective object restated: §§361 and cognate sections “are intended more for the protection of the minors and persons of unsound mind themselves than of the rights of the guardians” (State v. Harbansing Kisansing, Gajendragadkar J.).
Principles evolved
  1. “Taking” requires causal agency: the accused’s act must be the cause of the minor leaving the guardian’s keeping; a minor of mature understanding who voluntarily joins the accused is not “taken”.
  2. Earlier-stage inducement suffices: solicitation/persuasion at any prior stage, operating on the minor’s mind, converts voluntary-joining into taking/enticing (the bridge later used in Vadgama).
  3. No duty to restore: the section prohibits taking, not non-restoration; harbouring a self-leaver, without more, is not kidnapping.
  4. Ward-centred object: the offence protects the minor, not merely the guardian’s custodial rights.

PART VAbduction — BNS § 138 ↔ IPC § 362

Statute — BNS § 138 / IPC § 362 (identical)
“Whoever by force compels, or by any deceitful means induces, any person to go from any place, is said to abduct that person.”

5.1 Ingredients

  1. Any person — no age or mental-state limit; unlike kidnapping, the victim may be a major of sound mind.
  2. Compulsion by force (actual or constructive) or inducement by deceitful means — the two alternative modes; a person who goes willingly, without force or deceit, is not abducted.
  3. Going from any place — movement procured by either mode.

5.2 Character of the offence (DU exam material)

  • Not a substantive offence: abduction “is not substantive offence” — it becomes punishable only when coupled with a further intent/purpose prescribed elsewhere (e.g., BNS §87/IPC §366 compel marriage or illicit intercourse; §140/§§364–367 murder, ransom, confinement, grievous hurt/slavery; §141/§366B importation).
  • Continuing offence: “It is continuing offence” — it persists as long as the compelled/deceived movement continues.
  • Consent matters here: if the person accompanies willingly and without deceit, §138 is not attracted — the mirror image of kidnapping, where the child’s consent is immaterial.

PART VIDU Case Law II — Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat

Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat
AIR 1973 SC 2313 · Coram: I.D. Dua, J. · Appeal by special leave against Gujarat High Court partly allowing appeal (full text in DU Casebook, Unit 8, pp. 246–254)
Facts (prosecution version, accepted by courts below)
  • The appellant, an industrialist of Jamnagar (factory at Bunder Road with adjoining bungalow), was introduced to Mohini’s family during the 1965 Pakistan bombardment when they shifted to Bhrol. On 18.12.1965 (her birthday) he presented Mohini — a school-going girl below 15 — with a Parker pen (returned at her mother’s instance).
  • He then took Mohini, her father, his manager Tribhovandas and Tribhovandas’ children on trips to Baroda (land-purchase negotiation; impression created that her father would manage the new factory) and, at Christmas 1965, to Bombay, where Mohini, Malati and the appellant slept in one room and intercourse is alleged to have occurred on two nights; skirts and waistbands were bought for Mohini and Malati.
  • Further alleged intercourse in March 1966 at his bungalow; a summer-1966 trip to Mahabaleshwar/Mount Abu where, at 3 a.m., Mohini’s mother saw the appellant sleeping by Mohini’s side with his hand on her head; the Dattatraya temple episode in which he placed his hands on Mohini’s and his own daughter Rekha’s heads and swore Mohini was like his daughter; thereafter intercepting Mohini returning from school and taking her to the bungalow.
  • From July 1966 the parents escorted Mohini to school; the mother’s letter of 26.09.1966 asked him to desist; in October 1966 he contacted her at a school camp in Ahmedabad. Mohini’s letters of Nov–Dec 1966 (Exs. P-20, P-16) complained of ill-treatment at home.
  • The culminating incident (16–17.01.1967): instead of school, Mohini went to the appellant’s factory per previous arrangement; he took her into the garage, made her write three letters on his dictation (to her father, the DSP and himself — alleging ill-treatment and stating she was leaving for Bombay after taking Rs 250); hid her in the dickey of his car for hours; had intercourse at midnight at the factory office; concealed her in a cellar and then an inner room of the garage; on hearing a car he sent her out to wait near Gandhinagar, where Sub-Inspector Chaudhary, arriving with the appellant, traced her at about 9 p.m. Her wet clothes, school books and exercise books were recovered from the dickey; unfinished food in the inner room bore the appellant’s utensils. The father’s complaint was lodged at 7.20 p.m. on 16.01.1967.
Procedural history

Sessions Judge, Jamnagar: convicted under §§366 and 376 IPC (18 months RI u/s 366; 2 years RI + Rs 500 fine u/s 376, concurrent). Gujarat High Court on appeal: acquitted of §376 (prosecutrix’s account of the charged night uncorroborated) but maintained the §366 conviction. Appeal by special leave by the accused.

Issues
  1. Whether, on the evidence, the appellant kidnapped (took/enticed) Mohini — a minor — out of the keeping of her lawful guardian with intent that she be forced or seduced to illicit intercourse (§366 IPC; cf. BNS §87).
  2. Whether S. Varadarajan protected him on the plea that Mohini came to him of her own accord, unhappy at home.
Arguments — Appellant (Shri Dhebar)
  • Mohini, feeling unhappy and harassed by unreasonably harsh parents, left home on her own accord and sought his help out of compassion; he was a mere refuge, not a kidnapper.
  • The parents’ erroneous suspicions and stern behaviour, not his conduct, induced her leaving; on Varadarajan a voluntary joining of the accused is not “taking”; the concurrent findings on evidence were untrustworthy.
Arguments — Respondent (State) / findings of courts below
  • The whole course of conduct — gifts, trips, shared rooms, the temple charade, secret correspondence, the dictated letters and the Rs 250 plant, the dickey concealment — shows a foundation laid by inducement and allurement operating on an immature schoolgirl’s mind, culminating in the 16 January departure; the dictated letters were stage-management against future prosecution.
  • Trial court (quoting Christian Olifier, 10 Cox 420): even if she had left at her own time, “if, finding she has left, he avails himself of that to induce her to continue away from her father’s custody… he is also guilty, if his persuasion operated on her mind so as to induce her to leave.”
Decision

Appeal dismissed; §366 conviction and sentence upheld (sentence “already very lenient” given the aggravating previous intimacy and the temple episode). The Supreme Court held:

  • Statutory semantics: “takes” = “to cause to go”, “to escort”, “to get into possession” (not necessarily force); “entice” = inducement/allurement raising hope or desire, which “may work immediately or… create continuous and gradual but imperceptible impression culminating after some time”; the two words “are… intended to be read together so that each takes to some extent its colour and content from the other”.
  • The Varadarajan reconciliation: “if the minor leaves her parental home completely uninfluenced by any promise, offer or inducement emanating from the guilty party, then the latter cannot be considered to have committed the offence as defined in Section 361… But if the guilty party has laid a foundation by inducement, allurement or threat, etc. and if this can be considered to have influenced or weighed with her in leaving her guardian’s custody… then prima facie it would be difficult for him to plead innocence on the ground that the minor had voluntarily come to him… the mere circumstance that his act was not the immediate cause of her leaving her parental home… would constitute no valid defence.”
  • Application: “The evidence of the constant behaviour of the appellant towards Mohini for several months preceding the incident… completely brings the case within the passage at p. 248 [of Varadarajan]… ample material showing earlier allurements and even of the appellant’s participation in the formation of Mohini’s intention and resolve to leave her father’s house.”
  • Evidentiary anchor: the letters (Exs. P-20, P-16) and the mother’s letter (Ex. P-27) — “how the mother of the girl belonging to a comparatively poorer family felt when confronted with a rich man’s dishonourable behaviour towards her young, impressionable immature daughter” — made the substratum of the prosecutrix’s story “probable and trustworthy”.
Principles evolved
  1. Foundation-by-inducement rule: earlier-stage allurement that weighs with the minor in leaving the guardian’s keeping satisfies “taking/enticing” even if the immediate departure looks voluntary — the accused cannot then shelter behind the minor’s willingness.
  2. Gradual enticement: inducement need not be immediate; a continuous, imperceptible impression culminating later suffices.
  3. Contextual reading of “takes” and “entices”: each colours the other; force is not required.
  4. Power-imbalance realism: the court weighed the rich-man/poor-family dynamic and the girl’s immaturity in assessing the truth of the enticement narrative (contrast the mature college student in Varadarajan).

PART VIIDU Case Law III — Anversinh alias Kiransinh Fatesinh Zala v. State of Gujarat

Anversinh alias Kiransinh Fatesinh Zala v. State of Gujarat
(2021) 3 SCC 12 · Coram: Surya Kant, J. (with M.R. Shah, J.) · Criminal appeal against Gujarat High Court order dated 28.07.2009 (full text in DU Casebook, Unit 8, pp. 255–258; heard via video conferencing)
Facts
  • On the night of 14.05.1998, complainant Kiransinh Jalamsinh (PW-1) learnt from his wife that their eldest daughter (PW-3, the prosecutrix), sixteen years old, Class-VII educated, working as a maid, had not returned from her second shift. A watchman said she was last seen leaving vacant Bungalow No. 4 of Ramjani Society with the appellant; the appellant had left for Surpur with her.
  • Police complaint on 16.05.1998; both traced to a farm near Modasa and brought back on 21.05.1998; medical examination and seizure of clothes followed.
  • Prosecutrix’s case: caught on her way to work, forcibly taken by auto-rickshaw to a bus stand, then by bus to the appellant’s village; repeatedly raped and pressurised into a marriage ceremony. In cross-examination she admitted being in love with the appellant, prior consensual intercourse, and previous meetings outside home. She sat with other passengers on the back seat of the auto (appellant in front); spent a week at his village working and living “akin to husband and wife”.
  • School record (PW-2): date of birth 08.02.1982 ⇒ 16 years, 3 months, 6 days at the occurrence. Panch witnesses PW-4 and PW-6 turned hostile; PW-5 (doctor) proved intercourse; the IO (PW-8) admitted the FIR omitted the force-abduction claim and that her §161 CrPC statement spoke of a month-long acquaintance, a regular physical relationship in a damaged bungalow, and running away together to work on Bhikabhai’s farm.
Procedural history

Additional Sessions Judge (16.12.2002): convicted under §§363, 366 and 376 IPC (1 yr + fine u/s 363; 5 yrs + fine u/s 366; 10 yrs + fine u/s 376). Gujarat High Court (28.07.2009): set aside §376 (love affair established; no protest despite opportunities) but sustained §§363/366 (undisputed minority; no evidence she consented to be taken from custody). Appeal to Supreme Court against the kidnapping convictions.

Issues
  1. Whether, despite a consensual love affair and the prosecutrix’s voluntary company, the ingredients of kidnapping (§§361/363; BNS §137) and §366 (BNS §87) stand proved.
  2. Whether the High Court’s twin findings (love affair ⇒ no rape; but no consent to leaving custody ⇒ kidnapping) are mutually contradictory.
  3. What sentence is appropriate on the special facts?
Arguments — Appellant
  • The High Court’s findings are mutually contradictory: having held a love affair and voluntary company (hence acquittal u/s 376), it could not hold she did not willingly leave custody.
  • S. Varadarajan: voluntary abandonment of home by a minor girl is not kidnapping; absent active involvement there is no “taking” or “enticing”; recovery of a missing minor from a stranger’s custody is not ipso facto kidnapping (King Emperor v. Gokaran; Emperor v. Abdur Rahman).
Arguments — Respondent (State)
  • Concurrent findings plus the plain statutory language: consent of a girl below 18 is no excuse in kidnapping u/s 361; the evidence shows the appellant induced the prosecutrix to reach a designated place to accompany him — enticement proved.
Decision

Conviction under §§363/366 IPC upheld; sentence reduced to the period of imprisonment already undergone; appellant set free; appeal partly allowed. The Court (Surya Kant, J.) held:

  • Ingredients restated: §361 requires an act of enticing or taking, minority, and the care/keep of a lawful guardian; “such ‘enticement’ need not be direct or immediate in time and can also be through subtle actions like winning over the affection of a minor girl”; mere recovery of a missing minor from a stranger does not ipso facto establish kidnapping.
  • Application: the appellant “unintentionally admitted his culpability” — recovery from his custody plus admitted sexual relationship and intention to marry; eye-witnesses showed he “had drawn the prosecutrix out of the custody of her parents”; the evidence “unequivocally suggests that the appellant induced the prosecutrix to reach at a designated place to accompany him”.
  • Consent immaterial: “Such a plea [consensual affair]… cannot be acceded to given the unambiguous language of the statute as the prosecutrix was admittedly below 18 years of age”; and unlike Varadarajan’s Savitri, there is “little to suggest that she was aware of the full purport of her actions or that she possessed the mental acuities and maturity to take care of herself” — young, little-educated, actions not thought through.
  • Sentencing (humane approach): applying State of M.P. v. Surendra Singh (proportionality; no mechanical reduction), five factors justified reduction to period undergone: (i) no force, pre-planning, weapon or vulgar motive; (ii) the appellant’s own young age (about 18–19) — “hope for reform, and not punitively”; (iii) 22 years’ delay, both parties now settled in their forties; (iv) crime of passion, no antecedents, low recidivism; (v) unlike Raja Ram and Vadgama, “no grotesque misuse of power, wealth, status or age” — same social class and cultural vicinity; “if not for the age of the prosecutrix, the two could have been happily married”; the offence is mala prohibita, not mala in se.
Principles evolved
  1. Minority is the statute’s core: where the child is below the protected age and the accused’s inducement is proved, the love-affair defence collapses — consent of the minor is immaterial (contrast Varadarajan on its mature-minor, no-inducement facts).
  2. Subtle enticement counts: winning over a minor girl’s affection and inducing her to a designated place is “enticing”.
  3. Varadarajan distinguished, not overruled: its “voluntary joining” exit applies only where the minor’s departure is uninfluenced and she has capacity to know the full import of her act.
  4. Sentencing realism: mala prohibita love-elopement cases without force or power-imbalance merit rehabilitative, not punitive, sentencing after long delay (Surendra Singh proportionality).
The trilogy synthesised — one rule, three fact-patterns Rule: kidnapping turns on the accused’s causal agency in the child’s departure — “taking” (cause to go) or “enticing” (inducement, immediate or gradual); the child’s consent never excuses. Varadarajan = departure wholly self-generated by a near-major, educated minor; accused’s later facilitation ≠ taking ⇒ acquit. Vadgama = months of gifts, trips and intimacy laid the foundation; her “voluntary” walk to his factory was the fruit of his inducement ⇒ convict. Anversinh = love affair plus proved inducement of an immature 16-year-old ⇒ convict, but sentence tempered to period undergone. In every problem answer, locate the facts on this axis first.

PART VIIITrafficking of Person — BNS § 143 ↔ IPC §§ 370, 370A

Statute — BNS § 143 (as per course gazette)
143. (1) Whoever, for the purpose of exploitation recruits, transports, harbours, transfers, or receives a person or persons, by–– (a) using threats; or (b) using force, or any other form of coercion; or (c) by abduction; or (d) by practising fraud, or deception; or (e) by abuse of power; or (f) by inducement, including the giving or receiving of payments or benefits, in order to achieve the consent of any person having control over the person recruited, transported, harboured, transferred or received, commits the offence of trafficking.
Explanation 1. — “exploitation” shall include any act of physical exploitation or any form of sexual exploitation, slavery or practices similar to slavery, servitude, beggary or forced removal of organs.
Explanation 2. — The consent of the victim is immaterial in determination of the offence of trafficking.
(2) Trafficking: rigorous imprisonment 7–10 years + fine. (3) More than one person: RI 10 years–life + fine. (4) Trafficking of a child: RI 10 years–life + fine. (5) More than one child: RI 14 years–life + fine. (6) Repeat conviction for child trafficking: life (remainder of natural life) + fine. (7) Public servant or police officer involved: life (remainder of natural life) + fine.

IPC §370 (inserted by Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 13 of 2013) defined trafficking similarly with 7–10 years / 10–life gradations; §370A punished exploitation of trafficked persons. BNS §144 (= §370A, changed) punishes engaging a trafficked child for sexual exploitation with RI 5–10 years, and a trafficked person with RI 3–5 years (check bare act for the adult limb).

8.1 Elements — the “act + means + purpose” triad (Palermo-style, now codified)

  1. Act: recruits, transports, harbours, transfers or receives a person/persons (any one).
  2. Means: threats; force/coercion; abduction; fraud/deception; abuse of power; or inducement/payments to obtain the consent of a controller of the victim (any one).
  3. Purpose: exploitation — physical exploitation, any sexual exploitation, slavery or slavery-like practices, servitude, beggary, or forced organ removal (Explanation 1).
  4. Consent irrelevant (Explanation 2) — and, where any listed means is used, the victim’s consent is legally void anyway.

PART IXChild-Protective Offences — BNS §§ 95, 98, 99 (↔ IPC 372, 373; §95 new)

Statute — BNS § 95 (NEW; no IPC counterpart)
“Whoever hires, employs or engages any child to commit an offence shall be punished with imprisonment of either description which shall not be less than three years but which may extend to ten years, and with fine; and if the offence be committed shall also be punished with the punishment provided for that offence as if the offence has been committed by such person himself.
Explanation.—Hiring, employing, engaging or using a child for sexual exploitation or pornography is covered within the meaning of this section.”
  • Elements: (i) hiring, employing or engaging; (ii) of any child (u/s 2(3): below 18); (iii) to commit an offence. The inchoate use of a child as an instrument of crime is itself the offence — minimum three years.
  • Double-liability clause: if the offence is actually committed, the hirer is punished for it too, as if he committed it himself — a statutory vicarious-extension overriding the child’s own immunity (BNS §§20–21 / IPC §§82–83).
  • Explanation: using a child for sexual exploitation or pornography is deemed within the section — dovetailing with §144 BNS, ITPA and POCSO.
Statute — BNS §§ 98 & 99 (↔ IPC §§ 372, 373)
98. Whoever sells, lets to hire, or otherwise disposes of any child with intent that such child shall at any age be employed or used for the purpose of prostitution or illicit intercourse with any person or for any unlawful and immoral purpose, or knowing it to be likely that such child will at any age be employed or used for any such purpose — imprisonment up to ten years + fine.
Explanation 1. — When a female under eighteen is sold, let for hire, or otherwise disposed of to a prostitute or to any person who keeps or manages a brothel, the disposer shall, until the contrary is proved, be presumed to have disposed of her with the intent that she shall be used for prostitution.
Explanation 2. — “illicit intercourse” = sexual intercourse between persons not united by marriage or by a union recognised by personal law/custom as a quasi-marital relation.

99. Whoever buys, hires or otherwise obtains possession of any child with the like intent or knowledge — imprisonment not less than seven years but up to fourteen years + fine.
Explanation 1. — A prostitute or brothel-keeper who buys, hires or obtains possession of a female under eighteen is presumed, until contrary proved, to have obtained her for prostitution. Explanation 2. — “illicit intercourse” as in §98.
  • §98 = supply side; §99 = demand side — the two sections criminalise the market in children for sexual and immoral exploitation; both turn on intent or knowledge of the likely use “at any age” (the futurity of the intended use is immaterial).
  • Reversed onus: the presumptions in the Explanations shift the burden to the disposer/buyer-brothel-keeper once transfer of a female under 18 to a prostitute/brothel is proved.
  • Sentencing asymmetry (BNS change): the buyer (§99: 7–14 years) is now punished more severely than the seller (§98: up to 10 years) — IPC §§372/373 both capped at 10 years; the legislature rated the demand side graver.

PART XActs Endangering Sovereignty, Unity & Integrity of India — BNS § 152 (replacing IPC § 124A sedition)

Statute — BNS § 152 (as per course gazette)
“Whoever, purposely or knowingly, by words, either spoken or written, or by signs, or by visible representation, or by electronic communication or by use of financial means, or otherwise, excites or attempts to excite, secession or armed rebellion or subversive activities, or encourages feelings of separatist activities or endangers sovereignty or unity and integrity of India; or indulges in or commits any such act shall be punished with imprisonment for life or with imprisonment which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine.
Explanation.––Comments expressing disapprobation of the measures, or administrative or other action of the Government with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means without exciting or attempting to excite the activities referred to in this section do not constitute an offence under this section.”

10.1 Lineage: from sedition to §152

  • IPC §124A (sedition) punished bringing “into hatred or contempt, or excit[ing]… disaffection towards the Government established by law”, with Explanations 2–3 saving lawful disapprobation. The DU comparative table records §124A as “Deleted” and §152 as a “New Section”.
  • Constitutional gloss (Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar, AIR 1962 SC 955 — in DU/KD Gaur material): §§124A and 505 are valid only if read down — they “apply only to speeches and writing having intention or tendency to incite others to create public disorder or to violence. Criticism of government, however strongly worded, does not fall within the ambit of these sections.” The KD Gaur commentary adds that sedition “means a stirring up of rebellion against the government… activities… calculated to disturb the tranquility of the State, and lead people to subvert the government established by law.”
  • What §152 changes: (i) the protected object is named constitutionally — sovereignty, unity, integrity, security (and economic security via counterfeiting limbs elsewhere) — plus secession, armed rebellion, subversive and separatist activities; (ii) new media limbs — electronic communication and use of financial means; (iii) mens rea expressly “purposely or knowingly”; (iv) punishment life or up to 7 years + fine (IPC §124A: life or up to 3 years); (v) the saving Explanation is retained in substance — disapprobation seeking lawful alteration is not an offence. The Kedar Nath incitement-to-violence threshold, read into the old provision as a condition of constitutionality, remains the interpretive anchor for the new one, as Vinod Dua (decided on §124A) demonstrates.

PART XIDU Case Law IV (asterisked) — Vinod Dua v. Union of India

Vinod Dua v. Union of India & Ors.
AIR 2021 SC 3239 · Writ Petition (Crl.) No. 154 of 2020 · Decided 03.06.2021 · Coram: Uday Umesh Lalit & Vineet Saran, JJ. (judgment by Uday Umesh Lalit, J.) · asterisked additional case in DU Unit-8 case list (summarised from the judgment; sedition lineage per KD Gaur course material)
Facts
  • The petitioner, a senior journalist of about four decades’ standing, hosted “The Vinod Dua Show” on YouTube. In the episode uploaded on 30.03.2020 (early COVID-19 lockdown), he critically appraised the Government’s preparedness and management: that terror attacks (Pathankot, Pulwama), surgical strikes and the Balakot air-strikes had been used as political events to garner votes; that India lacked adequate testing facilities, PPE kits, masks and ventilators, which were instead being exported until 24.03.2020; and that supply chains had disrupted.
  • Respondent No. 3 complained that these assertions (segments at 5:09 and 5:45 of the video) were false and malicious, spread fear/alarm, incited violence and disturbed public tranquillity; FIR No. 0053 dated 06.05.2020 was registered at Police Station Kumarsain, District Shimla, Himachal Pradesh, under IPC §§ 124A, 268, 501 and 505.
  • The petitioner invoked Article 32 seeking (a) quashing of the FIR, and (b) a direction that FIRs against media persons with 10+ years’ standing be registered only after clearance by a State-level committee including the Chief Justice of the High Court or his nominee, the Leader of the Opposition and the Home Minister.
Issues
  1. Whether the talk-show comments, critical of the Government’s pandemic management and of its use of security events in politics, disclose the offence of sedition (§124A) or the other FIR sections.
  2. Whether a journalist’s critical appraisal, absent incitement to violence or public disorder, is protected by Article 19(1)(a) as read down in Kedar Nath Singh.
Arguments — Petitioner
  • The transcript shows journalistic critical appraisal — “our job is to do critical appraisal of government’s work” — not incitement; on Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (AIR 1962 SC 955), sedition requires tendency/intention to incite violence or public disorder, which is absent.
  • Prosecution for such speech chills free speech and violates Art. 19(1)(a); the FIR is an instrument of harassment and deserves quashing; structural safeguards for journalists were sought.
Arguments — Respondents
  • The statements spread false information and panic during a pandemic, defeating the lockdown and inducing offences against the State and public tranquillity — within §§124A/505 and the complained provisions (including disobedience to lockdown orders, §188-type liability).
Decision

Writ petition allowed; FIR quashed. The Supreme Court held:

  • The two impugned segments, read in the full transcript, are critical appraisal of government policy; they do not excite or attempt to excite hatred, contempt or disaffection with the requisite tendency to violence or public disorder.
  • Kedar Nath governs: sedition is attracted only where speech has the intention or tendency to incite violence or disorder; strongly worded criticism without such tendency is protected — the Explanation to §124A (and its BNS §152 successor) expressly saves disapprobation seeking lawful alteration.
  • “Every journalist is entitled to protection” under the Kedar Nath verdict — the Court underscored the press’s role in a democracy and quashed the proceedings as violative of Art. 19(1)(a). (The broader relief of a pre-FIR screening committee for journalists was not granted as a blanket direction; the quashing itself rested on the absence of sedition ingredients.)
Principles evolved / significance for BNS §152
  1. Incitement threshold constitutionalised: the Kedar Nath reading-down survives the repeal of §124A; §152’s “purposely or knowingly… excites… secession/armed rebellion/subversion… or endangers sovereignty/unity/integrity” must be construed with the same violence-or-disorder threshold, reinforced by §152’s own Explanation.
  2. Dissent is not sedition: criticism of the government’s measures — however stark, satirical or politically pointed — is democratic dialogue, not an offence against the State.
  3. Press freedom: journalists enjoy the Kedar Nath protection as part of Art. 19(1)(a); penal provisions may not be used as tools of harassment for critical reportage.
  4. Exam bridge: in a §152 problem, first apply the Explanation (disapprobation for lawful alteration), then the Kedar Nath/Vinod Dua incitement test; only speech purposively exciting secession/armed rebellion/subversion or endangering unity/integrity — with a tendency to violence or disorder — crosses into the offence.

DU PYQ · Q1/2024 · Problem · 20 marks
“Aadya, a minor girl of age 17 years was living with her mother when she developed intimacy with a boy of 20 years old named Vijay who was working as a helper in the nearby grocery shop. Her parents were divorced. Her father often visited her during weekend and he did not like his daughter being close to Vijay. Vijay had another girlfriend but was also fond of Aadya. Vijay and Aadya decided to marry each other and eloped together before her 18th birthday party from her father’s house. Father filed an FIR against Vijay. After 15 days Vijay was seen by the police in the city along with Aadya and was arrested and prosecuted for the offence of Kidnapping. Decide whether Vijay can be convicted or acquitted. Will your answer differ if Aadya’s parents were not divorced and were living together?”
Model answer

Issue: whether elopement by a 17-year-old girl with her 20-year-old boyfriend, decided jointly, amounts to kidnapping from lawful guardianship; and whether the parents’ marital status alters the analysis.

Provision: BNS §137(1)(b) + §137(2) (IPC §§361, 363): taking or enticing a child (u/s 2(3): below 18 — Aadya at 17 qualifies; under IPC too, a female under 18) out of the keeping of the lawful guardian without the guardian’s consent; the child’s own consent is wholly immaterial (State of Haryana v. Raja Ram; Anversinh Zala).

Application — the Varadarajan/Vadgama axis: On the stated facts the intimacy was mutual and pre-existing (“developed intimacy”, “Vijay… was also fond of Aadya”), and the elopement was a joint decision (“Vijay and Aadya decided to marry each other and eloped”). Unlike Vadgama, there is no pleaded foundation of inducement, allurement or promise emanating from Vijay that operated on Aadya’s mind to leave home; unlike Anversinh, there is no proved inducement to a designated place. The fact-pattern is closest to S. Varadarajan: a minor who, knowing the import of her act, voluntarily leaves the guardian’s house and is then accompanied by the accused; “something more has to be shown… some kind of inducement held out by the accused person or an active participation by him in the formation of the intention of the minor to leave the house of the guardian.” Aadya, though a child in law, is on the mature side of the spectrum (17, city-bred) and the initiative appears shared. However — and this is the examiner’s trap — the prosecution may lead evidence of prior solicitation (messages, promises of marriage); if proved, Vadgama converts the “voluntary” elopement into enticement, and Anversinh reminds us that a love affair is no defence where minority and inducement coexist. On the bare facts given, the balance tilts to acquittal on the Varadarajan principle, with a caution that any proved earlier inducement reverses the result.

The divorce twist: it does not change the answer in law. “Lawful guardian” (Explanation to §137(1)(b)) includes any person lawfully entrusted with care or custody; both divorced parents remain lawful guardians — here the mother, with whom Aadya lived, and the father exercising visiting custody. Taking her out of either’s keeping without that guardian’s consent satisfies the ingredient; the father’s FIR and weekend custody make him a lawful guardian whose consent was absent. If the parents lived together, the same rule applies a fortiori (joint keeping). The marital status of guardians goes to who the lawful guardian is, not to whether the offence exists. (Contrast the Q5/2020 variant where the girl herself persuades the boy — pure Varadarajan acquittal.)

Conclusion: On the pleaded facts, Vijay should be acquitted of kidnapping (BNS §137(2)/IPC §363) for want of proved taking/enticing; the answer does not differ if the parents were not divorced, because lawful guardianship — not marital unity — is the statutory criterion. Add: if evidence of prior inducement emerges, conviction follows (Vadgama), and if compulsion or deceit were shown, §138 abduction with purpose under §87 would also arise.

DU PYQ · Q2(b)/2023 · Problem · 20 marks
“‘S’ and ‘P’ lived in the same slum in Bombay. ‘S’ aged 21 years enticed ‘P’ aged 15 years 6 months out of lawful guardianship and took her with him to Delhi. There he forced her to work as domestic help at Mr. K’s house. She stayed at Mr. K’s house for next three days and fourth day she got a chance to run away. While ‘P’ was loitering aimlessly on the streets of Delhi she met ‘N’ and narrated her ordeal to him. Sympathizing with her, ‘N’ took her to his house to contact and locate her parents. ‘P’ was finally recovered from N’s house by Police. Decide the liability of ‘S’ and ‘N’ if any by citing relevant case laws.”
Model answer

Liability of S: (i) Kidnapping from lawful guardianship — BNS §137(1)(b)/§137(2) (IPC §§361/363): P (15½) is a child; “enticed… out of lawful guardianship” is expressly pleaded — enticement = inducement/allurement raising hope or desire (Vadgama); guardian’s consent absent; P’s consent immaterial (Raja Ram). Offence complete on taking; continuing until return (Jeevan v. Rex). Punishment up to 7 years + fine. (ii) Super-added purpose offences: forcing her to work as domestic help — if by force/deceit, abduction (BNS §138/IPC §362, a continuing offence) read with the purpose limb; and on the exploitation purpose, consider trafficking (BNS §143/IPC §370: transporting + coercion/fraud for exploitation including servitude/beggary — Explanation 1; consent immaterial) with the 10-years-to-life child limb u/s 143(4); and BNS §95/— if she was engaged to commit any offence; forced labour also attracts BNS §146 (IPC §374, unlawful compulsory labour). (iii) Placement at K’s house: K, if knowingly harbouring/confineing a kidnapped child, faces BNS §142 (IPC §368) — but the question excludes K.

Liability of N: none. BNS §142 (IPC §368) punishes one who, knowing that a person has been kidnapped or abducted, wrongfully conceals or confines her with the same intention/purpose. N’s mens rea is the opposite — sympathy and the purpose of reuniting P with her parents; there is no wrongful concealment (a bona fide protective custody pending location of parents is not “wrongful”), and no kidnapping-purpose. Analogously, the Varadarajan principle that the law imposes no duty to restore but also criminalises only taking/enticing with a culpable purpose leaves N outside the net; his conduct is morally laudable and legally innocent.

Conclusion: S guilty of kidnapping (and, on the exploitation purpose, trafficking/forced-labour offences); N not guilty of any offence.

DU PYQ · Q2/2021 · Problem · 20 marks
“Suhail, a young man residing in Delhi became friendly with Mariyam, the 17 years old daughter of Mr. Feroz, who was his immediate neighbour. Mr. Feroz realising the intimacy between Suhail and his daughter, sent his daughter to his brother’s house in Jammu. But Suhail tracked Mariyam and persuaded her to accompany him to Kanpur, his native place with a promise to marry her. However, Suhail was arrested at the Jammu Railway Station along with Mariyam on a complaint by her father that she has been kidnapped. Decide the criminal liability of Suhail. Also, after separation from Suhail, Mariyam with an intention to kill herself took overdose of sleeping pills but was taken to hospital and survived. Has she committed any offence? Decide with the help of legal provisions and judicial precedents.”
Model answer

Part I — Suhail: Guilty of kidnapping. Mariyam (17) is a child (BNS §2(3); IPC: female u/18). She remained in her father’s keeping even at the uncle’s Jammu house — exactly as Savitri remained in her father’s keeping at K. Natarajan’s house in Varadarajan. The decisive fact is that Suhail “tracked Mariyam and persuaded her to accompany him… with a promise to marry her”: this is precisely the “something more” — inducement held out and active participation in the formation of her intention — that Varadarajan demands and that Vadgama finds in earlier allurements; the promise of marriage is a classic enticement raising hope/d desire (cf. Q2(b)/2018 Mira–Gopal, where “Gopal suggested… promised to marry her” = guilty). Her consent is immaterial (Raja Ram, Anversinh). Arrest at the railway station is immaterial — the offence was complete when she was taken/enticed out of keeping (continuing offence, Jeevan v. Rex). Liability: BNS §137(2)/IPC §363 (up to 7 years + fine); if the purpose was to compel marriage or force/seduce to illicit intercourse, add BNS §87/IPC §366 (up to 10 years + fine) as in Vadgama/Anversinh.

Part II — Mariyam’s suicide attempt: Under the IPC regime applicable in 2021, §309 IPC (attempt to commit suicide) was the provision; however §115(1) of the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 raises a presumption of severe stress and provides that a person who attempts suicide shall be presumed to be suffering from mental illness and shall not be tried and punished under §309 — in substance decriminalising the attempt and routing her to care. Under the BNS (post-1.7.2024) the position is clearer still: the BNS omits any offence of attempt to suicide altogether — there is no counterpart to §309 — so Mariyam commits no offence; her case is one for treatment, not prosecution. (Abetment of another’s suicide remains an offence: BNS §§107–108 / IPC §§305–306 — not attracted here.)

Conclusion: Suhail guilty of kidnapping (with §87/§366 if purpose proved); Mariyam guilty of no offence.

DU PYQ · Q5/2020 · Problem · 25 marks
“A minor girl of age 17 years was living with her parents when she developed intimacy with a boy of 20 years old who was working as a helper in the nearby grocery shop. She was frequently seen in the company of this boy and always looked for excuses to visit that particular grocery shop. One fine day she left her house on her own and reached the shop where she persuaded this boy to take her to some other town so that they both could marry and be with each other always. The accused took her to several places. He was then arrested and prosecuted for kidnapping. Decide, if he should be convicted or acquitted. Give reasons.”
Model answer

Issue: whether a boy who is persuaded by the minor herself to take her away commits kidnapping.

Rule: BNS §137(1)(b)/IPC §361 requires taking or enticing by the accused out of the guardian’s keeping; “taking or enticing away a minor out of the keeping of a lawful guardian is an essential ingredient” (Varadarajan); where the minor leaves home uninfluenced by any promise/inducement of the accused and, with capacity to know the import of her act, voluntarily joins him, there is no taking; mere subsequent facilitation (“taking her along with him from place to place”) “falls short of an inducement… and is, therefore, not tantamount to ‘taking’”.

Application: The girl “left her house on her own” and then “persuaded this boy” — the direction of inducement is reversed: it is the minor who solicits, mirroring Savitri telephoning Varadarajan and insisting on marriage. There is no earlier-stage solicitation by the boy (the Vadgama bridge is absent on these facts); the intimacy and shop-visits show her initiative, not his allurement. His carrying her to several places is facilitation of her design, not enticement out of keeping. Following Varadarajan (and the English authorities it adopts — Christian Olifir, Jarvi: “if she was determined to leave her home… and insisted on leaving with him… you must acquit him”), and the DU exam-material answer key to this very question, he should be acquitted.

Distinguish carefully in exam: (i) if the boy had earlier persuaded/solicited her (Q2/2021 Suhail; Q2(b)/2018 Gopal), conviction follows — Vadgama; (ii) if she were of tender years or immature understanding, her “voluntariness” would not avail him — Anversinh; (iii) her consent never validates the taking where he is the causal agent — Raja Ram.

Conclusion: Acquittal of kidnapping (BNS §137(2)/IPC §363); no abduction either, absent force or deceitful means (§138/§362).

DU PYQ · Q2(b)/2018 · Problem · 15 marks
“Mira, a girl of 16 years, was living with her aged father and step mother who used to ill-treat her. She would often narrate her woes to Gopal, her 22 year old neighbour. One day Gopal suggested that they go to Mumbai and start their lives fresh. He also promised to marry her. Mira agreed and as planned they boarded the train to Mumbai. What offence, if any, has been committed by Gopal?”
Model answer

Rule & application: Mira (16) is a child under both regimes (IPC female u/18; BNS u/18). Gopal’s suggestion (“let us go to Mumbai and start fresh”) plus promise of marriage is inducement/allurement raising hope and desire — “enticement” in the Vadgama sense; it operated on her mind to leave her father’s keeping without his consent. Ill-treatment at home explains her receptivity but does not negate his causal role: unlike Varadarajan, the initiative and the promise emanate from the accused; the home-unhappiness merely supplied fertile ground, as in Vadgama where Mohini’s letters of grievance were exploited by the accused’s earlier allurements. Her consent is immaterial.

Offences: kidnapping from lawful guardianship — BNS §137(1)(b)/§137(2) (IPC §§361/363); and, the promise of marriage indicating intent that she be compelled/induced to marry or be seduced, BNS §87 (IPC §366) — up to 10 years + fine. (DU answer key: “It was Gopal who ‘enticed’ Mira… He also promised to marry her. Gopal is guilty of offence of kidnapping.”)

DU Pattern · Problem · 20 marks
Q6. R runs a “placement agency”. He recruits 12 girls aged 15–17 from villages by promising parents well-paid schooling-cum-work in the city, transports them to Delhi, harbours them in a locked flat and transfers them to households for unpaid domestic work; two are sexually exploited by a household head. A constable tips him off about raids for a monthly fee. Discuss R’s liability under the BNS, naming every section attracted and the applicable punishment tiers.
Model answer

Trafficking — §143: act = recruits, transports, harbours, transfers; means = fraud/deception (false promises to parents), abuse of power, coercion (locked flat); purpose = exploitation (physical exploitation, servitude, sexual exploitation — Explanation 1); consent of victims/parents immaterial (Explanation 2; parental consent procured by fraud is void in any event). Tiers: more than one person → §143(3) RI 10 yrs–life; children → §143(4) RI 10 yrs–life; more than one child → §143(5) RI 14 yrs–life + fine (12 children ⇒ this tier).

Sexual exploitation of trafficked children — §144(1): engaging trafficked children for sexual exploitation: RI 5–10 yrs + fine (the household head independently liable; R as abettor/transferor within §§143–144).

Kidnapping limb — §137(1)(b): each girl enticed/taken out of lawful guardianship by deceit without guardian’s true consent (consent vitiated by misconception, §28/§90) ⇒ §137(2) up to 7 yrs + fine; if any were maimed or used for begging, §139 tiers apply.

Child-as-instrument / forced labour: §95 if any child was engaged to commit an offence; §146 (IPC §374) unlawful compulsory labour.

Public-servant limb — §143(7): the constable, a police officer involved in trafficking, faces life = remainder of natural life + fine; R’s bribery of him may additionally attract the bribery provisions and §143(7) jurisprudence.

Conclusion: R liable under §§143(5), 144(1), 137(2), 146 (and §95 if made out), with concurrent special-law liability (ITPA, POCSO) noted; the graded minima make §143(5) the sentencing anchor.

DU Pattern · Problem · 15 marks
Q7. M, needing cash, agrees with a broker to “place” her 16-year-old daughter with a brothel-keeper in Delhi for ₹50,000; the broker pockets a commission; the brothel-keeper takes the girl into his brothel. All three are prosecuted. Analyse under BNS §§98, 99 and 143, including the statutory presumptions.
Model answer

M (seller): §98 — sells/lets to hire/otherwise disposes of a child with intent (or knowledge of likelihood) that she be used for prostitution: up to 10 yrs + fine. Explanation 1 presumption: a female under 18 disposed of to a prostitute/brothel-keeper is presumed disposed for prostitution “until the contrary is proved” — the burden shifts to M.

Broker: abettor/facilitator of §98 (and of §143 trafficking: transfer/receipt by inducement-payments “to achieve the consent of any person having control over the person” — clause (f)); also within §143(1) means (fraud/inducement) and purpose (sexual exploitation) ⇒ §143(4) child tier RI 10 yrs–life.

Brothel-keeper (buyer): §99 — buys/hires/obtains possession of a child with prostitutive intent: 7–14 yrs + fine; Explanation 1 presumption applies squarely (brothel-keeper obtaining a female u/18). Plus §143(4) trafficking (receipt + harbour for sexual exploitation) and §144(1) if engaged for sexual exploitation; ITPA offences concurrently.

Conclusion: the three-tier market is fully covered: supply (§98), intermediation (§143 clauses (d)/(f)), demand (§99), exploitation (§144); presumptions reverse the onus on intent at both ends.

DU Pattern · Theory · 15 marks
Q8. “Sedition is dead; long live §152.” Explain the offence of acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India under BNS §152, its relationship with IPC §124A and the Kedar Nath–Vinod Dua constitutional threshold. Would a TV debate calling for a region’s separation, and a newspaper editorial demanding reversal of a farm policy, fall within §152?
Model answer

Provision & elements: §152 punishes whoever, purposely or knowingly, by spoken/written words, signs, visible representation, electronic communication or use of financial means, or otherwise, excites or attempts to excite secession, armed rebellion or subversive activities, encourages feelings of separatist activities, or endangers sovereignty or unity and integrity of India, or indulges in/commits any such act — life or up to 7 years + fine. The Explanation saves comments expressing disapprobation of governmental measures/administrative action with a view to obtain their alteration by lawful means.

Lineage: IPC §124A (sedition) stands deleted per the DU comparative table; §152 is its successor with widened media and finance limbs, express mens rea and higher upper sentence. Kedar Nath Singh (AIR 1962 SC 955) read §124A down to acts with tendency/intention to incite violence or public disorder — “criticism of government, however strongly worded”, being outside it; Vinod Dua (AIR 2021 SC 3239) applied that shield to a journalist’s COVID critique and quashed the FIR, holding every journalist entitled to Kedar Nath protection. That constitutional threshold travels with the concept into §152.

Applications: (i) TV debate calling for a region’s separation: if the speaker purposely excites/encourages separatist activities or secession — the very objects named in §152 — the Explanation offers no cover (it saves disapprobation of measures, not advocacy of secession); subject to proof of purpose/knowledge and, on Kedar Nath, a tendency towards disorder/violence where the speech is merely abstract advocacy, the section is attracted. (ii) Editorial demanding reversal of a farm policy: squarely within the Explanation — disapprobation of measures seeking alteration by lawful means — and within Vinod Dua’s protected criticism; no offence.

Conclusion: §152 criminalises purposive excitement of secession/rebellion/subversion and endangerment of unity/integrity (including by e-communication and money), while democratic dissent seeking lawful change remains constitutionally protected.

DU Pattern · Short notes · 5 marks each
Q9. Write short notes on: (a) “keeping of the lawful guardian”; (b) taking vs enticing; (c) abduction as a non-substantive, continuing offence; (d) the Exception to BNS §137(1)(b).
Model answer

(a) Keeping: the guardian’s “charge, protection, maintenance and control” (Raja Ram, quoted in Vadgama); physical possession unnecessary — continuous control suffices; a child placed with a relative, schoolmaster or employer remains in the keeping of the lawful guardian (Explanation; Varadarajan; Fatti v. Emperor on “lawful” vs “legal”).

(b) Taking vs enticing: “takes” = cause to go, escort, get into possession — force not required; “entice” = inducement/allurement exciting hope or desire, immediate or gradual (Vadgama); read together, each colouring the other; a minor’s wholly self-generated departure, voluntarily joining the accused, is neither, absent earlier solicitation (Varadarajan).

(c) Abduction: §138/§362 defines, but does not itself punish, compulsion by force or inducement by deceitful means of any person to go from any place; punishment attaches only via purpose sections (§§87, 140, 141 BNS); it is a continuing offence during the compelled movement; free consent negatives it — unlike kidnapping where the child’s consent is immaterial.

(d) Exception: protects one who in good faith believes himself the father of an illegitimate child, or entitled to lawful custody of the child — e.g., a parent asserting custodial claim — unless the act is for an immoral or unlawful purpose; the good-faith belief must be honest and reasonable, and the immoral-purpose proviso prevents abuse (e.g., taking a child ostensibly under a custody claim for sale or exploitation).
