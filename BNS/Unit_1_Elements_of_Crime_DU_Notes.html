Nature and Definition of Crime

“What is a crime?” — the question with which the DU Case Material opens (edited from R.C. Nigam, Law of Crimes in India 25–37 (1965))

1.1 Why the law begins with the question “What is law?”

Traditionally, law is known to be a command enjoining a course of conduct — the command of a sovereign or of political superiors to political inferiors, or of a duly constituted legislature to all members of society. A crime may therefore be an act of disobedience to such a law. But disobedience of all laws is not crime — disobedience of civil laws, laws of inheritance or contract does not make one a criminal. A crime therefore means something more than mere disobedience to law:

“It means an act which is both forbidden by law and revolting to the moral sentiments of the society.” — R.C. Nigam (DU Case Material, General Introduction)

Thus, robbery or murder is a crime because it is revolting to the moral sentiments of society; a breach of revenue or contract law is not. But “moral sentiments of society” is itself a flexible term — it changes with the growth of public opinion and the social necessities of the times, and differs from country to country.

1.2 Crime is a relative and changing concept

  • Heresy — punished with burning a few centuries ago; today no civilised nation punishes religious belief.
  • Adultery — a crime under the Penal Code (until Joseph Shine v. Union of India, (2018) 8 SCC 186, which decriminalised §497 IPC as between adults); the DU CM records the classic contrast: “adultery is a crime according to our penal code, while it is a civil wrong according to English law”.
  • Suttee (burning of a widow on the husband’s pyre) — long considered a virtue in India; now a grave crime.
  • Polygamy — not a crime for Hindus until the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 made it so.
  • Post-independence new crimes — untouchability, forced/bonded labour, marrying below a certain age — “since Independence, many acts have now been enacted into crimes which we could not even have conceived of”. The categories of crimes are not closed; a socialist state conceives of more anti-social behaviour as crime.
Exam line: A crime of yesterday may become a virtue tomorrow, and a virtue of yesterday may become a crime tomorrow — hence all attempts to define crime, “beginning with Blackstone down to Kenny in modern times”, have proved abortive.

Since no such definition is possible across all countries and ages, the CM adopts two routes: (i) distinguish crime from civil and moral wrongs; (ii) critically examine the jurists’ definitions.

1.4 Crime as a public wrong — the private-to-public migration of wrongs

Crimes are public wrongs: the State, as protector of the rights of its subjects, pursues the offender — often in spite of the injured party. Historically, the process of “turning private wrongs into public ones” is still going forward: maiming or killing another’s cattle became crimes in the Hanoverian reign; breach of trust by a trustee became a crime only in 1857; incest became a crime in England only in 1908.

Three classic situations show that a “crime” need not injure any particular individual (contra Blackstone’s sweeping claim that “universally every crime is a civil injury”):

  1. Offences against the State — a seditious libel, or enlisting recruits for a foreign belligerent: no particular individual is injured.
  2. Inchoate/instrumental offences — an intending forger found in possession of a block for forging a trade mark, bank-note or currency note: serious offence, no individual injured.
  3. Self-regarding offences — attempted suicide: the only sufferer is the accused himself, who cannot claim compensation against himself.

1.5 Crime versus moral wrong

Criminal wrongs are narrower than moral wrongs. Law never punishes ingratitude, hard-heartedness, absence of natural affection, habitual idleness, avarice, sensuality or pride — “they might be the subject of confession and penance, but not of criminal proceeding”. Criminal law applies only to definite acts of commission and omission, capable of being distinctly proved, causing definite evils to definite persons or to the community. Yet: (i) where law and morals unite in condemning an act (murder, rape, arson, robbery, theft) punishment is enhanced; (ii) many acts are criminal though morally blameless (breach of statutory regulations — “failure to have a proper light on a bicycle or keeping of a pig in a wrong place”); and (iii) many grossly immoral acts are not crimes in some systems (adultery in England; incest in India, historically).

“The relation between criminal law and morality is not in all cases the same… wherever and so far as it is possible, they ought to harmonise with and support one another… The sentence of the law is to the moral sentiment of the public in relation to any offence what a seal is to hot wax. It converts into a permanent final judgement what might otherwise be a transient sentiment.” — Sir James Fitzjames Stephen, as reproduced in the DU Case Material

1.6 Crime versus ethics

Ethics studies the supreme good and absolute ideals; law concerns social relationships, not individual excellence. The DU CM’s two illustrations: (i) a starving neighbour beside your full granary — you commit no crime by not helping; (ii) standing by while a woman having an epileptic fit drowns — ethically wrong, but not criminal (no legal duty to rescue). The CM closes with Sheikh Saadi’s 13th-century line — “If you see a blind man proceeding to a well, if you are silent, you commit a crime” — as the direction in which humanitarian criminal law may one day travel.

1.7 Are crimes and torts complementary?

Yes. Crimes and torts “are not sharply separated groups of acts but are often one and the same act viewed from different standpoints, the difference being not one of nature but only of relation”. Stephen’s illustration: to ask “is this a crime or a tort?” is no wiser than to ask of a man “Is he a father or a son? For he may be both.” Many acts (assault, defamation, nuisance, fraudulent conversion) belong to both classes, and torts are constantly being “erected into crimes” when the civil remedy is felt to be inadequate.

Elements of Crime

The four constituent elements (edited from R.C. Nigam 38–43 & Atchuthen Pillai’s Criminal Law 42–47) + the four stages of a crime

The two core elements of crime are mens rea and actus reus. To these, two more indispensable elements are added: “a human being under a legal obligation to act in a particular way and a fit subject for the infliction of appropriate punishment”, and “an injury to another human being or to the society at large”. 2.1 Element 1 — A human being (the wrongdoer)

“The first element requires that the act should have been done by a human being before it can constitute a crime punishable at law. The human being must be ‘under a legal obligation to act, and capable of being punished.’” In early law, animals and even inanimate objects were punished (a pig burnt in Paris for devouring a child; a horse killed for kicking a man) — today the animal’s owner, not the animal, answers. The offender must have capacity: children below 7 (IPC §82 ↔ BNS §20), persons of unsound mind (IPC §84 ↔ BNS §22) are protected by general exceptions. A company can be criminally liable: Standard Chartered Bank v. Directorate of Enforcement (2005) 4 SCC 530 (fine on company permissible); Iridium India Telecom v. Motorola (2011) 1 SCC 74 (mens rea of its officers attributed).

2.2 Element 2 — Mens rea (the subjective element)

“In the entire field of criminal law there is no more important doctrine than that of mens rea. The fundamental principle of English criminal jurisprudence is actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea — the act alone does not make a man guilty unless his mind is also guilty.” Mens rea is the state of mind indicating culpability which is required by statute as an element of a crime — some blameworthy mental condition, constituted by intention or knowledge or otherwise, the absence of which negatives criminal intention. Full treatment in Section 4.

2.3 Element 3 — Actus reus (the objective element)

Russell calls it the “physical event”; Kenny defines it as “such result of human conduct as the law seeks to prevent”. It is a physical result of human conduct which criminal policy regards as sufficiently harmful to prohibit and penalise. It includes:

  • Acts of commission — a positive deed;
  • Acts of omission — IPC §32 ↔ BNS §3(4): “words which refer to acts done extend also to illegal omissions”. But no one is liable for omission unless a legal duty to act is cast on him — IPC §43 ↔ BNS §2(15): a person is “legally bound to do” whatever it is illegal in him to omit. (Parent starving child; railway gate-keeper omitting to shut the gate; doctor’s contractual duty.)
  • The act must be one forbidden by law — the executioner who hangs a condemned prisoner, and the surgeon who operates with due skill and care resulting in death, commit no crime: no mens rea + act sanctioned by law.
  • The act must be voluntary — a reflex, convulsive or coerced act (actus not voluntas) is not actus reus; a sleeping/unconscious movement is not.

2.4 Element 4 — Injury to another human being or society

The injury should be illegally caused to any person in body, mind, reputation or property — the very words of IPC §44 ↔ BNS §2(14) (“injury denotes any harm whatever illegally caused to any person, in body, mind, reputation or property”). Where no individual is hurt, the act may still be criminal because harm is caused to society at large — all public offences, especially offences against the State (treason, sedition, waging war), are of this kind and are punished severely.

2.5 When the four-element template breaks down

  • No mens rea, yet a crime → strict liability offences (Section 5).
  • No consummated actus reus / no injury, yet a crime → inchoate crimes: attempt, abetment, conspiracy (IPC Ch. V-A, §511 ↔ BNS §§45–62).
  • Only the first two elements present (person + intention), yet a crime → exceptional crimes of grave danger to public peace (e.g., unlawful assembly with common object — IPC §149 ↔ BNS §190(4); membership of prohibited organisations) where the law punishes even before harm.

Distinction between Civil and Criminal Liability

Six distinctions in the DU Case Material + the full exam table (12 points)
CrPC 1973 ↔ BNSS 2023CPC 1908

“Crimes are graver wrongs than torts.” Why? (i) they constitute greater interference with the happiness of others and affect the well-being of the community as a whole, not only of the individual wronged; (ii) the impulse to commit them is often very strong and the advantage, facility and low risk of detection tempt human nature; (iii) they are ordinarily deliberate acts directed by an evil mind and hurt society by the bad example they set. Hence crimes are singled out for punishment with a four-fold object: making an example of the criminal, deterring him from repeating the act, reforming him by eradicating the evil, and satisfying society’s feeling of vengeance. Civil wrongs are less serious — their effect is supposed to be confined mainly to individuals.

The Principle of Mens Rea

“Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea” — the guilty mind, its forms, its statutory presumption, and its exceptions
IPC §§ 24, 25, 39, 40, 44BNS §§ 2(7), 2(9), 2(29), 2(33), 3(6)

4.1 The maxim and the concept

The maxim — “the act does not make one guilty unless the mind is also guilty” — has “been familiar to lawyers following the common law for several centuries”. Mens rea is the state of mind indicating culpability, which is required by statute as an element of a crime — commonly some blameworthy mental condition constituted by intention or knowledge or otherwise, the absence of which negatives the intention of a crime. The term is given to volition, the motive force behind the criminal act. Even in crimes of strict liability some mental element (a voluntary act) is required.

“Generally, subject to both qualification and exception, a person is not criminally liable for a crime unless he intends to cause, foresees that he will probably cause, or at the lowest, foresees that he may cause, the elements which constitute the crime in question.” — DU Case Material (Atchuthen Pillai’s Criminal Law)

4.2.1 Intention vs Motive

“Motive is something which prompts a man to form an intention; intention is the fixed direction of the mind to a particular object.” The mental element of a crime ordinarily involves no reference to motive. Motive is not an essential element of any offence — but it is relevant evidence of intention. In Om Prakash v. State of Uttranchal (2003) 1 SCC 648 and State of U.P. v. Arun Kumar Gupta (2003) 2 SCC 202 the Supreme Court held that failure to prove motive is irrelevant where the guilt of the accused is otherwise proved. Conversely, the noblest motive is no defence (a mercy-killing is still murder).

4.2.2 Intention vs Knowledge

Both appear in the definitions of culpable homicide and murder (IPC §§299–300 ↔ BNS §§101–102) as alternative ingredients. “The demarcating line between knowledge and intention is no doubt thin” — a person may know a result is likely without desiring it (the well-jumping mother). Intention without knowledge is impossible; knowledge without intention is common.

4.3 Mens rea in the IPC and the BNS — an unstated essence

“Though the word mens rea as such is nowhere found in the IPC, its essence is reflected in almost all the provisions of the Code”: guilt is fastened on intention, knowledge or reason to believe, and the Code qualifies its offences with — wrongful gain or wrongful loss, dishonestly (IPC §24 ↔ BNS §2(7)), fraudulently (IPC §25 ↔ BNS §2(9)), reason to believe (IPC §26 ↔ BNS §2(29)), criminal knowledge or intention, intentional cooperation (IPC §37 ↔ BNS §3(6)), voluntarily (IPC §39 ↔ BNS §2(33)), malignantly, wantonly. The BNS carries this vocabulary forward in §2 (definitions) and §3 (general explanations). The mens rea must co-exist with the actus reus in time (concurrence) and must extend to all parts of the act — the doing, the circumstances and the consequences.

4.4 The presumption of mens rea in STATUTORY offences — and its displacement

When Parliament creates an offence in a statute without saying “knowingly/wilfully”, the common law applies a presumption that mens rea is an essential ingredient. This presumption — of English origin, heartily adopted in India — is, however, rebuttable.

“There is a presumption that mens rea, an evil intention, or a knowledge of the wrongfulness of the act, is an essential ingredient in every offence; but that presumption is liable to be displaced either by the words of the statute creating the offence or by the subject-matter with which it deals, and both must be considered.” — Wright, J., Sherras v. De Rutzen (1895) 1 QB 918, 921 — quoted in the DU CM in Mayer Hans George
“It is of the utmost importance for the protection of the liberty of the subject that a court should always bear in mind that, unless a statute either clearly or by necessary implication rules out mens rea as a constituent part of a crime, the Court should not find a man guilty of an offence against the criminal law unless he has a guilty mind.” — Lord Goddard, C.J., Brend v. Wood (1946) — adopted by the Privy Council in Srinivas Mall Bairoliya and by the Supreme Court in Ravula Hariprasada Rao v. State (1951) SCR 322
Indian anchor cases (all discussed inside the DU CM’s Mayer Hans George extract):
  • Nathulal v. State of M.P., AIR 1966 SC 43 — under the unamended §7 Essential Commodities Act, mens rea held essential.
  • Amendment of 1967 — “whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise” inserted in §7 ECA → strict liability (as explained in Swastik Oil Industries and applied in State of M.P. v. Narayan Singh).
  • Amendment of 1974 — words deleted; §10-C inserted: court presumes the culpable mental state (intention, motive, knowledge, belief); accused may rebut beyond reasonable doubt.
  • Ravula Hariprasada Rao v. State, (1951) SCR 322 — SC approved Brend v. Wood.
  • Lim Chin Aik v. Queen (1963) AC 160 (PC) — strict liability will not be inferred merely because the statute targets a “grave social evil”; the person must be able, by supervision or inquiry, to promote observance — “no luckless victim” rule.
  • Bruhn v. King (1909) AC 317 (PC) — revenue/prohibition statutes: prohibition operates absolutely; “he will be adjudged guilty of the offence though in fact he knew nothing of the prohibition”.
  • Indo-China Steam Navigation Co. v. Jasjit Singh — §52-A Sea Customs Act: mens rea excluded, else the provision becomes a dead letter.
  • Emperor v. Isak Solomon Macmull (1948) 50 Bom LR 190 (Chagla, C.J.) — master not vicariously liable absent mens rea unless the statute compels it by clear words or necessary implication.

4.5 Exceptions — offences where mens rea is NOT required

  • Public-welfare / regulatory offences — food adulteration (Prevention of Food Adulteration Act), essential commodities, narcotics possession, motor traffic, exchange control (Mayer Hans George), polluter regimes.
  • Offences against the State — waging war (IPC §121 ↔ BNS §147): the very act, done with the requisite intention to overawe the Government, suffices; “knowingly” is not an ingredient of sedition as defined.
  • Public nuisance — IPC §268 ↔ BNS §270: harm, not intention, is the gist.
  • Kidnapping/abduction — IPC §§361–363 ↔ BNS §§137–139: taking a minor from lawful guardianship is complete without dishonest intent (except aggravated forms).
  • Counterfeiting-related possession offences and statutory possession offences.
  • Statutorily presumed mens rea — e.g., §10-C Essential Commodities Act (presumption of culpable mental state, rebuttable only beyond reasonable doubt) — Narayan Singh.
  • Corporal result-only offences — e.g., bigamy under some statutes; marital rape exception-adjacent strictness; polluter-pays regimes (absolute liability — M.C. Mehta).

Strict and Absolute Liability

“In some cases a crime is constituted although there is no mens rea at all — these are known as cases of strict liability”
Rylands v. Fletcher (1868)M.C. Mehta v. UOI (1987)Mayer Hans George · Narayan Singh

5.1 The concept

A strict-liability offence is one where the prosecution needs to prove only the prohibited act (actus reus); neither intention nor negligence (nor even knowledge) need be shown, and mistake or ignorance of law/fact is no defence. Such offences are described as “quasi-criminal” — “where certain acts are forbidden by law under a penalty… the legislature has thought it so important to prevent the particular act that it absolutely forbids it; and if it is done the offender is liable to a penalty whether he had any mens rea or not” (Channell, J., in Pearks, Gunston & Tee v. Ward, quoted in the DU CM in Mayer Hans George).

Why strict liability? (i) public-welfare statutes protect large publics (health, food, currency, environment) who cannot protect themselves; (ii) proof of the supplier’s state of mind would be practically impossible and would make enforcement futile — Mayer Hans George: the Act’s “effectiveness as an instrument for the prevention of smuggling would be entirely frustrated” if knowledge had to be proved; (iii) deterrence of high-risk activity (pollution, hazardous industry).

5.4 Criticism and safeguards

  • Liberty concern — punishing the morally innocent (Brend v. Wood caution; Srinivas Mall Bairoliya: “surprising” to visit vicarious liability with 3 years’ imprisonment on a morally innocent master).
  • Constitutional caution — strict penal liability must be clearly spelled by the statute or necessary implication (Ravula Hariprasada Rao); a purely penal statute is construed against the State (lenity).
  • Legislative calibration — Parliament itself oscillates (ECA: 1967 strict → 1974 presumption), showing that strictness is a policy dial, not a doctrine of general application.
  • Modern view — strict liability acceptable where: minor/penal-in-name offences, truly regulatory context, defendant better placed to prevent harm (Lim Chin Aik), and penalties proportionate/fines rather than long imprisonment (R. v. St. Margarets Trust).

6.2 Section 4 — the six punishments BNS § 4 ↔ IPC § 53

BHARATIYA NYAYA SANHITA, 2023 — § 4 (Punishments) “The punishments to which offenders are liable under the provisions of this Sanhita are—
(a) Death;
(b) Imprisonment for life;
(c) Imprisonment, which is of two descriptions, namely:— (1) Rigorous, that is, with hard labour; (2) Simple;
(d) Forfeiture of property;
(e) Fine;
(f) Community Service.
  • IPC §53 listed only five punishments (Firstly–Fifthly): death; transportation for life (renamed imprisonment for life in 1955); imprisonment (rigorous/simple); forfeiture of property; fine. The BNS adds “Community Service” as a sixth — the first new punishment in 163 years NEW.
  • Imprisonment for life = imprisonment for the remainder of the convict’s natural life (see BNS §103: “imprisonment for life which means imprisonment for the remainder of a person’s natural life”); for fractional computation it is reckoned as 20 years — BNS §6 ↔ IPC §57.
  • Forfeiture of property survives (e.g., offences like IPC §§126–127 ↔ BNS §§150–151-type preparations against the State) but is virtually obsolete in practice.
  • Community service — defined not in the BNS but by the Explanation to §23, BNSS, 2023: “work which the Court may order a convict to do as a form of punishment that benefits the community, for which he shall not be entitled to any remuneration.” Full treatment in Section 8.

6.3 Section 5 — Commutation of sentence (a quiet revolution) BNS § 5 ↔ IPC §§ 54, 55, 55A

BNS § 5 (Commutation of sentence) “The appropriate Government may, without the consent of the offender, commute any punishment under this Sanhita to any other punishment in accordance with section 474 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. Explanation.— ‘appropriate Government’ means— (a) the Central Government, where the sentence is of death or is for an offence against a law relating to a matter within the Union’s executive power; (b) the State Government within which the offender is sentenced, in other cases.”

Change from IPC: IPC §54 (death → any other punishment) and §55 (life → ≤14 years) permitted commutation only with the consent of the offender. The BNS §5 read with BNSS §474 drops the consent requirement and sets the statutory ladder — death → imprisonment for life; life → imprisonment of not less than 7 years; 7-years+ → not less than 3 years; less than 7 years → fine; rigorous → simple. Executive clemency under Arts. 72 & 161 continues to operate above this scheme.

6.4 Sections 6 to 8 — rigour, fractions, fine and default BNS §§ 6–8 ↔ IPC §§ 57, 60, 63–70

BNS § 6 (Fractions of terms of punishment) — ↔ IPC §57 “In calculating fractions of terms of punishment, imprisonment for life shall be reckoned as equivalent to imprisonment for twenty years unless otherwise provided.” (E.g., for calculating one-third of the sentence in enhanced-punishment or default situations.)
BNS § 7 (Sentence may be wholly or partly rigorous or simple) — ↔ IPC §60 “In every case in which an offender is punishable with imprisonment which may be of either description, it shall be competent to the Court… to direct in the sentence that such imprisonment shall be wholly rigorous, or wholly simple, or that any part… rigorous and the rest simple.”
BNS § 8 (Amount of fine, liability in default of payment of fine, etc.) — ↔ IPC §§63–70 (1) Where no sum is expressed to which a fine may extend, the fine is unlimited but shall not be excessive. (2) Court may direct imprisonment in default of payment of fine (in excess of any other imprisonment). (3) Default term ≤ one-fourth of the maximum imprisonment for the offence. (4) Imprisonment in default of payment of fine or in default of community service may be of any description available for the offence. (5) Where the offence is punishable with fine or community service, default imprisonment shall be simple and shall not exceed — (a) 2 months if the fine ≤ ₹5,000; (b) 4 months if the fine ≤ ₹10,000; (c) 1 year in any other case. (6) Default imprisonment terminates when the fine is paid/levied; proportional payment earns proportional release (Illustration: fine of ₹1,000 with 4 months’ default — pay ₹750 after one month, go free). (7) Unpaid fine leviable within 6 years; death does not discharge attachable property.
Monetary modernisation NEW: IPC §67 (fine-only offences) fixed default scales at ₹50 → 2 months, ₹100 → 4 months, else 6 months. BNS §8(5) lifts these to ₹5,000 / ₹10,000 / 1 year, and adds default rules for community service — a completely new enforcement tail for the new punishment.

6.5 Sections 9 & 10 — one act, several offences BNS §§ 9–10 ↔ IPC §§ 71–72

BNS § 9 (Limit of punishment of offence made up of several offences) — ↔ IPC §71 Where an offence is made up of parts, each itself an offence, the offender is punished for only one of them unless expressly provided. Illustrations — A gives Z fifty strokes with a stick: one punishment for the whole beating (not fifty); but if A, mid-beating, deliberately strikes Y, the blow to Y is a distinct offence → two punishments.
BNS § 10 (Person guilty of one of several offences, judgment stating doubt) — ↔ IPC §72 Where the judgment is that the accused is guilty of one of several offences specified but it is doubtful which, he shall be punished for the offence carrying the lowest punishment (if punishments differ). (In dubio mitius — the benefit of the doubt goes to the accused even at the sentencing stage.)

6.6 Sections 11 & 12 — Solitary confinement BNS §§ 11–12 ↔ IPC §§ 73–74

BNS § 11 (Solitary confinement) — ↔ IPC §73 On conviction of an offence punishable with rigorous imprisonment, the Court may order solitary confinement for any portion(s) of the sentence, not exceeding 3 months in the whole, on the scale: (a) ≤ 1 month if the term ≤ 6 months; (b) ≤ 2 months if the term exceeds 6 months but ≤ 1 year; (c) ≤ 3 months if the term exceeds 1 year. (IPC §73’s obsolete reference to whipping — abolished 1955 — is gone.)
BNS § 12 (Limit of solitary confinement) — ↔ IPC §74 Solitary confinement shall never exceed 14 days at a time, with intervals between periods of not less duration than such periods; where the imprisonment exceeds 3 months, solitary shall not exceed 7 days in any one month, with like intervals.

6.7 Section 13 — Enhanced punishment after previous conviction BNS § 13 ↔ IPC § 75

BNS § 13 (Enhanced punishment for certain offences after previous conviction) Whoever, having been convicted by a Court in India of an offence punishable under Chapter X (offences by public servants) or Chapter XVII (offences against property) of this Sanhita with imprisonment of either description for 3 years or upwards, is again guilty of any offence punishable under either of those Chapters with like imprisonment, is liable for every subsequent offence to imprisonment for life, or imprisonment of either description for a term up to 10 years.

Note the renumbering: IPC §75 referred to Chapter XII (offences by/relating to public servants) and Chapter XVII (property offences); in the BNS these are Chapters X and XVII. This is preventive deterrence of recidivism (habitual offenders).

7.2 The governing tests — from Bachan Singh to Manoj

Bachan Singh (1980) — the normative frame:
  • Death only in the “rarest of rare” cases, when the option of life imprisonment is unquestionably foreclosed;
  • Circumstances of the offender must be considered along with the circumstances of the crime;
  • Life imprisonment is the rule, death the exception;
  • A “balance sheet” of aggravating vs mitigating circumstances must be drawn — mitigating factors include: offence committed under extreme mental or emotional disturbance; youth or old age; probability that the accused would not be a continuing threat; probability of reformation and rehabilitation (the State must prove the convict cannot be reformed).
Machhi Singh (1983) — the operational categories: death is permissible where the collective conscience of the community is so shocked that it will expect the holders of judicial power to inflict death. Five categories: (i) manner of the murder — extremely brutal, grotesque, diabolical, revolting or dastardly, arousing intense and extreme indignation; (ii) motive — total depravity and meanness; (iii) anti-social or socially abhorrent nature — e.g., murder of a Scheduled Caste/minority to terrorise the community, bride-burning for dowry; (iv) magnitude — enormous proportion, e.g., multiple murders of a family or a large number of persons; (v) personality of the victim — innocent child; helpless woman or old/infirm person; a public figure; a victim in a relationship of trust with, or under the control of, the murderer.
Manoj (2022) — the procedural turn: the tests are the “crime test”, the “criminal test” and the “R–R test” — not a crude balancing: the crime test must be satisfied 100% (aggravating circumstances fully made out) and the criminal test must show 0% mitigating circumstances before death can be considered; the probability (not mere possibility) of reformation must be negatived by the State on material; jail conduct is relevant contemporaneous evidence of the possibility of reform.

7.3 The retention debate — argue both sides in the exam

FOR retention (BNS retains — §4(a))
  • Constitutional validation — Jagmohan, Bachan Singh: not violative of Arts. 14, 19, 21.
  • Deterrence — the gravest crimes (terrorism, waging war, serial/mass murder, child rape-murder) demand the State’s gravest response.
  • Retribution/proportionality — “collective conscience” (Machhi Singh): some crimes shock society so deeply that only death expresses communal condemnation.
  • Narrow application — the rarest-of-rare filter + Manoj safeguards already minimise arbitrariness.
  • Legislative wisdom — 35th Law Commission Report (1967) favoured retention; Parliament retained it through three criminal-law overhauls (1860 → 1973 → 2023).
  • Victim-justice — for the families of victims of the most heinous crimes.
AGAINST retention / for abolition
  • No proven deterrence — 262nd Report (2015): no evidence death deters more than life; recommended abolition for all but terror/war offences.
  • Irreversibility & wrongful conviction — appellate acquittals in capital cases reveal grave trial error.
  • Arbitrariness & “judge-centric” sentencing — identical facts, different outcomes; crime-centric misuse of Machhi Singh categories (Santosh Bariyar; Swamy Shraddananda).
  • Class-caste skew — death row is disproportionately populated by the poor, marginalised and mentally ill (NLU-D Project 39A studies).
  • International trend — most nations have abolished (ICCPR & Second Optional Protocol direction).
  • Life means life now — imprisonment for the remainder of natural life (BNS §103) makes death unnecessary for incapacitation.

7.4 The BNS, 2023 position — retention, rationalised

  • Death retained as the first punishment — BNS §4(a); roughly 16 offences now carry it (13 under the IPC), e.g., murder (§103(1)), mob lynching — murder by five or more persons on grounds of race, caste, community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground (§103(2), NEW), murder by a life-convict (§104), waging war (§147), aggravated rape of a child below 12 (§65(2)), and attempt-to-murder variants (§109(2)).
  • Mithu-compliance — §104 (murder/attempt by a life-convict) offers death or life (the old IPC §303’s mandatory death was unconstitutional).
  • Mandatory minimums introduced in several grave offences sharpen general deterrence — but the death penalty remains judicially discretionary and rarest-of-rare-bounded.
  • Conclusion for the exam: the BNS’s answer to the retention debate is a two-track design — maximum retribution (death, retained and even extended to mob lynching) at one end, and maximum reformation (community service, introduced) at the other, with Manoj-style procedural safeguards (mandatory mitigation dossiers) disciplining the interface.

Introduction of Community Service

8.1 The concept

Community service requires an offender to perform unpaid work that benefits the community as a condition or form of punishment. It is the classic restorative / reformative sanction: the offender remains in society, repairs the harm by contributing labour to the community, avoids the criminogenic effects of prison, and the State saves prison costs. It marks the BNS’s declared shift “from punishment towards Nyaya” (DU CM course-objectives) and aligns India with jurisdictions where community orders (UK: 40–300 unpaid hours; Spain: 31–180 days; Canada, USA) are standard alternatives to short custody.

Explanation to § 23, BNSS, 2023 (definition of community service) “…‘community service’ shall mean the work which the Court may order a convict to do as a form of punishment that benefits the community, for which he shall not be entitled to any remuneration.” (The BNS itself does not define the term — BNS §2(39) borrows meanings from the BNSS.)

Acting on the High Court’s directions, the GNCTD Home Department, by Gazette Notification (June 2025), notified a list of 12 permissible community-service activities that Delhi courts may impose on persons convicted of minor offences — the first operational framework of its kind in India.

  • Objectives: reformative justice; restitution to the community; decongesting prisons; avoiding stigma and criminogenic exposure of petty offenders to hardened criminals; cost-effective sentencing; “course-correction” for young offenders.

8.5 Critique / challenges (write a balanced conclusion)

  • No national framework yet — BNS does not define types of work, supervising authority, monitoring or verification; the Delhi HC/GNCTD guidelines fill this only for Delhi (judicial creativity pending legislation — as in Vishaka tradition).
  • Dignity & Art. 23 concern — service must not become forced labour or humiliating spectacle (no cleaning in uniform before cameras).
  • Inequality risk — the poor may serve while the rich pay fines; uniformity needed (sentencing councils suggested).
  • Weaker deterrence — for some offenders community service may be no deterrent at all; suitability screening is essential (violent, sexual, repeat offenders excluded).
  • Supervision infrastructure — probation machinery (BNSS §360 ↔ CrPC §360; Probation of Offenders Act 1958) must be strengthened; courts need compliance reports.

CASE CARD 1 · MENSI REA & STRICT LIABILITY · DU CASE MATERIAL p.15

State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George

(1965) 1 SCR 123 : AIR 1965 SC 722 · Supreme Court of India · Decided 24 August 1964 · Appeal by special leave from Bombay High Court
Bench
Three-Judge Bench — K. Subba Rao, N. Rajagopala Ayyangar and J.R. Mudholkar, JJ. (opinion of the Court by Ayyangar, J.; dissent by Subba Rao, J.)
Provisions
§8(1) & §23(1-A)(a), Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1947; §24(1) (burden of proving permission); §167(8)(i), Sea Customs Act, 1878; RBI notifications of 25.08.1948 & 08.11.1962 (published in the Gazette on 24.11.1962)
Subject
Is mens rea an essential ingredient of an offence under a socio-economic regulatory statute which prescribes imprisonment?
Facts
  • Mayer Hans George, a German national by birth, a sailor by profession, was engaged at Hamburg by an international gang of gold-smugglers to clandestinely transport gold from Geneva/Zurich to the Far East, wearing a specially designed jacket — he claimed several earlier successful trips.
  • On 27 November 1962 he boarded a Swiss aircraft at Zurich, bound for Manila, carrying about 34 kilograms of gold (bars of ~1 kg each) concealed in 19 of the 28 compartments of his jacket. The flight touched Santa Cruz Airport, Bombay, at 6.05 a.m. on 28 November 1962; the respondent did not disembark.
  • Customs officers, acting on prior information, first examined the aircraft’s manifest — no gold was declared. Finding the respondent seated inside, they searched him (“what gold?” was his first response) and recovered the gold. The gold was not entered in the manifest or any other document.
  • Time-line of the law: under the RBI’s general permission (notification of 25.08.1948), gold on through transit to a place outside India could be brought in without removal from the aircraft (except transhipment). By a notification dated 8 November 1962 (published in the Gazette of India on 24 November 1962) the RBI added a second proviso: such gold must be declared in the manifest for transit as “same bottom cargo” or “transhipment cargo”. George left Zurich on 27 November 1962 — three days after gazette publication in India, and it was not disputed that he had no actual knowledge of the new condition.
  • He was prosecuted under §8(1) r/w §23(1-A)(a) FERA and §167(8)(i) Sea Customs Act. The Presidency Magistrate, Bombay convicted him and sentenced him to one year’s rigorous imprisonment. On appeal, the Bombay High Court acquitted him: (i) the second proviso did not apply to gold carried on the person of a passenger (it spoke of “cargo”); (ii) mens rea was a necessary ingredient and the respondent, unaware of the notification, had none. The State appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.
Issues
  1. Is mens rea an essential ingredient of the offence under §23(1-A)(a) FERA — i.e., must the prosecution prove the accused knowingly contravened the law?
  2. Was the RBI notification of 8 November 1962 — delegated legislation published only in the Gazette of India — binding on a foreigner in transit who had no means of knowing it? (Does the maxim ignorantia juris non excusat apply to unpublished/unnotifiable delegated legislation?)
  3. Did the second proviso (declaration in the manifest as cargo) apply at all to gold carried on the passenger’s person?
Arguments of the parties

Appellant — State of Maharashtra (Solicitor-General):

  • FERA was enacted to prevent smuggling of gold in the interests of the economic stability of the country; hence in construing its provisions “there is no scope for applying the presumption of common law that mens rea is a necessary ingredient” — the object of the statute and the mandatory terms of its provisions rebut the presumption.
  • The second proviso’s condition (declaration in the manifest) applied to all through-transit gold — gold carried on the person included — so the general permission stood withdrawn unless declared; failure to declare = contravention of §8(1) punishable under §23(1-A).
  • Relied on Indo-China Steam Navigation Co. v. Jasjit Singh (§52-A Sea Customs Act: mens rea excluded, else the provision is a dead letter) and Bruhn v. King (revenue prohibitions operate absolutely).

Respondent — Mayer Hans George (counsel: Mr. Sorabjee, per the DU CM text):

  • Common-law presumption: unless a statute clearly or by necessary implication rules out mens rea, no one should be found guilty without a guilty mind — Sherras v. De Rutzen (Wright, J.); Brend v. Wood (Goddard, C.J.); applied by the Privy Council in Srinivas Mall Bairoliya and by this Court in Ravula Hariprasada Rao.
  • Lim Chin Aik v. Queen: it is not enough to label the statute as dealing with a grave social evil; strict liability is not inferred where the accused could do nothing to promote observance — here the accused, a foreigner in transit, could not practicably know of a condition published only in the Indian Gazette three days before his flight; prosecuting him makes him a “luckless victim”.
  • Delegated legislation binds only when brought to the notice of affected persons — the notification cannot be “law” for him; the maxim ignorantia juris cannot apply to unpublished subordinate legislation.
  • The second proviso speaks of “cargo” (same bottom cargo / transhipment cargo) — goods entrusted to the carrier — and cannot apply to gold worn on the person.
Decision

Majority (Ayyangar and Mudholkar, JJ. — opinion by Ayyangar, J.): appeal allowed 2:1; conviction restored, sentence reduced to the period already undergone.

  • Mens rea — the method: “Where the statute does not contain the word ‘knowingly’, the first thing to do is to examine the statute to see whether the ordinary presumption that mens rea is required applies or not” — examining the language, object, subject-matter and the nature of the act (R. v. Tolson: the mens rea rule is “not an inflexible rule”).
  • Language: §8(1) lays an absolute embargo on bringing gold without the RBI’s general or special permission; §24(1) throws on the accused the burden of proving permission. The conscious physical act of “bringing” itself constitutes the contravention — no further mental element is postulated. (Only an involuntary bringing — gold slipped unknown into one’s pocket, or a forced landing of a through flight — would be outside the section.)
  • Subject-matter: the Act conserves foreign exchange “essential to the economic life of a developing country”; its penal provisions are “aimed at eliminating smuggling” — reading in a knowledge requirement “would entirely frustrate” the Act (adopting Bruhn v. King and R. v. St. Margarets Trust: breach of the flood-defences cannot be excused because done innocently).
  • Lim Chin Aik distinguished: under the Singapore Ordinance entry was lawful unless prohibited by an unpublicised individual order — “the position under §§8 and 23 of the Act is just the reverse”: bringing gold is unlawful unless permitted; hence mens rea cannot be imported. The RBI’s permission-notification was duly published and conditioned a standing permission.
  • Notification & maxim: the notification, published in the Gazette, conditioned the general permission — the convict’s ignorance did not excuse the failure to declare.

Dissent (Subba Rao, J.): appeal dismissed — acquittal affirmed, though on different reasoning. Mens rea is presumed in every statutory offence unless clearly excluded; the FERA provisions do not exclude it; and following Lim Chin Aik, since there was no prescribed mode of publication of the RBI’s order, the maxim ignorantia juris could not be invoked — the prosecution had to prove knowledge, which it conceded was absent (“an experienced smuggler of gold… [but] I have reluctantly come to the conclusion that the accused has not committed any offence”).

Principles evolved
  • The Indian statement of the mens-rea presumption method: presumption of mens rea in statutory offences (Sherras), displaced by the words of the statute or its object/subject-matter — both must be considered.
  • Socio-economic/regulatory legislation (exchange control, customs, smuggling) may create strict liability — only the voluntary, conscious act (actus reus) need be proved.
  • Where a statute prohibits absolutely, the burden of proving the licence/permission is on the accused (§24(1) FERA; Bruhn v. King).
  • Distinguishing “lawful unless prohibited” statutes (Lim Chin Aik — mens rea/knowledge needed) from “unlawful unless permitted” statutes (FERA — strict liability).
“The very object and purpose of the Act and its effectiveness as an instrument for the prevention of smuggling would be entirely frustrated if… the accused should be proved to have knowledge that he was contravening the law before he could be held to have contravened the provision.”

Tottenham Food Control Committee & Pearks, Gunston & Tee v. Ward (1902/1919); Rex v. Jacobs (1944); Brend v. Wood (1946); Srinivas Mall Bairoliya v. King-Emperor (PC, 1947); Lim Chin Aik v. Queen (PC, 1963); R. v. Tolson; R. v. St. Margarets Trust (1958); Ravula Hariprasada Rao (1951); Indo-China Steam Navigation Co. (1964); Emperor v. Isak Solomon Macmull (1948); State of Coorg v. P.K. Assu; State v. Sheo Prasad; C.T. Prim v. State (1961).

CASE CARD 2 · STRICT LIABILITY + STAGES OF CRIME · DU CASE MATERIAL p.37

State of Madhya Pradesh v. Narayan Singh

(1989) 3 SCC 596 · Supreme Court of India · Natarajan, J. (with Ahmadi, J.) · Two appeals (Crl. A. Nos. 49 & 24 of 1978) decided together
Provisions
Fertilisers (Movement Control) Order, 1973 — cl. 2(a) (“export” = to take or cause to be taken out of any place within a State to any place outside that State) & cl. 3 (“No person shall export, or attempt to export, or abet the export of any fertilisers from any State”); Essential Commodities Act, 1955 — §§3, 7 (as amended in 1967 and 1974), 10-C; CrPC §378(3)
Subject
(1) Is mens rea necessary for conviction under §7 ECA? (2) Was the conduct mere preparation, or an attempt, to export fertilisers without a permit?
Facts
  • Criminal Appeal 49/1978: a lorry (MPP 3668) carrying 200 bags of fertilisers, proceeding from Indore to Maharashtra, was intercepted on 12.02.1974 at the Sendhwa sales-tax barrier on the Agra–Bombay highway (National Highway 3), barely 8 miles from the Maharashtra border. The driver and two cleaners possessed invoices and records — but no export permit under the FMC Order.
  • Criminal Appeal 24/1978: a lorry (MPM 4866) from Indore carrying 170 bags was intercepted at the same barrier on 30.10.1973; driver and coolie — again invoices, no permit.
  • The accused did not deny the transport, the interception, or the absence of permits. Their defence: they did not know the contents of the documents seized from them and were not knowingly engaged in exporting fertilisers in violation of the Order.
  • Both Trial Magistrates acquitted: without evidence of the transport company’s employee there was no proof of attempted export; at best it was mere preparation. The High Court refused leave to the State to appeal against acquittal (§378(3) CrPC). The State came to the Supreme Court by special leave.
Issues
  1. Must the prosecution prove mens rea of the accused in exporting fertilisers without a valid permit to secure conviction under the FMC Order r/w §§3 & 7 ECA?
  2. On the facts, did the evidence establish only preparation, or did it cross into attempt to export (which cl. 3 itself forbids)?
Arguments of the parties

State (appellant):

  • Section 7(1) ECA (as amended by Act 36 of 1967) punishes a person “who contravenes whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise” any §3 order — the words expressly dispense with mens rea; every contravention, intentional or not, is an offence.
  • The lorries were intercepted 8 miles from the border, carrying loaded fertiliser bags under cover of sales invoices for consignees in Maharashtra — if not intercepted, the export would have been completed: this is attempt, not preparation.

Respondents (accused):

  • Following Nathulal v. State of M.P., AIR 1966 SC 43, an offence under §7 ECA is committed only by intentional contravention — mens rea is essential; the drivers’ assertion that they were unaware of the documents’ contents stood unrebutted.
  • The evidence showed at best a case of preparation to export — the actus reus of attempted export was not complete; mere transport short of the border is not an attempt.
Decision
  • Mens rea (Question 1) — against the accused: The trial court and High Court “failed to comprehend and construe §7(1) in its full perspective.” The section is “comprehensively worded” — it takes within its fold contraventions done knowingly or intentionally “or otherwise”, i.e., even unintentional contraventions. Nathulal no longer holds the field: the legislature reversed it by the 1967 amendment (words “whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise” inserted) — approving the Gujarat High Court’s analysis in Swastik Oil Industries v. State (1978).
  • The Court also noted the second legislative step (Ordinance/Act 30 of 1974): the 1967 words were deleted and §10-C inserted — in any prosecution requiring a culpable mental state, the court shall presume such mental state; the accused may rebut only by proving its absence beyond reasonable doubt (Explanation: “culpable mental state” includes intention, motive, knowledge of a fact, and belief in or reason to believe a fact) — the presumption operates against the accused, the burden “lies heavily” on him.
  • Attempt vs preparation (Question 2) — against the accused: “In the commission of an offence there are four stages — intention, preparation, attempt and execution. The first two stages would not attract culpability but the third and fourth would.” The accused were caught in the act of exporting: bags procured, loaded, moving under invoices towards consignees, stopped just 8 miles from the border — “surely no one can say that the respondents were taking the lorries with the fertiliser bags for innocuous purposes or for mere thrill or amusement”. This was attempted unlawful export, not preparation (cl. 3 expressly forbids “export, or attempt to export, or abet”).
  • Disposition: appeals allowed; the acquittals declared erroneous and unsustainable; the High Court ought to have granted leave. However, no punishment was awarded — fifteen years had elapsed since the acquittals, and the State’s counsel was “more interested in having the correct position of law set out than in securing punishment”.
Principles evolved
  • Parliament may create strict liability by express words (“whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise”) in regulatory statutes — and courts must give effect (“the legislature made its intention explicit and nullified the effect of the Supreme Court dicta in Nathu Lal”).
  • Statutory presumption of culpable mental state (§10-C ECA model): mens rea is presumed unless the accused rebuts it beyond reasonable doubt — the modern legislative technique between full mens rea and full strict liability.
  • The classical four-stage doctrine of criminal liability: intention and preparation are generally non-punishable; attempt and commission attract culpability — attempt is reached when the accused is caught in the act, moving inexorably towards completion (proximity test on the facts).
“They were clearly cases of attempted unlawful export of the fertiliser bags and not cases of mere preparation alone.”
CASE CARD 3 · RAREST OF RARE / DEATH PENALTY · DU CASE MATERIAL p.43

Machhi Singh and Others v. State of Punjab

(1983) 3 SCC 470 : AIR 1983 SC 957 · Supreme Court of India · Three-Judge Bench · Judgment by M.P. Thakkar, J.
Provisions
IPC §302 (↔ BNS §103) — three counts; IPC §149 common object (↔ BNS §190(4)); CrPC §354(3) — “special reasons” for death sentence (↔ BNSS counterpart)
Subject
What is the “rarest of rare” case justifying the death penalty after Bachan Singh? In what categories may courts impose death?
Facts
  • A bitter family feud raged between Machhi Singh’s group and the family of Amar Singh and his sister Piaro Bai (relations of Machhi Singh’s side had earlier been killed).
  • On the night of 12–13 August 1977, in a series of five incidents across five villages of Punjab committed in succession the same night, Machhi Singh and his associates (armed bands) murdered seventeen persons — men, women and children, all related to Amar Singh and Piaro Bai — and injured three, to avenge the earlier killings.
  • The victims were unarmed, defenceless, and caught unawares — several were dragged out and shot dead in cold blood (the incidents shocked the entire community).
  • Trials ended in conviction of several accused: four sentenced to death (including Machhi Singh, Kashmir Singh and Jagir Singh @ Jaggar Singh) and nine to life imprisonment; the Punjab & Haryana High Court confirmed. Appeals to the Supreme Court were decided by this judgment.
Issues
  1. Whether, on these facts, the cases fell within the “rarest of rare” category so as to merit the death sentence consistently with Bachan Singh and §354(3) CrPC?
  2. What categories/indicators should guide courts in identifying the rarest of rare case — i.e., when does the community “expect” the judicial power to impose death?
  3. Whether each appellant individually deserved the death sentence (individualised sentencing)?
Arguments of the parties

Defence / appellants:

  • Bachan Singh: life imprisonment is the rule, death the exception; the option of life imprisonment is never “unquestionably foreclosed” where there is any probability of reform; Machhi Singh and co-accused had no proven history making reformation impossible.
  • The crimes, however grave, arose out of a private family feud — not anti-social or socially abhorrent crimes of the kind that terrorise communities; the “collective conscience” argument was misplaced.
  • As to individual accused — one appellant was barely 18 years old; as to Mahinder Singh, there was doubt whether he used the weapon attributed to him (attribution resting on weak circumstances).

State / prosecution:

  • Seventeen innocent, defenceless persons of one family — including women and children — exterminated in one night across five villages in a premeditated, calculated, brutal campaign of vengeance: the magnitude, manner and motive place the case squarely in the rarest of rare category.
  • The brutality and scale evinced total depravity; the community’s collective conscience was profoundly shocked; anything less than death would defeat the community’s sense of justice.
Decision
  • The Supreme Court upheld the death sentences of Machhi Singh, Kashmir Singh and Jagir Singh @ Jaggar Singh; Mahinder Singh’s death sentence was commuted to life imprisonment (doubts about the weapon/role attributed to him) — demonstrating that within one massacre each convict is sentenced individually.
  • The Court first articulated the societal foundation of capital punishment: society is built on “reverence for life”; one who violates it by murdering another may forfeit the protection of the humanistic approach — but only in the rarest of rare cases where the collective conscience of the community is so shocked that it will expect the holders of the judicial power to inflict death, irrespective of their personal opinion.
  • The Court then laid down the famous five categories (see table below) in which the community may so expect the death sentence — viewing the crime from the platform of the motive, manner, anti-social nature, magnitude, or victim’s personality.
  • Both the circumstances of the crime and of the criminal must be considered; the sentencing court must draw a balance-sheet of aggravating and mitigating circumstances and record “special reasons” under §354(3) — a real special-reasons hearing, not a formality.
Principles evolved — the five Machhi Singh categories
CategoryWhen death may be “expected” by the community
I. Manner of the murderCommitted in an extremely brutal, grotesque, diabolical, revolting or dastardly manner so as to arouse intense and extreme indignation of the community (torture, burning alive, dismemberment).
II. Motive for the murderMotive evincing total depravity and meanness — e.g., hired assassination; murder for insurance or robbery; murder by one in whom the victim reposed trust.
III. Anti-social / socially abhorrent natureMurder of a member of a Scheduled Caste or minority etc., not for personal reasons but to terrorise such persons/community; bride-burning for dowry; murders designed to “reverse past injustices” or restore “social balance”.
IV. Magnitude of the crimeEnormous proportion — multiple murders of a family or of a large number of persons (a “crime of immense proportion”).
V. Personality of the victimVictim is an innocent child; a helpless woman or an old/infirm person; a public figure loved by the community; or a victim in a position of trust or domination vis-à-vis the murderer.
“When the collective conscience of the community is so shocked, it will expect the holders of the judicial power centre to inflict death penalty… irrespective of their personal opinion…” — and the converse: where the murderer acts under extreme mental/emotional disturbance, or is extremely young or old, or reform is probable, life must be preferred.
Later trajectory (bonus marks) Santosh Bariyar (2009) and Swamy Shraddananda (2008) showed that the Machhi Singh categories were being misused as a crime-centric shortcut that “considerably enlarged the scope for imposing death penalty” beyond Bachan Singh; Manoj (2022) restored the criminal-centric balance (Case Card 4). Mention this evolution in death-penalty answers.
CASE CARD 4 · CAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEDURE · DU CASE MATERIAL p.43

Manoj & Ors. v. State of Madhya Pradesh

2022 INSC 606 : (2022) 9 SCALE 67 : 2022 SCC OnLine SC 677 : (2023) 2 SCC 353 · Supreme Court of India · Decided 20 May 2022
Bench
U.U. Lalit, S. Ravindra Bhat and Bela M. Trivedi, JJ.
Provisions
IPC §§302 (three counts), 397 r/w 34, 449 r/w 34; Arms Act §§25(1-B)(b), 27 (↔ BNS §103 and robbery/house-trespass equivalents); CrPC §§235(2), 354(3)
Subject
Procedural framework for collecting mitigating circumstances in death-penalty cases; relevance of jail conduct; the “crime test, criminal test and R–R test”.
Facts
  • Three appeals by three accused — Manoj (about 35), Rahul @ Govind (about 20) and Neha Verma (about 22) — convicted by the First Additional Sessions Judge, Indore for the murder of three women of three generations (aged about 22, 46 and 76 years) in their own home.
  • The prosecution case: the three entered the house to rob it; on meeting unexpected protest from the women, they subjected them to frenzied knife attacks, killing all three. Convictions: §302 (×3) r/w 34, §397 r/w 34, §449 r/w 34 IPC + Arms Act offences; death sentences were awarded and confirmed by the Madhya Pradesh High Court (Indore Bench), which held the crime shocked the collective conscience.
  • Before the Supreme Court, the appeals raised conviction-points (test identification parade, shoeprint report, DNA quality, §313 CrPC examination) and the core sentencing question — whether death could be imposed without proper collection and consideration of the convicts’ mitigating circumstances.
Issues
  1. Was the death sentence justified on the Bachan Singh/Machhi Singh framework on these facts?
  2. What must a sentencing court do, procedurally, to gather the accused’s mitigating circumstances — and on whom does the burden of proving impossibility of reform lie?
  3. Is the convict’s conduct in jail after the crime a relevant mitigating (reform-indicating) circumstance?
Arguments of the parties

Appellants (Manoj, Rahul, Neha):

  • Sentencing was mechanical (effectively same-day conviction-and-sentence), with no psychiatric, psychological, social-background or probation material on record — the aggravating circumstances seemed “far more compelling or overwhelming” only because mitigation was never documented.
  • Mitigation existed: young age (Rahul 20, Neha 22); no criminal antecedents (except a petty-theft allegation against Manoj); the frenzied, unskilled stabbing suggested unfamiliarity with the weapon; overall good jail conduct; probability of reform not foreclosed.

Respondent — State of M.P.:

  • The crime was pre-planned and grotesque — three generations of defenceless women slaughtered in their home during a robbery; the collective conscience of society was shocked; the case was squarely rarest of rare.
  • The convicts gave false answers in §313 CrPC examination, showing no remorse; the High Court had already considered the sentence; the mitigation now claimed was too little, too late.
Decision
  • Convictions upheld on the evidence; but the death sentences were set aside and commuted to imprisonment for life, with a minimum of 25 years’ actual imprisonment before remission could be considered — because the option of life imprisonment was “certainly not foreclosed”.
  • The tests: while awarding death the court applies the “crime test”, the “criminal test” and the “R–R test” — not a mere balancing test. The crime test must be fully satisfied (100%) — aggravating circumstances established to the fullest; the criminal test must show 0% mitigating circumstances; even then the court must apply the rarest-of-rare test. Any circumstance favouring the accused — lack of intention, possibility of reformation, young age, no menace to society, clean record — tilts the criminal test against death.
  • Jail conduct is relevant: contemporaneous conduct in prison (work done, activities, behaviour) is legitimate material on the probability of reform; if the appeal is heard long after conviction, a fresh jail-conduct report must be called for.
  • The Manoj protocol (practical guidelines): the trial court must, at the sentencing stage: elicit information from the accused; require the State to produce — preferably collected beforehand — psychiatric and psychological evaluation of the accused; obtain reports from probation officers (nature, family and social background, education, conduct); call jail authorities’ reports on conduct and work; and give the accused an equal opportunity to rebut the State’s material. Sentencing must be individualised — “ad-hoc impressions” replaced by “verifiable data”.
  • The Court connected its guidelines to the 262nd Law Commission Report’s findings on the inconsistency of capital sentencing and to the pending reference in Death Sentence Mitigating Circumstances Guidelines, In re.
Principles evolved
  • Individualised, data-driven capital sentencing is part of Articles 14 and 21 due process — a death sentence without a real mitigation inquiry is procedurally infirm.
  • The State bears the burden to prove, on material, that the convict cannot be reformed — the test is probability, not possibility, of reformation.
  • Jail conduct / contemporaneous post-crime conduct is a relevant consideration at sentencing.
  • Aftermath: in Vasanta Sampat Dupare v. Union of India (2025), the Supreme Court held that death sentences passed in violation of the Manoj protocol may be reopened under Article 32 — the protocol (probation-officer, psychiatric/psychological, and jail-conduct reports) is now part of the Article 21 fair-trial guarantee.
“The unfortunate reality is that in the absence of well-documented mitigating circumstances at the trial level, the aggravating circumstances seem far more compelling, or overwhelming, rendering the sentencing court prone to imposing the death penalty, on the basis of an incomplete, and hence, incorrect application of the Bachan Singh test.”
CASE CARD 5 · COMMUNITY SERVICE IN OPERATION · DU CASE MATERIAL p.50

Delhi High Court Guidelines for Community Service, 2025

Guidelines framed by the High Court of Judicature of Delhi for awarding community service under §4(f), BNS 2023 — implemented by GNCTD Home Department Gazette Notification (June 2025)
Nature
Not merely a litigated judgment but a judicially framed sentencing framework (reproduced in the DU Case Material at p.50) — the model for operationalising BNS §4(f) pending legislative rules.
Provisions
BNS §4(f) (community service as a punishment); BNS §§202, 209, 226, 303(2) proviso, 355, 356(2) (the six offences); BNS §8(4)–(5) (default); BNSS §23 Explanation (definition)
Background & ‘facts’ of the development
  • From 1 July 2024, the BNS/BNSS made community service a statutory punishment for six minor offences — but left the mode of execution, nature of work, duration, supervision and verification unprovided. Courts faced the question: what exactly does a “community service” sentence look like?
  • The Delhi High Court framed guidelines for awarding community service; pursuant to the High Court’s directions, the Home Department, Government of NCT of Delhi, issued a gazette notification (June 2025) notifying the permissible activities and durations for Delhi courts.
  • The step follows the Delhi High Court’s record of reformative orders — e.g., directing accused persons to render service at Gurudwara Rakab Ganj Sahib (July 2024, in a case involving insult to a neighbour’s wife’s modesty) and at LNJP Hospital — and the Supreme Court’s use of community service in Sanjeev Nanda (2012).
The ‘issues’ the guidelines answer
  1. What activities may a court order as “community service” under §4(f)?
  2. For how long, and for which class of convicts?
  3. Does a guideline list limit judicial discretion?
The guidelines (as implemented by the June 2025 GNCTD notification)
  • 12 notified activities, including: cleaning and maintenance of hospital wards and their peripheral areas; sanitation duties in public spaces; maintenance/restoration of public parks; cleaning of parks and open spaces; weeding of roadsides and medians; arranging, cataloguing and binding of books in public libraries; and similar civic-welfare assignments.
  • Duration: 1 day to 31 days, or 40 to 240 hours, depending on the nature of the offence and the court’s discretion.
  • Eligibility: convicts of the six minor offences — public servant’s unlawful trade (§202); non-appearance after proclamation (§209); attempt to suicide to compel (§226); first-time petty theft under ₹5,000 with property restored (§303(2)); misconduct in public by a drunken person (§355); defamation (§356(2)) — the paradigm non-violent, first-time, petty offender.
  • Discretion preserved: the list is expressly illustrative — the activities are “not intended to curtail the discretion of the Court in awarding any other mode of community service under §4(f) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023”.
  • Enforcement: default is met by BNS §8(4)–(5) — simple imprisonment on the ₹5,000 / ₹10,000 / other scale.
Principles / significance
  • First operational framework in India for the newest punishment — a template other High Courts and State Governments are expected to replicate (Delhi’s 2023 Dwarka pilot had already resolved cases through community-service mandates).
  • Encapsulates restorative justice: accountability without incarceration; the community is the beneficiary; the offender retains employment, family and dignity.
  • Judicial guideline-making filling a legislative vacuum (the Vishaka technique) — practice guidance binding as a matter of judicial discipline pending statutory rules.
  • Interpretive bridge: the definition flows from BNSS §23 (unremunerated work benefiting the community); yet the sentence remains a true punishment (not probation), enforceable by imprisonment in default.
Exam connect Pair this card with BNS §4(f) & §8: “Community service — introduced for the first time by BNS §4(f), defined in BNSS §23, available for six minor offences, given teeth by §8(4)–(5), and operationalised for Delhi by the High Court guidelines of 2025 (12 activities; 1–31 days or 40–240 hours).”

Q1
“Crime is a relative and changing concept.” Define crime, examine its nature, and discuss the essential elements that constitute a crime. [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term (Law of Crimes–I) — recurring long question · Topics: Nature of crime + Elements of crime
Introduction — why the question is difficult

The DU Case Material opens with the question “What is a crime?” and immediately observes that to answer it one must first know what law is, since a crime is an act of disobedience to law. Yet disobedience of all laws is not crime — a breach of contract or revenue law creates no criminality. Hence a crime is something more: “an act which is both forbidden by law and revolting to the moral sentiments of the society.” Since moral sentiments vary with time, place and society, all attempts to define crime — from Blackstone down to Kenny — “have proved abortive” (Russell: “to define crime is a task which so far has not been satisfactorily accomplished by any writer”).

I. The relativity of crime

Crime is conditioned by the moral values approved by a particular society in a particular age: heresy was once punished with burning; suttee was accounted a virtue and is now a grave crime; polygamy became criminal for Hindus only by the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955; adultery was a crime under our Penal Code (now decriminalised for adults after Joseph Shine v. Union of India, 2018); and since Independence untouchability, forced labour and marrying below the prescribed age have been criminalised. The categories of crime are not closed.

II. Leading definitions

Blackstone: an act committed or omitted in violation of public law forbidding or commanding it. Kenny: acts forbidden by law and punishable — the differentia is punishment. Stephen: an act both forbidden by law and revolting to the moral sentiments of society. Salmond: a public wrong. Wechsler: penal law is the law of ultimate reliance against the deepest injuries human conduct can inflict. A crime is thus best described (not defined) as human conduct prohibited by the State under a threat of punishment, prosecuted by the State irrespective of the victim’s wishes.

III. The four essential elements
  1. A human being — under a legal obligation to act and a fit subject for punishment; must have capacity (children under 7 — IPC §82 ↔ BNS §20; unsoundness of mind — IPC §84 ↔ BNS §22). A company too is a “person” (IPC §11 ↔ BNS §2(26)).
  2. Mens rea (subjective element) — the guilty mind: intention, knowledge, recklessness or (as treated in Indian law) rashness/negligence. Maxim: actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea. Though the word does not appear in the IPC/BNS, its essence pervades the codes through “dishonestly” (IPC §24 ↔ BNS §2(7)), “fraudulently” (IPC §25 ↔ BNS §2(9)), “voluntarily” (IPC §39 ↔ BNS §2(33)), etc.
  3. Actus reus (objective element) — Kenny: “such result of human conduct as the law seeks to prevent”; includes acts of commission and illegal omission (IPC §32 ↔ BNS §3(4)); must be a voluntary act forbidden by law.
  4. Injury — illegally caused to any person in body, mind, reputation or property (IPC §44 ↔ BNS §2(14)), or harm to society at large (treason, sedition, waging war).
IV. Departures from the template

The four elements co-exist only as a general rule: (i) strict liability offences punish without mens rea (Mayer Hans George; Narayan Singh); (ii) inchoate crimes (attempt, abetment, conspiracy) punish though the actus reus is incomplete and no injury results; (iii) some grave offences punish intention allied to circumstances (unlawful assembly) though no injury has yet occurred.

Conclusion

Crime being a relative, changing concept, the law wisely defines it not by an abstract formula but by the concrete elements — a legally responsible human being, a guilty mind, a guilty act, and an injury to person or society. The examiner’s ideal close: quote Russell’s warning and state that the elements approach gives practical, adjudicable content to the concept.

“Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea.” Explain this maxim and discuss whether mens rea is an essential ingredient of an offence under a statute, with the help of State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George. [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — mens rea & statutory offences · Core DU CM case (p.15)
Meaning of the maxim

“The act does not make a man guilty unless his mind is also guilty.” A crime is the product of the union of a guilty mind with a guilty act: mere wrongful consequence, without a blameworthy mental state, does not attract criminal liability. Mens rea is “the state of mind indicating culpability which is required by statute as an element of a crime” — intention, knowledge, or, at the lower end, recklessness/negligence.

Mens rea in the Penal Codes

The IPC/BNS nowhere use the term, but its essence is reflected in almost every provision — “intentionally”, “knowingly”, “voluntarily” (IPC §39 ↔ BNS §2(33)), “dishonestly” (IPC §24 ↔ BNS §2(7)), “fraudulently” (IPC §25 ↔ BNS §2(9)). The mens rea must co-exist with the actus reus in point of time — a guilty mind before or after the act does not criminalise it.

The problem of statutory offences — the presumption

When a statute creates an offence silently on the mental element, the common law presumes mens rea is essential. Sherras v. De Rutzen (Wright, J.): “There is a presumption that mens rea… is an essential ingredient in every offence; but that presumption is liable to be displaced either by the words of the statute creating the offence or by the subject-matter with which it deals, and both must be considered.” Brend v. Wood (Goddard, C.J.): unless a statute clearly or by necessary implication rules out mens rea, no conviction without a guilty mind. India adopted this line in Ravula Hariprasada Rao v. State (1951) and Nathulal v. State of M.P. (AIR 1966 SC 43 — Essential Commodities Act, as then unamended).

Mayer Hans George — displacement of the presumption

Facts: A German carrier of an international gold-smuggling ring was found at Bombay’s Santa Cruz airport (28.11.1962) in transit from Zurich to Manila with 34 kg of gold concealed in a jacket, undeclared in the aircraft manifest, in violation of the RBI notification of 8.11.1962 (published in the Gazette on 24.11.1962) issued under §8(1) FERA, 1947; punishable under §23(1-A)(a). The Presidency Magistrate convicted (1 year R.I.); the Bombay High Court acquitted him holding mens rea necessary and absent. Held (majority — Ayyangar and Mudholkar, JJ.; Subba Rao, J. dissenting): conviction restored (sentence reduced to period undergone).

Principles: (i) where the statute does not use “knowingly”, examine its language, object, subject-matter and the nature of the act; (ii) §8(1) FERA laid an absolute embargo — bringing gold without RBI permission is unlawful unless permitted; §24(1) cast the burden of proving permission on the accused; the conscious physical act of bringing is the offence — no further mental element; (iii) FERA conserves foreign exchange essential to a developing economy and combats smuggling; reading in a knowledge requirement “would entirely frustrate” the Act; (iv) Lim Chin Aik distinguished — under the Singapore Ordinance entry was lawful unless prohibited, the reverse of FERA. The offence was one of strict liability; ignorance of the duly gazetted notification was no defence. (Subba Rao, J., dissenting, would have applied Lim Chin Aik and acquitted.)

Conclusion

Mens rea is the rule; strict liability the exception. The exception arises where the statute’s words or its regulatory object exclude the presumption — particularly in public-welfare and socio-economic legislation — but, per Brend v. Wood and Lim Chin Aik, courts will not be “astute” to find exclusion: absolute liability “is not to be presumed, but ought to be established”.

Q4
What is mens rea? Explain its forms — intention, knowledge, recklessness and negligence — with illustrations, and distinguish intention from motive and from knowledge. [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — DU CM “Constituent Elements of Crime”
Definition

Mens rea — the guilty mind — is “the state of mind indicating culpability, which is required by statute as an element of a crime… commonly taken to mean some blameworthy mental condition, whether constituted by intention or knowledge or otherwise, the absence of which on any particular occasion negatives the intention of a crime.” It is the volition, the motive force behind the criminal act.

The forms
  • Intention — “a fixed purpose to reach a desired objective… the state of mind of a man who not only foresees but also desires the possible consequences of his conduct.” Expressed by ‘intentionally’, ‘deliberately’, ‘voluntarily’, ‘wilfully’. Transferred malice: A poisons B’s bedside milk; C drinks it — A guilty of C’s murder.
  • Knowledge — awareness of the consequences of the act; “there may be knowledge of the likely consequences without any intention to cause them.” CM illustration: the mother who leaps into a well clutching her child — the child dies, she survives: culpable homicide on knowledge though without intention (IPC §§299/300 ↔ BNS §§101/102).
  • Recklessness — “Intention cannot exist without foresight, but foresight can exist without intention”; the actor foresees possible/probable harm yet does not desire it, and runs the risk unjustifiably — the ‘rash’ driver (IPC §304A ↔ BNS §106; IPC §279 ↔ BNS §281).
  • Negligence — blameworthy inadvertence: the consequence is neither foreseen nor willed, but a reasonable man would have foreseen it. Advertent negligence (foreseen but not willed) is wilful negligence/recklessness; inadvertent is simple negligence — CM illustration: the physician who treats improperly “through ignorance or forgetfulness” (simple) versus “to save himself trouble, or by way of scientific experiment with full recognition of the danger” (wilful). Criminal liability for pure inadvertence is exceptional and statutory.
Intention vs motive

Motive is what prompts the formation of an intention; intention is the fixed direction of the mind to the object. Motive is never an essential ingredient of an offence, but is relevant evidence of intention: Om Prakash v. State of Uttranchal (2003) 1 SCC 648 and State of U.P. v. Arun Kumar Gupta (2003) 2 SCC 202 — failure to prove motive is irrelevant where guilt is proved otherwise. Equally, a laudable motive (mercy killing) is no defence to murder.

Intention vs knowledge

Both are alternative mens rea ingredients in culpable homicide/murder. “The demarcating line is thin”: intention includes desire; knowledge is bare awareness of likelihood. Knowledge without intention suffices for the lesser offence (IPC §304 Part II ↔ BNS §105); intention pushes the case into murder (IPC §300 ↔ BNS §102 “thirdly” clause).

Conclusion

The ladder of culpability — intention → knowledge → recklessness → negligence — calibrates the grade of offence and sentence; the statute supplies the particular word, and the court fixes its meaning “in the context in which it appears”.

Q5
PROBLEM: A German national, in transit through Delhi airport on a flight from Zurich to Bangkok, is found wearing a jacket concealing 20 kg of undeclared gold. RBI’s latest notification (published in the Gazette four days before he boarded at Zurich) requires all transit gold to be declared in the aircraft manifest. He pleads: (i) he never left the aircraft; (ii) he had no knowledge of the notification; (iii) the gold was on his person, not ‘cargo’. Decide. [20 marks]
DU problem-question pattern (modeled directly on the DU CM case — p.15)
Issues

(1) Does bringing gold into India without declaration, while in transit and without disembarking, contravene the exchange-control law? (2) Is knowledge of the notification (mens rea) required for conviction? (3) Does a manifest-declaration condition apply to gold worn on the person?

Rule — State of Maharashtra v. Mayer Hans George, AIR 1965 SC 722

The facts are materially those of Mayer Hans George itself. The Supreme Court (majority) laid down: (i) an Explanation to §8(1) FERA-type provisions deems the bringing of goods into India, even when intended to be taken out without removal from the conveyance, to be “bringing into India” — not disembarking is irrelevant; (ii) on the question of mens rea, the presumption of mens rea (Sherras v. De Rutzen) is displaced where the statute’s language and object show absolute prohibition: the conscious physical act of bringing is the offence; the burden of proving permission is on the accused; ignorance of a duly gazetted notification is no defence; (iii) the manifest-declaration condition governs all transit gold including gold on the person — the general permission is conditional.

Application
  • Transit: the aircraft touched Indian territory with the gold aboard his person — deemed “bringing into India”; staying seated does not help (that was precisely George’s case).
  • No knowledge: since the offence is one of strict liability in a socio-economic statute, absence of knowledge of the notification is no defence; the notification, duly published in the Gazette, conditioned the general permission. (Only an involuntary bringing — gold slipped into his pocket unknown to him, or a forced landing — would be outside the section.)
  • ‘Not cargo’: following the majority’s construction, the declaration requirement is not confined to goods handed to the carrier; the benefit of the general permission is available only on declaration.
  • Counter-view (for balance): Subba Rao, J., dissenting, applying Lim Chin Aik, would hold that where subordinate legislation is not required to be, and was not, brought to the notice of the affected person, the maxim ignorantia juris cannot apply — knowledge must be proved. Also note Srinivas Mall Bairoliya — a court should not readily convict the morally innocent. A good answer presents the majority as the law, with the dissent as the humanitarian counterpoint.
Conclusion

The German national is liable: his conscious act of bringing undeclared gold into India contravened the prohibition read with the notification; mens rea is not an ingredient; conviction is sustainable (subject, per the majority’s practice in George, to a sentencing discretion mindful of the short lapse since publication).

Q6
“Strict liability offences are an exception to the doctrine of mens rea.” Explain strict liability in criminal law, the judicial technique for identifying it, and the legislative technique of presuming a ‘culpable mental state’, with reference to decided cases. [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — strict liability · Mayer Hans George + Narayan Singh (DU CM pp.15, 37)
Concept

In strict-liability (‘quasi-criminal’) offences the prosecution need prove only the prohibited act; mens rea, negligence, and even mistake are immaterial. Rationale (Pearks v. Ward, cited in the DU CM): “the legislature has thought it so important to prevent the particular act that it absolutely forbids it to be done; and if it is done the offender is liable to a penalty whether he had any mens rea or not.” Typical habitat: public-welfare, regulatory and socio-economic statutes — food adulteration, weights and measures, exchange control, essential commodities, traffic, pollution.

Judicial technique of identification
  1. Presumption of mens rea in every statutory offence — Sherras v. De Rutzen; Brend v. Wood; Ravula Hariprasada Rao.
  2. Displacement by the words of the statute (“whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise”) or by its object and subject-matter — Mayer Hans George: FERA’s absolute embargo, the reverse-onus provision, and anti-smuggling object excluded mens rea; “the conscious physical act of bringing constitutes the offence”.
  3. The Lim Chin Aik safety-valve: strict liability is not inferred merely from the “grave social evil” label; it must aid enforcement — the accused must be able to do something (supervision, inquiry) to promote observance; otherwise he is a “luckless victim”.
  4. Where imprisonment of the morally innocent would be “surprising”, courts resist — Srinivas Mall Bairoliya (no vicarious liability of the innocent master).
Legislative technique — presumption of culpable mental state

State of M.P. v. Narayan Singh traces the Essential Commodities Act saga: Nathulal (1966) read mens rea into §7; the 1967 amendment added “whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise” — full strict liability (Swastik Oil Industries approved); the 1974 amendment deleted those words and inserted §10-C — in prosecutions requiring a culpable mental state the court presumes it (intention, motive, knowledge, belief), and the accused must rebut it beyond reasonable doubt. Thus the statute achieves near-strict liability while preserving a rebuttable channel.

Strict vs absolute liability

Strict liability (Rylands v. Fletcher — with defences: plaintiff’s default, stranger, vis major, statutory authority) differs from absolute liability — M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) 1 SCC 395 (oleum leak): hazardous enterprises liable without any of the Rylands exceptions; compensation commensurate with the magnitude of the enterprise.

Conclusion

Strict liability is a policy exception grafted on the mens rea doctrine: justified for regulatory enforcement of public-welfare laws, but policed by the courts through the presumption-of-mens-rea canon, the Lim Chin Aik enforceability test, and proportionality in sentencing.

Q7
Explain the four stages in the commission of a crime. At what stage does criminal liability attach, and what are the exceptions where even preparation is punishable? Refer to State of M.P. v. Narayan Singh. [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — stages of crime · DU CM p.37
The four stages
  1. Intention (mens rea) — the guilty purpose formed in the mind. Never punishable by itself: “mens rea is not punishable” — the law does not punish thoughts; A who intends to kill B and merely buys a weapon commits no offence thereby.
  2. Preparation — arranging means and measures to execute the intention. Generally not punishable: the actor may repent and withdraw, and the harm is still remote.
  3. Attempt — an act done with intent to commit the offence, in furtherance of it, which falls short of consummation. Punishable — the general provision: IPC §511 ↔ BNS §62; specific attempts: attempt to murder (IPC §307 ↔ BNS §109), attempt to suicide to compel (IPC §309 ↔ BNS §226), etc.
  4. Commission/execution — the completed offence with its resultant harm; full punishment.
State of M.P. v. Narayan Singh, (1989) 3 SCC 596

Lorries carrying 200 and 170 bags of fertilisers from Indore towards Maharashtra, without export permits under the Fertilisers (Movement Control) Order, 1973 r/w §§3 & 7 Essential Commodities Act, were intercepted at the Sendhwa barrier, 8 miles from the Maharashtra border. The trial court and High Court treated it as mere preparation and acquitted. The Supreme Court (Natarajan, J.): “In the commission of an offence there are four stages viz. intention, preparation, attempt and execution. The first two stages would not attract culpability but the third and fourth stages would certainly attract culpability.” The accused were caught in the act of exporting — bags procured and loaded, lorries moving under invoices towards consignees; had they not been intercepted the export would have been completed. “Surely no one can say that the respondents were taking the lorries… for mere thrill or amusement.” Hence attempted unlawful export, not mere preparation — acquittals set aside (though no punishment was imposed as 15 years had passed; the State sought only the correct statement of law). The Court also held (on the mens rea question) that §7 ECA as amended (“whether knowingly, intentionally or otherwise”) created strict liability, and that under §10-C the culpable mental state is presumed.

Why liability begins at attempt — the tests

Proximity test (last-act/nearness: the act must be proximate to completion); locus poenitentiae — until preparation the actor may still withdraw with ease; after the attempt the danger to society has crystallised. Distinguish: preparation = arranging the means; attempt = beginning the execution with the intention to complete.

Exceptions — punishable preparations

Where the danger itself is grave and detection after completion difficult, statutes punish preparation: waging war — collecting men/arms (IPC §122 ↔ BNS §149); preparation to commit dacoity (IPC §399 ↔ BNS §310(4)); possession of counterfeit instruments (IPC §§242–243, 255–256); modern possession offences (NDPS, UAPA, arms). Note: an attempt requires mens rea to commit the complete offence — in Narayan Singh, since the underlying order forbids attempt expressly, strictness of the parent provision carried into the attempt.

Conclusion

The staircase of liability (intention → preparation → attempt → commission) reflects the escalation from thought to deed; the Criminal Law draws the punitive line at attempt — save where the legislature, in its wisdom, moves the line up to preparation for the gravest dangers.

DU LL.B. I Term — post-BNS syllabus question (Types of Punishments, §§4–13)
Framework

BNS Chapter II (“Of Punishments”, §§4–13) replaces IPC §§53–75. §4 prescribes six punishments: (a) death; (b) imprisonment for life; (c) imprisonment — rigorous (with hard labour) or simple; (d) forfeiture of property; (e) fine; (f) community service.

Section-wise analysis (with IPC parallels)
  • §4 ↔ IPC §53: IPC had five punishments; the BNS adds community service — the first new punishment since 1860; “imprisonment for life” now means the remainder of natural life (see §103).
  • §5 ↔ IPC §§54–55A: commutation of sentence by the appropriate Government without the offender’s consent, per the BNSS §474 ladder (death→life; life→≥7 years; 7+→≥3 years; <7→fine; rigorous→simple). IPC §§54–55 required consent — a deliberate change.
  • §6 ↔ IPC §57: life reckoned as 20 years for computing fractions.
  • §7 ↔ IPC §60: sentence may be wholly/partly rigorous or simple.
  • §8 ↔ IPC §§63–70: fine unlimited where not expressed “but not excessive”; default imprisonment ≤ one-fourth of the maximum term; default scales raised from ₹50/₹100/6 months (IPC §67) to ₹5,000→2 months, ₹10,000→4 months, else 1 year; and default imprisonment now attaches to default of community service as well.

  • §11 ↔ IPC §73: solitary confinement scale — ≤1 month (term ≤6 months), ≤2 months (6 months–1 year), ≤3 months (above 1 year); whipping reference omitted.
  • §12 ↔ IPC §74: limits — never >14 days at a time with intervals of at least equal length; ≤7 days in any month where imprisonment >3 months.
  • §13 ↔ IPC §75: enhanced punishment (life or up to 10 years) for repeat offenders convicted again of 3-year-plus offences under Chapters X (public servants) and XVII (property) — IPC’s Chapter XII renumbered.
Theories reflected

Death and enhanced recidivist punishment express deterrence-prevention; life imprisonment expresses incapacitation; fine is retribution-compensation hybrid; community service expresses the reformative-restorative ideal — the DU CM itself frames the shift “from punishments towards Nyaya”. Nigam’s four-fold object (example, deterrence, reformation, satisfaction of vengeance) is thus embodied across the chapter.

Conclusion

The BNS conserves the classical punitive toolbox, rationalises it into ten sections, modernises monetary scales, liberalises commutation, and adds a reformative sixth punishment — a two-track penal philosophy: death for the rarest of rare, community service for the petty offender.

Q9
Critically examine the retention of the death penalty under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, with reference to the “rarest of rare” doctrine (Bachan Singh, Machhi Singh). Should the death penalty be abolished? [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — “Retention of Death Penalty” · DU CM pp.43 ff.
Constitutional foundation

Jagmohan Singh (1973) upheld the death penalty; Bachan Singh (1980, 4:1) refined: death only in the “rarest of rare” case when the alternative of life imprisonment is “unquestionably foreclosed”; the circumstances of the offender along with those of the crime must be weighed; life is the rule, death the exception; special reasons must be recorded (CrPC §354(3)). Mithu (1983) struck down mandatory death (IPC §303). BNS §104 now offers death or life for murder by a life-convict — Mithu-compliant.

Machhi Singh (1983) — operationalising the doctrine

Death is permissible where the collective conscience of the community is so shocked that society expects the judiciary to inflict death. Five categories: manner (brutal, grotesque, diabolical); motive (total depravity); anti-social/abhorrent nature (terrorising SC/minority; dowry burnings); magnitude (mass/family murders — Machhi Singh’s own case: 17 killed across five villages in one night; death upheld for three, commuted for Mahinder Singh); victim’s personality (child, helpless woman, public figure, victim of trust).

Retention under the BNS

§4(a) retains death; ~16 offences carry it (13 under IPC) — murder (§103(1)); mob lynching — murder by 5+ persons on identity grounds (§103(2), new); murder by life-convict (§104); waging war (§147 ↔ IPC §121); aggravated child rape (§65(2)); certain attempt-to-murder and terrorism/organised-crime provisions. The legislature thus answered the abolition debate by retention plus procedural discipline (Manoj safeguards) rather than abolition.

The case for abolition

Law Commission 262nd Report (2015): abolition for all crimes save terrorism-related and waging war — no evidence of greater deterrence; wrongful convictions and arbitrariness; death row’s class-caste skew (Project 39A data); international abolitionist trajectory (ICCPR Second Optional Protocol); “life means natural life” now incapacitates as effectively.

The case for retention

Constitutional validation (Bachan Singh); proportional retribution for crimes that “shock the collective conscience”; deterrence for terror and mass violence; public confidence in justice; the rarest-of-rare + Manoj safeguards already minimise arbitrariness; the legislature’s repeated democratic choice (1967 35th Report; 2023 BNS).

Critical conclusion

The Indian model is a compromise: the penalty survives in the statute book but is hemmed in by (i) the substantive rarest-of-rare filter, (ii) the Machhi Singh categories as illustrative — not mechanical — tests (Santosh Bariyar), and (iii) the Manoj procedural protocol. Whether that compromise suffices is debatable — but on current law, the answer to “should it be abolished?” must weigh both the 262nd Report and the legislature’s considered retention. A first-class answer takes a reasoned position (either way) supported by these authorities.

Q10
Discuss the guidelines laid down in Manoj & Ors. v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2022) regarding the collection and consideration of mitigating circumstances in death penalty cases. What is the significance of jail conduct in such cases? [20 marks]
DU LL.B. I Term — post-2022 syllabus case (DU CM p.43)
Context

Three death-row convicts (Manoj, Rahul @ Govind, Neha Verma) convicted of the murder of three generations of women during a robbery at Indore. The Supreme Court (Lalit, Bhat, Trivedi, JJ.) noticed the systemic defect: in the absence of well-documented mitigation at trial, aggravating circumstances appear “far more compelling, or overwhelming”, making courts “prone to imposing the death penalty on the basis of an incomplete, and hence incorrect application of the Bachan Singh test”.

The tests restated

Death requires satisfaction of the crime test (aggravating circumstances 100% established), the criminal test (0% mitigating circumstances — young age, lack of intention, no antecedents, probability of reform all absent), and even then the R–R test. The State must prove, on material, the probability (not mere possibility) that the convict cannot be reformed and remains a continuing menace.

The guidelines (the Manoj protocol)
  1. At the sentencing stage the trial court must elicit information from the accused (a genuine mitigation hearing — never same-day sentencing).
  2. The State must produce, preferably collected beforehand, the accused’s psychiatric and psychological evaluation.
  3. Reports of probation officers — nature of the offence, family and social background, education, prior conduct — must be before the court.
  4. Jail authorities’ reports on the convict’s conduct, work done, and activities must be obtained; where the appeal is heard long after conviction, a fresh contemporaneous report must be called.
  5. The accused must have an equal opportunity to rebut the State’s material — replacing ad-hoc impressions with verifiable data.
Jail conduct

Contemporaneous prison behaviour is direct, continuous evidence of the probability of reformation — the very question Bachan Singh/§354(3) procedure asks. In Manoj, the convicts’ overall good conduct, young age and absence of antecedents led the Court to hold the option of life imprisonment “certainly not foreclosed”: death commuted to life with a minimum of 25 years before remission.

Significance & aftermath

The protocol operationalises individualised sentencing as an Article 14/21 guarantee; in Vasanta Sampat Dupare v. UOI (2025) the Court held death sentences passed in breach of the protocol can be reopened under Article 32 — the guidelines are part of the fair-trial guarantee. (Statistics show, however, that trial courts still frequently fail to call for the reports — a critical point to add.)

Q11

Introduction

BNS §4(f) introduces community service as the sixth punishment — the first addition since 1860. It is not defined in the BNS; the Explanation to §23 BNSS defines it: “the work which the Court may order a convict to do as a form of punishment that benefits the community, for which he shall not be entitled to any remuneration.”

Object

Shift from purely punitive to reformative-restorative justice (“from punishments towards Nyaya” — DU CM course objective): accountability without incarceration; restitution to the community; decongesting prisons; avoiding the criminogenic stigma of imprisonment for petty offenders; cheaper administration.

Scope — the six offences

The High Court of Delhi framed guidelines for awarding community service under §4(f); the GNCTD Home Department, by gazette notification (June 2025), operationalised them: 12 permissible activities (cleaning and maintenance of hospital wards and peripheral areas; weeding roadsides and medians; maintaining/cleaning public parks and open spaces; arranging and binding books in public libraries; sanitation of public spaces; and similar tasks), duration 1–31 days or 40–240 hours, for minor offences, with judicial discretion expressly preserved — the list “is not intended to curtail the discretion of the Court”. Critical evaluation

Merits: proportionate response to petty criminality; restorative and reformative; reduces prison overcrowding; international convergence (UK community orders, Spain, Canada). Concerns: no national statutory framework — nature of work, supervising agency, monitoring and verification are unspecified (Delhi’s guidelines fill the gap only locally); risk of humiliating or degrading assignments (Articles 14, 21, 23 concerns — no forced labour or public shaming); inconsistent application across judges; weaker deterrence for the unscrupulous; potential inequality (rich pay fines, poor sweep streets); probation machinery needs strengthening.

Conclusion

Community service is the BNS’s clearest reformative statement — a “second chance” track for the petty offender, provided its execution is standardised (Delhi HC 2025 model), dignified, and monitored. As the Delhi guidelines themselves show, judicial creativity will keep refining the legislature’s sketch.

(a) Solitary confinement — BNS §§11–12 (IPC §§73–74)

A sentence of rigorous imprisonment may include solitary confinement for portions of the term, never exceeding 3 months in the whole, on the scale: ≤1 month if the term is ≤6 months; ≤2 months if it exceeds 6 months but not 1 year; ≤3 months if it exceeds 1 year (§11). §12 sets humanitarian limits: solitary shall never exceed 14 days at a time, with intervals between periods at least as long as the periods; where the imprisonment exceeds 3 months, solitary shall not exceed 7 days in any one month. Its severity is the reason for the strict caps (the “separate confinement” jurisprudence of the 19th century). The IPC §73 reference to whipping — abolished in 1955 — is dropped in the BNS.

(b) Commutation — BNS §5 (IPC §§54, 55, 55A)

The appropriate Government may commute — without the offender’s consent — any punishment to another in accordance with BNSS §474: death → any other punishment (in practice life); life → imprisonment not less than 7 years; imprisonment of 7 years or more → not less than 3 years; imprisonment under 7 years → fine; rigorous → simple. “Appropriate Government”: the Centre for death sentences and Union-law offences; otherwise the State of sentencing. Departure from the IPC: §§54–55 IPC required the offender’s consent. Constitutional clemency (Arts. 72/161) operates above this statutory power (Kehar Singh; Shatrughan Chauhan — undue delay in mercy is a supervening constitutional ground for commutation).

(c) One act, several offences — BNS §§9–10 (IPC §§71–72)

§9: where an offence is made up of parts each of which is itself an offence, only one punishment may be imposed unless expressly provided — A’s fifty blows with a stick = one offence of hurt; but a deliberate blow to a bystander mid-episode is a separate offence. §10: where the judgment is that the accused is guilty of one of several specified offences but it is doubtful which, he is punished for the offence with the lowest punishment — the in-dubio-mitius principle at the sentencing stage.

(d) Presumption of culpable mental state — §10-C, Essential Commodities Act (as discussed in Narayan Singh)

Where an offence under the Act requires a culpable mental state, the court presumes its existence; the accused may escape only by proving its absence beyond reasonable doubt (not on preponderance). “Culpable mental state” includes intention, motive, knowledge of a fact, and belief in or reason to believe a fact. This legislative device, which reversed Nathulal, stands between full mens rea and full strict liability — and was applied in State of M.P. v. Narayan Singh.

(e) Transferred malice / transferred intention

Where a person intends a particular crime and brings about all its elements, he is guilty though the crime takes effect on an unintended victim — A poisons B’s bedside glass of milk; C drinks it and dies: A is guilty of C’s murder. The mens rea “transfers” from the intended to the actual victim. Recognised in Indian practice through the “thirdly” clause of murder (IPC §300 ↔ BNS §102 — intention to kill one person makes the killing of another murder) and applied in inchoate-offence scenarios.
