PART IIITheoretical Foundation — Mens Rea, Rashness and Negligence

Before entering § 106, the DU casebook builds the mental-element ladder in its general-principles chapter. Negligence occupies its lowest rung — and it is the only rung on which a person may be punished for harm he neither intended nor foresaw.

3.1 The four mental states (DU Casebook, general principles)

  • Intention — the desired consequence; foresight plus desire.
  • Knowledge / recklessness — “Intention cannot exist without foresight, but foresight can exist without intention. For a man may foresee the possible or even probable consequences of his conduct and yet not desire this state of risk of bringing about the unwished result. This state of mind is known as ‘recklessness’. The words ‘rash’ and ‘rashness’ have also been used to indicate this same attitude.” (Law of Crimes-I Casebook, p. 18)
  • Negligence — “If anything is done without any advertence to the consequent event or result, the mental state in such situation signifies negligence… The word ‘negligence’, therefore, is used to denote blameworthy inadvertence.” (ibid.)
  • No fault / pure accident — harm that a reasonable man would not have foreseen or avoided: “the harm must lie where it falls.”
Casebook theory — why negligence is punished at all “Strictly speaking, negligence may not be a form of mens rea. It is more in the nature of a legal fault. However, it is made punishable for a utilitarian purpose of hoping to improve people’s standards of behaviour. Criminal liability for negligence is exceptional at common law; manslaughter appears to be the only common law crime which may result from negligence.” And: “the wilful wrong doer is liable because he desires to do the harm; the negligent wrong doer is liable because he does not sufficiently desire to avoid it… it is on this ground that negligence is treated as a form of mens rea, standing side by side with wrongful intention as a formal ground of responsibility.” (Law of Crimes-I Casebook, pp. 18–19)

Advertent vs inadvertent negligence (wilful negligence = recklessness)

“Advertent negligence is commonly termed as wilful negligence or recklessness… In the former the harm done is foreseen as possible or probable but it is not willed. In the latter it is neither foreseen nor willed.” The casebook’s illustration: the physician who treats a patient improperly through ignorance or forgetfulness is guilty of simple (inadvertent) negligence; but if he does the same to save himself trouble, or by way of scientific experiment with full recognition of the danger, his negligence is wilful. Advertent negligence shades into the “rashness” of § 106; inadvertent negligence of a gross degree is the “negligence” of § 106.

Principle Criminal negligence is not a separate mental state but a degree-qualified one: the same carelessness that founds a civil suit founds a prosecution under §106/304A only when it is so gross that it exhibits a reckless disregard of human life — “a very high degree of negligence” (Lord Atkin) or, in Indian formulation, “gross and culpable neglect” (Straight, J., Empress v. Idu Beg).

PART IVCausing Death by Negligence — BNS § 106 ↔ IPC § 304A

4.1 Legislative history

  • The original Penal Code of 1860 had no provision punishing death caused by negligence — liability for causing death was confined to murder and culpable homicide not amounting to murder. Section 304A was inserted by the Indian Penal Code (Amendment) Act 27 of 1870 to cover cases “wherein a person causes the death of another by such acts as are rash or negligent but there is no intention to cause death and no knowledge that the act will cause death.” (DU E-Notes / Manupatra IPC commentary)
  • The BNS, 2023 re-enacts it as § 106, raises the maximum from two to five years, adds a separate sentencing limb for registered medical practitioners (two years), and adds an altogether new sub-section (2) for hit-and-run drivers (ten years).
  • Rationale for enhancement (KD Gaur commentary in course material): India’s roads are “lethal” — with ~1% of the world’s vehicles India accounts for ~10% of global road fatalities; the two-year ceiling of 1860 vintage was considered grotesquately inadequate.
Statute — IPC § 304A (predecessor)
“Whoever causes the death of any person by doing any rash or negligent act, not amounting to culpable homicide, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both.”
Statute — BNS § 106 (as per course gazette)
106. (1) Whoever causes death of any person by doing any rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to five years, and shall also be liable to fine; and if such act is done by a registered medical practitioner while performing medical procedure, he shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years, and shall also be liable to fine.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this sub-section, “registered medical practitioner” means a medical practitioner who possesses any medical qualification recognised under the National Medical Commission Act, 2019 and whose name has been entered in the National Medical Register or a State Medical Register under that Act.

(2) Whoever causes death of any person by rash and negligent driving of vehicle not amounting to culpable homicide, and escapes without reporting it to a police officer or a Magistrate soon after the incident, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description of a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

Classification (exam supplement): §106(1) — cognizable, bailable, triable by Magistrate of the first class; §106(2) — cognizable, non-bailable, triable by Magistrate of the first class. IPC §304A was cognizable, bailable, non-compoundable, triable by any Magistrate.

4.2 Ingredients / elements

Synthesising the DU exam-format material and the Manupatra/E-Notes commentary:

  1. There must be homicide — the death of a human being must actually be caused; the section has no application where only injury results.
  2. The accused must have caused such death — by an act (or by a negligent illegal omission).
  3. The death must be caused by doing a rash or negligent act — rashness or negligence is the mens rea of the offence.
  4. The act must not amount to culpable homicide — i.e., the ingredients of BNS §100 / IPC §299 (intention or knowledge of likelihood of death) must be absent; “Sec 106 of BNS apply only when it is proved that Sec 100 is not applicable.”
  5. Direct nexus (causa causans) — the rash/negligent act must be the proximate and efficient cause of death, “without the intervention of another’s negligence”; it must be the causa causans, not merely the causa sine qua non.

4.3 Rashness and Negligence — the twin mental states

Neither term is defined in the IPC or the BNS; their content is judge-made. The controlling definitions in the DU material are those of Straight, J. in Empress v. Idu Beg (1881) 3 All 776, adopted by the Supreme Court in Bhalchandra Waman Pathe v. State of Maharashtra (1968) and restated in S.N. Hussain:

Rashness (advertent)
“Rashness consists in hazarding a dangerous or wanton act with the knowledge that it is so, and that it may cause injury. The criminality lies in such a case in running the risk of doing such act with recklessness or indifference as to the consequences.”

And the classical formulation in the DU exam material: “Culpable rashness is acting with the consciousness that the mischievous and illegal consequences may follow, but with the hope that they will not, and often with the belief that the actor has taken sufficient precaution to prevent their happening.” A rash act is “primarily an overhasty act and is opposed to a deliberate act, even if it is partly deliberate; it is done without due thought and caution.”

Negligence (inadvertent)
“Criminal negligence … is the gross and culpable neglect or failure to exercise that reasonable and proper care and precaution to guard against injury either to the public generally or to an individual in particular, which, having regard to all the circumstances out of which the charge has arisen, it was the imperative duty of the accused person to have adopted.”

Tort baseline (Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks): “the omission to do something which a reasonable man, guided by those considerations which ordinarily regulate the conduct of human affairs, would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do.”

4.4 Causation — causa causans, not causa sine qua non

  • “This section applies where there is a direct nexus between the death of a person and the rash or negligent act. The act must be the causa causans; it is not enough that it may have been the causa sine qua non.” (Manupatra/KD Gaur commentary)
  • The chain is broken where “death has arisen from any other supervening act or intervention which could not have been anticipated”, i.e., death “was not the direct or proximate result of the rash or negligent act”.
  • Contributory negligence is no defence in criminal law: “Where there is ample proof that the accused had brought about the accident by his own negligence and rashness, it does not matter whether the deceased was deaf, or drunk, or in part contributed to his own death.” (DU E-Notes/Manupatra) (Contrast S.N. Hussain, where the gateman’s negligence made the accident unavoidable for the driver — there the third party’s negligence negated the driver’s own culpable negligence rather than merely contributing to it.)
  • Accident simpliciter is outside the section: e.g., Waryam Singh v. Emperor, AIR 1926 Lah 554 — accused on a dark night believing a man to be a ghost killed him: no liability under §304A.

4.5 Rash or negligent driving — the working tests

  • Duty to control: “A person driving a motor car is under a duty to control the car; he is prima facie guilty of negligence if the car leaves the road and dashes into a tree. It is for the person driving the car to explain the circumstances… but if the driver is not rash, he is not liable for the death of a person who, while suddenly crossing the road, comes under the wheels of his vehicle.” (DU E-Notes/Manupatra)
  • Mere accident insufficient: “The mere fact that a fatal motor run-over accident took place would not by itself be enough to make the driver liable under section 304A… it must be found as a fact that a collision was entirely or at least mainly due to rashness or negligence on the part of the driver. It is not sufficient if it is only found that the accused was driving the vehicle at a fast speed” — State v. Hari Singh, AIR 1969 Raj 86.
  • Criteria for judging driving: “width of the road, the density of the traffic and the attempt to overtake other vehicles and the going on the wrong side” — Shakila Khader v. Nausher Gama, AIR 1975 SC 1324.
  • Error of judgment ≠ negligence: Syed Akbar v. State of Karnataka (1980) 1 SCC 30 — bus driver at moderate speed swerved to dodge a child suddenly crossing; the swerve itself showed the accident was an error of judgment, not culpable negligence.
  • No presumption from mere fall: Mohd. Aynuddin v. State of A.P. (2000) 7 SCC 72 — passenger fell from a boarding bus and died; scanty evidence could not fasten criminal negligence on the driver; conviction set aside.
  • Res ipsa loquitur is only a rule of evidence in tort; “it cannot be pressed in service for determining per se the liability for negligence within the domain of criminal law” — Jacob Mathew.

4.6 Medical negligence — the Jacob Mathew regime and BNS §106(1) proviso

Criminal liability of professionals is judged with special caution: “a great care should be taken before imputing criminal negligence to a professional man acting in the course of his profession. A doctor is not criminally responsible for a patient’s death unless his negligence shows such disregard for life and safety as to amount to a crime against the State” — Juggan Khan v. State of M.P., AIR 1965 SC 831 (there, a registered homeopath who administered 24 drops of stramonium and a dhatura leaf without studying its effect was convicted — grossness was present).

Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab & Anr.
(2005) 6 SCC 1 · AIR 2005 SC 3180 · Appeals (Crl.) 144–145 of 2004 · Decided 05.08.2005 · Bench: R.C. Lahoti CJI, G.P. Mathur & P.K. Balasubramanyan JJ. (full judgment in DU course file “Medical negligence”)
Facts

On 15.02.1995 the complainant’s father, Jiwan Lal Sharma — a terminal-stage cancer patient — was admitted to a private ward of CMC Hospital, Ludhiana. On 22.02.1995 at about 11 p.m. he felt difficulty in breathing; the duty nurse summoned doctors; none turned up for 20–25 minutes. Dr Jacob Mathew and Dr Allen Joseph then attended; an oxygen cylinder was connected but proved empty; no other cylinder arrangement could be made functional for 5–7 minutes; another doctor arrived and declared the patient dead. The FIR alleged death “due to carelessness of doctors and nurses and non-availability of oxygen cylinder and the empty cylinder was fixed on the mouth of my father”. An offence under §304A/34 IPC was registered; charge framed; revision to Sessions dismissed; §482 CrPC petition to quash dismissed by the Punjab & Haryana High Court (18.12.2002; recall application dismissed 24.01.2003); hence these appeals by special leave.

Issues
  1. Whether the averments in the complaint/FIR, even if taken at face value, disclose criminal rashness or negligence (as opposed to civil negligence) by the appellant-doctors?
  2. What is the degree of negligence required for a prosecution under §304A IPC, particularly against medical professionals?
  3. What safeguards/guidelines should govern the prosecution and arrest of doctors?
Arguments — Appellant (doctors)
  • No specific allegation of any act of omission or commission amounting to criminal negligence existed in the entire challan; the plea was answerable at trial but the charge itself was unsustainable.
  • Criminal negligence requires mens rea and a gross degree; mere civil-liability lack of care cannot found a prosecution (Andrews v. DPP).
  • Indiscriminate prosecution of medical professionals harms the profession and society; safeguards (independent medical opinion before prosecution/arrest) were necessary.
Arguments — Respondent (State/complainant)
  • The FIR itself disclosed carelessness: an empty oxygen cylinder fixed on the patient’s mouth and delay in attendance — sufficient to proceed under §304A read with §34.
  • Whether the negligence was gross was a matter of evidence for trial, not for quashing at the threshold.
Decision

Appeals allowed; the prosecution under §304A/34 IPC quashed. The Court held that even if all averments were proved, they did not establish criminal rashness or negligence: it was not alleged that the appellant was unqualified to treat the patient; the grievance was non-availability of an empty cylinder — a matter for which “probably the hospital may be liable in civil law… but the accused appellant cannot be proceeded against under Section 304A IPC on the parameters of Bolam’s test.” The principles of Dr. Suresh Gupta v. State of A.P. (2004) 6 SCC 422 were reaffirmed (without comment on its facts).

Principles evolved (the Jacob Mathew doctrine)
  1. Degrees of negligence: civil “simple lack of care” is not enough; criminal law demands “a very high degree of negligence” (Lord Atkin, Andrews v. DPP); negligence must be “culpable or gross and not… merely based upon an error of judgment”.
  2. Professional standard: a professional impliedly assures only that he possesses the requisite skill and will exercise it with reasonable competence — he does not assure the result; “a physician would not assure the patient of full recovery in every case”.
  3. Bolam test: a professional is not negligent if his practice conforms to that of “a responsible body of medical opinion” skilled in that field (Bolam v. Friern Hospital Management Committee [1957] 1 WLR 582, McNair J.); mere difference of professional opinion does not make a doctor negligent.
  4. Error of judgment: “an error of judgment may or may not be negligent”; it is to be tested against the conduct of an ordinary competent professional in like circumstances.
  5. Mens rea & grossness: for criminal liability the degree of negligence must be much higher — gross or of a very high degree; the words “rash or negligent act” in §304A are to be read as qualified by “grossly”.
  6. The “no professional would” test: it must be shown that the accused did something (or omitted something) “which in the given facts and circumstances no medical professional in his ordinary senses and prudence would have done or failed to do”, and the hazard taken must be such that injury was “most likely imminent”.
  7. Res ipsa loquitur: a rule of evidence of the civil/tort domain; limited application, if at all, in a criminal negligence trial.
Guidelines for prosecuting medical professionals
  1. A private complaint may not be entertained unless the complainant produces prima facie evidence in the form of a credible opinion given by another competent doctor supporting the charge of rashness/negligence.
  2. The investigating officer must, before proceeding against the doctor, obtain an independent and competent medical opinion — preferably from a doctor in government service qualified in that branch — applying the Bolam test to the facts collected.
  3. A doctor accused of rashness/negligence may not be arrested in a routine manner; arrest only if necessary for investigation/evidence, or if the officer is satisfied the doctor will not face prosecution unless arrested.

PART VDU Case Law I — Cherubin Gregory v. State of Bihar

Cherubin Gregory v. State of Bihar
AIR 1964 SC 205 : (1964) 4 SCR 199 · Coram: N. Rajagopala Ayyangar, J. · Appeal by special leave from Patna High Court (full text in DU Casebook, Unit 7, pp. 247–249)
Facts (as found by the courts below)
  • The appellant fixed a naked, uninsulated copper wire across the passage leading to his latrine at the back of his house, connected to the electrical wiring of the house so as to carry a lethal voltage, in order to make entry dangerous and thereby prevent intruders from using the latrine.
  • The motive: the latrine wall of the neighbouring house (of which the deceased, Mst. Madilen, was an inmate) had fallen down about a week before the occurrence (16 July 1959), exposing her latrine to public view; the deceased and others therefore began using the appellant’s latrine. The appellant resented this, protested, and gave oral warnings which proved ineffective — whereupon he electrified the passage.
  • There was no warning whatsoever that the wire was live.
  • The deceased entered the latrine without contacting the wire, but on her way out her hand touched it; she received a shock and died soon after.
  • The accused’s suggestion of sufficient warning — that it was past daybreak (enough light) and that an electric light burned some distance away — was rejected: “neither of these could constitute warning as the condition of the wire being charged with electric current could not obviously be detected merely by the place being properly lit.”
Procedural history

Charged under §304 IPC and §304-A IPC before the Sessions Judge, Champaran; acquitted of §304 (no intention to cause death) but convicted under §304-A; appeal dismissed by the High Court of Patna; hence appeal by special leave to the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Whether the appellant could claim the right of private defence of property (IPC §§97, 99, 103) for a death caused by the trap he laid?
  2. Whether, the deceased being a trespasser to whom (as allegedly in English common law) the occupier owed no duty of care, an act that is not a tort could yet be a crime under §304-A?
  3. Whether fixing the live naked wire without warning was a “rash act” within §304-A IPC (now BNS §106)?
Arguments — Appellant
  • Private defence: the death was caused in the course of exercising the right of private defence of property against trespassers.
  • No duty to trespasser / no tort, no crime: the deceased was a trespasser; an occupier owes no duty of reasonable care to a trespasser, who therefore would have had no cause of action for damages; if the act was not actionable in tort, it could not be a crime.
  • Warning: daylight and the burning electric light amounted to sufficient notice of the wire.
Arguments — Respondent (State) / findings relied upon
  • The wire was naked, uninsulated and carried lethal voltage; charging it was done in reckless disregard of serious consequences to anyone coming in contact with it.
  • No warning of the live wire existed; the trap was deliberate — laid precisely because oral warnings had failed.
  • The concurrent findings of fact by the Sessions Judge and the High Court concluded the factual disputes; even learned counsel for the appellant did not controvert them.
Decision

Appeal dismissed; conviction under §304-A IPC upheld. The Supreme Court held:

  • Rash act established: “The voltage of the current passing through the naked wire being high enough to be lethal, there could be no dispute that charging it with current of that voltage was a ‘rash act’ done in reckless disregard of the serious consequences to people coming in contact with it.”
  • Private defence rejected: the right under §97 is “subject to the provisions of Section 99”; “the type of injury caused by the trap laid by the accused cannot be brought within the scope of Section 99, nor of course of Section 103 of the Code.”
  • Trespasser/tort argument rejected: (a) where a Code “defines with particularity the ingredients of a crime and the defences open to an accused”, it is not proper “to permit the invocation of some Common Law principle outside that Code for the purpose of treating, what on the words of the statute is a crime, into a permissible or other than unlawful act”; (b) in any event the act was an actionable wrong — “A trespasser is not an outlaw, a caput lupinem… the occupier is not entitled to do wilfully acts such as set a trap or set a naked live wire with the deliberate intention of causing harm to trespassers or in reckless disregard of the presence of the trespassers.” The naked live wire, like a spring-gun, is in truth “an arrangement to shoot a man without personally firing a shot”.
  • The Court endorsed the UK Law Reform Committee’s summary: “The trespasser enters entirely at his own risk, but the occupier must not set traps designed to do him bodily harm or to do any act calculated to do bodily harm to the trespasser whom he knows to be or who to his knowledge is likely to be on his premises… This is no more than ordinary civilised behaviour.”
Principles evolved
  1. Lethal trapped wire = rash act: laying a naked live wire of lethal voltage across a passage, without warning, is a rash act done in reckless disregard of consequences → liability under §304-A IPC / BNS §106 even absent intention to kill (hence §304 acquittal but §304-A conviction).
  2. Trespasser’s limited position: a trespasser enters at his own risk and the occupier owes him no duty of reasonable care — but the occupier may not wilfully set man-traps, spring-guns or live wires to harm him; “a trespasser is not an outlaw”.
  3. Code is self-contained: common-law notions of civil actionability cannot convert a statutory crime into a lawful act; criminal liability under the IPC/BNS is judged by the Code’s own ingredients.
  4. Private defence has moral limits: §§97/99/103 do not protect deliberately laid lethal traps; the voltage used “precludes any contention that it was merely a reasonable precaution for the protection of private property”.

PART VIDU Case Law II — S.N. Hussain v. State of Andhra Pradesh

S.N. Hussain v. State of Andhra Pradesh
AIR 1972 SC 685 · Coram: D.G. Palekar, J. · Appeal by special leave against High Court of Andhra Pradesh reversing acquittal (full text in DU Casebook, Unit 7, pp. 249–252)
Facts
  • The appellant was the driver of R.T.C. bus APZ 1672, driving on 1 January 1966 from Kurnool to Vanaparthy. The bus left Kurnool at about 6.15 a.m. and reached the railway level-crossing gate between Alampur Road Station and Manopad railway station at about 6.30–7.00 a.m.
  • The level crossing was a manned crossing in charge of a gateman, whose duty was to close the gate when a train was expected. It is an admitted fact that the gate was open when the bus arrived; the appellant passed through and crossed the meter-gauge track.
  • Suddenly a goods train dashed against the rear side of the bus; the bus was thrown off; of about 43 passengers, one died on the spot, three died later in hospital, and about 21 received more or less severe injuries.
  • Charge under §§304-A, 337 and 338 IPC: that the appellant was rash or negligent in crossing the railway track when a goods train was about to pass the gate.

Six material circumstances found by the Supreme Court: (1) the bus was driven, and could only be driven, dead slow — the approach is a U-shaped road with two bends and a climb, the track lying at a much higher level (so admitted by conductor P.W. 13 and Sub-Inspector P.W. 56 seated beside the driver); (2) the manned gate was open, indicating no train was expected and inviting vehicles to pass; (3) the track was at a higher level and the road lined with babool trees, so an approaching train was not visible from the bus; (4) the bus, unfitted with a silencer, made huge noise; (5) because of a cold breeze some window screens were lowered; (6) no evidence that the train gave any whistle, or that any whistle was heard by occupants.

Procedural history

Tried before the Munsif Magistrate, Alampur, who accepted the defence and acquitted. The State appealed against acquittal; the High Court of Andhra Pradesh convicted under all three sections, sentencing him to two years’ rigorous imprisonment under §304-A (other sentences concurrent). Hence this appeal by special leave.

Issues
  1. Whether the appellant was rash or negligent in crossing the railway track when a goods train was about to pass?
  2. Whether the High Court was justified in reversing a reasoned acquittal on that evidence?
Arguments — Appellant
  • He was neither rash nor negligent; the accident was unavoidable; since the manned gate was open he crossed “absolutely oblivious of the fact that a train was approaching”.
  • The learned Magistrate had taken a very reasonable view; the High Court should not have interfered with the order of acquittal.
  • Most prosecution witnesses turned hostile; the few witnesses relied on by the High Court (gangman Sayanna, his wife Kanthamma, P.W. 15, son Samuel P.W. 16) had not really proved the charge; the High Court misread the evidence of Sub-Inspector Laxaman Rao (P.W. 56).
Arguments / reasoning of the High Court (respondent side)
  • The driver negotiated the crossing “in a spirit of bravado and absolutely callous and unmindful of the consequences of the impending collision” — passengers (Sayanna etc.) had shouted warnings before the bus crossed.
  • The driver was negligent in not stopping at the road signals on either side of the track, which required vehicles to stop; he should have come to a dead stop and satisfied himself that no train was coming.
Decision

Appeal allowed; conviction and sentence set aside; appellant acquitted. The Supreme Court held:

  • No rashness: on the evidence the driver “received no warning either from the approaching train or from the passengers in the bus in sufficient time to save the collision”. The bus was 21 feet long and the crossing about 18 feet across; the shouted warnings (even if true) came when the bus was already on the track — “too late to avoid the collision”. On seeing the train, accelerating to clear the track was “the best he could do… This cannot be regarded either as bravado or adventure.” The High Court “completely misread” P.W. 56’s evidence.
  • No criminal negligence: at an unmanned crossing a driver may be required to stop, look both ways and then cross; “but where a level crossing is protected by a gateman and the gateman opens out the gate inviting the vehicles to pass, it will be too much to expect of any reasonable and prudent driver to stop his vehicle and look out for any approaching train.” Where the gate is open and no train is scheduled, “the driver would be justified in driving his vehicle through the level crossing”. A passenger train’s schedule might put a regular route-driver on notice; but this was an unscheduled goods train. Failure to stop at the road signal, in these circumstances, was not culpable negligence: “Culpable negligence lies in the failure to exercise reasonable and proper care and the extent of its reasonableness will always depend upon the circumstances of each case.”
  • Conclusion: “This was a clear case of unavoidable accident because of the negligence of the gateman in keeping the gate open and inviting the vehicles to pass.”
Principles evolved
  1. Canonical definitions adopted: criminal rashness and criminal negligence as defined by Straight, J. in Empress v. Idu Beg (1881) 3 All 776, adopted in Bhalchandra Waman Pathe v. State of Maharashtra (1968), are the governing tests for §304-A / BNS §106.
  2. Unavoidable accident is not crime: where a third party’s negligence (the gateman) makes the collision unavoidable for a careful driver, the driver’s conduct is neither rash nor negligent — an error of the situation, not of culpability.
  3. Reasonable reliance on safety infrastructure: a driver is entitled to treat an open manned gate as an invitation to cross and need not stop dead and reconnoitre; the standard of care is context-sensitive (“depend upon the circumstances of each case”).
  4. Appellate restraint: reversal of an acquittal demands cogent grounds; misreading of evidence vitiates the conviction.
  5. Last-oppportunity conduct exculpatory: accelerating to clear the track upon sighting the train is prudent damage-control, not “bravado”.

PART VIIOrganised Crime — BNS § 111 (New Section; no IPC counterpart)

The IPC of 1860 contained no general offence of “organised crime”; syndicated criminality was addressed only by special statutes (MCOCA, 1999 for Maharashtra and its state analogues, UAPA, NDPS, etc.) and by ordinary provisions on conspiracy and membership of unlawful assemblies. The BNS, 2023 nationalises the concept by importing an MCOCA-style offence into the general penal code.

Statute — BNS § 111 (as per course gazette)
111. (1) Any continuing unlawful activity including kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offence, cyber-crimes, trafficking of persons, drugs, weapons or illicit goods or services, human trafficking for prostitution or ransom, by any person or a group of persons acting in concert, singly or jointly, either as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on behalf of such syndicate, by use of violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion, or by any other unlawful means to obtain direct or indirect material benefit including a financial benefit, shall constitute organised crime.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this sub-section,––
(i) “organised crime syndicate” means a group of two or more persons who, acting either singly or jointly, as a syndicate or gang indulge in any continuing unlawful activity;
(ii) “continuing unlawful activity” means an activity prohibited by law which is a cognizable offence punishable with imprisonment of three years or more, undertaken by any person, either singly or jointly, as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on behalf of such syndicate in respect of which more than one charge-sheets have been filed before a competent Court within the preceding period of ten years and that Court has taken cognizance of such offence, and includes economic offence;
(iii) “economic offence” includes criminal breach of trust, forgery, counterfeiting of currency-notes, bank-notes and Government stamps, hawala transaction, mass-marketing fraud or running any scheme to defraud several persons or doing any act in any manner with a view to defraud any bank or financial institution or any other institution or organisation for obtaining monetary benefits in any form.

(2) Whoever commits organised crime shall,––
(a) if such offence has resulted in the death of any person, be punished with death or imprisonment for life, and shall also be liable to fine which shall not be less than ten lakh rupees;
(b) in any other case, be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than five years but which may extend to imprisonment for life, and shall also be liable to fine which shall not be less than five lakh rupees.

(3) Whoever abets, attempts, conspires or knowingly facilitates the commission of an organised crime, or otherwise engages in any act preparatory to an organised crime — 5 years to life + fine ≥ 5 lakh.
(4) Any person who is a member of an organised crime syndicate — 5 years to life + fine ≥ 5 lakh.
(5) Whoever intentionally harbours or conceals a person who has committed organised crime — 3 years to life + fine ≥ 5 lakh; proviso: not applicable where the harbour/concealment is by the spouse of the offender.
(6) Whoever possesses property derived or obtained from organised crime or its proceeds — 3 years to life + fine ≥ 2 lakh.
(7) Any person who, on behalf of a member of an organised crime syndicate, is or has been in possession of movable or immovable property which he cannot satisfactorily account for — 3 to 10 years + fine ≥ 1 lakh.

7.1 Elements decomposed (five cumulative limbs of §111(1))

  1. Continuing unlawful activity — a course of criminal conduct, not a one-off: an activity prohibited by law, cognizable, punishable with ≥3 years’ imprisonment, evidenced by more than one charge-sheet within the preceding ten years of which a competent court has taken cognizance (economic offences included).
  2. Collectivity — “any person or a group of persons acting in concert, singly or jointly”; the syndicate needs only two or more persons.
  3. Syndicate nexus — done as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on behalf of such syndicate (thus reaching hired actors and benami operators).
  4. Unlawful means — violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion, or any other unlawful means.
  5. Profit objective — to obtain direct or indirect material benefit including financial benefit (the economic hallmark distinguishing organised crime from terror, which pursues political/ideological ends).

  • Inchoate reach: §111(3) punishes conspiracy, attempt, abetment, facilitation and even acts preparatory at the same 5-years-to-life scale as completed crime — a dramatic departure from the IPC’s graded attempt/conspiracy rules (§§109–110, 120B).

PART VIIIPetty Organised Crime — BNS § 112 (New Section)

Statute — BNS § 112 (as per course gazette)
112. (1) Whoever, being a member of a group or gang, either singly or jointly, commits any act of theft, snatching, cheating, unauthorised selling of tickets, unauthorised betting or gambling, selling of public examination question papers or any other similar criminal act, is said to commit petty organised crime.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this sub-section “theft” includes trick theft, theft from vehicle, dwelling house or business premises, cargo theft, pick pocketing, theft through card skimming, shoplifting and theft of Automated Teller Machine.

(2) Whoever commits any petty organised crime shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than one year but which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine.

8.1 Elements

  1. Membership of a group or gang — collective identity is the gateway; a lone thief remains under the ordinary theft provisions (BNS §303).
  2. Commission, singly or jointly, of any listed or analogous act: theft (in the nine explained forms), snatching, cheating, unauthorised ticket selling, unauthorised betting/gambling, selling of public examination question papers, “or any other similar criminal act”.
  3. No syndicate/benefit threshold: unlike §111, neither a “continuing unlawful activity” record (two charge-sheets in ten years) nor a material-benefit objective need be proved — this is the street-level counterpart.

DU E-Notes / Manupatra Question Bank · Theory · 10–15 marks
Q1. “Explain the terms negligence and rashness under IPC.” Distinguish the two and state the degree of negligence required for criminal liability.
Model answer

Provision: IPC §304A (now BNS §106) punishes whoever causes death “by doing any rash or negligent act, not amounting to culpable homicide”. Neither term is defined; both are judge-made mental states sitting below culpable homicide on the culpability ladder.

Rashness is advertent risk-taking: “culpable rashness is acting with the consciousness that the mischievous and illegal consequences may follow, but with the hope that they will not, and often with the belief that the actor has taken sufficient precaution to prevent their happening”; per Straight, J. in Empress v. Idu Beg, adopted in Bhalchandra Waman Pathe and S.N. Hussain v. State of A.P.: “Rashness consists in hazarding a dangerous or wanton act with the knowledge that it is so, and that it may cause injury. The criminality lies in … running the risk of doing such act with recklessness or indifference as to the consequences.” A rash act is overhasty, done without due thought and caution.

Negligence is inadvertent breach of a duty of care: “the omission to do something which a reasonable man … would do, or doing something which a prudent and reasonable man would not do” (Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks); criminal negligence is “the gross and culpable neglect or failure to exercise that reasonable and proper care and precaution … which … it was the imperative duty of the accused person to have adopted” (Idu Beg). The DU casebook adds that negligence denotes “blameworthy inadvertence”, punished for utilitarian reasons although strictly it “may not be a form of mens rea”.

Degree: criminal liability demands grossness — Lord Atkin in Andrews v. DPP: “a very high degree of negligence is required”; Jacob Mathew reads “rash or negligent act” in §304A as qualified by “grossly”; negligence based merely on an error of judgment is not culpable (Syed Akbar).

Difference in one line: the rash actor foresees the risk and runs it; the negligent actor never adverts to a risk he had an imperative duty to guard against; both are punished under the disjunctive “rash or negligent” formula, but never coexist in the same act.

DU E-Notes / Manupatra Problem · 20 marks
Q2. “X started from Delhi on his car to go to Gurgaon at about 11.00 p.m. He was driving at a reasonable speed and quite carefully. But unfortunately on the way he knocked down two coolies who were lying and sleeping on the road itself. X is prosecuted for causing death by rash and negligent driving under section 304A IPC. Can X be convicted? Discuss.” (BNS counterpart: §106.)
Model answer

Issue: whether a careful night driver who kills persons sleeping on the carriageway commits the offence of causing death by negligence.

Rule: §304A/§106 requires (i) death, (ii) caused by the accused’s act, (iii) rashly or negligently, (iv) not amounting to culpable homicide, with a direct nexus — the act must be causa causans. “The mere fact that a fatal motor run-over accident took place would not by itself be enough to make the driver liable… it must be found as a fact that a collision was entirely or at least mainly due to rashness or negligence on the part of the driver” (State v. Hari Singh). A driver who is not rash “is not liable for the death of a person who, while suddenly crossing the road, comes under the wheels of his vehicle”. Tests of rash driving are contextual: width of road, traffic density, overtaking, wrong side (Shakila Khader).

Application: The facts stipulate reasonable speed and careful driving at 11 p.m. — no conscious risk-taking (no rashness) and no breach of an imperative duty of care (no negligence): persons lying asleep on the road at night are not a peril a prudent driver can be expected to foresee and avoid in those conditions. The deaths, though causally connected in fact, are not the product of gross carelessness; the case falls on the “pure accident” side of the spectrum illustrated by S.N. Hussain (unavoidable accident) and Mohd. Aynuddin (no presumption of negligence from a mere accident). Contrast Cherubin Gregory, where the accused himself created the lethal instrument.

Conclusion: X cannot be convicted under §304A IPC / §106 BNS. (If, however, X had escaped without reporting the incident to a police officer or Magistrate, the aggravated §106(2) BNS would independently be attracted on its own elements.)

DU E-Notes / Manupatra Question Bank · Theory · 10 marks
Q3. “Explain the doctrine of contributory negligence” and its place in criminal liability under §304A IPC / §106 BNS.
Model answer

Doctrine (tort): where the plaintiff’s own want of care contributes to his damage, liability/damages are apportioned; in tort it is a partial defence.

Criminal law position: “The doctrine of contributory negligence does not apply to criminal liability. Where there is ample proof that the accused had brought about the accident by his own negligence and rashness, it does not matter whether the deceased was deaf, or drunk, or in part contributed to his own death.” The prosecution’s burden is to prove the accused’s gross negligence as the proximate cause; the victim’s carelessness neither negates it nor dilutes it.

Refinement from the DU casebook: S.N. Hussain shows the limit — there the gateman’s negligence did not “contribute” alongside the driver’s fault; it made the accident unavoidable for a driver exercising reasonable care, thereby negating the driver’s culpable negligence altogether. Distinguish therefore: (a) victim/third-party fault alongside the accused’s gross negligence — no defence; (b) third-party fault that eliminates any breach of duty by the accused — acquittal, because the ingredient of rashness/negligence itself fails.

DU Pattern (Cherubin Gregory line) · Problem · 20 marks
Q4. A, irritated by repeated trespass into his orchard, strings a naked copper wire across the only footpath to it and connects it to his mains supply at lethal voltage, posting no warning. B, a trespasser who knows A has previously warned trespassers orally, enters at dusk, and is electrocuted while climbing out through a gap. B dies. Advise A under the BNS, dealing specifically with (i) private defence of property, and (ii) the argument that a trespasser to whom no civil duty is owed cannot found a criminal charge.
Model answer

Issue: liability for death by a deliberately laid lethal trap; availability of private defence; relevance of the victim’s trespasser status.

Provision: BNS §106(1) (IPC §304A): death by rash or negligent act not amounting to culpable homicide — up to 5 years + fine. Ingredients satisfied: death; caused by A’s act (laying and energising the wire); rashness — “hazarding a dangerous or wanton act with the knowledge that it is so… with recklessness or indifference as to the consequences” (Empress v. Idu Beg); no intention/knowledge of likelihood of death, hence not culpable homicide (BNS §100).

Application — direct authority: Cherubin Gregory v. State of Bihar AIR 1964 SC 205 is squarely in point: “The voltage of the current passing through the naked wire being high enough to be lethal, there could be no dispute that charging it with current of that voltage was a ‘rash act’ done in reckless disregard of the serious consequences to people coming in contact with it.” Absence of warning is decisive; prior oral warnings aggravate rather excuse (they prove knowledge of the likelihood of intrusion). (i) Private defence fails: the right (BNS §37 / IPC §97) is subject to the limiting conditions (IPC §99 / BNS §39 regime; §103/§41 catalogue of harms); “the type of injury caused by the trap laid by the accused cannot be brought within the scope of Section 99, nor of course of Section 103”. (ii) Trespasser argument fails twice over: the Code is self-contained — common-law civil actionability cannot convert a statutory crime into a lawful act; and substantively, “a trespasser is not an outlaw”: the occupier, though owing no duty of reasonable care, “is not entitled to do wilfully acts such as set a trap or set a naked live wire with the deliberate intention of causing harm to trespassers or in reckless disregard of their presence” — the wire being “an arrangement to shoot a man without personally firing a shot”.

Conclusion: A is guilty under BNS §106(1) (IPC §304A); acquittal of murder/culpable homicide would be correct on absence of intention (Cherubin Gregory itself), but the negligence limb stands. Contributory negligence of B (knowing trespass) is no defence.

DU Pattern (S.N. Hussain line) · Problem · 20 marks
Q5. D drives a state-roadways bus on his regular route. At a manned level crossing the gateman has the gate wide open; no passenger train is scheduled at that hour. D crosses at dead-slow speed (the approach has two sharp bends and a gradient). An unscheduled goods train, giving no whistle, strikes the rear of the bus; two passengers die. Passengers shout a warning only when the bus is already on the tracks; D accelerates to clear them. Prosecuted under BNS §106 (IPC §304A). Decide, and explain whether the High Court could validly reverse a trial-court acquittal on these facts.
Model answer

Issue: rashness? negligence? and appellate interference with acquittal.

Rule: rashness = conscious hazarding with recklessness/indifference (Idu Beg); criminal negligence = gross and culpable failure to exercise the care which, having regard to the circumstances, it was the imperative duty of the accused to adopt; “the extent of its reasonableness will always depend upon the circumstances of each case” (S.N. Hussain v. State of A.P., AIR 1972 SC 685).

Application: (a) No rashness: dead-slow approach negates bravado; shouts came too late (the bus already on the track); accelerating to clear the crossing on sighting the train is prudent damage-control, “the best he could do”, not adventure. (b) No negligence: at a manned crossing an open gate is an invitation to cross — “it will be too much to expect of any reasonable and prudent driver to stop his vehicle and look out for any approaching train”; unlike a scheduled passenger train, an unscheduled goods train gives a route driver no notice; the gateman’s negligence made the collision an unavoidable accident. (c) Appellate restraint: reversing a reasoned acquittal demands cogent grounds; in S.N. Hussain the Supreme Court held the High Court had “completely misread” the key eye-witness evidence and restored the acquittal — the same defect would vitiate a reversal here.

Conclusion: D is not guilty of BNS §106/IPC §304A (nor of §§337/338-type hurt offences on the same reasoning); the trial acquittal ought to be restored. Contrast Cherubin Gregory where the accused created the risk: here the risk was created by the gateman.

DU Pattern (Jacob Mathew line) · Theory-cum-problem · 15–20 marks
Q6. “A doctor is not an insurer of the patient’s life.” When can a registered medical practitioner be criminally prosecuted for a patient’s death? Discuss the Jacob Mathew tests and guidelines and the effect of the proviso to BNS §106(1). Apply: Surgeon S, of acknowledged competence, chooses a recognised but minority surgical technique; the patient dies of a known risk of that technique occurring in 2% of cases. The relatives lodge an FIR alleging murder and demand S’s immediate arrest.
Model answer

Rule: criminal liability for medical negligence requires gross negligence: (i) degrees — “a very high degree of negligence” (Andrews v. DPP); §304A’s “rash or negligent act” is “read as qualified by the word ‘grossly’” (Jacob Mathew); (ii) professional standard — a doctor assures skill and reasonable competence, never the result; (iii) Bolam test — conduct approved by a responsible body of professional opinion skilled in that field is not negligence, and “one man clearly is not negligent merely because his judgment differs from other professionals’; (iv) the “no professional in his ordinary senses and prudence would have done/failed to do” test; (v) error of judgment ≠ negligence; (vi) res ipsa loquitur unavailable as a shortcut in criminal law. Juggan Khan shows conviction remains possible for disregard of life and safety amounting to a crime against the State (unstudied administration of a poisonous drug).

Procedural safeguards (Jacob Mathew guidelines): private complaint only with prima facie credible opinion of another competent doctor; investigating officer to obtain independent medical opinion (preferably a government doctor of the same branch) applying Bolam; no routine arrest — arrest only if necessary for investigation/evidence or to secure attendance. Murder (BNS §103/IPC §302) is wholly misplaced absent intention to cause death.

Statutory update: by the proviso to BNS §106(1) (inserted by the Bharatiya Nyaya (Second Amendment) Act, 2025), a registered medical practitioner (NMC Act 2019 register) causing death by rash/negligent act while performing a medical procedure faces a ceiling of two years + fine, against five years for others.

Application: S’s choice of a recognised minority technique is protected by Bolam — a responsible body of opinion supports it; the 2% known risk is an informed professional hazard, not gross carelessness; nothing suggests conduct no prudent professional would adopt. The FIR discloses no ingredient of §106, let alone murder; the demand for immediate arrest violates the Jacob Mathew arrest guideline.

Conclusion: no offence of murder; no offence under §106(1); the prosecution should be quashed/declined per Jacob Mathew; at most a civil claim could be explored, and even that would likely fail on Bolam.

DU Pattern · Theory · 15 marks
Q7. Define “organised crime” under BNS §111. Explain “organised crime syndicate”, “continuing unlawful activity” and “economic offence”, and set out the punishment structure including the allied offences in sub-sections (3) to (7).
Model answer

Definition (paraphrase with keywords): any continuing unlawful activity (kidnapping, robbery, vehicle theft, extortion, land grabbing, contract killing, economic offence, cyber-crimes, trafficking of persons, drugs, weapons or illicit goods or services, human trafficking for prostitution or ransom) by any person or group acting in concert, singly or jointly, as a member of an organised crime syndicate or on its behalf, by violence, threat of violence, intimidation, coercion or other unlawful means, to obtain direct or indirect material benefit including financial benefit.

Three defined expressions: (i) syndicate = group of two or more persons acting singly or jointly as a syndicate/gang indulging in continuing unlawful activity; (ii) continuing unlawful activity = activity prohibited by law, a cognizable offence punishable with ≥3 years, undertaken as member/on behalf of syndicate, with more than one charge-sheet filed within the preceding ten years of which a competent court has taken cognizance, and includes economic offence; (iii) economic offence = criminal breach of trust, forgery, counterfeiting of currency-notes/bank-notes/Government stamps, hawala, mass-marketing fraud or schemes to defraud several persons, or any act to defraud banks/financial institutions/organisations for monetary benefit.

Punishment ladder: 111(2)(a) death caused → death or life + fine ≥ ₹10 lakh; 111(2)(b) otherwise → 5 yrs–life + fine ≥ ₹5 lakh; 111(3) abet/attempt/conspire/knowingly facilitate/acts preparatory → same 5 yrs–life + ≥ ₹5 lakh; 111(4) membership → same; 111(5) harbour/conceal (spouse exempt) → 3 yrs–life + ≥ ₹5 lakh; 111(6) possession of proceeds → 3 yrs–life + ≥ ₹2 lakh; 111(7) unaccounted property held on behalf of a syndicate member → 3–10 yrs + ≥ ₹1 lakh.

Closing analysis: the section nationalises MCOCA-style syndicate liability; its continuity-record threshold (two charge-sheets/ten years/cognizance) is the prosecution’s first hurdle, and its inchoate and property limbs (§§111(3), (6), (7)) attack the syndicate’s manpower and economy rather than only its overt acts.

DU Pattern · Problem · 20 marks
Q8. A ring of six persons operates in a railway station area: two pick pockets, two skim cards at ATMs, and two resell stolen cards; each member has one prior charge-sheet for theft in the last four years, of which cognizance was taken. They also snatch chains from lone commuters. Discuss their liability under BNS §§111 and 112, explaining why one may apply where the other does not.
Model answer

§112 analysis (satisfied): all are “members of a group or gang” committing, singly or jointly, acts of theft — the Explanation expressly includes pick pocketing and theft through card skimming and theft of Automated Teller Machine — and snatching. No continuity record or benefit objective is required. Each member is liable for petty organised crime: 1–7 years + fine, in addition to the substantive theft/snatching/cheating provisions.

§111 analysis (fails on the continuity limb, arguable on others): the group is a “syndicate” of two or more persons acting in concert for material benefit, using unlawful means (snatching implies force). But “continuing unlawful activity” demands, in respect of the activity, more than one charge-sheet within the preceding ten years with cognizance taken; on the stated facts each member has only one prior charge-sheet. Unless the prosecution can aggregate charge-sheets against the syndicate’s activity as such (a point of construction to argue both ways), the §111 threshold is not met — precisely the gap §112 was enacted to fill: gang crime that is organised in fact but below the syndicate-record threshold.

Conclusion: convict under §112 (with ordinary theft/snatching sections); reserve §111 for proof of the two-charge-sheet continuity record or of syndicate-scale activities (e.g., cargo theft contracts, extortion). Note the sentencing contrast: §112 caps at 7 years with discretionary fine; §111 begins at 5 years with mandatory minimum fines and reaches death/life where death results.

DU Pattern · Theory · 15 marks
Q9. What is a “terrorist act” under BNS §113? Explain its mental element, the three limbs of the actus reus, the ancillary offences in sub-sections (3)–(7), and the role of the Superintendent of Police under the final Explanation. How does §113 differ from §111?
Model answer

Mental element: intent to threaten, or likelihood of threatening, the unity, integrity, sovereignty, security or economic security of India, or intent to strike terror, or likelihood of striking terror, in the people or any section of the people in India or in any foreign country.

Actus reus (any one): (a) using bombs, dynamite, explosive/inflammable substance, firearms, other lethal weapons, poisonous or noxious gases, chemicals, or any hazardous substance (biological, radioactive, nuclear or otherwise), or any other means of whatever nature, to cause or likely to cause: death/injury; loss/damage/destruction of property; disruption of essential supplies or services; damage to India’s monetary stability via counterfeit Indian currency (forensically certified as imitating key security features); or damage/destruction of defence or Government property; (b) overawing by criminal force or its show, or causing/attempting death of a public functionary (constitutional or Gazette-notified authorities); (c) detaining, kidnapping or abducting with threats to kill or injure, or any other act, to compel the Government of India, a State Government, a foreign Government, an international/inter-governmental organisation or any person to do or abstain from any act.

Punishments and ancillary offences: §113(2)(a) death caused → death or life + fine; (2)(b) otherwise 5 yrs–life + fine; (3) conspiracy, attempt, advocacy, abetment, advice, incitement, knowing facilitation or preparatory acts → 5 yrs–life + fine; (4) organising training camps or recruiting for terrorist acts → 5 yrs–life + fine; (5) membership of an organisation involved in terrorist act → up to life + fine; (6) voluntary harbour/concealment of a terrorist (spouse exempt) → 3 yrs–life + fine; (7) knowing possession of terror-derived property → up to life + fine.

SP gate: the final Explanation declares that an officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police decides whether to register the case under §113 or under the UAPA, 1967 — preventing duplicate/competing registrations between the code and the special law.

§111 vs §113: organised crime is economically motivated (material/financial benefit) and syndicate-based; terrorism is ideologically/politically motivated (terror, coercion of governments) and may be committed by a lone actor; both reach death-punishment where death results, but only §111 carries mandatory minimum fines and a continuity-record threshold.

DU Pattern · Short notes · 5 marks each
Q10. Write short notes on: (a) causa causans vs causa sine qua non; (b) res ipsa loquitur in criminal negligence; (c) civil vs criminal negligence; (d) unavoidable accident.
Model answer

(a) For §106/304A the negligent act must be the causa causans — the proximate, efficient and operative cause of death, without the intervention of another’s negligence or an unforeseeable supervening act; being a causa sine qua non (a but-for link in the chain) is insufficient (Bhalchandra Waman Pathe; KD Gaur/Manupatra commentary).

(b) Res ipsa loquitur (“the thing speaks for itself”) is a rule of evidence of the tort domain allocating onus; per Jacob Mathew it “cannot be pressed in service for determining per se the liability for negligence within the domain of criminal law” and has at most limited application in a criminal negligence trial.

(c) Civil negligence = breach of the standard of reasonable care, degree irrelevant to liability (only to damages), proved on preponderance of probability; criminal negligence = gross or very high degree of carelessness (Andrews v. DPP), proved beyond reasonable doubt, punished by imprisonment/fine (table in Part III above).

(d) Unavoidable accident: where, despite exercise of reasonable care, a third party’s fault or sheer misfortune produces the harm, neither rashness nor negligence exists — S.N. Hussain (gateman’s open gate), Waryam Singh (ghost-mistake on a dark night), Syed Akbar (error of judgment in emergency swerve). Acquittal follows because the mental element of §106/304A itself is absent.
