General Exceptions — The Conceptual Framework

BNS Chapter III (§§ 14–44) ↔ IPC Chapter IV (§§ 76–106) — defences that "negative criminality completely"
BNS §§ 14–44IPC §§ 76–106BNS § 3(1) / IPC § 6BSA § 108 / IEA § 105

1.1 What is a "general exception"?

A general exception is a circumstance, defined in the criminal statute itself, in which an act that would otherwise satisfy the definition of an offence is not an offence at all. The exceptions are "general" because, unlike the special exceptions attached to particular offences (e.g., Exception 1 to § 101 BNS / § 300 IPC — grave and sudden provocation), they apply throughout the whole code, to every offence, unless excluded.

"General defences in Chapter IV, IPC, if applicable in a given case, negate criminality completely."— DU Case Material (2023 edn.), Unit 5 introduction
BNS § 3(1) ↔ IPC § 6 — DEFINITIONS SUBJECT TO EXCEPTIONS "Throughout this Sanhita every definition of an offence, every penal provision, and every Illustration of every such definition or penal provision, shall be understood subject to the exceptions contained in the Chapter entitled 'General Exceptions', though those exceptions are not repeated in such definition, penal provision, or Illustration."

Illustration (a): The sections containing definitions of offences do not express that a child under seven cannot commit them; the definitions are read subject to the general exception that nothing is an offence done by a child under seven.
Illustration (b): A, a police officer, without warrant, apprehends Z, who has committed murder. A is not guilty of wrongful confinement — he was bound by law to apprehend Z.
Two consequences follow: (i) the prosecution never needs to negative the exceptions in its charge — every offence is defined on the assumption that the exceptions may later be applied; and (ii) once the accused successfully brings his case within an exception, he is acquitted outright, not merely excused or given a lesser sentence. The exception destroys the actus reus or the mens rea element of the crime itself.

1.2 Justifications vs. excuses — how the defences differ in theory

Criminal-law theory divides defences into two families. Both produce acquittal, but for opposite reasons:

FamilyLogicExamples in BNS Ch. III / IPC Ch. IV
Justifications (the act was RIGHT)Society approves, or at least tolerates, what the actor did — the act avoids a greater evil or repels unlawful aggression. The law says: you may do this.Private defence (§§ 34–44 / 96–106); acts of judges & persons justified by law (§§ 15–17 / 77–79); necessity (§ 19 / 81); acts for the benefit of persons (§§ 26–27, 30 / 88–89, 92).
Excuses (the act was wrong, but the actor is NOT blameworthy)The actor lacked the capacity or fair opportunity to choose lawfully — punishing him would achieve nothing.Unsoundness of mind (§ 22 / 84); infancy (§§ 20–21 / 82–83); involuntary intoxication (§ 23 / 85); mistake of fact (§§ 14, 17 / 76, 79); accident (§ 18 / 80); compulsion by threats (§ 32 / 94).

The distinction matters for § 36/§ 98: against a justified act there is ordinarily no right of private defence (the act is lawful), but against an excused act (of an insane person, a child, an involuntarily intoxicated person) the victim does retain the right of private defence — a crucial asymmetry examined in § 4(C) below and tested in DU's Dec 2015 paper.

1.4 Burden of proving an exception — BSA § 108 ↔ Evidence Act § 105

BHARATIYA SAKSHYA ADHINIYAM § 108 ↔ INDIAN EVIDENCE ACT § 105 "When a person is accused of any offence, the burden of proving the existence of circumstances bringing the case within any of the General Exceptions in the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or within any special exception or proviso contained in any other part of the said Sanhita, or in any law defining the offence, is upon him, and the Court shall presume the absence of such circumstances."

Illustration (a): A, accused of murder, alleges that, by reason of unsoundness of mind, he did not know the nature of the act. The burden of proof is on A.

Three working rules govern this burden (settled in Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker v. State of Gujarat, AIR 1964 SC 1563, and applied in Bhosale and James Martin):

  1. The prosecution must prove the offence (including mens rea) beyond reasonable doubt — that burden never shifts.
  2. The accused's burden on an exception is only preponderance of probabilities — "no higher than that which rests upon a party to civil proceedings." He may discharge it through prosecution evidence itself (cross-examination), without adducing his own evidence.
  3. Even if the accused fails to establish the exception conclusively, the material may raise a reasonable doubt about an ingredient of the offence — the court must then acquit because the general burden is undischarged.
Exam nuance Do not write that the accused must prove insanity or private defence "beyond reasonable doubt" — that is the classic error. DU examiners specifically reward the Salim Zia formulation, quoted with approval in James Martin (case card 4): the accused "need not establish the plea to the hilt and may discharge his onus by establishing a mere preponderance of probabilities."

Unsoundness of Mind — BNS § 22 ↔ IPC § 84

"Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law."
BNS § 22IPC § 84M'Naghten Rules (1843)Srikant Anandrao Bhosale (2003)

2.1 The statutory text and its anatomy

BNS § 22 ↔ IPC § 84 — ACT OF A PERSON OF UNSOUND MIND "Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law."

The section is a single sentence carrying four cumulative ingredients — every one of which must be examined in a problem answer:

IngredientWhat must be shown
1. Time — "at the time of doing it"The decisive moment is the moment of the act, not before or after. Insanity before or after the offence is only relevant evidence from which the state of mind at the crucial moment is inferred — the holding on which Bhosale (case card 2) turns.
2. Cause — "by reason of unsoundness of mind"The incapacity must spring from mental disease (legal insanity), not from mere emotion, anger, passion, volitional weakness or moral depravity. "Every person suffering from mental illness is not exempted from criminal liability" (Surendra Mishra v. State of Jharkhand, (2011) 11 SCC 495).
3. Incapacity — "incapable of knowing"A total incapacity (not mere difficulty) in either of two cognitive spheres (see 4 below). Partial impairment, e.g., diminished responsibility or irresistible impulse, is not enough under Indian law.
4. Object of the incapacityEither (a) the nature of the act (physical character and consequences — that he is cutting a human being, not a vegetable), or (b) that the act is wrong or contrary to law (moral/legal wrongness). Either limb suffices.

2.2 The M'Naghten Rules — where the test comes from

M'Naghten's Case ((1843) 10 Cl & Fin 200) — Daniel M'Naghten, a Scottish woodturner labouring under the paranoid delusion that he was persecuted by Sir Robert Peel, shot and killed Edward Drummond, Peel's private secretary, mistaking him for Peel. The House of Lords posed five questions to the judges of the common-law courts; the answers, the M'Naghten Rules, remain the foundation of the insanity defence throughout the common-law world and of IPC § 84 (drafted by Macaulay's successors in step with them):

  1. Every man is presumed sane until the contrary is proved.
  2. To establish the defence, it must be clearly proved that at the time of the act the accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing; or
  3. if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong;
  4. where the accused labours under a partial delusion only, he must be considered in the same situation as if the facts with respect to which the delusion exists were real — a delusion that a man is attacking you licenses only such defence as would exist if he really were;
  5. medical insanity unsupported by impairment of cognitive faculties does not, in law, exempt from responsibility.
Why it matters for DU: § 84/§ 22 is deliberately narrower than the Rules' modern English successors (diminished responsibility, the "substantial capacity" Model Penal Code test). Indian courts remain faithful to the cognitive test only — a frequent critical note question: "the section adopts only the first two M'Naghten propositions and ignores disease of the volitions."

2.3 Legal insanity vs. medical insanity — the decisive distinction

The section speaks the language of legal, not medical, insanity. A diagnosis — schizophrenia, psychosis, paranoia — is merely the gateway; the defence succeeds only if the disease, at the moment of the act, produced one of the statutory incapacities. In Surendra Mishra the Supreme Court distilled the circumstantial indicators a court examines:

  • whether the act was devoid of any rational motive;
  • the history of mental illness before and after the offence;
  • the accused's conduct before, during and after the crime — flight, concealment, attempts to destroy evidence point to knowledge of wrongfulness;
  • the nature and brutality of the act (a senseless, bizarre attack on a helpless victim may evidence derangement).
"Every sane person — and in this case we are bound to take it that the appellant was sane — is presumed to have some knowledge of the nature of his act. This knowledge is not negatived by any mental condition short of insanity."— Gyarsibai v. The State (as reproduced in the DU Case Material, 2023 edn.)
Four classic traps (i) Anger is not insanity — extreme fits of anger, however violent, exclude § 84 (this was the State's whole case in Bhosale). (ii) Psychopathy/sadism is not insanity — knowing wrongdoing excludes the section. (iii) A lucid interval — an interval of cure or remission: an act done in a lucid interval is fully punishable. (iv) Medical certificate ≠ acquittal — the court tries the issue of legal insanity on the totality of circumstances, not on a hospital label.

2.4 Who proves what, and how well

Under BSA § 108 / IEA § 105 (§ 1.4 above) the accused bears the burden, but only to the civil standard — and the crucial time is the time of the offence. The operation of this burden is best learnt from Srikant Anandrao Bhosale v. State of Maharashtra (case card 2): a constable with a nine-year documented history of paranoid schizophrenia who killed his wife with a grinding stone in broad daylight and made no attempt to flee. The Supreme Court held that unsoundness before and after the incident, when taken with the totality of circumstances, supported the inference of a schizophrenic delusion at the moment of the act — the anger theory did not rule out a schizophrenia attack — and gave him § 84.

2.5 Consequence of success — acquittal, but not necessarily freedom

An acquittal under § 22/§ 84 is not a licence to walk out. Because the finding records that the accused did the act but lacked capacity, the procedural code provides for protective detention:

§ 374§ 335If the act is found committed (and would, but for incapacity, have been an offence), the court shall order the person detained in safe custody, or otherwise dealt with as it directs — he may be received into a mental-health establishment.

Society's protection interest re-enters through the procedural code. (The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 supplies the modern framework for evaluation and care of such accused persons.)

Intoxication — BNS §§ 23–24 ↔ IPC §§ 85–86

Drunkenness is never a blanket licence: the law splits intoxication into involuntary (a complete excuse in the same terms as insanity) and voluntary (knowledge imputed; intention examinable)
BNS § 23 (involuntary)BNS § 24 (voluntary)IPC §§ 85–86Basdev v. State of PEPSU (1956)

3.1 The two sections

BNS § 23 ↔ IPC § 85 — INVOLUNTARY INTOXICATION (COMPLETE DEFENCE) "Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, is, by reason of intoxication, incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong, or contrary to law; provided that the thing which intoxicated him was administered to him without his knowledge or against his will."
BNS § 24 ↔ IPC § 86 — VOLUNTARY INTOXICATION (LIMITED RULE) "In cases where an act done is not an offence unless done with a particular knowledge or intent, a person who does the act in a state of intoxication shall be liable to be dealt with as if he had the same knowledge as he would have had if he had not been intoxicated, unless the thing which intoxicated him was administered to him without his knowledge or against his will."
Drafting watch-point (BNS § 23) The Gazette text of § 23 reads "…what is neither wrong, or contrary to law" — an apparent drafting slip against IPC § 85's "either wrong, or contrary to law." Since the two are otherwise in pari materia, courts will read § 23 as reproducing the IPC test (incapacity to know the act is wrong or unlawful). In the exam, quote the BNS text but construe it with IPC § 85 — that observation itself earns marks.

3.2 Involuntary intoxication — the ingredients

  • Intoxication at the time of the act — by drink, drug, or any intoxicating substance;
  • from a thing administered without his knowledge or against his will — spiked drinks, forcibly forced liquor, drugged food; voluntary heavy drinking does not qualify, however "compulsive" the drinker's habit;
  • producing, by reason of that intoxication, incapacity to know the nature of the act or its wrongfulness — the same cognitive incapacity as § 22 / § 84.

If all three exist, the defence is complete — the accused is treated exactly like a person of unsound mind. (The commonest DU problem: A's drink is spiked at a party; in a drugged delirium he attacks a friend. Answer: § 23 / IPC 85 — nothing is an offence; if only the "spiking" is doubtful, § 24 analysis follows.)

3.3 Voluntary intoxication — the Basdev rule

Where intoxication is self-induced, § 24 / § 86 splits the mental element in two:

ElementRuleReasoning
KnowledgeImputed — the drunken man is dealt with as if he had the same knowledge as if sober.A man who deliberately disables his judgment cannot profit from the disablement; he takes the risk of what he does while drunk. Hence offences defined by knowledge (e.g., that the act is likely to cause death) are not softened by drink.
IntentionGenuinely examined — evidence of drunkenness is admissible to show the accused was incapable of forming the specific intention essential to the offence.Intention is a fact to be gathered from the attending circumstances of the case; if the intoxication was so extreme as to destroy the capacity to form intent, the specific-intent offence is not made out.

In Basdev v. State of PEPSU (AIR 1956 SC 488 — case card 1) the Supreme Court adopted the classical English rules (from DPP v. Beard [1920] AC 479, as summarised in R v. Monkhouse):

  1. Insanity, whether produced by drunkenness or otherwise, is a defence to the crime charged — if drink has made the accused insane, § 22 / § 84, not § 24, is the answer (drunkenness at that depth is effectively involuntary in its consequences);
  2. evidence of drunkenness which renders the accused incapable of forming the specific intent essential to the crime must be taken into account with the other facts to determine whether he had that intent;
  3. evidence of drunkenness falling short of proved incapacity — merely showing that the mind was affected so that the accused more readily gave way to passion — does not rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts.
The one-line exam formula: Voluntary drunkenness is relevant only to intention, never to knowledge; and it must amount to incapacity before it touches even intention — "mere drunkenness is no defence; drunkenness equivalent to insanity may be."

3.4 Applying § 24 / § 86 to murder (§ 101 BNS / § 300 IPC)

Murder is defined by intention and knowledge (clauses (a)–(d) of § 101/§ 300). The Basdev mechanics: knowledge that a revolver fired into a boy's abdomen at close range is likely to cause death is imputed to the drunkard — so the knowledge-based clauses (secondly) remain satisfied. Only if the evidence showed incapacity to form the intention to kill could the offence fall to culpable homicide (§ 105/§ 304 Part I). On Basdev's own facts (he walked and talked, fled after the shot, and begged forgiveness when caught) the Court held he was far from incapable — conviction under § 302 IPC affirmed. Note the converse lesson the case teaches: post-offence conduct is the best evidence of preserved capacity.

Private Defence — BNS §§ 34–44 ↔ IPC §§ 96–106

"Self-preservation is the prime instinct of every human being" — the right of private defence: preventive and protective, never punitive (DU Case Material)
BNS §§ 34–44IPC §§ 96–106Deo Narain (1973)James Martin (2004)Darshan Singh (2010)

4.1 Foundation and philosophy of the right

Why does a legal system that monopolises force through the State license the individual to use force at all? Because the State's protection is not omnipresent: between the commencement of an attack and the arrival of the police there is a gap that only the victim can fill. The right of private defence — argued from man's inherent right of self-preservation and from Bentham's "natural protector" duty — is the citizen's temporary, emergency licence to repel unlawful aggression. Darshan Singh (case card 5) contains the fullest modern exposition, adopted from Russell on Crime and a century of Supreme Court authority.

"a man is justified in resisting by force anyone who manifestly intends and endeavours by violence or surprise to commit a known felony against either his person, habitation or property. In these cases, he is not obliged to retreat, and may not merely resist the attack where he stands but may indeed pursue his adversary until the danger is ended, and if in a conflict between them he happens to kill his attacker, such killing is justifiable."— Russell on Crime (11th Edn., Vol. 1, p. 49), quoted in James Martin (para 19) and Darshan Singh (para 24)
When enacting sections 96 to 106 of the IPC, the Legislature "clearly intended to arouse and encourage the manly spirit of self-defence amongst the citizens, when faced with grave danger. The law does not require a law-abiding citizen to behave like a coward when confronted with an imminent unlawful aggression. As repeatedly observed by this Court there is nothing more degrading to the human spirit than to run away in face of danger."— Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab (para 24; DU Case Material)
"It is a noble movement of the heart, that indignation which kindles at the sight of the feeble injured by the strong… It concerns the public safety that every honest man should consider himself as the natural protector of every other." — Bentham, quoted in Darshan Singh (para 29). But "such protection must not be extended beyond the necessities of the case, otherwise it will encourage a spirit of lawlessness and disorder."— Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab (DU Case Material)
The two considerations that limit the right (Darshan Singh, para 31): (i) the same right is claimed by all other members of society — if each may use any force, security is destroyed; and (ii) the State undertakes the responsibility for maintenance of law and order — private force is tolerated only where State aid cannot be had in time. Hence: the right is available only when the danger is sudden, imminent and not self-created, and only to the extent necessary.
"The right of private defence has come to be recognized by all civilized societies as a preventive and protective right where the state protection is not available; this right is essentially protective and preventive and never punitive."— DU Case Material (2023 edn.), Unit 5 introduction
Character of the right — six cardinal features (synthesis of §§ 34–44):
  • Defensive, never retributive — "a right to defend oneself and not to retaliate… not a right to take revenge" (Darshan Singh, para 30).
  • Available against offences only — the IPC confers the right when the wrongful act is an offence (Darshan Singh, principle (ix)); BNS § 36/IPC § 98 creates an exception for protected persons (4.3 below).
  • No duty to retreat — English retreat doctrine expressly rejected for India (Darshan Singh, para 28).
  • Begins on reasonable apprehension — the attack need not have begun; a present, imminent threat suffices (Deo Narain; § 40/IPC 102).
  • Proportionate — no more harm than necessary (§ 37(2)/IPC 99), but judged generously, not "in golden scales" (Deo Narain; Buta Singh).
  • Ends with the apprehension — once the threat is repelled or has fled, the right dies; later force is aggression (Jai Dev, AIR 1963 SC 612).

4.2 The scheme of §§ 34–36 / IPC 96–98 — scope and subjects

BNS § 34 ↔ IPC § 96 — THINGS DONE IN PRIVATE DEFENCE "Nothing is an offence which is done in the exercise of the right of private defence."
BNS § 35 ↔ IPC § 97 — SUBJECT-MATTER OF THE RIGHT "Every person has a right, subject to the restrictions contained in section 37 [IPC: 99], to defend—
(a) his own body, and the body of any other person, against any offence affecting the human body;
(b) the property, whether movable or immovable, of himself or of any other person, against any act which is an offence falling under the definition of theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass, or which is an attempt to commit theft, robbery, mischief or criminal trespass."
Reading § 35 / § 97 — four things examiners test:
  • "Every person" — no age, sanity, sex or status limit; even a person who could not himself be criminally liable (a child, an insane person) may exercise the right; and the defender needs no authority from the person defended — the right to defend any other person is a public duty (Bentham's "natural protector").
  • Defence of body — against any offence affecting the human body (from simple hurt upwards) — the widest possible subject on the body side.
  • Defence of property — only against four named offences (theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass) and their attempts — extortion, dacoity simpliciter or house-trespass alone do not qualify (house-trespass matters only through the house-breaking clauses of § 41(b)/IPC 103(2ndly)).
  • Movable or immovable — cattle, goods, land and buildings all covered; and property "of any other person" — a stranger's goods too may be defended.

4.3 § 36 / IPC § 98 — private defence against persons who commit no offence

BNS § 36 ↔ IPC § 98 — RIGHT OF PRIVATE DEFENCE AGAINST ACT OF A PERSON OF UNSOUND MIND, ETC. "When an act, which would otherwise be a certain offence, is not that offence, by reason of the youth, the want of maturity of understanding, the unsoundness of mind or the intoxication of the person doing that act, or by reason of any misconception on the part of that person, every person has the same right of private defence against that act which he would have if the act were that offence."

Illustration (a): Z, a person of unsound mind, attempts to kill A; Z is guilty of no offence. But A has the same right of private defence which he would have if Z were sane.
Illustration (b): A enters by night a house which he is legally entitled to enter. Z, in good faith, taking A for a house-breaker, attacks A. Z, attacking under this misconception, commits no offence. But A has the same right of private defence against Z which he would have if Z were not acting under that misconception.
The asymmetry — and DU's Dec 2015 problem The aggressor's excuse (insanity, infancy, involuntary intoxication, honest mistake) protects him from punishment; it does not disarm his victim. Two madmen may both be protected — the attacker by § 22, the defender by § 36. The DU Dec 2015 paper asked exactly Illustration (a): A attacked by Z (unsound, spear in hand) strikes Z dead with a stick — A gets § 96/§ 34 read with § 98/§ 36 and § 100(1stly)/(2ndly)/§ 38(a)-(b), because a spear attack reasonably causes apprehension of death or grievous hurt. But if the aggressor's act is justified (a public servant lawfully acting, or a person under a mistake protected by § 14/§ 17 justification), §§ 37/99 restrict the response — see 4.4.

4.4 § 37 / IPC § 99 — the statutory limits ("Lakshman Rekha")

James Martin opens by framing the whole problem: the right exists, but "where exercise of such rights is claimed, [the question is] whether the 'Lakshman Rekha', applicable to its exercise has been exceeded. Section 99 IPC delineates the extent to which the right may be exercised." BNS § 37 restates the section in structured form:

BNS § 37 ↔ IPC § 99 — ACTS AGAINST WHICH THERE IS NO RIGHT OF PRIVATE DEFENCE "(1) There is no right of private defence—
(a) against an act which does not reasonably cause the apprehension of death or of grievous hurt, if done, or attempted to be done, by a public servant acting in good faith under colour of his office, though that act may not be strictly justifiable by law;
(b) against an act which does not reasonably cause the apprehension of death or of grievous hurt, if done, or attempted to be done, by the direction of a public servant acting in good faith under colour of his office, though that direction may not be strictly justifiable by law;
(c) in cases in which there is time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities.
(2) The right of private defence in no case extends to the inflicting of more harm than it is necessary to inflict for the purpose of defence.

Explanation 1: A person is not deprived of the right against an act of a public servant unless he knows or has reason to believe that the person doing it is such public servant.
Explanation 2: A person is not deprived of the right against an act done by direction of a public servant unless he knows/believes the actor acts by such direction, or unless the actor states his authority, or produces written authority if demanded."
The four limits unpacked:
  • Public-servant bar (a), (b): good-faith official acts under colour of office — even if technically irregular — may not be forcibly resisted, unless they threaten death or grievous hurt. Reason: the law presumes the legality of official process; resistance must take the form of legal remedies. Note the double safeguard in the Explanations: the bar applies only where the citizen knew or had reason to believe he faced a public servant (an ununiformed plain-clothes arrest at night may lawfully be resisted).
  • Time bar (c): where there is time to seek the protection of the public authorities, private force is premature — the right is a right of necessity, not convenience. It arises only "when immediate aid from the State machinery is not readily available" (Darshan Singh, para 33).
  • Proportionality (2): "more harm than necessary" is forbidden — the outer Lakshman Rekha. But proportionality is judged humanely: no "golden scales" (Deo Narain), no "arithmetical exactitude" (Darshan Singh principle (v); Vidhya Singh), no "high-powered spectacles or microscopes to detect slight or even marginal overstepping" (Buta Singh, approved in James Martin, para 17).
  • Free fight: where both parties willingly fight, each is aggressor as against the other — the right of private defence is not available to either (an inflexible rule invoked across the case law; both Deo Narain's trial court and the HCs in the DU cases examine "who was the aggressor").

4.5 § 38 / IPC § 100 — when defence of the body may kill

BNS § 38 ↔ IPC § 100 — WHEN THE RIGHT OF PRIVATE DEFENCE OF THE BODY EXTENDS TO CAUSING DEATH "The right of private defence of the body extends, under the restrictions specified in section 37, to the voluntary causing of death or of any other harm to the assailant, if the offence which occasions the exercise of the right be of any of the descriptions hereinafter enumerated, namely:—
(a) such an assault as may reasonably cause the apprehension that death will otherwise be the consequence of such assault;
(b) such an assault as may reasonably cause the apprehension that grievous hurt will otherwise be the consequence of such assault;
(c) an assault with the intention of committing rape;
(d) an assault with the intention of gratifying unnatural lust;
(e) an assault with the intention of kidnapping or abducting;
(f) an assault with the intention of wrongfully confining a person, under circumstances which may reasonably cause him to apprehend that he will be unable to have recourse to the public authorities for his release;
(g) an act of throwing or administering acid or an attempt to throw or administer acid which may reasonably cause the apprehension that grievous hurt will otherwise be the consequence of such act."

4.6 § 39 / IPC § 101 — all other cases: harm short of death

BNS § 39 ↔ IPC § 101 "If the offence be not of any of the descriptions specified in section 38, the right of private defence of the body does not extend to the voluntary causing of death to the assailant, but does extend, under the restrictions specified in section 37, to the voluntary causing to the assailant of any harm other than death."

Thus against a simple assault (a slap, a fist aimed at the arm) the defender may repel with proportionate force — he may hurt, but if he kills when death/grievous hurt was not reasonably apprehended and no special clause applies, he has exceeded the right: complete protection falls away, and the partial shield of the "grievous hurt in excess of right" rules (BNS § 65(2)/IPC 300 Exception 2 analogy aside) may or may not arise on the facts.

4.7 § 40 / IPC § 102 — commencement and continuance (body)

BNS § 40 ↔ IPC § 102 "The right of private defence of the body commences as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence though the offence may not have been committed; and it continues as long as such apprehension of danger to the body continues."

This short section is the heart of Deo Narain v. State of U.P. (AIR 1973 SC 473 — case card 3). The High Court had reasoned that the right to inflict a fatal blow "arises only after the accused had sustained injuries" and had condemned the accused for using a spear against a lathi. The Supreme Court held both propositions to be misdirections of law:

"To say that the appellant could only claim the right to use force after he had sustained a serious injury… is a complete misunderstanding of the law. The right of private defence commences as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt or threat… though the offence may not have been committed." The threat must be "present and imminent" — not remote or contingent — and the right "continues as long as such apprehension continues." "Private defence is preventive and protective, not punitive; the right to punish vests in the State."— Deo Narain v. State of U.P. (DU Case Material)
The three time-zones: (i) before the attack matures — right exists once the apprehension is reasonable and imminent (a raised gandasa, a mob breaking down the door — DU Dec 2014 problem: X could fire before the mob entered); (ii) during the attack — the classic zone, judged with "golden scales" leniency; (iii) after the threat ends — right is exhausted: Jai Dev v. State of Punjab (AIR 1963 SC 612), cited in James Martin: "as soon as the cause for reasonable apprehension disappears and the threat has either been destroyed or has been put to rout, there can be no occasion to exercise the right of private defence." Chasing a fleeing aggressor to "punish" him is retaliation, not defence.

4.8 §§ 41–43 / IPC §§ 103–105 — private defence of property

BNS § 41 ↔ IPC § 103 — WHEN DEFENCE OF PROPERTY EXTENDS TO CAUSING DEATH "The right of private defence of property extends, under the restrictions specified in section 37, to the voluntary causing of death or of any other harm to the wrong-doer, if the offence, the committing of which, or the attempting to commit which, occasions the exercise of the right, be an offence of any of the descriptions hereinafter enumerated, namely:—
(a) robbery;
(b) house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise;
(c) mischief by fire or any explosive substance committed on any building, tent or vessel, which building, tent or vessel is used as a human dwelling, or as a place for the custody of property;
(d) theft, mischief, or house-trespass, under such circumstances as may reasonably cause apprehension that death or grievous hurt will be the consequence, if such right of private defence is not exercised."
BNS § 42 ↔ IPC § 104 "If the offence, the committing of which, or the attempting to commit which, occasions the exercise of the right of private defence, be theft, mischief, or criminal trespass, not of any of the descriptions specified in section 41, that right does not extend to the voluntary causing of death, but does extend… to the voluntary causing to the wrong-doer of any harm other than death."
BNS § 43 ↔ IPC § 105 — COMMENCEMENT AND CONTINUANCE OF THE RIGHT OF PROPERTY DEFENCE "The right of private defence of property—
(a) commences when a reasonable apprehension of danger to the property commences;
(b) against theft continues till the offender has effected his retreat with the property, or either the assistance of the public authorities is obtained, or the property has been recovered;
(c) against robbery continues as long as the offender causes or attempts to cause to any person death or hurt or wrongful restraint, or as long as the fear of instant death or of instant hurt or of instant personal restraint continues;
(d) against criminal trespass or mischief continues as long as the offender continues in the commission of criminal trespass or mischief;
(e) against house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise continues as long as the house-trespass which has been begun by such house-breaking continues."
Why "after sunset and before sunrise" survived into the BNS The 2023 code retains the night-house-breaking clause (§ 41(b), § 43(e)) verbatim from IPC § 103(2ndly)/§ 105 — a deliberate legislative judgment that a dwelling invaded in the dark presents a special peril (occupants asleep, no visual warning, no easy recourse to help) justifying the extreme defence. Note the asymmetry: day-time house-breaking supports property defence only up to harm short of death (unless clause (d)'s death/grievous-hurt apprehension exists).

4.9 § 44 / IPC § 106 — deadly assault and the risk to innocents

BNS § 44 ↔ IPC § 106 "If in the exercise of the right of private defence against an assault which reasonably causes the apprehension of death, the defender be so situated that he cannot effectually exercise that right without risk of harm to an innocent person, his right of private defence extends to the running of that risk."
Illustration: A is attacked by a mob who attempt to murder him. He cannot effectually exercise his right of private defence without firing on the mob, and he cannot fire without risk of harming young children who are mingled with the mob. A commits no offence if by so firing he harms any of the children.

This "risk to innocents" rule is the legal answer to the instinctive objection "but bystanders were hit" — and it maps directly onto James Martin (case card 4), where passers-by on the road were injured when the accused fired at the bandh mob that had invaded his compound: the Supreme Court nonetheless found the firing within the reasonable limits of private defence.

4.10 The evidentiary framework — how courts actually decide the plea

The most examinable paragraphs of James Martin (paras 13–17 — reproduced in the DU Case Material) set out the procedure for a private-defence plea. They should be quoted almost verbatim in any problem answer:

  • No formal plea needed: "It is not necessary for the accused to plead in so many words that he acted in self-defence. If the circumstances show that the right was legitimately exercised, it is open to the Court to consider such a plea" — even if never taken (Munshi Ram v. Delhi Administration, AIR 1968 SC 702; Darshan Singh principle (vii)).
  • Burden on the accused under IEA § 105 (now BSA § 108) — but he need not prove the right beyond reasonable doubt; it is enough "as in a civil case that the preponderance of probabilities is in favour of his plea" (Salim Zia v. State of U.P., AIR 1979 SC 391). He may discharge the burden entirely from prosecution evidence — by cross-examination — without calling any witness (Bai Fatima; Mohd. Musheer Khan; Mohinder Pal Jolly).
  • No presumption from injuries: "The number of injuries [on the accused] is not always a safe criterion for determining who the aggressor was" — the defence must show the injuries probabilise the version of private defence; conversely, prosecution's unexplained failure to explain the accused's injuries is "a very important circumstance," though not decisive where the injuries are minor and superficial (Lakshmi Singh v. State of Bihar, AIR 1976 SC 2263).
  • Relevant factors for availability: injuries received by the accused; imminence of the threat; injuries caused by the accused; whether there was time to have recourse to the public authorities (Biran Singh; Wassan Singh; Sekar).
  • It is a defence, not an offence: the right "should not be allowed to be pleaded or availed as a pretext for a vindictive, aggressive or retributive purpose… A right to defend does not include a right to launch an offensive, particularly when the need to defend no longer survived."
"Such situations have to be pragmatically viewed and not with high-powered spectacles or microscopes to detect slight or even marginal overstepping… the person facing a reasonable apprehension of threat to himself cannot be expected to modulate his defence step by step with any arithmetical exactitude… where self-preservation is the paramount consideration." — Butta Singh v. State of Punjab (AIR 1991 SC 1316), quoted in James Martin, para 17
"…situations have to be judged from the subjective point of view of the accused concerned, in the surrounding excitement and confusion of the moment, confronted with a situation of peril and not by any microscopic and pedantic scrutiny." — Vidhya Singh v. State of M.P. (AIR 1971 SC 1857), quoted in James Martin, para 18 and Darshan Singh, para 54
"Detached reflection cannot be demanded in the presence of an uplifted knife." — Justice Holmes, Robert B. Brown v. United States (1921) 256 US 335, quoted in Darshan Singh, para 34

4.11 The Darshan Singh decalogue — ten principles for every answer

No.Principle (Darshan Singh, para 58 — DU Case Material)
(i)Self-preservation is the basic human instinct, duly recognised by the criminal jurisprudence of all civilized countries; all free, democratic and civilized countries recognize the right within reasonable limits.
(ii)The right is available only to one suddenly confronted with the necessity of averting an impending danger not of self-creation.
(iii)A mere reasonable apprehension is enough — actual commission of the offence is not necessary; it suffices if the accused apprehended that the offence was contemplated and likely to be committed.
(iv)The right commences as soon as a reasonable apprehension arises and is co-terminus with its duration.
(v)It is unrealistic to expect a person under assault to modulate his defence step by step with any arithmetical exactitude.
(vi)The force used ought not to be wholly disproportionate or much greater than necessary for protection of the person or property.
(vii)Even if the accused does not plead self-defence, it is open to the court to consider the plea if it arises from the material on record.
(viii)The accused need not prove the right beyond reasonable doubt — preponderance of probabilities suffices.
(ix)The Code confers the right only when the unlawful/wrongful act is an offence (see § 36/98 for the excepted cases).
(x)A person in imminent and reasonable danger of losing life or limb may inflict any harm, even death, on the assailant — either when the assault is attempted or directly threatened.
How to use the decalogue in problems Structure every private-defence problem answer as: (1) Was there an offence/unlawful aggression? → § 35/97 + § 36/98 if the attacker was protected; (2) Did the right commence? → § 40/102 or § 43/105 — reasonable, present, imminent apprehension; (3) What quantum of force? → § 38/100 or § 41/103 if death was caused, else § 39/101 / § 42/104; (4) Any bar? → § 37/99 limits (public servant, time, proportionality, free fight, self-created conflict); (5) Evidentiary posture — preponderance, no formal plea needed, subjective standpoint, golden-scales leniency (Darshan Singh (i)–(x) + James Martin paras 13–17). CASE 1 — INTOXICATION · BNS § 24 ↔ IPC § 86

Basdev v. State of PEPSU

AIR 1956 SC 488 · Supreme Court of India (N.H. Bhagwati & Chandrasekhara Aiyar JJ.) · Criminal Appeal No. 147 of 1955, decided 17 April 1956 RESEARCH (prescribed in DU syllabus; not reproduced in case-book)
Subject
Whether drunkenness that falls short of destroying the capacity to form intention can reduce murder (IPC § 302) to culpable homicide (§ 304) — the interaction of IPC § 86 with the mental element of murder.
Provisions
IPC §§ 86, 300, 302, 304  ↔  BNS §§ 24, 101, 103 (renumbered equivalents).
Facts
  • The appellant, Basdev, a retired military Jamadar from village Harigarh, went with other villagers — including the deceased, Maghar Singh, a boy of about 15–16 — to attend a marriage in another village (12 March 1954). The marriage party had drunk heavily; the appellant was excessively drunk.
  • At the midday meal at the bride's house, guests had taken their seats. The appellant asked Maghar Singh to move aside so that he could get a convenient seat; the boy did not comply.
  • On the boy's refusal, the appellant pulled out his revolver and shot him in the abdomen. The boy died of the wound. One prosecution witness (Wazir Singh Lambardar) described the appellant as being so drunk as to be almost unconscious; the appellant staggered at times and his speech was at times incoherent.
  • After the shooting the appellant tried to escape, was caught a short distance away, and on being secured realised what he had done and asked for forgiveness.
  • The Sessions Judge, weighing the extreme intoxication and the complete absence of motive or premeditation, convicted him under § 302 IPC but sentenced him to transportation for life instead of death. The PEPSU High Court at Patiala dismissed his appeal. The Supreme Court granted special leave confined to the question whether the offence was § 302 or § 304, in the light of § 86.
Issues
  1. Does evidence of voluntary drunkenness rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts?
  2. How do § 86's two limbs — "intent or knowledge" (first part) and "knowledge" (second part) — operate on an offence (murder) defined by both intention and knowledge?
  3. Was the appellant's intoxication so extreme as to make him incapable of forming the intention to kill, reducing § 302 to § 304?
Arguments

For the appellant: The appellant was so deeply intoxicated ("almost unconscious") that he was incapable of forming the intention to kill; drunkenness which renders an accused incapable of forming the specific intent essential to the crime must be weighed with the other facts in determining whether he had that intent (the DPP v. Beard/R v. Monkhouse line); there was no motive and no premeditation; hence at highest § 304 Part I, not § 302.

For the State: The intoxication was self-invited; the appellant was not so drunk as to be unaware of the nature of his act — he walked unaided, chose a seat, argued, aimed at a vital part of an unarmed boy, tried to flee and pleaded for forgiveness. Voluntary drunkenness is no defence to murder unless it produces a condition equivalent to insanity; the presumption that a man intends the natural and probable consequences of his act stands.

Decision

Appeal dismissed; conviction under § 302 IPC and the sentence of transportation for life maintained. The Court laid down the operating rules of § 86 (now § 24 BNS):

  • Knowledge imputed: so far as knowledge is concerned, the intoxicated person must be attributed the same knowledge as if he were sober — the second part of § 86 admits no evidence of drunkenness.
  • Intention examined: so far as intention is concerned, it "must be gathered from the attending circumstances of the case" — evidence of drunkenness is relevant only if it renders the accused incapable of forming the specific intent.
  • The three English rules were adopted: (i) insanity produced by drunkenness is a defence; (ii) drunkenness capable of destroying the specific intent is to be considered with the other facts on the question of intent; (iii) drunkenness falling short of such incapacity — merely showing a mind more readily yielding to passion — does not rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts.
  • Application: the facts showed the appellant retained both knowledge and capacity — he moved about unaided, spoke coherently at times, selected a seat, fired at a vital part at close range, attempted escape and begged forgiveness. His drunkenness fell far short of incapacity; § 302 stood.
Principles evolved
  • The knowledge/intention dichotomy of § 86/§ 24 — the single most quotable proposition on intoxication in Indian criminal law.
  • Incapacity, not impairment, is the test — a drunkard gets no discount for a temper loosened by drink.
  • Post-offence conduct evidences capacity — flight, remorse, coherent pleading are proof that the drink had not destroyed the mind.
  • Standard for reduction: murder is not reduced to culpable homicide merely because drink supplied the stimulus; only proved incapacity to form the intent can achieve that.
Exam one-liner: Basdev — voluntary intoxication is no defence to knowledge; it may negative only specific intention, and only when it destroys the capacity to form it.
CASE 2 — UNSOUNDNESS OF MIND · BNS § 22 ↔ IPC § 84

Srikant Anandrao Bhosale v. State of Maharashtra

(2003) 7 SCC 748 · Supreme Court of India (Y.K. Sabharwal & H.K. Sema JJ.) · Appeal (Crl.) No. 180 of 2000, decided 26 September 2002 RESEARCH (prescribed in DU syllabus; not reproduced in case-book)
Subject
When does paranoid schizophrenia entitle an accused to the general exception of unsoundness of mind — the crucial time, the totality-of-circumstances test, and the civil-standard burden under IEA § 105 (now BSA § 108).
Provisions
IPC §§ 84, 302  ↔  BNS §§ 22, 103; Evidence Act § 105 ↔ BSA § 108; CrPC § 335 ↔ BNSS § 374.
Facts
  • The appellant was a police constable in Maharashtra. On the morning of 24 April 1994, during a quarrel, he struck his wife Surekha on the head with a grinding stone while she was washing clothes in the bathroom. She was rushed to hospital and declared dead. The killing was in broad daylight; he made no attempt to run away or to conceal the act.
  • The defence case was mental illness: the appellant had a family history of psychiatric illness (his father had been under psychiatric treatment); he himself had been treated for unsoundness of mind since 1992 and was diagnosed as suffering from paranoid schizophrenia; he had been an indoor patient in a government hospital, and his treatment and hospital visits continued after the incident.
  • Two government doctors (DW 2 — Dr. Arun, and DW 3 — Dr. Pramod) deposed on the medical records to the long-standing illness. The defence was conducted by Dr. Shyamla Pappu, Senior Advocate, as amicus curiae.
  • The Sessions Court convicted him under § 302 IPC (rigorous life imprisonment); the High Court affirmed. Neither court accepted the insanity plea. In the Supreme Court, leave was granted limited to the insanity question.
Issues
  1. At what point of time must unsoundness of mind be established for § 84/§ 22, and how is a state of mind at that moment to be proved?
  2. Does proved schizophrenia before and after the offence — without direct proof of a psychotic attack at the moment of the act — satisfy the accused's burden?
  3. What is the standard and order of the burden under IEA § 105 / BSA § 108, and the effect of the prosecution's "fit of extreme anger" theory?
Arguments

For the appellant (amicus curiae): The medical evidence — family history, diagnosis of paranoid schizophrenia since 1992, continued treatment, indoor hospitalisation, repeated visits shortly after the incident — established unsoundness of mind before and after the offence; the state of mind at the moment of the crime is a matter to be inferred from circumstances, and here the totality (weak motive, daylight killing, no flight, bizarre use of a grinding stone) reasonably inferred a schizophrenic delusion at the relevant time; the burden is only preponderance, and a reasonable doubt on mens rea must anyway result in acquittal.

For the State: The appellant was by nature an angry man who drank and quarrelled with his wife; that morning there was a fight, and in an extreme fit of anger he killed her — anger is not insanity; unsoundness before and after the offence is conceded, but the defence failed to prove unsoundness at the time of the offence, the only moment that counts (Sheralli Wali Mohammed v. State of Maharashtra; the absence of flight, daylight act and weak motive do not by themselves prove insanity).

Decision

Appeal allowed; conviction and sentence set aside — the appellant was held entitled to the benefit of § 84 IPC and "set at liberty forthwith." The Court's reasoning:

  • It reaffirmed the Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakker (AIR 1964 SC 1563) doctrine of burden, summarised in three propositions: (1) the prosecution must prove the offence, with mens rea, beyond reasonable doubt throughout; (2) there is a rebuttable presumption of sanity, which the accused may rebut by all relevant evidence — oral, documentary, circumstantial — his burden being no higher than that of a party to civil proceedings; (3) even where the accused fails to establish the exception conclusively, the evidence may raise a reasonable doubt as to mens rea, requiring acquittal on the general burden.
  • The crucial point of time is the time of the commission of the offence; whether the accused was in the protected state of mind "can only be established from the circumstances which preceded, attended and followed the crime."
  • Held on the facts: unsoundness of mind before and after the occurrence stood proved (the State rightly did not dispute it); the totality of circumstances — family history, diagnosis and continuous treatment, indoor hospitalisation, weak motive, daylight act, no flight — supported a reasonable inference that the appellant was under a delusion at the relevant time: "The anger theory on which reliance has been placed is not ruled out under schizophrenia attack." The Court was "unable to hold that the crime was committed as a result of an extreme fit of anger."
Principles evolved
  • Legal insanity is judged at the moment of the act, but proved circumstantially — preceding and following unsoundness are relevant facts from which the momentary state is inferred ("totality of circumstances" test).
  • Anger does not exclude schizophrenia — the anger theory and the disease theory can coexist; where medical evidence shows a continuing psychosis, the prosecution cannot defeat § 84 by merely labelling the act as rage.
  • The civil-standard burden in operation — a textbook demonstration of Dahyabhai propositions (2) and (3) producing acquittal.
  • Individual circumstances (no flight, daylight, weak motive) are not independently decisive, but contribute to the cumulative inference.
Exam one-liner: Bhosale — paranoid schizophrenia before and after the offence plus the totality of circumstances may raise the reasonable inference of a delusion at the crucial moment; the accused discharges his s. 105/BSA 108 burden on a preponderance, and anger does not oust the disease.
CASE 3 — PRIVATE DEFENCE OF BODY · BNS § 40 ↔ IPC § 102

Deo Narain v. State of U.P.

(1973) 1 SCC 347 : AIR 1973 SC 473 · Supreme Court of India (I.D. Dua J.)
Subject
Commencement of the right of private defence of the body (§ 102/§ 40): must the defender first suffer a serious injury? And is there a rule that a spear may not be answered to a lathi?
Provisions
IPC §§ 96, 97, 99, 100, 102  ↔  BNS §§ 34, 35, 37, 38, 40.
Facts
  • The parties were close relations divided by a land dispute. The complainant's party came out, on the prosecution case, to take possession of / obstruct cultivation of the disputed land; a marpeet (free fight) ensued between the two groups.
  • In the affray, Chanderdeo and Deo Narain (the accused-appellant) received superficial head injuries. Deo Narain's spear blow to the chest of the deceased caused his death. The accused were charged with murder.
  • The trial court acquitted all the accused holding they had acted in exercise of the right of private defence (having analysed the scheme of the private-defence sections, following G.V. Subramanyam v. State of Andhra Pradesh, (1970) 3 SCR 473).
  • On appeal by the State, the High Court convicted Deo Narain under § 304 IPC (5 years' R.I.) on two grounds: (1) the right of private defence to cause death could arise "only after a serious injury had actually been inflicted" on the accused; (2) the weapon disparity — the accused party used a spear against a lathi — showed excessive force. The High Court, however, gave the benefit to the co-accused.
Issues
  1. Does the right of private defence of the body commence before the defender actually suffers a serious injury?
  2. Can a weapon hierarchy (lathi vs. spear) be erected as a rule of law to measure excess?
  3. On an appeal against acquittal, how far may the High Court reappraise evidence and reverse the trial court's private-defence finding?
Arguments

For the appellant (Deo Narain): § 102 IPC commences the right on reasonable apprehension from an attempt or threat, before any offence is committed; the trial court's finding of a free fight initiated by the complainant party, with head injuries to two of the accused, showed imminent danger; the High Court's "wait for a serious injury" test contradicts the section, and the lathi/spear comparison is unrealistic in the heat of an affray.

For the State: The accused were the aggressors in the land grab; the injuries on Deo Narain and Chanderdeo were superficial, while the deceased was killed by a spear thrust — force wholly disproportionate to a lathi attack; the right, if any, extended no further than causing harm short of death (§ 101), so § 304 was the correct conviction.

Decision

Appeal allowed; Deo Narain acquitted. The High Court's two grounds were held to be misdirections in law:

  • On commencement (§ 102/§ 40): "To say that the appellant could only claim the right to use force after he had sustained a serious injury… is a complete misunderstanding of the law." The right commences on reasonable apprehension of danger from an attempt or threat though the offence may not have been committed, and continues while the apprehension lasts; the threat must be present and imminent, not remote. Private defence is "preventive and protective, not punitive" — the right to punish vests in the State.
  • On lathi vs. spear (§ 100/§ 38 overlooked): there is no general rule that a lathi causes only minor injury — it depends on the nature of the lathi, the part aimed at and the force used; a lathi blow on the head can be instantly fatal. Conversely, even a spear can cause a minor injury. In the moments of excitement, "the parties cannot be expected to weigh in golden scales" what blow suffices. The suggestion that Deo Narain should have used his spear as a lathi, or aimed at a less vital part, was "unrealistic and unpractical."
  • On appeal against acquittal: the High Court may reappraise, but where two reasonable views are possible, the trial court's view demands due consideration — a fortiori where the High Court itself gave the co-accused the benefit on the same evidence.
Principles evolved
  • No "first-injury" rule — the defender need not wait to bleed before defending; apprehension starts the clock.
  • No per se weapon hierarchy — proportionality is judged on the facts of the assault (weapon + target + force), not by tabular comparison.
  • The golden-scales dictum — the defender's response is measured generously, by the standard of a person in excitement and peril.
  • Preventive/protective — never punitive — the classic DU-CM formulation of the right's character.
Exam one-liner: Deo Narain — the right of private defence commences on reasonable and imminent apprehension, not on actual injury; a spear against a fatal lathi blow to the head is not excessive per se; defenders are not weighed in golden scales.
CASE 4 — PRIVATE DEFENCE: BODY + PROPERTY IN A BANDH MOB · BNS § 34 ↔ IPC § 96

James Martin v. State of Kerala

(2004) 2 SCC 203 · Supreme Court of India (Arijit Pasayat J.)
Subject
The evidentiary law of the private-defence plea — burden, preponderance, the injuries rule — and whether firing on a violent bandh mob that invaded a compound and manhandled the employee exceeded the right.
Provisions
IPC §§ 96, 97, 99, 100, 101, 102, 105; Evidence Act § 105  ↔  BNS §§ 34, 35, 37, 38, 39, 40, 43; BSA § 108.
Facts
  • A Bharat Bandh was sponsored by some political parties on 15 March 1988. Most shops and offices were closed and vehicles off the roads. Cheranelloor, a politically sensitive Kochi suburb, saw isolated acts of defiance. The appellant James Martin and his father Xavier — respected local businessmen with no political antecedents — ran a bread factory and flour mill in their residential compound; their success had drawn the enmity of a neighbour.
  • Five bandh activists (including the two deceased, Mohanan and Basheer) entered the compound through the unlocked gate, went to the operating mill and demanded that PW-15, the mill employee, shut it down; an altercation followed, the activists threatened, intimidated and assaulted PW-15 (who had to run away), and were armed with sharp-edged weapons; they had earlier beaten up one Jossy. On hearing the commotion, the accused directed them to leave; instead, the situation escalated.
  • According to the prosecution, the gate was locked from inside and the appellant, on his father's instigation, fired an S.B.B.L. gun through a south-room window at the activists near the gate: Basheer was hit and fell; the second, indiscriminate firing injured others; even some passers-by on the road sustained gunshot injuries. Mohanan died on the way to hospital; Basheer succumbed at City Hospital. A mob later set fire to the accused's residential building, mill, factory and vehicles.
  • The defence case: the firing occurred when their house and buildings were being set on fire by an angry mob that refused the unlawful closure demand. The trial court discarded the prosecution version that the deceased had entered through the gate — they had scaled the walls; their entry was unlawful; PW-15 was roughed up; but it held the right of private defence exceeded, convicting the appellant under § 304 Part I, § 326 and § 324 IPC (7 years + 2 years + 1 year and fines). The High Court endorsed this as to the appellant, but gave A-2 (father) benefit of doubt.
Issues
  1. What is the burden and standard of proof on an accused pleading private defence, and can the plea be considered though not formally pleaded?
  2. Is the number of injuries on the accused a safe criterion of who the aggressor was?
  3. Did the trial court and High Court err in holding the right exceeded on these facts — bandh mob, unlawful entry, assaulted employee, burning property?
Arguments

For the appellant: The activists forcibly entered the compound, assaulted PW-15 and threatened the family and property; the accused were outnumbered with only PW-15 (already manhandled) on their side; the entire episode showed an "explosive" situation; the accused acted within the right of private defence of person and property, and there was no question of exceeding it.

For the State: After analysing the facts both courts below concurrently held the right exceeded; two lives were lost and several injured, including innocent passers-by on the road — such indiscriminate firing cannot be within permissible limits; the connected SLPs sought acquittal of A-2 / conviction under § 304 Part I.

Decision

Appeal allowed; conviction and sentence set aside ("the bail bonds shall stand discharged"). The Court laid out the governing law and applied it:

  • Section 96 does not define the right — whether it was legitimately exercised is a question of fact on the totality of surrounding circumstances; no abstract test exists.
  • No formal plea required; burden on accused under IEA § 105, discharged on preponderance of probabilities (Munshi Ram; Bai Fatima; Mohd. Musheer Khan; Mohinder Pal Jolly; Salim Zia — "need not establish the plea to the hilt"). The plea may rest wholly on prosecution evidence.
  • Number of injuries not a safe criterion of aggressiveness; non-explanation of the accused's injuries is important but not decisive where injuries are minor/superficial (Lakshmi Singh). A plea "cannot be based on surmises and speculation"; it is irrelevant that the accused may have had a chance to inflict even graver injury on the aggressor.
  • Relevant considerations: injuries received by the accused; imminence of threat; injuries caused by the accused; whether there was time to recourse to public authorities.
  • Golden scales / subjective standpoint: quoting Butta Singh, Vidhya Singh and Russell on Crime (no duty to retreat), the Court held the right "should not be construed narrowly" and must not become "a pretext for a vindictive, aggressive or retributive purpose."
  • Application: the threat to life and property "was not only imminent but did not cease, and it continued unabated" — vandalism, use of explosives, assault on PW-15; the events "generated a sort of frenzy and excitement rendering the situation explosive and beyond compromise." To expect calm from the outnumbered accused "would be not only too much to be desired but being unreasonably harsh and uncharitable, merely carried away only by considerations of sympathy for the lives lost, on taking a final account of what happened ultimately after everything was over." The acts were within the reasonable limits of private defence under § 96 IPC.
Principles evolved
  • The preponderance standard for private defence, fully expounded (the Salim Zia passage).
  • The injuries-rule: injuries on the accused probabilise but do not prove the plea; their non-explanation by prosecution is a weighty, not conclusive, circumstance.
  • Hindsight prohibition: the right is measured at the moment of peril, not "after everything was over" with "sympathy for the lives lost."
  • Bandh mobs forcing closure at gunpoint of legitimate business are unlawful aggression against person and property justifying armed defence.
Exam one-liner: James Martin — firing on a violent bandh mob that unlawfully entered the compound, manhandled the employee and fired/explosively threatened property was within § 96/§ 34; the plea needs only preponderance and may arise from the prosecution's own evidence.
CASE 5 — PRIVATE DEFENCE: THE MODERN RESTATEMENT · BNS §§ 34–44 ↔ IPC §§ 96–106

Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab

(2010) 1 S.C.R. 642 : (2010) 2 SCC 333 · Supreme Court of India (Dalveer Bhandari J.)
Subject
The definitive modern synthesis of the law of private defence — foundation, scope, limits and evidence — distilled into ten principles; plus the standard for reversing a trial acquittal.
Provisions
IPC §§ 96, 97, 99, 100, 102  ↔  BNS §§ 34, 35, 37, 38, 40 (quoted in full in the judgment).
Facts
  • A family land dispute: the deceased Gurcharan Singh was the brother of Bakhtawar Singh and uncle of the appellant Darshan Singh; Gurcharan's son Gurdish (PW-7) was the informant. The fields of the two brothers adjoined; a year earlier a panchayat compromise over a common well and pathway had left a grievance festering in the accused party, who wanted repartition.
  • Prosecution version (15 July 1991, ~8 a.m.): while Gurcharan and Gurdish were irrigating and mending ridges, Darshan Singh (armed with a licensed 12-bore double-barrel gun) and Bakhtawar (with a gandasa) came raising lalkaras; Bakhtawar struck Gurcharan on the chest with the gandasa; Gurcharan returned a gandasa blow on Bakhtawar's head; Darshan Singh then fired two shots, hitting Gurcharan in the chest (pellets also allegedly grazed Gurdish's arm and Gurdev's thigh); Gurcharan died at the spot.
  • Defence version: Gurcharan first struck Bakhtawar on the head with a gandasa — a bone-deep incised wound 7 cm × 0.5 cm on the right parietal region — and, believing his father killed or about to be, Darshan Singh fired in defence of his own life as Gurcharan advanced on him gandasa in hand. In his § 313 CrPC statement Darshan Singh admitted the firing and claimed private defence.
  • The trial court acquitted both accused: the presence of Gurdish and Gurdev was doubtful — no pellets were recovered from their supposed injuries, the injuries were not mentioned in the inquest report, and the doctor admitted their injuries could be self-suffered/fabricated later with the investigator's connivance; the story that Gurcharan was cutting ridge-weeds with a gandasa was unbelievable; probabilities favoured the defence; Darshan Singh fired in the exercise of private defence.
  • The High Court reversed and convicted, doubting the trial court's version mainly because the complainant appeared truthful and the accused defence untenable. Appeal to the Supreme Court (Bakhtawar's appeal abated on his death).
Issues
  1. What is the scope, foundation and extent of the right of private defence — restated for current use?
  2. Was the appellant's apprehension of death or grievous hurt, when he fired after his father was felled by a gandasa blow, a reasonable apprehension within § 100(1stly)/(2ndly)/§ 38(a)–(b)?
  3. Could the High Court reverse the trial acquittal on the ground that the complainant's version "appears more truthful"?
Arguments

For the appellant: The trial court's findings — first attack by the deceased, bone-deep gandasa wound on Bakhtawar, fabricated pellet injuries of PW-7/PW-8, no pellets recovered, unbelievable weed-cutting story — showed Darshan Singh fired only when Gurcharan advanced on him gandasa in hand after felling his father; the High Court reversed a plausible view without cogent reasons.

For the State: The complainant truthfully explained even the injury on Bakhtawar and did not conceal his father's role; the accused came armed and raising lalkaras, showing premeditation; the two gunshots on the chest of an injured man were excessive and punitive, not defensive.

Decision

Appeal allowed; the High Court's judgment set aside and the trial court's acquittal restored — "the role attributed to the appellant is fully covered by his right of private defence."

  • The judgment first delivers a full doctrinal exposition ("Scope and foundation of the private defence" — paras 24–37): Russell's rule and the no-duty-to-retreat conclusion for India; the "manly spirit" passage; Bentham's natural-protector duty; the two limiting considerations; the right as preventive, not punitive; apprehension need not be actual injury; the golden-scales rule; Holmes's "detached reflection" dictum; and that killing is justified on a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt under § 100's first two clauses even though no blow may have been inflicted on the defender.
  • Applied to the facts (para 36): Darshan Singh's § 313 statement — Gurcharan struck his father's head with a gandasa, he believed him killed, Gurcharan advanced gandasa in hand, he feared he too would be killed, and fired — disclosed a serious apprehension of death or at least grievous hurt when he exercised the right.
  • Ten principles were distilled from the precedents (para 58 — reproduced at § 4.11 above), citing Kashmiri Lal, James Martin (para 48 — preponderance), Mahabir Choudhary, Munshi Ram, Ramesh, Vidhya Singh and Buta Singh.
  • Acquittal-reversal standard: a High Court may not set aside an acquittal merely because the complainant's version "appears more truthful"; it must find the trial view utterly perverse — no other view plausible. With no pellets recovered from PW-7/PW-8, the trial court's fabrication finding was sound; its view was "not only possible or plausible but… based on the correct analysis and evaluation of the entire evidence."
Principles evolved
  • The Darshan Singh decalogue — the standard checklist for every private-defence question in Indian courts (and exams) since 2010.
  • No first-blow rule at the level of apprehension — reasonable apprehension of death/grievous hurt suffices even before any blow lands on the defender.
  • English duty to retreat expressly inapplicable to India's codified, substantive right.
  • Protective rule for acquittals — the presumption of innocence is fortified by acquittal; reversal requires perversity, not mere preference for the other view.
Exam one-liner: Darshan Singh — private defence in ten principles: self-preservation, sudden danger not self-created, mere apprehension enough, co-terminus duration, no arithmetical exactitude, proportionality, plea may arise from record, preponderance standard, offence requirement, and death permissible on imminent danger to life or limb.

Q1
What are the circumstances wherein the right of private defence of body extends to voluntarily causing death?
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.2(a) · DU Faculty of Law
Introduction framing

The general rule is that private defence of the body permits only proportionate harm (§ 39 BNS / § 101 IPC). The Code carves out an extreme case: where the assault is of a specified gravity, the defender may voluntarily cause death. This is § 100 IPC, now § 38 BNS, read with the restrictions of § 99/§ 37.

The seven enumerated circumstances — § 38 / § 100
  1. (a) [Firstly] Assault causing reasonable apprehension of death — e.g., a loaded gun raised, a gandasa descending on the head. In Darshan Singh the Supreme Court held the defender need not wait: the apprehension must be judged from the weapon, the manner of assault and surrounding circumstances; the clause applies "even though the [assailant] may not have inflicted any blow or injury on him."
  2. (b) [Secondly] Assault causing reasonable apprehension of grievous hurt — Deo Narain: a lathi aimed at the head can be instantly fatal; a spear is not per se excessive against it.
  3. (c) [Thirdly] Assault with intention of committing rape — any person present may defend the victim to the death (Bentham's "natural protector").
  4. (d) [Fourthly] Assault with intention of gratifying unnatural lust.
  5. (e) [Fifthly] Assault with intention of kidnapping or abducting — protects children and wards; guardian or bystander may resist to death.
  6. (f) [Sixthly] Assault intending wrongfully to confine, where the victim reasonably apprehends he cannot reach the public authorities for release — the captive may kill to escape.
  7. (g) [Seventhly] Acid attack or attempt — added by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 and retained as § 38(g) BNS: throwing or administering acid (or attempting it) with reasonable apprehension of grievous hurt.
Conditions attached
  • The right arises only when the offence is one of these descriptions — for any other assault, death may not be caused (§ 39/§ 101).
  • The restrictions of § 37/§ 99 still apply: no right against good-faith public-servant acts not threatening death/grievous hurt; no right where there is time to seek public authorities; no more harm than necessary.
  • The apprehension must be reasonable and imminent — the right commences with it and lasts as long as it lasts (§ 40/§ 102).
  • Evidentially the accused need show only a preponderance of probabilities (Salim Zia; James Martin).
Conclusion

Death may be caused in defence of the body only within the seven-clause catalogue, subject to the § 37/§ 99 limits — measured not in golden scales but pragmatically, from the accused's standpoint in the moment of peril (Butta Singh; Vidhya Singh; Darshan Singh principles (i)–(x)).

Q2
A is attacked by Z, a person of unsound mind, who has a spear in his hand. In order to protect himself, A strikes Z with a stick on his head, resulting in his death. During trial A pleads the right of private defence. Decide with the help of the relevant legislative provision.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.3(b) · DU Faculty of Law
Provision

This is § 98 IPC / § 36 BNS — right of private defence against the act of a person of unsound mind — and the facts are the section's own Illustration (a): "Z, a person of unsound mind, attempts to kill A; Z is guilty of no offence. But A has the same right of private defence which he would have if Z were sane."

Analysis IRAC
  • Z commits no offence — under § 84/§ 22, an unsound person incapable of knowing the nature or wrongfulness of his act is protected. Z's excuse protects Z from punishment, but it does not disarm Z's victim: the excuse negates culpability, not the defender's right (see § 1.2 — the justification/excuse asymmetry).
  • A's right is the same as if Z were sane (§ 98/§ 36) — A gets the full § 96–106 / §§ 34–44 protection.
  • Quantum: Z attacked with a spear — an assault that reasonably causes apprehension of death or grievous hurt; § 100(1stly)/(2ndly) IPC / § 38(a)–(b) BNS therefore extends A's right to voluntarily causing death. A's stick blow to the head is proportionate armed response to armed attack; nothing suggests A had time to summon police, that Z was a public servant, or that more harm was inflicted than necessary (§ 99/§ 37).
  • Commencement: the right commenced the moment the spear attack threatened A (§ 102/§ 40 — Deo Narain: the right does not await actual injury) and continued while the attack lasted.
Conclusion

A's act is protected by § 96/§ 34 read with §§ 98, 100, 102 (BNS 34, 36, 38, 40) — A commits no offence and is entitled to acquittal. The same analysis governs infant and involuntarily intoxicated aggressors; the converse case — where the "aggressor" is a public servant lawfully acting under colour of office — is barred by § 99/§ 37 unless death or grievous hurt is reasonably apprehended.

Q3
Due to communal riots, a mob of community A reached the house of X (a member of community B), which he had locked to save himself and his family. The mob started knocking at his door. Before the mob could enter, X fired from his licensed revolver and killed a member of the mob. X is charged with murder, but pleads the right of private defence. Can he do so? Give reasons.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.4(a) · DU Faculty of Law
Issues

(i) Can the right of private defence commence before the attackers actually enter or strike? (ii) Does a communal-riot mob at the door generate a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt? (iii) Is X's licensed weapon relevant?

Law
  • § 102/§ 40 — commencement: the right begins "as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence though the offence may not have been committed." In Deo Narain the Supreme Court held it a "complete misunderstanding of the law" to require the defender to first suffer serious injury; the threat must be present and imminent — a rioting mob knocking down one's door is precisely that.
  • § 100(1stly)/§ 38(a) — death permitted where the assault reasonably causes apprehension of death; a communal mob attacking the house of a targeted family in a riot (loot, plunder and arson being the known accompaniments — cf. the DU Dec 2014 paper's own facts) creates a reasonable and imminent apprehension of death or grievous hurt to X and his family.
  • § 97/§ 35 — defence of any other person: X defends not only himself but his family — the right extends to the body of any other person.
  • § 99/§ 37 — time bar inapplicable: there was plainly no time to have recourse to the public authorities (police absent/overwhelmed in a riot); the "public servant" bar is also inapplicable — the mob is not a public servant.
  • § 106/§ 44: even had X's firing risked harming innocents, against a murderous assault he may run that risk.
Application

X did not fire in revenge or pursuit: he fired while the mob was breaking upon his door — the classic window of § 102/§ 40 (compare the § 44/§ 106 illustration of the murderous mob). The single shot from a licensed revolver at the leading member of the mob is neither disproportionate nor punitive; there is no free fight — X was inside his besieged home, the aggression was entirely the mob's; and his belief need only be reasonable, not correct.

Conclusion

Yes — X can successfully plead private defence. His act falls within § 96/§ 34 read with §§ 97, 100(1stly), 102 and 106 IPC (BNS §§ 34, 35, 38(a), 40, 44); being an act done in the exercise of the right, it is no offence, and the murder charge must fail. Had he fired after dispersing the mob at their backs, the right would have ended (Jai Dev) and liability would attach.

Q4
Discuss statutory limitations on the exercise of the right of private defence as laid down in the Indian Penal Code.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.4(b) · DU Faculty of Law
Framing

James Martin calls § 99 the "Lakshman Rekha" of the right: § 96 confers, § 99 controls — "the right given under sections 96 to 98 and 100 to 106 is controlled by section 99." The BNS reproduces the scheme as § 37. The limitations are of four kinds, plus judge-made refinements:

(1) The public-servant bar — § 37(1)(a),(b) / § 99

No right of private defence against an act which does not reasonably cause apprehension of death or grievous hurt, done or attempted by a public servant (or by another under a public servant's direction) acting in good faith under colour of his office, though the act may not be strictly justifiable by law. Policy: official process must be tested in court, not by force. Safeguards: the Explanation to § 99 (BNS § 37 Expl. 1–2) preserve the right unless the citizen knew or had reason to believe the actor was a public servant / acted under his direction; and where the official act does threaten death or grievous hurt, the right revives in full.

(2) The time bar — § 37(1)(c) / § 99(3)

No right "in cases in which there is time to have recourse to the protection of the public authorities." The right is an emergency substitute for State aid (Darshan Singh, para 33: available only when immediate aid from the State machinery is not readily available). Related judicial riders: the right must answer a present and imminent threat (Deo Narain), and dies when the apprehension dies (Jai Dev) — late force is retaliation.

(3) The proportionality rule — § 37(2) / § 99

"The right of private defence in no case extends to the inflicting of more harm than it is necessary to inflict for the purpose of defence." Judicially tempered: the force must not be wholly disproportionate or much greater than necessary (Darshan Singh principle (vi)), but the defender is not weighed in golden scales (Deo Narain), cannot be expected to modulate with arithmetical exactitude (Vidhya Singh), and the situation is viewed from his subjective standpoint (James Martin, para 18; Holmes, "detached reflection cannot be demanded in the presence of an uplifted knife"). Death, moreover, may be caused only within the enumerated clauses of §§ 100/103 (BNS 38, 41).

(4) Judge-made limitations
  • Aggressor rule / free fight — one who is himself the aggressor, or a willing participant in a free fight, cannot invoke the right (Deo Narain's trial court framing).
  • Self-created danger — the right protects one "suddenly confronted with the necessity of averting an impending danger not of self-creation" (Darshan Singh principle (ii)).
  • Defence, not retribution — the right may not be used as "a pretext for a vindictive, aggressive or retributive purpose" (James Martin, para 20); pursuit after the threat ends is unprotected.
  • Offence requirement — the aggression must be an offence (or within § 98/§ 36's protected-attacker cases) — Darshan Singh principle (ix).
Conclusion

The statute balances the citizen's self-preservation against the State's monopoly of force: wide enough to repel imminent unlawful aggression to the point of death, narrow enough to bar force against good-faith official action, force where State aid is available, and force in excess of necessity — a balance James Martin aptly calls the Lakshman Rekha.

Q5
Can a student leader on indefinite fast during a protest be force-fed in order to save his life? Discuss in the light of relevant case law.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.2(b) · DU Faculty of Law
Provision

The question turns on the general exceptions dealing with consent and necessity — now BNS § 30, formerly IPC § 92: nothing is an offence by reason of harm caused to a person for whose benefit it is done in good faith even without his consent, where circumstances make it impossible to obtain consent (or he is incapable of giving it) and there is no guardian from whom consent can be obtained in time — provided the act does not amount to intentional causing of death, and (subject to statutory provisos) does not extend to voluntary causing of hurt except for the purpose of preventing death or hurt.

Analysis
  • A hunger-striker who has fallen unconscious or whose faculties are failing is incapable of signifying consent; the person is in immediate peril of death; those in charge (custodial authorities, doctors) act in good faith for his benefit — artificial feeding is precisely the "act done in good faith for benefit without consent" that § 30/§ 92 shelters.
  • The classic authority is the English Leigh v. Gladstone (1909) 1 KB 73: force-feeding a hunger-striking suffragette prisoner was held lawful, being necessary to preserve life — the necessity justification. Indian courts apply the same reasoning; the practical Indian example is the force-feeding of the Manipuri activist Irom Sharmila during her long fast (while in custody), resorted to precisely to preserve her life.
  • The necessity principle of § 19/§ 81 (act likely to cause harm but done without criminal intent in good faith to prevent greater harm) points the same way: the harm of forcible feeding is risked to avert the greater harm — death.
  • Counter-considerations (for balance): Article 21 and the emerging law of patient autonomy (refusal of medical treatment; the Supreme Court's 2018 Common Cause passive-euthanasia ruling recognises refusal rights of competent adults) mean force-feeding of a competent protestor raises serious constitutional questions; the exception answers best where the striker's condition has so deteriorated that capacity is lost or the State's custodial duty of care is engaged. A court order is the prudent course.
Conclusion

Yes — in principle, force-feeding to save the life of a fasting student leader falls within the necessity/consent exceptions (BNS §§ 19, 30 / IPC §§ 81, 92), as Leigh v. Gladstone established; the defence is strongest where the striker is in custody or no longer capable of consenting, and the intervention is medically proportionate and judicially supervised. The refusal of a fully competent adult, however, must be weighed against his right of self-determination — the balance the examiner expects.

Q6
"General defences in Chapter IV, IPC, if applicable in a given case, negate criminality completely." Elaborate the nature, classification and effect of General Exceptions under the BNS (Chapter III) and the IPC (Chapter IV).
DU examination pattern (theory) · framed on the DU Case Material introduction
Nature

General Exceptions are statutory circumstances in which an act satisfying the definition of an offence is nonetheless "nothing… an offence." They are woven into every definition by § 3(1) BNS / § 6 IPC — read subject to the exceptions though not repeating them. Two consequences: the prosecution need never anticipate them in its charge; and their successful invocation results in complete acquittal, not mitigation. In theory they divide into justifications (private defence, necessity, acts of authority — society approves the act) and excuses (insanity, infancy, involuntary intoxication, compulsion — the actor is not blameworthy), a distinction that matters for § 36/§ 98 (defence is available against excused aggressors).

Classification (BNS Ch. III, §§ 14–44)
  • Mistake of fact and authority of law — §§ 14–17 (IPC 76–79): mistake of fact negates mens rea; mistake of law never does.
  • Accident and necessity — §§ 18–19 (IPC 80–81): lawful act + accident + proper care; choosing the lesser harm.
  • Incapacity — §§ 20–24 (IPC 82–86): infancy (under 7; 7–12 without sufficient maturity), unsoundness of mind (§ 22/84), intoxication (§ 23 involuntary; § 24 voluntary).
  • Consent and benefit — §§ 25–33 (IPC 87–95): consent (express/implied, above 18, no death/grievous hurt), consent of guardian for minors/insane, communication, compulsion, slight harm.
  • Private defence — §§ 34–44 (IPC 96–106).
Effect and procedure

The exceptions operate as defences of the accused: under BSA § 108 / IEA § 105 he proves the excepting circumstances on a preponderance of probabilities, the court presuming their absence; but the prosecution's general burden (offence + mens rea beyond reasonable doubt) never shifts — and even a failed exception may leave a reasonable doubt on mens rea requiring acquittal (Dahyabhai; Bhosale; James Martin). Success negates criminality completely — though for unsoundness of mind the BNSS §§ 373–374 (CrPC §§ 334–335) sequel of safe custody follows, showing the act is excused, not approved.

Q7
"Nothing is an offence which is done by a person who, at the time of doing it, by reason of unsoundness of mind, is incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that he is doing what is either wrong or contrary to law." Critically examine § 22 BNS / § 84 IPC with reference to the M'Naghten Rules and decided case law.
DU examination pattern (theory + critical note)
The test — legal, not medical insanity

Section 84/§ 22 embodies the M'Naghten Rules (1843): a presumption of sanity; a defence only where disease of the mind produced, at the time of the act, a defect of reason rendering the accused incapable of knowing the nature and quality of the act or its wrongfulness. Every ingredient is cognitive — "unsoundness of mind," "at the time of doing it," "incapable of knowing," and the alternative objects (nature / wrong or contrary to law). Courts examine: motive, history of illness, conduct before/during/after, and the nature of the act (Surendra Mishra, (2011) 11 SCC 495: "every person suffering from mental illness is not exempted"; the section is about legal insanity).

Operation in practice — Bhosale and the burden

Srikant Anandrao Bhosale (case card 2) shows the machinery working: paranoid schizophrenia proved before and after the offence; crucial time = time of offence, a state of mind inferred from "the circumstances which preceded, attended and followed the crime"; the accused's burden is only preponderance (Dahyabhai); the anger theory did not rule out a schizophrenic attack → benefit of § 84. Conversely, where only passion or perversity is shown, the section fails: knowledge "is not negatived by any mental condition short of insanity" (Gyarsibai, DU CM).

Critique
  • Cognitive only: the section ignores disease of the volitions — the person who knows the act is wrong but cannot control it (irresistible impulse) is outside the protection; the drafters knowingly chose certainty over psychiatry.
  • Outdated psychiatry: "unsoundness of mind" reflects the 1840s' unitary psychosis; modern medicine distinguishes schizophrenia, affective disorders, organic states — courts must translate into legal incapacity anyway.
  • No diminished responsibility: unlike England (Homicide Act 1957) there is no partial defence of substantially impaired mental responsibility reducing murder to manslaughter — Indian law is all-or-nothing.
  • Proof difficulties: retrospective diagnosis of "the moment" is guesswork; the burden, though civil, still trips poorly represented accused.
Conclusion

The section remains a defensible compromise: it trades psychiatric sophistication for a clear, verdict-apt test. Reform committees (including the Law Commission) have suggested incorporating "inability to conform conduct," but Parliament — and now the BNS draftsmen, who reproduced § 84 verbatim as § 22 — have retained M'Naghten. The safety valves are the Dahyabhai reasonable-doubt rule and the safe-custody provisions (BNSS §§ 373–374) that follow acquittal.

Q8
"Drunkenness is no defence — but it may be a defence." Examine intoxication as a general exception under the BNS/IPC, distinguishing involuntary from voluntary intoxication.
DU examination pattern (theory)
Involuntary intoxication — § 23 BNS / § 85 IPC

Where the intoxicant was administered without his knowledge or against his will, and it rendered him incapable of knowing the nature of the act or its wrongfulness, the defence is complete — the law treats him like an unsound person, for no free will produced the condition. All ingredients must be pleaded and proved (preponderance) by the accused.

Voluntary intoxication — § 24 BNS / § 86 IPC

Where a man knowingly drinks himself into intoxication, the section imposes the imputation of knowledge: for offences requiring particular knowledge or intent, he "shall be liable to be dealt with as if he had the same knowledge as he would have had if he had not been intoxicated." But his intention remains a fact to be gathered from the circumstances — evidence of drunkenness is admissible if it shows he was incapable of forming the specific intent (Basdev, adopting DPP v. Beard): (i) insanity produced by drunkenness is a defence; (ii) drunkenness destroying the capacity to form the specific intent is weighed on intent; (iii) drunkenness falling short of that — merely loosening temper — does not rebut the presumption that a man intends the natural consequences of his acts.

Application to homicide

Murder (§ 101/§ 300) is satisfiable by knowledge-based clauses — hence a drunkard who fires into a victim's abdomen at close range is fixed with knowledge that death is likely (imputed), even if intention is doubtful; only proof of incapacity to intend could reduce the offence to § 105/§ 304 Part I. In Basdev itself the drunk officer who shot the seat-refusing boy was held far from incapacity — he moved, spoke, fled, begged pardon — so § 302 stood.

Conclusion

The paradox in the quotation resolves: drunkenness of itself is never an excuse (whoever drinks takes the risk of what he does); but drunkenness to the point of (i) administration against will, (ii) produced insanity, or (iii) destroyed capacity to form the specific intent, may be a defence. Between those poles, the sober man's knowledge is imputed.

Q9
A military officer, heavily drunk at a wedding, asks a 15-year-old boy to vacate a seat; on the boy's refusal he shoots him dead with his service revolver. At trial he pleads that he was too drunk to have formed any intention to kill. Decide.
DU examination pattern (problem — modelled on Basdev v. State of PEPSU, AIR 1956 SC 488)
Issue

Whether voluntary intoxication (i) exempts, (ii) reduces murder (§ 101/§ 300; punishment § 103/§ 302) to culpable homicide (§ 105/§ 304), or (iii) is irrelevant.

Law — § 24 BNS / § 86 IPC + Basdev
  • The intoxication was voluntary (self-administered) → § 23/§ 85 is out of question.
  • Knowledge is imputed as if sober: firing a revolver into the abdomen of an unarmed boy at close range — knowledge that death is the likely consequence attaches notwithstanding drink.
  • Intention alone is examinable, and only to prove incapacity to form the specific intent; drink that merely loosens temper does not rebut the presumption of intending the natural consequences of one's acts.
Application

On Basdev's facts — identical — the Court noted the officer walked unaided, spoke coherently at times, chose a seat, tried to escape after the shooting and pleaded for forgiveness: conduct demonstrating preserved knowledge and capacity. No evidence shows the officer here was reduced to insanity or total incapacity; motiveless-ness (if it be so) does not negate the imputed knowledge. The murder clause founded on knowledge (§ 101 secondly/§ 300 secondly — intention + knowledge that the act is imminently dangerous) is satisfied.

Conclusion

The plea fails; the officer is guilty of murder (§ 103/§ 302). The court may consider the intoxication and absence of premeditation only in sentencing (as the Sessions Judge in Basdev did by sentencing him to imprisonment for life rather than death), never to reduce the offence. If, exceptionally, medical evidence had established that the drink had produced a state of insanity (fixed madness), § 22/§ 84 would have been the answer instead.

Q10
"The right of private defence commences as soon as a reasonable apprehension of danger to the body arises from an attempt or threat to commit the offence, though the offence may not have been committed." Explain with reference to the commencement and continuance of the right, and discuss whether a defender must first sustain injury before defending.
DU examination pattern (theory — Deo Narain-centred)
The statutory scheme

Body (§ 40/§ 102): the right commences on reasonable apprehension arising from an attempt or threat — though the offence is not yet committed — and continues as long as the apprehension continues. Property (§ 43/§ 105): commencement on reasonable apprehension of danger to property; continuance is offence-specific — theft (till retreat with property/police aid/recovery), robbery (while hurt/restraint or fear of instant harm lasts), criminal trespass or mischief (while it continues), night house-breaking (while the house-trespass begun by it continues).

The first-injury fallacy — Deo Narain

In Deo Narain the High Court had held that the right to cause death arose "only after a serious injury had actually been inflicted" on the accused. The Supreme Court called this "a complete misunderstanding of the law": the section's words ("attempt or threat… though the offence may not have been committed") make apprehension, not victimisation, the trigger. The threat must be present and imminent — not remote or speculative — and the right is "preventive and protective, not punitive." Equally, the Court refused to let the High Court construct a lathi-below-spear weapon hierarchy: a lathi blow to the head can be instantly fatal; parties in excitement cannot be expected to weigh "in golden scales" how much force suffices.

Cessation

The right dies with the apprehension: once the threat "has either been destroyed or has been put to rout, there can be no occasion to exercise the right" (Jai Dev, AIR 1963 SC 612, cited in James Martin). Force used thereafter — chasing a fleeing aggressor, "finishing off" a repelled attacker — is retaliation and punishable; the right is a shield, and once it has served, it may not become a sword.

Conclusion

The defender need not bleed first, need not wait for the blow to land, and need not calibrate his weapon — but he must act while the danger is live: before it matures is premature (§ 37(1)(c)/§ 99(3) time-bar), after it dissolves is aggression. Darshan Singh principles (iii)–(v) compress the rule: mere apprehension starts it; co-terminus duration; no arithmetical exactitude.

Q11
"The right of private defence is essentially protective and preventive and never punitive." Discuss the nature and scope of the right, the rule against free fight, and the principles laid down in Darshan Singh v. State of Punjab.
DU examination pattern (theory + principles)
Nature

The right is the citizen's emergency licence to repel unlawful aggression when State aid cannot be had in time — founded on self-preservation (Darshan Singh: "there is nothing more degrading to the human spirit than to run away in face of danger"; no duty to retreat in India) and on the social duty of every person as the "natural protector" of every other (Bentham). Being protective and preventive, it is exhausted when the danger is: further force is punishment — "the right to punish vests in the State" (Deo Narain); the right is "not a right to take revenge" (Darshan Singh, para 30) and may not be "a pretext for a vindictive, aggressive or retributive purpose" (James Martin, para 20).

Scope

Every person may defend (a) his own body or any other person's body against any offence affecting the human body, and (b) movable or immovable property (his own or another's) against theft, robbery, mischief, criminal trespass and attempts (§ 35/§ 97), subject to § 37/§ 99 limits, with death permitted only in the enumerated cases (§ 38/§ 41) and the right commencing on reasonable apprehension and lasting its duration (§ 40/§ 43). The right exists even against excused aggressors — unsound, infant, involuntarily intoxicated, mistaken (§ 36/§ 98).

Free fight and the aggressor

Where both parties willingly engage, each is an aggressor vis-à-vis the other and neither may invoke the right; the plea belongs only to one confronted with a danger "not of self-creation" (Darshan Singh principle (ii)). Courts therefore first ask who initiated and sustained the aggression — with the guard that the number of injuries on the accused is not a safe criterion (James Martin, para 14).

The Darshan Singh decalogue (para 58)
  1. Self-preservation is the basic human instinct, recognised by all civilized nations within reasonable limits;
  2. available only against sudden danger not of self-creation;
  3. a mere reasonable apprehension suffices — actual offence unnecessary;
  4. commencement and continuance co-terminous with the apprehension;
  5. no duty to modulate defence with arithmetical exactitude;
  6. force must not be wholly disproportionate or much greater than necessary;
  7. the plea may be considered even if not expressly taken;
  8. proof on preponderance, not beyond reasonable doubt;
  9. the right arises only against an offence (subject to § 36/§ 98);
  10. imminent danger to life or limb justifies even death.

Conclusion: read together, the decalogue operationalises the "protective, never punitive" formula: the moment force outlives the danger, it ceases to be defence and becomes the crime it claimed to resist.

Q12
During a forced bandh, five activists scale the walls of a compound, assault the only employee on the premises and demand closure of the flour mill; a violent mob gathers outside and begins to set the buildings on fire. The owner's son fires a gun from the window, killing two. He is prosecuted for murder and pleads private defence of person and property. Decide.
DU examination pattern (problem — modelled on James Martin v. State of Kerala, (2004) 2 SCC 203)
Issues

Whether unlawful bandh-enforcement + assault on the employee + arson generates rights of private defence of body and property extending to deadly force; and whether the accused exceeded the "Lakshman Rekha" (§ 37/§ 99).

Law
  • Unlawful aggression: scaling walls, trespass, criminal assault, arson — offences against body (any person's) and against property (criminal trespass, mischief by fire on a dwelling/custodial building — § 41(c)/§ 103(3rdly)).
  • Commencement: the right arose when PW-type employee was attacked and the mob turned violent — continuing unabated through the firing (§ 40/§ 102; § 43(a), (d)/§ 105).
  • Death permissible: armed intruders + outnumbered defenders + mob arson in progress ⇒ reasonable apprehension of death/grievous hurt (§ 38(a)–(b)/§ 100(1stly)–(2ndly)); mischief by fire on the dwelling independently supports death (§ 41(c)/§ 103(3rdly)).
  • Evidence: plea needs only preponderance; may rest on the prosecution record; injuries on the accused/employee probabilise it; the number of injuries is not a safe criterion; no time for public authorities; judged subjectively, without golden scales; even harm to innocents by the roadside is shielded by § 44/§ 106.
Application — James Martin itself

These are James Martin's facts. The Supreme Court held the threat to life and property "not only imminent but… continued unabated"; the events "generated a sort of frenzy and excitement rendering the situation explosive and beyond compromise"; expecting restraint from the outnumbered accused would be "unreasonably harsh and uncharitable, merely carried away… by considerations of sympathy for the lives lost, on taking a final account of what happened ultimately after everything was over." The firing was within the reasonable limits of § 96/§ 34.

Conclusion

The accused is entitled to acquittal — the acts were done in the exercise of private defence of person and property (§§ 34–35, 38, 40–41 BNS / §§ 96–97, 100, 102–103 IPC). Had he fired after the mob dispersed, or pursued the fugitives, the right would have lapsed (Jai Dev) and the plea would fail as retaliation.

Q13
Write short notes on any two: (i) Lucid interval; (ii) The 'golden scales' rule; (iii) Lakshman Rekha of private defence; (iv) Acid-attack clause of § 38 BNS; (v) Safe custody after acquittal on the ground of insanity.
DU examination pattern (short notes)
(i) Lucid interval

A period of remission or cure within a continuing mental illness, during which the patient regains capacity. An offence committed in a lucid interval is fully punishable — § 22/§ 84 requires incapacity "at the time of doing it." Evidence of the interval (coherent conduct, rational motive, planning, flight) rebuts the inference that the offence accompanied a psychotic attack; conversely, onset and relapse around the offence support it (Bhosale's totality test).

(ii) The 'golden scales' rule

From Deo Narain: persons in the excitement of a sudden affray "cannot be expected to weigh in golden scales" exactly how much force suffices; echoed in Butta Singh (no "high-powered spectacles or microscopes"), Vidhya Singh (no "microscopic and pedantic scrutiny") and Holmes ("detached reflection cannot be demanded in the presence of an uplifted knife"). Proportionality remains a limit (§ 37(2)/§ 99; Darshan Singh (vi)) but is applied from the defender's standpoint in the moment, not with courtroom hindsight (James Martin).

(iii) Lakshman Rekha of private defence

James Martin's metaphor for the boundary of § 99/§ 37: the right must not be exceeded — no force against good-faith public servants (absent death/grievous-hurt apprehension), none where there is time to seek public authority, none beyond necessity, none against protected justifications, none in a free fight, and none after the danger has passed. Crossing the Rekha converts defence into offence, exposing the actor to liability for the excess.

(iv) Acid-attack clause — § 38(g) BNS / § 100 'Seventhly' IPC

Inserted by the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013 and carried into the BNS: an act of throwing or administering acid, or attempting it, which may reasonably cause apprehension of grievous hurt, extends the right of private defence of the body to causing death. The clause responds to the acid-violence epidemic; "administering" covers deception (spiking), and the attempt limb protects before the liquid flies.

(v) Safe custody after insanity acquittal

Acquittal under § 22/§ 84 is protective, not exculpatory release: BNSS § 373 (CrPC 334) requires the judgment to state whether the accused committed the act; § 374 (CrPC 335) then mandates detention in safe custody (or other disposition, e.g., reception into a mental-health establishment) where the act would otherwise have been an offence — because the excuse negates culpability, not dangerousness.

7.5 Ten exam techniques for this unit

  1. Always cite both codes — "§ 38 BNS / § 100 IPC" — the DU course is taught on the BNS with IPC continuity; examiners expect the pair.
  2. Quote the section's operative words ("reasonable apprehension", "though the offence may not have been committed", "more harm than… necessary") — statutory language is the currency of this unit.
  3. Structure PD problems in five steps: aggression → commencement → quantum (death clause?) → limits (§ 37/§ 99) → evidence (preponderance, subjective standpoint).
  4. Name the case for every proposition — Deo Narain for commencement, Darshan Singh for the decalogue, James Martin for evidence, Basdev for intoxication, Bhosale for insanity.
  5. Never write "the accused must prove PD/insanity beyond reasonable doubt" — the standard is preponderance (Salim Zia; Dahyabhai).
  6. Distinguish excuses from justifications when the aggressor is insane/child/drugged — § 36/§ 98 then wins the extra mark.
  7. Check the timing twice — before (premature/§ 37(1)(c)) and after (retaliation/Jai Dev) are where problem questions hide the trap.
  8. Remember the 2013 acid clause — showing awareness of § 38(g)/§ 100 Seventhly signals updated knowledge; so does noting that the BNS retained "house-breaking after sunset and before sunrise".
  9. Answer insanity problems through the totality of circumstances (before–during–after), never just the medical certificate.
  10. Finish with the sequel — insanity acquittal leads to BNSS §§ 373–374 safe custody; PD acquittal is absolute. That closing distinction impresses.
