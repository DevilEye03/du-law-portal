Unit map and method

The property offences form an escalation chain. Theft is dishonest moving of movable property from possession without consent; extortion obtains delivery through fear; robbery is aggravated theft or extortion with immediate violence/fear; dacoity is robbery committed or attempted conjointly by at least five persons or with five including persons present and aiding.

Foundation: possession, movable property and dishonesty

Movable property

BNS §2(21) includes property of every description except land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth. A thing attached to earth becomes capable of being stolen when severed; severance and movement may occur through the same act under §303.

Dishonestly

BNS §2(7): doing anything with the intention of causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another. Wrongful gain/loss are gain/loss by unlawful means of property to which the gaining person is not legally entitled or the losing person is legally entitled.

Possession is the protected interest

Theft primarily protects possession, not absolute ownership. Therefore, a person may commit theft of his own property if it is in another’s lawful possession and he takes it dishonestly—for example, a pledged watch removed before the debt/security is discharged.

Constructive possession

BNS §3(3) treats property in the possession of a spouse, clerk or servant on account of another as being in that other person’s possession. This matters when an employee, agent or family member physically holds property for the master/owner.

Property offence answer = identify possession + property + mental element + method of obtaining + aggravating circumstance + correct punishment

Consent and injury

For theft, consent may be express or implied and may be given by the person in possession or someone with express/implied authority. Under BNS §2(14), “injury” includes harm illegally caused to body, mind, reputation or property; this broad definition helps explain extortion by threats to reputation, family or property.

Theft — BNS §303 / IPC §§378–382

Theft is complete at the point of dishonest movement for the purpose of taking. Actual escape from the premises, permanent deprivation, sale or personal profit is not necessary.

1 · Mental elementIntend wrongful gain/loss
2 · SubjectMovable property
3 · PossessionOut of another’s possession
4 · ConsentWithout express/implied consent
5 · MovementMove it for that taking

Five essential ingredients under §303(1)

  1. Dishonest intention. The intention must exist when the property is moved. A bona fide mistake or an honest belief of implied consent negatives theft. Later dishonest conversion may be BNS §314 criminal misappropriation, not theft at the initial taking.
  2. Movable property. The thing must be movable under §2(21). Land or a thing attached to earth cannot be stolen while attached, but severance itself plus movement can complete theft.
  3. Out of another’s possession. The section protects possession. Legal title is not decisive; a clerk, warehouse keeper, spouse or employer may have actual/constructive possession on the facts.
  4. Without consent. Consent may be express or implied and may come from the possessor or authorised person. Consent induced by a legally relevant fear/misconception is not genuine consent.
  5. Movement for the taking. Moving a thing by removing an obstacle, separating it, or causing an animal to move counts. The movement must be causally connected to the dishonest taking.

Explanations and illustrations that matter in exams

Severance

A cuts a tree intending to take it. Theft is complete when the tree is severed for that dishonest taking.

Animal movement

Inducing a dog to follow bait or driving a bullock carrying treasure can move the animal and the property moved through it.

Found property

A ring lying on a highway with no person in possession is not theft merely on taking; dishonest misappropriation may arise under §314.

Hidden property

Moving a ring from a table to a hidden place with intention to take later completes theft at the first movement, even if the accused repents later.

Owner can steal

A person who pawned a watch and removes it before discharging the lawful security may commit theft of his own property by dishonestly depriving the pawnee of possession/security.

Temporary removal

A temporary taking can create wrongful loss. Returning the item later is not a defence where the initial removal was dishonest.

Extortion — BNS §308 / IPC §§383–389

Extortion is delivery-based. The offender creates fear; the person put in fear then voluntarily delivers property or a legally valuable instrument. In theft the offender moves the property; in extortion the victim’s fear-caused delivery is the immediate mechanism.

Extortion = intentional fear of injury + dishonest inducement + delivery of property / valuable security / convertible signed-sealed thing

§308(1): essential ingredients

Intentional actPut a person in fear
FearInjury to that person or any other
DishonestyInducement is to cause wrongful gain/loss
DeliveryVictim delivers property, security or instrument
  1. Intentionally putting in fear. The fear must be caused deliberately; accidental anxiety is not enough.
  2. Fear of injury. Injury may be to the person threatened or another person. Because BNS §2(14) includes body, mind, reputation and property, threats to reputation or family may qualify if they cause legally relevant fear.
  3. Dishonest inducement. The fear must induce delivery and the object must involve wrongful gain/loss. A threat made with no dishonest demand may constitute another offence, but not extortion under §308(1).
  4. Delivery to any person. Delivery need not be to the accused; the section permits delivery to any person. The victim must deliver because of the fear.
  5. Subject of delivery. Any property, valuable security, or anything signed/sealed capable of being converted into a valuable security. A blank paper signed under threat of grievous hurt qualifies if it may become a valuable security.

Robbery — BNS §309 / IPC §390 and §§392–394

Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion. It is not a completely independent third category of property acquisition.

Robbery by theft — §309(2)
Theft + for the purpose of committing/carrying away the theft, voluntary causing or attempting death, hurt, wrongful restraint, or fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint.
Robbery by extortion — §309(3)
Extortion + offender present + fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint + immediate, then-and-there delivery.

Robbery by theft

  1. First establish the complete or attempted theft.
  2. The violence/fear must be voluntary and connected to committing the theft, carrying away/attempting to carry away the stolen property—not a wholly unrelated later act.
  3. The additional harm may be death, hurt, wrongful restraint or fear of instant death/hurt/wrongful restraint.
  4. Actual completion of the taking is not essential where the statute covers attempt and the robbery conduct is otherwise complete.

Robbery by extortion

  1. There must be extortion: fear + dishonest inducement + delivery.
  2. The offender must be present—sufficiently near to create fear of instant harm.
  3. The fear must be of instant death, hurt or wrongful restraint, to the victim or another.
  4. Delivery must be then and there. A demand for money to be sent later, without immediate presence/instant fear, remains extortion, not robbery.

Robbery is not dacoity merely because many people are present. Dacoity needs at least five persons and conjoint robbery. Four persons acting together commit robbery, not dacoity, even if their plan is organised.

Dacoity and connected gang offences — BNS §§310–313

Every dacoity is robbery; every robbery is not dacoity. The decisive additions are the minimum number of participants and conjoint action.

Dacoity = robbery / attempted robbery + five or more persons + conjoint commission/attempt/aiding

§310(1): definition

  1. Robbery must exist or be attempted. Mere planning to steal is not dacoity; however, special provisions criminalise preparation and assembly.
  2. Minimum five persons. Five is the minimum; there is no maximum.
  3. Conjoint action. The persons must jointly commit or attempt robbery, or the whole number of persons jointly committing/attempting plus persons present and aiding must total five or more.
  4. Presence and aiding can count. A person who is present and aids the robbery may fall within the total even if he does not personally take property.

§310(2)–(6): complete punishment ladder

§311, §312 and §313

§311 / IPC §397

At the time of committing robbery/dacoity, using a deadly weapon, causing grievous hurt, or attempting to cause death/grievous hurt attracts a minimum of 7 years. It is an aggravated punishment provision; apply the base robbery/dacoity ingredients first.

§312 / IPC §398

At the time of attempting robbery/dacoity, being armed with a deadly weapon attracts a minimum of 7 years. The offence remains attempt; actual completion is unnecessary.

§313 / IPC §401

Belonging to a gang habitually committing theft or robbery, but not a gang of dacoits, is punishable with RI up to 7 years + fine. It targets organised habitual association rather than a single theft.

Detailed DU case law

Pyare Lal Bhargava v. State of Rajasthan

AIR 1963 SC 1094 · IPC §§378–379 · BNS §§303(1)–303(2)

Facts

Ram Kumar Ram obtained permission from the former Alwar Government to supply electricity and later sought transfer of the licence to a company. Pyare Lal Bhargava was a Superintendent in the Chief Engineer’s Office. At Ram Kumar’s instance, Pyare Lal obtained the government file (Ex. PA/1) from the Secretariat through a clerk, took it to his house, made it available to Ram Kumar, and allowed documents to be removed and substituted. The file was then returned to the office. The document tampering was discovered.

Procedural history

The Sub-Divisional Magistrate convicted the accused on theft and document-related charges. The Sessions Judge set aside the §465 conviction but maintained Pyare Lal’s conviction under IPC §379 and Ram Kumar’s conviction for abetment of theft. The High Court set aside Ram Kumar’s conviction but confirmed Pyare Lal’s. Pyare Lal appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues
  1. Can a temporary removal of a file, intended to be returned, amount to theft?
  2. Was Pyare Lal in possession of the file so that he could not “take” it from himself?
  3. Did temporary deprivation cause wrongful loss/dishonest taking even though the accused did not permanently gain the file?
Arguments
  • Appellant: the Superintendent had possession of the file and could not steal from himself; the file was taken only to show documents to Ram Kumar and was returned the next day; there was no intention to keep it and no wrongful gain or permanent wrongful loss.
  • Prosecution/State: legal possession was with the Engineering Department/Chief Engineer, not an individual officer; Pyare Lal removed the file without authorisation and made it available for private substitution; temporary deprivation of the Department’s possession is wrongful loss and dishonest taking.
Decision

The Supreme Court dismissed the appeal and upheld the theft conviction. It held that the file was in the Department’s possession under the Chief Engineer, not in Pyare Lal’s personal legal possession. The fact that it was returned did not prevent theft. Temporary deprivation itself caused wrongful loss, and a dishonest purpose need not produce permanent profit.

Principle evolved: Theft does not require permanent deprivation, permanent gain or an intention never to return. Temporary dispossession can be wrongful loss. A person who has physical access to property by virtue of office is not necessarily in legal possession of it; an unauthorised removal from the Department’s possession can be theft.

Memory visual: Departmental possession → unauthorised temporary removal → private use → return. The loss of possession for a period is enough.

BNS application: Apply §303(1). If the file is proved to be Government property, consider whether aggravated §305(e) is attracted; do not assume the enhancement without proof of the statutory fact.

State of Karnataka v. Basavegowda

(1997) Cr. L.J. 4386 (Kant.) · IPC §§307, 325, 384 and 392 · BNS property mapping §§308, 309 and connected hurt provisions

Facts

About ten days after marriage, Basavegowda took his wife Bhagyamma to the Burudala Bore forest on the pretext of attending a friend’s wedding. He threatened to kill her unless she handed over her ornaments worth approximately ₹11,000. She removed and handed over the jewellery. He wrapped it in a handkerchief and put it in his pocket, then assaulted her with a large stone and fists. She raised an alarm; he continued until two persons approached, then fled. She was taken to hospital, where injuries—including a fractured rib—were recorded. The ornaments were later recovered from the accused wrapped in the handkerchief.

Procedural history

He was charged under IPC §307 for attempt to murder and §392 for robbery. The trial court acquitted him, treating Bhagyamma’s sole testimony as insufficient, particularly because many witnesses had turned hostile. The State appealed to the Karnataka High Court.

Issues
  1. Whether Bhagyamma’s testimony, supported by medical evidence, scene evidence and recovery, could prove extortion despite hostile witnesses.
  2. Whether the act constituted attempt to murder under §307 and robbery under §392, or a lesser hurt/extortion offence.
  3. Whether a husband’s possession of his wife’s ornaments could be treated as lawful possession.
Arguments
  • State: Bhagyamma’s prompt hospital statement was consistent with the FIR and later evidence; broken bangles, the stone, six injuries, the rib fracture and recovery of the entire jewellery set wrapped in the accused’s handkerchief corroborated her. The ornaments were her personal property and had been taken under threat. The State also argued that a large stone capable of causing death and a threat to kill justified §307 and, alternatively, extortion by threat of death under IPC §386.
  • Defence: Bhagyamma’s later divorce and remarriage showed hostility and motive to falsely implicate; most witnesses were hostile; the stone could not have caused the chest injury; a husband could lawfully keep his wife’s ornaments; if he intended to kill her, he would not have allowed escape; at most, the facts showed grievous hurt, not attempted murder, and did not justify robbery/extortion.
Decision

The High Court set aside the acquittal. It accepted Bhagyamma’s evidence as substantially corroborated and held that hostile witnesses did not automatically aid the accused. It rejected the motive argument: later divorce/remarriage did not prove fabrication. The medical objection failed because the doctor ultimately accepted that the stone could cause the injury. The Court held the conduct did not reach IPC §307 attempt to murder, but the fracture justified IPC §325 grievous hurt. As to property, the wife’s ornaments were her personal property; possession obtained by threat was unlawful. The Court convicted under IPC §384 extortion rather than the more serious §392/§386 formulation and awarded two years’ RI under each, concurrently, with set-off.

Principles evolved: (1) A credible victim’s testimony can be sufficient when supported by surrounding material; hostile witnesses do not by themselves create reasonable doubt. (2) A husband has no automatic lawful right to possess or appropriate a wife’s personal ornaments when they were taken under threat. (3) Extortion requires fear-induced delivery; robbery requires additional strict immediacy/presence conditions. (4) The actual act, weapon and injuries control attempt-to-murder analysis; a threat to kill is not conclusive of IPC §307.

Hypothetical visual of the evidentiary chain used by the Court; it is not a reproduction of evidence.

BNS application: The conduct maps to BNS §308(1) and the completed fear-of-death/grievous-hurt category in §308(5), subject to proof and charge. The robbery question must separately satisfy §309(3)’s presence, instant fear and then-and-there delivery requirements.

Q5/2021 — Vineet: provocation and temporary file removal

Question
Vineet’s superior Surat summons him, humiliates and insults him, including a remark about his childlessness. Vineet returns to his barrack, picks up his service gun and returns to shoot Surat dead. Separately, Vineet removes a file from his office to his home, where friend Daman tampers with it; Vineet returns it the next day. Decide liability with case law.

Model answer

Part I — homicide/provocation. Under BNS §101 Exception 1 (the IPC §300 Exception 1 correspondence), grave and sudden provocation may reduce murder to culpable homicide if the provocation deprives the accused of self-control and the death is caused while that deprivation continues, subject to the statutory exclusions. The insults may be personally grave, but the facts show that Vineet returned to the barrack, was able to pick up a gun and then went back to the store. That interval supplied an opportunity for reflection and preparation. The deliberate retrieval and return with a weapon makes the act unlikely to be the immediate product of sudden loss of self-control. He will not succeed in the grave-and-sudden-provocation defence; the appropriate homicide classification depends on the full proof of intention/knowledge, but the exception should not be granted on these facts.

Part II — file. BNS §303(1) requires dishonest intention, movable property, possession of another, absence of consent and movement for the taking. The file was in the possession of the Department/competent officer, not Vineet merely because he could access it as an employee. Removing it to enable Daman to tamper with documents is dishonest and causes wrongful loss by temporary deprivation. It is immaterial that the file was returned the next day. Pyare Lal Bhargava holds that temporary removal of a departmental file, even with intended return and without permanent profit, is theft. Vineet is therefore liable under §303(1); if the evidence proves that he was a clerk/servant and the property was in the master/employer’s possession, §306 may be considered. If Government ownership of the file is proved, §305(e) may also be examined, but the base proposition is §303 theft.

Conclusion. The provocation defence fails because of the cooling interval and weapon retrieval. Vineet is guilty of theft because temporary dishonest deprivation is enough.

Q6/2020 — A takes his own shoes from temple custody

Question
A deposits shoes with a temple guard, takes a token and later, tired of waiting in the queue, picks his shoes from the rack without giving the token. He says the shoes are his own property. Discuss.

Model answer

Under BNS §303(1), theft is dishonest movement of movable property out of another’s possession without consent. Although ownership is ordinarily A’s, the shoes were placed in the guard’s lawful custody for the temple’s safekeeping system. Therefore, the possession element may be satisfied even though title remains with A. An owner can commit theft of his own property if he dishonestly removes it from another’s lawful possession—for example, the pledged-watch illustration and BNS §303 illustration (k).

However, ownership does not automatically establish the mental element. A took the shoes because he was tired and wanted to avoid the queue. The facts do not say that he intended wrongful gain or wrongful loss, to deprive the temple of a security, or to evade a lawful lien. The token procedure regulates retrieval but does not transfer title to the temple. This resembles BNS §303 illustration (i), where an owner’s removal without dishonest intention is not theft, rather than the pledged-watch case where the owner intends to defeat a lawful security.

Conclusion. The better answer is that A should be acquitted of theft for want of dishonest intention, although the act may violate temple rules or amount to a civil/disciplinary wrong. If evidence showed that the shoes were being lawfully retained as security or that A intended to cause wrongful loss to the custodian by bypassing a legal right of retention, theft could be made out.

Q1(b)/2019 — Suresh and the valuable pen

Question
Suresh visits Ramesh’s house, waits, uses a pen from Ramesh’s table to write a message and accidentally drops it into his pocket while leaving. He later forgets about it. The pen is recovered from his house. Is he liable for theft?

Model answer

The pen was movable property in Ramesh’s possession. But theft under BNS §303(1) requires dishonest intention at the time of movement. Suresh took the pen to write a message and, according to the facts, put it in his pocket by force of habit and later forgot it. There is no stated intention to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss when he moved it. The initial use may even be consistent with implied consent in a friendly setting, though the stronger point is absence of dishonesty.

Value alone does not create theft. If Suresh later discovered the pen and then dishonestly retained or converted it, BNS §314 criminal misappropriation could arise, but the stated forgetfulness does not establish that later mens rea. Pyare Lal does not assist the prosecution because that case involved deliberate unauthorised removal to facilitate private tampering—a dishonest purpose from the beginning.

Conclusion. On the stated facts, Suresh is not guilty of theft. The prosecution cannot prove dishonest intention at the moment of movement. A different result would follow if evidence showed that he deliberately pocketed it intending to keep it.

Short answer

Q2(a)/2018 — diamond ring moved under the carpet

Question
X sees Y’s diamond ring on a table in Y’s house, puts it under the carpet intending to take it later, but then changes his mind and leaves it there. Has X committed an offence?

Model answer

Yes. The ring was movable property in Y’s possession. X moved it from the table and hid it with the dishonest intention of taking it later. Under BNS §303 Explanation 3, movement includes moving by removing an obstacle or otherwise; more importantly, the statutory illustration corresponding to this fact provides that the offence is complete at the first movement to the hiding place. Subsequent repentance does not undo a completed theft. X is liable under BNS §303(1), corresponding to IPC §§378/379. The fact that the ring remained in the house and was never ultimately removed is irrelevant.

Short answer

Q5(b)/2023 — fear of grievous hurt and signed blank paper

Question
A puts Z in fear of grievous hurt and dishonestly induces Z to sign or seal a blank paper and deliver it. The paper may be converted into a valuable security. What offence is committed?

Model answer

A has committed extortion under BNS §308(1). The ingredients are complete: intentional fear of injury (here grievous hurt), dishonest inducement and delivery of a signed/sealed thing capable of being converted into a valuable security. A blank paper can qualify because the statutory language is not confined to immediately existing money or goods. Since the completed extortion was achieved by fear of grievous hurt, the applicable punishment category is BNS §308(5), up to ten years and fine, subject to the charge and proof. If Z had not delivered the paper and A had only put/attempted to put Z in such fear to commit extortion, §308(4) would be considered. The equivalent IPC structure is §383 with punishment under §386 for completed extortion by fear of death/grievous hurt.

Problem question

Q4(b)/2017 — schoolboys, threat to expose drug use and group liability

Question
A, B, C, D and E see X and Y taking drugs. A, B and C threaten to tell their parents unless the boys give money. D remains silent and keeps guard. E walks away. X and Y hand over cell phones and ₹1,000, divided among A–D. Decide liability.

Model answer

Under BNS §308(1), extortion requires intentional fear of injury, dishonest inducement and delivery of property. A threat to expose conduct may create fear of reputational or mental injury under BNS §2(14), and the demand for phones and money is dishonest. The boys delivered property because of the threat. The basic offence of completed extortion is therefore made out; if the prosecution proves that the threat was an accusation of an offence attracting the punishment thresholds in §308(6)/(7), the aggravated accusation categories may also be considered. On the stated facts, the safer primary charge is §308(2).

A, B and C directly threatened and induced delivery. D’s silent guard duty and receipt of an equal share show intentional participation in the common plan; under BNS §3(5), where a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of common intention, each is liable as if he did it alone. D is therefore jointly liable. E withdrew before participation, made no threat, received no share and did not aid the transaction; mere presence before walking away does not establish common intention. E should be acquitted unless additional evidence proves earlier agreement and a legally relevant act of assistance.

Conclusion. A–D are liable for completed extortion, with the exact aggravated sub-section depending on proof of the offence accusation and its statutory punishment. E is not liable on the stated facts.

Long problem

Q2/2024 — planned jeweller theft, death of chowkidar and theft–extortion distinction

Question
A, B, C and D plan to steal money from a jeweller’s house at night. A, a mechanic, takes a car entrusted to him from his workshop. A and B search the ground floor, C collects things from the first floor and D keeps lookout. N, the chowkidar, raises alarm; D strikes him with a lathi and N dies. B takes the car and absconds. Decide A–D’s liability and distinguish extortion from theft.

Model answer

Initial property offence. A–D have a prior plan and participate in different roles: searching, collecting and lookout. Their common intention under BNS §3(5) can attribute acts done in furtherance of that plan. The jeweller’s property is movable; dishonest movement from the house without consent completes theft. Because it occurs in a building used as a dwelling/custody of property, BNS §305(a) may be attracted if the statutory building fact is proved.

Transformation into robbery. D voluntarily struck N to prevent alarm while the theft was being carried out. Under BNS §309(2), theft becomes robbery when, for committing the theft or carrying away the property, the offender voluntarily causes/attempts death or hurt, wrongful restraint or fear of instant harm. The lathi attack is connected to the theft and therefore supplies the robbery aggravation. Since only four persons are proved, the offence is robbery, not dacoity; the minimum five-person requirement in §310 is absent.

Death. D is individually liable for the homicide caused by the head blow, depending on proof of intention/knowledge under BNS §§100–103. A, B and C are liable for the robbery through their common intention and may additionally share liability for the fatal act only if the prosecution proves that the act was in furtherance of their common intention or that the relevant statutory group-liability rule applies. A plan merely to steal does not automatically prove a common intention to murder, but a planned operation involving resistance and violence can support the prosecution’s argument. The answer must distinguish the clear robbery liability from the fact-sensitive homicide attribution.

Roles and the car. A’s removal of the workshop car is a separate dishonest taking from the workshop’s possession. If A was a clerk/servant and the car was in the employer’s possession, BNS §306 is possible; otherwise it is ordinary §303 theft. B’s subsequent taking of the car and absconding is part of the common plan and may make him jointly liable for the property offences; independent receiving/conversion liability may also arise on different proof. C’s collection and D’s lookout role do not save them from liability: a lookout may intentionally cooperate in the offence.

Theft versus extortion. Theft is secret/unauthorised movement by the offender of movable property out of another’s possession without consent, with dishonest intention. Extortion is fear-induced delivery by the victim of property, valuable security or a signed/sealed convertible instrument. Theft requires no fear; extortion requires fear. Neither requires immediate presence. Robbery adds instant violence/fear to theft or immediate presence, instant fear and then-and-there delivery to extortion. In this problem, the jeweller’s property is taken by movement, so it begins as theft and becomes robbery through D’s violence; there is no extortion because no victim was induced by fear to deliver property.

Conclusion. A–D are jointly liable for the robbery (and aggravated theft/building provision if proved); D has the strongest individual homicide liability. The four-person number excludes dacoity. A may also face a separate theft/clerk-servant theft charge for the workshop car.

Cross-unit PYQ

Q4(a)/2019 — five-person bank robbery, guard injured, C kills V

Question
A, B, C, D and E plan to rob a bank. They collect cash from the cashier at gunpoint. While escaping, B causes grievous injury to the guard with a revolver. Outside, C sees his enemy V at a bus stop and shoots him dead. Discuss all liability.

Model answer

A–E jointly planned and executed a robbery. Because five persons conjointly commit robbery, the completed offence is dacoity under BNS §310(1), punishable under §310(2), in addition to the underlying robbery structure. B’s use of the revolver and grievous injury during the robbery attracts the aggravated weapon/hurt provision in BNS §311 and the hurt-in-robbery provision in §309(6), subject to the exact proof and charge. The other persons can be liable as persons jointly concerned in the robbery; the statute expressly reaches persons jointly concerned where hurt is caused in committing/attempting robbery.

C’s shooting of V requires separate analysis. It occurred after coming out of the bank and was motivated by a personal enemy relationship, not the common bank robbery. Unless the prosecution proves that the shooting was in furtherance of the common intention/common object, A, B, D and E are not automatically liable for C’s private act. C is individually liable for the appropriate homicide offence under the BNS. C remains liable for the dacoity/robbery itself.

Conclusion. All five are liable for dacoity; B additionally attracts aggravated robbery/hurt/weapon liability; C separately faces homicide liability for killing V; the others do not share that separate homicide merely because they were dacoity participants.

Answer-writing frameworks

IRAC for theft

  1. State BNS §303(1)/IPC §378 definition and §303(2)/§379 punishment.
  2. Identify the property as movable under §2(21)/IPC §22.
  3. Identify whose possession it was in; separate possession from ownership.
  4. Prove dishonest intention at the moment of movement.
  5. Apply absence of express/implied consent.
  6. Show movement for the purpose of taking; explain initial movement.
  7. Check temporary deprivation, owner’s property, mistake and later appropriation.
  8. Check §304–§307 aggravations.

IRAC for extortion

  1. Intentional fear.
  2. Injury to victim or another.
  3. Dishonest inducement.
  4. Delivery to any person.
  5. Property/security/convertible signed or sealed instrument.
  6. Classify completed or preparatory conduct under §308(2)–(7).
  7. Check whether presence + instant fear + immediate delivery upgrades it to robbery.

IRAC for robbery/dacoity

  1. Choose theft route or extortion route.
  2. For theft: link voluntary harm/fear to committing/carrying away.
  3. For extortion: prove offender present, instant fear and then-and-there delivery.
  4. Apply §309(4) punishment, §309(5) attempt or §309(6) hurt.
  5. Count participants; test conjointness.
  6. If 5+: §310 dacoity; apply murder/preparation/assembly/gang aggravations.
  7. Check deadly weapon/grievous hurt under §311 or weapon at attempt under §312.

Last-minute revision sheet

THEFT

D-M-P-C-M: Dishonest intention · Movable property · out of another’s Possession · without Consent · Movement. Temporary deprivation is enough.

EXTORTION

F-D-D: Fear of injury · Dishonest inducement · Delivery of property/security/instrument. Fear may concern another person and need not be instant.

ROBBERY

Theft + instant violence/fear, or extortion + offender present + instant fear + immediate delivery.

DACOITY

Robbery/attempt + 5 or more + conjoint action. Four people cannot commit dacoity.

PYARE LAL

Temporary removal of government file = theft where dishonest loss of departmental possession is proved. Return later is no defence.

BASAVEGOWDA

Threat-induced delivery of wife’s personal ornaments = extortion; credible victim evidence plus corroboration can suffice; threat to kill is not automatically attempt murder.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Writing “theft requires permanent taking” or “the offender must profit.” It does not.
  • Confusing ownership with possession; an owner may steal his own property from another’s lawful possession.
  • Calling every threat-based payment robbery. Extortion becomes robbery only with presence, instant fear and then-and-there delivery.
  • Calling every group robbery dacoity without proving five or more persons acting conjointly.
  • Forgetting BNS §304 snatching and the special BNS community-service proviso for first low-value theft.
  • Forgetting that §307 punishes preparation for violent theft even before robbery is complete.
  • Applying §311’s minimum seven years without first proving robbery/dacoity and the weapon/hurt/attempt condition.
  • Assuming hostile witnesses automatically acquit the accused; Basavegowda requires evaluation of the whole evidentiary chain.
  • Treating a later change of mind as undoing theft where dishonest movement already occurred.
