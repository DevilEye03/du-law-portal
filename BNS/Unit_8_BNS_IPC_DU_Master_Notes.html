02

Foundational concepts and vocabulary

Child and minority

BNS §2(3): “child” means any person below 18 years. BNS therefore uses a single, gender-neutral threshold in §137(1)(b). The IPC wording in §361 used the historic formulation—male below 16, female below 18, or a person of unsound mind. This difference must be stated in a BNS answer.

Lawful guardian and “keeping”

The BNS explanation includes any person lawfully entrusted with care or custody. “Keeping” is not limited to physical possession at every moment; the DU material explains it through charge, protection, maintenance and control. A child may remain in the lawful keeping of a parent even when temporarily placed with a relative.

Consent

Under kidnapping from lawful guardianship, the legally material consent is the guardian’s consent, not the child’s. In trafficking, BNS §143(1), Explanation 2 expressly makes the victim’s consent immaterial. BNS §28 separately explains when purported consent is vitiated by fear, misconception, incapacity or age.

Actus reus and mens rea

Kidnapping from lawful guardianship is built around the act of taking or enticing; force, fraud and the kidnapper’s ultimate purpose are not ingredients of the basic offence. Abduction requires force or deceitful means and becomes punishable through an accompanying intent. Trafficking requires exploitation-purpose plus a listed act and listed improper means.

The four-question kidnapping test

Q1 · PersonChild below 18 / unsound mind?
Q2 · ActTaking or enticing?
Q3 · CustodyOut of lawful guardian’s keeping?
Q4 · ConsentWithout guardian’s consent?
Exam discriminator: If Q2 is “no” because the child independently left and the accused merely allowed or assisted the child afterwards, Varadarajan points towards acquittal. If an earlier promise, allurement, shelter-offer, message, persuasion or other active participation formed or influenced the child’s decision, Vadgama, Raja Ram and Anversinh support liability.

Kidnapping — BNS §137 / IPC §§359–363

Statutory architecture

§137(1)(a)

From India

Convey any person beyond India’s limits without that person’s consent or the consent of someone legally authorised to consent for that person.

§137(1)(b)

From lawful guardianship

Take or entice a child or person of unsound mind out of the lawful guardian’s keeping without the guardian’s consent.

§137(2)

Punishment

Imprisonment of either description up to seven years and fine. The BNSS Schedule classifies it as cognizable and bailable.

§137(1)(a): kidnapping from India

  1. Conveyance: a physical act of taking/carrying/transporting.
  2. Territorial threshold: the person must be conveyed beyond the limits of India; the offence is complete on crossing the territorial limit, not only on reaching the intended foreign destination.
  3. Absence of valid consent: for a competent major, the person’s own consent is relevant; for a child or person unable to consent, the legally authorised person’s consent is relevant.
  4. Any person: unlike lawful-guardianship kidnapping, the provision is not confined to a child.

§137(1)(b): kidnapping from lawful guardianship

1 · Child / unsound mindBelow 18 under BNS; or person of unsound mind.
2 · Taking / enticingPhysical removal, escorting, getting into possession, persuasion or subtle allurement.
3 · Lawful keepingCharge, protection, maintenance and control of lawful guardian.
4 · No guardian consentChild’s consent does not cure the offence.

“Taking”

It may mean causing the child to go, escorting the child or getting into possession of the child. It does not necessarily require force or fraud. But some active role must be proved: mere passive permission or allowing the child to accompany the accused is not automatically “taking”.

“Enticing”

Inducement or allurement that gives rise to hope or desire. It may be a promise, offer of permanent shelter, marriage promise, gifts, messages, emotional manipulation or a gradual influence. It need not be immediately before the child leaves.

Guardian’s keeping

“Keeping” is broader than physical custody. A father may remain the lawful guardian even if the child is temporarily with a relative. In a divorced-parent problem, identify actual lawful custody/keeping, any custody order and who exercised charge and control on the facts.

Exception

The lawful-guardianship clause does not extend to a person who in good faith believes himself to be the father of an illegitimate child or believes himself entitled to lawful custody, unless the act is for an immoral or unlawful purpose.

Taking, enticing and allowing — the decisive distinction

ConductLegal effectLeading authority
Child independently leaves; accused does not solicit, persuade, arrange or influence departure; accused merely accompanies/allows or later shelters.May fall short of “taking”; prove the statutory act, not merely the child’s presence with the accused.S. Varadarajan

IPC bridge

IPC §359 classified kidnapping into kidnapping from India and kidnapping from lawful guardianship; §360 and §361 supplied the definitions; §363 supplied punishment. The BNS consolidates the definition and punishment in §137 and replaces the historic gendered “minor” formulation with “child” below 18 years. The old IPC distinction between a male below 16 and a female below 18 should be stated only as a comparison—not as the current BNS rule.

Base offence versus aggravated offence. If the facts show kidnapping/abduction with an additional purpose—compelling marriage or illicit intercourse, murder, ransom, secret confinement, grievous hurt, slavery or unnatural lust—cite the relevant aggravated provision as well: BNS §87 (closest to IPC §366) and BNS §140(1)–(4) (closest to IPC §§364, 364A, 365 and 367).

Abduction — BNS §138 / IPC §362

Statutory definition: whoever, by force compels, or by any deceitful means induces, any person to go from any place, is said to abduct that person.

Any personNo age or guardian requirement.
Force or deceitful meansForceful compulsion or misleading representation.
Going from a placeMovement is the immediate object.
Further intentAbduction becomes punishable through the accompanying offence.

Force

The DU IPC capsule explains force as actual force rather than a bare abstract threat; analyse the facts for compulsion and causal connection to the person’s going.

Deceitful means

A misleading statement or false representation used to induce the person to leave. It is the intention and the causal deception that matter, not merely that the accused was present.

Not a complete standalone offence

Abduction is an auxiliary act. Under the IPC/BNS scheme it becomes punishable when accompanied by a specified intention, such as murder, ransom, secret confinement, marriage/illicit intercourse, grievous hurt or slavery.

Continuing movement

The DU material describes abduction as continuing while the abducted person is removed from one place to another. Always identify the substantive provision that supplies punishment.

Kidnapping versus abduction

Trafficking — BNS §143 / IPC §370

Trafficking is not merely movement. It is an exploitation-purpose offence built around a person being recruited, transported, harboured, transferred or received through one of the listed coercive, deceptive or abusive means.

Trafficking = exploitation purpose + listed act + listed means; victim consent is immaterial

§143(1): element-by-element

A · PurposeFor the purpose of exploitation.
B · ConductRecruit, transport, harbour, transfer or receive.
C · MeansThreat, force/coercion, abduction, fraud/deception, abuse of power or inducement/payment/benefit.
D · ExploitationPhysical/sexual exploitation, slavery-like practice, servitude, begging or forced organ removal.

What the prosecution must connect

  1. Exploitation-purpose: the purpose must be the object of the transaction or movement; ordinary lawful recruitment or transport is not trafficking merely because a person later suffers hardship.
  2. One statutory act is enough in the alternative: recruitment, transport, harbouring, transfer or receipt. The provision is deliberately chain-oriented; different actors may occupy different points in the chain.
  3. One listed means: threats, force or other coercion, abduction, fraud/deception, abuse of power, or inducement including giving or receiving payments/benefits to secure the consent of a person controlling the recruited/transported/harboured/transferred/received person.
  4. Consent is immaterial: consent cannot be used to defeat the offence where the statutory exploitation and means are established.

§143(2)–(7): enhanced punishment ladder

Consent and agency point. Explanation 2 is a statutory rule for trafficking. Even where a guardian, controller or adult purports to consent in return for money or benefit, the prosecution may rely on the listed inducement/benefit limb and the exploitation-purpose. Analyse who had control over the person and how the benefit operated.

Trafficking compared with kidnapping and smuggling

Kidnapping

Protective custody/territorial movement offence. It can be complete without exploitation-purpose or force. The child’s consent does not cure unlawful removal from the guardian.

Trafficking

Exploitation-chain offence. It can exist even without a classic kidnapping from parental custody and can cover recruitment, harbouring, receipt or transfer.

Smuggling idea

Do not equate every cross-border movement with trafficking. The BNS §143 ingredients—exploitation purpose, a listed act and a listed means—must be identified.

Linked BNS provision

§144 punishes knowingly/with reason to believe engaging a trafficked child for sexual exploitation (5–10 years + fine) and a trafficked person generally (3–7 years + fine).

Specific offences against children

BNS §95 — hiring, employing or engaging a child to commit an offence

Statutory ingredients

  1. The victim/participant used is a child—below 18 under BNS §2(3).
  2. The accused hires, employs or engages the child.
  3. The object is to make the child commit an offence.
  4. If the offence is actually committed, the accused is also punished with the punishment for that offence as if it had been committed by that person himself.

Mens rea and scope

The verbs and purpose clause require a purposeful deployment of the child as the instrument/participant of criminal conduct. The Explanation expressly covers hiring, employing, engaging or using a child for sexual exploitation or pornography. Apply other special laws where facts attract them.

Dual consequence: §95 is not limited to the 3–10 year punishment. It creates an independent liability for using the child and adds the punishment for the underlying offence if that offence is committed. Do not merge the two in a problem answer.
Recruitment relationshipHire / employ / engage / use
Protected personChild below 18
Criminal purposeCommit an offence
Result3–10 years + fine; if completed, underlying punishment too

IPC bridge: the comparative table marks BNS §95 as a new section. On IPC facts, analogous liability might have to be built through abetment (§§107–109), conspiracy or the substantive offence; none is a word-for-word equivalent to §95.

BNS §98 / IPC §372 — selling child for prostitution, illicit intercourse, etc.

Actus reus

Sell, let to hire or otherwise dispose of a child. “Dispose of” is broad enough to capture transfer of control or possession beyond a formal sale.

Mens rea alternative

Either intent that the child will at any age be used for prostitution, illicit intercourse or another unlawful/immoral purpose, or knowledge/reason to know that such use is likely.

Presumption

Where a female under 18 is sold, let for hire or disposed of to a prostitute or a person keeping/managing a brothel, the person disposing of her is presumed to have intended prostitution, unless the contrary is proved.

Punishment

Imprisonment of either description up to 10 years and fine. It is cognizable, non-bailable and triable by the Court of Session under the BNSS Schedule.

“Illicit intercourse”

For §§98–99, it means sexual intercourse between persons not united by marriage or by a personal-law/customary union or tie recognised as a quasi-marital relationship, including the cross-community formulation in Explanation 2.

BNS §99 / IPC §373 — buying child for prostitution, illicit intercourse, etc.

Actus reus

Buy, hire or otherwise obtain possession of any child.

Purpose/knowledge

Intent that the child be used at any age for prostitution, illicit intercourse or unlawful/immoral purpose, or knowledge/reason to believe such use is likely.

Presumption

A prostitute or a person keeping/managing a brothel who obtains possession of a female under 18 is presumed to have done so with intent that she be used for prostitution, unless rebutted.

Punishment

Imprisonment of either description not less than 7 years and up to 14 years, and fine. It is cognizable, non-bailable and triable by the Court of Session.

BNS §152 — act endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India

BNS §152 is located in the BNS chapter on offences against the State. It is a new text in the uploaded comparative table. Its constitutional centre of gravity is the balance between national security/public order and Article 19(1)(a) freedom of speech and expression.

Statutory anatomy

Mental elementPurposefully or knowingly
Communication/conductWords, signs, visible representation, electronic communication, financial means or otherwise
Prohibited tendency/objectSecession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist feelings, or danger to sovereignty/unity/integrity
PunishmentLife or up to 7 years + fine

Ingredients to write in an answer

  1. Mens rea: the act must be purposeful or knowing. Do not reduce the section to “any criticism of the government”.
  2. Mode of expression: spoken/written words, signs, visible representation, electronic communication, financial means or any other means.
  3. Substantive national-security object: the conduct must excite/attempt to excite secession, armed rebellion or subversive activities; encourage separatist activities; endanger sovereignty, unity or integrity; or indulge in/commit such acts.
  4. Causal and contextual assessment: meaning, audience, context, intended effect, tendency, proximity and the presence/absence of incitement matter. A vague or hostile opinion cannot be mechanically equated with the statutory harm.
  5. Explanation: disapprobation of governmental measures or administrative action, with a view to obtaining alteration by lawful means, is excluded if it does not excite or attempt to excite the listed activities.
Within the protective explanation
“The policy is unjust; repeal it through constitutional and lawful means.” Evidence of criticism/disapproval without secession, armed rebellion, separatist mobilisation or subversive incitement.
Potential §152 case
Knowingly using electronic messages or funding to mobilise armed rebellion, separatist activity or secession, with the required purpose/tendency and factual nexus.

IPC comparison

The uploaded BNS–IPC comparative table records IPC §124A sedition as deleted and marks BNS §152 as a new provision. Therefore, use this formulation: “No direct IPC equivalent; the closest historical speech offence was §124A, but §152 has distinct words and triggers.” IPC §§153A, 505 and other provisions may also arise on particular facts, but they are not automatic substitutes.

Procedural position

The BNSS Schedule classifies §152 as cognizable, non-bailable and triable by the Court of Session. The punishment is life imprisonment or imprisonment up to seven years and fine.

Constitutional method. Read §152 with Article 19(1)(a) and the reasonable-restriction framework under Article 19(2). The court must separate strong criticism, satire or advocacy for lawful reform from purposeful/knowing incitement to secession, armed rebellion, subversive/separatist activities or danger to sovereignty, unity and integrity. Vinod Dua is especially useful for this method, while its direct holding concerned IPC §§124A/505.

Detailed case law

Each brief follows the DU exam order: facts → procedural history → issues → rival arguments → decision → principle → BNS application. Names are standardised as S. Varadarajan and Thakorlal D. Vadgama, though the uploaded outline contains spelling variants.

S. Varadarajan v. State of Madras

AIR 1965 SC 942 · IPC §361/§363 · BNS §137(1)(b)/§137(2) conceptual equivalent

Facts

Savitri, a second-year B.Sc. student and daughter of S. Natarajan, lived in the neighbouring house of the appellant, Varadarajan. She became friendly with him and expressed that she wanted to marry him. Her father, after learning of the relationship, placed her at the house of a relative, K. Natarajan, to keep her away from the appellant. The next morning Savitri herself telephoned Varadarajan, selected a meeting place and went there. She entered his car; they went to obtain a witness and then to the Registrar’s office, where their agreement to marry was registered. They subsequently stayed at different places and were eventually traced by police investigating the father’s kidnapping complaint.

Procedural history

The Presidency Magistrate convicted Varadarajan under IPC §363 and sentenced him to rigorous imprisonment for one year. The Madras High Court affirmed. The Supreme Court heard the appeal by special leave.

Issue

Whether Varadarajan had “taken” or “enticed” Savitri out of the keeping of her lawful guardian, although she was a minor and the guardian had not consented.

Arguments
  • Appellant/defence: Savitri left voluntarily; she called him, chose the meeting point, insisted on marriage and entered the car on her own. There was no threat, blandishment, prior solicitation or active part in her leaving.
  • Prosecution/State: Savitri was still a minor and remained in her father’s lawful keeping even though temporarily left with a relative; the father had not consented; taking her away and keeping her with the appellant satisfied the offence. Her own consent was not legally material.
Decision

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal and acquitted Varadarajan. The prosecution proved minority, lawful keeping and absence of guardian consent, but failed to prove the essential first element—taking or enticing.

Ratio / principle: “Taking” and “allowing” are not synonymous. Where a mature, educated minor voluntarily leaves the guardian’s protection, calls the accused, chooses the meeting and joins him without evidence of inducement or active participation in forming her intention, the accused cannot be said to have taken her away merely because he allowed her to accompany him or helped her not to return. Earlier solicitation or persuasion, even if not immediately before departure, would be sufficient if proved.

Hypothetical visual, not a reproduction of the evidence. Use it to remember the missing link: active inducement or participation in forming the intention.

BNS application: Under BNS §137, the threshold is now “child below 18”. The reasoning on the act of taking/enticing remains useful, but the BNS age definition is gender-neutral.

Thakorlal D. Vadgama v. State of Gujarat

AIR 1973 SC 2313 · IPC §§361, 366 and 376 · BNS §137 plus aggravated BNS §87

Facts

Vadgama, an industrialist with a factory and adjoining bungalow, became close to Mohini, a school-going girl who had entered her sixteenth year. Over a prolonged period he took her and her family on trips, gave gifts including a Parker pen and cultivated intimacy. Mohini’s parents became suspicious and attempted to prevent contact. Mohini wrote letters complaining of rebukes and ill-treatment. Vadgama learned of her emotional state and, a few days before 16 January 1967, told her to come to his bungalow and that he would keep her permanently. She left home with schoolbooks and went to him. He kept her in the garage/attached rooms, had letters written to create a false trail, attempted to mislead parents/police and concealed her. She was recovered when police traced her.

Procedural history

The Sessions Court convicted him under IPC §§366 and 376. The Gujarat High Court acquitted him of rape under §376 but maintained the §366 conviction. He appealed to the Supreme Court by special leave.

Issues
  1. Can the accused be guilty of kidnapping/inducing a minor even if she physically left her parent’s house on her own?
  2. Did the circumstances establish the additional intention to compel marriage or seduce/force illicit intercourse for IPC §366?
Arguments
  • Appellant/defence: Mohini came voluntarily; the family’s story was false or motivated by the alleged refusal to appoint her father as manager; the letters showed that she wanted to leave; there was no force or fraud in the departure; the prosecution evidence of sexual intercourse was unsafe for lack of corroboration; Varadarajan showed that voluntary departure is not kidnapping.
  • Prosecution/State: The relationship, gifts, prior conduct, promise of permanent shelter, her immature state, concealment in the garage, dictated letters and efforts to mislead the police showed a continuous course of allurement and active participation. The earlier influence need not be immediately before the departure. The circumstances established the §366 purpose even if the rape charge was not proved beyond reasonable doubt.
Decision

The appeal was dismissed. The Supreme Court upheld the conviction under IPC §366. It did not disturb the acquittal under §376 because there was no appeal against that acquittal and the High Court had found the rape charge not proved beyond reasonable doubt.

Ratio / principle: “Taking” means causing to go, escorting or getting into possession; it need not use force or fraud. “Enticing” involves inducement/allurement that creates hope or desire. It can be subtle, gradual and earlier in time. If the minor’s departure was influenced by a promise, offer or inducement emanating from the accused, the accused cannot escape by saying that the child walked out voluntarily. The case is distinguishable from Varadarajan because the evidence showed prior allurement and participation in forming the child’s resolve.

Hypothetical visual of the legal reasoning: the chain is cumulative; the promise need not be made at the exact moment of departure.

DU exam line: “If the minor leaves her parental home influenced by any promise, offer or inducement emanating from the accused, the accused is guilty even though the enticement was not immediate prior.”

Anversinh alias Kiransinh Fatesinh Zala v. State of Gujarat

(2021) 3 SCC 12 · IPC §§363, 366 and 376 · BNS §137(2) and §87 mapping

Facts

On 14 May 1998, the complainant-father learned that his sixteen-year-old daughter, a domestic worker educated up to Class VII, had not returned. A watchman had last seen her coming out of a vacant bungalow with Anversinh. The appellant had gone to his village with her. Police located both at a farm near Modasa and brought them back. The prosecutrix said she was forcibly taken in an auto-rickshaw and bus, pressured into marriage and sexually assaulted. In cross-examination, however, she admitted that she was in love with the appellant, had earlier consensual intercourse with him and had met him outside home. The investigating officer’s statement under CrPC §161 recorded a pre-existing relationship, their leaving after being seen by a guard, farm work and cohabitation as husband and wife.

Procedural history

The Sessions Court convicted the appellant under IPC §§363, 366 and 376. The High Court set aside the rape conviction but sustained kidnapping convictions. Before the Supreme Court he challenged the kidnapping findings and relied on the consensual relationship and Varadarajan.

Issues
  1. Does a love affair or the prosecutrix’s voluntary consent defeat kidnapping of a girl below 18?
  2. Was there evidence of the accused’s active role in taking/enticing her out of parental custody?
  3. How should the lapse of time and the young age of the accused affect sentence after guilt is found?
Arguments
  • Appellant: the High Court accepted the love affair, frequent meetings and consensual sexual relationship while acquitting him of rape; it was contradictory to find that she did not willingly leave parental custody. The prosecutrix did not protest or raise alarm despite opportunities. Under Varadarajan, without active involvement there was no taking or enticing.
  • State: the girl was below 18; her consent could not be a defence. The concurrent findings, recovery from the appellant’s custody and evidence that he induced her to reach a designated place supported the statutory ingredients. The plain language of IPC §§361/366 controlled.
Decision

The Supreme Court found the kidnapping conviction sustainable. Enticement need not be direct or immediate and may operate through subtle acts such as winning the affection of a minor. Mere recovery from a stranger’s custody would not itself prove kidnapping, but here the evidence demonstrated an active role and inducement. The Court upheld guilt under the kidnapping provisions and did not grant acquittal. It nevertheless reduced the sentence to the period already undergone, considering no force or weapon, the appellant’s age (about 18–19), the love affair, long delay of more than 22 years, rehabilitation, low recidivism risk and absence of grotesque abuse of power.

Ratio / principle: Consent of a minor below the statutory age is no defence to kidnapping. But prosecution must still prove taking/enticement; mere recovery is not ipso facto enough. Guilt and sentence are distinct inquiries: youth, delay, rehabilitation and proportionality may reduce sentence without erasing conviction.

Hypothetical exam visual: first prove the act of taking/enticement; only then discuss the effect of consent and sentence.

BNS application: For a post-BNS problem, “child” is below 18 for both sexes. The case is useful for the proposition that voluntary love or consent does not by itself defeat §137, but the prosecution still carries the burden on taking/enticement.

Vinod Dua v. Union of India

AIR 2021 SC 3239 · W.P. (Cr.) No. 154 of 2020 · IPC §§124A, 268, 501, 505 · bridge to BNS §152

Facts

Journalist Vinod Dua uploaded an episode of The Vinod Dua Show on YouTube on 30 March 2020, critically discussing the government’s preparedness and handling of the COVID-19 pandemic, testing facilities, PPE and masks, export of ventilators/sanitiser, migrant labour and the lockdown. The complainant alleged that the programme spread false and malicious news, accused the Prime Minister of using deaths/terror attacks to garner votes, incited violence, created panic and disturbed public tranquillity.

Proceedings and reliefs sought

FIR No. 0053 dated 6 May 2020 at Kumarsain Police Station, Himachal Pradesh invoked IPC §§124A (sedition), 268 (public nuisance), 501 and 505. Dua filed an Article 32 petition seeking quashing of the FIR and a direction that FIRs against media persons with at least ten years’ standing not be registered without clearance by a committee. The Court had earlier protected him from arrest and permitted video-conference cooperation during investigation.

Issues
  1. Whether the allegations, read with the video in its entirety, made out sedition or allied offences, or were protected critical analysis under Article 19(1)(a).
  2. Whether a special committee/preliminary clearance regime for FIRs against experienced journalists could be judicially created.
Arguments
  • Petitioner: the video was critical analysis of government functioning and did not incite violence or public disorder; the FIR attributed statements he had not made; §§124A and 505 had to be read according to Kedar Nath Singh; the prosecution abused criminal process and violated free speech. A committee safeguard was sought by analogy with professional-negligence cases.
  • State/Union/complainant: the FIR should not be quashed at the investigation stage; the statements were misinformation likely to create panic during a pandemic; food-riot fears and migrant reporting could disturb public order; whether statements were deliberate or innocent required investigation. The proposed committee was said to encroach on the legislative field.
Decision

The Supreme Court quashed the FIR as an abuse of process. It reiterated from Kedar Nath Singh that a citizen may criticise or comment on government measures and functionaries so long as there is no incitement to violence or intention to create public disorder; only words with pernicious tendency or intention to create public disorder/disturbance of law and order attract §§124A/505. It rejected the requested ten-year-journalist committee as an impermissible judicial encroachment into the legislative domain.

Ratio / exam use: Criminal speech restrictions must be read narrowly and in conformity with Article 19(1)(a). Mere disapprobation or critical appraisal is not sedition. The case directly concerns IPC §124A/§505, not BNS §152. For §152, use the judgment as a constitutional method: identify the specific statutory national-security trigger and prove purposeful/knowing incitement or endangerment, while respecting the explanation’s protection of lawful disapprobation.

Hypothetical analytical visual; the actual case arose under IPC speech offences.

One-line distinction: “Dua protects criticism from IPC sedition; it does not grant immunity for purposeful/knowing BNS §152 conduct satisfying the new national-security ingredients.”

DU-listed comparator: State of Haryana v. Raja Ram

(1973) 1 SCC 544 · included because the uploaded DU syllabus/IPC capsule lists it under lawful-guardianship kidnapping

Facts and issue

Santosh Rani, aged about 14, was allegedly promised by Jai Narain that he would keep her like a queen with clothes, food and a servant. After her father prevented Jai Narain from visiting, Jai Narain sent messages through Raja Ram. Raja Ram persuaded Santosh to go to his house, brought Jai Narain and handed her over. The issue was whether Raja Ram could be convicted even though he did not personally go to the parental house to bring her.

Decision and principle

The Supreme Court restored the conviction. The child’s consent or willingness was immaterial; guardian’s consent was the relevant consent. It was not necessary to prove force or fraud. Raja Ram actively participated in forming Santosh’s intention to leave, and that was enough. A messenger or intermediary can therefore be liable when his persuasion materially contributes to the child’s removal.

Contrast map: Varadarajan = voluntary departure with no proved inducement; Raja Ram = active persuasion, even through an intermediary; Vadgama = earlier and subtle allurement; Anversinh = consent/love cannot defeat proved active removal.

Long answer

Q1/2024 — Aadya, Vijay and divorced parents

Question
Aadya, aged 17, lived with her mother. Her parents were divorced. Her father visited at weekends and disliked her closeness with Vijay, aged 20. Vijay and Aadya decided to marry and eloped from her father’s house before her 18th birthday. The father filed an FIR. Decide whether Vijay is guilty of kidnapping. Would the answer differ if the parents lived together?

Model answer

Issue. Whether Vijay took or enticed a child below 18 out of the keeping of a lawful guardian without that guardian’s consent, contrary to BNS §§137(1)(b) and 137(2).

Rule. BNS §2(3) makes every person below 18 a child. Section 137(1)(b) requires: (i) taking or enticing; (ii) a child/person of unsound mind; (iii) out of the keeping of the lawful guardian; and (iv) absence of guardian consent. The child’s consent or willingness does not by itself cure the offence. Under Varadarajan, mere allowing after a child independently leaves is not taking. Under Vadgama, an earlier promise or inducement that influenced the decision is enticement even if not immediately prior. Anversinh confirms that a minor’s love/consent is no defence once active removal/enticement is proved.

Application. Aadya is 17 and therefore a child. Vijay is 20 and the father did not consent. Divorce does not automatically remove a biological parent from the category of lawful guardian; the more precise question is whose keeping—charge, protection and control—existed at the time. Because Aadya left from the father’s house and the father had regular weekend custody, the prosecution has a plausible case that she was in his keeping. If a custody order or facts show that the mother alone had lawful custody and the father had no control, this element would require closer proof.

The decisive question is taking/enticement. If “Vijay and Aadya decided to marry” means Vijay participated in forming the plan, arranged the elopement, induced her, promised shelter/marriage or actively took her from the father’s house, the case falls within Vadgama/Raja Ram and Vijay is guilty; her consent to marriage is immaterial. If the evidence merely shows that Aadya, independently and with a mature decision, left and Vijay only allowed her to accompany him—as in Varadarajan—the prosecution fails on the first ingredient and he must be acquitted. The phrase “decided to marry and eloped together” is stronger than mere passive allowing, so on a full-proof reading the likely conclusion is conviction, subject to proof of Vijay’s active role beyond reasonable doubt.

Divorce variation. If parents lived together, the father’s lawful keeping and lack of consent would be even clearer. The result would not differ in legal principle: active taking/enticement remains necessary, and the child’s consent remains immaterial. Divorce changes the factual custody inquiry, not the statutory test.

Conclusion. Vijay should be convicted under BNS §137(1)(b) read with §137(2) if the prosecution proves active participation/inducement in the elopement; otherwise, under Varadarajan, he should be acquitted of basic kidnapping. On the stated “joint decision and elopement” facts, conviction is the better exam conclusion, with the caveat that criminal guilt cannot rest on Aadya’s presence with Vijay alone.

Problem question

Q2(b)/2023 — S, P and N

Question
S, aged 21, enticed P, aged 15 years 6 months, out of lawful guardianship and took her to Delhi, where he forced her to work as domestic help. She escaped and met N, who sympathised, took her home to contact and locate her parents. Police recovered P from N’s house. Decide liability of S and N with case law.

Model answer

S. P is a child below 18 under BNS §2(3). S actively enticed her and removed her from the lawful guardian’s keeping without consent. The elements of BNS §137(1)(b) are complete; S is punishable under §137(2). Her age makes her consent immaterial. The facts may also support other offences depending on proof: forcing her to work may raise BNS §146 (unlawful compulsory labour), and exploitation/transport for an exploitative purpose may require analysis under §143. Do not automatically label every kidnapping as trafficking; prove the §143 exploitation-purpose and listed means separately. Vadgama supports liability because persuasion/inducement need not be immediate. Raja Ram confirms that active persuasion is enough and force/fraud is unnecessary. S is therefore liable at least under §137(2), with related charges subject to proof.

N. N learned P’s ordeal and took her to his home to contact and locate her parents. BNS §142 (corresponding to IPC §368) punishes a person who, knowing that someone has been kidnapped/abducted, wrongfully conceals or confines that person, with punishment as if he had kidnapped/abducted with the same intention or purpose. N’s conduct was protective, temporary and directed to restoration/contacting parents; there is no wrongful concealment or confinement. Mere recovery from a stranger’s house is not ipso facto proof of kidnapping—an important lesson from Anversinh. On these facts N is not liable under §142 or §137. If evidence showed that N knowingly kept P hidden, exploited her or prevented contact with her lawful guardian, the conclusion could change.

Conclusion. S is guilty of kidnapping from lawful guardianship and may face additional liability if forced labour/trafficking ingredients are independently proved. N is acquitted because bona fide rescue and efforts to locate the parents lack wrongful concealment or confinement.

25 marks

Q5/2020 — 17-year-old girl asks the boy to take her away

Question
A 17-year-old girl lived with her parents and developed intimacy with a 20-year-old grocery-shop helper. She left home on her own, reached his shop, persuaded him to take her to another town so they could marry and live together. He took her to several places and was arrested for kidnapping. Should he be convicted?

Model answer

Law. The relevant rule is BNS §137(1)(b), corresponding to IPC §361, with punishment under §137(2)/IPC §363. Minority, lawful keeping and lack of guardian consent are insufficient by themselves; prosecution must prove that the accused took or enticed the child. The child’s consent is not a defence, but it remains necessary to examine whether the accused actively caused the departure. Varadarajan says mere allowing is not taking; Vadgama says earlier inducement/allurement, even subtle or gradual, is enough; Anversinh says love and consent do not defeat a charge where the accused actively induced/removed the child.

Application. The girl was 17 and in parental keeping; the father/mother did not consent. However, on the facts she left home on her own, reached the shop, and persuaded the boy to take her. There is no stated prior promise, threat, message, inducement or participation by him in forming her intention. His subsequent taking to other towns may facilitate her decision, but under Varadarajan facilitation after an independently formed intention is not necessarily taking.

Conclusion. On the facts as given, the boy should be acquitted because the prosecution has not established the act of taking or enticing. This is not because the girl’s consent legally authorises kidnapping; it is because the prosecution has failed to prove the actus reus. If additional evidence showed that he had earlier promised marriage, solicited her departure or deliberately cultivated the plan, Vadgama would reverse the result and support conviction.

Short/long answer

Q2(b)/2018 — Mira and Gopal

Question
Mira, aged 16, lived with her aged father and stepmother who ill-treated her. She narrated her woes to Gopal, aged 22. Gopal suggested that they go to Mumbai and start afresh and promised to marry her. She agreed; they boarded a train. What offence, if any, has Gopal committed?

Model answer

Mira is a child under BNS §2(3), and she was in the lawful keeping of her father/guardian. Gopal’s suggestion to go to Mumbai, his promise of marriage and the planned train journey show active inducement and participation in forming her intention. The fact that she agreed or was unhappy at home does not make the removal lawful. This is not the Varadarajan pattern of independent departure followed by passive permission; it is the Vadgama pattern of allurement creating hope/desire. Gopal is therefore guilty of kidnapping from lawful guardianship under BNS §§137(1)(b) and 137(2) (IPC §§361 and 363). If the prosecution also proves the statutory intent to compel marriage or force/seduce illicit intercourse, the aggravated BNS §87/IPC §366 may additionally apply. The essential answer is kidnapping because Gopal himself suggested the move and promised marriage.

10-step kidnapping/abduction IRAC template

  2. State the age and BNS §2(3) child rule.
  3. Identify the lawful guardian and factual “keeping”.
  4. State the absence/presence of guardian consent.
  5. Separate the child’s consent from guardian consent.
  6. Prove or disprove “taking” through active conduct.

  9. If force/deceit is present, analyse §138 and the aggravated purpose provision.

Last-minute revision sheet

KIDNAP

Child/unsound mind + take/entice + lawful keeping + no guardian consent.
Varadarajan: allowing ≠ taking.
Vadgama/Raja Ram: inducement can be earlier/subtle.
Anversinh: consent no defence if active role proved.

ABDUCT

Any person + force/deceit + make person go.
Not standalone; connect to murder, ransom, confinement, marriage/illicit intercourse, hurt/slavery.

TRAFFIC

Exploitation purpose + chain act + listed means.
Consent immaterial. Child trafficking: minimum 10 years; more than one child: minimum 14 years.

§95

Hire/employ/engage child for offence. 3–10 years + fine; if committed, underlying offence punishment too. New BNS provision.

§98/§99

§98 sells/disposes; §99 buys/obtains. Sexual/unlawful purpose or knowledge. Presumptions are rebuttable.

§152

Purpose/knowledge + specified communication/conduct + secession/armed rebellion/subversive/separatist activity or danger to sovereignty/unity/integrity. Lawful disapprobation protected.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Writing IPC age thresholds as if they were the BNS threshold. Under BNS, a child is below 18.
  • Assuming a child’s consent alone proves kidnapping, or assuming it alone disproves kidnapping. The decisive act is taking/enticement.
  • Calling every transport “trafficking” without identifying exploitation purpose and a listed means.
  • Calling §138 a complete offence without linking abduction to a punishable purpose/provision.
  • Equating BNS §152 with IPC §124A without noting the comparative table’s “deleted/new” distinction.
  • Using Varadarajan mechanically without checking prior promises or subtle influence; using Vadgama without proving that the inducement actually influenced departure.
  • Forgetting that Anversinh separates conviction from sentencing.
  • In a rescue fact pattern, treating a good-faith person like N as a wrongdoer without proving “wrongful” concealment or confinement under BNS §142.
