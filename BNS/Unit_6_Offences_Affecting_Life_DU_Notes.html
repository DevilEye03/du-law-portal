The Architecture — Genus, Species and the Three Degrees

Why the Code defines killing twice, how Punnayya's genus–species–degrees framework organises §§ 100–105/299–304, and the stage-wise method every homicide answer must follow.

1.1  Why two definitions?

The Indian Penal Code (and the Sanhita after it) does what English law does through judge-made manslaughter grades, but does it statutorily: it defines a genus — culpable homicide (§ 100/299) — and then carves out of that genus a graver species — murder (§ 101/300) — with five partial defences (Exceptions 1–5) that reduce murder back to culpable homicide not amounting to murder. The DU syllabus quotes the classic formulation:

"In the scheme of the Penal Code, 'culpable homicide' is the genus and 'murder' is its species. All 'murder' is 'culpable homicide' but not vice-versa. Speaking generally, 'culpable homicide' sans 'special characteristics of murder', is 'culpable homicide not amounting to murder'. For the purpose of fixing punishment, proportionate to the gravity of this generic offence, the Code practically recognises three degrees of culpable homicide. The first is what may be called 'culpable homicide of the first degree'. This is the greatest form of culpable homicide, which is defined in Section 300 as 'murder'. The second may be termed as 'culpable homicide of the second degree'… punishable under the first part of Section 304. Then, there is 'culpable homicide of the third degree'. This is the lowest type of culpable homicide and the punishment provided for it is also the lowest… punishable under the second part of Section 304." — Sarkaria J., State of A.P. v. R. Punnayya (DU CM), ¶ 12
DegreeContentProvisionPunishment

1.2  Punnayya's three-stage method — the answer skeleton

Punnayya ¶ 21 prescribes the sequence every court — and every DU answer — must follow:

  1. Stage 1 — Death: has the accused, by his act (or illegal omission — § 2(1)/s. 8), caused the death of another? (causation; Explanations 1–3; § 102/301 if the wrong person died)
  2. Stage 2 — Culpable homicide: does the act satisfy § 100/299 — intention to cause death (a), or intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death (b), or knowledge of likelihood of death (c)?
  3. Stage 3 — Murder: does the case fall within any of the four clauses of § 101/300? If yes and no Exception applies → murder (§ 103/302). If no, or an Exception applies → CHnAM (§ 105 Part I or II per the governing clause).

"The above are only broad guidelines and not cast-iron imperatives… But sometimes the facts are so intertwined and the second and third stages so telescoped into each other, that it may not be convenient to give a separate treatment to the matters involved." (Punnayya ¶ 22)

Culpable Homicide — the Genus (BNS § 100 / IPC § 299)

The text and its three clauses; the illustrations and explanations; the mens rea–actus reus relationship (Palani Goundan, Thavamani); and § 102/301 — killing the person you did not aim at (Mushnooru).

2.1  The text

BNS § 100 / IPC § 299 — Culpable homicide

Whoever causes death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that he is likely by such act to cause death, commits the offence of culpable homicide.

Illustration (a). A lays sticks and turf over a pit, with the intention of thereby causing death, or with the knowledge that death is likely to be thereby caused. Z, believing the ground to be firm, treads on it, falls in and is killed. A has committed the offence of culpable homicide.

Illustration (b). A knows Z to be behind a bush. B does not know it. A, intending to cause, or knowing it to be likely to cause, Z's death, induces B to fire at the bush. B fires and kills Z. Here B may be guilty of no offence; but A has committed the offence of culpable homicide.

Illustration (c). A, by shooting at a fowl with intent to kill and steal it, kills B, who is behind a bush, A not knowing that he was there. Here, although A was doing an unlawful act, he was not guilty of culpable homicide, as he did not intend to kill B, or to cause death by doing an act that he knew was likely to cause death.

Explanation 1. A person who causes bodily injury to another who is labouring under a disorder, disease or bodily infirmity, and thereby accelerates the death of that other, shall be deemed to have caused his death.

Explanation 2. Where death is caused by bodily injury, the person who causes such bodily injury shall be deemed to have caused the death, although by resorting to proper remedies and skilful treatment the death might have been prevented.

Explanation 3. The causing of the death of a child in the mother's womb is not homicide. But it may amount to culpable homicide to cause the death of a living child, if any part of that child has been brought forth, though the child may not have breathed or been completely born.

The three explanations are DU favourites : Expl. 1 = the egg-shell victim rule (blow to a diseased spleen — the blow-er takes his victim as he finds him); Expl. 2 = the "neglected wounds" rule (death by septicemia from the injuries is the accused's doing even though proper treatment might have saved him — the answer to Dec 2014 Q3(a), PYQ q2); Expl. 3 = the born-alive rule (killing a child in the womb is not homicide; but once "any part has been brought forth" it can be — § 93's exposure and §§ 88–92 govern the unborn).

2.2  Mens rea and actus reus — the "believed-dead" cases

Culpable homicide needs the concurrence of a guilty mind (intention/knowledge) with a guilty act (causing death). The classic Indian teaching cases are those where the accused's first act fells the victim, and his second act — done in the belief that the victim is already dead — actually kills:

CaseFactsHolding
Palani Goundan (1919 Mad)Ploughshare blow renders wife unconscious; husband, believing her dead, hangs her by a rope to stage suicide; death by hangingNot murder, not culpable homicide — the hanging was done "under the impression that she was already dead": intention must stand "in some relation to a person who… is alive, or who is believed by the accused to be alive". Conviction: grievous hurt (s. 326)
In re Thavamani (1943 Mad)Accused 2 held the woman's legs while accused 1 attacked her; believing her dead, both threw the body into a well; she died in the wellMurder — distinguished from Palani Goundan: here there was an intention to cause death from the start; "the two phases of the same transaction are so closely connected in time and purpose that they must be considered as parts of the same transaction"
Queen-Empress v. Khandu (1891 Bom, discussed in both)Three head blows with intent to kill; believing the victim dead, set fire to the hut to destroy evidence; death by burningNot one continuous act with the intention to kill → discussed within both judgments as the "separate transaction" branch
The exam line : where the killing intent operates on the final act — i.e., the accused attacks to kill and then completes the killing while merely mistaken about the earlier act's effect — it is murder (Thavamani: one transaction). Where the first act was only to injure and the second, fatal act is done on a belief that death has already occurred — the second act carries no homicidal mens rea (Palani Goundan) and liability is only for the first act (hurt/grievous hurt) plus any offence on the second (e.g., s. 201 causing disappearance of evidence). The dividing question is what intention existed at the inception.

2.3  § 102 / s. 301 — killing the person you did not aim at

BNS § 102 / IPC § 301 — Culpable homicide by causing death of person other than the person whose death was intended

If a person, by doing anything which he intends or knows to be likely to cause death, commits culpable homicide by causing the death of any person, whose death he neither intends nor knows himself to be likely to cause, the culpable homicide committed by the offender is of the description of which it would have been if he had caused the death of the person whose death he intended or knew himself to be likely to cause.

The "transferred malice" rule, statutised: aim at A, kill B — your guilt is measured by what you intended towards A. § 100's first illustration (the man-trap pit) shows the principle inside the genus; § 102 makes it express and fixes the grade of the offence by the intended victim's measure. Mushnooru is the great Indian authority — the majority (Benson J. and a concurring judge) applying it to the poisoned halva; Sundara Aiyar J. dissenting that the section cannot carry liability beyond the proximate and intended harm.

The three building blocks the 2023 syllabus ties together — "cause and effect":

  1. Causing death (actus reus): the accused's act must be the causa causans, not a mere link — Mushnooru's majority: the accused's act was "the efficient cause" of the girl's death though her own act of picking up the sweetmeat "contributed"; contributory action of the victim or a third person does not exculpate (Illustration (a) — the pit; Illustration (b) — the bush).
  2. Intention/knowledge need not target the actual victim: "It is enough if he 'causes the death' of any one, whether the person intended to be killed or any one else" (Mushnooru, on § 299) — but Illustration (c) marks the outer limit: the fowl-shooter who unknowingly kills B behind the bush does an unlawful act without the homicidal mental element, and is not guilty even of culpable homicide.
  3. Mens rea is judged by supposed circumstances: Palani Goundan (Wallis C.J.) — "the intention of the accused must be judged in the light… of what he supposed to be the circumstances"; a man is guilty of culpable homicide if he intends to kill what he believes to be a living person (even a tree-stump mistaken for a man), and not guilty if his intention was directed only to what he believed to be a corpse.
Mens rea theory hook (Dec 2015 Q1(a)) : the "rationale of punishing strict liability in the absence of guilty mind" question runs on the same rails: the IPC/BNS premise is that every offence defines both actus reus and mens rea (Palani Goundan — Mayne's view quoted by Napier J.: "every offence is defined and the definition states not only what the accused must have done but his state of mind"), with the general-exceptions chapter absorbing English mens rea defences; strict liability survives only in a few regulatory statutes, punished for reasons of public safety regardless of fault.

Murder — the Species (BNS § 101 / IPC § 300)

The four clauses, Punnayya's comparative table, the Virsa Singh four-step algorithm for clause (c)/Thirdly, and the knowledge clause (d)/Fourthly with its "without excuse" gate.

3.1  The text

BNS § 101 / IPC § 300 — Murder

Except in the cases hereinafter excepted, culpable homicide is murder —

(a) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing death; or

(b) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing such bodily injury as the offender knows to be likely to cause the death of the person to whom the harm is caused; or

(c) if the act by which the death is caused is done with the intention of causing bodily injury to any person, and the bodily injury intended to be inflicted is sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; or

(d) if the person committing the act by which the death is caused, knows that it is so imminently dangerous that it must, in all probability, cause death, or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death, and commits such act without any excuse for incurring the risk of causing death or such injury as aforesaid.

IPC's "Firstly…Fourthly" are now (a)–(d); nothing else changed. Note the two knowledge clauses: (b) — knowledge of the particular victim's frailty (the enlarged-spleen illustration (b)); (d) — knowledge of extreme danger to persons in general (the cannon-into-a-crowd illustration (d)).

3.3  Clause (c)/Thirdly — the Virsa Singh algorithm

The most litigated clause in Indian homicide law, and the single most examinable passage in this unit. The defence argument (a "favourite argument" — Bose J.) runs: the section requires the intention to inflict an injury sufficient to cause death — which is to say, an intention to kill — so the prosecution must prove the offender intended the fatal sufficiency. Bose J.'s answer: "the two clauses are disjunctive and separate" — the intention relates only to the injury; the sufficiency is an objective, inferential fact:

"To put it shortly, the prosecution must prove the following facts before it can bring a case under Section 300 'thirdly': First, it must establish, quite objectively, that a bodily injury is present. Secondly, the nature of the injury must be proved… Thirdly, it must be proved that there was an intention to inflict that particular bodily injury, that is to say, that it was not accidental or unintentional, or that some other kind of injury was intended. Once these three elements are proved to be present, the enquiry proceeds further and, Fourthly, it must be proved that the injury of the type just described… is sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature. This part of the enquiry is purely objective and inferential and has nothing to do with the intention of the offender… Once the four elements are established… the offence is murder under Section 300 'thirdly'. It does not matter that there was no intention to cause death.… No one has a licence to run around inflicting injuries that are sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature and claim that they are not guilty of murder." — Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab (DU CM), ¶¶ 14–20 (condensed)

The Five Exceptions to Murder — the Partial Defences

General defences (Chapter IV/§§ 14–44) negate criminality wholly; the Exceptions to § 101/300 reduce murder to culpable homicide — the law's "lenient view… in respect of murders committed on the spur of the moment" (2023 CM). Exception 1 (Nanavati) and Exception 4 (Ghapoo Yadav) are DU's prescribed deep-dives.

4.1  Exception 1 — grave and sudden provocation

Exception 1 to § 101 / s. 300

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, whilst deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation, causes the death of the person who gave the provocation, or causes the death of any other person by mistake or accident: provided that the provocation is not — (a) sought or voluntarily provoked by the offender as an excuse for killing or doing harm to any person; or (b) given by anything done in obedience to the law, or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of the powers of such public servant; or (c) given by anything done in the lawful exercise of the right of private defence.

Explanation. Whether the provocation was grave and sudden enough to prevent the offence from amounting to murder is a question of fact. Illustrations (a)–(f): killing the child of the provoker (a) is murder (wrong target, no accident); a stray shot killing a bystander (b) is only culpable homicide; arrest/bailiff (c), magistrate's words (d), resistance to private defence (e) = murder; and (f) the instigator who arms the provoked man is guilty of murder though the provoked man is not.

Nanavati's four principles (Sarkaria-era; Subba Rao J. in Nanavati itself, ¶ 20) — the exam statement of Indian law:

  1. The reasonable-man test: "The test of 'grave and sudden' provocation is whether a reasonable man, belonging to the same class of society as the accused, placed in the situation in which the accused was placed would be so provoked as to lose his self-control." No abstract standard — "customs, manners, way of life, traditional values… the cultural, social and emotional background of the society to which an accused belongs."
  2. Words and gestures suffice in India: "In India, words and gestures may also, under certain circumstances, cause grave and sudden provocation" — the Code's authors rejected the English mere-words rule: "gross insults by words or gestures have as great a tendency to move many persons to violent passion as dangerous or painful bodily injuries."
  3. The mental background: "The mental background created by the previous act of the victim may be taken into consideration in ascertaining whether the subsequent act caused grave and sudden provocation" (the Khogayi–Boya Munigadu–Balku–Babu Lal line: the "prolonged agony" cases, where the earlier discovery of infidelity and a fresh sight of the paramour or wife reignites passion).
  4. The cooling-off rule: "The fatal blow should be clearly traced to the influence of passion arising from that provocation and not after passion had cooled down by lapse of time, or otherwise giving room and scope for premeditation and calculation."

4.2  Exceptions 2–5

Exception 2 — exceeding private defence

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, in the exercise in good faith of the right of private defence of person or property, exceeds the power given to him by law and causes the death of the person against whom he is exercising such right of defence without premeditation, and without any intention of doing more harm than is necessary for the purpose of such defence.

Illustration: the horsewhip answered with a pistol. Note the double constraint — good faith (s. 52/§ 2(7)) plus no intent to exceed necessity. Full private defence (which negates the offence altogether) is §§ 30–44/ss. 96–106 (DU's Unit 8 in the 2025 material); Exception 2 catches only the honest excess.

Exception 3 — public servant exceeding powers

Culpable homicide is not murder if the offender, being a public servant or aiding a public servant acting for the advancement of public justice, exceeds the powers given to him by law, and causes death by doing an act which he in good faith believes to be lawful and necessary for the due discharge of his duty as such public servant, and without ill-will towards the person whose death is caused.

Exception 4 — sudden fight (Ghapoo Yadav's section)

Culpable homicide is not murder if it is committed without premeditation, in a sudden fight, in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel, and without the offender's having taken undue advantage or acted in a cruel or unusual manner. Explanation. It is immaterial which party offers the provocation or commits the first assault.

Ghapoo Yadav's five ingredients: "(a) without premeditation, (b) in a sudden fight; (c) without the offender's having taken undue advantage or acted in a cruel or unusual manner; and (d) the fight must have been with the person killed… all the ingredients mentioned in it must be found." A "fight" is mutual — "It takes two to make a fight… a combat between two and more persons whether with or without weapons" — distinguished from Exception 1: "in Exception 1 there is total deprivation of self-control, in Exception 4 there is only that heat of passion which clouds men's sober reason and urges them to deeds which they would not otherwise do."

Exception 5 — consent

Culpable homicide is not murder when the person whose death is caused, being above the age of eighteen years, suffers death or takes the risk of death with his own consent. (Illustration: instigating a child's suicide is not covered — the child cannot consent to death; euthanasia's modern law — Common Cause (2018) passive euthanasia — is built against this backdrop.)

5.1  The Mithu story — § 303 to § 104

IPC § 303 mandated death for murder by a person serving life — a mandatory capital sentence with no judicial discretion. In Mithu v. State of Punjab (1983, 3 SCC 273) a Constitution Bench struck it down as unconstitutional: a sentence "depriving the courts of their sane discretion… and refusing to recognize the individuality of the offender" violates Articles 14 and 21; the classification (life-convicts) bore no rational relation to a death-only rule, and a mandatory death penalty cannot be "judge-proof" in a system of individualised sentencing. The BNS now gives § 104 the constitutional form: death, or imprisonment for the remainder of the convict's natural life — the "remainder of natural life" phrase echoing the post-Gopal Vinayak Godse line that a life sentence ordinarily means life unless remitted. § 109(2) mirrors this for the life-convict who attempts to murder.

5.2  § 103(2) — the anti-lynching clause, from Tehseen to text

"…a group of five or more persons acting in concert commits murder on the ground of race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground, each member of such group shall be punished with death or with imprisonment for life, and shall also be liable to fine." — BNS § 103(2), Second Schedule text (emphasis on "each member" and the identity grounds)

The genealogy is Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India (2018) 6 SCC 72: "Mob violence and lynching are an affront to the rule of law and to the collective conscience of the nation… Hyperbolic protests against crime cannot be de facto justice; the victims of mob violence are often persons belonging to weak and marginalised sections." The Court issued preventive–remedial–punitive guidelines and recommended that Parliament create a separate offence of lynching with "adequate and severe punishment" — the Jan Vishwas-era draft anti-lynching bills (Rajasthan/Madhya Pradesh/Jharkhand 2019 state acts) pressed the same point, and § 103(2) is the answer. Its novelties to underline:

  1. Concert of five or more: guilt without the prosecution proving which hand struck the fatal blow — an individualised § 34/§ 149 (BNS § 3(5)/§ 190) principle hard-wired into the definition.
  2. Identity-ground motive: "race, caste or community, sex, place of birth, language, personal belief or any other similar ground" — hate as an element of murder, echoing §§ 196–197 BNS (promoting enmity) and the Court's direction in Tehseen that incitement (153A IPC/§ 196 BNS) must be punished on parity with the lynching itself.
  3. Each member: the section drops the "in furtherance of common object" machinery — membership of the concert is enough.
Where the death penalty stands (bridge to Unit 7's syllabus) : § 103(1)/302 retains "death or life". The choice is governed by Bachan Singh (1980) — death only in the "rarest of rare" cases, with special reasons (s. 354(3) CrPC/§ 242 BNS judgment) and the mitigating circumstances of Manoj v. State of M.P. (2022). Machhi Singh's five categories (manner, motive, anti-social nature, magnitude, victim's identity) are the shorthand — but note for the exam that the DU paper asks you to argue, not recite: the syllabus's critical questions ("morality and the machinery of death") anticipate the abolitionist line.

6.1  Rawalpenta Venkalu v. State of Hyderabad

AIR 1936 Mad 706 DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 1

Facts

A village faction-feud in the erstwhile Hyderabad State. The accused Rawalpenta Venkalu and his brother Venkataswami, armed, lay in wait for their enemy Balaiah, shut him up in a hut, set fire to the hut, and when the victim tried to break out, repelled his rescue by threatening and beating back the persons who attempted to save him. Balaiah died of burns. There was also a co-accused, G. Kistayya, alleged to have been present and acting in concert; the case against him rested on a retracted confession and on the testimony of the eyewitnesses, who were themselves partisans of the deceased's faction. The Sessions Court convicted Venkalu and Venkataswami of murder; Kistayya's conviction rested on the weaker material.

Issues

  • Does § 300/Firstly-type "intention to cause death" require direct proof of intention, or can it be inferred from the deliberate design of the act?
  • Where several persons act in concert to burn a man alive, is each responsible for the death although the actual act of setting fire cannot be attributed to each individually?
  • Can a retracted confession, plus interested eyewitnesses, sustain a conviction against the co-accused?

Arguments

For the appellants: no one saw precisely which accused applied the fire; the eyewitnesses belonged to the rival faction and their word was unworthy of credit; Kistayya's confession was retracted and, being the only material against him, could not be made the foundation of a capital conviction. The intention, at highest, was to frighten or assault — fire in a village hut could not be presumed aimed at death.

For the State: the design spoke for itself — the hut was locked from outside, the victim was shut in, the fire was set, and when the doomed man tried to escape, rescuers were beaten back. "The intentional setting fire to the hut of a man who is inside… and the preventing of that man from coming out" left no escape from the inference of intention to kill.

Held

Venkalu and Venkataswami — murder affirmed. Intention is to be gathered from the acts done and the circumstances: locking, firing, and repelling rescue are acts which admit of no other rational inference than the intention to cause death — the design is the proof of the mens rea. Persons acting together in such a common design are severally liable for the murder: where the concerted act is one and indivisible, it is idle to ask whose hand struck the fatal spark. Kistayya: conviction rested on the retracted confession and interested testimony; the confession, though admissible against its maker, is weak evidence — his conviction could not stand on that footing (the strictness with which courts treat retracted confessions — the rule later codified in the spirit of IEA s. 24–30/BSA §§ 22–25 — is part of the case's teaching).

Principles evolved

  • Intention may be proved circumstantially: a deliberate course of conduct (trap, fire, prevention of rescue) is itself the proof of clause (a)/Firstly — no confession or direct evidence of intent is needed.
  • Common intention liability (s. 34/§ 3(5)): participants in a murderous design are answerable as principals for the resulting death.
  • Evidentiary caution: partisanship of eyewitnesses and retracted confessions demand scrupulous scrutiny — proof of the design must be individualised except where the concert itself is indivisible.

DU-exam hooks

The case is the cleanest illustration that clause (a)/Firstly intention is inferential, not testimonial. Use it (i) for any "locked hut / burnt alive" problem, (ii) to open a "how is intention proved?" essay, (iii) alongside Punnayya for the § 100(a)→§ 101(a) ladder, and (iv) for common-intention severability. In the BNS, add § 3(5) (common intention) and § 190 (unlawful assembly) where a mob fact-pattern appears.

6.2  Palani Goundan v. Emperor

AIR 1919 Mad 409 (57 Mad 158) — Wallis C.J. and Napier J. DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 2

Facts

The husband, wishing to get rid of his wife, struck her on the head with a ploughshare. Believing her dead — she was in fact only unconscious — he suspended the body by a rope from the roof to simulate suicide by hanging. Medical evidence showed the woman was alive when strung up, and that death was caused, or at any rate completed, by the hanging. The question was whether the husband was guilty of murder of the wife, or of any homicide offence at all.

Issues

  • Where the fatal act (hanging) is done in the honest belief that the victim is already dead, is there the mens rea for culpable homicide?
  • Is intention to be judged by objective reality or by the accused's own view of the facts (his "supposed circumstances")?
  • What is the liability where the first, intended act (the ploughshare blow) fell short of killing?

Arguments

For the Crown: a man who deals a mortal blow and then hangs the body has caused death by his acts taken together; the transaction is one, and the murderous disposition shown towards the wife should fix him with murder.

For the accused: he intended by the blow to kill, but he believed he had succeeded; the subsequent hanging was directed at a corpse — an act done on the belief that its object was dead cannot carry an intention to kill; there can be no murder of a person the accused believes to be already dead.

Held

Not murder; not even culpable homicide. Wallis C.J. (with Napier J.): for an offence under s. 299 "the offence must be committed with the intention, or knowledge, of causing death or such bodily injury as is likely to cause death; and this intention or knowledge must be in some relation to a person who… is alive, or who is believed by the accused to be alive". A man who, "intending to kill, inflicts what he believes to be a mortal injury, and then, thinking his victim dead, does an act to disguise the crime which in fact kills him", is not guilty of murder — nor even of culpable homicide, because the second act is done under the honest belief that life was already extinct. Liability confined to the first act: grievous hurt by the ploughshare blow (s. 326; § 122 BNS), plus, if charged, s. 201 (§ 238 BNS) for the attempt to destroy evidence of his own offence.

Principles evolved

  • The belief-dead doctrine: homicidal mens rea must relate to a living (or believed-living) victim; acts on a supposed corpse carry no intention to kill.
  • Subjective reckoning of intention: "the accused must be judged according to what he supposed to be the circumstances" — the mirror-image of mistake-of-fact doctrine (s. 76/§ 15): a man who fires at a tree-stump in the belief it is his enemy may be guilty of an attempt; a man who mangles a man in the belief he is a corpse is guilty of no homicide.
  • Mens rea as the definition's core: Napier J. quoted Mayne — every offence's definition states both the act and the state of mind; whatever Parliament has not expressly made strict is presumed to require mens rea.

DU-exam hooks

Pair immediately with Thavamani (the distinction is a standing exam question) and with Illustration (c) of § 100. The "supposed circumstances" line is also your authority for the mens-rea essay (Dec 2015 Q1(a)). Modern charge-sheet: § 122 BNS (grievous hurt by dangerous weapons) + § 238 BNS (causing disappearance of evidence).

6.3  In re Thavamani

(1943) 2 Mad LJ 245 (42 Mad 547) — Horwill J. DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 3

Facts

Accused 1 attacked a woman with an aruval (bill-hook) while accused 2 held her legs; the blows on the head and neck were, in the event, not immediately fatal. Believing her dead, the two threw the "body" into a neighbouring well. Death was caused by drowning in the well. The trial court convicted both of murder; the question on revision/reference was whether the Palani Goundan rule reduced the case.

Issues

  • Does Palani Goundan govern a case where the initial attack was itself aimed at killing, the fatal second act being only the completion of that same murderous purpose?
  • When do two acts merge into "one continuous transaction" for the purposes of mens rea?

Arguments

For the accused: on Palani Goundan, the throwing into the well was done in the belief that death had already occurred; the drowning therefore carried no intention, and the accused could answer only for the head injuries.

For the prosecution: unlike the husband in Palani Goundan, these men set out to kill — one held the victim while the other hacked her; the disposal in the well was the concluding step of a single murderous enterprise. The intention to kill, formed at the inception, had not spent itself when the second act was done.

Held

Murder affirmed. Horwill J. distinguished Palani Goundan: there, the husband, even at the time of the first act, intended only to cause bodily injury — the intention to kill was never established as existing at the inception; here, "the accused intended to cause death from the very commencement of their attack". The subsequent mistake as to the stage of their success could not dissolve the continuing intention: "the first act of assault and the second act of throwing into the well were so closely connected in time and purpose that they must be considered as parts of the same transaction." The mens rea — intention to kill — attached to the whole transaction, including its fatal conclusion.

Principles evolved

  • The Palani–Thavamani line: the belief-dead doctrine spares only the accused whose killing-intent never existed (or expired) before the second act; where the intention to kill exists at the inception and persists through a continuous transaction, murder is complete though death came by the second, mistaken act.
  • One-transaction doctrine in homicide: time, purpose and continuity of the enterprise — not the mechanical count of acts — determine whether mens rea infects the fatal act (the same reasoning later deployed in DPP v. Smith-adjacent Indian cases and in s. 301 analyses).

DU-exam hooks

This pair is the examiner's favourite "believed-dead" trap. The crisp answer structure: (1) What was the intention at inception? (2) Was the second act part of one continuous transaction? Kill-intent + continuity = murder (Thavamani); hurt-intent or fresh purpose = hurt + evidence-offence (Palani). Add Khandu (head blows then hut fire, treated as separate) as the third rail of the distinction.

6.4  Emperor v. Mushnooru S. Murthy

(1892) ILR 15 Mad 365 — full bench: Benson J., Sundara Aiyar J. (dissent), third judge concurring with Benson J. DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 4

Facts

A government official entrusted with stores grew rich rapidly; suspicion of theft fell on him. To screen himself, he placed poisoned halva (sweetmeat) in his office cupboard, intending that the vermin — or perhaps a search party's dog — should be destroyed, or (the majority's reading) that whoever found and ate it would be removed. A little girl, the watchman's daughter, found the halva, ate it, and died of the poison. The accused was charged with her murder; the death was unquestionably caused by his act of placing the poison, through the intervening agency of the child's own act of eating.

Issues

  • Is a person guilty of culpable homicide when the death is of a person he neither intended nor knew to be likely to die — the victim's own intervening act completing the chain?
  • What does "causes death" in s. 299 require — the accused's act as causa causans (efficient cause), or merely as a link in the chain?
  • How does s. 301 (now § 102) operate — and does it extend liability where the intended victim (or object) survives and another dies?

Arguments

For the accused: he aimed only at the destruction of vermin (or at protecting his stores from exposure); the girl was outside the range of any intention or knowledge; her own act of eating the sweetmeat was an independent intervening agency that broke the causal chain; s. 301 applies only where the accused intended to kill a particular person and killed another — not where he intended no human death at all.

For the Crown: poison placed where human beings were likely to find and eat it was an act "with the knowledge that it is likely to cause death" — the child's act in taking the bait was the very contingency the poisoner had to contemplate; s. 299's words are general: "whoever causes death by doing an act…" — no clause confines the death to the intended victim.

Held (majority — Benson J. and the third judge; Sundara Aiyar J. dissenting)

Conviction affirmed (302/§ 103(1) territory — murder, transportation of life retained). (1) S. 299 needs no intention toward the actual victim: "It is enough if he 'causes the death' of any one, whether the person intended to be killed or any one else" — the section's language is general; Illustration (a) (the man-trap pit) and Illustration (b) (inducing B to fire into the bush) both acquit strangers and convict the designer whose act causes the death. (2) Efficient cause: the accused's act was the causa causans — the efficient cause of the death; that the victim's own act (taking the bait) contributed, or that the chain included her innocent agency, did not exculpate: the man who sets a man-trap is not absolved because the victim's own step leads him into it. (3) S. 301's office: where A intends or knows his act likely to cause the death of B, and it causes the death of C, the culpable homicide is of the description it would have been had B died — the grade follows the intention toward the intended victim; the majority read it as confirmatory of, and additional to, the general words of s. 299 rather than as a restriction upon them. (4) English law the same: Agnes Gore (the poisoned apple intended for a husband found by a child), Saunders' Case (poisoned porridge, eaten by another), Michael's Case (poisoned flour baked and eaten by neighbours) — all held murder where the poisoner's act, through another's innocent agency, killed a person other than the one aimed at.

Sundara Aiyar J. (dissent): an intervening independent agency — especially the victim's own act — may break causation; s. 301 should be confined to cases where the person intended to be killed does not die and another does, and should not be stretched to make a man answerable for deaths outside the reasonable contemplation of his design; on his reading the accused's guilt at highest would fall short of murder.

Principles evolved

  • "Causing death" is a causal, not a targeting, concept: the actus reus is complete when the accused's act is the efficient cause of a death — whoever dies, and whatever innocent agencies intervene.
  • Contributory acts of the victim no bar: the pit-faller, the bush-shooter's inducer, the poisoner — all take the risk of the intermediate steps by which the death is brought about.
  • S. 301's grading function: transferred malice fixes the degree of culpable homicide by the intention toward the intended victim — the ancestor of § 102 BNS, word for word.
  • The outer limit (Illustration (c)): where the act was done with no homicidal intention or knowledge at all (the fowl-shooter), there is no culpable homicide — Mushnooru's majority expressly preserved this boundary.

DU-exam hooks

The case anchors three separate questions: "cause and effect" (causation essays — cite the efficient-cause passage and the three English cases), § 102/301 problems (aim at A, kill B), and the Illustrations (a)–(c) as a set. If the paper gives a poisoned-food or man-trap problem, open with Mushnooru, apply Illustrations (a)/(b)/(c) as the three possible mental states, and grade through § 102. Note the dissent — examiners reward knowing Sundara Ayyar J.'s narrower causation.

6.5  Kapur Singh v. State of PEPSU

Punjab & Haryana line of cases reproduced in the DU Case Material 2023 SUPPLEMENTARY — DU CM 2023, Unit 2/3 doctrinal set

Facts

The accused attacked his victim with a gandasa (heavy chopping blade) inflicting eighteen injuries — but every one of them on non-vital parts (limbs, shoulders). The injuries were individually and even collectively not shown to be sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; the medical picture was of a man hacked to maim, not to kill. Death was not the immediate result, and the inference sought was that the intention was confined to causing grievous hurt.

Issues

  • Does a large number of injuries, or the use of a lethal weapon, compel the inference of intention to kill or of Thirdly murder?
  • Can a court, on the pattern and situation of the injuries, hold the intention to be "to maim, not to kill" — s. 299(b)/§ 100(b) — and convict only under s. 304 Part I/§ 105 Part I?

Arguments

For the State: eighteen blows with a gandasa bespeak murderous fury; the weapon is deadly; the court should presume the intention to kill.

For the accused: the intention must be read from the injuries actually inflicted — all on non-vital parts, none sufficient to cause death; the man hacked to disable, not to kill; clause Thirdly fails at Virsa Singh's steps 2 and 4; the case is s. 304 Part I, not 302.

Held

S. 304 Part I (culpable homicide of the second degree, intention to cause bodily injury likely to cause death). The number of injuries and the dangerousness of the weapon are not conclusive: where the injuries' situation and pattern show that death was neither intended nor (in the ordinary course) the natural consequence of the intended injuries, the case falls in s. 299(b)/§ 100(b) with intention only to inflict dangerous hurt — conviction under Part I, rigorous imprisonment; where death ensued on that footing, transportation for life could follow (the 2023 CM reproduces the case for this grading).

Principles evolved

  • Injuries speak the intention: the situation of the injuries (non-vital parts) is objective evidence of the scope of the intention — the accused is judged by the injury he intended, not by the weapon's abstract potential.
  • Eighteen is a number, not a necrology: multiplicity of blows does not, by itself, establish an intention to kill — but note the counter-principle immediately below.
  • The Punnayya contrast (write both together): in Punnayya the Supreme Court rejected the same argument — 19 injuries (9 grievous) on the limbs of a 55-year-old, held cumulatively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death (death by shock and haemorrhage): "bodily injury" in Thirdly includes its plural, and the doctor's cumulative-sufficiency opinion completes Virsa Singh's fourth step. Kapur Singh survives only where the cumulative medical evidence does not show sufficiency — the examiner's real question is which of the two lines the facts track.

DU-exam hooks

Deploy in the "many injuries, no fatal injury" problem: state Kapur Singh (pattern of injuries limits intention), then immediately the Punnayya correction (cumulative sufficiency can still make it Thirdly), and resolve on the doctor's evidence. This exact tension is the Dec 2014 Q3(a) stove/septicemia problem's second limb.

6.6  Virsa Singh v. State of Punjab

AIR 1958 SC 465 — Vivian Bose J. DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 5

Facts

A marriage party; the accused, drunk and armed with a spear, was dancing before it. He made an offensive remark to the complainant's wife; words followed; the bridegroom's father, Mohd. Sidik, stepped in to pacify. The accused then thrust his spear into Sidik's abdomen — a single thrust. The wound was about 2¼ inches by ½ to ⅜ inch; three coils of intestine came out. Sidik gathered them in and walked home; peritonitis set in; he died the next morning. The trial court and High Court convicted the accused of murder under s. 302, Thirdly; the defense contended the conviction was unsustainable because there was no proof he intended to cause an injury sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death — indeed, that Thirdly requires an intention approximating an intention to kill.

Issues

  • Does clause Thirdly (§ 101(c)) require the offender to know, or intend, that the injury he inflicts is sufficient to cause death?
  • What must the prosecution prove, and of what kind (objective/subjective) is each element?

Arguments

For the accused: Thirdly speaks of "the intention of causing bodily injury… sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death" — on its face the sufficiency must be intended; a rustic drunk cannot be credited with anatomical knowledge of the abdomen; at highest the case is s. 304. (The argument, Bose J. records, had found favour with Beaman J. in Sardarkhan's case in Bombay — intent linked to the seriousness of the injury intended.)

For the State: the clause's two limbs are separate: the intention relates to the bodily injury; the sufficiency is a matter of objective medical fact. The accused intended the abdominal thrust he gave; the injury he intended was, in the ordinary course of nature, sufficient to kill; that is all Thirdly demands.

Held (Vivian Bose J. — appeal dismissed)

Murder affirmed; the four-step formulation. The clauses of s. 300 are disjunctive and separate; Beaman J.'s reading (intent must extend to the injury's fatal quality) rejected: it would read "intention to cause death" into a clause framed without it. The prosecution's four requirements (set out in full at § 3.3 above) — injury present; nature proved; intention to inflict that injury; sufficiency in the ordinary course of nature — the fourth "purely objective and inferential and has nothing to do with the intention of the offender." Knowledge of anatomy irrelevant: "the inquiry… should not be narrow and pedantic… broad-based and simple": the man who thrusts a spear into another's abdomen intends the injury he inflicts; he cannot claim he did not know where the abdomen is. The escape hatch: if the accused shows he intended a lesser injury — "a superficial scratch" — or that the fatal injury was accidental, Thirdly fails; but here there was no explanation at all — it would be "perverse" to hold he did not intend the very thrust he deliberately delivered (Steane v. State's reasoning accommodated: doubt about intention benefits the accused; absence of explanation permits the natural inference). Death by peritonitis three days later was death in the ordinary course of nature from the injury.

Principles evolved

  • The Virsa Singh test is the canonical proof-recipe for clause (c)/Thirdly — quoted in every Indian murder judgment since, and expressly adopted in Punnayya, Jassa Singh, State of A.P. v. Rayavarapu Punnayya's progeny and beyond.
  • Division of labour: medicine proves steps 1, 2, 4; only step 3 belongs to the mind — and it asks less than the defence claims: intention as to the kind of injury, not its consequence.
  • Objective sufficiency: "sufficient in the ordinary course of nature" is judged on the post-mortem findings, not on the offender's foresight.

DU-exam hooks

Every spear-knife-lathi-death problem routes here. Quote the four steps verbatim, then run the facts through them in a numbered mini-table. If injuries are on limbs and multiple — import Punnayya's cumulative-sufficiency gloss; if the weapon penetrated a vital organ — Virsa Singh alone completes Thirdly. Note the drunk-state point: s. 85/§ 21 (voluntary intoxication) is no excuse where the basic intention to inflict the injury is proved.

6.7  State of Andhra Pradesh v. R. Punnayya

AIR 1976 SC 1822 = (1974) 4 SCC 381 — Sarkaria J. DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 6

Facts

A factional feud village in Andhra. The victim Kotam Raju, aged about 55, was set upon by the accused — A-1 Gangula Satyanandam (since dead; his appeal abated) and A-2 R. Punnayya — near the field channel. The two beat and cut him with sticks, slippers and knives until he fell; the post-mortem disclosed 19 external injuries, of which 9 were grievous — incised wounds, contusions and abrasions — all on the limbs and other non-vital parts; death was attributed to shock and haemorrhage from the cumulative injuries. The trial court convicted both of murder. The High Court acquitted A-1's companions and reduced A-2's conviction to s. 304 Part II (knowledge only), reasoning that the injuries were on non-vital parts, that there was no haemorrhage from a vital organ, and that death by "shock" could not ground Thirdly. The State appealed against the reduction.

Issues

  • Where injuries are individually non-fatal and confined to non-vital parts, but death results from their cumulative effect (shock and haemorrhage), does clause Thirdly apply?
  • What is the correct method for placing a case between s. 299 and s. 300, and between the parts of s. 304?
  • Were the High Court's reasons (non-vital situs, absence of internal haemorrhage, death by shock) sustainable?

Arguments

For A-2 (Punnayya): the injuries were deliberately confined to the limbs; a man who avoids the vital parts evinces an intention to maim, not to kill; none of the injuries was individually sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; the death, by "shock", was an unfortunate sequel — the case at highest s. 304 Part I, properly Part II.

For the State: 19 injuries, 9 grievous, on a 55-year-old, in a concerted factional assault — the injuries were cumulatively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; the medical officer's testimony supported Thirdly; the pattern of the attack (overwhelming violence) and the common intention to finish the victim made it murder.

Held (Sarkaria J. — appeal allowed, 302 restored)

(1) Framework: the genus-species passage and the three degrees (quoted at § 1.1); the "keywords" comparison (reproduced at § 3.2); knowledge under Fourthly must "approximate to a practical certainty". (2) Cumulative sufficiency: the High Court erred in dissecting the injuries one from the other — "bodily injury" in Thirdly takes in the plural: the question is whether the injuries intended and inflicted were collectively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; the doctor's evidence that death was the cumulative result of shock and haemorrhage satisfied Virsa Singh's fourth step; Anda's case approved. (3) Intention: A-2 was a factional leader taking vengeance — the ferocity, number and concert of the blows evidenced the intention to inflict the very injuries that were inflicted. (4) Result: A-2's conviction under 302 restored (imprisonment for life); the High Court's 304 Part II set aside. A-1's appeal had abated on his death.

Principles evolved

  • Cumulative-sufficiency rule: Thirdly is satisfied where the ensemble of intended injuries is sufficient to kill — non-vital situs is not a talisman.
  • The three-degree doctrine and the keyword method for 299/300 — the standard examination framework for the whole subject.
  • 304 Part II (knowledge) cannot be the refuge where intention to inflict grievous injuries is proved and the injuries are cumulatively sufficient — that is murder; if intention is proved but sufficiency is not, it is Part I, not Part II.

DU-exam hooks

The single most quotable case in the unit. Memorise the genus-species paragraph and the three degrees; deploy the keyword table; and use the cumulative-sufficiency point to defeat every "only limb injuries" defence — with Kapur Singh as the honest counter-example. Also the answer's closing line for Dec 2014 Q3(a).

6.8  Emperor v. Mt. Dhirajia

AIR 1940 All 275 — Allahabad High Court DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 7

Facts

A young wife, six months pregnant, lived with her husband in her father's house; her father-in-law and husband had earlier driven her out, and there had been quarrels and threats. One dawn, while the household slept, she took her six-month-old baby and slipped out to go to her parents. The husband woke, pursued, called out; she, in terror of being caught and of what would follow, either jumped or fell into a well, clutching the baby. She was rescued alive; the baby died. She was convicted under ss. 302/304 and 309 IPC (attempt to commit suicide).

Issues

  • Does leaping (or falling) into a well with a child, to escape a pursuing husband, amount to murder under Fourthly — an act "so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death"?
  • What is the relation between intention and knowledge — can panic exclude intention yet leave knowledge intact?
  • Is jumping into a well in panic an "attempt to commit suicide" requiring a conscious endeavour to die?

Arguments

For the prosecution: whatever her fear, a sane woman leaping into a well with a baby knows the act must in all probability cause the baby's death; Fourthly is satisfied; and her own jump proves the deliberate taking of her life (309).

For the accused: she acted in blind panic — no capacity to reason, hence no intention; no cool choice of death, hence no attempt to die; her flight was from her husband's threats, not toward the well.

Held (acquittal on 302; 304 Part II; 309 acquittal affirmed)

(1) Knowledge present: "Some degree of knowledge must… be attributed to every sane person", however primitive or however frightened — jumping into a well with a baby in arms can "but for a miracle" have one conclusion; the knowledge clause of s. 299(c) was satisfied. (2) Intention excluded by panic: "In order to possess and to form an intention there must be a capacity for reason. And when by some extraneous force the capacity for reason has been ousted, it seems to us that the capacity to form an intention must have been unseated too." (3) Fourthly fails on its own words: the clause requires the act to be done "without any excuse for incurring the risk" — "She feared her husband and she had reason to fear him… We think she had excuse and that that excuse was panic or fright." Not murder; 304 Part II — culpable homicide by knowledge alone. (4) s. 309: attempt to suicide requires a conscious endeavour to die; a panic-stricken leap is not the formation of that purpose — Lukada (leaping into a well after a scolding, held 309) distinguished as involving an actual, present purpose to die; acquittal on 309 restored. (Note: s. 309/§ 108 BNS — attempt to commit suicide — survives only as to its penal limb for others; § 108 preserves it, and note the MVA-assisted dying discussion in Common Cause for the modern frame.)

Principles evolved

  • Intention needs reason; knowledge is universal: panic, fright, coercion of circumstance may oust intention while leaving intact the knowledge that makes an act s. 299(c)/§ 100(c) culpable homicide.
  • "Without any excuse" is a real element of Fourthly: a genuine, humanly intelligible excuse (panic from real threat) downgrades Fourthly murder to third-degree culpable homicide — the law's safety valve.
  • Attempt to suicide demands conscious endeavour: reflexive or panic flight into danger is not an attempt at self-murder.

DU-exam hooks

The case to cite whenever a mother-child drowning/well/burning problem appears — always pair with Gyarsibai (next card): same clause, opposite result, and the pair teaches that "excuse" is a fact-question measured at the moment of the act. Also the standing authority for the intention/knowledge distinction essay.

6.9  Gyarsibai v. The State

AIR 1953 M.B. 61 — Madhya Bharat High Court DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 8

Facts

A young wife harassed by her sister-in-law. One day she announced she was going to drown herself, took her three children (aged about 7, 5 and 1½) to a well and threw herself in with them. The children died; she survived. Convicted under ss. 302 and 309; transportation for life.

Issues

  • Where a mother deliberately jumps into a well with her children, is the act done "without any excuse" within Fourthly?
  • From whose standpoint is the excuse judged — the distraught actor's, or the reasonable, sane person's?

Arguments

For the accused: she was a harassed young mother, provoked beyond endurance by the sister-in-law's cruelty — Dhirajia's panic-excusse should apply; at highest 304.

For the State: she proclaimed her intention, collected her children, and executed it — deliberation, not panic; no excuse can justify taking three children's lives; Fourthly is complete.

Held

Conviction under 302 affirmed. The knowledge that jumping into a well with three children must in all probability cause death is undeniable — she announced the very act in advance. On excuse: "we must take into account the state of mind of a reasonable and legally sane person and then determine whether the risk of the kind referred to in the fourth clause… could have been avoided" — the sister-in-law's attitude, however cruel, was no excuse for taking away the lives of three children. Distinguished from Dhirajia: there, sudden panic in flight; here, an announced and deliberate act. Murder stands; but the court recommended commutation of the capital element — transportation for life, with a recommendation for commutation to about 3 years' rigorous imprisonment, recognising the mitigation of her domestic provocation in the sentence, if not in the offence. Conviction under 309 affirmed: unlike Dhirajia, here there was a conscious effort at self-destruction — she had announced it and carried it out.

Principles evolved

  • The objective-excuse standard: Fourthly's "excuse" is measured from the standpoint of a reasonable, sane person — genuine panic may qualify (Dhirajia), but deliberation, however emotionally distressed, does not.
  • Announcement = deliberation: prior declaration of intent to jump removes the case from the refuge of sudden panic.
  • Offence vs sentence: the court's power to recommend commutation shows the two-track response — full murder liability with humane sentencing — the pattern modern law follows for battered and provoked offenders.

DU-exam hooks

Always argue Dhirajia and Gyarsibai as a matched pair on the "excuse" element. Exam formulation: "The difference between panic and resolve is the difference between 304-II and 302." Cite also for the 309 conscious-endeavour rule, and as the foil in any infanticide-by-mother problem.

6.10  K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra

AIR 1962 SC 605 — Subba Rao J. (for the Court) DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 9

Facts

Commander K.M. Nanavati, a naval officer, returned from a voyage; his English-born wife Sylvia confessed to him (18 April 1959) that she was in love with Prem Ahuja, a wealthy Bombay businessman, and that Ahuja had used her. On 27 April, after a family lunch and a cinema programme, Nanavati dropped his wife and children at the Metro cinema (about 1.30 p.m.), went to his ship, obtained a revolver and six cartridges from the ship's stores on the false pretext that the property was being stolen and he needed to check it, drove to Ahuja's office and then, not finding him there, to his flat. He entered Ahuja's bedroom, sent away the servant, shut the door, and confronted Ahuja (then in a towel after his bath) with "Will you marry my wife?… Will you look after my children?" Ahuja's answer — "I am not going to marry every woman I sleep with" (or, in the defence version, an abusive retort) — was followed by three shots from Nanavati's revolver at point-blank range; Ahuja died. Nanavati then went to a Parsi trustee and confessed, handed over his revolver, and surrendered to the police. A jury (9–3) acquitted him. The High Court (on reference under the then s. 307 CrPC) set the verdict aside as perverse and convicted him of murder; he appealed to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  • Was the jury's acquittal perverse — unsupported by the evidence and the law?
  • Did the killing fall within Exception 1 (grave and sudden provocation) — or within the third proviso's bar (provocation sought as an excuse) or the rule that premeditation defeats the exception?
  • What is the correct statement of the Indian law of provocation (words, class-of-society standard, cooling time)?

Arguments

For Nanavati: a wronged husband, deceived by a predator who exploited his absence at sea; the confession of adultery (analogous to the sight of adultery) was grave provocation; the final abusive answer in the bedroom was the spark; he had no intention to kill when he went to the flat — he took the gun innocently (to deposit it safely); the shots were fired "in the heat of the moment". The jury, the judges of fact, had believed him.

For the State: the sequence of conduct was murderous by design — the false pretext to obtain the revolver, the loading, the drive, the shutting of the bedroom door, the despatch of the servant; the provocation (if any) had cooled between 18 and 27 April; the bedroom exchange, if there was one, came after he had already resolved and prepared to shoot — the provocation was "sought" as an excuse (proviso (a)).

Held (Subba Rao J. — appeal dismissed; murder affirmed, life imprisonment)

(1) Jury verdict perverse: the acquittal was "so unreasonable" that no reasonable body of persons could have reached it on the evidence; the High Court rightly re-appreciated the evidence. (2) The four principles of Indian provocation law (§ 4.1 above) — reasonable man of the same class of society; words and gestures can suffice (Indian law rejecting the English mere-words rule); the mental background (Khogayi, Boya Munigadu, Balku, Babu Lal — prolonged passion re-ignited by a fresh cause); fatal blow before passion cools, without premeditation. (3) Application: even assuming Sylvia's confession of 18 April was grave provocation, by 27 April — after dropping the family at the cinema, going to the ship, drawing and loading the revolver, driving to the office and the flat — "the accused had time to regain his self-control"; the three-hour window and the false pretext and the loaded gun showed deliberation and preparation: "the murder was a deliberate and calculated one." Entering the bedroom to confront Ahuja meant any exchange there followed the resolution to shoot — provocation voluntarily sought as an excuse (first proviso); Ahuja's answer, at highest, only "equally abusive" words after the design was formed. (4) Intention proved: three shots at point-blank range through the towel into the chest — intention to kill beyond doubt; "the doctrine of provocation… is negatived by proof of intention to kill" (Mancini, Holmes, Duffy applied). Result: conviction under 302 restored — life imprisonment; the mercy plea to the Governor (the last jury trial's political aftermath) lay outside the judgment.

Principles evolved

  • The Indian provocation formula — the four principles — is the exam statement of Exception 1 law.
  • Cooling-off as fact of design: time enough to prepare (gun, pretext, journey) proves reassertion of self-control and converts "provocation" into "premeditation".
  • Words suffice — but must operate: India's rejection of the English mere-words rule sits beside the rule that a reply engineered by the killer cannot provoke.
  • Provocation and intention are opposites: reliance on a formed intention to kill defeats the exception as surely as lapse of time.

DU-exam hooks

The most famous trial in Indian criminal law — use it for Exception 1 essays AND for the critical/gendered reading (O'Donovan): the "reasonable man of the same class" includes husbands of the officer class, but battered women who kill after years of abuse still struggle to fit the "sudden" frame — the reform argument the syllabus's critical questions invite. Also the answer-frame for Dec 2015 Q3(a)-type problems (police provoke + knife).

6.11  Ghapoo Yadav v. State of Madhya Pradesh

Supreme Court (DU CM 2025 extract) DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 10

Facts

A village affray: the deceased and his group on one side, the accused (seven tried) on the other. A sudden quarrel erupted (over a trivial everyday matter); blows were exchanged on the spot — the classic mutual combat. In the melée the accused party assaulted the deceased with fists, feet and lathis; only one of the seven injuries was grievous; no deadly weapons of the kind that bespeak premeditation were deployed by the accused. The deceased died. The trial court convicted the accused under 302 read with s. 149 (common object); the High Court affirmed; the accused invoked Exception 4.

Issues

  • When does a killing in a brawl fall within Exception 4 (sudden fight) rather than murder?
  • Are the ingredients of Exception 4 conjunctive or alternative — and who bears the consequence of uncertainty?
  • What is the sentence for CHnAM under Part I where the exception applies?

Arguments

For the accused: no premeditation — the quarrel arose on the spur of the moment; the fight was mutual (blows given and received); no undue advantage was taken — no weapons against unarmed men, no cruelty; the case is squarely Exception 4, punishable under 304 Part I.

For the State: seven men against one; the group had come prepared; the assault was one-sided despite the initial exchange — a "fight" requires equality of combat; the injuries (including one grievous) show a determination beyond heat of passion.

Held

Exception 4 applied — 302/149 set aside; 304 Part I substituted. The Court restated the ingredients: the killing must be (a) without premeditation; (b) in a sudden fight; (c) in the heat of passion upon a sudden quarrel; (d) without the offender taking undue advantage or acting in a cruel or unusual manner; and the fight must have been with the person killed. "All the ingredients mentioned in it must be found co-existing" — conjunctive, not alternative. A "fight" presupposes mutual provocation and combat: "It takes two to make a fight… an exchange of blows… whether with or without weapons"; heat of passion means "that passion which clouds men's sober reason and urges them to deeds which they would not otherwise do" — distinguished from Exception 1's total deprivation of self-control. Undue advantage = unfair advantage: attacking with weapons an unarmed man, striking a fallen foe, numerical ambush — none present here; sudden fight is a question of fact in each case (the Explanation's point: it matters not who struck first or provoked). On sentence: 10 years rigorous imprisonment under Part I, following Sukhbir Singh v. State of Haryana ((2002) 3 SCC 327) — where Exception 4 reduces murder to Part I CHnAM, ten years is the normative sentence (and remission below requires special reasons).

Principles evolved

  • Conjunctive-ingredients rule for Exception 4 — and, by parity, for every partial defence: the court must find each element on the facts.
  • "Takes two to make a fight": mutuality of combat is of the essence; a one-sided beating is not a "fight" (though the first provoker and first striker are irrelevant, per the Explanation).
  • Exc 1 vs Exc 4 articulated: total loss of self-control (provoker's person) vs heat of passion on equal footing (mutual combat) — a standing distinction question.
  • Sukhbir Singh norm: 10 years as the standard Part I sentence in exception cases — the sentencing peg for all such answers.

DU-exam hooks

The first case to reach for in any "sudden quarrel + melée + death" problem. Structure: check each ingredient (a)–(d) separately; a failure of any one keeps the case in murder. Contrast with Nanavati (Exception 1's psychology) and with Dhirajia (Fourthly's excuse). In the BNS: § 105 Part I (5–10 yrs or life) + Sukhbir Singh's 10-year norm as the working sentence.

6.12  Tehseen S. Poonawalla v. Union of India

(2018) 6 SCC 72 — Dipak Misra C.J. (with A.M. Khanwilkar and D.Y. Chandrachud JJ.) DU CASE MATERIAL — 2025, Unit 6, Case 11

Facts

A wave of mob lynchings 2015–2018 — cattle-transport rumours, child-kidnapping WhatsApp forwards, "gau rakshak" vigilantism — claimed lives across the country (Dadri 2015; Jharkhand, Haryana, Rajasthan, Karnataka, Maharashtra). Petitions (Tehseen Poonawalla and others, including Tushar Gandhi) sought directions against mob violence and hate speech. The Union and States were heard; the Court undertook to frame preventive, remedial and punitive guidelines and to consider whether a special offence was needed.

Issues

  • How should the constitutional order respond to lynching — violence by private mobs on rumours and identity grounds?
  • What directions can be issued to the police and civil administration (preventive and remedial), and what accountability follows deliberate official failure (punitive)?
  • Should Parliament create a separate offence of lynching?

Arguments

For the petitioners: lynching had become systematic; rumours of cattle-theft and child-kidnapping circulated on social media were triggering mob murders; the State police stood by or were complicit; existing offences (302, 147–149, 153A) were inadequate because the mob's collective anonymity defeated individual attribution; the Court should lay down guidelines (as in Vishaka) and compel a special law.

For the Union/States: law and order is a State subject; existing provisions sufficed; the Centre had issued advisories; a judicially-crafted offence would trench on the legislature's domain.

Held

The vision: "Mob violence and lynching are an affront to the rule of law and to the collective conscience of the nation… Hyperbolic protests against crime cannot be de facto justice"; the mob cannot replace courts; "the victims of mob violence are often persons belonging to weak and marginalised sections" — hate violence attacks the constitutional values of equality and dignity. The guidelines (now the template § 103(2) operationalises): A. Preventive: a Nodal Officer (SP-rank) in every district, assisted by DSP-rank officers, to identify areas prone to lynching (hot-spot mapping, within three months); police patrolling; dissemination of warnings against rumour-mongering; s. 129 CrPC (now BNSS § 163) dispersal of unlawful assemblies; FIRs under s. 153A/§ 196 BNS against those inciting mobs; curbs on inflammatory broadcasts and social-media forwards. B. Remedial: immediate FIR on any lynching incident; investigation monitored by the Nodal Officer and Superintendent of Police; chargesheet within the statutory time; compensation to victims' families under the s. 357A CrPC scheme (BNSS § 396 successor). C. Punitive: departmental action against police/officials whose deliberate negligence or connivance enabled lynching — following Arumugam Servai v. State of Tamil Nadu ((2011) 6 SCC 403, suo motu action against officials who fail to prevent); inquiries to conclude within six months. Compliance: States and the Centre to implement within four weeks; the measure in compliance reports to be reviewed. On the special offence: the Court "recommend[ed] to the Central Government and the State Governments to consider enacting a special law prescribing severe punishment for lynching" — deliberately leaving definition and sentence to Parliament. The BNS's § 103(2) is the legislative answer: five or more persons, acting in concert, murdering on any of the listed identity grounds — each member liable to death or life.

Principles evolved

  • Lynching as a rule-of-law offence, not a law-and-order event: the private mob's assumption of the judicial function is itself the constitutional harm.
  • Judge-made governance guidelines in the Vishaka tradition — the triple preventive/remedial/punitive template now standard for institutional-failure cases.
  • Incitement punished at parity: rumour-mongers and 153A/§ 196 offenders are accessories to the mob's crime, not mere speakers.
  • The genealogy of § 103(2): judicial recommendation → legislative enactment — the modern example (with Vishaka) of the dialogue between Court and Parliament that the DU syllabus's "critical questions" invite you to discuss.

DU-exam hooks

Any lynching question (a 2024+ exam certainty after the BNS) must run: Tehseen's account of the harm → the three guideline families → § 103(2)'s text (five or more, concert, identity grounds, each member) → the evidentiary simplification it achieves over § 190/149. Pair with Rawalpenta (concert/individual responsibility) and with the s. 3(5)/§ 34 general principles for the "whose hand struck" problem.

Q1
Arguments between X (husband) and W (wife) on repayment of loan to the Bank, turned ugly. Husband slapped his wife and not satisfied with this threw a burning stove (kerosene oil) on her. The oil with flame resulted into fire in which the wife was engulfed. Husband tried to dose it off. However, the burn injuries were beyond 70% which resulted into death of the wife four days later in the hospital. Determine the liability of the Husband X in the case.
LL.B. I Term, December 2014 — Q.2(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION · 15-marker pattern
Stage 1 — Causation

Death was caused by the husband's act (throwing the lit kerosene stove); that he "tried to dose it off" afterwards is irrelevant to causation — Explanation 2 to § 100/s. 299: where death is caused by bodily injury, the inflictor is deemed to have caused it "although by resorting to proper remedies and skilful treatment the death might have been prevented". Four days' survival in hospital does not break the chain.

Stage 2 — Culpable homicide

Throwing a burning kerosene stove at a person is, at minimum, an act done with the knowledge that it is likely to cause death — § 100(c)/299 Thirdly is satisfied a fortiori if intention is found.

Stage 3 — Murder? the clause analysis
  • Clause (a)/Firstly (intention to cause death): an escalating quarrel ("not satisfied" with a slap, he resorted to fire) supports an inference of intention, but a court may hesitate — the subsequent attempt to extinguish the flames is evidence again it. Arguable, not essential.
  • Clause (c)/Thirdly (Virsa Singh — the certain route): (1) bodily injury — 70% burns, present; (2) nature — medically proved; (3) intention to inflict that very injury — throwing the burning stove is a voluntary act aimed at the person; no explanation offered, so the natural inference stands ("no one has a licence to run around inflicting injuries sufficient to cause death…"); (4) sufficiency in the ordinary course of nature — 70% burns are ordinarily fatal. Murder under § 101(c)/300 Thirdly — intention to kill unnecessary (Virsa Singh).
  • Clause (d)/Fourthly (alternative): hurling flaming kerosene at a person in a house is "so imminently dangerous that it must in all probability cause death", and a domestic quarrel over loan repayment is no excuse within the meaning of the clause (Gyarsibai line).
Exceptions?

None: the quarrel was mutual and verbal — a "sudden quarrel" presupposes mutual combat ("it takes two to make a fight" — Ghapoo Yadav) but a one-sided attack with a lethal agent on an unarmed spouse is both undue advantage and cruel, defeating Exception 4; provocation from a loan-repayment argument is neither grave nor sudden enough (Nanavati's reasonable-man test).

Conclusion

X is guilty of murder under § 103(1)/s. 302 (death or life imprisonment + fine), via clause (c)/Thirdly primarily and (d)/Fourthly in the alternative. If the court were to accept that he acted in sudden heat without the intention to inflict the burns as inflicted, the fallback is § 105 Part I/304 Part I — but on these facts (burning stove thrown at close range) murder is the sound conclusion.

Q2
Due to rivalry arising out of landed property between A and B, A caused multiple injuries to B on various parts of body to teach him a lesson. B was admitted to the hospital, where he was treated and discharged. When B was on his way to recovery, he became negligent about his medicines. He, therefore, developed fever and septic of two wounds. B died a week later. State the liability of A. Cite relevant legal provisions and decided cases.
LL.B. I Term, December 2014 — Q.3(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
Two issues in the problem

(1) Does the victim's own negligence about his medicines break the causal chain? (2) Are "multiple injuries to teach a lesson" murder or second-degree culpable homicide?

Issue 1 — Causation: the victim's negligence

Explanation 2 to § 100/s. 299 settles it: where death is caused by bodily injury, the inflictor is deemed to have caused it, "although by resorting to proper remedies and skilful treatment the death might have been prevented". A fortiori where the victim's own carelessness lets the wounds turn septic — the death is still A's act. The line the Explanation draws protects the accused from medical failure, not from his own injury's natural progress: septicemia is the "ordinary course" sequel of neglected wounds. A's act remains the causa causans (Mushnooru: contributory acts — even of the victim — do not exculpate). A cannot plead B's negligence as a novus actus; contrast a genuinely independent intervening agency (Sundara Aiyar J.'s dissent, rejected by the majority).

Issue 2 — The mental element: "teach a lesson"

The declared object — to teach a lesson by a beating — evidences intention to cause hurt, not to kill: the § 100(b)/299 Secondly-type mens rea. The grading then turns on the injuries' sufficiency:

  • If the injuries (individually or cumulatively) were sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death — clause (c)/Thirdly applies even without any intention to kill: Virsa Singh steps 3–4 (intention to inflict the injuries + objective sufficiency); and Punnayya adds the cumulative-sufficiency rule — 19 injuries on non-vital parts were held Thirdly murder where death by shock and haemorrhage followed a factional beating.
  • If they were not so sufficient — Kapur Singh: hacking confined to non-vital parts with a "maim, not kill" intent is s. 304 Part I/§ 105 Part I (second degree), because Thirdly fails at the sufficiency step.

Since death came only through the sepsis of two wounds after treatment and discharge, the sufficiency question is genuinely medical: if the doctor testifies the wounds were likely/certain to cause death if untreated, the Punnayya route to § 103(1)/302 opens; the safer and more defensible conclusion on "teach a lesson" facts is § 105 Part I/s. 304 Part I — intention to cause grievous injury likely to cause death — unless the prosecution proves cumulative sufficiency.

Conclusion

A has caused B's death with the intention of causing bodily injury likely to cause death: liable for culpable homicide not amounting to murder, second degree — § 105 Part I/s. 304 Part I (life, or 5–10 years + fine under the BNS); if the medical evidence shows the multiple injuries were cumulatively sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, the liability escalates to murder, § 103(1)/s. 302 (Virsa Singh; Punnayya). The victim's negligence does not exculpate (Explanation 2).

Q3
Accused X was running a bus at a high speed on a dusty and damaged road. While negotiating a curve with the same speed, without applying brakes, the bus over turned, killing a pedestrian and injuring some of the passengers. Prosecution is interested to prosecute X under Section 304-A of IPC. Can they do so and will they succeed? Give reasons.
LL.B. I Term, December 2014 — Q.3(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
The line the problem tests

§ 106(1)/s. 304A punishes death by a rash or negligent act — its boundary with culpable homicide is mental: if the actor knew the act was likely to cause death, § 100(c)/299 Thirdly takes over; if he merely failed to notice or take care (no such knowledge), it remains rashness/negligence. The examiner wants this demarcation, then the application.

Application

X drove at high speed on a visibly damaged, dusty road, took a curve at the same speed and did not apply brakes — a passenger-laden bus is a lethal instrument on such a road. Two readings:

  • Rash/negligent (304A/§ 106(1)): speed and failure to brake are classic rashness — the risk was run heedlessly; there is no material that X actually knew death was likely (he was, after all, driving himself). On this reading the prosecution succeeds — the section is attracted and a conviction can stand.
  • Culpable homicide (299(c)/§ 100(c)) : if the evidence showed X consciously appreciated that negotiating that curve at that speed without braking would probably kill, knowledge (which is "attributed to every sane person" — Dhirajia) would raise the case above 304A. But subjective awareness of likely death, as distinct from recklessness about it, must be proved; mere high speed does not import it.

Better view for the answer: the prosecution can proceed and will succeed under 304A/§ 106(1) — this is the archetypal rash-driving death: the driver took an obvious and grave risk without evidence of actual knowledge of likely death. (Cite also: the BNS's new § 106(2) hit-and-run aggravated clause — up to 10 years — applies only where the driver escapes without reporting; X, who overturned on the spot, is outside it.)

Conclusion

Yes — s. 304A/§ 106(1) is the correct offence; conviction sustainable on proof of rashness (high speed on a damaged road, no brakes on the curve). The passengers' injuries ride along as hurt by rash act (s. 337/§ 125(1)); had X possessed knowledge of likely death, the charge would graduate to § 100(c) with § 105 Part II/304 Part II.

Q4
Due to breaking of communal riots between community A and B, members of community A caused loot, plunder and fire of the property belonging to members of community B. X, who was a member of community B was also targeted. He tried to remain within closed door, to save himself and his family. However, the mob reached and started knocking at his door. Before the mob could enter, X fired from his licensed revolver and killed a member out of the mob. Mr. X is charged of murder, however he pleads his right of private defence against his charge. Can he do so? Give reasons.
LL.B. I Term, December 2014 — Q.4(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
The plea available — and its two forms

X's plea runs on two levels and the answer must keep them apart:

  • Complete private defence — §§ 30–44/96–106: under § 36/s. 100 (right of private defence of body extending to death) X may cause death in defence of his person against an assault causing reasonable apprehension of death; under § 43/s. 102 (defence of property) the right is available against robbery, mischief by fire (§ 40/s. 100's fifthly-equivalent — house by fire) and house-breaking after sunset. A rioting mob that has looted and burned the neighbourhood's property, has targeted his house and is breaking at his door, raises a reasonable apprehension of death or grievous hurt to him and his family — apprehension, not actual commencement of the attack, suffices (§ 37/s. 97; the attacker's own unlawful conduct creates the defender's right). The right extends to death against such an assembly (§ 39/s. 101-102 read together). If the court holds the defence complete — the force used necessary and proportionate to the reasonably-apprehended harm — X is acquitted outright; no homicide offence survives.
  • Exception 2 to § 101/300 — honest excess: if the court thinks X exceeded the necessity of the case (e.g., fired before the door gave, or kept firing as the mob recoiled), the excess in good faith is not murder but culpable homicide not amounting to murder — § 105 Part I/304 Part I — because the excess was without premeditation and without intention to do more harm than necessary (Exception 2's words).
Application

Riot context (§§ 189–191/147–149 mob), mob at the door at night after a rampage of loot and arson — the reasonable-man-on-the-spot standard (the fear must be judged from X's "supposed circumstances" — Palani Goundan's subjective-reckoning principle, transposed): he was entitled to act on the apprehension the mob itself created. Firing before the mob entered is legitimate — the law does not require the defender to wait for the door to be broken (§ 38/s. 98's aid; the right commences as soon as a reasonable apprehension arises and continues as long as it lasts). One shot from a licensed revolver, killing one member of the mob, is arguably within the necessity of defence against an assembly capable of overwhelming the household.

Conclusion

Yes, X can plead private defence. If the apprehension of death/grievous hurt from the rioting mob is accepted (it should be, on these facts), the defence is complete — acquittal. If the court finds a good-faith excess, the charge reduces from murder to CHnAM, § 105 Part I/s. 304 Part I under Exception 2. The murder charge cannot stand in either event.

Q5
Raghav Ram, a film actor, was returning from a party past midnight when he dozed off and the car that he was driving ran over two persons sleeping on the pavement killing them and thereafter rammed into a pole. He was jolted out of sleep by the impact of the accident when his car hit the pole. Tests confirmed high dosage of alcohol in his blood. Discuss his liability for the death of those two persons.
LL.B. I Term, December 2015 — Q.1(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (modelled on the real Salman Khan hit-and-run litigation)
The mental-element ladder
  • Knowledge-based culpable homicide — § 100(c)/299 Thirdly: a heavily intoxicated driver who takes the wheel at midnight has, imputably, the knowledge that his driving is likely to cause death (Dhirajia: knowledge "must be attributed to every sane person"). Drunkenness is voluntary (s. 86/§ 22: intoxication self-incurred is no excuse), so the knowledge cannot be dissolved by the self-induced sleep. On this footing: § 105 Part II/s. 304 Part II (up to 10 years).
  • Rash/negligent death — § 106/s. 304A: if the court holds that dozing off negates the appreciation of risk at the relevant moment, the case sinks to negligence (up to 5 years; § 106(2) hit-and-run up to 10 years if he fled without reporting — here he rammed into a pole and was presumably secured at the scene).
  • No intention: nothing suggests an intention to kill or to inflict likely-fatal injury — clauses (a)–(c) of § 101/Firstly–Thirdly are out; Fourthly/(d) ("so imminently dangerous… without excuse") arguably fits driving dead-drunk at speed past a pavement — but "excuse" and the degree-of-probability calibration make the knowledge route (Part II) the realistic conviction, not murder.
The real-world anchor

The facts restate the Salman Khan (State of Maharashtra v. Mohd. Abdul Naim Noor Mohammad Shaikh) litigation — the Bombay High Court's culpable-homicide reasoning (a drunk driver knows pavements are used by sleepers) versus the Supreme Court's ultimate Rash driving intention reading. For the exam: present the § 100(c) vs § 106(1) contest as the live issue, decide on the evidence of the alcohol dosage and the conscious taking of the wheel.

Conclusion

Raghav Ram is liable for the two deaths. The defensible charge: § 100(c) → § 105 Part II (s. 304 Part II) — culpable homicide by knowledge — since he voluntarily drank, knew his condition, and drove anyway; alternatively § 106(1)/304A if the court finds no operating knowledge at the moment of the accident. Additional: driving while intoxicated (s. 185 MV Act), and the BNS's § 106(2) if flight-without-reporting is established.

Q6
A, a police sub-inspector, in exercise of his lawful powers goes to the house of a murder suspect, B, to arrest him. The sub-inspector behaves in an unusually high-handed manner that provokes B. Due to this, B picks up a kitchen knife lying nearby and thrusts it into the abdomen of A resulting in grievous injury and ultimately death of A. During trial B pleads the defence of grave and sudden provocation. Decide.
LL.B. I Term, December 2015 — Q.3(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
The trap in the question — proviso (b)

Exception 1 to § 101/s. 300 itself disables the plea: the provocation must not be "given by anything done in obedience to the law, or by a public servant in the lawful exercise of the powers of such public servant". A goes to arrest a murder suspect in exercise of his lawful powers — the arrest itself cannot, as a matter of law, furnish the provocation.

The second line — the "high-handed manner"

The proviso bars provocation by acts done in lawful exercise of powers; if the public servant exceeds those powers by gratuitous high-handedness (abuse, humiliation, unnecessary violence), the excess is not "in obedience to law", and a grave and sudden provocation arising from it can in principle qualify. B must then satisfy the Nanavati tests: grave and sudden (a reasonable man of B's class losing self-control); fatal act before cooling; not sought by B as an excuse.

Application

Verbal high-handedness accompanying a lawful arrest rarely crosses the "grave" threshold; and the immediate resort to a kitchen knife thrust into the abdomen — an act with an independent, lethal object — evidences retaliatory violence rather than passion: "the doctrine of provocation… is negatived by proof of intention to kill" (Nanavati, on Mancini/Holmes). The intention to inflict the abdominal injury (Virsa Singh step 3) plus its sufficiency (step 4) makes the homicide Thirdly murder; and an attack on an officer executing an arrest also answers § 101/300 without any exception. At highest, if the court finds genuinely grave and sudden provocation from the officer's excess, Exception 1 reduces it to § 105 Part I/s. 304 Part I.

Conclusion

The plea fails: the arrest being lawful, its provocations are barred by proviso (b); the high-handed behaviour, unless proved as an unlawful excess of a grave character, cannot support the exception. B's liability: murder, § 103(1)/s. 302 (Thirdly, Virsa Singh) — reduced at best to § 105 Part I/304 Part I if the excess and loss of self-control are established; the separate offence of voluntarily causing grievous hurt to deter a public servant (s. 332/§ 121(2)) merges in the homicide.

Q7
Explain the rationale behind punishing a person guilty of a strict liability offence in the absence of guilty mind.
LL.B. I Term, December 2015 — Q.1(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (essay)
The general rule — mens rea is presumed

"The fundamental principle of criminal liability is that there must be wrongful act combined with wrongful intention" (Dec 2014 Q1(a) — the companion essay). The Code's structure assumes it: every offence's definition states both the act and the state of mind (Palani Goundan, quoting Mayne: whatever Parliament has not expressly made strict presumes mens rea). The homicide provisions are the perfect illustration — § 100/299 is a menu of mental states (intention×2, knowledge), and the believed-dead cases acquit where the mind's guilt is absent (Palani Goundan).

The exception — strict liability

Some offences dispense with mens rea: the act alone, done in the prohibited circumstances, convicts. Rationales to develop:

  • Public welfare and regulation: the classic strict liabilities (adulterated food, dangerous drugs, pollution, traffic) regulate ongoing, hazardous activity of enterprises; requiring proof of fault would defeat the regulatory scheme (the standard of care must be pitched at "the highest possible" for dangerous trades).
  • Difficulty of proving fault: where the harm is grave and the fault lies hidden in the defendant's internal arrangements (corporate records, technical processes), insisting on mens rea makes enforcement illusory.
  • Deterrence of the highest care: absolute liability forces the actor to internalise the full risk — he cannot plead "I did not mean it" for releasing poison into a river (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, 1987: absolute liability for hazardous enterprise, no Rylands v. Fletcher "reasonable care" escape).
  • Punishment of the risk, not the result: strict liability's principle is prophylactic — the offence is completed by the dangerous act regardless of outcome or intent, so the law marks the outer boundary of permitted conduct.
The critique (which the DU paper rewards)

Hart's objection — punishing the blameless is unjust and ineffective; the compromise: strict liability only for minor/regulatory penalties, not imprisonment; statutory reversal of burden (the defendant proves due diligence — a common Indian drafting pattern); and the constitutional check that severe punishments require fault (the Mithu spirit: punishment proportioned to culpability). In the IPC/BNS itself, true strict liability is virtually absent — even the rash/negligent provisions (§ 106/304A) require rashness or negligence, a fault element; the Code's method is graded mens rea (intention → knowledge → negligence), not its abolition.

Conclusion

Strict liability punishes without guilty mind because the legislature's judgment is that the activity regulated is so hazardous, and the public interest in compelling the highest caution so compelling, that the act itself — with its power to prevent — is enough; the tension with the presumption of innocence is confined to narrow regulatory fields, policed by proportionate penalties and due-diligence defences.

Q8
Write a note on: Grave and Sudden Provocation.
LL.B. I Term, December 2014 — Q.8(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (note)
The doctrine

Exception 1 to § 101/s. 300 reduces murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder where the offender, "whilst deprived of the power of self-control by grave and sudden provocation", kills the provoker (or another by mistake/accident). It is a partial defence: it reduces, never acquits — mitigation, not justification.

Ingredients (state as a list)
  • Grave — measured by the reasonable man "belonging to the same class of society as the accused" (Nanavati principle 1).
  • Sudden — the provocation must strike into a mind not prepared; prior knowledge and brooding destroy the suddenness.
  • Loss of self-control — the killing must be the passion's product, before cooling (Nanavati principle 4: "not after passion had cooled… giving room and scope for premeditation").
  • Target — the person who gave the provocation, or another by mistake/accident (Illustrations (a)–(b); killing the provoker's child intentionally is murder — Illustration (a)).
  • Provisos — provocation (a) sought/voluntarily provoked as an excuse, (b) in obedience to law or by a public servant lawfully acting, (c) in lawful exercise of private defence, is no provocation at all.
The Indian departures (the note's marks)

Words and gestures suffice — the Code rejected the English mere-words rule (Nanavati principle 2; Macaulay's Note); mental background counts — the Khogayi–Boya Munigadu–Balku–Babu Lal line: prolonged agony (a wife's repeated infidelity, sight of the paramour) can ripen into fresh sudden provocation (principle 3); class-of-society standard — the reasonable man is not abstract but situated. English contrasts: Mancini (time and manner of retaliation), Holmes (revenge is provocation's opposite), Duffy ("sudden and temporary loss of self-control").

Application authorities

Nanavati — exception fails on cooling (3-hour gap, false-pretext revolver, preparation); Ghapoo Yadav — contrasted with Exception 4's mere heat of passion; Dec 2015 Q3(a) (police provoke — proviso (b) bars). Close with the reform critique (O'Donovan: the suddenness frame misfits battered women who kill — the cumulative-abuse argument for a graduated doctrine).

Q9
A deals his wife a severe blow on the head with an iron rod. Believing her to be dead, and fearing discovery, he drags the 'body' to a well and throws it in. The autopsy reveals that the wife was alive when thrown and died by drowning. Discuss A's liability. Would your answer differ if A's original attack had been a concerted one with his brother — A restraining the wife while the brother struck repeated blows with an axe declaring 'she shall not live'?
PATTERN PROBLEM — Palani Goundan v. Thavamani (the believed-dead doctrine, both branches)
Branch 1 — the solo husband

This is Palani Goundan exactly. The intention question is judged by the accused's supposed circumstances: the throwing into the well was done under the honest belief that the woman was already dead — an act done on a supposed corpse carries no intention to kill and no knowledge of likely death (the mens rea must relate to a person "alive, or believed by the accused to be alive"). The first act (iron rod to the head) supports at highest an intention to cause grievous injury likely to cause death — § 100(b)/299(b) — but death did not result from it; death resulted from the drowning, which lacks the mental element. Liability: grievous hurt for the blow (s. 326/§ 122) + s. 201/§ 238 (causing disappearance of evidence of the offence); no homicide offence.

Branch 2 — the concerted axe attack

This is Thavamani: where the intention to cause death exists at the inception — the declared object "she shall not live", one holding while the other strikes — the subsequent mistake about the stage of success cannot dissolve the murderous intention; the assault and the disposal are "so closely connected in time and purpose that they must be considered as parts of the same transaction". Both brothers are liable for murder, § 103(1)/s. 302 — the second through common intention (s. 34/§ 3(5)), the transaction's mens rea infecting its fatal conclusion.

The examiner's switch

The difference between the branches is the intention at inception: hurt-only (Palani) vs kill (Thavamani) — and the continuity of the transaction. Note Khandu as the third rail: blows intended to kill, then a fire set later in a distinct purpose — courts have treated the second act as separate.

Q10
X, to destroy a rival shopkeeper Y, leaves a box of poisoned sweets on Y's counter with a note 'Compliments — for your customers'. Y's five-year-old daughter finds the box first, eats the sweets and dies. Y is unharmed. X is charged with her murder. He contends that he neither intended nor knew the child would eat the sweets, and that the person he meant to harm is alive. Decide.
PATTERN PROBLEM — Emperor v. Mushnooru + § 102/s. 301 (transferred malice, efficient cause)
X's two contentions, answered
  • "I did not aim at the child": Mushnooru (majority) — s. 299's words are general: "It is enough if he 'causes the death' of any one, whether the person intended to be killed or any one else". Illustration (a) (the man-trap) and Illustration (b) (the induced bush-shooter) both convict where the design kills a person outside its target. § 102/s. 301 then fixes the grade: the culpable homicide is of the description it would have been had the intended victim died — X's intention toward Y (death) makes the child's death murder.
  • "Her own act of eating broke the chain": the majority's efficient-cause doctrine — the poisoner's act was the causa causans; the child's innocent act of taking the bait is the very contingency the trap presupposed (Agnes Gore, Saunders, Michael at English law: poison placed for one, eaten by another — murder). Contributory acts of the victim do not exculpate.
The honest boundary (Illustration (c))

X will cite the fowl-shooter — but that illustration spares only the actor with no homicidal intention or knowledge at all. X placed poison for a human being's customers — an act with the intention of causing (at least) a death-like harm to Y's trade through Y's circle; the design was homicidal. The boundary case would be poison set genuinely for rats, unknowable to children — there the fowl-shooter logic (unlawful act, no homicidal mind) might reduce murder to a negligence/hurt outcome — but the note 'for your customers' destroys that refuge.

Conclusion

X is guilty of the child's murder — § 103(1)/s. 302 read with § 102/s. 301 (the grade measured by his intention toward Y), on Mushnooru's majority reasoning; Sundara Aiyar J.'s dissent (intervening agency; § 301 confined to intended-victim-survives cases) is the contrary academic view to note and reject as the minority.

Q11
W, terrorised by her husband's midnight threats of 'consequences', flees the house at dawn with her infant child. Seeing the husband give chase and hearing him shout that he would 'finish' her, she leaps into a river with the baby in her arms. Bystanders save the mother; the baby dies. In a separate case, W2, persecuted for years by her in-laws, announces to the neighbourhood that she is 'leaving with the children', and throws herself and her three children into a canal; the children die. Compare the criminal liability of W and W2.
PATTERN PROBLEM — Dhirajia v. Gyarsibai (Fourthly, "without any excuse")
The common ground

Both mothers knew — imputably to any sane person (Dhirajia's principle) — that leaping into water with children "can, but for a miracle, have only one conclusion": the knowledge element of Fourthly is complete in both.

The decisive difference — the excuse gate
  • W (the flight case): the leap was in panic from a pursuing, threatening husband — "She feared her husband and she had reason to fear him… she had excuse, and that that excuse was panic or fright" (Dhirajia). Intention is excluded (panic ousts the capacity to reason); the excuse excludes Fourthly; the knowledge clause of § 100(c) alone remains → CHnAM, third degree — § 105 Part II/s. 304 Part II (up to 10 years). Her own survival negates any question of § 108/309 complicity in self-death; and no conscious endeavour to die existed.
  • W2 (the announced leap): the prior announcement and the collection of the children evidence deliberation, not panic — measured "from the standpoint of a reasonable and legally sane person", persecution by in-laws is no excuse for taking three children's lives (Gyarsibai) → murder — § 103(1)/s. 302 via Fourthly (with the Gyarsibai court's own humane recommendation of commutation as the sentencing note).
The principle to extract

Fourthly's "without any excuse" is a real, fact-specific safety valve: panic engendered by real and present terror excuses; sorrow, harassment and resolve do not. The line between the two mothers is the line between 304-II and 302 — the exam's favourite calibration.

Q12
On a rumour spread on the village WhatsApp group that members of a nomadic community are child-lifters, a crowd of about forty gathers, drags S — a visitor waiting at the bus stand — from the shelter, and beats him to death with sticks and rods 'to teach the outsiders a lesson'. Six identified participants are prosecuted. The defence argues that no one can say whose blow was fatal, that the mob believed the rumour, and that in any case there was no pre-existing conspiracy. Analyse the liability under the BNS.
PATTERN PROBLEM — § 103(2) + Tehseen Poonawalla (the post-2023 lynching answer)
The charge architecture
  • § 103(2) (mob lynching): a group of five or more persons acting in concert commits murder on the ground of, inter alia, community, place of birth or "any other similar ground" — each member liable to death or life. The identity-ground element is satisfied: 'teach the outsiders a lesson' is a community/place-of-birth ground; the rumour (child-lifting) is the trigger, the identity the selection rationale.
  • "Whose blow was fatal" is no defence: the section's "each member… acting in concert" design eliminates the attribution problem that s. 34/149 (§ 3(5)/§ 190) machinery would otherwise have to solve — though those sections ride along: the forty-strong crowd is an unlawful assembly (§ 189) with the common object of assault; every identified participant is constructively liable (§ 190) for the death done in prosecution of it.
  • Mistake of fact (the rumour): no mistake vitiates the mob's guilt — the belief that S was a child-lifter, even if genuine, would at highest make the object an unlawful one (vigilante 'punishment' is not private defence: apprehension of a crime is not a crime in progress — §§ 34–35/§§ 32–33's immediacy and proportionality; there was no attack by S at all). Punishment is the State's monopoly — Tehseen: "hyperbolic protests against crime cannot be de facto justice".
  • No pre-existing conspiracy needed: concert — acting together on the spot — suffices; conspiracy (§ 61(2)) would be an additional count, not a prerequisite.
The Tehseen overlay (essay marks)

The incident is exactly the pathology Tehseen addressed: preventive (nodal officers, hot-spots, rumour-control, dispersal, § 196/153A FIRs against the rumour-mongers), remedial (immediate FIR, monitored investigation, compensation), punitive (departmental action against police who failed to prevent). The answer should note that the WhatsApp forwarders can separately face § 196 (promoting enmity) and § 353(2)-type offences, and that the police failure engages the Arumugam Servai accountability line.

Conclusion

The six identified participants are guilty of murder under § 103(2) (each liable to death or life) — alternatively and cumulatively § 103(1) with § 3(5)/§ 190; the rumour is no defence; the absence of a prior conspiracy is immaterial.

Q13
"All murder is culpable homicide, but not all culpable homicide is murder." Explain with reference to the scheme of BNS §§ 100–105 (IPC §§ 299–304), the tests the courts employ, and the partial defences that reduce murder to culpable homicide not amounting to murder.
CORE ESSAY — the unit's guaranteed question (genus-species, Punnayya framework)
Introduction — the double definition

Macaulay's Code defines the genus (culpable homicide, § 100/299) and the species (murder, § 101/300) separately — statutory recognition that the moral guilt of killing is graded. The classic statement — Punnayya: "'Culpable homicide' is the genus and 'murder' is its species… The Code practically recognises three degrees of culpable homicide": first (murder, § 103/302), second (§ 105 Part I/304 Part I), third (§ 105 Part II/304 Part II).

Body I — the elements of the genus

§ 100/299's three clauses (intention to cause death; intention to cause injury likely to cause death; knowledge of likely death), the three explanations (egg-shell victim; neglected-wounds; born-alive), and the actus reus (causing death — efficient cause: Mushnooru; believed-dead exceptions: Palani Goundan/Thavamani; wrong victim: § 102/301).

Body II — the elevation to murder

The four clauses of § 101/300 and the keyword shifts (Punnayya's table): (a) intention to kill; (b) intention + knowledge of the particular victim's frailty; (c) intention to inflict the injury + objective sufficiency — Virsa Singh's four steps (the intention is only as to the injury; the sufficiency "purely objective and inferential… nothing to do with the intention"; cumulative sufficiency per Punnayya); (d) knowledge approximating practical certainty + without excuse (Dhirajia/Gyarsibai). Knowledge must "approximate to a practical certainty" to graduate from § 100(c) to § 101(d).

Body III — the descent: Exceptions 1–5

The five partial defences (grave-sudden provocation — Nanavati's four principles; good-faith excess of private defence; public servant exceeding powers; sudden fight — Ghapoo Yadav's conjunctive ingredients, Sukhbir Singh's 10-year norm; consent of an adult) — each converts murder into CHnAM, § 105 Part I. Contrast the general defences (§§ 14–44/ss. 76–106) which acquit outright.

Body IV — the residual grade below the genus

Where even § 100's mental element is absent, rash/negligent death — § 106/304A — or pure accident/insanity (general exceptions) takes over: the Dec 2014 bus problem (Q3) and the Raghav Ram problem (Q5) illustrate the § 100(c)-vs-§ 106 line.

Conclusion

The scheme is a ladder of culpability — intention to kill → intention + knowledge → intention + objective sufficiency → knowledge without excuse → knowledge → rashness/negligence — each rung with its own punishment (§ 103 → § 105 I → § 105 II → § 106). The lawyer's task (Punnayya's three-stage method) is to locate the facts on the ladder: cause → § 100 → § 101 clause → exception. Every problem in this unit resolves by walking those stages.

9.5  Traps the examiner sets

  • "Likely" appears in both § 100(b) and § 101(b) — the difference is the offender's knowledge of the particular victim's frailty (the spleen illustration), not the probability level.
  • Fourthly needs "without any excuse" — an element, not rhetoric: Dhirajia's panic was an excuse; Gyarsibai's resolve was not.
  • Exception 1 vs 4 — total loss of self-control vs heat of passion on equal footing; provoker's person vs mutual fight.
  • § 102 fixes grade, not guilt — the guilt comes from § 100 (any victim); § 102 only measures the degree by the intended victim.
  • Believed-dead is two doctrines — hurt-intent (Palani: no homicide) vs kill-intent + one transaction (Thavamani: murder).
  • 304A is not a lesser included offence of 302 — it is a separate rashness/negligence offence; the moment knowledge of likely death exists, it is displaced.
  • Excuses reduce to Part I, never acquit — and Exception 5 needs the victim to be 18+.
  • BNS minimums: § 105 Part I now starts at 5 years; § 104 speaks of "remainder of natural life"; § 106 raised the ceiling to 5 (10 for hit-and-run).
