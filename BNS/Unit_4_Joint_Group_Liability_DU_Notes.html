0.1  The unit's central idea

The DU course material introduces the unit with this passage — quotable in any answer :

"Provisions for providing for group liability in crimes including sections 34 and 149 of the IPC are exceptions to the general rule of criminal liability that a man should be held liable for his own criminal acts and not for those of others. These provisions providing for vicarious liability/group liability are intended to deter people from committing offences in groups and to spare the prosecution [the burden] to prove specific actus reus of each member of the group." — Unit 8 (Joint and Group Liability), DU Course Material, Law of Crimes–I

Two liabilities, two logics: common intention (s. 34 IPC / s. 3(5) BNS) is participatory — a shared mind plus a shared criminal act, each answering for the whole; common object (s. 149 IPC / s. 190 BNS) is membership-based — the unlawful assembly as such becomes an engine of the offence, and every member answers for what it did in prosecution of its object. Both are rules of liability, not offences — they ride on an offence ("302 r/w 34", "302 r/w 149" — under BNS, 103 r/w 3(5), 103 r/w 190).

The Joint/Group Liability Framework — Exceptions to Individual Liability

Why the law abandons "each man for his own act" in group crime; the family of liability provisions; and how the two stars of this unit — common intention and common object — divide the field.

1.1  The principle and its exceptions

The bedrock rule of criminal law is individual responsibility: actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea — and the act and the mind must be yours. Group crime breaks the rule's premise: when five men with one object attack, or three confederates execute one plan, the "criminal act" is a single composite event produced by many hands. Holding each actor only for his own blow would make conviction impossible whenever the evidence cannot attribute the fatal injury to a particular fist (the Maina Singh problem), and would under-price the social danger of concerted violence. Hence two statutory exceptions, both older than the Constitution and both carried into the BNS:

  • Common intention — s. 34 IPC / s. 3(5) BNS: when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each is liable for it as if it were done by him alone. The psychological glue: a shared, pre-conceived (or on-the-spot formed) design plus participation.
  • Common object — s. 149 IPC / s. 190 BNS: if any member of an unlawful assembly commits an offence in prosecution of the common object of the assembly — or such as the members knew to be likely to be committed in its prosecution — every member of that assembly at the time is guilty of it. The structural glue: membership of a five-plus assembly with a proscribed object.

Both are, as Sethi J. put it in Suresh (quoting a century of authority), rules of evidence and vicarious liability, not substantive offences: a charge reads "302 r/w 34" or "302 r/w 149" (today, 103 r/w 3(5) or 103 r/w 190 BNS), never s. 34 or s. 149 alone.

Common Intention — BNS § 3(5) ↔ IPC § 34

The text and its essential ingredients; the cognate family (ss. 3(6)–(9) / IPC 35–38); common intention distinguished from similar intention; and the great overt-act debate settled in Suresh.

2.1  Text and essentials

BNS § 3(5) / IPC § 34 — Acts done by several persons in furtherance of common intention

When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone.

IPC § 33 (for the record): the word "act" denotes as well a series of acts as a single act; "omission" as well a series of omissions as a single omission — so the "criminal act" of s. 34 is the whole composite transaction, not any one blow.

EssentialExplanation (authority in brackets)
1. A criminal actOne offence-result produced by a plurality — "a single act or the conglomeration of a series of acts" (Suresh, Thomas J.); includes illegal omissions (IPC 32 / BNS 3(4))
2. Done by several personsNot by one person alone — Thomas J.'s first "indispensable postulate"; the section is not a vicarious hook for a lone doer's secret well-wishers
3. In furtherance of the common intention of allA pre-arranged plan (Mahboob Shah) — which may form "previously or in the course of occurrence and on the spur of the moment" (Suresh, Sethi J.), but must precede the act done (Shatrughan Patar)
4. Participation of the accusedSome act — overt or covert, even a facilitating omission — with nexus to the offence; presence alone may not suffice (Suresh, Thomas J.; Pavitri Devi)
5. Proof by inferenceDirect proof of prior concert rare; common intention "a question of fact… proved mainly as a matter of inference from the circumstances" (Suresh); incriminating facts "must be incompatible with the innocence of the accused and incapable of explanation on any other reasonable hypothesis" (Pandurang)

2.2  The cognate family — BNS § 3(6)–(9) / IPC §§ 35–38

Suresh calls these "one cognate group wherein different positions when more than one person [is] participating in the commission of one criminal act are adumbrated". The BNS preserves all four as General Explanations:

BNS § 3(6) / IPC § 35 — Act criminal only by knowledge or intention

Whenever an act, which is criminal only by reason of its being done with a criminal knowledge or intention, is done by several persons, each of such persons who joins in the act with such knowledge or intention is liable for the act in the same manner as if the act were done by him alone with that knowledge or intention.

Contrast with § 3(5)/34: no common intention needed — it is enough that each joiner has his own criminal knowledge or intention (Suresh, ¶ 17: the section "differs from Section 34 only regarding one postulate").

BNS § 3(7) / IPC § 36 — Effect caused partly by act, partly by omission

Wherever the causing of a certain effect, or an attempt to cause that effect, by an act or by an omission, is an offence, it is to be understood that the causing of that effect partly by an act and partly by an omission is the same offence.

Illustration. A intentionally causes Z's death, partly by illegally omitting to give Z food, and partly by beating Z. A has committed murder.

BNS § 3(8) / IPC § 37 — Co-operation by doing one of several acts

When an offence is committed by means of several acts, whoever intentionally co-operates in the commission of that offence by doing any one of those acts, either singly or jointly with any other person, commits that offence.

Illustrations. (a) A and B agree to murder Z by severally giving him small doses of poison; Z dies of the combined doses — both guilty of murder though their acts were separate. (b) A and B, joint jailors on alternate six-hour shifts, each illegally omit to feed Z; Z dies of hunger — both guilty of murder. (c) A starves Z and is dismissed; B, without collusion, continues the starvation — B guilty of murder, A only of attempt (no co-operation).

BNS § 3(9) / IPC § 38 — Persons engaged in one criminal act may be guilty of different offences

Where several persons are engaged or concerned in the commission of a criminal act, they may be guilty of different offences by means of that act.

Illustration. A kills Z under grave provocation (would be culpable homicide not amounting to murder); B, without provocation and with ill-will, assists — B is guilty of murder, A only of culpable homicide.

Why this family matters in the exam : it shows that s. 34/3(5) is only one cell of a matrix. § 3(8)/37 punishes the divider of labour; § 3(6)/35 the parallel knowledge; § 3(9)/38 the divergent blameworthiness of confederates in one act. A "discuss s. 34" answer that opens with this map before diving into Suresh covers twice the syllabus in the same words.

2.3  Common intention ≠ similar intention

The distinction, pressed by the Privy Council in Mahboob Shah and repeatedly affirmed (including in Suresh, ¶¶ 53–54, and Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab, AIR 1963 SC 174), is exam-favourite territory:

"The common intention implies a pre-arranged plan but in a given case it may develop at the spur of the moment in the course of the commission of the offence. Such common intention which developed at the spur of the moment is different from the similar intention actuated by a number of persons at the same time. The distinction between 'common intention' and 'similar intention' may be fine but is nonetheless a real one and if overlooked may lead to miscarriage of justice." — Suresh v. State of U.P. (DU CM), ¶ 53

2.4  The overt-act debate — the two opinions in Suresh

Does an accused need to have done an overt act to be caught by s. 34/3(5)? Unlawful Assembly & Common Object — BNS §§ 189–191 ↔ IPC §§ 141–149

The five objects that make an assembly unlawful; who is a member; the punishment ladder; the two limbs of § 190/149 (the Mizaji doctrine); and rioting.

3.1  The unlawful assembly — BNS § 189(1) / IPC § 141

BNS § 189(1) / IPC § 141 — Unlawful assembly

An assembly of five or more persons is designated an "unlawful assembly", if the common object of the persons composing that assembly is —

(a) to overawe by criminal force, or show of criminal force, the Central Government or any State Government or Parliament or the Legislature of any State, or any public servant in the exercise of the lawful power of such public servant; or

(b) to resist the execution of any law, or of any legal process; or

(c) to commit any mischief or criminal trespass, or other offence; or

(d) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to any person, to take or obtain possession of any property, or to deprive any person of the enjoyment of a right of way, or of the use of water or other incorporeal right of which he is in possession or enjoyment, or to enforce any right or supposed right; or

(e) by means of criminal force, or show of criminal force, to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or to omit to do what he is legally entitled to do.

Explanation. An assembly which was not unlawful when it assembled, may subsequently become an unlawful assembly.

BNS changes: the IPC's Firstly–Fifthly become clauses (a)–(e); clause (d) adds the words "or to enforce any right or supposed right" — squarely covering the Mizaji-type land-grab where the assembly claims a title; and the Explanation codifies the case-law that an assembly can degenerate into unlawfulness mid-course.

"Common object" : the object must be common to the assembly — but, per Mizaji (¶ 6), "it is not necessary that there should be a pre-concert in the sense of a meeting of the members of the unlawful assembly as to the common object; it is enough if it is adopted by all the members and is shared by all of them". The object is deduced from the members' weapons, words and conduct — the "go away otherwise they would be finished" testimony in Mizaji is the model.

3.2  Who is a member — and the punishment ladder

BNS § 189(2) / IPC §§ 142–143 — Member of an unlawful assembly

Whoever, being aware of facts which render any assembly an unlawful assembly, intentionally joins that assembly, or continues in it, is said to be a member of an unlawful assembly and such member shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both.

The two mental elements: (i) awareness of the facts that make the assembly unlawful (a person who unknowingly drifts into a crowd is not a member), and (ii) intentional joining or continuing. An innocent or accidental presence is protected — but the moment a member learns the object and stays, membership is complete.

3.3  Common object bites — BNS § 190 / IPC § 149 and the two limbs

BNS § 190 / IPC § 149 — Member of unlawful assembly guilty of offence committed in prosecution of common object

If an offence is committed by any member of an unlawful assembly in prosecution of the common object of that assembly, or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, every person who, at the time of the committing of that offence, is a member of the same assembly, is guilty of that offence.

The BNS reproduces IPC § 149 verbatim — the Mizaji doctrine of 1959 continues untouched under § 190.

ElementContent (authority in brackets)
1. Unlawful assemblyThe predicate — five-plus persons, an object within § 189(1)/(141)
2. Offence by a memberAny offence — from rioting to murder — committed by any member
3. Limb 1 — "in prosecution of" the common objectThe offence is the direct outcome of, done in carrying forward, the common object — immediately connected with it and not independent of it (Mizaji)
4. Limb 2 — "knew to be likely"Even if the offence is not in prosecution of the common object, membership attracts liability where all members knew it was likely to be committed in the prosecution of that object. "Knew" imports actual knowledge — the probabilities must be so high that knowledge may be presumed; a bare possibility is not enough (Mizaji)
5. Membership at the timeOne must be a member at the time of the offence — the unexplained presence of an accused with the armed group at the scene of attack is enough (Baladin v. State of U.P., AIR 1956 SC 181)
6. "Common object" ≠ "common intention"Object may be formed on the spot and need only be adopted and shared; intention requires prior concert or on-the-spot consensus plus participation (Mizaji ¶ 6; contrast Suresh)
"It is not necessary that there should be a pre-concert in the sense of a meeting of the members of the unlawful assembly as to the common object; it is enough if it is adopted by all the members and is shared by all of them… The expression 'in prosecution of the common object' [means] the offence committed was immediately connected with the common object of the assembly… and was not independent of it. The other part of the section is 'or such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object'… [T]he converse is however not true and it cannot be said that because the offence committed was not in prosecution of the common object, it would not fall under the second part." — Mizaji & Anr. v. State of Uttar Pradesh (DU CM, 2023)
Mizaji's illustrations of the second limb (memorise both) : (1) The abduction case — an armed body assembles to take a woman by force; violence and murder must be held to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object, so all are guilty of murder though the object was only abduction. (2) The land-possession case — an armed assembly to take forcible possession of land; shooting dead the resister is not in prosecution of possession-taking, but the members' knowledge that deadly violence was likely makes every member guilty of murder. Sabid Ali and Kapur C.J.'s spectrum make this the core of the exam answer on § 190/149.
Chikkarange Gowda v. State of Mysore (AIR 1956 SC 104, extracted in Mizaji): in a charge under § 149/190 the court must specify which limb is invoked — "if the latter part is invoked, the charge must also say that it was known to the members that the offence was likely to be committed in prosecution of the common object". The converse rule (per above) was settled against the Mysore view that once the offence is outside the first part the second part cannot apply. Cite this when discussing charge-framing defects on common object.

When the Co-Accused Walk — the Maina Singh Doctrine

Can one man be convicted "in furtherance of the common intention" or "in prosecution of the common object" of confederates who have been acquitted? The five-plus head-count, the unnamed-participant exception, and why Maina Singh's murder became grievous hurt.

4.1  The problem stated

Sections 3(5)/34 and 190/149 are relational — they distribute one criminal act among several doers. What, then, when the courtroom empties? A, B, C, D and E are charged as the unlawful assembly; B–E are acquitted for doubt; can A still be convicted of murder read with § 190/149 — or with § 3(5)/34 — on the reasoning that "someone must have been with him"? Maina Singh answers with a rule of remarkable practicality, built on a ladder of authorities:

AuthorityContribution to the doctrine
R. v. Plummer [(1902) 2 KB 339]Three indicted for conspiracy; two acquitted — conviction of the third "would be clearly illegal": the verdict finding an agreement with persons found not to have agreed is repugnant (following Harrison v. Errington, 1627, where a riot verdict against one of three, two being not guilty, was held void)
Topandas v. State of Bombay (AIR 1956 SC 33)Approves Plummer for India: of four named conspirators three acquitted → the fourth's conviction under 120-B "clearly illegal"
Mohan Singh v. State of Punjab (AIR 1963 SC 174)The pivot: if charge and evidence are confined to the named persons and acquittals leave fewer than five, § 149 cannot be invoked; but if evidence shows unidentified others swelled the assembly, acquittal of the named few does not displace § 149
Krishna Govind Patil v. State of Maharashtra (AIR 1963 SC 1413)Upholds Mohan Singh: the unnamed-participant conclusion "is really based on evidence" — court witnesses, defence witnesses, circumstances may prove the extra bodies

"So when the other four co-accused have been given the benefit of doubt and have been acquitted, it would not be permissible to take the view that there must have been some other person along with the appellant Maina Singh in causing the injuries to the deceased. It was as such not permissible to invoke Section 149 or Section 34 I.P.C." — Maina Singh v. State of Rajasthan (DU CM), ¶ 19
The exception that saves § 149 : acquittals destroy the assembly only when the prosecution case is a closed list. Where "evidence may nevertheless show that the unlawful assembly consisted of some other persons as well who were not identified and so not named" (Mohan Singh), the head-count of five is satisfied by the unidentified deadwood, and the convicted minority can still be hung with § 190/149. The burden is on the evidence, not on judicial speculation — "it was also possible that someone else might have been with him" is precisely the kind of non-categorical finding Maina Singh rejects.
Why murder fell to grievous hurt : once 302/34 fell, Dr Shanker Lal's testimony became decisive — the injuries ascribed to Maina Singh (gunshot + blunt) were collectively but not individually sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death, and the possibly-fatal sharp-weapon injury (No. 12) could not be imputed to him. Conviction altered to IPC 326 / BNS § 118(2) (voluntarily causing grievous hurt by dangerous weapons or means) — 10 years' RI. A perfect illustration that constructive liability is not a cosmetic label: it decides whether a man hangs or serves a decade.

5.1  Suresh & Anr. v. State of Uttar Pradesh

(2001) 3 SCC 673 — Three-Judge Bench (Thomas J.; Sethi J. concurring, with Agrawal J.) DU CASE MATERIAL DU LLB CRIMINAL LAW-II · CRIME-4 CM 2025 · UNIT 4

Facts

Land-hunger between brothers. A-1 Suresh claimed a strip of land adjacent to the house of his brother Ramesh; Ramesh clung to it, and the animosity "eventually grew alarmingly wild". On the night of 5 October 1996, armed assailants entered Ramesh's house as the family slept and hacked the entire household to pieces — six dead: a one-year-old whose skull was cut in two, a three-year-old, nine-year-old Monisha, the mother Ganga Devi (six injuries, skull broken to pieces), and Ramesh himself with four neck wounds "just short of decapitation". The sole survivor was seven-year-old Jitendra (PW 3), who woke with his father's blood in his mouth, and told the trial court he saw his uncle Suresh and brother-in-law A-2 Ramji "acting like demons, cutting the sleeping children with axe and chopper", and his aunt — Suresh's wife and Ramji's sister — A-3 Pavitri Devi clutching his mother's hair and "yelling like a demoness in thirst for the blood of the entire family". PW 1 (Lalji, uncle) and PW 2 (Amar Singh, neighbour) corroborated the presence of the three — but attributed no overt act to Pavitri Devi beyond her presence on the road near the scene.

The Sessions Court convicted Suresh and Ramji of murder (302/34) and sentenced them to death, and convicted Pavitri Devi of murder with the aid of s. 34. The Allahabad High Court confirmed the death sentences but acquitted Pavitri Devi, accepting PW 3's testimony only so far as it was corroborated by PWs 1–2. All three outcomes reached the Supreme Court: the two condemned men's appeals, and the State's appeal against Pavitri Devi's acquittal. A two-Judge Bench, doubting "whether and to what extent" s. 34 could be invoked, referred the matter to a larger Bench.

Issues

  • What is the true scope of s. 34 IPC (BNS § 3(5)): is an overt act by each accused necessary, or is participation enough — and can presence alone suffice?
  • Is "common intention" different from "similar intention"? Can common intention form on the spot?
  • Was Pavitri Devi's acquittal sustainable, given her admitted presence near the scene?
  • (For the condemned:) did the case fall in the rarest-of-rare category?

Arguments

For the appellants (K.B. Sinha, Senior Counsel): on the evidence of PWs 1–2 he could not dent the murder case, so he concentrated on (i) Pavitri Devi — her acquittal was justified because the exaggerated version of PW 3 about her catching the mother's hair was unsupported by PWs 1–2, and mere presence at the scene cannot attract s. 34; and (ii) the death sentence — the case did not fall in the category compelling the extreme penalty.

For the State: the massacre was pre-planned butchery of a family over land — the worst of the rarest of rare cases; and Pavitri Devi's presence with the killers, her relationship to both, and her exhortation showed shared common intention; the High Court's acquittal should be reversed.

Held — Thomas J. (for the Court)

(a) The anatomy of s. 34. The section must be read with ss. 32–33 (acts include illegal omissions; an act is a series of acts): "one criminal act, composed of more than one act, can be committed by more than one person, and if such commission is in furtherance of the common intention of all of them, each would be liable". He recast a caricature section — "when a criminal act is done by one person in furtherance of the common intention of several persons…" — to show that s. 34 is not such a vicarious provision: "all the co-accused have also done something to constitute the commission of a criminal act". Two indispensable postulates: (1) the criminal act was done not by one person; (2) each individual act cumulatively resulting in the offence was done in furtherance of the common intention of all.

(b) Participation is the sine qua non — but not presence. The accused "should have done some act which has nexus with the offence. Such an act need not be very substantial" — guarding the scene suffices; the act "need not necessarily be overt, even if it is only a covert act it is enough"; even an illegal omission can qualify. Physical presence is not even necessary: the co-accused may "remain a little away and supply weapons… by catapulting them", or — the celebrated modern illustration — "oversee[] the actions from a distance through binoculars and give instructions to the other accused through mobile phones as to how effectively the common intention can be implemented". But mere presence is not enough: the participant who does nothing, "who only keeps the common intention in his mind", cannot be convicted with the aid of s. 34 — "such a person can be convicted only under Section 120-B or Section 109" (conspiracy or abetment).

(c) The case-law ladder. Thomas J. surveys the entire arc — Barendra Kumar Ghosh (the counter-boy: "they also serve who only stand and wait"; "Section 34 deals with the doing of separate acts, similar or diverse, by several persons"; the abettor's presence converts abetment into the offence — s. 114 Evidence Act, "evidentiary, not punitory"); Mahboob Shah (common intention "implies a pre-arranged plan… done in concert pursuant to the pre-arranged plan"); Pandurang (the common intention must be incompatible with innocence and incapable of explanation on any other reasonable hypothesis; similar intention ≠ common intention); Shreekantiah (passive participation — "standing by a door… readiness to play his part"); Tukaram ("overt or covert, by active presence or by distant direction"); Lalai (spear-holder at the scene); Ramaswami (guard-duty; "simultaneous consensus of the minds"); Rambilas; Iftikhar Khan (four walking together — two with pistols, two with lathis — firing and fleeing: prior concert inferred); Krishnan v. Kerala ("establishment of an overt act is not a requirement of law to allow Section 34 to operate inasmuch as the section gets attracted when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of common intention of all"); Surender Chauhan (the two-factor test: common intention + participation).

(d) Application to Pavitri Devi. PW 3's version that she caught her mother's hair was exaggerated and uncorroborated by PWs 1–2; when the witnesses arrived she was standing on the road while the men were busy inside. Mere presence, "in the absence of other evidence, direct or circumstantial", cannot make her guilty with the aid of s. 34. Acquittal affirmed.

Held — Sethi J. (concurring, with Agrawal J.) — the doctrinal counterweight

Sethi J. re-read the entire case law (with full extracts of Barendra Kumar Ghosh, Mahboob Shah and R. v. Salmon — three marksmen negligently firing at a target, one killing a boy: "the death resulted from the action of the three and they are all liable") and insisted: (i) s. 34 is a rule of evidence, not a substantive offence — it only lays down the principle of joint liability; (ii) no overt act is necessary: "it is not the requirement of law that he should have actually done something" if common intention is proved — for "the essence of Section 34 is simultaneous consensus of the minds… such consensus can be developed at the spot"; a pre-arranged plan is not confined to a prior meeting of minds — it "may develop in the course of the occurrence"; (iii) in cases of violence physical presence at the actual commission — for the purpose of facilitating the crime — "is itself tantamount to actual participation", and "culpability under Section 34 cannot be excluded by mere distance from the scene of occurrence"; (iv) common intention is provable only by inference from conduct, and incriminating facts must be incompatible with innocence; (v) common ≠ similar intention — "the distinction… may be fine but is nonetheless a real one and if overlooked may lead to miscarriage of justice". On Pavitri Devi he agreed on the facts (common intention not proved) — but with the crucial caveat: had the prosecution proved her common intention, "she could not have been acquitted of the charge… only on the ground that she had actually not done any overt act."

Decision

Appeals of Suresh and Ramji dismissed (death sentence maintained); State's appeal against Pavitri Devi's acquittal also dismissed. Both opinions together give the modern, complete statement of s. 34.

Principles evolved

  • Participation in the criminal act — overt, covert, or by illegal omission — is the sine qua non of s. 34; presence at the scene is neither necessary nor sufficient.
  • Common intention may be a pre-arranged plan or develop on the spot — but must precede the act done; similar intention is not common intention.
  • S. 34 is a rule of evidence/rule of joint liability, not a substantive offence — it creates no separate punishment.
  • The non-participant sharer of the design falls to be dealt with under conspiracy (120-B / BNS § 61) or abetment (109 / BNS § 49), not s. 34.
  • Mere presence of a person — especially one not implicated by reliable evidence — cannot sustain a s. 34 conviction (Pavitri Devi).

DU-exam hooks

"They also serve who only stand and wait" (Barendra Kumar Ghosh, via Suresh) · the binoculars-and-mobile-phone illustration · "participation in the crime in furtherance of the common intention is a sine qua non" · "no overt act is necessary… the essence of Section 34 is simultaneous consensus of the minds" · "the distinction between common and similar intention may be fine but is nonetheless a real one" · the Surender Chauhan two-factor test. This is the case to lead with in ANY common-intention answer.

5.2  Maina Singh v. State of Rajasthan

(1976) 2 SCC 827 : AIR 1976 SC 1084 — Shinghal J. DU CASE MATERIAL DU LLB CRIMINAL LAW-II · CRIME-4 CM 2025 · UNIT 4

Facts

Smuggling suspicions poisoned the relations between Maina Singh and his neighbour Amar Singh of chak No. 77 GB, Ganganagar (Rajasthan), who — Maina suspected — was informing the police about his smuggling. On 29 June 1967, at sunset, Amar Singh, his son Ajeet Singh (PW 2) and the mason Isar Ram (PW 3) went to the diggi (pond) in murabba 35 for a bath. Maina Singh and his three sons Hardeep, Jeet and Puran, along with Narain Singh, arrived — Maina with a 12-bore gun, Puran with a takua, the others with gandasis (sickles). Maina fired at Amar Singh, missed, but hit Ajeet in the legs (12 gunshot wounds in all); Ajeet dived into a dry watercourse and then ran six miles to lodge the report at 10 p.m. Amar Singh fled to the sugarcane field crying for help; the five followed; Maina fired again and Amar fell; the others gave gandasi blows and Maina struck a blow or two with the butt of his gun, which broke — its pieces were later recovered near the body, with the empty cartridges. Amar Singh died on the spot.

The charge named all five as the unlawful assembly (302/149). The Sessions Judge disbelieved the widow (PW 7, unmentioned in the FIR) and found the ocular testimony of PWs 2–3 inconsistent as to which of the four associates did what: "it cannot be ascertained which one of the accused was with him and it was also possible that someone else might have been with him" — so Hardeep, Jeet, Puran and Narain were acquitted on benefit of doubt. But PWs 2–3 were consistent against Maina Singh, and the cartridges, the recovered gun with its missing butt, the medical evidence and his absconding clinched his guilt: convicted of 302/34 (life) for Amar Singh's murder and 326 (3 years RI + ₹ 100) for the injuries to Ajeet. The Rajasthan High Court dismissed both appeals — the State's against the four acquittals and Maina's against his conviction.

Issue

Can a single accused remain convicted of murder read with s. 34 (or s. 149) when all the co-accused through whom the common intention/assembly was constituted have been acquitted — the trial court itself having found the participation of "someone else" unproved and unidentifiable?

Arguments

For Maina Singh (Harbans Singh): he did not contest the evidence of his own violence; but with the four co-accused acquitted and the Sessions Judge himself holding that no conviction under 302/149 or 148 was possible, it "could not be held, in law, that they formed an unlawful assembly or that any offence was committed… in prosecution of the common object". A fortiori, the court could not treat the crime as done "in furtherance of the common intention of the other accused" when those accused were no other than the four who stood acquitted. All that was permissible was to convict him of what he did "in his individual capacity, without reference to the participation of any other person".

For the State (S.M. Jain): the acquittals rested only on benefit of doubt, and the trial court had recorded that "one or more of the accused persons or some other person might have participated in the crime along with Maina Singh" — that finding of possible companionship sufficed to sustain the common-intention conviction.

Held — the doctrine and its ladder

Shinghal J. dismantled the State's argument by marching through the authorities (the full ladder is set out in § 4 above): Plummer and Harrison v. Errington (a verdict against one of three rioters, two being not guilty, is void) → Topandas (approving Plummer for conspiracy) → Mohan Singh (the closed-list rule: if charge and evidence are confined to the named accused and acquittals leave fewer than five, s. 149 cannot be invoked — but unidentified others may save it) → Krishna Govind Patil (the unnamed-participant conclusion "is really based on evidence") → Ram Bilas Singh (the definitive restatement) → Yashwant and Sukh Ram (distinguished, no departure) → Dharam Pal (evidence showed the attackers were five-plus; no conflict). Applying the rule: the charge throughout named only the five; the evidence ran no wider; there was no direct or circumstantial proof of any unnamed participant; the trial court's "someone else might have been with him" was a suspicion, not a categorical finding. Therefore, with the four acquitted, "it was as such not permissible to invoke Section 149 or Section 34 I.P.C."

Consequence on sentence: Dr Shanker Lal (PW 5) testified that the injuries attributed to Maina (gunshot and blunt-weapon) were collectively — but not individually — sufficient in the ordinary course of nature to cause death; the possibly fatal sharp-weapon injury (No. 12, frontal-bone fracture) could not be imputed to him. So murder fell; but injury No. 26 (gunshot) was grievous → conviction altered to s. 326 IPC (voluntarily causing grievous hurt by dangerous means — now BNS § 118(2)) with 10 years' rigorous imprisonment; his separate 326 conviction for Ajeet's injuries (3 years RI) was already unchallenged and stood.

Principles evolved

  • Where the charge and the evidence are confined to named co-accused and those co-accused are acquitted, neither s. 149/190 nor s. 34/3(5) is available against the survivor.
  • The court may not speculate an unnamed companion into existence — only evidence (court witnesses, defence witnesses, circumstances) can show the assembly numbered five-plus including unidentified persons (Mohan Singh/Krishna Govind Patil).
  • The solitary convicted accused answers only for the offence proved against him individually — and the individual proof must satisfy the offence's own ingredients (here: no individually fatal injury → 326, not 302).

DU-exam hooks

"It was as such not permissible to invoke Section 149 or Section 34" · the closed-list vs open-list distinction · the Plummer–Topandas–Mohan Singh–Ram Bilas Singh chain · "someone else might have been with him" is not evidence. Pair it with Suresh: Suresh decides who shares liability when all are guilty; Maina Singh decides what remains when all but one go free.

5.3  Mizaji & Anr. v. State of Uttar Pradesh

1959 Supp (1) SCR 940 : AIR 1959 SC 572 — Kapur J. (with Bose and Sinha JJ.) DU CASE MATERIAL (CM 2023) DU LLB CRIMINAL LAW-II · UNIT 8 (2023 CM) / UNIT 4

Facts

A land-grab that turned lethal. Field No. 1096, the Sukhna field, stood recorded in the revenue papers in the cultivating possession of Rameshwar (the deceased) and four others (Ram Sarup, Jailal, Sita Ram, Saddon). On 18 April 1957 the recorded tenant Banwari sold the field to Tej Singh, who applied for mutation; Rameshwar's party opposed it. At sunrise on 27 July 1957, five men — Tej Singh and his son Mizaji, nephew Subedar, cousin Machal, and servant Maiku — came to the field armed: Tej Singh with a spear, Mizaji with a pistol concealed in the fold (phent) of his dhoti, the rest with lathis, bringing a plough, patela and bullocks. Maiku began ploughing and overturning the standing jowar crop while Tej Singh stood guard with his spear. Bateshwar (PW 7) carried word to Ram Sarup, who came to the field with Rameshwar, Jailal and Israel — unarmed. Ram Sarup asked Tej Singh why he was damaging the field; Tej Singh replied he had purchased it and would do "what he was doing"; an altercation followed; the four cutters came up; and on Tej Singh's instigation, Mizaji drew the pistol from his dhoti-fold and fired, hitting Rameshwar, who died within half an hour. The accused fled; the FIR (7:30 a.m., Nawabgunj PS) named all five.

The Sessions Judge found the assembly's common object was "to take forcible possession of the field and to meet every eventuality even to the extent of causing death if they are interfered with", and convicted all five of 302 read with 149 (Mizaji — death; the rest — life), rioting, and armed rioting (148 for Tej Singh and Mizaji, 147 for the lathi-men). The Allahabad High Court confirmed, adding that even if the common object was only possession, "the accused fully knew, considering the nature of weapons with which they were armed", that murder was likely in its prosecution.

Issues

  • Was the assembly's common object confined to taking forcible possession — and if so, could the murder still be brought within s. 149?
  • What do the two limbs of s. 149 — "in prosecution of the common object" and "such as the members knew to be likely" — each mean, and is the second limb narrower than the first?
  • Did the "knowledge" limb require proof that every member actually knew a murder was likely?
  • Was the death sentence appropriate for Mizaji, who fired at his father's instigation?

Arguments

For the appellants: the common object, at highest, was forcible possession of the field — the murder of Rameshwar was neither the object nor "in prosecution of" it; s. 149's second limb could not stretch beyond the first (the submission drawn from the Mysore High Court's view in Chikkarange Gowda, that once the offence is outside the first part of s. 149 the second part cannot apply); at any rate "knowledge" of a likely murder could not be attributed to every member merely from their weapons; and the defence case was that no occurrence took place at all — Rameshwar was killed in a dacoity at Ram Sarup's house.

For the State: the party went armed — spear, hidden pistol, lathis — to enforce a "supposed right" by show of criminal force; they "went there fully prepared to meet any eventuality even to commit murder if it was necessary for the accomplishment of their common object"; considering their relationship (father, son, nephew, cousin, servant) it was impossible that the others did not know Mizaji carried a pistol; so every member either prosecuted the common object to its murderous end or knew murder likely in its prosecution.

Held — Kapur J.

(a) Common object needs no pre-concert: "it is not necessary that there should be a pre-concert in the sense of a meeting of the members… it is enough if it is adopted by all the members and is shared by all of them" — object deduced from weapons, words and conduct.

(b) The two limbs distinguished. First limb: the offence must be "immediately connected with the common object of the assembly… and not independent of it". Second limb: catches offences outside the object — but only where "the probabilities were such that the knowledge of the likelihood of the commission of that offence can be attributed to the members of the assembly"; a bare possibility is not knowledge.

(c) The converse rule — rejecting the Mysore view. Relying on Sabid Ali (which had adopted the Federal Court's analysis in Pancham v. Emperor (1940 FCR 166) and Mohan Singh v. Emperor), the Court held "the converse is however not true": failure of the first limb does not exclude the second. Chikkarange Gowda v. State of Mysore was read as laying down only a charge-framing rule — the court must specify which limb is invoked and, for the second, that the likelihood was known to the members. Illustrations: an armed body assembled to take a woman by force — murder must be held likely; an armed assembly to take forcible possession — the shooting of a resister makes all members guilty under the second limb.

(d) Application. The High Court's concurrent finding — that the five went fully prepared for any eventuality and must be taken to have known murder was likely, given the hidden pistol and the family nexus — supported conviction under 302/149 for all. (e) Sentence: Mizaji's death sentence upheld — the extreme penalty was appropriate for a killing done in cold blood at a land dispute; that he fired "at the instance of his father" Tej Singh was no mitigation. Convictions and sentences (death for Mizaji; life for the rest; 148/147 sentences) confirmed.

Principles evolved

  • Common object may be adopted on the spot — no meeting or pre-concert needed (contrast common intention's consensus requirement).
  • First limb of 149/190: offence immediately connected with the common object, not independent of it.
  • Second limb: members' actual knowledge of likelihood — probabilities so high that knowledge can be attributed; bare possibility insufficient.
  • The second limb is not confined to offences that would also satisfy the first — the converse proposition is false.
  • Charge-framing discipline: specify the limb invoked (Chikkarange rule, approved).
  • S. 149/190 liability extends to every member — even the lathi-carriers — once knowledge/assembly is proved; and "supposed right" enforcement by armed force is squarely within § 189(1)(d).

DU-exam hooks

The two illustrations (abduction-posse; land-possession-posse) · "immediately connected with the common object… and not independent of it" · "the converse is however not true" · pistol in the dhoti fold · "adopted by all the members and is shared by all of them". Lead with Mizaji in every § 190/149 answer — it remains the canonical two-limbs statement under the BNS because § 190 reproduces § 149 verbatim.

Q1
"Establishment of an overt act is not a requirement of law to allow section 34 to operate in as much as the section gets attracted when a criminal act is done by common intention of all." Explain.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.7(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (quoted from Krishnan v. State of Kerala, extracted in Suresh)
Introduction frame

The proposition, taken verbatim from Krishnan v. State of Kerala (extracted in Suresh v. State of U.P., the DU case material), states the settled position on IPC s. 34 / BNS § 3(5): a formal overt act by each accused is not a legal precondition; what the section demands is a criminal act done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all. The 2001 three-Judge decision in Suresh contains both a judgment affirming the proposition and a doctrinal counterweight, and together they give the complete answer.

The text and its two postulates

S. 34 / § 3(5): "When a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of the common intention of all, each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone." As Thomas J. explains, the section presupposes (i) a criminal act (a single act or a series — s. 33) done not by one person, and (ii) every constituent act done in furtherance of the common intention of all. It is emphatically not a vicarious provision: Thomas J.'s "caricature section" — liability when one person acts "in furtherance of the common intention of several persons" — is the law's opposite. Each confederate must have contributed something.

Why "overt act" is not the test
  • Barendra Kumar Ghosh (PC): the counter-clerk who kept watch during the post-office murder was hanged with the killers — "they also serve who only stand and wait"; s. 34 "deals with the doing of separate acts, similar or diverse… by several persons". The watch-keeping was not an "overt act" of shooting, yet it was the act of participation.
  • Krishnan v. State of Kerala: precisely this question was answered — "establishment of an overt act is not a requirement of law to allow Section 34 to operate inasmuch as the section gets attracted when a criminal act is done by several persons in furtherance of common intention of all".
  • Surender Chauhan v. State of M.P.: the two-factor test — common intention + participation. "If a common intention is proved but no overt act is attributed to the individual accused, Section 34 will be attracted… essentially, the offence of the accused who did not commit any overt act is to be determined by ascertaining whether he shared the common intention."
  • Sethi J. in Suresh: "it is not the requirement of law that he should have actually done something"; s. 34 "is a rule of evidence and does not create a substantive offence"; presence at the scene for the purpose of facilitating the crime "is itself tantamount to actual participation", and "culpability under Section 34 cannot be excluded by mere distance from the scene".
  • Shreekantiah (standing at the door "with a readiness to play his part"), Tukaram ("overt or covert, by active presence or by distant direction"), Ramaswami (guard duty), Lalai (spear-holder): each found participation without an overt act of violence.
The counterweight — participation is still the sine qua non

Thomas J. in Suresh insists that although the act "need not necessarily be overt, even if it is only a covert act it is enough, provided such a covert act is proved to have been done… in furtherance of the common intention" — and even an illegal omission qualifies (s. 32/§ 3(4)): a confederate's deliberate silence when a warning would have saved the victim is itself a criminal act. Physical presence is neither necessary nor sufficient: the co-accused may stand afar and catapult weapons, or watch "through binoculars" and direct "through mobile phones"; but one who "only keeps the common intention in his mind" and does nothing — the mere by-stander — cannot be convicted under s. 34; his liability, if any, lies under conspiracy (120-B / § 61) or abetment (109 / § 49). This is exactly why Pavitri Devi's acquittal was affirmed: her presence on the road, uncorroborated as to any act, did not prove shared intention.

Conclusion wrap

The statement is correct as it stands: the law requires participation, which is a broader category than the overt act. An overt act is one species of participation; covert acts, guard duty, exhortation, weapon-supply and illegal omissions are others. What s. 34/§ 3(5) cannot survive without is some act having nexus with the offence done in furtherance of the common intention — proved, like the intention itself, by inference from conduct (Pandurang: incriminating facts "must be incompatible with the innocence of the accused").

Q2
A, B, C, D and E, all members of an unlawful assembly, decided to attack X, who was member of a rival political party. Each one of them was explained that the attack should not exceed to cause the death. While all of them went on one night to attack X, on reaching D and E found that Mr. X is the same person who has deprived them earlier of some rightful claim, hence they decided to take revenge from him (X). While A, B and C attacked X by using hockey sticks, D and E had hidden pistol, they directly shot X dead. Can A, B, C, D and E be held guilty under Section 149 of the Indian Penal Code? Explain your answer.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.7(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (problem solved with Mizaji + Suresh)
Step 1 — Is there an unlawful assembly? s. 141 / BNS 189(1)

Five persons (A–E) with the common object of attacking X — a member of a rival party — to inflict hurt. The object falls within s. 141's Fifthly / § 189(1)(e) (compelling a person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or overawing him by show of criminal force) and/or clause (c) ("other offence" — voluntarily causing hurt). The assembly is unlawful; and since A–E acted with force in prosecution of that object, all are at least guilty of rioting (s. 147 / § 191(2)) — armed with hockey sticks and pistols, s. 148 / § 191(3) for those so armed.

Step 2 — The common object does NOT include murder

The instructions were that "the attack should not exceed to cause the death" — i.e. the object was an assault stopping short of homicide. The shooting was the product not of the common object but of D and E's private, supervening revenge motive, discovered only on reaching the spot. So the first limb of s. 149/§ 190 — an offence committed "in prosecution of the common object" — does not cover the murder: the killing was not, in Mizaji's words, "immediately connected with the common object… and not independent of it"; it was independent of it.

Step 3 — The knowledge limb, tested against each group

Under the second limb — "such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object" — Mizaji requires actual knowledge, "probabilities such that the knowledge of the likelihood… can be attributed", a bare possibility being insufficient.

  • A, B and C: they attacked with hockey sticks under an express instruction not to go to the length of death; D and E's pistols were hidden; the revenge design was formed secretly on arrival. The likelihood of murder was never communicated to them — indeed the contrary was. Murder was a bare possibility, not knowledge. A, B and C cannot be held guilty of murder under s. 149. They are liable for voluntarily causing hurt/grievous hurt by hockey sticks read with s. 149 (ss. 323–326 / §§ 115, 117, 118(1)–(2) as applicable) — for that offence was in prosecution of the common object — plus rioting/armed rioting.
  • D and E: they knew of the pistols (they carried them); they knew their own secret intent to kill. For them the second limb is satisfied — but their liability is cleaner under a different route: between the two of them a common intention to murder X was formed on the spot (the revenge decision), and the shooting was done in furtherance of it. "A pre-arranged plan… may develop in the course of occurrence" (Suresh, Sethi J.; Mahboob Shah). D and E are guilty of murder (s. 302 / § 103(1)) read with s. 34 / § 3(5) — two persons being "several" enough — and not merely under s. 149.
Step 4 — Answer to the question posed

So, can all five be held guilty under s. 149 of murder? No. The murder was neither in prosecution of the assembly's common object (limited to non-fatal assault) nor within the knowledge of A, B and C (hidden pistols + express restraint + secret revenge). D and E alone had the murderous knowledge/intention; they answer for murder r/w s. 34. This divergence also illustrates s. 38 / § 3(9) — persons "engaged in one criminal act may be guilty of different offences": one night's violence yields murder from D–E and grievous hurt r/w 149 from A–C.

Authority audit cite-bank
  • Mizaji v. State of U.P. — two limbs; "immediately connected… not independent"; knowledge ≠ bare possibility; lathi-men hang with the gunman only when the knowledge limb is satisfied.
  • Suresh — common intention can develop on the spot between fewer than all (D–E); participation/participationless presence distinctions.
  • Chikkarange Gowda — the charge under 149 must specify the limb; a defect here is a real objection point.
  • Maina Singh — (converse check) had A–C also known of the pistols, the second limb would have swept all five into murder, however "unfair" that seems; membership, not muscle, is the test.
Q3
Distinguish between common intention (Section 34 IPC / Section 3(5) BNS) and common object (Section 149 IPC / Section 190 BNS). Can both provisions be invoked in the same case?
DU pattern (the unit's most-examined long answer) — full table in § 6.1
Core contrast lead with this

S. 34/§ 3(5) fastens liability on the sharer of a mind who participates in the criminal act; s. 149/§ 190 fastens liability on the member of a crowd whose unlawful assembly commits an offence. The first looks inward (consensus), the second outward (the five-plus head-count and the enumerated object).

  • Numbers: "several" (even two) for s. 34; five or more for s. 149 (Maina Singh — the head-count is jurisdictional).
  • Mental element: common intention = prior concert or on-the-spot consensus, but always preceding the act (Mahboob Shah; Shatrughan Patar); common object = may be "adopted… and shared by all" without any pre-concert (Mizaji ¶ 6) — the object may even change mid-course (Explanation to § 189(1)).
  • Participation vs membership: s. 34 requires an act with nexus — overt or covert (Suresh); s. 149 requires only intentional membership with awareness (§ 189(2)/142) — the passive lathi-bearer of Mizaji needed no personal act at all.
  • Reach: s. 149's second limb ("knew to be likely") can catch offences beyond the design — broader than s. 34, which is confined to acts in furtherance of the common intention.
  • Nature: s. 34 is a rule of evidence/joint liability (not an offence); s. 149 also operates as an aid to liability but sits inside the substantive unlawful-assembly chapter.
  • Charge: s. 149 charges must specify the limb (Chikkarange); s. 34 charges simply allege the reading.
Composite invocation

Yes — the two may be charged in the alternative and even tried together (courts routinely charge "302/34/149"), because they are complementary routes to constructive liability. But each must be independently proved: if the assembly count fails, 149 falls (though 34 may survive among those whose common intention is proved — Yashwant, Sukh Ram); if the shared mind fails, 34 falls (though 149 may survive on membership alone). If both fail, the accused answers only for his individual acts (Maina Singh: 302/34 altered to 326).

One-line clincher

Exam memory-hook: s. 34 convicts the accomplice who willed; s. 149 convicts the accomplice who joined.

Q4
Define "unlawful assembly". What are its essential ingredients, and when may an assembly not initially unlawful become one?
DU pattern — BNS § 189(1)–(2) / IPC §§ 141–143
Definition statute

BNS § 189(1) / IPC § 141: an assembly of five or more persons is an unlawful assembly if its common object is (a) to overawe by criminal force or its show the Union/State Government, Parliament or a Legislature, or any public servant in the exercise of lawful power; (b) to resist the execution of any law or legal process; (c) to commit any mischief or criminal trespass or other offence; (d) by criminal force or its show, to take/obtain possession of property, to deprive a person of a right of way, water or other incorporeal right, or to enforce any right or supposed right (the BNS addition — squarely covering the Mizaji land-grab); or (e) to compel any person to do what he is not legally bound to do, or omit what he is legally entitled to do.

Essentials
  • Five or more — the count is of participants, not of the accused on the charge-sheet; unidentified participants count (Mohan Singh; Dharam Pal).
  • A common object falling within one of the five clauses — "it is enough if it is adopted by all the members and is shared by all of them" (Mizaji); no pre-concert or meeting required.
  • Object must be common to the assembly — proved by weapons, words and conduct (the "go away otherwise they would be finished" pattern).
  • Membership (for liability): awareness of the facts rendering the assembly unlawful + intentional joining or continuing (§ 189(2)/142) — the innocent drifter is protected; the man who stays after knowing is not.
Subsequent unlawfulness

The Explanation to § 189(1) (codifying the case law): "an assembly which was not unlawful when it assembled, may subsequently become an unlawful assembly." A lawful procession becomes unlawful the moment its members adopt, say, the object of pulling down a structure (clause (c)) or forcing a shop's closure (clause (e)). Each member who then continues with knowledge becomes a member of the unlawful assembly. Distinguish also dispersal: those who withdraw before the object is adopted escape; those who remain are members for everything thereafter done in prosecution of the object or within the members' knowledge.

Punishment snapshot

Membership: 6 months (§ 189(2)/143); continuing after dispersal order: 2 years (§ 189(3)/145); armed member: 2 years (§ 189(4)/144); rioting: 2 years (§ 191(2)/147); armed rioting: 5 years (§ 191(3)/148 — enhanced from 3 under the BNS).

Q5
"Common intention implies a pre-arranged plan, but in a given case it may develop at the spur of the moment." Explain with reference to case law, and distinguish common intention from similar intention.
DU pattern — Mahboob Shah + Suresh (¶¶ 52–54)
The pre-arranged plan baseline

Mahboob Shah v. Emperor (PC): "common intention within the meaning of the section implies a pre-arranged plan, and to convict the accused… it should be proved that the criminal act was done in concert pursuant to the pre-arranged plan." The words "in furtherance of the common intention of all" were inserted by the Act of 1870 precisely to require concert — before that, mere common participation could mislead.

The spur-of-the-moment qualification

But prior concert does not demand a prior meeting. Suresh (Sethi J., ¶ 52): "The essence of Section 34 is simultaneous consensus of the minds… Such consensus can be developed at the spot and thereby intended by all of them" — the design may form "in the course of commission of the offence and on the spur of the moment", as when D and E of the Dec 2014 problem (Q2 above) decide on the spot to shoot. The only chronological rule is that the consensus must precede the act done in furtherance of it (Shatrughan Patar v. State of Assam): intention cannot be reconstructed backwards from the act. In Iftikhar Khan, four men walking in a body, two with pistols and two with lathis, firing and fleeing together without explanation — the prior meeting of minds was inferred from conduct; in Lalai, the spear-carrying brother's presence to "overcome any outside interference" implied on-the-spot concert.

Common ≠ similar intention

Suresh (¶ 53): "The common intention which developed at the spur of the moment is different from the similar intention actuated by a number of persons at the same time. The distinction… may be fine but is nonetheless a real one and if overlooked may lead to miscarriage of justice." In Pandurang, five men simultaneously struck the victim, each intending to kill on his own account — held: each liable only for the injuries he inflicted; no s. 34 without concert. The contrast pair to memorise: Iftikhar Khan (concert inferred → all for everything) vs Pandurang (parallel intentions → each for his own).

Evidentiary note

Common intention is "a question of fact… proved mainly as a matter of inference from the circumstances" (Mahboob Shah) — conduct before, at and after the occurrence; and under BSA § 10 / IEA § 10, acts done "in reference to their common intention" by one conspirator are evidence against all (Suresh ¶ 54 contrasts the two wordings).

Q6
Problem: A, B, C, D and E are charged with the murder of V, the charge alleging that they composed an unlawful assembly whose common object was to kill V, and alternatively that the murder was in furtherance of the common intention of all. The evidence names no participant other than the five. The court acquits D and E, giving them the benefit of doubt, and convicts A, B and C under s. 302 read with s. 149 and alternatively s. 34. Maintainable?
DU pattern (problem on the Maina Singh doctrine — mirrors DU's Maina Singh full text)
The s. 149/§ 190 count fails

Apply Maina Singh v. State of Rajasthan. The charge and the evidence are confined to the five named accused — a closed list. With D and E acquitted, the assembly at bar numbers three; s. 149/§ 190 requires five or more. Following the ladder — Plummer (verdict against one of three rioters, two acquitted, is void) → Topandas → Mohan Singh (closed list: "if both the charge and the evidence are confined to the persons named… and out of the persons so named two or more are acquitted leaving before the court less than five persons… then Section 149 cannot be invoked") → Ram Bilas Singh — the conviction under 302/149 cannot stand.

The exception the prosecution will invoke — and its limits

Mohan Singh/Krishna Govind Patil/Dharam Pal: if the evidence (including court witnesses, defence witnesses and circumstances) shows that unidentified persons swelled the attacking party to five-plus, the acquittal of the named few does not displace s. 149. But that conclusion "is really based on evidence" — it cannot rest on the speculation that "someone else might have been with him" (the very finding Maina Singh rejected). On our facts the evidence names no one else: the exception fails.

What survives: s. 34 — and individual acts
  • 302 r/w 34/§ 3(5) against A, B, C can survive — three is "several", and the common intention need only be shared by those convicted (cf. Sukh Ram: acquittal of co-accused no bar to 302/34 where the case never involved unknown persons; Yashwant: remaining accused convicted with s. 34). The court must, however, independently prove the triad's common intention to kill and each one's participation.
  • Individual liability as the floor: whatever be the fate of the constructive provisions, each convict answers for his own proved acts — if, as in Maina Singh itself, no single accused's act is individually proved sufficient to cause death in the ordinary course of nature, murder gives way to grievous hurt (326 / § 118(2), 10 years' RI in Maina Singh).
  • Charge audit: the conviction order must specify whether it rests on 34 or on 149; under Chikkarange the 149 charge had to specify its limb — a further vulnerability of the 149 count.
Conclusion

Conviction under 302/149: not maintainable. Conviction under 302/34: maintainable only on fresh findings of common intention among A, B, C plus each one's participation; failing that, only for the individual offences proved against each. Riots and unlawful-assembly convictions against the three must likewise fall, the assembly having dissolved below five.

Q7
Explain the two limbs of Section 149 IPC / Section 190 BNS. Is the second limb narrower in scope than the first? Refer to Mizaji v. State of U.P.
DU pattern — Mizaji (DU case material, 2023 CM Unit 8) is the set text
The two limbs

§ 190 / s. 149 attracts liability if the offence is committed by a member of an unlawful assembly (i) "in prosecution of the common object", or (ii) "such as the members of that assembly knew to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object".

  • First limb — in prosecution of: the offence is the direct outcome of the object — "immediately connected with the common object of the assembly… and not independent of it" (Mizaji). If the object is to evict a tenant by force and the assembly beats him to death in doing so, the murder is in prosecution of the object.
  • Second limb — knew to be likely: where the offence exceeds the object, membership still attracts liability if all members actually knew the offence was likely in the prosecution of the object. Knowledge is attributed only when "the probabilities were such that the knowledge of the likelihood… can be attributed to the members" — a bare possibility is not knowledge.
The Mysore heresy and its rejection

Chikkarange Gowda v. State of Mysore had been read (in the Mysore High Court's original formulation) to mean that once the offence is found not to be in prosecution of the common object, s. 149's second part cannot apply at all — making limb two narrower than limb one. Mizaji rejects this: "the converse is however not true" — the second limb catches a class of cases where the first does not. Relying on Sabid Ali (which drew on the Federal Court's Pancham and Mohan Singh), the Court held the two parts cumulative, not mutually exclusive. What survives of Chikkarange is only the charge-framing rule: the charge must specify the limb invoked.

Mizaji's illustrations (the heart of the answer)
  • The abduction posse: an armed body assembles to take a woman by force — "violence and even murder must be held to be likely to be committed in prosecution of that object" — all guilty of the murder if it occurs, though the object was only abduction.
  • The land-possession posse: an armed assembly to take forcible possession of land — shooting dead the resister is not "in prosecution of" the object of possession, but the members' knowledge that deadly violence was likely (pistol, spear, preparedness) draws all — lathi-men included — into murder.
Application in Mizaji itself

The five (Tej Singh with spear, Mizaji with a pistol in his dhoti-fold, three with lathis) went to plough the disputed Sukhna field and "to meet every eventuality even to the extent of causing death"; Mizaji shot Rameshwar at Tej Singh's instigation. Even on the view that the common object was only forcible possession (clause (d) — "to enforce any right or supposed right"), the family nexus and the weapons made murderous likelihood known to all: conviction of all five under 302/149 affirmed; Mizaji's death sentence upheld — filial obedience was no mitigation.

Conclusion

The second limb is not narrower than the first; it is a distinct, overlapping net. Limb one asks whether the offence was the object's direct child; limb two asks whether every member knew it was the object's likely by-product. Under § 190 (which reproduces s. 149 verbatim) the doctrine is unchanged.

Q8
Write a short note: Rioting and affray — with the BNS's punishment changes.
DU pattern short note — BNS §§ 191–192 / IPC §§ 146–160
Rioting § 191 / s. 146

Whenever force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly, or by any member of it, in prosecution of the common object, every member of that assembly is guilty of rioting — the crime of the crowd itself. Punishment: 2 years (§ 191(2)/147); if armed with a deadly weapon (or anything likely to cause death used as a weapon): 5 years under § 191(3)/148 — enhanced from the IPC's 3 years, the BNS's signature change signalling zero tolerance for armed mobs. Every member is liable even if he personally used no force — the Mizaji lathi-men were all convicted of rioting and armed rioting alongside the killer.

Affray § 192 / s. 159

Two or more persons fighting in a public place, thereby disturbing the public peace — the crime of the brawling pair, no assembly and no common object required. Punishment: 3 months / fine (§ 192/160; the former s. 160's 6-month figure was trimmed to 3 months under the BNS with a fine added).

Contrast table

Numbers (five-plus vs two-plus) · predicate (unlawful assembly vs public place) · essence (force in prosecution of common object vs fighting) · punishment (2/5 years vs 3 months). An affray can escalate: if five join with a common object, the affray ripens into an unlawful assembly and rioting.

Q9
"Section 34 IPC is a rule of evidence and not a substantive offence." Explain, and state its consequences for charging, conviction and sentence.
DU pattern — Sethi J. in Suresh; Barendra Kumar Ghosh
The proposition

S. 34/§ 3(5) creates no offence and prescribes no punishment. It is a rule of joint liability — Sethi J. in Suresh: the section "is a rule of evidence and does not create a substantive offence". It supplies the mode (the "read with") by which the participation of each is referred to the collective act: once common intention plus participation are proved, "each of such persons is liable for that act in the same manner as if it were done by him alone".

Consequences
  • Charging: one charges "302 r/w 34" (now "103(1) r/w 3(5)") — never s. 34 alone. There is no conviction "under s. 34" simpliciter; a conviction order reading only "Section 34" is defective.
  • Punishment: the sentence is for the substantive offence — s. 34 adds nothing to, and subtracts nothing from, the scale.
  • Proof: like an evidentiary rule, it operates only when the substantive ingredients are proved; the common intention itself is "a question of fact… proved mainly as a matter of inference" (Mahboob Shah).
  • Two accused suffice: because it is not an assembly provision, "several" means even two — unlike s. 149's five.
  • Historical footnote (Barendra Kumar Ghosh, extracted in Suresh): the presence of an abettor at the scene made him liable as if the principal — through the then s. 114 Evidence Act presumption, which was "evidentiary not punitory" — the doctrinal ancestor of the rule-of-evidence reading.
Q10
Problem: P, knowing that Q and R intend to murder S, supplies them with pistols, and on the appointed night stands at a distance from S's house to signal if the police approach. Q and R shoot S dead. Q is caught; R escapes and is never identified or traced. What is P's liability? Would it differ if P merely stood by, watching his neighbours' quarrel, without more?
DU pattern (problem on Suresh — covert participation, distance, and mere presence)
Liability of P the first limb

Murder (s. 302 / § 103(1)) r/w s. 34 / § 3(5). Thomas J. in Suresh: the co-accused "should have done some act which has nexus with the offence. Such an act need not be very substantial… The act need not necessarily be overt, even if it is only a covert act it is enough", and presence at the scene is not even necessary — one may "remain a little away and supply weapons… by catapulting them", or oversee operations "through binoculars" and direct "through mobile phones". P did both species: he supplied the instruments of death and stood as the look-out — the Ramaswami/Lalai guard-duty participation. His act was done in furtherance of the common intention of all three (supply and signal-service were part of the plan). R's non-apprehension is irrelevant: s. 34 operates among those before the court; it is not a counting provision like s. 149 — even P and Q alone (two persons) suffice.

The alternative formulations

If the plan's details are thin, P's conduct still satisfies abetment by intentionally aiding (s. 109 / § 49 — "intentionally aids" being the Thirdly clause of s. 107 / § 45) — with the offence committed, the abettor "shall be punished with the punishment provided for the offence"; and if an agreement is provable, conspiracy (120-B / § 61(2)). Suresh's allocation: the participant is caught by s. 34; the non-participant well-wisher — "who only keeps the common intention in his mind" but does nothing — falls to 120-B/109, not s. 34.

The second limb — the onlooker

If P "merely stood by" without supplying, signalling or agreeing, he is in Pavitri Devi's position: mere presence, "in the absence of other evidence, direct or circumstantial", cannot fasten s. 34 liability — however morally satisfying suspicion might feel. Unless his presence itself facilitated (e.g., a show of numbers terrifying the victim — Sethi J.: presence to facilitate "is itself tantamount to actual participation"), he is not guilty of the murder; at most a failure-to-report question arises, which the Penal Code does not (save in specified statutes) punish.

Conclusion

First version: P — murder r/w s. 34 (alternatively 109/120-B; § 3(5)/§ 49/§ 61). Second version: no liability for the murder; presence alone is not participation.

Q11
Explain the cognate family of Section 34: Sections 35, 36, 37 and 38 IPC (BNS §§ 3(6)–(9)), with illustrations. How do they interlock?
DU pattern — the "family" analysis from Suresh (Thomas J., ¶¶ 16–19)
The map Suresh calls them one cognate group
  • s. 35 / § 3(6) — parallel knowledge or intention: where an act is criminal only because done with criminal knowledge or intention, everyone who joins with his own such knowledge/intention is liable as if the act were his alone. No common intention needed — the contrast with s. 34 is exactly this postulate (Suresh ¶ 17).
  • s. 36 / § 3(7) — act + omission: if causing an effect (or attempting it) by act or omission is an offence, causing it partly by act and partly by omission is the same offence. Illustration: A starves Z and also beats him; Z dies — murder.
  • s. 37 / § 3(8) — division of labour: when an offence is committed by means of several acts, whoever intentionally co-operates by doing any one of those acts (singly or jointly) commits the offence. Illustrations: the divided poison doses (A and B severally administer small doses which combined kill — both murderers); the alternate jailors (A and B each illegally omit to feed Z on their six-hour shifts — both murderers); the dismissed jailor (A starves, is dismissed, B without collusion continues the starvation — B guilty of murder, A only of attempt: no co-operation, though identical conduct!).
  • s. 38 / § 3(9) — divergent guilt: several persons engaged in one criminal act may be guilty of different offences by means of it. Illustration: A kills under grave provocation (culpable homicide), B assists without provocation and with ill-will (murder).
Interlocking

Read together they map the four dimensions of group crime: shared mind (34), parallel mind (35), mixed means (36), distributed means (37) and graded blameworthiness (38). The Dec 2014 problem (Q2) is a live demonstration of s. 38/§ 3(9): one night's attack yielding murder from D–E and grievous hurt from A–C.

Q12
Critically examine the BNS 2023 restructuring of the group-liability provisions: IPC §§ 32–38, 141–149 into BNS §§ 3(4)–(9), 189–191. What has changed, and what has been deliberately preserved?
DU pattern (IPC↔BNS comparative answer) — the s. 0 master table is the summary
The relocation structural

The constructive-liability family of the IPC's Chapter II (General Explanations) moves into BNS Chapter II (General Explanations), § 3(4)–(9): 32→3(4) (acts include omissions), 34→3(5), 35→3(6), 36→3(7), 37→3(8), 38→3(9) — with the IPC's s. 33 (act/omission as series) absorbed into the drafting. The public-tranquillity chapter (141–149) becomes BNS §§ 189–190, and rioting/affray become §§ 191–192.

What changed
  • § 189(1)(d) adds "or to enforce any right or supposed right" — codifying the Mizaji fact-pattern (armed enforcement of a claimed title) as an enumerated object.
  • New Explanation: an assembly lawful when assembled "may subsequently become an unlawful assembly" — codifying the degeneration doctrine.
  • Consolidation: IPC 142 (who is a member) and 143 (punishment) merge into § 189(2) — definition and six-month sentence in one sub-section.
  • Armed rioting (§ 191(3)): punishment raised from 3 years to 5 years — the signal enhancement of the chapter.
  • Affray (§ 192): 6 months → 3 months + fine.
What was preserved — deliberately

§ 190 reproduces s. 149 verbatim — both limbs, both expressions — so the entire Mizaji doctrine carries across undisturbed; likewise § 3(5) = s. 34 word for word, preserving Suresh. The five-object enumeration, the five-plus head-count, the two-limb structure, and the membership test (awareness + intentional joining) are all intact. The exam point: the BNS re-arranged the furniture and sharpened punishments, but the doctrine — Suresh, Mizaji, Maina Singh — governs as before.

Critical appraisal

Positives: the (d)-addition and the Explanation remove doubts that litigation had generated; the armed-riot enhancement answers mob-violence anxieties. Critiques: the five-plus threshold and verbatim § 190 retain the old over-breadth (passive members hang with killers on the knowledge limb); the section-count balloons (189(1)–(9)) without clarity gains; and the much-discussed deletion debates (e.g., sedition/thin-skull provisions elsewhere) leave this chapter conspicuously uncontroversial.

Q13
Problem: H and his wife W, and H's brother-in-law G, are tried for the murder of V. The sole surviving eyewitness (a child) says W "caught V's wife by the hair and exhorted the others to finish everyone"; two adult witnesses speak only to W's presence on the road outside while H and G butchered the family inside. W is convicted under s. 302 r/w 34. Discuss.
DU pattern (problem drawn on the Pavitri Devi facts in Suresh — the DU case material itself)
Frame the two voices of Suresh

This is Pavitri Devi's case. The issue: presence plus an interested child's exaggeration — is that participation in a common intention?

  • Thomas J. — participation is the sine qua non: some act with nexus to the offence (overt or covert, even an omission). Exhortation to kill would obviously qualify — if proved. But the child's account (hair-clutching, exhortation) was uncorroborated by PWs 1–2, who spoke only to her presence on the road; the High Court had accepted the child's evidence only to the extent of that corroboration. Presence, "in the absence of other evidence, direct or circumstantial", cannot attract s. 34. Conviction unsustainable.
  • Sethi J. — agrees on the facts but marks the boundary the other way: had the common intention been proved, "she could not have been acquitted of the charge framed against her only on the ground that she had actually not done any overt act". For violence cases he would insist on physical presence at the commission "for the purpose of facilitating" the crime — which, when coupled with the shared design, "is itself tantamount to actual participation".
  • Evidence audit: an interested/child witness's uncorroborated particulars; PWs consistent only as to presence; the accused's house across the road (innocent explanation for presence); the benefit of the doubt on intention.
Answer

W's conviction under 302/34 must be set aside: (i) her presence is as consistent with innocent proximity (her own home across the road) as with complicity — Pandurang's rule: incriminating facts "must be incompatible with the innocence of the accused and incapable of explanation on any other reasonable hypothesis"; (ii) the only act attributed to her rests on uncorroborated child testimony; (iii) exhortation, had it been proved, would have sufficed (participation by instigation — even Thomas J. lists "exhortation… guarding the scene" as participation). The correct verdict: acquittal, as the High Court and the Supreme Court both held in Suresh. Note for completeness: with W out, the two men's conviction stands on their own proved acts and shared intention; their liability does not dilute.

Quote-bank for this answer

Revision Capsule — the Unit in Sixty Seconds

The one-screen revision map: definitions, distinctions, quotes, case one-liners and exam strategy.

8.1  The architecture in five lines

The skeleton: Group liability = two engines + one safety valve. Engine 1 — common intention (§ 3(5)/34): shared mind + participation → each as if done by him alone. Engine 2 — common object (§§ 189–190/141, 149): five-plus assembly + enumerated object + membership → even the passive member, through either of the two limbs. Safety valve — Maina Singh: acquit the confederates, audit the head-count; the engines stall without fuel (people).
The gate-keeper questions (ask in order): (1) How many? (5+ → assembly route; 2+ → intention route) (2) What mind? (prior concert/on-spot consensus vs adopted object) (3) What role? (participation with nexus vs mere membership vs mere presence) (4) Which limb of 190/149? (prosecution-of vs knew-likely) (5) Who survived the trial? (closed list → Maina Singh).

8.4  Ten exam tips for this unit

  1. Always cite both codes on first mention — "s. 34 IPC / § 3(5) BNS", "s. 149 IPC / § 190 BNS" — the examiner is reading two codes at once this decade.
  2. Lead every common-intention answer with Suresh and every common-object answer with Mizaji — those are DU's own prescribed texts for this unit.
  3. The 34-vs-149 table (§ 6.1) is the single highest-yield page of this unit — reproduce six of its rows and you have a full 15-mark answer.
  4. In problems, run the five gate-keeper questions (§ 8.1) in order and show your working — DU examiners award structure.
  5. Always end § 149 problems with the Chikkarange charge audit — it is a ready-made critical observation when facts are thin.
  6. When co-accused are acquitted, the magic words are closed list (Maina Singh) vs open list (Mohan Singh/Dharam Pal) — decide which, and the answer writes itself.
  7. Use the BNS value-adds: clause (d) "supposed right" (Mizaji codified), the subsequent-unlawfulness Explanation, armed-riot enhancement to 5 years, § 190 = s. 149 verbatim.
  8. Quote at least one judicial sentence verbatim per answer — the quote-bank (§ 8.2) exists for this.
  9. Remember the cognate family (§§ 3(6)–(9)/35–38) for "discuss the liability of A, B, C who did different things" questions — s. 38/3(9) is the divergent-guilt key.
  10. Finish with a two-line IRAC conclusion naming the exact offence + section pair for each actor — e.g., "D and E: murder r/w 3(5); A–C: grievous hurt r/w 190 + rioting; W: acquitted (presence ≠ participation)".
