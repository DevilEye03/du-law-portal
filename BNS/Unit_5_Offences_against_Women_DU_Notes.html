The Chapter-V Framework — Why the Code has a Special Chapter for Women

The architecture of BNS Chapter V ("Of Offences against Woman and Child"), the four waves of reform that produced today's text, and how the three syllabus blocks divide the field.

1.1  The architecture

BNS Chapter V carries the title "Of Offences against Woman and Child" and is organised in three blocks — exactly the three blocks of the DU syllabus:

BlockSections (BNS)The organising idea
Sexual offences§§ 63–73The most serious violations of sexual autonomy — rape and its aggravated, custodial, gang and repeat forms (63–71), the below-rape offences (67–69), and identity protection (72–73)
Criminal force & assault against women§§ 74–79Non-penetrative sexual violations — outraging modesty, sexual harassment, disrobing, voyeurism, stalking, insulting words/gestures
Offences relating to marriage§§ 80–87Crimes rooted in the institution of marriage — dowry death, deceitful cohabitation, bigamy, fraudulent ceremony, enticing a wife, cruelty, kidnapping to compel marriage
The unifying principle : these offences punish what the general hurt/defamation chapters cannot reach — the violation of sexual autonomy and bodily integrity (Gurmit Singh: rape "is often destructive of the whole personality of the victim… a murderer destroys the physical body; a rapist degrades the very soul"), of dignity and modesty (KPS Gill), and of the security of the marital home (SAFMA). The old IPC scattered them across Chapters XVI (hurt), XX (marriage) and XIV/XIX; the BNS gathers the women-specific ones into one chapter and, for the first time, gives the protection an explicit child dimension.

1.3  The three doctrinal threads to hold

  1. Consent — the axis of §§ 63–69: will vs consent vs passive submission; s. 90/§ 28's two-part structure; the false-promise problem (Tukaram → Gurmit Singh → Chhoteylal → Deepak Gulati).
  2. Modesty — the axis of §§ 74–79: an elastic, fact-driven concept that the courts refuse to freeze into definition (KPS Gill).
  3. Institutional protection — the axis of §§ 80–87: marriage as a protected (and exploited) institution — dowry death's deemed causation, 498A's controversial breadth (SAFMA).

Rape — Definition and Ingredients (BNS § 63 / IPC § 375)

The four acts, the seven circumstances, the statutory definition of consent, the s. 90/§ 28 backdrop, and Exception 2 — the marital rape exception read down in Independent Thought.

2.1  The text

BNS § 63 / IPC § 375 — Rape

A man is said to commit "rape" if he —

(a) penetrates his penis, to any extent, into the vagina, mouth, urethra or anus of a woman or makes her to do so with him or any other person; or

(b) inserts, to any extent, any object or a part of the body, not being the penis, into the vagina, the urethra or anus of a woman or makes her to do so with him or any other person; or

(c) manipulates any part of the body of a woman so as to cause penetration into the vagina, urethra, anus or any part of body of such woman or makes her to do so with him or any other person; or

(d) applies his mouth to the vagina, anus, urethra of a woman or makes her to do so with him or any other person,

under the circumstances falling under any of the following seven descriptions:

(i) against her will;

(ii) without her consent;

(iii) with her consent, when her consent has been obtained by putting her or any person in whom she is interested, in fear of death or of hurt;

(iv) with her consent, when the man knows that he is not her husband and that her consent is given because she believes that he is another man to whom she is or believes herself to be lawfully married;

(v) with her consent, when, at the time of giving such consent, by reason of unsoundness of mind or intoxication or the administration by him personally or through another of any stupefying or unwholesome substance, she is unable to understand the nature and consequences of that to which she gives consent;

(vi) with or without her consent, when she is under eighteen years of age;

(vii) when she is unable to communicate consent.

Explanation 1. "Vagina" shall also include labia majora.

Explanation 2. Consent means an unequivocal voluntary agreement when the woman by words, gestures or any form of verbal or non-verbal communication, communicates willingness to participate in the specific sexual act: Provided that a woman who does not physically resist to the act of penetration shall not by the reason only of that fact, be regarded as consenting to the sexual activity.

Exception 1. A medical procedure or intervention shall not constitute rape.

Exception 2. Sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape.

Evolution: Macaulay's 1860 text spoke only of penile-vaginal penetration ("Explanation: penetration is sufficient"); the 2013 Amendment (post-Nirbhaya) expanded to the four acts and added the seventh circumstance; the age in clause (vi) rose from 16 to 18 (2013) and Exception 2's age from 15 to 18 (2017 reading-down in Independent Thought, now codified in the BNS). The pre-2013 six-description version, quoted in extenso in Chhoteylal, remains exam-relevant for understanding what changed.

2.2  Ingredients unpacked

IngredientContent (authority in brackets)
1. Only a manThe offender under §§ 63–71 must be a man; the victim, a woman. (A woman can be prosecuted for abetment — e.g., Nushi-type guarding facts.)

(i) and (ii) are the battlegrounds of the consent doctrine (§ 6); (vi) is strict-liability as to age — "with or without her consent" — so consent of a 17-year-old is no answer (Chhoteylal: where the girl is under 16, "the question of age… would pale into insignificance" beside the consent inquiry); (vii) protects the unconscious/disabled victim.

2.3  The consent backdrop — s. 90 IPC / § 28 BNS

BNS § 28 / IPC § 90 — Consent known to be given under fear or misconception

A consent is not such a consent as is intended by any section of this Sanhita, — (a) if the consent is given by a person under fear of injury, or under a misconception of fact, and if the person doing the act knows, or has reason to believe, that the consent was given in consequence of such fear or misconception; or (b) if the consent is given by a person who, from unsoundness of mind or intoxication, is unable to understand the nature and consequence of that to which he gives his consent; or (c) unless the contrary appears from the context, if the consent is given by a person who is under twelve years of age.

Deelip Singh's two-part reading (quoted in Deepak Gulati): "The factors set out in the first part of Section 90 are from the point of view of the victim. The second part… enacts the corresponding provision from the point of view of the accused… The requirements of both the parts should be cumulatively satisfied." Both the woman's misconception AND the man's knowledge/reason-to-belief of it must be proved.

The two consent definitions, reconciled : s. 90/§ 28 tells you when consent is vitiated (fear, misconception, incapacity, minority — from both parties' perspectives); Explanation 2 to § 63 tells you what positive consent is ("unequivocal voluntary agreement… willingness to participate in the specific sexual act") and settles that non-resistance is not consent. Exam formula: "consent = voluntary participation of a capable mind, communicated by word/gesture; submission is not consent; and the proviso buries the 'she didn't scream' defence."

2.4  Exception 2 — the marital rape exception

Exception 2 says a husband's intercourse with his own wife (she being above the threshold age) is not rape. In the IPC the threshold stood at 15 — with the grotesque result that a husband could have non-consensual intercourse with a 16-year-old "wife" while the same act with any other 16-year-old was rape twice over (clause sixthly, then under-16). Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017) read the exception down to 18 for girl wives, and the BNS codifies: "the wife not being under eighteen years of age." The adult-woman marital rape exception survives — the Court expressly left "the wider issue of marital rape of women 18+" open — and remains the most debated lacuna in Indian criminal law (full treatment in the case card, § 7.6).

3.3  The below-rape trio — §§ 67, 68, 69

BNS § 67 / IPC § 376B — Sexual intercourse by husband upon his wife during separation

Whoever has sexual intercourse with his own wife, who is living separately, whether under a decree of separation or otherwise, without her consent, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than two years but which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine. ("Sexual intercourse" = all four acts of § 63.)

BNS § 68 / IPC § 376C — Sexual intercourse by a person in authority

Whoever, being (a) in a position of authority or in a fiduciary relationship, or (b) a public servant, or (c) superintendent or manager of a jail, remand home, place of custody, women's or children's institution, or (d) on the management or staff of a hospital, abuses such position to induce or seduce any woman either in his custody or under his charge or present in the premises to have sexual intercourse with him, such intercourse not amounting to rape, shall be punished with rigorous imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than five years, but which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

The offence catches induced "consent" — consent obtained by the abuse of position is no true consent, but the legislature grades it below rape.

BNS § 69 — Sexual intercourse by employing deceitful means, etc. (NEW OFFENCE)

Whoever, by deceitful means or by making promise to marry to a woman without any intention of fulfilling the same, has sexual intercourse with her, such sexual intercourse not amounting to the offence of rape, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years and shall also be liable to fine.

Explanation. "Deceitful means" shall include inducement for, or false promise of employment or promotion, or marrying by suppressing identity.

The Uday/Deepak Gulati line codified: where the false promise does NOT vitiate consent under s. 90 (because the man did not know/have reason to believe the woman consented only because of the promise, or the promise was not false from inception), intercourse is not rape — but since 2024 it is a distinct offence under § 69 carrying up to ten years. In the IPC era, such cases fell to nothing (as in Deepak Gulati's acquittal) or to § 69's cousins (493 where a sham marriage was staged).

3.4  Identity protection — §§ 72–73 / s. 228A

Printing or publishing the name or identity-revealing matter of a rape victim (offences under §§ 64–71) is an offence (up to 2 years + fine), except for police-investigation purposes, with the victim's written authorisation, or (victim dead/child/unsound mind) the next of kin's authorisation to a recognised welfare institution. Publishing court-proceeding matter without the court's permission is separately punishable (§ 73) — though printing High Court/Supreme Court judgments is exempt. Gurmit Singh adds the judicial layer: courts should withhold the prosecutrix's name in orders — "the anonymity of the victim of the crime must be maintained as far as possible throughout."

Criminal Force and Assault against Women (BNS §§ 74–79 / IPC §§ 354, 354A–D, 509)

The modesty jurisprudence of § 74, the 2013 quartet (harassment, disrobing, voyeurism, stalking), and the word-gesture offence — the KPS Gill chapter of the unit.

4.1  § 74 / s. 354 — outraging modesty

BNS § 74 / IPC § 354 — Assault or criminal force to woman with intent to outrage her modesty

Whoever assaults or uses criminal force to any woman, intending to outrage or knowing it to be likely that he will thereby outrage her modesty, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than one year but which may extend to five years, and shall also be liable to fine.

Ingredients: (1) assault or criminal force (ss. 349–351 IPC / §§ 128–130 BNS — intention/knowledge to use force); (2) against a woman; (3) intent to outrage or knowledge of likelihood of outraging her modesty. Punishment: minimum 1 year — the 2013 addition that ended the era of token sentences.

What is "modesty"? The Code does not define it, and the courts have wisely refused a straitjacket. The classical statement is from Major Singh v. State of Punjab (AIR 1967 SC 63) — the case of the 7½-month-old girl-child whose vagina was pierced by a candle: the modus operandi of the accused indicated knowledge that he was outraging the girl's modesty. Hidayatullah J.'s formulation: the essence of a woman's modesty is her sex; the culpable intention is the violation of that modesty, and it can be outraged by touching her person — "from her very birth [a woman] possesses the modesty which is the attribute of her sex." The Punjab & Haryana High Court in the Rupan Deol Bajaj prosecution added that modesty is a virtue attaching to a woman as a consequence of her sex, irrespective of her age. KPS Gill applies the section without any definitional ceremony: the posterior slap in a public gathering was itself the proof.

4.2  The 2013 quartet — §§ 75–78 / 354A–D

BNS § 75 / IPC § 354A — Sexual harassment (the Vishaka offences)

A man committing any of the following acts: (i) physical contact and advances involving unwelcome and explicit sexual overtures; or (ii) a demand or request for sexual favours; or (iii) showing pornography against the will of a woman; or (iv) making sexually coloured remarks, shall be guilty of the offence of sexual harassment. Punishment: (i)–(iii) — rigorous imprisonment up to 3 years, or fine, or both; (iv) — imprisonment up to 1 year, or fine, or both.

Born from the Supreme Court's Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) guidelines against workplace harassment, legislated in 2013. Note the two mental elements: "unwelcome" (no welcome by the woman) and "sexual" (the overtone). Repeated harassment at the workplace is also an offence under the POSH Act, 2013, with its ICC machinery.

BNS § 76 / IPC § 354B — Assault or use of criminal force to woman with intent to disrobe

Whoever assaults or uses criminal force to any woman or abets such act with the intention of disrobing or compelling her to be naked, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which shall not be less than three years but which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine. ("Compelling her to be naked" is the BNS's addition.)

BNS § 77 / IPC § 354C — Voyeurism

Whoever watches, or captures the image of a woman engaging in a private act in circumstances where she would usually have the expectation of not being observed either by the perpetrator or by any other person at the behest of the perpetrator, or disseminates such image, shall be punished on first conviction with 1–3 years + fine, and on a second or subsequent conviction with 3–7 years + fine.

Explanation 1 — "private act": an act watched in a place reasonably expected to provide privacy, where the victim's genitals/posterior/breasts are exposed or covered only in underwear, or she is using a lavatory, or doing a sexual act not ordinarily done in public. Explanation 2 — where the victim consents to capture but not to dissemination, dissemination is still voyeurism.

BNS § 78 / IPC § 354D — Stalking

Any man who (i) follows a woman and contacts, or attempts to contact, such woman to foster personal interaction repeatedly despite a clear indication of disinterest by such woman; or (ii) monitors the use by a woman of the internet, e-mail or any other form of electronic communication, commits the offence of stalking. Punishment: first conviction — up to 3 years + fine; second or subsequent — up to 5 years + fine.

Statutory defences (proviso): (i) pursued for preventing/detecting crime by a person entrusted with that responsibility by the State; (ii) pursued under any law or to comply with a legal condition; (iii) in the particular circumstances, the conduct was reasonable and justified.

BNS § 79 / IPC § 509 — Word, gesture or act intended to insult the modesty of a woman

Whoever, intending to insult the modesty of any woman, utters any words, makes any sound or gesture, or exhibits any object in any form, intending that such word or sound shall be heard, or that such gesture or object shall be seen, by such woman, or intrudes upon the privacy of such woman, shall be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years, and also with fine.

No physical contact needed — the "verbal/gestural" cousin of § 74; "intrudes upon her privacy" was added in 2013 (and the maximum raised from one year to three). KPS Gill was convicted under BOTH 354 and 509 — the slap (force) plus the whole public demeaning of the officer (insult).

Offences relating to Marriage (BNS §§ 80–87 / IPC §§ 304B, 366, 493–498A)

Dowry death and its statutory web; the deceit/bigamy group; enticing a married woman; and s. 498A's cruelty — the provision whose misuse and protection both reached the Supreme Court in SAFMA.

5.1  § 80 / s. 304B — Dowry death

BNS § 80 / IPC § 304B — Dowry death

(1) Where the death of a woman is caused by any burns or bodily injury or occurs otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of her marriage, and it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected to cruelty or harassment by her husband or any relative of her husband for, or in connection with, any demand for dowry, such death shall be called "dowry death", and such husband or relative shall be deemed to have caused her death.

(2) Whoever commits dowry death shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than seven years but which may extend to imprisonment for life. ("Dowry" as defined in s. 2, Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961.)

Ingredients: (1) death by burns/bodily injury/abnormal circumstances; (2) within 7 years of marriage; (3) cruelty or harassment soon before death; (4) by husband or his relative; (5) for or in connection with dowry demand. The section then works its alchemy: the perpetrators are deemed to have caused her death — a rare statutory presumption of causation.

The evidentiary twin : IEA § 113B / BSA § 118 — where a woman dies unnaturally within 7 years of marriage and it is shown she was subjected to cruelty/harassment for dowry soon before death, the court shall presume that the husband/relative caused the dowry death. Charge under § 80 must specify the death as dowry death (the presumption then switches the burden). Read the trio together: § 80 (substantive) + § 118 BSA (presumption) + Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 ss. 2, 3, 4 (the demand itself punishable).

5.2  The marriage-integrity group — §§ 81–84

BNS § 81 / IPC § 493 — Cohabitation caused by deceitful inducement of belief of lawful marriage

Every man who by deceit causes any woman who is not lawfully married to him to believe that she is lawfully married to him and to cohabit or have sexual intercourse with him in that belief, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

The "sham marriage" offence — a ceremony defective in law (void marriage) staged with deceit. Distinguish from § 69 (deceit below rape, no marriage ceremony belief) and from § 63(iv) (mistaken identity of the man).

BNS § 82 / IPC §§ 494–495 — Bigamy

(1) Whoever, having a husband or wife living, marries in any case in which such marriage is void by reason of its taking place during the life of such husband or wife, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine. Exception: void-declared marriages; and the seven-years-absent-and-unheard-of spouse, provided the subsequent spouse is informed of the facts.

(2) Whoever commits the offence under sub-section (1) having concealed from the person with whom the subsequent marriage is contracted the fact of the former marriage, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine.

Prosecution requires a complaint by the aggrieved party (CrPC 198 — now BNSS § 222 complaint requirement). The offence protects the monogamous marriage — the second spouse's knowledge decides the grading.

BNS § 83 / IPC § 496 — Marriage ceremony fraudulently gone through without lawful marriage

Whoever, dishonestly or with a fraudulent intention, goes through the ceremony of being married, knowing that he is not thereby lawfully married, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine.

§ 81 = deceit on the woman to make her believe the marriage is real; § 83 = fraudulent ceremony without even inducing her belief (both parties may know it is sham) — e.g., the staged marriage for immigration or to legitimise a live-in.

BNS § 84 / IPC § 498 — Enticing or taking away or detaining with criminal intent a married woman

Whoever takes or entices away any woman who is and whom he knows or has reason to believe to be the wife of any other man, with intent that she may have illicit intercourse with any person, or conceals or detains with that intent any such woman, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine, or with both.

5.3  §§ 85–86 / s. 498A — Cruelty by husband or relatives

BNS §§ 85–86 / IPC § 498A — Husband or relative of husband subjecting a woman to cruelty

§ 85. Whoever, being the husband or the relative of the husband of a woman, subjects such woman to cruelty shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to three years and shall also be liable to fine.

§ 86. "Cruelty" means — (a) any wilful conduct which is of such a nature as is likely to drive the woman to commit suicide or to cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health (whether mental or physical) of the woman; or (b) harassment of the woman where such harassment is with a view to coercing her or any person related to her to meet any unlawful demand for any property or valuable security or is on account of failure by her or any person related to her to meet such demand.

Enacted 1983 (Act 46 of 1983) to reach the torture that fell between 304B's deaths and the hurt chapter's bruises. Cognizable, non-bailable, non-compoundable. Clause (a) is the mental-cruelty limb; clause (b) the dowry-harassment limb. The BNS splits the old Explanation into a standalone definitional section — a drafting clean-up, no substantive change.

The 498A controversy (SAFMA's subject) : the very stringency that makes the section work (cognizable + non-bailable + non-compoundable) made it, in the Supreme Court's words in Arnesh Kumar, a provision "used as weapons rather than shields by disgruntled wives" — NCRB 2012: 1,97,762 arrests in one year. SAFMA balances: the offence stays as the legislature made it; the safeguards lie in arrest discipline (BNSS § 35 / CrPC 41), not in disarming the section. Full analysis in the case card, § 7.7.

5.4  § 87 / s. 366 — Kidnapping/abducting a woman to compel marriage

BNS § 87 / IPC § 366 — Kidnapping, abducting or inducing woman to compel her marriage, etc.

Whoever kidnaps or abducts any woman with intent that she may be compelled, or knowing it to be likely that she will be compelled, to marry any person against her will, or in order that she may be forced or seduced to illicit intercourse, or knowing it to be likely that she will be forced or seduced to illicit intercourse, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to ten years, and shall also be liable to fine; and whoever, by means of criminal intimidation or of abuse of authority or any other method of compulsion, induces any woman to go from any place with intent that she may be, or knowing it to be likely that she will be, forced or seduced to illicit intercourse with another person shall also be punishable as aforesaid.

The bridge offence between the kidnapping chapter and the women's chapter — Gurmit Singh's and Chhoteylal's charges under 366 show it riding along with § 63 in abduction-rape cases (the girl taken away "in order that she may be forced… to illicit intercourse").

The Consent Doctrine — the Unit's Cross-Cutting Synthesis

Will vs consent vs passive submission; the false-promise-of-marriage jurisprudence from Uday to Deepak Gulati; custodial consent and the 114A presumption — the single thread on which five of the seven cases hang.

6.1  Will · consent · submission — the three registers

Chhoteylal gives the classic exposition, drawing on Jowitt's Dictionary and American authority: consent "is an act of reason, accompanied with deliberation, the mind weighing, as in a balance, the good or evil on either side… consent supposes three things — a physical power, a mental power, and a free and serious use of them." And: "consent of the will and submission under the influence of fear or terror cannot amount to real consent" (Hallmark v. State). The three registers:

RegisterMeaningAuthority
WillThe wish, desire, inclination — "purely and solely a mental process," ascertained only from what the woman said and did (State v. Schwab, quoted in Chhoteylal)Circumstance (i) — "against her will": intercourse despite resistance and opposition
ConsentReasoned, voluntary assent after exercise of intelligence and a real choice between resistance and assent; express or implied, by word, gesture or conductCircumstance (ii) — the Expl. 2 definition; Mango Ram: "Submission of the body under the fear of terror cannot be construed as a consented sexual act… after having fully exercised the choice between resistance and assent"
SubmissionPassive, helpless surrender of the body induced by threats, fear or circumstance — no exercise of choice at allMathura's High Court ("mere passive or helpless surrender… cannot be equated with the desire or will") — right in principle, wrong in application; Chhoteylal restores it: "The circumstances made her submissive victim and that does not mean that she was inclined and willing"
The synthesis for problems : a woman who does not resist may still not have consented — fear, isolation, captivity, the presence of an armed man (Chhoteylal's "any alarm at unknown place might have endangered her life") convert non-resistance into submission, and submission into circumstance (i) or (ii). The proviso to Expl. 2 makes this statutory: "a woman who does not physically resist… shall not by the reason only of that fact, be regarded as consenting."

6.2  The false-promise-of-marriage ladder

Where a woman submits to intercourse because the man promises to marry her, is her consent vitiated by "misconception of fact" (s. 90/§ 28)? The line of authority all DU's cases traverse:

Deelip Singh v. State of Bihar (2005)s. 90's two-part anatomy: the victim's misconception AND the accused's knowledge/reason-to-believe must both be shown — "the requirements of both the parts should be cumulatively satisfied"

Such cases must be treated differently." — Deepak Gulati v. State of Haryana (DU CM), ¶ 21

6.3  Custodial and coercive contexts

  • Mathura's lesson: a police station at midnight is not a neutral site — the 1983 Amendment (custodial rape clauses in § 64(2)) and the IEA 114A / BSA 120 presumption (in aggravated prosecutions, where intercourse is proved and the woman says she did not consent, the court shall presume she did not) are the law's answer to Tukaram's "no fear of death or hurt" formalism.
  • Position-induced consent: § 68/376C punishes intercourse procured by abuse of authority where it falls short of rape — the consent is treated as induced, not free.
  • Captivity: Gurmit Singh (locked tube-well kotha, guards outside) and Chhoteylal (weeks in a rented room, armed captor) — consent obtained in such settings is presumptively no consent; the courts assess the woman's situation, not her abstractions ("the circumstances made her submissive victim").
  • Prior sexual history: irrelevant. Gurmit Singh: even if the prosecutrix "has been promiscuous in her sexual behaviour earlier, she has a right to refuse to submit herself to sexual intercourse to anyone and everyone because she is not a vulnerable object or prey." IEA 53A / BSA 48 makes the point evidentiary.

7.1  Tukaram & Anr. v. State of Maharashtra (the Mathura case)

AIR 1979 SC 185 — Koshal J. (with Kania and Tulzapurkar JJ. concurring) DU CASE MATERIAL (CM 2023, Case 24 — with the Open Letter as prescribed reading)

Facts

Mathura, an orphan tribal girl aged 14–16 (doctor's estimate), lived with her brother Gama; both worked as labourers. Through her visits to the house of Nushi, Mathura had come to know and fall in love with Nushi's nephew Ashok; the two "decided to become husband and wife". On 26 March 1972, Gama lodged a report at Desai Gunj police station alleging Mathura had been kidnapped by Nushi, her husband and Ashok. Head Constable Baburao recorded the lovers' statements at about 10:30 p.m. and left for his evening meal. As Mathura, her brother, Nushi and Ashok were leaving, the two appellants — Tukaram, Head Constable, and Ganpat, Constable — asked Mathura to wait and told the others to go. What followed: Ganpat took Mathura into the rear latrine, "loosened her underwear, lit a torch and stared at her private parts", then dragged her to the chhapri (back verandah), felled her and "raped her in spite of protests and stiff resistance". He left; Tukaram, "seated on a cot nearby", came, fondled her private parts, and "wanted to rape her but was unable to do so for the reason he was in a highly intoxicated condition". Outside, the companions grew suspicious at the lights going off and the door being bolted; a crowd gathered; Mathura emerged and told Nushi and Gama that Ganpat had raped her. Medical examination the next evening: no injuries on her person; hymen revealed old ruptures; vagina admitted two fingers easily; no matting of pubic hair; no semen on vaginal smears or pubic hair — but semen detected on her clothes and on Ganpat's pyjama.

Course below

Sessions Judge (acquittal): age not proved below 16; Mathura was "a shocking liar" whose testimony "is riddled with falsehood and improbabilities"; the farthest one could go was that she had intercourse "in all probability" with accused 2 — but "there is a world of difference between sexual intercourse and rape"; she was habituated to intercourse; the story was invented to "sound virtuous" before her lover Ashok. Bombay High Court (Nagpur Bench): reversed — the girl was in a police station at the dead of night, in the clutches of strangers in authority; the initiative must have come from the accused; "mere passive or helpless surrender of the body and its resignation to the other's lust induced by threats or fear cannot be equated with the desire or will"; conviction: Ganpat — 376 (5 years RI), Tukaram — 354 (1 year RI).

Issues

  • Does intercourse with a girl in police custody, where she offers no resistance, amount to rape — is "passive submission" consent?
  • Must the fear that vitiates consent under clause Thirdly be fear specifically of death or hurt?
  • Is the absence of bodily injuries and of semen in the smears inconsistent with rape?

Held (Koshal J.) — the acquittals

The Court allowed the appeal. (1) No marks of injury — "their absence goes a long way to indicate that the alleged intercourse was a peaceful affair"; the cries and alarm were "a tissue of lies". (2) Clause Thirdly requires the consent be obtained by putting her in fear of death or of hurt; the High Court had made no finding of any such fear; circumstantial evidence must "lead to no reasonable inference other than that of guilt" — here it "actually derogates" from the HC's inference. (3) The girl was leaving the police station with her brother, aunt and lover at the entrance when Ganpat caught her — "her natural impulse would be to shake off the hand that caught her and cry out for help"; her failure to do so made the consent not one "which could be brushed aside as passive submission". (4) The onus never shifts — every ingredient of s. 375 had to be proved by the prosecution. (5) As to Tukaram: the girl had gone back at trial on her FIR allegations against him (the torch, the latrine) — "where is the assurance that her word is truthful?"; his conviction under 354 was also set aside.

The aftermath — why this case is a monument

The judgment triggered the most famous academic protest in Indian legal history: "An Open Letter to the Chief Justice of India" ((1979) 4 SCC (J) 17 — Upendra Baxi, Lotika Sarkar, Raghunath Kelkar, Vasudha Dhagamwar), accusing the Court of treating Mathura's consent as presumed because she was "habituated to sex", and exposing the law's blindness to custodial coercion. The consequences, in law: (1) the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 1983 — custodial rape made an aggravated offence with a floor sentence (now § 64(2)(a)–(b)); consent-questioning curtailed by the IEA s. 114A presumption (now BSA § 120) where intercourse is proved in the aggravated cases and the woman says she did not consent; (2) s. 376(2) extended to public servants, superintendents of institutions, hospital staff; (3) in-camera trial provisions (s. 327(2)–(3) CrPC, now BNSS § 356) — Gurmit Singh's charter. In legal culture: the death of the "she was habituated, so she consented" inference — expressly buried in Gurmit Singh.

Principles evolved (and un-evolved)

  • As decided: passive submission is not per se absence of consent; clause Thirdly demands proof of fear of death or hurt; the onus of proving every ingredient of rape lies always on the prosecution.
  • As superseded: the "no injuries = peaceful affair" and "natural impulse to resist" reasonings are no longer good law — Expl. 2's proviso (non-resistance ≠ consent), the custody aggravators, and the BSA § 120 presumption have reversed the practical burden in custodial cases.
  • Historical principle: rape law in India is caselaw-plus-statute responding to scandal — Mathura (1983), Vishaka (1997 guidelines → 354A in 2013), Nirbhaya (2013 amendment).

DU-exam hooks

"Mere passive or helpless surrender of the body… cannot be equated with the desire or will" (the High Court, quoted in the CM text — and vindicated in principle by Chhoteylal/Gurmit Singh) · "a world of difference between sexual intercourse and rape" (Sessions) · the Open Letter · the 1983 Amendment trio. Use Mathura as the opening or closing frame of every custodial-rape answer.

7.2  Kanwar Pal Singh Gill v. State (Admn., U.T. Chandigarh) — the Rupan Deol Bajaj case

(2005) 6 SCC 161 — K.G. Balakrishnan J. DU CASE MATERIAL DU LL.B. · CRIME CM 2025 · UNIT 5 / CM 2023 CASE 23

Facts

On 18 July 1988, a senior IAS officer (Financial Commissioner & Secretary, Punjab) hosted a dinner at his Sector 16, Chandigarh residence for IAS/IPS officers, advocates (including the Advocate General) and journalists. The ladies sat in a semi-circle slightly away from the male guests. The accused K.P.S. Gill, Director General of Police, Punjab — then at the height of his anti-militancy fame — came and occupied a vacant chair among the ladies, called Rupan Deol Bajaj, a senior IAS officer, to sit beside him, and pulled her chair close; embarrassed, she pulled it back. About ten minutes later he asked her to come along with him; she strongly objected; he blocked her way — and then "slapped [her] on the posterior… in the presence of other guests". She complained at once to the host ("his behaviour was obnoxious… he was not fit for decent company"), to the Joint Director IB present there, and to her husband; the next day she recounted the incident to the Chief Secretary, the Advisor to the Governor, the Secretary to the Governor and the Governor himself; on 29 July 1988 she gave a written police complaint — no action followed. After about four months, her husband filed a complaint before the CJM, Chandigarh under ss. 341, 342, 352, 354, 355 and 509 IPC. The High Court quashed the complaint under s. 482 CrPC (now BNSS § 528); the Supreme Court set that aside and directed cognizance under 354 and 509. After full trial: conviction — 3 months + ₹500 (354), 2 months + ₹200 (509). The Sessions Judge confirmed but released him on probation, enhancing the fine to ₹50,000; the High Court maintained the conviction and enhanced the fine to ₹2,00,000 payable to the prosecutrix + ₹25,000 costs.

Issues

  • Does a single slap on the posterior, delivered publicly, amount to "assault or criminal force… with intent to outrage her modesty" (354/§ 74) and an insult to modesty (509/§ 79)?
  • Do the delay in the complaint, the interestedness of witnesses and the absence of some witnesses discredit the prosecutrix?
  • Was probation (in lieu of custody) sustainable for a senior police officer convicted of outraging a colleague's modesty?

Arguments

For Gill: no such incident occurred; the case was a conspiracy to malign him — his anti-militant operations had earned him enemies among top bureaucrats; the complaint came three months late; the witnesses were interested; the most relevant witnesses were not examined.

For Rupan Deol Bajaj (Indira Jaising, senior counsel): crimes against women are on the rise; a top police officer's conviction calls for exemplary, not probationary, treatment.

Held

(1) Conviction affirmed. The delay was fully explained — she had complained immediately and up the entire hierarchy of the State administration, from the host to the Governor, before being driven to the criminal court; there was "nothing to suggest… she hatched a conspiracy to malign the accused". His conduct "was not consistent with the high standard expected of a top-ranking police officer". The finding that he "gently slapped on the posterior of the prosecutrix in the presence of some guests… would certainly constitute the ingredient of Section 354" — criminal force with intent to outrage, and knowledge: "he knew fully well that gently slapping on the posterior… in the presence of other guests would embarrass her… Knowledge can be attributed to the accused that he was fully aware that touching the body of the prosecutrix at that place and time would amount to outraging her modesty." The clinching common-sense test: "Had it been without any culpable intention on the part of the accused, nobody would have taken notice of the incident." The prosecutrix's immediate hue and cry was "very much relevant". (2) Probation maintained — the incident was 1988; probation completed without default; "the various courts found him guilty… that by itself is setting a model for others and would enhance the faith in the judicial system". (3) Compensation — Rupan Deol Bajaj declined to receive the ₹2 lakh; the Court left it to the Chief Justice of Punjab & Haryana to direct it to a women's organization.

Principles evolved

  • Modesty is contextual, not sartorial: the offence is complete on proof of criminal force + intent/knowledge to outrage modesty — however "gentle" the touch; the public setting aggravates the outrage (embarrassment before guests is part of the actus).
  • Immediate complaint chain cures delay: complaints to the host, IB officer, Chief Secretary, Advisor, Governor — a documented contemporaneous trail beats the "3-month delay" argument.
  • Rank is no defence: "the accused being a police officer of the highest rank should have been exceedingly careful" — the standard rises with office.
  • The reaction of the woman is relevant evidence of the act's character (the hue and cry).

DU-exam hooks

"Had it been without any culpable intention… nobody would have taken notice of the incident" · the DGP at the dinner party — the perfect illustration for ANY § 74/354 problem · pair with Chhoteylal for the consent side and with Major Singh's "essence of her modesty is her sex" for the definition. Note also the case's modern significance: s. 354's then-maximum was two years; the 2013 Amendment (1–5 years, minimum one) is the legislature's answer to exactly this genre of token sentencing.

7.3  State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh & Ors.

(1996) 2 SCC 384 — Dr. A.S. Anand J. DU CASE MATERIAL DU LL.B. · CRIME CM 2025 · UNIT 5 / CM 2023 CASE 25

Facts

The prosecutrix, a village girl below 16 (X-class student at Government High School, Pakhowal), was abducted at 12:30 p.m. on 30 March 1984, a hundred karmas from her examination centre, by three young men in a blue Ambassador — Gurmit Singh, Jagjit Singh @ Bawa and Ranjit Singh: Ranjit caught her arm and pushed her in, Jagjit gagged her mouth, Gurmit threatened to kill her if she raised alarm. They drove her to the tube-well kotha of Ranjit Singh; the driver left. Gurmit forced her to drink liquor "misrepresenting to her that it was juice", removed her salwar and shirt, and raped her on a cot "while his companions guarded the kotha from outside"; she cried out in pain and was threatened into silence; then Jagjit and Ranjit each raped her in turn; all three subjected her to intercourse again during the night. At 6 a.m. she was dropped near the school; she sat her Hygiene examination, reached her village Nangal-Lalan at noon, and told her mother (PW 7). Her father (PW 6) consulted the Sarpanch; a panchayat of the two villages tried to settle the matter on 1 April; when it failed, the family set out for Police Station Raikot and met the police en route — statement Ex. PD, FIR registered. Medical examination (Dr Sukhwinder Kaur, PW 1, 2 April): hymen lacerated with fine radiate tears, swollen and painful; pubic hair matted; intercourse "one of the reasons" for the laceration; "possibility cannot be ruled out that [she] was not habitual to intercourse earlier". Chemical examiner: semen on the vaginal-smear slides (none on pubic hair/salwar). Charges: 363, 366, 368, 376 IPC. Defence: false implication — Jagjit blamed a hostile Sarpanch (his Canadian marriage); Gurmit alleged 7–8 years of civil litigation between the families and a beating exchanged with PW 6 on 1–2 April; Ranjit offered no reason.

Course below

Additional Sessions Judge: acquittal — the girl could not tell an Ambassador from a Fiat or Master; no alarm at the bus adda; the IO's "pitiable negligence" in not tracing the car/driver; delay in FIR (panchayat consultations "rendered the matter doubtful"); medical evidence doubtful (the speculum admission = "the possibility that the prosecutrix was habituated… could not be ruled out"); no corroboration; enmity. He even held she "was fighting her imagination to rope in the accused" and was "a girl of loose character" who "wanted to dupe her parents". High Court: conviction — the Sessions Judge's approach "rebels against realism". In appeal to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  • Is the testimony of a rape prosecutrix enough to convict without corroboration?
  • How are delay in FIR, absence of alarm, ignorance of the car's make, and the investigating officer's negligence to be treated?
  • Can a girl's supposed sexual habituation be used to discredit her — or to found a "loose character" finding?
  • What are the trial court's duties in conducting rape trials?

Arguments

For the accused (supporting acquittal): the multiplicities of doubt — car's make, no alarm, delayed FIR after panchayat consultation, no corroboration, medical doubt, admitted enmity and cross-beatings between the families.

For the State: the trial court's reasons are stereotypes, not evidence; the girl's account is consistent and corroborated by the medical and chemical evidence; the panchayat route explains the FIR timing; a father would never stake an unmarried daughter's reputation on a false rape case.

Held — Dr. Anand J. (the reform charter)

(1) The trial court's findings "rebel against realism." Whether a village girl knows Ambassador from Fiat is "wholly irrelevant"; she was threatened with death if she raised alarm — "to discredit the prosecutrix for not raising an alarm… is a travesty of justice"; the IO's negligence cannot be visited on her — "the prosecutrix had no control over the investigating agency". (2) Delay natural and explained: "in sexual offences delay in the lodging of the FIR can be due to variety of reasons particularly the reluctance of the prosecutrix or her family members… It is only after giving it a cool thought that a complaint of sexual offence is generally lodged"; the panchayat effort and the parents' consultation about "the reputation and future prospects of the marriage… of their daughter" are the natural conduct of a tradition-bound family. (3) The corroboration rule: "Seeking corroboration of her statement before relying upon the same as a rule in such cases amounts to adding insult to injury… Why should the evidence of a girl or a woman who complains of rape… be viewed with doubt, disbelief or suspicion?" Her evidence "stands almost on a par with the evidence of an injured witness and to an extent is even more reliable… a woman or a girl subjected to sexual assault is not an accomplice to the crime but is a victim of another person's lust… Corroboration… is not a requirement of law but a guidance of prudence under given circumstances… Courts cannot cling to a fossil formula." (adopting Chandraprakash Kewalchand Jain). (4) The medical evidence was positive — hymen lacerated, pubic hair matted, semen on the slides: the trial court's "habituated to intercourse" inference was drawn without any specific question put to the doctor and was "a wholly irresponsible finding". (5) Enmity cuts both ways: "enmity is a double-edged weapon, which may be used for false implication as well as to take revenge" — and "No father could stoop so low as to bring forth a false charge of rape on his unmarried minor daughter with a view to take revenge". (6) The stigma ban: "Even if the prosecutrix, in a given case, has been promiscuous in her sexual behaviour earlier, she has a right to refuse to submit herself to sexual intercourse to anyone and everyone because she is not a vulnerable object or prey… it is the accused and not the victim of sex crime who is on trial." (7) Conviction restored — all three: 376 (5 years RI + ₹5,000 each), 363 (3 years RI), concurrent — tempered by the 11-year lapse and their clean record since acquittal. (8) The trial-conduct charter: courts "must deal with such cases with utmost sensitivity"; must control cross-examination so it is not "a means of harassment or causing humiliation"; must not be "swayed by minor contradictions or insignificant discrepancies… not of a fatal nature"; and must hold rape trials in camera as the rule under s. 327(2)–(3) CrPC (now BNSS § 356) — "Trial of rape cases in camera should be the rule and an open trial… an exception"; consider women judges for such cases; withhold the victim's name in orders — "the anonymity of the victim… must be maintained as far as possible throughout."

"A murderer destroys the physical body of his victim; a rapist degrades the very soul of the helpless female… Rape is not merely a physical assault, it is often destructive of the whole personality of the victim." — State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh (DU CM), ¶ 20

Principles evolved

  • A prosecutrix is not an accomplice; her sole testimony, if it inspires confidence, sustains a conviction — corroboration is prudence, not law.
  • Delayed FIR, no alarm, car-ignorance, IO negligence, and non-examination of witnesses are not ipso facto fatal; each must be tested against the girl's situation.
  • Prior sexual behaviour/habituation is irrelevant to consent and can never support a "loose character" finding.
  • Enmity is a double-edged weapon — it can as easily explain the crime as the false case.
  • In-camera trial, controlled cross-examination, victim anonymity, sensitivity — the procedural charter (now BNSS § 356).

DU-exam hooks

"Adding insult to injury" · "not a vulnerable object or prey" · "fossil formula" · "a rapist degrades the very soul" · "enmity is a double-edged weapon". Gurmit Singh is THE answer-skeleton for any "corroboration in rape" or "evidentiary approach" question, and the authority of first resort for trial-conduct and in-camera trial points.

7.4  State of U.P. v. Chhoteylal

AIR 2011 SC 697 — Markandey Katju J. DU CASE MATERIAL DU LL.B. · CRIME CM 2025 · UNIT 5

Facts

On 19 September 1989, a young illiterate "rustic" woman (stated age 13; found 17½ by the trial court) went out to relieve herself in the evening in her village in Hardoi district, U.P. Ram Kali (A-3, female) followed her; on her return, near Vijai Bahadur's plot, Chhoteylal (A-1) and Ramdas (A-2) came from behind; A-1 caught her, and when she raised alarm, showed her a firearm and gagged her mouth. A-1 and A-2 took her to village Sahora (A-3 parting company), kept her overnight in the house of Girish and Saroj Pandit, and next day took her by bus to Shahajahanpur, where she was kept in a rented room for a few weeks while A-1 repeatedly had forcible intercourse with her — "whenever prosecutrix asked for return to her house, A-1 would gag her mouth and threaten her". Her brother Rampal complained to the SP Hardoi on 28 September; FIR 30 September; she was recovered on 13 October from the Shahabad–Pihani road near the Jalalpur culvert, medically examined, and her s. 164 statement recorded on 17 October. A-1 chargesheeted under 363, 366, 368, 376 (A-2 under the first three; A-3 under 363, 366).

Course below

III Additional Sessions Judge, Hardoi: A-3 acquitted; A-2 died (abatement); A-1 convicted under 363/366/368/376 — 7 years RI under 376 plus concurrent sentences, age found "about 17½". Allahabad High Court (Lucknow Bench, 2003): acquittal on three cryptic reasons — (1) the report was lodged after ten days with no explanation; (2) medical evidence put her at 17, "she could be even 19"; (3) "no internal or external injury was found on her body and she was habitual to sexual intercourse". The Supreme Court: "We are indeed surprised by the casual approach… The judgment of the High Court is not only cryptic and perfunctory but it has also not taken into consideration the crucial evidence… On flimsy grounds, the accused convicted of a serious crime of kidnapping and rape has been acquitted."

Issues

  • Is "habituated to sexual intercourse" + absence of injuries inconsistent with rape?
  • Can the High Court add two years to the doctor's age estimate to defeat clause Sixthly?
  • "Against her will" (clause First) vs "without her consent" (clause Secondly) — what is the difference, and which governs a 17-year-old captive?

Held

(1) Age: agreeing with the trial court — about 17½; "There is no such rule much less an absolute one that two years have to be added to the age determined by a doctor" (citing the 3-Judge Bench in State of Karnataka v. Bantara Sudhakara); Mussauddin Ahmed (best-evidence-withheld) inapplicable — the brother testified. Even so, clause Sixthly (under-16) was not attracted — so the case turned on clauses First/Secondly. (2) Will vs consent — the celebrated exposition: "The expressions 'against her will' and 'without her consent' may overlap sometimes but surely… have different connotation and dimension. The expression 'against her will' would ordinarily mean that the intercourse was done by a man with a woman despite her resistance and opposition. On the other hand, the expression 'without her consent' would comprehend an act of reason accompanied by deliberation." Consent (Jowitt) "supposes three things — a physical power, a mental power, and a free and serious use of them"; submission under fear or terror is not consent (Hallmark); and per Mango Ram (3-Judge): "Submission of the body under the fear of terror cannot be construed as a consented sexual act… after having fully exercised the choice between resistance and assent." (3) The habituation myth rejected: no injury + habituation proved nothing — the woman was a captive: "she had been kidnapped by two adult males, one of them wielded a fire-arm and threatened her… In the circumstances, it made sensible decision not to raise alarm. Any alarm at unknown place might have endangered her life… The circumstances made her submissive victim and that does not mean that she was inclined and willing… She had no free act of the mind during her stay with A-1 as she was under constant fear." (4) Corroboration — Gurmit Singh and Bharwada Bhoginbhai (the "lenses tinged with doubt, disbelief or suspicion" passage and the twelve reasons why Indian women rarely fabricate rape charges) applied: "an Indian woman traditionally will not concoct an untruthful story and bring charges of rape for the purpose of blackmail, hatred, spite or revenge. The stigma that attaches to the victim of rape in Indian society ordinarily rules out the leveling of false accusations." (5) The contradictions in her testimony were "minor" and natural for an illiterate rustic woman. Result: High Court reversed; conviction restored; the accused "shall now surrender within two months… to serve out the remaining sentence."

Principles evolved

  • Against her will ≠ without consent: "will" is the mental wish evidenced by resistance; "consent" is the reasoned, deliberate act of a free mind — two distinct limbs of § 63/375, both satisfied by a captive's submission.
  • Submissive victim ≠ willing victim: captivity, an armed captor, and unfamiliar surroundings explain the absence of alarm; non-resistance is the rational response, not proof of consent.
  • No "+2 years" medical-age rule — the doctor's opinion stands unless displaced by real evidence (Bantara Sudhakara).
  • Habituation and absence of injuries are not exculpatory — the Gurmit Singh stigma-ban applied to the High Court itself.
  • Appellate acquittals must engage with the evidence — a "cryptic and perfunctory" reversal is itself a ground for Supreme Court correction.

7.5  Deepak Gulati v. State of Haryana

(2013) 7 SCC 675 — Dr. B.S. Chauhan and Dipak Misra JJ. DU CASE MATERIAL DU LL.B. · CRIME CM 2025 · UNIT 5

Facts

The appellant Deepak and Geeta, a 19-year-old 10+2 student of Government Girls Senior Secondary School, Karnal, "had known each other for some time"; he met her in front of her school "in an attempt to develop intimate relations". On 10 May 1995 he induced her to go with him to Kurukshetra to get married — she agreed, collected her school certificate as proof of age, telephoned him when he did not show at the pre-decided spot, and went with him by bus to Karna Lake. There, per her court deposition, he took her into the bushes and raped her ("she neither raised any objection, nor any hue and cry"); then to Kurukshetra, where they stayed with his relatives 3–4 days and (she said) he committed rape again, postponing marriage "on one pretext or the other". On the fourth day she was thrown out; she took shelter in the Kurukshetra University girls' hostel (without authorisation); the warden grew suspicious; the family was informed; she left, met Deepak again at Birla Mandir, agreed once more to go to Ambala to marry him in court — and at the old bus stand found her father and the police waiting. FIR by the father under 365/366, later converted to 365/376. Medical examination (17 May): intercourse, "habitual to the same". The Sessions Judge convicted Deepak of 365 and 376 (3 years RI + ₹2,000; 7 years RI + ₹5,000); the High Court dismissed his appeal.

Issues

  • Does intercourse on a promise of marriage vitiate consent as "misconception of fact" under s. 90 — when does a false promise convert consensual sex into rape?
  • Where the girl is a willing adult who elopes, stays, and sets out again to marry, can rape stand?

Arguments

For the appellant (no counsel appeared; the Court examined the record with State counsel): the prosecutrix left home voluntarily, raised no complaint anywhere at any stage, lived and travelled with him for days, and was setting out to marry him when apprehended — the intercourse was consensual; any failure of the marriage plan was a breach of promise, not a deception that induced consent.

For the State: he had taken her on the pretext of marriage, exploited her for days, and thrown her out — the "false promise" made her consent a non-consent under s. 90.

Held

The Court marshalled the false-promise line — Uday v. State of Karnataka ("no straitjacket formula… each case has its own peculiar facts"), Deelip Singh (s. 90's two parts, cumulatively), Yedla Srinivasa Rao, Pradeep Kumar Verma — and distilled the governing tests: (1) consent is "an act of reason, accompanied by deliberation, the mind weighing, as in a balance, the good and evil on each side"; (2) "there is a distinction between the mere breach of a promise, and not fulfilling a false promise"; (3) rape requires proof "that at the relevant time, i.e., at initial stage itself, the accused had no intention whatsoever, of keeping his promise to marry" — "An accused can be convicted for rape only if the court reaches a conclusion that the intention of the accused was mala fide, and that he had clandestine motives"; (4) a promise that fails "due to reasons… beyond his control" is not a misconception; the misconception "must have an immediate relevance". Application: the girl was 19, "of adequate intelligence and maturity"; she telephoned him, waited, went willingly, complained to no one, stayed, was even then proceeding to Ambala to marry him: "we fail to understand on what basis the allegation of 'false promise of marriage' has been raised… we also fail to comprehend the circumstances in which a charge of deceit/rape can be leveled against the appellant." Convictions (which had already cost him over 3 years in prison) set aside; benefit of doubt.

Principles evolved

  • Consent obtained by a promise of marriage is vitiated only if the promise was false from inception, with the man's knowledge — mala fide intention and clandestine motive are the gist.
  • Breach ≠ deception: a genuine promise defeated by circumstances (family, caste, unforeseen events) leaves the intercourse consensual.
  • Both parts of s. 90/§ 28 must be proved cumulatively (Deelip Singh): her misconception AND his knowledge of it.
  • The adult woman's own conduct — willingness, continued association, renewed attempts to marry — is the decisive evidentiary matrix.
  • Legislative afterlife: the case's "gap" (the man who is not a rapist but is no innocent) is now filled by BNS § 69 — deceitful means/false promise to marry "without any intention of fulfilling the same… not amounting to… rape", up to 10 years.

DU-exam hooks

"Distinction between mere breach of a promise, and not fulfilling a false promise" · "mala fide… clandestine motives" · the Uday quartet · the § 69 codification point (examiners in the BNS era will reward the IPC-case → BNS-section bridge). This is THE case for the Dec 2014 Q5(a) false-promise problem (PYQ q1).

7.6  Independent Thought v. Union of India

(2017) 10 SCC 800 — 3-Judge Bench: Madan B. Lokur J. (for himself and R.K. Agrawal J.); Deepak Gupta J. concurring DU CASE MATERIAL (CM 2025 TOC — prescribed without full text) RECONSTRUCTED FROM THE REPORTED JUDGMENT

Facts

Independent Thought, an NGO working on child rights, petitioned under Article 32 challenging the constitutional validity of Exception 2 to s. 375 IPC insofar as it immunised a husband from prosecution for rape of his "wife" aged between 15 and 18 — the exception then read: "Sexual intercourse by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under fifteen years of age, is not rape." The anomaly was stark: clause Sixthly made intercourse with any girl under 16 rape "with or without her consent" (raised to 18 in 2013); POCSO 2012 criminalised penetrative sexual assault on any child under 18 regardless of marriage; the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 treated a child marriage as voidable; yet the married girl child of 15–18 had no rape protection at all — a husband's non-consensual intercourse with her was not even an offence, save under the hurt provisions.

Issues

  • Is Exception 2, insofar as it permits a husband's non-consensual intercourse with a wife aged 15–18, violative of Articles 14, 15 and 21?
  • Should the exception be struck down, or read down to 18 — and does the Court thereby "create a new offence"?
  • (Expressly excluded:) the marital rape of adult women — "The Court is not concerned here with the marital rape of a woman who is 18 years of age and above."

Arguments

For the petitioner: the exception is arbitrary and discriminatory — it denies the married girl child protection that every unmarried girl child enjoys; it conflicts with POCSO (s. 5 aggravated penetrative sexual assault), the 2013 IPC amendment (age 18), PCMA, and India's obligations under the CRC; child marriage cannot operate as a licence to assault.

For the Union: the age of 15 in Exception 2 was a considered legislative choice protecting the "husband-wife relationship" in child marriages; the husband, though exempt from rape, remains punishable for any accompanying physical violence (ss. 323–325); raising the threshold would destabilise the institution of marriage and prosecutions could be misused.

Held — Lokur J.

The Court catalogued the "apparent conflict or incongruity": "the rape of a married girl child (a girl child between 15 and 18 years of age) is not rape under IPC… but it is an offence of aggravated penetrative sexual assault under Section 5(n) of the POCSO Act and punishable under Section 6 of that Act" — an absurdity demanding constitutional correction. It surveyed the five possible options and chose reading down over striking down, so as to preserve the exception for adult wives while extending rape protection to girl wives. The grounds: Exception 2, as applied to girl children, is "(i) arbitrary, capricious, whimsical and violative of the rights of the girl child and not fair, just and reasonable and, therefore, violative of Articles 14, 15 and 21; (ii) discriminatory and violative of Article 14" — drawing an irrational line between the married and unmarried girl child, both minors, one shielded and one exposed. The operative reading: "Exception 2 to Section 375 IPC is read down as follows: 'Exception 2 — Sexual intercourse or sexual acts by a man with his own wife, the wife not being under eighteen years of age, is not rape.'" The judgment is prospective in effect; prosecutions for intercourse with a wife below 18 must follow the s. 198(6) CrPC procedure (complaint by the girl or specified kin); and the Court's self-description: "What has been done is only to read down Exception 2… to bring it in consonance with the Constitution and the POCSO Act. The Court is not creating any new offence but only removing what was unconstitutional and offensive." Deepak Gupta J. concurring: the exception is "violative of fundamental rights" — dignity, bodily integrity and autonomy of the girl child; marriage is not a licence to assault a minor.

Principles evolved

  • Marriage is not a defence to child rape: Exception 2 cannot shield non-consensual intercourse with a wife under 18 — the marital exception and the age-of-consent clause are now harmonised at 18.
  • Reading down over striking down: where severance of the unconstitutional application preserves the constitutional remainder, the court cures rather than demolishes — and no new offence is created (answer to the "legislating" objection).
  • Statutory harmonisation: IPC read in the light of POCSO, PCMA, and Article 21's dignity/bodily-integrity core.
  • Codification: BNS § 63 Exception 2 now states the threshold as "not being under eighteen years" — Independent Thought became the text of the statute on 1 July 2024.

DU-exam hooks

"The Court is not creating any new offence but only removing what was unconstitutional and offensive" · the IPC-vs-POCSO "incongruity" passage · Articles 14/15/21 tripod · the adult-marital-rape question expressly left open (contrast the pending debate). Every "marital rape exception" answer should run: Exception 2 → Independent Thought (child wives) → § 67 (separated wives) → the open adult question → BNS's silence as continuation.

7.7  Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar & Anr. v. Union of India

(2018) 10 SCC 443 : AIR 2018 SC 4273 — Dipak Misra CJI (with Khanwilkar and D.Y. Chandrachud JJ.) DU CASE MATERIAL DU LL.B. · CRIME CM 2025 · UNIT 5

Facts (the two petitions and the impugned judgment)

The case is a constitutional audit of s. 498A IPC's administration (now BNS §§ 85–86). Petitioners (including women's organisations) approached under Article 32: hundreds of women suffer horrific domestic violence and dowry extortion; there is "no concrete data" for the alleged misuse; the courts, by "whittling down the stringency" of 498A, were defeating the legislature's protective design; the offence had been "practically made bailable" through judicial glosses; the police hesitate to arrest though the offence is cognizable and non-bailable; and courts brand women "hyper-sensitive". Meanwhile, a second stream of litigation complained of the opposite vice — 498A as a "weapon rather than shield", with 1,97,762 persons arrested in 2012 alone (NCRB) — culminating in Rajesh Sharma v. State of U.P. (2018) 10 SCC 472, where the Supreme Court had laid down a battery of guidelines: Family Welfare Committees (FWCs) in every district (composed of para-legal volunteers, social workers, retirees, "wives of working officers") to vet every 498A complaint before any arrest; designated investigating officers; disposal of settled cases by the District Judge; bail directions; restraint on passport impounding/Red Corner Notices; exemption from personal appearance. The present petitions sought implementation — and the Court appointed V. Shekhar, senior counsel, as amicus, who argued that Rajesh Sharma's directions were themselves illegitimate.

Issues

  • Do the Rajesh Sharma directions — an extra-judicial Family Welfare Committee interposed before arrest — square with the CrPC (now BNSS)?
  • Can a court, "in the process of interpretation", create a third agency and gate-keep a cognizable, non-bailable offence?
  • How is the balance to be struck between the victim-wife's statutory protection and the accused family's liberty?

Arguments

Petitioners: misuse is unproven; even where it exists, "misuse by itself cannot be a ground to repeal a penal provision or take away its teeth"; dilution destroys the social purpose; the 498A mechanism includes its own checks (s. 41 CrPC arrest discipline).

Amicus (V. Shekhar): the FWC is an extra-judicial committee with "nothing to do with the Code"; its veto on arrest till report is contrary to the statutory scheme; entrusting disposal to the District Judge is impermissible since 498A is not compoundable — only the High Court under s. 482 CrPC (BNSS § 528) can quash on settlement (per Gian Singh v. State of Punjab (2012) 10 SCC 303); the bail/passport/appearance directions intrude on judicial discretion.

Held — the 498A balance sheet

(1) The statute stands. "The legislature in its wisdom has made the offence under Section 498-A IPC cognizable and non-bailable." Courts may not rewrite it: "Function of this Court is not to legislate but only to interpret the law" (quoting Rajesh Sharma itself) — and per Suresh Seth, no court can direct legislation or create what the Code does not contain. (2) The fault lies in administration, not the section: "The fault lies with the investigating agency which sometimes jumps into action without application of mind." (3) The FWC directions fall: "In Rajesh Sharma… there is introduction of a third agency which has nothing to do with the Code… the constitution of the Family Welfare Committees by the District Legal Services Authority and the power conferred on the Committee [is] impermissible." (4) Settlement route corrected: a non-compoundable offence can be closed only by the High Court under s. 482 CrPC / BNSS § 528 applying Gian Singh — the parties may so move; the District Judge has no such power. (5) The protective directions survive: restraint on passport impounding/RCNs (19.15); clubbing of connected cases (19.16); exemption from personal appearance through ss. 205/317 CrPC (BNSS §§ 275/352); and "recovery of disputed dowry items may not by itself be a ground for denial of bail" (19.14). (6) The governing framework reaffirmed: Joginder Kumar ("No arrest can be made in a routine manner on a mere allegation… Denying a person of his liberty is a serious matter"); D.K. Basu (the arrest-memo, notification, medical-examination guidelines — reproduced in full in the judgment); Lalita Kumari (FIR registration; preliminary enquiry permissible in matrimonial disputes); and above all Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar (2014) 8 SCC 273 — the "why arrest?" checklist under s. 41/BNSS § 35: "a police officer before arrest must put a question to himself, why arrest? Is it really required? What purpose it will serve?" — with the directions that police not arrest automatically in 498A cases, that the s. 41(1)(b) checklist be filled and furnished to the Magistrate, that the Magistrate record satisfaction before authorising detention, that non-arrest decisions be sent to the Magistrate within two weeks, and that violation invites departmental action and contempt. (7) Forward directions: investigating officers to be guided by Joginder Kumar, D.K. Basu, Lalita Kumari and Arnesh Kumar; DGP of each State to give IOs rigorous training on arrest principles in 498A cases.

"The directions issued in Arnesh Kumar (supra) are in consonance with the provisions contained in Section 41 CrPC and Section 41-A CrPC… The purpose has been to see that the investigating agency does not abuse the power and arrest people at its whim and fancy." — Social Action Forum for Manav Adhikar (DU CM), ¶ 37

Principles evolved

  • Misuse is not a ground to disarm a penal provision: the remedy for 498A abuse lies in arrest discipline and judicial discretion, not in erecting extra-statutory filters or making the offence toothless.
  • Judicial legislation forbidden: courts cannot, "by the process of interpretation", create committees, agencies or procedures the Code does not contain (Suresh Seth; the FWC fall).
  • Quashing on settlement belongs to the High Court alone (s. 482 CrPC / BNSS § 528; Gian Singh) for non-compoundable offences.
  • Arrest is not automatic: the Arnesh Kumar checklist (s. 41 CrPC / BNSS § 35) and s. 41A notice procedure (BNSS § 35(3)) govern every 498A case — protecting the innocent without disarming the victim.
  • The judgment is the BNS-era map for § 85–86 cruelty practice: same offence, same safeguards, new numbers.

Is it really required?" · "third agency which has nothing to do with the Code" · Gian Singh/482 as the settlement route. The perfect pairing for any 498A question: SAFMA (safeguards) + Gurmit Singh (sensitivity) — protection and procedure, both.

Q1
Mr. B and Ms. A, class fellows from Class X, developed infatuation towards each other. They carried the relationship for 6 long years till the boy did his master's course and was employed in a good multinational. They had promised to marry each other and were firm to do so, knowing fully well they belonged to different castes. On the basis of this promise and long relationship they entered into sexual relationship many a times. However, finally, when the boy disclosed it to his parents, they refused on the basis of caste; threatened the boy of social ostracisation and even death of both in case they do so. Compelled by these reasons, the boy married another girl. A charges the boy B of having committed rape with her. Decide the fate of B.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.5(a) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (solved with Uday/Deepak Gulati)
Framing the offence

A's case must be that her consent to each act of intercourse was obtained by a misconception of fact — the promise of marriage — within s. 90 IPC / § 28 BNS read with s. 375 Secondly / § 63(ii). If the misconception vitiates consent, every act is rape (s. 376/§ 64(1)).

The governing tests
  • Deelip Singh (quoted in Deepak Gulati): s. 90 has two parts — (i) the woman's consent was given under a misconception of fact; (ii) the man knew or had reason to believe it was so given. Both must be proved cumulatively.
  • Deepak Gulati: "there is a distinction between the mere breach of a promise, and not fulfilling a false promise… An accused can be convicted for rape only if the court reaches a conclusion that the intention of the accused was mala fide, and that he had clandestine motives" — i.e., the promise was false from inception.
  • Uday v. State of Karnataka: no straitjacket formula; each case on its facts — and on almost identical facts (adult couple, caste resistance, marriage defeated by family) Uday held the consent not vitiated: the woman was "conscious of the fact that her marriage may not take place owing to various considerations, including the caste factor".
Application

(1) A is an adult (six years from Class X; the man completed a master's and employment — both well past 18), of full understanding — clause (vi) age-rape is out. (2) The promise was mutual and long-standing — made when both "were firm to do so"; the rupture came from the parents' caste refusal and threats, events beyond B's control — precisely Uday's caste-refusal facts and Deepak Gulati's "circumstances which he could not have foreseen, or which were beyond his control" exception. (3) There is no material to show B never intended to marry — no mala fide at inception, no clandestine motive; the sexual relationship flowed from "love and passion… and not solely on account of misrepresentation". (4) A's knowledge of the caste obstacle shows she consented with her eyes open to the risk. Therefore consent was not vitiated; B is not guilty of rape.

The BNS twist the 2024+ answer must add

Under the BNS, even where rape fails, the court must examine § 69 — intercourse "by making promise to marry to a woman without any intention of fulfilling the same", not amounting to rape. On these facts § 69 also fails for the same reason — the intention to fulfil existed and its defeat was parent-driven. But note the grading: had B promised from the start with no intention (a mere seduction device), the modern charge would be § 69 (up to 10 years) rather than rape — the Deepak Gulati gap, closed by the legislature.

Conclusion

B is acquitted of rape (s. 376/§ 63-64); his liability, if any, is civil/societal, not criminal. Support with Uday, Deelip Singh, Yedla Srinivasa Rao (contrast), Pradeep Kumar Verma (contrast), Deepak Gulati.

Q2
Annoyed due to insufficient dowry brought by X, her mother-in-law and husband deliberately starved and ill-treated her by locking her in a room. As a result X's health deteriorated. One day she managed to escape and reached a hospital where she was admitted. The doctor refused the husband's request to send her home in such critical condition. It took more than 10 months for the lady to recover. Discuss the offence the mother-in-law and husband have committed. Give reasons.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2014 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.2(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (§§ 85–86 / 498A web)
The primary offence — cruelty s. 498A / §§ 85–86

Both are within the section's net: the husband simpliciter; the mother-in-law as "relative of the husband". The conduct — deliberate starvation, locking in a room, ill-treatment — is (i) wilful conduct "of such a nature as is likely to… cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health (whether mental or physical)" — clause (a)/§ 86(a); and (ii) since the motive was dissatisfaction with dowry, harassment… on account of failure… to meet [an unlawful] demand — clause (b)/§ 86(b). Punishment: up to 3 years + fine; cognizable, non-bailable, non-compoundable.

The accompanying offences
  • Wrongful confinement — locking her in a room (ss. 342–343 IPC / § 127(2)–(3) BNS; the "locking in a room" is the classic confinement actus).
  • Hurt by starvation — the deliberate denial of food causing health deterioration: s. 323/325 as appropriate (§§ 115(2), 117 BNS); if her condition verged on danger to life, s. 328 IPC? No — that is hurt by poison; but attempt to commit culpable homicide arguments arise if starvation was intended to endanger life — on these facts, injury and intimidation, not death, appear intended.
  • Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 — the "insufficient dowry" grievance presupposes a demand: ss. 3 (giving/taking dowry) and 4 (dowry demand punishable, min 6 months to 2 years + fine) ride along.
Had she died — the counterfactual the examiner loves

Starvation-death (or any unnatural death) within 7 years of marriage + the proved soon-before cruelty for dowry = dowry death, s. 304B/§ 80 — the perpetrators "deemed to have caused her death" (7 years to life), with the BSA § 118/IEA 113B presumption flipping the burden. The escape and hospital admission are what keep this a cruelty case; the doctor's refusal to discharge her becomes the corpus of the danger-to-health element.

Procedure note

Arrest of the mother-in-law and husband is governed by Arnesh Kumar / BNSS § 35 discipline (SAFMA) — but the 10-month hospitalisation evidence removes any "why arrest?" doubt. Also note Gurmit Singh's sensitivity principle applies to her testimony as the sole witness of the household's interior.

Conclusion

Husband and mother-in-law: guilty of cruelty under s. 498A/§ 85 (both limbs of the Explanation/§ 86) + wrongful confinement + relevant hurt offences + Dowry Prohibition Act ss. 3/4 — sentences concurrent; the matrimonial-home setting is the aggravating circumstance running through them all.

Q3
"Rape is a question of Law." Explain the essentials of the crime of rape. How is this law different from the law against sexual assault provided under the POCSO Act?
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.7(b) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION
"A question of law" — what the phrase captures

Unlike most offences, rape's definition itself adjudicates the woman's inner state: each of the seven circumstances is a legal standard ("will", "consent", "fear of death or hurt", "unable to understand", "unable to communicate") that the court must apply to proved facts. Chhoteylal: will is "purely and solely a mental process… there is no other manner by which her will can be ascertained" except her acts and statements — so the court translates fact into law through the s. 375/§ 63 grid. The ingredients to state:

  • (1) A man — the offender must be male; (2) one of the four acts — penile penetration "to any extent" of vagina/mouth/urethra/anus, object/body-part penetration, manipulation, mouth application (2013 expansion; earlier only penile-vaginal — "penetration is sufficient");
  • (3) one of the seven circumstances — against her will; without her consent; fear-vitiated consent; mistaken-husband consent; incapacity consent (unsoundness/intoxication/stupefying substance); under 18 (with or without consent); unable to communicate consent;
  • (4) consent defined (Expl. 2): "unequivocal voluntary agreement… willingness to participate in the specific sexual act"; the proviso: non-resistance ≠ consent. Exceptions: medical procedure; the marital exception (wife not under 18 — post-Independent Thought/BNS).
  • Evidence law within the definition: s. 90/§ 28's two-part test (Deelip Singh); BSA 120/IEA 114A presumption in § 64(2) cases; corroboration not a rule of law (Gurmit Singh).
POCSO contrast the second half of the marks

The seven-row comparison table in § 8.1 is the model answer: victim (woman vs child of either sex); offender (man vs any person); the acts; consent wholly irrelevant under POCSO vs the centrepiece of § 63; no marital exception under POCSO (s. 5(n) aggravated penetrative sexual assault — the Independent Thought lever); presumptions of guilt (ss. 29–30) vs the one-shot BSA 120 presumption; special courts and child-friendly procedure; punishments (POCSO minimum 20 years post-2019; § 64 minimum 10); s. 42/42A — greater punishment prevails, POCSO otherwise overrides. Conclude: the IPC/BNS protects women's sexual autonomy (consent the fulcrum); POCSO protects children absolutely (consent legislatively abolished as a defence) — a deliberate double standard in the child's favour.

Q4
Write short notes (any two): (i) Stalking; (ii) Voyeurism; (iii) Disrobing.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015, Q.6(b) — and December 2014, Q.5(b)(ii)–(iii) · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (both papers)
(i) Stalking — § 78 BNS / s. 354D IPC

Born of the 2013 Amendment. Any man who (a) follows a woman and contacts or attempts to contact her to foster personal interaction repeatedly despite a clear indication of disinterest, or (b) monitors her use of the internet, e-mail or any other electronic communication, commits stalking. Punishment: first conviction — up to 3 years + fine; second or subsequent — up to 5 years + fine. Statutory defences (proviso): State-entrusted crime prevention/detection; conduct under a law or legal requirement; conduct "reasonable and justified" in the circumstances. The offence's genius: it punishes persistence after refusal — the first advance may be innocent; the repetition after "a clear indication of disinterest" is the crime; electronic monitoring covers cyber-stalking.

(ii) Voyeurism — § 77 BNS / s. 354C IPC

Whoever watches, or captures the image of, a woman in a private act where she expects not to be observed (by the perpetrator or anyone at his behest), or disseminates such image. "Private act" (Expl. 1): watched in a place reasonably expected to give privacy, where genitals/posterior/breasts are exposed or in underwear, lavatory use, or a sexual act not ordinarily public. Expl. 2: consent to capture ≠ consent to dissemination — disseminating anyway is still voyeurism. Sentencing is progressive: first conviction 1–3 years; repeat 3–7 years. The only offence of the quartet where observation without contact is enough — the hidden camera, the peephole, the leaked intimate image.

(iii) Disrobing — § 76 BNS / s. 354B IPC

Assault or use of criminal force to any woman — or abetment of such act — with the intention of disrobing or compelling her to be naked: imprisonment not less than 3 years, up to 7, + fine. Essentials: criminal force (or abetment); the specific disrobing intent (mere stripping intent "to outrage modesty" falls to § 74 — the graver section demands the stripping purpose); BNS adds "compelling her to be naked". Exam link: the mob-stripping cases (and the Nirbhaya chronology) explain the 2013 separation of this conduct from the mother-section 354.

Q5
Six persons enter a house at night to commit theft. While the others are busy looking for valuables on the ground floor, one of them climbs up to the first floor and, finding the maid-servant sleeping alone there, rapes her and threatens to kill her if she raises an alarm. Then he comes down and joins his associates in collecting valuables after which they all leave the house. Discuss the liability of all of them for the offences of theft and rape.
LL.B. I Term Examination, December 2015 — Law of Crimes–I, Q.5 · REAL DU PAPER QUESTION (group liability × sexual offence)
The rapist's own liability

The man who climbed up: rape (s. 376/§ 64(1)) — intercourse against her will, plus the threat ("threatens to kill her") satisfies circumstance Thirdly/63(iii) independently. He is also guilty of criminal intimidation (s. 506/§ 351(2)) and, with the others, of theft r/w common intention and house-trespass. In BNS terms: § 63 r/w § 64(1); § 303(2) (theft); house-trespass by night — §§ 329–331 (IPC 448/456).

The other five — theft: YES

All six entered with the common intention to commit theft: each is liable for the theft (s. 379 r/w s. 34 / § 303(2) r/w § 3(5)); if five-plus acted with force in prosecution of the common object, the assembly route adds ss. 147–148/§ 191 (rioting; armed if weapons carried) and s. 149/§ 190 for the offences in prosecution of the object. The house-entry at night aggravates (lurking house-trespass by night — s. 456/§ 331(2)).

The other five — rape: the 149/190 two-limb audit
  • Limb 1 — "in prosecution of the common object"? No. The common object was theft; the spontaneous rape of the maid was independent of it — not "immediately connected" (Mizaji's test, your Unit-4 material). The rapist exceeded the object for his private lust.
  • Limb 2 — "knew to be likely"? The test: did all members know rape was likely in the prosecution of a theft-object? On these facts — no prior plan touching the occupants, no sharing of intent, the others not even present on the floor — a bare possibility is not knowledge (Mizaji). The five escape rape.
  • s. 34 / § 3(5)? No common intention to rape — the fifth man's design was formed alone, on the spot, and never shared. (Contrast Gurmit Singh, where all three took turns while the others guarded — shared design + participation by guarding = all guilty r/w s. 34, and gang rape on the BNS facts.)
  • Gang rape § 70/376D? No — "constituting a group or acting in furtherance of a common intention" to rape is absent; one rapist + five thieves is not a gang rape.
Refinements the examiner rewards

(1) The maid's sleep = "unable to communicate consent" now § 63(vii). (2) If evidence showed the plan included "using" any woman found (pre-arranged), the analysis flips — all six for rape r/w 149/34 and gang rape (§ 70). (3) Dec 2014's mirror problem (Q7(b), your Unit-4 PYQ) uses the same limb-2 logic in reverse — study them as a pair.

Conclusion

The rapist: rape (376/§ 64) + criminal intimidation + theft r/w 34 + house-trespass. The other five: theft r/w 34 (379/§ 303(2)), house-trespass/rioting as proved — but not rape, the act being neither in prosecution of the common object nor within their knowledge as likely.

Q6
Critically examine the marital rape exception (Exception 2 to s. 375 IPC / BNS § 63) in the light of Independent Thought v. Union of India. Should the exemption for wives above 18 survive?
DU pattern (the unit's set debate — bridges Independent Thought and the BNS)
State the exception and its history framework

Exception 2: intercourse by a man with his own wife (not under 18 — post-Independent Thought/BNS; 15 in the original IPC) is not rape. Its intellectual pedigree is Hale's seventeenth-century proposition that marriage carries the wife's irrevocable consent — a doctrine the common law has since abandoned (R v. R, 1991, England).

Independent Thought's achievement
  • The anomaly exposed: the married girl child of 15–18 had less protection than an unmarried girl (clause Sixthly at 16/18) and none under POCSO's logic — "an apparent conflict or incongruity between the provisions of IPC and the POCSO Act".
  • The grounds: arbitrary, capricious, whimsical — Articles 14, 15, 21; dignity and bodily integrity of the girl child.
  • The method: reading down, not striking down — "The Court is not creating any new offence but only removing what was unconstitutional and offensive"; prospective operation; s. 198(6) CrPC procedure preserved.
  • The codification: BNS Exception 2 now says 18 — the judgment became the statute.
The surviving exemption — the debate

For retaining (the Union's old stance): marriage presumes consent; protecting the marital relationship from criminalisation; misuse risk; the civil remedies and §§ 85–86 cruelty + § 74 assault + § 67 (separated wife) already cover the violent husband. For abolishing: autonomy and dignity under Article 21 cannot stop at the wedding (the Lokur J. reasoning extended); consent is iterative, not a one-time grant; § 67 already concedes the principle — the separated wife IS protected, so the exemption is only about the cohabiting wife's presumed availability; the violence-is-punished argument misses that rape's wrong is the non-consent, not the injury; international law (CEDAW committee criticisms). Examiner's line: the Supreme Court in Independent Thought expressly left the adult question open; subsequent proceedings continue; the BNS (2023) retained the exemption — a legislative choice now carrying the constitutional burden the Court identified.

Conclusion

Independent Thought demolished the exception's foundation (marriage as licence) for child wives; the logic presses on the adult exemption, but as the law stands: child wife — rape; separated wife — § 67; cohabiting adult wife — exempt (with §§ 85–86, 74 as the parallel remedies). State both the law and the critique.

Q7
"The essence of a woman's modesty is her sex." Discuss the offence of assault or criminal force to a woman with intent to outrage her modesty (s. 354 IPC / BNS § 74), with reference to Kanwar Pal Singh Gill v. State.
DU pattern (the § 74 long answer anchored on the DU case material)
Ingredients statute

(1) Assault or criminal force (force used with intention/knowledge — ss. 349–351/§§ 128–130); (2) to a woman; (3) intent to outrage her modesty or knowledge of likelihood of so outraging it. Punishment: 1–5 years + fine (minimum introduced 2013). "Modesty" is undefined — deliberately: Major Singh (the candle-infant case): "the essence of a woman's modesty is her sex"; she possesses it "from her very birth"; the accused's knowledge that he was violating it is the culpable element. Modesty is thus contextual — attachable to age, place, manner, audience.

KPS Gill applied

The dinner-party slap: (1) criminal force — the slap on the posterior, preceded by chair-pulling and way-blocking; (2) to a woman — a senior IAS officer, which cuts any suggestion that "modesty" belongs only to the sheltered; (3) knowledge — "he knew fully well that gently slapping on the posterior… in the presence of other guests would embarrass her… touching the body… at that place and time would amount to outraging her modesty"; the public setting is part of the outrage; and the negative test: "Had it been without any culpable intention… nobody would have taken notice of the incident." The immediate hue and cry, the complaint up the administrative ladder (host → IB → Chief Secretary → Governor) cured the later complaint's "delay"; rank ("a police officer of the highest rank should have been exceedingly careful") aggravated rather than excused. Conviction under 354 AND 509 (the insult limb) affirmed.

Grading and neighbours

§ 74 sits on the ladder: § 79 (no contact) below; §§ 75–78 beside it (the 2013 quartet); § 76 above (disrobing intent); § 63 above all (penetration). KPS Gill's era sentence (3 months → probation → fine) explains the 2013 minimum-1-year: the case is its own reform argument.

Conclusion

Model answer shape: definition → Major Singh's modesty thesis → KPS Gill's four application steps (force, woman, knowledge, context) → the evidence rules (immediate complaint; reaction of the woman relevant) → the punishment evolution.

Q8
"Corroboration of the prosecutrix's statement is not a requirement of law but a guidance of prudence." Explain with reference to State of Punjab v. Gurmit Singh. How should courts treat delay in FIR, absence of injuries, and alleged sexual habituation?
DU pattern (the Gurmit Singh evidentiary charter)
The rule lead quote

Gurmit Singh (adopting Chandraprakash Kewalchand Jain and Bharwada Bhoginbhai): the prosecutrix is not an accomplice but "a victim of another person's lust"; her evidence "stands almost on a par with the evidence of an injured witness and to an extent is even more reliable"; "Seeking corroboration… as a rule in such cases amounts to adding insult to injury"; corroboration is "not a requirement of law but a guidance of prudence under given circumstances"; "Courts cannot cling to a fossil formula." If her testimony inspires confidence, conviction follows on it alone; a hesitant court may look for assurance "short of corroboration required in the case of an accomplice".

The three classic attacks, answered
  • Delay in FIR: "in sexual offences delay… can be due to a variety of reasons particularly the reluctance of the prosecutrix or her family members to go to the police… It is only after giving it a cool thought that a complaint… is generally lodged"; the panchayat-consultation route (Gurmit Singh) and family consultation about "reputation and future prospects of the marriage" are natural conduct, not doubt-makers.
  • Absence of injuries / no alarm: the girl was threatened with death — "to discredit the prosecutrix for not raising an alarm… is a travesty of justice"; see also Chhoteylal: "Any alarm at unknown place might have endangered her life… The circumstances made her submissive victim." Expl. 2's proviso (non-resistance ≠ consent) is the statutory form.
  • Habituation / character: the trial court's "girl of loose character" finding was "wholly irresponsible"; "Even if the prosecutrix… has been promiscuous… earlier, she has a right to refuse to submit herself… because she is not a vulnerable object or prey… it is the accused and not the victim of sex crime who is on trial." (IEA 53A/BSA 48 makes prior history irrelevant.)
The institutional directions

In-camera trial as the rule (s. 327(2)–(3) CrPC / BNSS § 356); women judges "wherever possible"; victim anonymity in orders; controlled cross-examination — the court "should not sit as a silent spectator"; broader probabilities over "minor contradictions or insignificant discrepancies… not of a fatal nature".

Q9
Problem: A girl of 15 is brought to a police station at midnight when her brother lodges a kidnapping report against her lover. After the others leave, the head constable and a constable ask her to wait; the constable takes her to the rear of the station and forcibly has intercourse with her despite her protests; the head constable, too drunk to perform, gropes her. She raises no alarm at the time. Medical examination next day shows old hymenal ruptures and no injuries. Discuss the liability, and the law's modern answers.
DU pattern (the Mathura problem — IPC-era outcome vs BNS-era outcome)
The 1979 answer (Tukaram) — state it to expose it

The Sessions court: intercourse probable but "a world of difference between sexual intercourse and rape"; habituation told against her. The Supreme Court: no injuries = "a peaceful affair"; clause Thirdly needs fear of death or hurt specifically; she was leaving with her brother and lover when caught — "her natural impulse would be to shake off the hand that caught her"; onus always on the prosecution. Acquittals.

What the law has since done to each plank
  • Custody is itself the aggravator: § 64(2)(a)/(b)/376(2)(a),(b) — rape by a police officer in the police station/on a woman in custody: 10 years to life (natural life). The constable: aggravated rape.
  • The presumption: BSA § 120/IEA 114A — intercourse by the accused proved + she says she did not consent → the court shall presume absence of consent in these aggravated prosecutions. The 1979 "prove her fear" burden is inverted.
  • Non-resistance: Expl. 2 proviso to § 63 — non-resistance is not consent; Chhoteylal: captivity makes "a submissive victim", not a willing one; the 15-year-old at midnight in a police station is the paradigm.
  • Habituation/old ruptures: irrelevant — Gurmit Singh's stigma ban + IEA 53A/BSA 48.
  • Age 15: § 63(vi) — under 18, "with or without her consent": statutory rape regardless, plus § 65(1) (under 16: minimum 20 years). The entire consent inquiry vanishes.
  • The drunk officer: the 1979 court's 354 conviction was overturned for her credibility shifts — today the groping is § 63(b)–(c) (penetration by body part/manipulation) if proved, else § 74; and being "too drunk to perform" is no defence to attempt/assault.
Conclusion

Constable: aggravated rape (custodial + under-16) — § 64(2)(a) r/w 65(1), with the BSA 120 presumption. Head constable: § 63(b)/(c) or § 74 for the groping; his rank and presence complete the custodial colour. The Open Letter's question — "consent presumed because she was habituated?" — is now answered by statute.

Q10
Problem: Three men abduct a schoolgirl of 15 in a car, keep her overnight at a tube-well room where each has forcible intercourse with her while the others guard the room; they repeat the acts during the night and drop her near the school in the morning. The girl names all three; medical examination confirms intercourse and semen; the trial court acquits, doubting her because she could not name the car's make and did not cry out. Decide.
DU pattern (the Gurmit Singh problem restated as a decision question)
Classify the offences the charge sheet

Each man: rape — s. 376 IPC (aggravated by her being under 16 under the pre-2013 clause Sixthly / now § 65(1): minimum 20 years); abduction to compel illicit intercourse — s. 366/§ 87; kidnapping from lawful guardianship — s. 363/§ 137; wrongful confinement — ss. 342–344 / § 127(2)–(4) BNS (the locked kotha); and because they acted in furtherance of a common intention with the guarding constituting participation, each is liable for all the acts r/w s. 34/§ 3(5) — Gurmit Singh's own holding. Post-2013/2018 and under BNS: this is also gang rape — § 70(1)/376D ("a group… acting in furtherance of a common intention" — each "deemed to have committed the offence"), minimum 20 years; victim under 18 → § 70(2) — life or death.

Answer the acquittal's reasons (the Gurmit Singh audit)
  • "Could not name the make of the car" — "wholly irrelevant and immaterial" for a village schoolgirl; her captivity explains everything else.
  • "Did not cry out" — she was threatened with death on abduction ("she would be done to death" if she raised alarm) — "to discredit the prosecutrix for not raising an alarm… is a travesty of justice."
  • Guarding the kotha = participation — the concert of design is proved by the shared watch-keeping, the turns, the night repetition, the common drop-off.
  • Corroboration existed anyway (medical + chemical + father/mother) — but conviction could rest on her testimony alone, it being "consistent and reliable".
Result and sentence

Acquittal set aside; convictions under 363/366/368/376 (or §§ 63, 65(1), 70(1), 87, 137 r/w 127(2)–(3) as the case may be); sentence tempered (as Gurmit Singh itself: 5 years RI + fine under the IPC; the modern court would start at 20 years under § 65(1)/70(1)); in-camera trial, anonymity, and sensitivity directions apply.

Q11
Problem: Within four years of her marriage, a woman dies of burn injuries in her matrimonial home. Neighbours depose that her husband and mother-in-law had repeatedly taunted her family for bringing "an insufficient wedding" and, a week before the death, had locked her in a room after a quarrel over a car for her brother-in-law. The post-mortem shows 70% burns "caused otherwise than under normal circumstances". The accused plead that the stove burst accidentally. Advise the prosecution on the charge.
DU pattern (the dowry death problem)
The charge: dowry death — s. 304B/§ 80 build the ingredients

(1) Death by burns — the statutory head itself; (2) "otherwise than under normal circumstances" — the post-mortem's own words; (3) within seven years of marriage — four years, squarely inside; (4) cruelty/harassment soon before death — the locking-in a week before, the running taunts over the "insufficient wedding" and the car demand; (5) by the husband or his relative — both; (6) for or in connection with dowry demand — the car demand is "in connection with" (dowry = property/valuable security demanded "in connection with marriage" per Dowry Prohibition Act s. 2 — demands can be pre-, at-, or post-marriage). All six made out → the section deems them to have caused her death; punishment 7 years to life.

The presumption and the stove defence

BSA § 118/IEA 113B: the ingredients shown → "the court shall presume" the accused caused the dowry death — the burden shifts to them. The accidental-stove plea must now be affirmatively established against the presumption; the locked room a week before, the demand history and the 70% burns make it judicially unbelievable. If the presumption were unavailable (no dowry link), the alternative charge would be murder (302/§ 103) or 304A/§ 106 (negligence) — with the ordinary burden on the prosecution.

Riding charges

Dowry Prohibition Act ss. 3–4 (demand/taking); cruelty § 85 (merged in outcome); if she had survived — §§ 85–86 + hurt offences (mirror PYQ q2). Procedure: SAFMA/Arnesh Kumar discipline governs arrest of relatives; the conviction itself must rest on the demand-cruelty-death nexus, not on the mere fact of death in the matrimonial home (the safeguard against over-reach).

Conclusion

Charge both under § 80/304B with the § 118 BSA presumption riding; in the alternative, § 103/302. The week-before cruelty is the case's spine; the stove story faces the presumption it must dislodge.

Enhanced
  • Simple rape floor: 7 → 10 years (§ 64(1)) — Gurmit Singh's tempered 5-year sentence impossible today.
  • Exception 2: 15 → 18 — Independent Thought codified; the judgment literally became the statutory text.
  • Armed-riot-style severity throughout: 65(2) (under-12), 66, 70(2), 71 — the death penalty stations.
  • Fine-to-victim provisos (65, 70) — Delhi Domestic Working Women's Forum/Gurmit Singh compensation thinking statutoryised.
Newly created

§ 69 — deceitful means/false promise to marry (up to 10 years): the Deepak Gulati gap filled — the seducer who is not a rapist (no s. 90 vitiation) but is no innocent; "deceitful means" includes false employment/promotion promises and marriage by suppressed identity. Plus: "compelling her to be naked" in § 76; "fiduciary relationship" in § 68; consent's positive definition (Expl. 2) and labia majora (Expl. 1) in § 63.

Deleted / restructured

s. 377 omitted — Navtej Singh Johar's decriminalisation made structural; non-consensual acts are reached by § 63 (all persons' conduct now within the general chapters) and special laws. The 376(1)–(2) sub-section sprawl consolidated into 64(1)–(2) with the 13 aggravators lettered; 376A–E redistributed to §§ 66–71; 228A split to §§ 72–73.

Critique the "critically" marks

Praises: doctrinal continuity (Suresh→Mizaji-style case law survives untouched), the § 69 innovation, the child-wife correction. Critiques: the adult marital-rape exemption survives untouched (Independent Thought's logic left mid-way); Chapter V's title says "Woman and Child" but the child's protection still lives mainly in POCSO — the BNS adds little for boy victims; gendered offender ("a man") retains the female-abuser gap (reached only via abetment); § 69's "not amounting to rape" proviso may re-open litigation over which side of the line a seduction falls.

Q13
Answer with reasons: (a) A landlord, refused by his tenant's daughter, repeatedly presses against her in the stairwell and sends lewd messages for months; she has told him to stop each time. (b) A software engineer secretly records his flatmate bathing through a phone camera; he later shows the clip to a friend. (c) A jilted man begins tracking his ex-fiancée's location and reads her private chats by installing spyware on her phone.
DU pattern (the § 75–78 application set)
(a) § 75/354A + § 74 + § 79 physical + digital

The stairwell pressing = physical contact with unwelcome and explicit sexual overtures — § 75(1)(i)/354A(i) (up to 3 years RI); each contact is also criminal force outraging modesty — § 74 (1–5 years) if the sexual purpose is proved; the lewd messages = sexually coloured remarks — § 75(1)(iv)/354A(iv) (up to 1 year) and § 79 if intended to insult modesty; the months-long persistence after clear disinterest adds stalking § 78 (contact "repeatedly despite a clear indication of disinterest" — the messages count as contacting "to foster personal interaction"). Continued below-rape liability adds up to several counts; the 2013 quartet was designed for exactly this harassment continuum.

(b) § 77/354C voyeurism — both capture and dissemination

Recording her in a private act (bathing — a place where she expects not to be observed) = capturing the image of a woman engaging in a private act — § 77 first limb; showing the clip to a friend is dissemination — the second limb; Expl. 2 would cover even her consent to recording without consent to dissemination. First conviction: 1–3 years + fine. Note also IT Act s. 66E/67 if transmitted electronically — the greater/special provisions run alongside.

(c) § 78/354D stalking — the electronic limb

Monitoring "the use by a woman of the internet, e-mail or any other form of electronic communication" — spyware reading her chats is the provision's second limb in its plainest form; location-tracking plus unwanted contact after the engagement's end = the first limb. The proviso defences fail: he is not State-entrusted, not under any law, and jealousy is neither "reasonable" nor "justified". First conviction: up to 3 years + fine. (If he publishes intimate material to coerce, the IT Act and § 75/77 layers stack.)

Method note

In each, run: the actus → the section's mental element → the statutory exceptions → sentencing grade (first/subsequent). The 2013 quartet's shared architecture (unwelcome-ness, repetition, privacy, pursuit) is the connective tissue.

10.4  The trap list

  • Exception 2 ages: IPC as enacted said 15; Independent Thought read it to 18; the BNS says 18. Never write "15" as current law; never write "the marital rape exemption is abolished" — only the child-wife part fell.
  • 376 minimums: IPC 376(1) minimum was 7; BNS § 64(1) is 10. Gurmit Singh's 5-year sentence was a 1996 exercise in temperance, not the statutory floor.
  • § 69 is not rape: it punishes intercourse "not amounting to… rape" by deceitful means — do not merge it into § 63; the exam answer must classify first.
  • 354 is not the home of everything: disrobing → § 76; penetration by object/finger → § 63(b); forced oral → § 63(d); words alone → § 79. Post-2013, 354/§ 74 is the residue, not the umbrella.
  • "Habituated to sex" proves nothing — not consent, not credibility (Gurmit Singh; IEA 53A/BSA 48).
  • No injuries ≠ no rape — and the reverse is also true: injuries are not an ingredient of rape at all.
  • Delay: explained delay (panchayat, shame, family consultation — Gurmit Singh; 10 days of captivity — Chhoteylal) is no ground for acquittal; only unexplained falsity-oriented delay matters.
  • Corroboration: "guidance of prudence", never "requirement of law" — get the phrase exactly right.
  • POCSO s. 42: greater punishment prevails — do not say POCSO "overrides" simpliciter; 42A is subject to 42.
  • 498A procedure: Family Welfare Committees are gone (SAFMA struck them down); the Arnesh Kumar checklist survives — do not cite Rajesh Sharma's FWCs as living law.
  • s. 377: omitted in the BNS after Navtej Singh Johar — consensual homosexual acts are not an offence; do not answer "unnatural offences" questions with IPC text alone.
