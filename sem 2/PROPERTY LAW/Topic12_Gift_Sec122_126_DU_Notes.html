1. Concept of gift and the s.122 definition

s.122. "Gift" defined. — "Gift" is the transfer of certain existing moveable or immoveable property made voluntarily and without consideration, by one person, called the donor, to another, called the donee, and accepted by or on behalf of the donee.
Acceptance when to be made. — Such acceptance must be made during the lifetime of the donor and while he is still capable of giving.
If the donee dies before acceptance, the gift is void.

A gift is the only gratuitous mode of transfer recognised in the Act (sale, exchange, lease and mortgage are all for value). It is nevertheless a bilateral transaction — an offer by the donor and an acceptance by the donee — and in that sense partakes of the character of a contract (hence s.126 borrows the contractual grounds of rescission, and the Contract Act's rules on free consent apply — Kartari). It differs from a will in that it operates inter vivos and takes effect immediately, whereas a will is ambulatory and speaks from death. Chapter VII (ss.122–129) governs gifts of both movable and immovable property (the Act's other chapters mostly confine themselves to immovables), but s.129 saves Muslim law of hiba and donatio mortis causa of movables.

Element (s.122)Analysis
1. "Transfer"A transfer of ownership — the donor must divest himself of the whole of his interest in the thing given and vest it in the donee (Exam Capsule: "the transferor must divest himself of the absolute interest"). A gift of a limited interest (life interest) is possible as a transfer of that interest, but a gift in which the donor retains dominion and power to recall is no gift (s.126 — revocable at will = void). Transfer here is a "conveyance"; s.5 TPA (living persons) applies.
2. "Certain existing … property"(a) Certain — ascertained or ascertainable subject-matter; (b) existing at the date of the gift — a gift of future property is void (s.124; contrast s.6 which allows future property to be dealt with by contract); an expectancy (spes successionis, chance of an heir apparent) cannot be gifted (s.6(a)); (c) movable or immovable, corporeal or incorporeal — land, house, shares, jewellery, money, actionable claims (s.130 — gift of an actionable claim by writing); (d) must be transferable under s.6.
3. "Made voluntarily"Free will of the donor — the donor's consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake (ss.14–22 Contract Act imported). Voluntariness is the very soul of a gift; a gift extracted from an aged, ailing, illiterate woman by relatives who take a leading part in its preparation is set aside (Kartari v. Kewal Krishan). For pardanashin/illiterate donors the burden is on the donee to prove the deed was explained to and understood by the donor.
4. "Without consideration"Gratuitousness — no consideration in the sense of s.2(d) Contract Act. Love and affection, spiritual or moral benefit, past services, gratitude, expectation of care are motives, not consideration (Exam Capsule). Even a nominal or small price turns the transaction into a sale or exchange, not a gift. A gift "in lieu of services" (as recited in the Kartari deed) remains a gift as regards form, but the recital may be examined for genuineness. Where the deed imposes an obligation on the donee (maintain the donor), the transaction is still a gift — an onerous gift or gift with a direction — and default in that obligation is treated as "want or failure of consideration", which s.126 expressly excludes as a ground of revocation (Tila Bewa).
5. "By one person, called the donor"Competence: the donor must be competent to contract (s.7 TPA read with s.11 Contract Act) — major, of sound mind, not disqualified — and must have title/authority. A minor or lunatic cannot make a gift; a guardian cannot gift the ward's property (except for pious/religious purposes within very narrow limits under Hindu law); a trustee cannot gift trust property unless authorised; a karta may make gifts of small portions of joint family property for pious purposes/affection within reasonable limits. Company: gifts need authority under its constitution.
6. "To another, called the donee"Any person capable of holding property — need not be competent to contract: a minor, a lunatic, an unborn person (through s.13 machinery), a juristic person (idol, deity, company, registered society) may be donees. The donee must be an ascertainable person or persons; a gift to "the public" or to an unregistered society or to an indefinite class is void for uncertainty (though it may take effect as a charitable trust/dedication).
7. "Accepted by or on behalf of the donee"Acceptance is essential — a gift cannot be thrust upon a person. It may be express or implied: taking possession, collecting rents, mutation, retaining the deed, paying tax, exercising acts of ownership — or acceptance through a guardian/natural guardian for a minor (Tila Bewa: accepted by the plaintiff "through her husband"), through a manager/shebait for an idol. Mere joint residence of donor and donee on the property is not by itself acceptance (Exam Capsule). Acceptance may even be inferred from acts prior to execution. A presumption of acceptance arises where the gift is beneficial and the donee is a minor.
8. Timing of acceptanceMust be made during the donor's lifetime and while he is still capable of giving (i.e., before he loses competence, e.g., by insanity, or transfers to someone else). If the donee dies before acceptance, the gift is void — the donee's heirs cannot accept for him. If acceptance has been made but the donor dies before registration, the gift is valid: registration may be completed after death (s.123 read with s.47 Registration Act — a registered document operates from its execution; Exam Capsule; Kalyanasundaram Pillai v. Karuppa Mooppanar AIR 1927 PC 42).
Examples. (i) A executes a registered gift deed of his Rohini flat to his daughter D, who is abroad; A dies the next week before D returns; D had emailed her thanks and asked her brother to collect the deed — accepted during A's lifetime; valid. (ii) Same, but D dies in an accident before hearing of the deed — void; her children take nothing. (iii) A gifts "all the wheat my field will yield next season" — void (s.124, future property). (iv) A "gifts" his car to B for Rs 1 — a sale, not a gift. (v) A grandmother gifts a house to her 10-year-old grandson, the deed being accepted and kept by his father — valid, acceptance on behalf of the donee.

3. Free consent — undue influence, fraud and the protection of weak donors

Indian Contract Act, 1872 (imported by "voluntarily" in s.122 and by the second paragraph of s.126).
s.14 — Consent is free when not caused by coercion (s.15), undue influence (s.16), fraud (s.17), misrepresentation (s.18) or mistake (ss.20–22).
s.16(1) — A contract is induced by undue influence where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage. s.16(2) — position to dominate presumed where a person (a) holds real or apparent authority or stands in a fiduciary relation, or (b) contracts with a person whose mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected by reason of age, illness, or mental or bodily distress. s.16(3) — where a person in a position to dominate enters a transaction which, on its face or on the evidence, appears unconscionable, the burden of proving that it was not induced by undue influence lies on the person in the position to dominate.
s.19, 19A — agreement voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused; court may set aside on such terms as it thinks just.
s.111 Evidence Act — where there is a question as to the good faith of a transaction between parties one of whom stands to the other in a position of active confidence, the burden of proving good faith is on the party in active confidence.

3.1 The three-stage test — Subhas Chandra Das Mushib v. Ganga Prosad Das Mushib AIR 1967 SC 878 (applied in Kartari)

Stage 1 — Are the relations between donor and donee such that the donee is in a position to dominate the will of the donor? (authority, fiduciary relation, donor's age/illness/distress, dependence, isolation from natural advisers)
Stage 2 — Has the donee used that position to obtain an unfair advantage? (beneficiary took a leading part in preparing/registering the deed; natural heir excluded; donor left with nothing; secrecy; haste)
Stage 3 — Onus probandi: if the transaction appears unconscionable, the burden shifts to the donee to prove that it was not induced by undue influence (s.16(3)); otherwise the burden remains on the party alleging it.
If the donee fails to discharge that burden → gift set aside as voidable for undue influence (Kartari); relief may be moulded (s.19A).

3.2 Pardanashin, illiterate and ignorant donors

  • Rule. Where the donor is a pardanashin woman, the person relying on the deed must prove that it was explained to and substantially understood by her and that it was her "mental act" as its execution was her "physical act" (Farid-un-nisa v. Mukhtar Ahmad AIR 1925 PC 204, quoted in Kartari). Mere execution, even without protest, is no proof of understanding; evidence that the deed was read over and, if necessary, translated and explained is the natural proof; independent advice is strong but not indispensable.
  • Extension. The same protection extends to illiterate and ignorant women who are not pardanashin (Chinta Dasya v. Bhalku Das AIR 1930 Cal 591, adopted in Kartari: the object is "to protect the weak and helpless" and cannot be confined to a class; otherwise rich women would be favoured over poor). Modern courts extend it to illiterate/aged persons generally.
  • Suspicious circumstances doctrine borrowed from probate. Vellasawmy Servai v. L. Sivaraman Servai AIR 1930 PC 24 — where the propounder/beneficiary takes a leading part in giving instructions, procuring execution and registration, suspicion is excited and the court must scrutinise "with great vigilance"; the beneficiary must remove the suspicion. Kartari applies this to gifts.
  • Sub-Registrar's endorsement (s.58–60 Registration Act) that the executant admitted execution raises only a rebuttable presumption made "in a routine fashion" (Kartari).

3.3 Fraud v. undue influence — pleading and proof

Fraud (s.17) must be specifically pleaded with particulars (O.VI r.4 CPC) and strictly proved; undue influence (s.16) is likewise to be pleaded (O.VI r.4) but once the relationship and unconscionability are shown the burden shifts. In Kartari, particulars of fraud were not given and fraud was held not established, "but nonetheless the case of undue influence was proved and that is sufficient to set aside the document". Where the donor did not know the nature of the document at all (thought she was signing a treatment paper or a power of attorney), the plea is non est factum and the deed is void, not merely voidable.

Real-world pattern. Elderly parents in Delhi transfer houses to a son by "gift deed" on the promise of care; after the transfer they are neglected or evicted. Remedies: (i) civil suit to set aside for undue influence/fraud (Kartari), (ii) if a revocation clause exists, revocation under s.126 (Tila Bewa shows a bare maintenance direction is not enough), and (iii) — the modern statutory answer — s.23 of the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007: where a senior citizen has transferred property by gift or otherwise subject to the condition that the transferee shall provide basic amenities and physical needs and the transferee refuses/fails, the transfer is deemed to have been made by fraud, coercion or undue influence and may be declared void by the Maintenance Tribunal. The Supreme Court in Sudesh Chhikara v. Ramti Devi (2022) held that the condition of maintenance must actually be part of the transfer (express or clearly implied) — in effect, the Tila Bewa requirement of a real condition survives even under s.23; Urmila Dixit v. Sunil Sharan Dixit (2025) 1 SCC read s.23 beneficially and restored possession to the mother.

4. Mode of execution — s.123

s.123. Transfer how effected. — For the purpose of making a gift of immoveable property, the transfer must be effected by a registered instrument signed by or on behalf of the donor, and attested by at least two witnesses.
For the purpose of making a gift of moveable property, the transfer may be effected either by a registered instrument signed as aforesaid or by delivery.
Such delivery may be made in the same way as goods sold may be delivered.
RequirementDetail
Immovable property — writing + registration (mandatory)Irrespective of value (contrast sale — s.54 permits delivery for tangible immovables under Rs 100). s.17(1)(a) Registration Act, 1908 makes "instruments of gift of immovable property" compulsorily registrable; s.49 — an unregistered gift deed does not affect the property and is inadmissible to prove the gift (admissible only for collateral purposes). Registration must be within four months of execution (s.23), by presentation by the donor or his agent (s.32); the Sub-Registrar records admission of execution (ss.58–60). Once registered the deed operates from the date of execution (s.47) — so registration after the donor's death is valid if he executed it and the donee accepted in his lifetime. Registration is not a substitute for acceptance: a registered deed the donee never accepted is no gift; equally, acceptance and possession without registration confer no title (Exam Capsule: "mere delivery of possession without registered instrument cannot confer any title").
Signature "by or on behalf of the donor"Donor's signature or thumb-impression, or that of an agent under a power of attorney (which, if it authorises gift of immovable property, should itself be registered/authenticated — s.33 Registration Act). Signature by a person who does not understand the document is not a "signature" of a consenting donor (Kartari).
Attestation by at least two witnesses"Attested" — s.3 TPA: each witness must have seen the executant sign or affix his mark, or seen another person sign in the executant's presence and by his direction, or received from the executant a personal acknowledgment of his signature; and each must sign in the presence of the executant; the witnesses need not be present together; no particular form. The witness must sign animo attestandi — a scribe or registering officer who signs only in that capacity is not an attesting witness unless he also intended to attest (Exam Capsule: "a scribe is a competent witness if he has animus to attest"). Proof: s.68 Evidence Act — a document required by law to be attested shall not be used as evidence until at least one attesting witness has been called to prove its execution, if alive and available (the proviso exempting registered documents applies only where execution is not specifically denied — and it does not apply to wills; for gift deeds, if execution is specifically denied, an attesting witness must be called). In Kartari, the attesting witness Jakha Ram (D.W.2) was the donee's own man and Shero, though brought as a witness, was "given up".
Movable property — registered instrument OR deliveryDelivery "in the same way as goods sold may be delivered" — s.33 Sale of Goods Act, 1930: actual delivery, symbolic delivery (keys of a godown), constructive delivery (attornment by a bailee), or, where the donee is already in possession, a change in the character of possession. Shares — transfer deed and registration in the company's register; money — handing over, cheque encashed, bank transfer; a promise to give money in future is not a gift (nor an enforceable contract, lacking consideration — s.25 Contract Act, unless registered and made out of natural love and affection between near relatives, s.25(1)).
Stamp dutyA gift deed is chargeable as a conveyance (Art. 33, Schedule I, Indian Stamp Act) on the market value; several States concessionally stamp gifts to close relatives (Delhi: 4%/6% conveyance rates apply; Maharashtra: Rs 200 for residential/agricultural gift to specified relatives). Unstamped/under-stamped deeds are inadmissible until impounded (s.35).
Muslim donors (s.129)Chapter VII does not affect Muslim law: a hiba is valid on (i) declaration, (ii) acceptance, (iii) delivery of possession — without writing or registration even for immovable property (Hafeeza Bibi v. Shaikh Farid (2011) 5 SCC 654). If a Muslim chooses to reduce a hiba to writing, the document is not compulsorily registrable under s.17 merely because it records the gift, though a deed that itself creates the gift is treated as an instrument of gift by many High Courts.

4.1 When is a gift "complete"?

Immovable property: (1) deed executed by donor (signed) → (2) attested by two witnesses → (3) accepted by donee during donor's lifetime → (4) registered (may follow donor's death, relates back — s.47 Registration Act).
All four present → title vests in donee from execution; donor cannot unilaterally cancel, even by a registered "deed of cancellation" (Ext. A in Tila Bewa was ineffective). Possession not essential.
Any missing → no title: unregistered (void); unattested (void); not accepted / donee predeceased (void); donor incompetent (void); consent vitiated (voidable — Kartari).
Possession and mutation are not requirements. The donor's continued residence on the property (Tila Bewa: "the fact that the donor, who had no other property, stayed on the property even after the gift, does not show that the gift had not taken effect") or absence of mutation (revenue entries do not confer or deny title) does not defeat a completed gift. Conversely a "cancellation deed" executed by the donor has no legal effect; the donor's only remedy is a suit to set aside on a legal ground (Tila Bewa para 6: "he has got to get it set aside in a court of law").

5. Sections 124, 125, 127, 128, 129

SectionText (substance)Explanation & example
s.124 — Gift of existing and future propertyA gift comprising both existing and future property is void as to the latter.Severable: the gift stands for the existing part. "Future property" = property not in existence or not owned by the donor at the date of gift (next year's crop; property expected on inheritance; a house yet to be built). E.g., A gifts B his present house and "any house I may buy hereafter" — valid for the house, void for the future acquisition. Contrast Muslim law where a hiba of future property is wholly void as to that property likewise.
s.125 — Gift to several, of whom one does not acceptA gift of a thing to two or more donees, of whom one does not accept it, is void as to the interest which he would have taken had he accepted.Acceptance is individual; the non-accepting donee's share does not accrue to the others but remains with the donor. E.g., A gifts a house to B and C equally; C refuses — B takes half, A retains half. (Under Muslim law a hiba to two donees without division — mushaa — has its own rules.)
s.127 — Onerous gifts(1) Where a gift is a single transfer of several things of which one is burdened by an obligation and others are not, the donee takes nothing unless he accepts it fully. (2) Where the gift is in the form of two or more separate and independent transfers, the donee may accept one and refuse the others. (3) Onerous gift to disqualified person — a donee not competent to contract who accepts burdened property is not bound; but if after becoming competent and aware of the obligation he retains the property, he becomes bound.Illustration (a): A gives B all his shares in companies X (prosperous) and Y (heavy calls expected) in one gift; B refuses Y — he cannot take X. Illustration (b): A gives B a lease at a rent higher than the house can fetch, and separately a sum of money; B refuses the lease — he keeps the money. Principle: qui sentit commodum sentire debet et onus — he who takes the benefit must bear the burden. Applied to a minor: minor donee of a house with a mortgage may repudiate on majority, but if he keeps it knowing of the mortgage he is bound. Relevance to Tila Bewa: a gift with a direction to maintain the donor is an onerous gift; acceptance binds the donee to the obligation — but breach gives the donor an action for maintenance, not a right of revocation.
s.128 — Universal doneeSubject to s.127, where a gift consists of the donor's whole property, the donee is personally liable for all the debts due by and liabilities of the donor at the time of the gift, to the extent of the property comprised therein.Protects creditors (complementing s.53 fraudulent transfer). "Whole property" — movable and immovable; if any portion is retained the donee is not a universal donee. Liability is capped at the value of the gifted property and confined to debts existing at the date of gift. The donor in Kartari gave away her entire landed property, keeping nothing for maintenance — the court treated that very fact as evidence of unconscionability. A concept unknown to English law; borrowed from the Roman donatio omnium bonorum/Hindu law.
s.129 — Saving of donations mortis causa and Muhammadan lawNothing in Chapter VII relates to gifts of movable property made in contemplation of death, or affects any rule of Muhammadan law.Donatio mortis causa — governed by s.191 Indian Succession Act, 1925: a person may dispose of movable property by gift made in contemplation of death (illness expected to cause death), with delivery of possession, which takes effect only if the donor dies of that illness, and may be resumed if he recovers; immovable property cannot be so gifted. Muslim law — hiba (declaration, acceptance, delivery; no writing/registration needed), hiba-bil-iwaz (gift for return), hiba-ba-shart-ul-iwaz, sadaqah, marz-ul-maut gifts (limited to one-third without heirs' consent), mushaa doctrine. The saving applies where both donor and donee are Muslims (Exam Capsule) — and, per most High Courts, where the donor is Muslim.

6. Suspension and revocation of gifts — s.126

s.126. When gift may be suspended or revoked. — The donor and donee may agree that on the happening of any specified event which does not depend on the will of the donor a gift shall be suspended or revoked; but a gift which the parties agree shall be revocable wholly or in part, at the mere will of the donor, is void wholly or in part, as the case may be.
A gift may also be revoked in any of the cases (save want or failure of consideration) in which, if it were a contract, it might be rescinded.
Save as aforesaid, a gift cannot be revoked.
Nothing contained in this section shall be deemed to affect the rights of transferees for consideration without notice.
Illustrations. (a) A gives a field to B, reserving to himself, with B's assent, the right to take back the field in case B and his descendants die before A. B dies without descendants in A's lifetime. A may take back the field. (b) A gives a lakh of rupees to B, reserving to himself, with B's assent, the right to take back at pleasure Rs 10,000 out of the lakh. The gift holds good as to Rs 90,000, but is void as to Rs 10,000, which continue to belong to A.

6.1 The scheme — only two doors to revocation

GroundRequirementsAuthority / example
Door 1 — Revocation by agreed condition subsequent (para 1)(i) An agreement between donor and donee (express in the deed, or contemporaneous and proved) — not a unilateral reservation; (ii) that on a specified event the gift shall be suspended or revoked; (iii) the event must not depend on the will of the donor (otherwise void to that extent); (iv) the condition must be valid under ss.10–12, 25, 31–34 TPA (a condition wholly restraining alienation, or an unlawful condition, is void; a condition subsequent must be strictly fulfilled — s.29). On the happening of the event the gift is revoked (property reverts) or suspended; the donor need not sue, but in practice must sue for possession if the donee resists.Illustration (a) — reverter if donee's line fails in donor's lifetime; a gift to a daughter-in-law "which shall stand revoked if she fails to reside with and maintain me" — a genuine defeasance clause (the missing element in Tila Bewa). Illustration (b) — revocable at pleasure as to Rs 10,000 → void as to that part only.
Door 2 — Revocation as a contract might be rescinded (para 2)Grounds on which a contract may be rescinded (Contract Act ss.19, 19A): coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation — gift voidable at the donor's option; must be set aside by suit within limitation (Art. 59 Limitation Act — three years from when the facts entitling cancellation become known); mistake making consent unreal (ss.20–22) renders the gift void. Expressly excluded: "want or failure of consideration" — since a gift has no consideration, the donee's failure to perform a promised return (maintenance, services, funeral rites) is not a ground of revocation.Kartari v. Kewal Krishan — undue influence (and want of comprehension) → gift set aside, natural heir gets possession. Tila Bewa — failure to render seva and maintenance = at most failure of consideration → no revocation; remedy is a suit for maintenance.
Residual rule (para 3)"Save as aforesaid, a gift cannot be revoked." A completed gift is irrevocable: no revocation for ingratitude, changed circumstances, donor's subsequent poverty, donee's remarriage, or donor's "apprehension" that the donee may waste the property (the actual motive of the cancellation deed in Tila Bewa). A unilateral "deed of cancellation" is a nullity.Tila Bewa — deed of cancellation (Ext. A) ineffective. Under Muslim law, by contrast, a hiba is revocable by court decree before delivery and in many cases after, save specified exceptions (spouses, blood relations within prohibited degrees, donee dead, subject-matter destroyed/transferred/increased in value, return received).
Protection of bona fide purchasers (para 4)Revocation does not affect the rights of transferees for consideration without notice from the donee — parallel to s.41 and s.19 proviso Contract Act.If the donee in Illustration (a) had sold the field to X, a purchaser for value without notice of the reverter, before B's death, A cannot recover from X (though A may proceed against B for value).

6.2 Conditional gift v. gift with a "pious wish" — the Tila Bewa construction rules

Gift subject to a condition / liability (enforceable; revocable only if defeasance clause)Absolute gift with pious wish (not revocable)
The deed begins by stating that it is made with the object of providing for the donor's maintenance and this statement is followed by the operative clause — the gift is "subject to the liability to maintain the donor" (an onerous gift; donor may sue to enforce the charge/obligation).The deed makes an out-and-out transfer in the operative portion ("from today you are full owner in possession" — "Aja dina than sampurna malik dakhalke karai"), and afterwards directs the donee to render services (sebadharma), maintain (bharan poshan), perform funeral rites — such directions are only "an expression of pious wish".
Revocation is possible only if the deed further contains a defeasance/default clause: "if the donee fails to maintain, the gift shall be void/revoked" — then the s.126 condition is satisfied (event not dependent on donor's will).Without a defeasance clause, failure to comply gives no right to revoke; the failure is a "want of consideration" excluded by s.126 — the donor's remedy is to recover maintenance (e.g., under s.125 CrPC/BNSS s.144, Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act s.20, the 2007 Senior Citizens Act) or to sue for damages/specific performance of the obligation.
A restriction that the donee shall not sell/mortgage during the donor's lifetime without consent is a partial restraint — valid as a condition (Tila Bewa treated it as "the only condition attached") but its breach is not a ground of revocation unless the deed so provides.Reservation by donor of a right to remain in possession for life, if not in the deed, cannot be implied (the appellate decree in Tila Bewa giving the donor possession for life was set aside).
Common error. Students write that "a gift for maintenance is a conditional gift revocable on failure to maintain". Tila Bewa holds the opposite unless (a) the condition appears from the language of the deed and (b) the deed provides for revocation on its breach. Only the 2007 Senior Citizens Act (s.23) has changed this for senior-citizen donors, and even there the Supreme Court (Sudesh Chhikara, 2022) insists that the condition of maintenance be part of the transfer.

CASE 1 — Tila Bewa v. Mana Bewa, AIR 1962 Ori 130

Bench Orissa High Court, S. Barman J., second appeal by the plaintiff (donee daughter-in-law) and cross-appeal by the defendant (donor mother-in-law). Result: plaintiff's appeal partly allowed — gift held absolute and irrevocable; plaintiff entitled to immediate possession; cross-appeal dismissed.

Facts

  • The parties: plaintiff Tila Bewa is the daughter-in-law; defendant Mana Bewa is the mother-in-law. The defendant's son Natabar had a first wife who never came to live with him; the defendant then arranged his marriage with the plaintiff. During the marriage negotiations, to induce the plaintiff to come and live with her son, the defendant executed a registered deed of gift (Ext. 1) dated 10 May 1951 of the suit lands in the plaintiff's favour.
  • The plaintiff married Natabar, remained in possession of the suit lands and lived with him till his death in 1953; thereafter she lived with the mother-in-law till 1954, when, being neglected, she left for her father's house. She applied for mutation of the suit lands in her name. On 31 May 1954 the defendant executed a deed of cancellation (Ext. A) of the gift. On 19 September 1954 the plaintiff sued for declaration of title and possession. The plaintiff had by then remarried.
  • The deed (Ext. 1), as construed by the court: in its operative portion the donor made the plaintiff "full owner in possession from this day" ("Aja dina than sampurna malik dakhalke karai"); after that it expressed the wish that the plaintiff would render "Sebadharma and Bharan Poshan" — services and maintenance to the donor during her lifetime — and perform her funeral rites after death; it declared that the donor and her heirs would have no right in the lands and any claim by them would be invalid; the only condition was that the plaintiff would not sell or mortgage without her husband's consent / during the donor's lifetime, failing which such alienation would be invalid. There was no clause providing for revocation on any contingency and no reservation of possession to the donor.

Defence

(i) The gift was executed only to induce the marriage and was not acted upon (no mutation); (ii) it was a conditional gift — on condition that the plaintiff would maintain her — and the plaintiff having failed, the donor was entitled to revoke, which she did by Ext. A; (iii) the plaintiff, having remarried, could not claim under the deed; (iv) (in cross-appeal) the deed was vitiated by undue influence, fraud and coercion and was an unconscionable bargain.

Procedural history

CourtFinding
Munsif, Cuttack (trial)Gift genuine and valid; accepted by the plaintiff through her husband (then alive); plaintiff entitled even after remarriage; suit decreed.
Subordinate Judge, Cuttack (first appeal)No clause for revocation under any contingency; plaintiff rendered seva till husband's death; no fraud/coercion/undue influence/mistake/misrepresentation; donor knew the terms; transaction not nominal — acted upon; gift cannot be revoked; but declared title subject to the condition that the donor would remain in possession till her death — plaintiff to get possession only thereafter.
High Court (second appeal + cross-appeal)Gift absolute; possession-for-life condition set aside (not in the deed); plaintiff entitled to immediate possession; cross-appeal dismissed.

Issues

  1. Whether a completed registered gift can be revoked by the donor for the donee's failure to maintain/serve her, in the absence of an express revocation clause — scope of s.126.
  2. Whether the directions for seva and maintenance made the gift conditional, or were merely pious wishes.
  3. Whether the lower appellate court could impose a condition (donor's possession for life) not found in the deed.
  4. Whether the deed was vitiated by coercion, undue influence or fraud; whether want of mutation showed that it was not acted upon; effect of the donee's remarriage.

Arguments

Plaintiff-appellant (donee)
  • The deed was registered and attested as s.123 requires; on acceptance the gift became complete (s.122).
  • Under s.126 a gift can be revoked only if the donor and donee agreed that on a specified event not dependent on the donor's will it would be suspended or revoked; there was no such agreement; the deed of cancellation is a nullity.
  • On a plain reading, the deed nowhere reserves possession to the donor for life; the appellate court rewrote the deed.
Defendant-respondent (donor) — Mr S. Mohanty
  • The gift was an unconscionable bargain obtained by coercion, undue influence and fraud; the donor did not know its implications; it was not acted upon as no mutation was effected.
  • The gift was conditional upon sebadharma; relying on Balbhadar Singh v. Lakshmi Bai AIR 1930 All 669 — under Hindu law, a gift made in expectation that the donee will do something is revocable if the donee fails to do it.
  • Having remarried and left the house, the plaintiff can no longer render seva; the object of the gift has failed; the donor may revoke.

Judgment — Barman J.

  • The settled law on s.126 (para 6). "A gift, subject to the condition that the donee should maintain the donor, cannot be revoked under section 126 … for failure of the donee to maintain the donor, firstly for the reason that there is no agreement between the parties that the gift could be either suspended or revoked; and secondly, this should not depend on the will of the donor; again, the failure of the donee to maintain the donor as undertaken by her in the document is not a contingency which should defeat the gift; all that could be said is that the default of the donee in that behalf amounts to want of consideration; Section 126 thus provides against the revocation of a document of gift for failure of consideration; if the donee does not maintain the donor as agreed to by the donee, the latter (donor) could take proper steps to recover maintenance; it is not open to a settlor to revoke a settlement at his will and pleasure and he has got to get it set aside in a court of law by putting forward such pleas as bear on the invalidity of a deed of gift."
  • Completion and possession. Under s.122 a gift is complete when accepted by or on behalf of the donee; where there is evidence of acceptance, the fact that the donor, who had no other property, stayed on the property after the gift does not show that it had not taken effect; where no right is reserved to the donor, a clause that the donee should maintain the donor does not show that the donor continued to be the beneficial owner; "a direction in a gift deed that the donee should maintain the donor till his death will not make the gift a conditional one; if the terms … were that there had been an absolute transfer … such a direction for maintenance shall be regarded only as an expression of pious wish."
  • The construction rule — pious wish v. condition. "Where a gift deed, after the operative portion, provided that the donee was to render services to the donor and to meet the donor's funeral expenses, such directions are only pious wishes and do not give any right to the donor to revoke the gift if the conditions are not observed; … on the other hand if the gift deed starts with a statement that it is made with the object of providing for maintenance of the donor, and this statement is followed by the operative clause, there can be no doubt that the gift is subject to the liability to maintain the donor."
  • Application (para 7). The deed makes a complete gift in the operative portion ("full owner in possession from this day"); only afterwards does the donor express her wish for sebadharma and bharan poshan and the performance of funeral rites; it then negatives any right of the donor or her heirs; the only "condition" is the restraint on sale/mortgage during the donor's lifetime. "Reading the document as a whole, it is clear that it was an out and out gift, and that the directions as to her maintenance and Sebadharma are only pious wishes."
  • Vitiating factors (para 8). No specific issue had been framed on coercion, undue influence, fraud, mistake or misrepresentation; the cross-appeal "has no substance". Want of mutation does not mean the gift was not acted upon (the plaintiff had in fact possessed the lands till 1954).
  • Balbhadar Singh distinguished (para 9). The Allahabad court itself had observed that "in the absence of an express power of revocation for failure of the condition the gift cannot be impugned or revoked"; it was decided on its own facts. Here, "there is no agreement that on failure on the part of the plaintiff to perform any of the conditions, namely, Sebadharma etc. the gift will be invalid. In other words, there must be a defeasance or default clause in order to make the gift revocable; if there was a condition that on failure to perform any of the conditions the gift will be void, then certainly the gift could have been revoked; the document does not make any provision to that effect." Moreover the deed of cancellation itself showed that the donor revoked "in apprehension that the plaintiff might waste the property by transfer" — not for failure of seva; and remarriage was no ground. The court "expected" the plaintiff nonetheless to respect the pious wishes out of the income of the lands.
  • Relief (para 10). The appellate decree was modified: the clauses postponing possession to the donor's death were set aside; the plaintiff declared entitled to immediate possession; rest confirmed; cross-appeal dismissed; parties to bear own costs.
Principles
  1. A gift once complete under ss.122–123 is irrevocable except under s.126; a unilateral deed of cancellation is void; the donor must sue and prove a legal ground.
  2. Revocation under s.126 para 1 requires (a) an agreement (defeasance clause) that (b) on a specified event (c) not dependent on the donor's will the gift shall be suspended or revoked.
  3. The donee's failure to maintain/serve the donor is "want or failure of consideration" — expressly excluded from the grounds of revocation; the donor's remedy is to recover maintenance.
  4. Construction rule: directions to maintain placed after an absolute operative clause are pious wishes; a recital of the maintenance purpose before the operative clause makes the gift subject to the liability — and even then revocation needs an express default clause.
  5. The donor's continued residence or absence of mutation does not negate completion; possession is not a requisite; a court cannot add to the deed a reservation of possession for life.
  6. Pleas of undue influence/fraud must be raised as issues and proved; a donee's remarriage is irrelevant to her title.

CASE 2 — Kartari v. Kewal Krishan, AIR 1972 HP 117

Bench Himachal Pradesh High Court, D.B. Lal J., second appeal by the plaintiff (daughter of the donor) against the District Judge, Kangra, who had reversed the Sub-Judge, Una. Result: appeal allowed; gift deed held obtained by undue influence; set aside; plaintiff's suit for possession decreed with costs throughout.

Facts

  • Shrimati Basanti, an old, feeble, ailing, illiterate, pardanashin widow, was the exclusive owner of 20 kanals 16 marlas of land in Mauza Badhara, P.S. Una. Her only child was the plaintiff Kartari, herself a widow (widowed within four years of marriage), issueless, without landed property of her own, who usually lived with and looked after her mother and managed her properties.
  • In March–April 1961, while Kartari was away at her husband's house, the defendants Kewal Krishan and Mula Ram — collaterals in the fourth degree of Basanti's late husband — took Basanti to Una "under the pretext of getting her treated by some doctor". There, on 4 April 1961, a gift deed of her entire land in their favour was scribed, executed, attested and registered the same day. The deed recited that the donees had been serving her and that the gift was made "in lieu of that service"; it stated her age as 60. Kewal Krishan engaged the scribe, brought the attesting witnesses from the village (Jakha Ram, D.W.2 — his own man; Shero, D.W.3, was brought but "given up" as a witness), and presented her for registration.
  • On her return Kartari learnt of "some transaction" from villagers; her mother "did not remember anything but simply asserted that she was made to sign some transfer deed". On 24/25 April 1961 mother and daughter went to Una and had a complaint scribed by Sant Ram (P.W.1) to the Superintendent of Police, Hoshiarpur, stating that a transfer deed had been obtained by undue influence and fraud and would not bind them; the complaint (bearing Basanti's thumb impression) was entered in the scribe's register (Ex. P.W.1/1) and — significantly — described the transfer as being in favour of all four brothers, whereas the deed was in favour of only two, showing she did not know the details. Three or four days later Basanti died. The defendants took possession.
  • Kartari sued for cancellation of the gift deed and recovery of possession as the natural heir, pleading undue influence and fraud.

Defence

Basanti was neither old nor feeble nor incapable; she fully understood the deed; she did not want her property to descend to her daughter's heirs but wished it to go to the heirs of her deceased husband — hence the gift to the collaterals; no undue influence or fraud.

Procedural history

CourtDecision
Sub-Judge First Class, UnaFound for the plaintiff; cancelled the gift deed; decreed possession.
District Judge, Kangra (24-6-1968)Reversed and dismissed the suit — plaintiff had not cross-examined the scribe on whether the deed was read over; Jakha Ram said the lady walked to Una so she was "hale and hearty"; the Sub-Registrar's endorsement showed admission of execution; particulars of fraud not pleaded.
High Court (second appeal)Reversed the District Judge; suit decreed.

Issues

  1. Whether the gift deed of 4-4-1961 was procured by undue influence (and/or fraud) so that Basanti's consent was not free.
  2. On whom lay the burden of proof, and whether the District Judge misplaced it.
  3. Whether the protective rules for pardanashin women apply to an illiterate, ignorant woman, and whether the donees proved that the deed was explained to and understood by her.
  4. Admissibility and effect of the scribe's register entry of the police complaint; weight of the Sub-Registrar's endorsement.

Arguments

Plaintiff-appellant (Kartari)
  • The donees were in a position to dominate an aged, ailing, illiterate widow isolated from her only daughter and taken away on a pretext; they took a leading part in scribing, attesting and registering the deed on the same day — classic suspicious circumstances (Vellasawmy Servai).
  • The transaction is unconscionable: the donor gave away everything, kept nothing for her own maintenance, and disinherited her dependent widowed daughter in favour of remote collaterals who had rendered no service; under s.16(3) Contract Act / Subhas Chandra Das Mushib the burden shifted to the donees.
  • Basanti's own contemporaneous complaint to the police (proved by the scribe's register) shows she never consented and did not even know the contents.
  • Being illiterate and ignorant she was entitled to the pardanashin protection (Chinta Dasya; Farid-un-nisa): the donees had to prove the deed was read over, explained and understood — they did not.
Defendants-respondents (Kewal Krishan & Mula Ram)
  • The donor was competent, walked to Una on foot (Jakha Ram), and admitted execution before the Sub-Registrar, whose endorsement carries a presumption of correctness.
  • She had a rational motive — to keep the land in her husband's line rather than let it pass to the daughter's heirs.
  • The plaintiff never cross-examined the scribe or the witnesses on whether the deed was read over, nor suggested what other document was intended; particulars of fraud were not pleaded (O.VI r.4 CPC), so the plea must fail.
  • The register entry is secondary evidence of a complaint whose original was never summoned; inadmissible.

Judgment — D.B. Lal J.

  • The test (para 4). "The Court trying a case of undue influence must consider two things to start with, namely: (i) Are the relations between the donor and the donee such that the donee is in a position to dominate the will of the donor? and (ii) Has the donee used that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the donor? Upon the determination of these issues, a third point emerges, which is that of the onus probandi. If the transaction appears to be unconscionable, then the burden of proving that the contract was not induced by undue influence is to lie upon the person who was in a position to dominate the will of the other" (Subhas Chandra v. Ganga Prasad AIR 1967 SC 878).
  • Position to dominate — findings. Age: plaint said 90, deed said 60, witnesses 60–75 — "near about 70 … sufficiently an advanced age, specially when she was ailing"; Shero (D.W.3) himself admitted the defendants brought her to Una for treatment but could give no detail of any treatment — "she was taken to Una under the pretext of giving her a treatment"; the deed was obtained "while the daughter was absent" — "the defendants avoided the presence of the plaintiff at all relevant time"; as male collaterals of her husband's family who physically took charge of her, "they were in a position to dominate the will of the lady, at any rate during that short period". The recital of past "service" by the donees was unsupported by "evidence worth the name".
  • Unfair advantage — the beneficiary's leading part. Kewal Krishan "engaged his scribe … took witnesses from the village … presented the lady for registration." Applying Vellasawmy Servai v. Sivaraman Servai AIR 1930 PC 24 (a will case whose ratio "is equally applicable"): where the propounder is the principal beneficiary and takes a leading part in instructions, execution and registration, "the circumstances are such as would excite the suspicion of any … Court and require it to examine the evidence … with great vigilance and scrutiny". "The defendant being the principal beneficiary thus took a leading part … and this by itself is sufficient to prove that he dominated the will of the lady and exercised his influence in obtaining an unfair advantage inasmuch as he deprived the natural heir namely the plaintiff of the entire properties."
  • Unconscionability. "The natural affection of the mother should have been for the daughter who was a widow and not under affluent circumstances" — issueless, no land, no male member to look after her (Sarpanch P.W.2; admitted by Kewal Krishan D.W.4). If the motive was really to keep the property in the husband's line, "why it was not mentioned in the deed itself?" — the deed instead recited service. "The transaction of gift was itself unconscionable inasmuch as the mother deprived her dependent daughter of her entire share … Besides this, the donor herself never kept any land for her maintenance. Had she remained alive … she would have been entirely dependent for livelihood upon the defendants. She would not have agreed to such a transaction."
  • The police complaint / register entry. Two documents came into existence — the complaint and the scribe's register entry (kept in the regular course of business, bearing her thumb impression); the register entry "is by itself a primary evidence of a document" (s.62 Evidence Act) and was admissible. It proved that Basanti "had her own objection" to the deed and did not know its details (she thought all four brothers were donees).
  • District Judge's reasons rejected (para 5). No cross-examination about "another document" was possible because the plaintiff never set up one; Jakha Ram was the defendant's "own person"; the scribe's claim that he wrote "whatever the lady said" was "obviously incorrect because the language utilised … could not have been stated by her"; the Sub-Registrar's endorsement is made "in a routine fashion" and its presumption "can be rebutted by proper evidence"; absence of particulars meant "a case of fraud was not established, but nonetheless the case of undue influence was proved and that is sufficient to set aside the document."
  • Burden (para 6). "The burden of proof thus lay upon the defendants to establish that undue influence was not exercised and this they failed to establish. The learned District Judge placed a wrong burden of proof upon the plaintiff." Hence the gift deed "was obtained by undue influence and need be set aside."
  • Pardanashin rule extended (para 7). "Rules regarding transactions by 'pardanashin' women are equally applicable to illiterate and ignorant women though not 'pardanashin'" (Chinta Dasya v. Bhalku Das AIR 1930 Cal 591 — the rule protects "the weak and helpless" and cannot be confined to a class, else "rich women" would gain "an unfair advantage" over "poor women"). Quoting Farid-un-nisa v. Mukhtar Ahmad AIR 1925 PC 204: "the disposition made must be substantially understood and must really be the mental act, as its execution is the physical act, of the person who makes it … The parties to prove the state of the settlor's mind are the parties who set up and rely on the deed … mere execution … is in itself no real proof of a true understanding mind … Evidence to establish such comprehension is most obviously found in proof that the deed was read over to the settlor and, where necessary, explained." "In the instant case, it was the duty of the defendants to prove that the lady substantially understood the document and her physical act of signing … coincided with the mental act of approval of its contents. This the defendants have failed to establish."
  • Conclusion (paras 9–10). The deed "did not convey a free and full consent of Shrimati Basanti. A valid contract never came into existence." She herself objected and complained to the police. "The defendants could not derive any title under the gift-deed. The plaintiff being the natural heir … is entitled to get possession." Appeal allowed; District Judge's decree set aside; suit for possession decreed with costs throughout.
Principles
  1. A gift must be voluntary; the Contract Act tests of undue influence (s.16) apply to gifts, and a gift so procured is liable to be set aside (s.126 para 2).
  2. Three-stage inquiry: position to dominate → use of that position for unfair advantage → if unconscionable, burden on the donee to disprove undue influence; misplacing this burden is an error of law correctable in second appeal.
  3. A beneficiary who takes a leading part in preparing, executing and registering the deed, excluding the natural heir, invites the probate "suspicious circumstances" scrutiny; that participation "by itself" can establish domination and unfair advantage.
  4. Gift of the donor's entire property leaving nothing for her maintenance and disinheriting a dependent natural heir is a hallmark of unconscionability.
  5. The pardanashin protection — donee must prove the deed was read over, explained and substantially understood ("mental act" must coincide with the "physical act") — extends to illiterate and ignorant women (and, by later cases, to illiterate/aged persons generally).
  6. Failure to prove fraud with particulars does not defeat a proved case of undue influence; the Sub-Registrar's endorsement raises only a rebuttable presumption; a scribe's register entry is primary evidence.
Comparing the two DU cases. Tila Bewa — a valid, freely made gift: donor's later dissatisfaction, donee's alleged failure to serve, remarriage, apprehension of waste — none permits revocation (s.126 para 1 & 3). Kartari — a gift vitiated at inception by undue influence: set aside under the contractual door (s.126 para 2) without any revocation clause. Together they map s.126: defeasance clause or vitiated consent — nothing else.

Real examples. (1) Delhi's "family settlement by gift deed" — parents gift a floor of the house to each child; stamp duty at conveyance rates (Delhi gives no relative concession); registration at the Sub-Registrar with two witnesses; once done, the parents cannot "cancel" because a child stops visiting (Tila Bewa) — unless they go to the Maintenance Tribunal under s.23 and the deed contained a maintenance condition. (2) Property frauds on elderly persons — signature on a "medical/bank paper" that turns out to be a gift deed — are litigated on Kartari lines; courts look for who arranged the scribe, witnesses and registration and whether an independent person explained the deed. (3) Gifts of shares/mutual-fund units to children are movables — delivery/transfer suffices; income-tax clubbing (s.64) and s.56(2)(x) exemptions for relatives apply. (4) A Muslim father's oral hiba of a shop to his daughter with keys handed over is complete without any deed (s.129).

11. DU semester examination questions — model answers

Q.1 — DU LL.B. May/June 2025, Q.6(a) (short note, "Explain any two"): Essentials of a valid gift.
Model answer. s.122 TPA defines a gift as the transfer of certain existing movable or immovable property made voluntarily and without consideration by the donor to the donee and accepted by or on behalf of the donee. Its essentials are: (1) Parties — a donor competent to transfer (s.7; major, sound mind, owner/authorised) and an ascertainable donee (who may be a minor, an idol or a company but not the public at large). (2) Subject-matter — certain, existing, transferable property; a gift of future property is void as to that property (s.124); an expectancy cannot be gifted (s.6(a)). (3) Transfer of ownership — the donor must divest himself of the interest given; a gift revocable at the donor's mere will is void (s.126). (4) Voluntarily — free consent unvitiated by coercion, undue influence, fraud or misrepresentation (ss.14–19 Contract Act); a gift extracted from an aged illiterate widow by relatives who dominated her will and took a leading part in the deed was set aside in Kartari v. Kewal Krishan, where the burden lay on the donees to prove that the deed was explained to and understood by her. (5) Without consideration — gratuitous; love, affection, services or expectation of maintenance are motives, not consideration; any price makes it a sale/exchange. (6) Acceptance — by or on behalf of the donee, express or implied (possession, mutation, retaining the deed), during the donor's lifetime and while he is capable of giving; if the donee dies before acceptance the gift is void (s.122). (7) Mode — s.123: immovable property only by a registered instrument signed by or on behalf of the donor and attested by at least two witnesses (registration may follow the donor's death — s.47 Registration Act); movable property by such instrument or by delivery. An unregistered gift of immovable property is void even with possession. (8) Irrevocability — once complete, revocable only under s.126: an agreed defeasance event not dependent on the donor's will, or contractual grounds of rescission other than failure of consideration; a mere direction to maintain the donor is a pious wish and its breach gives no right of revocation (Tila Bewa v. Mana Bewa). Muslim gifts (hiba) and donations mortis causa of movables are outside these rules (s.129).
Q.2 (Problem — DU pattern). M, a widow, executes a registered gift deed of her lands in favour of her daughter-in-law D at the time of D's marriage to her son. The deed makes D "full owner in possession from today" and thereafter states that D shall render service to M, maintain her for life and perform her funeral rites, and shall not sell the land during M's lifetime. After the son's death D, being neglected, goes to her father's house and remarries. M executes a registered deed of cancellation, pleading that the gift was conditional on service and maintenance, and that D's remarriage defeats it. D sues for declaration and possession. Decide. Would your answer differ if the deed had opened with the words "in order to provide for my maintenance" and provided that on D's failure to maintain the gift shall stand revoked?
Model answer. The problem is Tila Bewa v. Mana Bewa. (1) Completion: the gift satisfies ss.122–123 (registered, attested, accepted — D took possession); the donor's continued presence or absence of mutation is irrelevant. (2) Revocation — s.126: a gift may be revoked only (a) on an agreed specified event not dependent on the donor's will, or (b) on grounds on which a contract may be rescinded, excluding want or failure of consideration; "save as aforesaid, a gift cannot be revoked." Here (a) there is no agreement/defeasance clause that failure of service or maintenance shall revoke the gift; (b) failure to maintain is at most failure of consideration, which s.126 expressly excludes — the remedy is a suit for maintenance. (3) Construction: the deed makes an absolute transfer in its operative portion and the directions to serve, maintain and perform funeral rites follow it; per Barman J. such directions are "pious wishes", not conditions. The restraint on sale during M's life is at most a partial restraint, not a ground of revocation. (4) Remarriage and M's apprehension of waste are legally irrelevant. (5) The deed of cancellation is a nullity — a donor cannot revoke "at will and pleasure" but must get the deed set aside by a court on a valid ground; M pleaded no undue influence or fraud with issues framed. Decree for D: declaration of title and immediate possession (the HC even set aside an appellate condition postponing possession to M's death). Alternative deed: if the deed opened with the maintenance purpose followed by the operative clause, the gift would be "subject to the liability to maintain"; and if it further provided that on default the gift shall stand revoked, that is a valid agreement under s.126 para 1 — an event (D's default) not dependent on M's will — so M could revoke on proof of default (subject to bona fide purchasers for value without notice). Today, if M is a senior citizen, s.23 of the 2007 Act would also allow the Tribunal to declare such a conditional transfer void (Sudesh Chhikara requires the condition to be part of the transfer).
Q.3 (Problem). B, an ailing illiterate widow aged about 70, lives with her only daughter K, a poor widow who manages B's land. While K is away, R and S, remote collaterals of B's late husband, take B to town saying she will be shown to a doctor; there a gift deed of B's entire land in their favour, reciting past services, is scribed by R's scribe, attested by R's men and registered the same day. B on return tells K she was made to sign "some paper"; both lodge a police complaint; B dies four days later. R and S take possession. K sues to set aside the deed and for possession. The first appellate court dismisses the suit because K did not prove fraud with particulars, did not cross-examine the scribe about reading over, and the Sub-Registrar's endorsement shows admission. Advise K in second appeal.
Model answer. Kartari v. Kewal Krishan. (1) Law: a gift must be made "voluntarily" (s.122) and may be set aside on any ground on which a contract may be rescinded (s.126 para 2) — undue influence under s.16 Contract Act. The three-stage test (Subhas Chandra Das Mushib): position to dominate; use of it for unfair advantage; if the transaction is unconscionable, burden on the donee (s.16(3)). (2) Position to dominate: age ~70, illness, illiteracy, isolation from her natural adviser (daughter deliberately kept out), physical custody of the collaterals under a pretext. (3) Unfair advantage: the donees engaged the scribe, procured witnesses and registration on the same day — the beneficiary's leading part "by itself is sufficient" (Vellasawmy Servai suspicious-circumstances rule applied to gifts); natural heir wholly excluded; recital of "services" unproved. (4) Unconscionability: the donor gave her entire property, kept nothing for maintenance and disinherited a dependent widowed daughter — she "would not have agreed to such a transaction". Hence the burden shifted to R and S. (5) Pardanashin rule: B being illiterate and ignorant, R and S had to prove that the deed was read over, explained and substantially understood — that the "mental act" coincided with the "physical act" (Farid-un-nisa; Chinta Dasya extending the rule beyond pardanashin women). They led no such proof; the scribe's claim that he wrote what she dictated is incredible given the legal language. (6) Appellate court's errors: failure to plead particulars of fraud is fatal only to fraud, not to undue influence; no cross-examination on "another document" was needed since K set up none; the Sub-Registrar's endorsement is a routine, rebuttable presumption; the scribe's register entry of the police complaint (thumb-marked, kept in course of business) is primary evidence and shows B's contemporaneous objection and ignorance of the contents; the court misplaced the burden on K — an error of law under s.100 CPC. Advice: the second appeal should succeed; the deed be set aside as obtained by undue influence; K, as natural heir (Hindu Succession Act s.15 — daughter is Class I heir of a female Hindu), gets possession with costs.
Q.4 (Essay). "Save as aforesaid, a gift cannot be revoked." Explain the circumstances in which a gift may be suspended or revoked under the Transfer of Property Act, with the help of illustrations and decided cases.
Model answer (outline). Introduce the irrevocability principle and its rationale (a completed transfer vests title; the donee's security; contrast Muslim law). Door 1 — agreed condition (para 1): requirements — agreement of both parties; specified event; not dependent on the donor's will; valid condition (ss.10–12, 25, 31–34); Illustration (a) (reverter on failure of donee's line); Illustration (b) (revocable at pleasure → void pro tanto); suspension v. revocation. Door 2 — contractual rescission (para 2): coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation (ss.15–19A Contract Act); mistake; Kartari in detail (three-stage test, burden, pardanashin rule); exclusion of "want or failure of consideration" — Tila Bewa (maintenance direction as pious wish; need for defeasance clause; remedy is maintenance suit; cancellation deed a nullity). Para 3 — no revocation for ingratitude, remarriage, changed circumstances, apprehension of waste. Para 4 — bona fide transferees for value without notice protected. Procedure — revocation for vitiated consent requires a suit within three years (Art. 59 Limitation Act); statutory addition — s.23 Senior Citizens Act 2007 (Sudesh Chhikara; Urmila Dixit). Conclude with the flowchart logic: defeasance clause or vitiated consent — nothing else.
Q.5 (Short notes — any two). (a) Mode of making a gift (s.123); (b) Onerous gift; (c) Universal donee; (d) Gift of future property and gift to several donees; (e) Pardanashin donor.
Model answer (outline). (a) Immovable: registered instrument (s.17(1)(a) Registration Act; no value threshold) signed by/for donor, attested by ≥2 witnesses (s.3 definition of "attested"; scribe with animus; proof under s.68 Evidence Act); delivery of possession neither necessary nor sufficient; registration may follow donor's death (s.47); movable: instrument or delivery as goods sold (s.33 SOGA); Muslim hiba exempt (s.129). (b) s.127 — single transfer of several things, one burdened: accept all or nothing (Illustration (a) shares in X and Y); separate independent transfers: may pick (Illustration (b)); minor donee not bound until he retains after majority with knowledge; maxim qui sentit commodum. (c) s.128 — gift of whole property makes donee personally liable for donor's debts and liabilities existing at the date of gift up to the value of the property; protects creditors; Kartari donor gave everything — evidence of unconscionability. (d) s.124 — void as to future property (severable); s.125 — non-accepting donee's share remains with donor. (e) Farid-un-nisa, Chinta Dasya, Kartari — burden on person relying on the deed to prove it was explained and understood; independent advice; extends to illiterate/ignorant women and, today, illiterate/aged persons; interacts with s.16(3) Contract Act and s.111 Evidence Act.
Q.6 (Problem). A executes a registered gift deed of his house in favour of his nephew N and hands the deed to N, who thanks him and starts collecting rent from tenants. Before mutation, A's son objects and A executes a "revocation deed" stating that N has been ungrateful and that A "always intended to keep the right to take the house back". A dies. N sues the son for declaration. Decide. Would it matter if the gift deed had contained a clause "the donor may at any time cancel this gift"?
Model answer. The gift is complete: competent donor, existing property, gratuitous, registered and (presumably) attested deed, acceptance evidenced by N's receipt of the deed and collection of rents (implied acceptance) in A's lifetime — ss.122–123. Mutation is not a requisite. Under s.126 the gift cannot be revoked: there was no agreed defeasance event; ingratitude is not a ground; A's unexpressed intention to retain a power of recall is no agreement, and a revocation deed is a nullity (Tila Bewa); no vitiating factor is alleged. N succeeds against the son (who takes only what A owned at death). If the deed itself had said "the donor may at any time cancel this gift", the gift would be revocable "at the mere will of the donor" and therefore void (s.126 para 1; Illustration (b)) — the house would never have left A and would pass to the son on A's death. Note also s.128 if the house was A's whole property (N would be liable for A's debts to that extent).

Case one-liners: Tila Bewa — "full owner from today" first, seva later = pious wish; no default clause → irrevocable; cancellation deed void. Kartari — old illiterate widow, collaterals, doctor pretext, same-day registration, everything given, daughter excluded → undue influence; burden on donees; pardanashin rule extended.
Twelve lines to remember. 1. Gift = gratuitous inter vivos transfer of existing property, accepted by donee (s.122). 2. Acceptance in donor's lifetime and capacity; donee dying earlier → void. 5. Immovable: registered + attested deed compulsory; possession irrelevant; registration after donor's death OK (s.47). 6. Movable: deed or delivery; Muslim hiba and donatio mortis causa saved (s.129). 7. Future property void pro tanto (s.124); non-accepting co-donee's share stays with donor (s.125). 8. Onerous gift: single transfer — all or nothing (s.127); universal donee liable for debts (s.128). 9. s.126: revocation only by agreed defeasance event (not at donor's will) or contractual rescission grounds. 10. Failure to maintain/serve = failure of consideration → no revocation (Tila Bewa); remedy: maintenance suit / s.23 Senior Citizens Act. 11. Undue influence: dominate → unfair advantage → unconscionable → burden on donee (Kartari); pardanashin rule for illiterate donors. 12.
