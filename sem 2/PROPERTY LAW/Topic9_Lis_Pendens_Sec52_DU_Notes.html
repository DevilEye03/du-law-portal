1. The doctrine — meaning, maxim, origin

Etymology. "Lis" means an action or a suit; "pendens" is the present participle of "pendo", meaning continuing or pending. The doctrine of lis pendens "may be defined as the jurisdiction, power, or control that courts have, during the pendency of an action, over the property involved therein" (34 American Jurisprudence 360, quoted in Govinda Pillai, para 5).

Maxim. Pendente lite nihil innovetur — "during litigation nothing new should be introduced." The Capsule: "Where a suit or proceeding is pending between two parties with respect to an immovable property, and one of the parties thereto sells, or otherwise transfers, the subject-matter of the litigation, then the transferee will be bound by the result of the suit or proceeding, whether or not he has notice of the suit or proceeding."

Origin and history (34 Am. Jur. 365, quoted in Govinda Pillai, para 6): "The doctrine of lis pendens is of ancient lineage. Originating, it is said, in the civil law, it seems to have been operative at an early date as the basis of the common law rule by virtue of which the judgment in a real action was regarded as over-reaching any alienation made by the defendant during its pendency. In the course of time the doctrine was adopted by equity, being embodied in one of Lord Bacon's ordinances 'for the better and more regular administration of justice in the court of Chancery'." Bacon's Twelfth Rule: "no decree bindeth any that cometh in bona fide by conveyance from the defendant before bill is exhibited, and is made no party neither by bill nor order; but where he comes in pendente lite, and while the suit is in full prosecution and without any colour of allowance or privity of the court, there regularly the decree bindeth; but if there were any intermission of the suit, or the court made acquainted with the conveyance, the court is to give order upon the special matter according to justice."

Lord Cranworth L.C. in Bellamy v. Sabine (1857) 1 De G & J 566 (the classic statement, adopted by the Privy Council in Faiyaz Husain Khan v. Prag Narain (1907) 34 IA 102 and by the SC in G.T. Girish): "It is scarcely correct to speak of lis pendens as affecting the purchaser through the doctrine of notice, though undoubtedly the language of the courts often so describes its operation. It affects him not because it amounts to notice, but because the law does not allow litigant parties to give to others pending the litigation rights to the property in dispute so as to prejudice the opposite party… The necessities of mankind require that the decision of the court in the suit shall be binding not only on the litigant parties, but also on those who derive title under them by alienation made pending the suit, whether such alienees had or had not notice of the pending proceedings. If this were not so there could be no certainty that the litigation would ever come to an end."
Simple example. A sues B in the Civil Judge's court at Prayagraj for a declaration of title to and possession of a house. While the suit is pending, B sells the house to C, who knows nothing of the suit. A wins. C cannot resist A's decree by saying "I am a bona fide purchaser without notice"; C's title is subject to A's decree. Between B and C, however, the sale is perfectly good — C can sue B for the price or damages. If A had lost, C's purchase would stand unaffected.

2. Basis of the doctrine — two theories

TheoryContent (34 Am. Jur. 363, quoted in Govinda Pillai)Status
Notice theory"A pending suit must be regarded as notice to all the world, and … any person who deals with property involved therein, having presumably known what he was doing, must have acted in bad faith and is therefore properly bound by the judgment rendered."Rejected as the true basis (though courts' language often uses it); it explains why constructive notice cannot be pleaded against s.52
Necessity / public policy theory"The doctrine is not founded on any theory of notice at all, but is based upon the necessity, as a matter of public policy, of preventing litigants from disposing of the property in controversy in such manner as to interfere with execution of the court's decree. Without such a principle … all suits for specific property might be rendered abortive by successive alienations of the property in suit, so that at the end of the suit another would have to be commenced, and after that another, making it almost impracticable for a man ever to make his rights available by a resort to the courts of justice."Accepted — Bennett on Lis Pendens: "The foundation for the doctrine of lis pendens does not rest upon notice, actual or constructive; it rests solely upon necessity — the necessity that neither party to the litigation should alienate the property in dispute so as to affect his opponent." Bellamy v. Sabine; Govinda Pillai; Capsule ("based upon expediency"); G.T. Girish (2022) ("based on equity and public policy … pours complete efficacy to the adjudicatory mechanism").
Consequences of the "necessity" basis. (1) Notice, good faith and consideration on the part of the transferee are irrelevant (G.T. Girish, para 99: "Neither the fact that the transferee had no notice nor the fact that the transferee acted bona fide … are relevant for applying Section 52 … unlike the requirement of Section 19(1)(b) of the Specific Relief Act"). (2) The doctrine has been described as an extension of res judicata — the decree binds the transferee as a person claiming under a party (Manjeshwara Krishnaya v. Vasudeva Mallya AIR 1918 Mad 578, cited in G.T. Girish). (3) It is a rule of procedure/public policy, applied even where the TPA did not extend (Travancore — Govinda Pillai, para 5, "the general principle governing such matters") and to agricultural land/areas excluded from the Act, as a rule of justice, equity and good conscience. (4) In England (Land Charges Act 1925/1972) and in Maharashtra and Gujarat (s.52 as amended by Bombay Act 14 of 1939 — notice of lis pendens must be registered under the Registration Act for the doctrine to bind a transferee) the rule has been modified — noted by Mulla and by the SC in G.T. Girish.

3. Section 52 — text, legislative history and element-by-element analysis

Section 52. Transfer of property pending suit relating thereto. — "During the pendency in any Court having authority within the limits of India excluding the State of Jammu and Kashmir* or established beyond such limits by the Central Government, of any suit or proceeding which is not collusive and in which any right to immoveable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party to the suit or proceeding so as to affect the rights of any other party thereto under any decree or order which may be made therein, except under the authority of the Court and on such terms as it may impose.

Explanation. — For the purposes of this section, the pendency of a suit or proceeding shall be deemed to commence from the date of the presentation of the plaint or the institution of the proceeding in a Court of competent jurisdiction, and to continue until the suit or proceeding has been disposed of by a final decree or order and complete satisfaction or discharge of such decree or order has been obtained, or has become unobtainable by reason of the expiration of any period of limitation prescribed for the execution thereof by any law for the time being in force."
*The J&K exclusion became otiose after the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019 (the TPA now extends there; the Central Act was made applicable from 31-10-2019).

3.2 Element-by-element analysis

ElementMeaning and rules
"During the pendency"Time-frame fixed by the Explanation (see Part 7). Transfers before presentation of the plaint in the competent court or after full satisfaction are outside the section — even if a suit is imminent or an appeal is later filed (subject to the "appeal is a continuation" rule once filed).
"in any Court having authority within the limits of India or established beyond such limits by the Central Government"Civil courts, revenue courts, tribunals exercising judicial power over rights to immovable property, the Supreme Court and High Courts (original and appellate), courts established by the Central Government abroad (e.g., former consular courts). Foreign courts are not covered (a suit in a foreign court does not create a lis pendens in India).
"of any suit or proceeding"Suits (plaint), appeals, revisions, execution proceedings, insolvency proceedings, probate/letters of administration proceedings, arbitration references before court, land-acquisition references, proceedings under special statutes before tribunals (see Part 5).
"which is not collusive"Excludes sham suits brought by secret arrangement (Part 6). Burden of proving collusion lies on the transferee who asserts it.
"in which any right to immoveable property is directly and specifically in question"Title, possession, mortgage, charge, lease, easement, pre-emption, partition, specific performance of a contract to transfer — the right must be the subject-matter of the lis, not incidentally involved (Part 8). Movable property is not covered by s.52 (though a similar principle may apply in equity).
"the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with"Any transfer (sale, mortgage, lease, gift, exchange, charge, release, surrender) and any other dealing (creating a tenancy, partition, dedication, contract to sell, even an involuntary court sale) — Part 9.
"by any party to the suit or proceeding"Plaintiff, defendant, their legal representatives brought on record, persons added under O.I r.10 (from the date they are added); not strangers or persons claiming under a paramount title (G.T. Girish — the "sine qua non").
"so as to affect the rights of any other party thereto under any decree or order which may be made therein"The transfer is not void; it simply cannot prejudice the rights the opposite party ultimately obtains under the decree. If the transferor wins, the transfer stands fully; if he loses, the transferee is bound (Part 10). A transfer that affects only the transferor's own rights (e.g., co-plaintiff selling his own undisputed share) is unaffected.
"except under the authority of the Court and on such terms as it may impose"The court seised of the suit (or the executing court) may permit a transfer pendente lite — e.g., sale to pay maintenance or to prevent deterioration — usually on terms such as deposit of the price in court, substitution of security, or undertaking that the purchaser will be bound. Such a transfer is protected.

6. Collusive suits

The 1929 amendment substituted "which is not collusive" for "contentious". A collusive suit is one in which the parties are, by secret agreement, not really adverse — the litigation is a sham used as an instrument to defeat third parties. It is distinct from a fraudulent suit, which is a genuine contest by which one party seeks to defraud a stranger — lis pendens does apply to a fraudulent (but contested) suit, since the contest is real.

Capsule illustration. A and B secretly agree that B will file a suit against A in respect of a house in A's possession; that A will not seriously contest; and that during the pendency B will sell the house to D and the money will be divided between them. B files the suit; sells to D pendente lite believing D's sale deed will later be set aside as made during the suit; B's suit is decreed. The suit being collusive, the transfer to D will not be set aside — D keeps the house. Section 52 protects honest litigants, not conspirators; the collusive decree is itself liable to be ignored by D (cf. s.44 Evidence Act / s.38 BSA — a judgment obtained by fraud or collusion may be shown to be so).

8. "Right to immovable property directly and specifically in question"

  • Directly — the right must be the very subject of adjudication; not collaterally or incidentally involved. A suit for rent, for damages for trespass, for accounts, or for a money decree does not put a right to the property directly in question — until attachment before judgment/in execution, when s.64 CPC (private alienation after attachment void against claims enforceable under the attachment) takes over.
  • Specifically — the property must be identifiable from the pleadings (described in the plaint/schedule). A suit relating to "all the properties of X" without specification, or a claim to an unascertained share of a fluctuating fund, is not "specific". In a partition suit, each item of joint property described in the plaint is specifically in question; a pre-emption suit is covered "where shares are not ascertained" too, because the right to the specific land is claimed.
  • "Any right" — includes ownership, possession, mortgage/charge, lease, easement, right of pre-emption, right to specific performance (s.40 TPA obligation annexed to ownership), right of maintenance charged on specific property (s.39), shebaiti right in specific property. A mere personal right (e.g., a contractual licence) is not enough.
  • Immovable — s.3 TPA / s.3(26) General Clauses Act / s.2(6) Registration Act (Topic 1). Movables are outside s.52; the equitable principle may still be applied by analogy in some decisions, but as a rule attachment (O.XXXVIII, O.XXI) is the remedy.
Examples. (i) M sues to enforce a mortgage over a theatre — right to the theatre directly and specifically in question (Supreme General Films). (ii) P sues for specific performance of an agreement to sell Plot 21 — covered (G.T. Girish: Explanation applied by the High Court; the SC applied the "party" limb). (iii) L sues T for arrears of rent of a shop — not covered; the shop is only the background. (iv) W sues H for maintenance and asks that it be charged on H's house — the right to a charge on the specific house is in question; sale by H pendente lite is subject to the charge decreed. (v) A sues B for a declaration that a decree obtained by B against C is fraudulent — no right to any specific immovable property in issue.

9. "Transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party" — voluntary and involuntary alienations; antecedent rights

9.1 Voluntary alienations

Every transfer by act of parties — sale (s.54), mortgage (s.58), charge (s.100), lease (s.105), exchange (s.118), gift (s.122), release, surrender, family settlement, partition, dedication to a deity, contract to sell coupled with possession (s.53A) — and "otherwise dealt with" covers dealings which are not technically "transfers of property" under s.5, such as creating a tenancy, granting a licence coupled with interest, or a compromise creating new rights (Supreme General Films: lease executed pursuant to a compromise decree in a separate suit).

9.2 Involuntary alienations

Transfers by operation of law or through court — court sales in execution of another decree, sales under the SARFAESI Act or Revenue Recovery Acts, transfers on insolvency — are also subject to s.52 in the sense that the auction-purchaser takes subject to the result of the pending suit (the Privy Council: a purchaser at a court sale in execution of a money decree against the party is as much bound as a private purchaser — Moti Lal v. Karrab-ul-Din (1897) 24 IA 170; Radhamadhub Holdar v. Monohur Mookerji (1888) 15 IA 97). The section itself says "except under the authority of the Court" — i.e., a transfer that the court seised of the lis authorises is exempt; a sale by another court in execution of an unrelated decree is not "authority of the Court" for s.52 purposes. Partition by metes and bounds, mutation, or succession on death are not "transfers" but the heir/LR steps into the shoes of the party (O.XXII CPC) and is bound anyway.

9.3 Antecedent (pre-existing) rights v. new rights — Supreme General Films

Section 52 strikes only at rights created pendente lite. If the transaction merely gives effect to a right that existed before the suit — e.g., a sale deed executed pendente lite in performance of a pre-suit registered agreement, redemption of a pre-suit mortgage, a lease-renewal exercised under a pre-suit covenant — the transferee's right relates back and is not defeated (the SC accepted this principle, citing Bishan Singh v. Khazan Singh AIR 1958 SC 838, but found on the facts that the 1956 lease "purported to create entirely new rights pendente lite", the 1940 unregistered lease having expired in 1946 and the 1948 letter/1956 compromise being suspect). Compare: a transferee under a pre-suit agreement to sell who obtains a sale deed pendente lite is protected only to the extent of the antecedent contractual right — and only if that right itself was enforceable (s.40 TPA; s.19(b) SRA).

9.4 "By any party"

  • Plaintiff or defendant — including a decree-holder in possession who sells while the losing side's appeal/SLP is pending (Dalip Kaur — Lachhman sold after obtaining possession under his decree; his vendees were bound by the SC's reversal and liable to restitution under s.144 CPC).
  • Legal representatives after substitution; persons impleaded under O.I r.10 from the date of impleadment.
  • Not: strangers; a person claiming under an independent paramount title; a co-owner who was never impleaded; the son in G.T. Girish who sold before being made a party and after the time for substitution had expired — the SC held the "cardinal and indispensable requirement" is that the transfer be by a party, and reversed the High Court's finding of lis pendens against him (though the plaintiff lost anyway on other grounds).
  • The transferee pendente lite is not a necessary party but may be impleaded under O.I r.10 / O.XXII r.10 CPC (devolution of interest) at the court's discretion; he is bound by the decree even if not impleaded and cannot resist execution (O.XXI r.102 CPC expressly denies a transferee pendente lite from the judgment-debtor the right to object under rr.98–100).

10. Effect of the doctrine

Voidable, not void. "A transfer or a dealing by a party to a suit during the pendency … is not ipso facto void. It only cannot affect the right of any other party to the suit under any decree or order that may be made … Section 52 creates only a right to be enforced to avoid a transfer made pendente lite, because such transfers are not void but voidable and that too at the option of the affected party to the proceedings" (Capsule). G.T. Girish (para 93, 100): "when a transaction is done … pending a case, the transaction is, as such, not annulled … as between the transferor and the transferee, it does not lie in the mouth of the transferor to set up the plea of lis pendens to defeat the disposition … The right of the party to the suit who conveys his right by a sale is extinguished. All that Section 52 … provides is that the transfer … is subjected to the final result of the litigation."

CASE 1 — Supreme General Films Exchange Ltd. v. H.H. Maharaja Sir Brijnath Singhji Deo

AIR 1975 SC 1810 : (1975) 2 SCC 530 · Supreme Court of India · Y.V. Chandrachud J. · Appeal by special leave (Art. 136) from the Madhya Pradesh High Court affirming the District Judge, Jabalpur · DU material pp. 120–121

Theme A registered lease executed by mortgagors during the pendency of the mortgagee's suit for sale, in purported performance of an earlier agreement and a compromise decree in a separate suit, creates new rights pendente lite and is struck by s.52; the "antecedent right" exception and the "s.65A is a special provision" argument rejected.

Facts

  • The property: Sunder Vilas Theatre (later Plaza Talkies), Jabalpur, owned by Jiwan Das Bhatia and sons ("the Bhatias").
  • The mortgage: The Bhatias borrowed Rs 2,50,000 from the plaintiff-respondent, a Maharaja, against security of bales of cotton. As the pledged goods proved insufficient, on 29 December 1951 they executed a registered mortgage deed of the Plaza Theatre in his favour. Unable to recover, the Maharaja filed Civil Suit No. 15A of 1954, which ended in a compromise decree on 7 May 1960 under which the dues were to be realised by sale of the theatre.
  • The second decree: The Central Bank of India, another creditor, had filed Civil Suit No. 3B of 1952 and obtained a decree for Rs 1,24,000 on 29 April 1952; rights under it were assigned to the Maharaja. In execution, the theatre with other properties was attached on 4 May 1955.
  • The tenant: The appellant company had run a cinema in the theatre under an unregistered lease of 27 February 1940, which expired on 10 April 1946; it continued as a tenant holding over. On 20 November 1954 the company sued the Bhatias (Suit No. 16A of 1954) for specific performance of an agreement to lease contained in a letter of 19 July 1948; a compromise decree was passed on 24 March 1956 — the Maharaja was not a party to that suit. Pursuant to it, on 30 March 1956 the Bhatias executed the impugned registered lease deed: eight years from 10 February 1956 to 10 February 1964, with an option of renewal to 10 February 1970.
  • The suit: The Maharaja sued in the District Judge's court, Jabalpur, for a declaration that the 1956 lease was void and ineffective against his rights under the two decrees, and that an auction-purchaser under either decree would take free of any obligation to the company under the lease. He relied on three provisions: s.52 TPA (lis pendens), s.65A TPA (mortgagor's limited power to lease) and s.64 CPC (alienation after attachment void).
  • The company denied that the plaintiff could claim under these provisions and pleaded that a suit for such a declaration did not lie at all under s.42 of the Specific Relief Act, 1877. The trial court and the High Court decreed the suit; the company appealed by special leave.

Issues

  1. Whether the lease of 30 March 1956, executed during the pendency of the mortgage suit (1954–1960), was hit by s.52 — or fell outside it as being merely the enforcement of an antecedent right (the 1948 agreement / 1956 compromise decree).
  2. Whether s.65A(2)(e) (mortgagor's lease not to exceed three years), being a special provision, displaces s.52.
  3. (Also raised) Maintainability under s.42 SRA 1877, and applicability of s.64 CPC — decided against the company (paras 7–17, not reproduced in the DU extract).

Arguments

Appellant (the Company)

  • The lease of 1956 was executed in satisfaction of an antecedent claim — the agreement to lease of 19 July 1948, which predated the mortgage suit of 1954 — and merely carried out the compromise decree of 24 March 1956 in the specific-performance suit. A transfer that enforces a pre-existing right is not a transfer "pendente lite" creating new rights; reliance on Bishan Singh v. Khazan Singh AIR 1958 SC 838.
  • Alternatively, s.65A(2)(e), which specifically regulates a mortgagor's leases (limit of three years) is a special provision that displaces the general s.52; the mortgagee's remedy lies only under s.65A.
  • The plaintiff's suit for a bare declaration was not maintainable under s.42 SRA 1877; the plaintiff had no present right to the theatre entitling him to such a declaration.

Respondent (the Maharaja)

  • The right to the theatre was directly and specifically in question in the mortgage suit (1954–1960); the lease of 1956 was executed by the mortgagors, parties to that suit, during its pendency, and was therefore subject to the decree for sale — an auction-purchaser must take free of it.
  • The alleged antecedent right was illusory: the 1940 lease was unregistered and had expired in 1946; the 1948 "agreement" was a letter; the specific-performance suit and its compromise, to which the mortgagee was not impleaded, were a device to manufacture a lease behind the mortgagee's back.
  • Further, the lease violated s.65A (beyond three years, not in ordinary course of management) and s.64 CPC (executed after the attachment of 4 May 1955).

Decision (Chandrachud J.)

  1. Antecedent-right argument rejected (para 18). "The contention that the case fell outside the purview of Section 52 … as the lease was executed in purported satisfaction of an antecedent claim rests upon the terms of an agreement of 1948, embodied in a letter, on the strength of which the defendant-appellant had filed his suit for specific performance. We find that the terms of the compromise decree in that suit and lease-deed of 1956 purported to confer upon the defendant-appellant new rights. Indeed, there are good grounds for suspecting that the compromise in the suit for specific performance was adopted as a device to get round legal difficulties in the execution of the lease of 1956 … We are unable to accept the argument, sought to be supported by the citation of Bishan Singh v. Khazan Singh, that the lease was merely an enforcement of an antecedent or pre-existing right. We think that it purported to create entirely new rights pendente lite. It was, therefore, struck by the doctrine of lis pendens, as explained by this Court in Jayaram Mudaliar v. Ayyaswami [AIR 1973 SC 569], embodied in Section 52."
  2. s.65A does not displace s.52 (para 19). The argument "overlooks the special objects of the doctrine of lis pendens which applies to a case in which litigation, relating to property in which rights are sought to be created pendente lite by acts of parties, is pending." Moreover, the argument assumes s.65A(2)(e) applies; "if that was so, it would make no substantial difference to the rights of the defendant-appellant, which would vanish before the suit was filed if Section 65A applies" (a lease limited to 3 years from 1956 would have ended in 1959). "We, however, think that, as the special doctrine of lis pendens is applicable here, the purported lease of 1956 was invalid from the outset" as against the plaintiff; unnecessary to decide s.65A.
  3. Appeal dismissed with costs (para 21). The declaration stood: the auction-purchaser under either decree takes free of the 1956 lease.
Capsule's gloss. "Since the earlier right created in favour of the tenant was with the help of a document that was incapable of taking effect in law [unregistered lease], the lease of 1956 for the first time created an interest in the property in their favour. If he had an antecedent right created prior to the commencement of the suit, he would have been able to retain possession as per the terms of the lease. But since the lease was executed while the suit was pending … the rights of the tenant were subject to the decision of the court … the tenant was to take the lease subject to the outcome … The result would be that he would have to vacate the premises."

Principles

  1. A lease (or any dealing) by a mortgagor-defendant during the pendency of the mortgagee's suit for sale is a transfer pendente lite and is subject to the decree; the auction-purchaser takes free of it.
  2. Antecedent-right exception: a transaction pendente lite escapes s.52 only if it genuinely gives effect to a right that existed before the suit; where the pre-suit document created no enforceable interest (unregistered/expired lease; mere letter) and the pendente-lite deed confers new rights, the exception fails. Courts will look through devices such as a collusive compromise decree in a separate suit to which the opposite party was not impleaded.
  3. Section 52 is a doctrine with "special objects" and is not displaced by other special provisions like s.65A; more than one provision (s.52, s.65A, s.64 CPC) may concurrently strike a transaction.
  4. A declaratory suit lies at the instance of a mortgagee-decree-holder to clear the title of the property for sale.
Example. A bank sues a borrower in 2023 for sale of a mortgaged shop in Civil Lines, Prayagraj. In 2024 the borrower grants a 9-year registered lease of the shop to his cousin, citing a 2019 "letter of intent". Following Supreme General Films, the lease creates new rights pendente lite; the auction-purchaser under the bank's decree can evict the cousin. Had the cousin held a registered 12-year lease executed in 2019 (before the suit and, if before the mortgage, binding on the bank), his possession would be an antecedent right unaffected by s.52.

CASE 2 — Govinda Pillai Gopala Pillai v. Aiyyappan Krishnan

AIR 1957 Ker 10 · Kerala High Court · M.S. Menon J. · Execution second appeal · DU material pp. 122–126

Theme Commencement of pendency. A plaint presented in a court without pecuniary jurisdiction and returned for presentation to the proper court does not start the lis; a gift deed executed after the first (wrong) presentation but before re-presentation in the competent court is not hit by lis pendens. The Rangoon view (Ma Than, 1927) is no longer good law after the 1929 Explanation.

Facts

  • In execution of a decree, the appellant (decree-holder) applied for delivery of one acre in Survey Plot No. 201/1, Kanjirappally North Pakuthy, with the building thereon.
  • The respondent (102nd defendant) objected: his father, the 35th defendant, had no rights in the property even on the date of suit; the 35th defendant had gifted it under Ext. I dated 3-6-1095 (Malayalam Era ≈ 1920) to the respondent and his mother; the mother had gifted her share to him under Ext. II in 1101 (≈1926); he had been in possession under independent title ever since; neither he nor his mother was a party to the decree, which therefore did not bind him.
  • Crucially, the plaint had originally been presented in a court lacking pecuniary jurisdiction; after a preliminary finding it was returned and was filed in the District Court, Kottayam, only on 29-11-1095 — i.e., after the gift of 3-6-1095. (The gift thus fell in the interval between wrong-court presentation and competent-court presentation.)
  • The executing court upheld the objection; the decree-holder appealed.
  • Note: The Transfer of Property Act was not in force in Travancore at the relevant time, so the court applied "the general principle governing such matters", taking s.52 as amended as its correct statement.

Issue

"Whether the gift deed dated 3-6-1095 is affected by the rule of lis pendens" — which turned on whether pendency commenced on the presentation of the plaint in the wrong court or only on its presentation in the competent court on 29-11-1095.

Arguments

Appellant (decree-holder)

  • Relied on Mulla (4th edn, p. 228): "If the plaintiff's valuation is disputed and the plaint returned after inquiry for presentation to a Court of higher grade, an alienation effected in the interval is affected by the doctrine of lis pendens" — based on Ma Than v. Maung Bagyan AIR 1927 Rang 145 (Heald J.: a plaintiff who presented in the wrong court and re-presented "without undue delay" was "actively prosecuting" the suit throughout; Cunliffe J. relying on Tangor Majhri v. Jaladhar Deari AIR 1919 Mad 755: active prosecution even in "an inappropriate tribunal" suffices; "a sound principle of equity").
  • The suit was one continuous litigation from the first presentation; the donee, a son of a defendant, should not benefit from a technicality of valuation.

Respondent (donee / 102nd defendant)

  • On the date of the gift no suit was pending in any court of competent jurisdiction; s.52 as amended in 1929 (and the general principle it embodies) fixes commencement at "presentation of the plaint … in a Court of competent jurisdiction".
  • The Rangoon decision was under the old "active prosecution" wording and is no longer good law: Gouri Dutt v. Shanker AIR 1933 Sind 117; Nathusingh v. Anandrao AIR 1940 Nag 185.
  • He and his mother were never parties; the decree cannot bind them or their property.

Decision (M.S. Menon J.)

  1. The doctrine explained (paras 5–7). Etymology ("lis" = suit; "pendens" from "pendo"); definition ("jurisdiction, power, or control that courts have, during the pendency of an action, over the property involved therein" — 34 Am. Jur. 360); the two theories (notice v. necessity — 34 Am. Jur. 363); origin in civil law, common law real actions and Bacon's Twelfth Ordinance (34 Am. Jur. 365); Bennett on Lis Pendens and Lord Cranworth in Bellamy v. Sabine: the rule "affects him not because it amounts to notice, but because the law does not allow litigant parties to give to others pending the litigation rights to the property in dispute so as to prejudice the opposite party"; "the foundation … rests solely upon necessity."
  2. The 1929 amendment (para 4). The section originally spoke of "active prosecution" of a "contentious" suit; Act 20 of 1929 substituted "pendency" and "any suit or proceeding which is not collusive" and added the Explanation fixing the time of pendency.
  3. The Rangoon view is obsolete (paras 8–13). "Whatever may be the correctness of the decision on the basis of Section 52 before the amendments … it cannot be considered as a correct interpretation of the section as it stands today." Quoting Rupchand AJC in Gouri Dutt: "The legislature has thought fit to amend … to make it abundantly clear that the pendency … shall be deemed to commence from the date of presentation of the plaint … in the Court of competent jurisdiction. The Rangoon case is therefore no longer good law." And: if a suit filed in a wrong court remained "the suit", s.14 of the Limitation Act (exclusion of time spent bona fide in a court without jurisdiction) would be redundant; the settled position is that limitation runs to the date of presentation in the proper court, with s.14 available as an exemption. In Nathusingh (Pollock J.): where a minor's partition suit was filed in a wrong court and the father mortgaged the property before re-presentation, "the suit in which the decree for partition was passed was not instituted until after the mortgage was executed, and therefore the doctrine of lis pendens cannot apply."
  4. Application (para 14). "We take the view that Section 52 … as it stands today embodies a correct version of the rule of lis pendens and that it is that rule that should be applied in this case. If the said rule is applied there can be no doubt that there was no suit pending in a court of competent jurisdiction prior to 29-11-1095 and that Ext. I dated 3-6-1095 should hence be held as not vitiated by the rule of lis pendens."
  5. Appeal dismissed with costs; the donee retained the property.

Principles

  1. Pendency commences only on presentation of the plaint in a court of competent jurisdiction (pecuniary, territorial, subject-matter). Presentation in a wrong court, even if followed by diligent re-presentation, does not start the lis; a transfer in the interval is unaffected.
  2. The 1929 Explanation is declaratory of the correct rule of lis pendens and is applied even where the TPA does not formally extend, as the embodiment of justice, equity and good conscience.
  3. The basis of the doctrine is necessity/public policy, not notice — a legislative and judicial choice which cuts both ways: the transferee cannot plead want of notice, but the plaintiff cannot extend the doctrine to a period when no competent court was seised.
  4. Analogy with Limitation Act s.14: a suit in a court without jurisdiction is not "the suit" for either limitation or lis pendens.
Example. P files a title suit over a house worth Rs 60 lakh before a Civil Judge (Junior Division) at Prayagraj whose pecuniary limit is lower. On 1 March the plaint is returned under O.VII r.10; P re-files before the Civil Judge (Senior Division) on 20 March. D gifts the house to his daughter on 10 March. The gift is not hit by s.52 (Govinda Pillai). P's protection lay in filing correctly, seeking an interim injunction, or — after the gift — impleading the daughter.

CASE 3 — Dalip Kaur v. Jeewan Ram

AIR 1996 P&H 158 · Punjab & Haryana High Court · Jawahar Lal Gupta J. · Regular Second Appeal from orders rejecting objections to a restitution application under s.144 CPC · DU material pp. 127–128

Theme Whether an appeal to the Supreme Court by special leave under Art. 136 is a continuation of the original suit so that lis pendens (and restitution) apply to a sale made by the decree-holder while the SLP/appeal was pending. Held: yes. Basis of DU 2025 Q.8.

Facts

  • Lachhman (respondent 24) sued for possession by pre-emption of 9 kanals 9 marlas of land sold to respondents 1–5 (the original vendees). Part of the land had been re-sold by respondents 1–5 to respondents 6–21.
  • The pre-emption suit was decreed on 22 August 1983; Lachhman took possession on 6 October 1983. The vendees' first appeal was dismissed by the District Judge on 18 March 1985; their second appeal was dismissed by the High Court on 26 September 1985.
  • Respondents 1–5 filed an SLP under Art. 136; leave was granted; the Supreme Court allowed the appeal on 5 October 1989 and dismissed Lachhman's suit.
  • The original vendees and respondents 6–21 then applied under s.144 CPC for restitution of possession.
  • The appellants (Dalip Kaur and others) with respondents 22–23 objected: they had purchased the suit land from Lachhman (while the Supreme Court proceedings were pending) as bona fide purchasers for consideration; the s.144 petition was therefore not competent against them. The vendees replied that the matter was governed by lis pendens.
  • The trial court framed issues (maintainability of objections; relief), rejected the objections on 23 February 1994; the first appellate court affirmed; hence the second appeal.

Issue

"Are the proceedings in a civil appeal before the Supreme Court in pursuance to the grant of special leave under Art. 136 of the Constitution of India not a continuation of the proceedings in the original suit and is the principle of lis pendens not applicable to such proceedings?"

Arguments

Appellants (Dalip Kaur etc. — Mr J.R. Mittal)

  • Sole contention: lis pendens does not apply to proceedings before the Supreme Court under Art. 136. Art. 136 is an extraordinary, discretionary remedy, "not in the ordinary line of appeal"; the suit had ended with the High Court's dismissal of the second appeal; a purchase from the decree-holder in possession thereafter was a purchase from an owner, not a transfer pendente lite.
  • Firm reliance on Mewa Singh v. Jagir Singh AIR 1971 P&H 244, where the court had declined to restore possession from a transferee not party to the Supreme Court appeal.
  • They were bona fide purchasers for value and not parties before the Supreme Court; a decree reversed in proceedings to which they were strangers cannot be executed against them by restitution.

Respondents (original vendees and sub-vendees)

  • The appeal to the Supreme Court, once leave is granted, is an appeal in the same lis; the decree of the trial court merged in the Supreme Court's order; the litigation continued until 5 October 1989. A transfer by Lachhman in that period is pendente lite.
  • The purchasers' interests were fully represented by their vendor, Lachhman, who was a party; being bound through him, they must restore what he took under the reversed decree (s.144 CPC).
  • Mewa Singh was decided on its peculiar facts (compromise; conscious deletion of a clause regarding the transferee) and itself recognised the general rule.

Decision (Jawahar Lal Gupta J.)

  1. Nature of the Supreme Court's jurisdiction (para 7). "The Supreme Court is at the head of the 'pyramid' of the judicial system in this country. It exercises original and appellate jurisdiction. It has the power to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter [Art. 142] … The law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all Courts [Art. 141]. Under Art. 136 … the Supreme Court has the discretion to grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order in any cause or matter … Their Lordships can even interfere with an interlocutory order." The power is invoked "not only in cases where substantial questions of law are involved but even in those where the High Court has come to a wrong conclusion from the evidence"; the SC "has interfered with the orders passed by the High Court in Second Appeals or Revision Petitions."
  2. Application. "In the present case, the decree which had been passed by the trial court and affirmed by the lower appellate Court as well as this Court in Second Appeal, was reversed by their Lordships. The decree having been reversed, the parties were clearly entitled to restitution of possession. The mere fact that the present appellants were not a party before the Supreme Court, is of no consequence as their interests were duly represented by their vendor who was admittedly a party. Still further, there appears to be no warrant for the view that the proceedings are not a continuation of the original suit. The mere fact that the leave to appeal has to be obtained under the Constitution does not mean that the doctrine of lis pendens would not apply or that the decree holder shall not be entitled to the restoration of possession."
  3. Mewa Singh distinguished (para 8). There the dispute "was not decided on merits but in terms of the compromise"; the appellants had recognised that they were not entitled to get back possession from the transferee (Purshottam Das Rattan) who was not a party, in view of the insertion and conscious deletion of clause (iv) of para 10 of the compromise petition — "clearly a case on its own facts". Even there the court observed that "there is no doubt that the transferee during the pendency of a suit or other proceedings is bound by the result thereof" — "This is precisely what has happened in the present case."
  4. Holding (para 9). "The question posed at the outset is answered in the negative. It is held that proceedings before the Supreme Court are a continuation of those in the original suit and that the principle of lis pendens as well as restitution shall apply to the proceedings." Appeal dismissed.

Principles

  1. Pendency under s.52 continues through every appellate tier — first appeal, second appeal and an appeal to the Supreme Court under Art. 136 (once leave is granted, the SLP becomes an appeal in the same lis; even the SLP stage is treated as part of the continuum). "Appeal is a continuation of the suit" applies to constitutional appeals as well.
  2. A decree-holder in possession who transfers during the pendency of the losing party's appeal transfers pendente lite; his transferees are bound by the reversal and must submit to restitution under s.144 CPC, even though they were not parties — they are represented by their vendor.
  3. Bona fides and consideration are no defence to lis pendens or restitution.
  4. Earlier decisions refusing restitution against transferees (e.g., Mewa Singh) rest on special facts such as compromise terms, not on any exception to the doctrine.
Example. K obtains a pre-emption decree over 5 bighas in Kaushambi in 2019, takes possession, loses in the District Judge's court in 2021 but wins in the High Court in 2023. The vendee files an SLP; leave is granted in 2024. In 2025 K sells the land to Z. In 2026 the Supreme Court reverses and dismisses K's suit. Z is bound: pendency continued through the Art. 136 appeal (Dalip Kaur); the vendee may recover possession from Z under s.144 CPC; Z's remedy is against K.

CASE 4 — G.T. Girish v. Y. Subba Raju (D) by LRs & Anr.

2022 LiveLaw (SC) 61 : (2022) 12 SCC 321 · Supreme Court of India · K.M. Joseph & P.S. Narasimha JJ. · Decided 18 January 2022 · Appeals from the Karnataka High Court (which had decreed specific performance) · Added to the DU list (handwritten "2022 LiveLaw (SC) 61")

Theme The Supreme Court's modern restatement of s.52: basis (equity and public policy, not notice); transfer pendente lite not void but subject to the result; good faith and notice irrelevant (contrast s.19(1)(b) SRA); the "party" requirement is the sine qua non — a sale by an heir before he was impleaded (and after the time for substitution had run) is not hit; and ultimately no specific performance because the agreement to sell a BDA-allotted site within the non-alienation period was unlawful (s.23 Contract Act).

Facts

  • On 4 April 1979 the Bangalore Development Authority (BDA) allotted a site to the first defendant (a lady) under a lease-cum-sale agreement under the Bangalore Rules of Allotment, 1972; possession on 14 May 1979. Rule 18(2) prohibited alienation of the site for ten years from allotment; conveyance by BDA to follow after the lease period.
  • On 17 November 1982 the first defendant agreed to sell the site to the plaintiff (Y. Subba Raju) for Rs 50,000 (Rs 30,000 advance by cheque; balance later paid — the plaintiff said the whole consideration was paid), the sale deed to be executed within three months of the vendor obtaining the sale deed from the BDA.
  • Letters of 1 March 1983 and 26 April 1984 from the plaintiff calling for performance; the vendor's letter of 8 May 1984 alleging the plaintiff's breach, lapse of the agreement and forfeiture. After notice on 14 February 1985 the plaintiff sued for specific performance (and injunction).
  • The first defendant filed her written statement in 1986 and died on 18 July 1994 pending suit. The plaintiff impleaded only her husband as defendant 1(a) (order of 25 August 1995). The husband gave a "no objection" enabling the BDA to convey the site to the son (later defendant 1(b)) by sale deed of 19 June 1996. On 19 September 1996 the son sold the site to the second defendant, G.T. Girish (a 20-year-old agriculturist/milk vendor from Nagamangala taluk) for a stated Rs 4,50,000. Only in 1997 were the son and Girish impleaded.
  • Trial court: refused specific performance; ordered refund of Rs 50,000 with 9% interest; found Girish a bona fide purchaser. High Court: reversed — suit maintainable; Girish not a bona fide purchaser without notice (constructive notice under s.3 TPA: no inquiry about original documents/possession certificate; negotiations in June 1996 with the father, a party, present; transaction even a sham); the sale to Girish hit by s.52 because the estate of the deceased defendant was represented by defendant 1(a) and the son could not deal with it free of the lis (relying on Nallakumara Goundan v. Pappayi Ammal AIR 1945 Mad 219); decreed specific performance directing defendants 1(a), 1(b) and 2 to convey.

Issues

  1. Whether the agreement of 17 November 1982 was lawful and enforceable, given the ten-year non-alienation rule (r.18(2)) — s.23 Contract Act / public policy; whether the suit was premature/barred by limitation.
  2. Whether the sale by the son (not then a party) to the second defendant on 19 September 1996 was hit by lis pendens under s.52.
  3. Whether the second defendant was a bona fide purchaser for value without notice (s.19(1)(b) SRA), and what relief the plaintiff should get.

Arguments

Appellants (second defendant — Smt Kiran Suri, Sr Adv; defendants 1(a)/1(b))

  • The agreement was unlawful — opposed to public policy and one which "would defeat the provisions of law" (s.23 Contract Act), being an agreement to alienate a BDA site within the ten-year embargo under r.18(2) and the lease-cum-sale agreement; no court can grant specific performance of an illegal bargain (Kedar Nath Motani v. Prahlad Rai AIR 1960 SC 213; Narayanamma v. Govindappa (2019) 19 SCC 42).
  • The suit was premature — performance was due only after the BDA conveyance, which came in 1996 — and, alternatively, time-barred.
  • The sale of 1996 was made by the son who was not a party to the suit (impleaded only in 1997, long after the period for substitution) — s.52 requires a transfer "by any party"; the High Court wrongly imported notice/constructive notice into s.52.
  • The second defendant was a bona fide purchaser for value without notice; the trial court's finding should not have been disturbed.

Respondents (plaintiff's LRs — Shri R. Basant, Sr Adv)

  • The agreement only contemplated conveyance after the vendor obtained title from the BDA — i.e., after the embargo; it did not offend r.18; the whole price had been paid; nothing remained for the plaintiff to do; equity favoured specific performance.
  • The estate of the deceased first defendant was represented in the suit by her husband (s.2(11) CPC; O.XXII); the son's sale during the suit's pendency was a dealing with the very estate in litigation and was hit by s.52 (Nallakumara Goundan); the husband's "no objection" was itself a dealing by a party.
  • Girish had constructive notice (s.3 TPA) of the agreement and the suit; the sale was a sham engineered to defeat the plaintiff; he could not claim s.19(1)(b) SRA protection.

Decision (K.M. Joseph J., for the Bench)

  1. Illegality (the decisive ground). After an extensive review of the Rules (rr.7, 8, 11, 12, 17, 18) the Court held that the scheme of public allotment — selection of eligible persons by a committee, fixed price, compulsory construction, and r.18's prohibition of alienation for ten years save by mortgage to specified institutions or surrender to the BDA — meant that an agreement to sell the site within the period was one which would defeat the provisions of law and was opposed to public policy under s.23 of the Contract Act; a court cannot lend its aid to enforce it (following the Motilal–Ferrodous Estates line). The suit for specific performance therefore had to fail on this ground alone (limitation not decided).
  2. Lis pendens — restatement (paras 92–94). "The Doctrine of Lis Pendens is based on the maxim 'pendente lite nihil innovetur' … Section 52 … is based on equity and public policy. It pours complete efficacy to the adjudicatory mechanism … by finding that any disposition of property … by a party to the litigation will not in any way detract from the finality of the decision rendered by the court. It is clear that it is not based on the ground of Notice as laid down by Lord Cranworth in Bellamy v. Sabine, which has been followed by the Privy Council in 34 IA 102" — quoting Mulla (12th edn): "The rule is, therefore, based not on the doctrine of notice, but on expediency, i.e., the necessity for final adjudication. It is immaterial whether the alienee pendente lite had, or had not, notice … no longer the case in England, or in Gujarat and Maharashtra, where the doctrine only affects transactions pendente lite if the lis has been duly registered." Para 93: "when a transaction is done … pending a case, the transaction is, as such, not annulled … as between the transferor and the transferee, it does not lie in the mouth of the transferor to set up the plea of lis pendens … Equally, the Principle of Lis Pendens is not to be confounded with the aspect of good faith or bona fides … the transferee … cannot set up the case that he acted bona fide." Para 94: "The cardinal and indispensable requirement … is that the transfer or dealing … is carried out by a party to the proceeding. Section 52 uses the word 'party' twice … the Special Bench of the Madras High Court in Manjeshwara Krishnaya v. Vasudeva Mallya (AIR 1918 Mad 578) puts the Doctrine of Lis Pendens as an extension of the Doctrine of Res Judicata. Thus, the sine qua non … is that the transfer is made … by a person who is a party to the litigation … The person/party who finally succeeds … can ask the court to ignore any transfer … by any party … made during the pendency of the lis."
  3. Application (paras 95–100). The first defendant died 1994; her husband was substituted in 1995; the son sold on 19 September 1996; son and purchaser were impleaded only on 9 April 1997 — "The transfer … was, therefore, made at a time when the son of the first defendant was not a party to the Suit." The High Court erred in invoking notice/constructive notice: "Neither the fact that the transferee had no notice nor the fact that the transferee acted bona fide … are relevant for applying Section 52 … This is unlike the requirement of Section 19(1)(b) of the Specific Relief Act." Nallakumara Goundan "turned on its own facts" — a transfer by an LR within the period for substitution; here the period had long expired. Even treating the father's "no objection" as the father having "otherwise dealt with" the property, "it would still not invalidate the sale. At best, the plaintiff can contend that, should he be entitled for a decree of performance, the sale in favour of the second defendant should be subjected to such decree." The High Court "was not correct in finding that the sale by defendant 1(b) in favour of second defendant was hit by lis pendens."
  4. Bona fide purchaser. The Court reviewed the High Court's findings (constructive notice, no inquiry into originals, meeting the father, age and means of the purchaser, unexamined broker) and, without disturbing the finding that Girish was not a bona fide purchaser without notice, held that since the agreement was unenforceable the question of relief against him did not arise; the sale was not a sham.
  5. Relief (paras 113–114). Appeals allowed; High Court judgment set aside; suit for specific performance dismissed; but "to do complete justice" (the site being admittedly worth Rs 2.5 crore and the plaintiff having paid Rs 50,000 decades earlier), the appellants to pay Rs 20,00,000 to the plaintiff's LRs within three months, failing which with 8% interest; parties to bear their costs.

Principles

  1. Basis: s.52 rests on equity, public policy and the necessity of final adjudication — not on notice (Bellamy v. Sabine; PC 34 IA 102) — and operates as an extension of res judicata.
  2. Effect: a transfer pendente lite is not void; it binds transferor and transferee; it is merely subordinated to the decree; the successful party may ask the court to ignore it.
  3. Notice, good faith and consideration are irrelevant to s.52 — unlike s.19(1)(b) SRA; a High Court errs in reasoning from constructive notice (s.3 TPA) to lis pendens.
  4. Party requirement is the sine qua non: a transfer by a person who was not a party at the time (e.g., an heir not brought on record, after the substitution period) is not hit — even though he later becomes a party. (Nallakumara — LR's transfer within the time for substitution — is confined to its facts.)
  5. Lis pendens helps only a plaintiff entitled to a decree: if the underlying agreement is unlawful (s.23 Contract Act — statutory non-alienation clauses in public allotments), no decree follows and the pendente-lite purchaser keeps the property; equitable compensation may be moulded under Art. 142.
Example. S agrees in 2015 to sell an Avas Vikas plot in Prayagraj to P in breach of a 10-year non-transfer condition; P sues in 2017. S dies in 2019; P brings only S's widow on record. In 2021 S's son (never impleaded) obtains the freehold deed and sells to T; P impleads son and T in 2023. Per G.T. Girish: (a) the son's sale is not hit by s.52 as he was not a party when he sold; (b) T's knowledge is irrelevant to s.52 either way; (c) P's agreement is unenforceable under s.23 Contract Act; P may at most recover his money with compensation.

13.1 Lis pendens and the doctrine of notice (Topic 3)

Under s.3 TPA a purchaser is fixed with constructive notice of what he would have learnt by reasonable inquiry — including, arguably, a pending suit if the record were searched. But s.52 does not depend on this: "it is immaterial whether the alienee pendente lite had, or had not, notice" (Mulla; G.T. Girish). Conversely, notice of a pending suit does not by itself bind a transferee from a non-party. Where s.52 is unavailable (e.g., the Govinda Pillai interval; a transfer before suit), the plaintiff may fall back on s.40 TPA / s.19(b) SRA (transferee with notice of a prior contract bound) or s.53.

13.2 Lis pendens and res judicata (s.11 CPC)

A decree binds "parties and persons claiming under them" (Explanation VI, s.11; s.47; O.XXI r.102). A transferee pendente lite claims under a party by a title arising after the suit began and is therefore bound as if he were a party — hence the description of lis pendens as an extension of res judicata (Manjeshwara Krishnaya, approved in G.T. Girish).

Everyday scenarios. (1) Flat buyers: before buying a flat in Naini, the buyer's lawyer searches the civil court records and the sub-registrar's index — because a pending title suit against the seller will bind the buyer regardless of the clean encumbrance certificate (s.52). (2) Builder–landowner disputes: a landowner sues the builder for cancellation of a development agreement; flats sold by the builder to third parties during the suit are subject to the decree — hence RERA promoters must disclose litigation (RERA 2016, s.4(2)(b)). (3) Bank auctions: an auction-purchaser under SARFAESI buying property that is the subject of a pending partition suit between the borrower and his siblings takes subject to that suit. 15. DU semester PYQs with model answers

Q1 (DU LL.B. May/June 2025, Q.8). "Proceedings before the Supreme Court are a continuation of those in the original suit and the principle of lis pendens shall apply to the proceedings." Elaborate upon the aforesaid statement, discussing in detail the concept of lis pendens.
Model answer.

Introduction. The statement is the holding of the Punjab & Haryana High Court in Dalip Kaur v. Jeewan Ram (AIR 1996 P&H 158), applying the doctrine of lis pendens codified in s.52 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 to an appeal before the Supreme Court under Art. 136 of the Constitution.

Concept of lis pendens. Lis = suit; pendens = pending. The maxim pendente lite nihil innovetur — nothing new should be introduced during litigation. The doctrine is "the jurisdiction, power or control that courts have, during the pendency of an action, over the property involved therein" (Govinda Pillai v. Aiyyappan Krishnan, AIR 1957 Ker 10, quoting American Jurisprudence). Its historical roots lie in Bacon's Twelfth Ordinance in Chancery. Its basis is not notice but necessity and public policy: Lord Cranworth in Bellamy v. Sabine (1857) — the law "does not allow litigant parties to give to others pending the litigation rights to the property in dispute so as to prejudice the opposite party … if this were not so there could be no certainty that the litigation would ever come to an end." The Supreme Court in G.T. Girish v. Y. Subba Raju (2022) reaffirmed that s.52 "is based on equity and public policy" and is an extension of res judicata.

Section 52. During the pendency in any court having authority in India of any suit or proceeding which is not collusive and in which any right to immovable property is directly and specifically in question, the property cannot be transferred or otherwise dealt with by any party so as to affect the rights of any other party under any decree or order therein, except under the authority of the court and on its terms. The Explanation (Act 20 of 1929) fixes pendency from the presentation of the plaint in a court of competent jurisdiction until final decree and complete satisfaction, or until execution becomes time-barred.

Essentials (Capsule): (i) pendency of a suit/proceeding; (ii) in a competent court — filing in a wrong court does not start pendency (Govinda Pillai: gift executed between return of plaint and re-presentation not hit); (iii) not collusive; (iv) right to immovable property directly and specifically in question (mortgage, partition, pre-emption, specific performance suits); (v) transfer by a party — not by a stranger or a person not yet impleaded (G.T. Girish); (vi) the transfer must affect the other party's rights. Effect: the transfer is not void but voidable at the instance of the affected party; transferee bound irrespective of notice or good faith; applies to voluntary and involuntary alienations; new rights pendente lite are struck even if dressed up as enforcement of an antecedent claim (Supreme General Films v. Brijnath Singhji Deo, AIR 1975 SC 1810 — lease by mortgagor during mortgage suit).

The statement — Dalip Kaur. Facts: Lachhman obtained a pre-emption decree (1983) and took possession; the vendees lost the first and second appeals but obtained special leave; the Supreme Court allowed their appeal (1989) and dismissed the suit. Meanwhile Lachhman had sold the land to Dalip Kaur and others. On the vendees' application for restitution under s.144 CPC the purchasers objected that they were bona fide purchasers and that lis pendens does not apply to Art. 136 proceedings, relying on Mewa Singh v. Jagir Singh. Held (Jawahar Lal Gupta J.): the Supreme Court sits at the head of the judicial pyramid with plenary appellate power under Arts. 136 and 142; a decree reversed by it entitles the parties to restitution; "there appears to be no warrant for the view that the proceedings are not a continuation of the original suit. The mere fact that the leave to appeal has to be obtained under the Constitution does not mean that the doctrine of lis pendens would not apply"; the purchasers, though not parties before the Supreme Court, were represented by their vendor; Mewa Singh turned on a compromise and its own facts and itself recognised that "the transferee during the pendency of a suit or other proceedings is bound by the result thereof." Therefore proceedings before the Supreme Court are a continuation of the original suit and both lis pendens and restitution apply.

Rationale. The Explanation's words "until … complete satisfaction" contemplate one continuous lis from plaint to satisfaction. An appeal is a rehearing and continuation of the suit; the trial decree merges in the appellate decree; if a transfer during an SLP escaped s.52, a decree-holder could render the Supreme Court's reversal nugatory by alienating pendente lite — precisely the mischief the doctrine prevents. The same logic covers execution and restitution proceedings, and O.XXI r.102 CPC bars a pendente-lite transferee from resisting delivery of possession.

Conclusion. The statement correctly states the law: pendency for s.52 spans the whole litigation, including constitutional appeals, until the decree is fully worked out; a purchaser from a party at any stage takes at his peril and can be dispossessed by restitution when the decree is reversed, his remedy lying only against his vendor.

Q2. "The doctrine of lis pendens is not founded on notice but on necessity." Discuss with reference to the basis, essentials and effect of s.52, and the decisions in Govinda Pillai and G.T. Girish.
Outline. Two theories (notice v. necessity) from 34 Am. Jur.; Bennett; Lord Cranworth in Bellamy v. Sabine; PC in Faiyaz Husain Khan; Capsule ("based upon expediency … necessity for final adjudication … prevention of multiplicity"). Consequences: notice/good faith/consideration irrelevant (G.T. Girish para 99, contrasting s.19(1)(b) SRA; High Court's error in using constructive notice); the doctrine does not extend to periods when no competent court is seised even if the defendant knew of the plaintiff's claim (Govinda Pillai); the transfer is not void — valid inter partes, subject to decree; extension of res judicata (Manjeshwara Krishnaya). Note the Bombay/Gujarat registration model and England as alternatives based on notice. Conclude: the necessity basis explains both the rigour (binds innocent purchasers) and the limits (party, competent court, specific property) of the rule.
Q3 (problem). M mortgaged his cinema hall to R in 2015. R sued for sale in 2018. In 2020, M executed a registered ten-year lease of the hall in favour of T, who had been running the cinema since 2005 under an unregistered lease that expired in 2010, and who had obtained a compromise decree against M in 2019 in a suit for specific performance of a 2012 letter promising a lease. R's suit was decreed in 2022 and the hall is to be sold. T claims that the auction-purchaser must respect his lease. Decide.
Answer. Facts mirror Supreme General Films Exchange v. Brijnath Singhji Deo (SC 1975). The mortgage suit (2018–2022) put a right to the hall directly and specifically in question; M is a party; the 2020 lease is a transfer pendente lite creating new rights: the 2005 unregistered lease (s.17 Registration Act; s.107 TPA) never created a valid interest beyond a tenancy from month to month and had expired in 2010; the 2012 letter and the 2019 compromise decree in a suit to which R was not a party cannot create an antecedent right binding R — the Supreme Court treated such a compromise as a "device" and rejected Bishan Singh v. Khazan Singh reliance. s.65A (mortgagor's leases limited to 3 years) does not displace s.52; if anything it too invalidates the lease as against R; s.64 CPC may also apply if the hall was attached. Result: the lease is subject to R's decree; the auction-purchaser takes free of it; T must vacate, with his remedy in damages against M.
Q4 (problem). A filed a suit against B for possession of a house in the court of a Civil Judge, which returned the plaint on 5 January for want of pecuniary jurisdiction. A presented it in the District Court on 25 January. On 15 January B gifted the house to his son C. Is the gift hit by lis pendens? Would it matter that C knew of the suit?
Answer. Not hit. Under the Explanation to s.52 pendency commences only on presentation of the plaint in a court of competent jurisdiction — 25 January. On 15 January no suit was pending in such a court (Govinda Pillai Gopala Pillai v. Aiyyappan Krishnan, Ker 1957, following Gouri Dutt and Nathusingh, and holding Ma Than (Rang 1927) no longer good law after the 1929 amendment; analogy with s.14 Limitation Act). C's knowledge is irrelevant to s.52 because the doctrine rests on necessity, not notice — knowledge neither brings a case within s.52 nor takes it out (G.T. Girish). A's remedies: implead C (O.I r.10) and seek relief against him on the merits of title; the gift being gratuitous, s.53 may be pleaded if made to defeat A as a creditor, and s.41 cannot protect a donee. Result: gift valid so far as s.52 is concerned.
Q5 (short notes). (a) Collusive suit; (b) Commencement and conclusion of pendency; (c) Voluntary and involuntary alienations under s.52; (d) Effect of transfer pendente lite.
Points. (a) Secret arrangement, no real contest, to defeat a third party — s.52 inapplicable (1929 words "which is not collusive"; Capsule A–B–D example); distinguish fraudulent-but-contested suit (covered) and compromise (covered unless collusive). (b) Explanation: plaint in competent court → final decree + complete satisfaction / execution time-barred; wrong court (Govinda Pillai), rejected plaint, appeals and SLP (Dalip Kaur), execution and restitution. (c) Voluntary — sale, mortgage, lease, gift, exchange, charge, compromise (Supreme General Films); involuntary — court sales, insolvency; both subject to the lis; court-authorised transfers exempt. (d) Not void; valid inter partes; subject to decree; transferee bound regardless of notice; cannot obstruct execution (O.XXI r.102); restitution (s.144); may be impleaded (O.XXII r.10); helps only the party who succeeds (G.T. Girish).
Q6. Distinguish lis pendens (s.52) from fraudulent transfer (s.53) and from the rule protecting transferees from ostensible owners (s.41).
Points. Use the Part 13 table: basis (necessity v. anti-fraud v. estoppel); trigger (pending suit v. intent to defeat creditors v. consent of real owner); notice/good faith (irrelevant v. relevant v. essential); effect (subject to decree v. voidable by creditors v. valid against real owner); beneficiaries. Illustrate: a sale pendente lite to a bona fide purchaser is bound by the decree though it would be protected under s.41/s.53.
