1. Concept of “Property”

The Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (TPA) nowhere defines “property”. Judicial and juristic writing treats it as the most comprehensive of all legal terms: it denotes every possible interest which a person can have in a thing, and in Indian usage it is used in three senses:

  1. Property as the thing — the land, the house, the car (the res).
  2. Property as ownership — the aggregate of rights over the thing (the “bundle of rights”).
  3. Property as a particular interest in the thing — e.g. the mortgagee’s interest, the lessee’s interest, a right of way, an equity of redemption.

Ownership as a “bundle of rights” (Salmond / Austin)

Ownership is not one right but a bundle: (i) right to possess (jus possidendi); (ii) right to use and enjoy (jus utendi / fruendi); (iii) right to exclude others; (iv) right to alienate / destroy (jus disponendi / abutendi); (v) right of indeterminate duration and residuary character. These sticks may be vested in different persons at the same time — e.g. in a usufructuary mortgage, the mortgagee has possession and enjoyment while the mortgagor retains the residuary ownership and the equity of redemption. Section 6 TPA (“property of any kind may be transferred…”) uses “property” in this generic sense, so that even a limited interest (a lease, a mortgagee’s interest, a life interest) is “property” capable of transfer.

Why the “concept of property” matters for Topic 1. The Shantabai and Ananda Behera line of cases turns on the difference between (a) a purely contractual right to obtain future goods (a right in personam — not “property” against the State, and if the State is not a party, the remedy is only damages against the grantor), and (b) a proprietary interest in land — a right in rem, which is “immovable property”, must be created by a registered instrument, and (before 1978) was protected under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The whole Article 32 litigation in these cases failed because the petitioners could not show that they had property as opposed to a contract.

2. Classification: Movable vs. Immovable — why the distinction matters

English law classifies property into real and personal — a historical classification based on the remedy (real action for recovery of the thing itself vs. personal action for damages). Because this classification produced anomalies (a 999-year lease of land is “personal” property), the Indian legislature adopted the physical, civil-law classification of movable and immovable property. The TPA itself governs transfers of both, but Chapters III–VIII deal only with specific transfers (sale, mortgage, lease, exchange, gift) of immovable property, and sale of movables is governed by the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.

3. Statutory framework — all the definitions, side by side

The TPA gives only a negative definition of immovable property and no definition of movable property. Courts therefore read the TPA together with the General Clauses Act and the Registration Act (the Supreme Court did precisely this in Shantabai, Titaghur and, through the A.P. High Court, in Bamadev).

Transfer of Property Act, 1882 — s. 3 (Interpretation clause) “immoveable property” does not include standing timber, growing crops or grass; … “attached to the earth” means— (a) rooted in the earth, as in the case of trees and shrubs; (b) imbedded in the earth, as in the case of walls or buildings; or (c) attached to what is so imbedded for the permanent beneficial enjoyment of that to which it is attached.
General Clauses Act, 1897 — s. 3 (26) “immovable property” shall include land, benefits to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth, or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth; (36) “movable property” shall mean property of every description, except immovable property. s. 4 — the definitions in s.3 apply to all Central Acts made after 3 January 1868 (and, by s.4(1), the definitions of “immovable property” and “movable property” apply also to the TPA) unless there is anything repugnant in the subject or context.
Registration Act, 1908 — s. 2 (6) “immovable property” includes land, buildings, hereditary allowances, rights to ways, lights, ferries, fisheries or any other benefit to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth, or permanently fastened to anything which is attached to the earth, but not standing timber, growing crops nor grass; (9) “movable property” includes standing timber, growing crops and grass, fruit upon and juice in trees, and property of every other description, except immovable property.
Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — s. 2(7) “goods” means every kind of movable property other than actionable claims and money; and includes stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale. Indian Penal Code, 1860 — s. 22 (now BNS 2023, s.2(21)) “movable property” is intended to include corporeal property of every description, except land and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything which is attached to the earth. Indian Easements Act, 1882 — s. 4 An easement is a right which the owner or occupier of certain land possesses, as such, for the beneficial enjoyment of that land, to do and continue to do something, or to prevent and continue to prevent something being done, in or upon, or in respect of, certain other land not his own.

Rule of priority between definitions (from Shantabai, para 23). Vivian Bose J.: “In the absence of a special definition, the general definition must prevail. Therefore, trees (except standing timber) are immoveable property.” In other words: where the TPA speaks (e.g. standing timber is excluded) the TPA governs; where the TPA is silent, the General Clauses Act fills the gap. Trees are therefore immovable property under the TPA because of the GCA — a point examiners like.

4. Anatomy of “Immovable Property” — the three components

5. Component I — Land

“Land” in its legal sense is far wider than the surface. It includes:

  • the surface of the earth, whether or not covered by water (so a tank, pond or lake-bed is land — Ananda Behera concerned Chilka Lake);
  • the column of space above the surface (cujus est solum ejus est usque ad coelum), subject to statutory regulation (aviation, building bye-laws);
  • the ground beneath the surface — sub-soil, minerals, coal (subject to the State’s statutory rights over mines and minerals);
  • all objects on or under the surface in their natural state — rocks, natural water, trees (subject to the standing-timber exception);
  • all objects placed on or under it by human agency with the intention of permanent annexation — buildings, walls, fences, tube-wells (this is where the doctrine of fixtures enters).
Example. A owns a plot on which there is a mango orchard, a bore-well and a boundary wall. A sale of the “plot” carries the trees (rooted), the wall (imbedded), the bore-well pump (attached for permanent beneficial enjoyment) and the sub-soil water rights, without separate mention (s.8 TPA). A cannot afterwards say “I sold only the earth”.

6. Component II — “Benefits to arise out of land” / Profit à prendre

A “benefit to arise out of land” is an interest in land and is therefore immovable property (GCA s.3(26); Reg. Act s.2(6) — “rights to ways, lights, ferries, fisheries or any other benefit to arise out of land”). The Registration Act lists illustrations; the courts have added many more. This is the head under which the right in Shantabai, Ananda Behera, Mahadeo and Titaghur was classified.

6.1 Meaning of profit à prendre (Halsbury, 4th Edn., Vol. 14, paras 240–242 — quoted in Titaghur, para 98)

“A profit à prendre is a right to take something off another person’s land. It may be more fully defined as a right to enter another’s land and to take some profit of the soil, or a portion of the soil itself, for the use of the owner of the right. … A profit à prendre is an interest in land, and for this reason any disposition of it must be in writing. A profit à prendre which gives a right to participate in a portion only of some specified produce of the land is just as much an interest in the land as a right to take the whole of that produce.”

Subject-matter (Halsbury para 242): animals including fish and fowl on the land; vegetable matter growing or deposited on the land by an agency other than man; any part of the soil itself including mineral accretions. Recognised English profits: to take acorns and beech mast, fern, heather, turf and peat, boughs of growing trees, rushes, freshwater fish, stone, sand and shingle, ice from a canal, pasture, shooting pheasants. (No right to take sea-coal from the foreshore.)

Servitude — the wider concept. Titaghur (para 99): a profit à prendre is a servitude because it burdens the ownership of land by separating off fragments of the right of ownership to be enjoyed by persons other than the owner. “Servitude” includes both easements and profits. Real-life example. The Forest Department of Uttarakhand grants XYZ Pulp Ltd. the exclusive right, for 15 years, to enter a reserved forest, cut bamboo as it matures, build depots and roads, and pay a minimum annual royalty regardless of quantity cut. This is a profit à prendre (benefit arising out of land) — immovable property, not a sale of goods; the State cannot levy purchase tax on the royalty (Titaghur). Contrast: a contractor buys “the 2,000 sal trees marked in Coupe No. 7, to be felled and removed this season” — this is a sale of goods (things attached to land agreed to be severed before sale — SGA s.2(7)); purchase tax is leviable.

7. Component III — “Attached to the earth” & the Doctrine of Fixtures

The expression “attached to the earth” is the TPA’s codification of what English law calls the law of fixtures. A fixture is a chattel (movable) which has been so annexed to land or to a building that in law it becomes part of the land — quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit (“whatever is planted in the soil belongs to the soil”) and quicquid inaedificatur solo, solo cedit (“whatever is built into the soil belongs to the soil”). If the attachment is merely for the beneficial enjoyment of the chattel itself, then it remains a chattel, even though fixed for the time being so that it may be enjoyed. The question must in each case be decided according to the circumstances.”

8. The three exclusions — Standing timber, Growing crops, Grass

All three are physically rooted in the earth; the legislature nevertheless treats them as movables because they are, by their nature, destined to be severed from the land — their value lies in their severance, not in their continued growth. The Registration Act s.2(9) also adds “fruit upon and juice in trees”.

8.1 Standing timber vs. tree — the Shantabai test

“Timber” = “wood suitable for building houses, bridges, ships etc., whether on the tree or cut and seasoned” (Webster’s, quoted by Bose J.). Note the exclusion is of “standing timber”, not of “timber trees”. Hence a tree becomes standing timber (movable) only when three conditions coincide:

  1. it is a timber tree (species whose wood is used for construction — teak, sal, sheesham, neem, babul; not a fruit-bearing tree like mango, mahua, date, jackfruit grown for fruit);
  2. it has reached a state fit to be used as timber if cut now; and
  3. the parties intend it to be felled at a reasonably early date, so that it can “already be looked upon as timber for all practical purposes even though it is still standing”.

Rationale (Bose J., paras 25–27): a living tree draws nourishment from the soil; if it is to be cut soon, the nourishment it takes is negligible and can be ignored; if it is to be left standing for an appreciable time so that the grantee benefits from further growth, the grantee is taking a profit from the soil itself — and that is an interest in land. Bose J. candidly said the rule is “grounded not so much on logical abstractions as on sound and practical common sense” developed “empirically from instance to instance”.

Mulla (4th Edn., quoted in Shantabai): p.16 — “Standing timber are trees fit for use for building or repairing houses. This is an exception to the general rule that growing trees are immoveable property.” p.21 — “Trees and shrubs may be sold apart from the land, to be cut and removed as wood, and in that case they are moveable property. But if the transfer includes the right to fell the trees for a term of years, so that the transferee derives a benefit from further growth, the transfer is treated as one of immoveable property.” English law (p.21) — “an unconditional sale of growing trees to be cut by the purchaser has been held to be a sale of an interest in land; but not so if it is stipulated that they are to be removed as soon as possible.”
Marshall v. Green (1875) 1 CPD 35 — the English formulation (quoted in Titaghur, para 106): “Wherever at the time of the contract it is contemplated that the purchaser should derive a benefit from the further growth of the thing sold, from further vegetation and from the nutriment to be afforded by the land, the contract is to be considered as for an interest in land; but where the process of vegetation is over, or the parties agree that the thing sold shall be immediately withdrawn from the land, the land is to be considered as a mere warehouse of the thing sold, and the contract is for goods.”

8.2 Growing crops

“Growing crops” means crops standing in the field — wheat, barley, paddy, sugarcane, indigo, potato, and also creepers such as pan (betel) and grape vines. Though a collection of plants rooted in earth, they have no existence apart from their produce and every crop is bound to be cut when ripe. They are movable. But a right to grow crops on land in future years (e.g. a cultivating lease) is a benefit arising out of land — immovable. In English law crops were split into fructus industriales (produced by annual labour — chattels) and fructus naturales (natural growth — part of the soil until severed); the Supreme Court in Mahadeo and Titaghur (para 53) held that this distinction does not exist in Indian law; the only question is whether the transaction concerns goods/movables or immovables.

8.3 Grass

Grass rooted in the earth is movable because its only use is as fodder — to be cut or grazed; no further vegetative growth is intended. However, a right to cut grass on another’s land over a period is a right exercised upon the land — a benefit arising out of land — and hence immovable (Seeni Chettiar v. Santhanathan Chettiar, (1897) ILR 20 Mad 58 (FB) — grant for four-plus years of produce of trees on a tank bank, grass and reeds: not a lease (no exclusive possession) but an interest in land requiring registration). Exam trap: “grass = movable; right to cut grass = immovable”.

9. Doctrine of Fixtures — English law vs. Indian law

9.1 English law: the two tests of Holland v. Hodgson (1872) LR 7 CP 328

Looms bolted to the stone floor of a worsted mill by nails driven into wooden beams were held to be fixtures that passed with a mortgage of the mill. Blackburn J.:

“The general maxim of the law is, that what is annexed to the land becomes part of the land; but it is very difficult, if not impossible, to say with precision what constitutes an annexation sufficient for this purpose. It is a question which must depend on the circumstances of each case, and mainly on two circumstances, as indicating the intention, viz. the degree of annexation and the object of the annexation.” … “An article which is affixed to the land even slightly is to be considered as part of the land, unless the circumstances are such as to show that it was intended all along to continue a chattel, the onus lying on those who contend that it is a chattel.” Conversely, an article resting on the land by its own weight is not part of the land unless the circumstances show it was intended to be — the onus then lying on those who assert it is part of the land.

Test 1 — Degree / mode of annexation

  • Rests by its own weight → prima facie chattel (a stack of stones; a “Dutch barn” resting on brick pillars).
  • Affixed even slightly by nails, bolts, cement → prima facie part of land.
  • Can it be removed without substantial damage to itself or the land?
But a dry-stone wall built of unmortared stones is part of the land; blocks of stone piled in a builder’s yard are not — showing that degree alone is not decisive.

Test 2 — Object / purpose of annexation

  • To effect a permanent improvement of the land/building → fixture.
  • For the better enjoyment of the chattel as a chattel (temporary, ornamental) → remains a chattel.
  • Who annexed it? An owner is presumed to intend permanence; a tenant or temporary occupier is presumed to intend removal.
In modern English law the object test dominates (Leigh v. Taylor; Elitestone v. Morris [1997]).

  • Trade fixtures — things fixed by a tenant for the purpose of his trade (engines, vats, shop counters) may be removed by him during the term.

  • Agricultural fixtures — by statute (Agricultural Holdings Acts).

9.3 Indian position — no absolute rule; the maxim has “no strict application”

Because India has statutory definitions (GCA, TPA, Registration Act), the Indian courts treat the English tests only as guidelines. The A.P. High Court in Bamadev (para 12, 16) said in terms: “The English law of fixtures has no strict application to the law in India relating to machinery attached to the earth … in view of the statutory definitions”, and that “there is no statutory test or guideline having universal application”. Three concrete departures:

  1. No quicquid plantatur as an inflexible rule. The Privy Council in Narayan Das Khettry v. Jatindra Nath Roy Chowdhury (1927) 54 IA 218 observed that there is nothing in the laws or customs of India to show any trace of the absolute rule that whatever is affixed or built on the soil becomes part of it; each case is decided on its circumstances. A person in lawful occupation (not a trespasser) who builds on another’s land has two rights: (i) to remove the structure on leaving, restoring the land; or (ii) if the owner retains it, to compensation for its value.
  2. Section 108(h) TPA — a lessee “may even after the determination of the lease remove, at any time whilst he is in possession of the property leased but not afterwards, all things which he has attached to the earth: provided he leaves the property in the state in which he received it” — a statutory version of the tenant’s-fixtures rule. See also s.108(i) (lessee’s right to growing crops on determination of a lease of uncertain duration) and s.51 (bona fide transferee who makes improvements — option of the evictor to pay compensation or sell).
  3. Section 8 TPA — on a transfer, land passes with “all things attached to the earth”; machinery attached to earth passes with its movable parts; a house passes with “locks, keys, bars, doors, windows and all other things provided for permanent use therewith”.
Modern excise-law cousin (for a holistic view). The same “attached to earth” problem arises in Central Excise / GST — whether a plant erected at site is “goods”. Sirpur Paper Mills v. CCE (1998) 1 SCC 400 (paper machine attached to earth for operational efficiency, removable and saleable — goods) was distinguished in Duncans; later, Triveni Engineering v. CCE (2000) 7 SCC 29 and CCE v. Solid & Correct Engineering (2010) 5 SCC 122 refined the test: attachment merely for wobble-free operation does not make a plant immovable, but a plant that cannot be removed without dismantling and is intended to be permanent is immovable. These cases apply the very tests from Bamadev/Duncans.

11. Movable property

The TPA does not define movable property. It is the residuary category: “property of every description except immovable property” (GCA s.3(36)); the Registration Act s.2(9) expressly adds standing timber, growing crops, grass, fruit upon and juice in trees. The Sale of Goods Act s.2(7) defines “goods” as movable property other than actionable claims and money, and — importantly for Titaghur — includes “things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale”. Thus a standing tree that is agreed to be severed before sale is not only movable but “goods” taxable by the State.

Examples of movable property: furniture, vehicles, machinery not permanently fixed, standing timber, growing crops, grass, fruit and juice on trees, stocks and shares, government securities, decrees, royalty, right to recover maintenance, copyright/patent, a hut resting on the ground, a road-roller, an anchor, cinema equipment in a touring talkies.

CASE 1 — Smt. Shantabai v. State of Bombay

AIR 1958 SC 532 : (1959) SCR 265 · Supreme Court · Constitution Bench of five Judges (S.R. Das C.J., Bhagwati, S.K. Das, A.K. Sarkar and Vivian Bose JJ.) · Writ Petition under Article 32 · DU Case Material pp. 1–5 (Bose J.’s judgment is what is prescribed)

1Background & Facts

  • The petitioner Shantabai’s husband, Balirambhau Doye, was the Zamindar of Pandharpur (Bhandara district, then Madhya Pradesh / C.P. & Berar; later Bombay State after reorganisation).
  • On 26 April 1948 he executed an unregistered document which “called itself a lease” in favour of his wife. It gave her the right to enter upon certain areas of the zamindari to cut and take out bamboos, fuel wood and teak, subject to restrictions: certain trees could not be felled; teak trees under 1½ feet girth were not to be cut (cl. 5); clause 4 spoke of a first, second and third cutting; clause 8 forbade re-cutting an area already worked, and required cutting “right at the land surface” without leaving furrows or holes; clause 3 reserved certain “pasas” (coupes 16, 17, 18) for the proprietor’s own cutting; clause 7 said she was to “conduct yourself only as a lease holder” and, in the absence of the Malik, look after the estate as manager.
  • Term: 26 April 1948 to 26 December 1960 (about 12½ years). Consideration: Rs 26,000.
  • She worked the forests till 1950. Then the Madhya Pradesh Abolition of Proprietary Rights (Estates, Mahals and Alienated Lands) Act, 1950 came into force (26 January 1951); under s.3 all proprietary rights vested in the State from the notified date — 31 March 1951. She was stopped from cutting trees.
  • She applied to the Deputy Commissioner, Bhandara under s.6(2) of the Act to validate the lease. On 16 Aug 1955 he held s.6(2) inapplicable (it covered transfers after 16 March 1950 only), but that the Act did not affect transfers before that date; he found the lease genuine and ordered that she be allowed to work the forests subject to the lease and the rules under s.218-A, C.P. Land Revenue Act.
  • She had claimed compensation for being kept out from 1951–55 but gave it up on the understanding that she would be allowed to work the forests for the rest of the term.
  • The Divisional Forest Officer did not give effective permission (only a letter that her claim “was being examined”). She then entered the forest and started cutting. On 19 March 1956 the DFO proceeded against her for unlawful cutting, cancelled her name and forfeited the cut material.
  • Her representation to Government (27 Sept 1956) produced nothing; on 26 Aug 1957 she filed a petition under Article 32 alleging violation of her fundamental rights under Arts. 19(1)(f) and (g) and 31(1).

2Issues

  1. What is the true nature of the deed of 26 April 1948 — a contract simpliciter, a lease, a licence coupled with a grant, a transfer of proprietary interest? Does it relate to movable or immovable property?
  2. Is a right to enter land and cut and carry away trees a benefit arising out of land (immovable) or a sale of standing timber (movable)?
  3. What is the distinction between “standing timber” and a “tree”?
  4. Did the unregistered deed convey any right or interest which could be enforced as a fundamental right under Article 32?

3Arguments

Petitioner (Shantabai)

  • The grant was of standing timber — movable property under s.3 TPA and s.2(6) Registration Act — so no registration was needed and the unregistered deed was fully effective.
  • Alternatively, her rights flowed from a contract/licence, and on the authority of Chhotabhai Jethabai Patel v. State of M.P., AIR 1953 SC 108 (three Judges) a writ lay to restrain the State from interfering with such rights, since they were not “proprietary rights” that vested under the Abolition Act.
  • The Deputy Commissioner had already held the lease genuine and unaffected by the Act; the forest officers’ action was illegal.

Respondent (State)

  • The deed conveyed an interest in immovable property — a benefit arising out of land — worth Rs 26,000; being unregistered it conveyed no title or interest (Registration Act ss.17, 49).
  • Even if it conveyed a share of the proprietor’s rights, those rights vested in the State under s.3 of the 1950 Act.
  • If it was a mere contract, the State was not a party; refusal to honour it is at most a breach of contract, not a breach of a fundamental right — Ananda Behera v. State of Orissa, AIR 1956 SC 17 (five Judges).
  • If it was a licence, the licence ended with the extinction of the licensor’s title.

4Decision & Reasoning — Vivian Bose J. (paras 14–31)

  1. Label not conclusive (para 15). “The document calls itself a ‘lease deed’, but that is not conclusive because the true nature of a document cannot be disguised by labelling it something else.”
  2. Clause 7 confers no proprietary right (para 17) — it merely recites the wife’s existing role as manager in the husband’s absence and restricts her from altering the deed; “there are no words of transfer or conveyance”.
  3. Not a lease but a licence coupled with a grant / profit à prendre (para 18). Clause 5 — “Only the lease of the forest woods is given to you” — shows the deed “confers no rights of enjoyment in the land”. It “only confers a right to enter on the lands in order to cut down certain kinds of trees and carry away the wood.” Following Chhotabhai and Ananda Behera, such a transaction is “a licence to enter on the land coupled with a grant to cut certain trees on it and carry away the wood. In England it is a profit à prendre because it is a grant of the produce of the soil like grass, or turves or trees.” Key distinction: “In a lease, one enjoys the property but has no right to take it away. In a profit à prendre one has a licence to enter on the land, not for the purpose of enjoying it, but for removing something from it, namely, a part of the produce of the soil.” (It is not a “transfer of a right to enjoy the immoveable property” within s.105 TPA.)
  4. Article 32 requires a fundamental right (paras 19–21). Insofar as the claim rests on contract simpliciter, it fails on the reasoning of Ananda Behera: a right to obtain future goods under a contract is “a purely personal right arising out of a contract to which the State … is not a party”; refusal to perform may be breach of contract but “cannot be regarded as a breach of any fundamental right”. To invoke Art. 19(1)(f) or 31(1) “something more must be disclosed, namely, a right to property of which one is the owner or in which one has an interest apart from a purely contractual right.” Chhotabhai (3 Judges) must yield to Ananda Behera (5 Judges), which “lays down the law aright”.
  5. Benefit arising out of land (para 21–22). “A right to enter on land for the purpose of cutting and carrying away timber standing on it is a benefit that arises out of land. There is no difference there between the English and the Indian law.” Under s.3(26) GCA it is immovable property “because it is a benefit that arises out of the land and also because trees are attached to the earth”. But s.3 TPA and s.2(6) Registration Act say standing timber is not immovable property. Which prevails?
  6. Interplay of definitions (para 23). Trees are immovable property because rooted in the earth; the TPA excludes only “standing timber”; hence “trees attached to earth (except standing timber) are immoveable property, even under the Transfer of Property Act, because of Section 3(26) of the General Clauses Act. In the absence of a special definition, the general definition must prevail.”
  7. Standing timber vs. tree (paras 24–27). The exclusion is of “standing timber”, not of “timber trees”. Timber = wood suitable for building houses, bridges, ships etc. Therefore “standing timber” must be (a) a tree in a state fit for such purposes and (b) a tree “meant to be converted into timber so shortly that it can already be looked upon as timber for all practical purposes even though it is still standing. If not, it is still a tree because, unlike timber, it will continue to draw sustenance from the soil.” The legal basis of the rule “is that trees that are not cut continue to draw nourishment from the soil and that the benefit of this goes to the grantee”; if felled at a reasonably early date the nourishment drawn is negligible. The rule is grounded on “sound and practical common-sense”, having grown “empirically from instance to instance”.
  8. Application to the deed (paras 28–29). The grant is for twelve years; “trees that will be fit for cutting twelve years hence will not be fit for felling now. Therefore, it is not a mere sale of the trees as wood. It is more … not just a right to cut a tree but also to derive a profit from the soil itself, in the shape of the nourishment in the soil that goes into the tree and makes it grow till it is of a size and age fit for felling as timber.” Clause 5 (no felling of teak under 1½ ft until it reaches that girth) and clause 4 (first, second, third cuttings) confirm this; even trees fit to cut now need not be cut — they may be left standing “till such time as the petitioner chooses to fell them”, so “they are to be regarded as trees and not as timber”.
  9. Result (para 30). Trees that were standing timber at the date of the deed (by size, girth and intention to fell early) would be movable; but “the remaining trees that are also covered by the grant will be immoveable property, and as the total value is Rs 26,000, the deed requires registration. Being unregistered, it passes no title or interest and, therefore, as in Ananda Behera case the petitioner has no fundamental right which she can enforce.”
  10. The majority (para 31 and as summarised in Titaghur, para 110). S.R. Das C.J. and the other three Judges preferred to leave open whether the deed was a lease or a licence coupled with a grant, because on every possible view the petition failed: (i) if it conveyed a share of proprietary rights — none passed without registration, and in any case such rights vested in the State under s.3; (ii) if it was a licence coupled with a grant — the right was a profit à prendre, an interest in land requiring registration, so nothing passed (Ananda Behera); (iii) if a purely personal contractual right — the State had neither acquired nor taken possession of the contract; she could sue the grantor (or the State, if bound) for damages. The majority added that had this aspect been noticed in Chhotabhai, “we have no doubt that case would not have been decided in the way it was done.” Petition dismissed with costs (unanimous).

5Principles evolved

  1. Nomenclature is irrelevant; the court looks at the substance of the document.
  2. Lease vs. profit à prendre: a lease is a transfer of a right to enjoy land with exclusive possession; a right to enter land only to remove produce is a licence coupled with a grant — a profit à prendre.
  3. A profit à prendre is a “benefit to arise out of land” and hence immovable property (GCA s.3(26)) requiring a registered instrument if its value is ≥ Rs 100.
  4. Standing timber test: a tree is standing timber (movable) only if fit for timber and intended to be felled reasonably early; where the grantee is to benefit from further growth/nourishment from the soil over a term, it is a tree — immovable.
  5. Priority of definitions: the special TPA exclusion governs standing timber; otherwise the GCA general definition supplies the meaning of immovable property for the TPA.
  6. Article 32: a contractual right to future goods is not “property” for Arts. 19(1)(f)/31; an unregistered deed of immovable property gives no enforceable fundamental right.
  7. Precedent: a larger Bench (Ananda Behera, 5 JJ.) prevails over a smaller (Chhotabhai, 3 JJ.).

6Note on the status of Bose J.’s opinion

In Titaghur (para 111) the State argued that Bose J.’s analysis was not “the judgment of the Court” because the majority did not decide the nature of the document. Madon J. accepted that it would be difficult to call it the decision of the Court as such, but held that Bose J.’s judgment is a “considered and reasoned judgment” and its analysis of standing timber vs. tree has been consistently followed (and was expressly adopted in Titaghur itself, para 51). It is therefore treated as authoritative on the standing-timber point.

CASE 2 — State of Orissa v. Titaghur Paper Mills Co. Ltd.

AIR 1985 SC 1293 : 1985 Supp SCC 280 · Supreme Court · Three Judges (D.P. Madon J. for the Court, with Y.V. Chandrachud C.J. and A.N. Sen J.) · Civil Appeals 219–220 of 1982 from Orissa High Court · DU Case Material pp. 6–24

1Background & Facts

  • On 23 May 1977 the Orissa Finance Department issued two notifications under the Orissa Sales Tax Act, 1947: SRO 372/1977 (under s.3-B) making “bamboos agreed to be severed” and “standing trees agreed to be severed” liable to purchase tax w.e.f. 1 June 1977, and SRO 373/1977 (under proviso to s.5(1)) fixing the rate at 10%. After the Orissa Sales Tax (Amendment) Ordinance 1977 (later Amendment Act 1978), two fresh notifications of 29 Dec 1977 — SRO 900 and 901/1977 — re-enacted the levy w.e.f. 1 Jan 1978 (Entries 2 and 17 of the Schedule).
  • 209 writ petitions under Art. 226 challenged these “impugned provisions”. Petitioners fell into two classes: (i) holders of the “Bamboo Contract” — agreements with the State “for the purpose of felling, cutting, obtaining and removing bamboos from forest areas for the purpose of converting the bamboo into paper pulp or for purposes connected with the manufacture of paper”; and (ii) holders of “Timber Contracts” — agreements for the purchase of standing trees for a particular felling season.
  • Salient terms of the Bamboo Contract (paras 102–105): described as “a grant of exclusive right and licence to fell, cut, obtain and remove bamboos”; long term — 14, 13 and 11 years for different contract areas, with an option to renew for 12 more years; covered bamboos existing and bamboos to grow in future; royalty unrelated to the quantity cut, with a minimum royalty always exceeding the royalty due on bamboos actually cut; rights to use all lands, roads and streams within and outside the contract areas for ingress/egress; rights to make dams, cut canals, water-courses, irrigation works, roads, bridges, buildings, tramways; right to use timber and other forest produce at schedule rates; right to extract fuel for employees’ domestic use; and under clause XXV the Government was bound, if required, to lease sites within the contract areas for storehouses, sheds, depots, bungalows, staff offices, etc.
  • The Orissa High Court (19 Sept 1979, Titaghur Paper Mills v. State of Orissa, (1980) Tax LR 1643) allowed all petitions and quashed the notifications, holding that they taxed an agreement to sell rather than a sale, and, as to the Bamboo Contract, taxed a profit à prendre. The State appealed.

2Issues

  1. Are the impugned notifications constitutionally valid — i.e., do they tax “goods” within Entry 54, List II?
  2. Is the Bamboo Contract a contract of sale of goods (bamboos agreed to be severed) or a grant of a profit à prendre — a benefit to arise out of land — i.e., immovable property?
  3. Can the Bamboo Contract be bifurcated into a sale of existing bamboos and a sale of future goods?
  4. What is the correct method of interpreting such documents — the whole document, or only clauses relevant to the statute in issue?
  5. What is the status of Chhotabhai (1953) and Orient Paper Mills (1977)?

3Arguments

Appellant (State of Orissa)

  • Under SGA s.2(7) and s.2(d) Orissa Act, “goods” includes “things attached to or forming part of the land which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale”; bamboos and standing trees agreed to be severed are therefore goods and their purchase is taxable.
  • The Bamboo Contract is in substance a sale of bamboos with an ancillary licence to enter; at worst it is a composite contract — one part a sale of existing bamboos (goods) and the other a sale of future goods — and the former is taxable.
  • Relied on Chhotabhai Jethabai Patel v. State of M.P., AIR 1953 SC 108 (forest contracts are mere licences, not interests in land) and State of M.P. v. Orient Paper Mills, AIR 1977 SC 687 (Krishna Iyer J. — a 20-year bamboo “lease” held to result in a sale of trees after severance; “royalty” is “a feudalistic euphemism for price”).
  • Bose J.’s opinion in Shantabai was not the judgment of the Court and is not authority that such a document is a profit à prendre.
  • Mahadeo v. State of Bombay is distinguishable on its terms.

Respondents (Titaghur Paper Mills; M/s M.M. Khara)

  • The notifications sought to create and tax a new class of goods unknown to law and to tax an agreement to sell, beyond the State’s legislative competence.
  • The Bamboo Contract confers a benefit to arise out of land — a profit à prendre — an interest in immovable property; a State cannot levy purchase tax on it (Entry 54 covers only goods).
  • The contract is integral and indivisible; its long duration, coverage of future growth, minimum royalty and ancillary rights over land show it is not a sale of goods.
  • Relied on Ananda Behera, Shantabai, Mahadeo, Yakinuddin — larger Benches which had overruled Chhotabhai.

4Decision & Reasoning (Madon J.)

  1. Definitions surveyed (paras 49–51). The Court set out “goods” (SGA s.2(7); Orissa Act s.2(d)), “movable property” (GCA s.3(36); Orissa GCA; Reg. Act s.2(9)) and “immovable property” (TPA s.3; GCA s.3(26); Reg. Act s.2(6)) and “attached to the earth” (TPA s.3). The TPA clause “is strictly speaking not a definition … it does not tell us what immovable property is but merely tells us what it does not include”. Conclusion: “while trees rooted in the earth are immovable property … if they are standing timber [they] are moveable property, [and] trees (including bamboos) rooted in the earth which are agreed to be severed before sale or under the contract of sale are not only moveable property but also goods.” The Court adopted Bose J.’s standing-timber analysis: “trees which are ready to be felled would be standing timber and, therefore, moveable property.”
  2. English fructus naturales / industriales distinction (paras 52–53) — explained (Statute of Frauds 1677 ss.4, 17; SGA 1893 s.62; SGA 1979 s.61) and held, following Mahadeo, not to exist in Indian law. In India the only question is whether the transaction concerns goods/movable or immovable property, and its importance is twofold: (1) registration (Reg. Act ss.17, 49, 50) and (2) State taxing power (Entry 54 List II — “a State cannot levy a tax on the sale or purchase of any property other than ‘goods’”).
  3. Validity of the notifications upheld (para 59). The High Court had “confused the question of the interpretation of the impugned provisions with the interpretation of Timber Contracts and the Bamboo Contract”. The notifications validly tax the purchase of goods; if a particular transaction (e.g. a profit à prendre) is not a purchase of goods, the action under the notification is illegal, but the notification itself is not invalid. “If a notification is invalid, all actions taken under it would be invalid also. The converse, however, is not true.” The constitutional challenge therefore failed. (The Timber Contracts — agreements for the sale of standing trees for a felling season — were held to be contracts for sale of goods and taxable.)
  4. Nature of profit à prendre (paras 98–101). Quoting Halsbury (paras 240–242, 43) and s.4 Easements Act: a profit is a right to enter another’s land and take some profit of the soil; it is an interest in land; a servitude; distinct from an easement (which only confers a right to use); under Indian law a profit granted for the beneficial enjoyment of a dominant heritage is an easement, otherwise not. By GCA ss.3(26) and 4 the definition of immovable property applies to the TPA (enacted 15 years earlier); Ananda Behera held a profit à prendre to be a benefit arising out of land and immovable property. Mulla’s list of benefits arising out of land was reproduced.
  5. The Bamboo Contract is a profit à prendre (paras 102–105). Unlike the Timber Contracts (“an agreement for the sale and purchase of forest produce” for one season), the Bamboo Contract is “a grant of exclusive right and licence to fell, cut, obtain and remove bamboos”; it is long-term with renewal; it embraces future bamboos; the royalty bears no relation to quantity cut and a minimum royalty is payable; it carries extensive ancillary rights over land (roads, dams, canals, buildings, tramways, fuel extraction, right to lease sites). “The terms and conditions of the Bamboo Contract leave no doubt that it confers upon the respondent company a benefit to arise out of land; it would thus be an interest in immovable property.” It is not a lease — no demise, no exclusive possession, only a right to enter and take part of the produce. It is one integral and indivisible contract and cannot be split into existing and future bamboos. Being worth over Rs 100 it was compulsorily registrable, but as a Government grant it is exempt under s.90 Registration Act. Not being for the beneficial enjoyment of any land of the company, it is not an easement. Any attempt to tax amounts payable under it is “not only ultra vires the Orissa Act but also unconstitutional as being beyond the State’s taxing power under Entry 54”.
  6. Review of authorities (paras 106–124) — see the precedent table below. Chhotabhai was held to be “not good law and has been overruled by decisions of larger benches”; Orient Paper Mills was held “not good law”, “given per incuriam”, and its rule of interpretation “wrong in law”.
  7. Rule of interpretation laid down (paras 117–119, 124). Rejecting Krishna Iyer J.’s approach of “confining the focus to that statutory compass” and looking only at “the stage when bamboos have already been felled”, the Court held that a document must be construed “as a whole with reference to all its terms and all the rights conferred by it and not with reference to only a few terms or with just one of the rights flowing therefrom”. To interpret a document so as to bring it within a particular statute, looking only at convenient clauses, “is fraught with considerable danger and mischief as it may expose documents to the personal predilections and philosophies of individual judges” — the same document could become a benefit arising out of land in a land-reform case and a sale of goods in a tax case. The Court also criticised Orient Paper Mills for its oblique disapproval of State forest contracts, noting that such policy, industrial promotion and local employment (50% local labour clause) were matters for the State.
  8. Result. Appeals allowed in part: notifications valid; but the Bamboo Contract is a profit à prendre and the amounts payable under it cannot be taxed.

6Principles evolved

  1. A long-term grant of an exclusive right to enter forest land and cut and remove bamboos including future growth, with minimum royalty and ancillary rights over the land, is a profit à prendre — a “benefit to arise out of land” — and hence immovable property; it is neither a sale of goods nor a lease.
  2. Trees/bamboos rooted in earth are immovable; if standing timber, movable; if agreed to be severed before or under the contract of sale, they are goods (SGA s.2(7)).
  3. The English fructus naturales / fructus industriales dichotomy has no place in Indian law.
  4. Importance of the movable/immovable question: (i) registration and (ii) the limits of State sales-tax power (Entry 54, List II).
  5. Such a contract is integral and indivisible — it cannot be split into existing goods and future goods.
  6. Canon of construction: a document must be read as a whole, with all its terms and rights, not selectively to fit a statute; the same document cannot bear different characters under different statutes.
  7. Validity of a notification is independent of the legality of a particular action taken under it.
  8. Chhotabhai (1953) and Orient Paper Mills (1977) are not good law.
  9. Government grants of interests in land are exempt from registration (Reg. Act s.90).

CASE 3 — Bamadev Panigrahi v. Monorama Raj

AIR 1974 AP 226 · Andhra Pradesh High Court · Division Bench (Kondaiah J.) · Appeal from decree of the Additional Subordinate Judge, Srikakulam in O.S. No. 76 of 1966 · DU Case Material pp. 25–31

1Background & Facts

  • The plaintiff’s husband, Profulla Kumar Raj, and the defendant Bamadev Panigrahi were friends.
  • On 1 Sept 1957 Profulla Kumar obtained a possessory (usufructuary) mortgage for Rs 4,000 from the Raja of Mandasa over a site of about 3.51 acres called “Pula Thota”, containing a bungalow, with a view to running a touring cinema there.
  • From 1952 to 1959 he allegedly advanced about Rs 15,000 to the defendant to meet the latter’s obligations under forest contracts with the Raja.
  • He built a temporary cinema structure and a temporary pandal on part of the site. On 17 Feb 1958 he purchased on hire-purchase from Commercial Credit Corporation, Madras a cinema projector with accessories for Rs 16,327, and the same day a diesel oil engine with accessories for Rs 3,506. These were “imbedded and installed in the earth by constructing foundations” for running “Kumar Touring Talkies”.
  • Lacking time, he entrusted management to the defendant “out of trust and confidence”. The defendant allegedly colluded with the Raja, got an endorsement of discharge on the 1957 mortgage bond and obtained a fresh mortgage in his own name on 6 March 1961.
  • On 5 May 1961 Profulla Kumar sent a notice demanding accounts, repayment of Rs 15,000 and delivery of the entire concern (machinery, equipment, records, site). By reply dated 2 June 1961 the defendant denied all liability. No suit for the equipment was filed in Profulla Kumar’s lifetime (he did sue for the Rs 4,000 mortgage money — O.S. 124/1961, decreed ex parte). He fell ill in 1963 and died on 7 Aug 1965.
  • The defendant claimed he was the real owner (mortgage and hire-purchase taken benami in the plaintiff’s husband’s name), that he paid the instalments, and that he had removed the projector and engine in Dec 1961–Jan 1962; his attempt to get a cinema licence in his own name failed because of the husband’s opposition.
  • The cinema had a licence for one year only; the structure was a temporary shed built partly of zinc sheets (cabin) and partly of oil cloth (tent).
  • On 20 July 1966 the widow Monorama Raj sued for (a) a declaration that she owned the cinema equipment (projector, diesel engine etc.) “embodied in the plaint schedule site”, (b) a direction to the defendant to remove and deliver it, or alternatively (c) Rs 19,833 as its value with interest.
  • Trial court: held the equipment, being embedded in the earth, was immovable property, hence the suit was within limitation (12 years); found the husband to be the true owner; decreed Rs 19,833. The defendant appealed.

2Issues

  1. Whether the suit for recovery of possession of the cinema projector, diesel oil engine and accessories, or alternatively their value, is barred by limitation — which depends on whether the property is movable (3 years) or immovable (12 years)?
  2. Whether the plaintiff’s husband (and after him the plaintiff) was entitled to the equipment? (Not decided, in view of the answer to Issue 1.)

3Arguments

Appellant / Defendant (Mr S. Ramamurty)

  • The projector and oil engine are movable property; they do not become immovable merely by being embedded or fastened, because the intention and object of fixing them was the beneficial enjoyment of the equipment itself, not to benefit the land.
  • The claim being one to movables, the suit had to be filed within 3 years from the denial on 2 June 1961; filed on 20 July 1966, it is time-barred.
  • In any event the appellant, not the plaintiff’s husband, was the real owner.

Respondent / Plaintiff (Mr Gangadhara Rao)

  • The property whose possession is sought is immovable (embedded in earth with foundations), so the suit for declaration of title is within 12 years and maintainable.
  • The trial court’s findings of fact on ownership should not be disturbed.
  • Alternatively, even if the declaration prayer is withdrawn, the other reliefs should survive as the suit as framed relates to immovable property.

4Decision & Reasoning (Kondaiah J.)

  1. Limitation framework (para 9). A suit for declaration of title to or possession of immovable property lies within 12 years from denial; for movable property, within 3 years. The suit, filed 5 years after denial, is in time only if the property is immovable. The Limitation Act does not define the terms; the court therefore turned to the GCA, TPA and Registration Act (para 10).
  2. Statutory position (para 11). Things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth are immovable. The machinery “does not fall within any of the categories of immovable property. Though it is really movable property, it may become immovable property if it is attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything which is attached to the earth. The enquiry should be not whether the attachment is direct or indirect, but what is the nature and character of the attachment and the intendment and object of such attachment are.”
  3. English law of fixtures — guidelines only (paras 12–14). It “has no strict application” in India given the statutory definitions, but the tests of Holland v. Hodgson (degree and object of annexation), Leigh v. Taylor (tapestries affixed for ornament by a tenant for life remained chattels — “unless it has become part of the house in any intelligible sense”) and Spyer v. Phillipson were noticed.
  4. Indian authorities (para 15) — the nine machinery cases tabulated in Part 10.1 above (Narayana Sa, Meghraj, Subrahmanian Firm, Mohammed Ibrahim, Venkataswami Naidu, Perumal Naicker, J.H. Subbiah, Chetty & Co.), with Wadsworth J.’s formulation from Subrahmanian Firm quoted.
  5. Principles (paras 16–17). It is a mixed question of fact and law; there is no universal statutory test; the cumulative effect of all circumstances is the guide. The two tests: (i) the intendment, object and purpose of installing the machinery — beneficial enjoyment of the building/land, or of the machinery itself? (ii) the degree and manner of attachment. Presumptions: where machinery and land belong to the same person, infer (unless contrary proved) that the object is beneficial enjoyment of the entire building/land; where they belong to different persons, presume the installer intends “to exploit the benefit of the machinery alone, as he is not interested in the building or the land”. A lessee installing machinery for the term intends its beneficial enjoyment during the lease and will “in all probability … remove the oil engine or machine from the land the moment his object … is achieved” — such a fixture is not permanent. The nature of the structure also matters: machinery in a temporary shed or tent → enjoyment of the machinery; a projector and engine in a permanent cinema theatre → “the purpose and object of installing the same must invariably be the beneficial enjoyment of the very cinema theatre”.
  6. Application (para 18). (a) It is a touring talkies — “not a pucca cinema hall, but only a temporary shed built partly with zinc sheets and partly with oil cloth”; the very name indicates a temporary concern which “will be moved freely from place to place depending upon the demand and the convenience of the proprietor”. (b) Permission to exhibit was under a temporary licence for one year, with no assurance of renewal; in fact, because of the disputes, no one obtained a licence after the first year. (c) The land belonged to the Raja of Mandasa; the installer (whoever he was) was only a usufructuary mortgagee and not the owner, and “must have intended to have only the beneficial enjoyment of the cinema equipment but would not have intended to benefit the very land which was not owned by him”. (d) The equipment was in fact removed subsequently. (e) The projector and engine “are not rooted in the earth as in the case of trees and shrubs, or imbedded in the earth as in the case of walls or buildings or attached to what is so imbedded for the permanent beneficial enjoyment of that to which they are attached” — so they are not “attached to the earth” under s.3 TPA, nor permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth.
  7. Holding. The machinery is movable property; the suit, filed beyond three years from the denial of 2 June 1961, is barred by limitation. The argument that withdrawal of the declaration prayer saves the other reliefs was rejected — the declaration itself concerned only the equipment (movables), not the talkies as a whole (para 19). The plaintiff’s delay was itself “a material factor” (para 20). Issue 2 (ownership) was left undecided (para 21). Appeal allowed; suit dismissed with costs throughout (para 22).

5Principles evolved

  1. Machinery is movable by nature and becomes immovable only if “attached to the earth” or “permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth”; the enquiry is into the nature and character of the attachment and the intendment and object of it — not whether the attachment is direct or indirect.
  2. Two tests: (i) object/purpose of annexation — enjoyment of the land/building vs. enjoyment of the chattel; (ii) degree and manner of annexation.
  3. Presumptions from ownership: owner of both land and machinery → permanence; installer who is a tenant/licensee/mortgagee of another’s land → temporary, for the chattel’s own enjoyment.
  4. Nature of the structure and duration of the interest (temporary shed, one-year licence) are relevant; subsequent removal confirms intention.
  5. The English law of fixtures has no strict application in India; it is a mixed question of fact and law; totality of circumstances governs.
  6. Practical consequence: the classification determines the limitation period — 3 years (movables) vs. 12 years (immovables).

CASE 4 — Duncans Industries Ltd. v. State of U.P.

(2000) 1 SCC 633 : AIR 2000 SC 355 · Supreme Court · Two Judges (N. Santosh Hegde J., with S.P. Bharucha J.) · Civil Appeal from Allahabad High Court (CMWP 9170/1995, decided 7-7-1997) · DU Case Material pp. 32–36

1Background & Facts

  • ICI India Ltd. executed an agreement of sale dated 11 Nov 1993 agreeing to transfer, on an “as is where is” basis and “as a going concern”, its fertilizer business (manufacturing, marketing, distribution and sale of urea) at Kanpur to Chand Chhap Fertilizer and Chemicals Ltd. (CCFCL) — later renamed Duncans Industries Ltd., Fertilizer Division — for a “slump price” of Rs 70 crore. “Transfer date” was 1 Dec 1993.
  • “Fertilizer business” was defined to include: (i) demised land (Plots 2-B, 5 and part of Plot 2) of 243.4387 acres with buildings and structures; (ii) freehold land and residential building “Chandralok” (94 flats); (iii) freehold land and building “Chandrakala” (guest house + 3 flats); (iv) “plant and machinery relating to the fertilizer business including the ammonia-manufacturing plants, the captive power plant and all other moveable capital assets including vehicles, furniture, air conditioners, standby systems, pipelines, railway siding etc.”
  • The agreement provided that assets capable of transfer by actual/constructive delivery would be delivered on the transfer date, and estates requiring documents would be conveyed by deed; possession of plant and machinery was purportedly handed over separately as movables on 11 Dec 1993.
  • A deed of conveyance dated 9 June 1994 was then executed, drafted so as to appear to convey only the land, while clauses 10, 11 and 13 referred to the earlier agreement to transfer the factory as a going concern, and Schedule III annexed a plan delineating the machinery comprising the fertilizer factory.
  • On presentation for registration, the Sub-Registrar referred the deed to the Collector under s.47-A(2), Indian Stamp Act, 1899 (U.P. amendment) for non-compliance with s.27 (facts affecting duty not fully set out) and for determination of market value. The Collector (20 Feb 1995) levied stamp duty of Rs 37,01,26,832.50 and penalty of Rs 30,53,167.50. In revision under s.56, the Chief Controlling Revenue Authority set aside the penalty and modified the duty to Rs 36,68,08,887.50. The Allahabad High Court dismissed the writ petition (7 July 1997), relying on Reynolds v. Ashby & Son [1904] AC 466 and Official Liquidator v. Sri Krishna Deo, AIR 1959 All 247. Appeal to the Supreme Court.

2Issues

  1. Whether the plant and machinery of the fertilizer factory are immovable property?
  2. Whether, by the conveyance deed of 9 June 1994, the plant and machinery were also transferred (so that their value counts for stamp duty), or only the land?
  3. Whether the valuation accepted by the High Court was correct? (Held not seriously challenged below; not entertained.)
  4. (Before the High Court) whether the Sub-Registrar had “reason to believe” to make a reference under s.47-A — decided against the appellant and not pressed in the SC.

3Arguments

Appellant — Duncans Industries (Mr M.L. Verma, Sr. Adv.)

  • The High Court erred in treating plant and machinery as immovable property; the parties, by the agreement of 11-11-1993, had treated them as movables and delivered possession of them as movables on 11-12-1993.
  • The conveyance deed of 9-6-1994 conveyed only the land; paras 10 and 11 merely referred to the earlier agreement, and “mere reference to some earlier transaction in a document does not amount to incorporation” of its terms.
  • Hence the value of plant and machinery could not be included in the market value of the property conveyed; the valuation was also wrong in law.
  • Relied on Sirpur Paper Mills Ltd. v. CCE, (1998) 1 SCC 400, where paper-making machinery attached to earth for operational efficiency was held not immovable — “if the appellant wanted to sell the paper-making machine it could always remove it from its base and sell it.”

Respondent — State of U.P. (Mr Gopal Subramanium, Sr. Adv.)

  • The agreement contemplated transfer of the fertilizer business itself as a going concern, expressly including the first, second and third stream urea plants and the ammonia plants; para 1(c)(i) defining “fertilizer business” shows the intention to transfer all properties comprised in it.
  • The machinery is permanently embedded to run a fertilizer factory — immovable property; it could not physically be “delivered” dehors the land.
  • The deed was structured to reduce market value and evade duty; the appellant had itself declared the plant as immovable property worth Rs 70 crore before the Income-tax authorities.
  • The valuation was not seriously challenged in the High Court and should not be reopened.

4Decision & Reasoning (Santosh Hegde J.)

  1. Plant and machinery are immovable property (para 8). The Court agreed with the High Court that the machinery forming the fertilizer plant was “permanently embedded in the earth with an intention of running the fertilizer factory”; while embedding it “the intention of the party was not to remove the same for the purpose of any sale … either as a part of a machinery or scrap and in the very nature of the user of these machineries, it was necessary that these machineries be permanently fixed to the ground.” Test: “The question whether a machinery which is embedded in the earth is moveable property or an immovable property, depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case. Primarily, the court will have to take into consideration the intention of the parties when it decided to embed the machinery, whether such embedment was intended to be temporary or permanent.” The agreement, the conveyance deed, the attendant circumstances and “the nature of machineries involved” showed they were “definitely embedded permanently with a view to utilise the same as a fertilizer plant”; the schedule’s description showed they were “set up permanently … and the same was not embedded to dismantle and remove the same for the purpose of sale as machinery at any point of time.” Nor could such plant be “transferred by delivery of possession on any date prior to the date of conveyance of the title to the land.”
  2. Sirpur Paper Mills distinguished (paras 8–9). That decision (excise; paper machine attached only for operational efficiency and removable for sale) turned on its own findings of fact. The Court “will have to take into consideration the intention of the parties which embedded the machinery and also the intention of the parties who intend alienating that machinery.” On the documents here, the plant “cannot but be described as an immovable property.”
  3. Title to plant and machinery passed under the conveyance deed (paras 10–13). Intention is to be gathered from all the material including the antecedent agreement, which sold “the entire business of fertilizer on an ‘as is where is’ basis including the land, building thereon, plant and machinery”. The appellant never claimed the machinery was dismantled and delivered; “nor is it possible to visualise from the nature of the plant … that such a possession dehors the land could be given”. “It is obviously to reduce the market value of the property the document in question is attempted to be drafted as a conveyance deed regarding the land only.” Clauses 10, 11 and 13 implicitly refer to sale of the factory as a going concern, and Schedule III (plan of the machinery) shows “it is the land with standing fertilizer factory which is being conveyed … though an attempt to camouflage this part of the property sold is made in the recitals”. Corroboration: the appellant’s own application under s.269-UL(3) Income-tax Act (Form 37-I, supplementary affidavit dated 8-9-1993) disclosed the market value of the immovable property transferred as Rs 70 crore and listed “plant and machinery, railway siding and other immovable properties” as part of the undertaking. The certificate issued by the appropriate authority evidenced this.
  4. Conclusion (paras 13, 16). The vendor conveyed by the deed of 9-6-1994 “not only … the land in question but also … the entire fertilizer business on ‘as is where is’ condition including the plant and machinery standing on the said land”; the authorities rightly included their value for stamp duty. Appeal dismissed with costs.

5Principles evolved

  1. Machinery permanently embedded in earth to constitute and run a factory, with no intention of dismantling/removal for sale, is “attached to the earth” and is immovable property.
  2. The primary test is intention at the time of embedding — temporary or permanent — judged from the documents, attendant circumstances and the nature of the machinery; also relevant is the intention of the parties alienating it.
  3. The two requisites of limb (c) of “attached to the earth” (as the class notes and Exam Capsule distil the case): (a) permanent attachment — intended to be used in perpetuity or for the life of the attachment; (b) the attachment constitutes a permanent improvement of / is for the beneficial enjoyment of the thing to which it is attached.
  4. Parties cannot alter the legal character of property by labelling it “movable” in their agreement or by the device of “separate delivery of possession”; a conveyance drafted to camouflage the transfer of a factory as a transfer of bare land will be read with the antecedent agreement and schedules.
  5. Consequences: (i) stamp duty is payable on the market value of land plus the embedded plant; (ii) title to immovable plant passes only by a registered conveyance — “physical delivery of possession, without a written, attested and registered document could not convey any title” (Exam Capsule summary); (iii) admissions before other authorities (Income-tax) may be used to establish intention.
  6. Excise cases (Sirpur) turning on operational-efficiency attachment do not lay down a general rule; each case turns on its facts.
Answer to the 2025 DU question embedded here: where a fertilizer factory is sold “as is where is” as a going concern, the parties cannot validly split the transaction into one deed for “plant and machinery” (as movables, by delivery) and another for “land and building” so as to escape stamp duty/registration: the embedded plant is itself immovable property and passes only with the land under a registered conveyance; the revenue may look through the device (Duncans).

15. Real-life examples & problem practice

  1. The builder’s tower crane. A crane bolted to a concrete base at a construction site for 18 months is movable — the object is to use the crane, not to improve the land, and it will be dismantled (cf. Bamadev, Sirpur).
  2. Rooftop solar plant. Panels bolted to a factory roof by the factory owner with a 25-year life and grid connection — immovable (permanent, for beneficial enjoyment of the factory: Duncans reasoning). The same panels leased by a third-party vendor under a 5-year removable arrangement — arguably movable (different ownership, temporary).
  3. Petrol pump. Underground storage tanks and dispensers embedded with foundations by the dealer-owner — immovable; a portable generator on wheels — movable.
  4. Mobile tower on a rented rooftop. The telecom company installs a tower on a rooftop under a 10-year licence, with an express right to remove it on termination — movable (tenant’s fixture; s.108(h) analogy), though the courts have treated some towers as immovable when built with deep foundations by the land owner — shows the fact-specific nature of the test.
  5. Sale of “the apple crop of 2026” in a Himachal orchard — goods (growing crops). Lease of the orchard for 10 years to pluck fruit — immovable (benefit arising from land; the lessee benefits from future growth).
  6. Sand mining lease from a river-bed for one year — right to take part of the soil itself = profit à prendre = immovable, so it needs a registered instrument (unless a Government grant under s.90 Registration Act).
  7. A tenant’s split AC. Indoor/outdoor units screwed to walls by a tenant — movable; he may remove them while in possession (s.108(h)), restoring the wall.

Q.1 below is reproduced verbatim from the DU LL.B. II Semester Examination, May/June 2025 (Paper Code LB-204, Q.P. 8736) uploaded with this project. PYQ 1 — DU May/June 2025, Q.1 (20 marks)

“A fertilizer factory is being sold on a ‘as is where is’ basis. Is it possible for the parties to enter into two separate deeds, one for the sale of plant and machinery and other for the land and building? Distinguish between movable and immovable property, elaborating upon the concept of profits à prendre and the doctrine of fixtures.”

Model Answer

1. Introduction and the issue. The question raises the classification of property under s.3 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 (TPA) read with s.3(26) of the General Clauses Act, 1897 and s.2(6) of the Registration Act, 1908, and specifically whether plant and machinery permanently embedded in a factory can be treated as movable property so as to be conveyed separately (by mere delivery, without a registered instrument and without ad valorem stamp duty). The facts are squarely those of Duncans Industries Ltd. v. State of U.P., (2000) 1 SCC 633.

2. Statutory framework. The TPA defines immovable property only negatively — it “does not include standing timber, growing crops or grass” — but defines “attached to the earth” as (a) rooted in the earth (trees, shrubs), (b) imbedded in the earth (walls, buildings), or (c) attached to what is so imbedded for the permanent beneficial enjoyment of that to which it is attached. The General Clauses Act, whose definitions apply to the TPA by virtue of s.4 GCA (Titaghur), positively provides that immovable property “shall include land, benefits to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth, or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth”; movable property is “property of every description except immovable property”. The Registration Act adds hereditary allowances, rights to ways, lights, ferries, fisheries “or any other benefit to arise out of land”. Reading these together (as Madon J. did in Titaghur and Bose J. in Shantabai), immovable property has three components — land, benefits arising out of land, and things attached to the earth — less the three exclusions.

3. Distinction between movable and immovable property. [Give the comparative table: definition; physical/intention test; mode of transfer — registered instrument under ss.54, 59, 107, 123 TPA and s.17 Registration Act vs. delivery/oral under s.9 TPA and Sale of Goods Act; limitation 12 vs 3 years (Bamadev); stamp duty on market value (Duncans); State taxing power confined to “goods” under Entry 54 List II (Titaghur); s.8 TPA incidents.] Illustrations: land, buildings, trees, fisheries, mortgage debt, equity of redemption, right to collect rent — immovable; standing timber, crops, grass, royalty, shares, decrees — movable.

4. Profit à prendre (benefits to arise out of land). Define per Halsbury (quoted in Titaghur): a right to enter another’s land and take some profit of the soil or part of the soil itself. It is an interest in land, a servitude, and in India a “benefit to arise out of land” = immovable property (Ananda Behera v. State of Orissa — right to catch fish in Chilka Lake). Distinguish from a lease (enjoyment with exclusive possession — s.105 TPA; “in a lease one enjoys the property but has no right to take it away; in a profit à prendre one has a licence to enter … for removing something from it” — Bose J.), from an easement (right to use, not to take; s.4 Easements Act), and from a bare licence. In Shantabai v. State of Bombay an unregistered 12-year grant to enter a zamindari forest and cut bamboo, fuel-wood and teak (teak under 1½ ft not to be cut) was a profit à prendre, not a sale of standing timber, because the grantee was to benefit from the further growth and nourishment drawn from the soil; standing timber (movable) is only a timber tree fit to be felled and meant to be felled reasonably early. In State of Orissa v. Titaghur Paper Mills the 11–14 year bamboo contract (renewable; covering future bamboos; minimum royalty; rights to build roads, dams and depots) was a profit à prendre — an integral, indivisible interest in immovable property — and not a sale of “goods”, so the State could not levy purchase tax on it; documents must be read as a whole and not selectively to fit a statute.

5. Doctrine of fixtures. A fixture is a chattel so annexed to land that it becomes part of it (quicquid plantatur solo, solo cedit). English tests — Holland v. Hodgson (1872): degree of annexation and object of annexation; Leigh v. Taylor (1902): tapestries affixed for ornament remain chattels. In India there is no absolute rule (Narayan Das Khettry v. Jatindra Nath, PC); the English law “has no strict application” given the statutory definitions (Bamadev). Indian tests (Bamadev Panigrahi v. Monorama Raj, AIR 1974 AP 226): (i) intendment, object and purpose of installation — enjoyment of the land/building or of the machinery itself; (ii) degree and manner of attachment; presumptions from ownership (owner → permanence; tenant/mortgagee of another’s land → temporary); nature of the structure (touring talkies in a zinc-and-oil-cloth shed under a one-year licence → projector and diesel engine were movable; suit barred by 3-year limitation). Also s.108(h) TPA (lessee may remove things he has attached to the earth while in possession) and s.8 TPA (land passes with things attached to the earth; a house with doors, windows and things provided for permanent use).

6. Application — can two separate deeds be executed? In Duncans Industries ICI sold its urea fertilizer business at Kanpur to CCFCL (Duncans) “as is where is” and “as a going concern” for a slump price of Rs 70 crore; the parties treated the plant (ammonia plants, captive power plant, pipelines, railway siding) as movables, purported to deliver possession separately, and drafted the conveyance deed as one of land alone. The Supreme Court (Santosh Hegde J.) held: (a) machinery permanently embedded with the intention of running the factory, not to be dismantled and sold, is immovable property — “primarily, the court will have to take into consideration the intention of the party when it decided to embed the machinery, whether such embedment was intended to be temporary or permanent”; the requisites are permanent attachment and permanent beneficial improvement of the thing attached; Sirpur Paper Mills (attachment merely for operational efficiency) was distinguished; (b) a plant of this nature cannot be delivered “dehors the land”; the deed was drafted “obviously to reduce the market value” and the attempt to “camouflage” failed — clauses 10, 11, 13 and Schedule III (plan of the machinery), the antecedent agreement and the appellant’s own Form 37-I declaration to the Income-tax authorities (immovable property worth Rs 70 crore including plant and machinery) showed the whole factory was conveyed; (c) stamp duty (~Rs 36.68 crore) was payable on land plus plant.

Conclusion. Therefore the parties cannot effectively split the sale of a fertilizer factory sold “as is where is” into a deed for plant and machinery (as movables) and a deed for land and building. The embedded plant is “attached to the earth” and is immovable property; title to it can pass only by a registered conveyance together with the land (ss.54 TPA, 17 Registration Act), on which stamp duty is payable on the combined market value. Only genuinely movable items (vehicles, furniture, loose equipment, stock) may be transferred separately by delivery. The label the parties choose is immaterial; the courts look to the intention at the time of embedding and to the substance of the transaction (Shantabai; Titaghur; Duncans).

PYQ 2 — “There is no comprehensive definition of the term ‘Immovable property’ in the Transfer of Property Act. Each case has to be decided on its own merits.” Comment. (DU, earlier years — asked repeatedly)

Model Answer (outline with full reasoning)

Thesis. The statement is correct on both limbs: (i) the TPA gives no positive definition; and (ii) because the operative concepts — “benefit to arise out of land”, “permanent beneficial enjoyment”, “standing timber” — depend on intention and circumstances, classification is a mixed question of fact and law decided case by case.

(A) Absence of a comprehensive definition. Quote s.3 TPA (negative); Madon J. in Titaghur: “this is strictly speaking not a definition … it merely tells us what it does not include.” The gap is filled by GCA s.3(26) (applied to the TPA via s.4 GCA) and Reg. Act s.2(6); the first Indian Law Commission (1879) said it “abstained from the almost impracticable task of defining the various kinds of interests in immovable things”. Bose J. in Shantabai: the general GCA definition prevails except where the TPA makes a special exclusion. Thus the working definition is a judicial synthesis of three statutes — itself proof that no single comprehensive definition exists.

(B) Why each case turns on its own facts — the three variable elements.

  1. Trees vs. standing timber. The same teak tree is movable if fit to be felled and meant to be felled soon, but immovable if left to grow for the grantee’s benefit (Shantabai — 12-year term, girth restriction, staggered cuttings; Marshall v. Green’s “warehouse” test; Seeni Chettiar). Bose J. admitted the rule rests on “practical common sense” developed “empirically from instance to instance”.
  2. Benefit arising out of land vs. sale of goods / growing crop. A right to pluck tendu leaves for a season is a growing crop (Ananda Behera explaining Chhotabhai; Ansari), but tendu-leaf rights spread over years with rights to occupy land and take other produce are proprietary interests (Mahadeo); a season’s timber contract is a sale of goods but a 14-year bamboo contract with future growth and ancillary rights is a profit à prendre (Titaghur). The Court insisted the entire document be read; the same document cannot be goods for tax and land for land-reform purposes.
  3. Fixtures — things attached to the earth. Identical cinema machinery is movable in a touring talkies erected by a mortgagee under a one-year licence (Bamadev; Venkataswami Naidu; Subrahmanian Firm — tenant) but immovable in a permanent theatre or factory owned by the installer (Mohammed Ibrahim; Chetty & Co.; Perumal Naicker); a fertilizer plant embedded to run the factory is immovable (Duncans) whereas a paper machine bolted for operational efficiency and removable for sale is goods (Sirpur). Bamadev (para 16): “There is no statutory test or guideline having universal application … the cumulative effect or the totality of the material facts and circumstances must be taken as a fair and reasonable guide.” Duncans (para 8): “depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case … primarily … the intention of the party when it decided to embed the machinery.” The Privy Council in Narayan Das Khettry rejected any absolute quicquid plantatur rule for India.

(C) Criticism / balance. Some certainty exists: the three statutory exclusions; the settled categories in Reg. Act s.2(6) and Mulla’s list; the settled tests (degree + object of annexation; ownership presumptions; further-growth test). The flexibility serves the purposes of the classification — registration and revenue — by preventing evasion through labels (Shantabai “lease”; Duncans “movables”).

Conclusion. The definition is deliberately open-textured; courts apply settled tests to varying facts, so that while the principles are certain, their application is necessarily case-specific.

PYQ 3 — Discuss with reference to decided cases whether the following are movable or immovable property: (i) sale of a wheat crop which would be ready for harvest after one month; (ii) transfer of a right to collect sand from a river-bed for one year; (iii) a right to work in mines and carry away the product for three years; (iv) a ceiling fan fixed by a tenant; (v) water pipes fixed by the owner of a house to get supply from the MCD; (vi) a right to cut mango trees (timber trees) for two years.

Model Answer

Framework (2–3 lines): s.3 TPA + GCA s.3(26) + Reg. Act s.2(6), (9); tests from Shantabai (standing timber/further growth), Ananda Behera/Titaghur (benefit arising out of land), Bamadev/Duncans (object and degree of annexation; ownership).

  1. Wheat crop ready in a month — MOVABLE. Growing crops are expressly excluded by s.3 TPA and included in movable property by Reg. Act s.2(9); they are “goods” under SGA s.2(7) (growing crops; things attached to land agreed to be severed before sale). The purchaser takes no benefit from the soil beyond ripening; the land is a mere “warehouse” (Marshall v. Green). No registration; sale by agreement/delivery.
  2. Right to collect sand from a river-bed for one year — IMMOVABLE. Sand is part of the soil itself; a right to enter land and take part of the soil is the classic profit à prendre (Halsbury para 242 — “stone, sand and shingle”), i.e. a benefit arising out of land (Ananda Behera; Titaghur). Duration is short but what is taken is the land itself, not a crop; hence immovable — the grant, if worth ≥ Rs 100, requires a registered instrument (unless a Government grant, s.90 Reg. Act).
  3. Right to work mines and carry the product for three years — IMMOVABLE. Minerals are part of land; the right to extract them over a term is a profit à prendre / interest in land; also the grantee acquires possessory rights akin to a lease (mining leases are treated as leases of immovable property). Registration required.
  4. Ceiling fan fixed by a tenant — MOVABLE. Limb (c) of “attached to the earth” requires attachment for the permanent beneficial enjoyment of the building; a fan is attached for the enjoyment of the fan, is easily removable without damage, and is installed by a tenant, who is presumed to intend removal (Bamadev; Subrahmanian Firm; Leigh v. Taylor). Under s.108(h) TPA the lessee may remove it while in possession.
  5. Water pipes fixed by the owner for MCD supply — IMMOVABLE. Imbedded/attached by the owner permanently for the beneficial enjoyment of the house (presumption of permanence — Mohammed Ibrahim; Duncans); analogous to doors and windows and “things provided for permanent use” that pass under s.8 TPA. Removal would damage the structure and defeat its purpose.
  6. Right to cut mango trees (timber trees) for two years — DEPENDS, prima facie IMMOVABLE. Apply Shantabai: standing timber = timber tree + fit to be felled + intended to be felled reasonably early. If the trees are mature and the two-year period is merely a reasonable time to remove them, they are standing timber (movable) and no registration is needed. But if the grantee may leave them standing to grow, or immature trees are included, he derives benefit from further growth and the right is a benefit arising out of land (immovable) requiring registration. Mango trees being ordinarily fruit trees (not standing timber) reinforces the immovable characterisation unless clearly sold as wood for immediate felling (Mathura Das v. Jadu Bir — trees sold to be cut and removed as wood are movable).

PYQ 4 — Ashok purchased from Ravi, by a registered sale deed, a house for Rs 5 lakh. Before purchase Ashok inspected the house and saw six ceiling fans and a booster pump. On taking possession he found Ravi had removed all the fans and the booster pump. Ravi says he sold only the house. Advise Ashok.

Model Answer

Law. (i) s.8 TPA: unless a different intention is expressed or necessarily implied, a transfer passes all the transferor’s interest in the property and its legal incidents; where the property is a house, these include “the locks, keys, bars, doors, windows and all other things provided for permanent use therewith”, and where it is land, “all things attached to the earth”. (ii) s.3 TPA limb (c): a thing attached to what is imbedded for the permanent beneficial enjoyment of the building is immovable property. (iii) Tests: object and degree of annexation; owner-installer presumed to intend permanence (Holland v. Hodgson; Bamadev; Duncans); attachment for the chattel’s own enjoyment keeps it a chattel (Leigh v. Taylor; Subrahmanian Firm).

Booster pump. A booster pump is normally fixed to the house’s water line/overhead-tank system on a foundation or bracket, by the owner, to make the house habitable; it is “provided for permanent use” with the house and is attached for the beneficial enjoyment of the house, not of the pump. It is therefore part of the immovable property and passed to Ashok under s.8 read with s.3, notwithstanding that the deed did not name it. Ravi’s removal is wrongful; Ashok may sue for its return/restoration or damages (and the contract of sale may also have been breached).

Ceiling fans. Fans are screwed to hooks in the ceiling for the enjoyment of the fan as a chattel; they are removable without injury to the house and are conventionally treated as movables (the Exam Capsule and Mulla give “electric fans or window blinds” as the standard example of non-permanent attachment). Prima facie they did not pass merely as incidents of the house. However, Ashok should examine: (a) whether the agreement to sell / sale deed listed fittings; (b) whether the price was fixed on the basis of the inspected condition and there was a representation that fittings were included — in which case he has a contractual remedy (breach/misrepresentation) though not a proprietary claim under s.8.

Advice. Ashok has a strong proprietary claim to the booster pump as immovable property passing with the house; his claim to the fans depends on the contract and is weak in property law. He should sue for recovery of the pump (or its value) and, if the agreement covered fittings, for damages for the fans as well.

PYQ 5 — “Standing timber, growing crops and grass are movable property, but a right to enjoy them may be immovable property.” Explain with reference to Shantabai v. State of Bombay and State of Orissa v. Titaghur Paper Mills.

Model Answer (outline)

  1. The exclusion: s.3 TPA; Reg. Act s.2(9); rationale — destined for severance, no further benefit from soil intended.
  2. Standing timber ≠ timber tree: Bose J.’s three conditions; “timber” per Webster; nourishment rationale; Mulla pp.16, 21; Marshall v. Green.
  3. Growing crops and grass: examples; no fructus distinction in India (Mahadeo, Titaghur para 53).
  4. The right to take them over time is a benefit arising out of land: GCA s.3(26); Ananda Behera (fish); Shantabai (12-year cutting right → immovable; unregistered → no title; Article 32 fails); Seeni Chettiar (right to cut grass/reeds for 4+ years); Titaghur (bamboo contract with future growth, minimum royalty, ancillary rights → profit à prendre; indivisible; not taxable as goods; Timber Contracts for a season → goods).
  5. Consequences: registration; State taxing power; nature of remedy (contract vs. property).
  6. Conclusion: the thing may be movable while the right to take it, if it involves benefit from the soil over a period, is immovable — “grass = movable; right to cut grass = immovable”.

PYQ 6 — Problem: P, a usufructuary mortgagee of land belonging to R, set up a touring cinema in a tent, embedding a projector and a diesel engine in foundations, under a one-year licence. Q, his manager, denied P’s ownership in June 2019. P’s widow sued in July 2024 for recovery of the equipment or its value. Q pleads limitation. Decide.

Model Answer (outline)

Issue: whether the equipment is movable (Art. 91/68 Limitation Act — 3 years from denial) or immovable (Arts. 64/65 — 12 years). Law: s.3 TPA “attached to the earth”; GCA s.3(26); tests in Bamadev Panigrahi v. Monorama Raj (on identical facts): object and purpose (enjoyment of machinery vs. land); degree of annexation; ownership of land vs. machinery (mortgagee, not owner → presumption of temporariness); nature of structure (tent/zinc shed → temporary); one-year licence; subsequent removal. Application: every factor points to temporary annexation for the enjoyment of the machinery itself; not rooted, not imbedded like a wall, not attached for permanent beneficial enjoyment of any building → movable. Contrast Duncans (owner; permanent factory; no intention to remove) and Perumal Naicker/Chetty & Co.. Decision: suit filed five years after denial is barred; dismissed. Note also that the plaintiff’s delay is a “material factor”.

Ten lines to remember in the exam hall
  1. TPA s.3 is a negative definition; GCA s.3(26) fills it (Bose J.: general definition prevails absent special one).
  2. Label of the deed is never conclusive (Shantabai; Orient Paper Mills; Duncans).
  3. Lease = enjoy with possession; profit à prendre = enter to take; easement = use, not take; licence = personal permission.
  4. Standing timber: fit for timber + felled reasonably early; else a tree (immovable) because it draws nourishment from the soil.
  5. A right to take produce over a period, benefiting from further growth = benefit arising out of land = immovable (Ananda Behera, Shantabai, Titaghur).
  6. Things agreed to be severed before/under a contract of sale are “goods” (SGA s.2(7)) — taxable; a profit à prendre is not.
  7. Read the whole document; it cannot be goods for tax and land for land-reform (Titaghur rejecting Orient Paper Mills).
  8. Machinery: intention at embedding is primary; owner → permanent; tenant/mortgagee/temporary structure → movable (Bamadev v. Duncans).
  9. Limb (c) needs permanence and beneficial enjoyment of the structure, not of the chattel (Subrahmanian Firm; Leigh v. Taylor).
  10. Consequences: registered instrument (s.54 TPA/s.17 Reg. Act), 12-year limitation, ad valorem stamp duty, no State sales tax; unregistered/oral grant of immovable interest passes nothing and supports no fundamental-right claim.
