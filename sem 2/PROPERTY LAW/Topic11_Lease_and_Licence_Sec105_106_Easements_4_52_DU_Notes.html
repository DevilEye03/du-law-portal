1. Lease — concept and s.105 definition

s.105. Lease defined. — "A lease of immoveable property is a transfer of a right to enjoy such property, made for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity, in consideration of a price paid or promised, or of money, a share of crops, service or any other thing of value, to be rendered periodically or on specified occasions to the transferor by the transferee, who accepts the transfer on such terms.
Lessor, lessee, premium and rent defined. — The transferor is called the lessor, the transferee is called the lessee, the price is called the premium, and the money, share, service or other thing to be so rendered is called the rent."

A lease is one of the five modes of transfer under the Act (sale, mortgage, lease, exchange, gift). Unlike a sale, ownership does not pass; unlike a mortgage, the transfer is not by way of security. What passes is a right to enjoy — possession and enjoyment of the property for a time — leaving with the lessor the reversion. Subba Rao J. in Associated Hotels (para 28): "Under Section 108 … the lessee is entitled to be put in possession of the property. A lease is therefore a transfer of an interest in land. The interest transferred is called the leasehold interest. The lessor parts with his right to enjoy the property during the term of the lease, and it follows from it that the lessee gets that right to the exclusion of the lessor."

ElementAnalysis
1. Parties — lessor and lesseeLessor must be competent to transfer (s.7) and must have a transferable interest of at least the duration granted — an owner, a lessee (sub-lease), a mortgagee in possession (s.76(a)), a karta, a guardian with sanction. A person with no interest, or a tenant statutorily forbidden to sub-let, cannot carve out a tenancy (Cobb v. Lane, Romer L.J.; Delta, proposition 5; Rajbir Kaur). A lease to oneself is impossible (merger — s.111(d)). Lessee — any person capable of holding property; a minor may be a lessee through guardian but cannot execute a lease deed with obligations (s.107 requires execution by both).
2. Immovable propertys.3 TPA; s.3(26) General Clauses Act. Land, buildings, benefits arising out of land (right to collect market dues, fishery), things attached to earth. A "lease" of movables (a car, machinery) is a bailment/hire under the Contract Act. Where premises are let with fittings and business (a running petrol pump, a cinema with equipment), the dominant intention decides whether it is a lease of immovable property or a licence/hire of a business (Uttamchand v. S.M. Lalwani AIR 1965 SC 716, cited in Delta).
3. Transfer of a right to enjoyThe core — a jus in re aliena; a partial transfer (s.5). It gives the lessee (a) the right to possession (s.108(b)), (b) the right to use and take the profits (s.108(d)–(e) crops, accessions), (c) a right exclusive of the lessor for the term, (d) a right good against the world, including transferees of the lessor (s.109). This is what distinguishes a lease from a licence, which "does not create in his favour any estate or interest in the property" (Associated Hotels). A grant of a right to enjoy a part of the benefits only (right to graze, to fish, to put up hoardings) may be a licence or a profit à prendre rather than a lease.
4. Duration — "for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity"The term must be certain or ascertainable at the commencement: a fixed term (11 months; 99 years); a periodic tenancy (month to month — the period is certain though the total duration is not); implied by s.106; or perpetual (permanent lease — Indian law, unlike English law, permits a lease in perpetuity). A lease "for the life of the lessee" or "for so long as the lessee pays rent" is for an ascertainable time (life; the tenancy continues subject to determination). A lease "until the lessor needs the property" without more is uncertain and operates only as a tenancy at will. See Part 2.
5. Consideration — premium and/or rentPremium = price paid or promised (lump sum, "salami", "pagri" — often for long leases). Rent = money, share of crops (batai), service (a chowkidar's rent-free quarters in return for service — a service tenancy), or any other thing of value rendered periodically or on specified occasions. Consideration is essential — a gratuitous grant of possession is a licence or a gift, not a lease (Samir Kumar Chatterjee — the plaintiff's case of a compassionate, rent-free permission; Rajbir Kaur: "essential to the creation of a tenancy that … the grant be for consideration"). The label given to the payment ("compensation", "licence fee", "commission") is not decisive (D'Souza; Delta).
6. Acceptance by the transferee "on such terms"A lease is bilateral — a contract plus a conveyance. The lessee must accept; hence s.107 requires a registered lease to be executed by both lessor and lessee, and an oral lease must be accompanied by delivery of possession (acceptance by conduct).
Examples. (i) A lets his flat in Vasant Kunj to B for 11 months at Rs 40,000 p.m. by an unregistered agreement + possession — a lease (s.107, second para). (ii) A gives B, a farmer, 5 bighas on batai (half the crop) for the kharif season — a lease, rent in kind. (iii) A allows his driver to live in the servant quarter as part of his wages — a lease for service, or, if occupation is required for the better performance of duties and A retains control, a service licence (see Part 6). (iv) A permits B to use A's godown wall for a hoarding at Rs 5,000 p.m. — a licence (no possession of the godown passes). (v) A grants B "the right to take all the mangoes of my orchard this season for Rs 1 lakh" — a licence coupled with a grant/profit, not a lease of the orchard.

2. Classification of leases

2.1 Absolute and derivative leases

KindMeaningIllustration / law
Absolute (head) leaseGranted by the owner out of his absolute ownership; the lessor retains the reversion (ownership minus the leasehold).Abhiram Mullick (owner) → Mallika Investment Co. in Delta; Ashar (owner) → DSS in Quality Cut Pieces.
Derivative lease (sub-lease / under-lease)Granted by a lessee out of his leasehold; the sub-lessee's interest is carved out of, and can never exceed, the head-lessee's interest (nemo dat). Hence the recurring litigation pattern: a tenant who has parted with possession pleads "licence", the landlord pleads "sub-letting".
Assignment v. sub-leaseAn assignment transfers the lessee's entire remaining interest — the assignee steps into privity of estate with the lessor (s.108(j), s.109); a sub-lease transfers a lesser term (even one day less) and keeps the lessee in the chain.Clause 8 of the Associated Hotels deed (transfer of the "licence" with consent) was held "destructive of any theory of licence" — assignability is a hallmark of an interest in property; conversely non-assignability pointed to licence in Quality Cut Pieces (cl. 34).

3. Section 106 — duration of certain leases in absence of written contract or local usage; notice to quit

s.106 (as substituted by the Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act, 2002, w.e.f. 31-12-2002).
(1) In the absence of a contract or local law or usage to the contrary, a lease of immovable property for agricultural or manufacturing purposes shall be deemed to be a lease from year to year, terminable, on the part of either lessor or lessee, by six months' notice; and a lease of immovable property for any other purpose shall be deemed to be a lease from month to month, terminable, on the part of either lessor or lessee, by fifteen days' notice.
(2) Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force, the period mentioned in sub-section (1) shall commence from the date of receipt of notice.
(3) A notice under sub-section (1) shall not be deemed to be invalid merely because the period mentioned therein falls short of the period specified under that sub-section, where a suit or proceeding is filed after the expiry of the period mentioned in that sub-section.
(4) Every notice under sub-section (1) must be in writing, signed by or on behalf of the person giving it, and either be sent by post to the party who is intended to be bound by it or be tendered or delivered personally to such party, or to one of his family or servants at his residence, or (if such tender or delivery is not practicable) affixed to a conspicuous part of the property.
PointExplanation
When s.106 appliesOnly where the lease itself (or local law/usage) is silent about duration — typically oral leases, unregistered "agreements" that cannot prove the term (s.107 + s.49 Registration Act; see Supreme General Films, Topic 9), holding-over tenancies (s.116 imports s.106), and leases whose term has expired. It is a rule of presumption supplying the term, not a rule overriding an express term.
"Agricultural or manufacturing purposes"Year to year → six months' notice (traditionally expiring with the end of a year of tenancy under the old section; after 2002, the notice simply needs to give six months from receipt). Manufacturing = bringing into existence a new, commercially distinct article (Allenbury Engineers v. Ramkrishna Dalmia AIR 1973 SC 425 — repair/assembly is not manufacture). Burden is on the party asserting the manufacturing purpose.
"Any other purpose" (residential, commercial, storage, etc.)Month to month → fifteen days' notice. Before 2002 the notice had to expire with the end of a month of the tenancy, which generated enormous technical litigation; sub-ss.(2)–(3) abolished that: the period now runs from receipt and a short notice is cured if the suit is filed after the statutory period.
Form and service — sub-s.(4)Writing + signature + one of the modes (post — presumption of service under s.27 General Clauses Act / s.114 Evidence Act; personal tender; delivery to family/servant at residence; affixation as last resort). An oral notice is invalid. A notice to one of several joint lessees is generally sufficient if they hold jointly.
Effect of Rent ActsWhere a Rent Control Act applies, determination under s.106 does not enable eviction; a ground under the Rent Act must be proved, and a s.106 notice is not a precondition (V. Dhanapal Chettiar, 7-judge bench). This is why, in D'Souza, once the SC held the transaction a lease, the eviction suit in the civil court failed under s.56 of the Goa Act, and in Associated Hotels the fixing of standard rent turned on whether the premises were "premises" let to a tenant.
Waiver of notice (s.113)A notice to quit is waived, with the express or implied consent of the person to whom it is given, by any act of the person giving it showing an intention to treat the lease as subsisting (e.g., accepting rent for a period after expiry of the notice — Illustration (a)).
Example. A lets a shop orally to B at Rs 10,000 p.m. in 2019. In March 2026 A serves a signed notice by registered post, received 3 March, requiring B to vacate by 15 March (12 days). B does not vacate; A sues on 25 March. Before 2002 the notice was bad (too short; not ending with the month of tenancy). After 2002: under s.106(2)–(3) the notice is valid because the suit was filed after 15 days from receipt. If, however, the Delhi Rent Control Act applies (rent ≤ Rs 3,500), A must still prove a ground under s.14 before the Rent Controller.

4. Section 107 — leases how made; ss.108, 111, 112, 116 in outline

s.107. A lease of immoveable property from year to year, or for any term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent, can be made only by a registered instrument. All other leases may be made either by a registered instrument or by oral agreement accompanied by delivery of possession. Where a lease is made by a registered instrument, such instrument (or each of them) shall be executed by both the lessor and the lessee. Proviso: State Government may by notification exempt classes of leases.
  • Three categories needing registration — (i) year to year; (ii) term exceeding one year (an 11-month lease escapes; a lease for exactly one year escapes; one year + one day does not); (iii) reserving a yearly rent (even if the term is short). Read with s.17(1)(d) Registration Act, 1908 (compulsory registration of leases from year to year, for any term exceeding one year, or reserving a yearly rent) and s.49 (an unregistered compulsorily registrable document does not affect the immovable property or be received as evidence of the transaction, but may be looked at for a collateral purpose — e.g., the nature and character of possession, as in Supreme General Films and Anthony v. K.C. Ittoop (2000) 6 SCC 394: an unregistered long lease + possession + rent → monthly tenancy under s.106).
  • Written unregistered lease for ≤1 year — s.107 second paragraph speaks of "registered instrument" or "oral agreement accompanied by delivery of possession"; the courts read an unregistered writing for one year or less as valid (the writing evidences the oral agreement), a view accepted in Anthony v. Ittoop.
  • Execution by both parties — inserted 1929; reflects the bilateral nature of the lease (acceptance in s.105). A "patta" signed only by the lessor and a "kabuliyat" signed only by the lessee together satisfy the requirement.
  • Stamp — a lease is chargeable under Art. 35, Schedule I, Indian Stamp Act, 1899 (duty varies with term and whether premium is paid); an insufficiently stamped lease is inadmissible until impounded and penalty paid (s.35). A "leave and licence" agreement attracts lower duty (in Maharashtra, Art. 36A Bombay Stamp Act) — one more commercial reason for the label.

5. Licence — s.52 Indian Easements Act 1882; s.4 easement contrasted; incidents (ss.53–64)

s.52. "Licence" defined. — "Where one person grants to another, or to a definite number of other persons, a right to do, or continue to do, in or upon the immovable property of the grantor, something which would, in the absence of such right, be unlawful, and such right does not amount to an easement or an interest in the property, the right is called a licence."
ElementAnalysis
Grant by one person to another or "a definite number of other persons"A licence is personal — granted to identified persons (not to the public at large; a public right of way is not a licence). It is a personal privilege, hence non-transferable (s.56) and ends with the grantor's transfer of the property (s.59) or, generally, the licensee's death.
"Right to do, or continue to do, in or upon the immovable property of the grantor"Positive acts of user on another's land: to enter, to reside, to trade from a stall, to park, to put up a hoarding, to watch a film, to attend a hotel; "continue to do" covers a licence ratifying an existing user.
"Something which would, in the absence of such right, be unlawful"The licence is a defence to trespass — its whole legal function (Vaughan C.J. in Thomas v. Sorrell (1673): "A dispensation or licence properly passeth no interest, nor alters or transfers property in anything, but only makes an action lawful, which without it had been unlawful"). Subba Rao J.: "if a document gives only a right to use the property in a particular way or under certain terms while it remains in possession and control of the owner thereof, it will be a licence. The legal possession, therefore, continues to be with the owner of the property, but the licensee is permitted to make use of the premises for a particular purpose. But for the permission, his occupation would be unlawful."
"Does not amount to an easement or an interest in the property"The negative, residuary part of the definition — the two things a licence is not: (i) not an easement (s.4 — which needs a dominant and servient heritage and is annexed to land), and (ii) not an interest in property (so not a lease, mortgage, charge, profit à prendre). If the grant confers possession of the property with the right to exclude everyone including the grantor, an interest has passed and it is a lease, whatever the label.

5.1 Section 4 — easement contrasted

s.4. "Easement" defined. — "An easement is a right which the owner or occupier of certain land possesses, as such, for the beneficial enjoyment of that land, to do and continue to do something, or to prevent and continue to prevent something being done, in or upon, or in respect of, certain other land not his own." Dominant and servient heritages and owners — the land for the beneficial enjoyment of which the right exists is the dominant heritage and its owner the dominant owner; the land on which the liability is imposed is the servient heritage and its owner the servient owner. Explanation: "land" includes things permanently attached to the earth; "beneficial enjoyment" includes possible convenience, remote advantage and mere amenity; "to do something" includes removal and appropriation of any part of the soil or its produce (profit à prendre — e.g., right to take fish or grass). Illustrations: (a) right of way over B's land annexed to A's house; (b) right to go on B's land and take water; (c) right to stop B building so as to obstruct light; (e) right to graze cattle on B's land.

s.64Licensee's right to compensation where licence for consideration is revoked before the term or purpose without his fault.
Irrevocable and contractual licences. A licence for consideration for a fixed term may be revoked in breach of contract; the licensee's remedy is damages (s.64), and English law (Winter Garden Theatre v. Millennium Productions [1948] AC 173; Errington v. Errington) may restrain a wrongful revocation by injunction. In India, a licensee in settled possession cannot be thrown out by force — the licensor must sue (Krishna Ram Mahale v. Shobha Venkat Rao (1989) 4 SCC 131); a dispossessed licensee may sue under s.6 Specific Relief Act, 1963 within six months. Maharashtra has a dedicated summary remedy: s.24 Maharashtra Rent Control Act, 1999 (recovery of possession from a licensee on expiry of licence, with double damages) and compulsory registration of leave-and-licence agreements (s.55) — the legislative answer to the Quality Cut Pieces-type situation.

Caveat — the "nomenclature trap". Every case in this topic has a document calling itself a "licence"/"leave and licence" or a payment called "commission"/"compensation"/"licence fee". Conversely the words "landlord", "rent", "demise" or "sub-letting" used in a purported licence are strong pointers to a lease (D'Souza), but "demised premises" was explained away in Delta from context.

7. Tests for distinguishing a lease from a licence — the evolution

7.1 English background as received in the Indian cases

AuthorityProposition

7.2 The Indian synthesis

Associated Hotels of India v. R.N. Kapoor (1959) — Subba Rao J., para 27: the four propositions
  1. To ascertain whether a document creates a licence or a lease, the substance of the document must be preferred to the form;
  2. the real test is the intention of the parties — whether they intended to create a lease or a licence;
  3. if the document creates an interest in the property, it is a lease; but if it only permits another to make use of the property, of which the legal possession continues with the owner, it is a licence; and
  4. if under the document a party gets exclusive possession of the property, prima facie he is considered to be a tenant; but circumstances may be established which negative the intention to create a lease.
Sohan Lal Naraindas v. Laxmidas Raghunath Gadit (1971) 1 SCC 276 (as summarised in Quality Cut Pieces and Delta) Intention of the parties to an instrument must be gathered from the terms of the agreement examined in the light of the surrounding circumstances. The description given by the parties may be evidence of the intention but is not decisive. Mere use of words appropriate to the creation of a lease will not preclude the agreement operating as a licence; a recital that the agreement does not create a tenancy is also not decisive. The crucial test is whether the instrument is intended to create or not to create an interest in the property; if it is in fact intended to create an interest, it is a lease; if it does not, it is a licence. In determining this, exclusive possession is not decisive (Shah J.). Vaze J. treated this as the current Indian test and applied it to the stall-holders.
Rajbir Kaur v. S. Chokesiri & Co. (1989) 1 SCC 19 — Venkatachaliah J. (as extracted in Delta, para 22) Possessory licences exist; the distinction between a lease and a "possessory licence" is often "difficult to draw". The grant of exclusive possession is not conclusive; "the solution that would seem to have been found is, as one would expect, that it must depend on the intention of the parties". A person with no power to grant a lease (e.g., a tenant forbidden to sub-let) — his grant would be a licence (Rajbir Kaur para 32; Delta proposition 5 leaves final decision open). The "Court must have regard to the substance of the transaction and not its form".
Delta International v. Shyam Sunder Ganeriwalla (1999) — M.B. Shah J., para 17: the six-point synthesis (the current authoritative statement)
  1. To find out whether the document creates a lease or licence the real test is to ascertain the intention of the parties: whether they intended to create a lease or a licence.
  2. Intention is to be gathered from the document itself. If the document expressly and specifically states that the parties are not creating a lease, then that is normally decisive — unless it is pleaded and proved that the document is a camouflage to conceal the real transaction. The words alone are not decisive where the relevant test is not satisfied, but where the parties have a definite intention, effect must be given.
  3. Where there is no written document, or the document is ambiguous, the intention is to be gathered from the surrounding circumstances and conduct; there exclusive possession is the most relevant, though not conclusive, circumstance.
  4. Where the landlord is not a party to the document between his tenant and a third party, the landlord is not bound by the recital "licence"; he can prove that the transaction is in fact a sub-lease (Rajbir Kaur para 32; M.N. Clubwala). Conversely, as between the parties who executed the document, the recital binds.
  5. Where the grantor had no power to grant a lease (a sitting tenant barred from sub-letting), the grant would ordinarily be a licence — the court did not finally decide this.
  6. Where a document is capable of two constructions, one lawful and one unlawful, the lawful construction is to be preferred (applied via s.107 construction and the parties' knowledge that a sub-lease without consent was illegal).

8. The rent-control context — why the label matters; camouflage; burden of proof

Why litigants fight over the label. A "tenant" under a Rent Control Act cannot be evicted except on statutory grounds and can have "standard rent" fixed (Associated Hotels — s.7 Delhi & Ajmer-Merwara Rent Control Act 1947); civil-court eviction suits against a tenant are barred (D'Souza — s.56 Goa, Daman & Diu Act 1968; s.50 Delhi Rent Control Act 1958; s.28 Bombay Rent Act 1947). A tenant who sub-lets without consent commits a ground for his own eviction (s.14(1)(b) DRC Act; s.13 W.B. Act; s.13(1)(e) Bombay Act) — hence tenants like Dewar's/Delta and Laxmi & Co. insist that their grantees are licensees, while landlords (or grantees seeking protection) call them sub-tenants. A licensee gets no rent-control protection (except Maharashtra s.15A for licensees in occupation on 1-2-1973 — the very reason Vaze J. noted the stall-holders' silence before 1966).
"Contracting out" and camouflage. A statutory protection cannot be waived in advance; a device whose real purpose is to evade the Rent Act may be disregarded as a sham/camouflage (D'Souza: Rent Acts to be construed "to advance the object and suppress the mischief"; drafting skill cannot defeat protection). But — per Somma/Shell-Mex as applied in Quality Cut Pieces, and per Delta proposition 2 — parties free to choose may genuinely create a licence, and the mere fact that a licence avoids the Rent Act does not make it a sham; the party alleging camouflage must plead and prove it.

8.1 Burden of proof (Evidence Act ss.101–103) — Samir Kumar Chatterjee

  • Where the plaintiff seeks eviction on the plea that the defendant is a licensee, and the defendant pleads tenancy, the initial onus is on the plaintiff to prove that the defendant was inducted as a licensee (he who asserts the special relationship must prove it); after the plaintiff adduces evidence the onus shifts, and once evidence from both sides is in, the question of onus loses importance (Kalyan Chandra Sarkar-type reasoning; also s.102 Evidence Act — the burden lies on the person who would fail if no evidence were given).
  • A court that misplaces the onus and decides on that footing commits an error of law, justifying interference in second appeal (s.100 CPC) even against concurrent findings of fact (Dilbagrai Punjabi v. Sharad Chandra AIR 1988 SC 1858; Bhairab Chandra Nandan).
  • Whether a document creates a lease or licence is a mixed question of law and fact — the construction of the document is a question of law (D'Souza: not a pure finding of fact binding in second appeal; the earlier "concurrent findings" bar did not apply).
  • Evidence pointers: rent receipts / counterfoils (comparison of signatures by the court under s.73 Evidence Act is "hazardous" — Samir Kumar), postal receipts, school certificates, electoral rolls, ration cards showing residence; absence of rent receipts explained by fear of exposing an unlawful sub-tenancy.
  • Appellate duty: O.XLI r.31 CPC — the first appellate court must state points for determination, decision, reasons; failure vitiates its judgment (Samir Kumar).

CASE 1 — Associated Hotels of India Ltd v. R.N. Kapoor, AIR 1959 SC 1262 : (1960) 1 SCR 368

Bench & opinions S.K. Das, A.K. Sarkar and K. Subba Rao JJ. Three opinions: Subba Rao J. (lead — held lease; dissented on the hotel-room exemption), S.K. Das J. (agreed it was a lease; but held the rooms exempt), Sarkar J. (held rooms exempt; found it unnecessary to decide lease v. licence). Result: appeal allowed by majority on the s.2(b) exemption point; the lease–licence propositions of Subba Rao J. (with S.K. Das J. concurring) are the ratio for this topic.

Facts

  • The appellant company owned and ran the Imperial Hotel, New Delhi. The respondent R.N. Kapoor carried on the business of a hair-dresser under the name "Madam Janes". By a document dated 1 May 1949, styled a "deed of licence", the hotel gave him the use of two rooms — spaces in the Ladies' and Gentlemen's cloak rooms of the hotel for his hair-dressing saloon.
  • Terms of the deed (as recited in the judgment): (1) licence to use the space for one year from 1-5-1949, renewable; (2) the licensee to pay Rs 9,600 per year (later reduced by agreement to Rs 700 per month), whether business was carried on or not, in four quarterly instalments in advance; (3) the licensee to keep the premises in good condition, bear the cost of electricity and water through separate meters; (4) not to make alterations or additions without consent; (5) on default of any instalment the hotel could terminate without notice and charge 12% interest; (6) the hotel entitled to the whole of the payments for the year even if the licence was terminated early; (7) the licensee to use the premises only for the saloon; (8) the licensee could transfer the "licence" with the consent of the hotel.
  • Kapoor applied to the Rent Controller under s.7(1) of the Delhi and Ajmer-Merwara Rent Control Act, 1947 for fixation of standard rent, alleging the agreed Rs 9,600 was excessive. The hotel resisted: (a) Kapoor was only a licensee, not a tenant; and (b) in any event the rooms were part of a hotel and therefore not "premises" under s.2(b) of the Act, which excluded "a room in a dharamshala, hotel or lodging house".

Procedural history

ForumDecision
Rent Controller, DelhiHeld Kapoor a tenant and the rooms "premises"; fixed standard rent at Rs 94 per month.
District Judge (appeal)Reversed — the document was a licence; further the rooms were "rooms in a hotel" and exempt under s.2(b); Rent Controller had no jurisdiction.
Punjab High Court (Khosla J.) in revisionRestored the Rent Controller's order — the transaction was a lease and the rooms were not exempt.
Supreme Court (special leave)Appeal allowed by majority (S.K. Das and Sarkar JJ.) on the ground that the rooms were rooms in a hotel within s.2(b); Subba Rao J. dissented on that point. On lease/licence: Subba Rao and S.K. Das JJ. held lease; Sarkar J. did not decide.

Issues

  1. Whether the document dated 1-5-1949 created a lease or a licence.
  2. Whether the two rooms were "a room in a … hotel" excluded from "premises" under s.2(b) of the 1947 Act, so that the Rent Controller had no jurisdiction to fix standard rent.

Arguments

Appellant (hotel)
  • The document is expressly a "deed of licence"; the parties, both commercially experienced, deliberately chose that form; the payment is a licence fee, not rent.
  • The hotel never parted with legal possession: the spaces were inside its cloak rooms, within the hotel building and subject to hotel management; the user was restricted to a saloon — a permitted user of a portion of the hotel, akin to the shops and services a hotel provides for guests.
  • Restrictions (no alterations; specific purpose; termination without notice on default) are typical of a personal privilege.
  • Alternatively, under s.2(b) a "room in a hotel" is outside the Act; a hair-dressing saloon within the hotel is a room in a hotel; the Act intended to exclude hotels altogether from rent control because they are governed by other machinery (the hotel-rates part of the Act/other law).
Respondent (Kapoor)
  • Substance prevails over form: the document, whatever its label, gives him exclusive possession of defined rooms for a fixed term at a fixed periodical payment payable irrespective of business — every ingredient of s.105.
  • The obligations imposed — to maintain, to pay for electricity/water through his own meters, not to alter, forfeiture for non-payment with interest, and above all the right to transfer the "licence" — are covenants of a lease; a bare permission is personal and cannot be transferred (s.56 Easements Act).
  • The rooms are not "rooms in a hotel" in the sense of s.2(b): the exemption is for rooms let to guests as part of hotel accommodation; a saloon carried on by an outsider as an independent business is "premises" let for business.
  • Rent legislation is protective and must be construed to prevent evasion by drafting.

Judgment — Subba Rao J. on lease v. licence (paras 26–29 of the SCR report)

  • Statutory contrast. "There is a marked distinction between a lease and a licence. Section 105 … defines a lease … Under s.108 … the lessee is entitled to be put in possession of the property. A lease is therefore a transfer of an interest in land … the lessee gets that right to the exclusion of the lessor. Whereas s.52 of the Indian Easements Act defines a licence … Under the aforesaid section, if a document gives only a right to use the property in a particular way or under certain terms while it remains in possession and control of the owner thereof, it will be a licence. The legal possession, therefore, continues to be with the owner of the property, but the licensee is permitted to make use of the premises for a particular purpose. But for the permission, his occupation would be unlawful. It does not create in his favour any estate or interest in the property."
  • The dividing line. "There is, therefore, clear distinction between the two concepts. The dividing line is clear though sometimes it becomes very thin or even blurred. At one time it was thought that the test of exclusive possession was infallible and if a person was given exclusive possession of a premises, it would conclusively establish that he was a lessee. But there was a change and the recent trend of judicial opinion is reflected in Errington v. Errington…" He then reviewed Errington (Denning L.J.), Cobb v. Lane (Somervell and Romer L.JJ.) and Glenwood Lumber (Lord Davey) and observed that the Court of Appeal had again "swung back to the view" that exclusive possession is a strong indicator subject to contrary intention.
  • The four propositions (para 27) — set out in Part 7.2 above: substance over form; intention the real test; interest created = lease, permission with legal possession in owner = licence; exclusive possession → prima facie tenant unless negativing circumstances.
  • Application (para 28). Reading the document as a whole: the respondent was given possession of two rooms for a term; the payments were fixed monthly sums payable whether business was done or not — "the payment of a fixed sum irrespective of the business done is more consistent with the concept of rent"; the obligations to keep in good condition, to pay water and electricity through his own meters, the prohibition on alterations without consent and the right of forfeiture "are covenants usually found in a lease"; and the clause allowing him to transfer the right with consent is "destructive of any theory of licence". The hotel's contention that it retained possession because the rooms were within its building was rejected — "the respondent had exclusive possession of the rooms" and the circumstances did not negative the creation of a lease. Held: the document created a lease, notwithstanding its description.

Judgment — the s.2(b) "room in a hotel" point

JudgeReasoning
Subba Rao J. (dissenting on this point)The exemption covers rooms let out as part of the hotel business — accommodation for guests — not every room in a building which happens to house a hotel. He gave five illustrations (e.g., a room in a hotel building let to a bank or a bookseller with independent access) to show that a literal reading would produce absurdity; a saloon run by an independent businessman is not "a room in a hotel". On s.2(b), the test is twofold: (i) the room must be physically part of the hotel building, and (ii) its user must be connected with the purposes of the hotel. A hair-dressing saloon within the hotel premises is an amenity for hotel guests and thus satisfies both conditions; exempt.

Principles (ratio for this topic)
  1. The label of a document is not decisive; the substance and the intention of the parties gathered from the whole document and circumstances determine whether it is a lease or a licence.
  2. Lease = transfer of an interest in immovable property carrying exclusive possession to the exclusion of the lessor; licence = permission to use while legal possession and control remain with the owner.
  3. Exclusive possession raises a prima facie inference of tenancy, rebuttable by circumstances negativing intention to create a tenancy (family, friendship, service, hotel guest, etc.).
  4. Indicia of a lease include: fixed periodic payment irrespective of business, term certain, repair/utility obligations, forfeiture and interest clauses, and especially transferability of the right.
  5. Rent control exemptions must be applied to the actual premises let; the "room in a hotel" question is one of fact and construction (majority: physical location, with S.K. Das J. adding the purpose nexus).
Illustration for exam. X runs a five-star hotel and gives Y a "licence" of a shop in the hotel arcade for 3 years at Rs 2 lakh p.m. payable whether or not Y trades; Y has his own lock, meters and may assign with consent. Applying Associated Hotels, Y is a lessee. Whether the Rent Act applies depends on the local statute's hotel exemption and rent ceiling; under the Delhi Rent Control Act 1958, premises above Rs 3,500 p.m. are outside the Act in any event.

CASE 2 — Quality Cut Pieces v. M. Laxmi & Co., AIR 1986 Bom 359

Bench Bombay High Court, Vaze J. — a batch of suits/appeals by 47 stall-holders of a departmental store at Dadar, Bombay, claiming to be tenants/protected under the Bombay Rents, Hotel and Lodging House Rates Control Act, 1947. Result: stall-holders held licensees, except two (Nathani, stall 6; Ramjeevandas, stall 7) as to whom licence was not proved.

Facts

  • In 1946 the Departmental Service Stores Ltd (DSS) took a building at Dadar on a 10-year lease (with a renewal option) from the owner Ashar to run a departmental store — a large hall on the ground floor subdivided into stalls/counters for different trades (cloth, cut-pieces, hosiery, provisions, medicines, etc.). The DSS business and lease later devolved on M. Laxmi & Co. (the respondent/plaintiff) as the "management".
  • Each stall-holder was inducted under a printed "commission agreement" (the specimen is Exh. Z-196). Key terms: the stall-holder deposited Rs 1,000; paid the management a "commission" of 2% of gross sales, subject to a minimum of Rs 135 per stall per month; had to submit monthly statements of sales; the management fixed the business hours, ran advertising for the store as a whole, ran rebate/discount schemes binding on all stalls, employed common staff, watchmen, sweepers; the stalls had no shutters or locks of their own — the entire hall was closed by a single collapsible gate whose key was with the management; cl. 34 declared that the stall-holder was not a tenant and had no interest in the premises; the agreement was non-assignable; the management could terminate on notice.
  • The arrangement ran from 1946 to 1965 — nearly twenty years — during which no stall-holder ever claimed to be a tenant. On 1 January 1966 the management served notices revoking the licences. The stall-holders responded (letters dated 10-1-1966) for the first time asserting tenancy, and filed suits (9-2-1966) for declaration that they were tenants and for injunction; the management sued for possession. The suits were tried together; the trial court held them licensees; the stall-holders appealed.
  • Two stall-holders — Nathani (stall 6) and Ramjeevandas (stall 7) — occupied stalls on the road-facing side with independent access from the street and their own shutters; and there was evidence that some other occupants of the building (e.g., Dr Patkar) were recognised as tenants of the management.

Issues

  1. Whether the "commission agreements" created leases (sub-tenancies) or licences.
  2. Whether an arrangement deliberately framed as a licence in order to keep the occupants outside the Rent Act is on that account a sham to be disregarded.
  3. Whether the two road-facing stall-holders stood on a different footing.

Arguments

Stall-holders (appellants)
  • Each had been in exclusive occupation of a defined stall for twenty years, with his own goods, staff and customers; under Associated Hotels exclusive possession is prima facie tenancy.
  • The "commission" was in reality rent — a fixed minimum of Rs 135 payable regardless of sales; the 2% formula was merely a mode of computing rent; the Rs 1,000 "deposit" was a tenancy deposit.
  • The document was a device to evade the Bombay Rent Act; clause 34 ("not a tenant") is a self-serving recital and not decisive (Sohan Lal Naraindas); rent legislation must be construed to defeat such evasion.
  • The management's controls (hours, advertising) were no more than the rules of a market; a market-stall holder can be a tenant.
Management (respondent)
  • The store was a single integrated business run by the management under one roof and one gate; the stall-holders were participants under a franchise-like arrangement, paying a turnover-based commission, submitting sales statements and bound by store-wide policies — wholly inconsistent with a tenancy.
  • No stall-holder had possession in the legal sense — no lock, no key, no shutter; access only when the management opened the collapsible gate; the management's staff could and did enter every stall.
  • The agreements were expressly non-assignable and expressly negatived tenancy; the parties, traders dealing at arm's length, intended a licence; the English authorities (Shell-Mex v. Manchester Garages; Somma v. Hazelhurst) show that parties may lawfully choose a licence and that avoidance of the Rent Act does not make the arrangement a sham.
  • Twenty years of silence — no claim of tenancy until the revocation notice — shows the parties' contemporaneous understanding.

Judgment — Vaze J.

  • Governing test. The court accepted Sohan Lal Naraindas v. Laxmidas Raghunath Gadit (1971) as laying down the Indian test: intention gathered from the terms in the light of surrounding circumstances; the description is evidence but not decisive; exclusive possession is not decisive; the crucial question is whether the instrument was intended to create an interest in the property.
  • English authorities. Vaze J. drew on Shell-Mex & B.P. v. Manchester Garages (petrol station "licence" upheld; Lord Denning — "personal privilege" v. "interest in land"; Buckley L.J. — the presence of a right to exclusive possession is only one factor), Somma v. Hazelhurst (a couple sharing a room under separate "licences" — the Court of Appeal refused to treat a genuine licence as a sham merely because the parties chose it to avoid the Rent Act: "we can see no reason why an ordinary landlord … should not be able to grant a licence to occupy … if the parties intend it"), and Marchant v. Charters (Lord Denning M.R.: the question is whether the occupier has "a stake in the room or … only permission for himself personally to occupy" — a resident of a service bed-sitting room was a licensee). The judge reasoned that the same freedom to contract for a licence exists in India, subject to the court's scrutiny of substance.
  • Application to the store. The structure of the arrangement — commission on turnover with monthly returns, management's control of hours, advertising, rebates and staff, the single gate and absence of locks, the deposit, the non-assignability and clause 34 — showed that the stall-holders had a personal privilege to trade from a counter within the management's store, not an interest in a defined piece of immovable property. The stall was more like a "pitch" than a demised shop. The conduct of the parties for twenty years confirmed this: no stall-holder asserted tenancy until the licences were revoked. The court rejected the sham argument — a genuine licence framed to keep the occupier outside the Rent Act is not on that account a camouflage; it is for the party alleging sham to prove that the document does not reflect the real transaction.
  • The two exceptions. As to Nathani (stall 6) and Ramjeevandas (stall 7), the stalls faced the road, had independent access and shutters, were not dependent on the management's gate, and were in their exclusive possession; moreover the management had admittedly recognised other occupants (Dr Patkar) as tenants, showing that it did create tenancies in the building. The management had therefore failed to prove that these two were licensees; their appeals succeeded.
Principles
  1. Parties dealing at arm's length may genuinely create a licence of business space; the fact that a licence keeps the occupier outside rent control does not by itself make the document a sham — the party alleging camouflage must prove it.
  2. Exclusive possession is not decisive (Sohan Lal); the totality of the arrangement — control of access, hours, common services, turnover-linked payment, non-assignability, express negation of tenancy, and long conduct — may show a mere personal privilege.
  3. The same building can house both tenants and licensees; each occupier's status depends on his own facts — physically independent, lockable units with exclusive possession lean toward tenancy.
  4. Denning M.R.'s "stake in the room" question is a useful working test.
Modern parallels. Shop-in-shop counters in Shoppers Stop/Big Bazaar ("concessionaires" paying a percentage of sales), kiosks in a mall's common area, food-court counters, co-working desks (WeWork), Delhi Haat stalls, and mall "licence" agreements are structured on the Quality Cut Pieces model — turnover-based fee, operator control of hours and access, no assignability. Contrast a locked shop unit in the same mall let for 9 years at fixed rent — a lease.

CASE 3 — Capt. B.V. D'Souza v. Antonio Fausto Fernandes, AIR 1989 SC 1816 : (1989) 3 SCC 574

Bench Supreme Court — L.M. Sharma and S. Natarajan JJ.; judgment by Sharma J. Result: appeal allowed; document held a lease; appellant a month-to-month tenant; civil suit barred by s.56 of the Goa, Daman and Diu Buildings (Lease, Rent and Eviction) Control Act, 1968.

Facts

  • The respondent (plaintiff) owned a building in Goa. By a document Ex. 20, described as a "leave and licence" agreement, he allowed the appellant, Capt. D'Souza, to occupy the premises. The document, however, described the plaintiff as "Landlord hereinafter called the Licensor"; provided for a payment of Rs 350 per month described as "compensation"; clause 5 prohibited the appellant from "sub-letting, under-letting or parting with possession" of the premises; clause 15 made the agreement, initially for a term (one year), renewable at the will of the licensee on giving one month's notice; the appellant was in exclusive occupation. The appellant continued in occupation for several years after the initial term, paying the monthly amount.
  • The owner then served a notice terminating the "licence" and filed a suit in the civil court for possession, contending that the appellant was a mere licensee and therefore outside the Goa Rent Act (which barred civil-court eviction suits against tenants — s.56).
  • The trial court, the first appellate court and the High Court of Bombay (Panaji Bench) in second appeal all held that the document was a licence and decreed the suit; the High Court treated the question as a concluded finding of fact.

Issues

  1. Whether the question "lease or licence" on a written document is a pure question of fact immune from review in second appeal/under Art. 136.
  2. Whether Ex. 20 created a lease or a licence.
  3. If a lease, whether the civil court had jurisdiction.

Arguments

Respondent-owner
  • Three courts had concurrently found a licence; the Supreme Court should not reappraise (Art. 136 / s.100 CPC).
  • The parties chose the "leave and licence" form; the payment was "compensation", not rent; the term was one year; the intention was to give a personal permission.
  • Following Shell-Mex, exclusive occupation is not decisive; a licensee can be given exclusive occupation.
Appellant-occupier
  • Construction of a document is a question of law; the courts below misdirected themselves in law by treating the label as decisive.
  • The document itself betrays a tenancy: it calls the plaintiff "Landlord"; clause 5 forbids sub-letting — a prohibition meaningful only against a tenant, since a licensee cannot sub-let in any case; clause 15 gives the occupier a right of renewal at his own will — an interest, not a revocable permission; exclusive possession for years.
  • The arrangement was a pure business transaction between strangers, with no element of family, friendship or charity to negative the inference of tenancy from exclusive possession (Facchini v. Bryson; Associated Hotels).
  • The Rent Act was enacted to protect tenants from landlords who dictate terms at inception; a "licence" label is the standard device of evasion and must be disregarded.

Judgment — Sharma J.

  • Nature of the question. "The question whether a particular document creates a lease or a licence is one of law arising on the interpretation of the document; it is not a pure question of fact" — hence open in second appeal and before the Supreme Court. The High Court erred in treating it as a concluded finding of fact.
  • The test. Applied Associated Hotels: substance over form; intention; interest v. permission; exclusive possession prima facie tenancy. The court accepted that after Shell-Mex exclusive possession is "not conclusive", but noted that even Buckley L.J. in Shell-Mex treated it as an important relevant factor; Indian law continues to regard exclusive possession as a strong, though rebuttable, indicator.
  • Construction of Ex. 20. (i) The words "Landlord" and "sub-letting" are inconsistent with a licence — "a licensee is not entitled to sub-let and therefore a covenant against sub-letting is appropriate only in a lease"; (ii) the renewal clause at the will of the occupier confers a right in the nature of an interest; (iii) the payment, though called compensation, was a fixed monthly sum in the nature of rent; (iv) the appellant had exclusive possession; (v) there was no relationship, friendship or generosity — "the arrangement was purely a business one" — so nothing negatived the inference of tenancy.
  • Rent legislation. "The main purpose of enacting the rent statutes is to protect the tenant from the exploitation of the landlord, who being in a dominating position is capable of dictating his terms at the inception of the tenancy, and the Rent Acts must receive that interpretation which may advance the object and suppress the mischief. … the landlord cannot be allowed to defeat the protection by using words of his choice in the document" — a camouflage will be seen through. (This passage was later cited by the respondent in Delta and distinguished there as inapplicable between two companies of equal bargaining power.)
  • Status and jurisdiction. The one-year term having expired long before the notice, and the appellant having continued with the owner's acceptance of the monthly payments, he was a tenant from month to month (s.116 read with s.106 TPA). Under s.56 of the Goa Act no civil court could entertain a suit for eviction of a tenant; the suit was therefore not maintainable and was dismissed.
Principles
  1. Lease-or-licence on a written instrument is a question of law (construction) — open in second appeal and under Art. 136.
  2. Internal indicia — "landlord", a covenant against sub-letting, renewal at the occupier's will, fixed monthly payment — reveal a lease despite the "leave and licence" heading and the word "compensation".
  3. Exclusive possession remains a strong pointer; it is displaced only by circumstances such as family, friendship, generosity or service.
  4. Rent Acts are construed purposively; drafting devices cannot defeat tenant protection where the substance is a tenancy (the "camouflage" doctrine later systematised in Delta).
  5. A fixed-term "licensee" who holds over with payments accepted becomes a monthly tenant; civil courts lose jurisdiction where the Rent Act vests eviction in special forums.
Delhi example. A Delhi flat owner signs a "leave and licence" with a paying occupant for 11 months at Rs 3,000 p.m. "compensation", with clauses forbidding sub-letting and permitting renewal on the occupant's request; the occupant stays for six years with payments accepted. On D'Souza, he is a monthly tenant; as rent is below Rs 3,500, the Delhi Rent Control Act applies and the owner must seek eviction before the Rent Controller on a s.14 ground.

CASE 4 — Samir Kumar Chatterjee v. Hirendra Nath Ghosh, AIR 1992 Cal 129

Bench Calcutta High Court, A.K. Nayak J., second appeal (s.100 CPC) by the defendant against concurrent decrees of eviction. Result: appeal allowed; suit dismissed — plaintiff failed to prove that the defendant was a licensee.

Facts

  • The plaintiff-respondent Hirendra Nath Ghosh was a tenant (rent Rs 70 p.m.) of premises No. 19, Gopal Chandra Chatterjee Road, Calcutta; later, on 22-4-1985, he purchased the premises.
  • His case: in September 1979, out of compassion, he allowed the defendant Samir Kumar Chatterjee (said to be in distress) to occupy one room as a gratuitous licensee for four months; the defendant did not vacate; the licence was revoked and the suit for eviction was filed treating him as a licensee (a proceeding to which the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act would not apply).
  • Defendant's case (written statement): he was never a licensee; his father had been a tenant in the premises much earlier; the defendant had been in occupation long before 1979; he was a tenant/sub-tenant under the plaintiff at Rs 20 per month, having paid Rs 2,000 as advance; he occupied a room, kitchen and toilet exclusively; no rent receipts were given because the plaintiff, himself a tenant, feared that written receipts would expose an unauthorised sub-tenancy to the landlord.
  • Evidence: plaintiff examined himself and two witnesses (PW 2 and PW 3 — held to be interested persons) and produced rent-receipt counterfoils to show that he alone paid rent; the defendant produced postal receipts/letters addressed to him at the premises and a school certificate of 1965 showing his residence there long before 1979, and examined witnesses including his former tutor Arun Sarkar.
  • Both the trial court and the first appellate court decreed the suit, placing the onus on the defendant to prove tenancy and disbelieving his witnesses; the appellate court compared signatures on the counterfoils itself.

Issues

  1. On whom lies the burden of proof when the plaintiff sues for eviction alleging licence and the defendant pleads tenancy?
  2. Whether the courts below erred in law in appreciating the evidence (misplaced onus; ignoring the plea of prior occupation; comparison of signatures; reliance on interested witnesses) so as to justify interference with concurrent findings in second appeal.
  3. Whether the defendant was a licensee or a tenant/sub-tenant.

Arguments

Plaintiff (respondent)
  • Concurrent findings of fact by two courts cannot be disturbed in second appeal.
  • The defendant had no rent receipt whatsoever; the counterfoils show the plaintiff alone paid rent to the landlord; the plea of a Rs 2,000 advance was unsupported; a person claiming tenancy must prove it.
  • Permissive occupation for a short period out of compassion is a classic gratuitous licence; on revocation the licensee must leave.
Defendant (appellant)
  • The plaintiff who asserts the special relationship of licensor–licensee must prove induction as licensee (s.101–102 Evidence Act); the courts wrongly cast the onus on the defendant.
  • Documentary evidence — school certificate (1965), postal receipts — proved residence at the premises long before the alleged 1979 induction, destroying the plaintiff's story; the courts ignored the pleaded case of prior occupation by the father and the defendant.
  • Exclusive occupation of a room with kitchen and toilet, payment of Rs 20 p.m. and Rs 2,000 advance are consistent only with sub-tenancy; absence of receipts is naturally explained by the plaintiff's fear of exposure.
  • The appellate court did not comply with O.XLI r.31 CPC and undertook a hazardous comparison of signatures under s.73 Evidence Act; its judgment is vitiated in law.

Judgment — Nayak J.

  • Onus. "Where the plaintiff/landlord sues for eviction on the plea that the defendant is a licensee, and the defendant/appellant asserts that he is a tenant, the initial onus is upon the plaintiff/landlord to prove that the defendant was inducted as a licensee. The question of onus, however, loses much of its importance when both sides have adduced evidence; once the plaintiff tenders evidence showing that the defendant was inducted as a licensee, the onus shifts to the defendant." The courts below "misplaced the onus on the defendant/appellant to prove his case that he was a tenant" and approached the evidence on that wrong footing — an error of law.
  • Evidence. (i) The courts ignored the written statement's plea that the defendant and his father were in occupation long before 1979, and the documentary proof (1965 school certificate; postal receipts) supporting it — which alone was enough to discredit the plaintiff's story of a 1979 compassionate induction; (ii) PW 2 and PW 3 were interested witnesses; (iii) the rent counterfoils were suspicious and, in any event, only showed the plaintiff's payments to his landlord, not the absence of a sub-tenancy; the appellate court's own comparison of signatures was "hazardous" (s.73 Evidence Act should be used with caution and not as the sole basis); (iv) the defence witnesses (e.g., the tutor Arun Sarkar) were disbelieved for insufficient reasons; (v) the absence of rent receipts was satisfactorily explained by the plaintiff's own position as a tenant apprehensive of sub-letting charges.
  • Nature of occupation. Exclusive occupation of a room with kitchen and toilet for years, coupled with payment, is consistent with a sub-tenancy and inconsistent with a four-month gratuitous licence; nothing negatived the inference of tenancy.
  • Second appeal. Concurrent findings can be interfered with when they are vitiated by misplacing the onus, ignoring material evidence/pleadings, or non-compliance with O.XLI r.31 CPC (relying on Dilbagrai Punjabi v. Sharad Chandra and Bhairab Chandra Nandan). The appeal was allowed and the suit dismissed.
Principles
  1. In an eviction suit founded on licence, the plaintiff bears the initial burden of proving induction as a licensee; misplacing the burden is an error of law.
  2. Where there is no written document, the nature of occupation is decided on conduct and circumstances — long exclusive possession of self-contained accommodation with payment points to tenancy (cf. Delta proposition 3).
  3. Documentary evidence of long residence, and a plausible explanation for the absence of receipts (unauthorised sub-tenancy), can outweigh oral testimony of interested witnesses and self-generated counterfoils.
  4. Appellate courts must comply with O.XLI r.31 CPC and be cautious in comparing signatures under s.73 Evidence Act.

CASE 5 — Delta International Ltd v. Shyam Sunder Ganeriwalla, AIR 1999 SC 2607 : (1999) 4 SCC 545

Bench Supreme Court — M.B. Shah and S. Rajendra Babu JJ.; judgment by Shah J. Result: appeals allowed; the deed of 18-7-1970 held a leave and licence, not a lease; Division Bench judgment set aside; Single Judge's decree restored.

Facts

  • Premises: 4-D, Council House Street, Calcutta — a petrol service station. Original owner Abhiram Mullick sold to Mallika Investment Co. (P) Ltd. Dewar's Garage (India) Ltd was a monthly tenant under Mallika and ran a petrol pump on the premises; Dewar's later amalgamated with Delta International Ltd (the appellant).
  • By a document dated 18 July 1970, Dewar's granted ESSO Standard Eastern Inc. (later Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Ltd, after nationalisation) a "leave and licence" of the premises with the petrol pump, equipment, fittings and fixtures. Key clauses: cl. 12 — the parties expressly agreed that the document was not a lease and did not create the relationship of landlord and tenant, and ESSO would not claim tenancy; cls. 13–17 — Dewar's would try to obtain the landlord's (Mallika's) consent and, if obtained, execute a sub-lease in ESSO's favour, whereupon ESSO would purchase the equipment in the Second Schedule for Rs 2,50,000 within one year; cl. 18 — "licence fee" of Rs 3,950 per month for the "demised premises", payable Rs 23,700 six months in advance; cl. 9 — licensor at liberty to revoke on default; ESSO could run the business in the licensor's name, could grant a sub-licence to a dealer, could instal machinery; the licensee was not liable for outgoings (rates, taxes) which remained with Dewar's; the licensee to keep plant in repair and insure; the deed used the words "successors and assigns".
  • The landlord's consent for a sub-lease was never obtained; no sub-lease was executed; the Rs 2,50,000 was never paid. HPCL put Shyam Sunder Ganeriwalla (respondent) in as its dealer to run the station.
  • In 1985 Delta terminated the licence and filed Suit No. 491 of 1985 on the Original Side of the Calcutta High Court for possession and damages/mesne profits at Rs 20,000 per month. Under s.14(1) of the West Bengal Premises Tenancy Act, 1956, a tenant could not sub-let without the landlord's prior written consent.

Procedural history

ForumDecision
Single Judge, Calcutta HC (Original Side)Decreed the suit — the document was a licence; possession to Delta.
Division Bench (2-12-1997)Reversed — exclusive possession had been handed over; the words "demised premises", "successors and assigns" showed a lease (sub-tenancy); suit dismissed.
Supreme CourtAppeals allowed; licence; Single Judge's order restored.

Issue

Whether the agreement dated 18-7-1970 between Dewar's Garage (a sitting monthly tenant) and ESSO was a lease (sub-lease) creating an interest in the premises, or a leave and licence — the answer determining whether Delta could recover possession.

Arguments

Appellant Delta (Mr Ashok Desai, Sr Adv.)
  • Under s.14(1) W.B. Premises Tenancy Act Dewar's had no power to sub-let without Mallika's written consent; a grantor who cannot lease can at most license (Rajbir Kaur para 32; Cobb v. Lane); the court should prefer the lawful construction.
  • Clause 12 expressly negatives tenancy; clauses 13–17 show that a sub-lease was contemplated only in future, conditional on consent, and with a separate price for the equipment — proving the parties knew the present deed was not a sub-lease.
  • The deed was a business licence to run a petrol station set up by the licensor, in the licensor's name, with the licensor bearing outgoings and keeping revocation rights (cl. 9) — indicia of licence; Uttamchand v. S.M. Lalwani (licence to run a business with premises is not a lease); M.N. Clubwala (notice clause not inconsistent with licence).
  • Between two large companies with legal advice there is no question of exploitation or camouflage; no camouflage was pleaded by the respondent.
Respondent Ganeriwalla / HPCL (Mr D.P. Gupta, Sr Adv.)
  • Construe the document as it is, disregarding legal consequences (Buckley J.; Lewison, Interpretation of Contracts); its terms show a lease: "demised premises" in cl. 18 (thrice), "successors and assigns", a long-term arrangement with advance payments, right to sub-licence and to instal machinery.
  • Exclusive possession had been handed over and enjoyed for 15 years; per Associated Hotels, Sohan Lal and D'Souza that is prima facie tenancy.
  • D'Souza: Rent Acts must be construed to suppress the mischief; the "leave and licence" label is a device.
  • Tulsi v. Paro: a licensee has no right to exclusive possession; long exclusive possession negates licence.

Judgment — Shah J.

  • Review of authorities (paras 10–16). The court traced Associated Hotels (four propositions), Sohan Lal Naraindas (exclusive possession not decisive), M.N. Clubwala v. Fida Hussain (stall-holders in a market under owner's control were licensees; notice clause not inconsistent with licence; s.62(c) Easements Act), Rajbir Kaur (possessory licences; no litmus test; substance over form; a person without power to lease creates only a licence), Booker v. Palmer, Errington, Cobb v. Lane, Uttamchand v. Lalwani.
  • The six propositions (para 17) — set out in Part 7.2: intention the real test; gathered from the document unless camouflage is pleaded and proved; absent a document, exclusive possession most relevant; landlord not party to the deed not bound by the recital; grantor without power to lease → licence (left open); prefer lawful construction where two are possible.
  • Application (paras 18–27).
    1. No camouflage pleaded. The respondent never pleaded that cl. 12 was a sham; it was executed between two companies fully aware of their rights. "In cases where a contract for licence is executed by handing over exclusive possession of the premises, the distinguishing line between the lease and the licence is absolutely thin. In such cases, the terms of the document are to be read as they are and it would be unreasonable to draw an inference that the parties intended to create a relationship of landlord and tenant despite express contrary terms in the deed which are binding between the parties" (para 21).
    2. Lawful construction. Accepting Buckley J.'s dictum that a contract is construed disregarding consequences, the court added: "when the terms of the document are ambiguous and are holding a double intendment then the meaning which is lawful is to be preferred. … It was known to them that without prior consent, creation of sub-tenancy would be illegal. Hence, it would not be correct to arrive at a conclusion which is contrary to the law and the express terms of the agreement" (para 23).
    3. "Demised premises" and exclusive possession. Exclusive possession "is not the sole indicia". The word "demise" indicates lease or conveyance depending on the terms (Stroud; Butterworths; Halsbury para 107; Young & Co. v. Liverpool Assessment Committee — "demise" prima facie establishes exclusive occupation but is rebuttable). Here the phrase was used only in cl. 18 for computing the "licence fee" (Rs 3,950 p.m.; Rs 23,700 six-monthly advance) — "If the intention of the parties was to create a lease, then the word 'rent' would have been easily used at all the places." "Demised premises" included plant, fixtures and the petrol station business (paras 24–25).
    4. Other clauses point to licence. The licence was to run a petrol station set up by the licensor; a sub-lease was reserved for the future subject to landlord's consent; the licensee bore no outgoings; it had to keep plant in repair and insure the business; it could carry on business in the licensor's name — "indicates that the premises were not let out, otherwise there was no question of permitting the use of the licensor's name"; cl. 9 allowed revocation on default; cl. 16 tied the purchase of equipment for Rs 2,50,000 to a future sub-lease that never happened. Clauses pointing the other way (sub-licence; instal machinery) had to be read in the context of a business licence (para 25).
    5. D'Souza distinguished (para 26): the rent-act mischief rationale presupposes an exploiting landlord dictating terms; here "Dewar itself was inducted by a tenant … the parties were under the impression that they would obtain the consent of the landlord for granting a sub-lease" — no exploitation between two companies "knowing full well their rights". Tulsi v. Paro distinguished — no document there; 20 years' possession and revenue records. M.N. Clubwala applied: "the decisive consideration is the intention of the parties … ascertained on a consideration of all the relevant provisions in the agreement. In the absence, however, of a formal document the intention … must be inferred from the circumstances and conduct".
    6. Conclusion (para 27–28). "The document contemplates three types of agreements, one, that of a leave and licence; secondly, in case a consent is obtained from the [landlord], for execution of a sub-lease which would create an interest in the property as a sub-tenant and thirdly, in case of a sub-lease, for purchase of equipment, fitting and fixtures at a price of Rs 2,50,000. The second and third parts of the agreement never came into operation. Hence … we hold that the agreement dated 18-7-1970 is a deed of 'leave and licence' and not a 'lease'." Appeals allowed; Division Bench judgment of 2-12-1997 set aside; Single Judge's order in Suit No. 491 of 1985 restored.
Principles
  1. The current Indian test: intention of the parties, gathered from the document; an express negation of tenancy is normally decisive between the parties unless camouflage is pleaded and proved.
  2. Exclusive possession is "one of the most relevant factors" only where there is no (or an ambiguous) document; it "loses its importance" against clear contrary terms.
  3. Where the grantor lacks power to lease (a tenant barred from sub-letting) and both parties know it, the lawful construction (licence) is preferred.
  4. Words like "demise", a notice/revocation clause, or a right to sub-licence are construed in context; the label of the payment ("licence fee" v. "rent") is a relevant pointer.
  5. The D'Souza purposive rent-act approach applies where there is inequality of bargaining power/exploitation, not to arm's-length commercial deeds.
  6. A landlord who is not a party to his tenant's "licence" deed is not bound by its recitals and may prove a sub-letting (proposition 4) — so Delta's victory against ESSO/HPCL would not bind Mallika.
Reconciling the five cases. Associated Hotels and D'Souza: owner-grantors, documents whose own terms (transferability; sub-letting bar; renewal) betrayed a lease, plus rent-control evasion → lease. Quality Cut Pieces and Delta: business arrangements between commercial parties, genuine express negation of tenancy, grantor's retained control or lack of power to lease, no sham proved → licence. Samir Kumar: no document; onus on the party alleging licence; exclusive possession + payment → tenancy. The unifying thread is Delta para 17.

12. DU semester examination questions — model answers

The DU LL.B. May/June 2025 LB-204 paper has no direct lease–licence question (Q.7(b), on a lease renewal clause and perpetuity, belongs to Topic 7). The questions below follow the recurring DU pattern for this topic (essay + problem from the prescribed cases).
Q.1 (Essay). "The dividing line between a lease and a licence is clear, though sometimes it becomes very thin or even blurred." Define lease and licence, distinguish between them, and discuss the tests laid down by the Supreme Court for determining whether a document creates a lease or a licence. (DU pattern; 20 marks)
Model answer.
Definitions. s.105 TPA: a lease is a transfer of a right to enjoy immovable property for a certain time (express/implied) or in perpetuity, for premium and/or rent, accepted by the transferee. s.52 Easements Act: a licence is a right granted to a person to do something in or upon the grantor's immovable property which would otherwise be unlawful, the right not amounting to an easement or an interest in the property. A lease is thus a transfer of an interest (a leasehold estate) carrying exclusive possession (s.108(b)); a licence is a personal permission that leaves legal possession with the owner and is merely a defence to trespass (Thomas v. Sorrell).
Distinction (tabulate): interest v. permission; exclusive possession v. occupation; consideration essential v. not; transferable/heritable (s.108(j)) v. personal (s.56); binds transferee (s.109) v. not (s.59); determination only under s.111 with relief against forfeiture v. revocable (s.60) subject to two exceptions and deemed revocation (s.62); lessee can sue trespassers v. licensee cannot; registration (s.107) v. none; tenant protected by Rent Acts v. licensee not.
Tests. (1) Older English exclusive-possession test (Glenwood Lumber). (2) Shift to intention (Errington; Cobb v. Lane). (3) Associated Hotels v. R.N. Kapoor — Subba Rao J.'s four propositions: substance over form; intention is the real test; interest = lease, permission with possession in owner = licence; exclusive possession → prima facie tenant unless negativing circumstances. Applied to hold a "deed of licence" of hotel saloon rooms a lease (fixed payment irrespective of business, repair/meter covenants, transferability "destructive of licence"). (4) Sohan Lal Naraindas — exclusive possession not decisive; label and recital not decisive; crucial question is whether an interest was intended. (5) D'Souza — construction is a question of law; "landlord", covenant against sub-letting, renewal at occupier's will show a lease; Rent Acts construed to suppress the mischief; exclusive possession strong where no family/friendship. (6) Quality Cut Pieces — parties can genuinely contract for a licence; avoiding the Rent Act is not per se sham; store control, turnover commission and single gate → licence. (7) Delta International — six propositions: intention from the document; express negation of tenancy decisive unless camouflage pleaded and proved; without a document exclusive possession most relevant; non-party landlord not bound; grantor without power to lease → licence; lawful construction preferred. (8) Samir Kumar Chatterjee — where no document, onus on the party asserting licence.
Conclusion. The line is conceptually clear (interest v. permission) but factually thin because possession can be handed over under both. Indian law resolves it by intention, read primarily from the document (with camouflage as the safety valve), and, failing a document, from conduct where exclusive possession is the leading pointer.
Q.2 (Problem). H, a hotel company, executes a "deed of licence" allowing K to use two rooms in the hotel for a hair-dressing saloon for one year at Rs 9,600 payable in advance "whether business is done or not". K must keep the rooms in good repair, pay for electricity and water through his own meters, make no alterations without consent, and may transfer the licence with H's consent; on default H may terminate without notice. K applies for fixation of standard rent under the Rent Act. H contends (i) K is a licensee, (ii) the rooms are "rooms in a hotel" exempt from the Act. Decide.
Model answer. The facts are Associated Hotels of India v. R.N. Kapoor. (i) Lease or licence: apply the four propositions. The nomenclature "licence" is not decisive; K has exclusive possession of defined rooms for a term; the payment is a fixed sum irrespective of business (rent, not fee); the covenants to repair, to pay utilities through his own meters, not to alter, and forfeiture with interest are lease covenants; the power to transfer is "destructive of any theory of licence" (a licence is personal — s.56 Easements Act). Nothing (family, friendship, service, hotel-guest control) negatives the inference from exclusive possession. Hence K is a lessee/tenant (Subba Rao and S.K. Das JJ.). (ii) Exemption: on the 1947 Delhi Act, the majority (Sarkar J. — plain meaning: any room in a hotel building; S.K. Das J. — room physically in the hotel whose user is connected with the hotel's purpose, a saloon being an amenity for guests) held the rooms exempt, Subba Rao J. dissenting (exemption confined to rooms let as hotel accommodation). Result: the Rent Controller lacked jurisdiction to fix standard rent. Under the present Delhi Rent Control Act 1958, "premises" excludes "a room in a hotel or lodging house" (s.2(i)) and premises with rent above Rs 3,500 p.m. are outside the Act (s.3(c)), so today K would in any event not get standard rent; but as a lessee he retains all rights under Chapter V of the TPA (notice under s.106, relief against forfeiture, etc.).
Q.3 (Problem). D, a monthly tenant of a petrol-pump site in Calcutta, cannot sub-let without the landlord's written consent. D executes a "leave and licence" with an oil company E: clause 12 states the document is not a lease and E will not claim tenancy; clauses 13–17 provide that if the landlord consents D will execute a sub-lease and E will buy the equipment for Rs 2.5 lakh; clause 18 fixes a "licence fee" of Rs 3,950 p.m. for the "demised premises"; E may run the pump in D's name; D bears all outgoings. Consent is never obtained. Fifteen years later D revokes and sues for possession. E pleads sub-tenancy relying on exclusive possession and the words "demised premises". Decide. Would the position differ if the head-landlord sued D for eviction on the ground of sub-letting?
Model answer. Delta International v. Shyam Sunder Ganeriwalla. Apply the six propositions: intention from the document; cl. 12 expressly negatives tenancy and E has not pleaded/proved camouflage — between two companies with legal advice there is no exploitation, so D'Souza's purposive approach does not apply; exclusive possession is "one of the most relevant factors" only in the absence of a clear document and "loses its importance" against express terms; "demised premises" is read in context — used only to compute a "licence fee" (had a lease been intended, "rent" would have been used) and it included plant and business; the business was run in D's name, D bore outgoings, revocation on default (cl. 9) is consistent with licence (s.62(c); Clubwala); a sub-lease was expressly reserved for the future subject to consent and never materialised; and since both knew a sub-lease without consent was illegal (s.14(1) W.B. Act), the lawful construction is preferred. Held: licence; D recovers possession. Second part: the head-landlord is not a party to the deed and is not bound by the "licence" recital (prop. 4; Rajbir Kaur para 32; Clubwala); he may prove on the facts (exclusive possession for consideration; parting with possession) that D sub-let/parted with possession and seek eviction under the Rent Act. The label protects D against E but not against his landlord.
Q.4 (Problem). O lets P into his house under a "leave and licence" agreement for one year at Rs 350 p.m. "compensation". The document calls O "landlord (hereinafter called the licensor)", forbids P from "sub-letting, under-letting or parting with possession", and allows P to renew at his will on one month's notice. P stays for six years paying monthly. O sues P in the civil court for possession as a licensee. P contends he is a tenant and the civil court has no jurisdiction under the Rent Act. Decide.
Model answer. B.V. D'Souza v. Antonio Fausto Fernandes. The construction of the document is a question of law. Applying Associated Hotels: substance over form; the document itself reveals a tenancy — "landlord"; a covenant against sub-letting is appropriate only to a lease (a licensee cannot sub-let at all); renewal at the occupier's will confers an interest; the monthly "compensation" is rent; P has exclusive possession and the transaction is purely commercial with no friendship or family element to negative tenancy (Facchini v. Bryson). The Rent Act is construed to advance its object; the landlord cannot defeat it by words of his choice. The one-year term having expired and payments having been accepted for years, P is a month-to-month tenant by holding over (ss.116, 106). The civil suit is barred (s.56 Goa Act / s.50 DRC Act) and must be dismissed; O must proceed before the Rent Controller on a statutory ground. Distinguish Delta: here O is the owner-landlord with power to lease and superior bargaining position, and the document's internal terms contradict the label.
Q.5 (Problem). T, a tenant of a house, sues S for eviction alleging that in 1979 he permitted S, out of compassion, to occupy one room gratuitously for four months and S has not left. S pleads that his father and he lived there since the 1960s, that he is a sub-tenant at Rs 20 p.m. having paid Rs 2,000 advance, and occupies a room, kitchen and toilet exclusively; he produces a 1965 school certificate and postal receipts with the address. Both courts decree the suit, holding that S failed to prove tenancy and disbelieving his witnesses. In second appeal, advise S.
Model answer. Samir Kumar Chatterjee v. Hirendra Nath Ghosh. (1) Onus: where the plaintiff sues on the footing of licence and the defendant pleads tenancy, the initial burden is on the plaintiff to prove induction as a licensee (ss.101–102 Evidence Act); the courts below misplaced the onus — an error of law justifying interference under s.100 CPC despite concurrent findings (Dilbagrai Punjabi). (2) Evidence: the plea and documents of residence since 1965 destroy the story of a 1979 induction and were ignored; the plaintiff's witnesses are interested; his rent counterfoils only prove his own payments and the appellate court's own signature comparison (s.73) is hazardous; the absence of receipts is explained by T's fear of exposing an unauthorised sub-letting; O.XLI r.31 CPC was not complied with. (3) Nature of occupation: absent a document, intention is gathered from conduct — exclusive possession of self-contained accommodation for years with payment is a tenancy, not a four-month gratuitous licence (Delta prop. 3). Advice: the second appeal should succeed and the suit be dismissed; S is a sub-tenant and can be evicted only under the Rent Act (and the head-landlord, not T, may have a ground against T for sub-letting).
Q.6 (Short notes — any two). (a) Lease in perpetuity and periodic lease; (b) Section 106 TPA after the 2002 amendment; (c) Absolute and derivative lease; (d) Licence and easement distinguished; (e) "Exclusive possession is not decisive" — Quality Cut Pieces.
Model answer (outline). (a) s.105 permits a lease "in perpetuity" — permanent, heritable, transferable, determinable only by forfeiture/surrender/merger; presumed only on clear words or long user (Sivayogeswara Cotton Press); perpetual renewal clauses valid (Ram Baran Prasad). Periodic lease — year to year/month to month, each period certain, arising by contract, s.106 presumption or holding over (s.116); ends by notice (s.111(h)). (b) s.106: agricultural/manufacturing → yearly, 6 months' notice; others → monthly, 15 days; 2002 changes — period runs from receipt (sub-s.2), short notice cured if suit filed after statutory period (sub-s.3), written signed notice served by post/personal/family-servant/affixation (sub-s.4); Rent Act overrides (Dhanapal Chettiar). (c) Absolute lease by owner; derivative (sub-lease) by lessee out of his term, cannot exceed it, falls with head lease, no privity with head-lessor; s.108(j) permits sub-lease unless contract/statute (Rent Acts) forbids; assignment v. sub-lease. (d) Easement (s.4): right in rem annexed to dominant heritage over servient heritage; passes with land; created by grant/prescription; licence (s.52): personal, no dominant heritage, non-transferable (s.56), revocable (s.60), not binding on transferee (s.59); s.52 itself excludes easements. (e) Vaze J. applied Sohan Lal and English cases (Shell-Mex, Somma, Marchant): stall-holders with defined counters but under single gate, store hours, turnover commission, non-assignability, 20 years' acquiescence were licensees; parties can genuinely choose a licence; only the two road-facing independent stalls were tenancies — illustrating that possession is one factor within the overall intention.

13. Mnemonics and rapid revision

"T-I-P-C-A" — elements of a lease (s.105): Transfer of right to enjoy · Immovable property · Period certain or perpetual · Consideration (premium/rent) · Acceptance by lessee.
"P-I-N" — licence (s.52): Permission to do something on grantor's land · otherwise Illegal (trespass) · Not an easement, not an interest.
"S-I-I-E" — Associated Hotels' four propositions: Substance over form · Intention real test · Interest = lease / permission = licence · Exclusive possession prima facie tenancy.
"I-D-E-N-P-L" — Delta's six propositions: Intention · from Document (unless camouflage pleaded & proved) · Exclusive possession most relevant when no document · Non-party landlord not bound · No Power to lease → licence · Lawful construction preferred.
Case one-liners: Associated Hotels — saloon in hotel, "licence" but LEASE (transferable). Quality Cut Pieces — store stalls, commission, one gate → LICENCE (except road-facing two). D'Souza — "landlord… no sub-letting" → LEASE; civil suit barred. Samir Kumar — no document; onus on plaintiff; room+kitchen+toilet → tenancy. Delta — tenant can't sub-let; cl. 12; two companies; no sham → LICENCE.
Twelve lines to remember. 1. Lease = interest + exclusive possession + consideration + term; licence = personal permission, possession stays with owner. 2. Label never decisive; intention is; but an express negation binds the parties unless sham is pleaded and proved (Delta). 3. Exclusive possession: prima facie tenancy (Associated Hotels); not decisive (Sohan Lal); most relevant when no document (Delta). 4. Negativing circumstances: family, friendship, charity, service, hotel-type control, grantor's lack of power. 5. Indicia of lease: transferability, sub-letting bar, repair/tax burden, fixed rent regardless of business, renewal at grantee's will. 6. Indicia of licence: grantor's control (gate, hours, staff), turnover fee, business in grantor's name, grantor bears outgoings, non-assignability. 7. Rent Acts: tenant protected; licensee not; purposive construction where landlord dominates (D'Souza) but not between equal companies (Delta). 8. Onus: plaintiff alleging licence proves it (Samir Kumar); construction of document is a question of law (D'Souza). 9. s.106: yearly/6 months for agriculture-manufacture; monthly/15 days otherwise; 2002 — from receipt, short notice cured, written & served. 10. s.107: >1 year / yearly / yearly rent → registered deed signed by both; else oral + possession. 11. Licence incidents: ss.53 (who), 56 (non-transferable), 59 (transferee not bound), 60 (revocable; 2 exceptions), 62 (deemed revoked), 63–64 (time & compensation). 12. Easement (s.4) needs dominant + servient heritage and is in rem; s.52 excludes it from licence.
