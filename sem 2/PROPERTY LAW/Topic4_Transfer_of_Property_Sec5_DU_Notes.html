1. Section 5 — text and scheme

Section 5. "Transfer of property" defined. — In the following sections "transfer of property" means an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to himself, or to himself and one or more other living persons; and "to transfer property" is to perform such act. In this section "living person" includes a company or association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not, but nothing herein contained shall affect any law for the time being in force relating to transfer of property to or by companies, associations or bodies of individuals. [Words "or to himself" inserted and second paragraph added by the Transfer of Property (Amendment) Act 20 of 1929.]

1.1 Scope of the definition — "in the following sections"

  • The definition governs Chapter II onwards (ss.5–137). It is the gateway to the whole Act: s.6 (what may be transferred), s.7 (who may transfer), s.9 (oral transfer), s.10–12 (restraints), s.13–20 (unborn persons, perpetuities, vesting), s.53 (fraudulent transfer), s.53-A (part performance) and the specific transfers — sale (s.54), mortgage (s.58), lease (s.105), exchange (s.118), gift (s.122), actionable claims (s.130).
  • It is not exhaustive of every legal transfer — it defines only what the Act means by transfer. Transfers by operation of law (inheritance, insolvency, forfeiture, court sale) are outside s.5 and, by s.2(d), outside the Act except s.57 and Chapter IV.
  • The Act deals with transfers inter vivos — between living persons — as distinct from testamentary dispositions (governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925) and intestate succession (personal laws).
  • The preamble states the Act is "to amend the law relating to the transfer of property by act of parties" — the phrase that explains the whole definition.

4. "Living person" — natural and juristic persons

4.1 Natural persons and competency (s.7)

The transferor must be alive and competent: s.7 — "Every person competent to contract and entitled to transferable property, or authorised to dispose of transferable property not his own, is competent to transfer such property either wholly or in part, and either absolutely or conditionally, in the circumstances, to the extent and in the manner, allowed and prescribed by any law for the time being in force." Read with Contract Act ss.11–12: major (Indian Majority Act, 1875 — 18 years; 21 if a guardian was appointed under the Guardians and Wards Act before 1999 amendment), sound mind, not disqualified by law (e.g., insolvent, alien enemy, disqualified proprietor under Court of Wards Acts). A minor's transfer is void (Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose, 1903 PC), though a minor may be a transferee (donee, purchaser) since no obligation is imposed on him.

4.2 Juristic persons — second paragraph of s.5

The 1929 amendment clarified that "living person" includes a company, association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not — e.g., companies (Companies Act, 2013, s.9 — separate legal entity, perpetual succession), registered societies (Societies Registration Act, 1860), co-operative societies, statutory corporations (LIC, DDA, MCD), universities, partnership firms (for transactions, via partners — Partnership Act s.14, 19), trade unions, a Hindu undivided family (acting through its karta). The saving clause — "nothing herein contained shall affect any law … relating to transfer of property to or by companies…" — preserves special rules: a company's power to transfer is limited by its memorandum and the Companies Act (e.g., s.180 — sale of undertaking requires special resolution); charges must be registered under s.77 Companies Act; society property vests in the governing body (s.5, 1860 Act).

5. "Conveys" — creation of a new title or interest

"Conveyance means any act by the transferor by which certain new titles or interests are created in favour of the transferee … Before transfer of property, the transferee does not have that particular interest. After the transfer … the transferee gets that particular interest" (Exam Capsule). The word is wide enough to cover every form of assurance inter vivos — absolute or limited, immediate or future, for consideration or gratuitous. The crucial negative: if no new interest passes, there is no transfer, however the document is labelled. This single idea explains why partition, release, surrender, relinquishment, family arrangement, charge and easement are not "transfers" under s.5, and it is the analytical core of V.N. Sarin and Mohar Singh.

6. "In present or in future"

The phrase qualifies the verb "conveys", not the noun "property". A living person may convey property so that the transferee's interest arises now (a sale) or at a future date / on a contingency (a lease to commence next year; a gift to A for life and then to B — B's vested remainder; a conditional transfer under ss.19, 21, 25). What cannot be done is to convey property that does not yet exist or does not yet belong to the transferor: "future property" (next year's crop of a field the transferor does not own; an expected legacy; a share of a living person's estate — s.6(a) spes successionis). A purported transfer of future property operates, at most, as a contract to transfer (enforceable in equity when the property comes into existence — Holroyd v. Marshall) or attracts the estoppel of s.43 where the transferor misrepresented his title (Topic 5, Jumma Masjid).

Valid — conveyance operating in future
  • A leases his shop to B for 5 years from 1 Jan next year (existing property; future commencement).
  • A gifts his house to B for life, remainder to C (C's interest is vested now, enjoyment postponed).
  • A transfers to B on condition that B marries within 3 years (s.21 contingent interest in existing property).

  • Heir apparent sells "the property I will inherit from my father" (s.6(a)) — void.
  • A sells "all the fish I catch next season" — a contract only.
  • Transfer by a Hindu reversioner of his expectancy during the widow's lifetime — void (Jumma Masjid, subject to s.43).
Link with the Will cases. In Ramaiah the respondent argued that "a bequest under a Will is nothing but a 'transfer of property in future'" within s.5. The Karnataka High Court rejected this: the phrase "in future" refers to a living person conveying existing property with postponed operation, not to a disposition that takes effect only on the transferor's death and vests nothing meanwhile; "to treat a devise under a will as a transfer of an existing property in future, is contrary to all known principles relating to transfer of property and testamentary succession." The Bombay dictum in Dr. Anant Trimbak Sabnis (relied on in Kenneth Solomon) that a bequest is "a transfer in its generic sense" was in the rent-control context of "parting with possession", not s.5.

7. "Property"

The Act does not define "property". Judicially it bears the widest meaning: "the word 'property' includes all legal rights of a person except his personal rights which constitute his status or personal condition" (G.C. Jain J. in Kenneth Solomon, para 7, adopting Salmond). It includes: (i) corporeal things — land, buildings, goods; (ii) incorporeal rights — a lessee's right of enjoyment (Kenneth Solomon — tenancy rights are "immovable property" and pass under a residuary bequest of "all my moveable and immoveable properties"), a mortgagee's interest, an actionable claim (s.3), goodwill, copyright, shares, a decree; (iii) interests in property — ownership, life interest, remainder, reversion, easement (only with the dominant heritage). s.6 then carves out what may not be transferred — spes successionis, right of re-entry, easement in gross, restricted personal interest, right to future maintenance, mere right to sue, public office, pension, interests opposed to their nature or to public policy, untransferable tenancies. See also s.8 — a transfer passes all the interest the transferor can pass, with legal incidents (Topic 1).

8. "To one or more other living persons, or to himself"

  • Transferee must be a living person at the date of transfer (or, for interests created under s.13, in existence when the prior interest ends). A transfer to an unborn person directly is void; a company in the course of incorporation cannot take (pre-incorporation contracts bind only if adopted — Specific Relief Act ss.15(h), 19(e)).
  • "Or to himself" (inserted 1929) — enables a person to transfer property to himself in another capacity: e.g., the owner declares himself trustee of the property (Indian Trusts Act, 1882, s.6 — a trust may be created by the author "transferring the trust-property to the trustee", and the author may himself be the trustee); a karta partitioning to himself; a partner converting personal property into partnership property (Partnership Act s.14). Before 1929 it was doubted whether a man could "convey" to himself.
  • "To himself and one or more other living persons" — creation of co-ownership or joint trusteeship, e.g., A conveys his house to A and B as tenants-in-common.
  • Transferee's competency is not required — a minor or lunatic can be a transferee (donee/purchaser), because s.7 speaks only of the transferor; however a minor cannot be a lessee where the lease imposes obligations (a minor's covenant is void — Mohori Bibee), nor a mortgagor.

Example. Sunita executes a registered gift deed of her Noida flat to her son on Monday — the flat is his from Monday and she cannot take it back. Had she instead executed a Will leaving him the flat, she remains owner, may sell it to a stranger on Tuesday (the bequest then fails), may tear up the Will on Wednesday, and only if she dies with the Will unrevoked does the son take — by succession, not by transfer.

10. Status of partition of joint family property

10.1 Nature of coparcenary ownership

Under Mitakshara law the coparceners hold the joint family property with community of interest and unity of possession: no coparcener can say "this room is mine"; each has an undefined, fluctuating interest in the whole, which enlarges on deaths and diminishes on births. Gajendragadkar C.J. in Sarin (para 10): "each coparcener has an antecedent title to the said property, though its extent is not determined until partition takes place." Partition (Hindu law — by unequivocal declaration of intention, by agreement, by arbitration, by suit; Hindu Succession Act, 1956, ss.6, 30) is therefore the process that defines and severs those interests: "in lieu of the property allotted to individual coparceners they, in substance, renounce their right in respect of the other properties; they get exclusive title to the properties allotted to them and as a consequence, they renounce their undefined right in respect of the rest."

10.2 Why partition is not a "transfer" under s.5

  1. No new title is conveyed — Girja Bai v. Sadashiv Dhundiraj (AIR 1916 PC 104): "Partition does not give him a title or create a title in him; it only enables him to obtain what is his own in a definite and specific form for purposes of disposition independent of the wishes of his former co-sharers."
  2. Transformation, not transfer — "that joint title is by partition transformed into separate titles of the individual coparceners" (Sarin, para 10); "partition really means" a change from joint enjoyment to enjoyment in severalty.
  3. Exchange of joint rights — "a partition is not actually a transfer of property but would only signify the surrender of a portion of a joint right in exchange for a similar right from the other co-sharer or co-sharers" (Mohar Singh, para 9 — co-owners, not coparceners).
  4. Consistent with tax law — CIT v. Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel (AIR 1965 SC 866): oral partition of HUF property is "not a transfer in the strict sense" for s.16(3) Income-tax Act, 1922 (now s.64; Income-tax Act s.47(i) expressly excludes HUF partition from "transfer" for capital gains).

10.3 The contextual qualification — partition is a "transfer" for some statutes

The Supreme Court in Sarin was careful to "confine our decision to the narrow question" of s.14(6) DRC Act, acknowledging that "there is preponderance of judicial authority in favour of the view that a partition is a transfer for the purpose of s.53 of the Transfer of Property Act" (a collusive partition designed to defeat creditors may be avoided) and that for s.17(1)(b) Registration Act a partition deed is an instrument that "purports to create … right, title or interest" (so a written partition of immovable property worth Rs 100+ must be registered; an oral partition or a memorandum recording a past partition need not — Roshan Singh v. Zile Singh, AIR 1988 SC 881). The upshot: whether partition is a "transfer" depends on the purpose and context of the statute in question — the word carries its strict s.5 meaning (no) for rent-control acquisition bars and income-tax clubbing, but a wider meaning where the object is to prevent fraud or ensure public record.

CASE 1 — V.N. Sarin v. Ajit Kumar Poplai & Anr.

AIR 1966 SC 432 : (1966) 1 SCR 349 · Supreme Court of India · P.B. Gajendragadkar C.J. (for the Bench) · Appeal by special leave from the Punjab High Court (Delhi Bench)

Theme Is allotment of coparcenary property to a coparcener on partition an "acquisition by transfer" — s.14(6) Delhi Rent Control Act, 1958, read with s.5 TPA?

Facts

  • A bungalow at Racquet Court Road, Civil Lines, Delhi belonged to a joint Hindu family consisting of Mr B.S. Poplai (respondent 2, the father/karta) and his two sons, Major Ajit Kumar Poplai (respondent 1) and Vinod Kumar Poplai.
  • The appellant V.N. Sarin had been inducted as a tenant of part of the bungalow by the father, before partition, at Rs 80 p.m.
  • On 17 May 1962 the three coparceners partitioned the coparcenary property; the tenanted premises fell to the share of respondent 1.
  • Respondent 1 applied to the Rent Controller for the appellant's eviction under s.14(1)(e) DRC Act — bona fide requirement for his own residence and that of his dependent wife and children — impleading the appellant and his father.
  • The appellant resisted on three grounds: (i) respondent 1 was not his landlord — he was unaware of the partition; (ii) no bona fide need; (iii) respondent 1 had acquired the premises "by transfer" (the partition), so s.14(6) barred a s.14(1)(e) application until five years had elapsed from acquisition.

Procedural history

Rent Controller: respondent 1 exclusive owner and landlord by virtue of partition; s.14(6) not attracted — acquisition by partition is not acquisition by transfer; but bona fide need not proved — petition dismissed. Rent Control Tribunal: agreed on landlord status and s.14(6); reversed on bona fide need — eviction ordered. Punjab High Court (second appeal): upheld the Tribunal; on s.14(6) held that property allotted on partition is not "acquired by transfer". Appeal to the Supreme Court by special leave — the only question being the construction of s.14(6).

Statutory provisions

DRC Act s.14(1) — notwithstanding anything in any other law or contract, no order for recovery of possession shall be made in favour of the landlord against a tenant; proviso — the Controller may order recovery on the grounds in clauses (a)–(l); clause (e): premises let for residence required bona fide by the landlord for occupation as a residence for himself or his family, he being the owner and having no other reasonably suitable accommodation. s.14(6) — "Where a landlord has acquired any premises by transfer, no application for the recovery of possession of such premises shall lie under sub-section (1) on the ground specified in clause (e) of the proviso thereto, unless a period of five years has elapsed from the date of the acquisition." s.2(e) — "landlord" means a person who for the time being is receiving or is entitled to receive the rent of any premises…

Issue

Whether the partition of coparcenary property among coparceners, resulting in the allotment of the tenanted premises to respondent 1, can be said to be an "acquisition by transfer" within s.14(6) DRC Act — which depends on whether partition is a "transfer" in the sense of s.5 TPA.

Arguments

Appellant tenant (Mr Purshottam)

  • When an item of undivided family property is allotted to one coparcener on partition, the allotment in substance amounts to a transfer of that property to him — before partition he did not own it exclusively; after partition he does; so he "acquired" it "by transfer".
  • The preponderance of judicial authority treats partition as a "transfer" for s.53 TPA (fraudulent transfers), and the definition in s.5 — "an act by which a living person conveys property … to one or more other living persons" — is wide enough to cover it.
  • Under s.17(1)(b) Registration Act a partition deed is a non-testamentary instrument which "purports to create … right, title or interest" — so for the Registration Act too partition creates title; there is no reason to treat it differently under s.14(6).
  • The policy of s.14(6) — protecting tenants against a new landlord's bona fide-need claim for five years — applies equally when the landlord changes by partition.

Respondent landlord

  • The object of s.14(6) is to prevent landlords from transferring premises to purchasers as a device to evict tenants; it presupposes a purchaser who had no title before and becomes landlord by the transfer — a stranger. A coparcener is not a stranger; he had an antecedent title in the whole property.
  • Partition is not a conveyance: Girja Bai v. Sadashiv Dhundiraj (PC 1916) — partition "does not give him a title or create a title in him; it only enables him to obtain what is his own in a definite and specific form".
  • The Supreme Court in CIT v. Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel (AIR 1965 SC 866) had already held that a partition is not a transfer for the Income-tax Act's clubbing provisions.
  • Whatever the position under s.53 TPA or the Registration Act (each with its own object), s.14(6) must be construed by its own purpose.

Decision and reasoning (Gajendragadkar C.J.)

  1. Policy of s.14(6) (para 9). "The object which this provision is intended to achieve is to prevent transfers by landlords as a device to enable the purchasers to evict the tenants." A landlord unable to prove bona fide need might sell to a purchaser who could; the legislature made "an unqualified and absolute provision" to defeat that device, even at the cost of occasional hardship to genuine purchasers.
  2. Nature of coparcenary and partition (para 10). "Community of interest and unity of possession are the essential attributes of coparcenary property; and so, the true effect of partition is that each coparcener gets a specific property in lieu of his undivided right in respect of the totality of the property of the family … they, in substance, renounce their right in respect of the other properties; they get exclusive title to the properties allotted to them." Partition "involves the transfer of joint enjoyment … into an enjoyment in severalty." "Each coparcener has an antecedent title … though its extent is not determined until partition." Hence "that joint title is by partition transformed into separate titles" — not easy to hold that partition "must necessarily mean transfer". Girja Bai quoted.
  3. Section 53 and Registration Act arguments (paras 11–13). The Court noted the authority treating partition as a transfer for s.53 (which depends on s.5) and for s.17(1)(b) Registration Act, but declined to be governed by them: "we propose to confine our decision to the narrow question … the construction of Section 14(6)."
  4. Construction of s.14(6) (para 13). "What Section 14(6) provides is that the purchaser should acquire the premises by transfer and that necessarily assumes that the title to the property which the purchaser acquires by transfer did not vest in him prior to such transfer." A coparcener's property "did in a sense belong to him before". On partition "there is no doubt a change of the landlord … but the said change is not of the same character as the change which is effected by transfer of premises to which Section 14(6) refers. In regard to cases falling under Section 14(6), a person who had no title to the premises and in that sense, was a stranger, becomes a landlord by virtue of the transfer." Before partition the landlord was the undivided family (respondent 1, his father and brother); after it, respondent 1 alone. "It would be unreasonable to hold that allotment … as a result of partition is an acquisition of the said property by transfer."
  5. Keshavlal (para 14). There, self-acquired property thrown into the hotchpot and then orally partitioned among wife and minor son was held not an "indirect transfer" under s.16(3)(a)(iii)–(iv) Income-tax Act, 1922 — "a partition is not a transfer" in that context; the same considerations "apply with equal force" to s.14(6).
  6. Result (para 15). Appeal dismissed with costs; eviction order stands; s.14(6) no bar.

Principles

  1. Partition of Hindu coparcenary property is not a transfer: it is the transformation of an antecedent joint title into separate titles — a division/definition of existing rights, not a conveyance of new ones.
  2. A "transfer" (s.5 sense) presupposes that the transferee had no prior title; the transferee is a "stranger" to the property.
  3. The meaning of "transfer" is contextual: partition may be a "transfer" for s.53 TPA and s.17 Registration Act (anti-fraud/public-record purposes) yet not for s.14(6) DRC Act or income-tax clubbing.
  4. Rent-control "acquisition by transfer" bars aim at device transfers to purchasers, not at internal family re-arrangements.
  5. A coparcener to whom tenanted premises are allotted becomes landlord (s.2(e)) forthwith and may sue for eviction on bona fide need without waiting five years.
Example. The Sharma HUF (father + two sons) owns a Model Town house whose ground floor is tenanted. On partition in 2025 the ground floor falls to the younger son, who needs it for his own residence. He may file a bona fide-need petition immediately; the tenant cannot invoke the five-year bar (Sarin). Had the father instead sold the ground floor to a friend in 2025, the friend must wait until 2030.

CASE 2 — Kenneth Solomon v. Dan Singh Bawa

AIR 1986 Del 1 · Delhi High Court · G.C. Jain J. · Second appeal from the Rent Control Tribunal (order dated 28-10-1976)

Theme Are tenancy rights "property" that pass under a Will? Is a Will a "transfer" under s.5? Does bequeathing tenancy rights amount to "parting with possession" under s.14(1)(b) Delhi Rent Control Act?

Facts

  • Dr (Mrs) C.L. Sury was the contractual tenant of House No. 72, Babar Road, New Delhi under the respondent-landlord Dan Singh Bawa at Rs 37.82 p.m. She died in October 1967.
  • By a Will dated 31 March 1957 she had, in a residuary clause, bequeathed "all my moveable and immoveable properties, whatsoever, however, and wheresoever situated at the time of my death … to be equally divided by my Trustees among my two nephews: 1. Kenneth Solomon … 2. Pannel Richard Solomon". The Will made no specific mention of the tenancy.
  • On 22 April 1968 the landlord petitioned for eviction of the appellant Kenneth Solomon under proviso (b) to s.14(1) DRC Act, alleging that the tenant had left no heir and had in her lifetime parted with possession of the premises in favour of the appellant without the landlord's written consent.
  • Appellant's defence: the contractual tenancy had not been determined; the tenancy rights devolved on him and his brother under the Will, or alternatively on him as Dr Sury's nearest kinsman/heir (nephew).

Procedural history

Additional Rent Controller (18-12-1973): tenancy rights were not bequeathed by the Will; the appellant, as nephew, inherited them as heir; inheritance is not parting with possession — petition dismissed. Rent Control Tribunal (28-10-1976): reversed — the tenancy rights were bequeathed under the residuary clause, and the bequest amounted to parting with possession; eviction ordered. Second appeal to the High Court.

Issues

  1. Whether the tenancy rights were disposed of under the Will (construction of the residuary clause; whether a tenancy is "property").
  2. Whether bequeathing tenancy rights by Will amounts to "otherwise parted with the possession" within proviso (b) to s.14(1) — and how this relates to "transfer" in s.5 TPA.

Arguments

Appellant (Mr Vohra)

  • The Will nowhere mentions the tenancy; tenancy rights under a monthly tenancy are personal and were not intended to pass under a general residuary clause. If not bequeathed, they devolved by inheritance (appellant being the nearest heir) — and devolution by inheritance is not a volitional parting with possession (Nathu v. Devi Singh).
  • Even if bequeathed, making a Will is not a transfer: s.5 TPA covers only transfers inter vivos by a living person; a Will is revocable, ambulatory and vests nothing during the tenant's life. There was thus no act by the tenant "parting with possession" during her lifetime; what happened on her death was succession, outside proviso (b).
  • Proviso (b) is penal (forfeiture of statutory protection) and must be strictly construed against the landlord.

Respondent landlord

  • A lease is a transfer of an interest in immovable property (s.105 TPA); tenancy rights are therefore "immoveable property" and fall squarely within "all my moveable and immoveable properties"; nothing in the Will shows an intention to exclude them.
  • "Parted with possession" is not defined and is wider than "sublet" or "assigned"; it means relinquishing legal possession to a non-party to the lease. A bequest is a voluntary, volitional act of the tenant which, on her death, vests the tenancy in strangers to the lease — unlike intestate succession, which is involuntary. The Delhi Division Bench in Nathu v. Devi Singh (AIR 1966 Punj 266) expressly treated parting with possession by will as within the mischief; the Bombay High Court in Anant Trimbak Sabnis (AIR 1980 Bom 69) held a bequest of tenancy to be a "transfer in its generic sense".
  • Whether or not a Will is a "transfer" under s.5 TPA is beside the point — the Rent Act uses its own, wider expression.

Decision and reasoning (G.C. Jain J.)

  1. Construction of the Will; tenancy as property (paras 6–7). A Will is construed like any other document to find the testator's intention. A lease under s.105 TPA "is a transfer of a right to enjoy immoveable property … The right of enjoyment contemplated by this Section is an interest in the immoveable property." "The word 'property' includes all legal rights of a person except his personal rights which constitute his status or personal condition. The tenancy rights would definitely be included in the words 'all my moveable and immoveable properties'." Nothing indicated an intention to exclude them; the tenancy devolved on the appellant (and his brother) under the Will.
  2. Meaning of "parted with possession" (paras 8–10). Proviso (b) set out. "Parted with" means "to relinquish" (Chambers); Stroud (citing Stening v. Abrahams [1931] 1 Ch 470) — a covenant not to part with possession is broken only if the lessee entirely excludes himself from legal possession. Hence: "giving the legal possession acquired under the lease to a person who was not a party to the lease agreement … there must be vesting of possession … in another person by divesting himself not only of physical possession but also of a right to possession."
  3. Nature of a Will (para 11). Succession Act s.2(h) quoted; a Will is revocable and "ambulatory until the death of the testator … Till that event it is only an expression of intention … But the moment the testator dies it has the effect of vesting the property … in the devisee. At that point of time it would have the same effect as a transfer of possession by sale or mortgage. The process of parting with possession thus starts on the execution of the will but matures only on the death of the testator." The vesting of tenancy rights on death "would amount to parting with possession within the meaning of … proviso (b)."
  4. Authorities (paras 12–13). Nathu v. Devi Singh (Delhi DB, under s.13(1)(b) of the 1952 Act): "What is hit … is a volitional transfer by a tenant without the consent of the landlord. If on the death of a person holding contractual tenancy the suit premises come into the hands of the heirs … that is not an intentional or volitional transfer … The case of parting with possession by will is, however, clearly envisaged". Dr Anant Trimbak Sabnis v. Vasant Pratap Pandit (Bombay DB, s.15 Bombay Rent Act): a bequest "becomes effective only after the death of the testator and is liable to be revoked … This by itself however cannot make it anything but transfer. Even the restricted concept of 'transfer' inter vivos in Section 5 of the T.P. Act contemplated its becoming effective at some future date … Bequest does result in the passing of the property from the testator to the legatee … It is none-the-less, a transfer in its generic sense."
  5. Section 5 TPA and the ruling (para 15). "'Transfer of property' according to … Section 5 … means an act by which a living person conveys property in present or in future to one or more other living persons … True, these words exclude transfer by will, for a will operates after the death of the testator. The act of making a will in itself would not attract the provisions contained in proviso (b). No landlord can claim eviction, during the lifetime of the tenant, on the ground that the tenant had made a will disposing the tenancy rights … it can be revoked at any time. By itself it does not vest the legal possession in the devisee. However, there is no escape from the conclusion that by his voluntary act the tenant parts with the possession of the tenancy premises though from the date of his death in case the will remains unrevoked." Dr Sury, by bequeathing the tenancy, had parted with possession within proviso (b); the landlord was entitled to evict. Appeal dismissed.

Principles

  1. Tenancy (leasehold) rights are an interest in immovable property and hence "property" capable of devolving by Will under a general residuary clause.
  2. A Will is not a "transfer of property" under s.5 TPA — s.5 is confined to acts of living persons operating inter vivos; a Will is ambulatory, revocable and vests nothing during life.
  3. Nevertheless, for the rent-control expression "otherwise parted with possession" (DRC Act s.14(1)(b)), a bequest of tenancy rights is a volitional act of the tenant which, maturing on death, vests legal possession in a non-party to the lease — a ground for eviction. Intestate devolution on heirs is not.
  4. No cause of action arises during the tenant's lifetime merely from the making of the Will.
  5. Statutory expressions ("transfer", "parting with possession") take their colour from the statute's object — a rent-control anti-transfer clause is wider than s.5.
Critique / later law. The reasoning that a revocable Will "starts" the parting with possession is contestable and sits uneasily with Ramaiah (Karnataka) which insists a Will creates no right whatever during life. The result is better explained by the volitional character of testamentary succession as contrasted with intestacy (Nathu). Note also the 1988 amendment to DRC Act s.2(l): on a residential tenant's death the tenancy devolves only on specified heirs (spouse, son/daughter, parents, daughter-in-law) living with the tenant, and for limited periods — Gian Devi Anand v. Jeevan Kumar (AIR 1985 SC 796) held statutory tenancies heritable; the amendment restricted this.
Example. Tenant T of a Karol Bagh flat wills "all my property" to her nephew N who lives elsewhere. During T's life the landlord can do nothing. On T's death N claims the tenancy. Under Kenneth Solomon, the vesting in N is a "parting with possession" without written consent — a ground for eviction under s.14(1)(b). Had T died intestate and N been her heir, there would be no ground (involuntary devolution) — subject now to s.2(l)'s list of protected heirs.

CASE 3 — Mohar Singh v. Devi Charan & Ors.

(1988) 3 SCC 63 : AIR 1988 SC 1365 · Supreme Court of India · M.N. Venkatachaliah J. (for the Bench) · Appeal by special leave from the Allahabad High Court (Civil Misc. Writ No. 2280 of 1979, decided 28-3-1980)

Theme Can a co-owner who becomes exclusive owner of one of two jointly-let shops by partition decree evict the tenant from that shop alone? s.109 TPA (rights of lessor's transferee) and the rule against splitting the tenancy; whether partition is a "transfer" for s.109.

Facts

  • Respondent 1 Devi Charan was tenant of two adjacent shops under a single lease from two co-owners, Jado Ram (3/8th share) and Asha Ram (5/8th share).
  • The appellant Mohar Singh purchased Jado Ram's 3/8th share; Asha Ram's 5/8th share passed through an intermediary to Gyan Chand.
  • In a civil suit for partition between Gyan Chand and the appellant, a decree ended the co-ownership: one shop was allotted to the appellant as exclusive owner (the suit shop); the other to Gyan Chand.
  • The appellant sought release of the suit shop for his own bona fide need under s.21 of U.P. Act 13 of 1972 before the prescribed authority. The prescribed authority ordered release and possession; the District Judge, Muzaffarnagar dismissed the tenant's appeal (Rent Control Appeal No. 48 of 1978). Bona fide need thus stood concluded by concurrent findings.
  • In writ, the Allahabad High Court set aside the eviction: the transfer of a portion of the accommodation substituted two lessors for one; one of two joint lessors cannot sue for a portion; splitting the tenancy is impermissible without the tenant's consent. The High Court did not consider the effect of the partition decree.

Issues

  1. Whether the assignee of part of the reversion (owner of one of two shops let under one lease) can seek eviction from that part without the other lessor joining — i.e., whether the rule against splitting the integrity of a tenancy applies to him.
  2. Whether s.109 TPA — which speaks of the lessor "transferring" the property or part of it — applies where the exclusive ownership arose by partition, partition being "no transfer" under s.5.

Arguments

Appellant landlord

  • The rule against splitting a tenancy binds the original lessor because of his own contract; s.109 TPA is a statutory exception: the transferee of a part of the reversion "shall possess all the rights … of the lessor as to the property or part transferred" — he may enforce the lease as to his part, with attornment arising by operation of law, and without the tenant's consent (Kannyan v. Alikutti, AIR 1920 Mad 838 FB).
  • By the partition decree he became sole owner of the suit shop; the same High Court in Ram Chandra Singh v. Ram Saran (AIR 1978 All 173) had held s.109 applicable to partition; the single Judge was bound by it.
  • Concurrent findings of bona fide need should not have been disturbed in writ jurisdiction on a point the High Court decided without considering the partition.

Respondent tenant (Shri Uma Dutta)

  • It is trite law that a landlord cannot split the unity and integrity of a tenancy and recover part of the demised premises (Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy, AIR 1918 PC 102). The tenancy of the two shops was one and indivisible; without the tenant's consent, neither co-owner (nor his successor) could evict from one shop alone.
  • s.109 applies only where the lessor "transfers" the property or part; "partition is no 'transfer'" under s.5 read with s.109 — it is merely an adjustment of pre-existing joint rights; so whatever flows from a transfer under s.109 "cannot be predicated of a partition".
  • Therefore the appellant could at most sue jointly with the other co-owner for the whole, not alone for a part.

Decision and reasoning (Venkatachaliah J.)

  1. The rule and its statutory exception (para 7). "It is a trite proposition that a landlord cannot split the unity and integrity of the tenancy and recover possession of a part of the demised premises from the tenant. But Section 109 of the Transfer of Property Act provides a statutory exception to this rule and enables an assignee of a part of the reversion to exercise all the rights of the landlord in respect of the portion respecting which the reversion is so assigned … There is no need for a consensual attornment. The attornment is brought about by operation of law. The limitation on the right of the landlord against splitting … inhering in the inhibitions of his own contract, does not visit the assignee of the part of the reversion. There is no need for the consent of the tenant for the severance of the reversion and the assignment of the part so severed." Wallis C.J. in Kannyan v. Alikutti quoted: the original lessor cannot give notice for part; but where he "has parted in whole or in part with the reversion in part of the demised premises … such an assignment effects a severance, and entitles the assignee on the expiry of the term to eject the tenant from the land covered by the assignment."
  2. Partition and s.109 (paras 9–10). "It is true that a partition is not actually a transfer of property but would only signify the surrender of a portion of a joint right in exchange for a similar right from the other co-sharer or co-sharers. However, some decisions of the High Courts tend to the view that even a case of partition is covered by Section 109 and that, in any event, even if the section does not in terms apply the principle of the section is applicable as embodying a rule of justice, equity and good conscience." The Court did not finally decide the point, but noted that the Allahabad High Court itself in Ram Chandra Singh v. Ram Saran had held s.109 attracted to partition, that the single Judge was bound by it (or should have referred the matter to a Division Bench), and that its correctness was not challenged: "We should, we think apply the same rule to this case. Several other High Courts have also taken this view."
  3. Result (para 11). Appeal allowed; High Court order set aside; order of the Third Additional District Judge, Muzaffarnagar (release/possession) restored; no costs.

Principles

  1. Partition is not a transfer of property in the strict s.5 sense — it is "the surrender of a portion of a joint right in exchange for a similar right" (SC dictum, extending Sarin from coparcenary to co-ownership generally).
  2. Yet the benefit of s.109 — the right of a transferee of part of the reversion to enforce the lease against the tenant as to that part, with attornment by operation of law and no need for the tenant's consent — is available to a co-owner allotted the tenanted portion on partition, either because s.109 applies to partition (Ram Chandra Singh) or on the principle of justice, equity and good conscience.
  3. The prohibition on splitting the integrity of a tenancy binds only the original lessor (contractual inhibition); severance of the reversion by transfer or partition legitimately splits the tenancy.
  4. Again, "transfer" is read purposively: the strict definition does not prevent a beneficial provision from applying to an analogous situation.
Example. Two brothers jointly own a building with a single tenant occupying two adjoining shops on one rent. They partition: shop 1 to A, shop 2 to B. A needs shop 1 for his son's business. Under Mohar Singh, A alone may seek eviction from shop 1 on bona fide need; the tenant cannot insist that the tenancy is indivisible or that B must join — attornment to A for shop 1 arises by operation of law. The tenant now pays apportioned rent (s.109, last para — court may fix proportion if parties disagree).

CASE 4 — N. Ramaiah v. Nagaraj S. & Anr.

AIR 2001 Kant 395 · Karnataka High Court (Division Bench) · R.V. Raveendran J. · Appeal from order dated 6-1-1999 of a single Judge on I.A. VIII in Probate C.P. No. 8 of 1998

Theme Is a bequest under a Will a "transfer of property" (in future) under s.5 TPA? Does a court order to maintain status quo regarding property prohibit the party from making a Will of it — and is such a Will void?

Facts

  • Muni Narayanappa died leaving his widow Anjanamma and his brother's son S. Nagaraj (respondent). Nagaraj filed Probate C.P. No. 8/1998 for letters of administration with a Will dated 11-1-1998 allegedly executed by Muni Narayanappa in his favour. Anjanamma contested it as a got-up document, claiming she had succeeded as sole heir (Hindu Succession Act s.8, Class I).
  • On 16-3-1998 Nagaraj filed I.A. I seeking a temporary injunction restraining Anjanamma from alienating/encumbering the scheduled properties (Item A — land, building and running business of Cauvery Service Station, Khata 280-6-4B, Hennur, Bangalore; Item B — house occupied by Anjanamma and building occupied by Sitaram Agencies; Item C — bank balances) or withdrawing bank amounts. On 18-6-1998 the single Judge directed her to "maintain status quo in regard to the properties until further orders" — without specifying whether as to possession or title.
  • On 15-9-1998 Anjanamma executed a registered Will bequeathing the same properties (Schedule A — 1 acre Sy. No. 6/4B, Hennur Road with house and petrol bunk; Schedules B–C — bank deposits) to her brother, the appellant N. Ramaiah, and his children. She died on 11-12-1998.
  • The appellant filed I.A. VIII to be impleaded in place of the deceased Anjanamma as legatee under her Will, to continue her contest. Nagaraj objected.
  • Single Judge (6-1-1999) dismissed the impleading application: a status quo order is "a blanket prohibitory order" implying "a prohibition on the creation of new right, title or interest through the execution of any documents"; the Will executed "in rank defiance" was "wholly non est"; the appellant had no locus standi. (One Suguna, claiming to be an adopted daughter, sought impleadment in the appeal — rejected as unnecessary; she could pursue her own remedies.)

Issues (para 10)

  1. Whether a bequest of property under a Will is a transfer of the property.
  2. Whether a direction to a party to maintain status quo in regard to a property prohibits him from making a testamentary disposition; and whether a Will made during such an order is void and non est as regards the bequest of that property.

Arguments

Appellant (N. Ramaiah, legatee)

  • A status quo order regarding property does not bar the execution of a Will; a Will alienates nothing, creates no interest in anyone during life, is revocable and merely regulates succession on death; the order was aimed at alienation/encumbrance.
  • Anjanamma's right to make a Will is a personal right which no interim order can take away; on her death the legatee is entitled to come on record to continue her defence, otherwise the contest to Nagaraj's Will would go by default.

Respondent (S. Nagaraj)

  • The status quo order was made on an application to restrain alienation/encumbrance — it therefore barred her from "transferring or alienating the property in any manner".
  • s.5 TPA defines transfer as an act by which a living person conveys property "in present or in future"; "a bequest under a Will is nothing but a 'transfer of property in future'"; by executing the Will Anjanamma "effected a future transfer" in violation of the order; the Will and the bequest are therefore invalid, and the appellant has no locus standi.
  • Any document executed in defiance of a prohibitory order is non est (single Judge's reasoning).

Decision and reasoning (Raveendran J.)

  1. TPA deals only with transfers inter vivos (para 11). The Act concerns "the act of a living person, conveying a property in present or in future, to one or more living persons. The provisions of TP Act are inapplicable to testamentary successions which are governed by Indian Succession Act, 1925." s.2(h) Succession Act quoted.
  2. Transfer v. Will (para 12). "A transfer is a conveyance of an existing property by one living person to another (that is transfer inter vivos). On the other hand, a Will does not involve any transfer, nor effect any transfer inter vivos, but is a legal expression of the wishes and intention of a person in regard to his properties which he desires to be carried into effect after his death. In other words, a Will regulates succession … The concept of transfer by a living person is wholly alien to a Will. When a person makes a will, he provides for testamentary succession and does not transfer any property. While a transfer is irrevocable and comes into effect either immediately or on the happening of a specified contingency, a Will is revocable and comes into operation only after the death of the testator. Thus to treat a devise under a will as a transfer of an existing property in future, is contrary to all known principles relating to transfer of property and testamentary succession."
  3. No right created during life (para 13). The single Judge "proceeded on a wrong premise" that execution of a Will creates new right, title or interest: "By execution of a Will, no right or title or interest is created in favour of any one during the life time of the deceased." Point (i) answered in the negative.
  4. Scope of the status quo order (paras 14–16). The order was made on an application to restrain alienation/encumbrance; it "merely directed Anjanamma to maintain status quo" without clarifying possession or title. "No Court has the power to make an order, that too an interim order restraining an individual from exercising his right to execute a will and thereby regulate succession on his death. A direction to a party to maintain status quo in regard to a property does not therefore bar him from making a testamentary disposition … By making a will the testator neither changes title nor possession in regard to a property nor alters the nature or situation of the property nor removes or adds anything to the property. In short the testator, by making a will does not alter the existing state of things … making of a will … does not violate an order of status quo … and consequently the testamentary disposition is neither void nor voidable." Read in context, the order only barred alienation/conveyance.
  5. Impleadment (paras 23–24). If the appellant is not brought on record "there will be no one to continue the contest put up by Anjanamma"; he is a necessary party. Appeal allowed; order of 6-1-1999 set aside; I.A. VIII allowed.

Principles

  1. A Will is not a "transfer of property" under s.5 TPA — not even a "transfer in future"; the TPA governs transfers inter vivos, the Succession Act governs testamentary succession.
  2. Key distinctions: transfer = conveyance of existing property, irrevocable, operates now or on a contingency; Will = declaration of intention, revocable, operates only on death, creates no right during life.
  3. An order to maintain status quo (or an injunction against alienation/encumbrance) regarding property does not prohibit the party from making a Will of it; a Will so made is neither void nor voidable; the legatee may be impleaded to continue the litigation.
  4. No court can, by interim order, deprive a person of the right to regulate succession to his property by Will.
  5. Contrast: an actual alienation (sale, gift, mortgage) in breach of such an order is contumacious and may be ignored/set aside (CPC O.XXXIX r.2A; Contempt of Courts Act) — hence the sharp line between transfer and Will matters practically.
Example (DU 2025 Q.2(b)). R and G litigate over a plot; the court orders status quo. R executes a Will of the plot in favour of his daughter. Under Ramaiah, R is not in contempt: a Will transfers nothing, alters neither title nor possession, and can be revoked; the status quo is intact. Had R sold or gifted the plot, he would be in breach (O.XXXIX r.2A CPC) and the transfer liable to be disregarded.

14. Precedent map & the contextual approach

The unifying theme — "transfer" is a contextual word. All four judgments start from the strict s.5 meaning (an inter vivos conveyance of a new interest) and then ask what the particular statute intends: s.14(6) DRC Act targets purchaser-devices (partition excluded — Sarin); s.109 TPA confers a beneficial right that equity extends to partition (Mohar Singh); s.14(1)(b) DRC Act targets volitional parting with possession (bequest included — Kenneth Solomon); a status quo order targets alienation (Will excluded — Ramaiah). In an exam, state the strict definition first, then the contextual gloss.

PYQ 1 — DU LL.B. II Term, LB-204, May/June 2025, Q.2(b)

R and G were litigating about the ownership of a plot of land. The court ordered the parties to maintain status quo in regard to the property. R went ahead and made a Will of the property during the operation of the status quo order. Is R in contempt of the court's order? Explain with the help of relevant case law.

Issues. (i) Is the making of a Will a "transfer" or alienation of property? (ii) Does an order to maintain status quo regarding property prohibit a testamentary disposition, so that R is in contempt and the Will is void?

Law. s.5 TPA defines "transfer of property" as "an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to himself…". The Act therefore deals only with transfers inter vivos; testamentary succession is governed by the Indian Succession Act, 1925, s.2(h) of which defines a Will as "the legal declaration of the intention of a testator with respect to his property which he desires to be carried into effect after his death". In N. Ramaiah v. Nagaraj S. (AIR 2001 Kant 395) — on facts identical to the problem — a widow contesting probate proceedings was directed to "maintain status quo" regarding the disputed properties (on an application to restrain alienation/encumbrance); she then executed a registered Will of the properties in favour of her brother and died. The single Judge held the Will "non est" as executed in defiance of the order. The Division Bench (Raveendran J.) reversed, holding: (a) "a transfer is a conveyance of an existing property by one living person to another … a Will does not involve any transfer … but is a legal expression of the wishes and intention of a person in regard to his properties which he desires to be carried into effect after his death"; "the concept of transfer by a living person is wholly alien to a Will"; a transfer is irrevocable and operates at once or on a contingency, a Will is revocable and operates only on death; to treat a bequest as a "transfer in future" under s.5 "is contrary to all known principles"; (b) "by execution of a Will, no right or title or interest is created in favour of any one during the life time" of the testator; (c) "no Court has the power to make an order, that too an interim order, restraining an individual from exercising his right to execute a will"; (d) "by making a will the testator neither changes title nor possession … nor alters the nature or situation of the property"; hence making a Will "does not violate an order of status quo … and consequently the testamentary disposition is neither void nor voidable". The Delhi High Court in Kenneth Solomon v. Dan Singh Bawa (AIR 1986 Del 1) likewise recognised that the words of s.5 "exclude transfer by will, for a will operates after the death of the testator" and that no cause of action arises during the testator's life from the mere making of a Will, though it treated the eventual vesting of tenancy rights on death as "parting with possession" for the Delhi Rent Control Act — a rent-law concept, not a s.5 transfer.

Application. The status quo order required R and G not to alter the existing state of affairs — title, possession, nature of the plot. By making a Will R conveyed nothing to anyone; the legatee acquires no interest during R's life; R may revoke it at any time (Succession Act s.62); title and possession remain exactly as before. There is therefore no disobedience of the order (CPC O.XXXIX r.2A) and no wilful breach amounting to civil contempt under s.2(b) Contempt of Courts Act, 1971. Contrast: had R executed a sale, gift or mortgage of the plot — a s.5 transfer altering title — he would be in breach and the alienation liable to be disregarded.

Conclusion. R is not in contempt. A Will is not a transfer of property under s.5 TPA and does not violate a status quo order; the Will remains valid, and on R's death the legatee may step into R's shoes in the litigation (N. Ramaiah v. Nagaraj S.).

PYQ 2 — "Define 'transfer of property' under s.5 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and analyse its essential elements. Is partition of joint Hindu family property a transfer? Refer to decided cases." [DU, recurring]

Definition. s.5: "an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to one or more other living persons, or to himself, or to himself and one or more other living persons"; "living person" includes a company, association or body of individuals, incorporated or not (1929 amendment, which also added "to himself").

Elements. (1) An act — voluntary act of parties; transfers by operation of law (inheritance, insolvency, court sale) are excluded (s.2(d)). (2) Living person — the transferor must exist at the time of transfer; natural or juristic; competent under s.7; a Will, operating on death, is excluded (Kenneth Solomon; Ramaiah). (3) Conveys — creates a new title or interest in the transferee which he did not have before; transactions that merely define, sever or extinguish existing rights (partition, release, surrender, family settlement, charge) are not transfers. (4) Property — every legal right except personal rights of status; includes incorporeal interests such as tenancy rights (Kenneth Solomon); subject to s.6 exclusions. (5) In present or in future — the conveyance may operate now or later, but the property must exist and belong to the transferor; future property/spes successionis cannot be conveyed (s.6(a)). (6) To other living persons or to himself — transferee must be in existence (unborn persons only through ss.13, 20); transfer to oneself in another capacity (trustee) permitted.

Partition. In V.N. Sarin v. Ajit Kumar Poplai (AIR 1966 SC 432) tenanted premises of a joint family fell to a son on partition; the tenant pleaded s.14(6) DRC Act (five-year bar where the landlord "acquired premises by transfer"). Gajendragadkar C.J. held that coparcenary property is marked by community of interest and unity of possession; each coparcener has an antecedent title; partition merely converts joint enjoyment into enjoyment in severalty — "that joint title is by partition transformed into separate titles"; per Girja Bai v. Sadashiv Dhundiraj (PC 1916), partition "does not give him a title or create a title in him". A transfer under s.14(6) presupposes that title "did not vest in him prior to such transfer" — a stranger becoming landlord. Hence partition is not an acquisition by transfer; CIT v. Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel (AIR 1965 SC 866) to the same effect for income-tax. In Mohar Singh v. Devi Charan (AIR 1988 SC 1365) the Supreme Court extended the proposition to ordinary co-ownership: "a partition is not actually a transfer of property but would only signify the surrender of a portion of a joint right in exchange for a similar right from the other co-sharer", while applying the benefit of s.109 TPA to a co-owner allotted the tenanted shop on partition, on the principle of justice, equity and good conscience.

Qualification. The Court in Sarin confined itself to s.14(6) and acknowledged that partition is treated as a "transfer" for s.53 TPA (fraudulent transfers) and s.17(1)(b) Registration Act (partition deeds registrable). The meaning is thus contextual.

Conclusion. Partition of joint family property is not a "transfer of property" within s.5 because nothing new is conveyed — the sharer's pre-existing undefined interest is simply crystallised; but for anti-fraud and registration purposes the law treats it as a transfer.

PYQ 3 — "A Will is not a transfer of property within the meaning of s.5 of the Transfer of Property Act." Discuss with reference to Kenneth Solomon v. Dan Singh Bawa and N. Ramaiah v. Nagaraj. [DU pattern]

Statement of law. s.5 requires an act of a living person conveying property inter vivos. A Will (Succession Act s.2(h)) is a declaration of intention to take effect after death; it is ambulatory and revocable (s.62); it vests nothing during life. It therefore fails the "living person conveys" test and is outside the TPA, which is confined to transfers by act of parties between living persons.

Kenneth Solomon (Del 1986). Tenant Dr Sury bequeathed "all my moveable and immoveable properties" to her nephews; on her death the landlord sought eviction under DRC Act s.14(1)(b) ("otherwise parted with possession" without written consent). G.C. Jain J. held (i) tenancy rights are an interest in immovable property and passed under the residuary clause; (ii) on s.5: "these words exclude transfer by will, for a will operates after the death of the testator. The act of making a will in itself would not attract … proviso (b). No landlord can claim eviction, during the lifetime of the tenant, on the ground that the tenant had made a will"; yet (iii) the vesting on death is a volitional parting with possession by the tenant (following Nathu v. Devi Singh, and Anant Trimbak Sabnis — bequest is a transfer "in its generic sense"), unlike intestate devolution; eviction upheld. The case thus affirms that a Will is not a s.5 transfer while giving the rent statute's wider expression its own meaning.

N. Ramaiah (Kant 2001). A widow under a status quo order made a Will of the disputed property; the respondent argued it was a "transfer in future" under s.5. Raveendran J. held the TPA inapplicable to testamentary succession; "the concept of transfer by a living person is wholly alien to a Will"; a transfer is an irrevocable conveyance of existing property, a Will a revocable regulation of succession; no right is created during the testator's life; a status quo order does not bar a Will and the Will is neither void nor voidable; the legatee was impleaded.

Analysis. Both decisions concur on the core proposition; they diverge on the effect of the eventual vesting: Kenneth Solomon treats it as the maturing of a volitional act (for rent-control purposes), Ramaiah insists a Will creates no right whatever until death. The reconciliation is contextual — "transfer" and "parting with possession" are read according to each statute's object. Consequences of the rule: no stamp duty/compulsory registration for Wills; a Will of property under injunction is valid; a Will is not hit by s.6 restrictions or the rule against alienation in a tenancy; s.53 (fraudulent transfer) does not apply to Wills; but a Will cannot defeat rights already transferred inter vivos.

Conclusion. The statement is correct: a Will operates by succession on death, not by conveyance between living persons, and is outside s.5 TPA.

PYQ 4 — Problem: "X and Y, co-owners, let two adjoining shops to T under one lease. They partition the property; shop 1 falls to X, who files for T's eviction from shop 1 for his bona fide need. T contends (a) the tenancy is indivisible and cannot be split without his consent, and (b) X acquired shop 1 by partition, which is not a 'transfer', so s.109 TPA does not help him. Decide." [DU pattern]

Issues. (a) Applicability of the rule against splitting a tenancy; (b) whether s.109 TPA (rights of lessor's transferee) applies where severance of the reversion occurred by partition.

Law. s.109: if the lessor transfers the property leased "or any part thereof", the transferee, absent contrary contract, "shall possess all the rights … of the lessor as to the property or part transferred". In Mohar Singh v. Devi Charan (AIR 1988 SC 1365), on identical facts, Venkatachaliah J. held: the rule that a landlord cannot split the unity and integrity of the tenancy binds the original lessor by his own contract, but s.109 is a statutory exception enabling an assignee of part of the reversion to exercise all the landlord's rights as to that part; attornment arises by operation of law; no consent of the tenant is needed (Kannyan v. Alikutti, Mad FB). On the objection that partition is no transfer, the Court accepted that "a partition is not actually a transfer of property but would only signify the surrender of a portion of a joint right in exchange for a similar right", yet applied s.109 to partition following Ram Chandra Singh v. Ram Saran (All) — or, if the section does not apply in terms, its principle "as embodying a rule of justice, equity and good conscience".

Application. (a) T's objection that the tenancy is indivisible fails: the reversion has been severed and X, as exclusive owner of shop 1, may enforce the lease as to shop 1 alone; T attorns to X by operation of law and pays apportioned rent (s.109, last para). (b) Although partition is not a s.5 transfer (Sarin; Mohar Singh), the benefit of s.109 extends to X.

Decision. X's petition is maintainable; subject to proof of bona fide need, T is liable to be evicted from shop 1 (Mohar Singh).

PYQ 5 — Short notes: (a) Living person and juristic person under s.5; (b) Transfer by operation of law; (c) "In present or in future"

(a) The transferor and transferee under s.5 must be "living persons" — persons in existence at the time of the transfer. The second paragraph (1929) makes clear that this includes juristic persons: companies, associations and bodies of individuals, incorporated or not — hence a company, society, corporation, university, or a firm through its partners can transfer and take property; special laws governing them (Companies Act — ultra vires, s.180 restrictions, s.77 charge registration) are saved. Idols/deities are juristic persons capable of holding property through a shebait (Pramatha Nath Mullick; Yogendra Nath Naskar), but a dedication to a deity is not a s.5 transfer as there is no living transferee; it is governed by Hindu law. Unborn persons are not living persons — interests for them can be created only under ss.13–14, 20. A dead person cannot transfer — a Will is not a transfer (Ramaiah). Capacity of a natural transferor is governed by s.7 read with Contract Act s.11.

(b) A transfer by operation of law occurs without any act of the owner — inheritance, insolvency (vesting in Official Receiver), forfeiture, escheat, court sale in execution, statutory vesting. s.2(d) excludes such transfers from the Act "save as provided by section 57 and Chapter IV", so that s.57 (discharge of encumbrances) and ss.58–104 (mortgages and charges) apply to them — Laxmi Devi v. Mukand Kanwar; Ahmedabad MC v. Haji Abdul Gafur (s.100 applied to a court-auction purchaser). Formalities of s.54/59/123, competency under s.7 and restraints under ss.10–12 do not apply.

(c) The words qualify "conveys": the transferor may convey so that the transferee's interest arises immediately or at a future date/on a contingency (lease to commence later; vested remainder after a life interest; conditional transfers under ss.19–21, 25–34). They do not permit a transfer of future property — property not yet in existence or not yet the transferor's (spes successionis, s.6(a)); such a purported transfer is at most a contract, or may be saved by s.43 (feeding the grant by estoppel — Jumma Masjid). Nor do they convert a Will into a "transfer in future" — a Will vests nothing until death and is revocable (Ramaiah).

  2. "Living person" includes companies, associations and bodies of individuals (1929); the transferor must be competent (s.7).
  3. "Conveys" = creation of a new title/interest; where nothing new passes there is no transfer.
  4. Transfers by operation of law are outside the Act except s.57 and Chapter IV (s.2(d)).
  5. The TPA governs transfers inter vivos; Wills are governed by the Succession Act and are not transfers (Kenneth Solomon; Ramaiah).
  6. A status quo / non-alienation order does not prevent making a Will; the Will is valid (Ramaiah).
  7. Partition of coparcenary or co-owned property is not a transfer — antecedent title is merely defined (Sarin; Mohar Singh; Girja Bai).

  9. A co-owner allotted tenanted premises on partition gets s.109 rights and can evict from his part alone (Mohar Singh); and is not barred by the DRC Act five-year rule (Sarin).
