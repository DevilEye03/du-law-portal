1. Setting the scene — the unborn and the two-step machinery

s.5 defines a transfer of property as an act by which a living person conveys property to one or more other living persons. A person not in existence (an unborn child, including one not yet conceived) cannot be a direct transferee: there is no one in whom the interest can vest. Yet settlors constantly wish to provide for future generations — "to my son for life, then to his children". The Act reconciles the two by permitting a transfer "for the benefit of" an unborn person (s.13 — the language is deliberately different from "to") through the medium of a prior interest in a living person, subject to three controls:

ControlSectionContent
Form of the unborn's interests.13The interest given to the unborn must be the whole remaining interest of the transferor — an absolute interest; no life estate or other limited estate for an unborn; and it must be preceded by a prior interest created by the same transfer in favour of a living person.
Time of vestings.14Vesting cannot be postponed beyond life/lives in being + minority of the ultimate beneficiary (who must be in existence at the end of the last life). The unborn must be born before the last prior interest ends; vesting may be delayed at most till he attains 18.
Consequences of failure and connected rulesss.15, 16, 17, 18, 20Class gifts (s.15) — only the offending members fail; dependent ulterior interests fail with the void prior interest (s.16); accumulation of income limited (s.17); public-benefit transfers exempt (s.18); the unborn takes a vested interest on birth unless a contrary intention appears (s.20).
Historical note. Before 1882 Hindu and Muslim law knew no gift to an unborn person at all (Tagore v. Tagore (1872) IA Supp 47 — a Hindu could not give to a person not in existence at the testator's death). The TPA (ss.13–18) and the Indian Succession Act (ss.113–118) introduced the English "rule against perpetuities" in modified form. The Hindu Disposition of Property Act, 1916 (and the Madras Act of 1914) made ss.13–14-type dispositions available to Hindus; s.2 HSA/ s.57 ISA extend the Succession Act provisions to Hindu wills. Muslim law (except for wakfs and, per some views, a life-interest device) still does not permit gifts to unborn persons (a hiba to an unborn is void), and the Act's Chapter II does not override Muslim law (s.2, second proviso) — so ss.13–14 do not apply to Muslim gifts, though they apply to Muslim sales/leases.

2. Section 13 — text and element-by-element analysis

s.13. Transfer for benefit of unborn person.—Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created for the benefit of a person not in existence at the date of the transfer, subject to a prior interest created by the same transfer, the interest created for the benefit of such person shall not take effect, unless it extends to the whole of the remaining interest of the transferor in the property.
Illustration.—A transfers property of which he is the owner to B in trust for A and his intended wife successively for their lives, and, after the death of the survivor, for the eldest son of the intended marriage for life, and after his death for A's second son. The interest so created for the benefit of the eldest son does not take effect, because it does not extend to the whole of A's remaining interest in the property.
#ElementAnalysis
1"on a transfer of property"An inter vivos transfer within s.5 (sale, gift, settlement, trust, lease). Wills — s.113 Indian Succession Act (same rule, with the difference that in a will the prior interest need not be created by the same instrument in the same way and the relevant date is the testator's death).
2"an interest therein is created for the benefit of a person not in existence at the date of the transfer""Person not in existence" = unborn; includes a child en ventre sa mère? — a child in the womb is treated by law as in existence for the purpose of taking a benefit (s.20 speaks of "not then living"; Hindu law and s.112/113 ISA treat a child in the womb as born); hence the section is aimed at persons not even conceived. "For the benefit of" — the unborn cannot be a direct transferee; the interest is created for his benefit through the prior holder (or trustees).
3"subject to a prior interest created by the same transfer"There must be a prior interest in a living person (life estate; a series of successive life estates in living persons is permitted — Capsule: "the transferor is competent to create successive life interests in favour of several living persons at the same time"). The prior interest carries possession and enjoyment until it ends. A direct transfer to an unborn without any prior interest is simply void under s.5 (no transferee).
4"shall not take effect, unless it extends to the whole of the remaining interest of the transferor"The unborn must get everything the transferor had left after the prior interest(s) — i.e., an absolute interest (fee simple; full ownership). A life interest, a remainder for years, or an interest subject to a further gift over to someone else is void (illustration: eldest son "for life" then to A's second son — eldest son's interest fails). Rationale: to prevent the settlor from controlling the property through generation after generation of limited estates — the unborn, once he takes absolutely, can alienate; the chain stops.
5Effect of failureThe unborn's interest is void; the prior interest stands (s.30 analogy; Capsule ill. (iii): if the unborn is never born the property reverts to the transferor or his heirs on the life-holder's death); any interest created to take effect after or upon failure of the void interest also fails (s.16) — so in the illustration A's second son also takes nothing.
Capsule illustrations, expanded. (i) 1-1-1980: A creates a life interest for his unmarried brother Br, then absolutely to Br's first child UB. Br takes possession; child born 1985 → vested interest at birth (s.20), possession only on Br's death (2000). (ii) UB born 1985, dies 1987 in Br's lifetime → UB's vested interest passes to UB's heirs (s.19: "a vested interest is not defeated by the death of the transferee before he obtains possession"), who take possession in 2000. (iii) UB never born; Br dies 2000 → property reverts to A or A's heirs "as if A died in 2000 leaving behind this property". (iv) A → B for life → B's unborn first child UB1 for life → absolutely to B's second child UB2: UB1's interest void (limited interest to an unborn); UB2's interest, though absolute, fails under s.16 as dependent on a void prior interest; after B's death the property reverts to A/heirs.

3. Prior interest, absolute interest, vesting on birth

3.1 Prior interest — who and what
  • Must be in favour of a living person (or persons) at the date of transfer — e.g., the transferor himself, his wife, brother, an intended spouse (illustration: "A and his intended wife").
  • Usually a life interest; may be successive life interests (B for life, then C for life, then D for life — all living), but every life must be in being; the moment a life interest is given to an unborn person the chain breaks (s.13).
  • The prior holder enjoys possession and income; on the last prior death the unborn (now born and vested) takes possession.
  • The transfer may be through trustees (Indian Trusts Act, 1882, s.9 — an unborn person may be a beneficiary).
3.2 Absolute interest — "whole of the remaining interest"
  • The unborn must take all that the transferor has left: no reservation of a further remainder to a third party; no life estate; no "so long as" limitation; no power of revocation retained inconsistent with absolute ownership.
  • Conditions that do not derogate from absoluteness (e.g., a condition precedent of attaining 18 — permitted by s.14; a charge for maintenance) do not offend s.13.
  • Capsule: "no limited estate can be conferred for the benefit of the unborn person. If a limited interest is settled for him, the same would be void."
3.3 Vesting on birth — s.20. "Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created for the benefit of a person not then living, he acquires upon his birth, unless a contrary intention appear from the terms of the transfer, a vested interest, although he may not be entitled to the enjoyment thereof immediately on his birth." Consequences: (a) his interest is heritable and transferable from birth (s.6, s.19); (b) postponement of enjoyment until the life-holder's death does not make it contingent (s.19 Explanation); (c) a contrary intention — e.g., "to B's first son on attaining 18" — makes it contingent until then (s.21), and s.14 permits that postponement but no more.

4. Section 14 — the rule against perpetuity

s.14. Rule against perpetuity.—No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of such transfer, and the minority of some person who shall be in existence at the expiration of that period, and to whom, if he attains full age, the interest created is to belong.

4.1 Meaning and object

"Perpetuity" (Lewis, quoted in Ram Baran Prasad) is "a future limitation, whether executory or by way of remainder, and of either real or personal property, which is not to vest until after the expiration of, or will not necessarily vest within, the period fixed and prescribed by law for the creation of future estates and interests." Perpetuity may arise (a) by taking away the transferee's power of alienation (dealt with by s.10) or (b) by creating future remote interests (dealt with by s.14) (Capsule). The rule "is founded on the principle that the liberty of alienation shall not be exercised to its own destruction, and that all contrivances shall be void which tend to create a perpetuity or place property for ever out of the reach of the exercise of the power of alienation" (Kempraj, para 4, quoting the classic formulation). Objects: free circulation of property for trade, commerce and improvement; protection of owners who would otherwise be unable to sell in need; prevention of dynastic tying-up of wealth; public policy.

6. Rule of possible and actual events

The test. Validity under s.14 is judged at the date of the transfer by asking whether the interest might, on any possible course of events, vest beyond the permitted period — not by waiting to see what actually happens. Ram Newaz: "the fact that it happened to fall in within the legal limitation is not the test which is to be applied to these cases. What you have to see is whether the event can be postponed to beyond the period … and not what in fact happened." There the vendee's interest did in fact fall in within a life in being (Ram Charan's son died childless in 1918), yet the disposition was void because the lineal descendants "might have remained … for 100 or 200 years."

Consequences of the "possible events" approach (shared with English common law — the "fertile octogenarian", "unborn widow" and "magic gravel pit" conundrums): a gift "to the children of B who attain 25" is void even if B is 80 and all children are already adults; a gift "to B's widow for life, then to B's children then living" may be void because B's eventual widow might be a person unborn at the transfer. Indian courts have not adopted the English statutory "wait and see" reform; the Law Commission (70th Report, 1977) recommended retention of the possible-events rule with clarifications.

Example. In 2020 A settles a Lucknow house on his daughter D (aged 45) for life, then on "such of D's children as attain 21". D has two children aged 20 and 17. Under the possible-events test the gift to the children is void: D might have another child after 2020 who would attain 21 more than 18 years after D's death (if D died shortly after the birth). It does not matter that this is biologically improbable. (Class-splitting under s.15 does not help, because the vice — vesting at 21 — affects every member.) Redraft: "as attain 18".

8. Section 15 — transfer to a class

s.15.—If, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created for the benefit of a class of persons with regard to some of whom such interest fails by reason of any of the rules contained in sections 13 and 14, such interest fails in regard to those persons only and not in regard to the whole class. [Words "in regard to those persons only and not in regard to the whole class" substituted in 1929 for "as regards the whole class".]
  • A class is a body of persons answering a general description — "children of B", "grandchildren", "nephews" — whose number is uncertain at the transfer and is fixed by future events.
  • Before 1929 the section (following the English rule in Leake v. Robinson (1817)) made the gift fail for the whole class if it was void for any member; the 1929 amendment reversed this: the gift fails only as to the members in whom it cannot validly vest, and the rest take.
  • Application: A → B for life → "B's children who attain 25": every member's interest may vest beyond the period → all fail. But A → B for life → "the children of B and C" where B is living and C is unborn (say, an intended child of A): B's children (who will be born in B's lifetime) can take; the gift fails only as to C's children (grandchildren of an unborn — beyond s.13/14).
  • Related: s.22 (transfer to such members of a class as attain a particular age — vests only in those who attain it); s.115 ISA (the will counterpart; still on the pre-1929 footing for wills — a bequest to a class fails wholly if it may vest beyond the period as to any member, unless the interests are severable).

9. Section 16 — transfer to take effect on failure of prior interest

s.16.—Where, by reason of any of the rules contained in sections 13 and 14, an interest created for the benefit of a person or of a class of persons fails in regard to such person or the whole of such class, any interest created in the same transaction and intended to take effect after or upon failure of such prior interest also fails.
  • Codifies the English rule of dependent limitations (Monypenny v. Dering (1852); In re Abbott): a subsequent interest which is to take effect after a void prior interest, or on its failure, falls with it — even if, viewed alone, it would vest within the period.
  • Illustration to s.13: after the eldest son's (void) life interest, "for A's second son" — the second son's interest fails too (Capsule: "a transfer subsequent to, or dependent on a void transfer can also not take effect").
  • Ram Newaz: the vendee's ultimate interest ("if none of my lineal descendants is alive … the land shall be the property of the vendee") was to take effect on failure of the (void) interests of the unborn lineal descendants → it failed with them; property went to the reversioners.
  • Distinguish s.27 (where the prior disposition fails for reasons other than ss.13–14 — e.g., death of the prior taker, breach of condition — the ulterior disposition does take effect) and s.30 (an invalid ulterior disposition does not affect the prior one). s.16 is the converse: invalid prior (under ss.13–14) kills the dependent ulterior. Also s.116 ISA (will counterpart).
  • Exception: an ulterior interest that is independent — expressed to take effect at a fixed time or on an event unconnected with the void interest — may survive (the English "independent limitation" gloss).

10. Section 17 — direction for accumulation of income

s.17.—(1) Where the terms of a transfer of property direct that the income arising from the property shall be accumulated either wholly or in part during a period longer than—(a) the life of the transferor, or (b) a period of eighteen years from the date of the transfer, such direction shall, save as hereinafter provided, be void to the extent to which the period during which the accumulation is directed exceeds the longer of the aforesaid periods, and at the end of such last-mentioned period the property and the income thereof shall be disposed of as if the period during which the accumulation has been directed to be made had elapsed.
(2) This section shall not affect any direction for accumulation for the purpose of—(i) the payment of the debts of the transferor or any other person taking any interest under the transfer, or (ii) the provision of portions for children or remoter issue of the transferor or of any other person taking any interest under the transfer, or (iii) the preservation or maintenance of the property transferred; and such direction may be made accordingly.
PointAnalysis
MischiefAccumulation ties up income as the perpetuity rule ties up corpus; the English Thellusson Act, 1800 (after Thellusson v. Woodford — a will directing accumulation for the lives of all descendants living at death, projected to produce a colossal fund) limited accumulation periods; s.17 (as recast in 1929) is India's version.
Permitted periodThe longer of (a) the transferor's life, or (b) 18 years from the transfer. (Contrast ISA s.117 for wills: 18 years from the testator's death only.)
Effect of excessDirection void only as to the excess (unlike s.14, where the whole interest fails); at the end of the permitted period "the property and the income thereof shall be disposed of as if the period … had elapsed" — i.e., go to the person(s) entitled under the transfer, or, in default, revert to the transferor/heirs.
Three exceptions (accumulation may be directed for longer)(i) payment of debts of the transferor or of any person taking an interest under the transfer; (ii) portions (provision) for children or remoter issue of the transferor or of any beneficiary; (iii) preservation or maintenance of the property transferred (sinking fund for repairs, insurance).
Interaction with s.14An accumulation direction is itself subject to s.14 if it is coupled with a postponed vesting; s.19 Explanation — a direction to accumulate income until the time of enjoyment does not by itself make an interest contingent.
Example. In 2024 A (aged 60) transfers a rented building to trustees: "accumulate the rent for 30 years and then pay the fund and the building to my grandson G." The direction is valid for 18 years (or for A's life if he lives beyond 2042 — the longer period); the excess is void; from the end of the permitted period the rent goes to G (his interest being vested, s.19/20). If the direction had been "accumulate until my debts are paid" or "to provide a marriage portion for my granddaughter", it would be valid throughout (s.17(2)).

11. Section 18 and other exceptions

s.18. Transfer in perpetuity for benefit of public.—The restrictions in sections 14, 16 and 17 shall not apply in the case of a transfer of property for the benefit of the public in the advancement of religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety, or any other object beneficial to mankind.
  • Public benefit / charity. Endowments to temples, mosques, churches, gurdwaras, schools, universities, hospitals, dharamshalas, libraries, public parks; wakfs (Mussalman Wakf Validating Act, 1913; Waqf Act, 1995); trusts under the Charitable and Religious Trusts Act, 1920 and the Bombay/Maharashtra Public Trusts Act — may be perpetual and may direct perpetual accumulation. "Any other object beneficial to mankind" is read ejusdem generis with the listed heads and in line with the fourth head of charity in Commissioners v. Pemsel [1891] AC 531; cf. s.2(15) Income-tax Act ("charitable purpose"). Note that s.18 does not exempt from s.13 (a charitable gift does not involve unborn persons in the s.13 sense) or from s.15.
  • Personal covenants, contracts, options, leases, renewals — outside the rule altogether (Part 7).
  • Vested interests — s.14 is about remoteness of vesting, so an interest vested at once, with enjoyment postponed, is untouched (s.19 Expl.).
  • Gifts to unborn under Muslim law — void under personal law (not an "exception" but a stricter rule); Hindu law — ss.13–14 apply via the Hindu Disposition of Property Act, 1916.
  • Statutory transfers / transfers by operation of law (s.2(d)); Government grants (Government Grants Act, 1895, s.3 — grants take effect according to their tenor notwithstanding the TPA).

CASE 1 — Ram Newaz v. Nankoo

AIR 1926 All 283 · Allahabad High Court · Mears C.J. and Lindsay J. · Letters Patent appeal by the plaintiffs (reversioners) against a Single Judge's second-appeal judgment · DU material p. 102

Theme A vendor's reservation of 2 bighas "for my life, then for my aulad khas (lineal descendants) without power of transfer, and if none of my lineal descendants is alive, then to the vendee" — void for creating limited interests in successive unborn generations and postponing the vendee's interest beyond the perpetuity period; the possible-events test; s.13 + s.14 + s.16 in combination.

Facts

  • In 1884 Ram Charan, "in difficulties", owned a 9-pie 3-kauri 2-dant zamindari share in a village. On 12 Feb 1884 he executed a sale deed which, after the formal parts, stated that he had "absolutely sold, with the exception of 2 bighas of nankar land numbered as below (1460), the entire property."
  • Later in the deed: "Let this be known that the 2 bighas of nankar land which I have excluded from the sale shall remain in my possession for life and after my death in the possession of my aulad khas without payment of rent or Government revenue. I or my lineal descendants have no right to transfer the property excluded either permanently or temporarily. If none of my lineal descendants is alive in my family then the said land shall be declared to be the own property of the vendee and his heirs and the persons of my family shall have no claim to the same."
  • The schedule again described the share sold as the 9-pie share "excluding the 2 bighas No. 1460".
  • Ram Charan died; his son Mauzzam Ram succeeded and died childless in 1918. The lineal descendants thus came to an end within a life in being. The defendants (successors of the vendee) claimed the 2 bighas under the clause; the plaintiffs, as reversioners of Ram Charan, sued for possession.

Procedural history

Munsif decreed the suit for the reversioners; first appellate court confirmed; a Single Judge of the High Court in second appeal reversed (holding for the vendee's successors); plaintiffs appealed under the Letters Patent.

Issues

  1. Construction: was the 1884 deed an out-and-out sale of the whole 9-pie share (the 2 bighas included, subject to a life interest reserved), or a sale of the share minus the 2 bighas, with a future contingent interest in the 2 bighas in favour of the vendee?
  2. Validity: if the latter, was the disposition of the 2 bighas — life interest to the vendor, then to his lineal descendants generation after generation without power of transfer, then to the vendee on their extinction — valid, or repugnant to the rules against transfers to unborn persons and perpetuities (ss.13, 14, 16 TPA), so that the vendee's interest could never take effect?

Arguments

Defendants (vendee's successors)

  • The deed was an absolute sale of the entire 9-pie share; the 2 bighas were merely retained in the vendor's possession for his life and his descendants' lives as a nankar (rent-free) holding; the ownership passed in 1884, or at latest the clause gave them a good title on the extinction of the descendants.
  • In fact the descendants died out in 1918 — within a life in being (the son) — so the vendee's interest had vested within the period allowed by law; there was no actual perpetuity.
  • The reversioners had no better title: the vendor had himself excluded his family from any claim once the line failed.

Plaintiffs (reversioners)

  • On its plain terms the deed sold the share minus the 2 bighas ("excluding No. 1460"); the vendee got no present interest in them, only a possibility of becoming owner in the future.
  • That possibility depended on a chain of limited (life) interests for unborn lineal descendants "from generation to generation" — void under s.13 (an unborn may take only the whole remaining interest) — and might not fall in for "100 or 200 years" — void under s.14.
  • The vendee's ultimate interest was to take effect only on failure of those void interests; by s.16 it fell with them. What actually happened in 1918 is irrelevant — remoteness is judged by possible events at the date of the deed.
  • The clause being void, the 2 bighas remained Ram Charan's, and on the death of his son passed to his reversioners.

Decision (Mears C.J. and Lindsay J.)

  1. Construction. "Pausing there and putting the sale in the plainest possible terms it was a sale of the 9-pie odd share minus the 2 bighas specifically numbered." "The vendee got on the 12th of February 1884 … the 9-pie odd share with No. 1460, the 2 bighas definitely excluded, but that they had a possibility of becoming its owners at a future date provided that provision was one which the law would recognize. We can see in the document no indication whatever of the vendees having acquired the whole of the property … What we do find is an acquisition of the whole of the 9-pie odd share except that particular area of 2 bighas numbered 1460."
  2. The contest. "What is the position when a contest arises between the nearest reversioners and the successors of the vendees? … Ram Charan having died, he was succeeded by his son Mauzzam Ram, who in turn died childless in 1918, and therefore these 2 bighas of land would as it happened, if there was no law to the contrary, become the property of the vendees within a life or lives in being and twenty-one years after."
  3. Possible, not actual, events. "But the fact that it happened to fall in within the legal limitation is not the test which is to be applied to these cases. What you have to see is whether the event can be postponed to beyond the period of a life or lives in being and 21 years after and not what in fact happened."
  4. Application. "Now applying that test it is perfectly evident that these 2 bighas of nankar land might have remained with the lineal descendants of Ram Charan for 100 or 200 years, and that being so, we are of opinion that this was a condition repugnant to the law, and being so repugnant to the law the defendants could not set up this document on which they rely as entitling them to possession of the property."
  5. Result. The plaintiffs "were right in bringing this action"; the Single Judge's decision set aside; the decree of the first appellate court (confirming the Munsif) restored with costs on the higher scale. The reversioners recovered the 2 bighas.
Analysis in terms of the sections (Capsule & uploaded note). (a) The clause created a life estate for the vendor (living — valid), then successive life-type interests, without power of transfer, for his aulad khas — unborn persons taking limited interests, generation after generation → void under s.13; (b) the property was sought to be made inalienable for an unlimited number of generations — the vendee's interest "might" vest centuries later → void under s.14 (the court, following English phraseology, spoke of "a life or lives in being and 21 years"; the Indian period is lives in being + minority); (c) the vendee's interest was to arise on failure of the void prior interests → fails under s.16; (d) the restraint on transfer binding the descendants was also an absolute restraint (s.10). Result: reversion to the vendor's heirs.

Principles

  1. Rule of possible events: whether a disposition offends the rule against perpetuity is determined by what could happen under its terms as at the date of the instrument, "not what in fact happened".
  2. An interest for unborn persons must be absolute; a chain of limited interests for successive unborn generations is void (s.13), and a disposition tying property up for an indefinite number of generations is void for remoteness (s.14).
  3. An interest intended to take effect on the failure of such void interests also fails (s.16); the property reverts to the transferor and his heirs.
  4. A deed is construed as a whole; an express exclusion of a parcel from the sale prevails over general words of absolute sale.
Example. In 2015 R sells his farm in Barabanki to V "except plot 12, which shall remain with me for life, then with my sons, grandsons and their descendants for ever, none of whom may sell it; if my line dies out, plot 12 shall belong to V." R dies in 2018; his only son dies childless in 2024. V claims plot 12. Following Ram Newaz, the clause is void (ss.13, 14, 16) — even though the line failed within a life in being — and plot 12 goes to R's heirs under the Hindu Succession Act.

CASE 2 — Ram Baran Prasad v. Ram Mohit Hazra & Ors.

AIR 1967 SC 744 : (1967) 1 SCR 293 · Supreme Court of India · Ramaswami J. (for the Bench) · Appeal by special leave from the Calcutta High Court (F.A. No. 104 of 1954, 18-11-1959) affirming the Subordinate Judge, 5th Court, Alipore (T.S. No. 100 of 1952, 27-2-1954) · DU material pp. 103–108

Theme Whether a covenant of pre-emption in a partition award, unlimited in time, (i) binds assignees/successors of the original parties and (ii) is void as offending the rule against perpetuity. Held: it binds assignees with notice, and s.14 does not apply because a contract for sale/pre-emption creates no interest in property (ss.40, 54).

Facts

  • Two brothers, Tulshidas Chatterjee and Kishorilal Chatterjee, owned land and buildings on Paharpur Road, Mouza Garden Reach, Khidderpore (Calcutta suburbs). In 1938 Kishorilal sued for partition; the matter went to arbitration; the arbitrators' award of 16 Dec 1940 was made a final decree on 15 Mar 1941.
  • The award divided the property into four blocks: A and C to Tulshidas, B and D to Kishorilal; two common passages (X, Y) and a common drain (Z) were kept joint. It contained a pre-emption clause: "any party in case of disposing or transferring any portion of his share, shall offer reference to the other party, that is each party shall have the right of pre-emption between each other." Other clauses provided that the 6-ft passage X "shall ever remain as such to all the blocks the owners whereof shall have every right to take underground water pipes, electric connections etc.", that common walls "shall have to be maintained … by both parties", that the drainage pit "shall have to be maintained as such for ever", and fixed a one-year period for reconstruction.
  • Chain of transfers: 20 Aug 1941 — Tulshidas sold Block A to Nagendra Nath Ghosh, after Kishorilal had refused to pre-empt on being offered. 22 Apr 1942 — Kishorilal sold Blocks B and D (Kobala Ex. I) to Rati Raman Mukherjee & others. 21 Jun 1946 — the Mukherjees sold B and D to the plaintiffs (Ex. 1(a)). 20 Sep 1952 — Nagendra Nath Ghosh sold Block A to Defendant 1 (without offering it to the plaintiffs). 2 Dec 1952 — plaintiffs sued Defendant 1 for pre-emption of Block A. 7 Apr 1953 — pending suit, Defendant 1 sold Block A to Defendant 2; plaint amended to seek pre-emption against both, calling on them to convey on payment of the actual consideration.

Procedural history

Subordinate Judge (27-2-1954): covenant of pre-emption binding on the defendants, who had notice; not hit by the rule against perpetuities; enforceable against assignees of the original parties. Decree: plaintiffs to deposit Rs 14,000 within one month; defendants to execute and register a kobala in plaintiffs' favour within 15 days of deposit. Calcutta High Court (18-11-1959): appeal dismissed; decree affirmed. Supreme Court: special leave.

Issues

  1. Was the pre-emption clause merely a personal covenant between Tulshidas and Kishorilal, or does it bind their successors-in-interest and assignees (and enure for the benefit of the plaintiffs as assignees)?
  2. Does a covenant of pre-emption unlimited in point of time offend the rule against perpetuities (s.14 TPA) and is it therefore void even between the original parties?

Arguments

Appellant (Defendant/purchaser) — Mr Bishen Narain

  • The pre-emption clause is a personal covenant between the two brothers; it does not say it binds assignees or successors; the plaintiffs (third-hand purchasers of B and D) cannot enforce it, nor can it bind the appellant (third-hand purchaser of A).
  • Even if it binds, the covenant is unlimited in time and confers a right to call for a conveyance at any future date — under the leading English authority London & South Western Rly v. Gomm (1882) 20 Ch D 562 (Jessel M.R.) such an option creates an equitable interest in land and, being capable of exercise beyond the perpetuity period, is void. Indian cases before the TPA applied the same reasoning to pre-emption clauses in partition deeds (Tripoora Soonduree v. Juggur Nath Dutt (1874) 24 WR 321 — Markby J.; Allibhai Mahomed Akuji v. Dada Alli Isap AIR 1931 Bom 578; Maharaj Bahadur Singh v. Bal Chand AIR 1922 PC 165 — agreement to grant a temple site to Jains held to infringe the rule).

Respondents (plaintiffs) — Mr N.C. Chatterji

  • Rights under a contract are prima facie assignable; under the Specific Relief Act (old s.23(b), s.27(b)) specific performance may be obtained by a party's representative in interest and enforced against persons claiming under a party by a subsequent title, except bona fide purchasers for value without notice; under Contract Act ss.37, 40 promises bind representatives unless a contrary intention appears. The award, read as a whole ("parties", "owners" of blocks, passages to "ever remain", pits maintained "for ever"), shows the parties intended obligations and benefits to run to successors; the clause rests on vicinage, which points the same way.
  • The rule against perpetuities concerns interests in property, not contracts. After the TPA a contract for sale creates no interest in land (s.54); s.40 para 2 makes it an obligation annexed to ownership enforceable against transferees with notice — but expressly "not amounting to an interest". Gomm therefore has no application in India; the pre-1882 Indian cases proceeded on the now-abandoned doctrine that an agreement for sale created an interest in land (Fati Chand Sahu).
  • The defendants had notice of the clause; the plaintiffs are entitled to pre-empt on paying the actual price.

Decision (Ramaswami J.)

  1. Binding on assignees (paras 4–5). "It is true that the pre-emption clause does not expressly state that it is binding upon the assignees or successors-in-interest, but, having regard to the context and the circumstances in which the award was made, it is manifest that the pre-emption clause must be construed as binding upon the assignees or successors-in-interest of the original contracting parties." "Prima facie rights of the parties to a contract are assignable." After quoting ss.23(b) and 27(b) Specific Relief Act and ss.37 and 40 Contract Act: "a contract in the absence of a contrary intention express or implied will be enforceable by and against the parties and their legal heirs and legal representatives including assignees and transferees." Nothing in the award suggests a contrary intention; the other clauses (passage X to "ever remain"; owners of blocks; common walls; drainage pit "for ever") show "parties" must include legal representatives and assignees; the clause "was based upon the ground of vicinage", which "would also suggest" the same intention. First submission rejected.
  2. Nature of the rule (para 7). Lewis's definition of a perpetuity quoted. "The rule as formulated falls within the branch of the law of property and its true object is to restrain the creation of future conditional interest in property. The rule against perpetuities is not concerned with contracts as such or with contractual rights and obligations as such. Thus a contract to pay money to a person, his heirs or legal representatives upon a future contingency, which may happen beyond the period prescribed would be perfectly valid (Walsh v. Secretary of State for India). It is therefore well-established that the rule of perpetuity concerns rights of property only and does not affect the making of contracts which do not create rights of property."
  3. Personal contracts even if referring to land (para 8). South Eastern Rly v. Associated Portland Cement [1910] 1 Ch 12; Witham v. Vane (1883) — the Earl of Darlington's 1824 covenant to pay 6d per chaldron of coal shipped from the sold manor, enforced in 1883 by an assignee against the Earl's executors; Lord Selborne L.C. overruled the perpetuity plea because "though the covenant had relation to land, it did not amount to a reservation of any interest in land."
  4. English law contrasted (para 9). In England a contract for purchase creates an equitable interest; hence an option/pre-emption unlimited in time is void — Gomm (Powell's covenant to reconvey at any time for £100; Kay J.'s decree reversed by the Court of Appeal; Jessel M.R.: "does or does not the covenant give an interest in the land? If it is a bare or mere personal contract it is of course not obnoxious to the rule, but in that case … it cannot be enforced against the assignee … The right to call for a conveyance of the land is an equitable interest or equitable estate … the covenant giving the option must give the other an interest in land").
  5. Pre-Act Indian law (para 10). Before the TPA it was accepted that an agreement for sale created an interest in land (Fati Chand Sahu v. Lilambar Singh Das (1871) 9 Beng LR 433 (PC) — suit dismissed for want of registration); hence Tripoora Soonduree (Markby J.), Allibhai (Baker J.) and Maharaj Bahadur Singh v. Bal Chand (PC, 1872 agreement — society of Jains; agreement "conferred … no present estate or interest in the site, and was unenforceable as a covenant, since it did not run with the land, and infringed the rule against perpetuity"). All concerned pre-1882 contracts.
  6. The decisive reasoning — ss.40 and 54 (para 11). "The second paragraph of Section 40 taken with the illustration establishes two propositions: (1) that a contract for sale does not create any interest in the land, but is annexed to the ownership of the land and (2) that the obligation can be enforced against a subsequent gratuitous transferee from the vendor or a transferee for value but with notice. Reading Section 14 along with Section 54 … it is manifest that a mere contract for sale of immovable property does not create any interest in the immovable property and it therefore follows that the rule of perpetuity cannot be applied to a covenant of pre-emption even though there is no time-limit within which the option has to be exercised." s.40 para 2 "makes a substantial departure from the English law" — an obligation creating no interest but concerning land is enforceable against an assignee with notice; such a contract "does not stand on the same footing as a mere personal contract". There is "a superficial kind of resemblance" between the s.40 obligation and the English equitable interest (both defeated by a bona fide purchaser without notice), "but the analogy cannot be carried further and the rule against perpetuity which applies to equitable estates in English law cannot be applied to a covenant of pre-emption because Section 40 … does not make the covenant enforceable against the assignee on the footing that it creates an interest in the land."
  7. Result (para 12). "The covenant for pre-emption in this case does not offend the rule against perpetuities and cannot be considered to be void in law." High Court decision correct; appeal dismissed with costs. The plaintiffs' decree for pre-emption of Block A on deposit of Rs 14,000 stood.

Principles

  1. The rule against perpetuity (s.14) is a rule of the law of property: it restrains the creation of future contingent interests in property; it does not apply to contracts or contractual rights that create no interest in property, even if they relate to land.
  2. In India a contract for sale / covenant of pre-emption / option creates no interest in land (s.54; s.40 para 2) — therefore a pre-emption covenant unlimited in time is valid; the English rule in Gomm (option = equitable interest → perpetuity) does not apply.
  3. Such a covenant, being "annexed to the ownership" of the land (s.40), is enforceable by and against assignees and successors of the original parties — against transferees with notice or gratuitous transferees — unless a contrary intention appears (Contract Act ss.37, 40; SRA ss.15(b), 19(b)); a pre-emption clause founded on vicinage in a partition award is presumed to run.
  4. Pre-1882 Indian decisions treating pre-emption covenants as void for perpetuity rest on the superseded doctrine that an agreement for sale created an interest in land.
Example. Two sisters partition a Meerut house by registered deed in 2005 with a clause "neither party shall sell her portion without first offering it to the other at the price offered by a third party." In 2023 one sister's purchaser resells to a builder who had read the partition deed. The other sister (or her assignee) may sue the builder for pre-emption; the clause is not void for perpetuity though unlimited in time (Ram Baran Prasad), and binds the builder as a transferee with notice (s.40; SRA s.19(b)). Had the builder bought without notice from an unregistered chain, he would take free.

CASE 3 — R. Kempraj v. M/s Burton Son & Co.

AIR 1970 SC 1872 : (1969) 2 SCC 594 · Supreme Court of India · Grover J. (for the Bench) · Appeal by special leave from the Mysore High Court (second appeal), affirming the decrees of the trial court and first appellate court · DU material pp. 109–111

Theme Whether a clause giving the lessee an option to renew a 10-year lease "for further similar periods … as long as they desire" — i.e., a perpetual renewal option — is void as offending the rule against perpetuity (s.14). Held: no — s.14 applies only to a transfer of property creating an interest; a renewal covenant creates no such interest; and s.105 itself permits leases in perpetuity.

Facts

  • On 26 Oct 1951 the respondent-lessee, Burton Son & Co., took a lease from the appellant-lessor, R. Kempraj, of premises Nos. 8 and 9, Mahatma Gandhi Road (South Parade), Civil Station, Bangalore, for 10 years in the first instance from 1 Nov 1951 "with an option to the lessee to renew the same as long as desired as provided".
  • Clause 9: "The lessee shall have the right to renew the lease … at the end of the present period of ten years … on the same rental of Rs 450 per month, for a similar period and for further similar periods thereafter on the same terms and conditions …; and the lessee shall be permitted and shall have the right to remain in occupation of the premises on the same terms and conditions for any further periods of ten years as long as they desire to do so."
  • Clause 10: "The lessor shall not raise any objection whatsoever to the lessee exercising his option to renew the lease for a further period of ten years on the same terms and conditions as long as they desire to be in occupation, provided that the lessee shall not have the right to transfer the lease or alienate any right thereunder."
  • Before the first 10 years expired, the lessee wrote exercising the option for renewal from 1 Nov 1961. The lessor did not comply. After notice, the lessee sued for specific performance of the renewal covenant — praying that the lessor be directed to execute a registered lease deed (s.107 TPA), failing which the court should execute it.
  • The lessor pleaded, inter alia, that the renewal condition was hit by the rule against perpetuity.

Procedural history

Trial court decreed the suit; first appellate court and the Mysore High Court affirmed. Special leave to the Supreme Court.

Issue

"Whether an option given to a lessee to get the lease, which is initially for a period of 10 years, renewed after every 10 years is hit by the rule of perpetuity and is void."

Arguments

Appellant (lessor)

  • Conceded that a lease in perpetuity could validly have been created under s.105 — but this lease was only for 10 years.
  • The mischief lies in the renewal clauses, which are covenants running with the land; on their true construction they create, at each renewal, a fresh interest to arise in the indefinite future — a perpetual series of future leasehold interests — which attracts s.14 "immediately". An option exercisable for ever beyond any life in being + minority is void, as an option to purchase is in English law (Woodall v. Clifton [1905] 2 Ch 257 — proviso giving lessor an option to purchase the fee simple void for perpetuity).
  • The clause is therefore unenforceable and the lessee's tenancy ended in 1961.

Respondent (lessee)

  • s.14 applies only where there is a transfer of property creating a future interest; the lease transferred an interest for 10 years only; the renewal clause is a covenant/agreement to grant a further lease — it transfers nothing and creates no interest in property (cf. s.54: a contract for sale creates no interest — Ram Baran Prasad).
  • Since s.105 permits a lease in perpetuity outright, a lease renewable perpetually cannot be against the policy of the law; the reversion always remains in the lessor.
  • Even in English law covenants for perpetual renewal are enforced and "the objection of perpetuity had never been taken" to them — Muller v. Trafford [1901] 1 Ch 54 (Farwell J.: a covenant to renew is a covenant running with the land and "free from any taint of perpetuity because it is annexed to the land"); approved in Weg Motors Ltd v. Hales [1961] 3 All ER 181 (Lord Evershed M.R.).
  • Indian authority: Ganesh Sonar v. Purnendu Narayan Singha AIR 1962 Pat 201 — option to lessor to resume leasehold land held a personal covenant outside s.14; Woodall v. Clifton distinguished because in India a contract for sale creates no interest.

Decision (Grover J.)

  1. The rule and its basis (para 4). s.14 set out. "It is well known that the rule against perpetuity is founded on the principle that the liberty of alienation 'shall not be exercised to its own destruction and that all contrivances shall be void which tend to create a perpetuity or place property for ever out of the reach of the exercise of the power of alienation'." "Transfer of property" (s.5) means an act by which a living person conveys property, in present or in future, to other living persons — including a company or association. s.105: a lease is a transfer of a right to enjoy property "made for a certain time, express or implied, or in perpetuity"; "a lease is not a mere contract but it is a transfer of an interest in land and creates a right in rem. Owing to the provisions of Section 105 a lease in perpetuity can be created but even then an interest still remains in the lessor which is called a reversion."
  2. Renewal clause transfers nothing (para 5). "Section 14 is applicable only where there is transfer of property. Even if creation of a lease hold interest is a transfer of a right in property and would fall within the expression 'transfer of property' the transfer was for a period of ten years only by means of the indenture Exh. P-1. The stipulation relating to renewal could not be regarded as transferring property or any rights therein."
  3. Ganesh Sonar applied; Woodall distinguished (para 6). The Patna High Court "quite rightly" distinguished Woodall v. Clifton "on the ground that after the coming into force of the Act a contract for the sale of immovable property did not itself create an interest in such property as was the case under the English law"; the lessor's option to resume was "merely a personal covenant and was not a covenant which created an interest in land and so the rule against perpetuity … was not applicable. The same principle would govern the present case. The clauses containing the option to get the lease renewed on the expiry of each term of ten years can by no means be regarded as creating an interest in property of the nature that would fall within the ambit of Section 14."
  4. English law also enforces perpetual renewal covenants (para 7). "Even under the English law the court would give effect to a covenant for perpetual renewal so long as the intention is clear and it will not be open to objection on the ground of perpetuity." Muller v. Trafford — Farwell J.: a covenant to renew "had been held for at least two centuries to be a covenant running with the land. If so, then no question of perpetuity would arise"; "it is then in my opinion free from any taint of perpetuity because it is annexed to the land" (Rogers v. Hosegood [1900] 2 Ch 388) — quoted with approval by Lord Evershed M.R. in Weg Motors v. Hales.
  5. s.40 and covenants running with the land (para 8). The equitable rule that the burden of a covenant runs with the land is in s.40 (set out in full). Per Mulla (5th edn, p. 194) "Section 40 expressly says that the right of the covenantee is not an interest in the land bound by the covenant nor an easement. It is not an interest because the Act does not recognise equitable estates and it cannot be said as Sir George Jessel said in Gomm that if a covenant 'binds the land it creates an equitable interest in the land'." "Even on the footing that the clauses relating to renewal … contain covenants running with the land the rule against perpetuity contained in Section 14 of the Act would not be applicable as no interest in property has been created of the nature contemplated by that provision."
  6. Result (para 9). Appeal dismissed with costs; the decree for specific performance — execution of a registered renewal lease from 1 Nov 1961 on the same terms (Rs 450 p.m.) — stood.

Principles

  1. s.14 applies only where a transfer of property creates a future interest in property; a covenant for renewal of a lease — even one renewable perpetually at the lessee's option — transfers no property and creates no interest; it is a personal/land-annexed covenant outside the rule.
  2. Since s.105 expressly permits a lease in perpetuity, a perpetually renewable lease cannot offend the policy of the Act; the lessor's reversion always subsists.
  3. Indian law does not recognise equitable estates; s.40 covenants running with the land are not interests in land — so English perpetuity reasoning based on equitable interests (Gomm; Woodall v. Clifton) does not apply. Options to the lessor to resume land are equally outside s.14 (Ganesh Sonar).
  4. Classic statement of the rule's rationale: liberty of alienation "shall not be exercised to its own destruction".
Example. In 2012 a Bengaluru landlord leases a showroom for 10 years at Rs 2 lakh p.m., "renewable at the tenant's option for successive terms of 10 years on the same terms so long as the tenant desires". In 2022 the tenant seeks renewal; the landlord refuses citing s.14. Under Kempraj the clause is valid; the tenant can obtain specific performance of a registered renewal deed (SRA s.10; TPA s.107). Note the practical qualification: rent-control legislation and the parties' own terms (e.g., rent revision) govern the content of the renewed lease; and clause 10-type prohibitions on assignment (s.10 lease exception) remain valid.

16. DU semester PYQs with model answers

Q1 (DU LL.B., May/June 2025, Q.7(a)). A transfers property in favour of B for life, thereafter to B's unborn son for life, and finally to X absolutely. Is this a valid transfer? Discuss in the light of relevant provisions of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and decided cases.
Model answer.

Issues. (i) Validity of the life interest in favour of B's unborn son; (ii) validity of the ultimate absolute interest in favour of X; (iii) devolution of the property.

Law. An unborn person cannot be a direct transferee (s.5 — "living persons"), but s.13 permits an interest to be created for the benefit of an unborn person provided (a) it is preceded by a prior interest created by the same transfer in a living person and (b) it "extends to the whole of the remaining interest of the transferor" — i.e., is absolute. The illustration to s.13 (A → B and intended wife for life → eldest son of the marriage for life → A's second son) declares the eldest son's life interest void "because it does not extend to the whole of A's remaining interest." s.14 further requires vesting within lives in being plus the beneficiary's minority. s.16 provides that where an interest fails under ss.13–14, "any interest created in the same transaction and intended to take effect after or upon failure of such prior interest also fails." s.20 — a validly created unborn's interest vests at birth.

Application. (1) B's life interest is valid — B is living. (2) The interest to B's unborn son is only a life interest; it does not carry the whole remaining interest of A → void under s.13. The Capsule's example is identical ("a life estate for the benefit of B's unborn first child … would be void"); Ram Newaz v. Nankoo (AIR 1926 All 283) struck down a reservation for the vendor's lineal descendants as "a condition repugnant to the law" because it created limited interests in unborn generations and might tie the land up "for 100 or 200 years" — the court applying the possible-events test ("not what in fact happened"). (3) X's interest, although absolute and in favour of a living person, is "intended to take effect after" the void life interest of the unborn son → fails under s.16 (as A's second son's interest fails in the illustration; as the vendee's interest failed in Ram Newaz). (4) Result: B enjoys for life; on B's death the property reverts to A or A's heirs.

How it could have been drafted validly. "To B for life, then to B's first son absolutely" (valid — vests at birth, s.20; Capsule ill.); or "to B for life, then to X absolutely" (no unborn involved). Conclusion: the transfer is valid only as to B's life interest; the gifts to the unborn son and to X are void.

Q2 (DU LL.B., May/June 2025, Q.7(b)). A clause in a lease deed provides that the option of renewal of the lease shall remain with the lessee; the lessor shall not have the right to raise any objection to the lease renewal. Does this clause offend the rule against perpetuity? Discuss in the light of decided case law.
Model answer.

Issue. Whether a lessee's option of (perpetual) renewal, which the lessor cannot object to, creates an interest that may vest beyond the period allowed by s.14 and is therefore void.

Law. s.14: "No transfer of property can operate to create an interest which is to take effect after the lifetime of one or more persons living at the date of such transfer, and the minority of some person…" The rule "falls within the branch of the law of property and its true object is to restrain the creation of future conditional interests in property … [it] is not concerned with contracts as such" (Ram Baran Prasad v. Ram Mohit Hazra, AIR 1967 SC 744 — pre-emption covenant unlimited in time held outside s.14 because a contract for sale creates no interest in land, ss.40, 54). s.105 defines a lease as a transfer of a right to enjoy property "for a certain time … or in perpetuity".

The decided case. The clause is almost verbatim clauses 9–10 in R. Kempraj v. Burton Son & Co. (AIR 1970 SC 1872): lease of premises on M.G. Road, Bangalore, for 10 years from 1-11-1951 at Rs 450 p.m., with the lessee's right to renew "for further similar periods … as long as they desire", the lessor "not [to] raise any objection whatsoever". The lessor resisted the lessee's suit for specific performance of renewal on the ground of perpetuity. The Supreme Court (Grover J.) held: (i) the rule rests on the principle that "the liberty of alienation shall not be exercised to its own destruction"; (ii) s.14 "is applicable only where there is transfer of property"; the lease transferred an interest for ten years only, and "the stipulation relating to renewal could not be regarded as transferring property or any rights therein"; (iii) s.105 permits a lease in perpetuity, the reversion remaining with the lessor, so a perpetually renewable lease cannot offend the Act; (iv) Ganesh Sonar v. Purnendu Narayan Singha (AIR 1962 Pat 201) rightly distinguished Woodall v. Clifton because in India a contract concerning land creates no interest in it; (v) even in England perpetual renewal covenants are enforced — Muller v. Trafford (Farwell J.: "free from any taint of perpetuity because it is annexed to the land"), approved in Weg Motors v. Hales; (vi) s.40 covenants running with land are not interests in land, the Act not recognising equitable estates; hence "no interest in property has been created of the nature contemplated by [s.14]". Appeal dismissed; renewal enforced.

Conclusion. The clause does not offend the rule against perpetuity. It is a covenant for renewal, not a transfer creating a future interest; the lessee may obtain specific performance of a registered renewal deed (s.107 TPA; SRA s.10), subject to any rent-control law. (Contrast: an option to purchase the freehold would also be valid in India for the same reason — Ram Baran Prasad — though void in England under Gomm.)

Q3. Explain the rule against perpetuity under s.14 TPA. What is the period of perpetuity in India and how does it differ from English law? State the exceptions.
Model answer (outline). Definition (Lewis, via Ram Baran Prasad); rationale (Kempraj quotation; Capsule objects); text of s.14; five essentials (Capsule) — transfer; ultimate unborn beneficiary with absolute interest (s.13); prior life interests in living persons; beneficiary in existence at end of last life; vesting ≤ minority. Period: lives in being + (gestation) + minority (18 — Majority Act); vesting must not be capable of being delayed beyond it; illustrations (18 valid, 25 void, 10 valid). Possible-events test (Ram Newaz). England: lives in being + 21 years in gross; 1964 Act (wait and see, 80 years), 2009 Act (125 years); India retains strict rule. Effect: interest void in toto; s.16 consequences; s.15 class gifts. Exceptions/limits: s.18 public benefit; personal covenants/contracts/pre-emption (Ram Baran Prasad); lease renewal/lease in perpetuity (Kempraj); vested interests with postponed enjoyment (s.19 Expl.); charges; Muslim/Hindu personal-law and statutory contexts; wills — s.114 ISA.
Q4. "The rule against perpetuity is a rule of property law and does not apply to contracts." Discuss with reference to covenants of pre-emption and options in leases.
Model answer (outline). State the proposition (Ram Baran Prasad para 7). Explain why: s.14 speaks of a "transfer of property" creating an "interest"; s.54 — contract for sale creates no interest; s.40 para 2 — obligation annexed to ownership "not amounting to an interest", enforceable against transferees with notice; India does not recognise equitable estates (Mulla, via Kempraj). Contrast England: contract creates equitable interest → option/pre-emption void if unlimited (Gomm; Woodall v. Clifton); Indian pre-1882 cases followed this (Tripoora Soonduree; Allibhai; Maharaj Bahadur Singh) on the now-superseded Fati Chand doctrine. Facts/holding of Ram Baran Prasad (partition award 1940; blocks A–D; chain of sales; Rs 14,000; binding on assignees — Contract Act ss.37, 40; SRA ss.23(b), 27(b); vicinage). Facts/holding of Kempraj (10-year lease, perpetual renewal, s.105, Ganesh Sonar, Muller v. Trafford). Personal covenants to pay money (Walsh; Witham v. Vane). Limits: where the "contract" is in truth a transfer creating a future interest (e.g., a settlement) the rule applies; a covenant may still be defeated by a bona fide purchaser without notice. Conclusion.

Q6. In 1884 R sold his zamindari share to V "except 2 bighas", providing that the 2 bighas would remain with R for life, then with his lineal descendants for ever without power of transfer, and if his line failed, would belong to V. R's son died childless in 1918. Decide between V's successors and R's reversioners.
Model answer. This is Ram Newaz v. Nankoo. Construction: the deed sold the share minus the 2 bighas; V got only "a possibility of becoming its owner at a future date provided that provision was one which the law would recognize." Validity: the reservation created (i) a valid life estate in R, (ii) limited interests without power of transfer in unborn lineal descendants generation after generation — void under s.13 (unborn may take only the whole remaining interest) and s.14 (the land "might have remained with the lineal descendants … for 100 or 200 years"), (iii) V's ultimate interest, to arise only on failure of the line — void under s.16 as dependent on the void prior interests. The fact that the line actually failed in 1918, within a life in being, is irrelevant: "the fact that it happened to fall in within the legal limitation is not the test … what you have to see is whether the event can be postponed beyond the period." The condition being "repugnant to the law", V's successors cannot rely on the deed; the 2 bighas remained R's and pass to his reversioners, who succeed. (Add: the restraint on transfer binding descendants is also an absolute restraint under s.10.)

17. Mnemonics & rapid revision

"P-A-L" for s.13: Prior interest to a living person · Absolute interest to the unborn · Life estate to an unborn is fatal.
"LIVES + MINORITY, NOT 21": Indian period = lives in being + minority (18) of the ultimate beneficiary; English = lives + 21 years.
"MIGHT, not DID": possible-events test — Ram Newaz ("100 or 200 years").
"No INTEREST, no PERPETUITY": contracts, pre-emption (Ram Baran Prasad), renewal options (Kempraj), covenants to pay money — all outside s.14 because ss.40/54 give them no interest in land.
"15 splits, 16 sinks, 17 stops at 18, 18 sets charity free": s.15 class splitting · s.16 dependent interests sink with the void prior · s.17 accumulation limited to 18 years/transferor's life · s.18 public benefit exempt.
Ten lines to remember
  1. Unborn cannot be a direct transferee (s.5); s.13 lets an interest be created for his benefit behind a prior interest in a living person.
  2. The unborn must get the whole remaining interest (absolute); a life estate to an unborn is void — illustration to s.13; Capsule; Ram Newaz.
  3. s.14: vesting no later than lives in being + minority of the ultimate beneficiary, who must exist at the end of the last life; "at 25" void, "at 18" valid.
  4. Judge by possible events at the date of transfer, not by what happened (Ram Newaz).
  5. Failure under ss.13–14 drags down any interest to take effect after or on failure of it (s.16); class gifts fail only as to offending members (s.15, post-1929).
  6. Vesting on birth (s.20); enjoyment may be postponed without making the interest contingent (s.19 Expl.).
  7. Ram Baran Prasad: pre-emption covenant without time-limit valid — rule is of property law; contract for sale creates no interest (ss.40, 54); binds assignees with notice.
  8. Kempraj: perpetual renewal option in a lease valid — renewal covenant transfers nothing; s.105 permits leases in perpetuity; no equitable estates in India.
  9. s.17: accumulation beyond the longer of transferor's life / 18 years void as to excess — except for debts, portions, preservation.
  10. s.18: transfers for public benefit (religion, knowledge, commerce, health, safety, etc.) exempt from ss.14, 16, 17; wills — ISA ss.113–118.
