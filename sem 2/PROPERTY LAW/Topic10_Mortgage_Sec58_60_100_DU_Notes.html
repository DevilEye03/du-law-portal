1. Mortgage — concept, history and definition

1.1 The idea

A mortgage is a security transaction: the owner of immovable property (the mortgagor) transfers an interest in it to a creditor (the mortgagee) so that, if the debt is not repaid, the creditor can look to the property. Ownership is not parted with; only such interest as is necessary for the security passes. Lindley M.R. in Santley v. Wilde (1899) 2 Ch 474 (quoted in all three DU cases): "a mortgage is a conveyance of land or an assignment of chattels as a security for the payment of a debt or the discharge of some other obligation for which it is given. This is the idea of a mortgage: and the security is redeemable on the payment or discharge of such debt or obligation, any provision to the contrary notwithstanding."

1.2 Historical background — why "equity of redemption"?

Viscount Haldane L.C. in G. & C. Kreglinger v. New Patagonia Meat & Cold Storage Co. [1914] AC 25 (quoted in Ganga Dhar and Pomal Kanji): "The case of the common law mortgage of land was indeed a gross one. The land was conveyed to the creditor upon the condition that if the money he had advanced to the feoffor was repaid on a date and at a place named, the fee simple would revest in the latter, but that if the condition was not strictly and literally fulfilled he should lose the land for ever. What made the hardship on the debtor a glaring one was that the debt still remained unpaid and could be recovered from the feoffor notwithstanding that he had actually forfeited the land to the mortgagee. Equity, therefore, at an early date began to relieve against what was virtually a penalty by compelling the creditor to use his legal title as a mere security."

Thus at common law the mortgagor's right to get his land back ended on the "law day"; Chancery allowed him to redeem afterwards — this right was the "equity of redemption", itself an estate in land. In India, s.60 converts the equity into a statutory right to redeem: "Under the Indian law, the terms 'right to redeem' and 'equity of redemption' are synonymous … The mortgagor's right to redeem even after the expiry of the date fixed for payment is not an equity, it is a statutory right" (Capsule; Shivdev Singh, para 8).

1.3 Section 58(a) — definition

s.58(a). "A mortgage is the transfer of an interest in specific immoveable property for the purpose of securing the payment of money advanced or to be advanced by way of loan, an existing or future debt, or the performance of an engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liability. The transferor is called a mortgagor, the transferee a mortgagee; the principal money and interest of which payment is secured for the time being are called the mortgage-money, and the instrument (if any) by which the transfer is effected is called a mortgage-deed."
Element (Capsule: four essentials)Explanation
(i) Parties — mortgagor and mortgageeMortgagor must be competent to transfer (s.7) — owner or person with authority (guardian with court leave under HMGA s.8; karta for legal necessity; agent with power of attorney). Mortgagee may be any person capable of holding property, including a minor (through guardian) or a company. By s.59A, references to mortgagor/mortgagee include persons deriving title from them (assignees, heirs, purchasers of the equity of redemption — e.g., Sucha Singh in Shivdev Singh; Motilal's sons in Ganga Dhar).
(ii) Transfer of an interestNot a transfer of ownership; a jus in re aliena carved out of ownership. The quantum differs by kind — bare right of sale (simple), possession and usufruct (usufructuary), absolute transfer with proviso for re-transfer (English), ostensible sale (conditional sale). What remains with the mortgagor is the equity of redemption, which is itself transferable, heritable, mortgageable and attachable.
(iii) Specific immovable propertyThe property must be specified so as to be identifiable (a mortgage of "all my property" is bad for uncertainty; "my house No. 12, Katra, Prayagraj" is specific). Immovable property per s.3 TPA / s.3(26) General Clauses Act (land, benefits arising out of land, things attached to the earth — but not standing timber, growing crops or grass). Movables cannot be mortgaged under the TPA (they are pledged/hypothecated).
(iv) Purpose — security for (a) money advanced or to be advanced by way of loan; (b) an existing or future debt; (c) performance of an engagement which may give rise to a pecuniary liabilityThe transfer must be for the purpose of securing — the "consideration" is the debt/obligation. Covers: present loan; future advances (cash-credit/overdraft); antecedent debt (Ganga Dhar: Rs 5,750 left with the mortgagee to redeem a prior mortgage); contingent liability (a guarantee, a lessee's covenant, a government contractor's security bond). If the transfer is absolute and not by way of security, it is a sale even if the price is called a "loan"; conversely, a document styled "sale" may in substance be a mortgage — see s.58(c) proviso and s.92 Evidence Act.
Mortgage-money = principal + interest secured "for the time being" (so it includes accrued interest, and, by contract, costs, repairs — Ganga Dhar repairs clause; s.72 mortgagee's expenditure). Mortgage-deed — "if any" — because a mortgage by deposit of title deeds (s.58(f)) and a mortgage for under Rs 100 by delivery (s.59) need no deed.
Real example. A home loan from SBI: the borrower signs a registered "Memorandum of Deposit of Title Deeds" (in notified towns) or a registered simple/English mortgage deed; the bank does not take possession; on default, the bank proceeds under the SARFAESI Act, 2002 (s.13(4)) or files a mortgage suit under O.XXXIV CPC. The borrower's right to repay and get his documents back is his right to redeem (s.60); it survives even after the bank issues a s.13(2) demand notice — until the sale is completed (Mathew Varghese v. M. Amritha Kumar (2014) 5 SCC 610; s.13(8) SARFAESI as amended 2016).

3. Kinds of mortgages — s.58(b)–(g)

The Act "gives an exhaustive definition of mortgage and recognises six variants" (Capsule). The classification matters because the remedies of the mortgagee (foreclosure / sale / possession / personal decree — s.67, s.68) and the formalities (s.59) depend on the kind.

3.1 Simple mortgage — s.58(b)

"Where, without delivering possession of the mortgaged property, the mortgagor binds himself personally to pay the mortgage-money, and agrees, expressly or impliedly, that, in the event of his failing to pay according to his contract, the mortgagee shall have a right to cause the mortgaged property to be sold and the proceeds of sale to be applied, so far as may be necessary, in payment of the mortgage-money, the transaction is called a simple mortgage and the mortgagee a simple mortgagee."
ElementsNotes
1. No delivery of possessionMortgagor keeps possession and enjoyment; mortgagee cannot appropriate rents.
2. Personal covenant to payExpress or implied; enables a personal decree (s.68(a); O.XXXIV r.6 CPC) if the sale proceeds fall short.
3. Express or implied power to have the property sold"Cause … to be sold" — only through court (suit for sale, O.XXXIV rr.4–5); no power of private sale (s.69 applies only to English mortgages and specified cases) and no foreclosure (s.67 proviso (a)). Capsule: "He acquires only the 'right of sale', and that too, only through the Court."
Example. R borrows Rs 20 lakh from S and executes a registered deed: "I shall repay with 12% interest within 3 years; in default S may have my shop sold through court." R stays in possession. This is a simple mortgage. On default S sues for sale; if the shop fetches Rs 15 lakh, S gets a personal decree for the balance under O.XXXIV r.6.

3.2 Mortgage by conditional sale — s.58(c)

"Where the mortgagor ostensibly sells the mortgaged property — on condition that on default of payment of the mortgage-money on a certain date the sale shall become absolute, or on condition that on such payment being made the sale shall become void, or on condition that on such payment being made the buyer shall transfer the property to the seller, the transaction is called a mortgage by conditional sale …: Provided that no such transaction shall be deemed to be a mortgage, unless the condition is embodied in the document which effects or purports to effect the sale." (Proviso added by Act 20 of 1929.)
  • "Ostensibly" — "seeming or apparent, but not actually the same thing. It imports that it is really not a sale" (Capsule). The transaction wears the form of a sale but is in substance a security; there is a debt, though usually no personal covenant.
  • Three alternative conditions: (a) default → sale becomes absolute; (b) payment → sale becomes void; (c) payment → buyer re-transfers.
  • Proviso (1929) — the condition must be in the same document; if the re-conveyance agreement is in a separate document, the transaction is a sale with a condition of repurchase (and the option must be exercised strictly in time) — this proviso reversed the earlier practice of proving the mortgage character by a separate agreement. However, even a single document may be a sale out-and-out if the parties so intended; the proviso only excludes separate documents, it does not make every single document with a repurchase clause a mortgage (Chunchun Jha; Pandit Chunchun Jha; Tamboli Ramanlal v. Ghanchi Chimanlal AIR 1992 SC 1236; Vithal Tukaram Kadam v. Vamanrao Sawalaram Bhosale (2018) 11 SCC 172). Tests: relationship of debtor–creditor; price v. market value; possession; who pays taxes; period for repurchase; interest.
  • Remedy of the mortgagee: foreclosure only (s.67 proviso (a) — a mortgagee by conditional sale cannot sue for sale); no personal decree absent a covenant.
  • The clause in Ganga Dhar — "in case we do not redeem within six months … this very deed will be deemed to be a sale deed" — is an attempt to convert a usufructuary mortgage into a conditional-sale forfeiture; it was struck down as a clog.

3.3 Usufructuary mortgage — s.58(d)

"Where the mortgagor delivers possession or expressly or by implication binds himself to deliver possession of the mortgaged property to the mortgagee, and authorises him to retain such possession until payment of the mortgage-money, and to receive the rents and profits accruing from the property or any part of such rents and profits and to appropriate the same in lieu of interest, or in payment of the mortgage-money, or partly in lieu of interest or partly in payment of the mortgage-money, the transaction is called an usufructuary mortgage …" (words in italics inserted/substituted by Act 20 of 1929).
ElementsNotes
1. Delivery of possession (or binding to deliver)Actual or constructive (e.g., attornment of tenants). Mortgagee out of possession may sue for possession and mesne profits (s.68(d) — personal decree if not put in possession).
2. Retention until paymentNo fixed time-limit is necessary; the mortgage lasts until the money is paid out of the usufruct or by the mortgagor. Where the usufruct is to be appropriated towards the principal, the mortgage is self-liquidating and the mortgagor may recover possession when the debt is paid off (s.62(a)); where towards interest only, on payment/tender/deposit of the principal (s.62(b)).
3. Appropriation of rents and profitsIn lieu of interest / towards principal / both. The mortgagee must account (s.76(g)).
4. No personal liability"The mortgagor will not be personally liable unless there is a distinct agreement to the contrary" (Capsule). Consequently the mortgagee cannot sue for sale or foreclosure (s.67 proviso (a)); his remedy is to remain in possession.
All three DU cases concern usufructuary (or usufructuary-plus) mortgages: Ganga Dhar — shop usufructuarily mortgaged for Rs 6,300, rents in lieu of interest, 85 years; Pomal Kanji — usufructuary mortgage for 20,000 koris plus a further 10,000 koris at ½% p.m. interest payable at redemption, 99 years (a combination → anomalous); Shivdev Singh — 23 kanals of agricultural land mortgaged with possession for Rs 7,000, 99 years.

3.4 English mortgage — s.58(e)

"Where the mortgagor binds himself to re-pay the mortgage-money on a certain date, and transfers the mortgaged property absolutely to the mortgagee, but subject to a proviso that he will re-transfer it to the mortgagor upon payment of the mortgage-money as agreed, the transaction is called an English mortgage."
  • Elements (Capsule): loan; absolute transfer; proviso for re-transfer on payment; certain date for payment; personal covenant.
  • "Absolutely" is qualified by the proviso and by s.58(a) — only an interest passes; the mortgagor retains the equity of redemption. Mortgagee may take possession (unless otherwise agreed).
  • Remedies: suit for sale (not foreclosure — s.67 proviso (a)); personal decree; power of sale without court under s.69(1)(a) where neither party is a Hindu, Muslim or Buddhist (or member of a notified class), or (b) mortgagee is the Government, or (c) property in Calcutta/Madras/Bombay or notified towns; appointment of receiver (s.69A).
  • Distinguish from conditional sale: English mortgage has a personal covenant and an absolute conveyance with a covenant to re-convey; conditional sale has an ostensible sale that becomes absolute on default.

3.5 Mortgage by deposit of title-deeds ("equitable mortgage") — s.58(f)

"Where a person in any of the following towns, namely, the towns of Calcutta, Madras and Bombay, and in any other town which the State Government concerned may, by notification in the Official Gazette, specify in this behalf, delivers to a creditor or his agent documents of title to immoveable property, with intent to create a security thereon, the transaction is called a mortgage by deposit of title-deeds."
ElementsNotes
1. Debt (existing or future)Loan, overdraft, cash-credit, guarantee.
2. Deposit of documents of titleDelivery of the deeds (or material deeds) to the creditor or agent; constructive delivery (deeds already with the bank for another purpose + fresh intention) suffices.
3. Intention to create securityThe essence; may be proved by oral evidence and conduct. If a memorandum records the terms of the bargain (not merely the fact of deposit) it requires registration (s.17 Registration Act; Rachpal Mahraj v. Bhagwandas Daruka AIR 1950 SC 272; United Bank of India v. Lekharam Sonaram AIR 1965 SC 1591).
4. In a notified townDeposit must be made in Calcutta, Madras, Bombay or a notified town (most State capitals and commercial towns, e.g., Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Prayagraj are notified); the property may be anywhere (Capsule).

No writing, registration or attestation is needed (s.59 first para, "other than a mortgage by deposit of title-deeds"); s.96 applies the provisions relating to simple mortgages to it — hence remedy by suit for sale and personal decree; no foreclosure. It ranks with registered mortgages according to priority in time (s.48), subject to s.78 (postponement for fraud/gross neglect). Since 2016, many States require registration of the memorandum/notice of deposit (e.g., Maharashtra s.17(1)(f) Registration Act as amended) and CERSAI registration under SARFAESI ss.20–26 gives constructive notice.

3.6 Anomalous mortgage — s.58(g)

"A mortgage which is not a simple mortgage, a mortgage by conditional sale, an usufructuary mortgage, an English mortgage or a mortgage by deposit of title-deeds within the meaning of this section is called an anomalous mortgage."

A residuary category — combinations (simple + usufructuary, as in Pomal Kanji: possession delivered and interest payable on part of the principal; usufructuary + conditional sale, as attempted in Ganga Dhar) and customary forms (Malabar kanom, otti; Punjab lekha-mukhi; Bengal zar-i-peshgi lease). Rights and liabilities are governed by the contract and, so far as not, by local usage (s.98). Remedies per s.67: foreclosure or sale as the terms permit (s.67 proviso (a) allows an anomalous mortgagee to foreclose only if the terms so permit). Privy Council in Mohammad Sher Khan v. Seth Swami Dayal AIR 1922 PC 17 (quoted in Ganga Dhar): "An anomalous mortgage enabling a mortgagee after a lapse of time and in the absence of redemption to enter and take the rents in satisfaction of the interest would be perfectly valid if it did not also hinder an existing right to redeem."

4. Mode of execution of mortgages — s.59, s.59A, s.96

s.59. "Where the principal money secured is one hundred rupees or upwards, a mortgage other than a mortgage by deposit of title-deeds can be effected only by a registered instrument signed by the mortgagor and attested by at least two witnesses. Where the principal money secured is less than one hundred rupees, a mortgage may be effected either by a registered instrument signed and attested as aforesaid, or (except in the case of a simple mortgage) by delivery of the property."
RequirementDetail and related law
WritingA mortgage of Rs 100 or more (other than by deposit of title deeds) must be in writing; oral mortgage void; oral evidence of a mortgage inadmissible except under s.58(f) or s.59 para 2.
Signature of the mortgagorPersonally or by agent (power of attorney — Powers of Attorney Act 1882; s.33 Registration Act for registration through an attorney).
Attestation by at least two witnesses"Attested" defined in s.3 TPA (inserted 1926): each witness must have seen the executant sign/affix mark, or seen some other person sign in his presence and by his direction, or received from the executant a personal acknowledgment of his signature, and must sign in the executant's presence; witnesses need not be present at the same time; no particular form. A party to the mortgage or the scribe may not be a competent attesting witness if he signs only as scribe. Proof: s.68 Evidence Act (s.67 BSA) — at least one attesting witness must be called if alive and available (unless execution is admitted — proviso applies only to non-will registered documents where execution is not specifically denied). An unattested (or unregistered) deed creates no mortgage; the settled view is that it cannot be treated as a charge either, since the parties intended a mortgage and not a charge — it may at most evidence the personal debt.
Registrations.17(1)(b) Registration Act, 1908 — compulsory for non-testamentary instruments creating a right in immovable property of Rs 100 or more; s.49 — unregistered document does not affect the property nor is receivable as evidence of the transaction (except for collateral purposes / s.53A). Registration within 4 months (s.23); at the sub-registrar's office where the property is situate (s.28). Effective from execution (s.47) — priority dates from execution, not registration.
Stamp dutyIndian Stamp Act 1899, Sch. I Art. 40 (mortgage-deed — rates differ for possession/without possession; UP Stamp Act rates); Art. 6 (agreement relating to deposit of title deeds). Unstamped/insufficiently stamped instrument inadmissible until duty and penalty paid (s.35).
Mortgages under Rs 100May be by registered instrument, or by delivery of possession — hence not available for a simple mortgage (no possession passes).
Mortgage by deposit of title deedsExcepted from s.59 — created by mere deposit with intent; but a memorandum embodying the terms needs registration (Part 3.5).
s.59A (1929)References to mortgagors/mortgagees include persons deriving title from them — purchasers of the equity of redemption can redeem; assignees of the mortgage can enforce.
Agricultural land / State lawsMany States restrict mortgages of agricultural land to non-agriculturists or to certain periods (e.g., Punjab Land Alienation Act (repealed), Punjab Redemption of Mortgages Act 1913 — summary redemption before the Collector; UP Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act 1950, s.153 — a bhumidhar may mortgage only without possession, usufructuary mortgage prohibited).
Example. M signs a "mortgage deed" for Rs 5 lakh in favour of N before a single witness and does not register it. It is not a valid mortgage (s.59) and cannot affect the house (s.49 Registration Act). N can only sue M on the personal covenant/loan (Art. 19/22 Limitation Act, 3 years). Had M deposited his sale deed with N at Prayagraj (a notified town) with the intention to secure the loan, a valid s.58(f) mortgage would exist without any writing.

5. Rights of the mortgagor — Section 60 (right to redeem) and allied sections

s.60. Right of mortgagor to redeem. — "At any time after the principal money has become due, the mortgagor has a right, on payment or tender, at a proper time and place, of the mortgage-money, to require the mortgagee (a) to deliver to the mortgagor the mortgage-deed and all documents relating to the mortgaged property which are in the possession or power of the mortgagee, (b) where the mortgagee is in possession of the mortgaged property, to deliver possession thereof to the mortgagor, and (c) at the cost of the mortgagor either to re-transfer the mortgaged property to him or to such third person as he may direct, or to execute and (where the mortgage has been effected by a registered instrument) to have registered an acknowledgement in writing that any right in derogation of his interest transferred to the mortgagee has been extinguished:
Provided that the right conferred by this section has not been extinguished by act of the parties or by decree of a Court.
The right conferred by this section is called a right to redeem and a suit to enforce it is called a suit for redemption.
Nothing in this section shall be deemed to render invalid any provision to the effect that, if the time fixed for payment of the principal money has been allowed to pass or no such time has been fixed, the mortgagee shall be entitled to reasonable notice before payment or tender of such money.
Redemption of portion of mortgaged property. — Nothing in this section shall entitle a person interested in a share only of the mortgaged property to redeem his own share only, on payment of a proportionate part of the amount remaining due on the mortgage, except only where a mortgagee, or, if there are more mortgagees than one, all such mortgagees, has or have acquired, in whole or in part, the share of a mortgagor."
ClauseAnalysis (with DU case references)
"At any time after the principal money has become due"The right accrues when the principal becomes due — on the date fixed, or at once if no date is fixed, or on the happening of the event agreed (e.g., expiry of the term). "Due" was substituted for "payable" in 1929. Ganga Dhar (para 4, quoting Bakhtawar Begum v. Husaini Khanam AIR 1914 PC 36): "Ordinarily, and in the absence of a special condition entitling the mortgagor to redeem during the term for which the mortgage is created, the right of redemption can only arise on the expiration of the specified period." Hence a suit before the term is premature — unless the term itself is struck down as a clog (Pomal Kanji, Shivdev Singh). "At any time" — thereafter the right continues indefinitely, subject only to limitation (30 years, Art. 61(a)) and extinguishment; there is no "once due, redeem now or never".
"the mortgagor"Includes persons deriving title (s.59A): heirs (sons in Ganga Dhar, Pomal Kanji), purchaser of the equity of redemption (Sucha Singh), subsequent mortgagee, co-mortgagor, lessee, surety, judgment-creditor who has attached — see s.91 for other persons entitled to redeem.
"on payment or tender, at a proper time and place, of the mortgage-money"Mortgage-money = principal + interest + other sums secured (repairs under the deed — Ganga Dhar; costs). Tender must be unconditional and of the whole amount; the mortgagor may deposit in court (s.83) and stop interest (s.84). In Shivdev Singh the purchaser of only 19 of 23 kanals tendered the whole Rs 7,000 "realising that partial redemption was not permissible."
Three-fold requirement: (a) documents, (b) possession, (c) re-transfer / registered acknowledgement(a) deed and title deeds in mortgagee's power; (b) possession where mortgagee is in possession (usufructuary/English/anomalous) — subject to tenancy questions (Part 9); (c) re-conveyance at mortgagor's cost, or, where the mortgage was registered, a registered receipt/acknowledgement of extinguishment (Registration Act s.17(2)(xi)). Non-compliance → suit for redemption; costs.
Proviso — extinguishment "by act of the parties or by decree of a Court"The only two ways the right ends: (1) act of parties — e.g., mortgagee purchases the equity of redemption by a subsequent, independent transaction (not by a stipulation in the mortgage itself, which would be a clog), release, merger (s.101 exceptions); (2) decree — final decree for foreclosure (O.XXXIV r.3) or sale (r.5/8) — "before his equity of redemption has been actually foreclosed" (Capsule). Jayasingh Dnyanu Mhoprekar v. Krishna Babaji Patil AIR 1985 SC 1646 (quoted in Shivdev Singh): "the right of redemption … can come to an end only in a manner known to law. Such extinguishment … can take place by a contract between the parties, by a merger or by a statutory provision which debars the mortgagor from redeeming."
Reasonable noticeIf the due date has passed (or none was fixed) the deed may validly require reasonable notice before tender — protects the mortgagee's reinvestment; not a clog.
Partial redemption — the rule of integrity/indivisibility of the mortgageA co-mortgagor or purchaser of part cannot redeem his share on proportionate payment (the security is indivisible; s.67 last para — mortgagee cannot sue for part either), unless the mortgagee(s) have themselves acquired part of the equity of redemption (then the integrity is broken). Hence Sucha Singh, purchaser of a part, had to redeem the whole (with a right to contribution under s.82 / s.92 subrogation against the remaining owner).

6. Rights of the mortgagee — foreclosure and sale (s.67) and others

s.67. Right to foreclosure or sale. — "In the absence of a contract to the contrary, the mortgagee has, at any time after the mortgage-money has become due to him, and before a decree has been made for the redemption of the mortgaged property, or the mortgage-money has been paid or deposited as hereinafter provided, a right to obtain from the Court a decree that the mortgagor shall be absolutely debarred of his right to redeem the property, or a decree that the property be sold. A suit to obtain a decree that a mortgagor shall be absolutely debarred of his right to redeem the mortgaged property is called a suit for foreclosure. Nothing in this section shall be deemed — (a) to authorise any mortgagee other than a mortgagee by conditional sale or a mortgagee under an anomalous mortgage by the terms of which he is entitled to foreclose, to institute a suit for foreclosure, or an usufructuary mortgagee as such or a mortgagee by conditional sale as such to institute a suit for sale; or (b) to authorise a mortgagor who holds the mortgagee's rights as his trustee or legal representative … to institute a suit for foreclosure; or (c) to authorise the mortgagee of a railway, canal, or other work in the maintenance of which the public are interested, to institute a suit for foreclosure or sale; or (d) to authorise a person interested in part only of the mortgage-money to institute a suit relating only to a corresponding part of the mortgaged property, unless the mortgagees have, with the consent of the mortgagor, severed their interests under the mortgage."
  • Co-extensive rights. "It is well settled that the mortgagee's right to enforce the mortgage and the mortgagor's right to redeem are co-extensive" (Ganga Dhar, para 18) — both arise when the mortgage-money becomes due. Hence in Ganga Dhar the argument that the mortgagee could demand his money at any time while the mortgagor was locked in for 85 years was "plainly fallacious".
  • Foreclosure — decree debarring redemption; available only to a mortgagee by conditional sale and an anomalous mortgagee whose terms permit. Procedure: O.XXXIV r.2 preliminary decree (account; time ≥ 6 months to pay), r.3 final decree — extinguishes the right to redeem (the "decree of a Court" in s.60 proviso). Even after a preliminary decree the mortgagor may redeem until the final decree.
  • Sale — available to simple, English, deposit-of-title-deeds and (as terms permit) anomalous mortgagees; O.XXXIV rr.4–5: preliminary decree, then final decree for sale; mortgagor may redeem until confirmation of sale (O.XXI r.92) — in Mhadagonda Ramgonda Patil v. Shripal Balwant Rainade AIR 1988 SC 1200 and Mademsetty Satyanarayana v. G. Yelloji Rao — right subsists till sale is confirmed.
  • Personal decree — s.68 (where mortgagor binds himself personally; where security is destroyed/insufficient without mortgagee's fault; where mortgagee is deprived of security by wrongful act of mortgagor; usufructuary mortgagee not put in possession) and O.XXXIV r.6.
  • Power of sale without court — s.69 (English mortgage where neither party is Hindu/Muslim/Buddhist; Government mortgagee; property in presidency/notified towns); s.69A receiver. Today banks/FIs use SARFAESI Act s.13 — statutory power of sale without court for secured creditors (the mortgagor's right to redeem survives till publication of the auction notice — s.13(8) post-2016).
  • Other rights: s.70 accessions; s.71 renewed lease; s.72 expenditure (preservation, title defence, insurance) recoverable with interest — the statutory basis for repairs clauses; s.73 proceeds of revenue sale/compensation; s.76 liabilities of mortgagee in possession (manage prudently, collect rents, pay revenue, necessary repairs, not commit destructive acts, insure, keep accounts, apply receipts) — relevant to the "prudent management" factor in Pomal Kanji, para 27.
Limitation (Limitation Act 1963). Art. 61(a) — suit by mortgagor to redeem or recover possession: 30 years from when the right to redeem accrues (so in Ganga Dhar, if the 85-year term stood, the right accrued in 1984 and a redemption suit would lie till 2014); Art. 61(b) — to recover possession after foreclosure; Art. 62 — mortgagee's suit for money charged on immovable property (sale): 12 years from when the money becomes due; Art. 63(a) — foreclosure: 30 years; Art. 63(b) — possession: 12 years. Extinguishment of the mortgagor's remedy under s.27 Limitation Act extinguishes the right itself — the third statutory mode of extinguishment referred to in Jayasingh Dnyanu.

7. Clog on the equity of redemption — "once a mortgage, always a mortgage"

7.1 The rule

Ganga Dhar, para 6 (Sarkar J.): "The rule against clogs on the equity of redemption is that, a mortgage shall always be redeemable and a mortgagor's right to redeem shall neither be taken away nor be limited by any contract between the parties." Lindley M.R., Santley v. Wilde: "Any provision inserted to prevent redemption on payment or performance of the debt or obligation for which the security was given is what is meant by a clog or fetter on the equity of redemption and is therefore void. It follows from this, that 'once a mortgage always a mortgage'." Mulla (quoted in Pomal Kanji, para 21): "a mortgage being a security for the debt, the right of redemption continues although the mortgagor fails to pay the debt at due date. Any provision inserted to prevent, evade or hamper redemption is void." Collins M.R., Jarrah Timber v. Samuel (1903) 2 Ch 1: it is the right of a mortgagor on redemption "to get back the subject of the mortgage and to hold and enjoy as he was entitled to hold and enjoy it before the mortgage."

7.2 Why the rule exists — the equity of "want of conscience"

  • Lord Northington L.C., Vernon v. Bethell (1762) 28 ER 838: "This court, as a court of conscience, is very jealous of persons taking securities for a loan, and converting such securities into purchases. And therefore I take it to be an established rule, that a mortgagee can never provide at the time of making the loan for any event or condition on which the equity of redemption shall be discharged, and the conveyance absolute. And there is great reason and justice in this rule, for necessitous men are not, truly speaking, free men, but, to answer a present exigency, will submit to any terms that the crafty may impose upon them."
  • Viscount Haldane L.C., Kreglinger: the jurisdiction "was merely a special application of a more general power to relieve against penalties and to mould them into mere securities … the end … has always been to ascertain, by parol evidence if need be, the real nature and substance of the transaction, and if it turned out to be in truth one of mortgage simply, to place it on that footing. It was, in ordinary cases, only where there was conduct which the Court of Chancery regarded as unconscientious that it interfered with freedom of contract."
  • Sarkar J.'s synthesis (Ganga Dhar, para 15): "The reason then justifying the court's power to relieve a mortgagor from the effects of his bargain is its want of conscience. Putting it in more familiar language the court's jurisdiction … depends on whether it was obtained by taking advantage of any difficulty or embarrassment that he might have been in when he borrowed the moneys on the mortgage. Was the mortgagor oppressed? Was he imposed upon? If he was, then he may be entitled to relief."
  • Indian footing: s.60 "is unqualified in its terms, and contains no saving provision as other sections do in favour of contracts to the contrary" (Mohammad Sher Khan v. Swami Dayal, PC 1922 — quoted in Ganga Dhar and Shivdev Singh); the Capsule: "in Section 60 there are no such words as 'in the absence of a contract to the contrary'." The doctrine is "a rule of justice, equity and good conscience" (Pomal Kanji, para 22) and the right is "a statutory and legal right which cannot be extinguished by any agreement made at the time of mortgage as part of the mortgage transaction" (Shivdev Singh, para 8).

7.3 Scope and limits of the doctrine

PropositionSource
Applies only to stipulations made at the time of the mortgage and as part of the mortgage transaction; a subsequent, independent agreement (e.g., sale of the equity of redemption to the mortgagee for fresh consideration) is valid.Capsule ("it does not apply where they subsequently vary the terms"); Maganlal Chhotalal v. Bhalchandra (1974) 15 Guj LR 193 (cited in Pomal Kanji, para 35); Privy Council in Mehrban Khan v. Makhna AIR 1930 PC 142.
Two levels of relief: (1) a term that wholly takes away the right to redeem is void per se; (2) a term that restricts the right is relieved against only if unconscionable.Ganga Dhar, para 12: "If he has agreed to forfeit wholly his right to redeem in certain circumstances, that agreement will be avoided. But the courts have gone beyond this. They have also relieved mortgagors from bargains whereby the right to redeem has not been taken away but restricted … The extent of this power has … to be ascertained by having regard to its origin."
Whether a term is a clog is a mixed question of law and fact, judged on the totality of circumstances.Pomal Kanji, paras 2, 26–27; Ganga Dhar, para 16 ("essentially one of fact … each turns on its own facts").
Relief is equitable and should be sought within a reasonable time; delay may be considered (but the length of delay alone is not decisive; the relevant financial condition is that at the time of the mortgage).Bhika v. Sheikh Amir AIR 1923 Nag 60 (cited in Pomal Kanji, para 25); Ganga Dhar, para 17 (suit after 47 years); Pomal Kanji, para 36 (son becoming a Civil Judge irrelevant).
The doctrine "is also subject to the mortgagee's rights prior to the mortgage" and is analogous to the rule against perpetuities in that "no one can take away the right which the law vests in him."Capsule.
Severability: an invalid clog does not invalidate an independent term fixing the period of the mortgage.Ganga Dhar, para 9 ("This latter term stands quite apart … The invalidity of one does not make the other also invalid").

7.5 What is NOT a clog

  • A term fixing when the principal becomes due (even a long one) that is fair and freely made — Ganga Dhar.
  • A requirement of reasonable notice before payment (s.60, third para).
  • Interest at a rate agreed, or usufruct in lieu of interest; provision for necessary repairs with accounts.
  • A subsequent sale/release of the equity of redemption to the mortgagee for fresh consideration.
  • Collateral advantages that are reasonable, not unconscionable and do not survive redemption (Kreglinger).
  • The mortgagee's statutory right to foreclose by court decree (s.67) — the law itself provides for termination "by decree of a Court".

Reconciling the cases. Ganga Dhar is not overruled. All three agree that (i) the right to redeem is statutory and cannot be excluded; (ii) whether a restriction is a clog is a question of fact judged by the totality of circumstances; (iii) length of term is not by itself a clog. The shift is evidential: after Pomal Kanji, a "long term, very long term" in an inflationary economy "taken with other relevant factors would create a presumption" of a clog (para 29) — placing a practical burden on the mortgagee to show the bargain was fair; whereas in Ganga Dhar the mortgagor had to prove oppression and failed. The factors that distinguish the outcomes: mortgagor's financial distress at the time; interest accumulating to the end; reconstruction liberty; meagre consideration relative to value; whether the mortgage gave the mortgagor a benefit (freeing the kacheri in Ganga Dhar).

9. Extinguishment of the right to redeem; tenants inducted by the mortgagee

  • Modes (s.60 proviso; Jayasingh Dnyanu): (1) act of parties — subsequent contract (sale of equity of redemption, release), merger (s.101 — unless the mortgagee-purchaser keeps the mortgage alive against puisne encumbrancers); (2) decree of court — final decree of foreclosure; confirmed sale in a mortgage suit; (3) statute — e.g., extinction under s.27 Limitation Act after 30 years (Art. 61(a)); acquisition of the property by the State; special enactments.
  • Not by: mere efflux of the date fixed; mortgagee's long possession (his possession is permissive as mortgagee and cannot become adverse — Shivdev Singh: 26 years of usufruct no bar); a stipulation in the mortgage itself; a compromise that re-states a clog.
  • Tenants inducted by a mortgagee in possession — the second issue in Pomal Kanji: a mortgagee in possession may lease (s.76(a) prudent management), but such a lease ordinarily does not survive redemption; the tenants must deliver possession to the redeeming mortgagor and cannot claim protection of the Rent Act against him unless the Rent Act's definition of "tenant"/"landlord" expressly covers them or the mortgagor has accepted them as tenants (Capsule: "A tenant of a mortgagee in possession cannot continue in possession after redemption"). Followed in Sachalmal Parasram v. Ratanbai AIR 1972 SC 637; Mahabir Gope v. Harbans Narain Singh AIR 1952 SC 205 (exception — lease in the ordinary course of prudent management, e.g., agricultural tenancy under statute, may bind); Harihar Prasad Singh v. Deonarain Prasad AIR 1956 SC 305; All India Film Corpn. v. Raja Gyan Nath (1969) 3 SCC 79.

CASE 1 — Ganga Dhar v. Shankar Lal

AIR 1958 SC 770 : 1959 SCR 509 · Supreme Court of India · Sarkar J. (for the Bench) · Appeal from the Judicial Commissioner, Ajmer, who had reversed the Sub-Judge's preliminary decree for redemption · DU material pp. 129–134

Theme The classical statement of the rule against clogs. An 85-year term is not by itself a clog; relief against a restriction on redemption depends on "want of conscience" — whether the mortgagee took advantage of the mortgagor's difficulties; but a clause that after the term the mortgage must be redeemed within six months or the deed becomes a sale is a clog and void.

Facts

  • The mortgage: On 1 August 1899 Purshottamdas (mortgagor) executed a usufructuary mortgage of a four-roomed shop with appurtenances on a plot of 5 yards × 15 yards in Naya Bazar, Ajmer, in favour of Dhanrupmal (mortgagee) for Rs 6,300, of which Rs 5,750 was left with the mortgagee to redeem a prior mortgage covering the shop and another property (a share in a Kacheri). On redemption of the prior mortgage, the mortgagee was to take and retain possession of the shop, appropriating its rent in lieu of interest, while possession of the Kacheri share was to be made over to the mortgagor.
  • The disputed clause: "I or my heirs will not be entitled to redeem the property for a period of 85 years. After the expiry of 85 years we shall redeem it within a period of six months. In case we do not redeem within a period of six months, then after the expiry of the stipulated period, I, my heirs, and legal representatives shall have no claim over the mortgaged property, and the mortgagee shall have no claim to get the mortgage money and the lagat (repairs) expenses that may be due at the time of default. In such a case this very deed will be deemed to be a sale deed. There will be no need of executing a fresh sale deed. The expenses spent in repairs and new constructions will be paid along with the mortgage money at the time of redemption according to account produced by the mortgagee."
  • Dhanrupmal redeemed the prior mortgage, took possession of the shop; the Kacheri went to the mortgagor. On 12 April 1939 Dhanrupmal assigned the mortgage to Motilal, whose sons (respondents) now represent him. The mortgagor's estate is represented by his son, the appellant.
  • On 2 January 1947 (about 47 years after the mortgage; the Capsule says 43) the appellant sued for redemption in the court of the Sub-Judge, Ajmer. The assignee's sons contested: the suit was premature — no right to redeem for 85 years, i.e., till 1 August 1984.
  • Sub-Judge: following a decision of the Judicial Commissioner, held the 85-year postponement invalid as a clog; preliminary decree for redemption. Judicial Commissioner, Ajmer: distinguished that decision, reviewed many cases, held the provision was not a clog; allowed the appeal and dismissed the suit. Appeal to the Supreme Court.

Issues

  1. Whether the suit was premature under s.60 — i.e., whether the principal money had "become due" before expiry of the 85-year term.
  2. Whether the term that the mortgage shall not be redeemable for 85 years was a clog on the equity of redemption.
  3. Whether the term that after 85 years redemption must occur within six months, failing which the deed becomes a sale, was valid — and whether its invalidity affected the 85-year term.

Arguments

Appellant (mortgagor's son)

  • The covenant creating the long term of 85 years, "taken along with the provision that the mortgagor must redeem within a period of six months thereafter or not at all and the other terms of the mortgage and also the circumstances of the case, is really a clog on the equity of redemption and is therefore invalid"; consequently "the mortgage money had been due all along and the suit was not premature."
  • The bargain was hard: while the mortgagor cannot redeem for 85 years, the mortgagee is free to demand his dues at any time.
  • The mortgagee could spend any amount on repairs and new constructions and the mortgagor could redeem only after paying them, according to the mortgagee's own account — an oppressive burden.
  • All the terms were for the mortgagee's benefit; the existence of a prior mortgage shows the mortgagor's impecunious condition, which the mortgagee exploited.

Respondents (assignee-mortgagee's sons)

  • Under s.60 the right to redeem arises only "after the principal money has become due"; by the contract that is after 85 years (Bakhtawar Begum v. Husaini Khanam, PC 1914). The suit filed in 1947 is premature.
  • The 85-year term does not take away the right to redeem; it only fixes when it accrues. Long-term mortgages are common in India.
  • No evidence of oppression: the mortgage benefited the mortgagor by freeing the Kacheri from the prior mortgage; interest (rent) was not high; the mortgagee accepted a smaller security for a larger sum; the suit was filed only because property prices rose after the war.
  • (They did not challenge the Judicial Commissioner's view that the six-months/deemed-sale clause was invalid.)

Decision (Sarkar J.)

  1. Section 60 and accrual of the right (para 4). "Under Section 60 … at any time after the principal money has become due, the mortgagor has a right on payment or tender of the mortgage money to require the mortgagee to reconvey … that right can be exercised only after the mortgage money has become due." Quoting Bakhtawar Begum: "in the absence of a special condition entitling the mortgagor to redeem during the term …, the right of redemption can only arise on the expiration of the specified period." The term is 85 years; no stipulation permits earlier redemption; the term had not expired.
  2. The rule against clogs (paras 6–7). "A mortgage shall always be redeemable and a mortgagor's right to redeem shall neither be taken away nor be limited by any contract between the parties" — Santley v. Wilde. "The courts will ignore any contract, the effect of which is to deprive the mortgagor of his right to redeem." Therefore the term that on failure to redeem within six months "the mortgagor will have no claim over the mortgaged property, and the mortgage deed will be deemed to be a deed of sale … cannot be sustained. It plainly takes away altogether the mortgagor's right to redeem the mortgage after the specified period. This is not permissible, for 'once a mortgage always a mortgage' and therefore always redeemable. The same result also follows from Section 60" — citing Mohammad Sher Khan v. Swami Dayal (PC): the section "is unqualified in its terms, and contains no saving provision as other sections do in favour of contracts to the contrary."
  3. Six-months clause void; severable (paras 8–9). "Once the right to redeem has arisen it cannot be taken away. The mortgagor's right to redeem must be deemed to continue even after the period of six months has expired … The term … must be held to be invalid and ignored." But counsel "did not contend that the invalidity … of the term taking away the right to redeem … after six months makes the term fixing the period of the mortgage at eighty-five years invalid. This latter term stands quite apart. It only fixes the time when the principal sum is to become due, that is, when the right to redeem will accrue and has, therefore, nothing to do with a term which provides when that right will be lost. The invalidity of one does not make the other also invalid."
  4. Is postponement for 85 years a clog? (paras 10–12). The term "does not … take away the mortgagor's right to redeem and is not, therefore, in that sense, a clog … It does, however, prevent accrual of the right to redeem for the period mentioned." When the principal becomes due "must of course depend on the contract between the parties"; the appellant "says that he should be relieved from this bargain." The rule against clogs "no doubt involves that the courts have the power to relieve a party from his bargain. If he has agreed to forfeit wholly his right to redeem …, that agreement will be avoided. But the courts have gone beyond this. They have also relieved mortgagors from bargains whereby the right to redeem has not been taken away but restricted." The extent of that power "is limited by [the] reason" for which equity evolved it.
  5. Origin and limit of the jurisdiction (paras 13–15). Vernon v. Bethell ("necessitous men are not, truly speaking, free men") and Kreglinger (relief against penalties; interference only with "unconscientious" conduct). Hence: "The reason then justifying the court's power to relieve a mortgagor from the effects of his bargain is its want of conscience … the court's jurisdiction … depends on whether it was obtained by taking advantage of any difficulty or embarrassment that he might have been in when he borrowed the moneys on the mortgage. Was the mortgagor oppressed? Was he imposed upon? If he was, then he may be entitled to relief."
  6. Application — a question of fact (paras 16–20). "Essentially one of fact … each turns on its own facts." (a) Length of term: "does the length of the term — and in this case it is long enough being eighty-five years — itself lead to the conclusion that it was an oppressive term? In our view, it does not … It is not necessary for us to go so far as to say that the length of the term … can never by itself show that the bargain was oppressive … we have nothing here to show that the length of the term was in any way disadvantageous to the mortgagor. It is quite conceivable that it was to his advantage." The suit was brought "over forty-seven years after the date of the mortgage. It seems to us impossible that if the term was oppressive, that was not realised much earlier"; the Judicial Commissioner's view that the suit was prompted by the post-war rise in land prices was not wrong; long-term mortgages "not uncommon in various parts of India." (b) Circumstances: the property was under a prior mortgage which also covered the Kacheri; the new mortgage freed the Kacheri; the mortgagee "was satisfied with a smaller security" for a larger sum — "the mortgagee under this mortgage was not putting any pressure on the mortgagor"; no complaint that the interest (measured by rent) was high. "All these … indicate that the mortgagee had not taken any unfair advantage of his position as the lender, nor that the mortgagor was under any financial embarrassment." (c) Mortgagee free to demand at any time? "Plainly fallacious. There is nothing in the mortgage instrument permitting the mortgagee to demand any money, and it is well settled that the mortgagee's right to enforce the mortgage and the mortgagor's right to redeem are coextensive." (d) Repairs clause: the shop and plot were "very small", large expenditure impossible; over 85 years repairs would be necessary; it is not hard on the mortgagor to pay for repairs of which he gets the benefit; "according to the account produced by the mortgagee" means only that the mortgagee "will have to show from his account that he spent these moneys. It is really a safeguard for the mortgagor." (e) All terms for the mortgagee's benefit? No — "the bargain appears to us to have been fair and one as between parties dealing with each other on equal footing." (f) Prior mortgage = impecuniosity? "Every debtor is not necessarily impecunious"; the mortgagor gained by freeing the Kacheri "and he has been in enjoyment of it ever since. That … indicates that the bargain had been freely made."
  7. Conclusion. "The bargain was a reasonable one and the eighty-five years' term of the mortgage should be enforced. We then come to the conclusion that the suit was premature and must fail." Appeal dismissed with costs.

Principles

  1. The right to redeem under s.60 arises only when the principal money becomes due — ordinarily at the end of the agreed term; a suit before that is premature.
  2. "Once a mortgage, always a mortgage": any term that wholly deprives the mortgagor of the right to redeem after it has arisen (forfeiture, deemed sale, short redemption window) is void — s.60 admits no "contract to the contrary".
  3. Courts may also relieve against terms that merely restrict redemption, but only on the ground of want of conscience — oppression/imposition by exploiting the mortgagor's difficulties at the time of the loan. This is a question of fact.
  4. Length of term is not by itself a clog; delay in suing, benefit derived by the mortgagor, reasonable interest and absence of pressure negate oppression.
  5. The mortgagee's right to enforce and the mortgagor's right to redeem are co-extensive.
  6. A void clog is severable from an independent term fixing the period of the mortgage.
  7. Repairs-with-accounts clauses are not oppressive and protect the mortgagor.
Example. In 1995 a shop-owner in Chowk, Prayagraj mortgages his shop with possession for Rs 3 lakh (of which Rs 2.5 lakh discharges an earlier mortgage covering his shop and his house, freeing the house); the term is 60 years; rent in lieu of interest; repairs on account. In 2025 his son sues to redeem. On Ganga Dhar reasoning — benefit to the mortgagor, no distress, ordinary interest, 30 years' silence — the term is likely to be enforced and the suit premature, though after Pomal Kanji the court will also weigh the price spiral and a 60-year lock-in as prima facie indications of a clog.

CASE 2 — Pomal Kanji Govindji v. Vrajlal Karsandas Purohit

AIR 1989 SC 436 : (1989) 1 SCC 458 · Supreme Court of India · Sabyasachi Mukharji J. (for the Bench) · Civil appeals and SLP from the Gujarat High Court (Kutch mortgages) · DU material pp. 135–145

Theme Whether 99-year mortgages of urban property, with interest payable only at redemption and liberty to demolish and rebuild at the mortgagor's cost, executed by a financially distressed mortgagor, are clogs — and whether tenants inducted by the mortgagee can resist the redeeming mortgagor under the Rent Act. Held: clog; redemption allowed before term; tenants must vacate. The doctrine "must be moulded in the modern conditions" of inflation.

Facts (the typical case — SLP (C) No. 8219 of 1982)

  • Karsandas Haridas Purohit (father of the plaintiffs; died 1956) mortgaged the suit property — two delis (compounds) in Kutch containing residential houses and shops — by a registered mortgage deed dated 20 April 1943 (Ex. 103) to Kansara Soni Shivji Jetha and Lalji Jetha for 30,000 koris, executed through their power-of-attorney holder and manager Soni Govindji Narayanji (Defendant 3 is his heir). The mortgagees were abroad (heirs in London and New Delhi).
  • Terms: a usufructuary mortgage for 20,000 koris with delivery of possession; a further 10,000 koris advanced at interest of ½% per month; term 99 years; on expiry the mortgagor to pay 30,000 koris plus the interest on 10,000 koris — the interest was not payable periodically but only at the time of redemption; the mortgagees had liberty to spend any amount on improvement and to rebuild the entire property, to be reimbursed at redemption. The mortgagees inducted tenants (Defendants 4–9).
  • The plaintiffs alleged that at the time of the mortgage their father's economic condition was weak, he was heavily indebted, was residing in the suit property which was his only house, and the mortgagees exploited this to impose "harsh and oppressive conditions … incorporated … to ensure that the mortgagors were prevented forever from redeeming." After a registered notice went unanswered they sued (Regular Civil Suit No. 35 of 1972, Civil Judge, Bhuj) for redemption and actual possession from the tenants.
  • Defences: suit premature (99 years); tenants protected by the Bombay Rent Act; sisters of plaintiffs not joined; on a true computation (interest on 10,000 koris for 29 years = 17,400 koris; total 47,400 koris ≈ Rs 15,800) the Civil Judge lacked pecuniary jurisdiction; court-fee deficient; the mortgagor was not poor — his father was an advocate, he an advocate's clerk, and Plaintiff 1 was himself an advocate at the time (later a Civil Judge and Tribunal Chairman) who "would not have sat idle for 29 years" if the terms were oppressive; a custom in Kutch State of 99-year mortgages with reconstruction liberty; no direct contact/collusion as the mortgagees were abroad; the suit was prompted by the rise in property prices; alternatively interest on 10,000 koris should be awarded.
  • Course: ex parte preliminary decree (2 April 1974) set aside on the heirs' appeal; suit retried with Defendant 2/1's written statement; the trial court framed six issues (clog? jurisdiction? interest? Rent Act protection? decree proper? order?) and decreed the suit (28 September 1978); the Assistant Judge, Kutch (first appeal) affirmed, following Khatubai Nathu Sumra v. Rajgo Mulji Nanji AIR 1979 Guj 171 (99-year mortgage by a hard-pressed mortgagor with demolition/reconstruction terms — unconscionable) and applying Ganga Dhar; the Gujarat High Court dismissed the second appeal (26 April 1982). Connected appeals raised the same questions.

Issues

  1. "Whether long term mortgages in the present inflationary market in fast moving conditions are clogs on equity of redemption and as such the mortgages are redeemable at the mortgagors' instance before the stipulated period" — specifically, whether the terms of the deed of 20 April 1943 amounted to a clog.
  2. Whether tenants inducted by the mortgagees are protected from the redemption decree by the Bombay Rent Act (issue 4).

Arguments

Appellants — mortgagees and tenants (Shri Rajinder Sachar, Shri B.K. Mehta, Shri Dholakia)

  • Custom: in former Kutch there was a custom of 99-year mortgages; "when the period was long, naturally the mortgagee would be required to give full authority to repair and reconstruct … to keep pace with new demands"; the reconstruction clause was "a natural consequence of long term" and no clog.
  • Ganga Dhar: long term is not by itself a clog; relief requires proof that the mortgagee took advantage of the mortgagor's difficulties; here the consideration was reasonable (comparable instances proved), there was no direct contact (mortgagees abroad, transaction through attorneys), no collusion, and the mortgagor's family was legally literate.
  • Delay/motive: Plaintiff 1, an advocate and later a Civil Judge, sat idle 29 years; the suit was filed only because prices rose; equitable relief must be sought within reasonable time (Bhika v. Sheikh Amir AIR 1923 Nag 60).
  • Aziz Khan v. Duni Chand AIR 1918 PC 48: even an "undoubtedly improvident" transaction gives no relief "on grounds of simple hardship" absent evidence that the lender unduly took advantage.
  • Repairs/construction: such clauses keep the property in good condition and are not clogs (Chhedi Lal v. Babu Nandan AIR 1944 All 204 — freedom of contract unless vitiated by undue influence or pressure of poverty).
  • Tenants: protected by the Bombay Rent Act; they cannot be evicted merely because the mortgage is redeemed.

Respondents — mortgagors

  • The combination of terms — 99 years; interest on 10,000 koris accumulating for 99 years and payable only at redemption; liberty to demolish and reconstruct at any cost debited to the mortgagor; possession with the mortgagee — made redemption "for all practical probabilities … an impossibility"; the purpose was "to ensure that the right of redemption could never be exercised" (Kunjbiharilal v. Prag Narayan AIR 1922 Oudh 283; Rajai Singh v. Randhir Singh AIR 1925 All 643; Vadilal Chhaganlal v. Gokaldas AIR 1953 Bom 408; Sarjug Mahto AIR 1963 Pat 114).
  • The mortgagor was heavily indebted and mortgaged his only residential house — "if there was no pressure from the creditor, nobody would like to mortgage the only house which is sole abode on the earth"; what matters is the financial condition at the time of the transaction, not the son's later career.
  • Ganga Dhar itself recognises the court's power to relieve against restrictive terms that are unconscionable; and the economic context has changed — in an inflationary economy a 99-year lock-in at a 1943 price is oppressive per se.
  • Tenants of a mortgagee in possession derive title from the mortgagee and cannot hold over against the redeeming owner.

Decision (Sabyasachi Mukharji J.)

  1. Nature of the question (para 2, 26). "Whether the mortgages operate as clogs on equity of redemption is a mixed question of law and facts"; all cases involved urban immovable property.
  2. Restatement of Ganga Dhar (paras 17–20). The Court set out Sarkar J.'s judgment at length: the rule empowers the court "not only to relieve a mortgagor of a bargain whereby … his right to redeem … is wholly taken away, but also where that right is restricted. The extent of the latter power is, however, limited by the reason that gave rise to it, namely, the unconscionable nature of the bargain"; Santley v. Wilde, Vernon v. Bethell, Kreglinger quoted; length of term "not by itself oppressive." Then the pivot (para 20): "It has to be borne in mind that the English authorities relied upon by Sarkar, J. and the principles propounded … in Seth Ganga Dhar were in the background of a sedate and fixed state of affairs. The spiral and escalation of prices of the immovable properties was not then there. Today, perhaps, a different conspectus would be required to consider the right to redeem the property after considerable length of time pegging the price to a small amount of money, the value of which is fast changing."
  3. The doctrine restated (paras 21–22, 27–29). Mulla: "Any provision inserted to prevent, evade or hamper redemption is void." Jarrah Timber: the mortgagor's right "to get back the subject of the mortgage and to hold and enjoy as he was entitled to … before the mortgage." "The doctrine 'clog on the equity of redemption' is a rule of justice, equity and good conscience. It must be adopted in each case to the reality of the situation and the individuality of the transaction. We must take note of the time, the condition, the price spiral, the term bargain and the other obligations in the background of the financial conditions of the parties." Para 27 lays down the multi-factor test: "Whether or not in a particular transaction there is clog … depends primarily upon the period of redemption, the circumstances under which the mortgage was created, the economic and financial position of the mortgagor, and his relationship vis-à-vis the mortgagee, the economic and social conditions in a particular country at a particular point of time, custom, if any, prevalent in the community …, and the totality of the circumstances …, namely, circumstances of the parties, the time, the situation, the clauses for redemption either for payment of interest or any other sum, the obligations of the mortgagee to construct or repair or maintain the mortgaged property in cases of usufructuary mortgage to manage as a matter of prudent management, these factors must be co-related to each other and viewed in a comprehensive conspectus." Para 28: "It is an axiomatic principle of life and law that necessitous men are not free men … 'Once a mortgage must always remain a mortgage', and must not be transformed into a conveyance or deprivation of the right over the property." Para 29: "Though, long term by itself … is not necessarily a clog … but in the changing circumstances of inflation and phenomenal increase in the prices of real estates, in this age of population explosion and consciousness and need for habitat, long term, very long term, taken with other relevant factors, would create a presumption that it is a clog … the clause obliging the payment of interest even in case of usufructuary mortgage not periodically but at the time of ultimate redemption …, the clauses permitting construction and reconstruction of the building in this inflationary age and debiting the mortgagor with an obligation to pay for the same …, would amount to clog on equity."
  4. Freedom of contract v. social justice (para 24). Dealing with Chhedi Lal: "We must, however, observe that we live in a changed time. Freedom of contract is permissible provided it does not lead to taking advantage of the oppressed or depressed people. The law must transform itself to the social awareness. Poverty should not be unduly permitted to curtail one's right to borrow money on the ground of justice, equity and good conscience on just terms. If it does, it is bad. Whether it does or does not, must, however, depend upon the facts and the circumstances of each case."
  5. Delay and equitable relief (para 25). Bhika v. Sheikh Amir noted (relief must be sought within reasonable time; not granted as a matter of course) — but not applied to defeat the mortgagors.
  6. Statutory character (paras 30–32). "Section 60 … conferred on the mortgagor the right of redemption. This is a statutory right. The right of redemption is an incident of a subsisting mortgage and it subsists so long as the mortgage subsists." "A mortgage cannot be made altogether irredeemable or redemption made illusory. The law must respond and be responsive to the felt and discernible compulsions of circumstances … In the context of fast changing circumstances and economic stability, long term for redemption makes a mortgage an illusory mortgage, though not decisive. It should prima facie be an indication as to how clogs on equity of redemption should be judged."
  7. Application (paras 33–38). "In view of the long period for redemption, the provision for interest @ ½ per cent … payable on the principal amount at the end of the long period, the clause regarding the repairs etc., and the mortgagor's financial condition, all these suggest that there was clog on equity." The Gujarat decisions (Khatubai; Maganlal Chhotalal v. Bhalchandra (1974) 15 Guj LR 193 — "no contract … made at the time of the mortgage and as a part of the mortgage transaction … would be valid if it in substance and effect prevents the mortgagor from getting back his property on payment of what is due"; Soni Motiben v. Hiralal AIR 1981 Guj 120) and Macklin J.'s observation that "if the conditions in the mortgage taken as a whole and added together do create unnecessary difficulties in the way of redemption … that is a greater or less clog" apply "with greater force in the present inflationary market"; Vadilal (Bom), Sarjug Mahto (Pat) approved. On the facts: the son's later elevation to Civil Judge is irrelevant — "what was relevant was the financial condition at the time of the transaction"; the father lived in the suit property, his only house — "if there was no pressure from the creditor, nobody would like to mortgage the only house which is sole abode on the earth"; the requirement to pay the whole interest at the end of 99 years "will practically make the redemption impossible" (Kunjbiharilal; Rajai Singh). The first appellate court was right.
  8. Tenants (paras 39–51, summarised in DU extract/Capsule). A tenant inducted by a mortgagee in possession cannot continue after redemption; the Rent Act does not protect him against the mortgagor unless the lease was binding on the mortgagor as an act of prudent management or the Act expressly so provides.
  9. Result (para 52). "The appeals must fail and are dismissed."

Principles

  1. The doctrine of clog is a rule of justice, equity and good conscience that "has to be moulded in the modern conditions" — the Ganga Dhar principles stand but are applied to an inflationary economy.
  2. Multi-factor "comprehensive conspectus" test: period; circumstances of creation; mortgagor's economic position and relationship with the mortgagee; socio-economic conditions and price spiral; custom; interest clauses; construction/repair obligations; consideration — all "co-related to each other."
  3. Long term, very long term, with other factors, raises a presumption of clog; a long term "prima facie" indicates an illusory mortgage though not decisive.
  4. Specific badges of a clog: interest accumulating and payable only at redemption; liberty to demolish/rebuild at the mortgagor's cost; mortgagor's distress; only residence mortgaged; meagre consideration in an inflationary era.
  5. The relevant time for judging oppression is the date of the mortgage; the mortgagor's later prosperity or delay does not cure a clog, though delay is a factor.
  6. Freedom of contract yields where it exploits "the oppressed or depressed"; "necessitous men are not free men."
  7. A mortgagee's tenants must yield possession on redemption.
Example. In 2005 a widow in Bhuj, indebted after the earthquake, mortgages her only house with possession to a trader for Rs 2 lakh for 99 years; Rs 50,000 of the sum carries 1% monthly interest payable only at redemption; the trader may rebuild at her cost. Her son sues in 2025. Under Pomal Kanji: 99 years in an inflationary era + interest ballooning to the end + rebuild clause + distress + only home → presumption of clog; redemption decreed on payment of the principal (and interest as the court settles); the trader's tenants must vacate.

CASE 3 — Shivdev Singh v. Sucha Singh

(2000) 4 SCC 326 : AIR 2000 SC 1935 · Supreme Court of India · R.P. Sethi J. (for the Bench) · Appeal from the Punjab & Haryana High Court affirming concurrent decrees for redemption · DU material pp. 146–150

Theme A 99-year possessory mortgage of agricultural land for a "meagre" Rs 7,000, taken from a financially hard-pressed mortgagor, is a clog; the purchaser of part of the equity of redemption may redeem the whole before the term. The Court harmonises Ganga Dhar and Pomal Kanji.

Facts

  • Prakash Singh owned 23 kanals 2 marlas in Village Sansra, Tehsil Ajnala, Punjab. By a mortgage deed dated 19 March 1968 he mortgaged it with possession to Smt Basant Kaur for Rs 7,000, for a term of 99 years. On her death the appellants (Shivdev Singh and others) stepped into her shoes as mortgagees in possession, enjoying the usufruct.
  • By a registered sale deed dated 25 March 1987 Prakash Singh sold 19 kanals 2 marlas of the mortgaged land to the respondent Sucha Singh for valid consideration, the mortgage money of Rs 7,000 being kept with the purchaser as amanat (security) to be paid to the mortgagees.
  • Sucha Singh, "realising that partial redemption was not permissible," offered the whole Rs 7,000 to the mortgagees, who refused to deliver possession. He sued for possession by way of redemption in the court of the Additional Senior Sub-Judge, Ajnala, pleading that at the time of the 1968 mortgage Prakash Singh "was financially tight" and the mortgagees, "taking undue advantage of his poor financial condition and helplessness," got the 99-year term inserted, which was a clog; and that they had enjoyed the usufruct for more than 20 years. Prakash Singh (Defendant 3) was proceeded ex parte.
  • The appellants admitted the mortgage and possession since 1968 but pleaded that the suit before expiry of 99 years was premature.
  • Issues framed: (1) Is the land liable to be redeemed? (2) Is the 99-year period a clog? (3) Has the plaintiff locus standi? (4) Relief. Trial court: "The clause … providing for the mortgage of the land for a period of 99 years constitutes a clog on the equity of redemption and as such is illegal and void … The statutory right of redemption cannot be fettered by any condition which impedes or prevents the redemption"; decree for possession on payment of Rs 7,000 (less costs). First appellate court and High Court affirmed — the purchaser had become mortgagor (s.59A) and could redeem before 99 years.

Issue

Whether the clause prescribing a period of 99 years constituted a clog on the equity of redemption, so that the suit filed before expiry was maintainable and not premature under s.60.

Arguments

Appellants (mortgagees)

  • The clause prescribing the period "did not constitute a clog" and "the suit filed before the expiry of the stipulated time was premature in terms of Section 60" — relying on Ganga Dhar v. Shankar Lal: length of term is not by itself oppressive; the mortgagor must prove that the bargain was obtained by taking advantage of his difficulties.
  • Pomal Kanji (relied on by the High Court) was distinguishable — it concerned urban property with additional oppressive clauses (interest payable at the end, reconstruction at mortgagor's cost); here there was only a plain possessory mortgage of agricultural land with a long term.
  • The purchaser of only a part had no better right than his vendor, who had agreed to the term.

Respondent (purchaser-mortgagor)

  • The right of redemption is a statutory right under s.60 which cannot be extinguished or fettered by a stipulation in the mortgage; a 99-year lock-in of 23 kanals for Rs 7,000, exacted from a financially hard-pressed farmer, renders the mortgage illusory — a clog under Pomal Kanji.
  • Concurrent findings of fact by three courts on the mortgagor's distress and the mortgagees' advantageous position should not be disturbed under Art. 136.
  • As transferee of the equity of redemption (s.59A) he is "mortgagor"; he tendered the whole mortgage-money, respecting the indivisibility rule (s.60, last para).

Decision (R.P. Sethi J.)

  1. Section 60 explained (para 8). After setting out the section: "Such a right of the mortgagor is called, in English law, the equity of redemption. The mortgagor being an owner who has parted with some rights of ownership has a right to get back the mortgage deed or mortgaged property, in exercise of his right of ownership. The right of redemption recognised under the Transfer of Property Act is thus a statutory and legal right which cannot be extinguished by any agreement made at the time of mortgage as part of the mortgage transaction."
  2. Modes of extinguishment (para 9). Quoting Jayasingh Dnyanu Mhoprekar v. Krishna Babaji Patil AIR 1985 SC 1646: the right "can come to an end only in a manner known to law … by a contract between the parties, by a merger or by a statutory provision which debars the mortgagor from redeeming. A mortgagee who has entered into possession … will have to give up possession … when the suit for redemption is filed unless he is able to show that the right of redemption has come to an end or that the suit is liable to be dismissed on some other valid ground. This flows from the legal principle … 'Once a mortgage, always a mortgage'."
  3. Ganga Dhar restated (para 10). "Any provision incorporated in the mortgage deed to prevent or hamper the redemption would thus be void. A mortgage cannot be made irredeemable and the right of redemption not illusory." The Court quoted Ganga Dhar extensively — Santley v. Wilde; the six-months clause; Mohammad Sher Khan; Vernon v. Bethell; Kreglinger; the "want of conscience" test ("Was the mortgagor oppressed? Was he imposed upon?"); and noted that Sarkar J. had restricted his finding to the facts: "It is not necessary for us to go so far as to say that the length of the term of the mortgage can never by itself show that the bargain was oppressive."
  4. Pomal Kanji restated (paras 11–12). "Freedom of contract is permissible provided it does not lead to taking advantage of the oppressed or depressed people …"; the doctrine is "a rule of justice, equity and good conscience … adopted to the reality of situation and the individuality of transaction"; paras 26–27 (mixed question; multi-factor test) and 31 ("long term for redemption makes a mortgage an illusory mortgage, though not decisive. It should prima facie be an indication") quoted; "the doctrine of clog on the equity of redemption has to be moulded in modern conditions."
  5. Application (para 13). "In the present case all the courts below on facts held that the mortgage deed being for a period of 99 years was a clog on the equity of redemption. Such findings were returned keeping in view the facts and circumstances of the case and the financial position under which the mortgagor Shri Prakash Singh was placed at the time of execution of the mortgage deed on 19-3-1968. The appellants were found to be in an advantageous position qua the mortgagor. They were also found to be deriving the usufructs of the mortgaged land for a period of over 26 years at the time of filing of the suit on payment of a meagre sum of Rs 7000 only to the mortgagor. The findings of facts returned by the courts below do not require any interference particularly when the learned counsel appearing for the appellants has not contended that such findings were perverse or uncalled for or against the evidence."
  6. Appeal dismissed; decree for redemption of the entire land on payment of Rs 7,000 affirmed.

Principles

  1. The right to redeem is a statutory and legal right incidental to ownership; it can be extinguished only by (a) a subsequent contract, (b) merger, or (c) statute/decree — never by a stipulation in the mortgage itself.
  2. Ganga Dhar and Pomal Kanji are complementary: the test remains oppression/want of conscience judged on the totality of circumstances, but a very long term for a meagre sum in modern conditions is prima facie an indication of an illusory mortgage and a clog — even without additional oppressive clauses.
  3. The relevant financial condition is that at the time of the mortgage; the mortgagee's long enjoyment of usufruct on a small advance underscores the inequity.
  4. A purchaser of part of the equity of redemption is a "mortgagor" (s.59A) entitled to redeem, but must redeem the whole mortgage (indivisibility — s.60 last para).
  5. Concurrent findings on a clog, being findings of fact/mixed questions, will not be disturbed under Art. 136 unless perverse.
Example. In 2000 a small farmer in Kaushambi mortgages 3 bighas with possession for Rs 40,000 for 99 years to a moneylender. In 2024 he sells 2 bighas to P, leaving the mortgage money with P. P tenders Rs 40,000 for the whole and sues. Following Shivdev Singh: P is a mortgagor under s.59A; partial redemption is barred, so he correctly tenders the whole; the 99-year term for a meagre sum from a hard-pressed farmer is a clog; decree for possession of the whole 3 bighas on payment of Rs 40,000 (P then has a charge/contribution claim against the farmer for the 1 bigha). Note also UPZALR Act s.153 — a usufructuary mortgage of bhumidhari land is itself prohibited in UP, which would be an independent ground.

Real-world touchpoints. (1) Home loans — equitable mortgages by deposit of title deeds are the norm; the sanction letter's "prepayment penalty" clause is not a clog (it is a term about interest, and RBI has barred prepayment penalties on floating-rate loans to individuals). (2) Gold-loan v. property loan — gold is pledged (Contract Act), property is mortgaged (TPA). (3) Old family mortgages — many pre-1970 bhog-bandhak (usufructuary) mortgages in UP villages remain unredeemed; heirs can still redeem within 30 years of accrual; UPZALR s.153 now forbids new usufructuary mortgages of bhumidhari land. (4) Builder–lender — a project mortgage plus a "buy-back at fixed price on default" clause in the same loan document would be a clog; lenders instead take a separate pledge of shares/escrow. (5) Maintenance — a charge for maintenance under HAMA s.27/decree is a s.100 charge, not a mortgage.

14. DU semester PYQs with model answers

Q1 (DU LL.B. May/June 2025, Q.6(c) — short note). Clog on equity of redemption.
Model answer.

Meaning. A mortgage is a transfer of an interest in specific immovable property as security for a debt (s.58(a)). The mortgagor's right, on payment of the mortgage-money after it becomes due, to get back his documents, possession and a re-transfer is the right of redemption (s.60) — the Indian statutory counterpart of the English "equity of redemption", which Chancery evolved to relieve borrowers from the forfeiture of land on the "law day" (Kreglinger). Any stipulation in the mortgage which prevents, evades or hampers this right is a clog (fetter) on the equity of redemption and is void: "once a mortgage, always a mortgage" (Lindley M.R., Santley v. Wilde, adopted in Ganga Dhar v. Shankar Lal AIR 1958 SC 770).

Statutory basis. Section 60 is "unqualified in its terms, and contains no saving provision … in favour of contracts to the contrary" (Mohd. Sher Khan v. Swami Dayal, PC); the right may be extinguished only "by act of the parties or by decree of a Court" (proviso) — i.e., a subsequent independent contract, merger, or a final decree of foreclosure/sale (Jayasingh Dnyanu Mhoprekar, followed in Shivdev Singh). The doctrine applies only to terms forming part of the mortgage transaction, not to later variations.

Rationale. "Necessitous men are not, truly speaking, free men" (Vernon v. Bethell); the court relieves where the bargain shows "want of conscience" — "Was the mortgagor oppressed? Was he imposed upon?" (Ganga Dhar). It is "a rule of justice, equity and good conscience" (Pomal Kanji).

Illustrations of clogs. (i) Forfeiture/deemed sale on default — the "redeem within six months after 85 years or the deed becomes a sale deed" clause struck down in Ganga Dhar; (ii) redemption made impossible or illusory — 99-year mortgages with interest payable only at the end and liberty to rebuild at the mortgagor's cost (Pomal Kanji Govindji v. Vrajlal Karsandas Purohit AIR 1989 SC 436), or a 99-year possessory mortgage of 23 kanals for a meagre Rs 7,000 from a hard-pressed farmer (Shivdev Singh v. Sucha Singh AIR 2000 SC 1935); (iii) penal interest from the date of the mortgage; (iv) redemption conditional on paying other debts; (v) option to purchase granted in the mortgage (Samuel v. Jarrah Timber); (vi) collateral advantages surviving redemption (Noakes v. Rice). Not clogs: a fair long term freely agreed (Ganga Dhar — 85 years upheld; suit premature), reasonable notice before tender (s.60), necessary repairs on account, reasonable collateral advantages ending on redemption (Kreglinger), a later sale of the equity of redemption.

Long-term mortgages — the test. Length of term is not by itself a clog (Ganga Dhar); whether it is depends on the totality of circumstances — period, circumstances of creation, financial position of the mortgagor and his relationship with the mortgagee, socio-economic conditions and price spiral, custom, interest and reconstruction clauses (Pomal Kanji, para 27). In modern inflationary conditions "long term, very long term, taken with other relevant factors, would create a presumption that it is a clog"; it makes the mortgage "illusory, though not decisive" and is "prima facie an indication" (Pomal Kanji; Shivdev Singh). "Freedom of contract is permissible provided it does not lead to taking advantage of the oppressed or depressed people."

Effect. The offending term is void and ignored; the rest of the deed stands (severability — Ganga Dhar); the mortgagor may redeem at once (if the term itself is the clog) or after the term (if only the forfeiture clause is struck); the mortgagee's tenants must yield possession on redemption.

Q2. "Once a mortgage, always a mortgage." Explain the doctrine with reference to Ganga Dhar v. Shankar Lal and Pomal Kanji Govindji v. Vrajlal Karsandas Purohit. Has the Supreme Court changed its approach to long-term mortgages?
Outline. (1) Meaning and origin of the maxim (Parts 7.1–7.2). (2) s.60 — statutory right; no contract to the contrary; proviso. (3) Ganga Dhar in detail: facts (85 years + 6 months + deemed sale), two-tier analysis (total deprivation void per se; restriction relieved only for want of conscience), fact-based test, factors negating oppression (benefit of freeing the Kacheri; 47-year delay; smaller security; reasonable interest; repairs on account as a safeguard), co-extensive rights, severability; result — term upheld, suit premature. (4) Pomal Kanji in detail: 99 years + interest at end + rebuild + distress + only house; Ganga Dhar decided "in the background of a sedate and fixed state of affairs"; "different conspectus" in inflationary times; multi-factor test; presumption from very long term; "freedom of contract … oppressed or depressed people"; result — clog; tenants out. (5) Shivdev Singh as synthesis: applies both; 99 years for Rs 7,000 = clog. (6) Assessment: not a change of principle but of evidential emphasis — the burden has practically shifted to the mortgagee in very-long-term mortgages; the "want of conscience" test survives as the touchstone; length alone still not decisive. Conclude with policy: protection of necessitous borrowers v. sanctity of contract; parallels in Usurious Loans Act and s.16 Contract Act.
Q3 (problem). In 1990 A, in financial distress, mortgaged his only house to B with possession for Rs 50,000 for a term of 99 years. The deed provided that (i) A would pay interest at 1% per month on Rs 20,000 of the sum, the whole interest being payable at the time of redemption; (ii) B could demolish and rebuild the house at any cost, to be reimbursed by A on redemption; (iii) if A failed to redeem within one year after the term, the deed would operate as a sale. In 2024 A's son sues for redemption. B pleads the suit is premature. Decide.
Answer. Clause (iii) is a forfeiture converting security into purchase — void per se (Ganga Dhar; Vernon v. Bethell; s.60 proviso permits extinguishment only by subsequent act or decree). Clauses (i)–(ii) with the 99-year term replicate Pomal Kanji: interest on a part of the principal accumulating for 99 years (Rs 20,000 × 12% × 99 ≈ Rs 23.76 lakh simple interest) makes redemption "practically impossible"; unlimited reconstruction debited to the mortgagor in an inflationary era; distress; only residence — presumption of a clog; the term itself is struck, so the principal is due now and the suit is not premature. B's Ganga Dhar plea fails because, unlike that case, there is evidence of oppression and no benefit to the mortgagor; delay of 34 years does not cure a clog (the financial condition at 1990 is what matters — Pomal Kanji, para 36; Shivdev Singh, 26 years). Decree: preliminary decree for redemption (O.XXXIV r.7) on payment of Rs 50,000 with such interest as the court finds fair (the court may relieve against the accumulated interest as penal/unconscionable — Usurious Loans Act), and the cost of only necessary repairs (s.63A, s.72); B's tenants must deliver possession.
Q4. Define mortgage. Explain the essentials and the different kinds of mortgages recognised by the Transfer of Property Act, with the remedies available to each kind of mortgagee.
Outline. s.58(a) definition; four essentials (parties; transfer of an interest; specific immovable property; purpose of security for loan/debt/pecuniary engagement) with explanation; six kinds (ss.58(b)–(g)) — text, elements, illustration, remedy (use Part 3 and the comparative table): simple (no possession, personal covenant, sale through court); conditional sale (ostensible sale; three conditions; 1929 proviso — same document; foreclosure only; sale v. mortgage tests — Chunchun Jha); usufructuary (possession; usufruct in lieu of interest/principal; no personal liability; no sale/foreclosure; s.62); English (absolute transfer with proviso; certain date; personal covenant; sale; s.69); deposit of title deeds (notified towns; deposit + intent; no writing; s.96 — sale); anomalous (combinations; s.98; Pomal Kanji). Mode of execution — s.59. Conclude with s.67's scheme of remedies and the co-extensive nature of the rights (Ganga Dhar).
Q5. Distinguish between a mortgage and a charge. Can a charge be enforced against a bona fide purchaser for value without notice?
Outline. s.58(a) v. s.100 (text); the Part 10.1 table — transfer of interest v. none; act of parties only v. also operation of law; formalities; personal liability; remedies (sale only for charge; provisions of simple mortgage apply); kinds; redemption/clog; limitation. Illustrations: vendor's charge s.55(4)(b); maintenance charge (Rajesh Kanta Roy compromise decree); partition-money charge. Answer the second part: No — the last limb of s.100 (1929) provides that "no charge shall be enforced against any property in the hands of a person to whom such property has been transferred for consideration and without notice of the charge", save where a law expressly provides otherwise (e.g., statutory charges for land revenue/tax which bind all); contrast a mortgage which, being an interest in the property, binds transferees regardless of notice (registration itself being notice — s.3 Expl. I).
Q6 (problem). P mortgaged his land to Q for 99 years for Rs 7,000 with possession. Nineteen years later P sold three-fourths of the land to R, leaving the mortgage money with R. R tendered the whole Rs 7,000 and sued for redemption of the entire land. Q contends (a) R, a purchaser of part, cannot redeem the whole; (b) the suit is premature. Decide with reference to case law.
Answer. (a) R is a "mortgagor" under s.59A (person deriving title from the mortgagor) and s.91; the rule of indivisibility (s.60 last para) forbids partial redemption but not redemption of the whole by a part-owner — indeed it compels him to redeem the whole, which R did (Shivdev Singh v. Sucha Singh: purchaser of 19 of 23 kanals "offered the whole of the mortgage money … realising that partial redemption was not permissible"; decree upheld). R will have a right of contribution/charge against P for the share attributable to the unsold quarter (s.82; s.92 subrogation). (b) Prematurity depends on whether the 99-year term is a clog. On Shivdev Singh (identical facts) and Pomal Kanji, a 99-year possessory mortgage for a meagre Rs 7,000 from a financially tight mortgagor, with the mortgagee enjoying usufruct for two decades, is an illusory mortgage — clog; the term is void and the money is due; suit maintainable. Q's reliance on Ganga Dhar fails for want of the equalising features present there (fair bargain, benefit to mortgagor). Decree for redemption of the whole on payment of Rs 7,000.

15. Mnemonics & rapid revision

"P-I-S-S" — essentials of a mortgage: Parties · transfer of an Interest · Specific immovable property · for Security (loan / debt / pecuniary engagement).
"S-C-U-E-D-A" — six kinds: Simple · Conditional sale · Usufructuary · English · Deposit of title deeds · Anomalous.
Remedies: "Simple SELLS, Conditional FORECLOSES, Usufructuary HOLDS, English SELLS (even privately), Deposit SELLS, Anomalous AS AGREED."
"Rs 100 — Register, Sign, 2 witnesses" — s.59 (except deposit of title deeds; under Rs 100 delivery suffices except simple).
"Due → Tender → D-P-R" — s.60: after money is due, on tender, get Documents, Possession, Re-transfer/acknowledgement; ends only by Act of parties or Decree.
"Once a mortgage, always a mortgage" — G-P-S: Ganga Dhar (85 yrs OK, forfeiture void — want of conscience) · Pomal Kanji (99 yrs + interest at end + rebuild = clog; inflation presumption; multi-factor test) · Shivdev Singh (99 yrs for Rs 7,000 = clog; statutory right; part-purchaser redeems whole).
Charge = "No Transfer, No Notice-proof, No Foreclosure" — s.100.
Twelve lines to remember
  1. Mortgage = transfer of an interest in specific immovable property as security (s.58(a)); mortgagor keeps ownership + equity of redemption.
  2. Six kinds, s.58(b)–(g); remedies fixed by kind (s.67 proviso (a)); anomalous = combination/custom (Pomal Kanji).
  3. s.58(c) proviso: condition of re-transfer must be in the same document; substance test for sale v. mortgage.
  4. s.59: ≥ Rs 100 → registered instrument, signed, attested by 2 witnesses (s.3 "attested"; s.68 Evidence Act); < Rs 100 → delivery (not simple); deposit of title deeds needs no writing.
  5. s.60: right to redeem arises when the principal is due (ordinarily end of term — Bakhtawar Begum; Ganga Dhar); continues till extinguished by act of parties or decree; 30 years (Art. 61(a)).
  6. No partial redemption (indivisibility) unless mortgagee acquired part of the equity — Shivdev Singh.
  7. Mortgagee's right to enforce and mortgagor's right to redeem are co-extensive (Ganga Dhar); foreclosure/sale by court decree under O.XXXIV; SARFAESI for banks.
  8. Clog = any term made at the time of the mortgage that prevents, evades or hampers redemption — void; s.60 has no "contract to the contrary".
  9. Total deprivation (forfeiture, deemed sale, redeem-within-window) void per se; mere restriction void only if unconscionable — "Was the mortgagor oppressed? Was he imposed upon?"
  10. Length of term alone ≠ clog (Ganga Dhar), but very long term + other factors in an inflationary economy → presumption of clog (Pomal Kanji); "prima facie an indication" (Shivdev Singh).
  11. Factors: period, circumstances, mortgagor's finances and relationship, socio-economic conditions/price spiral, custom, interest clauses, reconstruction/repairs, consideration v. value; judged as at the date of the mortgage.
  12. Charge (s.100): security without transfer of interest; by act of parties or law; simple-mortgage provisions apply (sale); not enforceable against bona fide transferee for value without notice.
