1. The idea of "vesting"

Every interest created by a transfer must be classified by when the transferee's title becomes complete. An interest may be vested in possession (present right to present enjoyment — e.g., the life tenant in occupation), vested in interest (present right to future enjoyment — e.g., the remainderman after a life estate; the title is complete, only possession is deferred), or contingent (no present right at all; the title itself depends on an uncertain event which may never happen). Chapter II of the TPA (ss.19–24) codifies the English learning on this subject; the Indian Succession Act (ss.119–120) does the same for wills, and the Supreme Court in Rajesh Kanta Roy treats all four provisions as a single body of principle.

Why it mattersConsequence
AlienabilityA vested interest is property (s.6) and transferable at once even before possession; a contingent interest is also transferable (it is not a mere spes — s.6(a)), but what passes is only the chance that the contingency will occur.
Heritability"A vested interest is not defeated by the death of the transferee before he obtains possession" (s.19) — it passes to his heirs. A contingent interest generally fails if the transferee dies before the event (unless the contingency is one unconnected with his survival).
Attachability in executionA vested interest is "property" liable to attachment and sale under s.60 CPC; "a mere contingent interest though transferable inter vivos is not attachable" — Pestonjee Bhicajee v. P.H. Anderson AIR 1939 PC 6, applied in Rajesh Kanta Roy. This was the whole battle in the DU case: Santi Debi could execute her decree against Rajes's interest under the trust only if it was vested.
Perpetuity (s.14)The rule against perpetuity is concerned with remoteness of vesting; an interest vested at once (with enjoyment postponed) can never offend it.
Insolvency / taxation / stampA vested remainder passes to the Official Assignee/Resolution Professional; is includible in the estate for wealth/estate duty purposes; a transfer of it attracts stamp duty as a conveyance.
Acceleration and lapseA vested remainder is accelerated if the prior interest fails; a contingent interest cannot be accelerated before the event; in wills a contingent legacy lapses if the legatee dies before the event.

2. Section 19 — vested interest

s.19. Vested interest.—Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created in favour of a person without specifying the time when it is to take effect, or in terms specifying that it is to take effect forthwith or on the happening of an event which must happen, such interest is vested, unless a contrary intention appears from the terms of the transfer.
A vested interest is not defeated by the death of the transferee before he obtains possession.
Explanation.—An intention that an interest shall not be vested is not to be inferred merely from a provision whereby the enjoyment thereof is postponed, or whereby a prior interest in the same property is given or reserved to some other person, or whereby income arising from the property is directed to be accumulated until the time of enjoyment arrives, or from a provision that if a particular event shall happen the interest shall pass to another person.
#ElementAnalysis
1"on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created in favour of a person"Inter vivos transfer (s.5); the person must be living or, if unborn, the interest is created "for his benefit" and vests on birth (s.20). For wills — s.119 ISA.
2Three modes of vesting: (a) "without specifying the time when it is to take effect"; (b) "in terms specifying that it is to take effect forthwith"; (c) "on the happening of an event which must happen"(a) Silence → immediate vesting (e.g., "I transfer my house to B"). (b) Express immediacy ("to B from today"). (c) A certain event — death of a named person (Capsule ill. (a): Rs 100 to B "to be paid on the death of C" — vested, C's death is certain); expiry of a fixed term; determination of a prior life estate (Capsule ill. (b)). The event must be one that must happen, though its time may be uncertain.
3"such interest is vested"The transferee acquires a present proprietary right — ownership — even if possession/enjoyment is postponed. The Capsule's adoption example: a Hindu widow adopts a son under an agreement postponing his estate during her lifetime — the adopted son's interest is vested (takes effect on the widow's death, a certain event); "he has a present proprietary right in the estate, the right of possession and enjoyment being deferred; and therefore, he can transfer the property even during the widow's lifetime."
4"unless a contrary intention appears from the terms of the transfer"The section lays down a presumption, rebuttable by the instrument read as a whole. But courts approach construction "with a bias in favour of a vested interest unless the intention to the contrary is definite and clear" (Rajesh Kanta Roy, para 9, conceded by the Solicitor-General). The Explanation lists four provisions from which a contrary intention is not to be inferred.
5"not defeated by the death of the transferee before he obtains possession"Heritability: the interest passes to the transferee's legal representatives (Capsule: "If the transferee dies, his interest vests in his legal representatives whether or not he has obtained possession"). Contrast the contingent interest (Part 4).

3. The Explanation to s.19 — four things that do not prevent vesting

(i) Postponement of enjoyment
"To B, to be given possession when he attains 25" — B's interest is vested at once; only enjoyment is postponed. (Distinguish "to B if he attains 25" — a condition precedent → contingent, s.21.) English rule: where the gift is "in terms immediate, and the payment alone postponed, the legacy is vested" (Williams on Executors, quoted in Rajesh Kanta Roy).
(ii) A prior interest given or reserved to another
"To A for life, then to B" — B's remainder is vested from the date of transfer (A's death is certain); B may sell it during A's life; if B predeceases A, B's heirs take on A's death (Capsule ill. (b)).
(iii) Direction to accumulate income until the time of enjoyment
"To B, the rents to be accumulated and paid to him with the corpus at 21" — vested; the accumulation direction (subject to s.17) does not make the gift contingent.
(iv) A provision that on a particular event the interest shall pass to another
"To B, but if B dies before A, then to C" — B's interest is vested subject to divestment (a condition subsequent, ss.28–31), not contingent. B has ownership now; C's interest is the contingent one.
The principle behind the Explanation. Each of the four provisions concerns the manner or time of enjoyment, or a later divesting event — none of them makes the acquisition of the title itself depend on an uncertain event. Only a condition precedent that may never be fulfilled produces a contingent interest. Rajesh Kanta Roy (para 11): "where the enjoyment of the property is postponed but the present income thereof is to be applied for the benefit of the donee the gift is vested and not contingent."

4. Section 21 — contingent interest

s.21. Contingent interest.—Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created in favour of a person to take effect only on the happening of a specified uncertain event, or if a specified uncertain event shall not happen, such person thereby acquires a contingent interest in the property. Such interest becomes a vested interest, in the former case, on the happening of the event, in the latter, when the happening of the event becomes impossible.
Exception.—Where, under a transfer of property, a person becomes entitled to an interest therein upon attaining a particular age, and the transferor also gives to him absolutely the income to arise from such interest before he reaches that age, or directs the income or so much thereof as may be necessary to be applied for his benefit, such interest is not contingent.
#ElementAnalysis
1"to take effect only on the happening of a specified uncertain event" (positive contingency)"To B if he marries C"; "to B if he attains 21"; "to B if A dies without leaving a son". The event may or may not happen. Capsule example: estate to A until he marries, then to B — B's interest is contingent on A's marriage.
2"or if a specified uncertain event shall not happen" (negative contingency)"To B if C does not return from abroad within 5 years"; "to B if A has no son".
3"such person thereby acquires a contingent interest""Neither any proprietary interest nor a right of enjoyment is given at present, but both depend upon future uncertain events" (Capsule). It is nevertheless an interest — "something more than a simple chance or possibility"; it rests on "the present existence of an incomplete title" — hence transferable (s.6) though not attachable.
4Conversion into vested interestPositive contingency → vests on the happening of the event (A marries → B's interest vests). Negative contingency → vests when the event becomes impossible (C dies abroad → B's interest vests). Until then the transferor retains the reversion.
5FailureIf the event becomes impossible (positive) or happens (negative), or the transferee dies before the event where his survival is implicit in the condition, the interest fails and the property "reverts to the transferor" (Capsule) — or goes over under an ulterior disposition (ss.27–28).

5. The Exception to s.21

Where an interest is given "upon attaining a particular age" (which, standing alone, is a condition precedent → contingent), but the transferor also (a) gives the transferee absolutely the income arising from the interest before he reaches that age, or (b) directs the income "or so much thereof as may be necessary" to be applied for his benefit (maintenance, education), the interest is not contingent but vested. Rationale: a settlor who gives the whole present benefit of the property to the donee is treated as intending immediate ownership, the age being a mere postponement of full enjoyment. This is the statutory form of the English rule in Hanson v. Graham (1801) 6 Ves 239 and Fox v. Fox (1875) LR 19 Eq 286 (where the intermediate income is given, the gift "at 21" is vested), and of the second limb of s.120 ISA. Rajesh Kanta Roy extends the same reasoning beyond the age case: application of the income for the donee's benefit — including, for sons legally bound to pay their father's debts, application of income towards those debts — indicates vesting.

Examples. (1) "To my nephew N on attaining 21" — contingent; if N dies at 19 it fails. (2) "To N on attaining 21, the rents meanwhile to be paid to him" — vested (Exception); if N dies at 19 his heirs take. (3) "To N on attaining 21, the trustees to apply so much of the income as necessary for his education" — vested. (4) "To N on attaining 21, the income meanwhile to be paid to his mother" — contingent (income not given to N or applied for his benefit).

Example"To B if he becomes an advocate""To B, but if he ceases to practise as an advocate, to C"
Certain v. uncertain event. The line between ss.19 and 21 is whether the event "must happen" (death of a person; end of a term; coming of a date) or "may or may not happen" (marriage; attaining an age; a person dying without issue; payment of debts — arguably). Only the occurrence need be certain, not its time. In Rajesh Kanta Roy the settlor's death was conceded to be certain; the dispute was whether "liquidation of all the debts" was an uncertain event on which the sons' title depended — the Court held it was, in substance, a mere charge/postponement of enjoyment, not a condition precedent.
Presumption in favour of vesting. "There is a strong presumption that on a transfer of property the transferor intends to confer a vested interest" (Capsule); the court "has to approach the task of construction … with a bias in favour of a vested interest unless the intention to the contrary is definite and clear" (Rajesh Kanta Roy). Reasons: the law abhors intestacy/reversion and favours early vesting, certainty of title, alienability, and protection of the donee's family.

8. Incidents — transferability, heritability, attachability, insolvency, limitation

  • Transferability (s.6). Both vested and contingent interests are "property" transferable under s.6 (the Capsule: "when an interest is vested, it becomes the property of the transferee and is under Section 6 transferable by him even before he has obtained possession"; a contingent interest "is transferable"). A transfer of a contingent interest passes whatever the transferor gets when the contingency is resolved.
  • Heritability. Vested — always. Contingent — depends on whether the condition is personal to the transferee (survival to an age/event) or external (return of X from abroad; A dying without issue).
  • Attachability. CPC s.60(1) makes liable to attachment "all … saleable property … belonging to the judgment-debtor, or over which … he has a disposing power which he may exercise for his own benefit"; O.21 r.46 (attachment of debts/shares/other movable property not in possession) and r.54 (immovable property). The Privy Council in Pestonjee Bhicajee v. P.H. Anderson (AIR 1939 PC 6) held a mere contingent interest not attachable, though transferable inter vivos — the rule accepted as "well-settled" in Rajesh Kanta Roy (para 7). A vested remainder/reversion is attachable and saleable (subject to the prior interest).
  • Insolvency. A vested interest (including a vested remainder) vests in the Official Assignee/Receiver (Presidency-towns/Provincial Insolvency Acts; IBC 2016 for individuals when notified); a contingent interest is generally excluded until it vests, though after-acquired property provisions may catch it.
  • Limitation. For a remainderman/reversioner, time to sue for possession runs from when his interest falls into possession (Limitation Act, 1963, Art. 65 read with s.6–8; Art. 110 for a Hindu excluded from joint family property); a vested interest cannot be lost by the life-tenant's adverse possession.
  • Trust deeds. Under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882 (ss.55–58) a beneficiary with a vested interest may transfer it (s.58) and may, if sui juris and solely entitled, require the trustees to transfer the property to him (s.56 — the rule in Saunders v. Vautier); a contingent beneficiary cannot.

10. Rules of construction

Form of wordsPrima facie result
"To B" / "to B forthwith" / "to B on C's death" (C named, certain to die)Vested (s.19)
"To B, payable/to be delivered at 21" (gift immediate, payment postponed)Vested — enjoyment postponed (Expl. (i); Williams: "where the bequest is in terms immediate, and the payment alone postponed, the legacy is vested")
"To B if/when/upon he attains 21" (age part of the description of the gift)Contingent (s.21) — unless intermediate income is given/applied (Exception; s.120 ISA)
"To B after payment of my debts" / "when my debts are paid"Vested — the words of apparent postponement create only a charge (Jarman, 8th edn, vol. II p. 1373, quoted in Rajesh Kanta Roy; ISA s.119 ill. (v); Ranganatha Mudaliar v. Mohana Krishna Mudaliar AIR 1926 Mad 645) — but a clearly expressed intention that benefits "should not vest till his debts were paid" will be given effect (Jarman p. 1390; Bernard v. Mountague (1816) 1 Mer 422)
"To A for life, then to B"B vested remainder (Expl. (ii))
"To A for life, then to B if B survives A"B contingent (survival uncertain)
"To B, but if B dies before A, to C"B vested subject to divestment; C contingent (Expl. (iv))
"To B, the income to be accumulated till he is 25"Vested (Expl. (iii)), accumulation subject to s.17
Provision that if the donee dies before the event his heirs takeStrong indication of vesting — because a hallmark of a contingent interest is that "the heirs of such a person do not get the benefit of the gift" (Rajesh Kanta Roy, para 12)
Donee's own conduct — creating a charge on the interest, treating it as presently executableConfirmatory of vesting (Rajesh Kanta Roy, para 13)
Overarching rule. "Apart from any seemingly technical rules which may be gathered from English decisions and text-books … there can be no doubt that the question is really one of intention to be gathered from a comprehensive view of all the terms of a document" (Rajesh Kanta Roy, para 9). Read the whole scheme; identify the settlor's purposes; ask whether the uncertain event was intended as a condition of title or as a limit on enjoyment / a charge.

Leading Case (DU Case Material, Topic 8, pp. 112–118)

CASE — Rajesh (Rajes) Kanta Roy v. Shanti (Santi) Debi & Anr.

AIR 1957 SC 255 : 1957 SCR 77 · Supreme Court of India · Jagannadhadas J. (for the Bench) · Appeal from the Calcutta High Court, which had affirmed the Subordinate Judge's order overruling objections under s.47 CPC to execution · DU material pp. 112–118

Theme Whether the interest of a son under his father's trust deed — property to "devolve on" him "absolutely" only after (i) liquidation of all the settlor's debts and (ii) the settlor's death, with restricted monthly payments meanwhile — is vested (and therefore attachable in execution of a maintenance decree) or contingent (and therefore not attachable). Held: vested in title, restricted in enjoyment.

Facts

  • Ramani Kanta Roy, a man of "considerable properties" (Calcutta; Dayabhaga Hindu), had three sons: Rajes, Rabindra and Ramendra. Rabindra died childless in 1938 leaving a widow, Santi Debi.
  • In 1934 Ramani had endowed some properties to the family deity, appointing the three sons shebaits. In 1941 Santi Debi sued for a declaration that, as her husband's heir, she could act as shebait in his place; the suit ended in a compromise recognising her as co-shebait.
  • In 1944 Ramani, Rajes and Ramendra sued Santi Debi to declare that compromise decree void — one ground being that her marriage to Rabindra was a nullity as within prohibited degrees (a sagotra marriage, against which Ramani "had developed prejudice").
  • During that suit, on 26 July 1945, Ramani executed a registered trust deed of his entire properties, appointing Rajes sole trustee. Key clauses:
    • Cl. 3 — trustee "shall pay the present existing just debts of the settlor" (Schedule B debts: Rs 2,62,169-8-0; properties valued at Rs 5 lakh for stamp, cl. 17, "substantially higher" in reality).
    • Cl. 5 — during the settlor's life and until all debts are paid, trustee to pay monthly Rs 1,000 to the settlor, Rs 300 to Rajes, Rs 200 to Ramendra. Cl. 6 — after the settlor's death and before liquidation, Rs 800 to Rajes and Rs 700 to Ramendra per month. (Only Rs 1,500 p.m. set aside for the family; by implication the rest of the income goes to debts.)
    • Cl. 8–9 — payments out of income if Rajes or Ramendra dies before liquidation: Rajes's monthly sum to go to his widow, then his legal heirs. Cl. 10 — family residence while debts unpaid. Cl. 11 — trustee's power to sell, mortgage or grant long leases to pay debts.
    • Cl. 12 — "On the liquidation of all the debts of the settlor (including the debt, if any, that may be incurred by the trustee for payment of the settlor's debts) and after his death this trust shall come to an end and the properties … shall devolve as follows: (a) Lots I–IV … including the surplus income thereof shall devolve on the said Rajes Kanta Roy absolutely or if he be then dead then … on his heirs then living absolutely but subject to cl. (c) regarding Premises No. 44/2, Lansdowne Road; (b) Lot V … shall be enjoyed by Ramendra during his lifetime or if he be then dead … on his son or sons absolutely, failing whom on his grandson(s) absolutely."
    • Cl. 14 — if Rajes dies before liquidation, Rajes's wife and Ramendra become joint trustees, the survivor sole trustee; no provision for further devolution of trusteeship.
  • Ramani died (date not on record). On 3 Dec 1946 the 1944 suit was compromised: Santi Debi gave up her rights under the 1941 decree and was to receive Rs 475 per month for life from November 1946, with a charge over certain properties, and a term that on default she could realise it by execution of the decree.
  • Payments were made till February 1948, then defaulted. On 8 July 1949 Santi Debi applied for execution for arrears March 1948–July 1949 (Rs 8,075) against both brothers, by attachment and sale of Premises No. 44/2, Lansdowne Road, Ballygunge. Rajes objected under s.47 CPC; Ramendra did not contest.

Procedural history

Subordinate Judge overruled Rajes's objections (holding his interest under the trust "not a mere expectancy"). Calcutta High Court dismissed his appeal, treating the question as whether the interest was vested or contingent and holding it vested, relying on illustration (v) to s.119 ISA and Ranganatha Mudaliar v. A. Mohana Krishna Mudaliar (AIR 1926 Mad 645). Appeal to the Supreme Court (Rajes appellant; Santi Debi respondent 1; Ramendra respondent 2).

Issues

  1. (Construction of the compromise decree) Whether, a charge having been created for the monthly allowance, the personal remedy could be pursued only after exhausting the remedy by way of charge. [Decided against Rajes — paras 4–6, not reproduced in the DU extract.]
  2. Whether, on a true construction of the trust deed, Rajes's interest in the trust properties (and in Premises 44/2 in particular) is vested or merely contingent — and hence whether it is attachable.

Arguments

Appellant (Rajes) — the Solicitor-General

  • Under cl. 12 the sons take only when the trust "comes to an end", which requires two events: the settlor's death (certain) and liquidation of all debts — an uncertain event as to both factum and time. The interest, taking effect "only on the happening of a specified uncertain event", is contingent (s.21).
  • The settlor was "very particular" that the property should not go to the sons as owners until debts were paid — cls. 3, 5, 6, 8–11 all emphasise liquidation; the surplus income was to be accumulated; the sons received only fixed sums. Discharge of debts was a condition precedent to vesting.
  • Both s.19 TPA and s.119 ISA yield to "a contrary intention"; there is "no such inflexible rule of law" that a gift after payment of debts is always vested. Bernard v. Mountague (1816) 1 Mer 422 held payment of debts a condition precedent on the terms of that trust; Williams on Executors (13th edn, vol. 2, p. 658) and Jarman on Wills (8th edn, vol. II, p. 1390) recognise that where the testator clearly intends that benefits shall not vest till debts are paid, "the vesting as well as payment was held to be postponed."
  • A contingent interest, though transferable, is not attachable — Pestonjee Bhicajee v. P.H. Anderson (AIR 1939 PC 6). Alternatively, Rajes holds only as trustee (the s.47 petition's plea).

Respondent (Santi Debi)

  • The point of contingency was never taken in the s.47 petition (which pleaded only that Rajes had "no interest … except that of a trustee") nor in the grounds of appeal to the High Court; it should not be entertained; in any event its determination may depend on whether the debts have since been paid.
  • The strong presumption in favour of vesting (s.19; s.119 ISA) applies; a gift "after payment of debts" is vested, the words of postponement "being considered only as creating a charge" (Jarman p. 1373; ISA s.119 ill. (v); Ranganatha Mudaliar).
  • The scheme shows a present allotment of specific lots to each son: the sons receive monthly sums throughout; on a son's death before liquidation his payments go to his widow and heirs; the surplus income of each lot is to follow the corpus of that lot; cl. 14 assumes the debts will be cleared within three lives. What is postponed is enjoyment, not vesting (s.19 Explanation).
  • The sons themselves treated their interest as vested — in the 1946 compromise they created a charge on the properties for her allowance and agreed it was presently executable.

Decision (Jagannadhadas J.)

  1. Preliminary (para 7). The contingency point "has not been raised in the petition … under Section 47"; the plea actually taken (interest only as trustee) "is obviously untenable"; it is not disputed that a beneficial interest under the trust would be attachable provided it is vested. Since the High Court decided the point as one of pure construction, the Court proceeded to consider it.
  2. The operative clause (para 8). Under cl. 12(a)–(b) Lots I–IV go to Rajes and Lot V to Ramendra, "after the trust comes to an end", i.e., after (1) discharge of all scheduled debts and (2) the settlor's death. Death "is not by any means an uncertain event"; the argument centred on discharge of debts being uncertain "in the sense that neither the factum nor the time … can be predicated with any certainty." The Court acknowledged that the deed's clauses (3, 5, 6, 8–11, 12) show "the settlor attached great importance to the discharge of the debts becoming an accomplished fact before the two sons take the full benefit", restricting family enjoyment to Rs 1,500 p.m. "But can it be said that their interest in the property was made to depend on the event of the total discharge of the debts and that the discharge of the debts was contemplated as an uncertain event?"
  3. Governing principles (para 9). The question "has to be guided generally by the principles recognised under Sections 19 and 21 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, and Sections 119 and 120 of the Indian Succession Act, 1925." The English learning was reviewed: Williams — "where the bequest is in terms immediate, and the payment alone postponed, the legacy is vested", subject to the testator's intention and to the exception where he clearly intends no vesting till debts are paid; Jarman p. 1390 (same exception) but also p. 1373 — "it is now well established that such a devise [after payment of debts] confers an immediately vested interest, the words of apparent postponement being considered only as creating a charge." Conclusion: "the question is really one of intention to be gathered from a comprehensive view of all the terms of a document"; the Solicitor-General "frankly admitted this, and also that a court has to approach the task of construction in such cases with a bias in favour of a vested interest unless the intention to the contrary is definite and clear."
  4. Scheme and purposes of the deed (para 10). Two main purposes: (i) to give the property to the two surviving sons excluding Santi Debi (prejudice over the sagotra marriage); (ii) discharge of debts — by constituting the whole estate a trust, vesting management in Rajes with powers of alienation, and limiting family drawings to Rs 1,500 p.m. Debts were Rs 2,62,169; properties worth well over Rs 5 lakh; cl. 12(a) contemplated surplus income accumulating — so the settlor expected the debts to be cleared; cl. 14's silence on trusteeship beyond three lives indicates he expected liquidation "before the expiry of the three lives … Rajes, his wife and Ramendra." "There is nothing to show that he was apprehensive that the debts would remain undischarged … and that he contemplated the ultimate discharge of his debts to be such an uncertain event as to drive him to make the accrual of the interest to his sons … depend upon the event of the actual discharge." Three "provisions of great significance": (1) the sons are not excluded from benefit pending liquidation — fixed monthly sums; (2) on a son's death before liquidation those sums go to his widow and legal heirs; (3) "the most significant" — cl. 12(a) directs that the surplus income of each lot devolves with that lot; the lots being unequal, this shows "the corpus of these lots was earmarked for the two sons with the present income thereof but with a restriction on the enjoyment of the present income to specified sums, so as to facilitate orderly discharge of the debts."
  5. Application of income for the donee's benefit (para 11). "There can be no doubt about the rule that where the enjoyment of the property is postponed but the present income thereof is to be applied for the benefit of the donee the gift is vested and not contingent" (Explanation to s.19 TPA; Explanation to s.119 ISA; Williams; Jarman). Normally the rule operates where the entire income is applied for the donee; here only a portion was available for actual use. But the sons are persons who, on intestacy, "would be under an obligation to discharge his debts out of the properties which devolve upon them. It is only the surplus which would be legally available for division between them. In such a case, the balance of the income which is meant to be applied for the discharge of the debts is also an application of the income for the benefit of the donees." The scheme therefore "operates as nothing more than the present allotment of these properties themselves to the donees subject to the discharge of debts notionally in the same proportion." Summarised: (1) specified lots earmarked for each son; (2) present income applied to debts after monthly payments, notionally pro rata; (3) surpluses go to the very person who gets the corpus; (4) a son's monthly payments devolve on his heirs if he dies earlier. "These arrangements taken together clearly indicate that what is postponed is not the very vesting of the property in the lots themselves but that the enjoyment of the income thereof is burdened with certain monthly payments and with the obligation to discharge debts therefrom notionally pro rata, all of which taken together constitute application of the income for his benefit."
  6. The heirs clause (para 12). "One of the features of a contingent interest is that if a person dies before the contingency disappears and before the vesting occurs, the heirs of such a person do not get the benefit of the gift. But the trust deed … specifically provides in the case of Rajes … that even in the event of his death it is his heirs (then surviving) that would take the interest." Even treating this as a direct gift to the heirs then living, "this would make no practical difference": the parties are Dayabhaga Hindus, and "up to the third degree in the male line the principle of representation under the Hindu Law operates", so whenever the debts are discharged the taker "would, in the normal course, be the very heir (the lineal descendant then surviving or the widow) of Rajes. The actual devolution … would not be affected by the alleged contingency. That being so it is more reasonable to hold that the interest of Rajes under the deed is vested and not contingent."
  7. Confirmation from conduct (para 13). In the 1946 compromise decree both brothers created a charge for Santi Debi's monthly payment "and agreed to such charge being presently executable. This shows clearly that they themselves understood the interest available to them under the trust as a vested interest."
  8. Conclusion (paras 14–15). Other hypothetical possibilities canvassed did not displace this view. "It appears to us reasonably clear that the intention of the settlor is that as regards the interest of the sons with respect to all the properties comprised therein, it is vested in title but restricted in enjoyment so long as the settlor is alive and the debts are not discharged." Rajes's interest in Premises 44/2 Lansdowne Road (subject to cl. 12(c)) was likewise vested. Appeal dismissed with costs; execution by attachment could proceed.

Principles

  1. Whether an interest is vested or contingent is a question of the settlor's/testator's intention gathered from the whole instrument, guided by ss.19 and 21 TPA and ss.119 and 120 ISA, with a strong bias in favour of vesting unless a contrary intention is "definite and clear".
  2. Postponement of enjoyment, a prior interest, a direction to accumulate, a gift over, or a direction that the property devolve "after payment of debts" does not make the interest contingent; words of apparent postponement for payment of debts create only a charge (Jarman; ISA s.119 ill. (v)).
  3. Where the present income is applied for the benefit of the donee — including application towards debts which the donee would himself be legally bound to discharge — the gift is vested though enjoyment is restricted (Explanation to s.19; Exception to s.21 by analogy).
  4. A provision that the donee's heirs take if he dies before the event is a strong indicator of vesting, since the hallmark of a contingent interest is that heirs get nothing.
  5. A vested beneficial interest under a trust is attachable in execution; a contingent interest, though transferable inter vivos, is not (Pestonjee Bhicajee v. Anderson).
  6. The parties' own subsequent conduct (creating a presently executable charge) may confirm the construction.
Example. In 2020 a Kanpur businessman settles all his properties on his elder son as trustee: pay my bank loans (Rs 3 crore) from the rents; pay me Rs 1 lakh and each son Rs 50,000 monthly; on clearance of the loans and my death, Building A (with its accumulated surplus rent) to the elder son absolutely or, if dead, to his heirs then living; Building B to the younger son. In 2024 a decree-holder against the elder son seeks to attach Building A. Following Rajesh Kanta Roy, the elder son's interest is vested in title, restricted in enjoyment — the loan clearance is a charge/postponement, the income is applied for his benefit (debts he would be liable for), his heirs are expressly provided for — so Building A is attachable (subject to the trust and the charge for the loans). Had the deed said "Building A to my elder son if he is alive when the loans are cleared, otherwise to a charity", his interest would be contingent and not attachable.

15. DU semester PYQs with model answers

Q1 (DU LL.B., May/June 2025, Q.6(b) — short note). Difference between vested and contingent interest.
Model answer.

Vested interest — s.19. Created (a) without specifying the time when it is to take effect, or (b) to take effect forthwith, or (c) on the happening of an event which must happen — unless a contrary intention appears. It is ownership: a present proprietary right, though enjoyment may be deferred (e.g., Rs 100 to B payable on C's death; to A for life, remainder to B). It is not defeated by the transferee's death before possession, and is transferable (s.6), heritable and attachable (CPC s.60). The Explanation says vesting is not negatived merely by postponement of enjoyment, a prior interest to another, a direction to accumulate income, or a gift over on a later event.

Contingent interest — s.21. Created to take effect only on the happening, or non-happening, of a specified uncertain event (to B if he marries C; to B if C does not return within five years). The transferee has no present proprietary right — "a chance of becoming an owner" resting on an incomplete title; the interest becomes vested when the event happens (or, for a negative event, becomes impossible) and fails if the condition fails. It is transferable (it is more than a spes) but not attachable (Pestonjee Bhicajee v. Anderson, PC 1939), and generally not heritable if survival is implicit. Exception: an interest on attaining an age is not contingent if the intermediate income is given to the donee or applied for his benefit.

Distinction in brief. (1) Certain v. uncertain event; (2) present ownership v. mere expectancy on an incomplete title; (3) condition precedent absent v. present; (4) death before possession — no effect v. usually fatal; (5) heritable v. depends on contingency; (6) attachable v. not attachable; (7) never offends s.14 v. must vest within the period; (8) construction — strong presumption in favour of vesting. Case: Rajesh Kanta Roy v. Shanti Debi (AIR 1957 SC 255) — sons' interest under a trust deed, to devolve on liquidation of debts and settlor's death, with monthly payments meanwhile and a clause in favour of heirs, held "vested in title but restricted in enjoyment" and therefore attachable in execution of the widow's maintenance decree; the question is one of intention gathered from the whole document, approached "with a bias in favour of a vested interest unless the intention to the contrary is definite and clear."

Q2. R executes a trust deed of all his properties appointing his son S trustee, directing payment of his debts from the income, monthly allowances to himself and his sons, and providing that on liquidation of all debts and after his death the trust shall end and Lot A shall devolve on S absolutely "or if he be then dead, on his heirs then living". A decree-holder of S seeks to attach Lot A. S objects that his interest is contingent. Decide.
Model answer (outline). Identify issue: vested (attachable, CPC s.60) or contingent (not attachable, Pestonjee Bhicajee). Law: ss.19, 21 TPA; ss.119–120 ISA; Explanation to s.19; presumption of vesting; "after payment of debts" = charge (Jarman; ISA s.119 ill. (v)). Apply Rajesh Kanta Roy point by point: settlor's death certain; liquidation of debts — not intended as a condition of title because (i) sons receive income (monthly sums) meanwhile — application of income for their benefit, including the balance applied to debts they would themselves be bound to pay; (ii) heirs clause — contrary to the hallmark of contingency; (iii) surplus income follows corpus — present earmarking; (iv) settlor expected debts to be cleared (surplus contemplated, trusteeship for three lives only). Conclusion: "vested in title but restricted in enjoyment"; objection fails; attachment (subject to the trust and the debts) proceeds. Note the contrary case (Bernard v. Mountague) where a "definite and clear" intention postpones vesting.
Q3. "An intention that an interest shall not be vested is not to be inferred merely from a provision whereby the enjoyment thereof is postponed." Explain the Explanation to s.19 and the Exception to s.21 with illustrations and case law.
Model answer (outline). Text of s.19 and its Explanation; the four situations with examples (payment at 25; life estate then remainder; accumulation; gift over); rationale — they concern enjoyment/divesting, not acquisition of title; condition precedent v. subsequent (ss.26, 29). Exception to s.21 — age + income given/applied = vested; English origin (Hanson v. Graham; Fox v. Fox); ISA s.120 Exception. Rajesh Kanta Roy as the leading application: "where the enjoyment of the property is postponed but the present income thereof is to be applied for the benefit of the donee the gift is vested and not contingent" — even where only part of the income reaches the donee, if the rest is applied to his legal obligations (father's debts). Capsule adoption example (widow postponing adopted son's estate). Limits: clear contrary intention prevails (Bernard v. Mountague); "if/when he attains" without income → contingent (s.22).
Q4. Classify the following and give reasons: (i) A transfers property to B for life and after B's death to C; C dies during B's lifetime. (ii) A transfers Rs 5,000 to B if B marries D; B dies unmarried. (iii) A transfers a house to B "when B attains 21", directing that the rent be applied for B's education; B is 16. (iv) A transfers property to B, but if B dies before A, to C.
Points. (i) C's remainder vested (B's death certain; Expl. (ii)) — not defeated by C's death; C's heirs take on B's death. (ii) Contingent on marriage to D; B's death unmarried makes the event impossible → interest fails; property remains with A (s.21; Capsule). (iii) Vested — Exception to s.21 (income applied for B's benefit); if B dies at 18 his heirs take. (iv) B vested subject to divestment (Expl. (iv); s.28); C contingent; if A dies first, B's interest becomes indefeasible; if B dies first, C's vests (s.29 strict compliance).
Q5. Distinguish a contingent interest from spes successionis. Can either be transferred or attached?
Points. Contingent interest — created by a transfer; "present existence of an incomplete title"; transferable under s.6 (Capsule); becomes vested on the event; not attachable (Pestonjee Bhicajee). Spes — no interest; bare hope; non-transferable (s.6(a)); contracts to transfer void (s.23 Contract Act; Ananda Mohan Roy); neither transferable nor attachable; only s.43 may operate where a present title was misrepresented (Jumma Masjid). Vested interest — transferable, heritable, attachable.

16. Mnemonics & rapid revision

"MUST = VESTED; MAY = CONTINGENT." Event that must happen → s.19; event that may or may not → s.21.
"P-P-A-G" — the four non-contingencies of the s.19 Explanation: Postponed enjoyment · Prior interest to another · Accumulation of income · Gift over on a later event.
"AGE + INCOME = VESTED" — Exception to s.21 / ISA s.120.
"T-H-A": vested is Transferable, Heritable, Attachable; contingent is Transferable only (heritability depends; not attachable — Pestonjee Bhicajee).
"Debts = charge, not condition" — Rajesh Kanta Roy: "vested in title but restricted in enjoyment".
Ten lines to remember
  1. s.19: no time / forthwith / certain event → vested, unless contrary intention; not defeated by death before possession.
  2. Explanation: postponement, prior interest, accumulation, gift over ≠ contrary intention.
  3. s.21: only on a specified uncertain event (or its non-happening) → contingent; vests when event happens / becomes impossible.
  4. Exception: age condition + income given or applied for donee → vested.
  5. Vested = ownership now, enjoyment later; contingent = incomplete title, more than a spes, less than ownership.
  6. Both transferable (s.6); only vested is attachable (Pestonjee Bhicajee, PC 1939) and reliably heritable.
  7. Presumption/bias in favour of vesting; construe the whole instrument for intention (Rajesh Kanta Roy).
  8. "After payment of debts" → charge, vested (Jarman p. 1373; ISA s.119 ill. (v)) — unless a definite, clear contrary intention (Bernard v. Mountague).
  9. Rajesh Kanta Roy indicators: income applied for donee (incl. debts he would owe); heirs clause; surplus follows corpus; settlor expected debts cleared; donee's own conduct (charge).
  10. Related: s.20 (unborn vests at birth), s.22 (class at age), s.23 (no time fixed — event before precedent interest ends), s.24 (survivors), ss.25–34 (conditions), ISA ss.119–120, CPC s.60.
