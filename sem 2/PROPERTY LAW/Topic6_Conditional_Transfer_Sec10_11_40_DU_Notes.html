1. Conditional transfers — the scheme of the Act

A transfer of property may be absolute (the transferee takes the interest free of any fetter) or conditional — the creation, continuance or enjoyment of the interest is made dependent on some event or subjected to some direction. The Act deals with conditions in three clusters:

ClusterSectionsWhat they regulate
Conditions restraining alienation / enjoyment (this Topic)ss. 10, 11, 12; s. 40 (and s. 11 para 2)Whether the transferor can, while parting with property, dictate that the transferee shall not alienate it (s.10), shall not become insolvent or attempt to alienate on pain of forfeiture (s.12), shall enjoy it only in a particular manner (s.11), and when a restriction on use binds later purchasers (s.40).
Conditions precedent and subsequent (Topic 8 area, but constantly overlapping)ss. 25–34s.25 — a condition that is impossible, unlawful, fraudulent, injurious, immoral or opposed to public policy makes the interest fail; s.26 — substantial compliance suffices for a condition precedent; ss.27–30 — ulterior dispositions on failure of a prior one; s.29 — strict compliance required for a condition subsequent; ss.31–32 — an interest may be made to cease on a specified uncertain event, provided the event could lawfully be a condition of creation; ss.33–34 — time for performance.
Conditions in specific transferss.31 lease clause in s.10/12; s.111(g) forfeiture; s.126 (gift revocable on condition); s.60 (clog on redemption)Special statutory treatment of conditions in leases, gifts and mortgages.

1.1 "Condition" and "limitation"

Condition — a stipulation which qualifies an interest already defined: "to B absolutely, provided that B shall not sell." The estate is complete; the proviso seeks to cut it down. A condition precedent must be satisfied before the interest vests (s.26 — substantial compliance); a condition subsequent operates after vesting to divest or restrict (s.29 — strict compliance; ss.10–12 strike down particular kinds of condition subsequent).
Limitation — words which mark out the boundary/duration of the interest itself: "to B until he attempts to sell", "to B so long as he remains unmarried". Under English law a determinable limitation escaped the rule against conditions repugnant to a fee; s.10 and s.12 expressly include "condition or limitation", so in India the drafting device of a determinable estate cannot be used to evade the prohibition.
Effect of a void condition (s.10/11/12). Only the condition is void, not the transfer. The transferee takes the interest as if the condition had never been written — "he may sell or not as he pleases" (Capsule). Contrast s.25: where a condition precedent is unlawful, the interest itself fails. Contrast also a gift under Muslim law (a condition attached to a hiba is void and the gift is absolute — same result by a different route) and s.126 (gift revocable at mere will of donor is void as to revocation).

2. Ownership and the right of alienation — the rule against repugnancy

Ownership is a bundle of rights: the right to possess, to use and enjoy, to exclude others, to destroy, and — crucially — the right to alienate. "A right of transfer is incidental to, and inseparable from, the ownership of the property. An absolute restraint is repugnant to the nature of the estate and is an exception to the very essence of the grant" (Capsule). The economic rationale: "wealth should be in free circulation so as to get the greatest benefit from it. The law favours alienation or transfer, rather than accumulation." The historical rationale: the English statute Quia Emptores (1290) established free alienability of the fee simple; Coke (Co. Litt. 223a) treated a condition not to alien annexed to a fee as "repugnant and void"; Indian law adopted and codified the rule in s.10, adding the phrase "or limitation" and codifying two exceptions.

Related constitutional and statutory context. After the 44th Amendment (1978), property is a constitutional (not fundamental) right under Art. 300-A — "No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law." The Supreme Court in Zoroastrian Society (para 43) rejected the argument that a bye-law restricting transfer infringed Art. 300-A. Statutory restraints on alienation imposed by law — e.g., land-ceiling and tenancy Acts, restrictions on transfer of tribal land (Fifth Schedule regulations; e.g., s.46 Chhotanagpur Tenancy Act), restrictions on transfer of agricultural land to non-agriculturists (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Himachal Pradesh s.118), s.30 of the Gujarat Co-operative Societies Act, lock-in periods in allotment letters of development authorities (DDA, Noida) — are outside s.10, which strikes only at restraints imposed by the transferor in the transfer. Restraints in wills are governed by s.138 Indian Succession Act, 1925 (identically worded to s.10, without the lease/married-woman exceptions; applies to Hindus by s.57/Sch. III) — hence Rosher (a will) is applied by analogy.

3. Section 10 — text and element-by-element analysis

s.10. Condition restraining alienation.—Where property is transferred subject to a condition or limitation absolutely restraining the transferee or any person claiming under him from parting with or disposing of his interest in the property, the condition or limitation is void, except in the case of a lease where the condition is for the benefit of the lessor or those claiming under him: provided that property may be transferred to or for the benefit of a woman (not being a Hindu, Muhammadan or Buddhist), so that she shall not have power during her marriage to transfer or charge the same or her beneficial interest therein.
#ElementAnalysis
1"Where property is transferred"There must be a transfer within s.5 — sale, gift, exchange, lease, mortgage — inter vivos. Property may be movable or immovable. The section presupposes that the transferor has parted with an interest and is now attempting to control what the transferee does with it. It does not apply where there is no transfer — e.g., devolution by succession/inheritance (Zoroastrian: Respondent 2 "inherited it with the limitations thereon"; "there was really no transfer of property to Respondent 2"), a will (s.138 Succession Act instead), a partition or family settlement (not a "transfer" — Muhammad Raza: "not a deed of gift, or a conveyance… but in the nature of a contract between them as to the terms upon which the ladies were to take"), or a restraint imposed by statute or bye-laws on membership.
2"subject to a condition or limitation"The restraint must be part of the terms of the transfer (condition subsequent or determinable limitation). A separate, independent contract not to sell (e.g., a personal covenant supported by consideration) is judged by the Contract Act (s.27 — restraint of trade; s.23 — public policy), though courts apply the same policy. "Limitation" closes the English loophole of the determinable fee.
3"absolutely restraining"The heart of the section. Only absolute restraints are void; partial restraints are valid (Muhammad Raza; Zoroastrian). "Absolute" is judged by substance, not form: a restraint that takes away the power of alienation "completely or substantially" is absolute (Rosher — option to widow at one-fifth value = "equivalent to a restraint upon selling at all"). Tests, see Part 4.
4"the transferee or any person claiming under him"The restraint may be aimed at the transferee personally or at his heirs, legatees, purchasers (as in Rosher — "his heirs or devisees, or any person claiming through or under him"). Either way it is void if absolute.
5"from parting with or disposing of his interest in the property""Parting with or disposing of" covers every mode of alienation — sale, mortgage, gift, lease, exchange. A restraint on one mode only (e.g., "shall not gift") or on one class of transferees ("not to strangers"), or for a limited time, is partial. "His interest" — whatever interest was transferred; s.10 applies to a transfer of a limited interest as much as to an absolute one (this is the first distinction from s.11).
6"the condition or limitation is void"The transfer stands; the condition is struck out. The transferee (and persons claiming under him) may alienate freely and the transferor/beneficiary of the condition has no cause of action (Capsule on Rosher: "as if these conditions did not exist on paper"). A transferee from the transferee gets a good title.
7Exception — lease: "except in the case of a lease where the condition is for the benefit of the lessor or those claiming under him"Even an absolute restraint on assignment/sub-letting in a lease is valid, because the lessor retains the reversion and has a legitimate interest in the identity of his tenant. Breach → forfeiture under s.111(g) if the lease so provides (and notice under s.111(g)/s.114-A where applicable); otherwise damages. See Part 5.
8Proviso — married woman: "property may be transferred to or for the benefit of a woman (not being a Hindu, Muhammadan or Buddhist), so that she shall not have power during her marriage to transfer or charge the same or her beneficial interest therein"Codifies the English equitable device of the restraint on anticipation (protecting a wife's separate property from her husband's influence/creditors). Applies only to women not Hindu, Muslim or Buddhist — i.e., Christian, Parsi, Jewish women — because Hindu and Muslim women were already protected by their personal laws (stridhan; Muslim wife's independent proprietary capacity). Restraint operates only "during her marriage". See Part 5.
Real examples. (1) A builder sells a flat to B with a clause "purchaser shall not resell for 99 years" — void; B can sell tomorrow. (2) Father gifts a shop to son with a clause "if the son ever attempts to sell, the shop shall revert to me" — this is a limitation plus a forfeiture; void under s.10 and s.12; son takes absolutely. (3) A Christian father settles a house on his married daughter "so that she shall have no power to sell or mortgage during coverture" — valid under the proviso. (4) A DDA lease-hold allotment containing "the lessee shall not sell within 10 years without the lessor's permission" — valid: it is a lease condition for the lessor's benefit (and, being imposed by a statutory body under its rules, also outside s.10).

4. Absolute v. partial restraints

4.1 Jessel M.R.'s classification (In re Macleay (1875) LR 20 Eq 186 — cited in Muhammad Raza and the Capsule)

"You may restrict alienation in many ways: you may restrict alienation by prohibiting a particular class of alienation, or you may restrict alienation by prohibiting it to a particular class of individuals, or you may restrict alienation by restricting it to a particular time."

Each of these may be partial (valid) if, after the restriction, the transferee remains substantially free to alienate; each becomes absolute (void) if in practice it forecloses alienation. Note that In re Macleay upheld a devise "never to sell out of the family"; the later case of In re Rosher distinguished it on the ground that a price restriction at one-fifth value goes further than a family restriction.

4.2 The tests

  1. Substance over form. Look at the practical effect, not the words. A "fine", an "additional rent", or a fixed under-value price on sale which "effectually prohibit[s] the alienation" is "a circumvention of the law" and treated as absolute (Rosher).
  2. Range of permitted transferees. If alienation is confined to one named person (or the transferor alone) → absolute (Capsule illustration 2: "sell only to A's wife W" — void). If confined to a class — the family (Muhammad Raza), members of a community qualified for society membership (Zoroastrian), co-sharers — → partial.
  3. Modes of alienation excluded. Prohibiting one mode (gift; mortgage) while leaving others → partial (Capsule illustration 3). Prohibiting all modes ("shall not sell, mortgage, gift, lease or otherwise alienate") → absolute.
  4. Time. A restraint for the life of a person or in perpetuity → generally absolute (Rosher — "during the life of the widow you shall not sell"; the Capsule treats it as absolute even though limited to the widow's lifetime, because for that period it was total). A short, reasonable period (e.g., 3–5 years' lock-in) → usually partial, but a life-long or indefinite period is absolute.
  5. Price. Requirement to sell at a fixed price irrespective of market value (Capsule illustration 1 — "only for Rs 10,000") or at a gross under-value (Rosher: £3,000 for an estate worth £15,000) → absolute. A right of pre-emption at market value or at a price to be fixed by valuation → partial (valid).
  6. Purpose. "Shall not alienate except for religious purposes" → absolute (only a narrow window left). "Shall not alienate for religious purposes" → partial (Capsule).
  7. Source and context. A restraint that the transferee has voluntarily accepted as part of a family arrangement, compromise, partition or membership of a co-operative is more readily upheld as partial (Muhammad Raza; Zoroastrian paras 41–42; Gummanna Shetty v. Nagaveniamma AIR 1967 SC 1595).

5. The two exceptions to s.10

(i) Lease — condition for the benefit of the lessor

A lease transfers only a right of enjoyment (s.105); the lessor keeps the reversion and a continuing relationship with whoever occupies. Hence a covenant "not to assign, sub-let or part with possession" — even if absolute — is valid. Related provisions: s.108(j) (lessee may transfer his interest absent a contract to the contrary — the exception in s.10 is the "contract to the contrary"); s.111(g) (forfeiture on breach of an express condition providing for re-entry, with written notice); s.114-A (relief against forfeiture for breach of certain covenants — but not for a covenant against assignment/sub-letting); rent-control statutes (e.g., Delhi Rent Control Act s.14(1)(b)) make unauthorised sub-letting a ground of eviction. The condition must be "for the benefit of the lessor or those claiming under him" — a condition benefiting a stranger is outside the exception.

Example. L leases a shop to T for 20 years: "T shall not sub-let or assign without L's written consent." Valid. If T assigns to X, L may forfeit (if re-entry clause) and evict.

(ii) Married woman not being a Hindu, Muhammadan or Buddhist

Origin: the English Court of Chancery's "restraint on anticipation" (Lord Thurlow, 18th c.) — a settlor could give property to a married woman for her separate use "without power of anticipation", so that she could not, under her husband's pressure, sell or charge it. Abolished in England by the Married Women (Restraint upon Anticipation) Act, 1949, but still on the Indian statute book. Requirements: (a) transfer to or for the benefit of a woman (may be through trustees — cf. Married Women's Property Act, 1874, s.4 wages/earnings; s.8 suits); (b) she is not Hindu, Muslim or Buddhist (Christians, Parsis, Jews — and by extension, Sikhs/Jains being "Hindu" under s.2 HSA are excluded); (c) the restraint operates only during her marriage — on widowhood/divorce it ceases; (d) it may forbid "transfer or charge" of the property or her beneficial interest. Hindu and Muslim women were excluded because Hindu law (stridhan, now s.14 HSA — absolute ownership) and Muslim law (full proprietary capacity of the wife; mahr) did not need the device.

6. Restraints in family arrangements, partitions, compromises, pre-emption and co-operative societies

  • Family arrangements / compromise decrees. "Family arrangements are specially favoured in Courts of equity" (Muhammad Raza). Such a document is not a "transfer" by one party to another but a contract as to the terms on which each takes; a restraint accepted as one of those terms (e.g., "not to alienate to a stranger") binds the party and those claiming through a breach. The Supreme Court in Zoroastrian (para 42) restated: "partial restraints are valid if imposed in a family settlement, partition or compromise of disputed claims" — citing Muhammad Raza and Gummanna Shetty v. Nagaveniamma (AIR 1967 SC 1595). Related: s.17(2)(vi) Registration Act (compromise decrees), Order XXIII r.3 CPC, Kale v. Deputy Director of Consolidation (AIR 1976 SC 807) on family settlements.
  • Partition. A covenant among co-sharers that each shall offer his share to the others before selling to outsiders is a partial restraint and valid; it mirrors the statutory preferential right in s.22 Hindu Succession Act (heirs' right of pre-emption over interests in immovable property/business devolving under s.8) and the protection of dwelling-houses in s.44 TPA (second para) and s.4 Partition Act, 1893.
  • Pre-emption clauses. A right of first refusal at market/fair value is partial (valid); at a fixed or grossly under-value price it is absolute (Rosher). Muslim law shufa and statutory pre-emption (Punjab Pre-emption Act; struck down in part in Bhau Ram v. Baij Nath AIR 1962 SC 1476 and Atam Prakash v. State of Haryana AIR 1986 SC 859 on Art. 14/19 grounds) operate by law, not by the transfer, and are outside s.10.
  • Co-operative societies. Restrictions flow from (a) the statute (Gujarat Act s.30(2): transfer only after one year, only to the society/a member/an accepted applicant, and with committee approval; s.31 on death; Rule 12(2) — society cannot "without sufficient cause" refuse admission to a qualified transferee); (b) the registered bye-laws (membership qualification — Bye-law 7: Parsis; Bye-law 21: committee sanction for share transfer); and (c) the member's voluntary submission to both. Zoroastrian holds that s.10 "cannot have any application to transfer of membership" and that a qualified restriction accepted voluntarily, permitting transfer to any person qualified to be a member, is at most a partial restraint. Analogous: Maharashtra Co-op. Societies Act s.29, Delhi Co-op. Societies Act 2003 s.34/s.35; 97th Constitutional Amendment (Art. 19(1)(c) "co-operative societies"; Part IX-B) — reinforcing the associational-autonomy premise of Zoroastrian, though Part IX-B was partly struck down for States in Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021).

7. Section 12 — condition making interest determinable on insolvency or attempted alienation

s.12.—Where property is transferred subject to a condition or limitation making any interest therein, reserved or given to or for the benefit of any person, to cease on his becoming insolvent or endeavouring to transfer or dispose of the same, such condition or limitation is void.
Nothing in this section applies to a condition in a lease for the benefit of the lessor or those claiming under him.

s.12 is the forfeiture counterpart of s.10. Where s.10 strikes at a condition "you shall not alienate", s.12 strikes at a condition "if you attempt to alienate, or become insolvent, your interest shall cease" — a device which would otherwise defeat both the transferee's creditors (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 / Provincial & Presidency-towns Insolvency Acts — property of the insolvent vests in the official assignee/resolution professional) and the policy of free alienation. Points: (i) applies to interests "reserved" (to the transferor himself) as well as "given"; (ii) covers both insolvency and "endeavouring to transfer"; (iii) lease exception as in s.10 (a lessor may stipulate for re-entry on the lessee's insolvency or assignment — s.111(g) expressly recognises forfeiture on insolvency); (iv) s.31 (interest made to cease on a specified uncertain event) is expressly "subject to the provisions of section 12"; (v) the English "protective trust" (determinable life interest on bankruptcy) is thus not available in India except via a lease.

Example. A settles a house on B "for life, but if B becomes insolvent or attempts to sell, the house shall pass to C". Under s.12 the forfeiture clause is void; under s.30 the prior disposition to B stands; B's life interest is available to his creditors; C's ulterior disposition is void as it is founded on an invalid condition (ss.28, 32).

8. Section 11 — restriction repugnant to the interest created

s.11.—Where, on a transfer of property, an interest therein is created absolutely in favour of any person, but the terms of the transfer direct that such interest shall be applied or enjoyed by him in a particular manner, he shall be entitled to receive and dispose of such interest as if there were no such direction.
[Second paragraph, substituted by Act 20 of 1929] Where any such direction has been made in respect of one piece of immoveable property for the purpose of securing the beneficial enjoyment of another piece of such property, nothing in this section shall be deemed to affect any right which the transferor may have to enforce such direction or any remedy which he may have in respect of a breach thereof.
#ElementAnalysis
1"an interest … created absolutely"The section applies only to absolute transfers — sale, exchange, unconditional gift — where the transferor "vested … all the rights that he had in the property … without retaining any right in his own favour" (Capsule). It does not apply to a lease or mortgage or life-interest, where the transferor retains an interest and may legitimately direct the mode of enjoyment (e.g., "lessee shall use the premises for residence only" — valid; s.108(o)).
2"the terms of the transfer direct that such interest shall be applied or enjoyed in a particular manner"Directions as to use/enjoyment (not alienation — that is s.10): "shall use only as a residence", "shall not demolish the house", "shall keep the garden", "shall apply the income to charity", "shall reside in the house". Also directions to apply money in a certain way after an absolute gift of it.
3"he shall be entitled to receive and dispose of such interest as if there were no such direction"The direction is void/unenforceable; the transfer stands. Rationale: a direction as to enjoyment is repugnant to the absolute nature of the interest — one cannot give everything and yet keep control. Contrast s.10 which strikes down only absolute restraints; under s.11 any direction as to enjoyment falls, save the para 2 exception.
4Para 2 exception — direction for the beneficial enjoyment of another piece of immovable propertyWhere the transferor retains adjoining land and the direction is imposed on the transferred land for the benefit of the retained land — e.g., "shall not build within four feet of my boundary", "shall not open windows towards my house", "shall keep the drain in repair" — the transferor may enforce the direction (injunction, damages). This is the statutory basis of restrictive covenants between neighbours, and, read with s.40, of their enforcement against subsequent transferees with notice. Note: the exception preserves only the transferor's right (and, through s.40, his successors'); it is confined to immovable property; and it presupposes two pieces of property — a dominant (retained) and a servient (transferred) tenement — akin to, but short of, an easement (the right is contractual, "not amounting to an interest therein or easement thereon" — s.40).

8.1 Positive and negative covenants (Capsule)

Negative (restrictive) covenant — "not to do a particular thing": not to build on the four-foot strip; not to open windows; not to use the premises as a shop; to keep the garden "uncovered with any buildings" (Tulk). The covenantor incurs no expenditure in complying. Such covenants, being negative in substance, run with the land in equity and bind purchasers with notice (Tulk v. Moxhay; s.40).
Positive (affirmative) covenant — "to do a particular thing": to repair the drain; to keep the wall cemented and painted; to maintain the lawn; to pay for the upkeep of a common road. The covenantor incurs a financial burden. In equity the burden of a positive covenant does not run with freehold land (Haywood v. Brunswick Permanent Benefit Building Society (1881) 8 QBD 403; Austerberry v. Oldham Corpn (1885) 29 Ch D 750; affirmed in Rhone v. Stephens [1994] 2 AC 310); it binds only the original covenantor personally. In India, s.40 speaks of a right "to restrain the enjoyment" (negative) and of "an obligation arising out of contract and annexed to the ownership" — the latter has been read by some High Courts as capable of covering positive obligations against a transferee with notice, but the dominant view follows Tulk: only negative covenants bind successors.
Capsule example — four covenants on sale of land adjoining A's house: (i) leave four feet open so as not to obstruct light and air — negative; (ii) no open windows on the wall adjoining A's house — negative; (iii) that wall to be always cemented and painted — positive; (iv) maintain the front lawn as a garden — positive. (i) and (ii) bind B and every purchaser with notice; (iii) and (iv) bind B personally (contract) and, on the stricter view, not his purchasers. Test of substance: "not to let the garden fall out of repair" is positive in substance though negative in form; "to keep the ground open and unbuilt" (Tulk) is negative in substance though it may be framed as "to maintain … in an open state".

Interplay with ss. 25–32. (1) ss.10–12 deal with conditions subsequent attached to a completed transfer; s.25 deals with the condition on which the interest depends (usually a condition precedent) — if that is unlawful the interest fails, whereas under ss.10–12 the condition fails and the interest survives. (2) s.28 expressly subjects ulterior dispositions to ss.10 and 12 — so "to B, but if B sells, to C" gives C nothing. (3) s.31 permits an interest to cease on an uncertain event subject to s.12, and s.32 requires the event to be one that could lawfully be a condition of creation — so an interest cannot be made to cease on "attempting to alienate". (4) s.26 (substantial compliance) v. s.29 (strict compliance) shows the law's leniency towards vesting and strictness towards divesting — the same policy that animates ss.10–12. (5) A condition "not to marry" or "to desert husband" is dealt with under s.25/public policy (ill. 10. Section 40 — burden of obligation restricting use of land; obligations annexed to ownership

s.40. Burden of obligation imposing restriction on use of land.—Where, for the more beneficial enjoyment of his own immoveable property, a third person has, independently of any interest in the immoveable property of another or of any easement thereon, a right to restrain the enjoyment in a particular manner of the latter property, or
or of obligation annexed to ownership but not amounting to interest or easement.—where a third person is entitled to the benefit of an obligation arising out of contract and annexed to the ownership of immoveable property, but not amounting to an interest therein or easement thereon,
such right or obligation may be enforced against a transferee with notice thereof or a gratuitous transferee of the property affected thereby, but not against a transferee for consideration and without notice of the right or obligation, nor against such property in his hands.
Illustration.—A contracts to sell Sultanpur to B. While the contract is still in force he sells Sultanpur to C, who has notice of the contract. B may enforce the contract against C to the same extent as against A.
#ElementAnalysis
1Two limbsLimb 1 — restrictive covenants: a "third person" (i.e., someone other than the present owner of the burdened land — typically the original vendor/covenantee who retained adjoining land, or his successor) has, for the more beneficial enjoyment of his own immovable property, a right "to restrain the enjoyment in a particular manner" of another's property. This is the Tulk v. Moxhay right, and the counterpart of s.11 para 2. Limb 2 — contractual obligations annexed to ownership: a right to the benefit of an obligation "arising out of contract and annexed to the ownership of immovable property" — e.g., a contract for sale (illustration), an agreement for pre-emption, a covenant to renew a lease — "not amounting to an interest therein or easement thereon" (consistent with s.54: a contract for sale "does not, of itself, create any interest in or charge on such property").
2"independently of any interest … or of any easement"The right must be neither a proprietary interest (which would bind everyone under s.8/s.48 anyway) nor an easement (governed by the Indian Easements Act, 1882 — ss.4, 13; an easement binds all successors of the servient owner regardless of notice). s.40 fills the gap between property rights and mere personal contracts — the "equity" recognised in Tulk.
3"for the more beneficial enjoyment of his own immoveable property"The covenantee must own dominant land benefited by the restriction — in Tulk, "the plaintiff, who remained owner of several houses in the square". A covenant in gross (no land retained) is not within limb 1 and does not run (English law: LCC v. Allen [1914] 3 KB 642). The Capsule: "the transferor may impose conditions restraining the enjoyment of land if such restrictions are for the benefit of adjoining land of the transferor."
4"a right to restrain the enjoyment in a particular manner" (negative)Limb 1 is confined to negative covenants — "to restrain". A positive obligation to spend money is not a right "to restrain". Capsule: "Such covenant should be negative and this rule does not apply in case of positive or affirmative covenant."
5Against whom enforceable(a) a transferee with notice (actual or constructive — s.3; a registered deed containing the covenant gives constructive notice under Explanation I); (b) a gratuitous transferee (donee, legatee) even without notice — a volunteer takes subject to equities. Not against (c) a transferee for consideration without notice, "nor against such property in his hands" (the bona fide purchaser's plea — the same policy as s.41, s.43 para 2, s.53(1) proviso, s.53-A proviso).
6RemediesInjunction (Specific Relief Act ss.38–39; s.38(3)(b)/(c): no standard for damages/inadequacy; s.41 SRA limits); specific performance of the contract of sale against the purchaser with notice (SRA s.15(b) and s.19(b) — specific performance may be enforced against "any other person claiming under [the party] by a title arising subsequently to the contract, except a transferee for value who has paid his money in good faith and without notice of the original contract" — the statutory twin of the illustration to s.40); damages (Contract Act s.73) against the original covenantor.

  • s.40 limb 1 makes such a restrictive right enforceable against subsequent transferees with notice or gratuitous transferees — the burden runs in equity. CASE 1 — Rosher v. Rosher

(1884) 26 Ch D 801 · Chancery Division · Pearson J. · Special case stated by consent under Order XXXIV of the Rules of Court, 1875 · DU material p. 85

Theme A condition annexed to an absolute devise in fee simple requiring the devisee, if he wishes to sell or let, first to offer the property to the testator's widow at a gross under-value — is it a valid partial restraint or a void absolute restraint on alienation?

Facts

  • J.B. Rosher, by will dated 26 Nov 1872, devised "all my manor, commonly called Trewyn Manor, and all other my real estate" (in the counties of Monmouth and Hereford) to his son Jeremiah Lilburn Rosher, "his heirs, executors, administrators and assigns" — i.e., an absolute devise in fee simple.
  • The will then annexed three provisos operating during the lifetime of the testator's wife (the widow):
    1. Sale: if the son, his heirs or devisees, or any person claiming under him, "shall desire to sell" the manor and estates or any part, the widow "shall have the option to purchase the same at the price of £3,600 for the whole [Pearson J. refers to £3,000], and at a proportionate price for any part", and the same "shall accordingly be first offered to her at such price".
    2. Letting Trewyn House: if they desire to let Trewyn House, garden, buildings, land and premises for more than three years at any one time, the widow shall have the option of renting the whole for any such period at £25 a year, and it shall first be offered to her.
    3. Letting Lower Trewyn: if they desire to let Lower Trewyn for more than seven years, the widow shall have the option of renting it at £35 a year, first offer to her.
  • The testator died on 26 Nov 1874. The widow sued the son; a special case was stated by consent for the opinion of the Court.
  • Agreed values: the real selling value of the manor and estates at the date of the will and at death was £15,000 and upwards (so the option price was one-fifth of value); the real letting value of Trewyn House was £100 and upwards per annum (option rent £25); the real letting value of Lower Trewyn was £100 and upwards per annum (option rent £35).

Questions for the Court

  1. Whether the son was entitled to sell, mortgage or charge the devised estates or any part without first offering the widow the option to purchase at the named or proportionate price — or whether the provisions as to the option of purchase were null and void.
  2. Whether the son was entitled to let Trewyn House for more than three years, or Lower Trewyn for more than seven years, without first offering the widow the option of renting at the named rents — or whether those provisions were void and of no effect.

Arguments

For the widow (plaintiff)

  • The testator gave the son the fee simple but was entitled to attach conditions; the provisos do not forbid sale or letting — they merely give the widow a right of pre-emption / first refusal. The son remains free to sell or let if the widow declines.
  • A condition restricting alienation to a particular person or for a particular time (the widow's life) is a partial restraint, which the authorities from Coke onwards allow — In re Macleay (1875) (Jessel M.R.: a condition "never to sell out of the family" upheld); Doe d. Gill v. Pearson (1805).
  • The price fixed is a matter for the testator's bounty to his widow; the court should not rewrite the will merely because the bargain is favourable to her.

For the son (defendant)

  • The devise is absolute in fee; any condition repugnant to the incidents of a fee simple — of which free alienability is the chief — is void (Co. Litt. 223a; Quia Emptores).
  • The provisos must be judged by their practical effect: to compel a sale at one-fifth of value, or a letting at one-quarter of the rental value, is to make sale or letting economically impossible; it is a prohibition in disguise, an absolute restraint during the widow's life, and void.
  • A "fine" or "additional rent" imposed on alienation could otherwise be pitched so high as to defeat the rule — the law does not permit its "circumvention".
  • In re Macleay is distinguishable: a restriction to the family leaves a real market; a price restriction leaves none.

Decision (Pearson J., 28 May 1884)

  1. Framing. "The question I have to decide is whether, there being an absolute devise in fee simple to the son, the conditions annexed to it are valid."
  2. Construction of the selling condition. If the son or anyone claiming under him "is minded to sell during the lifetime of the testator's widow", the estate "must be offered to the widow at the price of £3,000 for the whole, or at a proportionate price for a part." Since the agreed value was £15,000, "it is, therefore, in effect a condition that, if the son desires to sell, he shall offer the estates to the widow, and that she is to be at liberty to buy them at one-fifth of their value."
  3. Characterisation — absolute restraint. "I consider that (and I mean to decide the case upon that conclusion) as an absolute restraint against sale during the life of the widow. I mean to treat it as if it had been done 'during the life of the widow you shall not sell,' because to compel him, if he does sell, to sell at one-fifth of the value, and to throw away four-fifths of the value of the estate is, to my mind, equivalent to a restraint upon selling at all."
  4. Anti-circumvention principle. "If a covenant be held good which in the event of a grantee in fee simple aliening the land, merely imposes a fine upon him (or an additional rent on the lands…) the general rule might be evaded and the principles of it violated by fixing such an amount of fine or additional rent as would effectually prohibit the alienation, which would clearly be a 'circumvention of the law.' To my mind, to compel the son in the present case, if he chose to sell, to sell at one-fifth of the value of the estate, is really a prohibition of alienation during the widow's lifetime."
  5. Letting conditions. On the same reasoning, the conditions requiring the premises to be offered to the widow at £25/£35 a year (against a letting value of £100+), whenever a letting beyond three/seven years was desired, were a restraint on the son's power to lease — a mode of alienation — and were likewise void (Capsule: "the beneficiaries … were not free to even give it on lease, as a lease for above the time period of three years could again entitle W to take the property at a very small rent").
  6. Result. Both sets of provisions were declared void; the son was entitled to sell, mortgage, charge and let the estates without first offering them to the widow — "as if these conditions did not exist on paper … without any cause of action arising in favour of W" (Capsule). [Pearson J. also held the mortgage/charge question in the son's favour, the restriction on "selling" being read as not extending to mortgages and in any case being void.]

Principles

  1. A restraint on alienation annexed to an absolute estate is tested by its substance and practical effect, not by its form. A right of pre-emption at a fixed under-value price (here one-fifth), or a "fine" on alienation, is an absolute restraint and void — "equivalent to a restraint upon selling at all".
  2. A restraint which is total for the duration of a life (the widow's) is absolute for that period and void; limitation in time does not save it where, during that time, alienation is effectively prohibited.
  3. The rule against restraints extends to letting (leasing is a mode of disposing of an interest) and to mortgaging/charging.
  4. "Any restriction which substantially takes away the power of alienation is void as being repugnant to the very conception of ownership. A partial restraint which does not deprive the owner of his power of alienation is valid" (Capsule).
  5. Indian application: the same result follows under s.10 TPA (transfers inter vivos) and s.138 Indian Succession Act (wills); Capsule illustrations 1 and 2 (fixed price; single named purchaser) are drawn from Rosher.
Modern example. A father's gift deed of a Gurgaon flat (market value Rs 2 crore) to his son provides: "if the donee wishes to sell during the lifetime of the donor's wife, he shall first offer it to her at Rs 40 lakh." Under s.10 read with Rosher, the clause is an absolute restraint — void; the son may sell to anyone at market price and the mother has no claim. Had the clause said "first offer to her at the prevailing market value as certified by a Government-approved valuer", it would be a valid partial restraint (pre-emption).

CASE 2 — Muhammad Raza v. Abbas Bandi Bibi

(1932) 59 IA 236 : AIR 1932 PC 158 · Privy Council · Sir George Lowndes (delivering the judgment) · On appeal from the Chief Court of Oudh (Raza and Pullan JJ., 4 Jan 1929) · DU material pp. 87–90

Theme A restriction in a compromise/family arrangement that the female takers "shall not have power to transfer this property to a stranger" — is it a void absolute restraint repugnant to ownership, or a valid partial restraint? Also: the nature of the estate conferred by "malik mustaqil".

Facts

  • In 1868 Sughra Bibi sued her cousin Afzal Husain (lambardar of Mahal Shadipur, Oudh) claiming a half share in immovable properties entered in his name at the post-Mutiny settlement.
  • The suit ended in a compromise on which a decree was passed on 19 Sept 1870. Terms: (i) Sughra Bibi's marriage by nikah with Afzal Husain to be performed next month, and "in view of a marriage settlement" the dispute over the share to end; (ii) since Afzal Husain's first wife Fatima Begam (daughter of Raja Syed Abbas Ali) was alive, both wives, "in their capacity as wives, from this very time" were declared "permanent owners" (malik mustaqil) of a moiety each of the entire Mahal Shadipur, to be entered in the public records as owners "half and half"; (iii) "The said females shall not have power to transfer this property to a stranger; but the ownership thereof as family property shall devolve on the legal heirs of both the above-named wives, from generation to generation"; (iv) management and collections to remain with the husband; on neglect, the wife's only remedy was management through the Court of Wards; neither wife could have her share managed by her father's family during Afzal Husain's life; (v) if Afzal Husain refused the nikah, Sughra Bibi would "remain owner of a moiety" (without restriction); if she broke the stipulation of nikah she would "cease to have any rights whatever"; (vi) even on divorce, ownership would remain with the wives "subject to the conditions mentioned above"; (vii) other properties (Chitoi, Nausanda, Musha in pargana Tanda; Halimpur, Lodhna, Nathupur in pargana Surhurpur) were to remain with Afzal Husain during his and his father Mir Teg Ali's lives and then "devolve on the two wives or their descendants (aulad) in equal shares."
  • Afzal Husain married Sughra Bibi and died childless in 1872; Fatima Begam had predeceased him in 1871. Sughra Bibi took possession of her share but sold or mortgaged all of it before her death on 26 July 1914. Her transferees (the appellants) remained in undisturbed possession for nearly twelve years.
  • On 26 March 1926 the respondent, Abbas Bandi Bibi — an heir of Sughra Bibi in right of her mother Zainab Bibi, sister of Afzal Husain — sued in the court of the Subordinate Judge, Fyzabad, to recover two-thirds of Sughra Bibi's share (the remaining third belonging to maternal relatives who made no claim). Her case: under the compromise Sughra Bibi took only a life estate without power of alienation, so the alienations were invalid and the share passed to her heirs.

Procedural history

Preliminary issue before the Subordinate Judge: "Was the restriction placed by the compromise deed dated September 19, 1870, upon Sughra Bibi's power of alienation valid and legally enforceable?" — answered No (restriction invalid and inoperative), after "a detailed but not very informing examination of the case law". Some 28 other issues were tried by another Subordinate Judge; suit dismissed as to some properties, decreed as to others. Chief Court of Oudh (Raza and Pullan JJ., 4 Jan 1929): discussed the meaning of "malik"; held that in view of the express provision against transfer to a stranger the ladies had only a "limited ownership" with a gift over to their heirs, valid under Shia law; allowed the respondent's appeal and dismissed the appellants' — suit decreed in full. Appeal to the Privy Council by the transferees.

Issues

  1. On the true construction of the compromise, did Sughra Bibi take a life estate (with remainder to heirs) or an absolute estate of inheritance?
  2. If an absolute estate, was the restriction "shall not have power to transfer this property to a stranger" an absolute restraint — repugnant and void — or a partial restraint, valid and binding?
  3. By what law is the question to be judged (Oudh Laws Act, 1876, s.3 — justice, equity and good conscience), and does that law invalidate such a partial restriction?

Arguments

Appellants (Sughra Bibi's transferees)

  • The document made the ladies full owners ("malik mustaqil" — permanent proprietors, to be entered as such in the public records from the date of execution); the attempt to restrict alienation is repugnant to that absolute estate and void — relying on Raghunath Prasad Singh v. Deputy Commissioner, Partabgarh (1929) 56 IA 372 (Sir Binod Mitter), where an absolute restraint attached to a testamentary gift was held void.
  • Internal indications of absolute ownership: if Afzal Husain refused the marriage she was to "remain owner of a moiety" free of restriction; the other properties were to "devolve on the two wives or their descendants" without restriction — so the draftsman knew how to create a life estate and did not; the words about devolution on heirs are merely explanatory of the restriction against strangers, not a gift over; if only life estates were intended the restriction would not have been confined to strangers.
  • Hence Sughra Bibi's sales and mortgages to them were valid and their 12-year possession good.

Respondent (Abbas Bandi Bibi)

  • "Malik" does not necessarily import full ownership (Privy Council decisions); reading the document as a whole the ladies took life estates with vested remainders in their heirs — "the ownership thereof as family property shall devolve on the legal heirs… from generation to generation".
  • Alternatively, even if an estate of inheritance, it was expressly saddled with a restriction against alienation to a stranger; the appellants are admittedly strangers; the restriction is partial, valid, and binds those who claim through its breach.
  • The document is a family arrangement / contract, not a gift; equity favours family arrangements and will not let strangers profit from a breach.
  • Under Shia law the arrangement (including the gift over) is valid.

Decision (Sir George Lowndes)

  1. Construction left open. "Their Lordships feel the weight of these contentions, and they might have some difficulty in holding that Sughra Bibi took nothing more than a life estate. But assuming in the appellants' favour that she took an estate of inheritance, it was nevertheless one saddled, under the express words of the document, with a restriction against alienation to 'a stranger.'" "Stranger" means "any one who is not a member of the family, and the appellants are admittedly strangers in this sense."
  2. Partial, not absolute. "On the assumption that Sughra Bibi took … an absolute estate subject only to this restriction, their Lordships think that the restriction was not absolute but partial; it forbids only alienation to strangers, leaving her free to make any transfer she pleases within the ambit of the family. The question therefore is whether such a partial restriction on alienation is so inconsistent with an otherwise absolute estate that it must be regarded as repugnant and merely void."
  3. Raghunath Prasad distinguished — "there the restriction against alienation was absolute and was attached to a gift by will."
  4. Nature of the document. "It is … important … to bear in mind that the document under which the appellants claim was not a deed of gift, or a conveyance, by one of the parties to the other, but was in the nature of a contract between them as to the terms upon which the ladies were to take. The title … was in dispute … In compromise of their conflicting claims what was evidently a family arrangement was come to, by which it was agreed that she should take what she claimed upon certain conditions. One of these conditions was that she would not alienate the property outside the family."
  5. Governing law. Section 3 of the Oudh Laws Act, 1876 — the earlier clauses having no application, "the Courts shall act according to justice, equity and good conscience," the ultimate test for all provincial courts in India. "Judging the matter upon abstract grounds, their Lordships would have thought that where a person had been allowed to take property upon the express agreement that it shall not be alienated outside the family, those who seek to make title through a direct breach of this agreement could hardly support their claim by an appeal to these high sounding principles, and … family arrangements are specially favoured in Courts of equity."
  6. Section 10 TPA as declaratory of principle. "But, apart from this, it seems clear that after the passing of the Transfer of Property Act in 1882, a partial restriction upon the power of disposition would not, in the case of a transfer inter vivos, be regarded as repugnant: see s. 10 of the Act. In view of the terms of this section, and in the absence of any authority suggesting that before the Act a different principle was applied by the Courts in India, their Lordships think that it would be impossible for them to assert that such an agreement … was contrary to justice, equity and good conscience."
  7. English law test. Lord Hobhouse in Waghela Rajsanji v. Shekh Masludin (1887) 14 IA 89, 96: "equity and good conscience" generally means English law if applicable to Indian society. In England "a partial restriction would not be regarded as repugnant even in the case of a testamentary gift": In re Macleay (1875) LR 20 Eq 186 (Jessel M.R. upheld a condition that the devisee should "never sell out of the family", "pointing out that this had been the law from the time of Coke"); Doe d. Gill v. Pearson (1805) 6 East 173 (Lord Ellenborough).
  8. Shia law point unnecessary. Whether Shia law permits a vested remainder in "such an indeterminate body as the heirs of a living person" — "this somewhat abstruse problem" — was left undecided.
  9. Result. "Sughra Bibi had no power to transfer any part of the properties to the appellants, and upon her death the respondent became entitled to the two-thirds share." Appeal dismissed; Chief Court decree of 4 Jan 1929 affirmed with costs.

Principles

  1. A restriction forbidding alienation only to strangers (outside the family) is a partial restraint, not repugnant to an absolute estate, and is valid and binding; transferees who take in breach acquire no title against the heirs.
  2. s.10 TPA recognises the validity of partial restrictions upon a power of disposition in transfers inter vivos; the Privy Council treated the section as declaratory of the principle of justice, equity and good conscience even for a pre-Act transaction outside the Act.
  3. Restrictions accepted in a family arrangement / compromise stand on a stronger footing than conditions in a gift or will — the document is a contract as to the terms on which property is taken; equity favours family arrangements; a person cannot found title on a direct breach of such an agreement.
  4. "Malik"/"malik mustaqil" does not conclusively denote absolute ownership; the document must be read as a whole (construction point left open).
  5. Capsule formulation: "A condition to sell only to specific persons is void, but a condition not to sell outside the family would be a partial restraint."
Example. On partition of ancestral property in Tanda (Ambedkar Nagar), the deed provides that no sharer shall sell his allotted plot "to any person outside the Khan family". A sharer sells to an outsider. Following Muhammad Raza, the restriction is partial and binding; the outsider gets no title as against the family; the family may sue for declaration/possession (subject to limitation). Had the clause said "shall sell only to Zafar (a named brother)", it would be an absolute restraint and void.

CASE 3 — Zoroastrian Co-operative Housing Society Ltd. v. District Registrar, Co-operative Societies (Urban) & Ors.

(2005) 5 SCC 632 · Supreme Court of India · P.K. Balasubramanyan J. (for the Bench with Ruma Pal J.) · Appeal by special leave from the Gujarat High Court (Single Judge, SCA 6226/1996, 16-1-1997; Division Bench, LPA 129/1997, 23-7-1999) · DU material pp. 91–99

Theme Whether a bye-law of a co-operative housing society confining membership to Parsis, and the consequent requirement that a member's plot/flats be sold only to persons qualified to be members (Parsis) with the society's consent, is (i) opposed to public policy, and (ii) an absolute restraint on alienation void under s.10 TPA.

Facts

  • The Zoroastrian Co-operative Housing Society was registered on 19-5-1926 under the Bombay Co-operative Societies Act, 1925 (application under s.9 with proposed bye-laws; Registrar satisfied bye-laws not contrary to Act/Rules; certificate under s.11). Objects: building, buying, selling, letting and developing land on co-operative principles for residential use of members, and social/educational work "in connection with its tenets".
  • Bye-law 7: original members were the signatories; other members to be elected by the Committee, "provided that all members shall belong to the Parsi community". Bye-law 21: a member's share could be sold only with previous sanction of the Committee, which had full discretion; no rights against the Society until the transfer was registered.
  • The Society got 6 acres 12 guntas in Ahmedabad acquired at its cost under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 (agreement with Government of Bombay under s.41, 17-2-1928, reciting acquisition "for the purpose of erecting houses thereon" and usefulness to the public), and allotted plots to members for residential houses. On States' reorganisation the Society came under the Gujarat Co-operative Societies Act, 1961 (in force 1-5-1962; s.169 deemed registration).
  • A member sold his plot (with bungalow) to the father of Respondent 2, a Parsi, with the Committee's consent; he was admitted as a member. On his death Respondent 2 became a member "of his volition". Respondent 2 sought permission to demolish the bungalow and build a commercial building — refused (bye-laws did not permit commercial use). He then sought permission to build residential flats to be sold to Parsis — granted on 17-5-1988, "making it clear that the flats constructed could only be sold to Parsis." He did not act for seven years.
  • Meanwhile: the Society had written to the Registrar apprehending sales to non-Parsis for commercial motives; the Registrar replied that sales must accord with the bye-laws; a Gujarat Government notification of 20-7-1982 disqualified builders, contractors, architects etc. from membership of housing societies. The Society passed a resolution reminding members that under Bye-law 7 only Parsis could become new members and plots could not be sold to non-Parsis.
  • Respondent 2 began negotiating with Respondent 3, a builders' association (non-Parsi). The Society filed a case before the Board of Nominees for an injunction restraining construction on Plot No. 7 and transfer to outsiders. Interim injunction granted, then vacated; the Board took the view that the Society could not restrict membership to Parsis and membership should remain open to all. Respondent 2 applied to transfer his share to Respondent 3 — rejected by the Society as contrary to the Act, Rules and bye-laws. The District Registrar / authorities under the Act directed the Society to admit Respondent 3.

Procedural history

Gujarat High Court, Single Judge (16-1-1997): dismissed the Society's writ; a bye-law limiting membership to Parsis is an "unfair restriction" that authorities may deal with under s.24 of the Act and Rule 12(2); further, such a bye-law "would amount to a restraint on alienation and hence would be hit by Section 10 of the Transfer of Property Act." Division Bench (23-7-1999): LPA dismissed, "more or less concurring". Supreme Court: appeal by special leave by the Society and its Chairman.

Issues

  1. Can a co-operative society under the Gujarat Act confine membership to persons of a particular community/persuasion by its registered bye-laws, or is such a bye-law opposed to public policy (s.4 proviso; constitutional values of equality) and overridden by open membership (s.24)?
  2. Is the bye-law requiring a member to transfer his plot/flats only to Parsis (persons qualified for membership) with the Society's prior consent an absolute restraint on alienation void under s.10 TPA, and an interference with the right to property under Art. 300-A?

Arguments

Appellants (Society) — Mr Soli J. Sorabjee

  • Art. 19(1)(c): Parsis have a fundamental right to form an association; that right is infringed by thrusting on the association members it does not want, against its bye-laws. Art. 29: right of the Parsi minority to conserve its culture (Art. 26 grounds not pursued).
  • Nothing in the Act or Rules precludes a society from restricting membership to persons of a particular persuasion, belief or tenet; Bye-law 7 was duly registered and valid so long as not contrary to the Act/Rules; the Society cannot be compelled to act against its bye-laws.
  • No absolute restraint on alienation to attract s.10 TPA; the restraint, if any, is partial and valid.
  • Nothing illegal in persons coming together to restrict membership or exclude the general public so as to carry on their objects smoothly. Part III rights pertain to State action; an individual may voluntarily join an association which restricts rights he might otherwise enjoy. No violation of public policy.

Respondents 2 & 3 — Mr Bobde

  • s.4 of the Act: no bye-law can be recognised which is opposed to public policy — read with the Constitution and the rights of individuals. A bye-law restricting membership to a denomination, community, caste or creed is opposed to public policy; the authorities and the High Court rightly rejected the Society's claim.
  • The bye-law operates as a restraint on alienation invalid under s.10 TPA; the member cannot sell to anyone but a Parsi, and even then only with consent.
  • A co-operative society is different from a voluntary association or a Societies Registration Act society — it enjoys State aid and is regulated by ss.4 and 24; the validity of its bye-laws is testable notwithstanding earlier registration; under s.14 the Registrar can direct amendment of bye-laws to delete the Parsi restriction.

Decision (P.K. Balasubramanyan J.)

  1. Statutory framework (paras 7–12). s.2(2) bye-laws; s.2(13) member; s.4 (societies for economic interests or general welfare of members or public, with a proviso against registration if opposed to public policy); s.24(1) — "No society shall, without sufficient cause, refuse admission to membership to any person duly qualified therefor under the provisions of this Act, the rules and bye-laws of such society" — "the bye-laws are not given the go-by in spite of the introduction of the concept of open membership"; s.30(2) — a member shall not transfer his share/interest unless held for one year, the transfer is to the society/a member/an accepted applicant, and the committee approves; s.31 (heir must obtain membership or take the value); s.32 (share not attachable); s.36 (expulsion); ss.44–46 (dealings with non-members); Rule 12(2) (no refusal without sufficient cause to admit a duly qualified transferee).
  2. History and premise of housing societies (para 14). The model bye-laws contemplated "a bond of common habits and common usage among the members" — in India "most frequently found in a community or caste"; the Society adopted this by Bye-law 7.
  3. Open membership not absolute (para 36). "The concept of open membership, as envisaged by Section 24 of the Act is not absolute on the very wording of that section. The availability of membership is subject to the qualification prescribed under the … bye-laws … no person, or aspiring member, can be heard to say that he will not be bound by that law which prescribes a qualification for his membership."
  4. Public policy (paras 37–39). The High Court "made a wrong approach". "So long as the approved bye-law stands and the Act does not provide for invalidity of such a bye-law or for interdicting the formation of cooperative societies confined to persons of a particular vocation, a particular community, a particular persuasion or a particular sex, it could not be held that the formation of such a society under the Act would be opposed to public policy." Constitutional goals of non-discrimination "ha[ve] to be achieved by legislative intervention and not by the court coining a theory that whatever is not consistent with … the Constitution … could be declared to be opposed to public policy." Gherulal Parakh v. Mahadeodas Maiya (AIR 1959 SC 781): public policy is governed by precedents; new heads only in exceptional, clear cases of harm to the public; expansion "inadvisable in the interest of stability of society." A minority community may preserve its culture and way of life by forming a society and prescribing that its members alone may be members; examples — women's societies, societies of handicapped persons, labourers, vegetarians: "It will be impermissible … to thrust upon the society of those believing in say, vegetarianism, persons who are regular consumers of non-vegetarian food." Maybe "it is time to legislate", but that cannot make the Society's qualification opposed to public policy.
  5. Section 10 TPA — inapplicable to membership (para 40). "Section 10 of the Transfer of Property Act cannot have any application to transfer of membership. Transfer of membership is regulated by the bye-laws … Section 30 of the Act itself places restriction in that regard. There is no plea of invalidity attached to that provision. Hence, the restriction … cannot be invalidated or ignored by reference to Section 10."
  6. Section 10 — scope and no "transfer" here (para 41). "Section 10 … relieves a transferee of immovable property from an absolute restraint placed on his right to deal with the property in his capacity as an owner … The section applies to a case where property is transferred subject to a condition or limitation absolutely restraining the transferee … For making such a condition invalid, the restraint must be an absolute restraint. It must be a restraint imposed while the property is being transferred to the transferee. Here, Respondent 2 became a member … on the death of his father. He subscribed to the bye-laws. He accepted Section 30 of the Act … There was really no transfer of property to Respondent 2. He inherited it with the limitations thereon placed by Section 31 of the Act and the bye-laws … These are restrictions in the interests of the Society and its members and consistent with the object with which the Society was formed. He cannot question that restriction. It is also not possible to say that such a restriction amounts to an absolute restraint on alienation."
  7. Self-imposed and, at best, partial (para 42). "The restriction, if any, is a self-imposed restriction. It is a restriction in a compact to which the father of Respondent 2 was a party and to which Respondent 2 voluntarily became a party … Moreover, it is not as if it is an absolute restraint on alienation. Respondent 2 has the right to transfer the property to a person who is qualified to be a member of the Society as per its bye-laws. At best, it is a partial restraint on alienation. Such partial restraints are valid if imposed in a family settlement, partition or compromise of disputed claims — Mohd. Raza v. Abbas Bandi Bibi (AIR 1932 PC 158); Gummanna Shetty v. Nagaveniamma (AIR 1967 SC 1595). So, when a person accepts membership in a cooperative society by submitting himself to its bye-laws and secures an allotment … and places on himself a qualified restriction in his right to transfer the property by stipulating that the same would be transferred back to the society or with the prior consent of the society to a person qualified to be a member of the society, it cannot be held to be an absolute restraint on alienation offending Section 10 … He has voluntarily submerged his rights in that of the society."
  8. Attachability and Art. 300-A (para 43). That the member's rights are attachable and saleable in execution does not make the bye-law an absolute restraint; the condition that voluntary transfer be only to the society or a qualified person with consent cannot "straightaway be declared to be an absolute restraint on alienation and consequently an interference with his right to property protected by Article 300-A." The High Court's finding "is reversed."
  9. Result (paras 44–45). Appeal allowed; High Court judgments and authorities' orders set aside; the Society's right upheld to insist that Respondent 2 deal with the property only per the bye-laws and assign it, wholly or in parts, only to persons qualified to be members; direction to admit Respondent 3 set aside; Respondent 3 restrained from entering or building on the property on the basis of any transfer in disregard of the bye-laws and without the Society's prior consent.

Principles

  1. s.10 applies only to a restraint imposed in and by a transfer of property; it has no application to (a) transfer of membership of a society, (b) limitations attaching to property acquired by inheritance, or (c) restrictions flowing from statute (s.30 Gujarat Act) and registered bye-laws.
  2. A restriction permitting transfer to any person qualified to be a member (a class), with the society's consent, is at most a partial restraint — valid.
  3. Self-imposed / voluntarily accepted restrictions in a compact (membership; family settlement; partition; compromise) are valid partial restraints — Muhammad Raza and Gummanna Shetty reaffirmed.
  4. Open membership under s.24 is subject to bye-law qualifications; a community-restricted co-operative is not per se opposed to public policy; the doctrine of public policy is not to be expanded by courts from constitutional aspirations (Gherulal Parakh); change must come by legislation.
  5. Art. 300-A is not infringed by a voluntarily accepted, partial restriction on transfer.
Critical note for essay answers. The decision has been criticised for permitting exclusionary housing on religious lines (cf. Art. 15(2); the Maharashtra and Gujarat Governments' later moves; the debate around Indian Medical Association v. Union of India (2011) on Art. 15(2) and private bodies). Mention that the Court itself flagged the need for legislation (para 39). For s.10 purposes, however, the ratio is narrow and orthodox: no transfer → no s.10; class-based, consent-based, self-imposed restrictions are partial.
Example. A Delhi group housing society's bye-laws (registered under the Delhi Co-operative Societies Act, 2003) restrict membership to serving/retired defence officers; a member's heir wants to sell the flat to a builder. Following Zoroastrian, the society can insist that the transfer be to a person eligible for membership and with its consent under s.34/35 of the Delhi Act; s.10 TPA is not attracted. But if the sale deed itself from a private seller to a buyer said "buyer shall never sell", that would be a s.10 case and void.

CASE 4 — Tulk v. Moxhay

(1848) 2 Ph 774 : 41 ER 1143 : (1843–60) All ER Rep 9 · Court of Chancery · Lord Cottenham L.C. (on motion to discharge the Master of the Rolls' injunction) · DU material pp. 100–101

Theme Whether a restrictive (negative) covenant as to the use of land, entered into by the original purchaser, can be enforced in equity against a subsequent purchaser who bought with notice, although the covenant does not "run with the land" at law and the later purchaser's own deed contains no such covenant. Foundation of s.40 TPA (and s.11 para 2).

Facts

  • In 1808 the plaintiff Tulk, owner in fee of a vacant piece of ground in Leicester Square, London, and of several houses forming the square, sold the ground — described as "Leicester Square Garden or Pleasure Ground, with the equestrian statue then standing in the centre thereof and the iron railing and stone work round the same" — to one Elms in fee.
  • The conveyance contained a covenant by Elms, for himself, his heirs and assigns, with Tulk, his heirs, executors and administrators: that Elms and his heirs and assigns "should, and would from time to time, and at all times thereafter, at his and their own costs and charges, keep and maintain the said piece of ground and square garden and the iron railing round the same in its then form, and in sufficient and proper repair as a square garden and pleasure ground, in an open state, uncovered with any buildings, in neat and ornamental order; and that it should be lawful for the inhabitants of Leicester Square, tenants of the plaintiff, on payment of a reasonable rent, to have keys … and the privilege of admission … into the said square garden."
  • The land passed "by divers mesne conveyances" to the defendant Moxhay, whose purchase deed contained no similar covenant with his vendor, but he admitted that he had purchased with notice of the covenant in the 1808 deed.
  • Moxhay "manifested an intention to alter the character of the square garden, and asserted a right, if he thought fit, to build upon it." Tulk, who still owned several houses in the square, filed a bill for an injunction.
  • The Master of the Rolls granted an injunction restraining Moxhay from converting or using the ground and garden "to or for any other purpose than as a square garden and pleasure ground in an open state, and uncovered with buildings." Moxhay moved to discharge that order before the Lord Chancellor.

Issues

  1. Whether a covenant restricting the use of land, which does not run with the land at law (the covenantee having no reversion — Keppell v. Bailey; the later Austerberry rule), can nevertheless be enforced in equity against a subsequent purchaser with notice.
  2. Whether the covenant — partly expressed in affirmative terms ("keep and maintain … in repair") — was in substance a negative covenant (keep "in an open state, uncovered with any buildings") enforceable by injunction.

Arguments

Defendant (Moxhay) — moving to discharge

  • The covenant was personal to Elms; it does not run with the land at law because there is no privity of estate between Tulk (a vendor in fee) and Moxhay; at common law the burden of a covenant does not pass with freehold land to an assignee (Keppell v. Bailey (1834) — Lord Brougham: owners cannot create novel incidents binding land in the hands of all comers).
  • His own deed contains no covenant; his only liability could arise from contract, and he is not a party to the 1808 contract.
  • The covenant is positive in nature (to maintain and repair at his own cost) — a burden on land enforceable only between the original parties, "not against the subsequent purchasers for value" (Capsule).
  • Equity should not interfere unless an action at law would lie.

Plaintiff (Tulk)

  • The court's jurisdiction to enforce a contract between the owner of land and his neighbour-purchaser that the purchaser shall use or abstain from using the land in a particular way is undoubted; the only question is whether a purchaser with notice can escape it.
  • If a covenantor could sell and his purchaser take free of the covenant, "it would be impossible for an owner of land to sell part of it without incurring the risk of rendering what he retains worthless."
  • The price paid by Elms reflected the covenant; it would be inequitable for the original purchaser to sell the next day at a higher price because his assignee could escape the burden.
  • An equity attached to property by its owner binds every purchaser with notice — Duke of Bedford v. British Museum Trustees (Lord Eldon); Mann v. Stephens; decisions of the Vice-Chancellor of England.

Decision (Lord Cottenham L.C.)

  1. Jurisdiction undisputed. "That this court has jurisdiction to enforce a contract between the owner of land and his neighbour purchasing a part of it that the purchaser shall either use or abstain from using the land purchased in a particular way is what I never knew disputed. Here there is no question about the contract."
  2. The real question — not whether the covenant runs, but notice. "It is said that, the covenant being one which does not run with the land, this court cannot enforce it, but the question is not whether the covenant runs with the land, but whether a party shall be permitted to use the land in a manner inconsistent with the contract entered into by his vendor, with notice of which he purchased."
  3. Policy — protection of retained land. "If that were so, it would be impossible for an owner of land to sell part of it without incurring the risk of rendering what he retains worthless."
  4. Inequity of escape. "Of course, the price would be affected by the covenant, and nothing could be more inequitable than that the original purchaser should be able to sell the property the next day for a greater price, in consideration of the assignee being allowed to escape from the liability which he had himself undertaken."
  5. Equity attached to the land binds purchasers with notice. "That the question does not depend upon whether the covenant runs with the land is evident from this, that, if there was a mere agreement and no covenant, this court would enforce it against a party purchasing with notice of it, for if an equity is attached to property by the owner, no one purchasing with notice of that equity can stand in a different situation from that of the party from whom he purchased." Lord Eldon's decision in Duke of Bedford v. British Museum Trustees (60 ER 1055) proceeds on this footing; in Mann v. Stephens (60 ER 665) the Lord Chancellor had upheld a like injunction.
  6. Keppell v. Bailey explained. Lord Brougham "never could have meant to lay down, that this court would not enforce an equity attached to land by the owner unless under such circumstances as would maintain an action at law. If that be the result of his observations, I can only say that I cannot coincide with it."
  7. Result. "I think this decision of the Master of the Rolls perfectly right, and, therefore, that this motion must be refused with costs." The injunction stood: Moxhay could not build on or alter the character of the garden.

Principles ("the rule in Tulk v. Moxhay", as refined by later cases and codified in s.40 TPA)

  1. A negative/restrictive covenant concerning the use of land, made for the benefit of land retained by the covenantee, is enforceable in equity by injunction against a subsequent purchaser who took with notice (actual or constructive), even though the covenant does not run with the land at law and the purchaser's own deed is silent.
  2. Basis: an equity attached to the land by its owner + notice; "no one purchasing with notice of that equity can stand in a different situation from that of the party from whom he purchased."
  3. Limits (later cases; reflected in s.40 and the Capsule): the covenant must be negative in substance (Haywood 1881; positive covenants do not run); the covenantee must retain dominant land benefited (LCC v. Allen 1914); a purchaser for value without notice is not bound; a gratuitous transferee is bound even without notice.
  4. Indian codification — s.40 TPA (limb 1) and s.11 para 2; remedies under SRA ss.38–39; notice under s.3 TPA. The Capsule states the rule as: for the beneficial enjoyment of his own immovable property a third person may, independently of any interest/easement, restrain the enjoyment of another's property in a particular manner; enforceable against a transferee with notice or a gratuitous transferee, not against a transferee for consideration without notice.
Example. In 2010 R sells the rear half of his Noida plot to S, the registered sale deed providing that S "shall not construct above two storeys and shall keep the 3-metre strip along R's boundary unbuilt" (to protect R's light and air). S sells to T in 2022; T's deed is silent but the 2010 deed is registered (constructive notice — s.3 Expl. I). T begins a four-storey building. R (or R's purchaser of the front half) may obtain a perpetual injunction against T under s.40/SRA s.38 — Tulk v. Moxhay. A covenant "S shall maintain the boundary wall at his own cost" is positive and would bind only S in contract.

14. Case map & synthesis

Synthesis. The four cases form a ladder. Rosher fixes the outer limit: whatever its form, a condition that in substance prevents alienation (under-value option, fine, life-long bar) is absolute and void. Muhammad Raza fixes the inner limit: a restriction to a class (the family), especially one accepted in a family arrangement, is partial and binding — and s.10 is the touchstone even outside its strict field. Zoroastrian applies both to modern collective ownership: no transfer → no s.10; class + consent + voluntary acceptance → partial. Tulk v. Moxhay turns from alienation to use: restrictions on enjoyment imposed for the benefit of retained land (s.11 para 2) survive and, if negative, follow the land into the hands of purchasers with notice (s.40).

16. DU semester PYQs with model answers

Q1 (DU LL.B., May/June 2025, Q.5). "An absolute restraint on the right to sell the property is not permissible; however, partial restraints are allowed." Discuss the aforesaid statement in the light of decided cases.
Model answer.

Introduction — the principle. The right to alienate is an inseparable incident of ownership; wealth should circulate freely. Hence s.10 TPA provides that where property is transferred subject to a condition or limitation absolutely restraining the transferee or any person claiming under him from parting with or disposing of his interest, the condition or limitation is void (except in a lease for the lessor's benefit, and a transfer to a married woman not being Hindu, Muslim or Buddhist). Only the condition falls; the transfer stands. s.12 strikes down forfeiture on insolvency/attempted alienation; s.138 Indian Succession Act applies the same rule to wills. The section, by its terms, spares partial restraints.

Absolute v. partial — the tests. Jessel M.R. (In re Macleay): alienation may be restricted by mode, by class of persons or by time; each may be partial or absolute according to its effect. A restraint is absolute if it takes away the power of alienation "completely or substantially" — judged by substance, not form. Indicators of an absolute restraint: prohibition of all modes; confinement to a single named transferee; a fixed or grossly under-value price; a perpetual or life-long duration; leaving only a narrow window (e.g., "except for religious purposes"). Indicators of a partial restraint: exclusion of one mode (gift); restriction to a class (family, co-sharers, qualified members); pre-emption at market value; a short period.

Decided cases. (1) Rosher v. Rosher (1884) 26 Ch D 801 — devise in fee to son with a proviso that if he wished to sell during the widow's life he must first offer the estate to her at £3,000 (value £15,000), and lettings beyond 3/7 years must be offered to her at £25/£35 (value £100+). Pearson J. treated the condition as "during the life of the widow you shall not sell": to compel a sale at one-fifth of value "is equivalent to a restraint upon selling at all"; a fine or additional rent on alienation could otherwise be pitched so as to prohibit it — "a circumvention of the law." Held absolute, void; letting conditions likewise. (2) Muhammad Raza v. Abbas Bandi Bibi (1932) 59 IA 236 — compromise decree making two wives "malik mustaqil" of a moiety each, "shall not have power to transfer this property to a stranger"; the second wife sold to strangers. The Privy Council (assuming an absolute estate) held the restriction "not absolute but partial; it forbids only alienation to strangers, leaving her free to make any transfer she pleases within the ambit of the family"; being a family arrangement (a contract as to the terms on which she took), it bound her; after the TPA "a partial restriction upon the power of disposition would not … be regarded as repugnant: see s.10"; English law agrees (In re Macleay; Doe d. Gill v. Pearson). Transferees got no title. (3) Zoroastrian Co-operative Housing Society v. District Registrar (2005) 5 SCC 632 — bye-law confining membership to Parsis and requiring a member to sell plots/flats only to Parsis with the Society's consent; High Court held it an absolute restraint hit by s.10. Supreme Court reversed: s.10 applies only to a restraint "imposed while the property is being transferred"; here the member inherited the plot with its limitations and voluntarily accepted the bye-laws and s.30 of the Gujarat Act — "there was really no transfer"; s.10 has no application to transfer of membership; in any event, the member could sell to anyone qualified to be a member — "at best, it is a partial restraint", and "such partial restraints are valid if imposed in a family settlement, partition or compromise of disputed claims" (Muhammad Raza; Gummanna Shetty AIR 1967 SC 1595). (4) Raghunath Prasad Singh (PC 1929) — absolute restraint in a will void (distinguished in Muhammad Raza).

Exceptions. Lease conditions for the lessor's benefit (s.10, s.12; s.108(j), s.111(g)); restraint on anticipation for a married woman not Hindu/Muslim/Buddhist.

Conclusion. The statement correctly states the law: s.10 voids only absolute restraints, tested by substance (Rosher); restraints confined to a class, mode or reasonable time, or accepted in family arrangements/partitions/co-operative compacts, are partial and valid (Muhammad Raza; Zoroastrian).

Q2. A devises his estate to his son S absolutely, providing that if S wishes to sell during the lifetime of A's widow W, he shall first offer it to W at Rs 10 lakh (market value Rs 50 lakh), and that S shall not let the house for more than three years without offering it to W at Rs 1,000 p.m. (market rent Rs 5,000 p.m.). S contracts to sell to P at Rs 50 lakh without offering to W. W sues. Decide.
Model answer (outline). Identify: absolute devise + conditions on sale and letting; law — s.10 TPA (by analogy; wills → s.138 Succession Act); test — substance; Rosher v. Rosher on all fours (one-fifth value; letting at a fraction of rental value); Pearson J.: equivalent to "during the life of the widow you shall not sell"; fine/additional rent = circumvention; letting is a mode of disposal; conclusion — both conditions void; S takes absolutely; W has no cause of action; P gets a good title. Contrast: if the option were at market value → partial, valid, W could enjoin a sale to P without first offer (and, P having notice, enforce against P under s.40 limb 2/SRA s.19(b)).
Q3. Distinguish between s.10 and s.11 of the TPA. Explain positive and negative covenants and state when a covenant restricting the use of land binds a subsequent purchaser. Refer to Tulk v. Moxhay.
Model answer (outline). (1) s.10 v. s.11 (Capsule): s.10 applies to any transfer (absolute or partial) and protects the power of alienation, voiding only absolute restraints; s.11 applies only to absolute transfers and protects the freedom of enjoyment, voiding any direction as to mode of enjoyment, save under para 2. Both leave the transfer intact. (2) Para 2 of s.11: direction on the transferred land for the beneficial enjoyment of the transferor's other immovable property is enforceable by him. (3) Covenants: negative (not to build/open windows/change user) — no expenditure; positive (repair drain, paint wall, maintain lawn) — financial burden; substance governs. (4) Running with land: at law burden does not run with freehold (Keppell, Austerberry); in equity Tulk v. Moxhay — facts (Leicester Square garden, 1808 covenant to keep open and unbuilt; Moxhay bought with notice, deed silent, intended to build); Lord Cottenham: question is not whether covenant runs but whether a purchaser with notice may use land inconsistently with his vendor's contract; otherwise a vendor could not sell part without rendering the rest worthless; inequitable to escape by resale; "no one purchasing with notice of that equity can stand in a different situation from the party from whom he purchased"; injunction upheld. Conditions: negative in substance; dominant land retained; notice. (5) s.40 TPA codifies: enforceable against a transferee with notice or a gratuitous transferee, not against a transferee for consideration without notice; illustration (Sultanpur contract); notice under s.3; remedies SRA ss.38–39, 19(b). (6) Example of the four covenants (Capsule).
Q4. "Section 10 of the Transfer of Property Act has no application to a restriction placed by the bye-laws of a co-operative housing society on a member's right to transfer his plot." Examine with reference to Zoroastrian Co-operative Housing Society v. District Registrar.
Model answer (outline). Facts (1926 Society; Bye-laws 7 and 21; land acquired 1928 under LA Act s.41; Respondent 2 inherited, sought to sell Plot 7 to a builders' association; authorities and High Court held bye-law opposed to public policy and hit by s.10). Issues. Arguments (Sorabjee — Art. 19(1)(c), Art. 29, partial restraint, Part III applies to State action; Bobde — s.4 public policy, s.10 TPA, s.14/s.24). Reasoning: s.24 open membership subject to bye-laws; public policy not to be judicially expanded (Gherulal Parakh); legislature's task; s.10 applies only to a restraint imposed in a transfer — here inheritance with limitations (s.31), voluntary acceptance of bye-laws and s.30; membership transfer outside s.10; even otherwise a partial restraint (transfer to anyone qualified) — valid like restraints in family settlements/partitions (Muhammad Raza; Gummanna Shetty); Art. 300-A not infringed. Result. Critical note (exclusionary housing; Art. 15(2) debate; Court's own call for legislation). Conclusion: statement correct as the ratio; but s.10 would still apply to a restraint inserted in a private sale deed.
Q5. Write short notes: (a) exceptions to s.10; (b) s.12 TPA; (c) "condition" and "limitation"; (d) family arrangement and restraint on alienation.
Points. (a) Lease — lessor's reversionary interest; s.108(j), 111(g), 114-A, rent-control eviction grounds; married woman not Hindu/Muslim/Buddhist — restraint on anticipation, only during marriage, historical reason (stridhan, Muslim wife's capacity), English abolition 1949. (b) Forfeiture on insolvency/attempted alienation void; interest "reserved or given"; lease exception; relation to ss.28, 30, 31, 32; insolvency law — property vests in assignee/RP; example. (c) Condition qualifies a defined estate; limitation marks its duration ("until", "so long as"); English determinable-fee loophole closed by "or limitation" in ss.10 and 12; condition precedent (s.26 substantial compliance) v. subsequent (s.29 strict). (d) Muhammad Raza — compromise is a contract as to terms of taking, not a conveyance; equity favours family arrangements; partial restraints therein binding; Zoroastrian para 42; Gummanna Shetty; Kale v. Q6. X sells the eastern half of his plot to Y by registered deed containing covenants that Y (i) shall not raise any structure within 5 feet of the common boundary, (ii) shall not use the land for any trade, and (iii) shall maintain the common drain. Y sells to Z, whose deed is silent. Z opens a workshop, builds up to the boundary and lets the drain choke. Advise X.
Model answer. Covenants (i) and (ii) are negative and were imposed for the beneficial enjoyment of X's retained western half — valid under s.11 para 2. Z is a transferee with constructive notice (the registered deed — s.3 Expl. I; Registration Act s.17), so under s.40 limb 1 and Tulk v. Moxhay X may enforce them against Z by perpetual injunction (SRA s.38) and a mandatory injunction to demolish the encroaching structure (s.39). Covenant (iii) is positive (expenditure); its burden does not run — X's remedy is in damages against Y (Contract Act s.73), unless the drain right can be framed as an easement of necessity/quasi-easement under the Easements Act (s.13) or Z's act amounts to nuisance. Had Z been a purchaser for value without notice (impossible here given registration), he would not be bound (s.40 last clause).

17. Mnemonics & rapid revision

"ALL or a LOT is ABSOLUTE; a SLICE is PARTIAL." — All modes / all persons but one / life-long / fixed low price → absolute (void). One mode, one class excluded, a class permitted, a short time, market-price pre-emption → partial (valid).
"LM" — the two s.10 exceptions: Lease (for lessor's benefit) · Married woman (not Hindu/Muslim/Buddhist).
"10 = SELL, 11 = USE, 12 = LOSE." — s.10 restrains selling; s.11 dictates use; s.12 forfeits on insolvency/attempted sale.
"NO-DN" for Tulk/s.40: covenant must be Negative · for the benefit of Owner's retained land · binds transferee with Donation (gratuitous) or Notice.
Cases in one breath: Rosher — one-fifth price = "shall not sell" · Raza — "not to a stranger" = partial, family arrangement · Zoroastrian — no transfer, self-imposed, class = partial · Tulk — Leicester Square, notice binds.
Ten lines to remember
  1. Free alienability is an incident of ownership; a condition repugnant to it is void — s.10 (transfers), s.138 ISA (wills).
  2. s.10 voids only absolute restraints (judged by substance) on alienation; only the condition is void, the transfer stands.
  3. Exceptions: lease conditions for the lessor's benefit; restraint on anticipation for non-Hindu/Muslim/Buddhist married women.
  4. Rosher: pre-emption at one-fifth value = prohibition; fine/extra rent = circumvention; letting covered.
  5. Muhammad Raza: "not to a stranger" = partial, valid; family arrangement = contract, favoured by equity; s.10 declaratory of justice, equity, good conscience.
  6. Zoroastrian: s.10 needs a transfer imposing the restraint; inheritance + bye-laws + s.30 = self-imposed, partial; membership outside s.10; public policy not judicially expanded.
  7. s.12: forfeiture on insolvency/attempted alienation void (lease excepted); s.31 subject to s.12.
  8. s.11: any direction as to enjoyment of an absolutely transferred interest is void — except a direction for the beneficial enjoyment of the transferor's other immovable property (para 2).
  9. Negative covenant for retained land binds purchasers with notice and volunteers, not BFP without notice — Tulk v. Moxhay; s.40; positive covenants bind only the covenantor.
  10. Related: ss.25–32 (conditions precedent/subsequent), s.3 notice, s.54, SRA ss.19(b), 38–39, Easements Act, HSA s.22, Co-operative Societies Acts, Art. 300-A.
