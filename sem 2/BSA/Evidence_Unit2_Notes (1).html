Relevancy and Admissibility of Facts

Method

Each limb of the syllabus is treated with: statutory text (BSA, with IEA original in brackets), ingredients, illustrations, case law exactly as printed in the DU case material (facts → issues → arguments → decision → principle), allied enactments at the point of use (IT Act 2000, BNSS 2023, BNS 2023, CPC 1908, CrPC 1973, Identification of Prisoners Act 1920), real examples and a diagram. Cases starred (*) are listed in the DU material but not printed in the case-book; they are summarised from the reported judgments and flagged as such.

(a)(i)Logically Relevant Facts — ss.3–7 & 9 BSA (ss.5–9 & 11 IEA)

1. The master gate: s.3 BSA [s.5 IEA] — "Evidence may be given of facts in issue and relevant facts… and of no others"

s.3 BSA (verbatim)

"Evidence may be given in any suit or proceeding of the existence or non-existence of every fact in issue and of such other facts as are hereinafter declared to be relevant, and of no others. Explanation.—This section shall not enable any person to give evidence of a fact which he is disentitled to prove by any provision of the law for the time being in force relating to civil procedure."

  • Ingredients: (1) evidence confined to facts in issue + declared-relevant facts; (2) "and of no others" imposes a duty on the court to exclude irrelevant material even without objection; (3) the Explanation subordinates the section to CPC pleading rules (a fact unpleaded cannot be proved — e.g., a bond not produced at first hearing per illustration (b); new plea barred, Order VI–VIII CPC).
  • Object: to confine the judicial re-enactment (Unit I) within "the bounds prescribed by general convenience" — trials must end, and end on legally material facts.
  • Constitutional gloss: irrelevant or unlawfully prejudicial material violates fair trial (Art. 21); the section is the statutory expression of that discipline.

2. s.4 BSA [s.6 IEA] — Res gestae: facts forming part of the same transaction

"Facts which, though not in issue, are so connected with a fact in issue or a relevant fact as to form part of the same transaction, are relevant, whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places." Illustrations: (a) words of A, B or bystanders at the beating; (b) acts of an armed insurrection though A absent; (c) letters forming the correspondence out of which a libel arose; (d) successive deliveries of goods.

  • Ingredients: (1) a fact in issue (the principal transaction); (2) accompanying facts so connected as to be part of it; (3) spontaneity/proximity — not necessarily same time/place, but no interval for fabrication; (4) it is a hearsay exception: statements of victim/bystanders otherwise inadmissible become admissible.
  • Why: a transaction cannot be sliced from its immediate accompaniments — the cries, blows and words are the event itself.
  • Tests in case-law (supplementary): the statement must be substantially contemporaneous and spontaneous; later narrations fail (Rattan Singh v. State of H.P. (1997) — statement made before occurrence to a distant person not res gestae; Sawal Das v. State of Bihar — child's delayed statement excluded — both supplementary, not in DU material).
Real example

A murders B in a market; bystander C shouts "A is stabbing B!" and victim B cries "save me, A has attacked me". Both cries are relevant under s.4 BSA [s.6 IEA] although C and B (dead) are not cross-examined on them — they form part of the transaction of murder [s.101/103 BNS; s.300/302 IPC]. Contrast: C's police-station narration two hours later is not res gestae (hearsay).

3. s.5 BSA [s.7 IEA] — Occasion, cause, effect, state of things, opportunity

"Facts which are the occasion, cause or effect, immediate or otherwise, of relevant facts, or facts in issue, or which constitute the state of things under which they happened, or which afforded an opportunity for their occurrence or transaction, are relevant." Illustrations: (a) B going to fair with money, showing it; (b) marks on the ground produced by a struggle at the murder spot; (c) B's health and habits known to A affording opportunity to poison.

  • Ingredients: backward-looking (cause/occasion) and forward-looking (effect) connections, plus the ambient state of things and opportunity.
  • The "last-seen" species: that the accused and victim were last seen together is a cause/occasion/effect fact — strong where the time-gap is minimal (see Dharam Deo Yadav* and Mukesh* briefs); weak where the gap allows other possibilities.
Real example

In a poisoning case [s.101 BNS/s.300 IPC "by poison"], B's prior health (sound), A's knowledge of B's meal habits, and A's procurement of poison are s.5 facts (state of things + opportunity); B's post-meal symptoms are the effect. Together they build the inferential chain.

4. s.6 BSA [s.8 IEA] — Motive, preparation, conduct (previous & subsequent)

"(1) Any fact is relevant which shows or constitutes a motive or preparation… (2) The conduct of any party… if such conduct influences or is influenced by any fact in issue or relevant fact, and whether it was previous or subsequent thereto." Explanation 1: "conduct" does not include statements unless they accompany and explain acts; Explanation 2: statements made to a person which affect his conduct are relevant. Illustrations (f): "the police are coming" + A runs away; (i): absconding, possession of crime-proceeds, concealment; (j)/(k): complaint after rape/robbery relevant, bare statement not (but may be s.26(1) dying declaration or s.160 corroborative).

  • Ingredients: motive (the why), preparation (the gearing-up), conduct (pre/post) — all relevant if they influence/are influenced by the fact in issue.
  • Statements-as-conduct discipline: a bare assertion "I was raped" is not conduct-evidence; a complaint (act of complaining + its terms) is — Explanation 1 prevents smuggling hearsay in as conduct.
Real example

Insurance-motive murder: A takes a huge life-policy on B (motive), buys cyanide (preparation), flees after the funeral with the policy (subsequent conduct) — all s.6 facts in A's trial for murder [s.302 IPC/s.103(1) BNS].

5. s.7 BSA [s.9 IEA] — Explanatory facts, identity, time & place, relation of parties

"Facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact, or which support or rebut an inference suggested…, or which establish the identity of anything, or person whose identity is relevant, or fix the time or place…, or which show the relation of parties…, are relevant in so far as they are necessary for that purpose." Illustration (d): "I am leaving you because B has made me a better offer" — explanatory of C's conduct (the very fact litigated in DU 2024 Q1(a)!).

  • Identity species — Test Identification Parade (TIP): identity of the offender being relevant, first identification of a stranger by witnesses in a parade is a s.7 fact; it is not substantive evidence but corroborative of courtroom identification; governed procedurally by s.54A CrPC (BNSS successor) and practice under the Identification of Prisoners Act, 1920; delay/unexplained exposure → weight drops.
  • Time/place species: CCTV timestamps, cell-tower pings fix time/place.

6. s.9 BSA [s.11 IEA] — Facts inconsistent; highly probable/improbable (the alibi section)

"Facts not otherwise relevant are relevant— (1) if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact; (2) if by themselves or in connection with other facts they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable." Illustrations: (a) A at Ladakh on the crime-day at Chennai; (b) closed-circle elimination (crime by A, B, C or D; facts excluding B, C, D relevant).

  • Plea of alibi: not a "defence" in the substantive sense but a denial by inconsistency — the accused asserts a fact (presence elsewhere) inconsistent with the fact in issue (his presence at the crime). Burden of establishing guilt never shifts; alibi evidence merely renders the prosecution case improbable; but the accused must raise it and lay a factual foundation.
  • Du case law: Jayantibhai Bhenkarbhai v. State of Gujarat (2002) 8 SCC 165 and Mukesh v. State (NCT of Delhi)* — briefed below; also Dudh Nath Pandey v. State of U.P. (supplementary): alibi must be proved to show it was impossible for the accused to be at the scene; mere probability insufficient.

(a)(ii)Special Class of Relevant Facts: Conspiracy — s.8 BSA [s.10 IEA]

s.8 BSA (verbatim)

"Where there is reasonable ground to believe that two or more persons have conspired together to commit an offence or an actionable wrong, anything said, done or written by any one of such persons in reference to their common intention, after the time when such intention was first entertained by any one of them, is a relevant fact as against each of the persons believed to be so conspiring, as well for the purpose of proving the existence of the conspiracy as for the purpose of showing that any such person was a party to it." Illustration: arms procured in Europe, money collected in Kolkata, writings at Agra, transfers Delhi→Singapore, letter narrating the conspiracy — each relevant against A though ignorant of them, strangers to him, and before he joined or after he left.

1. Ingredients & mechanics

  1. Condition precedent: the court must have reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy exists (prima-facie foundation, usually from independent evidence) — only then does s.8 switch on.
  2. Agency principle: each conspirator is the agent of the others in carrying out the common design (s.61(2) BNS [s.120B IPC] partnership in crime) — hence the act/statement of one binds all.
  3. Temporal window: from the time the intention was first entertained until the conspiracy ends (Mirza Akbar): things said/done while the conspiracy is on foot and in reference to the common intention.
  4. Double purpose: (a) prove the conspiracy itself; (b) rope in each member.
  5. Exclusions (Mirza Akbar): narratives, confessions or statements to third parties after completion or after arrest — the common intention is then in the past; such matter is admissible only against its maker as an admission (ss.15–16 BSA).

Case Brief No. 3 — Mirza Akbar v. Emperor (DU Case Material)

AIR 1940 PC 176 · Privy Council · Lord Wright

Facts
  • Ali Askar murdered on 23-8-1938 at Taus Banda by Umar Sher (caught running with a freshly discharged single-barrel shot gun; empty cartridge jammed in barrel).
  • Prosecution case: Umar Sher was a hired assassin; the conspiracy was between the appellant Mirza Akbar and the victim's wife Mst. Mehr Taja, who wished to marry each other.
  • Three love-letters (P.A. and P.B. by the woman; P.D. by Akbar) spoke of "getting rid of" Ali Askar, finding "money for the man", and marrying after "Amir Jan wailing over Ali Askar" — held only consistent with a murder-conspiracy.
  • When Umar Sher was caught, Akbar (coming from his field half a mile away) insisted Umar Sher was innocent and should be released — conduct relied upon.
  • Impugned evidence: a statement made by Mst. Mehr Taja before the Examining Magistrate after her arrest, in Akbar's absence, admitted against Akbar under s.10 IEA.
Issues
  1. (Jurisdiction) Whether a single Judge of the Judicial Commissioner's Court could confirm the death sentence when the acting judge was disqualified (held: yes, under Rule 3 — not material for evidence course).
  2. (Evidence) Whether Mehr Taja's post-arrest statement was admissible against Akbar under s.10 IEA.
Arguments
  • Appellant: the statement, made after arrest and in his absence, was outside s.10; its reception vitiated the trial.
  • Crown: s.10 applies to any conspirator's statement referring to the common intention; alternatively, the error caused no failure of justice.
Decision & Reasoning (Lord Wright)
  • s.10 embodies the English common-law rule in R. v. Blake (1844) 6 QB 126: admissible = things done/written/spoken in carrying out the conspiracy (a declaration accompanying an act, or words which are themselves acts in the course of it); inadmissible = a mere narrative/confession to a third party after the conspiracy is completed ("the common intention is in the past").
  • The words "in reference to their common intention" signify an intention existing at the time the thing was said/done/written. Statements after arrest or after the conspiracy ended are wrongly admitted against the co-conspirator.
  • Hence Mehr Taja's post-arrest statement was wrongly admitted — but it in fact denied the conspiracy, and even excluding it the letters + Akbar's conduct at the arrest scene sufficed; applying Pakala Narayana Swami, no failure of justice. Appeal dismissed; conviction (death) confirmed.
Principle evolved

The Mirza Akbar rule: s.10 IEA / s.8 BSA admits, against co-conspirators, only acts/words during the pendency of the common design and in reference to it; post-completion narratives bind only the maker (as admissions). The condition precedent is reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy exists.

Case Brief No. 4 — Badri Rai v. State of Bihar (DU Case Material)

1959 SCR 1141 : AIR 1958 SC 953 · Supreme Court · Sinha J.

Facts
  • Inspector (informant) seized ornaments/molten silver being melted by six strangers with implements supplied by Ramji Sonar (goldsmith) — suspected stolen property (22-8-1953); Ramji bailed same day.
  • 24-8-1953: both appellants (Ramji + Badri, a school-runner) accosted the Inspector on the road, proposing to hush up the case for a consideration — evidence of conspiracy to bribe [s.120B r/w s.165-A IPC; today s.61(2) BNS + BNS bribery provisions].
  • 31-8-1953: Badri came to the thana and offered Rs 500 in a newspaper packet, stating Ramji had sent it per the 24-8 talk, to hush up the case; several persons present; FIR and seizure followed.
  • Both convicted u/s 120B r/w 165-A IPC; Badri also u/s 165-A.
Issues
  1. Whether Badri's 31-8 statement ("Ramji sent me with the bribe") was admissible against Ramji under s.10 IEA.
  2. Whether the 120B charge was a device to attract s.10.
  3. Whether, the money having been tendered, the statement was "post-completion" (Mirza Akbar).
Arguments (appellant Ramji)
  • The statement was made after the act of offering and so outside s.10 (Mirza Akbar); the 120B charge was deliberately added to make it admissible.
Decision & Reasoning
  • The 24-8 incident gave reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy to bribe existed → s.10 attracted; the charge under 120B was properly framed, and s.10 applies regardless of charging tactics ("deliberately enacted… because of the nature of the crime — hatched in secrecy, executed in darkness"; agency principle).
  • Distinguishing Mirza Akbar: the statement accompanied the act of payment — it was part of the same transaction; and the object of the conspiracy (hushing up the case) was far from accomplished when made. Hence within s.10 and admissible against both.
  • Appeal dismissed; convictions upheld.
Principle evolved

(1) A statement made in the course of and accompanying an act done for the conspiracy binds co-conspirators; (2) the window closes only when the object of the conspiracy is accomplished, not when one step (e.g., tendering money) is done; (3) once reasonable ground exists, s.10 is not defeated by the plea that the conspiracy charge was "added".

Allied provisions at this point
  • BNS s.61(2) [IPC s.120B] — conspiracy definition/punishment: the substantive twin of s.8 BSA; BNS s.3(5) [IPC s.34] common intention.
  • BNSS — joinder of conspirators in one trial (successor of s.220 CrPC), enabling s.24 BSA-style use of confessions and s.8 evidence in a common trial.
  • IT Act / electronic conspiracy: chats/e-mails between conspirators are "things written" under s.8 — admissible as electronic records (ss.2(1)(d), 61–63 BSA) with s.8's temporal limits.

Case Brief No. 5 — Mohd. Khalid v. State of W.B. (DU Case Material)

(2002) 7 SCC 334 · Supreme Court · Arijit Pasayat J. · (Calcutta B.B. Ganguly Street blasts, 16-3-1993)

Facts
  • Thundering explosions on 16-3-1993 near midnight at 267/266/268-A B.B. Ganguly Street, Bow Bazar, Calcutta: one building demolished, two partially; 69 dead, many injured; nitroglycerine explosives; further explosion on 18-3-1993; 22 live bombs recovered.
  • FIR u/s 120B, 436, 302, 307, 326 IPC [now ss.61(2), 436-type mischief by explosive fire, 103(1), 109, 118-range BNS] + ss.3/5 Explosive Substances Act, 1908; later TADA ss.3(2)(1), 3(3).
  • Prosecution: conspiracy to manufacture bombs to strike terror and destroy communal harmony post-Bombay riots; star witnesses PWs 40, 67, 68 saw bomb-making at the "satta gali" premises and heard Rashid direct mass bomb-preparation; judicial confessions of Pannalal Jaysoara and Mohd. Gulzar before Magistrates (later retracted 3-2-1995); TIP identification of Pannalal.
Issues
  1. Reliability of star witnesses (rowdy antecedents; 2-day delay in recording; non-examination of Nausad/Osman).
  2. Whether the retracted judicial confessions could be used, and against co-accused (s.30 IEA / s.24 BSA).
  3. Whether "self-protection" negatived terrorist intent (TADA).
Arguments (defence)
  • Witnesses police-tutored; delay fatal; confessions coerced & retracted; confessions of co-accused not substantive evidence (s.30 requires joint trial + other credible evidence first); bomb-making was private-defence preparation ("shield not weapon"); explosion accidental.
Decision & Reasoning
  • Witnesses upheld: their presence in the satta gali natural; delay explained by rescue chaos (first priority = lives under debris); non-examination of some witnesses not fatal — "it is not the quantity but the quality of the evidence that is important"; prosecution may pick and choose material witnesses.
  • Voluntariness of confession: s.24 IEA [s.22 BSA] interdicts only induced/threatened/confession; confession must be voluntary — "the outcome of his own free will inspired by the sound of his own conscience" (Ibrahim v. R. per Lord Sumner); retraction an afterthought.
  • s.30 IEA [s.24 BSA] mechanics (locus classicus Bhuboni Sahu quoted): co-accused's confession is "evidence of a very weak type"; not "evidence" u/s 3; cannot be the foundation of conviction — only lends assurance to other evidence; proper approach: first marshal evidence excluding the confession; if independently believable, conviction may stand; if the judge is not prepared to act, the confession may be called in aid (Kashmira Singh; Haricharan Kurmi).
  • Conspiracy angle: "where trustworthy evidence establishing all links of circumstantial evidence is available the confession of a co-accused as to conspiracy even without corroborative evidence can be taken into consideration"; conspiracy inferable from acts/conduct; explosion of live bombs a clear indication of conspiracy (s.10 IEA / s.8 BSA zone).
  • TADA intent: preparation/storage of bombs per se illegal; "cover of self-protection when pierced unravels a sinister design to unleash terror"; no reasonable apprehension of attack → no private defence.
  • Convictions under 120B IPC, TADA 3(2)(1)/3(3), Explosive Act upheld; acquittals u/s 302/34, 436/34 IPC not disturbed; appeals dismissed. (Obiter: deprecates deferred cross-examination — s.309 CrPC spirit.)
Principles evolved
  1. Confession = voluntary or nothing (s.22 BSA/s.24 IEA test).
  2. Co-accused's confession: weak type; usable only in aid of other evidence, on joint trial (Bhuboni Sahu/Kashmira Singh restated).
  3. Conspiracy proved by circumstantial links + s.8/s.10 material; confessions of conspirators as to on-foot conspiracy reinforce it.
  4. Quality over quantity of witnesses; explained delay not fatal.

Case Brief No. 6 — Jayantibhai Bhenkarbhai v. State of Gujarat (DU Case Material)

(2002) 8 SCC 165 · Supreme Court · R.C. Lahoti & Brijesh Kumar JJ. · (the alibi locus classicus)

Facts
  • 6-7-1989, ~8 p.m., Village Singpur (Songadh, Gujarat): Lalubhai assaulted by an armed mob led by A-1 Singha Magan after a liquor-demand altercation; died; 22 injuries (incised + blunt); FIR 6 a.m. next day at Ukai PS (10–12 km).
  • Five convicted u/s 302/149, 147/148/452 IPC [murder + unlawful assembly: ss.103(1)/109, 189–193, 452-range BNS]; only A-9 Jayantibhai appealed.
  • Defence of alibi: on 6-7-1989 he attended an election-appeal hearing before the Additional Development Commissioner, Gandhinagar (11 a.m.–2.30 p.m.; signed attendance + Secretariat visitors' register ~5 p.m.; zoo tickets at Ahmedabad 4 p.m.; midnight bus to Songadh reaching only on 7-7-1989 morning); supported by advocate DW3, clerk DW5, documents of "unimpeachable veracity"; he raised the plea before the IO on 8-7-1989 itself.
Issues
  1. Whether the alibi stood proved enough to exclude his presence at 8 p.m. in Singpur (~300 km away).
  2. Whether prosecution evidence (4 eyewitnesses) specifically implicated him (overt act, recovery).
Arguments
  • Prosecution/HC: alibi not proved to the hilt; he did not inform police on 7-7 itself; Dineshbhai/Rahulbhai not examined; witnesses prove participation.
  • Defence: documentary alibi (hearing record, register, postcard, tickets, bus-tickets) makes presence at Singpur by 8 p.m. highly improbable (8–10 hr bus journey); no specific overt act attributed; no recovery from him.
Decision & Reasoning
  • s.11 IEA [s.9 BSA] alibi doctrine (paras 18–19): alibi "flows from Section 11" (Illustration (a)); it is not an IPC exception but a rule of evidence (inconsistent fact); burden never leaves the prosecution; alibi considered only where prosecution has discharged its burden; then the accused must prove it with certainty so as to exclude the possibility of his presence (s.103 IEA burden of particular fact); the court weighs both scales — if the defence evidence creates a reasonable doubt as to presence, the prosecution fails.
  • On facts: attendance at Gandhinagar till after midday + 300 km/8–10 hr journey ⇒ his reaching Singpur by 8 p.m. highly improbable; non-examination of two witnesses "pales into insignificance" against documentary alibi; eyewitnesses not specific about his overt act; FIR milieu (political rival present) leaves a slot for doubt.
  • Appeal allowed; A-9 acquitted (benefit of doubt). Note: with A-9 out, the five-member s.149 IPC [s.3(2) BNS] count fell below five, but non-appealing co-accursed left undisturbed (s.34 IPC [s.3(5) BNS] would sustain).
Principles evolved
  1. Alibi = s.11 IEA / s.9 BSA relevance by inconsistency; burden structure: prosecution first, then accused to exclude possibility of presence (s.103/s.106 lineage).
  2. Alibi need not be "perfect"; it succeeds if it raises a reasonable doubt that fits the slot in the prosecution case.
  3. Documentary alibi (official registers, hearing records, tickets) outweighs oral gaps.
Real example tying ss.3–9 & 11 BSA together

Train-blast case: (s.4) bystander's scream at the platform; (s.5) struggle-marks & burnt residue; (s.6) accused's prior purchase of detonators + absconding; (s.7) TIP + CCTV timestamp fixing identity/time; (s.8) WhatsApp plan between conspirators during the on-foot conspiracy (Mirza Akbar window); (s.9) his attendance register showing him 900 km away (Jayantibhai alibi). One case, every limb of (a)(i)–(ii). Offences: s.103(1) BNS murder [s.302 IPC], s.61(2) BNS conspiracy [s.120B], Explosive Substances Act ss.3/5.

(b)(i)Admissions — ss.15–21 & 25 BSA [ss.17–23 & 31 IEA]

s.15 BSA (definition)

"An admission is a statement, oral or documentary or contained in electronic form, which suggests any inference as to any fact in issue or relevant fact, and which is made by any of the persons, and under the circumstances, hereinafter mentioned."

1. Ingredients

  1. Statement — oral, documentary, or electronic (BSA's express inclusion; diary entries, e-mails, WhatsApp texts qualify — see V.C. Shukla & Bhogilal Chunilal: even an uncommunicated entry is a "statement").
  2. It must suggest an inference as to a fact in issue or relevant fact.
  3. Made by a person in ss.16–18 BSA [18–20 IEA]: (a) party or his authorised agent; (b) parties in representative character (only while holding it); (c) persons with proprietary/pecuniary interest (during continuance); (d) predecessors-in-interest; (e) persons whose position/liability must be proved (s.17); (f) persons expressly referred to for information (s.18 — "go and ask C").

2. Proof & effect

  • s.19 BSA [s.21 IEA]: provable against the maker; not by the maker, save three exceptions: (1) would be relevant u/s 26 BSA [s.32 IEA] if maker dead; (2) contemporaneous statement of state of mind/body + conduct rendering falsehood improbable; (3) relevant otherwise than as admission.
  • s.20 BSA [s.22 IEA]: oral admission of contents of a document irrelevant until secondary evidence is permissible, or genuineness in question.
  • s.21 BSA [s.23 IEA]: civil admissions made "without prejudice"/on condition of non-proof — barred.
  • s.25 BSA [s.31 IEA]: admissions are not conclusive proof but may operate as estoppel (ss.121–123 BSA) — the bridge to substantive effect.
  • Substantive evidence: an admission is substantive evidence of the fact admitted; it is not necessary to confront the maker in the witness-box (unlike s.145 IEA/s.149-range BSA prior statements used to discredit a witness) — Bishwanath Prasad.
  • Whole-statement rule: an admission must be taken as a whole (subject to the "relatable to distinct fact" severance seen in the confession-discovery context, Bodhraj).

Case Brief No. 7 — Bishwanath Prasad v. Dwarka Prasad (DU Case Material)

(1974) 1 SCC 78 : AIR 1974 SC 117 · V.R. Krishna Iyer J. · (partition suit; admissions in earlier suit)

Facts
  • Suit for partition of joint-family properties. Two clusters in dispute: (i) Schedule-C items covered by four usufructuary mortgages; (ii) a shop-room at plot 1238.
  • Both courts below held them to be the first defendant's exclusive property, relying on admissions by the first plaintiff (deposition Ex.G) and the eighth defendant (deposition Ex.G2 and written statement Ex.H) made in an earlier suit (T.S. 61 of 1945) that the properties had been allotted to the defendant's branch in a 1938 partition.
Issues / Arguments (appellants)
  • Admissions vague; the "admissions-case" was a new case not pleaded; the admissions were never put to the first plaintiff in cross-examination, nor was the eighth defendant (who kept away from the box) summoned to explain — hence reliance prejudiced them (s.145 IEA invoked).
Decision & Reasoning
  • "Admissions are usually telling against the maker unless reasonably explained"; no explanation offered; concurrent findings not disturbed.
  • Cardinal distinction (quoting Bharat Singh, AIR 1966 SC 405): (a) admission by a party = substantive evidence proprio vigore if it fulfils ss.17/21 IEA — no requirement of putting it to the party, whether or not he enters the box; (b) prior statement used to discredit a witness under s.145 IEA — must be put to him, and it is not substantive evidence.
  • No prejudice pleaded at earlier stages; plea of surprise rejected. Appeal dismissed (half costs).
Principle evolved

An admission, once proved, is substantive evidence against its maker irrespective of confrontation; s.145-style contradiction is a different, credibility-only, device. Judicial admissions in earlier pleadings/depositions bind in later litigation unless explained.

Case Brief No. 8 — Central Bureau of Investigation v. V.C. Shukla (DU Case Material)

AIR 1998 SC 1406 · (Hawala/Jain-diaries case) · M.K. Mukherjee J.

Facts
  • CBI search (3-5-1991) at J.K. Jain's Saket premises recovered diaries, notebooks, files with coded entries of receipts (hawala, Rs 53.5 cr from abroad) and payments to 115 persons incl. MPs/ministers (1988–91).
  • Charge-sheets: V.C. Shukla (Rs 38.85 l) and L.K. Advani (Rs 25 l) with the Jain brothers for s.120B IPC [s.61(2) BNS] + ss.7, 12, 13(1)(d)/13(2) Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 [today the PC (Amendment) Act 2018 version; BNS bribery ss.7–8 lineage].
  • Special Judge proceeded to frame charges; High Court u/s 482 CrPC quashed/discharged; CBI appealed. The entire edifice of the case = the diary entries.
Issues
  1. Admissibility of entries under s.34 IEA (books of account); s.10 IEA (conspiracy); ss.17/21 IEA (admissions) — and against whom.
  2. Whether prima-facie material justified charges.
Arguments
  • CBI: entries regularly kept business accounts (s.34); acts/writings of conspirators (s.10 — Sardul Singh/Bhagwan Swarup five-point test); admissions of the Jains provable u/s 21 (Bhogilal Chunilal — uncommunicated account-book entries are statements).
  • Respondents: not regular books of account; s.34 at best corroborative and there was no independent evidence; no iota of prima-facie conspiracy for s.10; no admission as to the respondents.
Decision & Reasoning
  • s.34: entries not books of account in regular course of business; and s.34 evidence is corroborative only — no independent proof of payments ⇒ no avail.
  • s.10: the condition precedent failed — witness statements only showed formal acquaintance; "no one has even spoke about" Advani ⇒ no reasonable ground to believe in a conspiracy between Jains and Shukla/Advani; entries therefore not receivable u/s 10.
  • ss.17/21: entries may be admissions of the Jains (Bhogilal Chunilal approved) but, being admissions and not confessions, cannot be used against Shukla or Advani (Monir's passage: a statement short of confession is admissible only against its maker; s.30 IEA requires a confession + joint trial).
  • And as against the Jains too, the very conspiracy charged (Jains + Shukla/Advani) collapsed for want of the other party ⇒ admissions of that conspiracy unprovable; appeals dismissed; discharge upheld (question whether MPs are "public servants" left open).
  • Confession law restated: Pakala Narayana Swami — confession must admit the offence or substantially all its facts; a conclusively incriminating admission (knife/revolver possession) is not a confession; Stephen's Digest definition rejected; followed in Palvinder Kaur, Om Parkash, Veera Ibrahim.
Principles evolved
  1. Diary/account entries = admissions against maker only, and only if they touch a fact in issue/relevant fact delineated by the charges.
  2. s.10 cannot be boot-strapped: reasonable ground of conspiracy must pre-exist the entries.
  3. Admission ≠ confession; only confessions travel to co-accused (s.24 BSA/s.30 IEA).
  4. s.34 is corroborative, never foundational.
Allied provisions at this point
  • CPC: s.58 (facts admitted need not be proved); Order VIII r.6 (admissions in written statement); Order XII (admission of case and documents — summary judgment on admissions); Order XXVI? (none) — admissions in pleadings are judicial admissions (conclusive unless withdrawn, per Nagindas Ramdas lineage — supplementary).
  • BNSS/CrPC: s.313 CrPC (accused's statement — usable, not on oath); s.27 BNSS-range disclosures (see confessions).
  • IT Act: electronic "statements" as admissions — BSA s.15's express "contained in electronic form".

(b)(ii)Confessions — ss.22–24 BSA [ss.24–30 IEA]

The four-provision architecture
  • s.22 BSA [s.24 IEA] — confession caused by inducement, threat, coercion or promise from a person in authority, relating to the charge, sufficient to give reasonable grounds for supposing temporal advantage/avoidance of evil → irrelevant; Proviso 1: admissible once the impression is fully removed; Proviso 2: not irrelevant merely because of promise of secrecy, deception, drunkenness, answers to questions he need not have answered, or absence of warning.
  • s.23(1) BSA [s.25 IEA] — No confession to a police officer shall be proved against an accused (absolute ban, any circumstance, even before investigation — Aghnoo Nagesia).
  • s.23(2) BSA [s.26 IEA] — confession while in police custody (to anyone) barred unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate.
  • s.23(2) proviso BSA [s.27 IEA] — discovery exception: so much of the information from an accused in custody as relates distinctly to the fact discovered may be proved.
  • s.24 BSA [s.30 IEA] — proved confession of one accused affecting himself and a co-accused in joint trial may be taken into consideration against the co-accused too (Explanation II: trial in absence of absconder/proclaimed person deemed joint; cf. BNSS s.84 proclamation).

1. What is a confession? (judge-made definition)

  • Not defined in either Act. Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor AIR 1939 PC 47 (approved in Palvinder Kaur, Om Parkash, Veera Ibrahim, V.C. Shukla): "A confession must either admit in terms the offence, or at any rate substantially all the facts which constitute the offence. An admission of a gravely incriminating fact, even a conclusively incriminating fact, is not of itself a confession" (knife/revolver possession example); Stephen's Digest definition rejected.
  • Veera Ibrahim: a statement containing self-exculpatory matter which, if true, negatives the offence cannot be a confession.
  • Aghnoo Nagesia: a confession may reveal motive, preparation, opportunity, weapon, concealment, subsequent conduct — every admission of an incriminating fact inside a confessional statement partakes of the character of confession; taint attaches to each part; no surgical severance into "non-confessional" bits (save s.27).
  • Confession is a species of admission (ss.24–30 IEA sit within "Admissions", ss.17–31); hence a statement short of confession remains usable against its maker as an admission u/s 19/s.21 (Veera Ibrahim: Ex.I admissible as admission; V.C. Shukla/Monir).

2. The voluntariness gate (s.22 BSA / s.24 IEA) — the Veera Ibrahim six facts

  1. the statement is a confession; (ii) by an accused; (iii) to a person in authority; (iv) caused by inducement/threat/promise proceeding from that person; (v) having reference to the charge; (vi) sufficient, in the court's opinion, to give reasonable grounds for supposing temporal advantage or avoidance of evil.

Statutory compulsion to speak truth (e.g., s.108 Customs Act) emanates from law, not from a person in authority; a perjury warning is not a "threat" within the section (Veera Ibrahim). Art. 20(3) applies only where a formal accusation exists (FIR/complaint stage — R.C. Mehta approved).

3. The police ban and the discovery valve

  • s.23(1)/s.25: absolute; covers a confessional FIR (Aghnoo Nagesia: whole FIR excluded except identity parts 1, 15, 18 + s.27 portions).
  • s.23(2)/s.26: custody includes constructive custody (assumed in Aghnoo); the Magistrate-presence route = s.164 CrPC / BNSS s.183 safeguards (warning, voluntariness, time for reflection).
  • s.27 — Kottaya canon: "fact discovered" ≠ object produced; it embraces the place of concealment and the accused's knowledge of it; information as to past user/history ("with which I stabbed A") is inadmissible; the discovery is "one link in the chain of proof; the other links must be forged in manner allowed by law"; the guarantee = the discovery vouches for the truth of the information.
  • Evidentiary yield of a valid s.27 statement: proof of the accused's knowledge of concealment + the discovery itself — a circumstance of consciousness of guilt, not a confession of the offence (Pulukuri Kottaya; Bodhraj).

Case Brief No. 9 — Veera Ibrahim v. State of Maharashtra (DU Case Material)

(1976) 2 SCC 302 : AIR 1976 SC 1167 · R.S. Sarkaria J. · (Customs Act s.108 statement)

Facts
  • Appellant (accused No.2) prosecuted on Assistant Collector of Customs' complaint for s.135(a)/(b) Customs Act, 1962 + s.5 Imports & Exports (Control) Act, 1947; convicted on 135(a); HC partly acquitted but maintained 135(a).
  • His statement Ex.I recorded by Inspector of Customs u/s 108 Customs Act was the footing of conviction; packages of contraband seized after truck skidded near Dongri PS; Rs 2,000 cash recovered.
Issues / Arguments
  • (a) Ex.I violated Art. 20(3) (he was "accused" — earlier police arrest u/s 124 Bombay Police Act); (b) Ex.I hit by s.24 IEA (compulsion of law + perjury warning = threat by person in authority).
Decision & Reasoning
  • Art. 20(3): "accused of an offence" = person against whom a formal accusation (FIR/complaint) is levelled (R.C. Mehta); a customs enquiry is not an accusation; accusation arose only at the complaint stage ⇒ no testimonial-compulsion breach.
  • s.24 IEA six-fact test (set out verbatim in judgment); failed on (i) — Ex.I was not a confession (contained exculpatory matter — "innocent traveller", unaware of contraband; admitted facts don't cover all ingredients, e.g., dutiable/contraband character); (iv)/(vi) — no inducement/threat/promise by the officer; statutory duty to speak truth proceeds from law, not the officer; perjury warning ≠ threat.
  • Hence Ex.I admissible as an admission of incriminating facts u/s 21 IEA; with evidence aliunde (seizure, contraband, cash, flight) the inference of knowing concern in fraudulent evasion irresistible. Appeal dismissed.
Principles
  1. Exculpatory statement ≠ confession; but usable as admission against maker.
  2. Special-statute enquiry officers (Customs) are "persons in authority" but statutory compulsion/perjury caution does not trigger s.24.
  3. Art. 20(3) requires formal accusation.

Case Brief No. 10 — Aghnoo Nagesia v. State of Bihar (DU Case Material)

(1966) 1 SCR 134 : AIR 1966 SC 119 · Bachawat J. · (confessional FIR)

Facts
  • Four brutal tangi murders (Ratni, Chamin, Somra, Dilu) on 11-8-1963; no eyewitness.
  • The appellant himself lodged the FIR at 3.15 p.m. containing a full confession (18 parts: motive — property dispute; killings; concealment of tangi; "I can point them out"); arrested at the PS; next day pointed out bodies and tangi; blood-stained chadar recovered from his house.
Issue

Whether the confessional FIR (or parts of it) is admissible against him — s.25 IEA (confession to police officer) vs. s.27.

Decision & Reasoning
  • s.25 is imperative: a confession to a police officer is barred in all circumstances (free or custody, pre- or post-investigation); "accused of any offence" = accused at the trial.
  • Indivisibility: a confessional statement is one whole; if tainted/excluded, every part — motive, opportunity, concealment, subsequent conduct — goes with it; severance into "non-confessional" fragments is impermissible (ss.24–26 would otherwise be robbed of content); the 304-A drunk-driver illustration.
  • s.27 lifts the ban partially: only information relating distinctly to the fact discovered (places of bodies/tangi) is provable; custody may be constructive (assumed in his favour).
  • Resulting admissible residue (information→discovery of bodies/tangi + chadar + morning-presence in forest) insufficient to convict u/s 302 IPC [s.103(1) BNS]. Appeal allowed; acquitted; death set aside.
Principles
  1. Confessional FIR to police = confession to police officer → s.25/s.23(1) BSA bar.
  2. Exclusion is of the whole confession, parts included; s.27 is the only valve.
  3. Discovery-evidence alone, without a confessional core, may be too thin a chain for murder.

Case Brief No. 11 — Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor (DU Case Material)

AIR 1947 PC 67 · Privy Council · Sir John Beaumont · (s.27 locus classicus; also s.162 CrPC breach)

Facts
  • Village-faction rioting & murder (29-12-1944); hostile eyewitnesses accepted by Sessions Judge; six appellants sentenced to death.
  • Two grounds: (i) breach of proviso to s.162 CrPC (Sub-Inspector's inquest note-book statements not supplied in time); (ii) wrongful admission of custodial confessions of accused Nos.3 & 6 (s.27 construction).
Decision & Reasoning
  • s.162 point: breach grave in principle but, on facts, no prejudice (statements ultimately supplied; Circle Inspector's diary same-day; no vital inconsistencies pressed) — curable irregularity (s.537 CrPC lineage) absent failure of justice.
  • s.27 canon: the section is a proviso to s.26; its rationale = discovery guarantees truth of information; "fact discovered" = object + place of concealment + accused's knowledge; past user/history inadmissible ("with which I stabbed A" out; "I hid the spear in my yard, I will show" in); the Madras FB Athappa Goundan overruled; discovery is "one link in the chain of proof; the other links must be forged in manner allowed by law".
  • Confessional sentences in Ex.P and the "I stabbed Sivayya" sentence wrongly admitted; duty under s.167 IEA: appellate court must ask whether the admissible residue sustains conviction. Appeal allowed; remitted to Madras HC for that enquiry.
Principles
  1. The Kottaya severance rule for s.27 (adopted by BSA s.23(2) proviso).
  2. s.162 CrPC (BNSS successor) breach quashes conviction only on prejudice.
  3. s.167 IEA [s.162-range BSA] appellate duty when inadmissible evidence slipped in.

Case Brief No. 12 — Bodhraj v. State of J&K (DU Case Material)

(2002) 8 SCC 45 · Arijit Pasayat J. · (finance-company murder; disclosures; last-seen)

Facts
  • Deceased Swaran Singh (financier), owed huge sums by A-1/A-2, lured on 3-8-1994 through a fake land-deal at Village Dhiansar and murdered by hired assailants (A-3 to A-10); A-1/A-2 silent spectators; getaway cars.
  • Evidence: eyewitnesses PWs 1, 2, 9, 18; TIP identifications; disclosures by A-3–A-7 leading to recoveries of weapons (toka, kirch, sword, revolver) and a bullet-holed pant; licensed revolver of A-1 recovered at A-6's (Rajesh's) instance; bullet injuries on some accused from deceased's return fire.
  • Trial court convicted 3, acquitted 4; HC reversed the acquittals; cross-appeals.
Issues
  1. Sufficiency of conspiracy proof; (2) reliability of recoveries on disclosure; (3) propriety of HC reversing acquittals; (4) last-seen evidence; (5) delayed FIR/examination.
Decision & Reasoning
  • Recoveries: practically no cross-examination on the recovery aspect; disclosure-led recoveries + injuries + identification formed a complete chain against the four.
  • Last-seen (para 31): the doctrine applies where the time-gap is so small that the possibility of anyone else being the author is excluded; with a long gap it is hazardous alone; here positive evidence of deceased with A-1/A-2 (PWs 14, 15, 18 + PWs 1, 2) — gap minimal.
  • Acquittal reversal: "when two views are possible, the one in favour of the accused has to be preferred. But where the relevant materials have not been considered… the High Court has a duty to arrive at a correct conclusion."
  • Delays in FIR dispatch (rains-blocked road) and staggered witness examination explained; no universal rule that delay is fatal. All appeals dismissed.
Principles
  1. Disclosure-recovery evidence, if unchallenged and witnessed, is a strong circumstantial link (s.23(2) proviso BSA/s.27 IEA).
  2. Last-seen theory calibrated by time-gap.
  3. Appellate reversal of acquittal legitimate where the trial court ignored material.
Allied provisions at this point
  • BNSS s.183 [CrPC s.164]: Magistrate's recording of confession — warning, voluntariness enquiry, time for reflection, female Magistrate for certain offences; audio-video recording now encouraged.
  • BNSS s.351 [CrPC s.313]: accused's examination — statements usable but not evidence on oath.
  • BNSS s.180 [CrPC s.161/162]: witness statements — only for contradiction, mirroring Kottaya's s.162 holding.
  • Constitution: Arts. 20(3), 21 (voluntariness as fair-trial value — Selvi lineage, supplementary).
  • IPC/BNS: murder s.302 IPC [s.103(1) BNS]; conspiracy s.120B [s.61(2)]; kidnapping-for-murder s.364 [s.137-range].

(b)(iii)Dying Declarations — s.26(1) BSA [s.32(1) IEA]

BSA s.26(1) [IEA s.32(1)] — statutory text (condensed)

Statements, written or verbal, of relevant facts made by a person who is dead, or who cannot be found, or who has become incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without an amount of delay or expense which appears unreasonable, are themselves relevant facts in the following case — (1) when the statement relates to the cause of his death or to any of the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death, in cases in which the cause of that person's death comes into question.

1. Rationale, maxim and essentials

  • Nemo moriturus praesumitur mentire — "a man will not meet his Maker with a lie in his mouth" (Justice A.V. Chandrashekar's article); admitted on the twin principles of necessity (declarant unavailable) and guarantee of veracity (in extremity — "every motive to falsehood is silenced", Laxman).
  • Oath and cross-examination dispensed with — hence close scrutiny: no tutoring/prompting/imagining; declarant must have had opportunity to observe and identify.
  • Three essentials (per the article): (i) declarant in danger of death; (ii) full apprehension of danger; (iii) death must ensue. Note the modern relaxation (Laxman): "the declarant need not necessarily be in imminent danger of death" — what matters is a fit state of mind; and if the declarant survives, the statement leaves s.26(1) and works instead as an admission/witness evidence (Sudhakar logic).
  • "Circumstances of the transaction" is wider than "cause of death" — Pakala Narayana Swami (deceased's statement to wife before leaving for Behrampur; body found in trunk). Contrast Sudhakar: statement 5½ months before an unexplained suicide = outside the transaction.

2. Who may record; in what form

  • Oral, written, or signs/gestures (Chandrasekera alias Alisandiri v. King — throat-cut victim's signs; "any adequate method of communication… provided the indication is positive and definite" — Laxman).
  • By Magistrate (highest value — questions & answers, declarant's own words), doctor (bed-head ticket — Khushal Rao), police (FIR/Ex.I admissible: s.162(2) CrPC exempts s.32(1) statements — Patel Hiralal; BNSS s.180(2)), or any witness.
  • Practical guidelines (article): doctor's fitness certificate desirable; signature/thumb impression with explanation if burnt fingers; no leading questions; record sent to court by special messenger, not police; no interested persons present; Q&A form preferred but not mandatory (Satishchandra v. State of M.P., (2014) 6 SCC 723).
  • Plural declarations: must be materially consistent; if severable, the false part alone may be discarded; between two versions, that before the doctor/Magistrate is more reliable.

3. Khushal Rao's six propositions (AIR 1958 SC 22)

  1. No absolute rule of law that a dying declaration cannot found conviction without corroboration.
  2. Each case on its own facts and the circumstances of the declaration.
  3. Not a general proposition that a dying declaration is weaker evidence.
  4. It stands on the same footing as any other piece of evidence.
  5. Magistrate-recorded, Q&A, own-words declarations stand on a much higher footing than oral hearsay versions.
  6. Reliability tests: opportunity to observe (light, if at night), unimpaired memory, consistency across versions, earliest opportunity, no tutoring.

Not to be equated with accomplice evidence or a retracted confession — "the maker of a dying declaration cannot be tarnished with the same brush" (para 15). Where the court is fully convinced of truth, "it is its duty to convict, notwithstanding that there is no corroboration in the true sense" (Ram Jas v. State of U.P., quoted with approval; accord Chandrasekera — "if believed… a conviction was abundantly justified and, indeed, inevitable").

4. Laxman — the Constitution Bench rule

Laxman v. State of Maharashtra (2002) 6 SCC 710 (G.B. Pattanaik J.) resolved the Paparambaka Rosamma vs. Koli Chunilal Savji conflict: a doctor's fitness certificate is a rule of caution, not a sine qua non. Where the Magistrate (or any recorder) himself satisfied the fitness of the declarant by questions, and eyewitnesses speak to consciousness, the declaration can be acted upon even without medical endorsement. Paparambaka Rosamma (1999) 7 SCC 695 declared not correctly decided; Koli Chunilal affirmed (see Case Brief No. 16).

Atbir Singh v. Govt. (NCT of Delhi), (2010) 9 SCC 1 — 10 guiding principles (para 22), per the article
  1. Can be sole basis of conviction if it inspires full confidence.
  2. Court must be satisfied declarant was fit and there was no tutoring/prompting/imagination.
  3. True and voluntary → conviction without corroboration.
  4. Corroboration is a rule of prudence, not law.
  5. Suspicious declaration → not without corroboration.
  6. Declaration by an unconscious person cannot found conviction.
  7. Want of full details is no ground for rejection.
  8. Even a brief statement is not discarded.
  9. If eyewitnesses affirm fitness, medical opinion cannot prevail (echo of Laxman).
  10. True, voluntary, coherent, consistent → sole basis possible.

Case Brief No. 13 — Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay (DU Case Material)

1958 SCR 552 : AIR 1958 SC 22 · Sinha J. · (three successive dying declarations)

Facts
  • Rival-faction violence in Nagpur's mill area; Baboolal attacked with swords/spears at 9 p.m. on 12-2-1956 in a narrow lane; rushed to Mayo Hospital at 9.25 p.m.
  • Three recorded dying declarations in ~2 hours: (i) doctor's bed-head ticket (9.25 p.m.) naming Khushal and Tukaram; (ii) Sub-Inspector's record at 10.15 p.m. (after doctor advised a Magistrate but SI feared delay); (iii) Magistrate's record 11.15–11.35 p.m. with doctor's fitness certificate. Baboolal died next morning.
  • Trial court: death sentence on Khushal u/s 302 IPC [s.103(1) BNS]; HC acquitted Tukaram (name/caste confusion) but confirmed Khushal's sentence.
Issue

Can conviction rest solely on a dying declaration without corroboration? (Eyewitnesses were partisan and rejected even for corroboration.)

Decision & Reasoning
  • Approved Madras FB and Ram Jas: no absolute rule of corroboration; dying declaration ≠ accomplice/approver evidence (Illustration (b) to s.114 IEA logic doesn't apply — untainted source).
  • Laid down the six propositions + six reliability tests (above, §3). Applied: consistent naming within half an hour of the occurrence; injuries consistent; light of electric lamp; fit mind; no coaching ⇒ fully truthful.
  • Acquittal of Tukaram (identification doubt) no way militated against the declarations' truth. Death sentence confirmed; appeal dismissed.
Principles
  1. Khushal Rao's six propositions — the locus classicus on dying declarations.
  2. Consistency across plural recorded declarations strongly evidences truth.
  3. A dying declaration standing alone can sustain even a murder conviction and death sentence.

Case Brief No. 14 — Sudhakar v. State of Maharashtra (DU Case Material)

(2000) 6 SCC 671 · R.P. Sethi J. · (statement 5½ months before death ≠ dying declaration)

Facts
  • Rakhi, a 20-year-old school teacher, allegedly gang-raped on 9-7-1994 by the headmaster and a co-teacher; FIR on 20-7-1994 (11 days later, delay explained); her statement Ex.P-59 to the ASI.
  • She committed suicide on 22-12-1994 (poisoning); charges added u/s 306 r/w 34 IPC [BNS s.108 r/w s.3(5)]. Courts below convicted on 376(2)(g) [BNS s.64(1)(d)] + 306, treating Ex.P-59 as a dying declaration.
Issue

Whether a statement made 5½ months before death, relating a past rape, is admissible u/s 32(1) as to "the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his/her death".

Decision & Reasoning
  • S.32 IEA is an exception to the hearsay rule, admitted on necessity; the statement must relate to the cause of death or the transaction resulting in death.
  • No legal evidence that at the time of Ex.P-59 she disclosed any intent to commit suicide; the prosecution did not even establish the cause of death link; "circumstances stated in Exhibit P-59 do not suggest that a person making such a statement would, under the normal circumstances, commit suicide after more than five-and-a-half months."
  • Once Ex.P-59 falls, no cogent evidence remained (delay, hostility of witnesses, her school attendance on 10–11 July, mother told only on 12-7); conviction cannot rest on conjecture; s.306 conviction must fall with the s.376 charge. Appeal allowed; acquittal; release ordered (with strictures on dilatory investigation/prosecution).
Principles
  1. The s.32(1)/s.26(1) link must be real and proximate — old statements narrating prior crimes are not "circumstances of the transaction which resulted in death".
  2. Admissibility of a dying declaration can be dispositive: if it goes, the case may collapse.
  3. Reaffirms Pakala's transactional test from the exclusion side.

Case Brief No. 15 — Patel Hiralal Joitaram v. State of Gujarat (DU Case Material)

(2002) 1 SCC 22 · K.T. Thomas J. · (clarificatory s.161 statement as dying declaration)

Facts
  • 21-10-1988, 10 a.m., public road at Patan: Asha Ben, returning to fetch her child from Bal Mandir, doused with combustible liquid from a can and set ablaze with a lighter by "Hiralal Patel" (scooter No. 3040; son-in-law of Nanavati) — over a quarrel about an alleged affair with her sister. She extinguished flames under a railway-station water column; died on 15-11-1988.
  • Statements: to husband; Executive Magistrate's DD (11.15 a.m.) naming Hiralal Patel + identifying particulars; FIR statement saying "Hiralal Lalchand"; Ex.67 clarificatory statement to the IO that "Lalchand" was stated inadvertently (it is her sister's father-in-law's name; the real father is Joitabhai; he is Nanavati's son-in-law).
  • Trial court acquitted; HC reversed the acquittal and convicted u/s 302 IPC [s.103(1) BNS], life.
Issues / Arguments
  • Appellant (U.R. Lalit): (i) wrong approach in an appeal against acquittal; (ii) DDs unreliable (doctor's statement omitted the name — no spontaneous naming); (iii) Ex.67 inadmissible (s.161/162 CrPC bar; not within s.32(1) as it relates only to parentage); (iv) alternatively culpable homicide not amounting to murder (death after a fortnight).
Decision & Reasoning
  • Acquittal-appeal standard (Dhanna v. State of M.P.): if the trial view is palpably wrong, reversal is legitimate.
  • Doctor's DD without name: "too much to expect a lady in such a condition to disclose the name… without being asked"; the doctor had no use for the name.
  • Ex.67: if it falls within s.32(1) it stands extricated from the s.162 CrPC ban by s.162(2) CrPC [BNSS s.180(2)]; the clarification ("Hiralal did it and not Hiralal Lalchand") is inextricably intertwined with the burning episode and her death ⇒ within s.26(1) BSA. Three identifying features (name; scooter 3040 — appellant himself sought its return; Nanavati's son-in-law) "almost conclusively" fixed identity.
  • Sentence: s.299 Explanation 2 + s.300 "secondly" — soaking a human being in inflammable liquid and igniting her necessarily imports knowledge of likelihood of death; the 14-day interval and her reflex dash for water do not mitigate. Appeal dismissed.
Principles
  1. A clarificatory statement made during investigation can itself be a dying declaration (s.162(2) exemption).
  2. DD identification details are corroborable by independent record (vehicle papers, relationships).
  3. Delayed death does not reduce murder to culpable homicide when the act was inherently fatal.

Case Brief No. 16 — Laxman v. State of Maharashtra (DU Case Material)

(2002) 6 SCC 710 : JT 2002 (6) SC 313 · G.B. Pattanaik J. · Constitution Bench (doctor's certificate)

Facts / Reference question
  • Conviction for a burn-death based on a Magistrate-recorded dying declaration; doctor certified only that the patient was conscious, not expressly that she was in a "fit state of mind".
  • Two 3-Judge Bench decisions conflicted: Paparambaka Rosamma (1999) 7 SCC 695 (risky to accept the Magistrate's subjective satisfaction absent medical certification of fitness of mind) vs. Koli Chunilal Savji (1999) 9 SCC 562 (declaration cannot be ignored where record shows the deceased was conscious and capable). Referred to a Constitution Bench.
Arguments
  • Appellant: without express medical certification of fitness, the DD cannot be the sole basis (Paparambaka). State: the Magistrate's own questioning + doctor's consciousness certificate suffice (Koli Chunilal; Ravi Chander; Harjit Kaur).
Decision
  • Medical certification is a rule of caution, not a rule of law: "what is essentially required is that the person who records a dying declaration must be satisfied that the deceased was in a fit state of mind… even without examination by the doctor the declaration can be acted upon provided the court ultimately holds the same to be voluntary and truthful."
  • No prescribed statutory form; no requirement that it be made to a Magistrate; oral, written or signs all suffice; where eyewitnesses vouch for fitness, medical opinion cannot prevail.
  • Paparambaka Rosamma "has been too broadly stated and is not the correct enunciation of law" — overruled; Koli Chunilal affirmed.
Principles
  1. Constitution-Bench authority: fitness may be proved otherwise than by a doctor's certificate.
  2. Recorder's subjective satisfaction, duly tested, is acceptable.
  3. Prevents acquittals on hypertechnical grounds in burn-death cases.
Allied provisions at this point
  • BNSS s.180(2) [CrPC s.162(2)]: police-recorded statements falling within s.32(1) IEA / s.26(1) BSA exempt from the s.180 ban — Patel Hiralal's Ex.67 route.
  • BSA s.43 [IEA s.113B]: dowry-death presumption — pairs with BNS s.80 [IPC s.304B]; the DD of a dowry victim is the classic vehicle.
  • BNS s.103(1) [IPC s.302] murder; BNS s.105 [IPC s.304] CH not amounting to murder (patent argument in Patel Hiralal); BNS s.108 [IPC s.306] abetment of suicide (Sudhakar); BNS s.64(1)(d) gang rape [IPC s.376(2)(g)].
  • Atbir Singh (2010) 9 SCC 1; Satishchandra (2014) 6 SCC 723; Krishnagopal AIR 1988 SC 2154 ("forensic probability must… rest on a robust common sense") — supplementary authorities via the DU article.

(c)Opinions of Third Persons — ss.39–45 BSA [ss.45–51 IEA]

Provision-by-provision
  • s.39(1) — Opinion of experts [s.45 IEA]: where the Court has to form an opinion on foreign law, science or art, any other field ("any other field" newly added by BSA), or the identity of handwriting or finger impressions, the opinions of persons specially skilled ("experts") are relevant facts. Illustrations: poison symptoms (a); unsoundness of mind (b) — cf. BNS s.22 [IPC s.84]; same-hand comparison (c). s.39(2): on matters of information transmitted/stored in any computer resource or electronic/digital form, the opinion of the Examiner of Electronic Evidence under s.79A of the IT Act, 2000 is relevant; Explanation: such Examiner is an expert. (Cf. BSA s.63/61 electronic records + s.79B certification.)
  • s.40 — Facts bearing upon opinions of experts [s.46]: facts, not otherwise relevant, become relevant if they support or are inconsistent with a relevant expert opinion (poison-symptoms in other victims; harbours without sea-walls obstructing at the same time).
  • s.41(1) — Opinion as to handwriting [s.47]: opinion of any person acquainted with the handwriting — three modes of acquaintance in the Explanation: (i) saw the person write; (ii) received documents purporting to be written by him in answer to documents written by himself/under his authority; (iii) in the ordinary course of business, such documents habitually submitted to him (Illustration: merchant B, clerk C, broker D — none saw A write; all competent). s.41(2): opinion of the Certifying Authority re electronic signature [s.47A IEA].
  • s.42 — General custom or right [s.48]: opinions of persons likely to know of the existence of the custom/right, if it existed; "general custom or right" = customs/rights common to any considerable class of persons (Illustration: villagers' right to use a well).
  • s.43 — Usages, tenets, foundations, local meanings [s.49]: opinions of persons with special means of knowledge as to (i) usages and tenets of any body of men or family; (ii) constitution and governance of any religious/charitable foundation; (iii) meaning of words/terms used in particular districts or by particular classes.
  • s.44 — Relationship [s.50]: opinion expressed by conduct as to the existence of a relationship, of any person who as a member of the family or otherwise has special means of knowledge; Proviso: such opinion shall not be sufficient to prove a marriage in proceedings under the Divorce Act, 1869 or in prosecutions under ss.82 and 84 BNS [ss.494–495 IPC, bigamy]. Illustrations: treated by friends as husband and wife; A always treated as B's legitimate son.
  • s.45 — Grounds of opinion [s.51]: whenever the opinion of a living person is relevant, the grounds on which it is based are also relevant (Illustration: an expert may give an account of experiments performed by him).

Working rules distilled

  • The court's opinion is the court's own; expert opinion is a relevant fact, not evidence in the sense of testimony to what the witness perceived (opinion ≠ perception). It never binds the court.
  • Expert evidence is fallible and must be received with great caution; it can be accepted if there is internal or external evidence supporting it, or where the court itself compares and is satisfied (Ram Narain, following Fakhruddin v. State of M.P.).
  • The court may itself compare admitted and disputed writings and direct specimen writing in court — BSA s.72 [IEA s.73] (applies to finger impressions too).
  • Character evidence (ss.46–49 BSA) sits just beyond this block: good character relevant in criminal cases (s.47); the rape-shield proviso (s.48) bars evidence of the victim's character/sexual history on consent in BNS ss.64–78 prosecutions — a modernised s.53A/s.146(3) IEA regime.

Case Brief No. 17 — Ram Narain v. State of U.P. (DU Case Material)

(1973) 2 SCC 86 : AIR 1973 SC 2200 · I.D. Dua J. · (conviction on handwriting expert's opinion)

Facts
  • 15-8-1964: six-year-old Mannu, son of Excise Inspector Gajendra Nath, went missing at Kanpur; reward of Rs 501 announced.
  • Two anonymous ransom letters — post-card (21-8-1964, Rs 1,000) and inland letter (21-10-1964, Rs 5,000) — demanding money for the boy's return.
  • December 1964: an ITI trainee, chasing the reward, traced the child, who was recovered on 11-1-1965 from a house at Pandeypur. Investigation fixed authorship of the ransom letters on the appellant Ram Narain; the custodians (Ganga Bux, Chandrabhushan) were acquitted on the s.368 charge; the appellant's conviction u/ss 384/511 IPC (attempted extortion; cf. BNS s.309 extortion range + s.8 attempt) rested solely on handwriting expert R.A. Gregory's testimony.
Issue & Arguments

Is a conviction legal and proper on the uncorroborated testimony of a handwriting expert? Appellant: expert opinion is not conclusive; per Shashi Kumar Banerjee (AIR 1964 SC 529) courts "usually look for corroboration"; Fakhruddin (AIR 1967 SC 1326) wrongly departs from the earlier rule.

Decision & Reasoning
  • Expert handwriting opinion is no less fallible than any other expert opinion — "has to be received with great caution". But it may be worthy of acceptance where internal/external evidence relating to the document supports the expert's view, or where the court itself compares the writings (Presiding Officer familiar with the language) and finds it safe to accept the opinion.
  • Fakhruddin is not inconsistent with Shashi Kumar (in which "all the probabilities" were against the expert).
  • Here all three courts compared the writings themselves and concurred with the expert: "the disputed hand-writings tally with the specimen hand-writing"; no serious infirmity shown ⇒ no interference (appreciation of evidence, Art. 136 restraint).
  • Sentence: 8+ years of proceedings, appellant on bail having served one month — ends of substantial justice met by reducing the sentence to the period undergone plus Rs 700 fine (3 months RI in default). Appeal partly allowed.
Principles
  1. Handwriting expert opinion can sustain a conviction when the court itself compares the writings and concurs — no rigid corroboration rule.
  2. The Fakhruddin–Shashi Kumar synthesis: caution is a rule of prudence, acceptance turns on the document's internal/external support.
  3. Demonstrates s.39(1)(c)/(d) BSA [s.45 IEA] + s.72 BSA [s.73 IEA] in tandem.

Case Brief No. 18 — Dharam Deo Yadav v. State of U.P. (reported judgment)

(2014) 5 SCC 509 : AIR 2014 SC (Cri) 1124 · K.S. Radhakrishnan & A.K. Sikri JJ. · decided 11-4-2014 · *listed in the DU case material but not printed — brief drawn from the reported judgment (Supreme Court of India, Cri. App. No. 369 of 2006)

Facts
  • Diana Clare Routley, a 22-year-old New Zealander, arrived Varanasi on 7-8-1997, stayed in room 103, Old Vishnu Guest House; left on 10-8-1997 at 7.00 a.m. for Darjeeling and went missing; the appellant, a tourist guide attached to the guest house, had been acting as her guide (8–10 August).
  • Her father Allan Jack Routley wrote to the SP (City) Varanasi suspecting "Dharam Dev Yadav who is a local guide"; later lodged the FIR on 28-7-1998 at P.S. Bhelupur; crime registered u/s 366 IPC [BNS s.137-range kidnapping].
  • On 19-8-1998 the accused was intercepted at Shivpur railway station by PW14 (SHO) and interrogated; he confessed to strangulating Diana and burying her body in his house at Village Brindaban, Dist. Ghazipur; taken there, he opened the lock with his own key, pointed out the burial spot, and himself dug out the skeleton; inquest by SDM (PW16); arrest; co-accused (Kali Charan, Sindhu Harijan, Ram Karan) arrested but acquitted.
  • Scientific identification of the skeleton: PM by team (PW19); second PM by Prof. Dr. C.B. Tripathi, BHU (PW20) — superimposition technique (skull vs. photograph matched exactly), dental records obtained from New Zealand via Interpol (extracted third molars, cavities/fillings matched), blood group A from bones matched medical record; femur & humerus sent to CDFD Hyderabad; STR DNA analysis (PW21, Dr. G.V. Rao) — profile matched the father's blood sample.
  • Trial court: convicted u/s 302/34 + 201 IPC [BNS ss.103(1) r/w 3(5); s.132-range destroying evidence], acquitted of 364/394; death sentence as "rarest of rare"; HC confirmed.
Issues & Arguments
  • Appellant: (i) case purely circumstantial — last-seen evidence (PWs 1,2,3,5,9,10 identified her from a photograph without the negative) insufficient; (ii) confession & recovery inadmissible — accused "not in custody" when statement made (Deoman Upadhyaya); search violated s.100(4)/(5) CrPC [BNSS s.103-range], no independent witnesses, planting possible; (iii) PM procedure flawed; (iv) DNA evidence controversial, degradation/contamination doubts; (v) not rarest of rare (poor rickshaw-puller, no antecedents, reformatory plea).
  • State: last-seen + disclosure + recovery complete the chain; "custody" u/s 27 includes surveillance/restraint (Gangula Satya Murthy); even otherwise admissible as conduct u/s 8 IEA [s.6 BSA] (Sandeep v. State of U.P.); unbroken sealing/custody chain; scientific proof of identity beyond doubt.
Decision & Reasoning
  • Circumstantial-evidence canon: Hanumant test — every circumstance fully established, consistent only with guilt, chain so complete as to exclude innocence; conviction may rest on a complete circumstantial chain even without eyewitnesses.
  • Last-seen: conviction cannot rest on last-seen alone; but where the prosecution reliably proves the missing person was last seen in the accused's company and never seen thereafter, the accused must explain the parting (Sahadevan); here the skeleton was found inside the accused's own house — his silence under s.313 CrPC [BNSS s.351] is an additional link (Anthony D'Souza); false answers complete the chain.
  • s.27 discovery: "custody" ≠ formal arrest — any police surveillance/restriction/restraint suffices; the disclosure-led excavation of the skeleton from his own house is admissible; discovery from one's house yields the inference of concealment by the accused (three-possibilities analysis), and he offered no explanation; even absent custody, the statement is admissible as conduct u/s 8 (Sandeep; State of Maharashtra v. Suresh).
  • Crime-scene & custody chain: no procedural error in digging, panchnama, sealing, transport (PWs 14,15,16,17).
  • Expert/scientific evidence (ss.45 IEA / s.39 BSA): landmark passage — in an age of science the legal foundation must be sound in science as well as law; oral evidence suffers human infirmities, forensic evidence does not; Daubert factors (testability, peer review, error rate, general acceptance) noted; DNA profile valid and reliable subject to lab quality control; teeth an excellent DNA source; STR match with the father + dental/superimposition evidence conclusively identified the skeleton as Diana; experts' competence never doubted ⇒ no reason to discard PWs 19–21.
  • Sentence — Shankar Kisanrao Khade's three tests (Crime Test, Criminal Test, RR Test): first two satisfied, but with no evidence of the manner of killing (no eyewitness), the RR test fails ⇒ not rarest of rare; death commuted to 20 years' rigorous imprisonment without remission, over and above the period already undergone.
Principles
  1. Last-seen + unexplained recovery from the accused's house + s.313 silence = complete chain.
  2. s.27 "custody" is custodial surveillance, not formal arrest; fallback admissibility as conduct u/s 8 IEA / s.6 BSA.
  3. DNA/STR, superimposition and dental-record evidence are expert opinions u/s 45 IEA / s.39 BSA — conclusive where procedure and expertise are unchallenged.
  4. Crime/Criminal/RR tests: absence of evidence of brutality in manner defeats the death sentence even in a strangulation-murder of a foreign tourist.

Case Brief No. 19 — Mukesh & Anr. v. State (NCT of Delhi) & Ors. — "Nirbhaya" (reported judgment)

(2017) 6 SCC 1 : AIR 2017 SC 2161 · Dipak Misra, R. Banumathi & Ashok Bhushan JJ. · decided 5-5-2017 · *listed in the DU case material but not printed — brief drawn from the reported judgment (Cri. App. Nos. 607–610 of 2017)

Facts
  • 16-12-2012, ~8.30 p.m.: 23-year-old paramedical student ("Nirbhaya") and her friend (PW-1, the informant) boarded chartered white bus DL-1P-C-0149 (yellow-green stripes; "Yadav" written on it) at Munirka; six occupants — Ram Singh (driver), Mukesh, Akshay, Vinay, Pawan and one juvenile — gang-raped and brutally assaulted both with iron rods; robbed; thrown from the moving bus near NH-8 Mahipalpur; the bus reversed to run them over but failed.

  • Investigation "faulty and biased"; dying declarations unreliable — (a) morphine administered (effect 3–4 hours, denied unconsciousness per PW-52 Dr. P.K.   • Dying declarations (s.32(1) IEA / s.26(1) BSA): three declarations — first to Dr. Rashmi Ahuja (PW-49) immediately on admission; second to SDM Usha Chaturvedi (PW-27) on 21-12-2012 (naming "Ram Singh, Thakur, Raju, Mukesh, Pawan and Vinay"); third to MM Pawan Kumar (PW-30) on 25-12-2012 by multiple-choice questions answered in nods, gestures and writings. Held: (i) gestures/nods DD admissible and of evidentiary value where the recorder (a Magistrate) correctly perceives the declarant's meaning and satisfies himself of mental alertness/fitness (paras 185, 188 — Chandrasekera/Laxman lineage); (ii) videography is only a measure of caution, its absence not fatal; (iii) minor variations between plural declarations do not destroy value absent material contradiction (Sandeep v. State of Haryana, para 176); (iv) morphine objection factually negatived (para 181).
  • TIP (paras 137–143): TIP belongs to the investigation stage; no right to claim one; it is not substantive evidence but corroborates court identification (Malkhansingh); court identification is the substantive evidence; failure/delay goes to weight, not admissibility — and here PW-1 identified Mukesh at the TIP and in court, backed by DNA confirmation of his presence (para 96).
  • Electronic evidence (s.65B IEA / BSA s.61–63 range): CDRs are electronic records inherently admissible; objection to absence of s.65B(4) certificate is an objection to mode of proof to be taken at the marking stage, when the court could have let the deficiency be cured; not raised then, and no prejudice shown, it cannot wreck the prosecution at appeal; CCTV footage was in any event proved with s.65B certificate (Ex.PW-26/A) and CFSL verification, and the trial court itself played the footage in cross-examination.
  • s.27 recoveries & conduct: disclosure-led recovery of the robbed phone, blood-stained clothes and route chart admissible; together with PW-1's consistent core (FIR ↔ s.164 ↔ testimony; minor discrepancies not material — Sunil Kumar Sambhudayal Gupta, para 74), the chain was complete.
  • Conspiracy: "prior meeting of minds" rightly inferred — conspiracy to commit gang rape proved (s.120B IPC [BNS s.61(2)]).
  • Sentence: rarest of rare (Bachan Singh; Machhi Singh) — diabolically conceived, brutally executed, gang-rape of a defenceless young woman followed by murder; death sentences confirmed (Misra & Bhushan JJ. for themselves; Banumathi J. separately on evidence and sentence).
Principles
  1. Dying declarations by signs/nods/gestures recorded by a Magistrate with fitness satisfaction are admissible and reliable; plurality with minor variations is no vice.
  2. TIP = corroborative; court identification substantive; weight not admissibility.
  3. s.65B(4) certificate objection is a mode-of-proof objection at marking; CDRs inherently admissible; curable deficiency absent timely objection and prejudice.
  4. CCTV + DNA + CDR + DD + TIP + s.27 recoveries together form the modern scientific-evidence paradigm endorsed in the judgment.
  5. Crime-test/RR-test satisfied on extreme brutality ⇒ death affirmed.

Q1 (a) — "I am leaving you because Y has made me a better offer."

In a dispute between X and Y for inducing A to break his contract of service, X produces A's statement. Discuss relevancy.

Deepened Model Answer

Provisions: s.5 BSA (cause & effect; occasion) [s.7 IEA]; s.6 BSA (conduct incl. statements accompanying/related to conduct) [s.8 IEA]; s.4 BSA (res gestae) [s.6 IEA]; s.3 BSA (relevant fact) [s.3 IEA]; Malkani's res-gestae treatment (Brief #2) and Prafulla Desai (Brief #1) on contemporaneous statements.

Step 1 — identify the fact in issue (s.3 BSA): whether Y induced A to break the contract (tort of inducement of breach of contract).

Step 2 — routes of relevancy: (i) the statement is the reason/cause of the departure — the effect of the alleged inducement — s.5 BSA; (ii) leaving employment is conduct and the statement accompanies and explains that conduct — s.6 BSA Explanation 1; (iii) spoken at the moment of departure it forms part of the same transaction — s.4 BSA (spontaneity test per Malkani: contemporaneity, not exact simultaneity).

Step 3 — the hearsay objection and its answer: the statement is not tendered to prove the truth of "Y made a better offer" but as an original fact — evidence that the statement was made and operated on A's mind (state-of-mind evidence; the classic "better offer" illustration itself appears under s.7 IEA / s.5 BSA). Hence no hearsay bar; compare the uncommunicated-entries rule in V.C. Shukla — a statement binds its maker as an admission but proves nothing about others unless communicated or falling within a special provision.

Step 4 — admissibility vs weight: admissible; weight depends on cross-examination, A's availability, and whether the words were really spoken at that time.

Conclusion: relevant on three independent grounds and admissible; the classic textbook illustration under ss.5–6 BSA / ss.7–8 IEA.

Q1 (b) — Railway ticket alibi

X is prosecuted for rioting at Dehradun on 15-04-2023; he produces a railway ticket showing travel Varanasi→Delhi the same day. Discuss relevancy.

Deepened Model Answer

Provision: s.9 BSA [s.11 IEA] — facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact, or if by themselves/in connection they make the existence/non-existence of a fact in issue highly probable or improbable.

Alibi doctrine (Jayantibhai brief, paras 18–19): s.11 IEA/s.9 BSA embodies the plea of alibi — physical presence elsewhere is inconsistent with presence at the scene. The burden to raise it lies on the accused but only to create reasonable doubt; where distance/time make presence impossible, the alibi defeats the prosecution case.

Evidentiary mechanics: the ticket is documentary evidence (s.2(1)(d), s.61 BSA); proof requires genuineness, the journey endorsement, and identity of the traveller (a ticket can be passed on). It is corroborable by PNR/UTS records, platform CCTV, co-passengers, and by the impossibility test (could X have reached Dehradun by the riot's hour?).

Caution: an alibi must be pleaded with particularity and proved by cogent evidence; a vague alibi is worthless, but a proved impossibility of presence is conclusive.

Conclusion: relevant u/s 9 BSA/s.11 IEA; admissible as a document; decisive if genuineness and identity are established.

Q2 — The two conspiracy letters

A writes to B asking for explosives to blow up a Metro station; B delays; A writes again demanding reasons; before it reaches B, A receives and uses the explosives. Are both letters admissible in a conspiracy case?

Deepened Model Answer

Provisions: s.8 BSA [s.10 IEA]; admissions ss.15–16 BSA [ss.17–18 IEA]; substantive conspiracy: BNS s.61(2) [IPC s.120B]; the metro-blast offence: e.g., BNS ss.103/109/113-range [IPC ss.302/307/121-range] with explosives.

The Mirza Akbar rule (S.482-483 of the judgment): s.10/s.8 is founded on agency — once a conspiracy is shown, everything said/done by a conspirator in reference to the common intention, while the conspiracy is still in operation, is evidence against all; but things said before the agreement or after its completion are not admissible against the others (R. v. Blake; contrast s.6/4 res gestae).

Letter 1: (i) against A — his own admission (s.15 BSA); (ii) against B — admissible u/s 8 BSA if the common design is first shown by independent evidence: B's actual supply of explosives (proved separately) demonstrates the agreement, and the request is a statement in reference to the common design while in operation. The court needs reasonable ground to believe the conspiracy existed — the Badri Rai prima-facie threshold (see also V.C. Shukla's "condition precedent" failure).

Letter 2: written after A received and used the explosives — the common design was executed; it is post-completion narration — not admissible against B under s.8 (Mirza Akbar). It remains admissible against A alone as an admission (s.15 BSA; Bhogilal Chunilal uncommunicated-entries logic in V.C. Shukla).

Conclusion: Letter 1 — admissible against both (subject to prima facie proof of the agreement) and against A as admission; Letter 2 — admissible against A only.

Q3 — Disclosure leading to recovery of stolen idols

"I will tell the place of deposit of three golden idols which I stole from Pakka Mahal on 11 April" — the accused then points out idols at a Dharmsala. How much of the statement is usable? What inferences follow?

Deepened Model Answer

Provisions: s.23(2) proviso BSA [s.27 IEA]; bars of ss.23(1)–(2) BSA [ss.25–26 IEA]; Kottaya, Aghnoo Nagesia, Bodhraj; theft: BNS s.303 [IPC s.379]; receiving/retaining stolen property BNS s.316 [IPC s.411-range].

Gate 1 (ss.23(1)–(2)): the statement was recorded by the SI — to the extent it is confessional it is wholly barred (Aghnoo: the bar is absolute and indivisible).

Gate 2 (s.27 valve — Kottaya canon): "fact discovered" = the object + place produced from + the accused's knowledge. Sever the statement: provable — "I will tell the place of deposit of three golden idols" + the pointing out and recovery; not provable — "which I stole from Pakka Mahal on 11 April" (past user/history: the theft, its place and date are not facts discovered).

Gate 3 (conditions): accused in custody (actual or constructive); information distinctly relating to the discovery; recovery in the presence of witnesses (Bodhraj: unchallenged recovery-witness evidence is strong; panchnama per BNSS s.100 [CrPC s.100] search formalities).

Inferences: exclusive knowledge of concealment ⇒ consciousness of guilt; with the theft's other circumstances the discovery is a link in the circumstantial chain — but it is not proof of the theft itself; the chain must be forged in the manner allowed by law (Kottaya) and be complete and inconsistent with innocence.

Conclusion: only the discovery-related portion is admissible; it proves knowledge of concealment, supporting — not establishing — guilt.

Q4 — Dying declaration: sole basis & incomplete statement

(a) "There is no absolute rule that dying declaration can be the sole basis of conviction unless corroborated." Comment. (b) A stabbed victim tells the doctor "Mr. P has stabbed me because……" and dies mid-sentence. Discuss relevancy and veracity in P's trial.

Deepened Model Answer

(a) Provision: s.26(1) BSA [s.32(1) IEA]. Answer with Khushal Rao's six propositions: no absolute rule of corroboration; same footing as other evidence; Magistrate-recorded Q&A declarations on a higher footing; reliability tests (opportunity, fitness, consistency, earliest opportunity, no tutoring). Add Laxman (fitness may be proved without doctor's certificate) and Atbir's ten principles. Contrast Sudhakar: where the DD fails the s.32(1) transaction-link, the whole case may collapse. Conclusion: a reliable dying declaration alone can convict; corroboration is prudence, not law.

(b) Relevancy: statement by a dead person as to the cause of death — squarely s.26(1)/s.32(1); cause of death is in question in P's murder trial (BNS s.103(1) [IPC s.302]).

Veracity (Khushal Rao/Laxman tests): doctor = neutral recipient; earliest opportunity (no tutoring window); clear identification ("Mr. P"); the incomplete tail ("because…") concerns motive, not the operative accusatory part — motive is not an essential of s.26(1); injuries corroborate inherently; fitness — the attending doctor may certify (or fitness may be otherwise proved — Laxman); abrupt termination requires scrutiny that the recorded portion reflects the intended statement (cf. the Ravinder Singh line of authority).

Conclusion: relevant and admissible; if found voluntary and truthful it may sustain conviction alone — here with the natural corroboration of the stab injuries.
