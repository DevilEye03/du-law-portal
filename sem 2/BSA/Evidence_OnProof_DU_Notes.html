PART 0The Architecture of “ON PROOF”

Part II of the Adhiniyam (like the 1872 Act) answers three sequential questions before a court reaches a verdict: (1) which facts may be proved (relevancy, Ss. 3–32 BSA); (2) which facts need not or cannot be proved — the subject of this Unit; and (3) how facts are proved — by which medium (oral, documentary, electronic), in which quality (primary/secondary), and with which exclusions (parol-evidence rule). Unit III “ON PROOF” is therefore the hinge between relevancy and production of evidence. A fact may be relevant and yet be (a) excused from proof (judicial notice, admission), (b) prohibited from proof (estoppel, privilege), or (c) required to be proved only through a prescribed medium (documentary/electronic, not oral).

Concept Ladder
  1. Relevancy — is the fact logically/statutorily connected to a fact in issue? (gateway)
  2. Admissibility — is the fact received by law, or excluded by a rule of exclusion (estoppel, privilege, hearsay, best-evidence rule)?
  3. Proof — the establishment of a fact by (i) evidence (oral/documentary/electronic), (ii) presumption, or (iii) judicial notice/admission (no evidence at all).
  4. Degrees of proof — “proved”, “disproved”, “not proved” (s. 2(1) BSA / s. 3 IEA).

PART A(i)Facts Which Need Not Be Proved

BSA Ss. 51–53IEA Ss. 56–58 Chapter III, BSA 2023 (“Facts which need not be proved”) = Chapter III, IEA 1872. DU notes (LB-201) treat this head as the three exceptions to the general rule that every fact must be proved by a party: (1) facts judicially noticeable (Ss. 51–52), (2) facts admitted by the party (s. 53, read with judicial admissions), and (3) facts presumed by law (presumptions — dealt with in Unit VI of the syllabus).

A(i).1 Section 51 BSA / Section 56 IEA — the declaratory rule

Statute
s. 51, BSA 2023 — “No fact of which the Court will take judicial notice need be proved.”
s. 56, IEA 1872 — identical.

Nature. The section is declaratory: it does not create an independent exception but announces the consequence of ss. 52/57. The combined effect of ss. 51 and 52 is that facts judicially noticeable need no proof. Judicial notice, per Taylor (as quoted in the DU notes), is “the cognizance taken by the Court itself of certain matters which are so clearly established that evidence of their existence is deemed unnecessary”. The judge may take aid of books or documents of reference, and even of the parties; in such cases the party is not bound to prove the fact.

Why the rule exists (rationale from DU notes)

  • Expediency: recognition of facts without formal proof is a matter of expediency; no one has ever questioned the need and wisdom of accepting matters unquestionably within public knowledge.
  • Common sense: “Shutting the judicial eye to the existence of such facts… is in a sense an insult to common sense and would tend to reduce the judicial process to a meaningless and wasteful ritual.”
  • Superior to proof: for notorious facts (date of a poll; death of a person of eminence; events that rocked the nation — e.g. COVID-19) judicial notice takes the place of proof and is of equal force; indeed as a means of establishing widely known facts it is superior to formal proof.

A(i).2.1 The list is NOT exhaustive — Onkar Nath v. Delhi Administration

Onkar Nath & Ors. v. The Delhi Administration

Supreme Court, decided 15 February 1977 (as briefed in DU notes, Ch. VII)
Facts

The appellants were convicted for speeches delivered during the period of the All-India railway strike of May 1974. The courts below assumed — without formal evidence — that a railway strike was imminent on the date of the speeches and that a strike intended to paralyse the civic life of the nation was in fact launched on 8 May 1974.

Issue

Whether the courts below were justified in taking judicial notice of the imminence and occurrence of the railway strike, i.e. whether s. 57 IEA permits judicial notice of facts outside its thirteen clauses.

Decision (Chandrachud, J.)

Yes. Section 57 is illustrative, not exhaustive. The purpose of the section is to provide that the Court shall take judicial notice of certain facts, “rather than exhaust the category of facts of which the Court may in appropriate cases take judicial notice”. The courts below were therefore justified in assuming, without formal evidence, that the railway strike was imminent on 5 May 1974 and that a strike intended to paralyse the civic life of the nation was undertaken on 8 May 1974.

Principle evolved

(1) Ss. 56/57 (now 51/52 BSA) operate together: judicial notice dispenses with proof. (2) The statutory list is a floor, not a ceiling — notorious facts of public history/current events may be judicially noticed. (3) Judicial notice in such matters “takes the place of proof and is of equal force”.

Connected law & further judicial-notice points (DU notes)
  • Municipal bye-laws: DU/DJS PYQ — “How will you prove a municipal bye-law?” Answer: bye-laws are “law in force” ⇒ judicial notice under cl. (a)/s. 52(1)(a); no formal proof needed.
  • Political facts: Tamil Nadu Cauvery Neerppasana Virudhi Porattu Kuzhu v. Union of India (SC, 4 May 1990) — judicial notice can be taken of the fact that the Central Government is run by one political party while the State Governments in question are run by different parties.
  • General Clauses Act 1897, s. 3(29) defines “Indian law” for the purpose of cl. (a); Constitution of India — courts take judicial notice of the Constitution itself and of constitutional facts (e.g. proclamation of Emergency, Art. 352/356 notifications published in the Gazette).
  • Armed Forces Acts (Army Act 1950 s. 134; Navy Act 1957 s. 132; Air Force Act 1950 ss. 93/133; BSF Act 1968 s. 88; ITBP Act 1992 s. 100; NSG Act 1986 s. 85; SSB Act 2007 s. 100) contain their own judicial-notice clauses — the “linked provisions” of s. 52 in the official BSA text.
  • Maxim: ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat — ignorance of fact is excused, ignorance of law is not; the rule applies to judges too, which is why laws must be judicially noticed.
  • Limit: judicial notice ≠ personal knowledge of the judge; the court may demand reference books under s. 52(2). Private facts (e.g. a particular local custom) still require evidence.

A(i).3 Section 53 BSA / Section 58 IEA — facts admitted need not be proved

Statute
s. 53, BSA 2023 (= s. 58 IEA) — “No fact needs to be proved in any proceeding which the parties thereto or their agents agree to admit at the hearing, or which, before the hearing, they agree to admit by any writing under their hands, or which by any rule of pleading in force at the time they are deemed to have admitted by their pleadings: Provided that the Court may, in its discretion, require the facts admitted to be proved otherwise than by such admissions.”
Ingredients (component-wise)
  1. Admission by whom: by the parties to the proceeding or their agents (e.g. counsel in open court, pleader with authority).
  2. Admission when / how: (i) agreed to admit at the hearing (oral judicial admission); (ii) agreed before the hearing by any writing under their hands; or (iii) deemed admitted by pleadings under any rule of pleading in force (constructive admission).
  3. Effect: the fact need not be proved — proof by evidence is dispensed with.
  4. Proviso (safeguard): the Court retains a discretion to require the admitted fact to be proved otherwise than by the admission — crucial in criminal matters and where public policy or third-party rights are involved; an admission is not automatically conclusive.

Judicial vs extra-judicial admissions (DU notes)

Judicial admission (s. 53 / s. 58)Made in the course of the proceeding (at or before the hearing, or via pleadings). Operates as waiver of proof; ordinarily binding and conclusive subject to the court’s discretion under the proviso, and subject to being shown to be mistaken/obtained by fraud.
Extra-judicial admission (Ss. 15–23, 31 BSA / 17–23, 31 IEA)Made outside the proceeding. It is evidence of the fact admitted (relevant under s. 15 BSA/s. 17 IEA) but does not by itself dispense with proof; it must itself be proved and is only a piece of the evidentiary material.
Connected law on admissions
  • CPC 1908, s. 58: “Facts admitted need not be proved” — the civil-side twin of s. 53 BSA: “No fact need be proved in any suit which the parties thereto or their agents agree to admit at the hearing…”.
  • Order VIII Rule 5 CPC: every allegation of fact in the plaint not specifically or implicitly denied in the written statement is deemed admitted (rule of pleading → constructive admission under s. 53).
  • Order XII Rule 6 CPC: judgment on admissions — a court may pronounce judgment on the basis of admissions even before trial.
  • Criminal side: admissions of an accused are governed by BSA Ss. 15–23 (confessions Ss. 22–28) and BNSS 2023 s. 313 / s. 330 (examination of accused); a judicial admission in a criminal trial (e.g. under s. 313 BNSS) is evaluated with the proviso’s caution — the court may still demand proof.
  • Oaths Act 1969 s. 6: affidavits are not “evidence” unless the court orders (s. 1(4) BSA excludes affidavits from the Adhiniyam); admissions in affidavits therefore operate as admissions in writing before the hearing.
Real examples
  • Judicial notice: In a prosecution under the NDPS Act arising from a raid on 26 January, the court need not prove that 26 January is a public holiday (notified Gazette holiday — cl. (h)); nor that the territory where the offence occurred is within India (cl. (i)); nor the text of the NDPS Act itself (cl. (a)).
  • Admission: In a suit on a promissory note, the defendant’s counsel states in open court, “my client admits execution of the note but disputes consideration”. Execution need not be proved (s. 53 + s. 67 BSA dispensed with); consideration remains in issue.
  • Proviso at work: In a custody dispute, both parents “admit” that the child is 16 years old to avoid producing the school record; the court may, in its discretion, still require proof of age (proviso to s. 53).
PYQ — DU / DJS pattern

Q. “Is a ‘railway strike’ such a fact of which a court is enjoined to take judicial notice?” (DJS 1982, as preserved in DU notes.) — Model answer: Section 57 IEA (s. 52 BSA) uses the mandatory word “shall” for its thirteen clauses, but the list is not exhaustive (Onkar Nath v. Delhi Administration, SC 15-2-1977). A nation-wide railway strike that paralyses civic life is a notorious fact of public history/current event; under s. 52(2) the court may aid itself by appropriate books/documents of reference (newspaper records, government notifications). Hence the court was justified in taking judicial notice — the fact “need not be proved” under s. 51/s. 56.

PART A(ii)Facts Which the Parties Are Prohibited From Proving — The Doctrine of Estoppel

BSA Ss. 121–123IEA Ss. 115–117 Chapter VIII BSA 2023. Estoppel is the statutory instance of a fact which, though relevant, a party is forbidden by law to prove — the mirror-image of judicial notice.

A(ii).1 Concept, maxim and origin

  • Maxim: allegans contraria non est audiendus — “a person alleging contradictory facts should not be heard”. The doctrine is “steeped in the principles of equity and good conscience: equity will not allow a person to say one thing at one time and the opposite of it at another time” (DU notes).
  • English origin: Pickard v. Sears (1832) 6 A & E 469 — “where one by his words or conduct wilfully causes another to believe the existence of a certain state of things, and induces him to act on that belief so as to alter his own previous position, the former is concluded from averring against the latter a different state of things as existing at the same time.” (quoted verbatim by the Supreme Court in Maddanappa through Garth C.J.’s speech in Ganges Manufacturing Co. v. Sourajmull, ILR 5 Cal 669).
  • Object: “to prevent fraud and secure justice between the parties by promotion of honesty and good faith” (Maddanappa).
  • Statutory form: in India the doctrine of estoppel by representation is codified — it is a rule of evidence enacted in s. 121 BSA/s. 115 IEA; no cause of action arises upon an estoppel itself; it is a shield (rule of exclusion of proof), not a sword.
Is estoppel a rule of evidence or of substantive law? (DU notes, Ch. 12)
Rule of evidence (adjective law) — the code view: the section merely shuts out proof of a fact previously represented; it operates inside a suit/proceeding “between himself and such person or his representative”; Garth C.J. in Ganges Manufacturing: the provisions are “in one sense a rule of evidence”; Maddanappa doubted whether a court “while determining whether the conduct of a particular party amounts to an estoppel, could travel beyond the provisions of Section 115”.
Rule of substantive law — the equity view: estoppel is “a complex legal notion involving a combination of several ideas” (Lord Mansfield’s line, cited in DU notes); developed by courts into promissory estoppel, which creates rights/enforceable obligations even without contract or consideration (Indo-Afghan Agencies; Motilal Padampat). Conclusion of DU notes: s. 115/121 codifies the evidential core; the substantive branch (promissory/equitable estoppel) is judge-made and operates outside the section.

A(ii).2 Section 121 BSA / Section 115 IEA — the operative provision

Statute
s. 121, BSA 2023 (= s. 115 IEA, identical) — “When one person has, by his declaration, act or omission, intentionally caused or permitted another person to believe a thing to be true and to act upon such belief, neither he nor his representative shall be allowed, in any suit or proceeding between himself and such person or his representative, to deny the truth of that thing.”
Illustration: A intentionally and falsely leads B to believe that certain land belongs to A, and thereby induces B to buy and pay for it. The land afterwards becomes the property of A, and A seeks to set aside the sale on the ground that, at the time of the sale, he had no title. He must not be allowed to prove his want of title. (Cf. s. 43 TPA — “feeding the grant by estoppel”.)
Elements — component-wise decomposition
  1. Representation by one person to another, by (i) declaration (words, written or spoken), (ii) act, or (iii) omission (silence/acquiescence where there is a duty to speak). “It is significant that it is not merely a positive or active declaration that can be the basis… but also an act or omission” (DU v. Ashok Kumar Chopra).
  2. Intention element: the representor must have intentionally caused or permitted the other to believe the thing to be true — wilfulness in causing belief.
  3. Belief element: the other person must actually have believed the thing to be true (the representation must be of an existing fact, not a promise de futuro — Chhaganlal).
  4. Reliance element: he must have acted upon such belief and thereby altered his position to his prejudice/detriment (Maddanappa: “the person claiming benefit of the doctrine must show that he has acted to his detriment on the faith of the representation”).
  5. Proximate cause: the representation must be the proximate cause of the prejudicial action (Chhaganlal).
  6. Ignorance of truth: the person claiming estoppel must show he did not know the true state of things; if he knew the truth or had the means of knowledge, no estoppel (Chhaganlal; Maddanappa — “where one person makes a misrepresentation… if that other person knew the true state of facts… he must consequently not have been misled”).
  7. Mutuality of parties: only the person to whom the representation was made (or for whom it was designed), and only in a suit/proceeding between the representor (or his representative) and that person (or his representative), can plead it; estoppel operates inter partes, not in rem.
  8. Burden: on the party claiming estoppel; the representation must be clear and unambiguous, not indefinite (DU notes).

University of Delhi v. Ashok Kumar Chopra

Delhi High Court, 9 October 1967 — DU’s own estoppel precedent (briefed in DU notes Ch. 12)
Facts

A student was admitted to the B.A. course at Deshbandhu College, University of Delhi. The University later sought to resile from the admission on the ground of the student’s alleged ineligibility, although the student had acted on the University’s own admission process and joined the course.

Issue

Whether the University, having granted admission, was estopped under s. 115 IEA from denying the validity of that admission.

Decision

The three ingredients — (1) declaration/act/omission by one person; (2) intentional causing or permitting of belief in the truth of a thing; (3) intentional causing or permitting of action upon such belief — must be cumulatively present; on the facts they were, and estoppel was available against the University; relief was given to the student.

Principle / caveat

Estoppel binds even statutory institutions — but the decision “has no application to a case where a student is guilty of fraud, deception or concealment of material particulars while seeking and obtaining admission” (the caveat that later explains Madhuri Patel and distinguishes Sanatan Gauda).

Chhaganlal Keshavlal Mehta v. Patel Narandas Haribhai

Supreme Court, 11 December 1981 (R.B. Mishra, J.) — the eight-ingredient formulation (DU notes Ch. 12)

“To bring the case within the scope of estoppel as defined in Section 115 of the Evidence Act: (1) there must be a representation by a person or his authorised agent to another in the form of a declaration, act or omission; (2) the representation must have been of the existence of a fact and not of promises de futuro or intention which might or might not be enforceable in contract; (3) the representation must have been meant to be relied upon; (4) there must have been belief on the part of the other party in its truth; (5) there must have been action on the faith of that declaration, act or omission… altering his former position to his prejudice or detriment; (6) the misrepresentation or conduct or omission must have been the proximate cause of leading the other party to act to his prejudice; (7) the person claiming the benefit must show that he was not aware of the true state of things — if he was aware of the real state of affairs or had means of knowledge, there can be no estoppel; (8) only the person to whom the representation was made or for whom it was designed can avail himself of it; a person is entitled to plead estoppel in his own individual character and not as representative of his assignee.”

A(ii).3 Sections 122–123 BSA / 116–117 IEA — estoppel of tenants, licensees, bailees, acceptors

Statute
s. 122 BSA — “No tenant of immovable property, or person claiming through such tenant, shall, during the continuance of the tenancy or any time thereafter, be permitted to deny that the landlord of such tenant had, at the beginning of the tenancy, a title to such immovable property; and no person who came upon any immovable property by the licence of the person in possession thereof shall be permitted to deny that such person had a title to such possession at the time when such licence was given.” BSA change IEA s. 116 said only “during the continuance of the tenancy”; BSA extends the estoppel to any time thereafter, settling the old controversy whether a tenant out of possession could challenge title.
s. 123 BSA (= s. 117 IEA) — “No acceptor of a bill of exchange shall be permitted to deny that the drawer had authority to draw such bill or to endorse it; nor shall any bailee or licensee be permitted to deny that his bailor or licensor had, at the time when the bailment or licence commenced, authority to make such bailment or grant such licence.” Expl. 1: the acceptor may deny that the bill was really drawn by the person by whom it purports to have been drawn. Expl. 2: if a bailee delivers the goods bailed to a person other than the bailor, he may prove that such person had a right to them as against the bailor.

Rationale (all three sections): the relationship (landlord–tenant, bailor–bailee, licensor–licensee, drawer–acceptor) is founded on the representee having been put into possession or given a benefit by the representor; having accepted that benefit, he cannot afterwards dispute the very title under which he holds — nemo allegans contraria…. The estoppel is against denying title at the commencement of the relationship; it does not bar proof that the title subsequently determined (e.g. landlord’s interest expired) or that possession was obtained by force/fraud.

Connected law on estoppel
  • Transfer of Property Act 1882, s. 43 — transfer by person unauthorised who afterwards acquires interest: the illustration to s. 121 BSA is the evidential twin of s. 43 TPA (“feeding the grant by estoppel”); also s. 51 TPA (improvements by bona fide holder — argued and rejected in Maddanappa).
  • CPC 1908, s. 11 — res judicata (estoppel by record); see comparison table below. Doctrine of approbate and reprobate (election of remedies) is a species of estoppel by conduct.
  • Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, ss. 120–122 — statutory estoppels against denying original validity of the instrument, capacity of payee to indorse, signature/capacity of prior party (the “linked provisions” to s. 123 BSA).
  • Registration Act 1908 / R.P. Act — estoppel by registered deed; a party claiming under a registered sale deed cannot deny its recitals (estoppel by deed).
  • Constitution: no estoppel against the Constitution or a statute — estoppel cannot confer a status/status-based benefit contrary to a Presidential notification (Madhuri Patel), nor validate what the law forbids.
  • BNSS 2023 / IPC: estoppel does not bar criminal prosecution; it is a civil-evidential rule, though issue-estoppel may protect an accused on an issue already found in his favour.

Promissory estoppel (the equitable extension)

Doctrine

Conditions (DU notes): (1) a clear and unequivocal promise (words or conduct) intended to create/affect a legal relationship in the future, knowing or intending that it will be acted upon; (2) the promisee acts on it and alters his position. No pre-existing relationship and no consideration required.

  • England: Central London Property Trust Ltd. v. High Trees House Ltd. [1947] KB 130 / [1956] 1 All ER 256 (Denning, J.) — the fountainhead.
  • India — Union of India v. M/s Indo-Afghan Agencies Ltd. (1968) 2 SCR 366: (i) whether the arrangement is executive or administrative, courts can in appropriate cases compel performance of obligations imposed by schemes upon departmental authorities; (ii) Government is not exempt from the equity arising out of acts done by citizens to their prejudice in reliance on representations of future conduct; (iii) even where the case falls outside s. 115 IEA and outside Art. 299 (formal contract), a party who acted on the Government’s representation can hold the Government to its promise.
  • Motilal Padampat Sugar Mills v. State of U.P. (AIR 1979 SC 621, Bhagwati, J.): on a published sales-tax exemption scheme for new units the mill set up a vanaspati plant; the State later withdrew the exemption. Held: promissory estoppel binds the State; the plea of waiver failed (not pleaded; no knowledge of right); limits — promissory estoppel cannot override statute, cannot be invoked against public interest, and yields where the promise is forbidden by law.
  • M/s Jit Ram Shiv Kumar v. State of Haryana (1981) 1 SCC 11: counter-weight — where repeated exemptions were varied by later sovereign/public-interest decisions, estoppel could not fettle the State’s legislative/fiscal power.
  • Limit re-asserted in Madhuri Patel: “There is no estoppel as no promise of the social status is made by the State when a false plea was put forth… When it is found to be a case of fraud played by the concerned, no sympathy and equitable considerations can come to his rescue.”

A(ii).4 Limits & exceptions to estoppel (DU notes Ch. 12.10)

  1. No estoppel against statute, Constitution or law: the doctrine cannot confer a right/status that the law denies (Madhuri Patel; Presidential notifications under Art. 342 are conclusive).
  2. No estoppel against truth known to the representee: one who knows the true state of facts cannot claim to have been misled (Maddanappa).
  3. No estoppel arising from fraud or concealment of material particulars: (DU v. Ashok Kumar Chopra caveat; Madhuri Patel).
  4. No estoppel without detriment: absent alteration of position to prejudice, the plea fails (Maddanappa, applying Carr v. London & N.W. Ry. and Sarad v. Gopal).
  5. No estoppel on questions of jurisdiction of courts and on pure questions of law (representation must be of fact).
  6. Estoppel by silence/acquiescence arises only where the party estopped was bound in equity and good conscience to speak and the claimant relied on the silence to his prejudice (DU v. Ashok Kumar Chopra; Ramsden v. Dyson line in Maddanappa).
  7. s. 121 BSA is exhaustive of estoppel by representation in evidential law: courts cannot “travel beyond” it under the label of “equitable estoppel” in a matter governed by the section (Maddanappa).

20. R.S. Maddanappa v. Chandramma

(1965) 3 SCR 283 : AIR 1965 SC 1818 — Mudholkar, J. — DU Case Material LB-201, pp. 114–119 (full text)
Facts

The plaintiff (elder sister) sued in the District Judge’s court, Bangalore, for a declaration of her half share in suit properties, partition and mesne profits, impleading her sister (D1) as a defendant because D1 would not join as co-plaintiff. The properties were the absolute property of their mother Puttananjamma; on her death they devolved on the two sisters as heirs. Possession was with their father R.S. Maddanappa (D2), his second wife Gangavva and her children (D3–D8). Maddanappa’s defence: the properties originally belonged to Gowramma (Puttananjamma’s mother) who had settled them orally on Puttananjamma and himself; that it was Puttananjamma’s last wish that he enjoy them as absolute owner; that the daughters had expressly/impliedly abandoned their rights; that his possession was adverse for over the statutory period; and that he had spent over Rs 46,000 on improvements bona fide believing himself owner, entitling him to the benefit of s. 51 TPA. D1 admitted the plaintiff’s claim and claimed her own half share. D3–D8 additionally pleaded that D1 was estopped by her conduct from claiming any share. The conduct relied on: (i) D1’s silence in reply to the plaintiff’s lawyer’s notice of 26-1-1948 proposing division; (ii) D1’s letter of 17-1-1941 to her step-mother: “I have no desire whatsoever in respect of the properties which are at Bangalore. Everything belongs to my father… We give our consent to anything done by our father”; (iii) D1 and her husband attesting Maddanappa’s will of 25-1-1941 (dated 3-10-1944) which devised the suit properties to D3–D8. The trial court decreed the plaintiff’s suit but held D1 estopped; the High Court of Mysore reversed as to D1 and decreed her half share plus future mesne profits. D3–D8 appealed to the Supreme Court (certificate limited to D1’s claim).

Issues
  1. Whether D1 was estopped by her conduct (silence, letter, attestation of will) from claiming her half share (s. 115 IEA / now s. 121 BSA).
  2. Whether a decree for possession could be passed in favour of a defendant not transposed as plaintiff.
  3. Whether future mesne profits could be awarded though not claimed in the plaint.
Arguments — Appellants (D3–D8, Shri Venkatarangaiengar)
  • D1’s non-reply to the 1948 notice, her 1941 letter disclaiming interest, and her attestation of the will were a continuous course of conduct representing that she had no interest; the father and the legatees acted on it (further improvements after the will); D1 is therefore estopped.
  • Even outside s. 115, “equitable estoppel” of English judges (acquiescence, Ramsden v. Dyson) applies.
  • A defendant who never sought transposition cannot be granted a decree; mesne profits not claimed cannot be awarded (Mohd. Amin v. Vakil Ahmed).
Arguments — Respondents (D1 / plaintiff)
  • Estoppel requires that the representee be misled: Maddanappa knew the true title all along (the properties were his deceased wife’s); a person who knows the truth cannot claim to have believed a false representation.
  • No detriment flowed from D1’s conduct: improvements were made even before the will, and Maddanappa was in possession and enjoyment throughout.
  • The will created no interest in D3–D8 until Maddanappa’s death; attestation merely fixed knowledge of its contents.
Decision (Supreme Court — appeal dismissed; High Court’s decree upheld)
  1. No estoppel against D1. (a) Silence in reply to the notice and non-cooperation cannot justify the inference of estoppel — it did not mean she impliedly admitted she had no interest. (b) The 1941 letter cannot assist because “admittedly the father knew the true legal position” — that the properties belonged to Puttananjamma and he had no authority to deal with them; his possession must be deemed, to his knowledge, on behalf of the daughters; “there was thus no possibility of an erroneous belief about his title being created in the mind of Maddanappa”. (c) Attestation of the will affixed D1 with knowledge of what Maddanappa was doing but could not operate as estoppel: the will could take effect only on his death, so no interest had accrued to D3–D8 even on the date of suit; and Maddanappa, knowing the true position, could not say an erroneous belief was created in his mind; nor was there anything to show he altered his position to his disadvantage because of D1’s conduct (improvements pre-dated the will).
  2. On “equitable estoppel”: the Court doubted whether, while determining whether conduct amounts to estoppel, a court “could travel beyond the provisions of Section 115 of the Evidence Act”; s. 115 itself embodies Pickard v. Sears; and even the general/equitable principle (Lord Chancellor in Cairncross v. Lorimer; Brett J. in Carr v. London & N.W. Ry., third proposition, approved in Sarad v. Gopal; Ramsden v. Dyson acquiescence) requires detriment: “the person who sets up an estoppel against the other must show that his position was altered by reason of the representation or conduct of the latter”. None existed here.
  3. Improvements / s. 51 TPA: “No man who, knowing fully well that he has no title to property, spends money on improving it can be permitted to deprive the original owner of his right to possession… except upon payment for improvements” not effected with the owner’s consent — D1 cannot be made to pay for the improvements.
  4. Transposition: the objection is “purely technical”; the court could have transposed D1 under Order I Rule 10(2) CPC even suo motu (Bhupendra v. Rajeshwar, PC: power ought to be exercised for complete justice); both sisters claim under the same title; s. 99 CPC bars appellate interference on such a ground.
  5. Future mesne profits: Mohd. Amin bars past mesne profits not claimed; but future mesne profits are governed by Order XX Rule 12 CPC, under which the court may direct an inquiry from the institution of the suit until delivery of possession even without a specific claim — hence the decree was valid.
Principles evolved (exam-critical)
  • Estoppel requires a misled representee: where the person against whom estoppel is pleaded knew the true state of facts, no erroneous belief can arise and estoppel fails.
  • Detrimental reliance is indispensable — under s. 115 IEA and even under the general equitable principle.
  • s. 115 IEA (s. 121 BSA) is the exhaustive code of estoppel by representation in evidential law; courts should not travel beyond it under the guise of “equitable estoppel”.
  • Acquiescence estops only where the party had a duty to speak and the other was misled into altering his position.
  • Collateral holdings: O. I r. 10(2) & O. XX r. 12 CPC; s. 51 TPA unavailable to a knowing trespasser.

21. Kumari Madhuri Patil v. Additional Commissioner, Tribal Development

AIR 1995 SC 94 — K. Ramaswamy, J. — DU Case Material LB-201, pp. 120–130 (full text)
Facts

Suchita and Madhuri, daughters of Laxman Pandurang Patil, claimed Scheduled Tribe status as “Mahadeo Koli”. Their father’s 1943 school admission register and his school/college certificates recorded his caste as “Hindu Koli” (an OBC in Maharashtra). Suchita applied (30-11-1989) through her father to the Tahsildar, Andheri, for a caste certificate as Mahadeo Koli; the Sub-Divisional Officer refused (22-6-1989). In her appeal, and in W.P. 3516/1990 before the Bombay High Court (time for MBBS admission running out), she relied on the Division Bench ruling in Subhash Ganpatrao Kabade v. State of Maharashtra (“Koli = Mahadeo Koli”); under the High Court’s directions she was admitted to MBBS and the Tahsildar was directed to issue the certificate. Madhuri, enclosing her sister’s High Court order, obtained a certificate from the Executive Magistrate, Greater Bombay (an officer without jurisdiction) and secured BDS admission in 1992. On verification, the Caste Scrutiny Committee (order 26-6-1992), applying the anthropological/ethnological proforma and the expert-committee report, found the appellants to be Koli (OBC), not Mahadeo Koli; certificates were cancelled and confiscated; the Additional Commissioner’s appellate order (30-4-1993) confirmed: the uncle’s 1954 certificate was from a Magistrate without jurisdiction; the aunt’s certificate rested on school-leaving certificate/ration card with no probative value; the father’s pre-independence entries as “Hindu Koli” carried “great probative value” and belied the claim; the caste-affirmation of the “Samaj” (caste association) was self-serving. The Division Bench dismissed the writ petition (17-8-1993).

Issues
  1. Whether the appellants were “Mahadeo Koli” (ST) entitled to certification and reserved-seat admissions.
  2. Whether the Kabade Division Bench approach was correct.
  3. Whether, having studied for years on the strength of the certificates/court orders, the appellants could claim equity / promissory estoppel to continue.
  4. What procedure should govern issuance, scrutiny and cancellation of social-status certificates.
Arguments — Appellants
  • The father’s affidavit explained the “Hindu Koli” entries: before 1950 there was no necessity to describe sub-caste; Mahadeo Koli was introduced as ST only by the SC & ST (Amendment) Act, 1976; certificates of the maternal uncle (1954) and aunt (1979) and school registers probabilise ST status; the “social mobility/modernisation” of the family explains failure of the affinity test.
  • Suchita had completed the course except the final year; Madhuri was mid-course; justice demanded their education not be dislocated (equity, promissory estoppel).
Arguments — State / Committee
  • The Presidential Notification of 1950 (Art. 342 read with Art. 366(25)) is conclusive; Mahadeo Koli was declared ST as early as 1933/1942 and reiterated in 1950 — it is a hill tribe, not a sub-caste of Koli; Kolis are fishermen (OBC). The Kabade assumption that Mahadeo Koli was first recognised in 1976 was “not relatable to reality”.
  • Genetic/anthropological traits pass generation to generation; the affinity test cannot be evaded by a plea of modernisation; the father’s 1943 entries are the best evidence of caste (caste is acquired by birth); the documents produced were “manipulated and fabricated” to grab seats meant for genuine tribals.
Decision (appeal dismissed, subject to limited relief)
  1. Cancellation of the social-status certificates upheld: the Committee’s finding, based on sociological, anthropological and ethnological material and the father’s own pre-independence records, is a finding of fact not vitiated in judicial review; the Kabade reasoning was “wholly perverse and untenable”.
  2. Suchita: having completed the course except the final examination, she was permitted to sit the final MBBS examination, but not with ST status and with no future benefits; the direction is personal and “should not be treated and used as a precedent”.
  3. Madhuri: having approached an officer without jurisdiction and secured the certificate on the strength of her sister’s court order, she cannot continue with ST status/benefits; if eligible as a general candidate she may continue.
  4. On equity/estoppel: “A party that seeks equity must come with clean hands. He who comes to the court with false claim cannot plead equity… There is no estoppel as no promise of the social status is made by the State when a false plea was put forth… When it is found to be a case of fraud played by the concerned, no sympathy and equitable considerations can come to his rescue. Nor the plea of estoppel is germane to the beneficial constitutional concessions…”
  5. Fifteen guidelines issued to streamline issuance/scrutiny (summarised below).
The Madhuri Patel guidelines (1–15, condensed)
  1. Applications for social-status certificates to the Revenue Sub-Divisional Officer/Deputy Collector/Deputy Commissioner — not at Taluk/Mandal level.
  2. Sworn affidavit by parent/guardian/candidate with caste, sub-caste, tribe, original place and prescribed particulars.
  3. Verification application to the Scrutiny Committee at least six months before admission/appointment.
  4. Committee of three officers: Additional/Joint Secretary or above of the department; Director of Social/Tribal/Backward Class Welfare; and (for SCs) an officer experienced in verification; for STs a Research Officer with knowledge of tribe identification.
  5. Vigilance cell in each Directorate (Senior DySP in charge + Inspectors) to investigate claims at the native place; examine school records, birth registration, anthropological/ethnological traits, deity, rituals, customs, marriage and death ceremonies.
  6. Show-cause notice with vigilance report; reply within two weeks (max 30 days); if hearing sought, Committee hears with opportunity to adduce evidence; public notice by beat of drum; reasoned order.
  7. Favourable report ends matter; if particulars false/fraudulent, para 6 procedure applies.
  8. Notice to parent/guardian also where candidate is a minor.
  9. Inquiry completed expeditiously, preferably day-to-day, within two months; if false, certificate cancelled and confiscated; result communicated within one month.
  10. If admission deadline nears, provisional admission/appointment on certificate or affidavit, subject to scrutiny result.
  11. Committee’s order final, subject only to Art. 226 proceedings.
  12. No suit or other proceeding before any other authority.
  13. High Court to dispose of such matters within three months; single-judge order appealable only by Art. 136.
  14. False claim ⇒ prosecution of parent/guardian/candidate; conviction an offence involving moral turpitude attracting disqualification for elective posts.
  15. On cancellation, immediate communication to institution/appointing authority to cancel admission/appointment without further notice and debar the candidate.
Principles evolved
  • No estoppel, equity or promissory estoppel against fraud or against a statutory/constitutional status: a false social-status certificate creates no equity; the State makes no “promise” of status.
  • Presidential specification under Art. 342/366(25) is conclusive; courts cannot add to it (Marri Chandra Shekhar Rao v. Dean, Seth G.S. Medical College; Constitution Bench in Action Committee on issue of Caste Certificate).
  • Pre-constitution school/service entries of the father carry the greatest probative value, caste being acquired by birth; caste-association certificates are self-serving.
  • Parental fraud may visit the child (“Parents’ misconduct visits the children also many a times”) — but extreme relief may be tempered by completed study, without creating precedent.

22. Sanatan Gauda v. Berhampur University

AIR 1990 SC 1075 — Sawant, J. (Sharma, J. concurring) — DU Case Material LB-201, pp. 131–135 (full text)
Facts

The appellant passed M.A. in July 1981 with 364/900 (>40%) in the aggregate, but with only 13/100 in one Group-II paper. In 1983 he was admitted to the three-year LL.B. course at Ganjam Law College (affiliated to Berhampur University), submitting his mark-sheet and degree certificate with the application. He completed the Pre-Law course, was promoted to Inter-Law (1984), appeared in the Pre-Law and Inter-Law examinations (1985), and was admitted to the Final Law course. The University then refused to declare his results, stating (30-10-1986) that under Regulation 1, Ch. VIII read with Regulation 10, Ch. V he was ineligible: a candidate with less than 25% in any M.A. paper must have that paper excluded from the aggregate, leaving him below 39.5%. The Board of Studies (29-10-1986) had recommended that M.A. pass-outs with over 40% aggregate be treated eligible notwithstanding a paper below 20%. The Orissa High Court dismissed his writ petition (30-7-1987); the Supreme Court granted interim permission to continue and appear.

Issues
  1. Whether the 40%/39.5% aggregate requirement in Regulation 1, Ch. VIII applied to post-graduates at all.
  2. If the University’s reading were right, whether the University was estopped from refusing to declare results after having admitted him, issued admit cards, and allowed him to appear and progress to the Final year.
Arguments — Appellant
  • On a plain reading, Regulation 1’s percentage requirement applies only to graduates (B.A., B.Sc.…); a post-graduate need only have passed the higher degree. Regulation 10’s proviso excludes the sub-25% paper from the aggregate, leaving 351/800+ — above the 36% pass mark — so he satisfied even the alternative test. Bar Council Resolution No. 123/1984 (30-10-1984) also prescribes 39.5% only for graduates.
  • In any event, having submitted his true mark-sheet, been admitted, examined and promoted, the University is estopped from raising ineligibility at the result stage; the same High Court had granted relief in an identical case (O.J.C. 2619/1986).
Arguments — University (Shri P.N. Misra)
  • The 40%/39.5% requirement governs post-graduates too; after excluding the 13-mark paper the appellant fell short; the College prospectus (“aggregate of 40 per cent and above in the B.A.… or any other university degree or higher examination”) supports this.
  • The College Principal wrongly assured the University that eligibility was verified and never sent the marks list for verification (Annexures F, G, I); the University cannot be blamed for the Principal’s negligence, and the appellant cannot take advantage of the University’s having allowed him to appear.
Decision (appeal allowed)
  1. Construction: the first paragraph of Regulation 1 requires the percentage only of graduates; “a post-graduate student like the appellant… is not required to satisfy further that… he has secured 40 per cent… It is enough if he has passed his post-graduate examination.” Regulation 10’s proviso excludes the 13-mark paper, giving an aggregate above 36% — so he was duly qualified on either footing. The prospectus and BCI Resolution are “on par” with Regulation 1. The distinction between graduates and post-graduates is rational (“a post-graduate student has a minimum of two years more of academic pursuit”). “It is the interpretation placed by the University on its own Regulations… which is at fault.”
  2. Estoppel (independent ground): the appellant submitted his mark-sheet with the application; the College admitted him; he studied two years; the University granted admit cards for Pre-Law and Inter-Law and permitted him to appear; he was admitted to the Final year. It raised the objection only at the stage of declaration of results. “The University is, therefore, clearly estopped from refusing to declare the results… or from preventing him from pursuing his final year course.”
  3. Concurring opinion (Sharma, J.): the University “cannot punish the student for the negligence of the Principal or the University authorities”; the appellant “cannot be accused of making any false statement or suppressing any relevant fact”; it was the University’s “bounden duty… to have scrutinized the matter thoroughly before permitting the appellant to appear”. Parting observation: universities should frame rules in clear terms — “It is a serious matter if a student who acts upon one interpretation of a rule and spends a considerable period of his youth, is later threatened by a possible alternative construction…”
Principles evolved
  • Estoppel against an institution where the candidate is blameless (no fraud, no concealment) and the institution’s own organs acted on the true facts — the DU v. Ashok Kumar Chopra line applied; contrast Madhuri Patel where the candidate’s side perpetrated fraud.
  • Institutional negligence (Principal’s/University’s failure to verify) cannot be visited on the student.
  • Estoppel here operates as a rule of fair procedure/good conscience supplementing the correct construction of the regulations.
PYQ — LL.B. II Term, May–June 2024 (Paper LB-201, Q.5)

Q.5. “Anshul appeared for LL.B. entrance test of DU and obtained 5th rank in the merit list. His result of qualifying exam was still pending. He was provisionally admitted to the graduate course in law. In the meantime, his result of qualifying exam was declared and he as per the marks card issued, secured 60% marks. He appeared in the First and Second Term of law and was promoted to the Third Term. The university issued a notice to Anshul stating that it was by error that he was shown getting 60% marks. In fact, he had obtained only 45% marks, hence was not qualified to be a student of graduate course in law. University proceeds to cancel his admission. Can the University do so? Explain with reasons.” (20 marks)

Model answer (examination format)

Issue: whether the University is estopped under s. 121 BSA (s. 115 IEA) from cancelling an admission granted on its own erroneous tabulation, where the student committed no fraud.

Rule: s. 121 BSA requires (i) representation by declaration/act/omission; (ii) intentional causing of belief; (iii) action upon such belief altering the representee’s position. Estoppel binds even universities (University of Delhi v. Ashok Kumar Chopra, Del HC 1967), but not where the student obtains admission by fraud or concealment (same case; Madhuri Patel v. Addl. Commissioner, AIR 1995 SC 94). Where the candidate discloses true facts and the institution itself errs and lets him progress, the institution is estopped (Sanatan Gauda v. Berhampur University, AIR 1990 SC 1075 — University held estopped from withholding results after admitting the student, issuing admit cards and promoting him; “cannot punish the student for the negligence of the Principal or the University authorities”).

Application: Anshul’s case is Sanatan Gauda on all fours, and stronger: (a) his qualifying result was pending and he was admitted provisionally on the University’s own process — the representation (provisional admission, promotion to Third Term) was the University’s own act; (b) he neither misrepresented nor concealed — the 60% figure was the University’s own marks card; (c) he acted on the belief to his detriment — he pursued two terms, sat examinations, forgone other admissions/career options; (d) there is no fraud attracting the Madhuri Patel exception; the error is unilateral institutional error. Equity and the rule in Motilal Padampat (clear representation intended to be acted upon + altered position) reinforce the statutory estoppel.

Answer: No — the University cannot cancel his admission. Having provisionally admitted him, declared/issued the 60% marks card, allowed him to appear in two terms and promoted him to the Third Term, it is estopped from resiling to his prejudice; the proper course, at most, was to verify before admission (Sanatan Gauda, concurring opinion). Distinguish Madhuri Patel: there the certificates were fabricated and the claim false — here the candidate’s hands are clean.

Value-add: cite s. 121 BSA ingredients per Chhaganlal Keshavlal Mehta (8 ingredients); note that estoppel operates as a rule of evidence shutting out the University’s denial of the represented state of affairs (that Anshul was a duly admitted student).

Real-life worked examples
  • Tenant’s estoppel (s. 122): T inducts himself into a flat under a registered lease from L and pays rent for six years. In an eviction suit T pleads that L never owned the flat (title was in L’s brother). T is estopped from denying L’s title at the beginning of the tenancy — and, under the BSA’s added words, “or any time thereafter”.
  • Estoppel by silence: A watches B mortgage A’s land believing B to be owner, saying nothing although A knows the truth and is present at registration. A’s silence, where he was bound to speak, estops him against the mortgagee (illustrative of omission-based estoppel; cf. Ramsden v. Dyson in Maddanappa).
  • No estoppel against statute: a municipal corporation cannot be estopped by its officer’s ultra vires permission into sanctioning a building contrary to the master plan — estoppel yields to statute.

A(iii).2 Marital privilege in depth (s. 128 BSA / s. 122 IEA)

Component analysis
  1. Two branches (per Verghese): (1) a married person shall not be compelled to disclose any communication made to him during marriage by his spouse; (2) he shall not be permitted to disclose it in evidence — even voluntarily — unless the maker (or his representative-in-interest) consents.
  2. Subject matter: “communication” — oral or written (letters qualify), made during marriage; the bar “attaches at the time when the communication is made, and its admissibility will be adjudged in the light of the status at that date and not the status at the date when evidence is sought to be given” (Verghese, applying Moss v. Moss) — hence a later decree of divorce/nullity does not lift the bar.
  3. Who is barred: the recipient spouse as witness. The section does not bar proof of the communication by other means — e.g. by a third person into whose hands the letter voluntarily came, or by the document itself if otherwise provable (Verghese; Rumping v. DPP [1964] AC 814 — letter intercepted before delivery admissible; wife not called).
  4. Exceptions (three): (i) consent of the maker/representative; (ii) suits between married persons; (iii) proceedings in which one married person is prosecuted for any crime committed against the other.
  5. Not a rule of “no publication” in defamation: the English common-law notion that husband and wife are one person (so that communication of a libel by one to the other is no publication — Wenhak v. Morgan) has not been adopted in Indian criminal law (Queen Empress v. Butchi; Abdul Khadar v. Taib Begum; Tiruvengadda Mudali v. Tripurasundari Ammal — IPC exceptions exhaustive; no common-law gloss).

23. M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Poonan

AIR 1970 SC 1876 : (1969) 1 SCC 37 — J.C. Shah, J. — DU Case Material LB-201, pp. 136–139 (full text)
Facts

Rathi, daughter of M.C. Verghese, was married to T.J. Poonan. On 18, 25 and 30 July 1964 Poonan wrote three letters from Bombay to Rathi (then living with her parents at Trivandrum) containing, it is claimed, defamatory imputations concerning Verghese. Verghese filed a defamation complaint (s. 500 IPC) before the District Magistrate, Trivandrum. Poonan raised two preliminary objections: (1) the letters were inadmissible — barred by s. 122 IEA; (2) publication of a libel by a husband to his wife is no publication in law (Wenhak v. Morgan), so no offence. The District Magistrate accepted both and discharged Poonan (s. 253(2) CrPC). Sessions Court (in revision by Verghese) reversed: the unity-of-spouses doctrine has no application in India and s. 122 does not prohibit proof by the complainant. The Kerala High Court restored the discharge: the letters, if not provable either by the wife or through the father in whose hands she voluntarily placed them, fell outside the court’s cognizance. Appeal to the Supreme Court on certificate.

Issues
  1. Whether publication of defamatory matter by a husband to his own wife amounts to “publication” for s. 499 IPC.
  2. Whether s. 122 IEA (s. 128 BSA) renders the letters wholly unprovable, so that the complaint must fail at the threshold.
Arguments — Poonan (respondent)
  • Husband and wife are one in the eye of the law; communication to the spouse is communication to oneself — no publication (Wenhak).
  • The sole publication pleaded is to the wife; she is prohibited by s. 122 from disclosing; therefore no evidence of the offence can ever be led and the accused must be discharged.
  • (Appellant’s counter-point on status:) since Rathi later obtained a decree of nullity (impotency), the marital bar no longer operates.
Arguments — Verghese (appellant)
  • The unity doctrine is a feudal English fiction not received into Indian criminal law; IPC is an exhaustive code — no common-law exemptions may be invented.
  • s. 122 bars only the recipient spouse as a witness; it does not sterilise the document itself or other independent proof; the letters are in the complainant’s possession and can be tendered.
Decision (appeal allowed; enquiry remanded to the District Magistrate)
  1. On publication: the English rule rests on policy (Wenhak), but “the rule that husband and wife are one in the eye of law has not been adopted in its full force under our system of law and certainly not in our criminal jurisprudence” (Queen Empress v. Butchi — wife can commit theft of husband’s property; Abdul Khadar; Tiruvengadda Mudali — exceptions to s. 499 exhaustive). The Court, however, refrained from recording a final opinion, leaving it to the trial.
  2. On s. 122: “The section consists of two branches” — compulsion and permission. “Section 122… only prevents disclosure in evidence in court of the communication made by the husband to the wife. If Rathi appears in the witness box… prima facie the communications may not be permitted to be deposed to… unless Poonan consents. That does not, however, mean that no other evidence which is not barred under Section 122… can be given.” A prima facie case having been set up in the complaint, discharge without trial was unjustifiable.
  3. On Rumping v. DPP: House of Lords — a confessional letter written by the accused husband to his wife, intercepted and handed to police before delivery, was admissible; the crew-member, captain and translator gave evidence; the wife was not called; at common law there was never a rule excluding marital communications except where the recipient-spouse witness claims statutory privilege.
  4. On the nullity decree: argument rejected — “If the marriage was subsisting at the time when the communications were made, the bar prescribed by Section 122 will operate”; admissibility is judged at the date of the communication (Moss v. Moss: incompetence continues after decree absolute/nullity for matters arising during marriage).
  5. Whether the complainant, having received the letters from the wife, is her “agent” barred derivatively — left open for trial.
Principles evolved
  • s. 122/s. 128 is a witness-disability, not a document-immunity: it seals the lips of the recipient spouse; it does not exclude the communication from proof by other lawful means (third-party possession, interception before receipt).
  • The bar is fixed at the date of the communication — subsequent divorce/nullity is irrelevant.
  • English marital-unity fiction inapplicable to Indian criminal law; IPC exceptions are exhaustive.
  • Prelinary discharge on evidentiary speculation is improper where a prima facie case exists.

A(iii).3 State privilege — affairs of State (s. 129 BSA / s. 123 IEA) and the procedure (s. 165 BSA / s. 162 IEA)

Statute & procedure

s. 129 BSA: “No one shall be permitted to give any evidence derived from unpublished official records relating to any affairs of State, except with the permission of the officer at the head of the department concerned, who shall give or withhold such permission as he thinks fit.” s. 130 BSA: official communications in official confidence — public officer not compellable to disclose where public interest would suffer.

s. 165 BSA (s. 162 IEA) — the three limbs (per Raj Narain): (1) a witness summoned to produce a document shall bring it to court notwithstanding objection to production or admissibility — the validity of the objection shall be decided by the court; (2) the court, if it sees fit, may inspect the document unless it refers to matters of State, or take other evidence to determine admissibility; (3) translation limb.

Harmonised procedure (Sukhdev Singh → Raj Narain): (a) objection by affidavit of the head of the department (court may require the Minister’s affidavit; defective affidavit ⇒ further/better affidavit; affiant may be cross-examined); (b) court holds a preliminary enquiry into the character/class of the document — whether it relates to affairs of State; (c) court cannot enquire into the possible injury to public interest — that is for the authority; but (d) if not satisfied on affidavits the court may inspect (Amar Chand Butail; Raj Narain) and may order disclosure of innocuous parts sealing the noxious remainder; (e) ultimately the court is the final arbiter — it weighs the public interest in secrecy against the public interest in the administration of justice (Mathew, J.).

24. State of U.P. v. Raj Narain

AIR 1975 SC 865 : (1975) 4 SCC 428 — A.N. Ray, C.J.I., K.K. Mathew & H.R. Khanna, JJ. — DU Case Material LB-201, pp. 140–157 (full text)
Facts

In Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 (Allahabad High Court) Raj Narain challenged the election of Smt. Indira Gandhi from Rae Bareli. On 27-7-1973 he applied for summons for documents: (i) from the Secretary, General Administration, U.P. — Home/Defence Ministry circulars on security and tour arrangements for the Prime Minister’s Rae Bareli tours (1, 24, 25 Feb 1971) and all Centre–State and PM–CM correspondence on police arrangements and expenses; (ii) the same from the Chief Secretary; (iii) from the Head Clerk, S.P. Rae Bareli — tour-programme and police-arrangement documents. S.S. Saxena (Under Secretary) appeared with the documents and orally claimed privilege over the “Blue Book” (“Rules and Instructions for the Protection of the Prime Minister when on tour or in travel”) and the correspondence; the documents were kept in a sealed cover; thereafter R.K. Kaul (Home Secretary/head of department) filed an affidavit (19-9-1973) claiming that disclosure would injure public interest (secrecy of security arrangements); the S.P. also filed an affidavit. The learned Single Judge held that no privilege could be claimed: the Blue Book was not an “unpublished official record” because Rule 71(6) had been quoted in Parliament and filed in proceedings; and the S.P.’s documents, owing their existence to the Blue Book, could not be privileged either. Appeal to the Supreme Court.

Issues
  1. Whether the Blue Book and the correspondence were “unpublished official records relating to affairs of State” within s. 123 IEA (s. 129 BSA).
  2. The respective roles of the head of department’s affidavit, the court’s power of inspection, and the final arbiter of the privilege claim under ss. 123 and 162 IEA.
  3. Whether partial publication (quotation of a rule) destroys the “unpublished” character.
Arguments — State of U.P. (appellant)
  • The documents relate to security of the Prime Minister — quintessential affairs of State; the head of department’s affidavit is conclusive; disclosure would frustrate the very object of the rules; partial quotation in Parliament/writ proceedings does not publish the Blue Book (Duncan v. Cammell Laird).
Arguments — Raj Narain (respondent)
  • No affidavit by the head of department was filed at the time of the oral claim; the Blue Book is not unpublished (Rule 71(6) quoted in Parliament without objection, filed by the Union in a writ petition, and used by the respondent herself); the court has power — indeed duty — to inspect and to decide; public interest in fair trial and in the people’s right to know outweighs routine secrecy (Conway v. Rimmer; Rogers v. Home Secretary; US Reynolds).
Decision (High Court’s order set aside; matter remanded with directions)

Ray, C.J.I.: (1) The foundation of ss. 123 & 162 is that injury to public interest is the reason for exclusion; “public interest which demands that evidence be withheld is to be weighed against the public interest in the administration of justice that courts should have the fullest possible access to all relevant materials”; the court will proprio motu exclude evidence contrary to public interest. (2) Class vs contents: some documents are privileged by contents, others by class (Cabinet papers, Foreign Office despatches, security-of-State papers, high-level inter-departmental minutes). (3) Practice: objection by affidavit of head of department; court may require Minister’s affidavit; if satisfied, matter ends; if still in doubt the court may inspect; if on inspection a part is innocuous, that part may be ordered disclosed (without distortion), the noxious part being sealed. (4) Partial publication: “Any publication of parts of the Blue Book which may be described as innocuous… will not render the entire document a published one” (Cammell Laird applied). (5) Directions: Single Judge to consider Kaul’s affidavit; opportunity for affidavit re S.P.’s documents; then satisfaction or inspection; then orders.

Mathew, J.: The executive “is not the organ solely responsible for public interest”; administration of justice is another element; the court, with reference to the pending litigation, “will be in a better position to decide where the weight of public interest predominates”; s. 162 confers an overriding power on the court to decide the validity of the objection finally; the words “as he thinks fit” in s. 123 do not deter the court from deciding afresh after examining the document.

Khanna, J. (the celebrated passage): “In a government of responsibility like ours, where all the agents of the public must be responsible for their conduct, there can be but few secrets. The people of this country have a right to know every public act, everything that is done in a public way, by their public functionaries. They are entitled to know the particulars of every public transaction in all its bearing. The right to know, which is derived from the concept of freedom of speech… is a factor which should make one wary when secrecy is claimed for transactions which can, at any rate, have no repercussion on public security. To cover with a veil of secrecy the common routine business is not in the interest of the public… The responsibility of officials to explain and to justify their acts is the chief safeguard against oppression and corruption.” Inspection should be insisted upon save where national-security danger is reasonably shown (United States v. Reynolds).

Principles evolved
  • Court is the final arbiter of a claim of State privilege; the head of department’s affidavit starts, not ends, the enquiry (s. 162 IEA / s. 165 BSA).
  • Two-stage enquiry: (i) is the document an unpublished official record relating to affairs of State (character/class)? — court decides; (ii) would disclosure injure public interest, and does that injury outweigh the public interest in administration of justice? — balancing by the court.
  • Inspection power confirmed (departing from the stricter reading in Sukhdev Singh; following Amar Chand Butail and Conway v. Rimmer); severance of innocuous parts permitted.
  • Partial/innocuous publication does not declassify the whole record.
  • Right to know (germ of the Art. 19(1)(a) jurisprudence) is the counterweight to secrecy — the seed of S.P. Gupta v. President of India and the RTI regime.

Q.6(b). “A, a client, says to B, an attorney: ‘I have committed forgery, and I wish you to defend me.’ Is this statement protected from disclosure?” — Model answer: Yes. Real-life worked examples
  • Judicial privilege: In a perjury prosecution based on testimony given before Magistrate M, the prosecution cannot examine M on what the accused said in court without the special order of the Sessions Court (s. 127, Illustration (b)); but if the accused assaults the public prosecutor in court, M may testify to what occurred in his presence (Illustration (c)).
  • Professional privilege vs crime-fraud: A company’s counsel learns during the retainer that the client is currently siphoning funds through forged invoices (crime/fraud since commencement) — not protected; but the client’s confession of a past offence for which he seeks defence — protected forever.
  • State privilege today: A journalist’s RTI application for the file notings behind a demolition drive will be tested under s. 8(1)(i) (Cabinet papers) and the Raj Narain balance; routine administrative notings cannot be withheld merely because they are official (“veil of secrecy over common routine business” — Khanna, J.).

B(i).1 The two media — s. 2(1) “evidence” and the two rules of oral proof

Statute
s. 2(1) BSA (s. 3 IEA): “evidence” means and includes — (1) oral evidence: all statements which the Court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses, in relation to matters of fact under inquiry; (2) documentary evidence: all documents including electronic records produced for the inspection of the Court. B(i).3 Electronic & digital records — the BSA’s centrepiece

s. 61 BSA — NEWLY INSERTED PROVISION (syllabus-flagged)
s. 61, BSA 2023: “Nothing in this Adhiniyam shall apply to deny the admissibility of an electronic or digital record in the evidence on the ground that it is an electronic or digital record and such record shall, subject to section 63, have the same legal effect, validity and enforceability as other document.”

Why it matters (analysis): (1) It constitutionalises, within evidence law, the parity principle of s. 4 & s. 5 IT Act 2000 (legal recognition of electronic records/e-signatures) — no document may be treated as inadmissible merely because it is electronic; (2) the parity is subject to s. 63 — i.e. the computer-output route and its certificate regime still gate secondary electronic proof; (3) read with Explanations 4–7 to s. 57, electronic originals (server files, broadcast copies, temp files) are primary evidence in their own right — resolving the old “is a computer printout primary or secondary?” controversy of Anvar P.V.; (4) it reverses the burden of objection: the objector must show non-compliance with s. 63 for secondary proof, not the tenderer show “document-ness”.

ss. 62–63 BSA (= ss. 65A–65B IEA)
s. 62: “The contents of electronic records may be proved in accordance with the provisions of section 63.” (= s. 65A IEA.)
s. 63(1): information contained in an electronic record printed on paper, stored/recorded/copied in optical or magnetic media or semiconductor memory, produced by a computer or communication device (“computer output”) is deemed also a document if the four conditions are satisfied, and is admissible without further proof or production of the original as evidence of any contents of the original or of any fact stated therein of which direct evidence would be admissible.
s. 63(2) conditions: (a) output produced during the period the computer/device was used regularly to create/store/process information for an activity regularly carried on by the person having lawful control; (b) information of the kind was regularly fed in the ordinary course of that activity; (c) throughout the material part of the period the computer was operating properly or any malfunction did not affect the record/accuracy; (d) the information reproduces or is derived from information so fed.
s. 63(3): where the function was performed by one or more computers — widened (a) standalone; (b) on a computer system; (c) on a computer network; (d) on a computer resource enabling creation/processing/storage; (e) through an intermediary — all are treated as one single computer.
s. 63(4) certificate: where it is desired to give a statement in evidence by virtue of the section, a certificate (a) identifying the electronic record and describing the manner of production; (b) giving particulars of the device to show it was produced by a computer/communication device within sub-s. (3); (c) dealing with the sub-s. (2) conditions; and (d) purporting to be signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the device or management of the activities — shall be submitted “along with the electronic record at each instance where it is being submitted for admission” emphasis added by BSA; the certificate is evidence of the matters stated, which may be stated to the best of knowledge and belief.

PART B(ii)Exclusion of Oral Evidence by Documentary Evidence

BSA Ss. 94–95IEA Ss. 91–92 Chapter VI BSA 2023 (“Of the exclusion of oral evidence by documentary evidence”). This is the parol-evidence / best-evidence rule in its exclusionary form: once parties have embodied their transaction in a document, the document becomes the sole repository of its terms, and oral evidence is shut out both as to proof of the terms (s. 94) and as to variation of the terms (s. 95).

B(ii).1 Section 94 BSA / Section 91 IEA — document as exclusive proof of its own terms

Statute
s. 94, BSA 2023 (= s. 91 IEA) — “When the terms of a contract, or of a grant, or of any other disposition of property, have been reduced to the form of a document, and in all cases in which any matter is required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, no evidence shall be given in proof of the terms of such contract, grant or other disposition of property, or of such matter, except the document itself, or secondary evidence of its contents in cases in which secondary evidence is admissible under the provisions hereinbefore contained.”
Exception 1: public officer required by law to be appointed in writing — where a person is shown to have acted as such officer, the writing of appointment need not be proved. Exception 2: Wills admitted to probate in India may be proved by the probate.
Explanation 1: applies whether the contract/grant/disposition is in one document or several. Explanation 2: where there are more originals than one, one original only need be proved. Explanation 3: a statement in any document of a fact other than the facts referred to in the section does not preclude oral evidence as to that fact.
Illustrations: (a) contract contained in several letters — all letters must be proved; (b) contract in a bill of exchange — the bill must be proved; (c) bill drawn in a set of three — one need be proved; (d) written indigo contract reciting payment for other verbally-contracted indigo — oral evidence that no such payment was made is admissible (fact outside the terms); (e) receipt for money — oral evidence of the payment is admissible (a receipt is not a dispositive document of the transaction).
Elements & scope (DU notes Ch. 10 + Roop Kumar)
  1. Trigger: terms of (i) a contract, (ii) a grant, (iii) any other disposition of property, reduced to documentary form; or (iv) any matter required by law to be in documentary form (e.g. registered sale under s. 54 TPA + s. 17 Registration Act; wills; leases ≥ one year; company articles).
  2. Effect: exclusive mode of proof — the document itself (or admissible secondary evidence); oral testimony of the terms is inadmissible, not merely of inferior weight.
  3. Width: applies to all documents, dispositive or not, bilateral and unilateral alike (Roop Kumar difference-table, col. 1); it is concerned solely with the mode of proof.
  4. Prima facie condition: ss. 94–95 apply only where the document on the face of it contains or appears to contain all the terms (Roop Kumar).
  5. Explanation 3 safety-valve: recitals of collateral facts (e.g. receipt of money, prior verbal bargain) remain open to oral proof — Illustrations (d), (e).

B(ii).2 Section 95 BSA / Section 92 IEA — no oral variation of documented terms

Statute
s. 95, BSA 2023 (= s. 92 IEA) — “When the terms of any such contract, grant or other disposition of property, or any matter required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, have been proved according to section 94, no evidence of any oral agreement or statement shall be admitted, as between the parties to any such instrument or their representatives in interest, for the purpose of contradicting, varying, adding to, or subtracting from, its terms:” followed by six provisos —
  1. Proviso 1 (invalidating facts): any fact may be proved which would invalidate the document or entitle a person to a decree/order relating to it — fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution, want of capacity, want or failure of consideration, mistake in fact or law (Illustrations (d) misrepresentation as to mine value; (e) rectification for mistake).
  2. Proviso 2 (silence of document): a separate oral agreement on a matter on which the document is silent and which is not inconsistent with its terms may be proved; the Court shall regard the degree of formality of the document (Illustrations (f) goods sold silent on time of payment — credit term provable; (g) “Bought of A a horse for thirty thousand rupees” — verbal warranty of soundness provable; (h) informal card “Rooms, ten thousand rupees a month” — verbal agreement as to partial board provable, but a formally drawn stamped yearly agreement would exclude it).
  3. Proviso 3 (condition precedent): a separate oral agreement constituting a condition precedent to the attaching of any obligation under the instrument may be proved.
  4. Proviso 4 (subsequent rescission/modification): a distinct subsequent oral agreement to rescind or modify may be proved — except where the instrument is by law required to be in writing or has been registered (Illustration (i)? — the registered-deed carve-out preserves s. 48 Registration Act’s policy).
  5. Proviso 5 (usage or custom): any usage or custom by which incidents not expressly mentioned are usually annexed to contracts of that description may be proved, provided (Proviso 6) the annexure is not repugnant to or inconsistent with the express terms.
  6. Proviso 6/7 (language-to-facts): any fact may be proved which shows in what manner the language of a document is related to existing facts (bridge to ss. 96–101 BSA on ambiguity).
Illustrations of the rule: (a) policy of insurance on goods “in ships from Kolkata to Visakhapatnam” — an oral exception of the particular ship cannot be proved; (b) written absolute promise to pay Rs 1,000 on 1 March — a simultaneous oral agreement postponing payment to 31 March cannot be proved; (c) sale of “the Rampur tea estate” with a map — oral claim that unmapped land was meant to pass cannot be proved.
Scope limits (DU notes)
  • Parties only: the bar operates “as between the parties to any such instrument or their representatives in interest” — a stranger to the document may lead oral evidence (cf. s. 102 BSA/s. 99 IEA: who may give evidence of agreement varying terms).
  • Bilateral/dispositive documents are s. 95’s field (Roop Kumar, difference 2).
  • Timing: s. 94 attacks proof of the terms at any time; s. 95 attacks oral agreements contemporaneous or prior to the document (subsequent agreements ride Proviso 4).
  • Not a rule of relevancy but of exclusion: the oral agreement may be true; the law simply refuses to hear it — hence its place in this Unit (“facts parties are prohibited from proving”).

Roop Kumar v. Mohan Thedani

SC, 2 April 2003 (MANU/SC/0276/2003) — DU notes Ch. 10.4 (the leading synthesis of ss. 91–92 / 94–95)
Propositions laid down
  • Supplementary: “Sections 91 and 92 in effect supplement each other. Section 91 would be inoperative without the aid of Section 92, and similarly Section 92 would be inoperative without the aid of Section 91.”
  • Prima facie completeness: both apply only when the document on the face of it contains or appears to contain all the terms of the contract.
  • Best-evidence foundation: both provisions are based on the “best evidence rule”.
  • Grounds of exclusion of extrinsic (oral) evidence: (i) to admit inferior evidence when the law requires superior would amount to nullifying the law; (ii) when parties have deliberately put their agreement in writing, it is conclusively presumed, as between themselves and their privies, that they intended the writing to be the full and final statement of their intentions — placed “beyond the reach of future controversy, bad faith and treacherous memory”.
  • Difference table (as extracted in DU notes): s. 91 applies to all documents (dispositive or not), bilateral and unilateral, and concerns the mode of proof; s. 92 applies only to dispositive, bilateral documents and limits only the parties.
Connected law on the parol-evidence rule
  • Registration Act 1908, s. 48: registered documents relating to property take effect against oral agreements to the contrary — the proprietary reinforcement of Proviso 4 to s. 95; s. 17 (compulsory registration) fixes the “required by law to be in writing” class for s. 94.
  • Transfer of Property Act 1882: ss. 54, 59, 106, 118, 123 (sale, lease, gift — registered instrument); s. 92 proviso lineage for redemption terms.
  • Indian Contract Act 1872: s. 92’s provisos mirror contract doctrine — fraud/mistake (ss. 17–22), condition precedent (s. 5?), usage (s. 13), novation/rescission (s. 62); Companies Act 2013 s. 27 (variation of contract terms in prospectus) is a linked provision to s. 94 BSA.
  • Indian Succession Act 1925: wills — Exception 2 to s. 94 (probate proves the will); s. 103 BSA (s. 100 IEA) saves s. 63-line provisions on proof of wills from s. 95’s bar.
  • Negotiable Instruments Act 1881: bill of exchange as exclusive proof of its terms (Illustrations (b), (c) to s. 94).
  • CPC 1908: Order VII (production of documents with plaint), s. 34 (interest) — procedural interfaces; rectification suits under Specific Relief Act 1963 s. 26 are the vehicle for Proviso 1 mistake/fraud facts.
Real-life worked examples
  • Registered sale deed reciting Rs 50 lakh: the vendor cannot orally testify that the true price was Rs 80 lakh (“the rest in cash”) — s. 95 bar; but the vendee may prove by oral evidence that the deed was obtained by fraud/coercion (Proviso 1) or that possession was to be delivered only after a temple festival (Proviso 3, condition precedent), or that the parties later orally rescinded — not provable, since the deed is registered (Proviso 4 carve-out).
  • Lease deed silent on parking: tenant may prove a contemporaneous oral agreement granting two parking slots — document silent, not inconsistent; but the more formal the deed (registered, stamped, lawyer-drawn), the heavier the scrutiny (degree of formality, Proviso 2).
  • Trade usage: a written contract for sale of 100 bales of cotton “ex-ship” may be supplemented by proof of the mercantile usage as to landing and weighing (Proviso 5), unless repugnant to express terms (Proviso 6).
  • Receipt vs contract: a mere receipt (“received Rs 5,000”) does not attract s. 94’s exclusivity for the underlying transaction — Illustration (e); oral evidence of the payment and of the bargain remains open.
Model Q&A (DU pattern)

Q. “Distinguish ss. 94 and 95 BSA (ss. 91–92 IEA) and state when oral evidence is nevertheless admissible.” — Answer skeleton: (1) both flow from best-evidence rule and the conclusive-presumption rationale (Roop Kumar); (2) s. 94 = rule of mode of proof (document alone proves its terms; all documents; bilateral & unilateral; Exceptions 1–2; Explanations 1–3); s. 95 = rule of exclusion of variation (parties/representatives only; dispositive bilateral documents; six provisos); (3) s. 94 precedes s. 95 (“have been proved according to section 94”); (4) admissible oral evidence: collateral facts (Expl. 3), the six provisos, strangers to the document (s. 102 BSA), and ambiguity-resolution evidence under ss. 96–101 BSA.

Model Answer 1

Q. “Estoppel is a rule of evidence rooted in equity. Discuss the doctrine under the BSA 2023 with its ingredients, kinds and limitations, referring to leading decisions.”

Answer

Introduction. Among “facts which the parties are prohibited from proving”, estoppel occupies the first place: though the fact be relevant, the law closes the mouth of the party who previously represented the opposite. The maxim is allegans contraria non est audiendus; the English fountainhead Pickard v. Sears; in India the doctrine is codified as a rule of evidence in s. 121 BSA (s. 115 IEA), with special estoppels in ss. 122–123 (ss. 116–117 IEA).

Provision & ingredients. Quote s. 121. Decompose: representation (declaration/act/omission, including silence where there is a duty to speak); intentional causing or permitting of belief; belief in an existing fact (clear and unambiguous, not a promise de futuro); action upon belief; alteration of position to detriment; proximate cause; ignorance of truth by the claimant; inter-partes operation; burden on the party pleading. (Cite the eight ingredients of Chhaganlal Keshavlal Mehta and the three cumulative requirements of University of Delhi v. Ashok Kumar Chopra.)

Kinds. By record (res judicata/issue-estoppel, CPC s. 11); by deed/writing (cf. ss. 94–95 BSA, Registration Act); in pais/by representation — the codified class; promissory estoppel (equity: High Trees, Indo-Afghan, Motilal Padampat, with Jit Ram as counterweight). Special statutory estoppels: tenant & licensee (s. 122 — note BSA’s addition “or any time thereafter”), acceptor/bailee/licensee (s. 123), and NI Act ss. 120–122.

Case law. (i) Maddanappa v. Chandramma: estoppel fails where the representee knew the truth; detriment indispensable; s. 115 is the exhaustive code — no free-roaming “equitable estoppel”; acquiescence (Ramsden v. Dyson) explained. (ii) Sanatan Gauda v. Berhampur University: estoppel against an institution where the student is blameless; institutional negligence not visited on the student. (iii) Madhuri Patel: no estoppel or equity against fraud or against a statutory/constitutional status; fifteen guidelines. (iv) Motilal Padampat: estoppel vs waiver distinguished; promissory estoppel against the State, subject to statute and public interest.

Limitations. No estoppel against statute/Constitution; none against known truth; none founded on fraud/concealment; none without detriment; none on pure questions of law or jurisdiction; representation must be of fact.

Critique & conclusion. The section is evidential in form but equitable in spirit; the BSA’s extension of the tenant’s estoppel (“or any time thereafter”) resolves a long controversy; the doctrine remains “a shield, not a sword” — it excludes proof, it does not create causes of action. Conclude with the balance struck by the three 1990s–60s decisions: honesty and good faith protected, fraud and statutory policy never defeated.

Q. “Privileged communications are relevant but inadmissible. Examine the categories of privilege under the BSA 2023, with special reference to marital and State privilege and the decisions of the Supreme Court.”

Answer

Introduction. Privilege subordinates the search for truth to higher policy values — the independence of the judiciary, the sanctity of marriage, the security of the State, the confidence of the client. Ss. 127–134 BSA (ss. 121–129 IEA) codify eight such privileges; each bars disclosure even of relevant matter, and the court must enforce the bar of its own motion.

Categories. Tabulate the eight (judicial; marital; affairs of State; official communications; informers; professional; no-waiver-by-volunteering; confidential communications with legal adviser) with holders and rationales; keep s. 126 BSA (spousal competency) distinct.

Marital privilege (s. 128/s. 122). Two limbs — no compulsion, no permission; consent of maker; exceptions: interspousal suits and prosecution of one spouse for crime against the other; bar fixed at date of communication (nullity/divorce irrelevant — Moss v. Moss applied in Verghese); the privilege is a witness-disability, not document-immunity: M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Poonan (intercepted/third-party proof survives; Rumping v. DPP); English marital-unity fiction (Wenhak v. Morgan) rejected in Indian criminal law (Queen Empress v. Butchi, Abdul Khadar, Tiruvengadda Mudali).

State privilege (ss. 129–130/ss. 123–124 + procedure s. 165 BSA/s. 162 IEA). Unpublished official records relating to affairs of State; head of department’s permission; the three limbs of s. 162; the harmonised procedure of Sukhdev Singh as refined in State of U.P. v. Raj Narain: affidavit first, court’s preliminary enquiry into character/class, power of inspection, severance of innocuous parts, and the ultimate balancing — “public interest which demands that evidence be withheld is to be weighed against the public interest in the administration of justice”; Khanna J.’s right to know; partial publication does not declassify; Mathew J.: court the final arbiter. Legacy: S.P. Gupta, RTI Act 2005 s. 8.

Professional privilege (s. 132/s. 126). Communication, documents, advice; survives retainer; two exceptions (illegal purpose; crime/fraud since commencement) with the three illustrations; extension to interpreters/clerks (s. 132(3)); waiver only by express consent (s. 133, s. 134); BCI Rules r. 17 and Advocates Act s. 35 as disciplinary twins.

Conclusion. Privileges are narrowly construed but firmly enforced; Verghese and Raj Narain together show the modern temper — privileges protect relationships and the State’s essential secrets, never the suppression of provable truth through technicality.

Q. “‘Oral evidence must be direct’ and ‘documents must be proved by primary evidence’ — explain these twin best-evidence rules and the regime for electronic and digital records under the BSA 2023, highlighting s. 61 as a newly inserted provision.”

Answer

Introduction. Evidence is oral or documentary (s. 2(1)); each medium has a best-evidence rule: s. 55 BSA (s. 60 IEA) for orality — the percipient witness; s. 59 BSA (s. 64 IEA) for documents — the original. Kalyan Kumar Gogoi supplies the analytical preamble (three senses of “evidence”).

Oral evidence. Four limbs of directness (seen/heard/perceived/opinion); two provisos (expert treatises; production of material things); the hearsay ban and its statutory inroads (res gestae s. 4; conspiracy s. 8; admissions/confessions; dying declarations; expert treatises) with Sukhar, Navjot Sandhu, Khushal Rao; State of Bihar v. Ajit Kumar (“direct evidence rule”); Dr. Praful Desai (video-conferencing satisfies directness).

Documentary evidence. s. 56 (contents by primary or secondary); s. 57 primary (Explanations 1–7, noting BSA’s electronic Explanations 4–7); s. 58 secondary (eight categories, three new); s. 59 rule; s. 60 gateways (a)–(g) + Explanation pairing gateways with species; s. 64 notice to produce; ss. 65–73 execution/identification; ss. 74–77 public documents and certified copies; presumptions ss. 78–93. Cases: Kalyan Singh v. Chhoti; Ashok Dulichand (photocopy without pedigree); R.M. Malkani (tape-recording = document).

Electronic records. s. 61 (new): parity clause — no inadmissibility merely because electronic; same legal effect subject to s. 63. s. 62 (=65A). s. 63 (=65B): computer output deemed document on four conditions; single-computer rule widened (standalone/system/network/intermediary); certificate under s. 63(4) to accompany the record at each instance of submission; case-law saga Anvar → Shafhi → Khotkar (certificate a condition precedent; oral evidence cannot substitute) → Ravinder Singh @ Kaku; compliant practice illustrated by Mukesh (Nirbhaya). IT Act 2000 ss. 3, 3A, 4, 5, 7, 11, 13, 14 as the definitional and recognition backbone.

Conclusion. The BSA completes the migration begun by the IT Act 2000: the medium is neutral (s. 61), the safeguards are procedural (s. 63), and the best-evidence instinct survives through Explanations 4–7 to s. 57 which treat the electronic original as primary.

Model Answer 4

Q. “When a contract is reduced to writing, oral evidence is excluded. Critically examine ss. 94–95 BSA (ss. 91–92 IEA) with their exceptions and the decision in Roop Kumar v. Mohan Thedani.”

Answer

Introduction. Chapter VI BSA embodies the parol-evidence rule: the document is the best and, as between parties, the exclusive evidence of its own terms — “to admit inferior evidence when law requires superior would amount to nullifying the law”, and a deliberate writing is conclusively presumed the full and final statement of the parties’ intention, placed “beyond the reach of future controversy, bad faith and treacherous memory” (Roop Kumar).

s. 94. Trigger (contract/grant/disposition/matter required by law to be written); exclusive proof by document or admissible secondary; Exceptions 1–2; Explanations 1–3 (multi-document contracts; multiple originals; collateral facts open to oral proof); Illustrations (letters; bill of exchange; receipt).

s. 95. After terms proved per s. 94, no oral evidence between parties/representatives to contradict, vary, add to or subtract from terms; the six provisos (invalidating facts — fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution/capacity/consideration, mistake; silent-matter oral agreement with degree-of-formality test; condition precedent; subsequent oral rescission/modification except where writing or registration is required; usage/custom; language-to-facts); Illustrations (insurance policy; postponed payment; Rampur tea estate map; horse warranty; rooms-and-board card).

Roop Kumar synthesis. Sections supplement each other; prima facie completeness of the document required; difference-table (s. 94 all documents/mode of proof; s. 92 dispositive bilateral/parties only); best-evidence foundation.

Critical examination. The rule protects certainty but can shelter fraud — hence Proviso 1; the “degree of formality” test injects judicial discretion; the registration carve-out in Proviso 4 aligns with s. 48 Registration Act; strangers remain free (s. 102 BSA); ambiguity provisions (ss. 96–101) complete the scheme. Conclusion: properly applied, ss. 94–95 secure transactions without sterilising justice.

Examiner’s traps to avoid
  • Citing IEA numbers without BSA equivalents (the 2024–25 papers expect BSA first).
  • Treating s. 61 BSA as a copy of s. 65B — it is a parity clause; the certificate regime is s. 63(4).
  • Saying secondary evidence of a public document can be a photocopy — only a certified copy (Explanation to s. 60; s. 75).
  • Claiming marital privilege bars all proof of a spousal letter — it bars only the recipient-spouse witness (Verghese).
  • Asserting the head of department’s affidavit is conclusive — the court remains final arbiter (Raj Narain).
  • Forgetting that estoppel needs detriment and fails against a party who knew the truth (Maddanappa).
  • Confusing s. 94 (mode of proof, all documents) with s. 95 (variation bar, parties to dispositive documents) — use the Roop Kumar table.
