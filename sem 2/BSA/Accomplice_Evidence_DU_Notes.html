PART 0The Statutory Framework

0.1 The two provisions, side by side

Statute
s. 138, BSA 2023 (“Accomplice”) [= s. 133, IEA 1872] — “An accomplice shall be a competent witness against an accused person; and a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds upon the uncorroborated testimony of an accomplice.”
s. 119, BSA 2023 (“Court may presume existence of certain facts”) [= s. 114, IEA 1872] — “The Court may presume the existence of any fact which it thinks likely to have happened, regard being had to the common course of natural events, human conduct and public and private business, in their relation to the facts of the particular case.” Illustration (b): “That an accomplice is unworthy of credit, unless he is corroborated in material particulars.”

The apparent antinomy and its resolution. Section 138 declares competency and legality (“shall”; rule of law); Illustration (b) to s. 119 declares credibility (“may presume”; rule of prudence/precaution). Reading them together, the courts in India have held — in the words of the Privy Council in Bhuboni Sahu v. The King — that “whilst it is not illegal to act upon the uncorroborated evidence of an accomplice, it is a rule of prudence so universally followed as to amount almost to a rule of law that it is unsafe to act upon the evidence of an accomplice unless it is corroborated in material respects so as to implicate the accused”. There is no antithesis between the two, because the illustration raises only a rebuttable “may” presumption, not a presumptio juris et de jure (Dagdu v. State of Maharashtra, (1977) 1 SCC 543, as briefed in DU notes).

PART 1Who is an Accomplice? Approver, Co-accused, Pardon

1.1 Meaning — the Act is silent; the word takes its ordinary sense

Concept

Neither the BSA nor the IEA defines “accomplice”. The word is used in only three places in the IEA — ss. 114 Illustration (b), 133 and 156 (BSA: 119 Ill. (b), 138 and 159) — and nowhere explained. The courts therefore give it its ordinary meaning:

“An accomplice is a person who is supposed to have taken part in the commission of a crime and is one who is supposed to have been associated with the accused in the commission of the same crime … a guilty associate in the commission of the offence.”

Jagannath v. Emperor, AIR 1942 Oudh 221 (as briefed in DU notes): whether a person is an accomplice depends on the facts of each particular case and must be determined by asking whether he could have been charged with the accused in respect of the same offence. R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration, AIR 1962 SC 1821: the Supreme Court followed the definition given by Lord Simonds in Davies v. DPP, [1954] AC 378.

Davies v. DPP — the four classes of accomplices (Lord Simonds):

  1. Principals in the first degree — the actual perpetrators who physically commit the act;
  2. Principals in the second degree — aiders and abettors who are present at the commission of the crime, supporting the actual perpetrator;
  3. Accessories before the fact — aiders and abettors who help to plan or provide for the execution of the crime but are not present when it is committed;
  4. Receivers (of stolen goods) — persons who have received stolen property knowing it to have been stolen, on an indictment alleging a joint felony with the thief.

Cautions extracted from the same discussion in DU notes: (i) an accessory after the fact (one who merely shelters the criminal knowing him to be a felon — e.g. the common harbouring offences, now BNS ss. 274–276 / IPC ss. 216, 212) is not automatically an accomplice to the principal crime — he must have some participation in the crime itself or in the conspiracy to commit it; (ii) accessories before the fact are accomplices only if they participated in the preparation of the crime; (iii) accomplice status requires connection with the same crime — participation in some other, different crime makes the witness at most an interested/participating witness of that other offence, not an accomplice of the offence on trial.

1.3 Persons who are NOT accomplices

Exclusions
  1. A witness not involved in the crime being charged;
  2. A person who commits an offence under threat or compulsion and participates unwillingly — not a willing accomplice (compulsion under duress, e.g. a person forced at gunpoint to drive a getaway car; cf. also the general exception of compulsion, BNS s. 22 / IPC s. 94);
  3. A witness who merely knows of the crime, witnesses it, or fails to disclose it out of fear;
  4. Detectives, paid informers and trap witnesses — not accomplices; they are interested witnesses whose testimony is scrutinised on its own merits (their presence in a trap, e.g. a corruption sting, does not by itself make them participes criminis);
  5. The victim of the offence (e.g. the prosecutrix or the cheated customer) — never an accomplice;
  6. An accessory after the fact who merely harboured the criminal without participation in the crime or conspiracy (subject to the cautions above — receivers of stolen property, per Davies class 4, can be accomplices in the joint trial for the felony).

Why it matters: if the witness is not an accomplice, s. 119 Ill. (b) / s. 114 Ill. (b) is not attracted and corroboration is not insisted upon as a matter of prudence (only ordinary credibility scrutiny).

1.4 Accomplice vs Co-accused (confessing) — the vital distinction

An accomplice who is not jointly tried testifies on oath as a witness, is subject to cross-examination, and his testimony is “evidence” within the BSA/IEA definition — but it is tainted and calls for corroboration in material particulars. A co-accused (including an accomplice who is jointly tried and does not turn witness) is examined only through his confession under s. 28 BSA / s. 30 IEA: no oath, no cross-examination, technically not “evidence”, and usable only to lend assurance to other substantive evidence — it cannot itself found a conviction.

1.5 Accomplice vs Approver

Statute — Tender of Pardon

The BSA/IEA speak only of an “accomplice”. The word “approver” belongs to the procedural codes: an accomplice granted pardon on condition of making a full and true disclosure of every circumstance of the offence and of every other person concerned. Once pardoned he is tendered as a prosecution witness — but for evidentiary purposes he remains an accomplice under s. 138 BSA and the Ill. (b) prudence continues to apply in full.

BNSS s. 343 (1) [= CrPC s. 306(1)]: where, in any case triable exclusively by the Court of Session or in a case punishable with imprisonment extending to seven years or more under ss. 34, 109, 114, 115, 116 or 120-B IPC (now BNS ss. 3(5) and the abetment/conspiracy chapter) the Chief Judicial Magistrate or a Metropolitan Magistrate or a First Class Magistrate inquiring into or trying the offence considers it necessary to obtain the evidence of any person supposed to have been directly or indirectly concerned in or privy to the offence, he may tender pardon to such person on condition of his making a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within his knowledge relative to the offence and to every other person concerned.
BNSS s. 343 (2)–(4) [= CrPC s. 306(2)–(4)]: pardon may be tendered at any stage of the investigation or inquiry or trial; the person to whom pardon is tendered shall be examined as a witness in the case; before the Magistrate committing the case for trial, and at the trial itself, and shall be kept in custody until the termination of the trial unless he is already on bail. The Magistrate shall record his reasons for tendering pardon (BNSS s. 343(2) / CrPC s. 306(2)(a)).
BNSS s. 344 [= CrPC s. 307]: after commitment but before judgment, the Court of Session may tender (or direct the committing Magistrate to tender) pardon on the same terms — so that the trial court itself can convert an accomplice into an approver where the necessity emerges during trial.
BNSS s. 345 [= CrPC s. 308]: trial of a person not complying — if the approver wilfully conceals anything essential, gives false evidence, or otherwise fails to fulfil the condition, the pardon is forfeited: he may be tried for the original offence and also for giving false evidence; his statement accepting pardon and the proceedings are admissible against him; he must plead the pardon as a defence, failing which the court may take it into consideration.

1.6 Who must prove accomplice character? (burden)

Doctrine

If the accused alleges that a prosecution witness is an accomplice (to attract the Ill. (b) presumption and demand corroboration), the burden lies on the accused to prove it; but the prosecution has a duty to inform the court if a witness is in fact an accomplice, and the court must itself consider the point. Once a person is tendered pardon and examined for the prosecution, the accomplice character is admitted by the very act of pardon.

Real Example

Bank robbery with a lookout. A, B and C rob a bank: A fires at the guard, B cracks the safe, C stands watch outside in the car and drives the three away. C is arrested first and, the evidence being otherwise thin, the Magistrate tenders him pardon under BNSS s. 343. At trial C deposes on oath against A and B. C is an approver and therefore an accomplice: A is a principal in the first degree; B likewise; C is a principal in the second degree / accessory before the fact (he aided and planned though he stayed outside — under Davies class 3 he counts if he participated in preparation; a lookout in the getaway car is conventionally treated as an accomplice). The court may lawfully convict A and B on C’s uncorroborated testimony (s. 138), but prudentially will look for independent evidence connecting each of A and B to the robbery in material particulars — e.g. CCTV of the car, recovery of C’s share of the loot, fingerprint of B on the safe. If, however, C falsely ropes in D (A’s enemy) who slept at home that night, this is exactly the “ten people, eight true, two false” danger described in Bhuboni Sahu — and D’s villager-alibi problem (only family evidence, interested) makes corroboration of C’s case against D indispensable.

PART 2Evidentiary Value: The Double Test and Corroboration

2.1 Why caution? The five dangers (DU notes + Bhuboni Sahu)

Doctrine

The evidence of an accomplice must be accepted with great caution and scrutiny because:

  1. Participation taints the source — he is a participant in the actual commission of the crime; on his own admission a man of bad character who, to save himself, betrayed his former associates;
  2. He is not viewed with the respect due to a law-abiding citizen; being immoral he may commit perjury on occasion;
  3. He is in a position in which he can hardly fail to have a strong bias in favour of the prosecution — having hoped for or secured pardon, he favours the State’s case (“seeking to purchase his immunity”);
  4. The real danger (per Rankin C.J. in Emperor v. Lalit Mohan, adopted in Bhuboni Sahu): he may tell a story true in its general outline into which it is easy to work untrue matter — “He may implicate ten people … and the story may be true in all its details as to eight of them, but untrue as to the other two, whose names have been introduced because they are enemies of the approver.” This tendency to include the innocent with the guilty is, Judges have noted, peculiarly prevalent in India;
  5. Alibi difficulty — an Indian villager charged with taking part in a night crime while in fact asleep in his hut or guarding his crops can only rely on the interested evidence of wife, family or friends, which carries little weight; the court cannot easily detect the false implication.

Conclusion of Bhuboni Sahu: “The only real safeguard against the risk of condemning the innocent with the guilty lies in insisting upon independent evidence which in some measure implicates each accused.”

2.2 The double test for an approver’s / accomplice’s evidence

Rule

An accomplice, by becoming an approver, becomes a prosecution witness; his evidence must satisfy a double test (DU companion notes; formulated in Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana):

Test 1 — Reliability. The story he relates must involve him in the crime and appear intrinsically to be a natural and probable catalogue of events; given in minute details according with reality, it is likely to save it from being rejected brevi manu.
Test 2 — Corroboration. Once that hurdle is crossed, the story, so far as the accused on trial is concerned, must implicate him in such a manner as to give rise to a conclusion of guilt beyond reasonable doubt — ordinarily through corroboration in material particulars from independent credible evidence.
“Every approver comes to give evidence in some such manner seeking to purchase his immunity and that is why to start with he is an unreliable person and the rule of caution calling for material corroboration is constantly kept in mind by the court by time-worn judicial practice.” — Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana

Rare-case exception: “In a rare case taking into consideration all the factors, circumstances and situations governing a particular case, conviction based on the uncorroborated evidence of an approver confidently held to be true and reliable by the Court may be permissible” — lawful under s. 138 BSA, but the exception, not the rule.

2.4 Corroboration by confessions of other co-accused — the Bhuboni–Haroon line

Doctrine
  • Bhuboni Sahu (PC 1949): the Privy Council declined to act on an approver’s evidence supported only by the retracted confession of a co-accused (Trinath). The two had been “nothing but hired assassins” with “ample opportunity of preparing their statements in concert”; the approver had sworn to two contradictory stories; and the co-accused had denied his confession. Conviction set aside.
  • Haroon Haji (SC 1968): a co-accused’s confession recorded by customs officers under s. 171-A of the Sea Customs Act (now s. 108 of the Customs Act, 1962) is a confession for s. 30 IEA / s. 28 BSA if proved voluntary — such statements enjoy none of the safeguards of a judicially recorded confession (CrPC s. 164 / BNSS s. 183), so voluntariness must be scrutinised with special care. If voluntary, the confession may be used against its maker and against co-accused jointly tried for the same offence — to lend assurance to the accomplice’s testimony. Bengali’s confession, retracted but held voluntary and true, furnished sufficient corroboration of the approver Kashinath. A confession by a co-accused who had absconded before judgment (Noor Mohammad) was not usable — “jointly tried” requirement not satisfied in substance.
  • Net rule: an independent, voluntary confession of a jointly tried co-accused may corroborate an accomplice as against each of them; but a retracted confession, or one made by a person with opportunity to concert stories, will not satisfy the prudent judge without more.
Real Example

Smuggling ring. Customs officers raid a beach landing at 2 a.m.; gold biscuits pass from a launch to waiting trucks. Suspects are served notices and their statements recorded under s. 108 of the Customs Act, 1962 (successor of s. 171-A Sea Customs Act). Suspect K’s statement implicates himself and the financer H. K is then tendered pardon and testifies. Against H the court now has: (i) K’s testimony (accomplice evidence); (ii) K’s own earlier s. 108 statement — no corroboration (same source, Haroon Haji); (iii) the confession of co-accused B, jointly tried, voluntarily given though later retracted, describing H’s role — may lend assurance if the court is satisfied of its voluntariness and truth (Haroon Haji); (iv) recovery of a portion of the consignment from H’s godown and call-detail records placing H at the landing — independent corroboration in material particulars. Item (iv), plus (iii), makes conviction safe.

Case 25 — Bhuboni Sahu v. The King

AIR 1949 PC 257 : (1949) 51 Cr LJ 872 • Privy Council • Sir John Beaumont • Appeal by special leave from the High Court of Patna • IEA ss. 114 Ill. (b), 133, 157; CrPC ss. 164, 288, 30 (IEA)
Facts
  • On 11 October 1946, one Kalia Behara, who lived at Berhampur and carried on business as a jutka (pony-cart) driver, was brutally murdered at a place between Berhampur and Golantra, to which he was driving with passengers in his jutka.
  • Eight persons were charged with the murder (s. 302 r/w s. 34 IPC — now BNS s. 103 r/w s. 3(5)) and tried by the Sessions Judge, who convicted six, including the appellant Bhuboni Sahu (accused No. 7) and Trinath. The High Court of Patna allowed some appeals but dismissed those of the appellant and Trinath. (The deceased’s son and one other, also charged, were acquitted.)
  • Kholi Behara (aged 20), who had taken part in the murder, was tendered pardon and became an approver. He was arrested on 12 October 1946 (the day after the offence), sent to the Magistrate on 14 October, who on 15 October recorded his statement under s. 164 CrPC. In it he described the murder and alleged that he and Trinath had been engaged as hired assassins; that the appellant gave him a khantibadi (grass-cutting instrument) which he passed to Trinath, who handed it over at the appellant’s house; and that the appellant paid each of them a sum of money.
  • At the Sessions trial the approver told a different story. The Sessions Judge brought his evidence before the Committing Magistrate on record under s. 288 CrPC, and both the Sessions Judge and the High Court preferred the earlier version.
  • Trinath, the co-accused, made a confession which was recorded but was retracted in the Sessions Court.
  • Recoveries: a loin cloth identified as the one worn by the deceased when assaulted, and a khantibadi, in circumstances alleged to implicate the appellant.
Evidence against Bhuboni Sahu (the three items)
  1. The evidence of the approver Kholi Behara (ss. 114 Ill. (b) & 133 IEA);
  2. The (retracted) confession of co-accused Trinath (s. 30 IEA);
  3. Recovery of the deceased’s loin cloth and the khantibadi.
Issues
  1. Was there any evidence upon which the appellant could lawfully be convicted?
  2. Can a conviction rest on accomplice evidence “supported” only by the retracted confession of a co-accused?
  3. Can the approver’s own earlier statements (s. 164 CrPC; evidence brought on record under s. 288 CrPC) serve as the required corroboration?
  4. Do the recoveries of the loin cloth and khantibadi corroborate the approver’s story against the appellant?
Arguments for the Appellant (Sir Valentine Holmes)
  • The approver’s evidence was accepted by two courts; his earlier s. 164 statement and his evidence before the Committing Magistrate (made admissible by s. 288 CrPC) showed consistency soon after the occurrence.
  • He relied on examples recognised by the Judicial Committee itself where confessions of co-accused given independently and without opportunity of previous concert were entitled to great weight — and contended that Trinath’s confession was independent of the approver’s statement and its truth was not weakened by its retraction.
  • The recoveries (loin cloth; khantibadi handed over at the appellant’s house and later found there) supplied the necessary corroboration in material particulars.
Arguments for the Crown / reasoning adopted
  • The rule of prudence requires independent evidence implicating each accused; the confession of a co-accused (himself an accomplice) is not such independent evidence.
  • Trinath’s confession was very short, gave only a bare outline, and was partly self-exculpatory; on the state of the evidence it was impossible to say the approver and Trinath were kept apart, so previous concert was highly improbable to exclude.
  • The khantibadi is an instrument commonly possessed by agriculturists — no blood stains, nothing to connect the particular khantibadi with the murder; the recovery of the cloth also failed to implicate the appellant as claimed.
Decision (Privy Council — appeal allowed; conviction set aside)
  • Combined reading of ss. 133 and 114 Ill. (b): it is not illegal to act on uncorroborated accomplice evidence, but the rule of prudence requiring corroboration in material respects implicating the accused is “so universally followed as to amount almost to a rule of law” that it is unsafe to act without it.
  • Corroboration must be independent and must implicate each accused — not merely confirm the occurrence. “The corroboration must be not only with regard to the occurrence but also as against each of the accused sought to be implicated in the crime.”
  • One accomplice cannot corroborate another: the High Court itself was right not to treat Trinath’s confession as sufficient corroboration of the approver’s evidence (and on that principle had acquitted Mata Simma, accused No. 6).
  • No self-corroboration: a s. 164 CrPC statement “can never be used as substantive evidence of the facts stated, but it can be used to support or challenge evidence given in court by the person who made the statement”. “The statement made by the approver under s. 164 plainly does not amount to the corroboration in material particulars… An accomplice cannot corroborate himself; tainted evidence does not lose its taint by repetition.” But the court was entitled to have regard to the early consistent statement when choosing between the approver’s two versions (Committing Magistrate vs Sessions Court).
  • Recoveries rejected as corroboration: the khantibadi was a common agricultural tool; nothing showed it was the murder weapon; the cloth’s recovery did not implicate the appellant in the manner deposed.
  • Confession of a co-accused under s. 30 IEA is “a very weak type of evidence”: it is not evidence within the s. 3 definition; not made on oath; not in the presence of the accused; not tested by cross-examination. Section 30 permits the court to “take into consideration” such a confession — but the section “does not say that the confession is to amount to proof. Clearly there must be other evidence”.
  • Danger rationale (adopting Sir George Rankin in Ambikacharan Roy v. Emperor and the passage from Emperor v. Lalit Mohan Chuckerbutty): the accomplice’s bad character and prosecution bias are not the real danger; the real danger is a story true in outline padded with falsehoods — “He may implicate ten people… true as to eight… untrue as to the other two, whose names have been introduced because they are enemies of the approver.” This tendency is peculiarly prevalent in India; an Indian villager is seldom able to prove an alibi cogently (only interested family evidence). “The only real safeguard… lies in insisting upon independent evidence which in some measure implicates each accused.”
  • Result: the approver’s evidence, supported only by Trinath’s retracted confession, could not be acted upon — the two were “nothing but hired assassins” with “ample opportunity of preparing their statements in concert”; the approver had sworn to two contradictory stories; Trinath denied his confession. They may have falsely claimed instigation by another to conceal the real instigator. Conviction of Bhuboni Sahu set aside.
Principle evolved

(1) ss. 133 and 114(b) IEA (138 and 119(b) BSA) must be read together — conviction on uncorroborated accomplice evidence is legal but unsafe; the prudence rule is “almost a rule of law”. (2) Corroboration must be by independent evidence and must implicate each accused separately in material particulars. (3) An accomplice cannot corroborate himself — prior statements (s. 164 CrPC / s. 288 CrPC material) are not corroboration, though they may help the court prefer one version to another. (4) One accomplice’s evidence cannot corroborate another’s. (5) A co-accused’s confession under s. 30 IEA / s. 28 BSA is very weak, is not “evidence” technically, and cannot found a conviction — other evidence must exist. (6) The classic statement of the danger of false implication and the villager-alibi rationale.

Case 26 — Haroon Haji Abdulla v. State of Maharashtra

(1968) 2 SCR 641 : AIR 1968 SC 832 • Supreme Court • M. Hidayatullah, J. • Decided 14 December 1967 • IEA ss. 30, 114 Ill. (b), 133; s. 171-A Sea Customs Act 1878; s. 120-B IPC; s. 167(81) Sea Customs Act; FERA 1947 • (syllabus citation “AIR 1975 SC 856” is an error — it belongs to Case 27)
Facts
  • Gold smuggling from the Persian Gulf: gold was brought in steam launches from Gulf ports, transshipped at sea into Indian boats standing out, brought to the Indian shore and taken away by persons waiting for it.
  • A raid on the night of 13 August 1961 caught a consignment coming in; many smugglers were arrested. On 14 August the Customs Authorities served notices under s. 171-A, Sea Customs Act (successor provision today: s. 108, Customs Act 1962). On 15 August, two customs officers — Karnik and Rane — recorded independently and almost simultaneously the statements of two suspects, Kashinath and Bengali. (Noor Mohammad’s statement was recorded by Randive on 19 August.)
  • Kashinath’s statement implicated himself and Haroon. Bengali’s statement was a confession of his own guilt and implicated Haroon. Bengali was jointly tried with Haroon. Haroon, though served with a notice, was unwilling to make any statement until he had seen what the others had said.
  • Eighteen accused (the appellant and 17 others) were tried before the Chief Presidency Magistrate, Esplanade Court, for conspiracy to smuggle gold — s. 120-B IPC (now BNS s. 61(2)) read with s. 167(81) Sea Customs Act and certain offences under the FERA, 1947.
  • At trial, Kashinath became a prosecution witness (PW 1) — an accomplice. Bengali (accused No. 1) retracted his confession alleging duress and torture; he was convicted by the judgment but had died after the conclusion of the case and before judgment, so the case against him abated. Noor Mohammad (accused No. 4) jumped bail just before judgment; the case against him was kept pending (trial separated). Accused Nos. 11, 12, 13 and 16 were acquitted.
  • Trial Court: some accused acquitted; the appellant convicted. High Court: confirmed the conviction relying on (i) Kashinath’s evidence corroborated by his own statement before the Customs authorities, and (ii) the retracted confession of Bengali.
Issues
  1. Whether Kashinath, an accomplice, could support a conviction without corroboration in material particulars, and what may corroborate him.
  2. Whether Kashinath’s own statement to the Customs authorities can corroborate him.
  3. Whether a statement recorded under s. 171-A Sea Customs Act is a “confession” usable under s. 30 IEA (s. 28 BSA) against a co-accused, and whether s. 30 is confined to Magistrate-recorded confessions.
  4. Whether Bengali (died before judgment) and Noor Mohammad (absconded before judgment) were still persons “jointly tried” with Haroon.
  5. Effect of retraction alleging duress and torture on the value of the confessions.
Arguments for the Appellant
  • Kashinath being an accomplice, no conviction could be based on his evidence unless corroborated in material particulars (Bhuboni Sahu).
  • His own statement before the Customs authorities cannot be corroboration — an accomplice cannot corroborate himself.
  • Bengali’s statement was not a confession within s. 30 IEA and, in any case, was obtained by inducement/duress/torture; being retracted, it was worthless even against its maker, and far more so against a co-accused.
  • Since Bengali died and Noor Mohammad absconded before the trial was finally concluded against them, their statements are not statements of persons “jointly tried” with Haroon and are inadmissible under s. 30.
  • A confession of a co-accused is no better than accomplice evidence; corroboration by another tainted source proves nothing.
Arguments for the State
  • Reliance on Ram Prakash v. State of Punjab (1959 SCR 1219, Imam J.): the Evidence Act nowhere provides that a retracted confession ceases to be usable against the maker or co-accused; its value is weak and corroboration is ordinarily needed, but admissibility is unaffected — credibility depends on the circumstances of each case.
  • Reliance on Subramania Goundan v. State of Madras (1958 SCR 428, Govinda Menon J.): a confession stands on a slightly higher level than accomplice evidence.
  • Bengali’s confession was voluntary and true; the retractions came months later; and the two statements (Kashinath and Bengali), recorded almost simultaneously by different officers within hours of the incident, agree remarkably in detail and are independently corroborated at numerous points.
Decision (Supreme Court — appeal dismissed)
  • Relation of ss. 133 and 114(b): settled law — a court may legally convict on the single evidence of an accomplice; Ill. (b) adds a rider of prudence, because an accomplice “who betrays his associates is not a fair witness” and may weave false details into a true story so that “there may be no means at hand to sever the false from that which is true”. Courts therefore insist on corroboration as to the offence itself and implicating, however slightly, each accused named — a rule “so ingrained… as to have almost the standing of a rule of law”.
  • Bhuboni Sahu distinguished/clarified: that case dealt with accomplice evidence sought to be corroborated only by a retracted confession; the Judicial Committee itself accepted that confessions given independently and without opportunity of previous concert might be entitled to great weight, while courts should be slow to depart from the prudence rule.
  • Confession of co-accused used against the co-accused stands LOWER than accomplice evidence, because accomplice evidence is at least tested by cross-examination while the confession is not (reading Subramania Goundan down; Nathu v. State of U.P.: confessions of co-accused are not evidence, but if there is other evidence on which conviction can be based, they can be referred to as lending some assurance to the verdict).
  • Retraction: “A retracted confession must be looked upon with greater concern unless the reasons given for having made it in the first instance (not for retraction, as erroneously stated in some cases) are on the face of them false”. Once the confession is satisfactorily proved (voluntary), the admission must be satisfactorily withdrawn or its making explained by fear, duress or promise from a person in authority. A retracted confession is a weak link against the maker and more so against a co-accused.
  • Voluntariness of s. 171-A statements: such statements enjoy none of the safeguards of s. 164 CrPC recordings; they must be scrutinised with special care — but if voluntary and true they are confessions usable under s. 30 IEA, which is not confined to Magistrate-recorded confessions.
  • “Jointly tried”: Bengali was fully tried jointly with Haroon; his statement is relevant under s. 30 read with s. 32(3) IEA despite his death before judgment. There is “difficulty” about Noor Mohammad’s statement, because his trial was separated (he absconded); the High Court did not rely on it, and the Supreme Court also “reluctantly left it out of consideration” — yet nothing else destroyed the conclusion about the truth of the accomplice evidence.
  • No self-corroboration: “We are not seeking corroboration of the accomplice from his own statements… We are only looking into the previous statement to see if it discloses any variation which would put us on further inquiry.” The real check was comparison with Bengali’s statement — a remarkable degree of agreement, recorded almost simultaneously by different officers only hours after the incident, so that tutoring/collusion was impossible; the defence’s own suggestion of collusion failed for want of time.
  • Independent corroboration: Kashinath’s account of the boats, owners, pilots, trips, participants and their roles, the residences of the Muslim co-accused, the furniture of the room where gold was secreted, the cars employed — all amply borne out by non-accomplice evidence; Bengali’s independent confession corroborated Kashinath as against Haroon.
  • Conduct of Haroon: his refusal to answer the s. 171-A notice until he saw what others had said was “peculiar conduct in a man who now claims that he was not concerned with the smuggling”.
  • Result: the High Court had applied the correct checks; regard being had to s. 133, no interference was warranted. Appeal dismissed; appellant to surrender to bail.
Principle evolved

(1) Confessions recorded under s. 171-A Sea Customs Act (cf. s. 108 Customs Act 1962) are confessions for s. 30 IEA / s. 28 BSA if voluntary — s. 30 is not limited to s. 164 CrPC confessions; but voluntariness demands special scrutiny absent magisterial safeguards. (2) A confession is usable under s. 30 only against a person jointly tried for the same offence — satisfied where the confessing co-accused was fully tried even if he dies before judgment (s. 30 r/w s. 32(3)); not satisfied where his trial stands separated. (3) A retracted but voluntary and true confession of a jointly tried co-accused may corroborate an accomplice as against the accused, though it can never alone found a conviction. (4) The accomplice’s own prior statement is not corroboration; it is relevant only to detect variations. (5) Used against a co-accused, a confession ranks below accomplice evidence (no cross-examination).

Case 27 — Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana

(1975) 3 SCC 742 : AIR 1975 SC 856 • Supreme Court • A.N. Goswami, J. • Cr appeal by right under s. 2, Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act 1970 • IPC ss. 302/34 (now BNS ss. 103 r/w 3(5)); CrPC ss. 306–308 (now BNSS ss. 343–345); IEA ss. 114(b), 133
Facts
  • On 30 July 1968, Bimla, “a hale and hearty young girl (19)”, the right, legitimate wife of the accused Ravinder Singh (23, an Air Force employee at Sirsa), accompanied her husband on a rail journey after his two months’ furlough. He returned to his Air Force Station without her and without the least concern. Next morning she was found near a wayside station between Rewari and Kosli, with acid burns on her face and body and multiple injuries, incapacitated by shock; she was taken to the Railway Hospital, Rewari, then the Civil Hospital, Rewari, and was pronounced dead at 8.45 p.m. on 31 July 1968. She spoke something about her journey but it was not sufficient to be treated as a dying declaration.
  • The whole story came from the accused’s friend, approver Jasbir Inder Singh (21) (PW 5), arrested along with the accused on 13 August 1968. Ravinder Singh and the approver worked together at the Air Force Station, Sirsa, and were good friends; Bhanu Parkash Singh (since acquitted in a separate trial) was the cousin of the accused.
  • Preparation: in early July the accused told friends he “would finish his wife one day”. On 29 July 1968 Bhanu Parkash Singh came to the accused’s house. On 30 July the accused told the approver, in Bhanu Parkash’s presence, that he would kill his wife that day. Five persons — Ravinder, Bimla, Satinder Kumar, Jasbir Inder Singh (approver) and Bhanu Parkash Singh — left for Sirsa by train from Sasni station (4–5 miles from Komri); the accused booked his cycle at Sasni and bought only two tickets (his wife’s and Bhanu Parkash’s).
  • Journey: left Sasni at 12 noon; reached Delhi at 6.30 p.m.; changed for Bhatinda; reached Rewari about 10.30 p.m.; their bogie was attached to the Sirsa–Bhatinda train. When the train left Rewari at 2.15 a.m. on 31 July 1968, there was no other passenger in the compartment except the five.
  • The murder: Ravinder threw his wife on the floor by catching her neck; the approver caught hold of her feet; Bhanu Parkash Singh “threw acid in her mouth”; Satinder Kumar took no part. They removed her pazebs and gold jumkas. They threw Bimla from the running train between the first and second stations beyond Rewari. Some acid drops fell on the hands of the accused and Bhanu Parkash Singh, and on their pants and the accused’s shirt.
  • Aftermath: the accused got down to buy tickets for Bhanu Parkash and Satinder Kumar, but Ticket Collector Raghbir Singh (PW 29) at Bhiwani detained him for ticketless travel of the three and he missed the train; the approver, Bhanu Parkash and Satinder went on to Sirsa (arriving ~10.30 a.m.; accused arrived 1.30 p.m.). On 4 August 1968 the accused reported acid burns on both hands to the First Aid Post at the Air Force Unit; Sergeant R.N. Singh (PW 50), the Medical Assistant, made the entry in the register (Ext. PT). The accused and the approver resumed duties on 2 August 1968; both were arrested on 13 August 1968.
  • Pardon: the approver was in police custody till 27 August; on 29 August 1968 he wrote through the Jail Superintendent to the Magistrate expressing willingness to give evidence as “sultani gawa” (originally “King’s witness”); conditional pardon was granted on 6 September 1968 and he was examined thereafter as a prosecution witness (CrPC s. 306; now BNSS s. 343).
  • Trial Court (Additional Sessions Judge): disbelieved the approver; held his statement uncorroborated in material particulars; motive not established; dying declaration not proved — husband acquitted. High Court (on the State’s appeal): found the approver’s statement free from defect and corroborated in material particulars by other evidence connecting the accused with the crime — convicted under s. 302 IPC; life sentence (“shrinking… from administering the extreme penalty”).
Issues
  1. Was the approver’s evidence reliable, and was it corroborated in material particulars connecting the accused with the crime?
  2. Can a conviction be based on the uncorroborated evidence of an approver?
  3. Did the acquittal of co-accused Bhanu Parkash Singh in a separate trial (pronounced the same day, because there the approver’s evidence was found uncorroborated against him) bar the appellant’s conviction on the principle of issue-estoppel?
Arguments for the Appellant
  • The approver is a purchased witness; the trial court rightly disbelieved him; the “corroboration” items (ticket collector, cycle booking, acid burns) are trivial or explicable.
  • The acid-burn explanation of the accused (burns from batteries he handled) was a plausible innocent explanation.
  • Issue-estoppel: relying on Lalta v. State of U.P., AIR 1970 SC 1381 — Bhanu Parkash Singh was acquitted on the same approver’s evidence being held uncorroborated; that finding should estop the State from convicting Ravinder on the same evidence; conviction would be inconsistent.
Arguments for the State
  • The approver’s account is self-implicating, natural, detailed, with no earlier contradiction on any material point; the High Court examined it with the care required of accomplice evidence and found ample corroboration aliunde.
  • Motive proved: the accused’s illegitimate intimacy and repeated threats (“would finish his wife one day”).
  • Issue-estoppel has no application: the parties in the two trials are different; and counsel frankly admitted that in Bhanu Parkash’s case the approver was not held unreliable — only uncorroborated against that accused.
Decision (Supreme Court — appeal dismissed; conviction upheld)
  • Approver’s starting point of unreliability: “Every approver comes to give evidence in some such manner seeking to purchase his immunity and that is why to start with he is an unreliable person and the rule of caution calling for material corroboration is constantly kept in mind by the court by time-worn judicial practice.” “An approver is a most unworthy friend if at all and he having bargained for his immunity, must prove his worthiness for credibility in court.”
  • The double test: (1) the story must involve him in the crime and appear intrinsically a natural and probable catalogue of events, in minute details according with reality, so as not to be rejected brevi manu; (2) it must implicate the accused on trial so as to raise a conclusion of guilt beyond reasonable doubt — ordinarily through corroboration in material particulars “bridging closely the distance between the crime and the criminal”.
  • Rare-case rule: in a rare case, considering all factors, circumstances and situations, a conviction on the uncorroborated evidence of an approver confidently held to be true and reliable may be permissible.
  • Corroboration found on facts (each item from an independent source):
    1. PW 2 (Miss V.K. Sharma, Nurse): the semi-conscious Bimla’s statement about her journey — confirming the accused’s presence with her;
    2. PW 29 (Ticket Collector Raghbir Singh, Bhiwani): the accused was detained at Bhiwani and missed the train — corroboration of the journey and the throwing of Bimla from the train beyond Rewari; his story fitted the actual number of tickets — “absence of any attempt at padding”;
    3. PW 26 (Yudhishter Kumar, Sirsa): the approver, Satinder Kumar and Bhanu Parkash came to him at Sirsa on 31 July ~10.30 a.m. without the accused;
    4. PWs 16 & 17 (A.S.Ms., Sasni): the accused booked cycle No. RK-162872 (Road King) from Sasni to Sirsa on 30 July 1968 (forwarding note Ext. PW 16/A) and personally filled the note — proving he came by train on 30 July, not 29 July as the defence claimed;
    5. PW 20 (Sirsa station): the accused took delivery of the cycle at Sirsa on 1 August;
    6. PW 50 (Sgt. R.N. Singh, Medical Assistant): the accused reported acid burns on both hands at 7 a.m. on 4 August 1968; the entry in the First Aid Post register (Ext. PT) proved; not denied by the accused.
  • Battery explanation rejected as an afterthought: burns from a few accidental battery-acid drops “may even be superficial”; PW 50 was not shaken; the explanation was invented to cover the acid injuries.
  • Motive accepted: the illegitimate intimacy of the accused.
  • Trial court’s error exposed: it used the disbelieved dying-declaration evidence of Sampat (PW 8) as a yardstick to judge the approver’s veracity — once Sampat’s evidence is rejected it cannot be used to test other evidence; its reasons for rejecting the Ticket Collector were tenuous.
  • Issue-estoppel rejected: quoting Dixon J. in King v. Wilkes (77 CLR 511, 518): issue-estoppel arises where the same point was determined in favour of a prisoner in a previous criminal trial and arises again in a subsequent proceeding by the Crown against the same prisoner. Here the parties were different (State v. Ravinder Singh; State v. Bhanu Parkash Singh); the approver was not held unreliable in the earlier case — only uncorroborated against Bhanu Parkash; there is no inconsistency between the two findings. Per Manipur Administration v. Thokchom Bira Singh, AIR 1965 SC 87: issue-estoppel does not prevent trial of an offence (as autrefois acquit does) but only precludes evidence being led to prove a fact-in-issue already specifically found in an earlier trial between the same parties. Corroboration is accused-specific — uncorroborated as against one co-accused, amply corroborated as against another.
  • Result: appeal dismissed; conviction under s. 302 IPC and life sentence upheld.
Principle evolved

(1) The classic formulation of the double test for approver evidence (reliability + corroboration bridging the distance between crime and criminal). (2) Corroboration need not be elaborate — small, independent, credible items (railway records, medical register, ticket collector) suffice if they connect the accused with the crime. (3) In a rare case conviction on uncorroborated approver evidence is permissible if the court confidently believes it. (4) Corroboration is accused-specific: failure against one co-accused does not defeat conviction of another who is independently corroborated. (5) Issue-estoppel in criminal law requires identity of parties and of the fact-in-issue previously determined in the prisoner’s favour; it bars re-proof of a specific finding, not the trial itself.

PART 5DU PYQs with Best Model Answers

PYQ — LL.B. II Term, Law of Evidence, May–June 2024, Q.7 (20 marks)

Question: “The law is not that the evidence of accomplice deserves outright rejection if there is no corroboration; rather caution and care are required in dealing with accomplice evidence. Though there is no legal necessity to seek corroboration of accomplice evidence, yet it is desirable that the court should look for reassuring circumstances to satisfy the judicial conscience that the evidence is true.” Elucidate.

Best Model Answer

1. Introduction — the statutory setting. Section 138 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (s. 133 IEA) provides that an accomplice “shall be a competent witness against an accused person; and a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds upon the uncorborated testimony of an accomplice”. Alongside stands Illustration (b) to s. 119 BSA (s. 114 IEA): the court “may presume” that an accomplice is unworthy of credit unless corroborated in material particulars. The quoted proposition captures exactly the combined effect of these two provisions: no outright rejection (s. 138 — rule of law), but caution and care (Ill. (b) — rule of prudence).

2. Rule of law vs rule of prudence. Section 138 is the only absolute rule of law on the subject: it makes the accomplice competent (unlike, say, the disqualifications once attached by the Oaths Act era) and legalises conviction on his uncorroborated testimony. Illustration (b) does not contradict it — it raises a rebuttable presumption (“may presume”), not a presumptio juris et de jure. In Dagdu v. State of Maharashtra (1977) the Supreme Court held there is no antithesis between s. 133 and s. 114(b): reading them together, a conviction may lawfully rest on uncorroborated accomplice testimony, yet the court is entitled — and in the generality of cases justified — to presume that no reliance can be placed on such evidence unless corroborated in material particulars.

3. The Privy Council’s classic formula. In Bhuboni Sahu v. The King (AIR 1949 PC 257), Sir John Beaumont held that the rule of prudence is “so universally followed as to amount almost to a rule of law” that it is unsafe to act on accomplice evidence unless corroborated in material respects implicating the accused. The reason is not merely the accomplice’s bad character, his betrayal of associates, or his prosecution bias; the real danger is that his story, true in outline, may be padded with falsehoods — he may implicate ten persons, the story being true as to eight and false as to two named out of enmity. This tendency is peculiarly prevalent in India, where a villager can seldom prove an alibi except through interested family evidence. Hence the only real safeguard is insisting on independent evidence which in some measure implicates each accused. Further: (i) an accomplice cannot corroborate himself — his s. 164 CrPC (BNSS s. 183) statement is not corroboration, for “tainted evidence does not lose its taint by repetition”; (ii) one accomplice cannot corroborate another; (iii) a co-accused’s confession under s. 30 IEA (s. 28 BSA) is a very weak type of evidence which cannot itself found a conviction.

4. “Reassuring circumstances” in modern application. In Haroon Haji Abdulla v. State of Maharashtra (AIR 1968 SC 832), a gold-smuggling conspiracy case, the Court accepted the accomplice Kashinath because: his prior statement was used only to detect variations, never as self-corroboration; his account was corroborated at numerous points by non-accomplice evidence; and the independent, voluntary (though retracted) confession of co-accused Bengali, recorded almost simultaneously, lent assurance against the appellant. Retraction was not fatal: once voluntariness is satisfactorily proved, the admission must be satisfactorily withdrawn or explained. In Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana (AIR 1975 SC 856), the Court crystallised the double test: (1) reliability — the approver’s story must involve him in the crime and appear a natural, probable catalogue of events in minute detail; (2) corroboration in material particulars “bridging closely the distance between the crime and the criminal”. Railway records (cycle booking), the ticket collector, and the medical register entry of acid burns were held sufficient reassuring circumstances. The Court also recognised that in a rare case, conviction on uncorroborated approver evidence confidently held true and reliable is permissible — which is precisely why the requirement is prudence, not legal necessity.

5. Conclusion. The law thus steers between two extremes: it refuses to reject accomplice evidence outright (s. 138/s. 133), and it refuses to act on it mechanically (Ill. (b)/s. 114(b)). The court must look for reassuring circumstances — independent corroboration in material particulars connecting each accused with the crime — to satisfy the judicial conscience that the evidence is true; where such circumstances are absent, conviction, though not illegal, will ordinarily be unsafe.

Expected / Practice PYQ 2 (10–15 marks)

Q. Who is an accomplice? Distinguish between accomplice, approver and a co-accused making a confession.

Model Answer (points)
  • Meaning: the Act does not define the word; ordinary sense — a guilty associate, a person supposed to have taken part in the commission of the same crime and who could have been charged with the accused (Jagannath v. Emperor, AIR 1942 Oudh 221). Fact-specific inquiry; burden on the party alleging accomplice character, though the prosecution must disclose it; the word appears only in ss. 114 Ill. (b), 133, 156 IEA (ss. 119 Ill. (b), 138, 159 BSA).
  • Classes (Davies v. DPP, [1954] AC 378; followed in R.K. Dalmia v. Delhi Administration, AIR 1962 SC 1821): (i) principals in the first degree; (ii) principals in the second degree (aiders/abettors present); (iii) accessories before the fact (planners/providers, absent at commission); (iv) receivers of stolen property in a joint trial for the felony. Accessories after the fact are accomplices only if they participated in the crime or conspiracy.
  • Not accomplices: uninvolved witnesses; unwilling participants under compulsion; persons who merely know of/witness the crime or conceal it out of fear; detectives, paid informers, trap witnesses (interested witnesses); victims.
  • Accomplice: competent witness (s. 138 BSA), testifies on oath, cross-examinable, evidence is “evidence” but tainted — corroboration in material particulars as prudence.
  • Approver: accomplice pardoned under BNSS s. 343/344 (CrPC 306/307) on condition of full and true disclosure; becomes a prosecution witness but remains an accomplice evidentially; breach ⇒ forfeiture and trial under BNSS s. 345 (CrPC 308). All approvers are accomplices; not vice-versa.
  • Co-accused (confession): no oath, no cross-examination, not “evidence” technically; only “taken into consideration” under s. 28 BSA / s. 30 IEA; cannot found a conviction; used against a co-accused it stands lower than accomplice evidence (Haroon Haji, citing Nathu).
Expected / Practice PYQ 3 (10 marks)

Q. Discuss the law relating to tender of pardon to an accomplice and the consequences of its breach.

Model Answer (points)
  • Power & stage: BNSS s. 343 (CrPC 306) — CJM/MM/First Class Magistrate, in cases triable exclusively by the Court of Session or punishable with 7+ years (or specified abetment/conspiracy offences), at any stage of investigation, inquiry or trial; BNSS s. 344 (CrPC 307) — Court of Session after commitment, before judgment; reasons to be recorded.
  • Condition: making a full and true disclosure of the whole of the circumstances within his knowledge relative to the offence and every other person concerned in it (as principal or abettor).
  • Position after pardon: examined as a witness (before the committing Magistrate and at the trial); kept in custody until the trial terminates unless already on bail; evidentially he remains an accomplice — the double test of Ravinder Singh applies; Art. 20(3) is not attracted because the disclosure is voluntary, accepted in his own interest.
  • Breach: BNSS s. 345 (CrPC 308) — wilful concealment of anything essential, false evidence, or non-compliance ⇒ pardon forfeited; he may be tried for the original offence and for giving false evidence; the statement accepting the pardon and the proceedings are admissible against him; the pardon must be pleaded as a defence.
  • Illustration: Ravinder Singh — approver wrote from jail as “sultani gawa” on 29.8.1968; conditional pardon granted 6.9.1968; evidence evaluated on the double test; conviction of the accused upheld.
Expected / Practice PYQ 4 (7–10 marks)

Q. “An accomplice cannot corroborate himself; tainted evidence does not lose its taint by repetition.” Explain with reference to decided cases. Can the retracted confession of a co-accused corroborate an accomplice?

Model Answer (points)
  • The maxim is from Bhuboni Sahu v. The King: corroboration must come from an independent source implicating each accused; the accomplice’s own earlier statement (s. 164 CrPC / BNSS s. 183) is never substantive evidence and cannot corroborate his own testimony, though it may support/challenge his credit and help the court prefer one of two versions.
  • Haroon Haji: the Supreme Court looked at the accomplice’s prior customs statement only to check for variations (“that does not advance accomplice evidence any further”); the real check was Bengali’s independent confession plus non-accomplice corroboration at numerous points.
  • Retracted confession of a co-accused: usable under s. 30 IEA / s. 28 BSA only against a person jointly tried for the same offence; must be proved voluntary (special scrutiny for s. 171-A/customs statements); retraction is not fatal unless the making of the confession is explained by fear, duress or promise (Ram Prakash; Subramania Goundan; Nathu); it is weak — it can lend assurance where other evidence exists (Bengali’s confession corroborated Kashinath in Haroon Haji), but cannot alone corroborate an accomplice or found a conviction (Bhuboni Sahu — Trinath’s confession insufficient; concert possible; two contradictory stories).
  • One accomplice cannot corroborate another — each needs independent corroboration.

6.3 Mnemonics

  • “C-A-R” for the framework: Competent (s. 138) → Almost a rule of law (Ill. (b) prudence) → Rare-case exception (Ravinder).
  • “I-M-P-L-I-C-A-T-E" checklist for corroboration — Independent source; Material particulars; Per-accused; Links crime & criminal; Integrity (no concert/tutoring); Cross-examinable preferred over confession; Accomplice cannot self-corroborate; Tainted repetition is not corroboration; Exceptions are rare and reasoned.
  • Davies’s four classes: “P-P-A-R” — Principal 1st degree, Principal 2nd degree, Accessory before the fact, Receiver.
  • Pardon trio: BNSS 343–344–345 = tender → direct → try on breach (CrPC 306–307–308).

6.4 Examiner’s traps (do not fall for)

Traps
  1. “Conviction on accomplice evidence is illegal without corroboration” — FALSE. It is not illegal (s. 138); it is only unsafe as a rule of prudence.
  2. Approver’s s. 164/BNSS-183 statement as corroboration — FALSE. No self-corroboration; use only for consistency/variation or s. 160 BSA credit purposes.
  3. Confession of any co-accused usable — INCOMPLETE. Only of a person jointly tried for the same offence (Bengali yes; Noor Mohammad no — separated trial), and only to lend assurance.
  4. Retraction destroys a confession — FALSE. Test voluntariness and truth; retraction weakens value, not admissibility (Ram Prakash, applied in Haroon Haji).
  5. Accomplice = approver — FALSE. Approver ⊂ accomplice ⊂ co-accused; pardon is procedural (BNSS), status is evidentiary (BSA).
  6. Trap witness/detective is an accomplice — FALSE. Interested witness, not particeps criminis.
  7. Corroboration needed for every detail — FALSE. Material particulars only, connecting each accused with the crime.
  8. Acquittal of co-accused on same approver evidence = estoppel — FALSE. Corroboration is accused-specific; issue-estoppel needs same parties & same fact-in-issue (Ravinder Singh).
  9. Citation trap: the DU syllabus prints “AIR 1975 SC 856” against Haroon Haji; the correct citation is (1968) 2 SCR 641 : AIR 1968 SC 832 (AIR 1975 SC 856 belongs to Ravinder Singh). Mentioning this correction earns credit.
