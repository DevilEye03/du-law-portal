(a)Re-enactment of Past Events — The Core Enquiry of Evidence Law

1. The foundational idea

Every legal dispute is, at its heart, a dispute about what happened in the past. A trial court never sees the transaction it must decide upon: the murder has already been committed, the contract already breached, the will already executed. The judge's task is therefore retrospective fact-finding — a re-enactment (reconstruction) of past events from the traces those events left behind (witnesses' memory, documents, electronic records, material objects, fingerprints, CCTV footage). From that reconstruction the law then deduces either the blameworthiness of a party (criminal side — e.g., whether A's act falls within s.101 BNS/ s.300 IPC and deserves punishment under s.103 BNS/s.302 IPC) or the entitlements of a party (civil side — e.g., whether the plaintiff has proved title and is entitled to a decree of partition or injunction).

This is why evidence law is often described as the "law of proof of facts" or the epistemic engine of adjudication. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 itself states its object in the long title: "An Act to consolidate and to provide for general rules and principles of evidence for fair trial." The reconstruction must therefore be not merely clever but fair — achieved through rules of relevancy, admissibility, burden of proof and cross-examination.

Anchor provisions
  • s.5 IEA / s.3 BSA: evidence may be given only of facts in issue and relevant facts "and of no others" — this confines the re-enactment to the legally material past.
  • s.3 IEA / s.2 BSA: the machinery's output — proved / disproved / not proved — is the court's verdict on the reconstruction.
  • s.118 IEA / s.124 BSA: who may supply the raw material (competency of witnesses).
  • s.101–114 IEA / s.104–113 BSA: burden of proof — who must drive the reconstruction.

3. Why it is the "core enquiry"

  • Substantive law is dormant without it: s.103 BNS (s.302 IPC) punishes murder only if murder is proved; a decree for possession follows only if title is proved. Evidence law supplies the "if".
  • Truth as the first duty: the DU case material's leading authority, State of Maharashtra v. Prafulla B. Desai (2003) 4 SCC 601, holds that "the first duty of the court is to do justice… courts must endeavour to find the truth… there would be failure of justice not only by an unjust conviction but also by acquittal of the guilty for unjustified failure to produce available evidence."
  • Probability, not certainty: human reconstruction yields probability; hence the standards — beyond reasonable doubt (criminal) and preponderance of probabilities (civil) — and the definitions of "proved/disproved" built on what "a prudent man ought… to act upon".
Real example — criminal reconstruction

In a murder trial under s.101 BNS [s.300 IPC], the prosecution re-enacts the night of the crime through: the spouse's testimony (s.6 BSA — res gestae), the victim's last-seen-with-the-accused evidence (s.8 BSA), the CCTV clip of the lane (electronic record, ss.2(1)(d)/(e), 61–63 BSA), the recovery of the knife on disclosure (s.23 BSA [s.27 IEA]), and the motive (s.9 BSA). Each item is a fragment of the past; together they must render the fact in issue — "A caused B's death" — proved beyond reasonable doubt.

Real example — civil reconstruction

In a partition suit, the plaintiff reconstructs a 40-year-old family arrangement through a registered sale deed (primary evidence, s.57 BSA), mutation entries (s.74 BSA — public documents), recitals in later deeds (s.13 BSA), and the testimony of the scribe (s.60 BSA). The outcome — entitlement to a share — depends entirely on the quality of that reconstruction.

(b)Historians, Authors, Media Persons and Lawyers — The Shared 'Past-Event' Enterprise

1. The common enterprise

The syllabus point draws on a well-known insight of evidence scholarship: a trial is not the only institution that reconstructs the past. Historians writing about the Revolt of 1857, a novelist building a plot around a real incident, an investigative journalist exposing a scam, and a lawyer prosecuting a riot case are all engaged in the same basic activity — assembling a credible account of events they did not witness, from incomplete and possibly biased sources. All four (i) gather sources, (ii) test credibility and consistency, (iii) look for corroboration, (iv) reason by inference and probability, and (v) produce a narrative that claims to correspond to what actually happened.

2. Similarities (the points of convergence)

  • Dependence on traces: the historian's archive = the lawyer's record; both work with what survived.
  • Credibility assessment: a historian asks whether a chronicle was written by a courtier or a critic; a lawyer asks whether a witness is interested or independent — the same skill of source-criticism.
  • Corroboration & consistency: a single source is treated with caution in both crafts (cf. the legal rule of prudence, e.g., accomplice evidence — illustration (b) to s.114 IEA / s.119 BSA).
  • Inferential reasoning: both move from circumstantial traces to conclusions (motive, opportunity, conduct — ss.8–9 IEA/ ss.6–8 BSA style reasoning is everyday historical method).
  • Narrative construction: the final output is a story that must cohere; authors and media persons make this explicit, lawyers do it in the final address, historians in the monograph.
  • Fallibility: all four reconstructions can be wrong — memory fades, documents are forged, bias distorts.

Bottom line: because the lawyer's reconstruction coercively decides liberty and property and must be reached within a finite time by a neutral umpire, the law super-imposes artificial rules (relevance ceilings, exclusion of hearsay, privilege, burden of proof) that the historian, author or journalist does not need. The rules of evidence are thus the price paid for authoritative, final and fair reconstruction.

Real example

The 1984 anti-Sikh riots or the 2020 Delhi riots can be studied by a historian (using police records, press accounts, oral histories — including hearsay), reported by a journalist, novelised by an author, and tried by a sessions court. The court alone must exclude the newspaper report as hearsay, insist on the eyewitness being cross-examined, and reach a verdict "beyond reasonable doubt" that binds the parties — showing why the lawyer's re-enactment is the most rule-bound of the four.

(c)Why the Rules of Evidence Have Different Significance under the Adversarial and Inquisitorial Systems

1. The two systems in outline

Adversarial system (Anglo-Indian tradition)

  • Two parties control the case; the judge is a neutral umpire ("the arena of trial is the parties' battlefield").
  • Evidence is party-produced and presented orally in open court; witnesses are tested by cross-examination (s.138 IEA / s.143 BSA).
  • Historically tied to the jury: lay triers had to be shielded from prejudicial material — hence exclusionary rules (hearsay, character, confessions).
  • Truth emerges from the clash of competing narratives; the judge rules on admissibility, the trier weighs.

Inquisitorial system (Continental/European tradition)

  • The judge investigates: directs the collection of evidence, compiles the dossier, examines witnesses himself.
  • Professional judges (often with lay assessors) are trusted to read everything — free evaluation of evidence (l'intime conviction).
  • Fewer exclusionary rules: the question is what the dossier shows, not what may be shown to a jury.
  • Truth is an official inquiry ("material truth"), supervised through broad appellate review of facts.

2. Why the significance of evidence rules differs

  1. Jury factor: exclusionary rules (e.g., hearsay, prior bad character — s.54 IEA/s.49 BSA) exist mainly to protect a lay jury from over-valuing unreliable or prejudicial material. A professional inquisitorial judge needs no such shielding; hence the rules matter less there.
  2. Party-control factor: in an adversarial trial the court sees only what the parties choose to lead; rules of relevance, burden of proof and admissibility are needed to discipline partisan proof. An inquisitorial judge collects evidence himself, so discipline is internal (judicial duty), not external (rules).
  3. Orality v. dossier: adversarial law is built around live testimony and cross-examination (s.60 IEA — oral evidence must be direct; s.138 IEA). Inquisitorial procedure works from written dossiers; its "evidence law" is therefore largely procedure of the file, not rules of exclusion.
  4. Finality v. accuracy: adversarial systems value finality of contests (issue estoppel, res judicata, rules of estoppel in ss.115–117 IEA/121–123 BSA); inquisitorial systems prioritise material truth with wider appeals on fact. Different goals give evidence rules different weight.
  5. Constitutional overlay: in adversarial common-law systems the accused's right to confront witnesses is a constitutional value (cf. Art. 21, and the Sixth Amendment "Confrontation Clause" discussed in Prafulla Desai); this elevates evidence rules to constitutional significance — unknown in classic inquisitorial settings.

3. India's position

India inherited the adversarial model, and Stephen's Act of 1872 is a code of adversarial evidence law. Yet Indian procedure contains inquisitorial streaks: the magistrate's role in recording confessions (s.164 CrPC / s.183 BNSS), the court's own power to question a witness (s.165 IEA / its BSA counterpart), the duty of the court to "endeavour to find the truth" (Prafulla Desai), and presumptions that shift the investigative burden onto the accused (ss.113A/113B IEA / ss.117–118 BSA). Consequently, in India the rules of evidence retain full adversarial significance plus a truth-finding gloss added by the Supreme Court.

(d)History of Statutory Evidence Law in India

1. Pre-British phase (source: general legal history; corroborated in the course material's account of repealed sources)

  • Hindu law: the Dharma-sutras and Smritis (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada) contained elaborate rules on sakshya (witnesses) — qualifications of a witness, oaths, ordeals (divya) such as fire, water, balance; documents (lekhya) and possession (bhukti) as means of proof.
  • Mohammedan law: rules of bayyina (evidence), number and competence of witnesses, oaths (qasama), and confession before the Qazi.

    • These personal-law evidence rules are precisely among the sources that s.2 IEA later repealed — the course material lists "The Hindu Laws" and "The Muslim Laws" as repealed sources.

2. Company and Crown period — the road to codification

  • Regulation of 1772 / 1781 and the Charter Acts set up courts applying, in the mofussil, justice, equity and good conscience — i.e., English common-law evidence in diluted form — while Presidency towns applied English law directly. This produced chaos and non-uniformity.
  • Piecemeal statutes on evidence were passed (e.g., early Acts on witness competence and on documents), but the law remained a patchwork of English common law, local regulations and scattered enactments — exactly the bundle repealed by s.2 IEA (see topic (e)).
  • 1868 — Sir Henry Maine's draft: a first codification attempt; per the course material, "Mr Henry Maine drafted evidence in 1868. But that was not suitable."
  • 1871 — Sir James Fitzjames Stephen redrafted the Bill; it became the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (Act 1 of 1872). Stephen is therefore the "Father of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872"; his own treatise, A Digest of the Law of Evidence, remains a key interpretive aid (the Privy Council and SC repeatedly use Stephen's definitions, e.g., of confession).
  • Original structure: 167 sections, 11 chapters and one Schedule; enforced on 1 September 1872 (the same day as the Indian Contract Act, 1872).

(e)Situation Pre and Post Indian Evidence Act, 1872

1. The pre-1872 situation (plural, patchwork, personal)

Before 1-9-1872 the rules of evidence in force in British India were an uncodified mixture of:

  • the English common law of evidence (in force in the Presidency towns and, via "justice, equity and good conscience", influential in the mofussil);
  • Hindu law and Muslim law rules before their respective communities;
  • rules having force under s.25 of the Indian Councils Act, 1861;
  • specific enactments listed in the Schedule to the IEA.
s.2 IEA (as originally enacted, quoted in Hira H. Advani v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 2010 SC 2914)

"On and from that day (1st September 1872) the following laws shall be repealed; (1) All rules of evidence not contained in any Statute, Act or Regulation in force in any part of British India; (2) All such rules, laws and regulations as have acquired the force of law under the 25th Section of the 'Indian Councils Act' 1861 in so far as they relate to any matter herein provided for; and (3) The enactments mentioned in the schedule hereto… But nothing herein contained shall be deemed to affect any provision of any Statute, Act or Regulation in force in any part of British India and not hereby expressly repealed."

Privy Council on the effect of s.2 — Sri Chandra Nandi v. Rakhalananda (1940), per Justice Atkin

"Section 2(1) of the Indian Evidence Act repeals the whole of the English common law on evidence so far as it was in force in British India before the passing of the Indian Evidence Act, and that provision of the law in effect prohibits the employment of any kind of evidence not specifically authorised by the Act itself." — i.e., the Act was exhaustive and exclusive.

2. The post-1872 situation (uniform, codified, exclusive)

  • One code for all India and all courts: the same rules now governed every judicial proceeding (s.1), ending the Hindu/Muslim/English duality.
  • Exclusivity: "and of no others" in s.5 made relevancy a closed list; courts could not import common-law categories.
  • Precision of concepts: fact, fact in issue, relevant, proved, disproved, not proved, court, document, evidence — all statutorily defined (s.3).
  • Limits of application recognised: not applicable to affidavits, arbitrators (s.1), administrative tribunals (Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957): tribunals need only follow natural justice).
  • Later pruning: since s.2 had already done its repealing work, the Repealing Act, 1938 removed s.2 and the Schedule — "once Section 2 was repealed in 1938 there was no effect on legal points because repealed Acts were not enacted later on" (course material).

(f)Changes Introduced in Evidence Law by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023

1. Constitutional-legislative facts

  • BSA = Act No. 47 of 2023; Presidential assent 25-12-2023; published in Gazette Extraordinary, Part II, s.1; commenced on 1-7-2024 by Central Government notification.
  • s.170(1) BSA: "The Indian Evidence Act, 1872 is hereby repealed." (with savings in s.170(2) for accrued rights, past proceedings, etc.).
  • New long title: "An Act to consolidate and to provide for general rules and principles of evidence for fair trial" — the IEA's title was only "to consolidate, define and amend the law of evidence". The fair-trial objective is newly elevated to the statute's face.

4. What did NOT change (continuity)

  • The conceptual grammar — fact / fact in issue / relevant / proved / disproved / not proved / court — is retained verbatim (s.2(1)(a)–(l) BSA).
  • The relevancy scheme (res gestae, motive, conspiracy, admissions, confessions, dying declarations, expert opinion), best-evidence rule, estoppel, privilege, accomplice rule, presumptions — all carried over with re-numbering (see Appendix A).
  • Judge-made gloss largely survives: the BSA's electronic-evidence provisions codify what Prafulla Desai and Malkani had already accomplished judicially.
Real example of the change

A WhatsApp chat offered in a defamation suit in 2022 required the s.65B IEA certificate route (Anvar P.V./Arjun Panditrao line). The identical chat offered after 1-7-2024 is, first of all, a "document" by definition (s.2(1)(d) + Illustration (vi) BSA), cannot be denied admissibility merely for being electronic (s.61), and if produced from proper custody is primary evidence (Explanation 5 to s.57) — the 2023 code thus converts what was an exception into the rule.

(g)s.2(2) BSA — Words Not Defined Here Take Meaning from the IT Act, 2000, BNSS 2023 and BNS 2023

Verbatim — s.2(2), Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023

"Words and expressions used herein and not defined but defined in the Information Technology Act, 2000, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 shall have the same meanings as assigned to them in the said Act and Sanhitas."

1. Elements of the provision

  1. Trigger: a word/expression is used in the BSA but not defined in the BSA.
  2. Source: the same word is defined in the IT Act, 2000 (Act 21 of 2000), the BNSS, 2023 or the BNS, 2023.
  3. Effect: the external definition is automatically imported ("shall have the same meanings") — no separate notification needed.
  4. Limit: "unless the context otherwise requires" (opening words of s.2(1)) — the imported meaning yields to a contrary context.

2. Why Parliament did this (object)

  • One organic code: the three Sanhitas + IT Act replaced the IPC/CrPC/IEA trio; cross-definition avoids duplication and drift between statutes.
  • Technology-neutrality: digital vocabulary evolves faster than amendments; borrowing from the IT Act keeps the BSA current (e.g., "electronic record", "computer resource", "computer network", "intermediary", "electronic signature", "secure digital record").
  • Procedural harmony: terms of investigation/trial used in the BSA (e.g., "investigation", "summons", "proclamation") carry BNSS meanings; offence-terms (e.g., "dowry death", "grievous hurt") carry BNS meanings.

(h)Role of the Judiciary — Particularly the Appellate Judiciary — in Updation of Evidence Law by Judicial Creativity

1. Why judges must be creative in evidence law

Evidence law deals with facts and technology, both of which change faster than legislation. Tape recorders, telephones, fax, microfilm, computers, e-mail, CCTV, DNA, video-conferencing — none existed in 1872. Since an "ongoing Act" is presumed to be "always speaking," the appellate judiciary (High Courts under Arts. 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Arts. 136, 141 & 142) has continuously updated the rules of evidence by construction, later ratified by Parliament (IT Act 2000 amendments; now BSA 2023).

2. The doctrinal toolkit (as deployed in the DU case material)

  • Updating construction (Bennion): approved in CIT v. Podar Cement (1997) 5 SCC 482 and State v. S.J. Choudhary (1996) 2 SCC 428 — "handwriting" in s.45 IEA held to include typewriting; "notice in writing" (s.138 NI Act) to include fax (SIL Import v. Exim Aides (1999) 4 SCC 567).
  • Rolling examples given by the SC in Prafulla Desai: "stage carriage" → electric tramcar; "steam tricycle" → locomotive; "telegraph" → telephone; "banker's books" → microfilm; "to take note" → use of tape recorder; "documents" → computer databases.
  • The "living tree" metaphor: Bhagwati J in National Textile Workers' Union v. P.R. Ramakrishnan (1983) 1 SCC 228: "We cannot allow the dead hand of the past to stifle the growth of the living present. Law cannot stand still…"
  • Truth-duty over technicality: Nageshwar Shri Krishna Ghobe v. State of Maharashtra (1973) 4 SCC 23 — failure of justice includes acquittal of the guilty for unjustified failure to produce available evidence.

Case Brief No. 1 — State of Maharashtra v. Prafulla B. Desai (Dr.)

(2003) 4 SCC 601 · Supreme Court of India · S.N. Variava, J. · [DU Case Material, Unit I/II anchor case]

Facts (as narrated in the DU case material)
  • The complainant's wife suffered terminal cancer. Dr. Ernest Greenberg of Sloan Kettering Memorial Hospital, New York, opined she was inoperable and should be treated only with medication.
  • The respondent, a consulting surgeon of 40 years' standing, nonetheless suggested surgery to remove the uterus; the operation (22-12-1987) was performed by Dr. A.K. Mukherjee; on opening the stomach ascetic fluids oozed; the respondent advised closing up; this resulted in intestinal fistula; the patient needed 20–25 dressings a day for 3½ months till death, suffering terrible agony.
  • The respondent never examined her post-operation and denied she was his patient — but the Bombay Hospital bill showed his fees, belying his case; the Maharashtra Medical Council held him guilty of negligence.
  • Case registered under s.338 IPC r/w ss.109, 114 IPC (grievous hurt by act endangering life, abetment — the BNS analogues being the BNS provisions on grievous hurt by dangerous acts and on abetment, s.61-range). Process issued; challenge to process dismissed by SC on 8-7-1996; six witnesses recorded.
  • On 29-6-1998 the prosecution applied to examine Dr. Greenberg by video-conferencing (he was willing to testify but refused to travel to India; no CrPC provision could compel him). Trial court allowed (16-8-1999); the Bombay High Court set aside that order, reading "presence" in s.273 CrPC as physical presence — hence these appeals.
Issues
  1. Whether evidence can be recorded by video-conferencing consistently with s.273 CrPC ("evidence… shall be taken in the presence of the accused")?
  2. Whether video-conferencing violates Articles 14 & 21 ("procedure established by law")?
  3. Whether the practical objections (oath, perjury, tutoring, costs, ss.274–275 CrPC) bar video-conferencing?
Arguments of the respondent (accused)
  • CrPC procedure is the "procedure established by law"; only the legislature may change it; any judicial departure breaches Art. 21.
  • s.273 is mandatory; "presence" means physical presence in flesh and blood (contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege — video-conferencing unknown when CrPC was enacted).
  • The only exceptions are commissions (ss.284–290), ss.295–296; analogy to the US Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause, which (per respondent) bars VC.
  • ss.274–275 require recording in open court by the Magistrate; VC at a VSNL studio violates this; witness beyond court's contempt/perjury control; risks of tutoring, interruptions; costs.
Reasoning of the Supreme Court (the creative core)
  • Constructive presence already in s.273: the section itself dispenses with personal attendance and permits recording "in the presence of his pleader" — so physical presence is not a must; "a plain reading… does not support the restrictive meaning."
  • Evidence definition is technology-neutral: "One must also take note of the definition of the term 'evidence' as defined in the Indian Evidence Act. Thus evidence can be both oral and documentary and electronic records can be produced as evidence. This means that evidence, even in criminal matters, can also be by way of electronic records. This would include video-conferencing."
  • First duty = justice/truth (Nageshwar Ghobe); rights of accused important but victims also have rights.
  • Updating construction (Bennion; Podar Cement; S.J. Choudhary; SIL Import) and the rolling examples (telegraph→telephone, banker's books→microfilm, documents→computer databases).
  • Basavaraj R. Patil v. State of Karnataka (2000) 8 SCC 740: even "personally" in s.313 CrPC does not always mean physical presence.
  • VC is actual reality, not virtual reality: like watching a live match on TV; "except for touching, one can see, hear and observe as if the party is in the same room"; the binoculars/telescope analogy; both parties are in each other's presence on screen.
  • Objects of s.273 fully met: demeanour observable (playback even better), effective cross-examination, confrontation with documents; no prejudice.
  • Safeguards for the practical objections: open-court VC only where the witness's country has an extradition treaty and punishes perjury/contempt; otherwise commission route (ss.284–289 CrPC) — commission to the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Mumbai, deputing a responsible (preferably judicial) officer at the VSNL studio; officer to administer oath, ensure no tutoring (room cleared; preferably Consulate/Embassy room), ensure accused + counsel + one assistant and their papers admitted; memo of non-cooperation; costs borne by the State.
Decision

Impugned High Court order set aside; evidence of Dr. Greenberg directed to be recorded by video-conferencing on commission with the above safeguards; trial to conclude within one year. Appeals allowed.

Principles evolved (ratio & obiter of lasting value)
  1. "Evidence" under the IEA includes electronic records; video-conferencing is a mode of giving evidence — the statute's concepts are technology-neutral.
  2. "Presence" in s.273 CrPC includes constructive/virtual presence.
  3. The Evidence Act and procedure codes are ongoing Acts entitled to updating construction; the dead hand of the past cannot stifle the living present.
  4. Video-conferencing satisfies "procedure established by law" (Art. 21) and, properly safeguarded, causes no prejudice to the accused.

Unit-I linkage: topics (a) [truth-finding as core enquiry], (h) [judicial creativity], (i) [meaning of "evidence" & "document"].

Case Brief No. 2 — R.M. Malkani v. State of Maharashtra

(1973) 1 SCC 471 : AIR 1973 SC 157 · Supreme Court of India · A.N. Ray, J. · [DU Case Material]

Facts
  • The appellant was Coroner of Bombay. A patient (operated by Dr. Adatia for acute appendicitis; gangrenous appendix; later "paralytic ileus and peritonitis") died on 13-5-1964; an inquest was registered in the Coroner's court.
  • The appellant, exploiting the inquest, demanded Rs 20,000 (later Rs 10,000, then Rs 15,000, of which Rs 5,000 allegedly for the Police Surgeon) from Dr. Adatia through Dr. Motwani as gratification for a favourable inquest.
  • The doctors complained to the Anti-Corruption Bureau (Mugwe, Director). With Dr. Motwani's consent, police attached a tape recorder to his telephone and recorded the telephonic conversations between Motwani and the appellant (7, 10 & 12 October 1964).
  • The appellant was charged under ss.161, 385, 420 r/w 511 IPC (public servant obtaining gratification; extortion by fear of injury; cheating and attempt — today see BNS: s.161-type offence ≈ BNS s.7; cheating = BNS s.318; attempt/abetment consolidated in BNS s.61). Convicted; sentenced to six months + fine of Rs 10,000.
Issues
  1. Whether the tape-recorded conversation was admissible although allegedly obtained in contravention of s.25 of the Indian Telegraph Act (illegally obtained evidence)?
  2. Whether the recording was hit by s.162 CrPC (statement to police during investigation)?
  3. Whether the conversations offended Arts. 20(3) & 21 (self-incrimination; privacy)?
  4. Whether the offence of attempt/extortion was made out; sentence.
Arguments of the appellant
  • Evidence obtained illegally (breach of s.25 Telegraph Act) is inadmissible.
  • The conversations were statements made during investigation, barred by s.162 CrPC (Mugwe had instructed Motwani what to say).
  • Recording violated Art. 20(3) (compelled self-incrimination) and Art. 21 (privacy of conversation).
  • No attempt proved — only "bargaining"; and leniency on sentence (heart attacks; fine already paid).
Reasoning of the Court
  • Relevancy, not pedigree, governs admissibility: "if evidence was admissible it matters not how it was obtained" — Kuruma v. R. [1955 AC 197]; R. v. Leatham ("it matters not how you get it; if you steal it even, it would be admissible"); Magraj Patodia v. R.K. Birta AIR 1971 SC 1295 (document procured by improper/illegal means admissible if relevant and genuine); R. v. Maqsud Ali [1963] 2 All ER 464; State of Maharashtra v. Nagree (tape in trap case).
  • Rule of caution (the golden rule): the judge may disallow evidence if strict admissibility operates unfairly; two cares — (i) the tape is genuine and free from tampering/mutilation; (ii) scrupulous police conduct is secured.
  • A tape record of an unnoticed conversation is like a photograph taken without knowledge — relevant and admissible; it is contemporaneous relevant evidence.
  • No s.162 bar: the telephonic conversation was between Motwani and the appellant — "neither made a statement to the police officer."
  • No Art. 20(3) violation: the conversation was voluntary, not extracted under duress; the recorder merely played "the role of an eavesdropper."
  • Art. 21 / privacy calibrated: "The telephonic conversation of an innocent citizen will be protected by Courts against wrongful or highhanded interference by tapping… The protection is not for the guilty citizen against the efforts of the police to vindicate the law and prevent corruption of public servants."
  • On facts, the escalating demands (20k→10k→15k) proved the offence; gravity and position negate leniency.
Decision

Appeal dismissed; conviction and sentence upheld. Tape-recorded conversation held admissible and corroborative of the doctors' testimony.

Principles evolved
  1. A tape-recorded conversation is admissible evidence (and, in the language of the Act, a species of document/real evidence) if (a) relevant, (b) genuine/tamper-free, (c) voluntary.
  2. Illegality in obtaining affects weight and invites judicial caution/discretion, not automatic exclusion.
  3. Arts. 20(3)/21 are not infringed where the speaker talks voluntarily; privacy shields the innocent, not the guilty evading lawful detection.

(i)The Core Concepts — 'Document', 'Evidence', 'Facts', 'Facts in Issue', 'Relevant Fact', 'Proved', 'Disproved', 'Not Proved' (s.3 IEA ↔ s.2 BSA)

The whole machinery of the Act rests on eight defined concepts. The table below is the master-map; each concept is then unpacked.

1. FACT — s.2(1)(f) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"fact" means and includes— (i) any thing, state of things, or relation of things, capable of being perceived by the senses; (ii) any mental condition of which any person is conscious. Illustrations: (i) objects arranged in a certain order in a certain place; (ii) that a person heard or saw something; (iii) that a person said certain words; (iv) that a person holds a certain opinion, has an intention, acts in good faith or fraudulently, uses a word in a particular sense, or is/was conscious of a particular sensation.

  • Two families: (a) physical/external facts — a knife at the scene, the arrangement of objects, spoken words; (b) psychological/mental facts — intention (mens rea), knowledge, fraud, good faith, sensation of pain. Criminal law constantly puts mental facts in issue (e.g., intention to cause death under s.101 BNS [s.300 IPC]).
  • Elements: (1) it must be a thing/state/relation or a mental condition; (2) perceptible by senses or conscious to a person; (3) it is assertive-neutral — a "fact" for the Act need not yet be true or disputed; truth-status is decided later via proved/disproved.
  • Examples: "A heard a gunshot" is a fact; "A said 'I will kill B'" is a fact; "A intended to kill B" is a fact; "A owns a house in Bihar" is a fact.

2. FACTS IN ISSUE — s.2(1)(g) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"facts in issue" means and includes any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature or extent of any right, liability or disability, asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding, necessarily follows. Explanation: whenever, under the law relating to civil procedure, a Court records an issue of fact, the fact to be asserted or denied in the answer to such issue is a fact in issue. Illustrations (murder trial): that A caused B's death; that A intended to cause B's death; that A received grave and sudden provocation from B; that A was, by unsoundness of mind, incapable of knowing the nature of the act.

  • Elements: (1) a fact asserted by one party and denied by the other (the disputed core); (2) from which right/liability/disability necessarily follows; (3) in civil suits it crystallises as the issues framed under Order XIV Rule 1 CPC; in criminal trials as the ingredients traversed by the charge (BNSS provisions on framing of charge, successor to ss.211–214 CrPC).
  • The murder illustration decoded: "A caused B's death" ↔ s.101 BNS [s.300 IPC] ingredient 1; "intended" ↔ mens rea; "provocation" ↔ exception 1 to s.300 IPC [BNS exceptions]; "unsoundness" ↔ s.84 IPC [s.22 BNS]. Each is a fact in issue because the verdict necessarily follows from it.
Classroom example (from the study material)

Husband A states in a matrimonial proceeding: (1) he owns a house in Bihar; (2) a car; (3) a YouTube channel; (4) that he did law from NLU Delhi. Wife Zaira admits 1–3 but denies 4, asserting he studied at the Law Faculty, DU. Only statement (4) is a fact in issue — because it alone is disputed; 1–3, being admitted, need no proof (cf. s.56 IEA / s.51 BSA — facts judicially noticeable/admitted need not be proved).

3. RELEVANT FACT — s.2(1)(k) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"relevant"—a fact is said to be relevant to another when it is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of this Adhiniyam relating to the relevancy of facts. (i.e., the chain ss.3–50 BSA / ss.5–55 IEA.)

  • Elements: (1) relational — relevance is always of one fact to another (usually to a fact in issue); (2) the connection must be one recognised by the Act (res gestae s.4 BSA/ s.6 IEA; cause-effect s.7; motive/conduct s.8; explanatory facts s.9; conspiracy s.10; admissions ss.15+; etc.) — logical relevance alone is insufficient.
  • Example: in a theft trial, the jewellery-shop CCTV footage showing persons entering/leaving around the time of theft is a relevant fact — it connects to presence (fact in issue) and can prove/disprove it.
  • Relevancy vs. admissibility (DU favourite):

Magraj Patodia v. R.K. Birta: a document procured by improper/illegal means is not barred from admissibility if relevant and genuine — admissibility is a question of law, relevance of logic.

4. EVIDENCE — s.2(1)(e) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"evidence" means and includes— (i) all statements including statements given electronically which the Court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses in relation to matters of fact under inquiry; such statements are called oral evidence; (ii) all documents including electronic or digital records produced for the inspection of the Court; such documents are called documentary evidence.

  • Elements: (1) oral evidence = witness statements permitted/required by the court (now expressly including electronic statements — video-conference testimony per Prafulla Desai); (2) documentary evidence = documents produced for inspection (now including electronic/digital records).
  • What is NOT "evidence" under the Act: affidavits (s.1 excludes them; though practice permits proof by affidavit where statutes allow), statements of counsel, contents of the charge-sheet, s.161 CrPC / BNSS police statements (not substantive evidence), s.313 BNSS (s.313 CrPC) accused's statements (not on oath).
  • Recognised extensions (case law): tape-recorded conversation (Malkani); video-conferenced testimony (Prafulla Desai); the study material also lists dog-tracking, narco-analysis (with consent per Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010) — supplementary) as evidentiary species.
  • Kinds of evidence (DU PYQ): direct (perceives the fact in issue itself — "I saw A stab B") vs circumstantial (perceives a relevant fact from which the fact in issue is inferred — last-seen, motive, recovery); conviction on circumstances alone is permissible if the chain is complete and excludes every other hypothesis (the classic DU/UP(J) PYQ, see PYQ section).

5. DOCUMENT — s.2(1)(d) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"document" means any matter expressed or described or otherwise recorded upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks or any other means or by more than one of those means, intended to be used, or which may be used, for the purpose of recording that matter and includes electronic and digital records. Illustrations: (i) a writing; (ii) words printed, lithographed or photographed; (iii) a map or plan; (iv) an inscription on a metal plate or stone; (v) a caricature; (vi) an electronic record on emails, server logs, documents on computers, laptop or smartphone, messages, websites, locational evidence and voice mail messages stored on digital devices.

  • Elements: (1) matter expressed/described/recorded; (2) upon any substance (paper, stone, metal, magnetic media — substrate irrelevant); (3) by letters, figures, marks or any other means; (4) intended or usable for recording that matter.
  • Document vs. material object: a ransom letter is a document (its contents matter); the same letter's paper-with-fingerprints is a material object; a blood-stained knife is a material object, not a document. Documents are proved by contents (primary/secondary evidence, ss.57–60 BSA); material objects by production and identification.
  • Case law (DU): Malkani — the tape-record is treated as admissible recorded evidence; DU PYQ 2003 affirms "a tape-recorded statement is a document"; BSA Illustration (vi) now settles emails/WhatsApp/server logs/locational data as documents.

6. PROVED / DISPROVED / NOT PROVED — s.2(1)(j), (c), (i) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"proved" — after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes it to exist, or considers its existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it exists. · "disproved" — the Court believes it does not exist, or considers its non-existence so probable that a prudent man ought to act upon the supposition that it does not exist. · "not proved" — neither proved nor disproved.

  • Elements: the test is the prudent-man probability test, not certainty; the standard flexes with "the circumstances of the particular case" — hence beyond-reasonable-doubt in criminal and preponderance in civil cases are both housed inside the same definition.
  • "Not proved" ≠ "disproved": a fact the prosecution simply failed to establish is "not proved" (prosecution loses on it) but not necessarily "disproved" (the court has not positively believed its non-existence). The distinction decides reverse-onus situations.

7. COURT — s.2(1)(a) BSA [s.3 IEA]

"Court" includes all Judges and Magistrates, and all persons, except arbitrators, legally authorised to take evidence.

  • Elements: (1) Judges; (2) Magistrates; (3) any person legally authorised to take evidence (e.g., commissioners, certain inquiry officers); (4) excludes arbitrators (s.1 also excludes proceedings before them).
  • Stephen's anatomy: "In every Court there must be at least three constituent parts — the actor (plaintiff), the reus (defendant), and the judex (the judicial power)."
  • Tribunals are not "Courts" under the Act: Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) — the IEA has no application to tribunal enquiries; only natural justice binds them. Hence a departmental enquiry officer is not a "Court" for the Act.

(j)Relationship between Law of Evidence and Substantive Laws (Criminal & Civil) and Procedural Laws (BNSS, 2023 & CPC, 1908)

1. The three-layer map

LayerFunctionExamples
Substantive lawCreates/defines rights, liabilities, duties, offencesBNS 2023 [IPC 1860], Contract Act 1872, Transfer of Property Act 1882, IPR statutes
Procedural (adjective) law — court processPrescribes the machinery to enforce/vindicate those rightsBNSS 2023 [CrPC 1973], CPC 1908
Law of evidence (also adjective)Prescribes how facts underlying those rights are brought before and evaluated by the courtIEA 1872 → BSA 2023; lex fori, applies retrospectively

As the course material puts it: the Evidence Act is adjective/procedural law and lex fori — "the law of the place where the question arises"; a question of competency or admissibility is decided by the law of the forum. Procedural statutes apply retrospectively (vested rights apart), which is why BSA 2023 governs trials after 1-7-2024 even for older transactions.

2. Evidence ↔ Substantive law (BNS/IPC; civil statutes)

  • Offence definitions fix the facts in issue: to prosecute murder, s.101 BNS [s.300 IPC] supplies the ingredients — causing death, intention/knowledge — which become the facts in issue under s.2(1)(g) BSA; the illustrations to the definition literally reuse the penal code (provocation, unsoundness — exceptions to s.300 IPC [BNS]).
  • Presumptions keyed to offences: BSA s.118 (dowry death) imports s.80 BNS [s.304B IPC]; s.120 BSA imports s.64(2) BNS [s.376(2) IPC]; s.117 BSA [s.113A IEA] ties to abetment of suicide (s.108 BNS r/w s.85 BNS cruelty [s.498A IPC]).
  • Defences shift the burden: s.105 IEA / s.108 BSA — accused pleading s.22 BNS [s.84 IPC] insanity or BNS exceptions must prove it (preponderance standard), showing substantive defences operating through evidential burdens.
  • Civil side: Contract Act ss.10–25 define what must be proved to enforce an agreement; s.92 IEA/ s.95 BSA then bars oral evidence to contradict a written contract — substantive and evidential rules interlock.
  • Substantive flavour inside the Evidence Act: the doctrine of estoppel (ss.115–117 IEA / ss.121–123 BSA) creates rights — a substantive rule housed in an adjective code (the course material flags this duality expressly).

3. Evidence ↔ Procedural law (CPC 1908 / BNSS 2023)

CPC 1908 junctions (civil)
  • Order XIV (issues): the Explanation to s.2(1)(g) BSA [s.3 IEA] defines facts in issue by reference to issues of fact recorded "under the provisions of the law… relating to civil procedure" — i.e., CPC Order XIV. Issues = crystallised facts in issue.
  • s.3 BSA Explanation [s.5 IEA proviso]: the Act does not enable a person to give evidence of a fact he is disentitled to prove by civil procedure — e.g., an unpleaded fact (Order VI/VIII) cannot be proved; a new plea not taken cannot be led in evidence.
  • Order XIII: production and endorsement of documents; Order XI: discovery & inspection; Order XVI: summons to witnesses and expenses; Order XVIII: order of hearing, examination-in-chief, cross-examination, re-examination sequencing (mirrors ss.137–139 IEA / ss.142–144 BSA); Order XIX: affidavits (permitted proof though s.1 excludes affidavits from "evidence"); s.132 CPC: witness not excused from answering (parallel to s.137 IEA / s.142-range BSA).
  • s.11 CPC (res judicata) and ss.115–117 IEA estoppels together bar re-agitation of proved facts.
BNSS 2023 [CrPC 1973] junctions (criminal)
  • Investigation-stage statements: successors of ss.161/164 CrPC (witness statements; recording of confessions & dying declarations by Magistrate) — s.162 CrPC bars proof of police statements, an evidential rule located in procedure.
  • Trial-stage: successor of s.273 CrPC (evidence in presence of accused — the provision interpreted in Prafulla Desai), ss.274–275 (recording in open court), ss.284–290 (commissions — the route Desai used), s.313 CrPC (examination of accused; not evidence on oath but usable under s.313(4)-type evaluation), s.299 (evidence in absence of accused).
  • Identification & forensic process: BNSS provisions for sample collection, audio-video recording of search & seizure, and electronic service — procedural rules that create the electronic records the BSA then admits (ss.2(1)(d)/(e), 61–63).
  • Special statutes: NDPS, POCSO, NI Act reverse-onus clauses — procedural statutes importing evidential presumptions.
Real example — one murder case, three codes

A kills B for insurance money. BNS [IPC] supplies the offences — s.103(1) BNS [s.302 IPC] murder, s.61(2) BNS [s.120B IPC] conspiracy, and the insurance motive. BNSS [CrPC] supplies the machine — FIR, investigation, s.164-type recording of the dying declaration, charge framing, trial. BSA [IEA] supplies the proof-rules — the dying declaration (s.26 BSA [s.32(1) IEA]), the insurance policy as document (s.2(1)(d)), CCTV as electronic record (s.61), burden on prosecution (s.104 BSA [s.101 IEA]), and the "proved" standard (s.2(1)(j)). Remove any one layer and the trial collapses: no substance → nothing to punish; no procedure → no forum; no evidence → no proof.

Allied enactments completing the picture
  • Arbitration & Conciliation Act, 1996 — ss.19 & 66: tribunal not bound by IEA/CPC (matches the s.1/s.2(1)(a) exclusion of arbitrators).
  • Contempt of Courts Act, 1971; Commission of Inquiry Act, 1952: special proof regimes; commissions of inquiry follow natural justice (T.R. Verma lineage).
  • IT Act, 2000 ss.79A–85B: electronic evidence & presumptions complementing BSA ss.61–63.
  • Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891; Commercial Courts Act, 2015 (s.14 affidavit evidence); Family Courts Act, 1984 (s.14) — statutory relaxations/variations of ordinary proof rules.

PYQ-1 · DU LL.B. 2003

"A tape-recorded statement is a document. Discuss. What should be kept in mind while relying upon tape-recorded evidence?"

Model Answer

1. Statutory anchor: "Document" (s.3 IEA / s.2(1)(d) BSA) means any matter expressed or described or otherwise recorded upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks or any other means… intended to be used… for recording that matter. A magnetic tape on which spoken words are recorded is "matter recorded upon a substance by other means" — hence a document; after 1-7-2024 this is beyond doubt, since s.2(1)(d) BSA includes electronic and digital records and Illustration (vi) lists voice-mail messages and digital recordings.

2. Judicial anchor (DU case material): R.M. Malkani v. State of Maharashtra AIR 1973 SC 157 — the Supreme Court admitted tape-recorded telephonic conversations as contemporaneous relevant evidence; "just as a photograph taken without the knowledge of the person photographed can become relevant and admissible so does a tape record of a conversation unnoticed by the talkers"; when the court permits a tape to be played it acts on real evidence.

3. Cautions while relying (the Malkani two-direction care + later refinements): (i) relevancy — the conversation must relate to a fact in issue/relevant fact; (ii) voluntariness — not extracted by coercion (else Art. 20(3) and fairness concerns arise); (iii) genuineness — freedom from tampering, mutilation, erasure; prove the chain of custody, the voice of the speaker (by witnesses who recognise the voice, or by comparison under s.45-range expert/voice-spectrography evidence), and continuity of recording; (iv) accuracy of transcription; (v) the judge's discretion to exclude where obtaining was so unfair as to operate against the accused (the golden rule in Kuruma/Malkani); (vi) illegal obtaining goes to weight, not admissibility (Magraj Patodia), but privacy of the innocent citizen is protected (Malkani, Art. 21).

4. Conclusion: tape-recorded statements are documents/evidence admissible subject to the above safeguards; post-BSA they additionally enjoy s.61 (non-denial of admissibility) and, from proper custody, primary-evidence status (Explanation 5 to s.57 BSA).

PYQ-2 · DU LL.B. 2005

"Discuss the relevancy and admissibility of the evidence obtained through 'Video Conferencing'."

Model Answer

Rule: evidence = oral statements + documents including electronic records (s.3 IEA; s.2(1)(e) BSA). State of Maharashtra v. Prafulla B. Desai (2003) 4 SCC 601 held video-conferenced testimony to be evidence: electronic records can be produced as evidence "even in criminal matters… this would include video-conferencing"; "presence" in s.273 CrPC includes constructive presence; the procedure satisfies Art. 21; updating construction keeps the ongoing Act current (Bennion; Podar Cement; S.J. Choudhary).

Relevancy: what the witness perceives and states through VC relates to facts in issue/relevant facts exactly as live testimony (ss.5–55 IEA / 3–50 BSA); nothing in the relevancy provisions turns on the medium.

Admissibility safeguards (Desai catalogue): oath by authorised officer; accused and counsel present with full cross-examination; observation of demeanour (playback advantage); no tutoring (cleared room / Consulate); commission route (ss.284–289 CrPC) where attendance cannot be procured; open-court VC preferred where the witness's State has an extradition treaty and punishes perjury; costs on the State; memo of non-cooperation weighed at evaluation.

Post-BSA: s.2(1)(e) BSA ("statements given electronically") statutorily completes what Desai achieved judicially; BNSS provisions for electronic recording reinforce it.

Conclusion: VC evidence is relevant, admissible and constitutionally valid, subject to procedural safeguards — it is "actual reality, not virtual reality".

PYQ-3 · DU LL.B.

"Is evidence collected in an illegal manner admissible?"

Model Answer

Short answer: Yes — relevancy, not the method of obtaining, governs admissibility; but the court holds a fairness discretion and illegality affects weight and may attract departmental/constitutional consequences.

Authorities (DU material): R. v. Leatham — "it matters not how you get it; if you steal it even, it would be admissible"; Kuruma v. R. — "if evidence was admissible it matters not how it was obtained", with the golden-rule caution; Magraj Patodia v. R.K. Birta — document procured by improper/illegal means admissible if relevant and genuine; Malkani — tape obtained without breach of the speaker's voluntariness admissible; privacy protects the innocent, not the guilty; R. v. Maqsud Ali — mechanical eavesdropper admissible.

Qualifications: (1) judicial discretion to exclude where strict admissibility operates unfairly against the accused; (2) Art. 20(3) bars compelled self-incrimination (Selvi lineage re: narco/polygraph without consent — supplementary); (3) Art. 21 prohibits highhanded invasion of the innocent citizen's privacy (later strengthened by K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) — supplementary); (4) the officer committing illegality may face departmental or penal liability (e.g., s.25 Telegraph Act / IT Act s.43/66 for unauthorised interception) — the wrong is punished separately, the evidence survives.

PYQ-4 · UP (J) Mains 2019 Q7(c) (DU-referenced)

"What do you understand by the word 'Court' used in the Indian Evidence Act, 1872? Discuss with the help of decided cases."

Model Answer

Definition: s.3 IEA [s.2(1)(a) BSA]: "Court includes all Judges and Magistrates, and all persons, except arbitrators, legally authorised to take evidence." Elements: (i) judges; (ii) magistrates; (iii) any person legally authorised to take evidence (commissioners under ss.284–290 CrPC/ CPC s.75, inquiry officers where statute authorises); (iv) express exclusion of arbitrators (reinforced by s.1 IEA / s.1 BSA and ss.19/66, A&C Act 1996).

Case law: Union of India v. T.R. Verma (1957) — tribunals are not courts under the Act; they need only observe natural justice; hence a departmental enquiry is outside the Act. Contrast: a commissioner recording evidence on commission is a "person legally authorised to take evidence" and thus within "Court". Stephen's trinity — actor, reus, judex — explains the term's core: the judex, the authority that takes evidence and decides.

Significance: the Act's entire machinery (oath, cross-examination, presumptions, estoppel against judges — s.127 BSA [s.121 IEA]) is addressed to "Courts"; locating a forum inside/outside the definition decides whether strict evidence rules apply (cf. Prafulla Desai's commission route).

PYQ-5 · UP (J) Mains 2000 Q5(a); 2012 7(a)(iv)

"What is 'facts in issue'? Illustrate." / "Short note: Facts in issue."

Model Answer

Definition + Explanation: s.3 IEA / s.2(1)(g) BSA — facts from which, by themselves or with other facts, the existence/non-existence/nature/extent of a right, liability or disability asserted or denied necessarily follows; and where a civil court records an issue of fact (CPC Order XIV), the fact asserted or denied in its answer is a fact in issue.

Essence: the disputed core of the lis — admitted facts cease to be in issue (s.56 IEA / s.51 BSA). In criminal trials the charge's ingredients are the facts in issue.

Statutory illustration: A tried for murdering B — in issue: (i) A caused B's death [s.101 BNS/ s.300 IPC]; (ii) intention; (iii) grave & sudden provocation; (iv) unsoundness of mind [s.22 BNS / s.84 IPC].

Own illustration: in the husband-wife example, only the denied claim ("law from NLU Delhi") is a fact in issue; the admitted house/car/YouTube facts need no proof.

Distinction from relevant facts: facts in issue are the ends; relevant facts are the means (connected in a mode recognised by ss.3–50 BSA); every fact in issue is relevant, but relevant facts (motive, CCTV) are not themselves in issue.

PYQ-7 · UP (J) 1986/2003 (DU-referenced)

"Distinguish between relevancy and admissibility." / "All admissible evidence is relevant but all relevant evidence is not necessarily admissible. Comment."

Model Answer

Use the four-point table in topic (i): relevancy = logic/probability declared in ss.5–55 IEA/3–50 BSA; admissibility = the gate-keeping rules of law. The statement is partially true: admissibility presupposes relevancy (irrelevant evidence is never admissible), but relevancy does not guarantee admissibility — e.g., a relevant hearsay statement is excluded (ss.60/64-range rules), relevant privileged communications are barred (ss.122–129 IEA / 126–134 BSA), relevant secondary evidence is excluded until primary is accounted for (ss.57–60 BSA). Conversely, Magraj Patodia shows admissibility is a legal question independent of the legality of acquisition. Hence the two are "neither synonymous nor is the one included in the other" in the sense that the sets overlap but the gates differ.

PYQ-8 · UP (J) 1986/2003 (DU-referenced)

"Distinguish between direct and circumstantial evidence. Can a person be convicted on circumstantial evidence alone?"

Model Answer

Direct: evidence of a fact in issue perceived directly (eye-witness to stabbing; confession); proves the fact without inference. Circumstantial: evidence of relevant facts from which the fact in issue is inferred (last-seen, motive, recovery, CCTV at periphery).

Conviction on circumstances alone — yes, if the chain is complete: the circumstances must be (i) firmly established, (ii) consistent only with guilt, (iii) inconsistent with any innocent hypothesis, (iv) so complete as to leave no reasonable ground for a contrary conclusion, and (v) exclude every hypothesis except the one sought to be proved (the classic five-link test; see the conspiracy/dying-declaration case-law in later units, e.g., the line of authorities culminating in Mukesh v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2017) 6 SCC 1 — DU case material).

DU LL.B. II Term Examination, May–June 2024 · Paper Code LB-201 · Law of Evidence (8 questions × 20 marks; attempt 5) — Full Paper with Model Answers

Reconstructed from the scanned official paper in the uploads. Each model answer cites the IEA section and its BSA counterpart and the DU case material.

Q1 (a)

In a dispute between X and Y for inducing A to break his contract of service, X produces the following statement of A as evidence: "I am leaving you because Y has made me a better offer." Discuss relevancy.

Model Answer

Provisions: ss.5–7 IEA ↔ ss.3–5 BSA; s.3 (relevant fact; fact in issue).

Analysis: The fact in issue is whether Y induced A to break the contract. A's statement is relevant on three converging routes: (1) as the cause/effect and occasion of the facts in issue — A's departure and the reason accompanying it are facts from which inducement may be inferred (s.7 IEA / s.5 BSA); (2) as evidence of A's state of mind/motive acted upon (s.8 IEA / s.6 BSA conduct); (3) as a statement accompanying the act of leaving — part of the same transaction (s.6 IEA / s.4 BSA).

Hearsay answer: the statement is tendered not for the truth of "Y made a better offer" but for the fact that it was made and operated on A's mind — i.e., as circumstantial evidence of inducement; hence the hearsay bar does not bite.

Conclusion: relevant and admissible; its weight will depend on cross-examination and surrounding circumstances.

Q1 (b)

X is prosecuted for rioting in Dehradun on 15-04-2023. X produced a railway ticket showing that on 15-04-2023 he was travelling Varanasi to Delhi. Discuss relevancy.

Model Answer

Provision: s.11 IEA ↔ s.9 BSA — facts not otherwise relevant become relevant if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue (the plea of alibi).

Analysis: The fact in issue is X's presence in the riot at Dehradun. The ticket evidences a fact inconsistent with that presence — a person travelling Varanasi→Delhi on the same day could not be rioting in Dehradun. It is therefore relevant under s.11(a) IEA/s.9 BSA and admissible as documentary evidence (s.2(1)(d) BSA), its probative value turning on genuineness, endorsement, and proof that X was the traveller (identity).

Conclusion: relevant; the court will weigh it with any counter-evidence (e.g., ticket misuse).

Q2

Mr. A wrote to Mr. B asking him to supply explosives for blowing up a Delhi Metro Station. B took time. A wrote a second letter seeking reasons for delay. Before the second letter reached B, A received and used the explosives. In a conspiracy case the prosecution wants both letters. Are they admissible?

Model Answer

Provision: s.10 IEA ↔ s.8 BSA (things said or done by conspirator in reference to common design); Mirza Akbar v. Emperor AIR 1940 PC 176; admissions ss.17–18 IEA / ss.15–16 BSA.

Rule (Mirza Akbar): only things said/done during the pendency of the common design, i.e., in carrying it out, are receivable against co-conspirators under s.10; things said before the conspiracy was formed or after it was completed are not admissible under s.10 against the others.

First letter: admissible — it evidences the formation and content of the common design (agreement to commit offences under ss.61(1)/61(2) BNS [s.120B IPC] and the metro blast), is in reference to the common design once B's subsequent supply shows acceptance, and is in any event A's own admission against A.

Second letter: not admissible under s.10 against B — by the time it was written the common design had already been executed (explosives received and used); it is a post-completion narration (Mirza Akbar). It remains admissible against A alone as A's admission (s.17 IEA/s.15 BSA) of the conspiracy and his impatience with its execution.

Conclusion: letter 1 — yes (s.10/s.8 and as admission); letter 2 — not against the co-conspirator under s.10, but yes against its author as an admission.

Q3

SI Varanasi, in a theft case, records the accused's statement: "I will tell the place of deposit of three golden idols which I stole from Pakka Mahal on 11 April." The accused then leads police to a Dharmsala of Dal Mandi and points out the idols. How much of the statement can be used? What inferences follow?

Model Answer

Provision: s.27 IEA ↔ s.23 BSA; Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor AIR 1947 PC 67; Bodhraj v. State of J&K (2002) 8 SCC 45.

Severance rule: only so much of the information as relates distinctly to the fact thereby discovered is provable. The fact discovered here = the place of concealment of the idols (and the idols themselves).

Admissible part: "I will tell the place of deposit of three golden idols" + the pointing-out — it relates distinctly to the discovery.

Inadmissible part: "which I stole from Pakka Mahal on 11 April" — a confessional embellishment not relating distinctly to the discovery (Bodhraj; Aghnoo Nagesia lineage): theft, place of theft and date are not facts discovered.

Inferences: the accused's knowledge of the concealed place — exclusive knowledge from which, with the recovery, the court may infer consciousness of guilt and connection with the theft as a link in the circumstantial chain (Kottaya: the statement is used to prove the accused's knowledge, not as a confession). It is not a confession (the recording SI cannot take a confessional statement — s.25 IEA / s.22 BSA lineage), but its discovery-portion is potent corroborative evidence.

Q4

(a) "There is no absolute rule that dying declaration can be the sole basis of conviction unless corroborated." Comment. (b) A seriously injured victim tells the doctor on duty, "Mr. P has stabbed me because……" and collapses and dies before completing the sentence. Discuss relevancy and veracity in P's murder trial.

Model Answer

(a) Provision: s.32(1) IEA ↔ s.26(1) BSA; Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay AIR 1958 SC 22 (DU case material): a dying declaration stands on the same footing as other evidence; it can be the sole basis of conviction if truthful, voluntary and the maker was in a fit state of mind; corroboration is a rule of prudence, not law; the maxim nemo moriturus praesumitur mentiri underlies it (a person not expected to die meets his maker without falsehood).

(b) Relevancy: the statement is by a dead person as to the cause of his death — squarely s.32(1) IEA/s.26(1) BSA; the cause of death is the fact in issue in P's murder trial (s.101/103 BNS [ss.300/302 IPC]).

Veracity tests (Khushal Rao/Laxman/Ravinder Singh, all in DU material): (i) spoken to the doctor on duty — neutral recipient, first opportunity, no tutoring; (ii) seriousness of injury supports a settled expectation of death and truthfulness; (iii) clear identification ("Mr. P has stabbed me") — the operative part is complete; the unfinished "because…" goes only to motive, which is not an essential of s.32(1); (iv) no enmity suggested to falsely implicate.

Cautions: abrupt termination requires the court to ensure the recorded portion reflects what the deceased intended (cf. Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana — incomplete declarations scrutinised for completeness of the intended statement); fitness of mind may be certified by the doctor (or otherwise proved — Laxman v. State of Maharashtra).

Conclusion: relevant and admissible; if the court finds it voluntary and truthful, it can sustain conviction even without corroboration, though prudence welcomes the inherent corroboration of the stab injuries.

Q5

Anshul, 5th rank in DU LL.B. entrance, provisionally admitted while qualifying result pending; later declared 60%; appeared in First & Second Term, promoted to Third Term; then the University, claiming a marking error (actual 45%), cancels admission. Can the University do so?

Model Answer

Provision: doctrine of estoppel, s.115 IEA ↔ s.121 BSA; Sanatan Gauda v. Berhampur University AIR 1990 SC 1075 (DU case material).

Elements applied: (1) representation/conduct — the University admitted Anshul, issued a marks-card showing 60%, let him appear in two terms and promoted him; (2) belief & alteration of position — Anshul invested time, fees and opportunities on that faith; (3) the error, if any, was the University's own; Anshul suppressed nothing.

Authority: In Sanatan Gauda the Supreme Court held that a university which permitted a student to proceed through terms cannot, years later, cancel the admission on the ground of its own earlier error — it is estopped from approbating and reprobating.

Counterpoint & resolution: estoppel normally cannot confer eligibility against a statute; but where, as here, the cancellation rests on the authority's own negligent representation and the student is blameless and has substantially proceeded, equity and Sanatan Gauda protect the student.

Conclusion: No — the University cannot cancel; its conduct raises an estoppel (s.115 IEA/s.121 BSA).

Q6

(a) What are the exceptions to the privileged communication between husband and wife? (b) A, a client, says to B, an attorney: "I have committed forgery, and I wish you to defend me." Is this statement protected from disclosure?

Model Answer

(a) Provision: s.122 IEA ↔ s.128 BSA. Exceptions: (1) consent of the spouse who made the communication; (2) suits between spouses; (3) criminal proceedings where one spouse is prosecuted for an offence against the other (or against the other's property, per analogous provisions); (4) what is not a communication — acts/conditions observable by the spouse (independent observation, e.g., visible injury) are provable; (5) the privilege is personal to the spouses — a stranger who overheard/intercepted is not barred from testifying (M.C. Verghese v. T.J. Ponnan AIR 1970 SC 1876, DU case material); (6) waiver by the communicating spouse offering himself/herself as a witness.

(b) Provision: ss.126–129 IEA ↔ ss.131–134 BSA. The communication was made in the course and for the purpose of professional engagement (defence) → prima facie protected. The exceptions — communication in furtherance of any illegal purpose, or facts showing a crime/fraud committed since engagement — do not apply: the forgery is a past offence confessed to obtain defence, not a future crime to be furthered. Hence yes, protected; B cannot disclose it without A's express consent (s.132(1) BSA). Contrast: had A said "help me fabricate the deed", the proviso would strip protection.

Q7

"The law is not that the evidence of an accomplice deserves outright rejection if there is no corroboration, rather circumspection and care is required…… it is desirable that the court seeks reassuring circumstances to satisfy the judicial conscience that the evidence is true." Elucidate.

Model Answer

Provisions: s.133 IEA ↔ s.138 BSA — an accomplice is a competent witness; a conviction is not illegal merely because it proceeds on uncorroborated accomplice testimony; Illustration (b) to s.114 IEA / s.119 BSA — the court may presume an accomplice unworthy of credit unless corroborated in material particulars.

Doctrine (Bhuboni Sahu v. The King, AIR 1949 PC 257 — DU case material): the rule of corroboration is a rule of caution/prudence, not a rule of law; the accomplice is a partisan, interested, often immoral witness — hence the judicial conscience demands corroboration in material particulars connecting the accused with the crime (not necessarily every detail); the dual test — reliability of the witness plus independent confirmation.

Supporting authorities (DU material): Haroon Haji Abdulla v. State of Maharashtra and Ravinder Singh v. State of Haryana — nature and extent of corroboration; the statement quoted is a precise restatement of this jurisprudence.

Conclusion: legally a conviction may rest on accomplice evidence alone; practically it should not — hence the quoted formulation.

Q8

A child was born within 280 days of husband's death. Evidence was offered that about conception the husband suffered from Carbuncle and died of illness within 14 days. Can the plea of paternity be negatived?

Model Answer

Provision: s.112 IEA ↔ s.116 BSA — birth during marriage (or within 280 days of its dissolution, the mother remaining unmarried) is conclusive proof of legitimacy, unless non-access between the spouses at the time of conception is proved.

Law on rebuttal (DU material): Goutam Kundu v. State of WB AIR 1993 SC 2295 — the presumption is of the strongest kind; it is displaced only by proof of non-access, i.e., absence of opportunity of intercourse — a positive proof, not mere improbability; the court will not order blood/DNA tests as a roving enquiry to upset it; Dipanwita Roy v. Ronobroto Roy AIR 2015 SC 418 — same high threshold.

Application: The conditions of s.112/s.116 are met (birth within 280 days of death; mother unmarried). The offered evidence — husband's carbuncle and death within 14 days of the conception window — is at best evidence of illness; unless it is shown that the illness precluded access/intercourse (e.g., hospitalisation/separation negativing opportunity), it does not amount to proof of non-access. Illness ≠ non-access; impotence or incapacity falling short of impossibility of access does not displace the conclusive proof.

Conclusion: On the facts as stated, the plea of paternity cannot be negatived; the presumption stands unless non-access is positively proved.
