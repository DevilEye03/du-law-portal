PART 0The Statutory Framework of Competence

0.1 “Witnesses are the eyes and ears of justice”

Foundation

Bentham’s aphorism, quoted at the opening of the DU chapter on competence: “Witnesses are eyes and ears of justice” — “but these eyes and ears must be fit and in working condition”. Section 124 BSA (s. 118 IEA) is the general provision deciding competency: it presumes every person competent, and incompetency is the exception, to be established only where the Court considers the person prevented — by tender years, extreme old age, disease (of body or mind), or any other cause of the same kind — from understanding the questions put to him or from giving rational answers to them. The Explanation protects the person of unsound mind (IEA: “lunatic”): he is not incompetent unless his unsoundness actually prevents understanding and rational answering — i.e. lucidity restores competence.

s. 124, BSA 2023 — “Who may testify” [= s. 118, IEA 1872]: “All persons shall be competent to testify unless the Court considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions put to them, or from giving rational answers to those questions, by tender years, extreme old age, disease, whether of body or mind, or any other cause of the same kind. — Explanation. A person of unsound mind is not incompetent to testify, unless he is prevented by his unsoundness of mind from understanding the questions put to him and giving rational answers to them.”

DU notes MCQ (reproduced): “Who among the following is treated absolutely incompetent under s. 118 IEA? (a) lunatic (b) child of tender years (c) person of extreme old age (d) none of the above” — Answer: (d). Incompetence under s. 124/118 is never absolute or status-based; it is always functional (understanding + rational answers).

0.3 Connected law at the threshold: the Oaths Act, 1969

Connected Law
s. 4(1), Oaths Act 1969 (repealing the Oaths Act 1873): “Oaths or affirmations shall be made by all witnesses, that is to say, all persons who may lawfully be examined, or give, or be required to give, evidence by or before any court… Provided that where the witness is a child under twelve years of age, and the court… is of opinion that, though the witness understands the duty of speaking the truth, he does not understand the nature of an oath or affirmation, the foregoing provisions of this Section and the provisions of Section 5 shall not apply to such witness; but in any such case the absence of an oath or affirmation shall not render inadmissible any evidence given by such witness nor affect the obligation of the witness to state the truth.”
  • Effect: oath is the rule; for a child under 12 who understands the duty of truth but not the nature of the oath, oath is dispensed with — yet the evidence remains admissible and the duty to speak the truth continues (reinforced by s. 7: omission or irregularity in administering the oath does not affect the obligation to state the truth or the admissibility of evidence).
  • Interplay with s. 124 BSA / 118 IEA: competence (understanding + rational answers) is a Evidence-Act question; oath (its form or dispensation) is an Oaths-Act question. Absence of oath goes to weight, not admissibility.
  • For dumb witnesses: oath may be administered “by appropriate means… with the assistance of an interpreter” (Darshan Singh); a person under a religious vow of silence is deemed unable to speak within s. 125/119 (Lakhan Singh v. Emperor, 1942 P 183) and may depose in writing without breaking the vow.

PART 1Child Witness — s. 124 BSA / s. 118 IEA

1.1 The rule: no age bar, only a capacity test

Doctrine
  • The Evidence Act fixes no minimum age for a witness. A child is competent if he can understand the questions put to him and give rational answers — the determining factor is capacity, not age (DU companion notes; P Ramesh).
  • The two-limb test (P Ramesh v. State, 2019): in criminal proceedings a person of any age is competent if she/he is able to (i) understand questions put as a witness; and (ii) give such answers to the questions that can be understood. “A child of tender age can be allowed to testify if she/he has the intellectual capacity to understand questions and give rational answers thereto. A child becomes incompetent only in case the court considers that the child was unable to understand the questions and answer them in a coherent and comprehensible manner.”
  • Voir dire (preliminary examination): “In order to determine the competency of a child witness, the judge has to form her or his opinion. The judge is at liberty to test the capacity of a child witness and no precise rule can be laid down regarding the degree of intelligence and knowledge which will render the child a competent witness. The competency… can be ascertained by questioning her/him to find out the capability to understand the occurrence witnessed and to speak the truth before the court.” — i.e. the court may put preliminary (voir dire) questions before administering oath/recording evidence.
  • Sequence: “A child has to be a competent witness first, only then is her/his statement admissible.”
  • Oath: child under 12 who understands the duty of truth but not the nature of oath — no oath required (Oaths Act s. 4(1) proviso); absence of oath does not make the evidence inadmissible.

1.2 Value of a child’s testimony — the three DU-note cases

State of Uttar Pradesh v. Krishna Master & Ors.

(2010) — Supreme Court, decided 3 August 2010 • child witness aged six years • DU notes Ch. XIII (13.1.1.2)
Facts

A gruesome murder of the child’s father, mother, brothers etc. was committed; the child, then six years old, witnessed it. His evidence was recorded with a gap of about ten years between the incident and the recording of evidence.

Observation of the Supreme Court
“A child of tender age is always receptive to abnormal events which take place in its life and would never forget those events for the rest of his life. The child would be able to recapitulate correctly and exactly when asked about the same in future. When a child of tender age witnesses gruesome murder of his father, mother, brothers etc. he is not likely to forget the incident for his whole life and would certainly recapitulate facts in his memory when asked about the same at any point of time, notwithstanding the gap of about ten years between the incident and recording of his evidence… it would be doing injustice to a child witness possessing sharp memory to say that it is inconceivable for him to recapitulate facts… Normally, a child aged six years is not expected to be out of house at the dead of night and he is expected to be in the company of his parents.”
Decision

Accused convicted for committing murder; the child’s testimony accepted.

Principle

Delay between incident and testimony does not by itself discredit a child of tender years; traumatic/abnormal events are indelibly imprinted on a child’s memory; presence of a six-year-old in the house at dead of night is natural.

Dattu Ramrao Sakhare & Ors. v. State of Maharashtra

decided 8 May 1997 • child witness Sarubai, aged about 10 years • DU notes Ch. XIII (13.1.1.3)
Facts

The entire prosecution case rested upon the evidence of Sarubai, a child witness aged about 10 years.

Observation of the Supreme Court
  • “A child witness can be the basis of conviction. In other words even in the absence of oath the evidence of a child witness can be considered under Section 118 of the Evidence Act provided that such witness is able to understand the question and able to give rational answers thereof.”
  • “The evidence of a child witness and credibility thereof would depend upon the circumstances of each case. The only precaution which the court should bear in mind… is that the witness must be a reliable one and his/her demeanour must be like any other competent witness and there is no likelihood of being tutored.”
  • “There is no practice that in every case the evidence of such a witness be corroborated before a conviction can be allowed to stand but, however, as a rule of prudence the court always finds it desirable to have the corroboration to such evidence from other dependable evidence on record.”
Principle (rule of law vs rule of prudence)

Rule of law: no corroboration is legally necessary for convicting on a child’s testimony. Rule of prudence: corroboration from dependable evidence is desirable before conviction. The touchstones are reliability, natural demeanour, absence of tutoring.

P Ramesh v. State, Rep. by Inspector of Police

decided 9 July 2019 • children aged eight and six years • murder of mother by father • DU notes Ch. XIII (13.1.1.3)
Facts

The appellant was tried for the murder of his wife. During trial the prosecution sought to adduce the evidence of the children of the appellant and the deceased; on 19 May 2015, when their evidence was to be recorded, one was eight years old and the other six years old.

Law applied

s. 118 IEA (competence) read with s. 4, Oaths Act 1969 (oath mandatory for all witnesses, except child witnesses under twelve within the proviso).

Observation & decision

The two-limb competency test and the voir dire liberty of the judge (quoted in 1.1 above); “no precise rule can be laid down”; competency ascertained by questioning the child about the occurrence and the duty to speak the truth.

Principle

Competency of a child witness is a judicial determination on facts, made by preliminary questioning; the standard is understanding + intelligible, rational answers, never a fixed age or IQ threshold.

Real Example

Domestic-violence murder, sole child eyewitness. A (the husband) kills his wife W at night in their home; their daughter D (7) sees A strike W with a rod. At trial D is first asked preliminary questions (her name, school, what “truth” means, whether she was present) — she answers coherently: competent under s. 124 BSA. She does not understand what an oath means: the court records her evidence without oath under the Oaths Act proviso; the evidence is still admissible and she remains bound to speak the truth. Her account is spontaneous, matches the injury pattern and the position of the rod, and shows no coaching: credible. The court may convict on her testimony alone (rule of law) but will look, as prudence, for supporting material — the neighbour who heard the quarrel, the FSL report on the rod — before convicting (Dattu Ramrao). Delay of four years in recording her evidence is immaterial: abnormal events are indelible in a child’s memory (Krishna Master).

PART 2Dumb Witness / Witness Unable to Communicate Verbally — s. 125 BSA / s. 119 IEA

2.1 The provision and the 2013 amendment

Statute
s. 125, BSA 2023 [= s. 119, IEA 1872 as amended in 2013]: “A witness who is unable to speak may give his evidence in any other manner in which he can make it intelligible, as by writing or by signs; but such writing must be written and the signs made in open Court, and evidence so given shall be deemed to be oral evidence: Provided that if the witness is unable to communicate verbally, (1) the Court shall take the assistance of an interpreter or a special educator in recording the statement, and (2) such statement shall be videographed.”
#Before the 2013 amendmentAfter the 2013 amendment
1Marginal note: “Dumb witnesses”Marginal note: “Witness unable to communicate verbally”
2No provisoProviso added: interpreter / special educator + mandatory videography

DU notes glosses: “deemed” = legal fiction — writing/signs are treated as “oral” for the purposes of the oral-evidence provisions (ss. 59–60, 91–92 IEA; ss. 56–57, 88–89 BSA); “open Court” connects to CrPC s. 327 (court to be open), securing transparency.

2.2 Scope and special situations (DU notes)

  • Object (Darshan Singh): deaf-and-dumb persons were once “contemplated in law as idiots”; modern science shows such persons are “generally found more intelligent, and susceptible to far higher culture than one was once supposed” — hence physical disability must not exclude them from witness status.
  • Vow of silence: a witness under a religious vow of silence is deemed unable to speak within s. 119/125 and may give evidence in writing in open court without being forced to break the vow (Lakhan Singh v. Emperor, 1942 P 183; Monir’s Textbook, cited in DU notes).
  • Signs, nods and gestures have evidentiary value: Meesala Ramakrishnan v. State of A.P. (1994) 4 SCC 182 — a dying declaration recorded by means of signs and nods of a person unable to speak is a verbal statement, relevant and admissible; the Court clarified that “verbal” does not mean “oral”; “signs and gestures made by nods or head are admissible and such nods and gestures are not only admissible but possess evidentiary value”.
  • Dumb child: both ss. 124 and 125 BSA (118 & 119 IEA) apply — s. 124 is the general competence gate (understanding + rational answers), s. 125 the manner-of-evidence gate (DU notes Q&A).

2.3 The Darshan Singh guidelines (State of Rajasthan v. Darshan Singh @ Darshan Lal, 21 May 2012)

State of Rajasthan v. Darshan Singh @ Darshan Lal

Supreme Court, decided 21 May 2012 • sole eye-witness Geeta (PW 16), deaf and dumb • DU notes Ch. XIII (13.2)
Facts

Geeta, wife of the deceased Kaku Singh, communicated by gestures that the accused Darshan Singh had stayed with them at night, gave Kaku Singh a pill with water making him unconscious, and that two more accomplices of Darshan came from outside and all three inflicted injuries on Kaku Singh with sharp-edged weapons. The deceased had an illicit relationship with the accused’s sister (motive). Geeta, being deaf and dumb, was the sole eye-witness; her statement was recorded in sign language with the help of her father Jaswant Singh as interpreter.

The six observations of the Supreme Court
  1. Object of s. 119: the historical equation of deaf-and-dumb persons with “idiots” has been abandoned in light of modern science (above).
  2. Duty of the Court: before examining a deaf-and-dumb person, the Court must exercise due caution and ascertain that he possesses the requisite intelligence and understands the nature of an oath.
  3. Oath: on being so satisfied, oath may be administered by appropriate means, with the assistance of an interpreter.
  4. Read-and-write preferred: if the person can read and write, it is most desirable to adopt that method, being more satisfactory than sign language; “the law requires that there must be a record of signs and not the interpretation of signs”.
  5. Credibility: a dumb person need not be prevented from being a credible and reliable witness merely due to physical disability; if literate he may convey himself through writing, otherwise through signs and gestures.
  6. Summing up: a deaf-and-dumb person is a competent witness; if oath can be administered, it should be; if literate, record statement in writing (questions in writing, answers in writing); if not literate, record in sign language with an interpreter if found necessary; the interpreter should be a person of the same surroundings, without interest in the case, and himself administered oath.
Principle

Physical disability of speech/hearing affects only the manner of giving evidence, never competence; the recording must preserve the witness’s own signs (record of signs, not interpretation), with interpreter-safeguards and (post-2013) videography.

Real Example

Shop assault, deaf-mute eyewitness. M, a deaf-and-mute shop assistant, witnesses a snatch-and-assault. She reads and writes Hindi fluently. Following Darshan Singh, the court first satisfies itself of her intelligence and that she understands the duty of truth; administers oath through a sign-language interpreter (a government-certified interpreter with no link to the parties, himself sworn); then puts questions in writing and records her answers in writing in open court, the whole proceeding being videographed per the s. 125 proviso. Her written deposition is deemed oral evidence and can found a conviction if credible — the disability goes to manner, not merit.

PART 3Hostile Witness — s. 157 BSA / s. 154 IEA

3.1 The provision

Statute
s. 157, BSA 2023 [= s. 154, IEA 1872] — “Question by party to his own witness”: “(1) The Court may, in its discretion, permit the person who calls a witness to put any questions to him which might be put in cross-examination by the adverse party. (2) Nothing in this section shall disentitle the person so permitted under sub-section (1), to rely on any part of the evidence of such witness.”

Analysis (DU companion notes): (i) the provision permits only those questions that could be asked in cross-examination; (ii) the law nowhere requires a formal declaration that the witness is “hostile” before the section is invoked; (iii) the request arises when the party examining feels the witness’s present testimony is against his duty to speak the truth / contrary to what was collected in investigation. Only the judge may grant the permission, on the request of the examining party.

Working definition (DU notes): during a trial, when the prosecution calls a person to witness in its favour and such person, when called upon, does not conform to his previous statement collected during investigation, he is called a hostile witness; at common law, one who is not desirous of telling the truth at the instance of the party calling him, or who testifies for the opposing party / offers adverse testimony to the calling party during direct examination.

3.2 Why the Indian Act avoids the word “hostile” — Sat Pal v. Delhi Administration (30 Sept 1975, SC)

Doctrine
  1. Origin: the terms “hostile witness”, “adverse witness”, “unfavourable witness”, “unwilling witness” are all terms of English law.
  2. Relaxation: the rigidity of the English rule prohibiting a party from discrediting or contradicting its own witness was relaxed by evolving “hostile” and “unfavourable” and drawing distinctions between them.
  3. Hostile witness: one who is not desirous of telling the truth at the instance of the party calling him.
  4. Unfavourable witness: one called by a party to prove a particular fact in issue or relevant to the issue who fails to prove such fact, or proves an opposite fact.
  5. Deliberate Indian silence: to steer clear of the English controversy, the authors of the IEA advisedly avoided all these terms; in India the grant of permission to cross-examine one’s own witness is not conditional on the witness being declared “adverse” or “hostile”. Whether under s. 142 (leading questions) or s. 154 (cross-questions), the Act leaves the matter entirely to the discretion of the court.
  6. Use of the discretion: the discretion under s. 154 is unqualified and untrammelled and apart from any question of “hostility”; it is to be liberally exercised whenever the court — from the witness’s demeanour, temper, attitude, bearing, or the tenor and tendency of his answers, or from a perusal of his previous inconsistent statement, or otherwise — thinks the grant expedient to extract the truth and to do justice. The grant is not an adjudication on veracity; therefore courts should avoid expressions like “declared hostile” in the order, with their “historical cobwebs… misleading legacy of confusion”.

3.3 Why witnesses turn hostile (DU companion notes)

Context
  • Absence of witness protection;
  • Prolonged trials;
  • Easy bail of rich accused;
  • Absence of adequate facilities to witnesses in courts;
  • Use of money and power by the accused;
  • Threats by the accused;
  • Other factors: fear of police or of the legal system, political fear, etc.

Connected instrument: the Witness Protection Scheme, 2018 (formulated pursuant to the Supreme Court’s directions in Mahendra Chawla v. Union of India and given statutory force through amendments/state adoption) — threat assessment, protection of identity, change of identity/relocation, in-camera proceedings — is the systemic answer to the first cause above; cite it wherever an exam question asks “why do witnesses turn hostile and what is the remedy?”

3.4 Evidentiary value of a hostile witness’s testimony — s. 157(2) and Baldev Singh

Rule

Sub-section (2) of s. 157 BSA (s. 154(2) IEA) preserves the calling party’s right to rely on any part of the witness’s evidence. Consequences, per Baldev Singh & Ors. v. State of H.P. (H.P. High Court, 29 April 2017), quoted in DU notes:

“It is settled that statement of hostile witnesses is not to be brushed aside in toto and Court can consider evidence of hostile witness to corroborate other evidence on record. It is also clearly well settled that mere fact that a witness is declared hostile does not make him unreliable witness so as to exclude his evidence from consideration altogether but the said evidence remains admissible in the trial and there is no legal bar to base conviction or acquittal upon testimony of hostile witness if corroborated by other reliable evidence.”
  • The testimony is therefore sifted: the part that supports the case may be used; the part that does not is discarded — “the grain from the chaff”.
  • The court may also permit the party to impeach the credit of its own witness with the court’s consent under s. 158 BSA / s. 155 IEA (unworthiness of credit, bribery, former inconsistent statements).
  • Former inconsistent statements (e.g. the s. 183 BNSS / s. 161 CrPC police statement, or s. 180 BNSS statement) may be used to contradict him (BNSS s. 322 / CrPC s. 145 procedure).

3.5 The stage at which permission may be sought — the Laloo Prasad rule

Rule

Permission under s. 157(1)/154(1) may in law be sought at any stage of the examination (Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat, AIR 1964 SC 1563, as relied upon in Laloo Prasad), but the grant remains a discretion, and the discretion is ordinarily exercised against a request made after cross-examination has closed where the witness had already resiled in the examination-in-chief itself. The matrix distilled from State of Bihar v. Laloo Prasad (full brief in Part 5):

3.6 Connected law around the hostile witness

Connected Law
  • BNSS s. 348 (= CrPC s. 311): “Any Court may, at any stage of any inquiry, trial or other proceeding… summon any person as a witness, or examine any person in attendance though not summoned… or recall and re-examine any person already examined; and the Court shall summon and examine or recall and re-examine any such person if his evidence appears to it to be essential to the just decision of the case.” This is the court’s truth-finding power — distinct from the party’s s. 157 route; in Laloo Prasad the SC treated recall for cross-questions as an unnecessary exercise given the prosecutor’s other options.
  • Contradiction by former statements: BNSS s. 322 (CrPC s. 145) — witness may be contradicted by his police/statement-in-writing; s. 160 BSA / s. 157 IEA — former statements to corroborate; s. 158 BSA / s. 155 IEA — impeachment of credit.
  • False evidence (perjury) deterrence: a witness who answers falsely after being denied contradiction may be charged with giving false evidence (s. 156 BSA / s. 153 IEA exception). Offences: IPC ss. 191 (giving false evidence) → BNS s. 227; IPC s. 192 (fabricating) → BNS s. 228; IPC s. 193 (punishment) → BNS s. 229; IPC s. 194 (false evidence causing conviction of innocent) → BNS s. 230; procedural gateway: CrPC s. 340 (complaint by court) with the corresponding BNSS provision.
  • Oaths Act 1969 ss. 4–5, 7: the sworn duty to speak truth underpins every stage; breach is perjury, not mere irregularity.
  • Witness Protection Scheme 2018: systemic cure for hostility caused by threats (see 3.3).
Real Example

Theft trial, eyewitness won. PW 1 gave a s. 180 BNSS (s. 161 CrPC) statement identifying T as the snatch-thief (IPC s. 379 → BNS s. 303). In court he says he “cannot identify”. If he says this in examination-in-chief, the prosecutor must move under s. 157(1) then; the court, seeing the prior inconsistent statement, will liberally permit cross-questions (Sat Pal). If instead he identifies T in chief but, in cross, admits “I was not really there”, the prosecutor’s application at the close of cross is timely and legitimate (Laloo Prasad’s second limb). If the prosecutor sleeps through both stages and applies only after re-examination, refusal is unimpeachable — his remedies are to disown PW 1 in arguments and to rely on any part still helpful (s. 157(2)), e.g. the admission that he saw T flee with the bag.

PART 4Examination, Cross-Examination & Re-Examination — ss. 142–144 & 158 BSA / ss. 137–139 & 155 IEA

4.1 The three stages defined (s. 142 BSA / s. 137 IEA)

Statute
s. 142, BSA 2023 [= s. 137 IEA]: “(1) The examination of a witness by the party who calls him shall be called his examination-in-chief. (2) The examination of a witness by the adverse party shall be called his cross-examination. (3) The examination of a witness, subsequent to the cross-examination, by the party who called him, shall be called his re-examination.”

Objects (DU companion notes):

  • Examination-in-chief: to make the witness depose to what he has been called to prove; must be confined to relevant facts; no leading questions (a leading question is one which suggests the answer).
  • Cross-examination: to impeach the accuracy, credibility and general value of the evidence given in chief; to sift the facts already stated; to detect and expose discrepancies; and to elicit suppressed facts supporting the cross-examining party’s case.
  • Re-examination: to give the calling party an opportunity of filling in lacunae or explaining inconsistencies which cross-examination has discovered in the examination-in-chief; hence limited to explanation of matters referred to in cross-examination.

4.2 Order and scope (s. 143 BSA / s. 138 IEA)

Statute
s. 143, BSA 2023 [= s. 138 IEA]: “(1) Witnesses shall be first examined-in-chief, then (if the adverse party so desires) cross-examined, then (if the party calling him so desires) re-examined. (2) The examination-in-chief and cross-examination must relate to relevant facts, but the cross-examination need not be confined to the facts to which the witness testified in his examination-in-chief. (3) The re-examination shall be directed to the explanation of matters referred to in cross-examination; and, if new matter is, by permission of the Court, introduced in re-examination, the adverse party may further cross-examine upon that matter.”
  • No cross without chief: a witness cannot be cross-examined unless first examined-in-chief (DU notes: “it cannot be so without examination-in-chief”).
  • Cross is the right of the adverse party; a party cannot cross-examine his own witness except with the court’s permission under s. 157 BSA / s. 154 IEA.
  • Incomplete examination: if the opposite party is unlawfully denied the opportunity to cross-examine, or the calling party is not allowed re-examination, the examination is treated as incomplete — and evidence not tested by cross-examination loses its probative force (DU companion notes).
  • s. 144 BSA / s. 139 IEA: a person summoned merely to produce a document does not become a witness by producing it and cannot be cross-examined unless and until he is called as a witness.

4.3 Leading questions (s. 146 BSA / ss. 142–143 IEA)

Statute
s. 146, BSA 2023: “(1) Any question suggesting the answer which the person putting it wishes or expects to receive, is called a leading question. (2) Leading questions must not, if objected to by the adverse party, be asked in an examination-in-chief, or in a re-examination, except with the permission of the Court. (3) The Court shall permit leading questions as to matters which are introductory or undisputed, or which have, in its opinion, been already sufficiently proved. (4) Leading questions may be asked in cross-examination.”

IEA counterparts: s. 142 (when leading questions must not be asked) and s. 143 (when they may be asked). Example of a leading question: “Were you at the tea-shop at 6 p.m. on 2 March?” (suggests the answer) vs the non-leading “Where were you on the evening of 2 March?”

4.4 Impeaching the credit of a witness (s. 158 BSA / s. 155 IEA)

Statute
s. 158, BSA 2023 [= s. 155 IEA]: “The credit of a witness may be impeached in the following ways by the adverse party, or, with the consent of the Court, by the party who calls him: (a) by the evidence of persons who testify that they, from their knowledge of the witness, believe him to be unworthy of credit; (b) by proof that the witness has been bribed, or has accepted the offer of a bribe, or has received any other corrupt inducement to give his evidence; (c) by proof of former statements inconsistent with any part of his evidence which is liable to be contradicted. — Explanation: A witness declaring another witness to be unworthy of credit may not, upon his examination-in-chief, give reasons for his belief, but he may be asked his reasons in cross-examination, and the answers which he gives cannot be contradicted, though, if they are false, he may afterwards be charged with giving false evidence.”
  • Note the consent route: the calling party may impeach his own witness with the court’s consent — a companion gateway to s. 157.
  • Companion provisions: s. 160 BSA / s. 157 IEA (former statements to corroborate later testimony); s. 159 BSA / s. 156 IEA (questions tending to corroborate); s. 156 BSA / s. 153 IEA (exclusion of evidence to contradict answers testing veracity, with exceptions for previous conviction and impeachment of impartiality).

4.5 Forbidden questions — the courtroom guardrails

Real Example

Walk-through of one witness, three stages. Prosecution calls E, an eye-witness to an assault (IPC s. 323/324 → BNS ss. 115/116). Chief: prosecutor asks open questions — “What did you see at 9 p.m. near the gate?” — no leading (s. 146(2)); defence objects if the prosecutor slips in “You saw X strike the blow, didn’t you?”. Cross: defence may range beyond the chief — E’s enmity with X, his presence at a liquor shop, his earlier s. 180 BNSS statement — because cross “need not be confined” (s. 143(2)); leading questions freely allowed (s. 146(4)); but “Is it true you were sleeping with the complainant’s wife?” may be forbidden as insulting/needlessly offensive (s. 155). Re-examination: prosecutor may only explain cross-matters — e.g. why E was at the liquor shop (his shift ended there) — and if, with permission, E volunteers new matter (a fresh identification), the defence gets a further cross on it (s. 143(3)).

PART 5Case 28 in Full Brief & Companion Cards

Case 28 — State of Bihar v. Laloo Prasad

(2002) 9 SCC 626 • Supreme Court of India • K.T. Thomas & S.N. Variava, JJ. • appeal by special leave against the High Court’s refusal to interfere • IEA s. 154 (now BSA s. 157); CrPC trial procedure; CBI prosecution • DU Case Material LB-201 (2025), pp. 176–177
Facts
  • In a criminal case prosecuted by the CBI, the Public Prosecutor examined Baleshwar Choudhary (PW 39) as a prosecution witness.
  • Examination-in-chief: PW 39 referred to a document executed in 1993, styling it a sale deed; and then stated that he had received the consideration thereof in 1983 — i.e. ten years before the deed. “The said last part of the chief examination is obviously not in consonance with the prosecution case.”
  • The Public Prosecutor did not, at that stage, seek the trial court’s permission under s. 154 IEA to put to the witness questions which might be put in cross-examination by the adverse party. The examination therefore proceeded to cross-examination by the adverse party.
  • Cross-examination: in cross, the witness gave further details of how he received the consideration — elaboration in favour of the accused and against the State.
  • After cross-examination was over, the Public Prosecutor requested permission to treat the witness as hostile and to cross-examine him.
  • Trial Judge: declined, by order, to permit the Public Prosecutor to cross-examine the witness. High Court: on the CBI’s challenge, declined to interfere. Hence the appeal by special leave.
Issues
  1. Whether the party calling a witness may seek the court’s permission under s. 154 IEA (s. 157 BSA) at any stage of the examination — including after cross-examination has concluded.
  2. Whether the trial Judge’s refusal to exercise the s. 154 discretion in favour of the prosecution, on a request made after cross-examination, was an error warranting interference.
  3. Whether the witness should be recalled so that the prosecution may put cross-questions to him.
Argument for the Appellant (State / CBI)
  • Reliance on Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat, AIR 1964 SC 1563: it is open to the party who calls the witness to seek the permission of the court (as envisaged in s. 154 of the Evidence Act) at any stage of the examination.
  • The witness’s answers favoured the defence; the prosecution must therefore be armed with cross-questions to test him; refusal paralyses the prosecution and occasions miscarriage of justice.
Reasoning of the Supreme Court (respondent-side position upheld)
  • Discretion is the heart of s. 154: “Nonetheless, a discretion has been vested with the court whether to grant the permission or not. Normally when the Public Prosecutor requested for permission to put cross-questions to a witness called by him the court used to grant it.”
  • The missed window: “Here if the Public Prosecutor had sought permission at the end of the chief examination itself the trial court would have no good reason for declining the permission sought for. But the Public Prosecutor did not do so at that stage. That is precisely the reason why the trial Judge declined to exercise his discretion when the permission was sought for after the cross-examination was over.”
  • Nature of the cross-testimony: “The witness has said only the details in cross-examination regarding the matter which he said in the chief examination itself.” There was thus no new resiling in cross.
  • The contrasting situation (the rule stated): “It would have been a different position if the witness stuck to his version he was expected to say by the party who called the witness, in the examination-in-chief, but he showed propensity to favour the adverse party only in cross-examination. In such case the party who called him has a legitimate right to put cross-questions to the witness. But if he resiled from his expected stand even in the chief examination the permission to put cross-questions should have been sought then.”
  • No wholly wrong exercise of discretion: “In the above situation we are unable to hold that the trial Judge has gone wholly wrong in declining to exercise the discretion envisaged under Section 154… in favour of the appellant.”
  • Options still open to the prosecutor (and why recall is unnecessary): “If the Public Prosecutor is not prepared to own the testimony of the witness examined by him he can give expression of it in different forms. One of such forms is the one envisaged in Section 154… The very fact that he sought permission of the court soon after the end of the cross-examination was enough to indicate his resolve not to own all what the witness said in his evidence. It is again open to the Public Prosecutor to tell the court during final consideration that he is not inclined to own the evidence of any particular witness in spite of the fact the said witness was examined on his side. When such options are available to a Public Prosecutor, it is not a useful exercise for this Court to consider whether the witness shall again be called back for the purpose of putting cross-questions to him.”
Decision

Appeal dismissed; no remedy granted to the State. The trial Judge’s refusal (and the High Court’s non-interference) stood; the Supreme Court declined to direct recall of PW 39 for cross-questions.

Principle evolved
  1. Permission under s. 154 IEA / s. 157 BSA may in law be sought at any stage (Dahyabhai), but its grant is judicial discretion, and the discretion is properly refused where the request is belated — made after cross-examination — in respect of a witness who had already resiled in the examination-in-chief.
  2. Timing rule of practice: resiling in chief ⇒ apply at the end of chief; resiling first in cross ⇒ apply at the close of cross (legitimate right).
  3. A prosecutor who does not own a witness’s testimony has alternative expressions: the s. 154 route at the proper time, or disowning the witness in final arguments; hence recall merely for cross-questions is not a necessary exercise.
  4. Appellate restraint: unless the trial court’s discretionary order is wholly wrong, superior courts will not substitute their own discretion.

5.2 Companion case cards (DU notes, Ch. XV & Ch. XIII)

Sat Pal v. Delhi Administration

decided 30 September 1975, Supreme Court • DU notes Ch. XV — the leading exposition of s. 154 IEA
Points (as briefed in DU notes)
  • The labels “hostile”, “adverse”, “unfavourable”, “unwilling” are English-law imports; the IEA deliberately avoids them.
  • Hostile = not desirous of telling the truth at the instance of the calling party; unfavourable = fails to prove the fact he was called to prove, or proves the opposite.
  • Permission under s. 154 is not conditional on a declaration of hostility; the discretion is unqualified and untrammelled, to be liberally exercised from demeanour, temper, attitude, bearing, tenor/tendency of answers, or previous inconsistent statements, whenever expedient to extract the truth and do justice.
  • The order granting permission is not an adjudication of veracity; avoid the phrase “declared hostile”.

Baldev Singh & Ors. v. State of Himachal Pradesh

Himachal Pradesh High Court, 29 April 2017 • s. 154(2) IEA — evidentiary value of hostile witness • DU notes Ch. XV
Points
  • Hostile witness’s statement not brushed aside in toto; usable to corroborate other evidence.
  • Declaration of hostility does not make the witness unreliable per se; evidence remains admissible; no legal bar to conviction or acquittal on it if corroborated by other reliable evidence.

Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat

AIR 1964 SC 1563 • cited in Laloo Prasad • DU notes Ch. XV
Point relied upon

It is open to the party who calls a witness to seek s. 154 permission at any stage of the examination — the proposition the appellant pressed in Laloo Prasad; the 2002 judgment reads it as subject to the court’s discretion and to the timing discipline explained above.

Model Q 1 (Essay, 20 marks — pattern-based)

Q. “All persons shall be competent to testify” — discuss the competence of a child witness under s. 124 BSA (s. 118 IEA) and the Oaths Act 1969, and evaluate the evidentiary value of a child’s testimony with reference to decided cases.

Best Model Answer

1. Statutory foundation. Section 124 BSA (s. 118 IEA) enacts the presumption of universal competence: all persons are competent unless the Court considers them prevented — by tender years, extreme old age, disease of body or mind, or any other like cause — from understanding the questions put to them or from giving rational answers. Incompetence is thus functional, never status-based: no person, not even a lunatic (Explanation: unsound mind), is absolutely incompetent; a lunatic testifies in lucid intervals. “Witnesses are the eyes and ears of justice” (Bentham), but the eyes and ears must be fit and working.

2. The child-witness test. The Act fixes no age limit. In P Ramesh v. State (2019) the Supreme Court held that competency is decided by the judge forming her/his own opinion through preliminary questioning — no precise rule of intelligence can be laid down — and stated the two limbs: the child must (i) understand the questions put as a witness, and (ii) give answers that can be understood; a child is incompetent only if unable to answer in a coherent and comprehensible manner. “A child has to be a competent witness first, only then is her/his statement admissible.”

3. The Oaths Act interface. Section 4(1), Oaths Act 1969 requires oaths of all witnesses, but the proviso dispenses with oath for a child under twelve who understands the duty of speaking the truth though not the nature of an oath; and “the absence of an oath… shall not render inadmissible any evidence given by such witness nor affect the obligation of the witness to state the truth” (see also s. 7: irregularity in oath ≠ inadmissibility). Oath goes to weight, competence to admissibility.

4. Evidentiary value. In Dattu Ramrao Sakhare v. State of Maharashtra (1997) the Court held a child’s evidence can be the basis of conviction even without oath, provided the understanding/rational-answer test is met; credibility depends on the circumstances; the precautions are reliability, natural demeanour, absence of tutoring; corroboration is not a rule of law but a rule of prudence. In State of U.P. v. Krishna Master (2010) a six-year-old’s account of the gruesome murder of his parents was accepted despite a ten-year gap: a child of tender years is “always receptive to abnormal events” and never forgets them; to doubt his recapitulation would be “injustice to a child witness possessing sharp memory”; and a six-year-old is naturally at home at dead of night.

5. Special positions. A child who is a victim (prosecutrix) stands higher: she is not equated with an accomplice and her evidence carries the weight of an injured witness (State of Maharashtra v. Chandraprakash Kewalchand Jain, 1990). A dumb child is governed by ss. 124 and 125 together.

6. Conclusion. Competence is a capacity question answered by voir dire; admissibility survives absence of oath; conviction on sole child testimony is lawful but prudence seeks dependable corroboration; tutoring is the fatal vice to exclude.

Model Q 2 (Short note / problem, 10 marks)

Q. A deaf-and-dumb eye-witness cannot read or write. How may her evidence be recorded? State the safeguards. What is the effect of s. 125 BSA deeming such evidence “oral”?

Best Model Answer (points)
  • Competence first: s. 124 BSA/118 IEA — the disability of speech does not affect understanding or rational answering; the court must first satisfy itself of her intelligence and that she understands the nature of an oath (Darshan Singh, 2012).
  • Manner: s. 125 BSA/119 IEA — evidence “in any other manner in which he can make it intelligible, as by writing or by signs”; writing/signs must be made in open Court (CrPC s. 327). Since she cannot read or write, the sign-language route applies, with an interpreter — a person of the same surroundings, disinterested, himself on oath; oath may be administered to her by appropriate means through the interpreter.
  • Post-2013 proviso: assistance of an interpreter or special educator is mandatory and the statement shall be videographed.
  • Record of signs, not interpretation: the record must preserve her signs; the interpreter’s gloss is not the evidence (Darshan Singh). Signs/nods possess evidentiary value and a statement by signs can be a verbal statement (e.g. dying declaration by nods: Meesala Ramakrishnan, 1994 — “verbal” ≠ “oral”).
  • Deeming fiction: evidence so given is deemed oral evidence — it satisfies the oral-evidence provisions (ss. 56–57, 88–89 BSA; ss. 59–60, 91–92 IEA) and can found a conviction like any spoken testimony (Geeta PW 16 in Darshan Singh was the sole eye-witness).
  • Vow of silence analogy: a witness under a religious vow of silence is within the same section and may depose in writing without breaking the vow (Lakhan Singh v. Emperor, 1942).
Model Q 3 (Essay, 20 marks — pattern-based)

Q. What is a hostile witness? Discuss s. 157 BSA (s. 154 IEA), the scope of the court’s discretion, the evidentiary value of a hostile witness’s testimony, and the stage at which permission should be sought, with reference to Sat Pal, Baldev Singh and State of Bihar v. Laloo Prasad.

Best Model Answer

1. Concept and statute. A hostile witness, at common law, is one not desirous of telling the truth at the instance of the party calling him; an “unfavourable” witness fails to prove the fact he was called to prove or proves the opposite (Sat Pal v. Delhi Administration, 1975). Indian law avoids both labels: s. 157(1) BSA (s. 154(1) IEA) simply empowers the Court, in its discretion, to permit the party calling a witness to put to him any questions which might be put in cross-examination by the adverse party; and s. 157(2) preserves that party’s right to rely on any part of the witness’s evidence. No formal “declaration of hostility” is a precondition.

2. The discretion. Per Sat Pal it is unqualified and untrammelled, independent of any finding of hostility, and to be liberally exercised where the witness’s demeanour, temper, attitude, bearing, the tenor and tendency of his answers, or a previous inconsistent statement, show that cross-questions are expedient to extract the truth and do justice; the order should avoid the phrase “declared hostile” because granting permission is not an adjudication on veracity.

3. Value of the testimony. The testimony is not wiped out: s. 157(2) allows reliance on any part; Baldev Singh v. State of H.P. (2017) confirms it is not to be brushed aside in toto, may corroborate other evidence, and — if corroborated by reliable evidence — may even found a conviction or acquittal. The court sifts the supporting grain from the hostile chaff; impeachment of credit is available with consent under s. 158 BSA (s. 155 IEA).

4. Stage of the application — Laloo Prasad. In State of Bihar v. Laloo Prasad, (2002) 9 SCC 626, PW 39 had resiled in examination-in-chief (sale deed of 1993 with consideration received in 1983); the prosecutor let cross-examination proceed and applied under s. 154 only after it closed. The Supreme Court (K.T. Thomas & S.N. Variava JJ.) upheld the trial Judge’s refusal: permission sought at the end of chief would have met “no good reason for declining”; but where the witness resiled in chief the application should have been made then. Conversely, where the witness keeps his chief version and first favours the adverse party in cross, the calling party has a legitimate right to cross-questions at the close of cross. The prosecutor’s residual tools — disowning the witness in final arguments and relying on helpful parts — made recall for cross-questions an unnecessary exercise; appeal dismissed.

5. Why witnesses turn hostile & systemic cure. Absence of witness protection, protracted trials, threats, money and power of the accused, fear of police/courts (DU notes) — addressed today by the Witness Protection Scheme 2018; deterrence by perjury law (IPC ss. 191–193 → BNS ss. 227–229).

6. Conclusion. Section 157 is a truth-extraction device governed by disciplined timing and untrammelled but reviewable discretion; hostility qualifies weight and mode of testing, never admissibility.

Model Q 4 (Problem, 10 marks)

Q. In a murder trial, PW 2 in his examination-in-chief states that he saw nothing. The prosecutor, taken by surprise, says nothing. The defence cross-examines PW 2, who then admits he was present but “cannot say who struck”. After re-examination the prosecutor applies under s. 157(1) BSA to cross-examine PW 2. The trial judge refuses. Advise the prosecutor, citing Laloo Prasad. Would your answer change if PW 2 had identified the accused in chief but retracted only in cross?

Best Model Answer (points)
  • First limb (as facts stand): PW 2 resiled in chief (“saw nothing” against his s. 180 BNSS statement). Per Laloo Prasad, the application “should have been sought then” — at the end of chief. The post-re-examination application is belated; refusal is a valid exercise of discretion, not “wholly wrong”; no appellate interference.
  • Residual remedies: (i) disown PW 2 in final arguments; (ii) rely on any part still helpful, e.g. the admission of presence (s. 157(2)); (iii) contradict him by his former statement (BNSS s. 322 / CrPC s. 145 procedure) and impeach credit with consent (s. 158 BSA); (iv) the court’s own power under BNSS s. 348 (CrPC s. 311) to recall if essential to a just decision — but that is the court’s tool, not the party’s.
  • Variant: if PW 2 had identified the accused in chief and retracted only in cross, the prosecutor’s application at the close of cross-examination is timely and his right is legitimate (Laloo Prasad, second limb); refusal in that situation would be an improper fetter on the discretion and open to challenge.
  • Practice note: move under s. 157(1) the moment the witness resiles; the order need not label him “hostile” (Sat Pal).

7.2 Mnemonics

  • “U-R-A” for competence: Understands questions → Rational answers → All persons presumed competent.
  • “C-O-D-E” for child witness: Capacity not age; Oath dispensable (<12); Demeanour & no tutoring; Evidence may convict (prudence: corroborate).
  • “W-S-V” for dumb witness: Writing preferred; Signs in open court (record of signs); Videograph + interpreter (proviso).
  • “H-T-V” for hostile witness: Hostility not required as label; Timing of application decisive (Laloo Prasad); Value survives in parts (s. 157(2), Baldev Singh).
  • “C-X-R” cycle: Chief (own party, no leading) → X (cross: wide, leading ok, credit-testing) → Re (explanation only; new matter ⇒ further X).

7.3 Examiner’s traps

Traps
  1. “A child’s evidence is inadmissible without oath” — FALSE: Oaths Act proviso preserves admissibility; absence of oath affects weight only.
  2. “Corroboration of a child witness is mandatory” — FALSE: rule of prudence, not law (Dattu Ramrao).
  3. “A dumb witness’s signs are not oral evidence” — FALSE: s. 125/119 deems them oral evidence; and per Meesala, verbal ≠ oral.
  4. “Interpreter’s translation is the evidence” — FALSE: there must be a record of signs, not the interpretation (Darshan Singh).
  5. “A hostile witness’s evidence is wiped out” — FALSE: s. 157(2)/154(2); Baldev Singh — sift, don’t discard.
  6. “Permission under s. 157 can never be refused” / “can be sought whenever convenient” — BOTH WRONG: discretion is real; Laloo Prasad disciplines the timing.
  7. “Declaring a witness hostile is a precondition of s. 157” — FALSE: no declaration required; Sat Pal discourages the label.
  8. “Re-examination can add new proof of the case” — FALSE: explanation of cross-matters only; new matter needs permission and triggers further cross (s. 143(3)).
  9. “A document-producer can be cross-examined” — FALSE unless called as a witness (s. 144 BSA / 139 IEA).
  10. “Leading questions are illegal in cross-examination” — FALSE: they are the native currency of cross (s. 146(4)).
