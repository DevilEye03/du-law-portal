1.1Definition of International Law

Every DU answer on Topic 1 should open by conceding a methodological point: there is no single definition of international law, because the discipline has changed faster than its definitions. As the DU course material puts it, "the impact of the 'context' and the social milieu on the definition and development of international law" is what matters.1 The definitional history runs from a State-only, European, war-centred law, to a universal, welfare-oriented, organisation-and-individual-inclusive law.

A. Oppenheim's Classical Definition (1905)

"Law of Nations or International Law is the name for the body of customary and conventional rules which are considered legally binding by civilised states in their intercourse with each other." L. Oppenheim, International Law: A Treatise, Vol. I (1st ed., 1905), p. 1 — the definition printed in the DU classroom material.

Oppenheim and his successors built the definition on four load-bearing elements, each of which later came under attack:

"states in their intercourse with each other"States are the only subjects; individuals are objects.International organisations, individuals, peoples, insurgents, ICRC, Holy See, multinationals hold some personality — the determinative test is functional (see §1.5).

B. The Five-fold Criticism of Oppenheim (the standard DU answer)

The DU-syllabus material identifies five defects — "every important element in it can now be challenged."

  1. International organisations are omitted. "It is now generally recognised that, not only 'states' but public international organisations, have rights and duties under International Law, even though they may not have all the rights and duties that states have." In fact "the future of International law is one with the future of international organisation." Authority: Reparation for Injuries (1949) — the UN is an international person; Effect of Awards of the UN Administrative Tribunal (1954).
  2. "Civilised states" is a discriminatory phrase. The criterion of "civilisation" was neither long history nor culture — it was Christian-European identity. Hence the 8th edition re-wrote the definition: "Law of Nations or International Law is the name for the body of customary and treaty rules which are considered legally binding by States in their intercourse with each other."
  3. Individuals and private persons are omitted. "More controversial but no longer untenable is the view that even individuals and other private persons may have some such rights and duties." Authority: UDHR 1948; ICCPR and its First Optional Protocol (individual petitions to the Human Rights Committee); European Convention on Human Rights 1950, Art. 34 (individual applications to the European Court). Wilfred Jenks' famous formulation: contemporary international law "must be regarded as the common law of mankind in an early stage of development."
  4. Sources are understated. International law consists "not only of customary and conventional rules but also of 'General Principles of Law'" — Art. 38(1)(c) ICJ Statute.
  5. "Body of rules" is static. The conception of international law as a fixed body of rules "now stands changed as static and inadequate." Law must be "flexible, adaptable and changeable."
Examiner's favourite quote

Sir Henry Maine: "The greatest function of the 'law of nature' was discharged in giving birth to modern International Law."
Jenks: "Contemporary International Law … must be regarded as the common law of mankind in an early stage of development."
Fenwick (quoted in DU material): international law is "the body of general principles and specific rules which are binding upon the members of the international community in their mutual relations."

E. Is International Law "True Law"? — the Austinian Challenge

1. The denial: Austin, Hobbes, Pufendorf, Holland, Bentham

John Austin's command theory holds that law properly so-called has two necessary features: (i) it is a command of a determinate sovereign, and (ii) it is enforced by a sanction. Because international law lacks both a world legislature and a coercive enforcement agency, Austin classed it as "positive international morality", analogous to "rules binding a club or society." Hobbes (man is "nasty, brutish and violent") and Pufendorf agreed; Holland called it "the vanishing point of jurisprudence"; Bentham too denied it the name of law.

Their six supporting arguments (as listed in the DU material):

  • No determinate superior political authority exists in international law;
  • No effective legislative machinery comparable to a legislature;
  • Sanction is an essential element of law, and international law lacks it;
  • No executive power enforces ICJ decisions or treaty obligations;
  • No potent judiciary with compulsory jurisdiction;
  • Hence some writers call it "quasi-law."

2. The defence: Starke's four arguments and the modern consensus

Professor Starke's four main arguments (printed in the DU material):

  1. Formal legislative authority is not essential to law. Many primitive communities had law without a legislature — Sir Henry Maine's historical school established this by research.
  2. International legislation exists. Law-making treaties and conventions — the Hague and Geneva Conventions, the UN Charter, the Vienna Conventions — are binding although they do not emanate from a sovereign.
  3. Authoritative agencies treat it as law. States themselves plead treaties, precedent and jurists, not morality; "there is no such thing as an International Lawlessness."
  4. The United Nations and the ICJ are based on the true legality of international law: Art. 38 ICJ Statute directs the Court to decide "in accordance with international law"; Art. 59 makes decisions binding; Art. 94 makes them enforceable.

Additional modern arguments: customary rules are treated as law (English common law itself is customary); Paquete Habana (1900) — "International law is a part of our law, and must be ascertained and administered by the courts of justice of appropriate jurisdiction"; Hart — "international law is law because States regard it as law. Nothing need be further proved"; Fitzmaurice — enforceability exists "even if only in a rough and rudimentary form"; Kelsen — in a decentralised society enforcement occurs through self-help, and international law makes reprisals and the use of force lawful as countermeasures while unlawful otherwise; hence it is true law.

The frequency-of-violation fallacy. Violations prove weakness of machinery, not absence of law — municipal law is violated daily too. Oppenheim conceded that "while the frequency of the violations of international law may strain its legal force to breaking point, the formal (though often cynical) affirmation of its binding nature is not without significance." Brierly: international law is "more honoured in the observance than in the breaches."

"The difference of origin and of the stages through which law and treaty pass is merged in the result… If the principle is binding at all — that is, if nations regard that a principle binds them — it is of no great moment whether the force is moral, ethical or physical. It seems … unscientific, if not positively absurd, to make the nature of a thing depend upon a mere form of punishment." James Brown Scott, "The Legal Nature of International Law," 1905 — one of the classic essays supplied in your project folder.
A pupil's caution — Zimmern's charge

Sir Alfred Zimmern wrote that for anyone trained in the British tradition the term 'International Law' "is never law as we understand it, and it often … comes dangerously near to being an imposture, a simulacrum of law, an attorney's mantle artfully displayed on the shoulders of arbitrary power." Goodhart (1936) and MacKenzie (1939) both devoted their Grotius/AJIL papers to answering this charge; MacKenzie's reply was that international law is the "law of the international community" and its function is conflict-resolution and value-allocation within that community. Cite this to show reading beyond the textbook.

F. The Basis of International Law — Jurisprudential Theories

"Basis" asks a different question from "definition": not what the rules are, but why are they binding — from where does international law derive its obligatory force? Two principal schools, each with sub-branches.

I. Naturalist School Law of Nature

Core: international law is part of the law of nature; States obey it because it is natural law applied to States.

Origins: Greek Stoics → Roman jus naturale and jus gentium (the law applied to foreigners in Rome, as against jus civile) → medieval divine law → 16th–17th century secularisation by Vittoria, Suarez, Gentilis → Grotius, who separated jus naturale from jus gentium and based the law of nations on nature, custom and treaties; natural law became "the dictate of right reason".

Sub-schools after Grotius: Naturalists (Pufendorf — natural law exclusively); Positivists (Bynkershoek — State consent/practice); Grotians or Eclectics (Wolff, Vattel — both).

Vattel: "We use the term 'necessary Law of Nations' for that law which results from applying the natural law to nations. It is necessary, because nations are absolutely bound to observe it."

II. Positivist School & Consent Theory Will of the State

Core: law is law as it is, not as it ought to be; the binding force comes from the consent / will of States, evidenced in custom and treaties.

Exponents: Bynkershoek → Zorn (international law as a branch of State law) → Triepel (obligatory force stems from agreement between States — Vereinbarung) → Anzilotti (pacta sunt servanda as the fundamental norm) → Jellinek and the auto-limitation / self-limitation theory; the "will of the State" idea traces to Hegel.

Auto-limitation: sovereign will is supreme; international law binds only to the extent the State has voluntarily restricted its own sovereignty. Oppenheim's own view: common consent of States is the basis.

Weakness: easy for treaties, hard for custom (a new State is bound by pre-existing custom without having consented — see §1.6); and it cannot explain jus cogens or obligations erga omnes. Also, the Lotus presumption (restrictions on State independence are not presumed) is the purest judicial statement of positivism.

The Doctrine of Fundamental Rights (a naturalist offshoot)

Deduced from the "essential nature" of the State, every State — like man — possesses certain fundamental or natural rights: self-preservation, independence, equality, respect, and intercourse. Brierly called it "merely the old doctrine of natural rights of man transferred to the State." It influenced the American Declaration of Independence and the classic rules on non-intervention.

Criticism (for the answer): (i) it confuses the State with the individual; (ii) a legal right presupposes a legal system — rights cannot pre-exist the system that validates them; (iii) it denies the possibility of growth by positing a fixed "order of nature"; (iv) it is subjective and detached from State practice, hence its decline. But note its revival: human rights and international criminal law are the modern heirs of natural-law thinking.

G. Sanctions and the Legal Character — Kelsen, Starke and the "law habit"

Kelsen's test: the distinguishing feature of law is that it is a coercive order — rules of law must be connected with a sanction, and force must be either a sanction or a delinquency. In the international field sanctions exist as war and reprisal (historically) and, today, as enforcement action, countermeasures and collective measures. The difference between municipal and international law is only one of degree of centralisation: "within the State, force is monopolised by the community; in the society of States a legitimate use of force may be made by the subjects of law — the individual States." Hence, international law is true law but a weak law.

Starke's three principal sanctions (as reproduced in the DU class material):

  1. Chapter VII of the UN Charter — if there is a threat to the peace or an act of aggression, the Security Council may take necessary action.
  2. Article 94 of the Charter — decisions of the ICJ are binding, and a party may have recourse to the Security Council for enforcement.
  3. Article 2(4) read with Article 51 — members have undertaken not to use force; the only permitted unilateral use is self-defence, and even that is subject to Security Council supervision.

Plus the informal sanctions: adverse public opinion (Rousseau's "the strongest fear"), economic sanctions, severance of diplomatic relations, suspension or expulsion from international organisations (Art. 5 and 6 UN Charter), and punishment of war crimes. Verma's list adds: fear of being branded a human-rights violator by NGOs such as Amnesty International, Asia Watch and Greenpeace.

Critical note (use this to lift the answer above average)

Sanctions are not co-extensive with international law. The Charter sanctions protect "international peace and security" — only a part of international law. There is no general machinery for breach of, say, a trade or environmental treaty; the only general remedy remains countermeasures (self-help), governed by the ILC Articles on State Responsibility 2001, Arts. 49–54. Also note the selectivity problem: Certain Expenses of the United Nations (ICJ Rep. 1962, p. 151) is cited in Verma to show that "political pressure may sometimes prevent the Security Council from applying such sanctions."

1.2Development of International Law

"In the long march of mankind from the cave to the computer a central role has always been played by the idea of law, the idea that order is necessary and chaos inimical to a just and stable existence. If it is applicable to a legal system, it is so equally with international law." Opening line of DU PIL Question Paper, 2000 — the examiner's own framing of this sub-topic.

The syllabus prescribes development under three heads: (a) the need for international peace and security; (b) the establishment of the United Nations; and (c) the development of different generations of human rights. Each is a separate exam question; the historical narrative below supplies the common backdrop.

A. Historical Development — a Five-Stage Timeline

Fig. 1.2 — Five stages in the development of international law (use this spine for any "development" essay)

Stage I — Antiquity: the classical roots

International law did not begin in 1648 or 1625. Every civilisation that dealt with other political communities produced something like it:

  • India: the dharmaśāstra tradition — the Ramayana, Mahabharata, Manusmriti and Kautilya's Arthashastra contain rules on the conduct of war (prohibition of poisoned weapons, protection of the wounded and of non-combatants, inviolability of envoys), treaties (sandhi) and neutrality. Ashoka's inscriptions record diplomatic missions to Hellenic kings. These are moral-religious rules, but the concept of a law binding even the king is genuinely Indian.
  • Greece: the amphictyonic leagues, the inviolability of heralds, the tradition of arbitration between city-States — and the Stoic doctrine of a universal natural law binding all men.
  • Rome: the jus fetiale (priestly rules on declaring war and concluding treaties) and, crucially, the jus gentium — the law applied to foreigners, later identified with jus naturale, "the principles which ought to control human conduct … founded in the very nature of man as a rational and social being."

Stage II — Grotius and Westphalia

Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), in De Jure Belli ac Pacis (1625), produced the first systematic framework of the modern science. He was influenced by Gentilis and by Vittoria and Suarez. He secularised natural law — "the dictate of right reason instead of divine prescriptions" — and based the law of nations on nature, custom and treaties. His enduring doctrines: just and unjust war; qualified neutrality; freedom of the seas (in Mare Liberum, against Selden's Mare Clausum); restitution of harm done by a State; and the value of periodic conferences.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) is the political counterpart: it ended the Thirty Years' War, recognised the territorial sovereignty and independence of States, and gave birth to the Westphalian model — a society of formally equal, sovereign States acknowledging no superior. Almost every modern "Third World" critique (see §1.6) attacks the legacy of this model's exclusions.

Before the 19th century, development was "effected mainly through usages, practices and writings of jurists." In the 19th century States began to legislate collectively:

Stage IV — The League experiment and its failure

The League of Nations (1920) was "the first international organisation designed to maintain international peace and promote cooperation among nations." Membership was open to "any" State — the first universalising move. It was joined by the Permanent Court of International Justice (1921) and by the ILO. The Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928) renounced war as an instrument of national policy but provided no machinery. The Hague Codification Conference (1930) failed on all three of its subjects. The system collapsed over Manchuria (1931) and Abyssinia (1935). The lesson drawn by the framers of the UN Charter: a prohibition of force without centralised enforcement is a paper prohibition.

The Preamble of the UN Charter opens with the determination "to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war, which twice in our lifetime has brought untold sorrow to mankind." C. Establishment of the United Nations

1. Genesis

The name "United Nations" was coined in the Declaration by United Nations of 1 January 1942 (Washington), signed by 26 States. The institutional blueprint was agreed at Dumbarton Oaks (1944) and at Yalta (1945, on the veto), and the Charter was signed at San Francisco on 26 June 1945, entering into force on 24 October 1945 (now United Nations Day). The ICJ was established in June 1945 and began work in April 1946; the first General Assembly met in London in January 1946.

2. Purposes and principles

Article 1 — Purposes
  1. To maintain international peace and security — collective measures for prevention and removal of threats, and adjustment or settlement of disputes by peaceful means and in conformity with justice and international law;
  2. To develop friendly relations among nations based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples;
  3. To achieve international co-operation in solving international problems of an economic, social, cultural or humanitarian character, and in promoting and encouraging respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms for all without distinction as to race, sex, language or religion;
  4. To be a centre for harmonizing the actions of nations in the attainment of these common ends.
Article 2 — Principles
  1. Sovereign equality of all Members;
  2. Fulfilment in good faith of Charter obligations;
  3. Peaceful settlement of disputes;
  4. Refrain from threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any State;
  5. Give the UN every assistance in any action it takes; refrain from giving assistance to any State against which the UN is taking preventive or enforcement action;
  6. Non-intervention in matters essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any State (subject to Chapter VII enforcement measures);
  7. Charter obligations prevail over conflicting obligations under any other international agreement (Art. 103).

1.3Codification of International Law: The Work of the International Law Commission

"The codification of international law aims at putting together the rules of law on a given subject in a systematic manner making its provisions clearer by removing all lacunas, and also modifying the rules in accordance with the changed conditions." S.K. Verma, An Introduction to Public International Law (2nd ed.), Ch. 1, §V — the DU-prescribed text.

A. Meaning and the Codification–Progressive Development Distinction

Because international law is largely unwritten and generated by the practice of nearly two hundred States, its defects are notorious: uncertainty, lacunae, conflicting State practice, and the absence of an authoritative text. Codification is the cure. The word itself derives from Bentham's "codification" — the reduction of law into a code.

The two operating concepts are defined by Article 15 of the Statute of the International Law Commission:

Table 1.7 — Codification vs. Progressive Development (Art. 15, ILC Statute)

Nature of the exerciseDeclaratory — recording lex lata (the law as it is).Legislative — creating lex ferenda (the law as it should be).
Typical subjectsDiplomatic relations; consular relations; law of treaties; law of the sea.State responsibility; jurisdictional immunities; nationality of natural persons; climate-related obligations.
The overlap — judge Sorensen's warning (quote it)

"Although theoretically clear and distinguishable, the two notions tend in practice to overlap or to leave between them an intermediate area in which it is not possible to indicate precisely where codification ends and progressive development begins."
Judge Max Sorensen, dissenting opinion, North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (1969) ICJ Rep. 3, at pp. 242–43.

The Commission itself follows the same procedure regardless of which label applies — a single set of draft articles, sent to Governments, revised, and then either adopted by a conference or "taken note of" by the General Assembly.

C. The International Law Commission — Constitution and Method

1. Legal basis

  • Charter, Article 13(1)(a): "The General Assembly shall initiate studies and make recommendations for the purpose of … encouraging the progressive development of international law and its codification."
  • GA Resolution 174(II) of 21 November 1947 established the ILC by a Statute "for the promotion of the progressive development of international law and its codification." The Commission held its first session in 1949 and in that year drew up a provisional list of 14 subjects for codification.

2. Composition

  • 34 members (originally 15, then 21, then 25; raised to 34 in 1981), elected by the General Assembly for five years, "having competence in international law", "representing the main forms of civilisation and the principal legal systems of the world" (Arts. 2, 3 and 8 of the Statute).
  • Geographic distribution (per Verma's footnote to Arts. 2, 3 and 8): Africa 8; Latin America and the Caribbean 6; Asia 7; Eastern Europe 3; Western Europe and Other States 8; plus two rotating seats.
  • Members sit in their individual capacity — not as representatives of Governments. Verma's sharp observation: "increasingly the professional diplomats from the respective States now are donning the Commission, which belies the apolitical character of the Commission."
  • An Indian, Sir Benegal Narsing Rau, was a member of the Commission and of the 1958 Law of the Sea Conference; India has been consistently represented (e.g., Special Rapporteurs on State responsibility such as Roberto Ago (Italy) and James Crawford (Australia)).

Critical appraisal — the marks-winning paragraph
  1. Success: the ILC has given the international community its most important law-making conventions; it has made customary law visible and citable; and its drafts shape State conduct even when un-adopted (the ARSIWA is treated by the ICJ as reflecting custom).
  2. Delay: the State-responsibility project took from 1949 to 2001 — over fifty years; some topics have been on the agenda for decades.
  3. Overload: "the Commission is already saddled with too many subjects and is confining itself mainly to traditional subjects where there exists considerable amount of non-controversial State practice" — hence the trend towards special codification bodies (UNCLOS III, the Rome Conference, the ad hoc committees on terrorism and on jurisdictional immunities).
  4. Third World view (see §1.6): the new States regard the ILC as too slow, too Western in composition and too conservative in its choice of topics; they prefer the General Assembly and special conferences where their "number power" counts. On the other side "the western countries are wary of the 'terror of majority' unleashed by new nations".
  5. Structural limitation: the ILC can only draft. Whether a rule becomes law depends on ratification — e.g., the 1986 Vienna Convention and the 2004 Jurisdictional Immunities Convention are still not in force.

1.4Sanctions of International Law

The syllabus lists eight categories: Security Council, General Assembly, ICJ, Human Rights Council, ITLOS, ICC, PCA, and sanctions under the different human rights treaties. Write the answer as an institutional survey — one heading per institution, each answering: (i) what is the legal basis? (ii) what sanction can it impose? (iii) is the sanction binding? (iv) illustration.

A. The Concept of Sanction and Its Classification

A "sanction" in international law is the consequence attached to a breach of an international obligation, designed to induce compliance. Two preliminary points must be made:

  • Sanctions are not co-extensive with international law. Chapter VII sanctions protect "international peace and security", which is only a part of international law. For the rest, the system relies on countermeasures, reciprocity, reputation and reciprocal treaty withdrawal.
  • Sanctions are decentralised. There is no world police. Kelsen's insight: in a decentralised legal order, the subjects of law themselves execute the law through self-help, and international law's contribution is to make such self-help lawful in defined circumstances and unlawful otherwise.

Real examples — the Security Council actually sanctioning
  • Southern Rhodesia (1966): SC Res. 232 imposed the first-ever mandatory economic sanctions under Art. 41, after the Unilateral Declaration of Independence; upheld in the Namibia advisory opinion context.
  • South Africa: an arms embargo (Res. 418, 1977) plus a voluntary oil embargo — the model of a sanctions campaign against apartheid; complemented by the 1976 UN sports boycott and the 1977 Gleneagles Agreement.
  • Iraq (1990–2003): Res. 661 (comprehensive economic sanctions), 678 ("all necessary means"), 687 (cease-fire terms, disarmament, the basis of the Oil-for-Food Programme) — the most-studied case, also criticised for its humanitarian consequences.
  • Libya (2011): Res. 1970 (referral to the ICC, arms embargo, asset freeze, travel ban) and Res. 1973 (authorising "all necessary measures" to protect civilians) — the controversial authorisation that produced the NATO air campaign.
  • Counter-terrorism: Res. 1267 (1999) Al-Qaida/Taliban Committee; Res. 1373 (2001) — a general, quasi-legislative obligation on all States; Res. 1540 (2004) on weapons of mass destruction.
  • Non-proliferation: Res. 1718 (2006) and successors on the DPRK; Res. 1929 (2010) on Iran. Iran–US: the ICJ in Alleged Violations of the 1955 Treaty (2023) held that US "snapback" of sanctions breached the Treaty of Amity.
  • Veto inaction: Syria (multiple vetoes), Crimea 2014, and the 2022 invasion of Ukraine — where the SC was deadlocked and the GA took over via the "Uniting for Peace" route. This is the standard criticism of the SC as a sanctions organ.

Two doctrines to cite:

  • Art. 103 — hierarchy: Charter obligations prevail over any other international agreement; SC sanctions thus override conflicting treaty rights (the basis on which the ECtHR in Al-Jedda and Nada v. Switzerland reconciled the 1267 regime with the ECHR).
  • Art. 2(7) — the limit: nothing in the Charter authorises intervention "in matters which are essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any State", but "this principle shall not prejudice the application of enforcement measures under Chapter VII". Human-rights situations are therefore sanctionable if the SC makes an Art. 39 determination.

D. The International Court of Justice

The ICJ is the only international court of general jurisdiction, but its sanctions are indirect. Structure the answer around four points:

  1. Contentious jurisdiction. Under Art. 36(1) — treaty-based compromissory clauses: under Art. 36(2) — the "Optional Clause" declarations, of which India's (still in force, with reservations excluding Commonwealth States and multilateral-treaty disputes where the other party accepted jurisdiction only within 12 months before the dispute) is a famous example. Over 70 States have made declarations.
  2. Binding force and enforcement. Art. 59 (decisions bind the parties and only in respect of that case); Art. 60 (final, without appeal); Art. 61 (revision). Art. 94(1) UN Charter: "Each Member of the United Nations undertakes to comply with the decision of the International Court of Justice in any case to which it is a party." Art. 94(2): if a party fails to perform its obligations, the other party may "have recourse to the Security Council, which may, if it deems necessary, make recommendations or decide upon measures to be taken to give effect to the judgment." This is the DU material's second sanction.
  3. Provisional measures (Art. 41). Long thought non-binding; now settled as binding in LaGrand (Germany v. United States), ICJ Rep. 2001, p. 466: "the object and purpose of the Statute … implies that [provisional measures] should be binding"; confirmed in Armed Activities (DRC v. Uganda) 2000 and applied in the 2017 Ukraine v. Russia Order. Notice also the paradox — a party that violates provisional measures is in breach twice over.
  4. Advisory jurisdiction (Art. 65). Not binding as such, but "the Court's advisory opinions carry great weight" (Tehran Hostages, 1980, para. 53); they are often the vehicle for the most important legal pronouncements — Reparation (1949), Reservations to the Genocide Convention (1951), Namibia (1971), Western Sahara (1975), Nuclear Weapons (1996), Wall in the Occupied Palestinian Territory (2004), Kosovo (2010), Chagos (2019), Climate Change (2024).
The weaknesses of the ICJ as a sanction — say all four

(i) Jurisdiction is consensual — no State can be brought to the Court without its consent; (ii) the Court has no enforcement machinery of its own — Art. 94(2) returns the matter to the Security Council, where the veto applies; (iii) compliance has been uneven — the US withdrew its acceptance of compulsory jurisdiction after Nicaragua, and withdrew from the Optional Protocol to the VCCR after Avena; (iv) apart from Nicaragua, "there has been hardly any case when the aggrieved State has approached the Security Council under Art. 94" (Verma). But note the counter-example of successful compliance: Corfu Channel (UK paid £843,947 to Albania in 1992), Temple of Preah Vihear (Thailand returned the temple in 1963), Jadhav (Pakistan allowed consular access and review), Chagos (partially implemented).

F. The International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS)

  • Basis: UNCLOS 1982, Annex VI (Statute of ITLOS). Seat: Hamburg. 21 judges elected for nine years by the States Parties. Functioning since 1996.
  • Jurisdiction: disputes submitted under Part XV; compulsory jurisdiction over (i) Part XI (deep seabed "Area") disputes — where the Seabed Disputes Chamber is compulsory for States Parties, the Authority and contractors; (ii) prompt release of vessels and crews (Art. 292 — a unique "summary" jurisdiction); (iii) provisional measures (Art. 290); (iv) any dispute where States have chosen ITLOS under Art. 287.
  • Enforcement: UNCLOS Art. 296 — decisions are final and binding; Art. 279–280 (peaceful means); Art. 298 (opt-out for maritime delimitation, military activities, SC law-enforcement action).
  • Landmark cases: M/V Saiga (No. 2) (1999 — prompt release; reparation); Bangladesh/Myanmar (2012 — first ITLOS maritime boundary case, applied the "equidistance/relevant circumstances" method); Arctic Sunrise (2013 — provisional measures against Russia, which did not appear yet partly complied); Enrica Lexie (Italy v. India) ended in PCA arbitration (2020); the 2024 Advisory Opinion on Climate Change and the Oceans — a major ITLOS statement that States must take measures to prevent marine pollution and protect the marine environment from climate impacts.
  • India connection: Dr. Neeru Chadha has served as an ITLOS judge since 2017 (first Indian woman elected to an international court); India took the Enrica Lexie dispute to arbitration under UNCLOS and in Republic of Italy v. Union of India, (2013) 4 SCC 721 the Indian Supreme Court held that the dispute was arbitrable and India's courts should await the arbitral award (the "Italian Marines" case, dropping the criminal proceedings against the marines under the 2014 Agreement).

G. The International Criminal Court (ICC)

  • Basis: the Rome Statute, adopted 17 July 1998 (120 votes in favour, 7 against, 21 abstentions); in force 1 July 2002; 125 States Parties (India, China, the USA, Russia, Israel and Pakistan are not parties). Seat: The Hague.
  • Jurisdiction (Art. 5): genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression (activated 2018).
  • Trigger mechanisms (Arts. 12–14): (i) State referral; (ii) SC referral under Chapter VII — used for Darfur (Res. 1593, 2005) and Libya (Res. 1970, 2011); (iii) proprio motu investigation by the Prosecutor with Pre-Trial Chamber authorisation (e.g., Kenya, Georgia, Bangladesh/Myanmar, Ukraine).
  • Complementarity (Arts. 1, 17): the ICC acts only where national courts are unwilling or unable — a jurisdictional concession to sovereignty.
  • Individual responsibility (Arts. 25, 27, 28): jurisdiction over natural persons; official capacity is irrelevant (Art. 27) — no immunity even for a serving Head of State; superior responsibility (Art. 28).
  • Sanction: arrest warrants, prosecution, imprisonment up to 30 years, or life imprisonment; reparations to victims (Art. 75) and the Trust Fund for Victims.
  • Landmark: Lubanga (2012 — first conviction); Ntaganda (2019); Ongwen (2021); the arrest warrant against President Omar al-Bashir (2009–10) — which triggered the immunity debate revisited in Jordan Referral re Al-Bashir (Appeals Chamber, 2019); the 2023 warrants against Russian officials (including the sitting Head of State) following the SC's inability to act on Ukraine; the 2024 warrants in the Gaza situation.
  • India's position (a favourite question): India voted against the Rome Statute in 1998 and has not signed. Reasons: (i) the crime of aggression and the definition of war crimes were too broad; (ii) Art. 12 gives the Court jurisdiction without State consent (non-consensual jurisdiction); (iii) the pre-trial role of the Security Council and the veto; (iv) the possibility of politicised prosecutions; (v) India insisted on the inclusion of nuclear weapons and terrorism as crimes. India does, however, participate in the ICC's Assembly as an observer and supported the SC referral of Darfur.

H. The Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA)

  • Basis: Convention for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes, adopted at the First Hague Peace Conference (1899), revised in 1907. The PCA began functioning in 1900 — the oldest global dispute-settlement institution.
  • Nature: despite the name, the PCA is not a court; it is an intergovernmental organisation providing registry and administrative support to arbitral tribunals, conciliation commissions and fact-finding commissions chosen by the parties. It maintains the "panel" of potential arbitrators (each State nominates up to four) and a list of standing "members of the Court" (Max Huber's famous 1928 Island of Palmas award was rendered by a PCA tribunal).
  • Consent as the basis: jurisdiction requires an arbitration agreement — either a compromis, a compromissory clause (e.g., UNCLOS Art. 287), or a general arbitration treaty (the 1899/1907 Conventions, or bilateral investment treaties).
  • Sanction-value: awards are binding on the parties, final, and not subject to appeal (a limited annulment is available only under ICSID/UNCITRAL rules); enforcement relies on the parties' good faith, and pecuniary awards are often paid to avoid commercial and reputational consequences.
  • Landmark modern cases: Bay of Bengal Maritime Boundary Arbitration (Bangladesh/India), PCA 2014 — India participated and complied; South China Sea Arbitration (Philippines v. China), 2016 — China refused to participate and rejected the award; Enrica Lexie (Italy v. India), 2020; Dispute concerning Coastal State Rights in the Black Sea (Ukraine v. K. A Composite Assessment — "Do sanctions determine the effectiveness of international law?"

This is literally a recorded DU question (2015). Build the answer on four moves:

  1. Yes, in part: sanctions give the law teeth. Without Chapter VII, Art. 2(4) would be exhortation; without the ICC, individual criminal responsibility would be a policy ideal; without ITLOS, UNCLOS disputes would go unresolved. The existence of a sanction is also Kelsen's defining feature of a legal order.
  2. But sanction is neither necessary nor sufficient. Customary and common law systems functioned without a sovereign enforcer; conversely, the SC's enforcement record is selective — Korea (1950) but not Tibet (1950–51), Kuwait (1990) but not Chechnya (1994–99).
  3. Veto and politics distort enforcement. The permanent members' veto means the strongest sanction is unavailable against the strongest States — the critique famously implied in Certain Expenses (1962) and demonstrated in Syria after 2011 and Ukraine in 2022.
  4. Compliance does not depend only on coercion. States obey international law because of the "law habit" (Fitzmaurice), reciprocity, reputation, domestic implementation and self-interest. Brierly: international law "is more honoured in the observance than in the breaches." Sanctions are the visible edge of a much larger structure of compliance.

1.5Subjects of International Law — States, International Organisations and Individuals

The syllabus puts this sub-topic in three layers: (a) the position of States; (b) international organisations — their establishment and personality; (c) individuals — the development of human rights and the position of individuals. The two prescribed cases both live here: Reparation (organisations) and Ukraine v. Russia (individuals).

A. Legal Personality — the Governing Test

The definition to memorise

"The subjects of law in any legal system are not necessarily identical in their nature or in the extent of their rights, and their nature depends upon the needs of the community." — Reparation for Injuries, ICJ Rep. 1949, p. 174, at 178.

"An entity is a subject of international law … capable of possessing international rights and duties, and … has the capacity to maintain its rights by bringing international claims." — ibid., at 179.

The DU classroom material (citing Gurdip Singh) defines personality as "the capacity to be a bearer of rights and duties under international law". Four constituents of legal capacity are traditionally tested:

  1. Capacity to make claims in respect of breaches of international law (procedural capacity / locus standi);
  2. Capacity to enter into treaties and other international agreements (treaty-making power);
  3. Capacity to enjoy privileges and immunities from the jurisdiction of national courts;
  4. Capacity to be subject to obligations and to be held responsible for their breach — the "duty" side of personality (e.g., individual criminal responsibility).

To this modern writers add the capacity to espouse claims on behalf of others (functional protection), and the capacity to be a party before international tribunals.

C. States — the Primary Subject

1. Criteria of Statehood

The Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States, 1933, Article 1 — the standard four criteria:

  1. Permanent population;
  2. Defined territory (not necessarily settled or fully delimited — North Sea Continental Shelf; Israel/India cases);
  3. Government — effective control, though "failed States" and Governments-in-exile complicate this (Aaland Islands 1920);
  4. Capacity to enter into relations with other States — i.e., independence and the absence of control by another State (hence the rejection of "puppet" claims; contrast the condominium of the New Hebrides/Vanuatu).

The Montevideo criteria are complemented by two modern additions: respect for self-determination and legality of the entity's creation (the "unlawful creation" doctrine applied to Northern Cyprus — Loizidou cases; Southern Rhodesia; and to the Bantustans).

3. Sovereign equality and its nine corollaries

UNGA Res. 2625 (XXV), 1970 — Declaration on Principles of International Law concerning Friendly Relations — elaborates the principle that all States enjoy sovereign equality, meaning: (i) juridical equality; (ii) the rights inherent in full sovereignty; (iii) the duty to respect the personality of other States; (iv) territorial integrity and political independence; (v) the right freely to choose and develop political, social, economic and cultural systems; (vi) the duty to comply fully and in good faith with international obligations and to live in peace.

An illustration you can add — act of State and sovereign immunity

A dramatic practical consequence of "the State as a subject" is the doctrine of the act of State and State immunity. In Kuwait Airways Corp. v. Iraqi Airways Co. [1995] 3 All ER 694 (in your folder), the House of Lords held that Iraq's seizure of KAC aircraft and their transfer to Iraqi Airways under Revolutionary Command Council Resolution 369 were acts of a sovereign character (acta jure imperii); accordingly, under s. 14(2)(a) of the State Immunity Act 1978 the airline could not claim immunity for them — because s. 14(2) withdraws immunity where a State entity is engaged in a commercial transaction — and the Court could look behind the State's own legislation; but the subsequent incorporation of the aircraft into the fleet was commercial use. Service of process on the Iraqi embassy was held invalid under s. 12(1) of the Act. The case captures perfectly the modern lesson: the State's personality confers immunity, but only for genuinely governmental acts.

D. International Organisations as Subjects

1. Establishment and types

Organisations began as nineteenth-century administrative unions — the Rhine and Danube river commissions, the International Telegraph Union (1865), the Universal Postal Union (1874). The 20th century produced the institutional generation of the League (1919), the ILO (1919) and finally the UN (1945), followed by the specialised agencies and a dense network of bodies: ILO, FAO, UNESCO, WHO, IMF, IBRD (World Bank), ICAO, IMO, ITU, WMO, WIPO, IFAD, UNIDO, IAEA (which reports to the GA and, where relevant, the SC), the WTO (1995), the ICC and ITLOS (created by treaty).

2. Personality: conferred, implied and objective

  • No express Charter provision creates UN personality. The Court in 1949 built one out of the Organisation's characteristics: a centre with organs (Art. 7), special tasks, membership duties (Art. 2(5)), capacity to make recommendations, legal capacity and privileges and immunities (Arts. 104, 105), and the power to conclude agreements (Art. 63).
  • Implied powers: an organisation "must be deemed to have those powers which, though not expressly provided in the Charter, are conferred upon it by necessary implication as being essential to the performance of its duties." The doctrine was solidified in Certain Expenses of the United Nations (1962) ICJ Rep. 151, at 168, and restated in Legality of the Use by a State of Nuclear Weapons in Armed Conflict (WHO Advisory Opinion, 1996) — where the Court refused to imply a WHO competence to address the legality of nuclear weapons because it was not necessary for WHO's constitutional functions. Use both cases together: the doctrine is powerful but bounded by functional necessity.
  • Objective personality: because 50 States representing the vast majority of the international community created the UN, it possesses personality erga omnes, "and not merely personality recognized by them alone" — so the UN may claim even against non-Member States.
  • Absolute or relative? Brownlie's caution (worth stating in an answer): the practice of the UN and other organisations remains essentially functional and limited — organisations do not enjoy sovereignty, territory, or general jurisdictional competence. The correct statement is that personality is derivative, functional and relative.

3. Consequences of organisational personality

  1. Treaty-making capacity — the 1986 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties between States and International Organisations (not yet in force) codifies it.
  2. Privileges and immunities — the General Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations, 1946 (s. 35 is expressly relied on in Reparation); the ICJ's Applicability of the Obligation to Arbitrate under Section 21 of the UN Headquarters Agreement (1988) — the PLO observer mission case.
  3. Capacity to bring claims — the holding of Reparation; also the Organisation's right to functional protection of its agents.
  4. Responsibility of organisations — the ILC's Draft Articles on the Responsibility of International Organizations (2011), mirroring the State-responsibility Articles.
  5. Limits — an organisation cannot be a party to contentious proceedings before the ICJ (Art. 34(1) ICJ Statute); its personality is not the same as a State's, "still less … a super-State."

E. Individuals as Subjects — Rights, Duties and Standing

1. The old orthodoxy: the individual as object

Under classical law the individual was an object of international law: diplomatic protection belonged to the State, piracy and slave-trading were suppressed through the medium of State responsibility, and no individual could appear before an international tribunal. The DU material records the traditional view with its textual support — Art. 34(1) of the ICJ Statute: "only States may be parties in cases before the Court."

4. Individual duties — international criminal responsibility

  • Nuremberg Principles 1950 (formulated by the ILC at the GA's request) — Principle I: any person who commits an act which constitutes a crime under international law is responsible therefor and liable to punishment.
  • Genocide Convention 1948, Art. IV — "persons committing genocide … shall be punished, whether they are constitutionally responsible rulers, public officials or private individuals."

5. How much subjecthood? The balanced formulation

The maximalist claim

"The rights and duties of States are in the final analysis rights and duties of individuals who compose the States and, therefore, individuals alone are the subjects of international law" (Kelsen).

Support: piracy; Nuremberg; individual petitions; the "human rights revolution"; obligations erga omnes and jus cogens; investment arbitration (individuals and companies may sue States directly under ICSID — the 1965 Convention on the Settlement of Investment Disputes, cited in the DU material).

The balanced (and correct) claim

"There is no general rule that the individual cannot be a 'subject of international law', and in particular contexts he appears as a legal person on the international plane" — Ian Brownlie (the exact quotation in the 2015 DU paper).

Limits: no general personality; no general treaty-making power; no standing before the ICJ (Art. 34(1)); rights are derivative, and are available only where a treaty confers them; nationality remains the principal connecting factor; enforcement still largely requires States.

Real-world illustrations of individual subjecthood
  • Diplomatic protection vs. functional protection: the classic contrast from Reparation — the State protects its national (diplomatic protection, with its prerequisites of continuous nationality and exhaustion of local remedies: Nottebohm (1955), Barcelona Traction (1970)); the organisation protects its agent, irrespective of nationality.
  • Refugees and stateless persons: the 1951 Refugee Convention (Art. 33 non-refoulement) and the 1961 Statelessness Convention give individuals directly invocable status; the ILC's Draft Articles on Diplomatic Protection, 2006, keep the rule that a State may protect only nationals.
  • Barcelona Traction (1970) para. 33: "an essential distinction should be drawn between the obligations of a State towards the international community as a whole, and those arising vis-à-vis another State… By their very nature the former are the concern of all States … they are obligations erga omnes." This is the doctrinal bridge from the individual-in-State to the individual-in-the-international-community.
  • India-context: Jadhav; Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) (CEDAW read into Arts. 14/15/19/21); Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996) (customary international environmental principles incorporated) — the individual's international rights working through municipal courts.

1.6Third World and International Law

The last line of the syllabus — and one that DU examiners love because it separates a textbook answer from a thinking one. The 2025 paper's question paper even highlights this head in red ink. The treatment below follows S.K. Verma's Chapter 1 (§IV "New States and International Law", pp. 19–23) and adds the TWAIL literature.

A. Who are the "Third World"? Definitions and Terminology

  • "Third World" — coined in the Cold War as a description of the non-aligned group (India, Yugoslavia, Egypt, Indonesia, Ghana), and used today to describe the developing States of Asia, Africa and Latin America, of which the great majority won independence after 1945.
  • "New States" — the term Verma uses, with a caveat that it "is considered a misnomer"; R.L. Friedheim and J.J.G. Syatauw suggest the more accurate term "dissatisfied States": States that accept much of the existing law, but reject the parts which were procured against them or in their absence.
  • "TWAIL" — Third World Approaches to International Law — a scholarly movement (R.P. Anand, B.S. Chimni, Upendra Baxi, Antony Anghie, Makau Mutua, James Gathii, Obiora Okafor) which argues that international law is not a neutral, benign body of rules, but a system whose categories (sovereignty, territory, statehood, the "standard of civilisation") were forged in, and continue to reproduce, colonial relations.

B. The Historical Grievance — Law as Colonial Instrument

  1. Exclusion from the "club." Oppenheim's definition — "civilised states" — illustrates the point. As Verma notes, the law was "European Public Law applicable among the Christian nations"; "even though China had 5,000 years of old culture, she was not included in the group of civilised states." Turkey became the first non-European subject only in the mid-nineteenth century; the League of Nations (1920) marked the "beginning of a new era" of universality.
  2. Doctrine as instrument. The critical narratives: terra nullius and the denial of sovereignty to indigenous polities (a doctrine the ICJ rejected, for the purposes of the Western Sahara advisory opinion, in the Sahul pleadings and finally in Western Sahara (1975); and which Australia repudiated in Mabo v. Queensland (No. 2) (1992)); the "standard of civilisation"; the "civilising mission"; the Berlin Conference (1884–85) and the "effective occupation" test; the uti possidetis juris doctrine; and, most vividly, the system of capitulations "by which their nationals were subject to their national consular courts rather than to the national law of the capitulating State, such as in China, Iran, Turkey."
  3. Colonial "consent." Because most Asian and African territories were not sovereign members of the international community in 1945, they were bound by the existing corpus of custom without having participated in its formation — a fact TWAIL writers say is fatal to the positivist account of consent, and which the law conceals by the technique of "tacit acquiescence."

D. The Structural Problem the Third World's Numbers Created

Verma presents the dialectic fairly — state both sides for full marks:

The Third World's gains
  • "They are closely associated in the codification and formulation of new norms of international conduct for States by their participation in the deliberations of the International Law Commission."
  • They "have helped in giving new directions to the law on State responsibility by not confining it only to the treatment of aliens, but also to other breaches of international law as well as affecting the rules concerning the use of force."
  • They obtained an equitable representation of "the principal civilisations of the world" on the ICJ (Africa 3, Asia 3, Latin America 2, Eastern Europe 2, Western Europe and others 5).
The enduring difficulty
  • "The explosion of new States and their unified stand on many issues … is making it increasingly difficult to adopt new rules."
  • "The western countries are wary of the 'terror of majority' unleashed by new nations."
  • "The first major casualty of this new phenomenon is the principle of unanimity for the adoption of the rules of international law."
  • It "not only makes difficult new customary rules to be evolved, but also the adoption and implementation of multilateral treaties."
  • Possible outcomes: agreement on the lowest common denominator — "some general, imprecise or ambiguous norms"; or a shift from universality to regional law (as in Latin America and the EU).

Verma's own synthesis — memorise this as the conclusion: "As States engage in varied transnational activities, some commonality of interest, howsoever limited, will always remain and that can become the basis for framing and evolving international law with universal application. With increasing interdependence among nations and the global effects of new technological developments affecting outer space, sea-bed and the environment, there has been a growing awareness about the need for universal law or the 'common law of mankind', which should transcend the national borders and should have within its ambit individuals, corporations, international institutions, States and other groups."

CASE 1 — Reparation for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the United Nations

Citation: Advisory Opinion, ICJ Reports 1949, p. 174 (11 April 1949).
Bench: President Basdevant; Vice-President Guerrero; Judges Alvarez, Hackworth, Winiarski, Zoričić, De Visscher, Sir Arnold McNair, Klaestad, Badawi Pasha, Krylov, Read, Hsu Mo, Azevedo, Fabela.
Prescribed in the DU syllabus at: "Reparation for Injuries Suffered in the Service of the United Nations Case. ICJ Rep. 1949, p. 174…" — and reproduced in full at pp. 7–12 of the DU LB-205 Case Material.

1. Facts

  • Count Folke Bernadotte, a Swedish national, was appointed United Nations Mediator in Palestine. On 17 September 1948, while engaged in the performance of his duties in Jerusalem, he was assassinated; a French observer, Colonel André Sérot, was killed in the same attack.
  • The persons responsible were never apprehended by the authorities of the State concerned (Israel, then not yet a UN Member). The UN believed reparation was due and, more importantly, wanted a legal answer about who could claim — it or the national State.
  • On 3 December 1948 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 258 (III). Its preamble records that the series of tragic events which had befallen agents of the United Nations "raises, with greater urgency than ever, the question of the arrangements to be made by the United Nations with a view to ensuring to its agents the fullest measure of protection in the future and ensuring that reparation be made for the injuries suffered"; and that "it is highly desirable that the Secretary-General should be able to act without question as efficaciously as possible with a view to obtaining any reparation due."
  • By that Resolution the GA submitted two legal questions to the Court for an advisory opinion.
The two questions, verbatim

Question I: "In the event of an agent of the United Nations in the performance of his duties suffering injury in circumstances involving the responsibility of a State, has the United Nations, as an Organization, the capacity to bring an international claim against the responsible de jure or de facto government with a view to obtaining the reparation due in respect of the damage caused (a) to the United Nations, (b) to the victim or to persons entitled through him?"

Question II: "In the event of an affirmative reply on point 1 (b), how is action by the United Nations to be reconciled with such rights as may be possessed by the State of which the victim is a national?"

2. Issues

  1. Does the UN possess international personality? The Charter nowhere says so expressly.
  2. If so, does personality include the capacity to bring an international claim (a) for damage to the Organisation itself, and (b) for damage to its agent?
  3. Can the claim be brought against a State that is not a Member of the United Nations?
  4. How is the Organisation's "functional protection" to be reconciled with the national State's right of diplomatic protection? Does the fact that the agent bears the nationality of the defendant State bar the claim?

3. Arguments on both sides

Table 1.22 — Competing positions before the Court
Party / organPrincipal submission
The Secretary-General (in support of an affirmative answer)The UN must enjoy the powers necessary for the effective discharge of its functions; without the capacity to claim, the Organisation cannot protect its agents or obtain redress; the Charter's silence is not a denial of capacity but an invitation to infer it from the Organisation's functions.
The argument from the text of the Charter (against)The Charter does not confer international personality in terms; Art. 104 speaks only of "legal capacity" in the territory of Members (i.e., municipal capacity); the Organisation is not a State, still less a "super-State"; a claim for injury to an individual is the national State's own right of diplomatic protection and cannot be exercised by anyone else.
The argument from the traditional rule of nationality of claims (against)Diplomatic protection is exercised by and only by the national State; a claim in respect of the victim is therefore the national State's claim; to allow the UN to bring it would displace or duplicate the State's right. (The Court accepted this rule as existing, but held it inapplicable — see below.)
The argument from interests (in support)Agents of the UN in disturbed parts of the world face unusual dangers; their national State may be unable or unwilling to claim; the agent must not have to rely on the protection of his own State, because that would compromise his independence and the impartiality of the Organisation — precisely the object of Art. 100 of the Charter.

4. Reasoning of the Court — step by step

  1. Method: infer from functions, not from the text. The Court began from the general proposition: "The subjects of law in any legal system are not necessarily identical in their nature or in the extent of their rights, and their nature depends upon the needs of the community." The Charter "was not settled by the actual terms of the Charter." It was therefore necessary to "consider what characteristics it was intended thereby to give to the Organization."
  2. Indicators of personality: the Charter made the Organisation a centre with organs and special tasks (Art. 1(4), Art. 7); it required Members to give the Organisation every assistance and to carry out SC decisions (Art. 2(5)); it empowered the GA to make recommendations; it conferred legal capacity and privileges and immunities (Arts. 104, 105) and provided for agreements between the Organisation and its Members. "Practice — in particular the conclusion of conventions to which the Organization is a party — has confirmed the character of an organization which occupies a position in certain respects in detachment from its Members." The Convention on the Privileges and Immunities of the United Nations, 1946, s. 35 "creates rights and duties between each of the signatories and the organization … It is difficult to see how such a convention could operate except upon the international plane and as between parties possessing international personality."
  3. Conclusion on personality: "the Organization was intended to exercise and enjoy, and is in fact exercising and enjoying, functions and rights which can only be explained on the basis of the possession of a large measure of international personality and the capacity to operate upon an international plane." Accordingly "the Organization is an international person."
    • But — and the Court was emphatic — "That is not the same thing as saying that it is a State, which it certainly is not, or that its legal personality and rights and duties are the same as those of a State. Still less is it the same thing as saying that it is a super-State…"
    • "What it does mean is that it is a subject of international law and capable of possessing international rights and duties, and that it has capacity to maintain its rights by bringing international claims."
  4. Question I(a) — damage to the Organisation itself. Yes. "The damage specified in Question 1 (a) means exclusively damage caused to the interests of the Organization itself, to its administrative machine, to its property and assets, and to the interests of which it is the guardian." The claim is based on the breach of an obligation owed to the Organisation; the defendant "cannot contend that this obligation is governed by municipal law"; and nobody can obtain reparation on the Organisation's behalf except the Organisation itself — "It cannot be supposed that … all the Members of the Organization, save the defendant State, must combine to bring a claim against the defendant for the damage suffered by the Organization."
  5. Question I(b) — damage to the agent ("functional protection"). Yes, by 11 votes to 4. Three reasons were given for rejecting the nationality-of-claims objection:
    1. The traditional rule "applies to claims brought by a State. But here we have the different and new case of a claim that would be brought by the Organization."
    2. "Even in inter-State relations, there are important exceptions to the rule, for there are cases in which protection may be exercised by a State on behalf of persons not having its nationality."
    3. The rule rests on two bases: first, the defendant State has broken an obligation towards the national State in respect of its nationals; second, only the party to whom an international obligation is due can bring a claim. "This is precisely what happens when the Organization, in bringing a claim for damage suffered by its agent, does so by invoking the breach of an obligation towards itself." Hence "the principle underlying this rule leads to the recognition of this capacity as belonging to the Organization."
    The Court then supplied the policy foundation of the new right: agents must be given adequate protection; the agent "must feel that this protection is assured to him by the Organization"; to ensure his independence and that of the Organisation, "it is essential that in performing his duties he need not have to rely on any other protection than that of the Organization … In particular, he should not have to rely on the protection of his own State," for otherwise his independence "might well be compromised, contrary to the principle applied by Article 100 of the Charter." This is "even more necessary when the agent is stateless."
  6. The juridical character of the claim: "The obligations entered into by States to enable the agents of the Organization to perform their duties are undertaken not in the interest of the agents, but in that of the Organization. When it claims redress for a breach of these obligations, the Organization is invoking its own right… In claiming reparation based on the injury suffered by its agent, the Organization does not represent the agent, but is asserting its own right, the right to secure respect for undertakings entered into towards the Organization." On reparation the Court recalled the PCIJ's statement in Factory at Chorzów (Judgment No. 8, Series A, No. 9, p. 21): "it is a principle of international law that the breach of an engagement involves an obligation to make reparation in an adequate form." The Court is not called upon to fix the measure; the reparation may include "the damage suffered by the victim or by persons entitled through him."
  7. Non-Member States — objective personality. "Fifty States, representing the vast majority of the members of the international community, had the power, in conformity with international law, to bring into being an entity possessing objective international personality, and not merely personality recognized by them alone, together with capacity to bring international claims." Hence the affirmative answer holds "whether or not the defendant State is a Member of the United Nations."
  8. Question II — reconciliation of the two protections (10 votes to 5). Where the victim has a nationality, "competition between the State's right of diplomatic protection and the Organization's right of functional protection might arise," and "there is no rule of law which assigns priority to the one or to the other, or which compels either the State or the Organization to refrain from bringing an international claim." Crucially: where the agent bears the nationality of the defendant State, that is no obstacle: "The action of the Organization is in fact based not upon the nationality of the victim but upon his status as agent of the Organization… the question of nationality is not pertinent to the admissibility of the claim." Customary practices about dual nationals do not apply because the Organisation does not act on the basis of nationality at all.
Operative findings (the exact results to quote)
  • Question I(a) — affirmative, unanimously, both as regards a Member State and a non-Member State: the UN has capacity to bring an international claim for damage to itself.
  • Question I(b) — affirmative, by eleven votes to four, both as regards a Member and a non-Member State: the UN has capacity to claim in respect of damage to the victim or persons entitled through him.
  • Question II — by ten votes to five: the two protections can co-exist; there is no rule of priority; the Organisation's claim rests on the agent's status, not nationality.
  • The Court implied that the measure of reparation would depend on the injury actually suffered, and included the damage suffered by the agent.

5. Principles evolved (the "take-aways" — write these as a numbered list in the exam)

  1. Functional necessity / implied powers: an international organisation possesses the powers "conferred upon it by necessary implication as being essential to the performance of its duties," even where the constituent instrument is silent.
  2. Relative, not uniform, personality: subjects of law are not identical in nature or extent of rights; personality depends on the needs of the community. The UN is an international person but is neither a State nor a super-State.
  3. Objective international personality: an organisation created by a vast majority of the international community possesses personality erga omnes, and not merely as against its own members.
  4. Functional protection: an independent right of the Organisation, parallel to but distinct from diplomatic protection, arising by necessary intendment from the Charter and justified by the agent's independence and by Article 100.
  5. Reparation as a general principle: the breach of an international engagement entails an obligation to make reparation in adequate form (Factory at Chorzów).
  6. Primacy of function over form: the identity of the claimant depends on whose right has been breached, not on the identity or nationality of the person injured.

6. Comment, criticism and subsequent application

  • The "super-State" anxiety. Judge Winiarski appended a declaration concerned that the concept of an "international person" with a capacity to claim might place the Organisation above its Members; the Court's careful formula (person, not State, not super-State) answers a real political fear — and has since been reaffirmed in the ILC's Draft Articles on the Responsibility of International Organizations (2011).
  • Applied in: Effect of Awards of Compensation made by the United Nations Administrative Tribunal (1954) — the GA cannot refuse to give effect to a UNAT award; the Tribunal's competence is an exercise of the Organisation's implied powers and its award possesses res judicata authority within the Organisation. Certain Expenses of the United Nations (1962) — the "implied powers" doctrine applied to peacekeeping financing. Legal Consequences for States of the Continued Presence of South Africa in Namibia (1971) — powers over the Mandate as an exercise of UN functional authority. Applicability of the Obligation to Arbitrate under Section 21 of the UN Headquarters Agreement (1988) — the UN's personality gives it standing to invoke the arbitration obligation against the US.
  • Limits made clear in Legality of the Use by a State of Nuclear Weapons in Armed Conflict (WHO AO, 1996): implied powers must be necessary for the functions of the organisation as defined in its constitution — the WHO could not borrow the UN's peace-and-security competence. Use this pair of cases together: Reparation (the doctrine at its strongest) and WHO (its limits).
  • Practical legacy: Israel ultimately paid compensation in 1950 (a modest sum, usually reported as about US$54,628, in respect of Bernadotte) without formally accepting the opinion's reasoning. All peacekeeping claims since then have been processed on the strength of the opinion. The 1994 Convention on the Safety of United Nations and Associated Personnel, and the Report of the Secretary-General's Panel on the safety of staff, build on it.
  • Modern doctrinal position: "In principle it is now well established that international organizations may indeed possess objective international legal personality" (the DU material's own summary). The League's successor organisations (ILO, FAO) and the specialised agencies have benefited directly.
How to use this case in each sub-topic
  • 1.1 Definition: proof that States are not the only subjects — the leading authority against Oppenheim's "States only" view; the source of the modern functional definition of personality.
  • 1.2 Development: the institutionalisation of international life after 1945; the UN's capacity to act on the international plane.
  • 1.4 Sanctions: the Organisation's capacity to "maintain its rights by bringing international claims" is itself a sanction mechanism distinct from diplomatic protection.
  • 1.5 Subjects: the foundational case — always the first case to cite for the personality of international organisations and for the position of individuals (functional protection).

CASE 2 — Application of the International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism and of the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (Ukraine v. Russian Federation)

Citation (as prescribed by DU): ICJ Reports (2019). The judgment prescribed is the Judgment on Preliminary Objections of 8 November 2019. Related stages: Provisional Measures Order of 19 April 2017; Merits Judgment of 31 January 2024.
Bench (2019): President Yusuf; Vice-President Xue; Judges Tomka, Abraham, Bennouna, Cançado Trindade, Donoghue, Gaja, Sebutinde, Bhandari, Robinson, Crawford, Salam, Iwasawa; Judges ad hoc Pocar (for Ukraine) and Skotnikov (for Russia).

1. Facts and procedural history

  • Background. The proceedings arose out of events from the spring of 2014 onwards: the annexation of Crimea by the Russian Federation and the armed conflict in eastern Ukraine (Donetsk and Luhansk regions), where Ukraine alleged that Russia was supporting, arming and financing armed groups (the self-styled "DPR" and "LPR").
  • 16 January 2017: Ukraine filed an Application in the Registry of the Court, invoking as the basis of jurisdiction Article 24(1) of the ICSFT 1999 (for the events in eastern Ukraine) and Article 22 of CERD 1965 (for the situation in Crimea). Both are "compromissory clauses" — treaty provisions conferring jurisdiction on the ICJ for disputes relating to the interpretation or application of the treaty.
  • 19 April 2017: the Court made an Order on provisional measures, finding that the conditions for the indication of measures were met only in part and only under CERD. It did not find the mens rea element of terrorism-financing offences to be "plausible." It ordered Russia, pending the final decision: (i) to refrain from maintaining or imposing measures that prevent the Crimean Tatar community from conducting its own representative institutions, including the Mejlis; (ii) to ensure the availability of Ukrainian-language education; and (iii) both Parties to refrain from any action aggravating the dispute.
  • 12 September 2018: the Russian Federation raised preliminary objections to the jurisdiction of the Court and to the admissibility of the Application. Hearings were held in June 2019. Judgment on the objections was delivered on 8 November 2019.

2. The two conventions relied upon

3. Arguments of both sides

Table 1.24 — Ukraine's case and Russia's four preliminary objections

Jurisdiction ratione materiae — ICSFT The alleged acts "fall within the provisions" of the ICSFT: Russia financed and armed terrorist groups and thereby breached the obligation to prevent and cooperate in the suppression of terrorism financing. Article 2's phrase "any person" is broad enough to cover State officials; and the financing of terrorism by a State falls within the Convention. Four sub-points: (i) "any person" in Arts. 2 and 18 means private persons only, not State officials; (ii) State responsibility for financing terrorism is outside the ICSFT (which is a law-enforcement/criminal-law instrument) — the heart of Ukraine's case therefore falls outside the Convention; (iii) Ukraine had never put forward a plausible case of intentional or knowing financing; (iv) the allegations do not meet the requirements of Art. 2(1).

4. Reasoning of the Court — on each objection

  1. Method for jurisdiction ratione materiae: the Court recalled that it must ascertain whether the acts of which the applicant complains "fall within the provisions" of the treaty containing the compromissory clause. This may require interpretation of the scope of the treaty. It added a caution that has since become doctrinally important: "at the present stage of the proceedings, an examination by it of the alleged wrongful acts or of the plausibility of the claims is not generally warranted."
  2. ICSFT — "any person" (para. 61). "The ICSFT applies to offences committed by 'any person', i.e. both individuals acting in a private capacity and those who are State agents." But the Court drew the crucial line: "while the commission by a State official of an offence does not in itself engage the responsibility of the State concerned under the Convention, the States parties to the ICSFT are under an obligation to take appropriate measures and to co-operate in the prevention and suppression of offences of financing acts of terrorism committed by whichever person. Should a State breach such an obligation, its responsibility under the Convention would arise."
  3. ICSFT — State financing of terrorism is outside the Convention (paras. 59–60). "The financing by a State of acts of terrorism is not addressed by the ICSFT. It lies outside the scope of the Convention." The consequences: Ukraine's central claim — that Russia itself financed terrorism — fell outside the Court's jurisdiction ratione materiae. But the Court immediately added that this finding "does not imply that such conduct is lawful under international law": State-sponsored terrorism remains governed by the Charter principles of non-intervention and non-use of force (this nuance is what the 2024 merits judgment then built upon, by finding a violation of the ancillary duty to investigate under Art. 9(1)).
  4. ICSFT — mens rea is a merits question (para. 63). The existence of the requisite intention or knowledge "raises complex issues of law and especially of fact that divide the Parties and are properly a matter for the merits"; the mens rea issue therefore did not affect the scope of the ICSFT and was not relevant to jurisdiction ratione materiae. This reversed the analytical move the Court had made at the provisional-measures stage in 2017, and it is one of the most commented-upon points of the judgment.
  5. ICSFT — preconditions of Art. 24(1) (paras. 70–77). For the first precondition, there must have been a genuine attempt to settle the dispute through negotiation which failed within a reasonable time; the diplomatic exchanges lasted about two years with little progress, so the precondition was met. For the second — organisation of an arbitration — negotiations were held and the parties were unable to agree within six months, so that precondition was also met. Hence jurisdiction exists under Art. 24(1). The Court noted (para. 76) that Ukraine's proposal of an ICJ ad hoc chamber was "one of the options put forward" during the arbitration-related negotiations, leaving the doctrinal point open.
  6. CERD — subject-matter jurisdiction. Both Parties agreed that Crimean Tatars and ethnic Ukrainians constitute ethnic groups protected under CERD. Given "the broadly formulated rights and obligations contained in the Convention," the Court held that the measures of which Ukraine complained — restrictions allegedly imposed on those communities — "are capable of having an adverse effect on the enjoyment of certain rights protected under CERD," and thus fall within the provisions of that Convention.
  7. CERD — are Art. 22's preconditions alternative or cumulative? (the constitutional holding) The Court held they are alternative, examining (i) the text: "or" can be read disjunctively, and that is not the only possible reading; (ii) the context: negotiation and the "procedures expressly provided for in the Convention" are two means to the same objective, so a cumulative reading would require a State to negotiate, then take the same dispute to the CERD Committee for further negotiation — which the context does not support; (iii) object and purpose: CERD aims "to eradicate all forms of racial discrimination effectively and promptly," and a cumulative reading would render that aim more difficult. Accordingly, it was enough to consider whether negotiation had been attempted — and the ~2 years of diplomatic correspondence and meetings showed a genuine attempt that had become "futile or deadlocked" by the time of the Application.
  8. Admissibility — exhaustion of local remedies. The Court recalled that local remedies must be exhausted "as a matter of customary international law in cases in which a State brings a claim on behalf of one or more of its nationals." But "in filing its Application, Ukraine does not adopt the cause of its nationals, but challenges, on the basis of CERD, the alleged pattern of conduct of the Russian Federation with regard to the treatment of the Crimean Tatar and Ukrainian communities in Crimea." The rule therefore does not apply, and the objection to admissibility was rejected.
Operative clause (8 November 2019)
  1. Rejects, by thirteen votes to three, Russia's preliminary objection that the Court lacks jurisdiction under Art. 24(1) ICSFT;
  2. Finds, by thirteen votes to three, that it has jurisdiction on the basis of Art. 24(1) ICSFT to entertain Ukraine's claims;
  3. Rejects, by fifteen votes to one, Russia's preliminary objection that the Court lacks jurisdiction under Art. 22 CERD;
  4. Rejects, unanimously, Russia's objection to the admissibility of the Application in relation to the CERD claims (non-exhaustion of local remedies);
  5. Finds, by fifteen votes to one, that it has jurisdiction on the basis of Art. 22 CERD, and that the Application is admissible in relation to those claims.

5. Principles evolved (extract these — they are the examinable "ratio")

  1. Treaty crimes and "any person": a criminal-law suppression convention may reach conduct by State agents as "persons," even though the State itself is not made responsible for the offence — the responsibility is for the State's failure to take measures and to cooperate (Art. 9(1)).
  2. State financing of terrorism lies outside the ICSFT — a limiting interpretation of a suppression convention, with the express saving that such conduct is not thereby lawful (governed by the Charter's non-intervention and non-use-of-force rules).
  3. Jurisdiction ratione materiae vs. merits: questions of plausibility and mens rea belong to the merits and should not generally be examined at the jurisdictional stage — a clarification of the Oil Platforms/Genocide jurisprudence.
  4. Compromissory-clause preconditions: negotiation and arbitration requirements in suppression conventions are justiciable procedural preconditions; "genuine attempt" is a flexible, fact-sensitive test; futility or deadlock suffices.
  5. CERD Art. 22's preconditions are alternative, not cumulative — a purposive, "effective and prompt" reading of an anti-discrimination treaty's dispute-settlement clause.
  6. Exhaustion of local remedies is inapplicable where a State challenges a pattern of conduct rather than espousing the cause of identified nationals — an important refinement of the diplomatic-protection analogy.
  7. For the law of subjects: the case demonstrates that individuals (including State officials) may be the direct addressees of international obligations under treaty regimes, while State responsibility under such treaties is engaged only through the State's own ancillary obligations — the modern, graduated picture of individual and State responsibility side by side.

6. Subsequent history, criticism and use in the answer

  • Merits Judgment, 31 January 2024 (highly relevant follow-up): the Court found, by 13 votes to 2, that the Russian Federation had breached Art. 9(1) ICSFT by failing to investigate facts contained in information received from Ukraine regarding persons who allegedly committed offences under Art. 2 of the Convention; and, by 13 to 2, that Russia had breached CERD Arts. 2(1)(a) and 5(e)(v) in the implementation of its school-education curriculum in Crimea. It rejected almost all the remaining claims (including the allegations of financing of the "DPR"/"LPR" and of killings of Crimean Tatars) and declined to award compensation, holding that the declaration of unlawfulness constituted just satisfaction. The judgment is criticised by both sides — Ukraine as "unfulfilled promises," Russia's ad hoc judge as an unjustified extension of Art. 9.
  • Critique worth one line each: (i) the Court's separation of "State financing" from the Convention reduced Ukraine's case to ancillary cooperation duties; (ii) the expansiveness of the Art. 9(1) duty combined with an extremely low evidential threshold attracted sharp dissent; (iii) the alternative reading of CERD Art. 22 has opened the ICJ's jurisdiction under other human-rights treaties with identically worded clauses (e.g., the Genocide, Torture, and CEDAW conventions) — a systemic consequence.
  • Indian relevance: the judgment's technique — using treaty preconditions to filter cases, then separating jurisdictional from merits questions — parallels India's own use of compromissory clauses (e.g., the VCCR Optional Protocol in Jadhav), and its treatment of individual rights informs the Indian debate over India's non-accession to the ICC.
  • How to deploy it in Topic 1:
    • Under 1.5 Subjects — the best modern authority for the proposition that individuals (including State officials) are direct addressees of treaty obligations; contrast with Reparation's organisation-centric personality.
    • Under 1.4 Sanctions — an illustration of a treaty-based enforcement chain: negotiation → arbitration → ICJ (Art. 24(1) ICSFT; Art. 22 CERD); the "sanction" is a declaratory judgment plus the political consequences of the finding.
    • Under 1.3 Codification — the dispute settlement clause is not an ILC product, but the case illustrates how multilaterally negotiated texts operate in practice and why precision in drafting compromissory clauses matters.

Model answer — 2025, Q.1 (Subjects / personality + the UN claim hypothetical), 20 marks

(i) Opening — the test. An entity is a subject of international law if it possesses international legal personality: "capable of possessing international rights and duties, and … the capacity to maintain its rights by bringing international claims" — Reparation for Injuries (1949). Legal personality comprises four capacities: to make claims; to conclude treaties; to enjoy privileges and immunities; and to be the bearer of obligations (and responsibility for their breach).

(ii) Theories. ① States only — Oppenheim (1905), Sir Frederick Smith; Art. 34(1) ICJ Statute. ② Individuals only — Kelsen. ③ The modern, functional view — States remain primary, but organisations, individuals and certain non-State entities hold graded personality. Cite Reparation (Organsiation); Barcelona Traction (States and companies); LaGrand/Jadhav (individuals).

(iii) The subject categories.
• States — Montevideo criteria 1933; sovereignty, equality (GA Res. 2625(XXV)); recognition (declaratory vs. constitutive, Lauterpacht; Haile Selassie (1939), Arantzazu Mendi (1939)).
• International organisations — established by treaty; personality inferred from functions ("implied powers"); objective personality (Reparation); privileges and immunities (1946 General Convention); limits (WHO Nuclear Weapons AO, 1996); UNAT Awards AO (1954).
• Individuals — as beneficiaries of rights (UDHR 1948; ICCPR OP1; ECHR Art. 34; VCCR Art. 36 — LaGrand, Avena, Jadhav) and bearers of duties (piracy; Nuremberg Principle I; Genocide Art. IV; Rome Statute Arts. 25, 27, 28) — but no general treaty-making power and no standing before the ICJ.
• Non-State entities — insurgents, national liberation movements, peoples (self-determination), the Holy See, the ICRC, indigenous peoples (UNDRIP 2007), MNCs (ICSID).

(iv) The hypothetical. A UN employee of nationality X is murdered by a secret service agency of State Y; the UN claims against Y. Applying Reparation:

  1. Maintainability ratione personae: the UN is an international person with objective personality, so it may bring a claim even if Y is a non-Member ("fifty States, representing the vast majority of the international community, had the power … to bring into being an entity possessing objective international personality").
  2. Question I(a): the UN may claim for damage to itself — its administrative machine, property, assets and "interests of which it is the guardian."
  3. Question I(b): the UN may also claim reparation for the damage suffered by the victim or persons entitled through him — because it "does not represent the agent, but is asserting its own right" that obligations due to it be respected; the nationality of the agent (even that of the defendant State) is "not pertinent to the admissibility of the claim," since the claim rests on the agent's status, not his nationality.
  4. Agency: the Court requires the injured person to be an "agent" of the Organisation, performing duties, with the damage arising "in circumstances involving the responsibility of a State." The employment relationship is the effective test of agency — his nationality is irrelevant.
  5. Question II: where the national State also has a right of diplomatic protection, "there is no rule of law which assigns priority to the one or to the other"; the two rights may be exercised independently, and Art. 100 independence justifies functional protection.
  6. Attribution: the acts must be attributable to Y (a secret service agency is a State organ — ILC ARSIWA Art. 4) and must constitute the breach of an international obligation owed to the UN; responsibility towards the UN rather than towards State X is the basis.

(v) Conclusion. Maintainable: the UN may bring a claim for both heads of damage; if Y is the State of nationality of the agent, the claim is still admissible because the UN asserts its own right. This is exactly the situation in which Reparation arose — Bernadotte, a Swedish national, was assassinated while serving as UN Mediator in Palestine.

B.1 Model answer — "'International law is not true law but positive international morality.' Discuss critically this statement of Austin and spell out the sanctions of international law in detail." 2011199819951994199319922000

1. Introduction. The question is not whether international law exists, but whether it satisfies the definition of "law". Austin's command theory (following Hobbes, Bentham, Pufendorf and Holland) holds that law is a command of a determinate sovereign enforced by sanction; international law lacks both a legislature and an enforcement agency, and is therefore only "positive international morality" — for Austin it is "law improperly so-called," analogous to rules binding a club.

2. Austin's six reasons. (i) no determinate superior political authority; (ii) no effective legislative machinery; (iii) no sanction; (iv) no executive to enforce ICJ decisions or treaties; (v) no potent judiciary with compulsory jurisdiction; (vi) hence it is "quasi-law".

3. Foundations of the command theory criticised. (a) Austin's own admission that "the essential part of constitutional law is nothing more than positive morality" (Goodhart, 1936) shows the theory over-excludes: British constitutional law, the basic norm of the British Constitution, and the common law itself cannot satisfy the test. (b) Sir Henry Maine's historical jurisprudence proved that in primitive societies there were laws without a sovereign. (c) Modern jurisprudence (Hart, Kelsen, Fitzmaurice) does not require a sovereign command — law is a system of rules recognised by officials, and in a decentralised order, sanctions operate through self-help.

4. Starke's four answers. (i) formal legislative authority is not essential to law (primitive communities; customary systems); (ii) international legislation exists — law-making treaties (Charter, Geneva, Hague, Vienna Conventions); (iii) authoritative agencies do not treat it as mere morality — States plead treaties and precedent; (iv) the UN and the ICJ are founded on the true legality of international law — Art. 38 ICJ Statute, Art. 59, Art. 94 UN Charter.

5. Further answers. Paquete Habana (1900) — "International law is a part of our law"; Hart — "international law is law because States regard it as law. Nothing need be further proved"; Kelsen — international law is a "coercive order" whose sanctions are reprisals and war, lawful only as countermeasures; Brierly — it is "more honoured in the observance than in the breaches"; and the frequency of violations no more disproves its legality than municipal crime disproves municipal law (Oppenheim: violations "may strain its legal force to breaking point; the formal … affirmation of its binding nature is not without significance").

6. Sanctions in detail — the second half of the question. Structure it in three layers:
(a) Centralised sanctions: Chapter VII of the Charter — Art. 39 determination, Art. 40 provisional measures, Art. 41 (non-military: interruption of economic relations and of rail, sea, air, postal, telegraphic, radio and other means of communication, and severance of diplomatic relations), Art. 42 (military action by air, sea and land forces); Art. 25 makes decisions binding (Namibia, 1971). Examples: Rhodesia (Res. 232, 1966 — the first mandatory sanctions), Iraq (Res. 661, 1990), Libya (1970, 1973), DPRK (1718), the targeted regimes 1267/1373/1540.
(b) Judicial and institutional sanctions: Art. 94(1) and (2) — ICJ judgments and recourse to the SC; Art. 41 ICJ Statute (provisional measures, now binding per LaGrand); GA Arts. 5, 6 and 19 (suspension, expulsion, loss of vote — the South Africa, Haiti and Somalia precedents); GA Res. 377(V) "Uniting for Peace"; HRC UPR and special procedures; ITLOS Art. 296; ICC arrest warrants (Art. 27) with SC referrals in Darfur and Libya; PCA awards enforced by consent and reputation.
(c) Horizontal and informal sanctions: retorsion; countermeasures under ARSIWA Arts. 22, 49–54 — must be proportionate (Art. 51; Air Services Agreement (1978); cf. Naulilaa (1928)) and may not involve force or breach fundamental human rights or humanitarian obligations (Art. 50); and adverse public opinion, by which "no State would like to be branded as the violator of human rights" (Verma) — the sanctions of any organised community that lacks a police.

7. Conclusion. Austin's theory rests on a definition of law that cannot survive the actual plurality of legal systems. International law is law — but law of a special, decentralised kind: as Brierly put it, it "operates in a decentralised political system. There is no world legislature, no international police, and no international court with compulsory jurisdiction." Hence Starke's conclusion: it is "weak law" — not no law.

B.2 Model answer — "A sharp contrast exists between the traditional and modern definitions of international law, yet they share some common features. Mark out the distinctions … and the way they have reoriented international law." 1999 (cf. 2002)

Structure. (i) Oppenheim 1905 as the traditional definition; (ii) five criticisms (organisations; "civilised"; individuals; general principles; static conception); (iii) the modern definitions — Oppenheim 8th and 9th editions, Fenwick, Jenks, Brierly, Kelsen; (iv) common features retained: a body of rules, binding, governing relations between distinct political communities, backed by the possibility of sanction; (v) the reorientation: from a European law of coexistence to a universal law of cooperation; from a State-only law to a law of a community of States and peoples; from a law about war to a law about peace and welfare; (vi) conclusion — the definition has followed the community, and the community has become universal ("ubi societas ibi jus").

Table to draw (one-liner): Traditional (State-centric; sources = custom and treaty; subjects = States; content = coexistence and war; geography = European; sanction = war and reprisal) vs. Modern (multi-actor; sources = + general principles, resolutions, unilateral acts; subjects = States, organisations, individuals; content = cooperation, human rights, development, environment; geography = universal 193 States; sanction = institutions, countermeasures and opinion).

B.3 Model answer — "Explain the basis of international law with the help of jurisprudential theories." 20151992

1. The question. "Basis" = the source of the binding force of international law; it is distinct from the question of sources (Art. 38). Three broad families of answers have been given: nature, will, and society.

2. Natural law school. Greek Stoics and Roman jus naturale/jus gentium; divine law in the medieval period; secularisation by Vittoria, Suarez, Gentilis and above all Grotius, for whom natural law was "the dictate of right reason". Sub-schools after Grotius: naturalists (Pufendorf), positivists (Bynkershoek) and Grotians (Wolff, Vattel — the "necessary Law of Nations"). The offshoot doctrine of fundamental rights — self-preservation, independence, equality, respect, intercourse — with Brierly's criticism that a right presupposes a legal system and that the doctrine denies growth. Maine's tribute: "the greatest function of the 'law of nature' was discharged in giving birth to modern International Law." Twentieth-century revival: human rights and international criminal law.

3. Positivism and consent. Law as it is; the will of the State as the source — Zorn (a branch of State law), Triepel (Vereinbarung/agreement), Anzilotti (pacta sunt servanda), Jellinek (auto-limitation), Oppenheim (common consent). Judicial expression: Lotus (1927) — restrictions on the independence of States cannot be presumed. Criticisms: cannot explain custom binding on new States (see the Third World problem, §1.6) or jus cogens; and "consent" is often a fiction (tacit acquiescence).

4. Sociological and policy schools. Duguit and Scelle (social solidarity); McDougal and Lasswell (authoritative decision-making for community values); Kelsen's pure theory (the hierarchy of norms and the Grundnorm; law as a coercive order operating through self-help).

5. The modern synthesis. Ubi societas ibi jus: the basis of obligation is the existence of an international community, whose members recognise the law as binding and habitually comply — the "law habit" (Fitzmaurice); Brierly's observation that violations are few compared to the vast body of observance; and the practical answer that the basis of obligation is a combination of consent (for treaties), practice and opinio juris (for custom), and the necessities of coexistence and cooperation for everything else.

6. Conclusion. No single theory explains the whole. The naturalists explain why States ought to obey; the positivists explain what they have agreed to; the sociological school explains why they do, in fact, obey. Together they describe a legal order that is real, binding, and decentralised.

B.4 Model answer — "In the long march of mankind from the cave to the computer a central role has always been played by the idea of law … explain the main reasons responsible for a systematic development of international law. How far do you agree that international law has by and large fulfilled its objective?" 2000 (cf. 2002)

Part A — reasons for systematic development (use the five-stage timeline of Fig. 1.2): (i) needs of commerce and communication (river commissions, IMO, ICAO, ITU, UPU); (ii) the destructiveness of war — Geneva 1864, St. Petersburg 1868, the Hague Conventions, the League, the Kellogg–Briand Pact, the UN Charter; (iii) the expansion of the international community beyond Europe (Turkey, Latin America, decolonisation; the League's "any State" and the UN's "peace-loving States"); (iv) institutionalisation — the League, PCIJ, UN, ICJ, specialised agencies, ICC, ITLOS, WTO; (v) the emergence of the individual as a subject and the human-rights movement (Versailles 1919, Nuremberg 1945, UDHR 1948, the Covenants 1966); (vi) new domains — air law 1919/1944, outer space 1967, the seabed and UNCLOS 1982, the environment (Stockholm 1972, UNCLOS, UNFCCC 1992, Paris 2015), cyber and climate; (vii) codification through the ILC and diplomatic conferences.

Part B — evaluation ("has it fulfilled its object?"): Successes — 193 States living under a common Charter; the prohibition of force in Art. 2(4) and the near-universal renunciation of conquest; decolonisation achieved largely through law and institutions; the human-rights corpus; the prohibition of genocide and the growth of individual criminal accountability; the settlement of thousands of boundary disputes; the law of the sea; a functioning criminal court. Failures — the veto and paralysed collective security (Syria, Crimea, Ukraine 2022); selectivity in enforcement; humanitarian catastrophes under sanctions (Iraq); failure to prevent genocide (Rwanda 1994, Srebrenica 1995); climate inaction; inequality of economic institutions; the persistence of the Third World's grievance (§1.6).

Conclusion. International law has not abolished war or injustice, but it has made violence costly, disputes justiciable and accountability possible (Nuremberg, ICTY, ICTR, ICC). Its object is not the elimination of conflict but its regulation. As Verma puts it: "Legal institutions can never guarantee lasting peace and security … what matters is that law responds to the changing needs of society."

B.5 Model answer — "What are the three generations of human rights? Discuss in detail the implementation of the human rights recognised by the ICCPR 1966 and the ICESCR 1966." 2015201320122011

Part A — three generations (Vašák's classification): ① First generation — civil and political rights (life, liberty, fair trial, expression, religion, vote); negative rights; roots in the 18th-century natural-rights philosophy of Locke and Rousseau; UDHR Arts. 3–21; ICCPR 1966 and its two Optional Protocols. ② Second generation — economic, social and cultural rights (work, fair wages, social security, health, education, food, housing, cultural participation); positive rights; roots in socialism and the Mexican (1917) and Soviet (1918) constitutions; UDHR Arts. 22–27; ICESCR 1966 and its Optional Protocol 2008. ③ Third generation — collective or solidarity rights (development, healthy environment, peace, self-determination, communication, humanitarian assistance); emerged in the 1970s with "the predominant support of developing countries"; the Declaration on the Right to Development 1986; Stockholm/Rio. Note the critique — many Western scholars doubt that group rights are human rights because they vest in no individual; and the positive duty aspects are hard to realise, as the fate of the NIEO shows. The two Covenants together with the UDHR and the protocols form the International Bill of Rights.

Part B — implementation of the ICCPR. (i) Reporting: State Parties report to the Human Rights Committee (18 independent experts) under Art. 40; (ii) Inter-State communications under Art. 41 (declaration-based; never used in practice); (iii) Individual communications under the First Optional Protocol — the Committee expresses "views" (adopted 66–2–38; in force 23 March 1976); (iv) General Comments interpreting the Covenant (e.g., GC 34 on freedom of expression); (v) the Second Optional Protocol on abolition of the death penalty (1989). India is party to the Covenant and to OP1, with a reservation on Art. 1 (self-determination). Illustrations of impact: Toonen v. Australia (1994) — decriminalisation of homosexuality in Tasmania; the Committee's jurisprudence on arbitrary detention, torture and the death penalty.

Part C — implementation of the ICESCR. (i) Reporting to the Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (since 1987); (ii) the Committee's General Comments (e.g., GC 14 on the right to health; GC 15 on water; GC 24 on business and human rights); (iii) the Optional Protocol (2008) allowing individual and inter-State communications and inquiries; (iv) "progressive realisation" and the minimum core (Art. 2(1)); (v) the special role of the softer machinery, and of national courts (India: Vishaka 1997 for workplace harassment; Puttaswamy 2017 for privacy; the right to food litigation under Art. 21, the National Food Security Act 2013).

Part D — assessment. The Covenant machinery is treaty-based, not coercive: "most of these instruments provide the machinery and procedure, albeit not so perfect, for the implementation and monitoring compliance … an individual does not have any direct recourse to any international organ to enforce its rights, and in certain cases … non-binding recommendations are generally made." Contrast the European Convention 1950, whose Art. 34 individual application and Art. 46 supervision by the Committee of Ministers give the individual a real remedy — "except under the European Convention, a similar situation exists under other regional treaties as well."

B.6 Model answer — "Discuss sanctions as a mode for enforcement of international law." 2013 / "Sanctions determine the effectiveness of international law. Comment." 2015 / "Sanctions in international law" (short note) 1999

1. Concept. A sanction is the consequence attached to a breach of an international obligation, intended to induce compliance. Because international society is decentralised, sanctions are plural: some are institutional, some judicial, some horizontal, some purely reputational. Kelsen's premise: the distinguishing feature of law is that it is a coercive order — in a decentralised society the sanction takes the form of self-help, which international law makes lawful only as a countermeasure.

2. The eight institutional sanctions of the syllabus. Treat them in the order set out in the syllabus itself (this is what earns full marks):
① Security Council — Chapter VII: Art. 39 determination, Art. 40 provisional measures, Art. 41 (economic, communications and diplomatic sanctions), Art. 42 (military enforcement); binding under Art. 25 (Namibia 1971); Art. 5/6 suspension and expulsion; Art. 94(2) enforcement of ICJ judgments. Examples: Rhodesia 1966, South Africa 1977, Iraq 1990, Libya 2011, DPRK, 1267/1373/1540. Critique: the veto produces paralysis and selectivity (Syria, Crimea, Ukraine 2022).
② General Assembly — Arts. 10–14 recommendations; Art. 19 loss of vote (South Africa 1986; Haiti; Somalia); Arts. 5 and 6 on SC recommendation; Res. 377(V) Uniting for Peace (Suez 1956; Ukraine 2022, ES-11/1); GA resolutions as evidence of custom (Nuclear Weapons AO 1996, para. 70; Nicaragua 1986).
③ ICJ — binding judgments (Arts. 59, 60); Art. 94(1) and (2); provisional measures binding (LaGrand, 2001); advisory opinions as authoritative pronouncements (Reparation, Namibia, Chagos). Limits: consensual jurisdiction; no enforcement of its own; uneven compliance (Nicaragua; Avena; but note Corfu Channel, Temple of Preah Vihear and Jadhav as compliance successes).
④ Human Rights Council — UPR (Res. 5/1, 2007), Special Procedures, complaint procedure, commissions of inquiry; no enforcement, but reputational and evidentiary effects.
⑤ ITLOS — UNCLOS Art. 296; Seabed Disputes Chamber; prompt release (Art. 292); provisional measures (Art. 290); M/V Saiga (No. 2); Bangladesh/Myanmar (2012); advisory opinion on climate change (2024).
⑥ ICC — Rome Statute Arts. 5, 12–14, 17, 25, 27; SC referrals (Darfur 2005, Libya 2011); arrest warrants (Al-Bashir 2009–10; the 2023 and 2024 warrants); complementarity.
⑦ PCA — 1899/1907 Hague framework; binding awards (Island of Palmas 1928; Bay of Bengal 2014; Enrica Lexie 2020); consent-based, so the "sanction" is consent plus reputation.
⑧ Sanctions under human rights treaties — ICCPR OP1 individual communications; CAT Art. 20 inquiries; CEDAW OP; ECHR Art. 34/46; ACHR and ACHPR — the strongest regional remedy being the European Court's binding judgment supervised by the Committee of Ministers.

3. Horizontal sanctions. Retorsion (unfriendly but lawful: expulsion of diplomats, withdrawal of aid, tariff measures) and countermeasures (ARSIWA 2001 Arts. 22, 49–54): must respond to a prior wrongful act; must be aimed at inducing compliance, not punishment; must be proportionate (Art. 51; Air Services Agreement 1978; Naulilaa 1928); may not involve force or violate fundamental human rights/humanitarian obligations (Art. 50); must be suspended when the wrongful act ceases (Art. 52(3)). Examples: Magnitsky-style targeted measures; US measures after Tehran Hostages; the 2022 sanctions on Russia.

4. Informal sanctions. Adverse public opinion (Rousseau's "strongest fear"); NGO naming and shaming (Amnesty, HRW, Greenpeace); reciprocity; the "law habit"; FATF grey-listing; suspension from sporting and cultural bodies (South Africa, Russia).

5. Evaluation — do sanctions "determine effectiveness"? Yes in part: without Chapter VII, Art. 2(4) would be exhortation; without the ICC, individual responsibility would be a slogan. But no: compliance in most cases precedes and exceeds enforcement; sanctions are politically selective and often counter-productive; and international law functions by consent, reciprocity and internalisation. Conclusion: sanctions are the visible edge of a larger structure of compliance — necessary to the credibility of the law, but neither necessary nor sufficient for its effectiveness.

B.7 Model answer — "Examine the role, functions and powers of the UN Human Rights Council." 2015

1. Origins. ECOSOC established the Commission on Human Rights (CHR) in February 1946 under Art. 68 of the Charter, assisted by the Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. The CHR was criticised for "allowing countries with poor human rights records to be members." The GA replaced it by Res. 60/251 of 15 March 2006, creating the Human Rights Council as a subsidiary organ of the GA, sitting in Geneva.

2. Composition. 47 members elected by the GA by secret ballot, distributed: Africa 13, Asia 13, Eastern Europe 6, Latin America and the Caribbean 8, Western Europe and Other States 7; three-year terms, no more than two consecutive terms; membership suspended by a two-thirds GA vote for gross and systematic violations (Libya, 2011). A candidate's human-rights record is supposed to be considered ("members shall uphold the highest standards").

3. Mandate and functions. (i) Promote universal respect for the protection of all human rights; (ii) address situations of violations, including gross and systematic; (iii) make recommendations to the GA; (iv) promote coordination of human-rights activities in the UN system; (v) work in cooperation and through dialogue; (vi) Universal Periodic Review (UPR) — every UN member State is reviewed every four-and-a-half years (India: 2008, 2012, 2017, 2022); (vii) Special Procedures — Special Rapporteurs, Independent Experts and Working Groups with country or thematic mandates (torture, arbitrary detention, freedom of expression, etc.); (viii) Complaint Procedure (Res. 5/1) for consistent patterns of gross violations; (ix) Advisory Committee — a think-tank; (x) special sessions — a third of members may requisition (used for Syria, DPRK, Myanmar, Ukraine).

4. Powers and limitations. No enforcement power: its outputs are recommendations, resolutions, reports and public findings. Its authority is judicial in method (fact-finding, legal analysis) but political in effect (reputational, agenda-setting, feeding into SC designations and ICC referrals, and into the universal human-rights treaty-body system). The political calibration of members and the practice of "bloc voting" are its standing criticisms; the UPR's value lies in universality — all States, including the powerful, are examined.

5. Conclusion. The Council is the successor to the CHR and the institutional centre of UN human-rights diplomacy. It has converted piecemeal treaty machinery into a universal, periodic accountability mechanism. But because it lacks coercive powers, its influence depends on the accuracy of its findings and the cost of reputational exposure — which is precisely why States contest its agenda.

B.8 Model answer — "Is the individual a subject of international law?" / "Discuss the status of individuals in international law." / Brownlie's view 20152013201220022001199919981995199419931992

1. The question framed. The controversy "whether the individual is a subject or an object of international law" turns on the definition of a subject. The accepted test is the Reparation formula — an entity "capable of possessing international rights and duties, and … the capacity to maintain its rights by bringing international claims." Legal capacity has four constituents: capacity to make claims; treaty-making capacity; capacity to enjoy privileges and immunities; and capacity to bear responsibility.

2. The classical position. Under classical law the individual was an object: the law of nations was "primarily a law of international conduct of States and not of their citizens" (Oppenheim); damage to a national was an injury to his State (Mavrommatis, 1924 — "by taking up the case of one of its subjects … a State is in reality asserting its own rights"); and only States may be parties before the ICJ (Art. 34(1) Statute).

3. The transformation — the sequence to reproduce. 1919 Treaty of Versailles (Mixed Arbitral Tribunals, ILO, minorities treaties) → 1945–46 Nuremberg and Tokyo Charters (individual criminal responsibility; "crimes are committed by men, not by abstract entities") → 1948 UDHR and Genocide Convention (Art. IV punishes rulers, public officials and private individuals) → 1950 ECHR Art. 34 (individual applications to the European Court) → 1963 VCCR Art. 36 (consular notification as an individual right) → 1966 ICCPR and the First Optional Protocol (individual petitions) → 1965 ICSID Convention (individuals and corporations may sue States directly) → 1979 CEDAW; 1984 CAT → 1993 ICTY; 1994 ICTR → 1998 Rome Statute.

4. Rights. The consular trilogy demonstrates the modern approach: LaGrand (ICJ Rep. 2001, p. 466) — Art. 36(1) VCCR "creates individual rights," enforceable by the national State before the ICJ; Avena (2004) — the obligation of review and reconsideration; Jadhav (2019) — Pakistan's breach and the requirement of effective review and reconsideration for an Indian national. Add the individual's ability to petition the Human Rights Committee, the Committee against Torture, the CEDAW Committee and the Inter-American and African Commissions, and the individual's standing before the European Court of Human Rights.

5. Duties. Piracy (long a crime under customary law — UNCLOS Arts. 100–107); slavery; war crimes; the Nuremberg Principles (ILC, 1950, Principle I: "Any person who commits an act which constitutes a crime under international law is responsible therefor and liable to punishment"); Genocide Convention Art. IV; the Rome Statute Arts. 25 (individual criminal responsibility), 27 (irrelevance of official capacity), 28 (superior responsibility). The limit: Arrest Warrant (DRC v. Belgium), ICJ Rep. 2002 — an incumbent Head of State/Government/Foreign Minister enjoys immunity before foreign municipal courts, but not before international criminal tribunals.

6. The balance — Brownlie's formulation. "There is no general rule that the individual cannot be a 'subject of international law' and in particular contexts he appears as a legal person on the international plane." So too the DU material: "Ordinarily international law deals with the rights and duties of the States … This does not however mean that other entities or individuals are outside the scope of international law." But note the limits: no general treaty-making power; no standing before the ICJ; rights ordinarily available only where a treaty confers them; nationality still the principal connecting factor; and enforcement still requires the State.

7. Conclusion. The individual is a subject of international law in specific contexts — a partial, derivative, functional subject. The classical dichotomy (States = subjects; individuals = objects) has been replaced by a graduated scale. As the final point of the answer: "the future of international law is one with the future of international organisation" and "the individual is the ultimate unit of all law" are not competing slogans but the two ends of the same spectrum.

B.9 Model answer — "Does the UN possess a legal personality in international law? Discuss with special reference to the Reparation for Injuries Case (1949)." 2015 / "Reparations Case has confirmed the opinion that international organizations are subjects of international law. Critically analyse the judgment and the grounds upon which international organizations have been held so." 2000 / "International Organisations as a subject of international law" (short note) 20011998

1. The problem. The UN Charter never uses the words "international legal personality." Yet no one doubts the UN may conclude treaties, sue and be sued, and claim reparation. The basis of that capacity had to be judicially constructed — and the construction is the Reparation opinion.

2. The facts and questions (GA Res. 258(III); Bernadotte's assassination, 17 September 1948; Questions I(a), I(b) and II — full text in §Case 1 above).

3. The reasoning. (i) subjects of law are not identical in nature or extent of rights — "their nature depends upon the needs of the community"; (ii) the Charter's silence is not decisive — the Court looked at the Organisation's characteristics: a centre with organs and tasks; Art. 2(5) obligations of Members; the GA's power of recommendation; Arts. 104–105 capacity, privileges and immunities; the power to conclude agreements; practice under the 1946 Convention on Privileges and Immunities (s. 35); (iii) hence "the Organization is an international person" — but "that is not the same thing as saying that it is a State … Still less … that it is a super-State"; (iv) Question I(a): affirmative, unanimously; (v) Question I(b): affirmative, 11 to 4 — the traditional rule of nationality of claims "applies to claims brought by a State," and the Organisation asserts its own right, not the agent's; (vi) non-Member States: the 50 founder States had the power to create an entity with objective international personality; (vii) Question II: no rule of priority between diplomatic and functional protection; the nationality of the agent, even that of the defendant State, is irrelevant.

4. The principles. Implied powers; functional necessity; relative personality; objective personality; functional protection; reparation as a general principle (Factory at Chorzów).

5. Critical analysis (this is where marks are won). (i) The opinion is a piece of creative judicial statecraft: functional necessity can be a slippery test — which necessities count? (ii) The Court's caution (not a State, not a super-State) was politically necessary to reassure States; Judge Winiarski's declaration recorded the anxiety. (iii) The limits were later made explicit in the WHO Nuclear Weapons opinion (1996): an implied power must serve the organisation's own constitutional functions. (iv) The ILC's 2011 Draft Articles on the Responsibility of International Organizations now mirror the State-responsibility Articles, but no convention has been adopted for organisations. (v) The practical result: organisations can act, claim, and be claimed against — but their personality remains derivative and functional.

6. Conclusion. The UN possesses international legal personality: not because the Charter says so, but because its functions make it indispensable. The opinion is the charter of the modern law of international organisations.

B.10 Model answer — "A subject of law possesses a distinct personality … What are the different constituents of legal capacity under international law? Whom out of the various entities possessing this capacity do you prefer most?" 1999 / "Describe the tests by which an entity can claim personality under international law." 2002 / "As a pre-requisite to be a subject of international law, an entity should be endowed with the procedural capacity to sue and be sued … can the following be considered subjects: (a) inter-governmental organisation; (b) individual?" 1998

Part A — constituents of legal capacity. ① Capacity to bring international claims (procedural capacity, locus standi); ② treaty-making capacity; ③ capacity to enjoy privileges and immunities; ④ capacity to bear obligations and responsibility; and, in modern writing, ⑤ capacity to espouse claims on behalf of others (functional protection); ⑥ capacity to be a party before international tribunals. Add the structural point from Reparation: capacity is relative — "the rights and duties of an entity such as the Organization must depend upon its purposes and functions."

Part B — apply to the two candidates. Inter-governmental organisations: they have all the capacities listed, in functional measure (Reparation; the 1986 Vienna Convention; the 1946 General Convention on Privileges and Immunities; the ILC 2011 Articles) but cannot be parties before the ICJ in contentious cases (Art. 34(1)) and do not hold sovereignty or territory. Individuals: they have no treaty-making capacity and no ICJ standing, but they do have claim-making capacity under ICCPR OP1, ECHR Art. 34, VCCR Art. 36 (as applied in LaGrand, Avena, Jadhav), plus direct rights under investment treaties (ICSID 1965), and obligations under the Nuremberg Principles, the Genocide Convention and the Rome Statute.

Part C — "Whom do you prefer most?" (a deliberately open question — you must choose and defend). The best-marked answer argues for the State as the primary subject — because it alone possesses plenary personality, is the principal law-maker (custom and treaty), provides the enforcement mechanism, and remains the vehicle through which most other entities (organisations, corporations, individuals) obtain their international standing. Then concede that this hierarchy is changing: the most dynamic development of the last seventy years has been the emergence of the individual and the organisation. Choose the State, but explain that personality is a spectrum, not a monopoly.
