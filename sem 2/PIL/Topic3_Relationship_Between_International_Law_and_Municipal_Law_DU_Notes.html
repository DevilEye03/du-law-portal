1. Learn one sentence

If you remember nothing else from this topic, remember this: the theories (monism and dualism) describe how the two systems are related; the practice of States is what actually decides whether a rule of international law will be applied in a national court. The syllabus asks for both, and the examiner is testing whether you can move from Kelsen to Berubari in one answer.

2. Learn the Indian skeleton

Custom: applied by Indian courts unless it conflicts with statute (incorporation, qualified). Treaties: binding on India internationally the moment the executive concludes them (Art. 53 read with Art. 73 and Entry 14, List I); enforceable in Indian courts only when legislated into domestic law (Art. 253), or read in as an aid to interpretation where there is no conflict and a gap (Vishaka, Vellore, Puttaswamy). Cession of territory: needs a constitutional amendment (Art. 368) — Berubari; but a boundary settlement or a servitude does not — Ram Kishore Sen, Sukumar Sengupta.

3. Learn the eight cases by cluster

Cession/boundary: Berubari, Ram Kishore Sen, Sukumar Sengupta. Treaty versus statute: Jolly George, Gramophone. Custom as part of the law of the land: Vellore. Treaties as an aid to interpretation: Vishaka, Puttaswamy. Every PYQ on this topic is answered by one or more of these clusters.

3.0The Problem Stated

A. Why this topic matters — and why it is nine hours of the syllabus

International law is made between States; but nearly everything it requires has to be done inside States — by legislatures, executives, police officers and courts. A treaty prohibiting torture is worth nothing unless a national judge can refuse to admit an extract obtained by torture. An arbitral award on a boundary is worth nothing until a municipal court accepts that the border has moved. It follows that every legal system has to answer a prior question: when, how, and to what extent does international law operate within the domestic legal order? That question is this topic.

Three facts explain the difficulty. First, there is no world legislature and no compulsory world court with general jurisdiction: international law has no machinery of its own to compel municipal application, so it must borrow the machinery of States. Secondly, the constitutions of the world take different views: some declare international law binding law of the land (the German Basic Law, Article 25), some subject treaties to ordinary legislation (the United Kingdom), some make treaties supreme law upon ratification (the United States), and one — India — says something at once directive (Article 51) and enabling (Article 253) without ever saying that international law is part of the law of the land. Thirdly, the two systems collide in ordinary litigation, not in the abstract: in a Calcutta execution petition (Jolly George), in a customs seizure of pirated cassettes (Gramophone), in a tannery-pollution PIL (Vellore), in a gang-rape writ petition (Vishaka), in a constitutional challenge to Aadhaar (Puttaswamy), and in a challenge to the transfer of Berubari (Berubari, Ram Kishore Sen).

The single most important framework. Verma opens the chapter with the sentence that supplies the whole structure of this topic: “Very often, municipal courts have to face situations calling for the application of the rules of international law... Similarly, international tribunals may be called upon to determine the precise status and effect of a rule of municipal law... There may also be situations which require theoretical analysis and determination of the limits between international law and municipal law.” Note the three directions in which the question runs: (i) international law in municipal courts; (ii) municipal law before international tribunals; (iii) the theory of the relationship. The syllabus gives you (iii) as §3.1 and (i) as §3.2, and you should always add (ii) — it is the part of the answer most candidates forget.

C. The two questions — do not confuse them

Examiners award marks for separating the two questions that run through this topic. Candidates who conflate them write vague answers; candidates who separate them can use the same material for either question.

  1. The question of APPLICATION (or reception): Is a rule of international law — a treaty rule or a customary rule — directly applicable in the courts of a State, of its own force (ex proprio vigore), or must it first be transformed into municipal law by legislation? This is the monist–dualist question in its practical form. It is answered by the constitution and practice of each State.
  2. The question of SUPREMACY (or primacy): Assuming the rule is applicable, and assuming that it conflicts with a statute, a precedent or the constitution of the State, which prevails? This is the question of hierarchy. It has three sub-answers in practice — international law prevails, municipal law prevails, or the two are reconciled by interpretation (the “harmonisation” principle).

E. Five practical stakes — what is actually at risk

  • Rights and remedies of individuals. Whether an individual can invoke a treaty right at all: contrast Jolly George (the Covenant could not itself be enforced, but it was used to mould CPC section 51) with LaGrand (the ICJ held that Art. 36 VCCR creates individual rights) and Medellín (yet not enforceable in Texas).
  • The constitutional power to cede and to acquire territory. In India this turned into constitutional litigation four times: Berubari (cession needs Art. 368), Ram Kishore Sen (restoring de jure Pakistan's territory is not cession), Maganbhai (a boundary award under an arbitral tribunal is self-executing), Sukumar Sengupta (a perpetual lease/servitude is not cession).
  • The validity of executive action. Can the Union executive bind India by a treaty without Parliament? In India, yes as to the international obligation (Manmull Jain, Nirmal Bose) — but the obligation may be unenforceable inside India until legislated (Menon, Birma, Shiv Kumar Sharma).
  • The reach of human-rights protection. The interpretive turn from ADM Jabalpur (1976: no relief because the Constitution said nothing) to Vishaka (1997) and Puttaswamy (2017: constitutional provisions “must be read and interpreted in a manner which would enhance their conformity with the global human rights regime”) is a shift of the first importance, and every one of those milestones is a Topic-3 case.
  • India's standing and responsibility internationally. If Indian law prevails internally, India still remains internationally responsible: Article 27 VCLT forbids pleading internal law to justify breach of a treaty; ARSIWA Article 3 says the characterisation of an act as wrongful “is not affected by its characterization as lawful by internal law”. The municipal court's judgment does not discharge the State's obligation — it moves the burden to the State to pay compensation or change its law.

Opening paragraph you can use in any answer on this topic. “International law and municipal law are two systems which are formally distinct but functionally interlocked. The theories — monism and dualism — describe the formal relationship; the practice of States determines the practical one. The essential questions are two: whether a rule of international law applies within a State of its own force or only after transformation into municipal law; and, if it applies, which prevails when it conflicts with a statute or the constitution. Every State answers these questions by its constitution, its legislation and its courts; no State answers them consistently by a single theory. As the prescribed text concludes, the controversy between monism and dualism is ‘more academic than real’, and the practice of States reveals ‘a mixture of international law supremacy, municipal law supremacy and coordination of legal systems’. The Indian position — custom received, treaties legislated, the Constitution supreme — illustrates that mixture precisely.”

3.1Theories of the Relationship

A. Why theory at all?

Because the two questions of §3.0 — application and supremacy — cannot be answered by looking at one country at a time. A lawyer who wants to argue that a treaty binds a national court, or that a custom overrides a statute, needs a general account of why international law should have effect inside a State at all. Two such accounts have dominated the literature for a century. They are not merely descriptive: each carries a political programme. Dualism was the theory of the sovereign State in the age of positivism; monism was the theory of the “unity of law” in the age of the League and the United Nations. As the DU classroom notes put it, “of all these theories the most popular are ‘Monism’ and ‘Dualism’ and they are diametrically opposed to each other” — and to those two the prescribed text adds three further theories (transformation, specific adoption, delegation) that explain how international law is applied within a municipal system.

B. Monistic Theory (syllabus 3.1.1)

1. The core proposition

“The theory of monism regards that both international law and municipal law have a common underlying legal basis and [it] derives its origin from the law of nature which binds equally the States and individuals. Thus, they are intrinsically the same and form part of that science of law which binds all human beings alike.” Both systems, in the end, “regulate the conduct of individuals, one immediately and the other mediately, though in the sphere of international law, the consequences of such conduct are attributed to the State” (Verma). The monist therefore regards law as “a single unified field of knowledge, consisting of rules, whether binding on States, individuals, or on entities other than States”, and holds that “the decisive point is whether or not international law is true law. Once it is accepted as true law, then there is no intrinsic difference between the two.”

2. The exponents and their arguments

  • Kelsen (the chief exponent; the “pure theory of law”). Law is a hierarchy of norms; each norm derives its validity from a higher norm, and the whole chain from a basic postulate, the Grundnorm. Since both international and municipal norms can be traced to a single hierarchy, the two systems form one legal order. Kelsen's philosophical premise, in Verma's words, is that “the behaviour of States is reducible to the behaviour of individuals representing the State”, so that “the alleged difference in subject matter between the two systems cannot be considered a difference”.
  • Lauterpacht — the most radical monist in practice: because the individual is the ultimate unit of all law, international law and municipal law are but two aspects of one system, in which international law is ultimately supreme; he used the theory to argue for the direct protection of human rights by international law.
  • Fitzmaurice and Starke — monists of a more modest kind: they accept that the two systems have their own sphere of operation, but deny that they are ever in a true conflict; where they appear to clash, the correct analysis is that one system has simply not yet been modified. Starke's federal analogy (Verma) is the clearest statement: there is an “international constitutional law” that conditions both State law and international law, much as a federal constitution conditions both provincial law and federal statutes.
  • Duguit and Wright (and, in the classroom notes, Wright alongside Kelsen and Duguit) — the sociological and philosophical variants: law is a social fact, and there is no reason why the fact of international solidarity should produce a different species of law from the fact of national solidarity.

3. The consequences monists draw

  1. No transformation is needed. International law is applicable within the State of its own force; the constitution of a State merely prescribes the machinery for giving effect to obligations, not the validity of the obligations themselves. This is the seed of the delegation theory (§3.1 F).
  2. Primacy of international law (for most, though not all, monists): if there is one system with international law at its apex, then an inconsistent statute is not “law” at the higher level — the State remains responsible for the breach.
  3. No non liquet in the domestic sphere either: a court may not refuse relief merely because the domestic statute is silent if international law supplies the rule.

4. The criticism (be accurate — the criticism is not that monism is illogical)

Verma's criticism is empirical, and the classroom notes agree: “in actual practice States do not follow this theory. They contend that Municipal Law and International Law are two separate systems of law. Further, each State is sovereign and as such is not bound by international law. States follow international law simply because they give their consent to be bound...” To this must be added the doctrinal objection: monism proves too much. If one accepts that State conduct is ultimately individual conduct, one has explained why both systems are “law” — but not why a judge sworn to the constitution of her State should apply an international rule in preference to a statute. The impossibility of deriving a rule of priority from the mere unity of the science of law is exactly the weakness Kelsen's own account displays (§3.1 E below).

C. Dualistic Theory (syllabus 3.1.2)

1. The core proposition

“Under the dualist theory, international law and municipal law operate on different levels. While international law regulates mainly the relations and obligations between sovereign and independent States, the municipal law operates and regulates the relations and obligations of individuals within a State” (Verma). The two are “two entirely distinct and self-contained legal systems to the extent to which the rules of one are not expressly received in the other.” In the classroom formula: “Dualism is based on the complete sovereignty of States”, its conception of the State-will being borrowed from Hegel.

2. Triepel's three differences (learn these)

Anzilotti's refinement. Anzilotti sought to explain the difference in terms of the fundamental postulate of each system. The fundamental principle of municipal law is that the law must be obeyed; the fundamental principle of international law is pacta sunt servanda — agreements are to be observed in good faith. The two systems are therefore distinct by construction, though each contains cross-references to the other (for example, by referring questions of nationality or of contract to municipal law). Verma records the Fitzmaurice caveat that Anzilotti has, for this reason, been “wrongly labelled” a pure dualist — a note worth including because examiners enjoy it.

3. The dualist conclusion

Because the systems and their postulates differ, an international rule cannot enter the municipal legal order except through the will of the State — that is, through legislation or judicial adoption. Hence the transformation theory, and hence the conclusion that in a conflict municipal law prevails internally (the State being internationally responsible for the consequences). Practical dualism is the constitutional doctrine of States that require statutes to give treaties internal effect: the United Kingdom before the Human Rights Act 1998 debates, India under Article 253, and (for non-self-executing treaties) the United States.

4. The criticism

  • The “subjects” objection: “It is not correct to contend that international law is binding only on States. In the modern period, international law is applicable to States, individuals and certain other non-State entities” — a point Verma also makes (individuals may be subjects of obligations and, in human-rights law, of rights; cf. Reparation, Topic 1).
  • The “will” objection: “the conception of State-will as the source of State law is incorrect. In fact State will is nothing but the will of the people who compose it”; and the “common will” of States is “totally misleading” — for it can create binding force only if we already know when a common will is decisive, and it cannot explain rules binding States against their will (Verma).
  • The customary-law objection: Triepel's theory, if taken strictly, dissolves general international law into a mass of tacit particular agreements — “a view that is at variance with reality”. Pacta sunt servanda alone “does not explain the binding force of customary rules of international law to which the States have not given their consent”.
  • The empirical objection: if dualism were literally true, a State's constitution could not reach its international obligations at all; but the case-law of this topic shows the reach of national constitutions over treaties and the reach of international law over national law — the interaction is constant.

E. The Question of Primacy — and Kelsen's difficulty

Both theories have to answer: which of the two prevails when they conflict? The syllabus does not name this head, but every case in §3.2 is an exercise in it, and it is examined.

  • Dualists give primacy to municipal law. “Since dualists attach significance to the sovereignty of the ‘State will’, they ascribe primacy to municipal law over international law. The basis of their view is that the State is independent, it enjoys the widest liberties and exercises almost complete sovereignty” (Verma). The practical translation: a national court applies the statute, and the State bears the international consequences.
  • Monists are divided — and this is the surprise in the prescribed text. “Some of them ascribe primacy to international law. But Kelsen... maintains that, in accordance with his hierarchical or Grundnorm doctrine, each rule is conditioned by a superior rule for its validity and thus, in turn, it derives validity from the fundamental postulate, i.e., the Grundnorm which might belong either to international law or State law. But without taking his thesis to its logical conclusion, he has ascribed primacy to State law because, in his opinion, the choice between them could not be made in a strictly scientific way.”
  • Why Kelsen's choice fails. Verma gives two reasons, both quotable: (i) to give primacy to State law “would tantamount to according primacy to legal systems of almost all the independent nations of the world with its attendant confusion and anarchy”; (ii) if international law derived its validity from State law, then “with the disappearance of State law (constitution), the rules of international law should also disappear. But State practice has invariably established that international law operates independent of internal changes or revolution in a State, or repeal or abolition of its constitution.” Here the Topic-3 case-law is the proof: Jolly George, Vishaka and Puttaswamy all invoke obligations India assumed years earlier, unaffected by changes in governments, statutes or constitutional amendments.
  • Starke's answer: “According to Starke, a monist, primacy lies with international law... there is thus an international constitutional law which conditions both State law and international law, just like the constitutional instrument in a federal State conditions both provincial law and federal statutes.” Verma's reply is the examiner's gift: this view “also begs certain questions: Has international law attained the maturity of a federal constitution? What are the contents of this constitution? What is the authority and procedure to determine the contents of such a constitution and resolve the disputes?”
  • The settled conclusion. “In fact, international law and municipal law are not comparable since both have their own sphere of operation and neither can be termed as subordinate to the other. The supremacy of international law in the international sphere is unchallenged in the same way as [that] of municipal law in the State matters... The supremacy of international law in [the] municipal sphere simply requires that if a State is in breach of its international obligations for which it is internationally responsible, it cannot shelter itself behind domestic law by way of absolution. International law simply does not purport to govern the contents of national law in the national sphere.” Learn this as the closing paragraph of any answer on primacy.

F. Theories of Application — Transformation (Specific Adoption) and Delegation

Verma distinguishes the theoretical question (what is the relationship?) from the practical question (how does international law get applied inside a municipality?). “Germinating from the above two theories... two theories have been propounded: (1) ‘Transformation’ or ‘Specific Adoption’ Theory, and (2) Delegation Theory. These theories are, in fact, the manifestation of the theories of dualism and monism” respectively.

1. Transformation / Specific Adoption Theory (dualist)

“According to positivists, the municipal law is logically a complete system. The rules of international law cannot impinge upon State law unless they undergo the process of transformation and be specifically adopted by, or incorporated into, the State law, there being no automatic application... The national legislature may ‘transform’ it into a rule of national law, which will be applied by the courts as a rule of national or municipal law.” The positivist image: “treaties are promises and municipal statutes are commands”; therefore “to make treaties binding municipally, their transformation into statute is formally and substantively necessary.”

Criticism (monist): the distinction between treaties and statutes “is merely of form and procedure, but the real object of both is the same, i.e., to stipulate certain determinate legal consequences for certain situations, and in this sense treaties are commands in the same way as are statutes. Hence, it is not correct to say that every treaty needs transformation to be binding internally.”

2. Delegation Theory (monist)

“The critics of the transformation theory, who favour monism, maintain that there is no need for international obligations to be transformed into rules of national law, and in case of any apparent conflict between the two, the international rule prevails. The application of rules of international law to the municipal sphere is a continuation of one single process which starts with the creation and acceptance of [the] international law rule by the State. The constitutional rules of international law delegate to each State constitution the right to determine the procedure and manner to make treaties effective at the municipal level.”

Its own limitation, stated by Verma: “the fact that national organs do not behave according to such rules indicates the weakness of international law, but does not invalidate the theory, since the State will incur international responsibility where it permits violations of international legal rules to occur.”

G. Which theory is nearer to reality? — the answer that earns full marks

The question is asked in different forms in 1992 ("stating clearly which is nearer to reality"), 1993 (state practices), 2002 ("an uneasy compromise between Monism and Dualism") and 2015 ("which theory is supported by their practice?"). The best answer has four steps.

  1. State the test, not the label. A State is not monist or dualist in the abstract; its practice is different for (i) customary law, (ii) treaties, and (iii) constitutional norms. Even within one State the answer differs by category — India receives custom but legislates treaties; the United States makes treaties supreme law on paper but subjects them to self-execution and later-in-time doctrines on the ground.
  2. Show that both theories fail as descriptions. Monism fails because States do not in fact treat international law as overriding their statutes (Mortensen v. Peters; Maclaine Watson; Jolly George; Gramophone). Dualism fails because States do not in fact treat international law as irrelevant inside their borders — custom is applied without legislation (Paquete Habana; Trendtex; Vellore), and constitutions like Germany's and the Netherlands' expressly receive it.
  3. Explain the practice by the constitutional text plus judicial technique. What decides the outcome in a real case is (a) the constitutional rule on reception, (b) the presence or absence of a clear statute, and (c) the canon of harmonious construction — the presumption that the legislature did not intend to breach international law. That is why the same formal position (say, dualism in the UK, India and Australia) produces different results: the technique of interpretation does the work. Vishaka is the Indian proof: the treaty was not legislated, yet it supplied the content of Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21; and Vellore is the customary twin.
  4. Conclude with the prescribed text. “Practice of international tribunals and municipal courts suggests that it is a mixture of international law supremacy, municipal law supremacy and coordination of legal systems that exists. It is not correct to compartmentalise States into ‘monist’ or ‘dualist’ groups, since there are as many ways of giving effect to international law as there are national legal systems. The constitution of a nation is the starting point... Hence, the practice varies among nations.” Add, as the practical conclusion: in the event of an irreconcilable conflict, the State is internationally responsible — Article 27 VCLT, Article 3 ARSIWA — so that dualism “wins” the municipal case and loses the international one.

Model skeleton — “State the various theories regarding the relationship between international law and municipal law. Discuss them with the help of State practice, stating clearly which is nearer to reality” (1992 Q.4; cf. 1993, 1994, 2002, 2015). (1) Two questions: application and supremacy (§3.0 C). (2) Monism — Kelsen, Lauterpacht, Fitzmaurice/Starke; consequences; criticism. (3) Dualism — Triepel's three differences (Table 3.4); Anzilotti's postulates; consequences; criticism. (4) The two theories of application — transformation/specific adoption and delegation (Fig. 3.3). (5) Primacy — dualists to municipal law; Kelsen's reluctant choice for State law and why it fails; Starke's international constitutional law and Verma's objections; the settled conclusion. (6) Practice — UK (custom received; treaties legislated), US (custom federal; treaties supreme but self-execution and later-in-time), India (custom received; treaties legislated; Constitution supreme), Germany/Netherlands (constitutional reception). (7) Which is nearer reality — neither, as a universal; the answer is “a mixture”, and the operative technique is harmonious construction. (8) Conclusion — the State's international responsibility survives whatever its courts do.

Verma adds Germany, France, the Netherlands, Greece and Russia, and those are summarised in §3.2 C for completeness, because a comparative sentence or two lifts an answer from adequate to distinguished.

What the examiner wants in this head. For each country, four things, in this order: (1) the constitutional starting point; (2) the position of customary international law; (3) the position of treaties; and (4) two or three decided cases showing the position in action. A bare recital of the incorporation doctrine without cases will not do, and a recital of cases without the constitutional starting point will not either.

A. The United Kingdom

1. The constitutional starting point

The United Kingdom has no written constitution. Parliamentary sovereignty means that Parliament “enjoys the supremacy in making or changing the law of the United Kingdom”, and “the conclusion and termination of treaties, and the conduct of foreign affairs, is the prerogative of the Crown” — that is, of the government, which is accountable to Parliament politically rather than legally (Verma). Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions: because the courts are creatures of the common law, they can receive customary international law without legislation; but because Parliament is sovereign, a treaty cannot change English law without an Act of Parliament. Hence the famous asymmetry, to be memorised as the answer to "the British position":

Custom is received automatically; treaties are not. The first is expressed by the incorporation doctrine (Blackstone); the second by the proposition that a treaty is res inter alios acta so far as individuals are concerned.

2. Customary international law — from incorporation to transformation and back

(a) Blackstone's doctrine. Sir William Blackstone wrote that “the law of nations... is here adopted in its full extent by the common law, and is held to be part of the law of the land” (Commentaries, Book IV, ch. 5). The doctrine was applied in the eighteenth century (Buvot v. Barbuit (1737); Triquet v. Bath (1764), where Lord Mansfield said “the law of nations, in its full extent, was part of the law of England”; Heathfield v. Chilton (1767)) and reaffirmed in Emperor of Austria v. Day and Kossuth (1861). This is the incorporation doctrine, and it is put to Indian use through Article 372(1) of the Constitution (§3.3 D).

(b) The retreat: R. v. Keyn (The Franconia), (1876) 2 Ex. D. 63. A German ship, the Franconia, collided with a British vessel less than three miles from the English coast; the British vessel sank and a life was lost; the German master was charged with manslaughter. The question was whether the English court had jurisdiction over an incident in the maritime belt. The court declined to apply the customary rule and held that English courts had no jurisdiction over crimes committed by foreigners within the three-mile belt “although such a right existed under customary international law”; the rule was “not applicable ex proprio vigore, but it was for the Parliament to legislate”. Parliament then passed the Territorial Waters Jurisdiction Act, 1878, reversing the decision. Keyn is the high-water mark of British transformation, and it is the English ancestor of the Indian debate in Jolly George and Gramophone.

(c) The partial return: West Rand Central Gold Mining Co. v. R. (1905) 2 KB 391. The court held that “whatever has received the common consent of civilised nations must have received the assent of our country... and as such will be acknowledged and applied by our municipal tribunals”, but added the crucial qualification that the expression “the law of nations forms part of the law of England” ought not to be construed “so as to include... [rules] as to which there is no evidence that Great Britain has ever assented, and a fortiori if they are contrary to the principles of her laws as declared by her courts”. (The facts in the classroom version of the case: gold seized by Boer officials in South Africa, later in British hands, claim for restitution; the Crown's declaration that it would not honour the predecessor's commitments prevailed — “municipal law prevailed”.)

(d) The qualifications: Chung Chi Cheung v. The King (1939) AC 160 (PC). A cabin-boy on a Chinese vessel shot and killed the captain while the vessel was in Hong Kong territorial waters; the Privy Council affirmed the colonial court's jurisdiction. Lord Atkin supplied the formula that Indian judges later borrowed: “so far as the courts of this country are concerned, international law has no validity save in so far as its principles are accepted and adopted by our domestic law... On any judicial issue they seek to ascertain what the relevant rule is and, having found it, they will treat it as incorporated into the domestic law, so far as it is not inconsistent with rules enacted by statutes or finally declared by their tribunals.”

(e) The modern position. The incorporation doctrine “does not find a blanket application”. In Thakrar v. Secretary of State for the Home Office (1974) QB 684 (CA) Lord Denning followed transformation; in Trendtex Trading Corporation v. Central Bank of Nigeria (1977) QB 529 (CA) he returned to incorporation, and Shaw L.J. explained why stare decisis does not freeze the content of international law: “What is immutable is the principle of English law that the law of nations (not what was the law of nations) must be applied in the courts of England. The rule of stare decisis... does not prevent a court from applying a rule which did not exist when the earlier decision was made if the new rule has had the effect in international law of extinguishing the old rule.” Incorporation was followed again in J.H. Rayner (Mincing Lane) Ltd. v. Department of Trade and Industry (1990) 2 AC 418 at 500; and in R. v. Jones (Margaret) [2007] 1 AC 136 the House of Lords held that even a crime recognised in customary international law (“the crime of aggression” as argued) is not thereby automatically a crime triable by English courts. Likewise R. v. Bow Street Metropolitan Stipendiary Magistrate, ex p. Pinochet Ugarte (No. 3) [2000] 1 AC 147: torture became an international crime, but English criminal courts acquired extra-territorial jurisdiction only on the passing of section 134 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988.

(iii) Prize courts: though municipal, they administer the international law of prize and are not bound by an executive order in Council that conflicts with it — The Zamora (1916) 2 AC 77 (a Swedish vessel carrying copper from New York to Stockholm, stopped in 1915 and referred to the Prize Court; the Privy Council held that the Prize Court must apply the customary and conventional international law of prize even against the Order in Council, though it would be bound by a British statute).

Direct effect and the individual: Bennett v. Horseferry Magistrates' Court (1993) 3 All ER 138. The accused had been abducted from South Africa with the collusion of the police; the House of Lords declined to exercise criminal jurisdiction, holding that where the law-enforcement agency bringing a prosecution has been enabled to do so only by participating in violations of international law and of the laws of another State, the court should take cognizance of that fact. This is the English ancestor of the doctrine of male captus, bene detentus-rejected — an example of a customary rule operating to protect an individual against the executive.

3. Treaties — the dualist rule and its five exceptions

The making of a treaty (negotiation, signature, ratification) is a prerogative act of the Crown. A treaty duly ratified binds the United Kingdom internationally, but “to have internal effect, in the sense of changing the existing law or to be a source of rights or obligations in domestic law, the treaty requires enabling legislation by Parliament”. The leading statements:

  • The Parlement Belge (1879–80) 5 PD 197 — the first-instance holding (unaffected on this point by the reversal on appeal) that the Crown cannot, by entering into a treaty, alter the law of England: the Belgian mail steamer sued for collision with the steam-tug Daring could not claim immunity under the unincorporated Anglo-Belgian Convention of 1876, because “a treaty cannot affect private rights unless it has been made part of British law by an Act of Parliament”. (The Court of Appeal reversed on the ground that the immunity was available in customary international law — a nice illustration of the difference between the two channels.)
  • Attorney-General for Canada v. Attorney-General for Ontario (1937) AC 326 (PC) — “the making of a treaty is an executive act, while the performance of its obligations, if they entail alteration of the existing domestic law, requires legislative action... Once the [obligations] are created, while they bind the State as against the other contracting parties, Parliament may refuse to perform them and so leave the State in default.” This Privy Council formulation is treated by Verma as “reflective of the Indian position”.
  • Maclaine Watson & Co. Ltd. v. Department of Trade and Industry (1988) 3 WLR 1033 (HL) — “Treaties... are not self-executing. [A] treaty is not part of English law unless and until it has been incorporated into the law by legislation. So far as individuals are concerned, it is res inter alios acta from which they cannot derive rights and by which they cannot be deprived of rights or subjected to obligations.”
  • J.H. Rayner (Mincing Lane) Ltd. v. Department of Trade and Industry (1990) 2 AC 418 (HL) (Lord Templeman) — “Except to the extent that a treaty becomes incorporated into the laws of the United Kingdom by statute, the courts of the United Kingdom have no power to enforce treaty rights and obligations at the behest of a sovereign government or at the behest of a private individual.”

Conflict, interpretation and the European Convention. Where a treaty has been legislated, it will prevail over an earlier conflicting statute (Ostime v. Australian Mutual Provident Society (1959) 3 All ER 245). Where it has not been legislated, it may still be referred to for the interpretation of a conflicting statute, but cannot prevail over a clearly worded statute that contradicts it: R. v. Secretary of State for the Home Department, ex p. Brind (1991) 2 WLR 588 (HL), where the House of Lords held that Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights (not then embodied in a statute) did not have the effect of law in England, but had “persuasive force in resolving ambiguities”. Where an Act is enacted in Parliament's own language to give effect to a treaty and is ambiguous, the treaty may be referred to so as to comply with its terms, because there is a prima facie presumption that Parliament does not intend to act in breach of its treaty obligations and of international law — Salomon v. Commissioners of Customs and Excise (1967) 2 QB 116 (CA) (customs valuation under the 1950 Convention). The Human Rights Act 1998 has since made the European Convention rights actionable domestically (section 2 requires courts to take into account the jurisprudence of the Strasbourg organs; section 3 requires legislation to be read compatibly so far as possible; section 4 provides for declarations of incompatibility) — the classic modern example of transformation by statute. Post-Brexit, the European Union (Withdrawal) Act 2018 and the Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023 illustrate the same principle from the other side: what Parliament gave, Parliament can take away — a point that disposes of any suggestion that the United Kingdom has become monist.

B. The United States of America

1. The constitutional starting point

Article II, section 2: the President makes treaties “by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, provided two-thirds of the Senators present concur”. Article VI, clause 2 (the Supremacy Clause): “all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, anything in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the contrary notwithstanding.” Treaties thus rank with federal statutes — above State law, below the Constitution. From this text flow six doctrines that together constitute the American practice: (i) custom is part of federal law; (ii) treaties are supreme law but only “made under the authority of the United States” — that is, self-executing ones; (iii) later-in-time repeal as between treaty and statute; (iv) the presumption against congressional override of treaty obligations; (v) the Constitution prevails over all; and (vi) executive agreements have much the same internal force as treaties, without Senate consent.

2. Customary international law — The Paquete Habana

The locus classicus is The Paquete Habana, 175 U.S. 677 (1900). Two small fishing boats, Lola and Paquete Habana, were seized as prize of war during the Spanish-American war while fishing off Havana. The Supreme Court, per Gray J., exempted them from capture by applying the customary rule exempting coastal fishing vessels, and laid down the American doctrine in words that every Indian student should be able to contrast with Gramophone:

“International law is part of our law, and must be ascertained and administered by the Courts of Justice of appropriate jurisdiction, as often as questions of right depending upon it are duly presented for their determination. For this purpose, where there is no treaty and no controlling executive or legislative act or judicial decision, resort must be had to the customs and usages of civilized nations...”
— 175 U.S. 677, 700 (1900)

Note the three exceptions built into the very sentence — no treaty, no controlling executive or legislative act, no judicial decision. That is why the American rule, though it sounds monist, is only a presumption in favour of applying custom, defeasible by the political branches. The modern refinements:

  • Custom is federal law and its determination by the federal courts binds State courts (Restatement (Third) of the Foreign Relations Law of the United States (1987), vol. I, p. 91).
  • Executive certificates control certain questions — recognition of States and governments, and immunity — and the courts follow them even where they depart from custom: in Garcia-Mir v. Meese, 788 F.2d 1446 (11th Cir. 1986), the prolonged detention of illegal immigrants, contrary to customary international law, was held lawful because of the Attorney-General's decision, a “controlling executive act”.
  • Conflict with a statute: if the statute is clear and unambiguous, the statute prevails (The Over the Top, 5 F.2d 842 (1925)); otherwise the courts harmonise, giving “great weight” to the views of the Executive on questions of international law (the Charming Betsy canon: “an act of Congress ought never to be construed to violate the law of nations if any other possible construction remains”, Murray v. Schooner Charming Betsy, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 64 (1804)).
  • The Constitution prevails over custom: international law “is regarded as subject to the Constitution and the courts will not give effect to a rule of international law which conflicts with the United States Constitution” (Tag v. Rogers, 267 F.2d 664 (1959)).

3. Treaties — self-executing and non-self-executing

The distinguishing American feature is the distinction between self-executing and non-self-executing treaties. A self-executing treaty “is one which, in the view of the court, does not expressly or by its nature require legislation to make it operative within the municipal field”; a non-self-executing treaty becomes applicable only after the consent of Congress through adoption by a specific statute, and where an agreement is given effect by a statute, the implementing legislation is regarded as United States law rather than the agreement itself.

4. Two episodes worth a paragraph each

(a) The PLO Observer Mission case (1988): international law invoked against the United States. Under the Anti-Terrorism Act, 1987 the United States closed the Observer Mission of the Palestine Liberation Organisation in New York (the PLO had been granted observer status by the General Assembly in 1974). This was contrary to the United Nations Headquarters Agreement, 1947. The General Assembly termed the 1987 Act inconsistent with the Headquarters Agreement (Res. 42/230 of 23 March 1988); the Secretary-General held that the Agreement took precedence over United States domestic law “on the basis that international law prevails over municipal law”; and the International Court of Justice, in its advisory opinion of 26 April 1988 (Applicability of the Obligation to Arbitrate under Section 21 of the United Nations Headquarters Agreement, ICJ Rep. 1988, p. 12), ruled against the United States' unilateral act. In the federal district court the judge conceded that Congress had found the PLO a terrorist organisation, but held that Congress had not meant to supersede the Headquarters Agreement — “the clearest of expressions on the part of Congress to override the treaty obligation” being required (United States v. Palestine Liberation Organization, 695 F. Supp. 1456, 1468 (S.D.N.Y. 1988)). Use this case to show that even the State with the strongest domestic-supremacy doctrines may be held internationally to account.

(b) Avena / Medellín (2008): the limits of the international judicial voice. In Avena and Other Mexican Nationals (Mexico v. United States), ICJ Rep. 2004, p. 12, the ICJ held that the United States had violated Article 36 VCCR in 51 cases and that the United States was obliged to provide “review and reconsideration” of the convictions and sentences. In Medellín v. Texas, 552 U.S. 491 (2008) the Supreme Court held that the ICJ's judgment, though deserving “respectful consideration”, was not binding domestic law and could not overcome State procedural-default rules barring further post-conviction challenges. Verma records the same point. This is the cleanest modern illustration of the topic's central proposition: the international obligation and the domestic remedy are two different things.

C. Germany, France, the Netherlands, Greece and Russia — reception written into the constitution

Several continental constitutions do expressly what the common-law constitutions leave to judicial doctrine: they provide in the text for the effect of international law. Verma generalises: “an increasing number of States have incorporated provisions about the implementation of international law in their constitutions”, and “it can generally be stated that the European nations accord supremacy to international law through constitutional provisions. Nevertheless, they support the positivist approach that to be binding municipally the international law need[s] to be specifically adopted by the municipal law as laid down in their constitutions.” That final clause is the point to carry into an examination answer: even these constitutions are, in their operation, “specific adoption” clauses.

The most strongly monist text in Europe: a treaty duly approved by the States-General and published may displace even later legislation. Two limits: (i) the superiority extends only to provisions that are self-executing by content (“een ieder verbindende bepalingen”) — so the Dutch device is, functionally, a constitutional rule of self-execution; and (ii) it is the courts (in the Harmonisation Act tradition) who decide what is “binding on all persons”.
Greece Constitution of 11 June 1975, Article 28(1): the generally recognised rules of international law, as well as international conventions, are an integral part of Greek law and prevail over any contrary provisions of law. But treaties relating to commerce, taxation, economic co-operation and participation in international organisations have no force without ratification by a law of Parliament. Custom and treaty are both “integral” parts of domestic law and both prevail over statute; but the category of treaties specified in Article 28(1) requires parliamentary sanction before it operates internally.
Russia Constitution of 1993, Article 15(4): “the generally recognised principles and norms of international law and international treaties of the Russian Federation shall constitute part of its legal system”; where an international treaty of Russia conflicts with a law, “the rules of the international treaty shall apply”. Article 86: the President concludes treaties; Article 106: both Chambers give consent by federal law. Clear priority for both custom and treaties “over both earlier and later national laws”; Russian courts increasingly apply international law directly, particularly on human rights. Three limits: (i) no priority over the Russian Constitution; (ii) to have binding force a treaty must be published; and (iii) the Constitutional Court may review, in advance, the compatibility with the Constitution of a treaty not yet in force for Russia. In its later case-law the Russian Constitutional Court has asserted the power to declare a treaty “inapplicable” in Russia if it is inconsistent with the constitutional order — the very dualist turn that Article 15(4) seemed to exclude.

The comparative lesson. Even where the constitution says that international law “forms part of the law of the land”, three questions remain live and are decided by courts: (i) is the rule self-executing (the Dutch/German/American test); (ii) does it rank above the constitution (in Germany, France and Russia: no); and (iii) who determines the content of the rule (in Germany, the Federal Constitutional Court under Article 100(2); in Russia, the Constitutional Court; in India, ultimately the Supreme Court, which has declared even a treaty's meaning for India — Berubari, Vishaka). Reception clauses thereby convert a conflict between systems into an argument about interpretation — which is exactly the conclusion Verma reaches.

3.3Indian Practice in Detail (syllabus 3.2: “Practice of States — India”)

The Indian position cannot be stated in one sentence, because India has neither a reception clause (like Germany's Article 25) nor a supremacy clause (like Article VI(2) of the United States Constitution), and has never enacted a general statute on the effect of treaties. What India has is a patchwork: a Directive Principle that exhorts (Article 51(c)), an enabling power that empowers (Article 253), a distribution of legislative heads that allocates (Entries 10, 13 and 14 of List I), a saving clause that carries forward the pre-1950 common law (Article 372(1)), and a rich and now fairly consistent body of judicial practice. The eight prescribed cases are the joints of that patchwork.

B. Treaty-making in India — an executive act, unfettered but not unlimited

The first proposition is that treaty-making is an executive act. In Union of India v. Manmull Jain, AIR 1954 Cal 615, the validity of the treaty relating to the transfer of Chandernagore, a former French possession, was questioned for want of prior parliamentary legislation. The Calcutta High Court answered:

“Making a treaty is an executive act and not a legislative act. Legislation may be and is often required to give effect to the terms of a treaty. Thus, if a treaty, say, provides for payment to a foreign power, legislation may be necessary before the money can be spent, but the treaty is complete without the legislation... The President makes a treaty in exercise of his executive power and no Court of law in India can question its validity.”

The Supreme Court has held that for the exercise of the executive power under Article 73, Union legislation is not a pre-requisite (Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab, AIR 1955 SC 549; Jayantilal v. Rana, AIR 1964 SC 648), and in Nirmal Bose v. Union of India, AIR 1959 Cal 506, that the Union Government has the right by executive action to enter into treaties and agreements with foreign countries. The Privy Council formulation in Attorney-General for Canada v. Attorney-General for Ontario (1937) AC 326 — which Verma treats as “reflective of the Indian position” — completes the picture: “Parliament, no doubt, has a constitutional control over the executive but it cannot be disputed that the creation of the obligations undertaken in treaties and the assent to their form and quality are the function of the executive alone.”

Therefore, and this is the line to write in an answer: the Indian Parliament has no share in treaty-making comparable to that of the United States Senate. There is no requirement of parliamentary approval, no statutory duty of prior consultation (as in the UK's Constitutional Reform and Governance Act 2010) and no general practice of laying treaties before Parliament, although the executive may (and in practice often does) inform or consult Parliament, and although treaties involving money, cession of territory, or alteration of law cannot be implemented internally without legislation. The famous illustration of the executive's freedom is the Indo-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement of 1974, which the executive signed without legislation and which was implemented only in 2015 after the Constitution (100th Amendment) Act, 2015 — the cession-requires-amendment rule of Berubari reasserting itself forty-five years later. (Read the 2015 exchange of enclaves as the living aftermath of the Topic-3 cases: the 1974 Agreement plus the 2011 Protocol were given effect only by the 100th Amendment, which transferred 111 Indian enclaves to Bangladesh and 51 Bangladeshi enclaves to India.)

C. Treaty-implementation in India — legislation, with a minority view and a judicial qualification

Once concluded, how does a treaty operate inside India? Verma records two views: (i) “treaties shall not be binding upon courts unless implemented by legislation” (the view of D.D. Basu); and (ii) the view of Alexandrowicz that “not all treaties require legislation for implementation and it is only treaties affecting private rights that must be enacted by legislation to become enforceable”. The second view found judicial support in the separate opinion of Shah J. in Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India, AIR 1969 SC 783:

“The obligations arising under the agreements or treaties are not by their own force binding on Indian nationals. The power to legislate in respect of treaties lies with the Parliament under Entries 10 and 14 of the Union List. But making of law under that authority is necessary when the treaty or agreement operates to restrict the rights of citizens or others or modifies the laws of the State. If the rights of the citizens or others which are justiciable are not affected, no legislative measure is needed to give effect to the agreement or treaty.”

“This approach of Shah, J. has put the treaties into two categories: self-executing and non-self-executing, akin to the American approach. But preponderance of judicial authority upholds the other approach” — namely, that legislation is necessary. That preponderance consists of:

D. Customary international law in India — received, but subordinate

The Indian position on custom is stated with unusual clarity in Krishna Sharma v. The State of West Bengal, AIR 1954 Cal 591, where the Calcutta High Court said that the Indian courts apply rules of internal law, which include (a) the Constitution, (b) statutes of Parliament and (c) statutes of State legislatures — and:

“If the [Indian statutes are] in conflict with any principle of international law... municipal courts of India have got to obey the laws passed by the Legislature of the country to which they owe their allegiance. In interpreting and applying municipal law, the courts will try to adopt such a construction as will not bring [it] into conflict with rights and obligations deducible from rules of international law. If such rules or rights and obligations are inconsistent with the positive regulations of municipal law, the municipal courts cannot override the latter.”

That single passage contains the whole Indian doctrine: custom is applied; it never overrides a statute; and it is used in interpretation to avoid conflicts. Its principal applications are:

E. The human-rights turn — treaties as an instrument of interpretation

The most significant Indian development of the last thirty years is the judicial use of unlegislated human-rights treaties to expand constitutional guarantees. The chain runs: Jolly George (1980: the Covenant constrains the interpretation of CPC section 51) → PUCL (1997: ICCPR and UDHR in the privacy context) → Vishaka (1997: CEDAW and the Beijing Statement supply the content of Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 where there is a legislative vacuum) → Apparel Export Promotion Council v. A.K. Chopra, (1999) 1 SCC 759 → Puttaswamy (2017: “India's commitments under international law” as an interpretive principle for the constitutional right to privacy). Verma's summary of the doctrine, drawn from Vishaka, deserves memorising:

“In the absence of domestic law occupying the field... the international conventions and norms are significant for the purpose of interpretation of the guarantee of gender equality in the Constitution... [A]ny international convention not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and in harmony with its spirit must be read into these provisions to enlarge the meaning and content thereof, to promote the object of the constitutional guarantee.”

Two consequences, and two limits:

  • Consequence 1 — legitimate expectations. “Because of the ratification of these conventions, they create ‘legitimate expectations’ of their observance in the absence of contrary legislative provisions.”
  • Consequence 2 — the custom/treaty distinction is blurred. “This approach of the Supreme Court has diminished the distinction between the rules of customary international law and international conventions to which India is a party. The courts in India may enforce treaties which are not inconsistent with the Indian law or when there is a gap in the law.”
  • Limit 1 — the statute still wins. “If a treaty which has not been implemented internally through legislation conflicts with a municipal statute, the statute will prevail” (Jolly George). And “a subsequent ratification of international treaties will not render the existing municipal laws ultra vires of those treaties in case of inconsistency”.
  • Limit 2 — the Constitution still wins. Treaties conflicting with Part III are “null and void” by inference from Article 13 and cannot be enforced; and Puttaswamy states the conflict rule for the interpretive exercise explicitly: where there is a conflict with a statute, the statute prevails; where there is none, the Constitution is to be read in conformity with international obligations (Fig. 3.11).

3.4Municipal Law before International Tribunals — the mirror image

The syllabus is written entirely from the municipal side — “relationship between international law and municipal law... practice of States”. But the relationship has two directions, and Verma devotes a full section to the other one: what an international tribunal does with a defence founded on national law. This section is the answer to the standard Indian-examination problem question (see the 2002 paper, reproduced below in the PYQ section) and to any question asking whether a State can “shelter behind” its own law.

A. The rule: a State may not plead its internal law

B. The leading authority: the Alabama Claims Arbitration (1872)

The Alabama Claims Arbitration (United States v. Great Britain), 1872

Arbitral Tribunal at Geneva · J.B. Moore, International Arbitrations, vol. 1, p. 653

Facts. During the American Civil War a number of ships were built in England for private buyers, and it was well known that they were to be used as warships to attack the federal maritime trade of the United States. These raiders — of which the Alabama was the most famous — did considerable damage to American shipping. The United States claimed compensation before the Arbitral Tribunal, alleging that Great Britain had violated its obligations as a neutral State by allowing the construction and sailing to occur within its jurisdiction, and that it was liable under the so-called “Three Rules of Washington”, which set out the duties of neutrals and had been accepted by both parties.

The defence and its rejection. In its defence, Britain contended that it was constrained by its constitutional law to interfere with the private construction concerned — in other words, that British domestic law did not furnish the executive with the means to detain the vessels before their departure. The Tribunal rejected the plea and upheld the supremacy of international law: “the Government of Her Britannic Majesty cannot justify itself for a failure in due diligence on the plea of insufficiency of the legal means of action which it possessed”; and, more pointedly, “a neutral government must take care... that its municipal law shall prohibit acts contravening neutrality.”

Principle. A State which has contracted international obligations must make such modification in its legislation, or enact such new legislation, as may be necessary to carry them out. A second authority to the same effect is the Finnish Ships Arbitration (UN RIAA, vol. 3, p. 1484), where the plea of the respondent's internal law was similarly rejected. The rule was put positively in the Case of the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, PCIJ Rep., Series B, No. 10, p. 20 (1925): the duty to make the necessary legal changes is “self-evident”. In practical terms, this is why the internal law of a State cannot excuse it — why Article 27 of the Vienna Convention and Article 3 of the Articles on State Responsibility are drafted as they are, why the United Kingdom's failure to enact the statute contemplated by R. v. Keyn earlier (had it persisted) would have been a breach, and why the story of Jolly George Varghese is a story of Parliament eventually doing what Article 11 ICCPR required — section 51 CPC being read down by the judiciary in the meantime, and Order 21 Rule 37 being amended, and the whole field thereafter converted by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 and the general abolition of arrest for debt in the commercial courts' practice.

C. Three nuances an examiner will reward

  1. International tribunals do not declare national laws invalid. “International tribunals generally do not declare national laws invalid merely because these laws or the way in which they have been applied are inconsistent with international law.” The municipal rule retains its internal validity; what follows is the State's international responsibility, not the nullification of the statute. That is precisely the structure of the Indian cases: section 51 CPC was not struck down in Jolly George — it was construed.
  2. No duty of recognition of the external effects of a wrongful rule. “The municipal rule, in breach of a State's international obligations, does not impose a duty on other States to recognise the external effects of an act under that rule, which is not in conformity with international law.” The illustration is Nottebohm (Liechtenstein v. Guatemala), ICJ Rep. 1955, p. 4: the Court upheld Liechtenstein's right to grant nationality to Nottebohm, but because the grant did not accord with the international requirement of a “genuine connection” between the individual and the State, it was not binding on Guatemala. The domestic act was valid internally and ineffective internationally — the purest statement of the dualist structure operating in the international forum.
  3. But tribunals must often apply municipal law. Sometimes the dispute turns on municipal law: LaGrand and the American rule of “procedural default”; the Brazilian Loans case (France v. Brazil), PCIJ Rep., Series A, No. 21 (1929), where Brazilian Government loans governed by Brazilian law fell to be interpreted; the Serbian Loans case, Judgment No. 14, PCIJ Rep., Series A, No. 20 (1929); and Barcelona Traction, Light and Power Co. (Belgium v. Spain), ICJ Rep. 1970, p. 3, where the Court applied the municipal-law concept that a company has a personality distinct from its shareholders, so that the State of the shareholders could not claim for injury to the company. The Court's classical statement of its power is that, while its main function is to decide disputes between States on the basis of international law, it also has jurisdiction to determine the existence of any fact which, if established, would constitute a breach of an international obligation — and that jurisdiction includes questions of municipal law. It is therefore “obliged to obtain knowledge of the municipal law, including the constitutional provisions or other State legislation and the decisions of the State municipal courts”, in order to decide the applicable rules and their precise limits — but it is not entitled to undertake its own construction of national laws, “with the danger of contradicting rulings of national tribunals”. The Court thus treats municipal law as fact and as a source of interpretive material, not as a source of international obligation. Finally, international tribunals “quite often resort to municipal laws to ascertain whether a customary rule of international law has evolved by concurrent and cumulative State practice” (cf. Lotus, PCIJ Rep., Series A, No. 10 (1927)) — the same technique used in Topic 2 to prove custom.

D. LaGrand — the case that proves both halves of the rule

In LaGrand (Germany v. United States of America) (Merits), ICJ Rep. 2001, p. 466, two German nationals, Karl and Walter LaGrand, were tried and sentenced to death in Arizona without being informed, as Article 36(1) of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, 1963 requires, of their right to consular notification and access, and without the German consulate being informed. Germany obtained an order of provisional measures on 3 March 1999 asking the United States to “take all measures at its disposal” to stay the execution until the case was decided; Walter LaGrand was executed as scheduled. On the merits the United States argued, among other things, that the Court was being invited to act as a “court of appeal of national criminal proceedings”, and relied on its domestic rule of “procedural default” (a procedural failing not raised at trial at State level cannot be argued at the federal level). The Court held that the United States had breached Article 36 and that the rule of procedural default did not in itself violate Article 36 — a fine distinction that preserves the Alabama rule while declining to sit in judgment on the design of American criminal procedure. Two lessons for this topic: (i) the international obligation survived the national rule and the national judgment; and (ii) the tribunal's remedy was not to invalidate the American rule but to require review and reconsideration of the individual convictions. The sequel — Medellín v. Texas, 552 U.S. 491 (2008), where the United States Supreme Court held that even a judgment of the ICJ is not domestic law in the United States absent implementing legislation, and the withdrawal of the United States in March 2005 from the Optional Protocol to the Vienna Convention conferring compulsory jurisdiction on the ICJ — is a reminder that the Alabama rule binds States qua international persons, while their courts may continue to say “no”.

Fig. 3.8 — What an international tribunal does with municipal law: it looks at it, refuses to be bound by it, and does not rewrite it

One-paragraph answer to “Can a State plead its municipal law to avoid an international obligation?” No. Article 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties provides that a party may not invoke its internal law to justify failure to perform a treaty, subject only to the narrow manifest-and-fundamental rule of Article 46; Article 13 of the ILC's 1949 Draft Declaration had said the same generally; and Articles 3 and 32 of the 2001 Articles on State Responsibility provide that internal law neither characterises nor excuses an internationally wrongful act, reparation being due regardless. The Alabama Claims Arbitration (1872) rejected Great Britain's plea that its constitutional law afforded no means of detaining the Alabama, holding that a neutral must ensure its municipal law prohibits acts contravening neutrality; the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations case (1925), LaGrand (2001) and the Avena case (2004) apply the same rule. The international tribunal will nonetheless examine municipal law as a matter of fact where the dispute requires it — Serbian Loans and Brazilian Loans (1929), Barcelona Traction (1970) — but without substituting its construction for that of the national courts, and it will not invalidate the national rule or compel other States to recognise the external effects of an internally valid act (Nottebohm, 1955). The municipal court may therefore decide for its own law (Gramophone; Jolly George), but the municipal State remains internationally responsible (Alabama; LaGrand) — dualism costs money, not obligation.

15. In re Berubari Union No. (I) — Special Reference No. 1 of 1959

AIR 1960 SC 845 : (1960) 3 SCR 250 · Supreme Court of India (Advisory Opinion under Article 143(1)) · Opinion delivered by P.B. Gajendragadkar, J. · Date of opinion: 14 March 1960 · DU case material, pp. 37–45

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it is the foundation-stone of Indian practice on the relationship between international law and municipal law in the field where the relationship matters most: territory. It decides (i) that a treaty of cession cannot be implemented by an ordinary law under Article 3 but requires a constitutional amendment under Article 368; (ii) that the treaty-making power is an attribute of sovereignty which the Constitution must channel; and (iii) incidentally, that the executive's treaty-making power is not dependent on parliamentary legislation, though its implementation may be. Every later case of the cluster — Ram Kishore Sen, Maganbhai, Sukumar Sengupta — is a refinement of the distinctions drawn here.

2. The facts — from the Radcliffe Award to the Nehru–Noon Agreement

(a) Partition and the Radcliffe Award. On 20 February 1947 the British Government announced its intention to transfer power in British India by June 1948; on 3 June 1947 it issued a statement on the method of transfer; and on 18 July 1947 the British Parliament passed the Indian Independence Act, 1947, which came into force on the “appointed day”, 15 August 1947, setting up the two independent Dominions of India and Pakistan. Section 2 defined the territories of each Dominion; section 3(1) provided that the Province of Bengal should cease to exist and two new Provinces, East Bengal and West Bengal, should be constituted in its place; section 3(3) provided that the boundaries of the new Provinces “shall be such as may be determined... by the award of a boundary commission”, and that until then the Bengal districts specified in the First Schedule should be treated as East Bengal and the remainder as West Bengal; section 3(4) defined “award” as the decision of the Chairman of the commission contained in his report to the Governor-General.

On 30 June 1947 the Governor-General announced that Bengal and Punjab would be partitioned. A boundary commission was appointed for Bengal, consisting of four judges of High Courts and a Chairman to be appointed later — and Sir Cyril Radcliffe was appointed Chairman. Its terms of reference required the demarcation of the boundaries of the two parts of Bengal “on the basis of ascertaining the contiguous areas of Muslims and non-Muslims”, while also taking into account “other factors”. The Commission made its award on 12 August 1947 — three days before the appointed day — known as the Radcliffe Award.

(b) Berubari Union No. 12. The Union with which the case is concerned “has an area of 8.75 sq miles and a population of ten to twelve thousand residents”. It is situated in Police Station Jalpaiguri, District Jalpaiguri, then part of the Rajshahi Division. It was not specified in the First Schedule to the Independence Act — so, on the face of that Schedule, it would have been part of West Bengal. As the Court explained, however, “the First Schedule to the Independence Act did not really come into operation at all”, because the Radcliffe Award was made before the appointed day.

(c) The Bagge Tribunal. Boundary disputes having arisen, the two Dominions agreed at the Inter-Dominion Conference held at New Delhi on 14 December 1948 to set up a tribunal “without delay and in any case not later than 31-1-1949” for their adjudication and final decision. This was the Indo-Pakistan Boundaries Disputes Tribunal, presided over by Lord Justice Algot Bagge, whose award — the Bagge Award — was made on 26 January 1950. Critically for the case: “no issue was raised about the Berubari Union. In fact no reference was made to the District of Jalpaiguri at all in the proceedings before the Tribunal.”

(d) The dispute and the Agreement. It was two years later, in 1952, that Pakistan raised the question of Berubari Union for the first time, alleging that under the award Berubari should really have formed part of East Bengal and had been wrongly treated as part of West Bengal. Throughout that period “the Berubari Union continued to be in the possession of the Indian Union and was governed as a part of West Bengal”. Correspondence between the Prime Ministers followed and the dispute remained alive until 1958, when the two Prime Ministers reached the Agreement of 10 September 1958 (recorded in a joint note of the Commonwealth Secretary, Ministry of External Affairs, India and the Foreign Secretary, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Pakistan). The two items material to the Reference were:

Item 3 (Berubari Union 12): “This will be so divided as to give half the area to Pakistan, the other half adjacent to India being retained by India. The Division of Berubari Union 12 will be horizontal, starting from the north east corner of Debiganj Thana. The division should be made in such a manner that the Cooch-Behar Enclaves between Pachagar Thana of East Pakistan and Berubari Union 12 of Jalpaiguri Thana of West Bengal will remain connected as at present with Indian territory and will remain with India. The Cooch-Behar Enclaves lower down between Boda Thana of East Pakistan and Berubari Union 12 will be exchanged along with the general exchange of enclaves and will go to Pakistan.”

Item 10 (Exchange of Enclaves): “Exchange of old Cooch-Behar Enclaves in Pakistan and Pakistan Enclaves in India without claim to compensation for extra area going to Pakistan, is agreed to.”

3. How the Reference arose

A doubt arose whether the implementation of the Agreement required legislative action — and if so, whether a law of Parliament relatable to Article 3 would suffice, or whether an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368 was necessary, “in addition or in the alternative”. Because of the likelihood of “avoidable and protracted litigation”, the President referred three questions to the Supreme Court under Article 143(1):

  1. Is any legislative action necessary for the implementation of the Agreement relating to Berubari Union?
  2. If so, is a law of Parliament relatable to Article 3 sufficient, or is an amendment of the Constitution in accordance with Article 368 necessary, in addition or in the alternative?
  3. Is a law of Parliament relatable to Article 3 sufficient for the implementation of the Agreement relating to the Exchange of Enclaves, or is an amendment under Article 368 necessary?

4. Arguments for the Union of India (the Attorney-General)

  • No legislative action is necessary at all for either part of the Agreement.
  • The Berubari arrangement is not cession but ascertainment of a boundary: the Agreement merely “ascertain[ed] or delineat[ed] the exact boundary about which a dispute existed... by reason of different interpretations put by them on the relevant description contained in the award”. It was “the recognition or ascertainment of the boundary which had already been fixed and in no sense... a substitution of a new boundary”. If, as a result, possession of some land had to be yielded, “it does not amount to cession of territory; it is merely a mode of settling the boundary”.
  • The exchange of enclaves is incidental to the Berubari settlement and partakes of its character.
  • Even if legislation was needed, Article 3 would suffice — Article 3 speaks of the alteration of the boundaries of States.
  • The executive power is co-extensive with Parliament's power in matters of treaty-making and implementation: Articles 53(1), 73(1)(a) and (b) and 74, read with Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955) 2 SCR 225, where Mukherjea C.J. had said that executive function includes “the determination of the policy as well as carrying it into execution... the direction of foreign policy”.
  • No change in the First Schedule is needed because Berubari Union was never legally included in the territorial description of West Bengal in that Schedule.
  • A precedent: Act 47 of 1951, by which the boundaries of Assam were altered consequent upon the cession of a strip of territory (about 32 square miles of the Dewangiri Hill Block, largely forest and sparsely inhabited by Bhotias) to the Government of Bhutan — an instance of cession given effect by a law passed under Article 3.
  • The South Australia v. Victoria analogy: State of South Australia v. State of Victoria (1914) 12 CLR 667 showed that a boundary mistakenly fixed and later corrected was not a cession.

5. Arguments against (Mr Chatterjee) — the “extreme contention”

Mr Chatterjee urged that even Parliament has no power to cede any part of Indian territory in favour of a foreign State, either by ordinary legislation or even by a constitutional amendment, so that the Agreement was void. He relied on two grounds: (i) the Preamble — “We, the people of India, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a sovereign democratic republic” — which, he said, irrevocably postulates that India must continue to be territorially as well as politically a republic, the makers of the Constitution having been “painfully conscious of the tragic partition”; and (ii) Article 1(3)(c), which contemplates that “the territory of India shall comprise such other territories as may be acquired” — an express provision for acquisition with no provision for cession, so that expressio unius est exclusio alterius.

7. The reasoning, step by step

  1. The Agreement is cession, not ascertainment. The agreement “does not purport to be based on the award and has been reached apart from, and independently of, it”. Therefore the Attorney-General's premise failed, and with it his argument that no legislation was needed: it had been “fairly conceded by him that this argument proceeds on the assumption that the Agreement is in substance and fact no more than the ascertainment of the disputed boundary already fixed by the award”.
  2. South Australia v. Victoria distinguished. In the Australian case, parties were not “independently determining the line with a view to settle the dispute between the two respective States”; they determined the line “in accordance with the provisions of a parliamentary statute”. Here the parties had independently negotiated the division — so the analogy failed.
  3. Berubari was Indian territory. Rejecting the argument that no change in the First Schedule was needed, the Court held that since the Radcliffe Award was announced Berubari “has remained in possession of India and has been always treated as a part of West Bengal and governed as such”, and therefore fell “within the territories which immediately before the commencement of the Constitution were comprised in the Province of West Bengal”.
  4. The power to cede is an attribute of sovereignty — but its exercise is governed by the Constitution. This is the heart of the opinion: “it is an essential attribute of sovereignty that a sovereign State can acquire foreign territory and can, in case of necessity, cede a part of its territory in favour of a foreign State, and this can be done in exercise of its treaty-making power. Cession of national territory in law amounts to the transfer of sovereignty over the said territory by the owner State in favour of another State... This power... is of course subject to the limitations which the Constitution of the State may either expressly or by necessary implication impose... Stated broadly the treaty-making power would have to be exercised in the manner contemplated by the Constitution and subject to the limitations imposed by it. Whether the treaty made can be implemented by ordinary legislation or by constitutional amendment will naturally depend on the provisions of the Constitution itself.”
  5. Mr Chatterjee's extreme contention rejected. “In our opinion, there is no substance in these contentions.” The Preamble does not freeze the territory, and Article 1(3)(c) (which speaks of acquisition) does not by implication forbid cession — a sovereign State has both powers. The Court also recorded Oppenheim's humanitarian observation that cession involves hardship for inhabitants who lose their citizenship, and noted that it may be mitigated by an option to emigrate within a stipulated period.
  6. Why Article 3 is insufficient. The implementation of the Agreement would “naturally involve the alteration of the content of and the consequent amendment of Article 1 and of the relevant part of the First Schedule to the Constitution, because such implementation would necessarily lead to the diminution of the territory of the Union of India”. Article 3 deals with the internal reorganisation of States — the formation of new States, and the alteration of areas, boundaries or names of States — and not with the diminution of the Union's own territory in favour of a foreign State. Hence a law under Article 368 is both competent and necessary. (In Ram Kishore Sen, six years later, the Court corrected an inadvertent error in this part of the opinion: it had proceeded on the footing that the word “State” in Article 3 excludes Union territories, which is not correct — see Case 16.)
  7. The Bhutan precedent rejected. Act 47 of 1951 had been cited as legislative practice. The Court held: “We do not think that this instance can be of any assistance in construing the scope and effect of the provisions of Article 3.”
  8. Two safeguards discussed. (i) The Attorney-General argued that proceeding under Article 368 would deprive the legislature of West Bengal of the opportunity to express its views under the proviso to Article 3; the Court answered that if, on a fair construction, Article 3 is inapplicable, that incidental consequence cannot be avoided, and that Article 368 has its own safeguard — the special majority of the total membership of each House and two-thirds of those present and voting. (ii) The Court added, significantly, that the amendment of Article 1 consequent on cession “does not attract the safeguard prescribed by the proviso to Article 368 because neither Article 1 nor Article 3 is included in the list of entrenched provisions... It is not for us to enquire or consider whether it would not be appropriate to include the said two articles under the proviso. That is a matter for the Parliament to consider and decide.” (Parliament did, in effect, reconsider the amendment procedure soon afterwards — the Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971 and Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, (1973) 4 SCC 225, which forged the basic-structure doctrine. Voluntarily handing over Indian territory is, on the logic of Kesavananda, subject to that doctrine — a proposition worth a sentence in a good answer.)

8. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) A treaty or agreement which involves cession — the transfer of sovereignty over Indian territory to a foreign State — cannot be implemented by ordinary legislation under Article 3; it requires an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368, because it involves a diminution of the territory of India and therefore an amendment of Article 1 and the First Schedule. (ii) The treaty-making power is an attribute of sovereignty which the State possesses as an incident of statehood, but whose exercise is governed by the Constitution. (iii) The distinction between cession and the ascertainment or settlement of a boundary is the pivot on which the constitutional requirements turn — a distinction the Court here applied against the Union, and later applied in its favour in Ram Kishore Sen and Maganbhai.

9. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • The Ninth Amendment. Acting on the opinion, Parliament passed the Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960 (in force from 28 December 1960), which amended the First Schedule to give effect to the transfer, defined the “appointed day” as the date the Central Government might notify, and defined the “Indo-Pakistan Agreements” to include those of 10 September 1958, 23 October 1959 and 11 January 1960.
  • The Agreement itself was never fully implemented. No “appointed day” was ever notified; the Ninth Amendment remained, in the words of the Calcutta High Court and the Supreme Court in Sukumar Sengupta (1990), a “dead letter” as regards the eastern sector. Berubari was never transferred; the Cooch-Behar enclaves were never exchanged. Half a century later, the whole story was completed in a different direction by the Indo-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement, 2015 (Constitution (100th Amendment) Act, 2015), which exchanged enclaves between India and Bangladesh — but without touching Berubari.
  • The doctrinal sequel: Ram Kishore Sen (1966) rejected writ petitions against the proposed transfer of Berubari and of the 512 acres of Chilahati, and corrected the inadvertent error in Berubari.

10. Relevance to the topic — and the international-law dimension

Berubari is often described as a case about Article 3 and Article 368. It is also, and more importantly for this topic, a case about dualism in action: the Agreement was a valid and binding treaty in the international plane (the Court did not doubt that India could cede territory and was bound by what it had agreed), yet the Union could not give it effect internally without altering its own constitution. That is the transformation theory operating at the constitutional level in its most dramatic form. Three further points are examinable: (a) the case accepts the executive's treaty-making power as complete (the Attorney-General did not need to rely on Article 73, and the Court left Ram Jawaya Kapur untouched); (b) the case establishes the constitutional rule that determines the fate of the Indo-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement 1974 and (c) it is the reason why the Indian Constitution contains a legislative (Article 253) but no receptive clause: the framers assumed that where a treaty requires changes in law, Parliament would make them — and where it requires a change in the Constitution, Parliament must amend it.

How to write Berubari: (i) facts in three sentences — Radcliffe Award, Bagge Award, 1958 Agreement dividing Berubari Union 12 and exchanging Cooch-Behar enclaves; (ii) the three questions; (iii) the holding: cession, not ascertainment; Article 3 incompetent, Article 368 necessary; (iv) the ratio in one sentence — “cessation of territory is an attribute of sovereignty, but its manner of exercise is what the Constitution prescribes, and under the Indian Constitution cession requires a constitutional amendment”; (v) three quotable lines: “essential attribute of sovereignty”; “diminution of the territory of the Union of India”; “no substance in these contentions” (on the argument that no cession is possible at all); (vi) close with the aftermath: the Ninth Amendment, the “dead letter” of the eastern sector and the 100th Amendment of 2015. Add the international-law dimension: under VCLT Article 27 India could not have pleaded Article 3 or Article 368 to escape the Agreement once ratified — the constitutional requirement is India's internal difficulty, not an excuse vis-à-vis Pakistan.

16. Ram Kishore Sen v. Union of India (the “second Berubari case”)

(1966) 1 SCR 430 : AIR 1966 SC 644 · Supreme Court of India (Civil Appeal from the Calcutta High Court) · Judgment delivered by P.B. Gajendragadkar, C.J. · DU case material, pp. 46–51

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it completes Berubari in three ways. First, it draws the line between cession (which needs a constitutional amendment) and the delivery of territory that was always de jure Pakistan's (which needs none) — the distinction that became the foundation of Indian practice for the next sixty years. Secondly, it corrects an error in the Berubari opinion, thereby telling us how the Supreme Court treats its own advisory opinions. Thirdly, it construes the crucial phrase “administered as if” in Entry 13 of the First Schedule — a pure constitutional-interpretation question into which international-law categories (de facto and de jure sovereignty, adverse possession) enter as arguments. For the present topic it is the leading Indian authority on the proposition that not every transfer of territory is cession, and therefore on how far the constitution's internal requirements reach into the implementation of treaties.

2. The facts

(a) The six appellants. The writ petition from which the appeal arose was filed by six residents of Thana Jalpaiguri in the District of Jalpaiguri, each of whom had a personal stake in the territory proposed to be transferred:

Table 3.16 — Who the appellants were and what they stood to lose
AppellantsTheir connection with the disputed territory
1 & 2Original inhabitants of villages Senpara and Deuniapara, within Berubari Union No. 12; they owned ancestral homes and cultivated lands there and lived in the homesteads.
3 & 4Originally residents of villages in Thana Boda adjoining Thana Jalpaiguri; when Thana Boda was transferred to Pakistan as a result of the partition of 1947, they moved to Senpara and Gouranga Bazar within Berubari Union No. 12, where they had since acquired land and built homesteads.
5 & 6Inhabitants of the village of Chilahati, said by them to be situated in Thana Jalpaiguri, where they had ancestral homes and cultivated lands.

The prayer was for writs or directions restraining the respondents (the Union of India and others) from transferring a portion of Berubari Union No. 12 and the village of Chilahati to Pakistan — the transfer being, they alleged, illegal.

(b) The Agreement and the Reference. The Agreement of 10 September 1958 between the Prime Ministers of India and Pakistan, and the three questions referred to the Supreme Court under Article 143(1), are as in Case 15. After the opinion, Parliament passed the Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960, in force from 28 December 1960, defining “appointed day” and “Indo-Pakistan Agreements” (those of 10 September 1958, 23 October 1959 and 11 January 1960) for the purposes of the transfer of territories to Pakistan.

(c) The findings of the single Judge. On the Berubari point he held that the plea of necessity for a law under Article 3 had been rejected by the Supreme Court, so that implementation of the Ninth Amendment was valid; on Chilahati he found that the village was part of Debiganj Thana and had been allotted to Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award; that the appellants' theory that the Chilahati being transferred was a different Chilahati was not made out; that a small area of 512 acres appertaining to that village had not been delivered over to Pakistan at partition; and that the respondents, in proposing to transfer it, were merely giving Pakistan what really belonged to her, so that no question of constitutionality arose. The alternative plea of adverse possession was rejected. The writ petition was dismissed, the Judge granted a certificate, and the appellants appealed.

3. The issues before the Supreme Court

  1. Was the trial Judge in error in holding that the map (Ext. A-1) on which the appellants relied to show that a horizontal division from the north-east corner of Debiganj Thana would leave no part of Berubari Union No. 12 in Pakistan was “neither relevant nor reliable”?
  2. Was the proposed transfer of the 512 acres of Chilahati constitutional, given (a) the history of the Radcliffe Award, (b) the appellants' reading of Entry 13 of the First Schedule (“administered as if...”) and (c) their plea of adverse possession?
  3. Whether the Ninth Amendment Act's failure to prescribe the precise manner of division made it defective.

4. Arguments of the appellants (Mr Mukerjee and Mr Dutt)

  • On Berubari: the division had to be by a strict horizontal line beginning at the north-east corner of Debiganj Thana and drawn east to west; if so drawn, no part of Berubari Union No. 12 would fall to the south of the line; the division intended by the Amendment Act was therefore impossible, and the map on which they relied proved it.
  • On the Ninth Amendment: the Schedule annexed to the Amendment Act should itself have shown how the division was to be made — more details and specific directions were required.
  • On Chilahati: private documents (transfer deeds ranging from 1925 to 1945) described Chilahati as forming part of District Jalpaiguri, showing that the Chilahati being transferred was not the Chilahati in Debiganj Police Station.
  • On Entry 13: even if the area had been allotted to Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award, it had been “administered as if” it formed part of the Province of West Bengal, and therefore must be deemed included in West Bengal within the meaning of Entry 13 and in the territory of India under Article 1; it could not now be ceded without following the Berubari procedure.
  • On adverse possession: the possession of West Bengal over the area since the Radcliffe Award had been adverse, so that Pakistan's title had been lost.

5. Arguments of the respondents (the Union of India)

  • The map was unreliable and the intended division was plainly a division into a northern portion (India) and a southern portion (Pakistan); maps produced by the respondents established that Chilahati lay in Debiganj Thana.
  • The Ninth Amendment merely recorded and made effective the decision of the two Prime Ministers, as the Supreme Court had itself suggested in its opinion; no further detail was required.
  • What was proposed was the transfer of territory which “really belongs to” Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award — an act of good faith, not a cession of Indian territory.
  • Neither the Union of India nor the State of West Bengal claimed the area; both expressly stated that it belonged to Pakistan under the Radcliffe Award and had to be delivered over.

6. The decision

The appeal was dismissed. Every one of the appellants' contentions failed: the map was properly rejected; the Ninth Amendment was not defective for want of detail; Chilahati was in Debiganj Thana and had been allotted to Pakistan; Entry 13 did not apply; and the plea of adverse possession was not open to the appellants (and had not even been pleaded in the writ petition).

7. The reasoning, in the Court's own structure

  1. The correction of the Berubari opinion (¶8). Before dealing with the arguments, the Court “advert[ed] to the opinion expressed by this Court in Re: The Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves with a view to correct an error which has crept into the opinion through inadvertence”. In Berubari the Court had, while discussing the significance of several clauses of Article 3, “assumed that the Union territories were outside the purview of the said provisions... on the basis that the word ‘State’ used in all the said clauses of Article 3 did not include the Union territories specified in the First Schedule”. That assumption, the Court now said, was erroneous. The true reason Article 3 was inapplicable in Berubari is the reason given at ¶46 of that opinion: “the power to acquire new territory and the power to cede a part of the national territory were outside the scope of Article 3(c)”, both being essential attributes of sovereignty. Learn this correction: it is a favourite examination point, and it shows the Court keeping its ratio while discarding an unnecessary premise.
  2. The map and the horizontal division (¶¶9–18). The Court held that the intention of the relevant provision was that “after Berubari Union No. 12 is divided, its northern portion should remain with India and the southern portion should go to Pakistan” — the phrase “starting from the north east corner of Debiganj Thana” being descriptive of the starting point of the division, not a mathematical direction that would defeat the division altogether. The map was neither relevant nor reliable.
  3. The Ninth Amendment needed no further detail (¶18). “All that the relevant provision has done is to record the decision reached by the Prime Ministers of the two countries and make it effective by including it in the Constitution Amendment Act as suggested by this Court in its opinion...” The contention was “clearly misconceived and must be rejected”.
  4. Chilahati was Pakistan's — and the Government's conduct deserved praise (¶20). The village of Chilahati in Debiganj Thana had been allotted to Pakistan; “through inadvertence, a part of it was not delivered to Pakistan on the occasion of the partition which followed the Radcliffe Award” — a mistake not surprising in the division of territories. “What the respondents now propose to do is to transfer to Pakistan the area in question which really belongs to her. In our opinion, this conduct on the part of the respondents speaks for their fair and straightforward approach in this matter.” The private transfer deeds of 1925–1945 were of no weight: individuals' recitals that Chilahati lay in Jalpaiguri could not displace the taluka and police-station maps, and no attempt had been made to identify the lands with the taluka maps to show a separate Chilahati.
  5. The construction of “as if” in Entry 13 (¶¶21–25). This is the constitutional heart of the judgment. When the Constitution was first adopted, the territory of West Bengal was described simply as the territory comprised in the Province of West Bengal; the words “as if” were used for the territories of other States — the territories which, by virtue of an order under section 290-A of the Government of India Act, 1935, were immediately before the commencement of the Constitution “being administered as if they formed part of that Province”. That clause did not originally apply to West Bengal; it was applied to West Bengal by the Constitution (Amendment of the First and Fourth Schedules) Order, 1950 (passed on 25 January 1950), which deleted the paragraph relating to West Bengal so that the general clause became applicable to it. On that history the Court held: “the words ‘as if’ have a special significance. They refer to territories which originally did not belong to West Bengal but which became a part of West Bengal by reason of merger agreements.” The clause “is clearly and specifically intended to refer to territories which merged with the adjoining States at the crucial time, and so, it cannot include a part of Chilahati that was administered by West Bengal under the circumstance to which we have just referred.” The Court's decisive sentence: “The West Bengal Government knew all the time that it was an area which belonged to Pakistan and which had to be transferred to it. That is, in fact, what the respondents are seeking to do; and so, it would be idle to contend that by virtue of the accidental fact that this area was administered by West Bengal, it has constitutionally and validly become a part of West Bengal itself.”
  6. Adverse possession rejected (¶26). The plea had not been raised in the writ petition; neither the Union of India nor the State of West Bengal claimed the area; and so it was unnecessary to decide “whether a plea of this kind can be made under international law”. (Note how the Court avoided an international-law holding while using the de facto/de jure vocabulary throughout — a useful illustration of the municipal court's caution.)

8. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) The transfer of territory which was de jure a foreign State's under a binding boundary award, but which has remained in India's de facto administration by accident, is not cession and requires neither a law under Article 3 nor an amendment under Article 368. (ii) The phrase “administered as if” in Entry 13 of the First Schedule is confined to territories that became part of a State by merger agreements; it does not cover territory administered under a mistake about the boundary. (iii) The correction — that Article 3 does extend to Union territories, and that the true ground of Berubari is that cession lies outside Article 3(c) — is now the authoritative reading of Berubari. (iv) Adverse possession has no application to a dispute between States over territory governed by an award.

9. What happened next — and the living aftermath

  • The writ petitions against the actual transfer of Berubari were dismissed in Union of India v. Sudhansu Mazumdar, AIR 1971 SC 1594, where the Court held that the cession of Indian territory to a foreign State does not bring about a transfer of ownership rights so as to attract Article 32(2).
  • The reasoning of Ram Kishore Sen on de facto/de jure sovereignty was applied by the Supreme Court in Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta (Case 19) to hold that Dahagram and Angarpota had remained Bangladesh territory throughout, so that no cession was involved in allowing Bangladesh to keep them.
  • The 2015 Land Boundary Agreement with Bangladesh finally settled the enclave question — by exchange, with a constitutional amendment, precisely because that was cession (of the Indian enclaves) and acquisition (of the Bangladeshi ones). The contrast between Ram Kishore Sen (no amendment needed) and the 100th Amendment (amendment needed) is the clearest possible teaching device for the cession/boundary distinction.

10. Relevance to the topic

The case shows the municipal court working out the domestic consequences of an international-law determination — where sovereignty lay de jure after the Radcliffe Award — and then applying its own constitutional rules to the consequences. It is the Indian counterpart of the principle stated in this topic's international-law materials: an international tribunal determines where sovereignty lies; a municipal constitution determines what its own organs must do about it. Note also the case's implicit acceptance of the executive's competence to act on the award: the Union was implementing an international determination, and the Court upheld the action, respecting the executive's conduct as “fair and straightforward”. For the theory debate, Ram Kishore Sen is an example of coordination rather than hierarchy: international law told the parties where the boundary was; Indian constitutional law told them what instruments were needed to give effect to it; no conflict between the two systems arose at all.

How to write Ram Kishore Sen: lead with the distinction, not the facts — “cession of Indian territory (Berubari) requires a constitutional amendment; the restoration of territory that was always de jure the other State's, or the settlement of a disputed boundary, does not”. Then give the facts briefly (six appellants; 512 acres of Chilahati; the “as if” argument). Then the two holdings: (a) the Entry 13 construction — “as if” refers to merger-agreement territories, not to land administered by mistake; (b) the correction of the Berubari error about Article 3 and Union territories. Close with the aftermath: Sudhansu Mazumdar (1971), Sukumar Sengupta (1990) and the 100th Amendment (2015).

17. Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin

AIR 1980 SC 470 : (1980) 2 SCC 360 · Supreme Court of India (Appeal by special leave, from the Kerala High Court) · Judgment delivered by V. R. Krishna Iyer, J. · DU case material, pp. 52–58

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it is the Supreme Court's most explicit holding on the transformation of treaty obligations into municipal law and, at the same time, its most imaginative use of an unlegislated treaty. The Court says, on the one hand, that the ICCPR cannot itself be enforced in an Indian court until municipal law is changed; and, on the other hand, it uses the Covenant to read down section 51 of the Code of Civil Procedure so that India's international obligation is honoured. It is the perfect case for an examination answer on the proposition that India follows the dualist (transformation) theory with a strong interpretative exception — and it is the seed from which Vishaka, Vellore and Puttaswamy grow.

2. The facts

  • The appellants (judgment-debtors) suffered a money decree against them in O.S. No. 57 of 1972 in a sum of Rs 2.5 lakhs, the respondent-Bank being the decree-holder. There were two other money decrees against them (O.S. 92 of 1972 and O.S. 94 of 1974), the total sum payable being over Rs 7 lakhs.
  • In execution of the decree in O.S. 57 of 1972, a warrant for arrest and detention in the civil prison was issued against the appellants under section 51 and Order XXI, rule 37 of the CPC on 22 June 1979. There had already been a similar warrant earlier in execution of the same decree.
  • Besides the warrant, the decree-holder had proceeded against the judgment-debtors' properties: all their immovable properties had been attached for sale in discharge of the decree debts, and the execution court had appointed a Receiver for the management of the attached properties. The enjoyment, and even the power to alienate, was therefore forbidden by court order.
  • On the earlier occasion a similar warrant had already been issued; the High Court, in a short order, “summarily dismissed the revision” filed by the judgment-debtors against the order of arrest.
  • Crucially: “We see no investigation having been made by the executing court regarding the current ability of the judgment-debtors to clear off the debts or their malafide refusal, if any, to discharge the debts.”

3. The issues

  1. Whether the personal freedom of a judgment-debtor can be held in ransom until repayment of a debt, and whether section 51 read with Order XXI, rule 37 CPC warrants such a step.
  2. If it does, whether the provision is constitutional — tested “on the touchstone of fair procedure under Article 21 and in conformity with the inherent dignity of the human person in the light of Article 11 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights”.
  3. Whether, in any case, section 51 had been complied with “in its enlightened signification”.

4. Arguments

For the appellants (judgment-debtors)

“Law and justice must be on speaking terms”: a debtor who is unable to pay must not be detained in the civil prison. India is a party to the ICCPR and Article 11 declares that “no one shall be imprisoned merely on the ground of inability to fulfil a contractual obligation”; Article 51(c) obliges the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations; Article 21 requires a fair, just and reasonable procedure before personal liberty can be taken away; the earlier warrant had been issued without any enquiry into present means; and the properties having been attached and a Receiver appointed, the debtors could not be said to be obstructing execution.

For the respondent-Bank

The decree is a decree of a competent court; section 51 expressly authorises execution by arrest and detention; the judgment-debtors had means after the decree and had not discharged it; international covenants, until legislated, confer no rights on individuals in municipal courts — counsel argued that international law is in this sense “inchoate” as far as the citizen is concerned; and the Court should not rewrite a statutory provision that the legislature had framed deliberately.

5. The decision

The Court set aside the judgment under appeal and directed the executing court to decide de novo the means of the judgment-debtors to discharge the decree in the light of the interpretation given. Section 51 CPC was read down (not struck down): detention can be ordered only where, apart from mere failure to pay, there is (a) an element of bad faith beyond mere indifference to pay, some deliberate or recusant disposition in the past, or (b) current means to pay the decree or a substantial part of it. The Court expressly left open, for another day, the question whether the proviso to section 51 read with Order XXI, rule 37, is “in excess of the Constitutional mandate in Art. 21 and bad in part”, because, the matter being remitted, “the stage has not yet arisen for us to go into the vires”.

6. The reasoning — on international law

  1. The Covenant is acknowledged but not self-executing. “India is now a signatory to this Covenant and Art. 51(c) of the Constitution obligates the State to ‘foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organised peoples with one another’. Even so, until the municipal law is changed to accommodate the Covenant what binds the court is the former, not the latter.” And: “the positive commitment of the States Parties ignites legislative action at home but does not automatically make the Covenant an enforceable part of the corpus juris of India.”
  2. The transformation theory is expressly adopted. The Court approved A.H. Robertson's statement in Human Rights in National and International Law “that international conventional law must go through the process of transformation into the municipal law before the international treaty can become an internal law. From the national point of view the national rules alone count.” But it added the second half of the rule: “With regard to interpretation, however, it is a principle generally recognised in national legal system[s] that, in the event of doubt, the national rule is to be interpreted in accordance with the State's international obligations.”
  3. The Kerala precedent — Xavier v. Canara Bank Ltd. (1969). The Court extracted the Kerala High Court's reasoning at length and with approval: a judgment-debtor of seventy, who had spent on illness the means he once had, could not be detained. The Kerala Court had said: “The remedy for breaches of International Law in general is not to be found in the law courts of the State because International Law per se or proprio vigore has not the force or authority of civil law, till under its inspirational impact actual legislation is undertaken... the Declaration of Human Rights merely sets a common standard of achievement... but cannot create a binding set of rules... individual citizens cannot complain about their breach in the municipal courts even if the country concerned has adopted the covenants and ratified the operational protocol.” But the Kerala Court had then done what the Supreme Court did here: it read section 51 consistently with Article 11.
  4. The Law Commission's 54th Report. The Court found that the Law Commission had itself “cognise[d] the Covenant, while dealing with s. 51 C.P.C.”, quoting the key passages of the Kerala ruling and endorsing its ratio — “We agree with this view” — and adding: “Imprisonment is not to be ordered merely because, like Shylock, the creditor says: ‘I crave the law, the penalty and forfeit of my bond.’ The law does recognise the principle that ‘Mercy is reasonable in the time of affliction, as clouds of rain in the time of drought.’” The Court concurred with the Law Commission's construction.
  5. The operative proposition (learn it verbatim). “Quondam affluence and current indigence without intervening dishonesty or bad faith in liquidating his liability can be consistent with Art. 11 of the Covenant, because then no detention is permissible under s. 51, C.P.C.” And on the words that hurt — “or has had since the date of the decree, the means to pay the amount of the decree”: “The simple default to discharge is not enough. There must be some element of bad faith beyond mere indifference to pay, some deliberate or recusant disposition in the past or, alternatively, current means to pay the decree or a substantial part of it... Here considerations of the debtor's other pressing needs and straitened circumstances will play prominently.”
  6. Article 21 as the constitutional anchor. “The high value of human dignity and the worth of the human person enshrined in Art. 21, read with Arts. 14 and 19, obligates the State not to incarcerate except under [a] law which is fair, just and reasonable in its procedural essence” — following Maneka Gandhi and developed in Sunil Batra, Sita Ram and Sunil Batra (II). And the famous sentence: “It is too obvious to need elaboration that to cast a person in prison because of his poverty and consequent inability to meet his contractual liability is appalling. To be poor, in this land of daridra Narayana, is no crime and to ‘recover’ debts by the procedure of putting one in prison is too flagrantly violative of Art. 21 unless there is proof of the minimal fairness of his wilful failure to pay in spite of his sufficient means and absence of more terribly pressing claims on his means such as medical bills to treat cancer or other grave illness.”
  7. The harmonising conclusion. “We would have, by this construction, sauced law with justice, harmonised s. 51 with the Covenant and the Constitution.” Because the provision could be read consistently with the Covenant and Article 21, the “lethal blow of Art. 21” could not strike it down.

7. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) An international covenant to which India is a party, but which has not been legislated into municipal law, is not directly enforceable in Indian courts — international conventional law must go through the process of transformation (Xavier v. Canara Bank; A.H. Robertson; the Law Commission's 54th Report). (ii) But where municipal law is capable of two constructions, it must be interpreted in conformity with India's international obligations, because that is the presumed intention of the legislature and the constitutional command of Article 51(c). (iii) Section 51 CPC (with Order XXI, rule 37) is therefore to be read as permitting detention for a decree debt only where there is proof of bad faith, deliberate evasion, or current means to pay — mere past affluence followed by present indigence will not do. (iv) Article 11 ICCPR thus becomes, through interpretation, an operative standard of Indian civil procedure without ever being “enforced” as such.

8. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • Statutory reform followed the judicial reading. Order XXI, rule 37 was amended (by the 1976/1999 amendments and the Commercial Courts Act 2015 regime) to require a show-cause notice and a hearing before arrest; and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 replaced the era of imprisonment for commercial debt with a collective insolvency process. The judgment's logic — that imprisonment for inability to pay is not permissible — is now the settled position.
  • Constitutional aftermath. The case is one of the pillars of the “fair procedure” jurisprudence under Article 21 (with Maneka Gandhi), and was cited in the line of cases that produced Puttaswamy (2017).
  • Topic-3 aftermath. Its method — dualism in doctrine, monism in interpretation — was adopted in Vellore (custom), Vishaka (CEDAW) and Puttaswamy (ICCPR and UDHR). Its language about transformation is quoted in the prescribed text and in every standard treatment of Indian practice. Note that the same Court later used the ICCPR more strongly: in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab, AIR 1980 SC 898, decided the same year, the Court held that the requirements of Article 6 ICCPR were substantially similar to Articles 20 and 21 of the Constitution and that Indian penal law was in accord with India's international commitments.

9. Relevance to the topic

This is the prescribed case that states the transformation theory in terms and simultaneously shows its limits being pushed back. For an examiner, three consequences should be spelled out. First, it settles that India is, formally, a dualist State for treaties: ratification creates an international obligation, not a domestic right. Secondly, it identifies the bridge by which international obligations cross into Indian law without legislation: the canon of harmonious construction, itself derived from Article 51(c) and from the presumption that Parliament does not intend to legislate in breach of international law. Thirdly, it shows the price of dualism: because the Covenant was not legislated, the Indian courts could give relief only by inventing a favourable construction of section 51 — and had the words of the section been intractable, the debtor would have had no remedy in India at all, even while India stood in breach internationally (VCLT Art. 27). Compare Medellín v. Texas (2008) for the same dilemma in the United States.

How to write Jolly George: (i) two sentences of facts — a Rs 2.5 lakh decree (aggregate over Rs 7 lakhs), a warrant of arrest under section 51 and Order XXI, rule 37 CPC, properties attached and a Receiver appointed, no enquiry into present means; (ii) the three questions; (iii) the two propositions: the Covenant is not ex proprio vigore enforceable (“ignites legislative action at home but does not automatically make the Covenant an enforceable part of the corpus juris of India”), yet section 51 must be read in conformity with it (harmonious construction, Article 51(c)); (iv) the operative test — bad faith, deliberate evasion or current means; (v) the Article 21 dimension (“to be poor... is no crime”); (vi) the aftermath — the Law Commission's 54th Report, the amendments to Order XXI rule 37, the IBC 2016, and the case's place in the Maneka–Puttaswamy line. Quote “transformation into the municipal law” and “what binds the court is the former, not the latter” — examiners look for these.

18. Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey

AIR 1984 SC 667 : (1984) 2 SCC 534 · Supreme Court of India (Civil Appeal No. 136 of the Constitution from the Calcutta High Court) · Bench: O. Chinnappa Reddy, E.S. Venkataramiah and R.B. Misra, JJ. · Judgment delivered by O. Chinnappa Reddy, J., on 21 February 1984

Provenance note. This case appears in the LB-205 case material's contents but no text of it is printed there; the treatment below is reconstructed from the reported judgment (the Supreme Court's own judgment of 21 February 1984 and the Calcutta High Court's judgment of 10 February 1983), and from its use in the prescribed text, S.K. Verma, An Introduction to Public International Law, at pp. 73–74.

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it is the Indian Supreme Court's clearest statement of the qualified incorporation doctrine for customary international law — the rule that custom is part of the law of the land unless it conflicts with an Act of Parliament — coupled with the rule of interpretation that national courts must strive to read statutes so as to avoid a confrontation with international law and treaty obligations. It is also the best Indian illustration of a court using international conventions to construe a domestic statute: the Copyright Act, 1957 was read in the setting of the Berne Convention for the Protection of Literary and Artistic Works, 1886, with the result that “import” in sections 51 and 53 was given a meaning wide enough to cover goods merely in transit through India to Nepal. The case therefore answers the two questions the syllabus asks in its 1998, 2002 and 2013 paper forms: “whether international law is of its own force drawn into the law of the land without the aid of a municipal statute and, second, whether so drawn, it overrides municipal law in case of conflict”.

2. The facts

  • Nepal is land-locked — its only access to the sea is across India. India, by treaty and by international convention, allows a right of transit to facilitate Nepal's international trade (the India–Nepal Treaty of Trade and Treaty of Transit; and the Convention on Transit Trade of Land-Locked States, 1965, to which India was a party).
  • A consignment of pre-recorded audio cassettes was being imported by a Nepali firm, M/s Sungawa Enterprises, from Singapore to Nepal through the port of Calcutta (Calcutta being the entry point for goods destined for Nepal by sea).
  • The Gramophone Company of India Ltd. was the owner of copyright in the recordings embodied in the cassettes — including recordings of well-known performing artists to whom it paid royalties. It learnt that the consignment consisted of pirated copies of its recordings, and that the cassettes lay at Calcutta port awaiting dispatch to Nepal.
  • The Company applied to the Registrar of Copyrights under section 53 of the Copyright Act, 1957 (which empowers the Registrar, on application, to order that infringing copies shall not be imported, and provides that copies to which such an order applies shall be deemed to be goods of which import is prohibited under section 11 of the Customs Act, 1962, and may be confiscated — but shall be delivered to the owner of the copyright, not vesting in the Government).
  • The Registrar was slow to act. The Company moved the Calcutta High Court: the single Judge allowed the writ petition and directed the Registrar to take appropriate action. On appeal, the Division Bench reversed: goods merely in transit to Nepal were not “imported” into India, and it relied on the treaties with Nepal and the “original package doctrine” that goods destined elsewhere do not become part of the mass of the property of the country of transit (Birendra Bahadur Pandey v. Gramophone Co. of India Ltd., AIR 1984 Cal 69). The Supreme Court granted leave and heard the Company's appeal.

3. The issues

  1. Whether goods brought into India from outside India in transit to Nepal are “imported” within the meaning of sections 51 and 53 of the Copyright Act, 1957 — that is, whether “import” is confined to importation for commerce, or extends to importation for transit.
  2. Whether the treaties with Nepal and the 1965 Convention on Transit Trade of Land-Locked States obliged India to allow the passage of the infringing copies, so that the interpretation adopted by the Division Bench was required by international law or, conversely, whether international law permitted India to protect the copyright-owner by seizing them.
  3. What is the position when a rule of international law and an Act of Parliament speak differently?

4. Arguments

For the appellant (Gramophone Company)

No treaty with Nepal required India to permit the transit of infringing goods; the Convention on Transit Trade of Land-Locked States, 1965 itself excepts, from the obligation of freedom of transit, traffic in narcotics and dangerous drugs, arms, and goods restricted for the protection of industrial, literary and artistic property, trade names and the like (Article 2 and its exceptions); the Berne Convention requires that infringing copies shall be liable to seizure in any country of the Union where the work enjoys legal protection; section 53 of the Copyright Act is to be read in the setting of these international conventions; the statute itself defines “import” by reference to the copies it prohibits, and there is nothing in the Act that excepts transit; and the pirated cassettes were in fact believed to be re-entering the Indian market in the guise of transit, to the prejudice of the copyright-owner and the revenue.

For the respondents (importers)

Goods merely in transit are not “brought into the country for the purpose of use, enjoyment, consumption, sale or distribution” and therefore are not “imported”; the object of the Copyright Act is to prevent unauthorised reproduction or exploitation in India, and that object is not frustrated by permitting transit; construing “import” literally would make even airborne goods passing through India liable to confiscation, causing “chaos and confusion”; and the treaties guarantee freedom of transit and exempt transit traffic from duties except reasonable charges.

5. The decision

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal. It held that the word “import” in sections 51 and 53 of the Copyright Act, 1957 means “bringing into India from outside India” and “is not limited to importation for commerce only but includes importation for transit across the country”. The interim custody of the cassettes was dealt with accordingly, and the appeal against the Division Bench's judgment was allowed. The confiscated copies — if confiscation followed — were, under the proviso to section 53, not to vest in the Government but to be delivered to the owner of the copyright.

6. The reasoning

  1. The statute's own definition supplies the answer. “We may look at Sec. 53, rather than elsewhere, to discover the meaning of the word ‘import’. We find that the meaning is stated in that provision itself. If we ask what is not to be imported, we find the answer is copies made out of India which if made in India would infringe copyright. So it follows that ‘import’ in the provision means bringing into India from out of India.” The Court declined to borrow the Customs Act's definition (“bringing into India from a place outside India”) and preferred to read section 53 with section 11 of the Customs Act as a single mechanism.
  2. The international setting decides the choice of meaning. “In interpreting the word ‘import’ in the Copyright Act, we must take note that while the positive requirement of the Copyright Conventions is to protect copyright, negatively also, the Transit Trade Convention and the bilateral Treaty make exceptions enabling the Transit State to take measures to protect Copyright. If this much is borne in mind, it becomes clear that the word ‘import’ in Section 53 of the Copyright Act cannot bear the narrow interpretation sought to be placed upon it to limit it to importation for commerce.” Hence: “Our interpretation, far from being inconsistent with any principle of International law, is entirely in accord with International Conventions and the Treaties between India and Nepal.”
  3. The treaties with Nepal did not require a contrary result. “It appears to us that the Treaty of Trade concerned itself with trade between India and Nepal and not with trade between Nepal and other countries.” The treaties “expressly contain reservations enabling each of the contracting parties to take measures in pursuance of general conventions for the protection of industrial, literary and artistic property”. The Court also observed that the freedom of transit is in the nature of a right of innocent passage — opening the judgment with the words: “Nepal is our neighbour. Unfortunately Nepal is land-locked... So, as one good neighbour to another with a view to ‘maintain, develop and strengthen the friendly relations’ between our two countries, by treaty and by International Convention, we allow a right of innocent passage in order to facilitate Nepal's international trade.”
  4. The international-law doctrine — the passage on which the syllabus turns. “The comity of nations requires that rules of international law may be accommodated in the municipal law even without express legislative sanction... The doctrine of incorporation also recognises the position that the rules of international law are incorporated into national law and considered to be part of the national law unless they are in conflict with an Act of Parliament. Comity of Nations or no, municipal law must prevail in case of conflict. National courts cannot say ‘yes’ if Parliament has said ‘no’ to a principle of international law. National courts will endorse international law but not if it conflicts with national law... But the courts are under an obligation within legitimate limits, so to interpret the municipal statute as to avoid confrontation with the comity of nations or the well-established principles of international law. But if conflict is inevitable, the latter must yield.” (This is the passage extracted by the prescribed text, and the one to quote.) On the facts, no true conflict arose: the international conventions themselves pointed to the same result as the Act properly construed — so the Court did not have to choose between them.
  5. The High Court's own formulation, useful for the transformation point. The Calcutta High Court had observed: “Though treaty may not be binding for construction of statute, it may be used to clarify the expression in statute in case of doubt or two meanings.” The Supreme Court's approach is the same, taken one step further: a statute enacted to give effect to a treaty must be read in the light of the treaty, and the meaning so found is the statutory meaning, not a gloss upon it.

7. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) The doctrine of incorporation applies in India to customary international law — and to the conventions of which a statute is the legislative expression — subject to the supremacy of an Act of Parliament in case of an irreconcilable conflict. (ii) Where a statute admits of two meanings, the court must choose the meaning which accords with India's international obligations and with the international conventions in whose setting the statute was enacted. (iii) “Import” in sections 51 and 53 of the Copyright Act includes importation for transit; the State of transit may enforce copyright against goods merely passing through its territory, because the Transit Trade Convention and the bilateral treaties reserve the power to protect industrial, literary and artistic property. (iv) The treaty partner's freedom of transit is a right of innocent passage, not a licence to import infringing goods.

8. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • Statutory consolidation. The Copyright Act, 1957 has since been amended (notably in 2012) and section 53 now speaks expressly of the power to order that infringing copies shall not be imported; the parallel power in the Customs Act, 1962 (sections 11 and 111) is exercised through notifications under section 11 that cover pirated and counterfeit goods and goods infringing trade marks, patents and designs — the very technique of “deemed prohibition” validated in this case. India's obligations today arise additionally under the TRIPS Agreement (Article 51, suspension of release by customs authorities of counterfeit and pirated goods) and the WIPO Copyright Treaty, 1996, to both of which India's regime responds.
  • The wider doctrine. The case is cited for the proposition — used in PUCL (1997) and referred to in Vellore — that customary international law is part of Indian law “unless it conflicts with an Act of Parliament”; and for the interpretive technique that later matured in Vishaka and Puttaswamy. It is also the Indian member of the family that includes The Paquete Habana (1900) in the United States and Trendtex (1977) in England — each of which received custom while preserving the supremacy of statute.
  • The Nepal transit relationship was renegotiated (the India–Nepal Treaty of Transit, 1999, replacing the 1978 Treaty, with subsequent revisions), and the problem of illicit diversion of goods in transit has been handled by agreement rather than by confiscation — the practical resolution of the tension the case exposed.

9. Relevance to the topic

Gramophone is the case in which the Indian Supreme Court says, in one paragraph, both halves of the classical doctrine: international law is part of the law of the land (the incorporation doctrine), and where an Act of Parliament is against it, municipal law prevails. It therefore demonstrates the qualified monism for custom and firm dualism for anything the legislature has spoken on that characterises Indian practice. Its specific contribution to the syllabus is the technique of “reading the statute in its international setting” — the technique by which a dualist State can honour its obligations without waiting for legislation. In an answer on “how far do Indian courts give effect to international law?” the standard progression should be: Gramophone (interpretation in the international setting) → Jolly George (harmonisation with a covenant) → Vellore (custom as law of the land) → Vishaka (conventions fill a legislative vacuum) → Puttaswamy (the constitutional right construed in conformity with the global human-rights regime).

How to write Gramophone: (i) facts — pirated cassettes from Singapore to Nepal, seized at Calcutta, Registrar of Copyrights, section 53 of the Copyright Act read with section 11 of the Customs Act, single Judge versus Division Bench; (ii) the issue — does “import” include transit? (iii) the answer — yes, because section 53 defines it, and because the Berne Convention and the Transit Trade Convention (with its literary-property exception) point the same way; (iv) the international-law passage — the incorporation doctrine “unless they are in conflict with an Act of Parliament”, and “municipal law must prevail in case of conflict”; (v) the interpretive obligation — courts must construe statutes so as to avoid confrontation with international law; (vi) the aftermath — TRIPS Article 51, the Customs Act notifications, and the case's place in the Indian progression. If the question is on the Blackstonian doctrine (2011, 2013, 2015, 2025 papers), pair Gramophone with Krishna Sharma and Vellore, and with Blackstone, R. v. Keyn, West Rand, Chung Chi Cheung and Trendtex on the English side.

19. Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta

AIR 1990 SC 1692 : 1990 Supp. SCC 545 · Supreme Court of India (Civil Appeal from the Calcutta High Court) · Bench: S. Mukharji, C.J., M.H. Kania, K.J. Shetty, K.N. Saikia and S.C. Agrawal, JJ. · Judgment of 3 May 1990

Provenance note. This case appears in the LB-205 case material's contents but no text of it is printed there; the treatment below is reconstructed from the reported judgment, the High Court judgments under appeal and the treatment in the prescribed text (S.K. Verma, at p. 78), which states the holding in these words: “The Court held that the implementation of these agreements, as far as ‘Teen Bigha’ was concerned, did not amount to cession of the said territory or transfer of sovereignty in respect of the same and did not require any constitutional amendment.”

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it completes the territorial trilogy and states, in the positive, what a servitude is: an arrangement by which a State grants to another State rights over its territory without transferring sovereignty. It is the case that decides that a “lease in perpetuity” of Indian territory for the benefit of a neighbouring State — the Teen Bigha corridor — could be implemented by mere executive action, with neither an Article 3 law nor an Article 368 amendment. It also contains a sharp lesson in the separation of powers: the High Court's direction that the Constitution and laws be amended was set aside as beyond the judicial function. For this topic, it is the case that shows how far the cession rule of Berubari does not reach.

2. The facts

  • The enclaves and the failure of the Ninth Amendment. Under the Agreement of 10 September 1958, the enclaves of Dahagram and Angarpota (Angerpota) were to be exchanged along with other enclaves. The Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960, passed in consequence of the Supreme Court's opinion in Berubari, was to give effect to the exchange — but it never took effect in the eastern sector, because no “appointed day” was ever notified by the Central Government. The Ninth Amendment therefore remained, in the words of the courts, a “dead letter” as regards these territories, with the result that “neither in fact nor in law... [was] there any accession to India in respect of the two enclaves”, and they “remained part of Pakistan (now Bangladesh) as they were before”.
  • 1971 and after. East Pakistan became the independent State of Bangladesh. Dahagram and Angarpota were and remained Bangladesh territory, but they lay inside Indian territory — so Bangladesh could not exercise full sovereignty over them without access through India.
  • The 1974 Agreement (16 May 1974). In the Indo-Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement, it was agreed that instead of exchanging these two enclaves, they would remain with Bangladesh; and, to give Bangladesh access to them, clause 14 provided that India would “lease in perpetuity” to Bangladesh a small area of Indian territory at “Teen Bigha” — a strip (about 178 metres by 85 metres, in the accounts) connecting the enclave of Dahagram with the Panbari mouza of the Bangladesh mainland.
  • The 1982 Agreement (Memorandum of Understanding). Entered into to implement the 1974 Agreement, it provided, among other things: (i) clause 2 — “sovereignty over the leased area shall continue to vest in India”; the rent for the leased area shall be Bangladesh Taka one per annum, which India waived; and (ii) clause 3 — “Bangladesh shall have undisturbed possession and use of the area leased to her in perpetuity”, with India having no jurisdiction over Bangladesh nationals in respect of offences committed in the area.
  • The challenge. The writ petitioners (of whom Sukumar Sengupta was one) challenged the validity of the agreements and the proposed implementation, contending that a “lease in perpetuity” of Indian territory to a foreign State amounted to cession, and that therefore an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368 and/or legislation was necessary; they also contended that the agreements had never been validly ratified and that clause 9 (the jurisdictional provision) reduced India's sovereignty.

3. The judgments below

The single Judge of the Calcutta High Court held that there was “no question of lease or exclusive possession of Bangladesh” of the area, and that conferring powers on Bangladesh and abstaining from the exercise of its own jurisdiction by India did not by itself amount to a transfer of sovereignty — but noted that, without amendment of the existing Indian law, it might not be legally possible to take away the existing jurisdiction of India's law-enforcement agencies and courts. The Division Bench affirmed that there was no cession of territory and no abandonment of sovereignty, but went further and directed that a constitutional amendment and appropriate legislation be undertaken in view of the Ninth Amendment not having come into force. The Union of India appealed against that direction, contending that the Attorney-General had only conceded that if the agreements amounted to cession an amendment would be needed — not that they did.

4. The issues

  1. Whether the 1974 Agreement read with the 1982 Agreement, in so far as they give Bangladesh the use of the Teen Bigha corridor, amount to a cession of Indian territory, a lease, or a surrender of sovereignty.
  2. Whether their implementation therefore requires a constitutional amendment under Article 368 and/or parliamentary legislation, or whether executive action suffices.
  3. Whether the High Court was competent to direct the Union to amend the Constitution or enact laws.

5. Arguments

For the Union of India (appellant)

The agreements do not transfer sovereignty; they confer limited transit rights — an international servitude — and sovereignty over Teen Bigha expressly continues to vest in India (clause 2 of the 1982 agreement). There is no “lease” in the legal sense, because no exclusive or legal possession is transferred: citizens of both countries retain free movement over the corridor, and the rent is a formal one taka per annum which India has waived. No constitutional amendment is needed because there is no cession, and because the Ninth Amendment — the only provision that could have required implementation — never became operative for the eastern sector, no “appointed day” having been notified (A.K. Roy v. Union of India, (1982) 1 SCC 271). The Division Bench exceeded judicial limits by ordering constitutional and legislative changes.

For the respondents (writ petitioners)

A perpetual lease of Indian territory to a foreign State is, in substance, a cession of sovereignty and, on the authority of Berubari, cannot be effected without an amendment under Article 368; the 1974 agreement required formal ratification which never occurred, so the 1982 understanding lacks a legal foundation; and clause 9 of the 1982 agreement — under which India disclaims jurisdiction over offences committed by Bangladesh nationals in the corridor — diminishes India's jurisdiction and thus its sovereignty.

6. The decision

The Supreme Court held that the implementation of the 1974 and 1982 agreements did not amount to cession of any Indian territory to Bangladesh, that there was no transfer of sovereignty, wholly or partially, and that no constitutional amendment was required; it further held that the arrangement did not amount to a lease or a surrender of sovereignty as understood in international law but at most to a servitude suffered by India in its own territory; and it set aside the direction of the Division Bench requiring amendment of the Constitution and legislation, holding that the Division Bench had “transgressed its limits to that extent”. The Court nevertheless noted that the jurisdiction of Indian law-enforcement agencies and courts over the corridor could not be taken away merely by agreement without amendment of the existing Indian law, and recorded that the Government had already taken steps to amend the law — while making clear that “the implementation of the agreements is not dependent on such steps being taken”. It also observed that the litigation was “really a fight over non-issue”.

7. The reasoning

  1. Cession requires an actual and physical transfer plus exclusive rights. The Court defined cession with precision: “Cession as understood in international law would result in an actual and physical transfer of the said area to Bangladesh following which Bangladesh would have the exclusive right to treat the said transferred territory as part of its own territory and exercise full control, dominion and right over the same. This is not the position or the situation which is contemplated under the agreements.”
  2. Sovereignty was expressly retained. Construing clauses 2 and 3 of the 1982 agreement, the Court held that rights granted to Bangladesh — mainly unfettered transit for its citizens, officials and security forces — “were not exclusive and the aggregation thereof [did] not amount to a lease, as is commonly understood, in favour of Bangladesh”; that “no exclusive possession of that area was sought to be transferred to Bangladesh and no legal possession at all was being transferred”; and that “there was no question of transfer of sovereignty, wholly or partially”. What was done was merely “to enable the Government of Bangladesh and its nationals to exercise certain rights in respect of the said area... which otherwise they would not have been entitled to do”.
  3. The factual foundation: Dahagram and Angarpota were never Indian. Following the finding of the High Court, confirmed by the Court as “a finding which is factually concluded”, both de facto and de jure Dahagram and Angarpota remained part of East Pakistan and then Bangladesh. Hence there was “undisputedly... no question of cession of any part or any territory by the agreements of 1974 and 1982”. This is the Ram Kishore Sen reasoning applied to enclaves.
  4. The Ninth Amendment was a dead letter. Since no “appointed day” was notified, the Ninth Amendment had “not come into effect” in the eastern sector; therefore the direction of the Division Bench “to amend the Constitution of India so that Berubari Union No. 12 is not transferred to Bangladesh” was unnecessary and unwarranted.
  5. Servitude, not lease. “The concessions given to Bangladesh over the said area might amount to servitudes suffered by India in its territory as known in international law.” The Court did not have to decide the classification definitively for the result, but the concept — a right in another State's territory falling short of sovereignty — is the analytical key to the case. (Compare the international-law literature's classic examples: rights of passage, fishing rights, and demilitarised zones.)
  6. Judicial restraint. “The Court having held that [the] 9th Amendment to the Constitution has not come into effect and there being no cession of any part or territory or abandonment of sovereignty, there was no cause to direct the legislature to amend or pass suitable laws. The Division Bench transgressed its limits to that extent.” Supporting authority cited: State of Himachal Pradesh v. Umed Ram Sharma, (1986) 2 SCC 68.
  7. The residual caveat (important for accuracy). The Court did not say that the executive could by agreement strip Indian courts and police of jurisdiction: it recorded that “merely by virtue of the agreement and without any amendment of existing Indian law it might not be legally possible to take away existing jurisdiction of the law enforcing agencies of India or the Indian Courts”, and that the Government had agreed to take steps to amend the law — while the implementation of the agreements did not depend on such amendment. This is a neat illustration of the topic's central point: the agreement binds India internationally at once; the internal legal consequences may require legislation; and the two tracks run in parallel.

8. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) A bilateral agreement granting another State rights of transit and use over a strip of Indian territory, with sovereignty expressly retained by India, does not amount to cession and requires no constitutional amendment. (ii) Such an arrangement is, at most, an international servitude, not a lease, because no exclusive or legal possession is transferred, and the rights granted are limited and non-exclusive. (iii) Cession, by contrast, requires an actual and physical transfer and the exclusive right in the transferee to treat the territory as its own. (iv) Courts may not direct the legislature to amend the Constitution: questions of amendment are for Parliament. (v) Where an agreement requires changes in Indian law to make the internal position consistent (for example, jurisdiction over offences in the corridor), the executive must take those steps, but the validity and implementation of the agreement do not depend upon them.

9. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • The corridor functioned. Teen Bigha continued to be used by Bangladesh to reach Dahagram and Angarpota; the jurisdictional arrangements were worked out in practice, and the corridor remains an illustration of a servitude operating in peacetime.
  • The enclave story ended in 2015. After the India–Bangladesh Protocol of 2011, the Constitution (100th Amendment) Act, 2015 gave effect to the Land Boundary Agreement: 111 Indian enclaves were transferred to Bangladesh and 51 Bangladeshi enclaves to India. Dahagram and Angarpota remained with Bangladesh. That the exchange required a constitutional amendment, while the Teen Bigha servitude did not, is the neatest possible confirmation of the distinction drawn in this case.
  • Doctrinal use. Sukumar Sengupta is cited for the proposition that the transfer of territory which was never India's is not cession; for the concept of international servitudes in Indian constitutional law; and for the limits of judicial directions to the legislature. In the 1991 and 2002 DU papers the problem questions on cession and lease of Indian territory to Pakistan and Bangladesh respectively (see the PYQ section) are answerable only by combining Berubari, Ram Kishore Sen and this case.

10. Relevance to the topic

For the monism/dualism debate, Sukumar Sengupta is the leading Indian case on the point that a treaty's validity in international law and its implementation in municipal law are distinct questions — and that the second question is answered by asking what, in substance, the treaty does. If it cedes territory, the Constitution must be amended (Berubari). If it settles a boundary or concedes a servitude, executive action suffices (Ram Kishore Sen, Sukumar Sengupta). The case is also authority for the proposition that the executive's treaty-making power under Articles 53 and 73 extends to agreements of this kind, and that the courts will not second-guess the wisdom or the internal legal housekeeping of such agreements save where the Constitution so requires.

20. Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India

(1996) 5 SCC 647 : AIR 1996 SC 2715 · Supreme Court of India (Writ Petition under Article 32) · Bench: Kuldip Singh and Faizan Uddin, JJ. · Judgment delivered by Kuldip Singh, J. · DU case material, pp. 59–62

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it is the prescribed authority for the proposition that rules of customary international law are part of the law of the land in India — the high-water mark of the incorporation doctrine in Indian environmental jurisprudence. Where Gramophone said that custom is received “unless it is in conflict with an Act of Parliament”, Vellore did something more: it identified two specific principles of customary international law — the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle — and declared that “once these principles are accepted as part of customary international law, there would be no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law”. It is therefore the case that answers the examination question, “How far have Indian courts given effect to customary international law?”

2. The facts

  • A public interest petition was filed under Article 32 by the Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum, directed against pollution caused by the “enormous discharge of untreated effluent” by the tanneries and other industries in the State of Tamil Nadu.
  • The tanneries were discharging untreated effluent into agricultural fields, roadsides, waterways and open lands; the untreated effluent was finally discharged into the River Palar, “the main source of water supply to the residents of the area”. The entire surface and subsoil water of the river had been polluted, with the result that potable water was not available to the residents.
  • According to a preliminary survey by the Tamil Nadu Agricultural University Research Centre, Vellore, nearly 35,000 hectares of agricultural land in the tanneries belt had become either partially or totally unfit for cultivation.
  • The tanneries use about 170 types of chemicals in the chrome tanning processes — including sodium chloride, lime, sodium sulphate, chromium sulphate, fat, liquor, ammonia and sulphuric acid, besides dyes — in large quantities; and nearly 35 litres of water are used for processing one kilogram of finished leather, resulting in “dangerously enormous quantities of toxic effluents being let out in the open”. These effluents “have spoiled the physico-chemical properties of the soil and have contaminated groundwater by percolation”.
  • A list annexed to the affidavit of the Deputy Secretary, Environment and Forests Department, Tamil Nadu, dated 21 July 1992, identified 59 affected villages in the three divisions of Thirupathur, Vellore and Ranipet, where there was an acute shortage of drinking water and the Government was making alternative arrangements for supply.
  • The counterweight: the leather industry in India had become a major foreign exchange earner, with Tamil Nadu the leading exporter of finished leather, accounting for approximately 80 per cent of the country's exports, and providing employment. The Court acknowledged this and then held: the industry “has no right to destroy the ecology, degrade the environment and pose as a health-hazard. It cannot be permitted to expand or even to continue with the present production unless it tackles by itself the problem of pollution created by the said industry.”

3. The issues

  1. Whether the tanneries and other industries could be permitted to continue discharging untreated effluent, having regard to the damage to land, water and health, balanced against their economic contribution.
  2. Whether the “precautionary principle” and the “polluter pays principle” are part of the law of India.
  3. Whether “sustainable development” is a norm of customary international law, and if so with what content.
  4. What remedial and institutional measures were required — in particular whether an authority should be constituted under section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 to implement these principles and to award compensation and the cost of restoration.

4. Arguments

For the petitioner (Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum)

Untreated effluents have made 35,000 hectares unfit for cultivation and polluted the River Palar and the groundwater; 59 villages face an acute drinking-water shortage; the industries are liable for the damage independently of whether they took reasonable care; the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle are recognised principles of international environmental law and should be given effect by Indian courts; and the State Government and its pollution control board have failed to act.

For the tanneries and industries (and the State)

The industry is a vital foreign-exchange earner and a large employer, contributing some 80 per cent of India's finished-leather exports; the technical and financial burden of treatment plants is heavy; closure would destroy the livelihood of thousands of workers; and pollution control measures were being taken progressively, with the State providing time and facilities.

5. The decision

The Court held that sustainable development is a part of customary international law, that its essential features — the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle — are part of the law of the land in India, and that the industries were absolutely liable for the harm caused and for the cost of restoring the environment. Concretely, the Court: (i) directed the Central Government to constitute an authority under section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 with the power to implement the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle, to assess the loss, to recover the cost of remediation and to order closure where necessary; (ii) directed that compensation be paid to the affected villagers and that steps be taken to provide potable water; (iii) required the tanneries to set up effluent treatment (including Common Effluent Treatment Plants) as a condition of continuing operation; (iv) required the State to ensure compliance on pain of closure; and (v) required measures to be adopted by all States on the same model, and the principles to be applied throughout the country. The Court's directions also contemplated the publication of the authority's orders and the payment of compensation to the “loss of ecology” caused.

6. The reasoning

  1. The environmental framework first. The Court set out the constitutional and statutory anchors: Article 21 (right to life — including the right to a healthy environment), Article 47 (duty of the State to raise the level of nutrition and standard of living and improve public health), Article 48A (duty of the State to protect and improve the environment) and Article 51A(g) (fundamental duty of every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment); the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974, the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981 and the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; and the principle of “absolute liability” laid down in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum gas leak), (1987) 1 SCC 395.
  2. Sustainable development: from Stockholm to Rio. “The traditional concept that development and ecology are opposed to each other is no longer acceptable. ‘Sustainable Development’ is the answer.” The Court traced the concept: the Stockholm Declaration, 1972 (where it came to be known internationally); the report of the World Commission on Environment and Development, “Our Common Future”, 1987 (the Brundtland Report, so called after Ms G.H. Brundtland, then Prime Minister of Norway); “Caring for the Earth”, 1991 (World Conservation Union, UNEP and WWF — a strategy for sustainable living); and the Earth Summit at Rio, June 1992, which saw two conventions signed by 153 nations (on biological diversity and on climate change) and three non-binding documents approved by consensus (a Statement on Forestry Principles, a declaration of principles on environmental policy and development initiatives, and Agenda 21). The Brundtland definition was adopted: “Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of the future generations to meet their own needs.” And the holding: “We have no hesitation in holding that ‘Sustainable Development’ as a balancing concept between ecology and development has been accepted as a part of the customary international law though its salient features have yet to be finalised by the international law jurists.”
  3. Its salient principles. “Some of the salient principles of ‘Sustainable Development’, as culled out from [the] Brundtland Report and other international documents, are Inter-Generational Equity, Use and Conservation of Natural Resources, Environmental Protection, the Precautionary Principle, Polluter Pays Principle, Obligation to Assist and Cooperate, Eradication of Poverty and Financial Assistance to the developing countries. We are, however, of the view that ‘The Precautionary Principle’ and ‘The Polluter Pays Principle’ are essential features of ‘Sustainable Development’.”
  4. The precautionary principle defined for municipal law. “The ‘Precautionary Principle’ — in the context of the municipal law — means: (i) Environmental measures — by the State Government and the statutory authorities — must anticipate, prevent and attack the causes of environmental degradation. (ii) Where there are threats of serious and irreversible damage, lack of scientific certainty should not be used as a reason for postponing measures to prevent environmental degradation. (iii) The ‘onus of proof’ is on the actor or the developer/industrialist to show that his action is environmentally benign.” (Note the third element: the burden of proof shifts — a procedural revolution in environmental litigation.)
  5. The polluter pays principle defined. The Court adopted its earlier decision in Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India, (1996) 3 SCC 212: “any principle evolved in this behalf should be simple, practical and suited to the conditions obtaining in this country”; and the rule that “once the activity carried on is hazardous or inherently dangerous, the person carrying on such activity is liable to make good the loss caused to any other person by his activity irrespective of the fact whether he took reasonable care while carrying on his activity. The rule is premised upon the very nature of the activity carried on.” Consequently the polluting industries are “absolutely liable to compensate for the harm caused by them to villagers in the affected area, to the soil and to the underground water”, and are bound to take all necessary measures to remove sludge and other pollutants. And the extension: “The ‘Polluter Pays Principle’ as interpreted by this Court means that the absolute liability for harm to the environment extends not only to compensate the victims of pollution but also the cost of restoring the environmental degradation. Remediation of the damaged environment is part of the process of ‘Sustainable Development’...”
  6. The incorporation holding — the sentence for which the case is prescribed. “[O]nce these principles are accepted as part of Customary International law there would be no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law. It is almost an accepted proposition of law that the rules of customary international law which are not contrary to the municipal law shall be deemed to have been incorporated in the domestic law and shall be followed by the Courts of Law.” This is the same proposition as in PUCL v. Union of India and it is the Indian application of Blackstone and of Trendtex.

7. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) Sustainable development, as a balancing concept between ecology and development, is part of customary international law. (ii) Its essential features — the precautionary principle (with its shifted onus of proof) and the polluter pays principle — are therefore part of the law of the land in India, no legislation being required, because customary international law not contrary to municipal law is deemed incorporated. (iii) An industry engaged in a hazardous activity is absolutely and strictly liable for harm, irrespective of reasonable care, and its liability extends to the cost of remediating the environment as well as compensating the victims. (iv) Where existing institutions are inadequate, the Court may direct the creation of a statutory authority under section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 to implement these principles. (v) Economic importance is no defence: an industry “has no right to destroy the ecology”.

8. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • Institutional follow-up. The Loss of Ecology (Prevention and Payment of Compensation) Authority was constituted under section 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 pursuant to these directions, and the Supreme Court has since supervised its assessment of compensation (including orders in 2003–2009 requiring the tanneries to pay for the loss of ecology and the failure to comply).
  • Constitutionalisation. The principles of Vellore were absorbed into Indian law by a line of cases: A.P. Pollution Control Board v. Prof. M.V. Nayudu, (1999) 2 SCC 718 (the precautionary principle explained and the burden of proof discussed, drawing on the Rio Declaration and international practice); M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath, (1997) 1 SCC 388 (the public trust doctrine); A.P. Pollution Control Board II v. M.V. Nayudu, (2001) 2 SCC 62; and Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Natural Resource Policy v. Union of India, (2005) 13 SCC 186 and others. The result is that the precautionary and polluter pays principles and sustainable development are today treated as part of Article 21 jurisprudence.
  • Statutory consolidation. The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 (section 20) expressly requires the Tribunal to apply “the principles of sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle” in deciding environmental disputes — the legislative adoption, in one phrase, of what Vellore had judicially received from customary international law.
  • Industrial consequence. Common Effluent Treatment Plants, reverse osmosis plants and zero-liquid-discharge regimes in the tannery clusters of Ranipet, Vellore and Thirupathur are the direct descendants of this litigation; the Court's direction that the polluter, not the taxpayer, must pay for remediation has become the fiscal principle of Indian environmental regulation.

9. Relevance to the topic

Vellore is the Indian case that most neatly demonstrates the reception of custom without legislation. It differs from Gramophone and Jolly George in one crucial respect: there the court used an international norm to construe a statute; here it applied international custom as law, and used it to impose liability and to create an institution. Read with Vishaka, it shows the two channels by which international law enters Indian law without an implementing statute: custom is received (Vellore, following PUCL and Krishna Sharma); treaty provisions are read in as interpretation (Vishaka, following Jolly George). Read with Puttaswamy, it completes a triad of techniques now standard in Indian constitutional adjudication. Its limits are equally instructive: the Court itself noted that the salient features of sustainable development “have yet to be finalised by the international law jurists” — an acknowledgement that the content of a custom may be controversial even while its normative force is accepted; and it accepted the norm only because it did not conflict with Indian municipal law (indeed, the Indian statutes and the Constitution pointed the same way).

How to write Vellore: (i) facts — tanneries in Tamil Nadu, untreated effluent into fields and the River Palar, 35,000 hectares ruined, 59 villages without drinking water, 170 chemicals, 35 litres of water per kg of leather, 80 per cent of India's leather exports; (ii) the international-law spine — Stockholm 1972 → Brundtland 1987 → Caring for the Earth 1991 → Rio 1992; sustainable development as customary international law; (iii) the two principles defined (precautionary principle with its three limbs and shifted onus; polluter pays with absolute liability and remediation costs); (iv) the incorporation holding — “once these principles are accepted as part of customary international law there would be no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law”; (v) the domestic anchors — Articles 21, 47, 48A, 51A(g) and the three environmental statutes plus absolute liability from the Oleum gas leak case; (vi) the aftermath — the Loss of Ecology Authority, the NGT Act 2010 section 20, and A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu.

21. Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan ★ highlighted in the syllabus as stressed by the teacher

AIR 1997 SC 3011 : (1997) 6 SCC 241 · Supreme Court of India (Writ Petition (Criminal) under Article 32) · Bench: J.S. Verma, C.J., Sujata V. Manohar and B.N. Kirpal, JJ. · Judgment delivered by Verma, C.J., on 13 August 1997 · DU case material, pp. 63–70

1. Why this case is highlighted in the syllabus

Because it is the single most important Indian authority on the use of international conventions to fill a legislative vacuum; because it converted an unlegislated treaty (CEDAW) into binding Indian law through the mechanism of Article 141; and because it produced the Vishaka Guidelines, which governed every Indian workplace for sixteen years until Parliament enacted the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013. For this topic it is the case that answers, definitively, whether India is “monist” or “dualist”: neither as a matter of doctrine, but a working combination of the two — the treaty could not be enforced as such, yet it supplied the content of the Constitution itself. The teacher's highlighting is a signal: expect it as a compulsory or short-note question.

2. The facts

  • The writ petition was filed by social activists and non-governmental organisations, as a class action, for the enforcement of the fundamental rights of working women under Articles 14, 19 and 21 of the Constitution, “in view of the prevailing climate in which the violation of these rights is not uncommon”, and with the aim of “focussing attention towards this societal aberration, and assisting in finding suitable methods for realisation of the true concept of ‘gender equality’” and of preventing sexual harassment of working women at all workplaces “through judicial process, to fill the vacuum in existing legislation”.
  • The immediate cause for the petition was “an incident of alleged brutal gang rape of a social worker in a village of Rajasthan”. The Court noted that the incident was the subject-matter of a separate criminal action and that no further mention of it was necessary — but observed that it “reveals the hazards to which a working woman may be exposed and the depravity to which sexual harassment can degenerate and the urgency for safeguards by an alternative mechanism in the absence of legislative measures”.
  • (The background, well known to students even though the judgment keeps it anonymous, is the gang rape of the social worker Bhanwari Devi in 1992 while she was working on a State-sponsored programme against child marriage in Rajasthan — the incident that made the absence of any law or mechanism on workplace sexual harassment intolerable.)
  • There was at the time no domestic law defining or providing a mechanism against sexual harassment at the workplace; the Indian Penal Code dealt with certain offences but not with the workplace relationship as such.

3. The issues

  1. Whether sexual harassment of a working woman violates her fundamental rights under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21, so as to attract the remedy under Article 32.
  2. Whether, in the absence of domestic legislation, the Court could lay down binding guidelines to fill the vacuum — and, if so, on what basis and with what authority under the Constitution.
  3. What the content of such guidelines should be, and what the position of the international conventions (CEDAW and the Beijing documents) is in giving them content.

4. Arguments

For the petitioners

Gender equality and the right to work with human dignity are guaranteed by Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21; the right to carry on an occupation depends on the availability of a safe working environment; India having ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) and participated in the Fourth World Conference on Women (Beijing, 1995), the contents of those international instruments must inform the meaning of the constitutional guarantees; in the absence of legislation, this Court should lay down guidelines to be observed at all workplaces, exercising its power under Article 32 read with Article 141.

For the respondents (the State of Rajasthan and others)

The incident was already the subject of criminal proceedings; the matter was for the legislature, not the courts; no enforceable statutory duty existed; and the Court should not legislate, the proper course being to await the statute that Parliament might enact.

5. The decision

The Court held that sexual harassment of a working woman violates Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21, and — there being no domestic law occupying the field — laid down the “Vishaka Guidelines”, to be treated as law under Article 141 and binding and enforceable in all courts “until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field”. It defined sexual harassment (including unwelcome sexually determined behaviour, physical contact and advances, a demand or request for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature), and required employers and other responsible persons to provide a mechanism: an express prohibition of sexual harassment notified and circulated; preventive and remedial measures including disciplinary action and criminal proceedings where the conduct amounted to an offence under the IPC; a Complaints Committee in every workplace, with a woman as head, with third-party or NGO participation, to hear complaints and to allow the complainant to seek support or to have a person of her choice present; a prohibition on victimisation of the complainant; and the extension of all of this to the unorganised sector, to establishments and to third parties. On the facts, the Court disposed of the petition with these directions and discharged the notice.

6. The reasoning — and the international-law engine of it

  1. The rights violated. “Each such incident results in violation of the fundamental rights of ‘Gender Equality’ and the ‘Right to Life and Liberty’. It is a clear violation of the rights under Articles 14, 15 and 21 of the Constitution. One of the logical consequences of such an incident is also the violation of the victim's fundamental right under Article 19(1)(g)... The fundamental right to carry on any occupation, trade or profession depends on the availability of a ‘safe’ working environment. Right to life means life with dignity.”
  2. The constitutional hooks for using international law. The Court set out in terms the provisions that permit and require the use of international norms — the Directive Principle in Article 51(c) (“foster respect for international law and treaty obligations”), the enabling power of Article 253 (Parliament may legislate to implement treaties), Entry 14 of the Union List (“entering into treaties... and implementing of treaties”), and the supporting provisions on work and dignity — Article 42 (just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief) and Article 51A(a) and (e) (including the duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women).
  3. The gateway passage. “In the absence of domestic law occupying the field, to formulate effective measures to check the evil of sexual harassment of working women at all workplaces, the contents of international conventions and norms are significant for the purpose of interpretation of the guarantee of gender equality, right to work with human dignity in Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution and the safeguards against sexual harassment implicit therein. Any international convention not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and in harmony with its spirit must be read into these provisions to enlarge the meaning and content thereof, to promote the object of the constitutional guarantee. This is implicit from Article 51(c) and the enabling power of Parliament to enact laws for implementing the international conventions... by virtue of Article 253 read with Entry 14 of the Union List...”
  4. The international instruments relied on. CEDAW — particularly the obligation to take all appropriate measures to eliminate discrimination against women in the field of employment (including the right to work, to equal opportunities, to free choice of profession, to equal remuneration, and to protection of health and safety in working conditions), and its mandate to eliminate prejudices and customary practices based on the idea of the inferiority of women; the General Recommendations of the CEDAW Committee (including General Recommendation No. 19, treating gender-based violence as a form of discrimination); the Fourth World Conference on Women (Beijing, 1995) and its Declaration and Platform for Action; and the Beijing Statement of Principles of the Independence of the Judiciary (1995) — the latter for the proposition that the judiciary must not remain passive where rights are violated for want of legislation. India's ratification of CEDAW is the foundation; the Court noted that it is a party — and that international conventions create “legitimate expectations” of their observance in the absence of contrary legislative provisions.
  5. Why the Court could make guidelines that bind. Two reasons: (i) the vacuum — the field was unoccupied by any domestic law on the point, so there was no statutory text with which the international norm could conflict; and (ii) the source of binding force — Article 141, under which “the law declared by the Supreme Court shall be binding on all courts within the territory of India”. Hence the guidelines operate as law until Parliament legislates, at which point they yield (which is what happened in 2013).
  6. The technique's limits — state them. The Court did not say that CEDAW was enforceable as such, nor that it overrode any statute; and it expressly rested its power on the absence of legislation. Had there been a conflicting statute, Jolly George and Gramophone tell us the statute would have prevailed, and the remedy would have been legislative. This is why the case supports, rather than undermines, the “dualist in form, permeable in practice” description of Indian practice.

8. The principle evolved

Ratio. (i) Sexual harassment of a working woman violates Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 of the Constitution, and a safe working environment is an incident of the right to carry on an occupation. (ii) Where there is no domestic law occupying the field, the contents of international conventions and norms — here CEDAW and the Beijing documents — are significant for the interpretation of the constitutional guarantees, and an international convention not inconsistent with fundamental rights and in harmony with their spirit must be read into Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 so as to enlarge their meaning and content. This follows from Article 51(c) and from Article 253 read with Entry 14 of the Union List. (iii) Ratification of such conventions creates “legitimate expectations” of their observance in the absence of contrary legislative provisions. (iv) In the absence of legislation, the Court may lay down guidelines which operate as law under Article 141 until Parliament legislates. (v) The Court thereby filled a vacuum without overriding any statute — the interpretive, not the monopolistic, use of international law.

9. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • The Guidelines governed Indian workplaces for sixteen years. They were applied and enforced through subsequent orders, most notably Apparel Export Promotion Council v. A.K. Chopra, (1999) 1 SCC 759 (sexual harassment was held to violate Articles 14, 15 and 21; the employer's obligation was recognised; and the Court set aside the punishment of censure as grossly inadequate) and Medha Kotwal Lele v. Union of India, (2013) 1 SCC 297 (directions for the effective implementation of the guidelines, with remedies for breach).
  • Legislation at last — the fate the Court itself anticipated. Parliament enacted the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 with the rules framed thereunder, which now occupy the field. The statutory definition of “sexual harassment” (section 2(n)) and the machinery of the Internal Complaints Committee and Local Committees (sections 4 and 6) are the statutory descendants of the Vishaka Guidelines; the guidelines ceased to have the force of law under Article 141 in so far as the Act occupies the field, and the Court's declaration has been replaced by national legislation — precisely the sequence the judgment contemplated: “until suitable legislation is enacted”.
  • Interpretive afterlife. The Vishaka technique has been applied repeatedly: to children's rights (M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu, (1996) 6 SCC 756, on child labour); to the rights of the disabled (NALSA v. Union of India); to the rights of sex workers and of persons living with HIV/AIDS; to prisoners' rights; and most recently to privacy in Puttaswamy. In Puttaswamy the Court cites Vishaka as an instance of the “interpretive principle” that constitutional provisions must be read consistently with India's international obligations.
  • International-law consequence. Had India not legislated, she would have remained in breach of CEDAW — the guidelines were a domestic remedy against an international exposure. This is the perfect illustration of the topic's closing theme: judicial creativity is not a substitute for legislation but a bridge to it.

10. Relevance to the topic

Vishaka demonstrates the interpretive entry of an unincorporated treaty. It is rightly placed together with Vellore: the two cases together show that international law enters Indian law without legislation by one of two routes — as custom received as law (Vellore, PUCL) or as convention read into a constitutional guarantee (Vishaka), always subject to the twin limits that it must not conflict with fundamental rights and that legislation, when it comes, occupies the field. The case also shows how an Indian court can give effect to a treaty obligation even under the transformation theory, because it never enforces the treaty: it enforces the Constitution, in the light of the treaty. Read with Berubari and Gramophone, it completes the picture of Indian practice: Berubari (ratify — but cession needs an amendment), Jolly George (ratify — but enforcement needs legislation), Gramophone (conventions interpret statutes), Vishaka (ratify — and the Constitution will be read in their light).

How to write Vishaka: (i) facts — class action by NGOs after a gang rape in Rajasthan; no domestic law on workplace sexual harassment; (ii) rights — Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g), 21 (safe working environment; life with dignity), plus Articles 42, 51A(e); (iii) the international-law engine — CEDAW, the Beijing Conference documents and the Beijing Statement on Judicial Independence; Article 51(c), Article 253 read with Entry 14, List I; (iv) the gateway passage — “any international convention not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and in harmony with its spirit must be read into these provisions to enlarge the meaning and content thereof”; (v) the guidelines — definition plus the Complaints Committee machinery; binding under Article 141 until legislation; (vi) aftermath — Apparel Export Promotion Council, Medha Kotwal Lele, the POSH Act 2013 and the continued use of the technique in Puttaswamy. For a short-note question, structure it as: Vishaka Guidelines → Article 141 → POSH Act 2013.

22. Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (the Privacy case; p. 71 of the case material)

(2017) 6 SCC 235 : AIR 2017 SC 4161 · Supreme Court of India (Writ Petitions under Article 32; nine-judge Bench) · Judgment delivered by Dr. D.Y. Chandrachud, J. · Decided 24 August 2017 · The relevant passages in the DU case material are paragraphs 1–2 and 129–133, at pp. 71–73 (the caption in the case material carries an obvious slip in the year, printing “2004”; the citation of the judgment is (2017) 6 SCC 235)

1. Why this case is in the syllabus

Because it is the most recent statement of the Indian Supreme Court's method for using international law, and it states the rule of priority and the rule of interpretation in one paragraph. Where Vishaka used a convention to fill a vacuum, and Vellore received a custom as law, Puttaswamy uses India's treaty commitments — the Universal Declaration and the ICCPR, together with the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — as an interpretive principle for the Constitution itself, and then states the conflict rule explicitly: where international law contradicts a domestic statute, the Court gives effect to the latter; where there is no contradiction, the Constitution must be read so as to enhance its conformity with the global human rights regime. The page reference (p. 71) in the syllabus is to paragraphs 129–133 of the judgment as printed, which is why the case material extracts only those paragraphs.

2. The facts and the proceedings

  • The petitions challenged the constitutional validity of the Aadhaar scheme — the collection, storage and use of biometric and demographic data of residents — and, at a deeper level, raised the question whether the Constitution protects an individual's right to privacy at all.
  • A three-judge Bench in 2015 referred the question to a larger Bench, because two earlier decisions — M.P. Sharma v. Satish Chandra, AIR 1954 SC 300, and Kharak Singh v. State of U.P., AIR 1963 SC 1295 — held (or were read as holding) that privacy is not a fundamental right, while Gobind v. State of M.P., (1975) 2 SCC 148, had taken a different view.
  • Nine judges assembled in 2017 to determine “whether privacy is a constitutionally protected value”. The Court observed that the issue “reaches out to the foundation of a constitutional culture based on the protection of human rights” and that if privacy is a protected value, it “would redefine in significant ways our concepts of liberty and the entitlements that flow out of its protection” — noting that privacy, in its simplest sense, allows each human being “to be left alone in a core which is inviolable”, while the autonomy of the individual is conditioned by relationships with society and the overarching presence of State and non-State entities.
  • The holding: privacy is a fundamental right, intrinsic to the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 and to the freedoms guaranteed by Part III; M.P. Sharma and Kharak Singh were overruled to the extent of any contrary view. The Aadhaar questions themselves were then decided in the follow-up judgment, K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar) v. Union of India, (2019) 1 SCC 1, which upheld the Aadhaar scheme in part and struck down several features of it. The paragraphs printed in the DU materials (129–133) are the Court's consideration of “India's commitments under International law”.

3. The issues relevant to this topic

  1. What is the significance of India's international obligations — under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948 and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, 1966 — in construing the constitutional right to life and personal liberty?
  2. What is the rank of international law as against a domestic statute and against the Constitution itself?

4. The reasoning — a paragraph-by-paragraph account of ¶¶129–133

Table 3.18 — Puttaswamy ¶¶129–133: India's commitments under international law

¶129The constitutional hook and the instruments. “The recognition of privacy as a fundamental constitutional value is part of India's commitment to a global human rights regime.” Article 51 of the Constitution — a Directive Principle — requires the State to endeavour to “foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organized peoples with one another”. Then the instruments: Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (“No one shall be subjected to arbitrary interference with his privacy, family, home or correspondence, nor to attacks upon his honour and reputation. The Court then turns to the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, “enacted by Parliament [which] refers to the ICCPR as a human rights instrument”: section 2(1)(d) defines “human rights” as “the rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in the International Covenants and enforceable by courts in India”; section 2(1)(f) defines “International Covenants” as the ICCPR, the ICESCR and such other Covenant or Convention adopted by the General Assembly as the Central Government may specify; and section 12(f) entrusts the National Human Rights Commission with the function of “studying treaties and other international instruments on human rights and making recommendations for their effective implementation”. This is the point most students miss: Parliament itself, in 1993, gave international human rights instruments a statutory role in Indian law.
¶130The tripartite obligation of the ICCPR. “The ICCPR casts an obligation on states to respect, protect and fulfil its norms. The duty of a State to respect mandates that it must not violate the right. The duty to protect mandates that the government must protect it against interference by private parties. The duty to fulfil postulates that government must take steps towards realization of a right.” The Court then invoked the Human Rights Committee's General Comment 16 on the right to privacy (“there is universal recognition of the fundamental importance, and enduring relevance, of the right to privacy and of the need to ensure that it is safeguarded, in law and practice”), and the report of the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights presented on 30 June 2014 to the same effect.
¶131The earlier Indian cases. In Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab, the Court had considered the obligations assumed by India under the ICCPR following ratification and held that the requirements of Article 6 of the ICCPR are substantially similar to the guarantees contained in Articles 20 and 21 of the Constitution, and that the penal law of India was in accord with India's international commitments. In Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi, (1981) 1 SCC 608, the Court had held that “there is implicit in Article 21 the right to protection against torture or cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment which is enunciated in Article 5 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and guaranteed by Article 7 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights”. And in NALSA v. Union of India, (2014) 5 SCC 438, dealing with the rights of transgender persons, the Court found that the international conventions were not inconsistent with the fundamental rights guaranteed by the Constitution and “must be recognised and followed”.
¶132The technique identified. “In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, this Court observed that in the absence of domestic law, the Convention on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) is applicable. In NALSA... the international conventions were not inconsistent with the fundamental rights guaranteed by the Constitution and must be recognised and followed.” — the Court thus lines up the Indian authorities in a single method: international instruments inform, and are followed in, the absence of inconsistent domestic law.
¶133The rule — quote it verbatim. “The position in law is well settled. Where there is a contradiction between international law and a domestic statute, the Court would give effect to the latter. In the present case, there is no contradiction between the international obligations which have been assumed by India and the Constitution. The Court will not readily presume any inconsistency. On the contrary, constitutional provisions must be read and interpreted in a manner which would enhance their conformity with the global human rights regime. India is a responsible member of the international community and the Court must adopt an interpretation which abides by the international commitments made by the country, particularly where its constitutional and statutory mandates indicate no deviation. In fact, the enactment of the Human Rights Act by Parliament would indicate a legislative desire to implement the human rights regime founded on constitutional values and international conventions acceded to by India.”

5. The decision and the principle evolved

Ratio (so far as it concerns this topic). (i) Parliament's own statutes — here the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — recognise the ICCPR and the ICESCR as containing “human rights... enforceable by courts in India”; the constitutional commitment in Article 51(c) and this legislative recognition together provide the basis for the interpretive use of international instruments. (ii) The ICCPR imposes on States the triple obligation to respect, protect and fulfil rights — a framework now routinely used in Indian constitutional adjudication. (iii) Conflict rule: between international law and a domestic statute, the Court gives effect to the statute. Interpretive rule: absent a contradiction, the Court will not presume inconsistency, and will read the Constitution so as to “enhance [its] conformity with the global human rights regime”. (iv) Accordingly, privacy is a constitutionally protected value, informed by Article 12 UDHR and Article 17 ICCPR.

6. What happened next — the living aftermath

  • The Aadhaar decision. In K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar-5J) v. Union of India, (2019) 1 SCC 1, the Court upheld the constitutionality of the Aadhaar Act in substance, applying the four-fold proportionality test articulated in the 2017 judgment (legality, legitimate State aim, proportionality, procedural safeguards), while striking down features such as the mandatory linking of Aadhaar for school admissions and mobile connections.
  • The data-protection statute. The judgment's insistence on a data-protection regime contributed directly to the enactment of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — the statutory counterpart, at last, of the constitutional right.
  • The method's continuation. Indian courts now routinely use CEDAW (against child marriage and in employment cases), the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, the ICCPR and the ICESCR in constitutional adjudication — the “interpretive principle” of Vishaka and Puttaswamy — while continuing to accept that a statute, and certainly the Constitution, prevails over an inconsistent international rule (Jolly George; Gramophone).

7. Relevance to the topic

Puttaswamy is the syllabus's own answer to the most modern form of the question. It does three things: (i) it treats international law as a legitimate constitutional source of meaning for fundamental rights; (ii) it states the hierarchical rule (statute over international law; Constitution over both; no presumption of inconsistency); and (iii) it identifies a legislative bridge — the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 — through which international human-rights instruments are already, by Parliament's own choice, part of the law administered by Indian courts. The result is a working synthesis of the two theories: monism in interpretation, dualism in hierarchy. That phrase is worth writing, and it is the phrase with which this topic should be concluded.

Answer 1 — 2025 Q.5 (and 2015 Q.6): the Blackstonian doctrine and the practice of applicability of treaty law and customary law in India

Question. “Explain the relevance of the Blackstonian doctrine while deciding the relationship between international law and municipal law. What is the practice of applicability of international treaty law and customary law in India vis-à-vis the Constitutional provisions of India? Elucidate your answer with the help of cases.” (2025, Q.5, 20 marks; the same question was set in 2015, Q.6)

Structure of the answer: Part I — the doctrine; Part II — its qualification in England; Part III — its reception in India through Article 372(1); Part IV — the practice for custom in India, with cases; Part V — the practice for treaties in India, with cases and constitutional provisions; Part VI — conclusion: the doctrine survives in a qualified form, subject to statute and to the Constitution.

Part I — What the Blackstonian doctrine is. Sir William Blackstone, in his Commentaries on the Laws of England (Book IV, ch. 5), wrote that “the law of nations... is here adopted in its full extent by the common law, and is held to be part of the law of the land”. This is the doctrine of incorporation. Its premises are that customary international law is not foreign law requiring proof, but the law of the land, applied by the courts of its own force (ex proprio vigore), to be taken judicial notice of without expert evidence, and to be construed so as to avoid a conflict with statute. The doctrine was applied in England from the eighteenth century (Buvot v. Barbuit (1737); Triquet v. Bath (1764), Lord Mansfield; Heathfield v. Chilton (1767); and Emperor of Austria v. Day and Kossuth (1861)). Its theoretical twin is monism: the doctrine is the practical face of the monist thesis that international law and municipal law form a single legal order, an idea later systematised by Kelsen in the Grundnorm hierarchy and defended by Lauterpacht, Fitzmaurice and Starke.

Part II — Its qualification in English practice. English courts did not apply the doctrine without limits. In R. v. Keyn (The Franconia), (1876) 2 Ex. D. 63, the court refused to apply the customary three-mile jurisdiction rule, holding that it was “for the Parliament to legislate” — and Parliament did so, by the Territorial Waters Jurisdiction Act, 1878. In West Rand Central Gold Mining Co. v. R. (1905) 2 KB 391, the court accepted custom as part of English law but only if Britain had assented to it and it was not “contrary to the principles of her laws as declared by her courts”. In Chung Chi Cheung v. The King (1939) AC 160, Lord Atkin stated the now-classical formula: international law is incorporated “so far as it is not inconsistent with rules enacted by statutes or finally declared by their tribunals”. The modern position, after Trendtex Trading Corporation v. Central Bank of Nigeria (1977) QB 529 (where Shaw L.J. explained that stare decisis cannot freeze a changing rule) and J.H. Rayner v. Department of Trade and Industry (1990) 2 AC 418, is that custom is received subject to two qualifications — (i) it must not be inconsistent with a British statute, earlier or later; and (ii) it must not be contrary to a rule already laid down by the highest court — with further exceptions for Acts of State and matters of the Crown's prerogative (recognition, diplomatic immunity), where the courts follow executive certificates.

Part III — Its reception in India. India had no reception clause in the Constitution, but the doctrine was already in force in British India and Article 372(1) continued “all the laws in force in the territory of India immediately before the commencement of this Constitution... until altered or repealed or amended”. Through this saving clause the common-law rule of incorporation survives; authority: T.K. Varred v. State of Travancore-Cochin, AIR 1956 SC 142. The other constitutional provisions relevant to the practice are Article 51(c) (the Directive Principle requiring the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations — the hook for interpretation, in Vishaka and Puttaswamy), Article 253 (the enabling power of Parliament to legislate to implement treaties, overriding the federal distribution), and Entries 10, 13 and 14 of List I. It should be candidly stated, as the prescribed text does, that “there is no support for the doctrine of incorporation in the Constitution of India, except finding its basis in Art. 372, though [that] Article has not been referred by the Court in its judgements”.

Part IV — Custom in Indian practice. The starting point is Krishna Sharma v. State of West Bengal, AIR 1954 Cal 591: Indian courts apply internal law (the Constitution and statutes) and, while they will construe statutes so as not to conflict with international law, they “cannot override” a positive statute with a rule of international law. Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey, AIR 1984 SC 667, states the doctrine and its limit in one passage: “the doctrine of incorporation also recognises the position that the rules of international law are incorporated into national law and considered to be part of the national law unless they are in conflict with an Act of Parliament. Comity of Nations or no, municipal law must prevail in case of conflict... But the courts are under an obligation within legitimate limits, so to interpret the municipal statute as to avoid confrontation with the comity of nations or the well-established principles of international law.” PUCL v. Union of India, AIR 1997 SC 568, holds that “rules of customary international law which are not contrary to the municipal law shall be deemed to have been incorporated in the domestic law”; Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand, (2005) 3 SCC 551, that “principles of international law whenever applicable operate as a statutory implication”; and Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India, (1996) 5 SCC 647, that sustainable development — with the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle — is part of customary international law and, therefore, “once these principles are accepted as part of customary international law there would be no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law”. The outer limit is A.D.M. Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla, AIR 1976 SC 1207: even if the Universal Declaration had become custom, “nothing which conflicts with the provisions of our Constitution could be enforced here under any disguise”.

Part V — Treaty law in Indian practice. Here the Blackstonian doctrine does not apply; India follows the British (dualist) approach. (i) Making a treaty is an executive act: Articles 53(1), 73 and 77, with Entries 10 and 14 of List I — Union of India v. Manmull Jain, AIR 1954 Cal 615 (Chandernagore); Nirmal Bose v. Union of India, AIR 1959 Cal 506; and the Privy Council's formulation in Attorney-General for Canada v. Attorney-General for Ontario (1937) AC 326. There is no parliamentary approval requirement. (ii) Implementation requires legislation under Articles 253 and 246 read with Entry 14: Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin, AIR 1980 SC 470 (“the positive commitment of the States Parties ignites legislative action at home but does not automatically make the Covenant an enforceable part of the corpus juris of India”); Birma v. State of Rajasthan, AIR 1951 Raj 127; Shiv Kumar Sharma v. Union of India, AIR 1968 Del 643; State of Madras v. G.G. Menon, AIR 1954 SC 517. (iii) A treaty affecting private rights yields to a statute: Jolly George (s. 51 CPC read down, not displaced). (iv) Cession of Indian territory requires a constitutional amendment: In re Berubari Union (I), AIR 1960 SC 845; while a boundary settlement or the restoration of de jure foreign territory does not — Ram Kishore Sen, AIR 1966 SC 644, and a lease or servitude does not — Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta, AIR 1990 SC 1692. (v) Where there is no statute and a gap exists, conventions are read into the Constitution: Vishaka, AIR 1997 SC 3011 (CEDAW; guidelines binding under Article 141 until legislation, now replaced by the POSH Act, 2013) and Puttaswamy, (2017) 6 SCC 235 (Article 12 UDHR; Article 17 ICCPR; the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993, ss. 2(1)(d), 2(1)(f), 12(f)), where the Court held that “where there is a contradiction between international law and a domestic statute, the Court would give effect to the latter”, but that absent a contradiction “constitutional provisions must be read and interpreted in a manner which would enhance their conformity with the global human rights regime”. (vi) Where the treaty conflicts with Part III, it cannot be enforced at all (the Article 13 inference in the prescribed text; A.D.M. Jabalpur).

Part VI — Conclusion. The Blackstonian doctrine therefore remains relevant in India, but in a qualified form: it is the source of the rule that customary international law is part of Indian law and may be applied without legislation (Vellore); it explains why courts take judicial notice of international law and construe statutes so as to conform to it (Gramophone); and it does not extend to treaties, which require legislation, nor to any rule, customary or conventional, that conflicts with a statute or with the Constitution. As the prescribed text concludes, what exists is “a mixture of international law supremacy, municipal law supremacy and coordination of legal systems”, and India's practice is best described as monism in interpretation and dualism in hierarchy.

Answer 2 — 2015 Q.2(a) and (b): constitutional provisions on treaty making and implementation; the role of the Indian judiciary

Question (a). Discuss the constitutional provisions on treaty making and its implementation in India. (b) Discuss the role of the Indian judiciary in the effectuation of international legal obligations. (2015, Q.2, 20 marks)

(a) Treaty-making. Explain that the word “treaty” is not defined in the Constitution; that Article 53(1) vests the executive power of the Union in the President; that Article 73(1)(a) extends it to matters on which Parliament may legislate (read with Entry 10, List I, “foreign affairs”, and Entry 14, “entering into treaties and agreements... and implementing of treaties”), while Article 73(1)(b) extends it to the exercise of rights, authority and jurisdiction exercisable by virtue of any treaty; that Article 77 prescribes the form of executive action; and that Article 246 read with Entry 14 gives Parliament the legislative competence. Conclusion: the executive may bind India internationally without prior parliamentary approval — Ram Jawaya Kapur; Manmull Jain; Nirmal Bose; the Privy Council in A.G. Canada v. A.G. Ontario; and there is no analogue of the United States Senate's two-thirds consent. Add the qualifications: implementation requires legislation; money obligations require legislative sanction; cession requires a constitutional amendment (Berubari); and the 100th Amendment of 2015 shows the sequence in practice (1974 Agreement → 2011 Protocol → constitutional amendment).

(b) Treaty-implementation and the judiciary's role. Set out the four judicial techniques: (i) enforcement after legislation — the ordinary case; (ii) harmonious construction — the presumption that Parliament does not intend to breach international law, used in Jolly George to read section 51 CPC down and in Gramophone to read the Copyright Act in the setting of the Berne Convention; (iii) reception of custom — Vellore (precautionary and polluter pays principles as law of the land), PUCL, Pratap Singh; and (iv) interpretation of the Constitution in the light of conventions — Vishaka (CEDAW), NALSA, Puttaswamy (UDHR and ICCPR). Add the counterweights: the courts will not enforce an unlegislated treaty against a statute; they will not apply international law against the Constitution (A.D.M. Jabalpur); they leave delicate questions of foreign policy and recognition to the executive; and where territory is involved they insist on the constitutional forms (Berubari; Sukumar Sengupta). Conclude that the judiciary's role is interpretive and gap-filling, not displacing: it effectuates international obligations through Indian law rather than above it — the “monism in interpretation, dualism in hierarchy” formula.

Answer 3 — 2013 Q.4: Indian practice on the adoption and implementation of international treaties

Question. Discuss the Indian practice on adoption and implementation of international treaties. (2013, Q.4, 20 marks)

Answer under six heads. 1. Adoption (conclusion and ratification): executive act under Articles 53, 73, 77 and Entry 14 — Manmull Jain, Nirmal Bose; no prior parliamentary consent; India follows the dualist approach and “has not yet legislated” a general statute on treaties, so that practice is to be gathered from the Constitution and the cases. 2. International effect: the treaty binds India at once (A.G. Canada v. A.G. Ontario); internal law cannot be pleaded against it (VCLT Arts. 27, 46; ARSIWA Arts. 3, 32; the Alabama Claims, 1872). 3. Domestic effect — the general rule: legislation is required where the treaty (i) affects the rights of citizens, (ii) modifies the law of the State, or (iii) creates financial obligations — Maganbhai (Shah J.) — and the preponderant view is that legislation is required generally: Birma, Shiv Kumar Sharma, Jolly George, G.G. Menon, Civil Rights Vigilance Committee (the Gleneagles Accord). 4. The minority view and its partial acceptance: the self-executing/non-self-executing dichotomy imported into Indian law by Shah J. in Maganbhai, and the self-executing character of the Kutch Award; Rosaline George (1994). 5. Cession and territory: Berubari (Art. 368), Ram Kishore Sen (boundary — no amendment), Sukumar Sengupta (servitude — no amendment), the Ninth Amendment, and the 100th Amendment of 2015. 6. The interpretive practice: Jolly George (ICCPR Art. 11 and s. 51 CPC), Gramophone (Berne Convention and “import”), Vellore (custom), Vishaka (CEDAW; Art. 141 guidelines; POSH Act 2013), Puttaswamy (UDHR, ICCPR, Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993). Conclude with the prescribed text's own five-point summary of Indian treaty practice (see Table 3.13) and with the observation that although judicial authority predominantly supports the need for legislation, “more recently, the Supreme Court has resorted to international treaties, which are ratified by India but not legislated, in the interpretation of the constitutional and statutory provisions, or in the absence of contrary legislative provisions, and thus making them part of the domestic law.”

Answer 4 — Short note: the Blackstonian doctrine (2013 Q.8(c); 2011 Q.(iv))

Question. Write a short note on the applicability of the Blackstonian doctrine. (2013, Q.8(c), 10 marks; 2011, Q.(iv), 10 marks)

Short-note content (five paragraphs). (1) What it is: Blackstone's proposition that “the law of nations... is adopted in its full extent by the common law, and is held to be part of the law of the land” — the incorporation doctrine, the practical expression of monism. (2) Its development in England: applied in Buvot v. Barbuit, Triquet v. Bath, Emperor of Austria v. Day; qualified in West Rand Central Gold Mining Co. v. R. (1905) and Chung Chi Cheung (1939); temporarily displaced by R. v. Keyn (1876) and restored by the Territorial Waters Jurisdiction Act, 1878; refreshed by Trendtex (1977) and Rayner (1990). (3) Its two qualifications: no inconsistency with statute; no inconsistency with a rule already declared by the highest court. (4) Its applicability in India: received through Article 372(1) (the pre-1950 law continues); applied in Krishna Sharma (1954), Gramophone (1984: “unless they are in conflict with an Act of Parliament”), PUCL (1997) and Vellore (1996: precautionary and polluter pays principles as part of the law of the land); limited by A.D.M. Jabalpur (no enforcement against the Constitution). (5) Its limits and its opposite: the doctrine applies to custom, not to treaties, for which India follows the transformation (dualist) approach — Jolly George; and where a treaty and a statute conflict, the statute prevails. Mention that the questions on the doctrine have been asked repeatedly (2011, 2013, 2015, 2025) — a sign that the examiners expect the Blackstone–Keyn–West Rand–Chung Chi Cheung–Trendtex sequence and the Indian cases.

Answer 5 — 2012 Q.4: do treaties affecting private rights ipso facto become part of the law of the land?

Question. Do treaties which affect private rights ipso facto become part of the law of the land in India? What is the practice in this regard in the U.K. and the U.S.A.? Discuss with reference to the constitutional provisions and the decided cases. (2012, Q.4, 20 marks)

Answer — India (no). The treaty-making power is executive (Arts. 53, 73, Entry 14, List I); implementation requires legislation (Art. 253). The leading statement is Jolly George Varghese: “until the municipal law is changed to accommodate the Covenant what binds the court is the former, not the latter” — international conventional law “must go through the process of transformation into the municipal law before the international treaty can become an internal law”. The one qualification judicially recognised is that of Shah J. in Maganbhai: “making of law... is necessary when the treaty or agreement operates to restrict the rights of citizens or others or modifies the laws of the State. If the rights of the citizens or others which are justiciable are not affected, no legislative measure is needed...” — which means, precisely, that treaties affecting private rights are the clearest case in which legislation is required. Support with Birma (AIR 1951 Raj 127), Shiv Kumar Sharma (AIR 1968 Del 643), G.G. Menon (AIR 1954 SC 517) and Civil Rights Vigilance Committee (AIR 1983 Kant 85). Add the exceptions/qualifications: a treaty may be used as an aid to interpretation where the statute is ambiguous (Gramophone; the UK's Brind rule); where there is no law occupying the field, a convention may be read into the constitutional guarantees (Vishaka); and where a treaty conflicts with Part III it cannot be enforced at all (Art. 13 inference).

United Kingdom (no — the classical dualist position). A treaty is made by the Crown as a prerogative act; “a treaty cannot affect private rights unless it has been made a part of British law by an Act of Parliament” — The Parlement Belge (1879–80) 5 PD 197; A.G. for Canada v. A.G. for Ontario (1937) AC 326; Maclaine Watson (1988): treaties “are not self-executing... So far as individuals are concerned, it is res inter alios acta from which they cannot derive rights and by which they cannot be deprived of rights or subjected to obligations”; Rayner (1990) (Lord Templeman). Legislation is required where the treaty affects private rights, modifies the common law or statute law, creates a financial burden or invests the Crown with additional powers, and for cession of British territory. An unincorporated treaty may still be referred to for the interpretation of an ambiguous statute — Brind (1991) — but cannot prevail over a clearly worded statute. Modern statutes (Human Rights Act 1998; and, from the other direction, the EU (Withdrawal) Act 2018 and the Retained EU Law Act 2023) confirm that internal effect is a matter of parliamentary choice. Note the contrast with custom: custom is received by incorporation (Blackstone, Chung Chi Cheung, Trendtex) — the UK's asymmetry.

United States (partly yes — the exceptional case). Article VI(2) makes treaties “the supreme Law of the Land”, so that a self-executing treaty operates without legislation and prevails over State law (Missouri v. Holland; Asakura v. City of Seattle); but a non-self-executing treaty needs implementing legislation, and the test is the intention of the parties (Sei Fujii v. State of California (1952) — the UN Charter human-rights provisions held non-self-executing). Between a treaty and a later federal statute, the later in time prevails (Edye v. Robertson), subject to the presumption that Congress does not intend to override treaty obligations (Cook v. United States; US v. PLO, 1988); the Constitution prevails over both (Tag v. Rogers); and even an ICJ judgment is not domestic law without legislation (Medellín v. Texas, 2008). So the American answer to the question posed is: only if the treaty is self-executing — a category which, in practice, has swallowed much of the rule.

Conclusion. In India and the UK, treaties affecting private rights do not become part of the law of the land ipso facto; in the United States, they do so only if self-executing. In all three systems the same protective technique appears — a treaty may be used to interpret, but not to displace, a clear statute — and in all three the State remains internationally responsible for the internal shortfall (VCLT Art. 27).

Answer 6 — 2011 Q.4: how far do international treaties bind Indian courts?

Question. How far do international treaties bind Indian courts? Discuss in detail the Indian practice of implementation of treaties in the municipal legal system. (2011, Q.4, 20 marks)

Answer. Begin with the proposition that a treaty binds the State (and therefore India as an international person) from the moment it is concluded — Articles 53, 73, Entry 14, List I; Manmull Jain; Nirmal Bose — irrespective of parliamentary approval, and that no internal law can be pleaded against it (VCLT Arts. 27, 46). Then answer the question actually asked, in four propositions.

  1. An unlegislated treaty does not bind Indian courts as law. “Treaties which are part of international law do not form part of the law of the land unless expressly made so by the legislative authority” (Birma, AIR 1951 Raj 127); “In India, treaties do not have the force of law and consequently obligations arising therefrom will not be enforceable in municipal courts unless backed by legislation” (Shiv Kumar Sharma, AIR 1968 Del 643); the Covenant “does not automatically make... an enforceable part of the corpus juris of India” (Jolly George, AIR 1980 SC 470); G.G. Menon (extradition under the 1881 Act refused); Civil Rights Vigilance Committee (Gleneagles Accord not enforceable at the instance of citizens).
  2. But an unlegislated treaty binds the courts in interpretation. The settled canon: where a statute is capable of two constructions, the court adopts the construction consistent with India's international obligations — because the legislature is presumed not to intend a breach — Jolly George; Gramophone (the Copyright Act read in the setting of the Berne Convention and the Transit Trade Convention); Krishna Sharma; and, in the UK, Brind. Note that this is not enforcement of the treaty but interpretation of the statute.
  3. Where there is a legislative vacuum, a convention may be read into the constitutional guarantees — Vishaka (CEDAW into Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21; guidelines binding under Article 141 until legislation; now the POSH Act, 2013); PUCL; NALSA; Puttaswamy (UDHR Art. 12 and ICCPR Art. 17; the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993). Custom, by contrast, is received directly as law where no statute or constitutional provision is against it — Vellore, PUCL.
  4. But a treaty never binds a court against a statute or the Constitution. Conflict with a statute: the statute prevails — Jolly George; Gramophone (“National courts cannot say ‘yes’ if Parliament has said ‘no’”); Puttaswamy ¶133. Conflict with the Constitution: the treaty is unenforceable — A.D.M. Jabalpur; the Article 13 inference (a treaty with Part III is “null and void”). Conflict over territory: the constitutional forms govern — Berubari (amendment), Ram Kishore Sen and Sukumar Sengupta (no amendment needed).

Conclusion with the five-point summary of Indian treaty practice from the prescribed text, and the contemporary statement that “the courts in India may enforce treaties which are not inconsistent with the Indian law or when there is a gap in the law” — the interpretive turn of the last three decades.

Answer 1 — 2002 Q.4(a): the theoretical relationship between national and international law

Question. “It is important to clear various underlying assumptions in the relation between national and international law.” Explain theoretically the relationship between national and international law, keeping in view the above. (2002, Q.4(a), part of a 20-mark question)

Answer plan. (1) Identify the assumptions that must be cleared: that international law and national law are comparable; that one must be subordinate to the other; that a State is either “monist” or “dualist”; and that the resolution of a conflict in a national court settles the international position. (2) Monism: one legal order, common origin, the individual at the base of all law — Kelsen's hierarchy and Grundnorm; Lauterpacht; Fitzmaurice; Starke's “international constitutional law”. Criticism: States do not behave monistically; unity of legal science does not yield a rule of priority. (3) Dualism: two systems, different subjects, sources and juridical origin — Triepel's trio of differences and Anzilotti's postulates (obedience v. pacta sunt servanda). Criticism: individuals and organisations are now subjects; “State will” reduces to the will of the people; custom binds without consent; the theory cannot explain general international law. (4) The theories of application: transformation/specific adoption (positivist) and delegation (monist) — Verma's point that they are “the manifestation of the theories of dualism and monism”. (5) Primacy: dualists to municipal law; monists divided; Kelsen to State law on non-scientific grounds and the two reasons his choice fails (the anarchy of plural primacies; the survival of international law through revolutions and the repeal of constitutions); Starke to international law and Verma's three objections. (6) The cleared position: the controversy is “more academic than real”; the two systems have their own spheres; the supremacy of international law in the international sphere is unchallenged, as is that of municipal law in State matters; what international law requires is that a State in breach “cannot shelter itself behind domestic law by way of absolution”. (7) Illustrate with the Indian material: Berubari (the treaty binds India; the Constitution channels the implementation), Jolly George (the Covenant binds India; the statute governs the court), Vishaka (the treaty informs the Constitution). Close with Verma's “mixture” conclusion.

Answer 2 — 2001 Q.3: monism and dualism as an “uneasy compromise”, in the light of Gramophone

Question. “The relationship between international law and municipal law has been an uneasy compromise between Monism and Dualism.” Explain the theoretical foundation of the relationship in the light of Gramophone Co. of India v. B. B. Pandey. (2001, Q.3, 20 marks)

Answer. Explain the two theories briefly (as in Answer 1), then devote the greater part of the answer to showing that Gramophone is itself the compromise in miniature. Facts and holding: pirated cassettes consigned from Singapore to Nepal and seized at Calcutta; “import” in sections 51 and 53 of the Copyright Act means “bringing into India from outside India” and includes importation for transit; the interpretation is supported by the Berne Convention and by the Convention on Transit Trade of Land-Locked States, 1965, whose exceptions permit the transit State to protect industrial, literary and artistic property. The monist strand: the Court accepts that “the doctrine of incorporation... recognises the position that the rules of international law are incorporated into national law and considered to be part of the national law”, and it interprets an Indian statute in the setting of international conventions, observing that “our interpretation, far from being inconsistent with any principle of international law, is entirely in accord with International Conventions and the Treaties between India and Nepal”. The dualist strand: the same paragraph contains the qualifications — “unless they are in conflict with an Act of Parliament”; “Comity of Nations or no, municipal law must prevail in case of conflict”; “National courts cannot say ‘yes’ if Parliament has said ‘no’ to a principle of international law” — with the duty to construe so as to avoid a confrontation, and the proviso that “if conflict is inevitable, the latter must yield”. The compromise explained: because the conventions and the statute pointed the same way, no choice had to be made; but the Court's method shows that India receives international law as an interpretive and gap-filling source (monist in spirit) while preserving the supremacy of Parliament (dualist in structure). Reinforce with Jolly George (same compromise in respect of the ICCPR), Vellore and Vishaka (the interpretive direction) and A.D.M. Jabalpur (the hard boundary). Conclude with the prescribed text's view that the compromise is not a defect but a description: practice shows “a mixture of international law supremacy, municipal law supremacy and coordination of legal systems”.

Answer 3 — 2000 Q.4: how far have the problems of municipal courts been reconciled? Anglo-Indian practice

Question. The relationship between international law and municipal law has posed difficult problems to municipal courts. How far have these problems been reconciled by the municipal courts? Construct your argument on the basis of Anglo-Indian practice. (2000, Q.4, 20 marks)

Answer plan. State the four problems: (i) Is international law applicable of its own force, or only after transformation? (ii) If applicable, does it prevail over statute? (iii) May an individual rely on it? (iv) What is the position where the State's own courts refuse to apply it? Then show the reconciliation techniques developed in Anglo-Indian practice: (a) the distinction between custom and treaty — custom received by incorporation, treaties requiring legislation (Chung Chi Cheung; Trendtex; Maclaine Watson; and in India Krishna Sharma, Gramophone, Jolly George); (b) the presumption of harmony — Parliament is presumed not to legislate in breach of international law, so ambiguous statutes are read conformably (R. v. Chief Immigration Officer, ex p. Salam; Salomon v. Commissioners of Customs and Excise; in India Gramophone, Jolly George, Vishaka, Puttaswamy); (c) the rule of evidence — judicial notice of international law; (d) executive certificates for recognition, immunity and boundary questions (UK) and the corresponding Indian practice of accepting executive statements on recognition and the extent of territory; (e) the classification of subject-matter — Acts of State and prerogative matters are outside judicial review; prize courts apply international law even against an Order in Council (The Zamora, 1916); (f) the self-execution device — the American distinction (Sei Fujii; Medellín) and Shah J.'s version in Maganbhai; and (g) constitutional reception and constitutional limits — Article 25 of the German Basic Law, Articles 93–94 of the Dutch Constitution, Article VI(2) of the US Constitution, Article 55 of the French Constitution, and in India Article 372 with the overriding supremacy of the Constitution. Assess how far reconciled: substantially in practice, but not by theory. The residual problems are three: the treaty that the legislature refuses to implement (the UK's Maclaine Watson, the US's Medellín, India's G.G. Menon); the individual who has no remedy despite a treaty right; and the question whether custom binds where there is no controlling statute, executive act or treaty — on which, the prescribed text notes, there is in India no judicial authority. Conclude with the standard formula: the courts have reconciled the systems by technique (interpretation) rather than by hierarchy, and the international responsibility of the State survives the reconciliation.

Answer 4 — 1999 Q.4: the criterion adopted by municipal courts

Question. “The relationship between international law and municipal law has posed a difficult problem to municipal courts, namely, to what extent may such courts give effect within the municipal sphere to rules of international law.” Clearly examine the criterion which has been adopted by municipal courts to resolve this issue. Refer to settled judicial precedents. (1999, Q.4, 20 marks)

Answer. Present the criterion as a hierarchy of four questions, which is the cleanest way to answer.

  1. What kind of rule is it — custom or treaty? Custom is generally received without legislation (The Paquete Habana, 175 U.S. 677: “international law is part of our law... where there is no treaty and no controlling executive or legislative act or judicial decision”; Trendtex; Chung Chi Cheung; in India Krishna Sharma, Gramophone, Vellore). Treaties, in the UK and India, require legislation (The Parlement Belge; Maclaine Watson; Jolly George); in the US they require it only if not self-executing (Sei Fujii).
  2. Is there a statute on the point? If the statute is clear, it prevails over custom and over an unlegislated treaty (Mortensen v. Peters, 1906; Gramophone; Jolly George; Puttaswamy ¶133). If the statute is ambiguous, it is construed in conformity with international law (Salomon; Brind; Gramophone).
  3. Is there a constitutional provision? The Constitution always prevails (A.D.M. Jabalpur; Tag v. Rogers; the German Basic Law's fundamental provisions; Article 54 of the French Constitution), although a reception clause may give custom or treaties rank above ordinary statutes (Article 25 German Basic Law; Articles 93–94 Dutch Constitution; Article 28(1) Greek Constitution; Article 15(4) Russian Constitution).
  4. Is the rule directly effective (self-executing) as regards individuals? If yes, the individual may rely on it (Paquete Habana; Trendtex; Bennett v. Horseferry Magistrates' Court, 1993 — the abduction case; Vishaka, where the convention supplied the content of the rights). If no, the individual's remedy is only through interpretation (Jolly George) or through the political organs (Xavier v. Canara Bank: “the individual cannot come to Court but may complain to the Human Rights Committee”).

Add the criterion's twin: the criterion applied by international tribunals is the reverse and flat — internal law is no excuse (VCLT Art. 27; ARSIWA Arts. 3, 32; the Alabama Claims; LaGrand). Close with the observation that the criterion is not a single rule but a technique of accommodation, and that its direction of travel in India since 1980 has been towards greater use of international norms.

Answer 5 — 1998 Q.4(a): applicability of customary international rules in the practice of India and the USA

Question. “Two questions arise, first, whether international law is of its own force, drawn into the law of the land without the aid of a municipal statute and, second, whether so drawn, it overrides municipal law in case of conflict” (Gramophone Co. of India v. B. B. Pandey, AIR 1984 SC 667). Discuss with reference to the applicability of customary international rules in the practice of India and the U.S.A. (1998, Q.4(a), part of a 20-mark question)

Answer. Take the two questions separately, and answer each for both countries.

Question 1 — is custom drawn in without a statute? India: yes, through the common-law doctrine of incorporation continued by Article 372(1) — Krishna Sharma (1954) (courts apply international law so far as not inconsistent with statute; they cannot override a statute), PUCL (1997) (“almost accepted proposition of law”), Vellore (1996) (sustainable development, precautionary principle and polluter pays principle accepted as part of the domestic law), Pratap Singh (2005) (international law as statutory implication). USA: yes, custom is part of federal law — The Paquete Habana (1900): “International law is part of our law, and must be ascertained and administered by the Courts of Justice of appropriate jurisdiction... where there is no treaty and no controlling executive or legislative act or judicial decision”; its determination by the federal courts binds State courts (Restatement (Third), 1987).

Question 2 — does it override municipal law in conflict? India: no. Gramophone itself supplies the answer — the rule is incorporated “unless [it is] in conflict with an Act of Parliament”; “municipal law must prevail in case of conflict”; the remedy is interpretation so as to avoid confrontation. A.D.M. Jabalpur adds the constitutional limit; Vellore itself accepts the principles only because they do not conflict with Indian law. USA: no, and with three exceptions carved into the Paquete Habana formula itself — no treaty, no controlling executive or legislative act, no judicial decision. Hence in Garcia-Mir v. Meese (1986) an Attorney-General's decision (a “controlling executive act”) displaced customary law on the detention of aliens; a clear statute prevails (The Over the Top, 1925); and the Constitution prevails (Tag v. Rogers, 1959). Where no such act exists, the courts harmonise — the Charming Betsy canon (1804): “an act of Congress ought never to be construed to violate the law of nations if any other possible construction remains”.

Conclusion. In both countries the answer to the first question is “yes” and to the second “no” — a qualified incorporation: custom is received as a rule of decision subject to the supremacy of the legislature and the constitution. India's practice is the more generous as regards the content of custom (Vellore), the USA's the more institutionalised as regards its federal character, and neither permits custom to defeat a clear statute. Note the further difference in treaty practice: India requires legislation generally, while the USA gives self-executing treaties the rank of supreme law.

Answer 6 — 1995 Q.3: Fitzmaurice, the unreal controversy, and the treaty practice of India and the USA

Question. According to Fitzmaurice, the entire monist–dualist controversy on the subject of international law and municipal law is unreal and does not exist. Both simultaneously have their spheres of activity. However, occasions come when they clash. Discuss the State practice relating to treaties in India and the United States of America. Which theory, on the relationship between international law and municipal law, is supported by their practice? (1995, Q.3, 20 marks)

Answer. Begin with Fitzmaurice's position as stated in the prescribed text: the two systems “operate without any conflict in their assigned spheres of action”; accordingly “the question of superiority or primacy of one system over the other does not arise”, and where a State organ fails to apply international law, that is “a manifestation of its weakness, but does not invalidate the theory, since the State will incur international responsibility”. (Recall also that Fitzmaurice considered Anzilotti to have been wrongly labelled a dualist — a nice point of scholarship.) Then give the treaty practice of the two States, and finally answer the “which theory” question.

India. Making: executive act (Arts. 53, 73, Entry 14) — Manmull Jain, Nirmal Bose. International effect at once; internal effect only on legislation (Art. 253) — Jolly George, Birma, Shiv Kumar Sharma, G.G. Menon, Civil Rights Vigilance Committee. Self-execution recognised in a minority view (Maganbhai, Shah J.; the Kutch Award). Cession requires an amendment (Berubari); boundary settlement and servitudes do not (Ram Kishore Sen; Sukumar Sengupta). Statutes prevail on conflict; conventions inform interpretation and fill vacuums (Gramophone, Vishaka, Puttaswamy).

United States. Making: President with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate (Art. II(2)); effect: “supreme Law of the Land” (Art. VI(2)) — but only if self-executing (Sei Fujii; Medellín); later-in-time rule as against federal statutes, with the presumption against congressional override (Edye; Cook; US v. PLO); treaties prevail over State law (Missouri v. Holland); the Constitution prevails over treaties (Tag v. Rogers); executive agreements have equivalent force (Belmont; Pink); custom is federal law, subject to controlling acts (Paquete Habana; Garcia-Mir).

Which theory is supported by their practice? Neither exclusively — and that is the answer the question is fishing for. On paper the United States looks monist (treaties are supreme law) but behaves dualistically (self-execution, later-in-time, Medellín); India looks dualist (Art. 253; Jolly George) but behaves monistically in interpretation (Vishaka, Vellore, Puttaswamy). Both are “dualist in hierarchy, monist in interpretation”, and each illustrates Fitzmaurice's point that the controversy is unreal in the sense that the systems rarely collide in an irreconcilable way — and that when they do, each answers within its own sphere, with the State bearing the international responsibility.

Answer 7 — 1994 Q.2: the customary practices of India, the UK and the USA compared

Question. In fact there is no reason to consider [the] relationship between international law and municipal law [a problem], as both the laws act in their respective spheres and there is never any clash between them. However, States do have different practices in this regard... Discuss, therefore, the customary practices of India, the UK and the USA in this regard to bring home the differences in their approach. (1994, Q.2, 20 marks)

Answer. This question is confined to custom (contrast Answer 6, which is about treaties) — a distinction worth stating expressly. United Kingdom: the Blackstonian incorporation doctrine (Blackstone; Buvot v. Barbuit; Triquet v. Bath), qualified by West Rand (1905) and Chung Chi Cheung (1939), interrupted by R. v. Keyn (1876) and restored by statute (Territorial Waters Jurisdiction Act, 1878), modernised by Trendtex (1977) and Rayner (1990); two qualifications (no inconsistency with statute; no inconsistency with a declared rule), plus the exceptions of Acts of State and the Crown's prerogative (executive certificates); harmonious construction (ex p. Salam) and judicial notice; prize courts apply international law even against an Order in Council (The Zamora). United States: custom is part of federal law (Paquete Habana), subject to three exceptions read into the doctrine itself (treaty, controlling executive or legislative act, judicial decision); federal determination binds State courts (Restatement (Third)); executive certificates on recognition and immunity; the Charming Betsy canon; the Constitution prevails (Tag v. Rogers). India: incorporation through Article 372(1) (T.K. Varred); custom applied so far as consistent with statute (Krishna Sharma, Gramophone, PUCL, Vellore, Pratap Singh); not enforceable against the Constitution (A.D.M. Jabalpur); the executive's statements accepted in matters of recognition and the extent of territory; no judicial authority yet on custom where there is no controlling statute, executive decision or treaty. Differences in approach — draw them out: the UK's is doctrinal (a common-law rule with judicial qualifications); the USA's is constitutional-federal (custom as federal law, subject to the political branches); India's is received and subordinate (via Article 372, but with no reception clause and a marked reluctance to let custom displace anything written). Conclusion: the “spheres” theory holds for custom more often than for treaties, because custom is generally consistent with domestic law; where it is not, all three systems prefer the domestic rule, and the State answers internationally.

Answer 8 — 1993 Q.3: no theory reflects State practice — examine the treaty practices of India, the USA and the UK

Question. None of the theories which explain the relationship between international law and municipal law reflects the correct position of State practices in this regard. We can know the exact position only by examining the State practices. Examine the treaty practices of India, the USA and the UK to know the position of their law vis-à-vis the law of nations. (1993, Q.3, 20 marks)

Answer 10 — 1991 Q.3: each supreme in its own sphere — customary practices of the USA, the UK and India

Question. In fact International law and Municipal law are each supreme in their respective spheres. Nevertheless State practices vary. Discuss the State practices in International customary law of the USA, U.K. and India. (1991, Q.3, 20 marks)

Answer. Begin by explaining the “sphere” thesis: as the prescribed text puts it, “international law and municipal law are not comparable since both have their own sphere of operation and neither can be termed as subordinate to the other. The supremacy of international law in the international sphere is unchallenged in the same way as of municipal law in the State matters.” Then give the three practices as in Answer 7, with emphasis on custom: the USA (Paquete Habana's formula and its three exceptions; Garcia-Mir; the Charming Betsy canon; federal character of custom); the UK (Blackstone; West Rand; Chung Chi Cheung; Trendtex; the two qualifications and the exceptions for Acts of State and the prerogative); India (Krishna Sharma; Gramophone; PUCL; Vellore; Pratap Singh; the constitutional limit in A.D.M. Group C — problem questions (and how to adapt one answer to many questions)

Problem 1 — 1992 Q.5: the Danish trawler and the British statute

Problem. The British Parliament passed a law prohibiting fishing in an area of the British maritime belt. A Danish trawler was captured while fishing in this area and was put on trial. The Captain of the trawler pleaded that International Law permitted it. Decide. (1992, Q.5)

Model answer. The problem is the classic Mortensen v. Peters situation, and it is decided by the rule that a clear statute prevails over customary international law within the municipal sphere. Facts of the real case: in Mortensen v. Peters, (1906) 8 F (J) 93, a Danish subject, Mortensen, master of the Norwegian steamer Rio Tinto, was convicted by the Court of Justiciary of Scotland of otter-trawling in the Moray Firth within an area covered by the Sea Fisheries Regulation (Scotland) Act, 1895. The Act prohibited trawling within an area extending more than seventy miles from the coast in the Moray Firth, whereas customary international law then recognised only a ten-mile (or, for the territorial sea proper, a three-mile) limit for bays and estuaries. Mortensen pleaded that international law permitted the fishing. Held: the statute was clear and unambiguous, and it was applied; the plea of international law failed; the appellant was liable. Reasoning: (i) the Blackstonian incorporation doctrine is subject to the qualification that custom must not be inconsistent with a British statute, whether earlier or later (West Rand; Chung Chi Cheung; the qualification identified in the prescribed text); (ii) where the words of a statute are clear, the court must give effect to them, even though the statute is in breach of international law (cf. Polites v. Commonwealth (1945) 70 CLR 60); the presumption that Parliament does not intend to legislate against international law operates only where the statute is ambiguous (ex p. Salam; Salomon; Brind); (iii) it is not for the court to correct the legislature: as the Indian Supreme Court was to put it much later, municipal law must prevail in case of conflict and “national courts cannot say ‘yes’ if Parliament has said ‘no’ to a principle of international law” (Gramophone, AIR 1984 SC 667). Consequence for the United Kingdom: the United Kingdom remained internationally responsible for any resulting breach — Article 27 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties and Article 3 of the Articles on State Responsibility — and international law would look to Parliament, not to the Court of Justiciary, for a remedy (as Parliament had done by the Territorial Waters Jurisdiction Act, 1878 after R. v. Keyn, and as it did in the twentieth century by the Territorial Sea Act, 1987 establishing a twelve-nautical-mile territorial sea consistently with Article 3 of UNCLOS, 1982). Answer to the "decide" instruction: convict the captain; the plea of international law is no defence against a clear statute, but the State may not invoke that statute to excuse the breach internationally. If the same problem arose in India, the result would be the same, on the authority of Krishna Sharma, Gramophone and Puttaswamy ¶133 — municipal law prevails on a real conflict.

Problem 2 — 1999 Q.5(ii): cession to Pakistan and lease to Bangladesh — ratification and its conditions

Problem. As part of continuing bilateral treaty making with its neighbours, India agrees to cede certain parts of its territory to Pakistan, and to lease certain parts to Bangladesh. How should these agreements be ratified to be binding under international law? Does international law put any conditions on the way ratification must be effected? (1999, Q.5(ii))

Model answer — in three steps.

Step 1: how the two agreements become binding internationally. Both are treaties within Article 2(1)(a) VCLT, 1969; the ordinary machinery of Articles 11–16 applies (signature, exchange of instruments, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, according to what the parties agree). Under India's constitutional practice, the making of the treaty is an executive act — Articles 53(1), 73 and 77, with Entries 10 and 14 of List I — and no prior parliamentary consent is required (Union of India v. Manmull Jain; Nirmal Bose; A.G. Canada v. A.G. Ontario). The instruments should be registered with the United Nations Secretariat under Article 102 of the Charter; non-registration does not invalidate the treaty but prevents invocation before UN organs. Once in force they bind India, and performance must be in good faith (pacta sunt servanda, Article 26).

Step 2: how the two agreements are implemented internally — the decisive difference. The cession to Pakistan: this is cession of Indian territory and requires an amendment of the Constitution under Article 368, a law under Article 3 being incompetent — In re Berubari Union (I), AIR 1960 SC 845; the precedent being the Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960, and, for the enclaves, the Constitution (100th Amendment) Act, 2015. The exception: if the territory was always de jure Pakistan's and India merely administered it (as with the 512 acres of Chilahati in Ram Kishore Sen, AIR 1966 SC 644), or if what is involved is the settlement of a disputed boundary or the implementation of an award (Maganbhai, AIR 1969 SC 783), no amendment and no legislation are needed; the executive may act. The lease to Bangladesh: on the authority of Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta, AIR 1990 SC 1692, a lease in perpetuity of Indian territory conferring rights of use while sovereignty continues to vest in India is neither a cession nor a transfer of sovereignty — at most an international servitude — and may be implemented by mere executive action; no Article 368 amendment and no Article 3 law are required. Where, however, the agreement purports to oust the jurisdiction of Indian courts and law-enforcement agencies over offences committed in the area, the position is different: as the Court observed in that case, that consequence may require a change in the existing Indian law, though the validity and implementation of the agreement do not depend on it.

Step 3: does international law impose conditions on the manner of ratification? Yes, but they are conditions of validity and effect, not of internal procedure. (i) Capacity and authority: the treaty must be concluded by a person with authority under Article 7 (heads of State or government, ministers for foreign affairs, heads of mission, or a representative holding full powers), and a person without authority cannot bind the State unless the State subsequently confirms the act (Article 8). (ii) Internal law: as a general rule internal law is irrelevant (Article 27), but consent may be invalidated if it was expressed in violation of a provision of internal law regarding competence to conclude a treaty, provided that the violation was manifest and concerned a rule of fundamental importance (Article 46) — a narrow exception of which the Indian rules on cession (Article 368) are exactly the kind, so that a cession effected without the amendment could raise an Article 46 question if the violation were manifest. (iii) Defects of consent: error, fraud, corruption of a representative, coercion of a representative and coercion of a State render consent voidable or void (Articles 48–52). (iv) Jus cogens: a treaty conflicting with a peremptory norm is void (Articles 53 and 64) — a condition of substance which applies to any territory agreement (for example, one purporting to deny self-determination). (v) Reservations must not be incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty (Article 19), and formal confirmation, acceptance and approval follow the same rules as ratification (Article 2(1)(b)). (vi) Registration under Article 102 of the Charter (above). Add that the population's consent to a cession is required by some constitutions (Article 53(3) of the French Constitution: “no cession, no exchange and no addition of territory shall be valid without the consent of the population concerned”) and is a relevant policy consideration under the human-rights instruments — the very hardship of which Oppenheim and the Berubari opinion speak, mitigated in practice by an option to emigrate.

Conclusion. Internationally, ratify both treaties in the ordinary way, register them, and perform in good faith; internally, the cession will require a constitutional amendment while the lease (servitude) will not — the distinction drawn by the three territorial cases; and international law permits an internal-law objection to the validity of a consent only in the narrow circumstances of Article 46.

Problem 3 — 1998 Q.4(b): implementation of the two territorial agreements under Indian law

Problem. As part of settlement of the issue of sovereignty over a disputed territory which was administered by India, India and Pakistan entered into an agreement whereby India agreed to hand over the territory to Pakistan. In another agreement, India leased certain other land to Bangladesh in perpetuity. How will these agreements be implemented under the Indian law? (1998, Q.4(b))

Model answer. Separate the two agreements, because Indian law treats them differently, and note that the question turns on substance, not form.

(1) The first agreement — handing over disputed territory administered by India. Ask the Berubari question: is what is being done cession, or the ascertainment of a boundary and the delivery of what the other side already owns? Three possibilities:

  • If the agreement settles a disputed boundary or implements an award, no legislation and no amendment are needed: Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India, AIR 1969 SC 783 (the Kutch Award was an operative, self-executing award; Hidayatullah C.J.: “Ordinarily an adjustment of a boundary which International law regards as valid between two nations should be recognised by the courts and the implementation thereof can always be with the Executive unless a clear case of cession is involved...”); and Ram Kishore Sen, AIR 1966 SC 644 (handing over 512 acres of Chilahati, de jure Pakistan's, was not cession).
  • If the territory was de jure India's and the agreement transfers sovereignty to Pakistan, it is cession: a law under Article 3 is incompetent and an amendment under Article 368 is necessary — Berubari, AIR 1960 SC 845; the Ninth Amendment of 1960 was the instrument; the 100th Amendment of 2015 shows the same requirement for the enclave exchange. Note also that a negotiated division “reached apart from, and independently of” the award is cession and not ascertainment — the very distinction on which Berubari turned and which counsel had failed to appreciate in that case.
  • If the agreement is really a grant of rights short of sovereignty (as with the Teen Bigha corridor), it is a servitude and requires neither — Sukumar Sengupta, AIR 1990 SC 1692.

(2) The second agreement — the lease in perpetuity to Bangladesh. This is squarely governed by Union of India v. Sukumar Sengupta: the Supreme Court held that such an arrangement — with sovereignty expressly remaining in India, no exclusive or legal possession transferred, and the rights granted being limited and non-exclusive — amounts at most to an international servitude, not a lease or a cession, and can be implemented by executive action alone. No Article 368 amendment, no Article 3 law. Two qualifications from that judgment should be stated: (i) the implementation of the agreement is not dependent on any change in Indian law, but where the agreement seeks to exclude Indian jurisdiction over offences in the leased area, the existing Indian law may need to be amended for that consequence to be given effect internally; and (ii) courts may not direct the legislature to amend the Constitution or enact laws — the Division Bench's direction to that effect was set aside.

(3) Common points. Both agreements bind India internationally upon conclusion (Arts. 53, 73; the making of a treaty is an executive act — Manmull Jain, Nirmal Bose); both should be registered under Article 102 of the Charter; both cannot be excused by pleading Indian law (VCLT Art. 27; ARSIWA Art. 3); and neither can be implemented in a manner that violates Part III of the Constitution, since a treaty in conflict with fundamental rights is unenforceable (the Article 13 inference in the prescribed text; A.D.M. Jabalpur). Finally, note the practical aftermath: the Ninth Amendment remained a “dead letter” because no appointed day was notified (Sukumar Sengupta; A.K. Roy, (1982) 1 SCC 271), and the enclave question was finally settled in 2015 by the 100th Amendment — the cession rule of Berubari reasserting itself after fifty-five years.

Problem 4 — 2001 Q.4(b): the Slavania problem (a national law contrary to international law)

Problem. Slavania has passed a national legislation authorising its President to give monetary and other support to a rebel guerrilla group in neighbouring Paktonia which is using force to overthrow the government there. Slavania is a member of the UN and of treaties banning intervention. A Slavanian national X approaches the Supreme Court of Slavania for a writ against the President, arguing that the legislation is contrary to customary international law, conventional law and the UN Charter, and that Slavania is bound to protect and promote human rights under the Charter. As a judge of the Supreme Court of Slavania, how will you decide the petition? (2001, Q.4(b))

Model answer — as a judgment.

(i) The international-law standards. The conduct alleged violates: Article 2(4) of the UN Charter (prohibition of the threat or use of force) and Article 2(1) and (7) read with the principle of non-intervention in the internal affairs of States; the Friendly Relations Declaration (UNGA Res. 2625 (XXV), 1970), which declares that no State shall organise, instigate, assist or participate in acts of civil strife or terrorist acts in another State, or acquiesce in organised activities within its territory directed towards the commission of such acts; the customary prohibition of intervention as authoritatively interpreted by the International Court of Justice in Military and Paramilitary Activities in and against Nicaragua (Nicaragua v. United States of America), ICJ Rep. 1986, p. 14, where the Court held that the principle of non-intervention “involves the right of every sovereign State to conduct its affairs without outside interference”, that assistance to rebels in the form of the provision of weapons or logistical or other support constitutes a breach, and that the prohibition of the use of force and the principle of non-intervention exist as customary law in parallel with the Charter; and the human-rights framework, since the supply of support to a guerrilla group is difficult to reconcile with the obligations to respect and ensure human rights.

(ii) The judge's dilemma — and how to resolve it. As a judge of the Supreme Court of Slavania I am bound by Slavania's Constitution and statutes, not by international law directly, unless the Constitution so provides. Therefore: (a) if the Constitution has a reception clause giving international law internal force (like Article 25 of the German Basic Law or Articles 93–94 of the Dutch Constitution) or ranks treaties above ordinary statutes (like Article 55 of the French Constitution or Article VI(2) of the US Constitution for self-executing treaties), the legislation may be tested and struck down; (b) if the Constitution is silent (as in India and the UK), the statute governs and cannot be displaced by international law — Gramophone; Krishna Sharma; Mortensen v. Peters; (c) even then, I must interpret the statute, so far as its language permits, so as not to conflict with Slavania's international obligations — the presumption that the legislature does not intend to breach international law (Jolly George; Brind; Vellore; Puttaswamy ¶133); and, if the statute is clear, I must apply it, while observing, as the Indian Supreme Court did in Gramophone, that “national courts cannot say ‘yes’ if Parliament has said ‘no’ to a principle of international law”. (d) If X's claim rests on a Charter provision on human rights, the answer is that the Charter's human-rights clauses (Preamble, Articles 1, 55, 56) are generally treated as non-self-executing so far as private rights of action are concerned (Sei Fujii v. State of California, 242 P.2d 617 (1952)), and that a private individual has no standing before the ICH unless a treaty confers rights on individuals (contrast LaGrand, where the ICJ held that Article 36 VCCR creates individual rights).

(iii) The order. I would dismiss the petition in the absence of a constitutional ground, while (i) construing the authorisation narrowly so that it does not extend to acts contrary to Slavania's Charter and customary obligations, and (ii) recording that Slavania remains internationally responsible for the law and the conduct; that the responsibility attaches to Slavania for the acts of all its organs, including the legislature and the executive (ARSIWA Articles 3, 4; Alabama Claims, 1872; LaGrand, 2001); that Paktonia may invoke Slavania's responsibility before the ICJ where jurisdiction exists, through the Security Council under Chapter VII, or by countermeasures subject to the law of State responsibility; and that “a party may not invoke the provisions of its internal law as justification for its failure to perform a treaty” (VCLT Article 27).

Alternative submission to score higher: if the hypothetical Constitution of Slavania contains a bill of rights (as most modern constitutions do), the petition can be framed domestically — as a claim that the legislation is unconstitutional because it is inconsistent with Slavania's commitments and with the constitutional value of peace and international law — the route taken, in effect, by Vishaka and Puttaswamy in India, and by the German Constitutional Court in its Völkerrechtsfreundlichkeit cases. That is the modern answer to the old dilemma: the domestic constitution, not the foreign obligation, is made the instrument of compliance.

The ten-sentence answer that fits any question on this topic. (1) International law and municipal law are formally distinct but functionally interlocked; the theories describe the relationship, and State practice decides it. (2) Monism (Kelsen, Lauterpacht, Fitzmaurice, Starke) treats them as one system; dualism (Triepel, Anzilotti) treats them as two. (3) The practical questions are two: application and supremacy. (4) For custom, the common-law tradition receives it by incorporation (Blackstone; Chung Chi Cheung; Trendtex), subject to statute and precedent. (5) For treaties, the United Kingdom and India require legislation (Maclaine Watson; Jolly George), while the United States gives internal force to self-executing treaties only (Sei Fujii; Medellín). (6) In India, Article 372(1) carries the incorporation rule forward; Article 51(c) supplies the interpretive obligation; Article 253 supplies the power to implement; and Part III and the constitutional scheme remain supreme. (7) Custom is applied unless inconsistent with statute (Gramophone; PUCL; Vellore). (8) Treaties may be read into constitutional guarantees where there is no law occupying the field (Vishaka), and the Constitution is construed to conform with India's international obligations (Puttaswamy ¶133). (9) But a statute, and a fortiori the Constitution, prevails where the conflict is real (Jolly George; Gramophone; A.D.M. Jabalpur), and cession of territory requires a constitutional amendment (Berubari) whereas a boundary settlement or servitude does not (Ram Kishore Sen; Sukumar Sengupta). (10) Whatever its courts decide, the State remains internationally responsible — Article 27 VCLT, Articles 3 and 32 ARSIWA, the Alabama Claims — so that Indian practice is best described as monism in interpretation and dualism in hierarchy.

Appendix C — Revision checklist: the ten questions you must be able to answer in sixty seconds

  1. Define monism and dualism in one sentence each, with their exponents. One legal order with a common basis (Kelsen; Lauterpacht, Fitzmaurice, Starke) versus two distinct, self-contained systems differing in subject-matter, sources and juridical origin (Triepel; Anzilotti).
  2. State the two questions of the topic. Application (ex proprio vigore or after transformation?) and supremacy (which prevails on conflict?).
  3. What is the Blackstonian doctrine and its two qualifications? Custom is part of the law of the land; subject to (i) no inconsistent statute and (ii) no contrary rule declared by the highest court.
  4. Does customary international law bind Indian courts? Yes, so far as not inconsistent with statute — Krishna Sharma, Gramophone, PUCL, Vellore, Pratap Singh — but never against the Constitution (A.D.M. Jabalpur).
  5. Does a treaty bind Indian courts? Only after legislation (Art. 253) — Jolly George, Birma, Shiv Kumar Sharma, G.G. Menon; but it may guide interpretation (Gramophone), fill a vacuum (Vishaka) and inform the Constitution (Puttaswamy).
  6. What happens on a conflict between a treaty and a statute? The statute prevails — Jolly George; Gramophone; Puttaswamy ¶133 — after an attempt at harmonious construction.
  7. What is the rule for territory? Cession → Article 368 (Berubari); boundary settlement or restoration of de jure foreign territory → no amendment (Ram Kishore Sen; Maganbhai); lease/servitude → no amendment (Sukumar Sengupta).
  8. Can a State plead its own law internationally? No — VCLT Arts. 27, 46; ARSIWA Arts. 3, 32; the Alabama Claims; LaGrand. But the tribunal treats municipal law as fact (Serbian and Brazilian Loans; Barcelona Traction) and does not rewrite it.
  9. What is the practice of the UK and the USA? UK: custom received, treaties legislated (Maclaine Watson). USA: custom federal (Paquete Habana), treaties supreme if self-executing (Sei Fujii; Medellín), later-in-time rule, Constitution supreme.
  10. Which theory is nearest reality? Neither as a universal description: practice is “a mixture of international law supremacy, municipal law supremacy and coordination”, and Indian practice is best captured as monism in interpretation, dualism in hierarchy.
