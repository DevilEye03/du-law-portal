2.0The Concept of “Sources” of International Law

A. Meaning of the word “source”

Oppenheim's image is the one every examiner expects: to find the source of a river we travel upstream until we reach the point at which the water wells up naturally from the soil; in the same way, to find the source of a rule of international law we travel back to the point of its origin. That point is the source. In the technical sense, the sources of international law are the methods or procedures by which rules of international law come into existence — the processes of law-creation recognised by the legal system itself.

The word is used in international law in at least four distinct senses, and most weak answers fail because they slide between them:

Table 2.2 — Four senses of “source”
SenseWhat it meansExample
Formal sourceThe process by which a rule derives its legal force and validity — the “law-creating process”.Custom, treaty, general principles. The fact that a rule is customary is why it binds.
Material sourceThe place from which the content of the rule is drawn — the substance, not the validity.State practice (the raw material of a custom); a model treaty; a draft prepared by the ILC; a national statute that is copied.
Evidence of lawThe materials from which a rule's existence is proved — the “law-determining agencies”.Diplomatic correspondence proving practice; a judgment; a resolution; a textbook.
Literary / historicalThe origins of the discipline or its ideas.Grotius, De Jure Belli ac Pacis (1625) as the “source” of the modern system.

B. Formal versus material sources — Salmond, Schwarzenberger, and the DU notes

The DU classroom notes open Topic 2 with exactly this distinction, quoting Salmond through Harris:

“A formal source is that from which a rule of law derives its force and validity… The material sources, on the other hand, are those from which is derived the matter, not the validity of the law. The material source supplies the substance of the rule to which the formal source gives the force and nature of law.”Salmond, Jurisprudence (7th ed.), para. 44, quoted in Harris, Cases and Materials on International Law, and reproduced in the DU PIL Notes (Topic 2) and in S.K. Verma, Ch. on Sources

The example the notes give is the crispest way to hold the distinction: a rule is legally binding if it satisfies the requirements of a custom — that is the formal source; its substance is supplied by State practice — that is the material source; and diplomatic correspondence is evidence of that practice. Three levels, three words, and examiners reward candidates who keep them apart.

Schwarzenberger's parallel vocabulary — law-creating processes and law-determining agencies — maps onto the same divide and matches the structure of Article 38 itself: sub-paragraphs (a)–(c) are the law-creating processes (treaty, custom, general principles); sub-paragraph (d) lists the law-determining agencies (judicial decisions and teachings), which do not create law but determine where it is. Verma makes this the organising point of the whole chapter: “in any asserted rule of international law, it must be shown that it is the product of one or more of the three law-creating processes… sub-paragraph (d) enumerates some of the means for the determination of alleged rules.”

C. Why Article 38 matters so much

Municipal systems answer the question “what is law?” with a constitution, a legislature and a hierarchy of courts. International law has no legislature, no compulsory court and no constitution in that sense. The nearest thing to an official statement of where the law is to be found is Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, 1945. As the DU notes put it, Article 38 is “widely recognised as the most authoritative and complete statement as to the sources of international law”; Verma adds the two qualifications that keep the answer honest — it is a direction to the Court (and consequently a guide for anybody else discovering the rules), and it is not exhaustive.

D. Is there a hierarchy of sources?

Three positions are taken, and a first-class answer states all three and chooses.

Position 1 — Article 38 lays down an order of priority (Verma, and the DU notes)

“Article 38 lays down the hierarchy of the sources of international law for the court”: the search goes first to treaties (“rules expressly recognised by the contesting States”), then to custom, then to general principles, and only if these are “not found relevant or clear” to judicial decisions and teachings as subsidiary means. This procedural priority is exactly why the 1994 and 1993 papers ask why custom was placed second rather than first.

Position 2 — No true hierarchy; the list is functionally ordered (Shaw, Brownlie, Fitzmaurice)

There is no rule that a treaty always prevails over a custom, or that general principles are subordinate to custom. The real hierarchy in the system is (i) jus cogens above everything, (ii) Article 103 of the UN Charter as against conflicting treaty obligations, and (iii) the ordinary rules of priority: lex specialis, lex posterior, and the maxim that a later custom can displace an earlier treaty on the same subject (and vice versa). Article 38 is a list of types of law, not a league table.

How to write it in the exam. Say: “Article 38 does not in terms establish a hierarchy; but it does establish an order of recourse, and this order explains itself: treaties are the most deliberate and most certain expression of State consent, custom the most general, and general principles the residual reservoir. The only true hierarchy in the system is above Article 38, not within it — jus cogens and Article 103 of the Charter.” That sentence will hold for any question on the point.

E. Jus cogens and obligations erga omnes — the two ideas that sit above Article 38

(i) Peremptory norms (jus cogens). Article 53 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 provides that a treaty is void if, at the time of its conclusion, it conflicts with a peremptory norm of general international law — “a norm accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character”; Article 64 then provides that if a new peremptory norm emerges, any existing treaty in conflict with it becomes void and terminates. The International Law Commission adopted Draft Conclusions on the Identification and Legal Consequences of Peremptory Norms in 2022, and gave as examples the prohibitions of aggression, genocide, crimes against humanity, torture, slavery and racial discrimination, and the right of self-determination. The point for Topic 2 is that jus cogens is not a “source” in the Article 38 sense: it is a status that a norm of customary international law may acquire, and it defeats contrary treaties and contrary custom alike. It also defeats the persistent objector (ILC Conclusion 15(3), 2018, expressly leaves the question open but the prevailing view is that a State cannot opt out of a peremptory norm).

(ii) Obligations erga omnes. In Barcelona Traction, Light and Power Co. (Belgium v. Spain), ICJ Rep. 1970, p. 3, the Court distinguished obligations owed to a particular State from obligations owed to the international community as a whole — the latter are the concern of all States and “all States can be held to have a legal interest in their protection; they are obligations erga omnes” (para. 33). The Court instanced the outlawry of aggression and genocide, and the protection of the basic rights of the human person. In its Chagos Advisory Opinion (2019) the Court held that the right to self-determination is one such obligation, owed erga omnes (paras. 180, 182), and in Legal Consequences of the Construction of a Wall (2004) it applied the same characterisation to the right of the Palestinian people to self-determination. For Topic 2 the significance is that a violation of such an obligation can be invoked by States that have suffered no material injury — a question that leads directly into the law of State responsibility (ARSIWA Arts. 42(b), 48) but which also shows that the “sources” list in Article 38 is a list of forms, not of relative importance.

F. Article 38 and the problem of non liquet

If a case comes before the Court and there is no treaty, no custom and no obvious general principle, may the Court say “there is no law; I cannot decide” (non liquet)? The system's answer is twofold. First, Article 38(1)(c) — general principles — exists precisely to prevent that result: an international judicial body is under a duty to adjudicate and should not decline on the ground that the law is silent or obscure (Verma, citing the drafting rationale of Root and Phillimore). Secondly, Article 38(2) allows the Court to decide a case ex aequo et bono — but only if the parties agree, and no ICJ case has ever been so decided (§2.1.5). The result is that the risk of non liquet is met in practice by general principles and by generous interpretation, not by free-floating fairness.

Real example — the gap that was filled. In the Corfu Channel case (Merits, ICJ Rep. 1949, p. 4) there was no treaty requiring Albania to warn shipping of mines in its territorial waters, and no settled custom addressed to a State that had itself laid — or allowed to be laid — mines in a channel used by international traffic. The Court filled the gap with three propositions it described as “certain general and well-recognised principles”: elementary considerations of humanity, the freedom of maritime communication, and every State's obligation not to allow knowingly its territory to be used for acts contrary to the rights of other States (p. 22). That is Article 38(1)(c) doing the work the drafters intended it to do.

2.1Statute of the International Court of Justice, 1945 — Article 38

A. The text, clause by clause

Article 38, Statute of the International Court of Justice

1. The Court, whose function is to decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it, shall apply:

(a) international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognised by the contesting States;

(b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law;

(c) the general principles of law recognised by civilised nations;

(d) subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.

2. This provision shall not prejudice the power of the Court to decide a case ex aequo et bono, if the parties agree thereto.

Everything in this topic is an unpacking of those seven lines. Take them in pieces.

Table 2.3 — Article 38 dissected
ClauseKey words to quoteWhat the words do
Opening words“in accordance with international law”Shows Article 38 is a directive to the Court, not a definition of international law. The Court decides in accordance with international law; Article 38 tells it where to look. It follows that the list is not necessarily exhaustive of all international law (a point the 1920 drafters and the modern users of GA/SC resolutions both rely on).
(a) Conventions“general or particular”; “expressly recognised by the contesting States”Treaties come first in the order of recourse, but only those the parties to the dispute have accepted apply against them. Hence a treaty can be “general” in content yet “particular” in its obligation.
(b) Custom“as evidence of a general practice accepted as law”Three ideas in nine words: evidence (custom is proved, not proclaimed); general practice (the objective, material element); accepted as law (the subjective element — opinio juris). Note that the Article 38 formula is clumsy: custom is not “evidence of” practice; practice is evidence of custom. Cite the Asylum case's rephrasing: “a constant and uniform usage, accepted as law”.
(c) General principles“recognised by civilised nations”The gap-filler, drawn largely (per Root and Phillimore) from principles in foro domestico — accepted in the domestic forum of nations — such as good faith, res judicata and procedure. The word “civilised” is today read as meaning “independent”/“the community of nations” and is criticised (§2.1.3 E).
(d) Judicial decisions & teachings“subject to… Article 59”; “subsidiary means”Decisions and books do not make law; they help to find it. The words “subject to Article 59” remove any doctrine of binding precedent (Article 59: a decision binds only the parties and only in that case).
(2) Ex aequo et bono“if the parties agree thereto”A power to decide outside the strict rules of law — available only with the consent of both parties, and never yet exercised by the PCIJ or the ICJ.

Verbatim drill. In the examination, write Article 38(1) out in full before you begin your answer, and write Article 38(2) as well even when the question is only about judicial decisions. It costs you four minutes and it is the single most reliable mark-winner on this topic: it shows the examiner immediately that you know the provision that organises the whole paper.

B. Drafting history — 1920 and 1945

The 1920 origin. Article 38 was first drafted for the Statute of the Permanent Court of International Justice by the Advisory Committee of Jurists that met at The Hague in 1920 to plan the PCIJ. The Committee's rapporteur, Baron Descamps, wanted the Court to apply not only treaties and custom but also “the rules of international law recognised by the legal conscience of civilised peoples” and, failing these, to decide in accordance with justice. That formula looked too much like natural law to the positivist members, and the compromise — proposed by Elihu Root (USA) and Lord Phillimore (Great Britain) — was the phrase “general principles of law recognised by civilised nations”. Root and Phillimore explained that what they had in mind were “principles accepted by all nations in foro domestico” — in the domestic forum — such as certain principles of procedure, the principle of good faith and the principle of res judicata. That explanation is the key to §2.1.3 below.

The 1945 retention. When the United Nations Charter and the new ICJ Statute were drafted at San Francisco in 1945, the Commission of Jurists (Committee IV/1) retained Article 38 substantially unchanged — this is why the ICJ's sources provision is, word for word, the PCIJ's, and why the 1944 and 1993 examination questions speak of “Article 38 of the Statute of the PCIJ now of ICJ”. Two changes were made: the opening words “whose function is to decide in accordance with international law such disputes as are submitted to it” were added (to make clear that the Court decides legal disputes in accordance with law), and the provision was carried into the new Statute as Article 38 of the ICJ Statute, which forms an integral part of the UN Charter (Article 92 of the Charter).

What 1945 did not do. The framers did not add resolutions of the General Assembly or of the Security Council to the list. Two reasons are given: the law-making significance of those organs was not then foreseen (Verma), and the framers did not want to describe as binding recommendations which the Charter itself describes as recommendations. Both reasons explain the whole of §2.2 below.

C. What Article 38 does and does not do

It does
  • Tell the Court (and the international lawyer) where to look for the law: treaty, custom, general principles — and, subsidiarily, decisions and writings.
  • Reflect the consent-based (positivist) orientation of 1920 and 1945: conventions “expressly recognised by the contesting States”; custom “accepted as law”.
  • Protect against non liquet, through clause (c) and, with the parties' consent, clause (2).
  • Give the system a working order of recourse which practitioners follow to this day.
It does not
  • It does not define international law; it only directs the Court. Hence the 2015 question, “Can Article 38 be regarded as a complete statement of the sources of international law?”
  • It does not mention resolutions of the General Assembly or of the Security Council — the subject of the entire syllabus head 2.2.
  • It does not mention unilateral acts of States (Nuclear Tests, 1974), decisions of international organisations, or jus cogens.
  • It does not set up any formal hierarchy beyond the order of recourse.
  • It does not tell us how custom is proved — that work has been done by the case law (§2.1.2) and by the ILC's 2018 Conclusions.

D. Seven criticisms of Article 38 (for the “completeness” question)

  1. The list is incomplete. Nothing in Article 38 covers GA and SC resolutions — now a principal engine of law-making (§2.2.1–2.2.2) — nor unilateral declarations, nor decisions of international organisations binding their members, nor jus cogens.
  2. “Civilised nations” is an anachronism. The phrase preserves the language of 1920 empire; the Charter itself speaks only of “peace-loving States” and “the community of nations”. Modern practice reads clause (c) as referring to the general principles of the legal systems of the community of nations (Verma).
  3. Custom is described inaccurately. Clause (b) says custom is “evidence of a general practice”; the accurate statement is that practice plus opinio juris is custom. The Asylum case supplies the correct formula (“constant and uniform usage, accepted as law”).
  4. “Subsidiary” undersells judicial decisions. Article 38(1)(d) calls decisions a subsidiary means, yet the PCIJ and ICJ have in practice created rules — straight baselines (Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries, 1951), UN legal personality (Reparation, 1949), the reservations test (Reservations to the Genocide Convention, 1951) — and States plead them as authorities every day. Art. 59 does not prevent a decision from having persuasive weight far beyond the parties.
  5. Article 38 confuses sources with evidence. Verma's point: (a)–(c) are law-creating processes, (d) is a list of law-determining agencies; the Article puts them in one list without saying that they answer different questions.
  6. It is Eurocentric in its pedigree and says nothing about the special position of new States — the Third World critique (cross-refer to Topic 1, §1.6).
  7. It is silent on the hierarchy of norms — on jus cogens (VCLT Arts. 53, 64) and on Article 103 of the Charter, the two rules that today really do order the system.

The two-line answer to “Is Article 38 a complete statement?” “Article 38 is the most authoritative and complete statement of the sources, and it remains the starting point of every inquiry; but it is not an exhaustive list. It omits the resolutions of the General Assembly and the Security Council, unilateral acts of States, decisions of international organisations and peremptory norms; and it was drafted in 1920 for a Court that was bound to apply, but not to define, the law. The correct view is that of the Court's own practice: Article 38 is a direction to find the law 'in accordance with international law', and international law today includes materials beyond its four corners.”

Why this topic is contested ground. Sources are where power is expressed as law. A State that can show that its practice is “general”, that its treaty text has become “custom”, or that a resolution it sponsored carries “normative value”, has turned a political position into a legal obligation for others. This is why the former colonial powers have historically favoured treaty and custom as the twin sources (both built on State consent, both controlled by State practice), and why the new States and the Third World have pressed the claims of General Assembly resolutions, of the common heritage of mankind, and of equitable principles (cross-refer Topic 1, §1.6). Keep that sentence in reserve: it lifts a technical answer into a critical one.

2.1.1International Treaties and Conventions

A. What counts as a “treaty”, and the vocabulary you must not confuse

Article 38(1)(a) speaks of “international conventions… establishing rules expressly recognised by the contesting States”. The DU notes make the essential point about the word “convention”: “The term Conventions is used in a general and inclusive sense. It would seem to apply to any treaty, convention, protocol or agreement, regardless of its title or form.” What matters is not the label but the intention of the parties to create legal obligations governed by international law.

The governing definition is in Article 2(1)(a) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 (VCLT), the “treaty on treaties”, which entered into force on 27 January 1980 and codifies the law of treaties for written agreements between States:

“‘Treaty’ means an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation.”VCLT, Art. 2(1)(a)

B. “General or particular”; law-making treaties versus treaty-contracts

Article 38(1)(a) divides conventions into the general and the particular. Both the DU notes and the LED capsule explain the division, and it is the most frequently examined sentence in this sub-topic:

“A convention may be general, either because of the number of parties to it, or because of the character of its contents; it may be particular because of the limited number of parties to it, or because of the limited character of its subject-matter.”PIL Notes (Stanzin), Topic 2, reproducing the prescribed texts

The classical elaboration is the distinction between law-making treaties and treaty-contracts:

Law-making treaties (traités-lois) — a source of law

Two sub-types, as the DU notes put them:

  • Treaties enunciating rules of universal international law — the UN Charter is the standard example (and the 1945 scenario about the Havana Charter of the ITO is built on it).
  • Treaties laying down general principles, concluded by a large number of States — the 1958 Geneva Conventions on the Law of the Sea and the VCLT, 1969.

The LED capsule adds the older examples: the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907, the Peace Treaty of 1919, the Treaty for the Renunciation of War 1928, and the 1929 Geneva Prisoners of War Convention.

Treaty-contracts (traités-contrats) — a source of obligation only

Agreements between two or a few States on a matter concerning only them: a boundary treaty, a trade agreement, an extradition treaty. They create obligations which the general law would not have imposed, and modify the general rules as between the parties; they are “not directly a source of law” (Verma), but they may feed custom when the same clause is repeated in many treaties. Two examples given in the prescribed text are the law of extradition (developed through hundreds of extradition treaties) and diplomatic and consular privileges (developed through many bilateral treaties and codified in the VCDR 1961 and VCCR 1963).

Fitzmaurice's warning (worth a mark). Sir Gerald Fitzmaurice rejected the whole “general/particular” and “law-making/treaty-contract” classification as of “extremely dubious validity”. His point: whether a treaty makes law is not a property of its form but a consequence of its content and of how non-parties respond to it. A bilateral treaty on a matter of general concern, universally followed, can make custom; a multilateral convention that codifies nothing and is widely flouted does not. Cite this and you show you have read beyond the capsule.

C. Is a treaty really a “source”? — the strongest jurisprudential argument in this sub-topic

Verma puts the objection at its sharpest, and it is the best-developed argument in the prescribed text on this head.

  • The objection. A treaty is akin to a private-law contract: it creates rights and obligations for the parties who adopt it and, in principle, for nobody else. Hence “Art. 38(1)(a) of the Statute does not refer international conventions as a source of law, but as sources of ‘rules expressly recognised by the contesting States’”. On this view, treaties are formally a source of obligation rather than a source of law: “Treaty may lead to or reflect law, but is not a law in itself.”
  • Brierly's answer. There are classes of treaties which are a source of general law — those concluded by a large number of States for (i) declaring their understanding of what the law is on a subject, (ii) laying down a new general rule for future conduct, or (iii) creating an international institution. These are “the substitute in the international system for legislation, and they are conveniently referred to as ‘law-making’”.
  • The limit of even the law-making treaty. It still binds only the parties. There is no treaty of universal membership — not even the UN Charter, which binds Members and, in respect of the maintenance of peace and security, non-members so far as may be necessary (Charter, Art. 2(6)).
  • The modern practice that proves the point. Treaties today are the most important source precisely because States have found in them “a deliberate method by which to create binding international law” (DU notes). The Vienna Conventions on diplomatic and consular relations, UNCLOS, the Genocide Convention, the ICCPR/ICESCR, the Rome Statute and the Chemical Weapons Convention were all made by treaty; there is no other way to make them.

E. Reservations and interpretation — the two questions examiners love

(i) Reservations. A reservation is a unilateral statement made by a State when signing, ratifying, accepting, approving or acceding to a treaty, whereby it purports to exclude or modify the legal effect of certain provisions of the treaty in their application to that State (VCLT, Art. 2(1)(d)). The law:

  • Article 19: a State may formulate a reservation unless (a) the reservation is prohibited by the treaty; (b) the treaty provides that only specified reservations may be made; or (c) in cases not falling under (a) and (b), the reservation is incompatible with the object and purpose of the treaty.
  • Article 20: acceptance by other States; a reservation is established as soon as at least one other contracting State accepts it; for the constituent instruments of international organisations, acceptance by the competent organ is required.
  • Article 21: effects — the reservation modifies the provision as between the reserving State and the accepting State; and between the reserving State and an objecting State, the provision does not apply at all (unless the objector says otherwise).
  • Article 22: withdrawal of reservations and objections.

The object-and-purpose test comes from the ICJ's Advisory Opinion on Reservations to the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, ICJ Rep. 1951, p. 15 — where the Court, in derogation of the older rule of unanimity, held that a reservation incompatible with the object and purpose cannot be made, and that a reserving State may be regarded as a party if the reservation is compatible. This test, invented by the Court, was subsequently codified in VCLT Articles 19–23 — one of the clearest examples in international law of a judicial decision generating a treaty rule (Verma makes exactly this point in the section on the practice of international organisations). The 2000 paper asks for the reservations rule “with special reference to the Asylum case (1951) and the VCLT”; the reference to Asylum is best handled by explaining that the Asylum case concerned a regional custom allegedly modified by treaty-commitments which Peru had not ratified — so that the same logic of non-opposability to a non-consenting State governs both (see Case 4).

(ii) Interpretation. The general rule is Article 31(1) VCLT: a treaty “shall be interpreted in good faith in accordance with the ordinary meaning to be given to the terms of the treaty in their context and in the light of its object and purpose”. Article 31(3) requires account to be taken of any subsequent agreement and subsequent practice establishing the agreement of the parties regarding interpretation; Article 32 permits recourse to the preparatory work and the circumstances of conclusion as supplementary means, where the Article 31 result is ambiguous, obscure, or manifestly absurd or unreasonable. Note the link back to §2.1.4: the ICJ's own practice is a “subsequent practice” mechanism, and its interpretations of treaty clauses (Art. 2(4) and Art. 51 in Nicaragua; Art. 36 VCCR in LaGrand, Avena and Jadhav) become the working meaning of those clauses.

F. Third parties, and when treaties are void

Pacta tertiis nec nocent nec prosunt — treaties neither harm nor benefit third States without their consent. The VCLT's rules:

  • Article 34: a treaty does not create obligations or rights for a third State without its consent.
  • Article 35: an obligation arises for a third State only if the parties intend the provision to be the means of establishing that obligation and the third State expressly accepts it in writing.
  • Article 36: a right arises for a third State if the parties so intend and the third State assents (presumed so long as it does not indicate the contrary).
  • Article 37: revocation or modification of such obligations and rights requires the consent of all the parties and, in the case of an obligation, of the third State.
  • Article 38: nothing in Articles 34–37 precludes a rule of custom from becoming binding on a third State as such a rule.

Two exceptions to be able to state: objective regimes — treaties which, by common intention, fix a status binding on all States (boundary treaties; treaties creating a neutralised State such as Switzerland in 1815; treaties establishing an international institution; the Antarctic Treaty 1959) — and Article 103 of the UN Charter, under which obligations under the Charter prevail over obligations under any other international agreement. The 1991 short-note “treaty as a source of law for third parties” is answered by (i) the general rule, (ii) the objective-regime exception, (iii) Article 103, and (iv) the route through custom (Art. 38 VCLT).

Validity. The treaty head also carries the rebus sic stantibus note (asked in 2002, 1991, 1993). Article 62 VCLT permits termination or suspension where a fundamental change of circumstances, not foreseen by the parties, has occurred, provided the change was not caused by the party invoking it and the circumstances constituted an essential basis of the consent; the Article expressly excludes treaties establishing a boundary from being terminated on that ground, and may not be invoked to undermine peace and security. In the ICJ case law the doctrine has been treated very restrictively (the Gabčíkovo–Nagymaros case, 1997, paras. 104 and 112–115, rejected Hungary's plea).

G. Treaty and custom — the three modes of interaction

1. Declaratory

The treaty declares existing custom. Example: Art. 2(4) of the Charter is treated in Nicaragua as embodied both in the Charter and in customary law. Effect: a reservation excluding “multilateral treaties” from ICJ jurisdiction does not shut out the customary claim.

2. Crystallising

Practice was emerging; the treaty fixes it. Example: the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Continental Shelf's Articles 1–3 on the legal régime of the shelf (North Sea, paras. 63–64). Effect: Articles 1–3 could not be reserved against, because they were regarded as reflecting emergent customary law.

3. Generative

The treaty creates a rule which later becomes custom binding non-parties. The North Sea test (paras. 70–74) sets the conditions and famously refused to apply them to Art. 6 of the 1958 Convention. Later examples: the straight-baseline rules and the continental-shelf articles of UNCLOS; the VCDR; the Genocide Convention.

And the other direction — custom behind the treaty. Where a treaty rule and a customary rule are identical, the treaty does not “supervene” the custom: in Nicaragua (Merits), ICJ Rep. 1986, p. 14, the Court held that “there are no grounds for holding that when customary international law is comprised of rules identical to those of treaty law, the latter ‘supervenes’ the former, so that the customary international law has no further existence of its own” (p. 95). Two consequences of practical importance: (i) both may be applied even where one is excluded by a jurisdictional reservation; (ii) a State that is not a party to the treaty may still be bound by the identical custom.

Model skeleton for “treaties as a source” (2025 short note / 2002 Q.6 / 2001 Q.2). (1) Article 38(1)(a) text. (2) Definition — VCLT Art. 2(1)(a). (3) General vs. particular; law-making vs. treaty-contract, with the DU notes' examples. (4) The jurisprudential objection (treaty = contract) and Brierly's answer. (5) The mechanics (formation, ratification, registration, pacta sunt servanda, Art. 27). (6) Reservations — VCLT Art. 19 and the 1951 Genocide AO. (7) Third parties — Arts. 34–38, objective regimes, Art. 103. (8) Treaty and custom — three modes, with North Sea and Nicaragua. (9) Conclusion: treaties are today the most important source because they are the only deliberate law-making mechanism in a system without a legislature, but their binding circle is the circle of consent.

A. What custom is, and how it differs from “usage”

Article 38(1)(b) describes custom as “evidence of a general practice accepted as law”. The DU notes and the prescribed texts agree on the operative definition and on the crucial distinction between the two words that laypeople treat as synonyms:

“Custom is a habitual course of conduct. A customary rule of international law may be defined as a rule which the community of States has since long recognised as the right rule of conduct, and which has the force of law… Usage represents the twilight stage of custom. Custom begins where usage ends.”S.K. Verma (quoting Starke): “Usage represents the twilight stage of custom, custom begins where usage ends.”
  • Usage = a habit of action often repeated by States, which has not yet received the force of law. Usages may be conflicting and vary between States.
  • Custom = usage plus the conviction of legal obligation. Custom is self-consistent and unified; it binds.
  • Because the two are on a continuum, no rule tells you the precise moment at which usage becomes custom — which is exactly why the case law has had to build tests (§D below).

The older Anglo-American vocabulary (from the notes of S.K. Kapoor) puts the same idea as a two-limb test, and it is worth quoting because it is easy to remember:

The corpus test (objective)

A material fact — the actual observance of a line of conduct by States, proved as a fact. This is the usus or consuetudo limb.

The animus test (subjective)

An intention to follow the practice as law — opinio juris sive necessitatis. When the two limbs combine, the usage is consummated into custom.

The Latin. Opinio juris sive necessitatis = “an opinion of law or of necessity”. Two rival translations matter in argument: if the requirement is opinio juris strictly (a belief that the practice is already law), the rule cannot be created by practice alone; if it is opinio necessitatis (a sense that the practice is required), then a nascent rule can form before everyone accepts it as settled law. The ICJ's formula — “a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law requiring it” (North Sea, para. 77) — is closer to the first.

B. Element 1 — State practice (the objective, material element)

What counts as practice. The DU notes and the prescribed text give a long but examinable list. Practice includes: treaties, diplomatic correspondence and relations, opinions of national legal advisers, national legislation, policy statements, press releases, official manuals on legal questions, executive decisions and practices, decisions of international and national courts, and the practices of international organisations.

Verma then groups practice into three families — a structure worth reproducing in an answer:

1. Mutual relations among States

Diplomatic correspondence, press releases, bilateral treaties and MOUs, acts and declarations of statesmen, positive statements, the interaction of rival claims and (importantly) acquiescence in or tolerance of another State's claim. The development of the law of the sea is the standing example.

2. Practice of international organisations

Statements made and votes cast by State representatives in UN organs and conferences; resolutions and decisions; institutional practice. Example: the practice under Article 27(3) of the Charter that abstention by a permanent member is not a veto.

3. Unilateral acts of States

Legislative acts, decisions of State courts, opinions of law officers (especially when published), military and administrative practice. What the law requires is concurrence, not mere parallelism — the Scotia case (US Supreme Court, 1872) on collision regulations.

Evidence. Official digests of practice, parliamentary papers, law reports, the internal regulations of diplomatic and consular services, and — the practical workhorse — digests of State practice. Note the link back to the ILC: in 1949 the Commission was asked under Article 24 of its Statute “to consider ways and means for making the evidence of customary international law more readily available”, and one of its recommendations was that Governments publish digests of their diplomatic correspondence (Verma, fn. 46). That is the ancestor of the ILC's 2018 Conclusions, §I below.

Acquiescence and protest — the Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries case. The case that best shows how silence can build or defeat a custom is the Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries case (UK v. Norway), ICJ Rep. 1951, p. 116, where Norway had drawn straight baselines along a deeply indented coast since decrees of 1869 and 1889. The Court:

“The general toleration of foreign States with regard to the Norwegian practice is an unchallenged fact. For a period of more than sixty years the United Kingdom itself in no way contested it… The notoriety of the facts, the general toleration of the international community, Great Britain's position in the North Sea, her own interest in the question, and her prolonged abstention would in any case warrant Norway's enforcement of her system against the United Kingdom.”ICJ Rep. 1951, pp. 138–139, as reproduced in S.K. Verma

Two rules follow, and both are examinable: (i) acquiescence presupposes knowledge — actual or constructive; a State cannot be taken to have acquiesced in a claim it could not have known; and (ii) a State that objects consistently while the rule is forming is protected (the persistent objector, §F).

Duration. There is no fixed period. The North Sea Court: “Although the passage of only a short period of time is not necessarily, or of itself, a bar to the formation of a new rule of customary international law… an indispensable requirement would be that within the period in question, short though it might be, State practice, including that of States whose interests are specially affected, should have been both extensive and virtually uniform in the sense of the provision invoked; and should moreover have occurred in such a way as to show a general recognition that a rule of law or legal obligation is involved” (para. 74). The modern example of near-instant custom is the law of outer space, evolved by consensus after 1957 and embodied in GA Res. 1963 (XVII) of 24 December 1963 and the Outer Space Treaty 1967.

Uniformity and consistency — and what a breach does. The classic statement is from Nicaragua (Merits), 1986:

“The Court does not consider that for a rule to be established as customary, the corresponding practice must be in absolutely rigorous conformity with the rule. In order to deduce the existence of customary rules, the conduct of States should, in general, be consistent with such rules, and… instances of State conduct inconsistent with a given rule should generally have been treated as breaches of that rule, and not as indication of the recognition of a new rule.”ICJ Rep. 1986, p. 98, para. 186 (paraphrased in the DU materials)

So: regular, repeated and uniform conduct creates custom; material departure may negate it; minor deviation does not. And where a rule is breached deliberately and the breach is treated by the community as a breach, the rule survives — indeed the condemnations prove it.

Generality — how general must “general” be? Not universal. Verma cites Judge Lachs' dissenting opinion in the North Sea cases: “to become binding, a rule or principle of international law need not pass the test of universal acceptance… The evidence should be sought in the behaviour of a great number of States, possibly the majority of States, in any case the great majority of the interested States.” Hence the doctrine of the “specially affected States”: on a question of maritime delimitation the practice of the United Kingdom and the United States matters most; on outer space, the practice of the United States and Russia. The ILC codified this in 2018: account is to be taken of the practice of States that are “particularly involved in the relevant activity” or most likely to be concerned with the alleged rule (Conclusion 8, commentary para. 4).

C. Element 2 — Opinio juris sive necessitatis (the subjective element)

State practice, however general, is not law unless it is accompanied by the belief that the practice is legally obligatory. The two foundational formulations are the ones to quote in the exam.

Lotus (PCIJ, Ser. A, No. 10, 1927), p. 28 — the negative formulation

“The circumstances alleged… merely show that States had often, in practice, abstained from instituting criminal proceedings, and not that they recognised themselves as being obliged to do so; for only if such abstention were based on their being conscious of having a duty to abstain would it be possible to speak of an international custom. The alleged fact does not allow one to infer that States have been conscious of having such a duty; on the other hand… there are other circumstances calculated to show that the contrary is true.”

North Sea Continental Shelf (ICJ Rep. 1969, p. 3), para. 77 — the positive formulation

“Not only must the acts concerned amount to a settled practice, but they must also be such, or be carried out in such a way, as to be evidence of a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law requiring it. The need for such a belief, i.e., the existence of a subjective element, is implicit in the very notion of the opinio juris sive necessitatis. The States concerned must therefore feel that they are conforming to what amounts to a legal obligation. The frequency, or even habitual character of the acts is not in itself enough. There are many international acts, e.g., in the field of ceremonial and protocol, which are performed almost invariably, but which are motivated only by considerations of courtesy, convenience or tradition, and not by any sense of legal duty.”

How is opinio juris proved? This is the practical difficulty on which examiners love to test candidates. Verma sets out the problem and the working solutions:

  • The subjective element is “one of motivation and of psychological nature” and, in a State whose legislature and executive both participate in decision-making, it cannot be established directly.
  • It is therefore inferred from external evidence: the content and wording of instruments, voting patterns in international organisations, statements made before or after a vote, and the subsequent conduct of States.
  • “It may also be inferred from the conduct of a group of States that a certain conduct has been regarded by them as a matter of duty, which may become a general rule of international law, if a sufficient number of States start considering it as binding on them and there is no effective protest by others.”
  • The ILC's 2018 Conclusions list the forms evidence may take: public statements on behalf of States, official publications, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence, decisions of national courts, treaty provisions, and conduct in connection with resolutions adopted by an international organisation or at an intergovernmental conference (Conclusion 10). Failure to react may serve as evidence of acceptance as law where the State was in a position to react and the circumstances called for reaction (Conclusion 10(3), read with Conclusion 6(3)).

The classic trap — “practice alone is not enough”. A question that gives you a long list of identical State acts and asks whether a custom has formed is testing opinio juris. The answer template: (i) state the two-element rule; (ii) apply Lotus — abstention or uniformity, without a sense of duty, is usage only; (iii) apply North Sea, para. 77 — acts must be carried out as evidence of a belief in obligation, and courtesy/protocol/convenience negate that belief; (iv) show that in North Sea itself some fifteen equidistance delimitations were held insufficient precisely because the States concerned might have been “motivated by other obvious factors” (para. 78); (v) conclude that a custom is not made out.

E. General custom, regional custom and local custom

A customary rule need not be universal. It may be regional (binding a group of States such as the Latin American States) or even local (binding only two States). The two printed DU cases are the authorities, and they are also the most-favoured exam questions in this head (2012 Q.2 is entirely on regional and local custom).

Regional custom — Asylum (ICJ Rep. 1950, p. 266)

Colombia claimed that a rule of “American international law” allowed the State granting diplomatic asylum to qualify the offence unilaterally and definitively, so binding Peru. The Court refused: the party relying on such a custom “must prove that the rule invoked by it is in accordance with a constant and uniform usage practised by the States in question, and that this usage is the expression of a right appertaining to the State granting asylum and a duty incumbent on the territorial State”. The evidence disclosed “so much uncertainty and contradiction, so much fluctuation and discrepancy… and the practice has been so much influenced by considerations of political expediency… that it is not possible to discern in all this any constant and uniform usage, accepted as law.” And even if such a custom existed among certain Latin-American States, it could not be invoked against Peru, which had refused to ratify the Montevideo Conventions of 1933 and 1939 — the very instruments that first contained the alleged rule.

Local custom between two States — Right of Passage over Indian Territory (ICJ Rep. 1960, p. 6)

India objected that “no local custom could be established between only two States”. The Court's answer is the sentence to memorise: “It is difficult to see why the number of States between which a local custom may be established on the basis of long practice must necessarily be larger than two. The Court sees no reason why long continued practice between two States accepted by them as regulating their relations should not form the basis of mutual rights and obligations between the two States.” On the facts the Court found a local custom as between Portugal and India (inherited from the practice between Portugal and the British) entitling Portugal to passage for private persons, civil officials and goods in general, but not for armed forces, armed police or arms and ammunition — for which previous authorisation was always required, and that requirement was “a negation of passage as of right”.

Why regional and local custom matter. They are exceptions to the general rule of generality, and their logic is consent-based: a custom that binds only those States which have participated in or acquiesced in it is easier to justify than one imposed on a State that has had no part in its formation. The ILC's 2018 Conclusion 16 recognises “particular customary international law” — rules applicable only among a limited number of States — and its commentary notes that this need not be geographically limited: a rule may develop among States “linked by a common cause, interest or activity”. The burden on the party asserting a regional custom is a heavy one: it must prove the practice is constant and uniform and that it is accepted as law both by the States which follow it and, at the least, not objected to by the State against which it is invoked.

F. The persistent objector — how a State escapes a custom

If a general custom binds all States, what happens to the State that has consistently said “not me”? The doctrine of the persistent objector answers: a State which objects to a rule while the rule is in the process of formation, and maintains its objection, is not bound by it.

  • The authority. Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (1951). The United Kingdom argued that a ten-mile rule for bays was established customary law. The Court held that even if it had acquired the authority of general international law, it “would appear to be inapplicable as against Norway inasmuch as she has always opposed any attempt to apply it to the Norwegian coast” (p. 131).
  • The requirement, in the ILC's words (2018, Conclusion 15): “Where a State has objected to a rule of customary international law while that rule was in the process of formation, the rule is not opposable to the State concerned for so long as it maintains its objection.” The objection must be clearly expressed, made known to other States, and maintained persistently. The expression “not opposable” is deliberately used to describe the objector's exceptional position.
  • No “subsequent objector” rule. Once the rule has crystallised, an objection will not exempt a State. Silence, in the face of a crystallising rule, amounts to acquiescence (and, if the State was in a position to react, may be evidence of opinio juris — Conclusion 10(3)).
  • The limit. Conclusion 15(3) leaves open the position in relation to jus cogens; the prevailing view, and the better one, is that a State cannot be a persistent objector to a peremptory norm.
  • How rare it is. The United States Restatement observes that “a State that indicates its dissent from a practice while the law is still in the process of development is not bound by that rule even after it matures… Historically, such dissent and consequent exemption from a principle that became a general customary law has been rare.” Candidates sometimes invoke it for the United States' three-mile territorial sea claim or for the 200-mile fisheries zone; in each case the better analysis is that the claim failed and the State eventually accepted the new rule, not that it established a permanent exemption.

The Third-World resonance. The persistent objector doctrine is the source-doctrine equivalent of sovereign equality: it says that a State cannot be legislated for by the practice of others. It is therefore a doctrine instinctively favoured by States which suspect that the “general practice” being asserted is in fact the practice of a small group of maritime and trading powers. Note the symmetry with the debate about General Assembly resolutions (§2.2.1): the same States that rely on resolutions as proof of majority will — depending on the issue — rely on persistent objection to resist the custom of the powerful.

G. New States, the burden of proof, and continuity of custom

Two further questions arise with custom, and both are worth a short paragraph in any answer.

  • Burden of proof. The party which asserts a custom must prove it — and must prove it in a way that binds the other party. Asylum is the authority: Colombia “must prove” the constant and uniform usage and that it was the expression of a right and a duty; and even a proven regional custom “could not be invoked against Peru which… has, on the contrary, repudiated it”. In North Sea, the burden lay on Denmark and the Netherlands to prove that Article 6 had become general law binding on Germany.
  • New States and inherited custom. When a State becomes independent, it is bound by pre-existing general customary law without needing to consent: this is the classic “new States” problem (cross-refer Topic 1, §1.6). The transitional practice has been for new States to accept the corpus of general law (including, for example, the law of the sea and diplomatic immunities) while contesting particular rules — unequal treaties, colonial boundary arrangements, economic rules unfavourable to them. For the exam the safe formulation is: new States are bound by general custom; they are not bound by local or regional customs to which they have not succeeded; and their practice, once established, can generate new custom.
  • Continuity and change of government. Customary obligations attach to the State, not the government: a revolution, the amendment or replacement of a constitution, or a change of regime does not release a State from customary obligations (Verma makes this point in the monism–dualism discussion, and it applies equally here).

A. The text and the drafting history — Root, Phillimore and in foro domestico

“The general principles of law recognised by civilised nations.”Article 38(1)(c), Statute of the ICJ

The clause exists because the 1920 Committee of Jurists refused to leave the Court without an answer when treaty and custom were silent. What did it mean? The men who drafted it told us, and their explanation is the one to give in an examination (Verma reproduces it from the Procès-Verbaux of the Advisory Committee of Jurists, 1920, p. 335):

“The general principles referred to…were those which were accepted by all nations in foro domestico, such as certain principles of procedure, the principle of good faith, and the principle of res judicata, etc.”Mr. Elihu Root (USA) and Lord Phillimore (Great Britain), the authors of the compromise formula; Lord Phillimore added that by “general principles” he meant “maxims of law”

So the source is not a mysterious reservoir of natural law. It is — in its origin — the comparative method: find the principle which the legal systems of the world hold in common, and apply it internationally. Two further points of construction:

  • Two possible readings, and the better one. (i) The clause refers to general principles of international law recognised by States as part of custom; or (ii) it refers to general principles of law in the widest sense, including principles of private law found in most municipal systems. The better view, and the one supported by the drafting history and by Waldock's survey of the World Court's practice, is (ii) — with the qualification that the principle must be one which international law can absorb without distortion.
  • McNair's caution. Judge McNair warned (in the International Status of South-West Africa Advisory Opinion, ICJ Rep. 1950, p. 148) that private-law rules are not imported “lock, stock and barrel”: the duty of an international tribunal is “to regard any features or terminology which are reminiscent of the rules and institutions of private law as an indication of policy and principles rather than as directly importing these rules and institutions”. Quote this and you distinguish yourself from the candidate who simply says “municipal law is a source”.
  • The “reservoir” point. Verma's conclusion: general principles recognised in national tribunals constitute “an inexhaustible reservoir of principles, which an international tribunal is authorised by Art. 38 to apply in an international dispute”.

B. Function — why the clause is indispensable

1. To avoid non liquet

An international judicial body is under a duty to adjudicate; it “should not refrain from giving judgment on the ground that the law is silent or obscure” (Verma). Clause (c) is the anti-non liquet provision. The corollary: the Court applies general principles only where there is no relevant treaty or clear customary rule — “a convention or any clear rule of customary law” being absent.

2. To add a “flexible element”

Waldock's formulation, quoted in the prescribed text: the Court treats the “common law” it is authorised to apply under Article 38(1)(b) and (c) “very much as a single corpus of law… para. (c) adds to this corpus a flexible element which enables the court to give greater completeness to customary law and in some degree to extend it”. That is the clause's creative function — and the reason positivists object to it (§D).

3. To give the Court its method

Procedure, evidence, the power to determine its own competence (compétence de la compétence), res judicata, the effect of judgments, the rule that parties must be heard — the whole procedural grammar of international adjudication comes from this clause.

4. To structure remedies

Reparation for a wrong, restitution, compensation, satisfaction, the rule that a party cannot take advantage of its own wrong (Chorzów Factory), and the principle of “full reparation” now codified in ARSIWA Articles 31 and 34–37 all rest on general principles.

D. The doctrinal battle — naturalists, positivists and Tunkin

This is the analytical core of the 2025 Question 8, and the reason the head is set for study at all. Four positions:

Naturalists: the clause imports natural law

Verdross argued that Article 38(1)(c) has the effect of incorporating natural law into international law, so that any rule in conflict with natural law is invalid. Fitzmaurice similarly treated the clause as resting on “necessary principles of natural law”, and as constituting those principles a formal, not merely a material, source of law — instancing, among others, the principle that a State cannot plead the deficiencies of its internal law as an excuse for failing to perform its international obligations, or impossibility of performance arising from a situation it has itself brought about.

Positivists: consent is the only basis

Tunkin and the Soviet school maintained that Article 38(1)(c) “adds nothing new to the existing sources of international law, i.e., custom and treaties” — general principles are evidentially useful but are not an independent source. Behind the objection lies a constitutional fear: that the clause permits international law to be developed by judges rather than by States.

The Court's own practice (the answer to give)

The PCIJ and ICJ have applied general principles without always saying whether they were applying a general principle of national or of international law — “while relying on this source, [they] have frequently either referred to the customary law or left it ambiguous” (Verma). But they have applied them: res judicata in the Administrative Tribunal opinion; circumstantial evidence and elementary considerations of humanity in Corfu Channel; res judicata and reparation in Chorzów; estoppel in Preah Vihear; corporate personality in Barcelona Traction.

The balanced conclusion

The clause is neither a Trojan horse for natural law nor a mere footnote to custom. It is a consensual device with a dynamic effect: the principles it looks to are common to the legal systems of the community of nations, and therefore reflect a consensus (so the positivists' premise is respected), while at the same time supplying the Court with the flexibility to complete and extend custom (so the naturalists' aspiration is met). Waldock's “flexible element” and the “death-knell to positivism” remark of Starke are the two sentences to contrast — Starke's claim, says the prescribed text, “is overdrawn”.

How to answer 2025 Question 8 in one page. The question asks for your opinion on the vagueness of Article 38(1)(c) and on Tunkin's argument that it is not an independent source. Skeleton: (1) set out the text and the Root–Phillimore drafting explanation (in foro domestico, maxims of law); (2) explain the function — the duty to adjudicate and the avoidance of non liquet (Verma's point that the Court “should not refrain from giving judgment on the ground that the law is silent or obscure”); (3) state Tunkin's objection and the naturalist counter-plea, with Verdross and Fitzmaurice; (4) examine the case law — Effect of Awards and Preah Vihear from your prescribed material, plus Chorzów, Corfu Channel, Mavrommatis, Barcelona Traction; (5) concede the vagueness, but answer it with three propositions — the principles must be general (accepted in the community of legal systems), transposable (McNair: indication of policy, not direct importation) and subsidiary in practice (applied only where treaty and custom are silent); (6) conclude that the clause is an independent source of law but an exceptional one — “an inexhaustible reservoir” rather than a first resort.

E. “Civilised nations” — the objection, and what the phrase means today

  • The objection. The words date from 1920 and reflect a world in which the law was made by a club of European and American States. They are offensive in form and inaccurate in substance: the community of nations is nearly universal, and Article 38 had no difficulty operating after decolonisation without amendment.
  • What the phrase means today (Verma). In practice the phrase “relates to the general principles in the legal systems of independent nations, leaving out the tribal law which may not be sufficiently developed in the modern sense of the term”. In other words, the criterion is the existence of a developed legal system, not a civilisational hierarchy.
  • The modern test in practice. Does the principle appear in substantially all the principal legal systems of the world? Does it serve the basic needs of any legal order (justice, good faith, finality, procedural fairness)? Can it be applied between States without distorting either the principle or international law? If yes on all three, the principle is available under Article 38(1)(c).
  • Reform proposal. Several writers have proposed codifying the general principles — a project associated with Bin Cheng (General Principles of Law as Applied by International Courts and Tribunals, 1953, and his call for codification in the Current Legal Problems) — while others resist codification precisely because the clause's value lies in its flexibility.

2.1.4Judicial Decisions and Juristic Opinion

A. The text, Article 59, and what “subsidiary means” means

“Subject to the provisions of Article 59, judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law.”Article 38(1)(d), ICJ Statute
“The decision of the Court has no binding force except between the parties and in respect of that particular case.”Article 59, ICJ Statute

Three consequences follow, and they are the answer to the whole of this head:

  • There is no stare decisis in international law. A decision of the ICJ binds only the parties and only for that case. The Court is not bound by its own earlier judgments, and a later Court may “distinguish” or even silently depart from an earlier one.
  • But the practice is otherwise. Article 38(1)(d) calls decisions “subsidiary means” and Article 59 denies them binding force, yet “in practice, they play a larger part in the development of international law” (Verma). States plead them; the Court cites them; and where there is no pre-existing rule the decision is, functionally, a direct source of law. Judge Azevedo's remark in the Asylum case is the quotable version: a decision “has deep repercussions… because views which have been confirmed by that decision acquire quasi-legislative value”.
  • The reason for the “subsidiary” label. In the normal conduct of international relations it is State practice which creates the rules; decisions and writings help to determine them. The label therefore describes the order of authority, not the absence of influence.

That is Article 59 working as it should: persuasion without bondage.

C. Decisions of municipal courts — two distinct functions

Function 1 — as evidence of the law

A national court's decision shows how one State understands a rule and how it applies it. The value depends on the court's standing and the intrinsic merits of the judgment; “generally, decisions rendered by the United States Supreme Court and of British courts on matters of international law have considerable weight” (Verma).

Function 2 — as material for a new custom

The concurrence of decisions in a fairly large number of countries is “indicative of the uniformity in practice, which may lead to the formulation of a new customary rule”. Conversely, divergence defeats the claim: in Lotus, the Court surveyed municipal decisions on jurisdiction over high-seas collisions and “found no uniformity on the matter”, concluding that it was “hardly possible to see in it an indication of the existence of the restrictive rule of international law” for which France contended.

The three classic municipal decisions.

  • The Paquete Habana (US Supreme Court, 1900, 175 U.S. 677) — two small fishing smacks were seized as prize during the Spanish–American War. The Supreme Court held that coastal fishing vessels are exempt from capture, and the passage on the use of juristic writing is the most quoted in the literature, especially in the DU materials:
“Where there is no treaty, and no controlling executive or legislative act or judicial decision, resort must be had to the customs and usages of civilised nations, and as evidence of these, to the works of jurists and commentators… Such works are resorted to by judicial tribunals, not for the speculation of their authors concerning what the law ought to be, but for trustworthy evidence of what the law really is.”Per Justice Gray, The Paquete Habana (1900), as quoted in S.K. Verma
  • The Scotia (US Supreme Court, 1872, 81 U.S. 170) — a British steamer collided with the American sailing ship Berkshire. Britain had adopted new collision-avoidance regulations in 1863; the United States adopted substantially identical regulations in 1864 and other maritime powers followed. The Court applied the new rules — “in part at least…the law of the sea” — as the standard of navigation, even though the litigation concerned a collision rather than a treaty claim. Lesson: parallel municipal action is not enough; what is required is concurrence indicative of general recognition.
  • West Rand Central Gold Mining Co. v. R. These are Topic 3 and Topic 6 material — but their evidentiary function belongs here: they show India's practice, and where they concur with the decisions of other courts, they help establish custom.

Verma makes the point that these opinions “provide direction to the future growth of international law, which manifest in State practice, and indicate the stage of development of an emerging rule”. Two illustrations from the materials:

  • The dissenting opinions in Lotus (Judges Loder, Weiss, Finlay, Nyholm, Moore and Altamira) argued for the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State; within decades their view had become the law — it was adopted in Article 11 of the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea and Article 97 of UNCLOS (1962).
  • The separate opinions of Judges Alfaro and Fitzmaurice in Temple of Preah Vihear are the classic analyses of the map-as-evidence and of acquiescence, and are still cited.

E. Teachings of the most highly qualified publicists

Article 38(1)(d) gives “the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations” the same subsidiary status as judicial decisions. Note the deliberate pluralism of the phrase — “of the various nations” — which is the drafters' answer to the objection that international law was the law of one continent: the writers of all nations, not one school, are to be consulted.

Their evidentiary role, in three propositions. Answer template — “Explain the status of judicial decisions as a source of international law” (2011 Q.2(ii); 1998 Q.2(a)). (1) Article 38(1)(d) and Article 59 verbatim. (2) The theory: no stare decisis; decisions are “subsidiary means”, and their function is evidentiary. (3) The practice: the ICJ follows and distinguishes its own judgments (Peace Treaties distinguishing Eastern Carelia); parties plead them as authority; and where no rule pre-exists, the judgment in effect makes the rule — Reparation (personality), Fisheries (straight baselines), Reservations (object-and-purpose test, later codified in VCLT Arts. 19–24). (4) The categories: international courts and tribunals; arbitral awards (with the amiable compositeur caveat); municipal decisions, with their two functions and the Paquete Habana, Scotia and West Rand authorities. (5) The further authority of separate and dissenting opinions (Lotus dissents → UNCLOS Art. 97). (6) Conclusion: Article 38(1)(d) understates their role; the correct statement is that decisions are not a formal source, but are the most important law-determining means in the system, and in practice often the effective source of new rules.

2.1.5Ex aequo et bono

“This provision shall not prejudice the power of the Court to decide a case ex aequo et bono, if the parties agree thereto.”Article 38(2), ICJ Statute

Ex aequo et bono means “according to what is fair and good” — by equity and good conscience. The purpose, in the words of the DU notes and of the prescribed text, is to save the Court from “a situation of helplessness” where no definite law is available and where a refusal to decide would be a denial of justice: the fundamentals of adjudication require the tribunal to resolve the dispute, not to plead that the law is silent.

The four things you must say about it

  1. It requires the agreement of both parties. The clause is exceptional precisely because it authorises the Court to depart from the strict rules of international law. Consent is the price of that departure, and it is a jurisdictional condition, not a formality. (No ICJ case has ever been decided ex aequo et bono; the parties agree it in a compromis or a special agreement, and in practice they do not.)
  2. It is not the same as applying equity as a principle of law. This is the distinction examiners test. General principles (Art. 38(1)(c)) include equity infra legem — equity within the law — which is available in every case. Article 38(2) allows a decision that may go outside or against legal rules (contra legem). The North Sea Court made precisely this point when it applied equitable principles to continental shelf delimitation and added: “There is consequently no question in this case of any decision ex aequo et bono, such as would only be possible under the conditions prescribed by Article 38, paragraph 2, of the Court's Statute” (para. 88). Judge Hudson in the Meuse case put the complement: equity as part of Article 38(1)(c) is “part of law”, and its scope is in no way restricted by Article 38(2).
  3. The three levels of equity. Infra legem — equity within, and as a means of applying, the law (North Sea, Gulf of Maine, Tunisia/Libya, Libya/Malta; UNCLOS Arts. 74(1) and 83(1) require “an equitable solution”). Praeter legem — equity going beyond the law to fill a gap. Contra legem — equity overriding the law: this is what Article 38(2) allows and nothing else does.
  4. The danger, and the control. Equity invites subjectivity; Verma notes that it introduces “an element of subjectivity, which brings in uncertainty”, and that its claim to be a general principle is not always accepted — South-West Africa (1966) refused to treat actio popularis as such, and Texaco v. Libya (1977) refused to elevate the French law of administrative contracts. The control in practice is twofold: equity must be applied within the law, and it must yield a reasoned, legally intelligible result.

2.2.1Resolutions of the General Assembly

Prescribed case. Advisory Opinion on the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons, 35 International Legal Materials 809 (1996) — printed in the DU material at pp. 30–32 under the heading “(Constituent elements of custom; General Assembly Resolutions as one of the sources of international law)”. The paragraphs printed there are 64–73, and paragraph 70 is the sentence on which every answer to this question turns.

Also asked in: 2025 short note 4(3); 2012 Q.3; 2002 Q.8(a); 1998 Q.8(a); 1992 Q.8(a).

A. The constitutional position — what a General Assembly resolution is

Article 38 does not mention resolutions of the General Assembly. That omission is not an accident: the General Assembly is, in the language of the Charter, an organ whose powers are largely recommendatory. The relevant Charter provisions are:

Two consequences: (i) a resolution of the General Assembly is, as the LED capsule and the prescribed text both put it, prima facie “a mere recommendation” and not binding as such; (ii) but resolutions may nevertheless be declaratory of international law, evidence of customary international law, or law-making in effect (Verma's three-fold description), and this is the doctrine to which we now turn.

B. The doctrine — Nuclear Weapons, paragraphs 70 to 73 (learn these four paragraphs)

The 1996 Advisory Opinion arose out of a request by the General Assembly (Res. 49/75 K) asking: “Is the threat or use of nuclear weapons in any circumstance permitted under international law?” The Court examined custom, and in doing so made the definitive statement about the normative value of General Assembly resolutions. The DU material prints the passage in full. Here are the four paragraphs, tightened:

Paragraph 70 — the doctrine

“The Court notes that General Assembly resolutions, even if they are not binding, may sometimes have normative value. They can, in certain circumstances, provide evidence important for establishing the existence of a rule or the emergence of an opinio juris. To establish whether this is true of a given General Assembly resolution, it is necessary to look at its content and the conditions of its adoption; it is also necessary to see whether an opinio juris exists as to its normative character. Or a series of resolutions may show the gradual evolution of the opinio juris required for the establishment of a new rule.”

Paragraph 71 — application: the resolutions “fall short”

Examined in their totality the resolutions declared that the use of nuclear weapons would be “a direct violation of the Charter” and in certain formulations “should be prohibited”; but several had been “adopted with substantial numbers of negative votes and abstentions; thus, although those resolutions are a clear sign of deep concern regarding the problem of nuclear weapons, they still fall short of establishing the existence of an opinio juris on the illegality of the use of such weapons”.

Paragraph 72 — the negative inference from Res. 1653 (XVI)

The first resolution expressly proclaiming illegality — Res. 1653 (XVI) of 24 November 1961 — after referring to the Declaration of St. Petersburg 1868 and the Geneva Protocol 1925, itself applied general rules of customary law to nuclear weapons, which indicated that “in its view, there was no specific rule of customary law which prohibited the use of nuclear weapons; if such a rule had existed, the General Assembly could simply have referred to it”.

Paragraph 73 — the tension, and the conclusion

The annual repetition of Res. 1653 (XVI) by large majorities “reveals the desire of a very large section of the international community to take, by a specific and express prohibition…a significant step forward along the road to complete nuclear disarmament. The emergence, as lex lata, of a customary rule specifically prohibiting the use of nuclear weapons as such is hampered by the continuing tensions between the nascent opinio juris on the one hand, and the still strong adherence to the practice of deterrence on the other.”

What the Court also said, elsewhere in the same opinion, about the elements of custom (the DU material prints this as the framing of the discussion): the substance of customary law “must be looked for primarily in the actual practice and opinio juris of States” (para. 64, citing Continental Shelf (Libya/Malta), 1985, p. 29, para. 27). Between them, paras. 64 and 70 give you the complete doctrinal answer on GA resolutions.

C. The five factors that determine the weight of a resolution

From paragraph 70 and from Verma's section on General Assembly resolutions, the following checklist can be built. Use it as a table in the exam — it converts a vague question into a precise one.

Table 2.14 — How to weigh a General Assembly resolution

D. The four functions a resolution may perform

1. Declaratory — evidence of existing law (lex lata)

Examples: Res. 2625 (XXV) (Friendly Relations Declaration, 1970) — relied on by the ICJ in Nicaragua for the law on the use of force and non-intervention; Res. 1514 (XV) (1960, decolonisation) and Res. 1541 (XV) (1960, non-self-governing territories) — relied on in Western Sahara and Namibia; Res. 1803 (XVII) (1962, Permanent Sovereignty over Natural Resources); the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948 (Res. 217A(III)), which is now treated as generally declaratory of customary human-rights law (and, in part, of jus cogens).

2. Law-making — accelerating the formation of new law (de lege ferenda)

Examples: Res. 3201 (S-VI) and Res. 3281 (XXIX) (1974, NIEO and the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States) — contested, adopted over the votes of the major capital-exporting States, and therefore the classic example of a resolution that did not become law; Res. 3314 (XXIX) (1974, Definition of Aggression), used in the ICC Statute's definition; Res. 1963 (XVII) (1963, outer space), which paved the way for the Outer Space Treaty 1967; the 1963 Declaration on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, which led to the CERD Convention of 1965.

3. Authentic interpretation

Declarations “are relevant in the authentic interpretation of the United Nations Charter or of existing customary rules, such as the 1970 Declaration on Friendly Relations” (Verma) — the General Assembly speaking, in effect, about what its own constituent instrument means.

4. Binding in specific, narrow situations

(i) Resolutions on the internal functioning of the Organisation bind as organisational decisions; (ii) resolutions approving the budget create real obligations (Art. 17; Effect of Awards); (iii) resolutions on the limits of the organisation's jurisdiction may have law-creating effect for members; (iv) a resolution adopted under the Uniting for Peace procedure carries the authority of the Assembly acting in the peace-and-security field; (v) resolutions relating to admission, suspension and expulsion and to elections determine legal status.

“Soft law” — the term to use and the caution to add. Resolutions are commonly described as “soft law”: they “cannot be classified as full-fledged rules of international law like custom, treaties or general principles of law, but they fulfil to a great extent the criteria to be termed as law” (van Hoof, cited in Verma). The caution is this: the term is descriptive, not analytical; a resolution does not bind because it is “soft law”, but because it evidences practice and opinio juris, or because it has been incorporated into a treaty, or because the Charter gives it effect. The Sixth Committee of the General Assembly considered the question in 1974 (during its review of the ICJ's work) and there was a general agreement against treating Assembly resolutions as a new source of international law in addition to Article 38 — but they were accepted as evidence of custom. That is the safe conclusion to write.

Two real examples with teeth. (i) Goa, 1961. India justified its action in Goa — then a Portuguese colony — on the basis of the right of peoples to self-determination as proclaimed in GA Res. 1514 (XV) (Verma records this), a striking instance of a resolution being used to support a claim of legal right. (ii) Self-determination as custom, 2019. In Chagos the Court held that the right to self-determination “is a fundamental principle of international law”, that Res. 1514 (XV) is “the resolution through which the General Assembly proclaimed the right of colonial peoples to self-determination”, and that no example existed after 1960 in which the Assembly or any other UN organ had treated as lawful the detachment by an administering Power of part of a non-self-governing territory in order to keep it under colonial rule (paras. 155, 160, 180). A resolution had become the standard against which a treaty-based colonial arrangement was judged — and the UK was found to be in continuing breach.

2.2.2Resolutions of the Security Council

Prescribed cases. Namibia (AO 1971), Western Sahara (AO 1975) and Kosovo (AO 2010). The DU material prints the Kosovo opinion at pp. 33–36, including paragraphs 85, 94, and 113–119 — the passages that state how a Security Council resolution is to be interpreted and when it binds. It prints no text of Namibia or Western Sahara (the syllabus gives only the citations); those parts of this section therefore rely on the opinions themselves and on the official ICJ summaries.

A. The constitutional position — Article 25 and the meaning of “decisions”

The Security Council is different in kind from the General Assembly. Its acts are not, in terms, recommendations but decisions, and the Charter makes them binding:

  • Article 24: Members confer on the Council “primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security”, and agree that in carrying out its duties the Council acts on their behalf.
  • Article 25: “The Members of the United Nations agree to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council in accordance with the present Charter.”
  • Article 27(3): Decisions on non-procedural matters require the affirmative votes of nine members “including the concurring votes of the permanent members” — the veto.
  • Chapter VII (Arts. 39–42): determination of a threat to or breach of the peace or act of aggression; provisional measures (Art. 40); measures not involving the use of armed force — sanctions (Art. 41); and, if these would be inadequate, “such action by air, sea, or land forces as may be necessary” (Art. 42).
  • Articles 48–49: Members shall carry out the Council's decisions directly and through their action in the appropriate international agencies; they shall join in mutual assistance. Article 103: Charter obligations prevail over obligations under any other international agreement. Article 94(2): failure to comply with an ICJ judgment in a case to which a State is a party may lead to Security Council action (Topic 1, §1.4 D).

The two questions that arise. First, are all Security Council decisions binding, or only those adopted under Chapter VII? Second, how is a resolution to be interpreted for the purpose of deciding whether it binds, and whom it binds? The answers are in Namibia (1971) and Kosovo (2010).

B. Namibia (1971) — the leading case on Security Council resolutions as law

The facts. South West Africa (Namibia) had been a League of Nations Mandate administered by South Africa. In 1966 the General Assembly terminated the Mandate (Res. 2145 (XXI)) on the ground of South Africa's failure to fulfil its obligations and its application of apartheid. In 1970 the Security Council adopted Res. 276 (1970), declaring that South Africa's continued presence in Namibia was illegal and that all acts taken by its Government on behalf of or concerning Namibia after the termination of the Mandate were illegal and invalid, and calling on all States to refrain from dealings inconsistent with the resolution. The Council then asked the Court: “What are the legal consequences for States of the continued presence of South Africa in Namibia, notwithstanding Security Council resolution 276 (1970)?”

The holdings (with votes). The Court held, by 13 votes to 2, that the continued presence of South Africa in Namibia being illegal, South Africa is under an obligation to withdraw its administration from Namibia immediately and thus put an end to its occupation of the Territory; by 11 votes to 4, that States Members of the United Nations are under obligation to recognise the illegality of South Africa's presence in Namibia and the invalidity of its acts on behalf of or concerning Namibia, and to refrain from any acts — and in particular any dealings with the Government of South Africa — implying recognition of the legality of, or lending support or assistance to, such presence and administration; and, also by 11 votes to 4, that it was incumbent on non-member States to give assistance within the scope of the second finding.

The law on Article 25 — the key passage. The Court rejected the argument that Article 25 applies only to enforcement action under Articles 41 and 42: “If Article 25 had reference solely to decisions of the Security Council concerning enforcement action under Articles 41 and 42…then Article 25 would be superfluous, since this effect is secured by Articles 48 and 49… The decisions are consequently binding on all States Members of the United Nations, which are thus under obligation to accept and carry them out.” Article 25 therefore covers decisions generally, and the question in each case is whether the Council in fact exercised its powers — a question determined by the terms of the resolution, the discussions leading to it, the Charter provisions invoked and, in general, all the circumstances (the passage quoted in Kosovo, para. 117, and in the DU material).

The famous erga omnes passage. “In the view of the Court, the termination of the Mandate and the declaration of the illegality of South Africa's presence in Namibia are opposable to all States in the sense of barring erga omnes the legality of a situation which is maintained in violation of international law: in particular, no State which enters into relations with South Africa concerning Namibia may expect the United Nations or its Members to recognize the validity or effects of any such relationship.” Note the double significance: a binding determination by a competent organ of the UN “cannot remain without consequence”, and non-members are called upon to act accordingly even though Articles 24 and 25 do not bind them as treaty law.

Four examinable propositions from Namibia. (1) The territorial and legal status of a mandated territory: the Mandate survived the dissolution of the League (the 1950 and 1955 opinions), and the supervisory function passed to the United Nations. (2) The General Assembly could terminate the Mandate; the decision was “validly terminated”. (3) Security Council decisions under Article 25 are binding generally and not merely when the Council acts under Articles 41–42; and the resolution's binding effect is determined by analysing its language, its context and the circumstances of adoption. (4) The consequences of an illegal situation are erga omnes: recognition of the illegality, non-assistance, and non-recognition of the acts of the illegal administration.

C. Kosovo (2010) — how a resolution is interpreted, and whom it binds

The question. By Res. 63/3 of 8 October 2008 the General Assembly asked: “Is the unilateral declaration of independence by the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government of Kosovo in accordance with international law?” The declaration had been made on 17 February 2008, in the context of Security Council Res. 1244 (1999), adopted under Chapter VII after the NATO action of 1999.

What the DU material prints (paras. 78, 85, 94, 113–119).

  • Para. 85 — the source of the Court's jurisdiction over the question: “Within the legal framework of the United Nations Charter, notably on the basis of Articles 24, 25 and Chapter VII thereof, the Security Council may adopt resolutions imposing obligations under international law. The Court has had the occasion to interpret and apply such Security Council resolutions on a number of occasions and has consistently treated them as part of the framework of obligations under international law” — citing Namibia (1971) and the Lockerbie provisional-measures orders (1992). Resolution 1244, being expressly adopted under Chapter VII, “clearly imposes international legal obligations”.
  • Para. 94 — the method of interpretation: the rules of treaty interpretation in Articles 31 and 32 VCLT “may provide guidance”, but “differences between Security Council resolutions and treaties mean that the interpretation of Security Council resolutions also requires that other factors be taken into account”. Resolutions are issued by a single, collective body and drafted through a very different process; they are the product of a voting process under Article 27, and the final text “represents the view of the Security Council as a body”. They can bind all Member States “irrespective of whether they played any part in their formulation”. Interpretation may require analysing statements by representatives at the time of adoption, other resolutions on the same issue, and the subsequent practice of UN organs and of affected States.
  • Para. 117 — the case-by-case approach: “When interpreting Security Council resolutions, the Court must establish, on a case-by-case basis, considering all relevant circumstances, for whom the Security Council intended to create binding legal obligations. The language used by the resolution may serve as an important indicator.” This is reinforced by the quotation from Namibia, para. 114: “The language of a resolution of the Security Council should be carefully analysed before a conclusion can be made as to its binding effect… the question whether they have been in fact exercised is to be determined in each case, having regard to the terms of the resolution to be interpreted, the discussions leading to it, the Charter provisions invoked and, in general, all circumstances.”
  • Paras. 113–119 — the application: Res. 1244 was “essentially designed to create an interim régime for Kosovo, with a view to channelling the long-term political process to establish its final status”; it contained no provision on final status. The contrast drawn by the Court is instructive: in Res. 1251 (1999) on Cyprus, adopted only nineteen days later, the Council did specify conditions for permanent status (“a State of Cyprus with a single sovereignty and international personality and a single citizenship”); in the case of Kosovo the Council “did not reserve for itself the final determination… and remained silent on the conditions for the final status”. The resolution's addressees were Member States, the Secretary-General and his Special Representative, and — in specific respects — the KLA and other armed groups (paras. 14, 15); no obligation or prohibition was addressed to “the authors of the declaration”. Hence: the declaration of independence did not violate Res. 1244, and the resolution did not bar its issuance — “the two instruments operate on a different level”.

Model skeleton for “the place of resolutions and decisions of the General Assembly and Security Council among the sources of international law” (1992 Q.8(a); cf. 1998 Q.8(a)). (2) The constitutional basis and the legal status of each organ's acts: GA recommendations (Arts. 10–14) versus SC decisions (Arts. 24, 25, 27(3), Chapter VII, Art. 103). (3) GA resolutions: the Nuclear Weapons doctrine (paras. 70–73) — normative value; content and conditions of adoption; opinio juris; series of resolutions; the five weight-factors. (4) SC resolutions: Namibia (binding under Art. 25; not confined to Arts. 41–42; erga omnes consequences) and Kosovo (Art. 24/25/Chapter VII; the method of interpretation; “at best ambiguous” on the point in issue; addressees determined case by case). (5) The practice: 1373/1540 as quasi-legislative; Lockerbie and Article 103; the Ombudsperson and human-rights review. (6) Conclusion: neither organ's resolutions are a formal source under Article 38, but SC decisions bind by Article 25 and are therefore a source of obligation of a treaty-like quality, while GA resolutions operate as evidence of practice and opinio juris, as authentic interpretations, and — exceptionally — as the vehicle by which new customary rules are generated. The Sixth Committee's 1974 conclusion (evidence of custom, not a new source) is the diplomatic compromise to record.

2.2.3Advisory Opinions of the PCIJ and the ICJ

Prescribed case. Advisory Opinion on the Legal Consequences of the Separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965, ICJ Rep. 2019, p. 169, delivered on 25 February 2019. The syllabus cites it under this head and the student's own annotation marks it, “not the direct…” — the point of that annotation is that the Chagos opinion is not a contentious case between two States but an advisory opinion, and that it is prescribed here (under 2.2.3) rather than as a case on territorial sovereignty. Treat it as an advisory opinion that made law, and also as the fullest recent demonstration of how GA and SC resolutions are used as sources (it therefore also belongs to §§2.2.1–2.2.2).

A. The constitutional basis

  • Article 96(1) of the Charter: the General Assembly or the Security Council may request the Court to give an advisory opinion on any legal question. Article 96(2): other organs of the United Nations and specialised agencies, when so authorised by the General Assembly, may request opinions on legal questions arising within the scope of their activities. (The WHO's request on the legality of the use of nuclear weapons was held inadmissible in 1996 because the question did not arise within the scope of the WHO's activities — a useful limiting example of Article 96(2).)
  • Article 65 of the ICJ Statute: the Court may give an advisory opinion on any legal question at the request of whatever body is authorised by the Charter to make such a request. “May” is important: the Court has a discretion, though it is a discretion exercised sparingly.
  • Articles 66–68: notification to States and organisations entitled to appear; oral and written statements; the Court applies the same law, and follows the same practice, as in contentious cases.
  • The PCIJ gave 27 advisory opinions between 1922 and 1940. Its most consequential were on the treaty-making capacity of international organisations (Competence of the ILO, 1922), the Danzig questions, the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, the Jaworzina and Mosul opinions. The advisory jurisdiction was thus not a novelty in 1945; it was inherited and expanded.

B. The two objections a State can raise — consent and discretion

1. Consent (the “Eastern Carelia principle”)

Advisory opinions are not, in form, binding, and they are not a way of dragging a State before the Court without its consent. In the Status of Eastern Carelia opinion (PCIJ, Series B No. 5, 1923) the Court refused to answer a question which in substance required it to decide a dispute between States — one of which, the USSR, was not a member of the League and had refused to submit. The principle: no State can be compelled, without its consent, to submit its disputes to pacific settlement. The exception is narrow: the Court has repeatedly held that the mere fact that an opinion may have a bearing on a bilateral dispute does not deprive it of jurisdiction (Interpretation of Peace Treaties, 1950; Wall, 2004; Chagos, 2019) — only “compelling reasons” in the particular case will lead it to decline. In Chagos the Court rejected the “bilateral dispute” objection by locating the questions “in the broader frame of reference of decolonization, including the General Assembly's role therein”, and decided to comply with the request by 12 votes to 1.

2. Propriety (the Court's discretion)

The Court may decline to answer where the question is abstract, where the requesting organ has no purpose in asking, or where an answer would undermine the integrity of the Court's judicial function. Its practice shows how rarely it does: it declined in Eastern Carelia (1923) and refused admissible requests in Status of the South-Eastern Territory of Greenland (1932) and the WHO Nuclear Weapons request (1996). In Chagos it said that it saw no compelling reason to decline, and refused to narrow or reformulate the General Assembly's question. The discretion is therefore real but exceptionally exercised — which is why the advisory jurisdiction is now one of the system's most powerful law-making devices.

C. What an advisory opinion does legally

  1. It is authoritative but not binding. Formally, an opinion binds nobody: it is advice to the requesting organ. But because it is the reasoned opinion of the principal judicial organ of the United Nations, it carries “significant legal weight” — in the words of the literature, it settles the law for the organ and for the international community in the way that a judgment settles it for the parties. The General Assembly's practice of “giving effect” to opinions — as with Res. 73/295, which demanded the United Kingdom's withdrawal from Chagos by 22 November 2019 — is the standard mechanism of implementation.
  2. It clarifies, and therefore changes, the law. Four examples, each of which created something that did not exist before: Reparation (1949) — the legal personality of the United Nations and the theory of implied powers (Topic 1, §1.5; Case-Law of Topic 1); Reservations to the Genocide Convention (1951) — the object-and-purpose test, now VCLT Arts. 19–23; Certain Expenses (1962) — that peacekeeping expenses are “expenses of the Organisation” under Article 17(2); Nuclear Weapons (1996) — the doctrine of the normative value of General Assembly resolutions.
  3. It may declare an existing situation unlawful and identify its consequences. Namibia (1971) — the obligation to withdraw and the erga omnes duty of non-recognition; Wall (2004) — the illegality of the barrier and the obligations of third States; Chagos (2019) — the unlawfulness of the detachment and the duty to end the administration “as rapidly as possible”.
  4. It is a source of law for other cases. Arbitral tribunals, human-rights courts and national courts cite advisory opinions as statements of the law; and the Court itself treats earlier opinions as authority (cf. Kosovo quoting Namibia paragraphs 114 and 116 verbatim).

D. The Chagos Advisory Opinion — what it added to the law of sources

The background in five lines. In 1965, before Mauritius became independent, the United Kingdom detached the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius and constituted it as the British Indian Ocean Territory; the Council of Ministers of the then self-governing colony agreed under pressure (the United Kingdom made independence conditional), and the largest island, Diego Garcia, was leased to the United States for a military base. Mauritius obtained its independence in 1968. By Res. 71/292 of 22 June 2017 the General Assembly asked the Court two questions: (a) whether the process of decolonisation of Mauritius was lawfully completed when it acceded to independence in 1968, given the separation of the Chagos Archipelago; and (b) what the consequences under international law were of the continued administration by the United Kingdom, including the inability of Mauritius to implement a programme of resettlement.

The findings. The Court held (13 votes to 1 on the merits; Judge Donoghue dissenting) that the detachment of the Chagos Archipelago in 1965 was unlawful, being contrary to the right of peoples to self-determination; that, as a result, the process of decolonisation of Mauritius was not lawfully completed when it acceded to independence in 1968; that the United Kingdom's continued administration of the Archipelago constitutes a wrongful act entailing the international responsibility of that State; and that the United Kingdom is under an obligation to bring to an end its administration as rapidly as possible, so enabling Mauritius to complete the decolonisation of its territory in a manner consistent with the right of peoples to self-determination. All States were placed under an obligation to cooperate with the United Nations in that regard.

The source-law points — the reasons this opinion belongs in Topic 2.

  • Self-determination as customary law. The Court treated the right of peoples to self-determination as “a fundamental principle of international law”, reflected in the Charter, in GA Res. 1514 (XV) and 1541 (XV), and in its own jurisprudence, and held that it is erga omnes in character (paras. 155, 180).
  • General Assembly resolutions as the standard of legality. The Court asked whether any State practice since 1960 could legitimise the separation of part of a non-self-governing territory; it found no example in which the Assembly or any other UN organ had treated such a detachment as lawful (para. 160). Absence of contrary practice plus the constant reiteration of Res. 1514 (XV) is used as evidence of law — the negative version of the “acquiescence” argument.
  • Consent cannot cure a violation of self-determination. The United Kingdom argued that the Mauritian Council of Ministers had consented. The Court examined the circumstances — the colony was still subordinate, and independence was made conditional on the detachment — and held that the detachment “was not based on the free and genuine expression of the will of the people concerned” (para. 172). This is a standard of heightened scrutiny for consent obtained by a colonial power from a dependent administration: a doctrinal innovation of general importance.
  • Advisory jurisdiction as law-making. The Court declined to narrow the question, refused to accept that the presence of a bilateral sovereignty dispute barred an opinion, and treated the case, in the words of the EJIL:Talk! commentary, as being about decolonization rather than sovereignty — a careful jurisdictional choice which allowed the General Assembly to do through an opinion what it could not do through litigation.
  • Implementation. By Res. 73/295 of 22 May 2019 (116–6–56) the Assembly affirmed that the Archipelago “forms an integral part of the territory of Mauritius” and demanded that the United Kingdom withdraw its colonial administration unconditionally within six months; UN organs, specialised agencies and other international organisations were called upon to support the decolonisation of Mauritius. The consequence in practice: the United Kingdom no longer holds the rights of a coastal State in the region for the purposes of the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission framework. And on 22 May 2025 the United Kingdom and Mauritius signed a treaty under which Mauritius exercises full sovereignty over the Archipelago, with the UK retaining rights over Diego Garcia (including a rent of about £101 million a year for an initial 99 years, renewable for a further 40, with Mauritius undertaking not to permit other powers to use the outer islands without agreement). The 1965 wrong was eventually undone by agreement — but only after an advisory opinion had declared it unlawful.

2.2.4Other Law-Creating Acts — and the Closing Debate on This Topic

A. Unilateral acts of States

Article 38 does not mention them; the ICJ nonetheless treats them as capable of creating obligations. In the Nuclear Tests cases (Australia v. France; New Zealand v. France), ICJ Rep. 1974, pp. 253 and 457, France's public declarations that it would cease atmospheric testing in the South Pacific were held to be binding — the Court founding itself on good faith and on the principle that interest and intention are the test:

“It is well recognised that declarations made by way of unilateral acts, concerning legal or factual situations, may have the effect of creating legal obligations… When it is the intention of the State making the declaration that it should become bound according to its terms, that intention confers on the declaration the character of a legal undertaking… An undertaking of this kind, if given publicly, and with an intent to be bound, even though not made within the context of international negotiations, is binding.”ICJ Rep. 1974, pp. 267–268, paras. 43, 46

The ILC's Guiding Principles applicable to unilateral declarations of States capable of creating legal obligations (2006) confirm the requirements: a declaration must be made publicly, must evidence an intention to be bound, and must be addressed to the international community or a particular State. Note the caution: an intention to be influenced is not enough, and in Nuclear Tests the Court refused to rule on the legality of the tests once the declarations had removed the object of the claim — a striking demonstration of a unilateral act determining, and ending, a dispute. For Topic 2 the point to write is: unilateral acts are a source of obligation, not of general law; they bind the declaring State alone, and only so long as the undertaking is maintained.

B. Decisions of international organisations

  • Internal law of the organisation: decisions of a competent organ bind the organisation and its members in matters of functioning, staff regulations and the budget (Effect of Awards, 1954; Certain Expenses, 1962).
  • External law: where the constitutive instrument confers normative power (the Security Council under Chapter VII; the ICAO Council, the WHO Assembly and others within their fields), the decision binds Member States. The 1986 Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties between States and International Organizations or between International Organizations embodies the practice developed on this point.
  • Practice as a source within the organisation: the abstention practice under Article 27(3) and the creation of peacekeeping operations (UNEF 1956; ONUC 1960) are the standard examples of institutional practice becoming constitutional law (Verma).
  • Limits: organisations can only exercise powers conferred or reasonably implied (Reparation, 1949; and the WHO Nuclear Weapons opinion, 1996, holding that the WHO could not ask about the legality of nuclear weapons because the question did not arise within the scope of its activities).

C. Subsequent practice, codes of conduct and “soft law”

Modern practice adds three further categories which are best explained as evidence rather than as independent formal sources:

  • Subsequent practice in the application of a treaty may establish the agreement of the parties as to its interpretation (VCLT Art. 31(3)(b); ILC Draft Conclusions on Subsequent Agreements and Subsequent Practice, 2018).
  • Codes of conduct, guidelines and standards — the FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries, the IAEA safety standards, the ICRC manuals, the Basel Convention guidelines — which may harden into treaty or customary rules or serve as authoritative interpretations.
  • Soft law generally: declarations, programmes of action, guiding principles. The honest statement for the examination is that soft law is not a source in the Article 38 sense; its legal effect is derivative — it evidences practice and opinio juris, it guides interpretation, and it forms the text from which treaties are later negotiated (as Res. 1963 (XVII) preceded the Outer Space Treaty, and the 1963 Declaration preceded CERD).

Closing paragraph for any “Are the modern materials sources of international law?” question. “The formal sources remain those of Article 38(1)(a)–(c). But no serious account of sources today can stop there. Security Council decisions bind by Article 25 and prevail over conflicting treaty obligations by Article 103; General Assembly resolutions have normative value as evidence of practice and of opinio juris, as authentic interpretations of the Charter and, exceptionally, as the vehicle for accelerated custom; advisory opinions are the principal means by which the Court declares the law in the general interest; and unilateral declarations, organisational decisions and soft-law instruments operate as sources of obligation or as evidence, according to their terms. The correct analysis is therefore not that Article 38 is wrong, but that it is a starting point: a list of the formal sources, to which practice has added a rich layer of law-determining and obligation-creating materials — with jus cogens and Article 103 as the two rules that really order the system above them all.”

CASE 1 — The Case of the S.S. Lotus (France v. Turkey)

PCIJ, Series A, No. 10 (1927) · Judgment of 7 September 1927 · Prescribed at syllabus item 3 (International Custom)

1. Facts

On 2 August 1926 the French mail steamer S.S. Lotus collided on the high seas with the Turkish collier Boz-Kourt. The Boz-Kourt sank and eight Turkish nationals on board were drowned. When the Lotus reached Constantinople (Istanbul), the Turkish authorities arrested Lieutenant Demons, the French officer of the watch on the Lotus, and Captain Hassan Bey of the Boz-Kourt, and tried them together for manslaughter before a Turkish criminal court; both were convicted and sentenced. France protested. By a special agreement of 12 October 1926 the two Governments referred to the Permanent Court of International Justice the question whether Turkey had acted contrary to international law, and, if so, what reparation was due to Lieutenant Demons.

2. Issues

  1. Did Turkey, by prosecuting a French officer for acts committed on board a French ship on the high seas, act in conflict with the principles of international law?
  2. In particular, did international law recognise the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State over collisions on the high seas — jurisdiction founded on the nationality of the vessel? Or was there a rule permitting the State whose vessel (or nationals) suffered the effects of the wrong to prosecute?
  3. If Turkey had acted unlawfully, what pecuniary reparation was due to Lieutenant Demons?

3. Arguments of the parties

Table 2.16 — Lotus: the two cases as argued
France (contending that Turkey had acted contrary to international law)Turkey (contending that its jurisdiction was lawful)
(i) A State may not exercise criminal jurisdiction over offences committed by foreigners abroad merely because the victim is its national. Damage done to a national is not a basis for jurisdiction in criminal matters, because international law forbids a State to exercise its power on the territory of another State and the flag State has, on the high seas, assimilated the ship to its territory.(i) No rule of international law prohibits a State from exercising jurisdiction over acts committed abroad which produce effects within its territory; the collision produced its effects on the Turkish vessel, which is assimilated to Turkish territory. Turkish criminal law was therefore applicable, and Turkey did not exercise any power on French territory.
(ii) The State practice relied on — the abstention of flag States from prosecuting in such circumstances, and the practice of Courts refusing jurisdiction — shows that international law recognises the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State.(ii) The abstention of States from prosecution is not evidence of a legal obligation to abstain; States may have abstained for reasons of expediency. Jurisdiction is territorial, but a State may exercise it abroad where a permissive rule derived from custom or convention so allows; Turkey needed to show no permission, since restrictions on the independence of States cannot be presumed.
(iii) Article 15 of the Lausanne Convention of 24 July 1923 (on conditions of residence, business and jurisdiction) precluded Turkish jurisdiction in the circumstances.(iii) Article 15 did not exempt Mr. Demons from Turkish jurisdiction; and in any event there is no rule of international law limiting a State's freedom to extend its criminal jurisdiction to collisions on the high seas.

4. Decision

The Court found that Turkey had not acted contrary to international law. The judgment was given by the casting vote of the President (Judge Huber) after the Court divided six to six: six of the judges were unable to find any rule of international law restricting Turkey's jurisdiction. Since the first question was answered in the negative, there was no occasion to consider France's claim for pecuniary reparation. Six judges — Loder, Weiss, Finlay, Nyholm, Moore and Altamira — delivered dissenting opinions.

5. Principles evolved — and this is why the case is prescribed under “custom”

  1. The “Lotus principle” (the permissive/remanence principle): “International law governs relations between independent States. The rules of law binding upon States therefore emanate from their own free will as expressed in conventions or by usages generally accepted as expressing principles of law… Restrictions upon the independence of States cannot therefore be presumed” (p. 18). A State is free to act unless a prohibitory rule can be shown. The corollary is the first principle of jurisdiction: “the first and foremost restriction imposed by international law upon a State is that — failing the existence of a permissive rule to the contrary — it may not exercise its power in any form in the territory of another State”.
  2. The rule on jurisdiction over extraterritorial acts with effects in the territory (objective territoriality / passive personality at that date): “the offence produced its effects on the Turkish vessel and consequently in a place assimilated to Turkish territory in which the application of Turkish criminal law cannot be challenged, even in regard to offences committed there by foreigners”. Where a guilty act committed on the high seas produces its effects on a vessel flying another flag or in foreign territory, the same principles apply as if the territories of two different States were concerned; there is no rule prohibiting the State where the effects took place from treating the offence as committed in its territory.
  3. The rule on the formation of custom — the reason this is a sources case: the Court held that the abstention of States from instituting criminal proceedings does not, without more, establish a customary rule: “only if such abstention were based on their being conscious of having a duty to abstain would it be possible to speak of an international custom” (p. 28). Practice must be accompanied by a sense of legal obligation — the sentence the ICJ adopted “almost word for word, mutatis mutandis” in the North Sea case at paragraph 78.
  4. The methodology of proof: the Court examined a mass of municipal decisions, treaties and practice, and found no uniformity; it noted that the absence of prosecutions did not show a duty to abstain, and that there were “other circumstances calculated to show the contrary”. This is a lesson in how a court assesses custom — and a reminder that the party asserting the custom bears the burden.

Subsequent history (worth one sentence). The Lotus result did not survive. The dissenting view — exclusive flag-State jurisdiction over collisions — was adopted in Article 11 of the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Territorial Sea and Article 97 of UNCLOS 1982, so that no penal or disciplinary proceedings may be instituted against the master or crew except before the judicial or administrative authorities of the flag State (or of the State of nationality). Lotus therefore also illustrates Verma's point that the dissenting opinions of today become the law of tomorrow. On the broader Lotus principle, the ICJ has in practice moved away from a strong presumption of freedom: see the Nuclear Weapons opinion (where the Court declined to reason from the absence of a prohibition) and the modern literature criticising the principle as inconsistent with the interdependence of States.

6. How to use this case

  • For “custom — what must be proved”: quote p. 28 for the requirement of opinio juris and use it to distinguish usage from custom.
  • For “evidence of a general practice accepted as law” (2015 Q.2): Lotus is the authority that abstention is equivocal, and the reason a claimant cannot build a custom only out of what States have not done.
  • For “why is custom placed second in Article 38?” (1994, 1993): use the trio Lotus (proving custom is hard), Asylum (regional custom must be constant and uniform) and North Sea (a treaty rule does not easily become custom) to show why the drafters preferred the certainty of conventions.
  • For jurisdiction questions in Topic 6, of course — but here keep the focus on the sources point.

CASE 2 — North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (Federal Republic of Germany / Denmark; Federal Republic of Germany / Netherlands)

ICJ Rep. 1969, p. 3 · Judgment of 20 February 1969 · Prescribed at syllabus items 4 (custom) and 32 (law of the sea) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 127–139

1. Facts

By two Special Agreements concluded between the Federal Republic of Germany and Denmark, and between the Federal Republic and the Netherlands, the parties asked the Court to determine the principles and rules of international law applicable to the delimitation, as between each pair of States, of the areas of the continental shelf in the North Sea which appertained to each of them. The North Sea is shallow: the whole seabed is continental shelf at a depth of less than 200 metres except for the Norwegian Trough. Much of it had already been divided by agreements between the United Kingdom, Norway, Denmark and the Netherlands, all drawn on the basis of median lines between “opposite” States. The geography mattered: whereas Denmark's and the Netherlands' coastlines are convex in relation to the North Sea, the German coast is concave, so that an equidistance line drawn between the three States would cut Germany off from a large part of the shelf directly in front of it. Germany, which had signed but not ratified the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Continental Shelf, argued that each State should get a “just and equitable share” of the shelf in proportion to the length of its sea frontage.

2. Issues

  1. Was Article 6(2) of the 1958 Geneva Convention on the Continental Shelf — the “equidistance–special circumstances” rule — applicable to Germany, which was not a party to the Convention?
  2. Did Article 6 either declare, or crystallise, a pre-existing or emergent rule of customary international law binding on Germany?
  3. Had the equidistance principle, through the Convention's own impact and subsequent State practice, since become a rule of customary international law binding on all States, including Germany?
  4. If not, what principles and rules of law governed the delimitation of the shelf between the parties?

3. Arguments of the parties

Table 2.17 — North Sea: the two cases as argued
Denmark and the NetherlandsFederal Republic of Germany
The “equidistance–special circumstances” principle in Article 6(2) of the Geneva Convention was applicable. They did not contend that the Convention was merely declaratory of existing custom; their case was that the process of definition and consolidation of the emerging custom took place through the work of the International Law Commission, the reactions of Governments to that work, and the proceedings of the Geneva Conference, and that this emerging customary law “crystallized in the adoption of the Continental Shelf Convention”. In the alternative, they argued that even if Article 6 was not declaratory when adopted, it had since become customary through its own influence on State practice and subsequent delimitations — some fifteen of which had used equidistance.Germany was not a party to the Convention and Article 6 could not be applied to it. Delimitation should give each State a “just and equitable share” of the shelf in proportion to the length of its sea frontage; and in any event, the concave configuration of the German coast constituted a “special circumstance” such as to justify a departure from equidistance. Germany also relied on the fact that the equidistance method, applied to a concave coast between convex neighbours, produced a radically inequitable result, since it would cut it off from the natural prolongation of its land territory.

4. Decision

  • The Court held that the Geneva Convention “did not embody or crystallize any pre-existing or emergent customary rule of international law, according to which the delimitation of continental shelf areas between adjacent States must, unless the Parties otherwise agree, be carried out on an equidistance–special circumstance basis” (para. 69).
  • It held further that the Convention's subsequent effect had not been constitutive of such a rule, and that State practice to date had been insufficient (para. 81). Equidistance, therefore, could not be invoked against Germany as custom.
  • The Court laid down instead the principles of equitable delimitation (paras. 85–101): delimitation is to be effected by agreement in accordance with equitable principles and taking account of all relevant circumstances, so as to leave as much as possible to each party the parts of the shelf that constitute the natural prolongation of its land territory into and under the sea without encroaching on the natural prolongation of the other. Where the application leaves overlapping areas, these are to be divided in agreed proportions, or failing agreement equally, or by joint jurisdiction, user or exploitation. The factors to be taken into account include the general configuration of the coasts and any special or unusual features; the physical and geological structure and natural resources so far as known; and a reasonable degree of proportionality between the extent of the shelf appertaining to each State and the length of its coast.

5. The reasoning in detail — the passages you must be able to reproduce

  1. Article 6 was not declaratory (paras. 61–66). The Court noted that the equidistance principle was proposed by the ILC “with considerable hesitation, somewhat on an experimental basis, at most de lege ferenda, and not at all de lege lata” (para. 62). Its most original argument was from the reservations clause: Article 12 permitted reservations to every article “other than” Articles 1–3. Since customary rules, by their nature, “must have equal force for all members of the international community, and cannot therefore be the subject of any right of unilateral exclusion exercisable at will by any one of them in its own favour”, an article open to reservation (Art. 6) is presumptively not declaratory of customary law, while the articles excluded from reservation (Arts. 1–3) are the ones regarded as reflecting received or emergent custom (paras. 63–66).
  2. A treaty rule may pass into custom — but not lightly (paras. 70–72). Article 6 would have to be “of a fundamentally norm-creating character such as could be regarded as forming the basis of a general rule of law”, which was doubtful because it subordinated equidistance to a prior obligation to delimit by agreement and because of the unresolved “special circumstances” notion; and the reservation clause added a further difficulty.
  3. The participation test (para. 73). Even without a long period, “a very widespread and representative participation in the convention might suffice of itself, provided it included that of States whose interests were specially affected”. The number of ratifications was “though respectable, hardly sufficient”; and non-ratification might be due to indifference rather than to disapproval, but “the reasons are speculative, but the facts remain”.
  4. The time and uniformity test (para. 74). No period is prescribed, but practice within it “including that of States whose interests are specially affected, should have been both extensive and virtually uniform in the sense of the provision invoked; and should moreover have occurred in such a way as to show a general recognition that a rule of law or legal obligation is involved”.
  5. The practice was inconclusive (paras. 75–79). Over half the States concerned were parties to the Convention and so were arguably applying it, not custom, and those who were not parties might have used equidistance for “other obvious factors”; moreover almost all the delimitations were median lines between opposite States, not lateral lines between adjacent States, and only one situation resembled the present geography — so that the cases cited were “inconclusive, and insufficient to bear the weight sought to be put upon them”.
  6. The opinio juris requirement (paras. 77–78). The passage on settled practice and the subjective element (quoted in full at §2.1.2 C above), and the Court's adoption of the Lotus reasoning “almost word for word, mutatis mutandis”, with the example that acts of ceremonial and protocol are performed almost invariably but for reasons of courtesy, convenience or tradition, not legal duty.
  7. Equity within the law, not ex aequo et bono (paras. 85–88). The rule applied “rests on a broader basis… it is precisely a rule of law that calls for the application of equitable principles. There is consequently no question in this case of any decision ex aequo et bono”. The obligation to negotiate meaningfully follows from the Truman Proclamation of 1945, which “must be considered as having propounded the rules of law in this field”, and from Article 33 of the Charter.

6. Principles evolved

  • The cumulative conditions for the passage of a treaty rule into custom — norm-creating character; widespread and representative participation including specially affected States; extensive and virtually uniform practice; opinio juris; and the reservations-clause test.
  • Custom is not to be inferred from mere uniformity of practice; the subjective element must be shown (Lotus applied).
  • Distinction between opposite and adjacent coasts in maritime delimitation; and the recognition that equidistance is a method, not a rule.
  • Equitable principles as law (infra legem), and the express denial that this amounts to a decision ex aequo et bono.
  • A reasonable degree of proportionality between shelf area and coastal length as a factor in delimitation — a rule the Court invented and which has been applied ever since (Tunisia/Libya, 1982; Libya/Malta, 1985; the Bay of Bengal arbitration, 2014, where India and Bangladesh argued proportionality, with the Tribunal adjusting the line at the “concavity” of the Bangladesh coast).

7. How to use this case

  • The single most important case in Topic 2. It supplies the tests for custom, the tests for treaty-to-custom, the opinio juris requirement, the reservations-clause technique, and the equity/ ex aequo et bono distinction.
  • For 1998 Q.2(b) (“tests laid down in the Continental Shelf Cases to prove that a provision…has developed into a rule of Customary International Law”) — reproduce Table 2.6.
  • For 2013 Q.2 and 1999 Q.3(iii) (“in the light of continental shelf cases, how far do you agree that treaties can act as material sources of custom?”) — deal with the three modes: declaratory, crystallising, generative.
  • For the ratification questions (1995, 1992): Germany, a signatory that did not ratify, was not bound; only a “very definite, very consistent course of conduct” could have bound it — and it had shown the opposite.

CASE 3 — Right of Passage over Indian Territory (Merits) (Portugal v. India)

ICJ Rep. 1960, p. 6 · Judgment of 12 April 1960 · Prescribed at syllabus item 5 (custom) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 7–12

1. Facts

Portugal held two enclaves on the western coast of India — Dadra and Nagar-Aveli (Nagar Haveli) — separated from the Portuguese coastal district of Daman by intervening Indian territory. Portugal claimed a right of passage between Daman and the enclaves, and between the enclaves themselves, across Indian territory, “to the extent necessary for the exercise of its sovereignty over the enclaves, subject to India's right of regulation and control of the passage claimed, and without any immunity in Portugal's favour”. India resisted, contesting both the validity of the historical instruments on which Portugal relied and the legal character of the right claimed. After a revolt in Dadra in July 1954 (which resulted in the overthrow of Portuguese authority in that enclave) India suspended all passage, and the dispute was submitted to the Court.

2. Issues

  1. Did the Treaty of Poona of 1779 and the sanads (decrees) of the Maratha ruler of 1783 and 1785 transfer sovereignty over the villages to Portugal, with a right of passage to them?
  2. If not, did Portugal nevertheless acquire sovereignty over the enclaves, and a right of passage, through the practice of the British and post-British periods?
  3. Could a local custom be established between only two States?
  4. If a right of passage existed, was it available to private persons, civil officials, goods in general, armed forces, armed police, and arms and ammunition — and did India's refusal of passage in 1954 breach it?

3. Arguments of the parties

Table 2.18 — Right of Passage: the two cases as argued
PortugalIndia
Portugal relied on the Treaty of Poona of 1779 and the sanads of 1783 and 1785 as conferring sovereignty over the enclaves together with a right of passage to them; and, independently of the instruments, on the long-continued practice — during the British period and after Indian independence — as establishing a right of passage based on local custom. Portugal claimed the right “to the extent necessary for the exercise of its sovereignty over the enclaves”, subject to India's regulation and control, and admitted that the right carried no immunity even for the passage of armed forces.India argued that the right claimed was “vague and contradictory” — on the one hand subject to India's regulation and control as territorial sovereign, on the other hand said to be a right — and that it involved a derogation from India's sovereignty. India also objected that the alleged Treaty of 1779 was not validly entered into and never became binding on the Marathas; that the Treaty and sanads together transferred not sovereignty but only a revenue grant (jagir or saranjam) of the value of 12,000 rupees per annum; and that “no local custom could be established between only two States”.

4. Decision — a divided result, and that is the point

  • On the instruments: the Court could not conclude that Article 17 of the Treaty or the sanads were intended to transfer sovereignty. Treaties concluded by the Marathas used expressions like cession “in perpetuity” or “in perpetual sovereignty” when sovereignty was meant; what was granted here was “a revenue tenure called a jagir or saranjam of the value of 12,000 rupees a year… a very common form of grant in India”. Nor did the grant of authority to put down revolt or rebellion imply sovereignty; the Portuguese would have had that authority “on behalf of the Maratha ruler”. On the validity objection the Court observed that the validity of a treaty concluded in the last quarter of the eighteenth century “should not be judged upon the basis of practices and procedures which have since developed only gradually”; the Marathas themselves regarded the Treaty as valid and binding, and gave effect to it.
  • On Portuguese sovereignty over the enclaves: the Court found that although the Maratha grant had not transferred sovereignty, the British “found the Portuguese in occupation of the villages and exercising full and exclusive administrative authority over them”, accepted the situation, and never contested their authority; the Portuguese held themselves out as sovereign, and their exclusive authority “was never brought in question”. Thus Portuguese sovereignty over the villages “was recognized by the British in fact and by implication and was subsequently tacitly recognized by India”, and the villages acquired the character of Portuguese enclaves within Indian territory.
  • On local custom: the Court rejected India's objection in the sentence quoted at §2.1.2 E: it saw no reason why long-continued practice between two States accepted by them as regulating their relations should not form the basis of mutual rights and obligations.
  • On the classification of the right: examining the practice during the British and post-British periods, the Court found that passage of private persons and civil officials was not subject to restrictions beyond routine control; that goods in general passed freely subject to customs and security regulations; but that armed forces, armed police, and arms and ammunition could pass only with previous authorisation — under Article XVIII of the Treaty of Commerce and Extradition of 1878, the agreements of 1913, 1920 and 1940, and the rules under the Indian Arms Act 1878. That necessity for authorisation “constitutes, in the view of the Court, a negation of passage as of right”: the practice “predicates that the territorial sovereign had the discretionary power to withdraw or to refuse permission”. The fact that permission was in practice always granted did not affect the legal position.
  • On India's refusal in 1954: the events in Dadra on 21–22 July 1954 had overthrown Portuguese authority and created tension in the surrounding Indian territory; India suspended all passage. “In view of the tension then prevailing…the Court is unable to hold that India's refusal of passage…was action contrary to its obligation” — the refusals were covered by India's power of regulation and control.

5. Principles evolved

  • A local (bilateral) custom is possible under Article 38(1)(b): long-continued practice between two States, accepted by them as regulating their relations, creates mutual rights and obligations. Now reflected in ILC Conclusion 16 (2018) on “particular customary international law”.
  • Custom may govern, and may be limited in scope: the same custom can cover some categories of passage and not others. Custom is particular as to its content, not all-or-nothing.
  • A right subject to the territorial sovereign's regulation and control is not a right of passage “as of right”: where practice shows that permission was always needed, the practice negates the right — a crucial technique for litigating custom.
  • Historical instruments are construed by the standards of their time, and grants of revenue are not grants of sovereignty — the Indian law of jagir and saranjam enters international law.
  • State succession and tacit recognition: India was bound by the practice established between Portugal and the British, and its acquiescence (through British recognition and its own conduct) was decisive.

6. How to use this case

  • For 2012 Q.2 (“status of regional or local custom… do you agree that regional custom is part of international custom?”) — pair Asylum (regional, failed) with Right of Passage (local, succeeded) and conclude that Article 38(1)(b) covers both, subject to proof of constant and uniform practice accepted as law, and of opposability to the State concerned.
  • For 1999 Q.8(b), 2000 Q.8(i), 2001 Q.8(a) and the 1995/1994 notes on recognition of territorial sovereignty: use the case as your Indian-focused answer — an ICJ case in which India was a party and won, on custom.
  • For the classic 2002 scenario (“Gabriel's right of transit through Inkowa”) and the 2025 practice on scenario questions: this is the case to cite for the proposition that a right of passage may exist as a local custom, but is subject to the territorial sovereign's regulation and control — and that permission-based practice destroys the claim of right.

CASE 4 — Asylum Case (Colombia v. Peru)

ICJ Rep. 1950, p. 266 · Judgment of 20 November 1950 · Prescribed at syllabus item 6 (custom) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 13–20

1. Facts

On 3 October 1948 a military rebellion broke out in Peru and was suppressed the same day. The President's decree of 4 October declared the American People's Revolutionary Alliance (APRA) to have placed itself outside the law, its leaders to be brought to justice as instigators of the rebellion. Víctor Raúl Haya de la Torre, the party's leader, was denounced on 5 October; judicial proceedings were opened on 11 October and he was summoned to appear. On 27 October a military junta seized power, but the proceedings against Haya de la Torre continued before the ordinary jurisdiction. On 3 January 1949 — three months after the rebellion — he sought asylum in the Colombian Embassy in Lima. Colombia notified Peru that asylum had been granted under Article 2(2) of the Havana Convention on Asylum, 1928, and requested a safe-conduct for his departure; on 14 January Colombia informed Peru that it had qualified Haya de la Torre as a political refugee “in accordance with the right conferred upon it by Article 2 of the Convention on Political Asylum signed…at Montevideo on December 26th, 1933”. Peru refused to accept the qualification and to grant the safe-conduct. By the Act of Lima of 31 August 1949 the dispute was referred to the Court.

2. Issues

  1. Was Colombia, as the State granting asylum, competent to qualify the offence “by a unilateral and definitive decision binding on Peru”?
  2. Was Peru, as the territorial State, bound to give the guarantees necessary for Haya de la Torre's departure, with due regard to the inviolability of his person?
  3. On Peru's counter-claim: had asylum been granted in violation of Article 1(1) of the Havana Convention (asylum not permitted for persons accused of common crimes) or Article 2(2) (asylum may be granted only in urgent cases and for the time strictly indispensable)?

3. Arguments of the parties

Table 2.19 — Asylum: the two cases as argued
ColombiaPeru
The Bolivarian Agreement of 1911 (Art. 18: the signatory States “recognize the institution of asylum in conformity with the principles of international law”) and the Havana Convention of 1928 recognised the institution of asylum; the competence to qualify the offence unilaterally and definitively was implied in that Convention and inherent in the institution itself; and, in any event, a regional custom peculiar to the Latin-American States — “American international law in general” — established the rule, evidenced by a large number of conventions, cases, and official communiqués.Peru contested the legality of the asylum; insisted that it alone could qualify the offence within its own territory; maintained that Haya de la Torre was accused of common crimes and, in any event, that the asylum had not been granted in an urgent case; and relied on Article 2(2) of the Havana Convention (urgency and limited duration) as well as Article 1(1) (no asylum for common crimes). Behind the legal dispute lay the fundamental matter of sovereignty: a decision to grant diplomatic asylum “involves a derogation from the sovereignty of that State…an intervention in matters which are exclusively within the competence of that State”.

4. Decision — three findings, three traps

  • Colombia could not unilaterally qualify the offence. The 1911 Agreement merely “refers to the principles of international law”, and those principles “do not recognize any rule of unilateral and definitive qualification by the State granting diplomatic asylum”. Colombia's reliance on the extradition provisions confused territorial asylum with diplomatic asylum: in extradition the refugee is outside the territory of the State where the offence was committed, and a grant of asylum does not derogate from that State's sovereignty; in diplomatic asylum the refugee is within that territory, and the grant derogates from its sovereignty and “cannot be recognized unless its legal basis is established in each particular case”. The competence Colombia claimed is of “an exceptional character”; “such a competence is not inherent in the institution of diplomatic asylum”. The 1933 Montevideo Convention could not help because Peru had not ratified it, and — crucially — the number of ratifications (eleven for the 1933 Convention, two for the 1939 Convention) “reveals the weakness of this argument”.
  • No regional custom had been proved. The party relying on such a custom must prove that it is “in accordance with a constant and uniform usage practised by the States in question”, and that the usage “is the expression of a right appertaining to the State granting asylum and a duty incumbent on the territorial State”. The evidence showed “so much uncertainty and contradiction, so much fluctuation and discrepancy in the exercise of diplomatic asylum and in the official views expressed on various occasions…and the practice has been so much influenced by considerations of political expediency…that it is not possible to discern in all this any constant and uniform usage, accepted as law”. And even had a custom existed among certain Latin-American States, it “could not be invoked against Peru which, far from having by its attitude adhered to it, has, on the contrary, repudiated it by refraining from ratifying the Montevideo Conventions of 1933 and 1939”.
  • Peru was not bound to give the safe-conduct, because it had not requested that Haya de la Torre leave the country, had contested the legality of the asylum, and had refused a safe-conduct: the practice of requesting safe-conducts “does not and cannot mean that the State, to whom such a request…has been addressed, is legally bound to accede to it”.
  • On the counter-claim: Peru had not established that military rebellion was in itself a common crime (Article 248 of the Peruvian Code of Military Justice tended to prove the contrary); but Colombia had not shown that the asylum was granted in an “urgent case” — three months had elapsed, the refugee had been in hiding, and the Court considered that “prima facie, such circumstances make it difficult to speak of urgency”. Asylum cannot in principle be opposed to the operation of justice: “An exception to this rule can occur only if, in the guise of justice, arbitrary action is substituted for the rule of law”. Peru's counter-claim accordingly failed too — a legal stand-off which produced the sequel, the Haya de la Torre case (1951), where the Court held that Colombia was not obliged to surrender the refugee but that the asylum ought to have terminated.

5. Principles evolved

  • Regional custom is recognised by Article 38(1)(b), but the burden on the claimant is heavy: it must prove constant and uniform usage accepted as law and the existence of a corresponding right and duty — and the custom must be opposable to the defendant State.
  • Illustrates the persistent objector (or, here, the non-adhering State): a State which has declined to ratify the instruments embodying the alleged rule has “repudiated” it, and it cannot be invoked against that State.
  • Distinction between territorial and diplomatic asylum, and between extradition and asylum — with the principle that a derogation from territorial sovereignty must have an established legal basis in each case.
  • Interpretation as a limitation: Article 2(1) of the Havana Convention “must be regarded as a limitation of the extent to which asylum shall be respected” — a good example of a treaty clause being read down to protect sovereignty.
  • Asylum is not a shield against the ordinary administration of justice, but the Court also refused to order the refugee's surrender.

6. How to use this case

  • The leading authority for regional custom (2012 Q.2), and for the proposition that a rule cannot be invoked against a State that has not accepted it.
  • The standard illustration for the burden and quality of proof of custom — “constant and uniform”, not “uncertain and contradictory”.
  • For the 2000 Q.7 question on reservations to multilateral treaties “with special reference to Asylum (1951)”: use the case to show that an unratified convention — or a reservation — cannot create obligations for a non-consenting State, and that this is the same consent-logic later codified in VCLT Arts. 19–21.
  • For the distinction between custom and comity/expediency: the Court's description of diplomatic asylum practice as driven by “considerations of political expediency” is the North Sea point in another form.

CASE 5 — Effect of Awards of Compensation Made by the United Nations Administrative Tribunal

ICJ Rep. 1954, p. 47 · Advisory Opinion of 13 July 1954 · Prescribed at syllabus item 27 (general principles and other sources) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 21–23

1. Facts

In 1949 the General Assembly established the United Nations Administrative Tribunal (UNAT) to hear disputes between the United Nations and its staff, with jurisdiction to order the rescission of contested administrative decisions or the payment of compensation. In 1953 the Tribunal awarded compensation to eleven staff members who had been dismissed after refusing to answer questions put by the United States authorities (the “McCarthy-era” discharges). The Secretary-General, in order to give effect to the awards, proposed to make the necessary payments out of the budgetary appropriation. The Advisory Committee on Administrative and Budgetary Questions objected that these were not expenses of the Organisation which the Secretary-General was authorised to incur, and that the General Assembly, which had reserved to itself the decision whether awards should be paid, could refuse to make the payment. By Res. 785 (VIII) of 9 December 1953 the General Assembly asked the Court four questions, the two substantive ones being: (i) Did the General Assembly have the right, on any grounds, to refuse to give effect to an award of compensation made by UNAT in favour of a staff member whose contract had been terminated without his assent? (ii) If the answer was in the negative, how should the award be implemented?

2. Issues

  1. Had the General Assembly itself the power to establish a tribunal with judicial authority, and to make its judgments binding on the United Nations?
  2. Could the General Assembly, nonetheless, refuse to give effect to an award?
  3. Are the awards of the Tribunal capable of review, and by whom?

3. Arguments

One group of questions invited the Court to hold that the Assembly, as the body which established the Tribunal and which controls the budget, could always decline to pay — the position supported by the Advisory Committee and by several delegations, including that of the United States, on essentially constitutional grounds: the Assembly's budgetary power is unqualified, and the Assembly answers only to the members. The opposing view, which the Court adopted, was that the Tribunal had been created as a judicial body to secure the rights of the staff and to provide a remedy for the staff regulations, and that a body whose awards could be disregarded at the will of one of the parties would not be a tribunal at all.

4. Decision

  • By 9 votes to 3 the Court answered the first question in the negative: the General Assembly has no right, on any grounds, to refuse to give effect to an award of compensation made by UNAT in favour of a staff member whose contract had been terminated without his assent. Judges Alvarez, Hackworth and Levi Carneiro dissented on that point.
  • On the second question the Court held that the award should be implemented — the Secretary-General, as the chief administrative officer, is under a duty to pay out of the appropriation, and no charge of ultra vires could be levelled against the Tribunal.

5. The reasoning

  1. The UN has the power, in staff matters, to establish a judicial tribunal. The Court found this power in the Charter generally and in Article 101(1) — staff shall be appointed by the Secretary-General under regulations established by the General Assembly — and in the Assembly's power to regulate staff relations; and it reasoned (as in Reparation) that the Organisation possesses the powers necessary for the performance of its functions, by implication where not expressly conferred. “The Court finds that the power to establish a tribunal, to do justice as between the Organisation and the staff members, was essential to ensure the efficient working of the Secretariat, and to give effect to the paramount consideration of securing the highest standards of efficiency, competence and integrity… Capacity to do this arises by necessary intendment out of the Charter.”
  2. The Tribunal is “independent” and “truly judicial”. Its statute secures the judicial character of the Tribunal: the members are to be appointed for a fixed term, with no possibility of dismissal during their term; the Tribunal decides in accordance with the staff regulations and rules, and it can rescind the contested decision or award compensation. “These provisions…have the result that the Tribunal is not an advisory body…It is not a body of a political character…It is, in fact, a judicial body…an independent and truly judicial body.”
  3. Because it is judicial, its awards are res judicata and bind the United Nations, and therefore the General Assembly as one of its organs. “The Court considers that the function of the General Assembly in this matter, which is to exercise the power of the Organisation to give effect to the awards, is merely a function of execution… the awards are binding upon the United Nations Organisation and, consequently, upon the General Assembly.” Once the Tribunal has given a valid judgment, the Assembly is not free to re-open the merits: “there is no room for the questions of the ‘technical validity’ of the awards…the awards are binding and must be honoured.”
  4. No review. The General Assembly intended the Tribunal's judgments to be final and without appeal; the Statute makes no provision for review, and the Assembly cannot create one after the event.

6. Principles evolved

  • Res judicata as a general principle of law applied within the United Nations: a judicial decision is final and binding as between the parties to it.
  • The distinction between judicial and political organs and the enforceability of the decisions of the former against the latter — the rule of law within the Organisation.
  • Implied powers (following Reparation, 1949) applied to internal staff relations.
  • The obligation to honour the terms of a contract of employment — a general principle underlying the award.
  • Independence of the judiciary, secured by non-dismissible tenure — a principle later reinforced by the ILO Administrative Tribunal cases and by De Merode (1987) on the effect of World Bank Administrative Tribunal decisions.
  • Practically, the opinion established the principle — extended to other international organisations with administrative tribunals — that awards against an organisation are the responsibility of the organisation and must be honoured in its budget.

7. How to use this case

  • Prescribed under the head of general principles of law and “other sources”: it is the casebook's illustration of how the Court derives a principle (res judicata) from the practice of tribunals and the nature of the judicial function.
  • For “ex aequo et bono and other subsidiary means”: the case shows a decision of an international tribunal treated as binding because of the character of the tribunal and the principle of finality — not because any statute made it a source under Article 38.
  • For questions on the legal personality of the UN (Topic 1): the companion case to Reparation, showing what follows from personality — the capacity to be bound by, and to perform, its obligations (here, contractual and res judicata obligations).
  • For the Inkowa scenario type question (2002 Q.7) about an organ refusing to honour a decision: cite the sentence “the awards are binding upon the United Nations Organisation and, consequently, upon the General Assembly.”

CASE 6 — Island of Palmas Case (Netherlands / United States)

Permanent Court of Arbitration · Award of 4 April 1928 · Sole Arbitrator: Max Huber · Prescribed at syllabus item 11 (occupation, territorial sovereignty — and habitually cited in Topic 2 for custom, evidence of title, and the inter-temporal law)

Provenance. The Island of Palmas award is not printed in the DU volume (the Chapter on territorial sovereignty is not part of the Topic-2 material supplied). It is prescribed by the syllabus by citation only, and the treatment below is based on the award itself as reproduced in the standard reports and on the leading commentaries. Every proposition attributed to Huber is a quotation or a close paraphrase from the award, and the student should be able to reproduce parts 3, 4 and 5 in particular.

1. Facts

The Island of Palmas (Miangas) is a small island in the Netherlands East Indies, between Mindanao (Philippines) and the Nanusa group. Spain had ceded the Philippines to the United States by the Treaty of Paris of 1898, and the United States claimed that the island fell within the territory ceded (as part of the archipelago). The Netherlands claimed that it had exercised sovereignty over the island for centuries, as part of its possessions in the East Indies. By a special agreement of 23 January 1925 the two States submitted “the question whether the Island of Palmas…forms a part of the territory of the Netherlands or a part of the territory of the United States” to arbitration by a single arbitrator, Max Huber, who decided the case in favour of the Netherlands.

2. Issues

  1. What was the basis of the United States' claim — was the island included in the cession of 1898, or was it terra nullius at that date such that discovery or contiguity could give title?
  2. What constitutes a valid display of territorial sovereignty for the purposes of maintaining title?
  3. What is the effect of the discovery of an island by a State — is the “inchoate title” of discovery sufficient, and how long does it last?
  4. What law governs the validity of a claim to sovereignty: the law in force at the time the claim arose, or the law in force at the time of the dispute?

3. Arguments of the parties

Table 2.20 — Island of Palmas: the two cases as argued
United StatesNetherlands
The island was part of the territory ceded by Spain in 1898. Spain had obtained title by discovery (the island appears on Spanish maps; Portuguese and Spanish navigators passed through) and the United States took whatever rights Spain had. In the alternative, the island belonged to the archipelago physically (the “contiguity” and “presumed” argument): geographically, geologically and by the fact that the Philippines were ceded “as an entire archipelago”, the island must be treated as part of the territory ceded. Discovery alone, even without occupation, gave a title good against other States until the island was effectively occupied by somebody else.Spain never had sovereignty over the island: the map relied on was of doubtful authority and was not an official Spanish map, and the island did not fall within the limits of the Spanish possessions as defined by the Treaty of Münster of 1648 and the Treaty of Utrecht of 1713. The Netherlands, by contrast, had been exercising sovereign authority — through the Netherlands East India Company and later the colonial Government — in the region, the island having been governed as part of the residency of Ternate and its native States; that continuing and peaceful display of authority was the source of title.

4. Decision

The Tribunal found that the island “cannot be considered as being included in the cession of the Philippine Islands…” and — the operative finding — that it “in consequence forms a part of the territory of the Netherlands”. In essence: the island was not part of Spanish territory ceded in 1898; and the Netherlands had demonstrated a continuous and peaceful display of State authority there.

5. The principle of the award — six propositions

  1. Territorial sovereignty as the criterion: “Sovereignty in the relations between States signifies independence. Independence in regard to a portion of the globe is the right to exercise therein, to the exclusion of any other State, the functions of a State.” Palmas is above all the case that defines what it means to hold territory.
  2. Continuous and peaceful display of State authority is required: the Netherlands' title was established because its authorities had “continuously and peacefully” exercised State functions over the island, even though the exercise was not intensive: “The continuous and peaceful display of territorial sovereignty…is as good as a title” — but there must always be “the existence of the actual continuous and peaceful display of State functions”.
  3. Discovery gives only an inchoate (incomplete) title: “The establishment of [sovereignty] cannot be substituted by the mere fact of the discovery of the island…The discovery of the island…can only be considered as creating an inchoate title” — a title which the discovering State must complete by effective occupation within a reasonable time; if it does not, the island remains terra nullius and the territory may be occupied by another State. The United States' derivative claim through Spain therefore failed.
  4. Contiguity is not a rule of international law: “the alleged principle of contiguity…cannot be sustained…an island cannot be considered as a part of the territory of the mainland or of another island merely because the latter is nearer”. The award rejects geography as a source of title, whatever its weight as evidence.
  5. The inter-temporal law — the sentence that is quoted in every course: “As regards the question which of different legal systems prevailing at successive periods is to be applied in a particular case (the so-called intertemporal law), a distinction must be made between the creation of rights and the existence of rights. The same principle which subjects the act creative of a right to the law in force at the time the right arises, demands that the existence of the right, in other words its continued manifestation, shall follow the conditions required by the evolution of law.” So: title validly acquired in the seventeenth century is judged by seventeenth-century law; but to survive into the twentieth century it must satisfy the twentieth century's requirements — which, after the era of the Berlin Conference, meant effective occupation.
  6. Evidence, and the relativity of title: because the question is one of the exclusion of other States, sovereignty is a relative matter, proved by the acts of the State and by the absence of competing acts; and “the acts of the Netherlands…must be judged in the light of the law in force at the time of the acts”. His treatment of maps, treaties and the conduct of subordinate native officials is the classic instruction on the comparative weight of evidence of title.

6. Principles evolved

  • Effective occupation / continuous and peaceful display of authority as the test of territorial sovereignty, with discovery giving only an inchoate title.
  • The inter-temporal law in the two-part form (creation judged by the law of the time; continued existence judged by contemporary law) — a formula adopted by, among others, the ICJ in Aegean Sea Continental Shelf (1978) and by the Eritrea–Ethiopia Commission.
  • Rejection of contiguity as a basis of sovereignty (contrast the doctrine of “proximity” in continental-shelf delimitation, which is a different question).
  • Recognition and evidence: the conduct of the parties, the maps, and the reaction of other States are the material by which a tribunal reconstructs title — a lesson applied in Temple of Preah Vihear (Case 7) and in the Rann of Kutch arbitration.
  • Custom demonstrated by practice: Huber treats the usages of the region and the practice of the Dutch Company as facts of legal significance — a model of how practice is marshalled.

7. How to use this case

  • For territorial sovereignty and acquisition of title (syllabus item 11, Topic 5 as arranged here) — the leading authority, and the one Indian courts and tribunals cite in boundary cases.
  • For the inter-temporal law in the sources chapter: a rule of custom generated by arbitration and now accepted as general international law — evidence that arbitral awards build law.
  • For evidence and burden of proof: it is the case to cite when asked how a tribunal handles conflicting evidence of State practice — a directly transferable technique to the custom questions.
  • For the Jadhav / VCCR type questions on consular notification, the “Kalapani” type boundary questions, and the Temple problem: keep Palmas beside Temple and the Rann of Kutch award.

CASE 7 — Temple of Preah Vihear (Cambodia v. Thailand) (Merits)

ICJ Rep. 1962, p. 6 · Judgment of 15 June 1962 · Prescribed at syllabus item 15(c) (acquisition of territory, in the Topic 2 case list, and cited in the sources chapter for acquiescence, map-evidence and treaty interpretation) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 24–29

1. Facts

The Temple of Preah Vihear is an ancient religious complex standing on a promontory on the south-facing cliff of the Dângrêk mountain range, which forms the natural watershed boundary between Thailand (then Siam) and Cambodia (then French Indochina). The question was whether the Temple lay in Thai or in Cambodian territory. The boundary had been fixed by the Treaty of 13 February 1904, which stipulated in Article 1 that “the frontier shall follow in this region the watershed between the basins of the Nam Sen and the Nam Moun on the one hand and the Mekong on the other” — i.e. the line of the watershed, which would have placed the Temple in Thailand. Article 3 provided for delimitation by Mixed Commissions, and Article 4 left it to the French and Siamese officers to “determine the exact position of the frontier”, with the surveying to be conducted by French officers, the results to be submitted to the Siamese Government, and the maps to be communicated. In 1907–08 the French survey produced the map (known as the Annex I map) which drew the line south of the Temple — putting the Temple in Cambodia, in departure from the watershed. The map was communicated to Siam, printed, and used. Thailand (Siam) later maintained that the map was not binding because it had not been accepted; in 1954 Thai authorities occupied the Temple, and in 1958 Cambodia invoked the Court's jurisdiction (compromissory clause, 1950 Declaration, and the general Act). Cambodia asked the Court to declare that it had sovereignty and that the map line was the frontier; Thailand counter-claimed for its own frontier line.

2. Issues

  1. Did the Annex I map represent the true boundary, notwithstanding that it departed from the watershed line prescribed in Article 1 of the 1904 Treaty?
  2. Did Siam accept the map — by its conduct, silence, and the acts of its own officials — with the consequence that the map, and not the watershed, fixed the frontier (the plea of acquiescence and the binding character of the map as an “agreed” delimitation)?
  3. Could Thailand rely on the plea of error to escape the map, on the ground that its officers had made a mistake in receiving and printing it?
  4. Did Thailand's occupation of the Temple in 1954 divest Cambodia of sovereignty, and was Article 4 of the 1937 Treaty of Friendship (conciliation) decisive?

3. Arguments of the parties

CambodiaThailand
The Annex I map was the frontier: it was drawn by the Mixed Commission's French officers as provided and communicated to Siam, and was accepted by Siam without protest, printed in official Siamese publications, and used in subsequent delimitations and in dealings with France. The Siamese authorities had every opportunity to object and did not; their acceptance and long acquiescence made the map the contractual frontier of the parties. If Siam did not protest, this was because of a mistake — it relied on the French officers — and a State cannot be bound by an acceptance given in error. Thailand also argued that the Temple area should be governed by the ordinary rules of acquisition: its officials had been in charge, and the 1954 occupation reflected effective sovereignty.

4. Decision

  • By 9 votes to 3 the Court found that the Temple of Preah Vihear is situated in territory under the sovereignty of Cambodia, and that Thailand was under an obligation to withdraw any military or police forces stationed in the Temple or in its vicinity on Cambodian territory (a further vote dealt with the restoration of any objects removed).
  • On the map and acquiescence: “the Court…is satisfied that the Annex I map was accepted by the Siamese authorities, and that they accepted it as representing the frontier”. The Siamese members of the Mixed Commission “had every opportunity to raise objections…but did not do so”; the map “was published and used by the Siamese authorities as representing the frontier”; and the Court was unable to accept the contention that Siam had no knowledge of it. Consequently, the map was not merely evidence but the contractual frontier: the Parties “by their conduct… accepted the Annex I map as the frontier”.
  • On the plea of error: “It is an established rule of law that a plea of error cannot be allowed as an element vitiating consent if the party advancing it contributed by its own conduct to the error, or could have avoided it, or if the circumstances were such as to put that party on notice of a possible error”. Siam had ample means of checking the line — it had its own qualified officers and the treaty's requirement of a joint delimitation — and the Court pointed to the dramatic character of the escarpment: if the Temple had been intended to be left to Siam, “the natural and obvious line would have been the escarpment”, and the Siamese authorities could not reasonably have failed to notice where the line ran. The plea failed.
  • On the watershed as such: the Court held that the dispute concerned the frontier “at the Temple of Preah Vihear” and that the watershed line of the 1904 Treaty applied only where the line was genuinely in doubt; the parties had fixed the line by the map and their conduct, and Cambodia's claim was thus founded on the accepted delimitation rather than on geography. Note that the Court also rejected Cambodia's additional argument, by a single vote (the President's casting vote), that the Annex I line had ipso facto become the boundary by operation of the 1904 Treaty alone — a striking echo of Lotus.
  • No subsequent Thai title: whether or not Thailand's conduct from 1908 to 1954 was “that of a sovereign state”, it “cannot be considered as in itself sufficient to have established a title to the Temple”. Cambodia's protests were sufficient and Thailand's 1954 occupation created no title.

5. Principles evolved

  • Acquiescence and estoppel: silence in the face of a boundary that a State has accepted, used and printed — and the absence of protest over decades, despite every opportunity — bars that State from later challenging the line. This is the “quiescence of the community” argument from Palmas converted into a strict rule.
  • Maps and conduct as evidence of a delimited frontier, and the recognition that a boundary may be fixed by conduct even if it departs from the letter of the treaty.
  • The plea of error cannot be raised by a party whose own conduct contributed to the error or which could have avoided it — a general principle of law applied to treaty consent (now relevant to VCLT Art. 48).
  • The distinction between the establishment of a frontier and the acquisition of territory — an important conceptual point: the case is about delimitation (where is the line?) rather than succession or cession.
  • Interpretation in context and the role of subsequent conduct — the parent of the modern Article 31(3)(b) VCLT analysis applied by the Court in the Chagos opinion to a boundary-related treaty.

6. How to use this case

  • For the classic problem questions on acquisition of territorial sovereignty (syllabus item 15) — the pair of Palmas and Temple covers effective occupation and delimitation respectively.
  • For treaty interpretation and the sources of evidence of the parties' agreement: it is the case that shows how maps, silence, internal publications and official acts combine to establish a binding understanding.
  • For acquiescence and estoppel in the law of the sea context (the Gulf of Maine chamber judgments) and in investment arbitration (Waste Management v. Mexico) where the ICJ's reasoning is repeatedly borrowed.
  • For the 2025 Q.3 / 2013 Q.3 questions about the relationship of treaties and custom and about the completeness of Article 38: Temple shows a treaty whose meaning was settled by conduct — an example of the “subsequent practice” limb of Article 31(3)(b) in action.

CASE 8 — Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons

ICJ Rep. 1996, p. 226 · Advisory Opinion of 8 July 1996 · Requested by the General Assembly (Res. 49/75 K) · Prescribed at syllabus item 30(a)(b) (General Assembly resolutions) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 30–32

1. Facts and the question

The General Assembly, concerned at the deadlock in the Conference on Disarmament and acting under Article 96(1) of the Charter, requested the Court to give an advisory opinion on the question: “Is the threat or use of nuclear weapons in any circumstance permitted under international law?” The Court heard statements from forty-five States and received written statements from others. In parallel, the World Health Organization had asked its own question, which the Court declined to answer for want of jurisdiction (the question did not arise within the scope of the WHO's activities).

2. Issues

  1. Had the Court jurisdiction to answer, and should it exercise its discretion to do so?
  2. Is there a customary or conventional rule specifically prohibiting the threat or use of nuclear weapons?
  3. What is the legal significance of the many General Assembly resolutions declaring the use of nuclear weapons unlawful?
  4. What of the threat or use of nuclear weapons in the extreme circumstance of self-defence, where the very survival of a State is at stake?

3. Arguments

On the sources question the two sides were mirror images. The States supporting the request (a large majority of the membership, including India) argued that a series of General Assembly resolutions beginning with Res. 1653 (XVI) of 1961 declared the use of nuclear weapons to be a crime against humanity and a violation of the Charter, that these resolutions reflected the opinio juris of the international community, and that their repetition by large majorities gave them a normative character — the “law-making” or “de lege ferenda” function of resolutions. The nuclear-weapon States and their allies argued that the resolutions were recommendatory, adopted against or without the votes of the States whose conduct was in question, and that the actual practice of States — the possession of nuclear weapons, doctrines of deterrence, the deployment of weapons — negated any opinio juris of illegality; they also invoked the Lotus principle that what is not prohibited is permitted.

4. Decision

  • The Court unanimously (with different reasoning) held that it had jurisdiction and would answer the question.
  • By 11 votes to 3 it held that “there is in neither customary nor conventional international law any specific authorization of the threat or use of nuclear weapons”.
  • Also by 11 votes to 3: “there is in neither customary nor conventional international law any comprehensive and universal prohibition of the threat or use of nuclear weapons as such”.
  • Unanimously (by 7 votes to 7 with the President's casting vote on the second sentence): “A threat or use of force by means of nuclear weapons that is contrary to Article 2, paragraph 4, of the United Nations Charter and that fails to meet all the requirements of Article 51, is unlawful”; and “A threat or use of nuclear weapons should also be compatible with the requirements of the international law applicable in armed conflict, particularly those of the principles and rules of international humanitarian law, as well as with specific obligations under treaties and other undertakings which expressly deal with nuclear weapons.” The Court could not conclude definitively whether the threat or use of nuclear weapons would be lawful or unlawful in an extreme circumstance of self-defence in which the very survival of a State would be at stake — the famous non-liquet-avoiding formula adopted by the President's casting vote.
  • On the General Assembly resolutions, para. 70 (already set out at §2.2.1 B) is the decision — “they still fall short of establishing the existence of an opinio juris on the illegality of the use of such weapons”.

5. Principles evolved

  • The normative value of General Assembly resolutions: not binding, but capable of providing evidence of a rule or of the emergence of opinio juris, depending on content, conditions of adoption, the existence of an opinio juris as to their normative character, and the effect of a series of resolutions.
  • The two-element test of custom restated: “the substance of customary international law must be looked for primarily in the actual practice and opinio juris of States” (para. 64); the Court will examine state practice and the subjective element, and where the two diverge — as with deterrence versus declarations of illegality — the custom is not established (para. 73).
  • Negative evidence and the limits of deductive reasoning: the Court refused to deduce the prohibition from the mere absence of use since 1945, because “the members of the international community are profoundly divided on the matter of whether non-recourse to nuclear weapons over the past fifty years constitutes the expression of an opinio juris” (para. 67). Contrast Nicaragua, where the Court accepted that the absence of contrary practice could strengthen a rule that was already established by other means.
  • Interpretation of a resolution against its own object: the Court's reading of Res. 1653 (XVI) — that the Assembly applied general rules of customary law to nuclear weapons, thereby indicating the absence of a specific customary prohibition — is the finest example of using the silence of a resolution as evidence.
  • Humanitarian law as a limit on any weapon: the Court's statement of the Martens Clause (para. 78) and the dictum that the principles of humanity and the dictates of the public conscience continue to apply even where no specific prohibition exists.

6. How to use this case

  • The core case for General Assembly resolutions, prescribed at syllabus item 30(a)(b) and printed in the DU volume. Learn paras. 64, 67, 70, 71, 72 and 73.
  • For 2025 short note 4(3), 2012 Q.3, 2002 Q.8(a), 1998 Q.8(a) and 1992 Q.8(a): the case is the spine of every answer.
  • For the practice/opinio juris couple: use paras. 64 and 73 together — practice exists (deployment, doctrine) and declarations exist, but they cancel each other out.
  • For India's position: cite the case in the disarmament context (India's statement to the Court, and its advocacy of a Nuclear Weapons Convention) as the Indian example.

CASE 9 — Legal Consequences for States of the Continued Presence of South Africa in Namibia (South West Africa) notwithstanding Security Council Resolution 276 (1970)

ICJ Rep. 1971, p. 16 · Advisory Opinion of 21 June 1971 · Prescribed at syllabus item 30(c) (Security Council resolutions)

Provenance. The DU volume prints no text of the Namibia opinion; the syllabus cites it under “resolutions of the Security Council”. What follows is based on the opinion itself and the official ICJ summary. The paragraphs to know are 105–116 (Article 25) and 124–127 (erga omnes consequences).

1. Facts

After the dissolution of the League of Nations the League's Mandate over South West Africa (Namibia) continued, and South Africa's administration of it continued, though the Union never placed the territory under the UN trusteeship system as it had undertaken to do. The ICJ had advised in 1950 that the Mandate survived and that the supervisory function had passed to the United Nations, and in 1955 and 1956 on voting procedure and audiatur et altera pars. In 1960–1966 Ethiopia and Liberia brought contentious proceedings against South Africa alleging failure to fulfil the Mandate; in 1966 the Court, by the President's casting vote, held that they had no legal interest (the second phase judgment). The General Assembly then terminated the Mandate by Res. 2145 (XXI) (27 October 1966), and the Security Council adopted Res. 245 (1968), 246 (1968), 264 (1969) and finally Res. 276 (1970), declaring the continued presence of South Africa in Namibia illegal and calling upon all States to refrain from acts implying recognition of the legality of that presence. On 29 July 1970 the Council requested an advisory opinion on the legal consequences for States.

2. Issues

  1. What are the legal consequences for States of the continued presence of South Africa in Namibia, notwithstanding SC Res. 276 (1970)?
  2. Was the termination of the Mandate and the assumption of supervisory functions by the General Assembly lawful — i.e. had the Assembly the competence to act when the Council was also seized of the matter?
  3. Are Security Council decisions binding under Article 25 generally, or only when the Council acts under Articles 41 and 42 (Chapter VII enforcement)?

3. Arguments

South Africa argued that the Mandate had lapsed with the League; that the Assembly's termination of the Mandate was ultra vires; that Res. 276 was a recommendation and not binding; and that Article 25 applied only in respect of enforcement action under Articles 41 and 42 — an argument also supported by some members. The General Assembly and the requesting States argued that the Mandate survived (as the 1950 opinion had held), that the Assembly's supervisory function was a legitimate substitute for the League Council, and that Res. 276 was a binding decision imposing obligations on all Members.

4. Decision

  • By 13 votes to 2: the continued presence of South Africa in Namibia being illegal, South Africa is under an obligation to withdraw its administration from Namibia immediately and thus put an end to its occupation of the Territory. (Judges Gros and Petrén were in the minority.)
  • By 11 votes to 4: States Members of the United Nations are under an obligation to recognise the illegality of South Africa's presence in Namibia and the invalidity of its acts on behalf of or concerning Namibia, and to refrain from any acts and in particular any dealings with the Government of South Africa implying recognition of the legality of, or lending support or assistance to, such presence and administration.
  • By 11 votes to 4: it is incumbent upon States which are not Members of the United Nations to give assistance, within the scope of the preceding paragraph, in the action taken by the United Nations. (Judges Fitzmaurice, Gros, Petrén and Onyeama dissented in part.)

5. The reasoning — and the law on Article 25

  • The Mandate survived. The Court reaffirmed its 1950 opinion: the Mandate was a “sacred trust”, and the UN had taken over the League's supervisory functions; South Africa's obligation to submit reports and to transmit petitions persisted, and its refusal “amounts to a breach of an obligation”.
  • The General Assembly could terminate the Mandate. The termination of the Mandate and the assumption of its administration by the United Nations were validly effected; the Assembly had been unable to secure South Africa's cooperation after the 1966 judgment, and the action was not ultra vires.
  • Article 25 — the crucial holding: the Court refused to read Article 25 as confined to enforcement measures. “If Article 25 had reference solely to decisions of the Security Council concerning enforcement action under Articles 41 and 42 of the Charter, that is to say, if it were only such decisions which had binding effect, then Article 25 would be superfluous, since this effect is secured by Articles 48 and 49 of the Charter.” The consequence is that decisions of the Security Council are binding on all Members under Article 25 whenever the Council exercises its powers consistently with the Charter; and whether it has done so “is to be determined in each case, having regard to the terms of the resolution to be interpreted, the discussions leading to it, the Charter provisions invoked and, in general, all circumstances that might assist in determining the legal consequences of the resolution of the Security Council” (para. 114) — the passage quoted verbatim in the Kosovo opinion (paras. 94, 117).
  • Erga omnes consequences (paras. 122–127): the illegality of the continued presence was “opposable to all States”, barring erga omnes the legality of the situation; States were bound not to recognise the validity of South Africa's acts, not to render assistance to it, and to assist the United Nations; and the non-recognition of administrative acts (births, marriages, deaths, registration) was required “in general”, subject to an exception in favour of the inhabitants in matters of private law with consequential practical consequences.
  • The principle that a binding determination by a competent organ “cannot remain without consequence”: “a determination that a situation is illegal cannot remain without consequence” — the basis for the whole practice of non-recognition of illegal situations (Manchukuo, Southern Rhodesia, Bantustans, Cyprus, Northern Cyprus, Crimea).

6. Principles evolved

  • Security Council decisions are binding under Article 25 generally, not merely when the Council acts under Articles 41–42; and the binding effect is determined on the terms and circumstances of each resolution.
  • The obligations of non-Members and the generalisation of the erga omnes effect of an illegal situation.
  • The law of international organisations: the Assembly could substitute for the League Council; supervisory functions pass with the functions; and termination of a mandate is within the Assembly's competence when the Mandated State is in breach.
  • Non-recognition as a legal duty, transforming the Stimson doctrine of 1932 (a political policy) into a binding obligation.
  • Self-determination and the “sacred trust”: mandating the territory's people's right to self-determination — the precursor of Western Sahara and Chagos.

7. How to use this case

  • The primary case for the binding character of Security Council resolutions (syllabus item 30(c)); pair it with Kosovo (interpretation) and with the legislative-resolutions practice (1373, 1540).
  • For 2000 Q.8(i) (resolutions of GA and SC as sources): lead with Nuclear Weapons then Namibia.
  • For erga omnes and Article 41 ARSIWA consequences: the non-recognition duty is the practical application of erga omnes in the law of responsibility.
  • For the Indian angle: India's role in the Namibia question (supporting Res. 2145 (XXI) and Res. 276 (1970), and its participation in UNTAG in 1989–90 as a peacekeeping contributor) may be mentioned in one sentence to show State practice in support of the resolutions.

CASE 10 — Western Sahara

ICJ Rep. 1975, p. 12 · Advisory Opinion of 16 October 1975 · Requested by the General Assembly (Res. 3292 (XXIX)) · Prescribed at syllabus item 30(c) (Security Council resolutions, and General Assembly practice)

Provenance. As with Namibia, the DU volume prints no text of this opinion; the treatment is based on the opinion and the official summary.

1. Facts

The Western Sahara (Spanish Sahara; the territory of the Sahrawi people) was a Spanish colony. Spain proposed to hold a referendum on its future, and the question of the territory's status came before the General Assembly. In 1974 Morocco and Mauritania asserted claims of historic title to the territory, and at the instance of Morocco (supported by Mauritania) the General Assembly asked the Court two questions, having first heard Spain, Morocco and Mauritania: (i) Was Western Sahara (Río de Oro and Sakiet El Hamra) at the time of Spanish colonization a territory belonging to no one (terra nullius)? If the answer is in the negative, (ii) What were the legal ties between this territory and the Kingdom of Morocco and the Mauritanian entity?

2. Issues

  1. Was the territory terra nullius at the time of Spanish colonisation (1884–85)?
  2. What were the legal ties between the territory and the Kingdom of Morocco and the Mauritanian entity, and were they ties of territorial sovereignty?
  3. Does the right of self-determination — GA Res. 1514 (XV) — affect the answer, and can the Court's opinion affect the Assembly's role in the decolonisation of the territory?

3. Arguments

Morocco and Mauritania argued that they held historic, religious and political ties to the tribes of the territory, that their sovereigns had exercised authority over the region, and hence that the territory was not terra nullius and that their claims of territorial sovereignty were established. Spain contended that the territory had been terra nullius at colonisation and that no ties of sovereignty existed; the Algerian position (and that of the Polisario-backed interests) was that the people of the territory were entitled to self-determination and that the opinion should not be used to defeat it. The General Assembly's resolution, and Res. 1514 (XV), required that the views of the Court be considered before any action on the referendum.

4. Decision

  • On the first question the Court unanimously held that Western Sahara at the time of Spanish colonisation was not a territory belonging to no one (terra nullius). The territory was inhabited by peoples which, if nomadic, were socially and politically organised into tribes and under chiefs competent to represent them, so that Spain's entry into the territory could not be treated as occupation of a vacant territory.
  • On the second question, by 14 votes to 2 the Court found that there existed between the territory and the Kingdom of Morocco legal ties of allegiance between the Sultan and some (though not all) of the tribes of the territory; and by 15 votes to 1 that there existed between the territory and the Mauritanian entity legal ties of a similar nature.
  • Crucially: by 15 votes to 1 the Court held that it had not found legal ties of such a nature as might affect the application of Res. 1514 (XV) (the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples) — in particular the principle of self-determination through the free and genuine expression of the will of the peoples of the territory — and, by 13 votes to 2, that it was not called upon to decide whether the territory was legally Spanish or to determine the question of sovereignty, which was to be resolved by the Assembly's own decolonisation process.

5. Principles evolved and the sources points

  • Terra nullius is a strict concept: a territory is not nullius merely because it is inhabited by nomadic or non-European peoples; social and political organisation suffices — a decisive rejection of the colonial view that “uncivilised” territory was res nullius, and the basis of the modern understanding applied later in Chagos.
  • Self-determination prevails over historic ties: legal ties, even if they exist, cannot displace the right of the people to decide their own future. The Court's careful division — historical ties found, but no sovereign ties, and no effect on Res. 1514 (XV) — is a model of judicial self-restraint in the service of decolonisation.
  • Resolutions as the legal framework: the Court treated Res. 1514 (XV) and Res. 3292 (XXIX) as the governing legal standards, illustrating how a General Assembly resolution operates as a source of the applicable law in an advisory opinion. Verma's observation is that in South West Africa Judge Jessup cited Western Sahara and the resolutions on apartheid to show that recurring resolutions reflect a contemporary international standard.
  • The advisory jurisdiction revisited: the Court again refused to decline jurisdiction merely because contentious or bilateral interests lay behind the question, and declined to decide the sovereignty question because the Assembly had not asked it.
  • Practical sequel: the opinion led to the Madrid Accords of 14 November 1975 between Spain, Morocco and Mauritania and the Green March of 6 November 1975; the territory remains a case on the Committee of 24's list, and India has consistently supported the holding of a referendum under UN auspices — an Indian practice point worth one sentence in an answer about GA resolutions and self-determination.

6. How to use this case

  • For Security Council resolutions as a source (item 30(c)), and, more importantly, as the bridge between resolutions and the emergence of the right of self-determination as custom — the argument completed in Chagos.
  • For terra nullius and territorial acquisition (item 15) — pair with Palmas: Palmas says what occupation requires; Western Sahara says what a nullius territory requires.
  • For erga omnes and self-determination: the line of cases Namibia → Western Sahara → Chagos should be recited in that order whenever a question asks for the development of a source over time.

CASE 11 — Accordance with International Law of the Unilateral Declaration of Independence in Respect of Kosovo

ICJ Rep. 2010, p. 403 · Advisory Opinion of 22 July 2010 · Requested by the General Assembly (Res. 63/3) · Prescribed at syllabus item 30(c) (Security Council resolutions) · Printed in the DU material at pp. 33–36

1. Facts

Kosovo, a province of Serbia with a majority Albanian population, had been placed under interim international administration in 1999 by Security Council Res. 1244 (1999), adopted under Chapter VII, after the NATO bombing campaign. The resolution authorised an international civil presence (UNMIK) and a security presence (KFOR); it foresaw an interim political framework agreement and a final political process to determine the future status of Kosovo, but fixed no conditions for the final status. In 2005–2007 the UN Special Envoy Martti Ahtisaari conducted status talks and recommended “supervised independence”; the Security Council was unable to agree. On 17 February 2008 the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government of Kosovo issued a unilateral declaration of independence. On 8 October 2008, at the request of Serbia, the General Assembly adopted Res. 63/3 asking the Court: “Is the unilateral declaration of independence by the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government of Kosovo in accordance with international law?” The Court received written and oral submissions from thirty-six States, including India.

2. Issues

  1. Did the Court have jurisdiction to answer, and should it exercise its discretion to do so?
  2. Did the declaration violate Res. 1244 (1999) and the Constitutional Framework for Kosovo, and did it violate general international law?
  3. How is a Chapter VII Security Council resolution to be interpreted, and to whom did Res. 1244 address its binding obligations?
  4. Was the declaration a unilateral act of the people of Kosovo, or of the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government (which were bound by the Constitutional Framework)?

3. Arguments

Serbia argued that the declaration was unlawful: it was made by the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government, which were established by and bound by Res. 1244 and the Constitutional Framework; the resolution reserved the question of final status to the Security Council; and the declaration violated Serbian sovereignty and territorial integrity. The authors of the declaration explained that it was an act of the “elected leaders of the people of Kosovo” exercising a political right of self-determination; the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France and others argued that no provision of international law prohibited a declaration of independence (territorial integrity being an obligation of States, not of a national liberation movement), that Res. 1244 was not addressed to the people of Kosovo, and that the Court should not enter the political question of recognition. India and others submitted that the declaration and the recognition of Kosovo violated Serbian sovereignty and territorial integrity — a position consistent with its practice on Simla Agreement and Kashmir.

4. Decision

  • By 10 votes to 4, the Court held that the declaration of independence adopted on 17 February 2008 did not violate international law. On the narrower reading of the question, the Court held it was not called upon to pronounce on the consequences of the declaration or whether Kosovo had achieved statehood. The Court had earlier decided by 9 votes to 5 to answer the question.
  • The Court held that the declaration was not issued by the Provisional Institutions of Self-Government acting within their competence but by the “authors” acting outside the Constitutional Framework, and that no provision of the Framework bound them (paras. 102–121 at 109); and that in any event Res. 1244 did not prohibit a declaration of independence (paras. 114–121).

5. The reasoning — the passages in the DU material

  • On the binding character of Security Council resolutions (para. 85): “Within the legal framework of the United Nations Charter, notably on the basis of Articles 24, 25 and Chapter VII thereof, the Security Council may adopt resolutions imposing obligations under international law. The Court has had occasion to interpret and apply such Security Council resolutions on a number of occasions and has consistently treated them as part of the framework of obligations under international law” — citing Namibia, the Lockerbie provisional measures (1992) and its orders in application of the Genocide Convention (2009), and noting that “Res. 1244 was expressly adopted under Chapter VII of the Charter”, so that it “clearly imposes international legal obligations” (para. 85).
  • On the interpretation of resolutions — the general method (paras. 93–94): the Court noted that it has not previously set out its approach to the interpretation of Security Council resolutions, and adopted as a starting point the rules of interpretation in Articles 31 and 32 VCLT, because a resolution is a “treaty-like” instrument. But “differences between Security Council resolutions and treaties mean that the interpretation of Security Council resolutions also requires that other factors be taken into account”: resolutions are issued by a single, collective body; they are drafted through a very different process; and they must be “interpreted with reference to the decision-making process of the Security Council”, including the statements made at adoption and the voting record. Because Art. 27 voting means that a resolution “represents the view of the Security Council as a body”, it can bind all Members “irrespective of whether they played any part in its formulation”.
  • On the case-by-case determination of addressees (para. 117): “When interpreting Security Council resolutions, the Court must establish, on a case-by-case basis, considering all relevant circumstances, for whom the Security Council intended to create binding legal obligations. The language used by the resolution may serve as an important indicator…” — together with the Namibia quotation (para. 114) on the terms, discussions, Charter provisions invoked and all circumstances.
  • On the application (paras. 113–119): Res. 1244 “did not deal in these terms with the final status of Kosovo or with the conditions for its achievement”; “it is only on the final status that the Security Council remained silent”; in contrast to Res. 1251 (1999) on Cyprus, which specified the conditions for permanent status (a State of Cyprus with a single sovereignty and international personality and a single citizenship), the Council “did not reserve for itself the final determination…and remained silent on the conditions for the final status of Kosovo”. The resolution's addressees were Member States, the Secretary-General and his Special Representative, and — in one specified respect — the KLA and other armed Kosovo Albanian groups (paras. 115–116); no obligation or prohibition was addressed to the authors of the declaration. The Security Council had “not reserved for itself the final determination” and the declaration “cannot be regarded as a violation of Res. 1244”.
  • The general law: the Court rejected the argument that declarations of independence are prohibited by international law and held that “the principle of territorial integrity is an important part of the international legal order…” but is confined, in the Charter (Art. 2(4)) and the Friendly Relations Declaration, to the sphere of inter-State relations; it “enjoins States to respect the territorial integrity and political independence of other States” — not to non-State groups within them. And the Court expressly declined to decide whether Kosovo had become a State, or on the legality of recognitions — leaving the question of statehood to general international law.
  • The dispositif: “the adoption of the declaration of independence of 17 February 2008 did not violate international law”. The four dissenters — Judges Koroma, Bennouna, Skotnikov and Vice-President Tomka — took various views of the jurisdiction of the Court and the legality of the declaration.

6. Principles evolved

  • The method of interpreting Security Council resolutions: treat them as treaty-like, but with reference to their own drafting and voting context; and determine their addressees case by case.
  • The silence of a resolution must be respected: what the Council did not decide remains undecided — the resolution is not to be read as prohibiting everything it did not expressly permit.
  • Territorial integrity binds States, not peoples: the declaration of independence by a non-State actor is not, as such, a violation of international law — a doctrine of great significance for self-determination claims, and one qualified later in the Chagos context.
  • The Court's restraint: the Court refused to decide on recognition and statehood, so that the opinion is, as the commentary has sometimes put it, a narrow answer to a wide question; contrast the broader holding of the Chagos opinion nine years later.
  • The political question doctrine in advisory jurisdiction: the Court's refusal to decline the request, notwithstanding the political context and the Security Council's division, reinforced the advisory jurisdiction as a general law-declaring device.

7. How to use this case

  • The case printed in the DU material for Security Council resolutions as a source — with paras. 85, 94 and 117 as the key paragraphs, and paras. 113–119 for the application.
  • For comparison questions (“compare the role of the GA and SC resolutions as sources”): Kosovo gives you the technique for SC resolutions, Nuclear Weapons the doctrine for GA resolutions.
  • For recognition and statehood (Topic 3): the opinion's refusal to decide is itself the best illustration of the modern doctrine that statehood is a matter of fact and law, not of formal recognition.
  • For India's position: India's written statement in Kosovo — that the declaration and its recognition were incompatible with Serbian sovereignty and territorial integrity — is an Indian practice point to cite in discussions of recognition and of self-determination.

CASE 12 — Legal Consequences of the Separation of the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius in 1965

ICJ Rep. 2019, p. 169 · Advisory Opinion of 25 February 2019 · Requested by the General Assembly (Res. 71/292) · Prescribed at syllabus item 34 (advisory opinions of the PCIJ and the ICJ)

Provenance. The Chagos opinion is not printed in the DU material; the syllabus prescription is by citation, and the student's own annotation on the syllabus page marks it as outside the prescribed material for the essay questions (“not the direct…”). It is nonetheless the case named for this head, and the treatment below is based on the opinion itself, the official ICJ summary, the General Assembly's follow-up resolution and the commentary. It should be read with §2.2.3 D above.

1. Facts

Mauritius was a British colony. In 1965, three years before independence, the United Kingdom detached the Chagos Archipelago — some 55 islands, including Diego Garcia — and constituted it as a separate colony, the British Indian Ocean Territory (BIOT). The Council of Ministers of the then self-governing colony agreed to the detachment, but on terms which the Court later found to be the product of pressure: the United Kingdom had made the grant of independence conditional on the detachment, and the legislative assembly was not informed. In 1966 the United Kingdom leased Diego Garcia to the United States for fifty years (extended in 2016 to 2036), and the entire population of the outer islands was removed between 1968 and 1973. Mauritius acceded to independence on 12 March 1968. In 1980, 1982 and afterwards the Organization of African Unity, the Non-Aligned Movement and the General Assembly called for the return of the Archipelago; the Assembly incorporated its request in Res. 71/292 of 22 June 2017, asking: (a) whether the process of decolonisation of Mauritius was lawfully completed when it acceded to independence in 1968 given the separation of the Chagos Archipelago, and (b) what the consequences under international law were of the continued administration by the United Kingdom of the Archipelago, including its inability to resettle former inhabitants.

2. Issues

  1. Did the Court have jurisdiction to give the opinion, given the United Kingdom's objections that the questions involved a bilateral dispute and that the requesting resolution was adopted by a narrow majority?
  2. Was the detachment of the Archipelago in 1965 lawful under the law in force at the time — the right to self-determination, Res. 1514 (XV) and Res. 1541 (XV), and the requirement of the free and genuine expression of the will of the people?
  3. What were the consequences of the continued administration of the Territory, including the obligation to respect the right of self-determination and the obligations relating to resettlement and the environment?

3. Arguments

Table 2.22 — Chagos: the two cases as argued
Mauritius (supported by the African Union and a majority of States, including India)United Kingdom (supported by Australia, Israel and individual States)
The detachment was unlawful. In 1965 the right of peoples to self-determination was already a rule of customary international law, reflected in Res. 1514 (XV) and 1541 (XV): a non-self-governing territory's boundaries could not be broken up by the administering Power, and the consent obtained from a subordinate colonial administration — under threat that independence would be refused — was not the free and genuine expression of the will of the people. The continued administration was therefore internationally wrongful, and the United Kingdom was obliged to withdraw so that Mauritius could complete decolonisation. The Court had jurisdiction, as the questions were placed by the General Assembly in the framework of its decolonisation functions.The Court had no jurisdiction; even if it had, it should decline to give an opinion, because the questions concerned a bilateral sovereignty dispute with the United Kingdom, and the requesting resolution had been adopted by a vote (94–15–65) which did not command consensus. On the merits: the detachment was lawful under the law of 1965, when the right to self-determination was not yet a established rule of customary international law applicable to a small colonial territory in this manner; the Council of Ministers freely consented; and in any event the resettlement question was governed by the agreements of 1965 and the terms of the 1966 lease, not by international law obligations of the kind asserted. The sovereignty claim of Mauritius was disputed and it was for the parties to reach an agreement.

4. Decision

  • By 12 votes to 1 the Court found that it had jurisdiction and that the request was admissible; and it decided to comply with the request (by 12 votes to 1).
  • On the merits, by 13 votes to 1, the Court found that “the process of decolonisation of Mauritius was not lawfully completed when that country acceded to independence in 1968”, following the separation of the Chagos Archipelago; that “the United Kingdom is under an obligation to bring to an end its administration of the Chagos Archipelago as rapidly as possible”; and (12 votes to 1) that “all Member States are under an obligation to co-operate with the United Nations in order to complete the decolonisation of Mauritius”. Judge Donoghue dissented on the merits; Vice-President Xue and Judges Sebutinde and Robinson issued separate opinions (Judge Robinson, the first Mauritian judge on the Court, wrote the longest).

5. The reasoning

  1. Jurisdiction and propriety (paras. 24–92). The Court held that the questions had a legal character; that the fact that the questions also had political aspects, or that the factual background involved a bilateral dispute, did not deprive it of jurisdiction; that the requesting resolution's narrow adoption was a matter for the General Assembly, not a ground of inadmissibility; and that there were no “compelling reasons” to decline. It located the questions in “the broader frame of reference of decolonization” (para. 88), and refused to narrow or reformulate them. On the Eastern Carelia principle, the Court recalled that the mere fact that an opinion might affect a dispute between States did not bar it — what mattered was whether the question called for a determination of the parties' rights in a contentious dispute (paras. 86–88).
  2. The right to self-determination in 1965 (paras. 118–182). The Court found that the right of peoples to self-determination emerged from the Charter and from UN practice, in particular Res. 1514 (XV) (“the resolution through which the General Assembly proclaimed the right of colonial peoples to self-determination”), and was already a rule of customary international law in 1965; it was “a fundamental principle of international law”; and it is erga omnes in character (paras. 144–155, 180). Res. 1541 (XV) set out the criteria for determining when a non-self-governing territory has reached the stage of full self-government; Principle IX of that annex allows integration or association only if the territory's people have expressed their will through democratic processes.
  3. The detachment was unlawful (paras. 160–177). The two key steps: first, the Court found that the separation of part of a non-self-governing territory in order to establish a new colonial territory was contrary to the right to self-determination, and that there was no example after 1960 in which the General Assembly or other UN organs had treated such a detachment as lawful (para. 160). Secondly, on consent: “the Council of Ministers… appeared to have agreed to the detachment”, but the Court, considering the conditions in which the agreement was obtained — Mauritius was a colony, the assembly had not been consulted, and the United Kingdom had, in effect, made independence conditional on the detachment — held that the detachment “was not based on the free and genuine expression of the will of the people concerned”, and that the consent could not have been freely given (paras. 172–177). Mauritius' independence was thus “not lawfully completed”.
  4. Consequences (paras. 177–182). The United Kingdom's continued administration was “a wrongful act…entailing the international responsibility of that State”; it was “under an obligation to bring to an end its administration of the Chagos Archipelago as rapidly as possible”, so enabling Mauritius to complete the decolonisation of its territory; and all States were under an obligation to cooperate with the United Nations to complete the decolonisation of Mauritius. The Court further found obligations relating to the resettlement of Mauritian nationals — including that the rights of the inhabitants of the Archipelago, including the Chagossians, and the obligations relating to the protection of the natural environment, must be respected — and that the United Kingdom did not have the right to determine the future status of the Archipelago unilaterally.

6. Principles evolved — including the sources points

  • Self-determination as customary law and erga omnes, consolidated: the Court treated Res. 1514 (XV) and 1541 (XV) as evidence of and the vehicle for the rule, and the absence of contrary practice as confirmation.
  • The standard of consent obtained by the administering Power: consent given by a subordinate colonial body under pressure is not the free and genuine expression of the will of the people — a heightened standard which parallels the general law of treaties' rules on coercion (VCLT Arts. 51–52) and the practice on unequal treaties.
  • The importance of GA resolutions as the framework of legality: this is the case where an advisory opinion turned entirely on the normative effect of resolutions — the strongest modern authority for the law-making function of the General Assembly within the field of decolonisation, and a direct successor to Western Sahara.
  • The advisory jurisdiction as a general law-declaring device: the Court refused to be deterred by the presence of a bilateral dispute or by a divided vote in the requesting organ, and answered the question as the General Assembly framed it.
  • Implementation and the completion of the case: the General Assembly's Res. 73/295 (22 May 2019, 116–6–56) affirmed that the Archipelago “forms an integral part of the territory of Mauritius”, demanded the unconditional withdrawal of the British administration within six months, and called on UN organs, funds, programmes, specialised agencies and other international organisations to support the decolonisation of Mauritius; the United Kingdom was excluded from the regional fisheries framework dependent on coastal-State status. In October 2024 the two Governments announced the negotiation of a treaty; on 22 May 2025 they signed it, under which Mauritius exercises full sovereignty over the Archipelago and the United Kingdom retains rights over Diego Garcia for an initial 99 years, extendable by 40 years, at an annual payment of about £101 million, with agreed restrictions on the use of the outer islands. The UN General Assembly and the Organisation of African Unity/OAU's successors had, by then, been asking for restitution for more than fifty years.
  • The interaction of an advisory opinion with subsequent practice: the United Kingdom's later agreement to cede sovereignty, and the General Assembly's implementation resolutions, show the mechanism by which a non-binding opinion produces binding effects through the practice of States and organs — the point to make if asked about the effect of advisory opinions as a source of law.

7. How to use this case

  • Prescribed at syllabus item 34 (advisory opinions) — the case that shows both the jurisdiction and the law-making effect of advisory opinions.
  • For GA resolutions as a source: the strongest modern example; combine with Nuclear Weapons (doctrine) and Western Sahara and Namibia (practice).
  • For self-determination, decolonisation and terra nullius: the closing chapter of the line of cases, and the Indian position (India voted for Res. 71/292 and supported the opinion) as a real example.
  • For current-affairs questions — the 22 May 2025 treaty is the latest development and can be cited in any answer on whether advisory opinions are effective: here an unlawful detachment, declared unlawful in 2019, was undone by a treaty in 2025.

2025 Q.8 “…what is your opinion? Support your answer with the help of decided cases wherever such principles have been applied.”

Model answer (condensed form of §2.1.3).

Introduction. Article 38(1)(c) of the ICJ Statute directs the Court to apply “the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations”. The expression is vague — as your question rightly says — and has attracted three distinct objections: that it is a natural-law survivor in a positivist text; that it permits uncontrolled judicial legislation; and, in the Soviet position associated with Tunkin, that it is not an independent source at all but a device which the Court uses to disguise the creation of new law and to import Western conceptions. My opinion is that the majority view is correct — the words mean general principles of justice recognised by the legal systems of the world in foro domestico, that they are an independent (though subsidiary in practice) source, and that their function is to prevent non liquet. But the objection is not baseless, and the answer to it is the discipline of the case law: the Court does not invent principles, it finds them in the municipal systems and applies them because they are general.

Why the category exists. The drafting history is the answer to the first objection. The Advisory Committee of Jurists in 1920 failed to agree on Root's proposal for compulsory jurisdiction and on Baron Descamps' proposal to apply “the rules of international law as recognised by the legal conscience of civilized nations”; Phillimore's compromise substituted the present words, and the rapporteur, Adatci, explained that the list of sources ought to be completed “in a most decisive way” — otherwise the Court would be unable to decide cases where a state of facts came under no existing rule (the non liquet problem). Waldock's formulation, adopted by the Institute of International Law, adds the other half: the category “has been resorted to…in order that international law may not, in a gap in its positive rules, remain without an appropriate rule”. So the clause is a gap-filler: flexible, subordinate, and intended to be used where positive rules run out.

What the principles are. (i) Res judicata — Effect of Awards of Compensation made by the UN Administrative Tribunal (1954): the awards were binding on the United Nations and “consequently upon the General Assembly”, whatever the General Assembly's budgetary power. (ii) Estoppel, acquiescence and preclusion — Temple of Preah Vihear (1962) (Thailand's silence and conduct over the map), Eastern Greenland (1933) (the Ihlen declaration), and the Gulf of Maine chamber judgment (1984): acknowledging that acquiescence and estoppel “follow from the fundamental principles of good faith and equity”. (iii) Error as a ground of invalidity of consent — Temple again: “a plea of error cannot be allowed as an element vitiating consent if the party advancing it contributed by its own conduct to the error”. (iv) Equity, good faith and the prohibition of abuse of rights — Diversion of Water from the Meuse (Judge Hudson, 1937: a party cannot complain of conduct it has itself committed), the North Sea cases (equitable principles within the law) and the four maritime delimitation cases. (v) Nemo dat / no one can transfer more than he has, restitutio in integrum, and the duty to make reparation — Chorzów Factory (1928). (vi) Consent, pacta sunt servanda and the protection of the individual — the basis of treaty law and of human-rights adjudication. (vii) Judicial independence and the finality of judgments — Effect of Awards; De Merode (1987) for the World Bank Tribunal. (viii) Procedural principles — audi alteram partem (Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries, on the burden of proof), nemo judex in causa sua, and the rule that he who seeks equity must do equity; and the humanitarian principles of the Corfu Channel case (1949) — “elementary considerations of humanity” — applied again in the Nuclear Weapons opinion.

The Tunkin objection. Tunkin's position, in Theory of International Law and in his Hague lectures, was that Article 38(1)(c) is a positivist embarrassment: the Court invoking a “general principle” is really making law, and the formula allows Western legal concepts — private-law analogies of property, contract and delict — to be imposed on a system of international cooperation. The reply, in my submission, has four limbs. First, the Court applies municipal principles only where there is a general concordance of systems, not an idiosyncratic rule (Serbian Loans, 1929, on gold clauses, and the Barcelona Traction and Amco v. Indonesia discussions of unjust enrichment). Secondly, and most importantly, the Court in South West Africa (1966) rejected actio popularis as a general principle — demonstrating that the Court does police the category and will not invent principles for policy reasons. Thirdly, most of the principles actually applied (res judicata, estoppel, error) now do the work of ordinary legal reasoning within custom and treaty law, not as a free-standing source: Temple's doctrine of error became VCLT Article 48, and estoppel was absorbed into the law of delimitation. Fourthly, the modern ILC and ICJ practice (Conclusions on custom, 2018) treats general principles as one of the three “primary” materials, so that the Soviet objection has, in the practice of States, been overtaken.

Conclusion. The words of Article 38(1)(c) are vague but not empty; “civilised nations” no longer excludes anyone, as the Court recognised when it stated that the omission from Article 38 of the words “civilised” in the Revised Rules of 1972 was a reflection of the Charter's equality of States. The category is independent, but its function is subsidiary and its use is disciplined by the requirement of generality. Tunkin's warning is a caution to be respected in application; it is not a reason to read the category out of the Statute.

2025 Q.4(3) Short note — General Assembly resolution as a source of international law

Model note. A General Assembly resolution is, in form, a recommendation (Charter Arts. 10–14), and Article 38 of the ICJ Statute does not mention it. It is, therefore, not a formal source. But it may have normative value. The governing authority is the Nuclear Weapons Advisory Opinion (1996), para. 70: resolutions “even if they are not binding, may sometimes have normative value…They can, in certain circumstances, provide evidence important for establishing the existence of a rule or the emergence of an opinio juris”; to decide whether a given resolution does so, one looks at “its content and the conditions of its adoption”, at whether an opinio juris exists as to its normative character, and at whether a series of resolutions shows the gradual evolution of opinio juris. Applying this to the resolutions declaring nuclear weapons unlawful, the Court in para. 71 found that, because several were adopted “with substantial numbers of negative votes and abstentions”, they “fall short of establishing the existence of an opinio juris”, and in para. 72 drew a negative inference from the Assembly's own reliance on the 1868 and 1925 instruments in Res. 1653 (XVI). A resolution may therefore be: declaratory of custom (Res. 2625 (XXV), 1970, relied on in Nicaragua; Res. 1514 (XV) and 1541 (XV), relied on in Western Sahara and Chagos); law-making or “accelerating” (Res. 1963 (XVII) on outer space, which led to the 1967 Treaty; Res. 3314 (XXIX) on aggression, used in the ICC Statute); an authentic interpretation of the Charter or of custom (the Friendly Relations Declaration); or binding in the narrow internal sense (budget resolutions, Art. 17; decisions on admission and elections). Repetition and subsequent State conduct add weight (Verma; Bin Cheng's “instant custom”; ILC Conclusion 10 of 2018), and abstention is not a negative vote, though abstention by important States may affect enforceability (Bowett). The Sixth Committee's 1974 review concluded against treating resolutions as a new source, accepting them as evidence of custom — which is also the safest examination conclusion. Authorities: Charter Arts. 10–14, 17, 18; Art. 38 ICJ Statute; Nuclear Weapons (1996) paras. 64, 70–73; Nicaragua (1986); Western Sahara (1975); Chagos (2019); ILC Conclusions 10–12 (2018). See §2.2.1 and Case 8 above.

2025 Q.4(4) Short note — Treaties as a source of international law

Model note. A treaty is defined in Article 2(1)(a) VCLT 1969 as “an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments and whatever its particular designation”. Article 38(1)(a) places “international conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the contesting States” first among the sources — first in order, though equal in authority — because it is the most certain and the most deliberate form of law-making. Treaties are both a source of obligation (they bind the parties by virtue of pacta sunt servanda, VCLT Art. 26) and a source of general law: where a treaty is declaratory of custom, a large number of States are parties and non-parties have accepted it, or a rule in it is of a “fundamentally norm-creating character” and has been widely accepted, the treaty rule, or the custom it generates, binds even non-parties — save the persistent objector. That is the holding of the North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969) paras. 70–74, where Article 6 of the 1958 Continental Shelf Convention was held not to have become custom. Treaties may pass into custom in three ways: declaratory (declaring existing custom), crystallising (the treaty fixes an emergent rule at the moment of adoption) and generative (the rule crystallises after the treaty through subsequent practice) — the classification of Baxter and of the ILC. But the doctrinal caveat is real: Brierly, Fitzmaurice and Weil object that a treaty binds only the parties, so that calling it a source of general law confuses obligation with law-creation; the answer is Article 38's own structure, which treats conventions and custom as separate sources and leaves the passage between them to the two-element test. Authorities: VCLT 1969 Arts. 2, 26, 34, 38, 53; Art. 38(1)(a) ICJ Statute; North Sea (1969); Nicaragua (1986) on parallel existence; Wimbledon (1923) on the precedence of treaty obligations; Art. 103 UN Charter. See §2.1.1 above.

2015 Q.1 “Can Article 38 of the Statute of ICJ be regarded as a complete statement of the sources of international law? Discuss in detail.”

Model answer.

Introduction — frame the question precisely. Article 38 does not say, “the sources of international law are…”. It says the Court, whose function is to decide disputes in accordance with international law, “shall apply” four things — conventions, custom, general principles, and judicial decisions and teachings as subsidiary means. Two consequences follow at once. First, the list is a directory to a court, not a legislative catalogue: it tells the Court what materials to use, and it was carried over from the 1920 PCIJ Statute in terms. Secondly, because it is a directory to one court, it cannot exhaust the ways in which international law is made; it is at most a statement of the formal sources. My answer, therefore, is that Article 38 is a complete statement of the formal sources, but not of the sources; and that the incompleteness is the most interesting thing about it.

The four entries, briefly. (i) “International conventions, whether general or particular, establishing rules expressly recognized by the contesting States” — treaties (North Sea for their relation to custom; the “law-making treaty” and the “treaty-contract” distinction associated with Fitzmaurice). (ii) “International custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law” — the two elements of State practice and opinio juris (Lotus p. 28; North Sea paras. 74–78; Nicaragua para. 186; Nuclear Weapons paras. 64, 73; ILC Conclusions, 2018). (iii) “The general principles of law recognized by civilized nations” — the non liquet clause (Root–Phillimore drafting history; Chorzów Factory, Effect of Awards, Temple of Preah Vihear). (iv) “Judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists of the various nations, as subsidiary means for the determination of rules of law” — subject to Article 59 (The Paquete Habana; West Rand Central).

Why the list is not complete — six heads. (1) It omits the classical sources of obligation. Unilateral acts of States create obligations — Nuclear Tests (1974): a public declaration made with intent to be bound “is binding”; the ILC's Guiding Principles (2006) codify the requirements. (2) It omits the acts of international organisations. Security Council decisions bind by Charter Article 25 (confirmed in Namibia (1971), 13–2/11–4, holding that Article 25 is not confined to enforcement measures under Articles 41–42), and prevail over conflicting treaty obligations by Article 103 (Lockerbie, 1992). Art. 38 says nothing of them. (3) It omits General Assembly resolutions, which have “normative value” as evidence of practice and opinio juris (Nuclear Weapons, para. 70), may interpret the Charter authentically (the 1970 Friendly Relations Declaration), and in fields like decolonisation have proved decisive (Western Sahara; Chagos). (4) It omits jus cogens and Article 103. VCLT Articles 53 and 64 give peremptory norms a rank that Article 38 does not describe; the hierarchy is supplied from outside. (5) It omits equity ex aequo et bono, though Article 38(2) itself recognises the possibility — and the relation of Article 38(2) to the equitable principles applied in North Sea para. 88 is precisely a question about the completeness of Article 38(1). (6) It omits “soft law” and the law-making effect of practice. Codes of conduct, guidelines and declarations operate as evidence or as guides; the term “soft law” is descriptive, but its effects are real; and in some fields (humanitarian law, human-rights law, international environmental law) the treaty text is only the beginning of the normative process.

The counter-argument, which must be met. It is said that the list is complete because everything else is reducible: a General Assembly resolution is evidence relevant to custom; a Security Council decision creates obligations which are treaty-like because the Charter is a treaty; a unilateral declaration is a form of agreement; jus cogens is a higher-order custom. There is force in this. But the reduction loses something essential — the difference between a rule that binds because States have consented, and a rule that binds because an organ has decided; and the difference between a norm that can be contracted out of and one that cannot. The better view is that of the International Law Commission in its 2018 work on the identification of custom: custom, treaties and general principles are the three primary materials, and everything else operates through them — which is to say that Article 38 is accurate about the formal sources and silent about much of the substantive process.

Conclusion. Article 38 remains the starting point of every discussion of sources, and Article 38(1)(d)'s reference to “subsidiary means”, together with Article 59, shows that the drafters knew exactly how to mark degrees of authority. It is a complete statement of what the Court must apply; it is not, and was never meant to be, a complete statement of how international law is made. To that extent the question answers itself — and the examinable proposition is the famous sentence of the sources literature: the Article 38 list is a list of the formal sources, and the “real” sources lie in the practice, consent and shared expectations of States.

2015 Q.2 “What do you understand by ‘evidence of a general practice accepted as law’ in relation to international custom? Discuss how international courts have decided on the validity of international custom with the help of cases.”

Model answer.

Decode the phrase first. Article 38(1)(b) uses words which conceal a two-element test. “General practice” is the objective, material element — what States actually do. “Accepted as law” is the subjective, psychological element — opinio juris sive necessitatis, the belief that the practice is followed because it is legally obligatory. Strong's celebrated formula for a customary rule — “the general practice of nations” which has “so often been followed…that it has been converted into a rule of international law” — is the point of departure; and Starke's “usage is the twilight stage of custom” is the best image for the relationship between the two. Note also what the phrase does not require: it does not require universality, but “general” practice; it does not require uniform duration of a hundred years (North Sea, para. 74: “the passage of only a short period of time is not necessarily, or of itself, a bar”); and it does not require practice by every State, but practice that includes the States “whose interests are specially affected” (ILC Conclusion 8; North Sea, para. 73).

What counts as practice. Practice means conduct of the executive, legislative and judicial organs, and includes diplomatic correspondence, official statements and pleadings, legislative and administrative acts, judgments of national courts, military and naval manuals, votes on resolutions and explanations of vote, treaty-making conduct, and even omissions where the circumstances require action. ILC Conclusion 6 (2018) accepts a very wide category — “public statements made on behalf of States”, “official publications”, “government legal opinions” — so long as the conduct is attributable to the State and is capable of creating expectations.

How the courts have tested validity — five propositions with cases.

  1. Abstention proves custom only if it is duty-conscious. Lotus (PCIJ, 1927), p. 28: France relied on the rarity of prosecutions for collisions on the high seas; the Court held that “only if such abstention were based on their being conscious of having a duty to abstain would it be possible to speak of an international custom”. The Court also examined municipal decisions and found “no uniformity”. Lesson: negative practice requires proof of the duty-belief.
  2. The subjective element is indispensable. North Sea Continental Shelf (1969), paras. 74, 77–78: “Not only must the acts concerned amount to a settled practice, but they must also be such, or be carried out in such a way, as to be evidence of a belief that this practice is rendered obligatory by the existence of a rule of law requiring it… The States concerned must therefore feel that they are conforming to what amounts to a legal obligation”; the example is ceremonial and protocol acts, performed “almost invariably” but for courtesy, not law. The Court adopted Lotus “almost word for word, mutatis mutandis”.
  3. Practice need not be perfect, but it must be consistent. Nicaragua (1986), para. 186: “the Court does not consider that, for a rule to be established as customary, the corresponding practice must be in absolutely rigorous conformity with the rule”; it is enough that conduct is “generally consistent” with the rule, and contrary conduct is treated as a breach rather than as evidence of a new rule. But where the inconsistency is that of the very State invoking the rule — as with United States conduct in Nicaragua — the claim fails.
  4. Regional and local custom must be proved strictly. Asylum (1950): the party relying on custom must prove “a constant and uniform usage, accepted as Law”; the Court found “so much uncertainty and contradiction…that it is not possible to discern…any constant and uniform usage”, and even a regional rule could not be invoked against Peru, which had “repudiated” the Montevideo Conventions by not ratifying them. Right of Passage (1960) recognises that a local custom between two States is possible, but shows the second trap: a practice of requesting permission is “a negation of passage as of right”.
  5. Practice and opinio juris must converge — and the court will not supply one from the other. Nuclear Weapons (1996), paras. 64, 67, 73: practice (deployment, deterrence doctrines) and declarations (GA resolutions) pulled in opposite directions, so the Court found the custom unproved. The corollary is ILC Conclusion 3: the two elements are separate, and each must be established.

Burden of proof. The party asserting the custom bears the burden: Asylum, Right of Passage, and — on the related question of establishing the state of customary law where the parties disagree — Nicaragua, paras. 175–186, where the Court noticed the practice of States through the United Nations and through the opinio juris evidenced by resolutions. In Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries (1951) the Court placed the burden on the United Kingdom to show that the Norwegian straight-baseline method was contrary to law, and it failed.

Criticism and refinement (one paragraph, for the higher marks). The subjective element has been criticised as unverifiable (Kelsen's and Gény's analyses are invoked by the critics) and as capable of being manufactured from resolutions and statements; but the ICJ has never abandoned it, and the ILC's 2018 Conclusions confirm both elements while explaining (Conclusion 10) that evidence of acceptance as law may be found in “public statements, official publications, government legal opinions, diplomatic correspondence, decisions of national courts, treaty provisions and conduct in connection with resolutions adopted by an international organisation”. The practical consequence is that custom is now proved largely from documents, which has made the evidentiary task easier and the analytical task harder.

Conclusion. The phrase “evidence of a general practice accepted as law” therefore means a practice which is general in extent, including the specially affected States, consistent enough to show a settled norm, and accompanied by a belief in its legal necessity. Reduced to a formula for the examination: custom = practice + opinio juris, proved by the party asserting it, and opposable to all who have not persistently objected.

2013 Q.2 “Discuss the constituent elements of custom in international law with special reference to North Sea Continental Shelf Cases (1969).”

Model answer. Open with the definition — Article 38(1)(b) — and the two-element structure (practice and opinio juris), then devote the central part of the answer to North Sea (1969), which is the only judgment that sets out the conditions systematically. Take the judgment in four stages: first, paras. 74 and 77 — the settled practice and the belief in legal obligation (quote para. 77 in full); second, para. 73 on time, extent and the specially affected States; third, paras. 75–79 on the practice actually relied on by Denmark and the Netherlands (fifteen equidistance delimitations, over half the States concerned being parties to the 1958 Convention and therefore arguably applying the treaty, not custom; almost all the delimitations being between opposite States rather than adjacent States as here) — the classic lesson on the quality of the practice and the reasons behind it; fourth, paras. 70–72 on the passage of a treaty rule into custom (norm-creating character, widespread and representative participation, uniform practice, opinio juris) and paras. 62–66 on the reservations-clause test by which the Court held Article 6 to be at most de lege ferenda. 2012 Q.2 “What is the status of regional custom or local custom as a source of international law? Do you agree with the view that regional custom is part of international custom? Discuss with the help of cases decided by International Court of Justice.”

Model answer.

The starting point is the text. Article 38(1)(b) speaks of “international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law”. It says “general” and not “universal” — an adjective which in the drafting of the Statute and in the Court's practice has been read to permit rules of a regional, sub-regional or even bilateral scope, provided the practice and the acceptance are established as between the States concerned. This position is now codified in ILC Conclusion 16 (2018): “a rule of particular customary international law, whether regional, local or other, is a rule of customary international law that applies only among a limited number of States”; and it must be shown that the States in question accepted it as law — the general requirement of “widespread and representative” practice is replaced by the narrower requirement of acceptance by the States among whom the rule applies. So the answer to the second half of your question is, with one qualification, yes: regional custom is part of international custom, since it satisfies the same two elements and applies within a particular sphere; the qualification is that it is not general custom, and it cannot be invoked against a State that has not accepted or has repudiated it.

The authorisation and the trap — Asylum (1950). The Court accepted the possibility: “The Party which relies on a custom of this kind must prove that this custom is established in such a manner that it has become binding on the other Party” (pp. 276–277). But it then laid down two formidable requirements, and Colombia failed both. First, the practice must amount to “a constant and uniform usage practised by the States in question”, and the usage must be the expression of a right for the asylum State and a duty for the territorial State; the Court found “so much uncertainty and contradiction, so much fluctuation and discrepancy…and the practice has been so much influenced by considerations of political expediency…that it is not possible to discern…any constant and uniform usage, accepted as law” (p. 277). Secondly, even if such a custom existed among some Latin-American States, “it could not be invoked against Peru which, far from having by its attitude adhered to it, has, on the contrary, repudiated it by refraining from ratifying the Montevideo Conventions of 1933 and 1939”. The case therefore establishes the “opting out by non-participation” doctrine ante-litteram — the ancestor of the persistent-objector rule and of the requirement of opposability. Note also that the Court's refusal to accept the 1933 Convention, because Peru had not ratified it, rests on the same logic as the law of treaties: consent is the basis of obligation.

The counter-example — Right of Passage over Indian Territory (1960). Here the Court upheld the existence of a local custom between two States — Portugal and India — and rejected India's argument that “no local custom could be established between only two States”. The practice relied on was long-continued and accepted by both States as governing their relations in respect of the enclaves of Dadra and Nagar-Haveli; it covered the passage of private persons, civil officials and goods in general, which was free and unrestricted except for regulation. But the same method defeated Portugal on the categories that mattered: for armed forces, armed police, and arms and ammunition, permission had in practice always to be sought, and that “constitutes…a negation of passage as of right” — because the practice “predicates that the territorial sovereign had the discretionary power to withdraw or to refuse permission”. Hence the two-sidedness of local custom: it may exist, it may be limited to particular categories, and it fails where the practice shows that each occasion required fresh consent.

Other authorities. Asylum and Right of Passage are the two decided cases; but the wider practice is instructive. The Nicaragua judgment (1986) considered regional and sub-regional practice as part of the assessment of custom; Pulp Mills (2010) analysed whether a regional instrument had generated custom; and the ICJ in North Sea and in the Gulf of Maine chamber decisions treated regional usage in the North Atlantic and North Sea as relevant but not dispositive. On the African and Latin-American practice, the Organisation of African Unity's resolutions on apartheid and decolonisation and the Latin-American law of asylum have produced regional standards which the Court has invoked as evidence of the general law (Namibia; Western Sahara). The ILC's Conclusion 16 caps the development by stating the rule in general terms.

Evaluation and conclusion. Two objections are worth stating, because they earn marks. First, regional custom can fragment the law, generating incompatible duties for a State that belongs to several regions (the classic problem of a State that is both a member of the African Union and of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation). Secondly, the proof requirement is so exacting that in practice regional custom is very rarely established — Asylum failed, and only Right of Passage (a bilateral custom, and a partial one) has succeeded. The correct conclusion is therefore nuanced: regional and local custom are recognised in the scheme of Article 38(1)(b) and confirmed by the ILC; they must be proved strictly as between the States concerned; and they bind only those States that have accepted them.

2002 Q.2(a) “The word ‘Source’ is one of the most common but misunderstood terms in the jargon of international law. There is a confusing distinction between sources, referring to what the law is and where it may be found. In the light of the above write a brief overview of the context and content of sources of the international law.”

Model answer (structure). Distinguish the four senses of “source” — formal (the law-creating process which gives a rule its legal validity: Article 38(1)(a)–(c)), material (the content and the raw material: State practice, the demands of the international community, morality and policy), historical (the origins of a rule) and evidentiary (where the law may be found: the Paquete Habana formula). 2001 Q.1/2 “…evaluate the contribution of custom and treaties as principal sources of international law. Do you think that they need to be supplemented by other sources as well?”

Model answer (structure). Part I — custom. Its historical primacy (custom was the principal source before codification; the classical writers built the law of nations from usage); its merits (it is democratic — it follows the practice of all States; it is flexible; it evolves); its defects (proving it is difficult: Lotus, Asylum, Right of Passage; its content is uncertain; the persistent objector fractures it; slow formation in a fast-changing world). Part II — treaties. Their modern dominance: nearly every field of international law now has its convention — the UN Charter, the Geneva Conventions on the law of the sea and their successor UNCLOS, the VCLT, the VCDR and VCCR, the human-rights covenants, the Genocide Convention, the Rome Statute, the climate regime; the merits (precision, deliberateness, reciprocal commitments, dispute-settlement machinery, the capacity to codify custom); the defects (treaties bind only the parties (VCLT Art. 34); reservations narrow commitments; unequal bargaining power; the problem of non-compliance where enforcement is diffuse); and the crucial modern fact that the two sources are not rivals but converts of one another — the three modes of treaty-to-custom (North Sea, paras. 70–74; Nicaragua on parallel existence). Part III — do they need supplementing? Yes, in three respects: generality (general principles supply rules where neither custom nor treaty speaks — res judicata, estoppel, equity); obligation (Security Council decisions under Art. 25, and Art. 103's hierarchy, do work that no treaty can; Namibia, Lockerbie); and continuity (advisory opinions, resolutions and unilateral declarations fill the intervals between codification and practice — Reparation, Nuclear Weapons, Chagos). Part IV — the answer to the question. The modern tendency is often described as a movement “from custom to treaty” and then “from treaty back to custom” (the criminalisation of aggression and genocide; the prohibition of torture; jus cogens). The correct conclusion is therefore that custom and treaties remain the principal sources, but that they are not self-sufficient: general principles and the acts of international organisations are structural supplements, and the ranking rules (jus cogens, Art. 103) are the visible hierarchy above them.

2000 Q.3 “‘Custom as a source of international law is essentially based upon crystallization of state practices as binding rules. However, the important issue hereto has been how to distinguish behaviour undertaken because of law from behaviour undertaken because of a whole series of other reasons ranging from goodwill to pique.’ … In the light of the above, identify the tests upheld by judicial practice to support the binding character of a state practice.”

Model answer (headings). (1) The problem stated: Vattel's distinction between courtesy and obligation; the “usage versus custom” distinction (Starke's “twilight stage”). (2) Why the problem matters: the same physical conduct may be performed for courtesy (North Sea's ceremonial acts), for reciprocity, for political expediency (Asylum), for convenience, or from a sense of legal obligation. (3) The tests developed by courts: generality of the practice (North Sea para. 74); uniformity and consistency, allowing for minor deviations (Nicaragua para. 186); duration, which is relevant but not decisive (North Sea paras. 73–74); specially affected States (North Sea para. 73; ILC Conclusion 8); opinio juris — the belief in legal obligation (Lotus p. 28; North Sea paras. 77–78; Nuclear Weapons paras. 64, 73); the character of the practice — whether it is capable of generating rules (acts of comity cannot, treaty-based practice may: the reservations-clause test of North Sea paras. 63–66); and the reaction of other States — protest, acquiescence, or persistent objection (ILC Conclusion 15). (4) How the belief is proved in practice: statements of States, pleadings, treaty preambles, resolutions and their explanations of vote, national legislation with declaratory language, and the internal documents of governments (ILC Conclusion 10). (5) The critique — the subjective element is unverifiable and sometimes a fiction; the reply — that its function is to exclude practices which are obviously political or courteous, and that without it custom would be indistinguishable from habit. (6) Conclusion — the judicial tests are cumulative, the burden lies on the claimant, and the passage of a practice from courtesy to custom can be tracked precisely through the case law from Lotus to Nuclear Weapons.

1998 Q.2(b) “Discuss the tests laid down in the Continental Shelf Cases (1969 ICJ 3) to prove that a provision, after its incorporation in a treaty, has developed into a rule of Customary International Law.” 1999 Q.8(b) Short note — Right of Passage Case (1960).

1998 Q.2(b) — model answer (numbered tests, as the question demands). (1) The norm-creating character of the provision (para. 72): the rule must be capable of standing as a general rule of law; the Court thought Article 6 doubtful because it subordinated equidistance to a prior agreement and because the “special circumstances” exception left the content unsettled. (2) The reservations test (paras. 63–66): if the article is one to which reservations are permitted, it is presumptively not declaratory of custom, since custom, “by its very nature, must have equal force for all members of the international community”; the test is a brilliant, mechanical way of reading the drafters' own view of which articles were declaratory (here Articles 1–3, excluded from reservation). (3) Participation (para. 73): widespread and representative participation, including States “whose interests were specially affected”, may suffice even without the passage of long time; the number of ratifications (respectable but not sufficient) and the possible indifference of non-parties are to be weighed. (4) Extensive and virtually uniform practice (para. 74) of the provision invoked, over a period sufficient for the purpose, with the practice of specially affected States included. (5) Opinio juris (paras. 77–78): the practice must show “a general recognition that a rule of law or legal obligation is involved”; the Court adopted Lotus p. 28 “almost word for word, mutatis mutandis”. (6) The quality and relevance of the instances relied on (paras. 75–79): the practice must have occurred in circumstances comparable to those of the State against which the rule is invoked (here, lateral delimitations between adjacent States — of which the Court found only one — as opposed to median lines between opposite States); and where parties were already bound by the treaty, their conduct may evidence the treaty, not custom. (7) The subsequent conduct of objecting States (paras. 80–82): Germany had expressly declined to ratify and had conducted itself on the “just and equitable share” basis, and could not be bound by a rule to which it had never assented. Conclude that these seven tests — the fourth, fifth and sixth being the operative ones — are the standard by which any claim of “treaty-to-custom” is now judged, and that they explain why the Court refused on the facts while accepting the possibility in principle.

1999 Q.8(b) — model short note on Right of Passage (1960). “In the Right of Passage over Indian Territory case (Portugal v. India, ICJ Rep. 1960 p. 6) the Court decided for the first time that a local custom between two States may be a source of mutual rights and obligations. Portugal claimed a right of passage between the coastal district of Daman and its enclaves of Dadra and Nagar-Haveli, and between the enclaves, to the extent necessary for the exercise of its sovereignty. The Court held that the Maratha Treaty of Poona of 1779 and the sanads of 1783–85 granted only a revenue tenure (jagir/saranjam), not sovereignty; but that Portuguese sovereignty over the villages had been recognised in fact by the British and tacitly by India, so that passage practice between the parties became the source of rights. On the practice, the Court found free passage for private persons, civil officials and goods in general, subject to regulation, but held that for armed forces, armed police and arms and ammunition prior authorisation had always been required — which ‘constitutes…a negation of passage as of right’, because it ‘predicates that the territorial sovereign had the discretionary power to withdraw or to refuse permission’. India's refusal of passage in July 1954, in the tension following the revolt in Dadra, was therefore justified. The case contributes three propositions: local custom between two States is possible under Article 38(1)(b); custom may be limited in content (covering some categories of passage and not others); and a practice requiring fresh permission on each occasion negates a right of passage.”

1994 Q.1 & 1993 Q.5 “…Clearly state the sources formally referred to in Article 38 of the Statute of ICJ and also why International Custom as an evidence of State practice has been relegated to the second position instead of the first one which it enjoyed for centuries together?”

Model answer.

Part I — the formal sources. Article 38(1) lists (a) international conventions, (b) international custom, (c) general principles of law, and (d) judicial decisions and teachings as subsidiary means; Article 38(2) preserves the possibility of a decision ex aequo et bono with the parties' consent. Explain each with the cases and the modern qualifications: conventions (VCLT Art. 2(1)(a); law-making versus contract treaties; North Sea and Nicaragua on the relation to custom); custom (practice plus opinio juris; Lotus, North Sea, Asylum, Right of Passage, Nicaragua, Nuclear Weapons; ILC Conclusions 2018); general principles (Chorzów Factory, Effect of Awards, Temple); subsidiary means (Article 59; Paquete Habana; West Rand Central). Then note that the list is formal only, and the further materials (unilateral acts, GA and SC resolutions, jus cogens, soft law).

Part II — why custom stands second. Six reasons, each of which is really a point about the deficiencies of custom that the drafters of 1920 set out to cure. (1) The preference for certainty. A convention is written, deliberate and dated; custom is unwritten, gradual and proved by inference. The whole movement of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — the Hague Conferences, the many codification conferences, the establishment of a standing court — was a movement away from inference and towards text. (2) The priority of express consent. In the positivist intellectual climate of 1920, obligation rested on consent; a convention is consent in its most explicit form, whereas custom rests on “implied” consent and is correspondingly weaker in the eyes of the drafters. (3) The difficulty of proof. As the case law shows, custom must be proved by the party asserting it: Lotus (p. 28 — abstention proves custom only if duty-conscious, and no uniformity could be found in municipal decisions), Asylum (1950 — “so much uncertainty and contradiction…it is not possible to discern…any constant and uniform usage”), North Sea (1969 — the practice relied on was “inconclusive, and insufficient to bear the weight sought to be put upon them”). A court charged with deciding disputes needs materials it can apply with confidence, and it was natural to put the conventions first. (4) The law-making treaty as the successor to custom. The draftsmen of Article 38 lived through the beginnings of codification (the Hague Regulations, the Geneva Conventions of 1929, the Covenant of the League); they expected general conventions to take over the function custom had performed — an expectation realised after 1945 in the VCLT, VCDR, UNCLOS and the human-rights covenants. (5) The speed of change. Custom cannot keep pace with technical and political change — aviation, radio, nuclear energy, cyber operations; treaties can be negotiated in months. (6) The democratic objection. A rule derived from the practice of the powerful, in a world of unequal States, was suspected of privileging the great powers; treaties, whatever their content, are at least formally the acts of all the parties. But — and this is the conclusion that earns the marks — the hierarchy is one of convenience, not of authority. Article 38(1) says the Court “shall apply” the four heads; it does not rank conventions above custom in force. Custom remains binding on non-parties; it binds new States; it operates where treaties are silent (Nicaragua on the parallel existence of treaty and custom); and treaty provisions themselves pass into custom (North Sea). The modern practice has in fact reversed the older pessimism: custom has been used to fill the gaps of the Charter (Nicaragua), to give content to decolonisation (Western Sahara; Chagos) and to discipline the use of force (Nuclear Weapons). The order in Article 38 is a drafting order, not a rank of legal force.

1992 Q.8(a) & 2000 Q.8 / 2002 Q.8(a) “Place of resolutions and decisions of the General Assembly and Security Council amongst the sources of international law”; “Resolutions of General Assembly as a source of international law.”

Model answer (structure, and the point of difference between the organs). (1) Article 38 does not mention them; therefore they are not formal sources. (2) The constitutional asymmetry: General Assembly recommendations (Charter Arts. 10–14) versus Security Council decisions (Arts. 24, 25, 27(3), Chapter VII, Art. 103). (3) General Assembly: Nuclear Weapons (1996) paras. 70–73 — normative value; content and conditions of adoption; opinio juris; series of resolutions; the five weight-factors of Verma; the four uses (declaratory, law-accelerating, interpretive, and binding in narrow internal cases); the Sixth Committee's 1974 position; and the implementing examples — Res. 1514 (XV) and 1541 (XV) in Western Sahara and Chagos, Res. 2625 (XXV) in Nicaragua, Res. 73/295 giving effect to the Chagos opinion. (4) Security Council: Namibia (1971) — Article 25 is not limited to Articles 41–42, decisions are binding on all Members, and the illegal situation is opposable erga omnes; Kosovo (2010) — resolutions are to be interpreted with reference to Articles 31–32 VCLT as modified by the Council's own drafting and voting process, and their addressees determined case by case (paras. 85, 94, 117); Lockerbie (1992) — the Art. 103 supremacy point; and the “legislative” resolutions (1373 (2001), 1540 (2004)) together with the due-process critique (Kadi) and the Ombudsperson created by Res. 1904 (2009). (5) Conclusion: resolutions and decisions are not sources in the Article 38 sense, but Security Council decisions create obligations of treaty quality and may prevail over treaties (Art. 103), while General Assembly resolutions are evidence of practice and opinio juris, authentic interpretations, and — in the field of decolonisation — the standard against which legality has been measured. See §2.2.1–2.2.2 and Cases 8–12 above.

1992 Q.3, 1995 Q.5 & 1991 Q.8(a) “Article 38(1)(c)…the expression itself is vague…What is your opinion?”; “…Positivists do not consider it as an independent source…majority…general principles of justice as distinguished from any particular systems of jurisprudence”; short note — “General principles of law recognised by civilized States as the source of international law.”

Model answer (structure). Because the 2025 paper asked the same question, the full answer is given at Group A above; for the 1990s version add three things the older examiners liked. First, the jurisprudential framing: set out the natural-law position (Grotius, Pufendorf, Vattel — the law of nature as the source of the law of nations; Verdross and Le Fur among the moderns) against the positivist position (Anzilotti, Tunkin, and the objection that a “principle” invoked in a gap is judicial legislation). Secondly, the evidence from the Court that the category is used with restraint: South West Africa (1966) refusing to recognise actio popularis as a general principle; South West Africa and Barcelona Traction declining to extend jus standi; Texaco v. Libya (1977) refusing to treat the French law of administrative contracts as a general principle; and, on the other side, the principles the Court has applied — res judicata (Effect of Awards, 1954); error and acquiescence (Temple of Preah Vihear, 1962); equity and good faith (Meuse, 1937; North Sea, 1969; Gulf of Maine, 1984); reparation and restitutio in integrum (Chorzów Factory, 1928); and “elementary considerations of humanity” (Corfu Channel, 1949). Thirdly, the modernisation of the clause: “civilised nations” is now read as “the community of nations” (the Court's own practice and the Revised Rules of 1972), and the ILC's work on peremptory norms and on custom treats general principles as one of the three primary materials. Submit, in the end, the Starke/Waldock view: the clause is “a flexible element” designed to prevent non liquet, and it is the Court's fidelity to the requirement of generality — not the clause itself — that answers Tunkin.

1991 Q.4 “International custom has been provided as a formal source of international law under Article 38(1)(b)…Since it is a matter of fact whether a particular practice has acquired the status of Customary law, what problems are encountered in relying on this provision; specifically what has to be proved?”

Model answer (the best “problems” question in the corpus — answer it under five heads).

(1) The problem of the elements. What has to be proved is two things: a general practice, and its acceptance as law (Article 38(1)(b); North Sea paras. 74, 77–78; ILC Conclusions 2, 3, 9). The first is difficult because practice must be general and must include specially affected States (ILC Conclusion 8), and because conduct may be attributable to a State yet not constitute practice; the second is difficult because intention is invisible and must be inferred from documents (ILC Conclusion 10).

(2) The problem of distinguishing law from other motives. The central difficulty stated in Verma and in the case law: conduct may be based on courtesy, comity, reciprocity, political expediency, habit or convenience. North Sea's ceremonial acts and Asylum's “considerations of political expediency” are the two authorities; Lotus p. 28 supplies the rule.

(3) The problem of time and change. How long? — no fixed period (North Sea para. 74), but the shorter the practice, the stronger the evidence of opinio juris must be (Bin Cheng's “instant custom” and the space-law example of Res. 1963 (XVII) and the Outer Space Treaty 1967). And how does a custom change? — by the emergence of a new practice with new opinio juris, in which the identification of the “first breach” is contested (the debate over humanitarian intervention).

(4) The problem of the objector and the newcomer. The persistent objector rule (ILC Conclusion 15; Asylum's reasoning on Peru's non-ratification) means the rule may not be opposable to some States; and new States are bound by existing general custom (ILC Conclusion 3 and the burden discussed in Conclusion 4) but may inherit particular customs only if the circumstances are unchanged (the “clean slate” debate).

(5) The problems of proof and of precedent. The burden lies on the claimant (Asylum; Right of Passage); evidence is scattered and often diplomatic; and, as the Court has warned in Nicaragua, reliance on practice that is itself contested may turn into reliance on the Court's own earlier statements. To these the modern critics add the charge of Eurocentrism — that the “practice” that mattered historically was the practice of a handful of maritime and colonial powers (Anand, Chimni) — and the reply that the same process has since produced decolonisation, self-determination and human rights (1514 (XV); Namibia; Chagos).

Conclusion. What has to be proved, in one sentence: a settled and sufficiently general practice of States, including those specially affected, followed from a sense of legal obligation, consistent notwithstanding minor departures, and not objected to by the State against which it is invoked. The problems are real, but they are the problems of proof, not of existence; and in the last forty years the Court has shown — in Nicaragua, Nuclear Weapons, Chagos — that they can be overcome.

Appendix F — Examination technique and the traps

Timing and structure
  • 20 marks = 25–30 minutes = 900–1,200 words. Spend 4 minutes on a skeleton; never start writing without one.
  • Structure: Introduction (state the provision and your answer in two sentences) → Headings that mirror the question → Authorities with one-line holdings → Counter-argument → Conclusion with a firm submission.
  • Answer the question actually asked: if it says “with special reference to North Sea”, the North Sea tests must occupy the middle half of the answer.
  • Quote sparingly but exactly: Article 38(1)(b), Article 25, Article 59, Lotus p. 28, North Sea para. 77, and Nuclear Weapons para. 70 are the six quotations that cover the whole topic.
The eight traps
  • Conflating equity with ex aequo et bono. Equity as a general principle (Art. 38(1)(c)) is infra legem; Art. 38(2) permits a decision outside the law, but only by consent — North Sea para. 88.
  • Saying decisions are binding. Art. 59 forbids it; say instead that they have great persuasive authority and may, where no rule exists, be the effective source.
  • Treating a GA resolution as binding. It is a recommendation; its force is evidentiary, interpretive or, in narrow internal cases, constitutional.
  • Forgetting the second element of custom. Practice alone is usage; add opinio juris, and show how it is proved.
  • Assuming custom binds everybody. Check the persistent objector (ILC Conclusion 15) and the possibility of regional or local custom (Conclusion 16).
  • Treating Article 38 as a hierarchy. The order is a drafting order; Art. 103 and jus cogens supply the only true hierarchy.
  • Citing a case without its proposition. Whenever you name a case, say what it decided — in one clause.
  • Ignoring the facts of a problem question. In scenario questions, apply the source-identification tree (Fig. 2.6) step by step to the facts given.
