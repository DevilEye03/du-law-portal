00OrientationHow this document is built & what to read first

Section 14 is one sentence long, and it is the most important sentence in the Act after the charging section itself. It says that all income, for the purpose of charge and for the purpose of computation, falls under one of five heads — and nothing else. Everything that follows in the statute (ss. 15 to 59) is really an elaborate set of instructions about which door a receipt walks through, and what deductions are available once it is inside. This document treats the topic the way an examiner expects it: the architecture of s. 14 → whether the heads are mutually exclusive (the question the syllabus asks in terms) → each head, its charging section and its computation rules → the fifteen prescribed cases in full → the Dec-2024 paper answered → a revision sheet.

The single most important idea in the topic

Classification is not an aesthetic exercise; it decides money. The head determines (i) whether the receipt is taxable at all, (ii) which deductions are allowed, (iii) in whose hands it is taxed, and (iv) which set-off and carry-forward rules apply. That is why the battles in the prescribed cases are always about entry into a head, not about arithmetic: whether a managing director's waived commission is "salary" or "business" (Ram Pershad), whether rent from shops in a market is "house property" or "business" (East India Housing), whether interest from an evacuee hotel is chargeable on the "owner" (Jodhamal Kuthiala), whether an irrecoverable advance is a "bad debt" or a "capital loss" (B.D. Bharucha, Mysore Sugar), and whether loom hours are an "asset" or an "operating cost" (Empire Jute).

01FrameworkSection 14 — the architecture of the charge

The Income-tax Act does not tax "income" in the abstract. It taxes income classified. Section 14 is the section that performs the classification, and ss. 15 to 59 are the working-out of it. An answer to any problem in this topic that does not begin by identifying the head is, in the examiner's eyes, an answer that has not begun.

◆1.1 The text, and the six slots it creates

Section 14. Heads of income. — Save as otherwise provided by this Act, all income shall, for the purposes of charge of income-tax and computation of total income, be classified under the following heads of income —

A.Salariesss. 15–17
B.Interest on securities — omitted by the Finance Act, 1988, w.e.f. 1-4-1989formerly s. 18
C.Income from house propertyss. 22–27
D.Profits and gains of business or professionss. 28–44
E.Capital gainsss. 45–55
F.Income from other sourcesss. 56–59

In the Income-tax Act, 2025 the same rule is s. 13, and the five heads occupy ss. 15–19 (salaries), 20–25 (house property), 26–66 (business or profession), 67–93 (capital gains) and 94–96 (other sources).

Three things about the section deserve to be noticed at once, because each of them is an exam point.

1 · "Save as otherwise provided"

The classification is subject to the rest of the Act. So an express exemption (ss. 10–13A), a special rate provision (ss. 111A, 112, 112A), a presumptive scheme (ss. 44AD, 44ADA, 44AE) or a special charge (s. 115JB) can displace the ordinary head-wise computation. Classification tells you the normal route; it does not override a specific provision.

2 · "For the purposes of charge … and computation"

The heads operate at two stages. At the stage of charge, the question is whether the receipt is taxable at all and under which head. At the stage of computation, the head fixes the arithmetic — which deductions are allowed and which are not (30% of annual value under s. 24(a); nothing except s. 57 items under "other sources"; s. 37(1) under business).

3 · "All income shall be classified"

There is no residuary unclassified category. If a receipt is income and is not exempt, it must be placed in one of the five. "Other sources" is the residuary head, but it is still a head, with its own charging section (s. 56) and its own computation rules (ss. 57–59).

◆1.2 Why classify at all? The rationale of s. 14

The syllabus asks for the rationale of the heads, and a full answer gives five reasons. They are not decoration: each one explains a concrete rule.

Five functions served by the head-wise classification
FunctionWhat the head doesWhere you see it in the statute
1 · It identifies the charging provision The head tells you which section imposes the charge and therefore which conditions must be satisfied before anything is taxed. Rent is not taxed because it is "income"; it is taxed because s. 22 says so, and only if the property is owned, not occupied for business, and its annual value is ascertainable. ss. 15, 22, 28, 45, 56 — each opens with the words "shall be chargeable". East India Housing (§4.8) turns on precisely this.
2 · It fixes the computation machinery Each head has its own permitted deductions. Salary gets three (s. 16); property gets two (s. 24); business gets the whole of ss. 30–37; capital gains gets ss. 48–55; other sources gets s. 57 and nothing else. A receipt wrongly classified does not merely change the label — it changes the deductions, and therefore the tax.
3 · It fixes the unit and the year of assessment Head-wise computation produces head-wise income or loss. Only head-wise results are aggregated (s. 5 read with s. 288A) into gross total income, and only head-wise losses are set off under ss. 70–80. s. 71 (intra-head), s. 72 (inter-head, business), s. 74 (capital gains), s. 78 (carry-forward and change in constitution).
4 · It allocates income between persons Several heads are taxed on the person entitled to the income or the person owning the source, not merely the person who receives it. "Owner" in s. 22 and "salary due" in s. 15 are examples. Jodhamal Kuthiala (§4.9) is entirely about who the "owner" is; L.W. Russel (§3.2) is about when salary is "due".
5 · It separates the rates Some heads attract special rates: long-term capital gains are taxed at a flat rate under ss. 112/112A instead of slab rates; winnings from lotteries under s. 115BB. Classification therefore drives the rate as well as the base. ss. 111A, 112, 112A, 115BB, 115BBH (virtual digital assets).
The clean way to state the rationale

"The heads of income are a device of legislative classification, adopted for the convenience of computation and for the orderly application of the charging and deduction provisions. They are not a taxonomy of economic sources but a set of computational compartments; and because each compartment has its own charging section and its own deductions, the classification of a receipt determines not merely the form but the quantum of the charge." That sentence, with East India Housing cited for the proposition that the heads are mutually exclusive, will answer any "rationale" question in full.

◆1.3 Are the heads mutually exclusive? — the question in terms

This is the exact question the syllabus asks, and it is the single most examined idea in the topic. The answer has to be given at three levels, because the short answer ("yes") is true but incomplete.

(a) Level one — one receipt, one head

Each item of income is chargeable under one and only one head. If it falls within a specific head, the fact that it might also be described by another head does not permit the Revenue to tax it under the latter. The Supreme Court stated the rule in East India Housing and Land Development Trust Ltd. v. CIT (reader p. 98) in words that should be memorised:

"The propositions laid down in these decided cases are these: (1) that the several heads of income enumerated in s. 6 of the Income-tax Act are mutually exclusive, and each head of income has a definite rule provided for computation of the income; and (2) that if the income from a source falls within a specific head set out in s. 6, the fact that it may indirectly be covered by another head will not make the income taxable under the latter head." CIT, West Bengal v. East India Housing & Land Development Trust Ltd., 42 ITR 49 (SC) — reader p. 99

(b) Level two — "mutually exclusive" has three different meanings, and only one of them is absolute

Three senses of "mutual exclusivity" — keep them apart in the answer
SenseRuleConsequence
As between receipts Absolute. A single receipt cannot be taxed under two heads. Double taxation of the same income is not permitted. If the Revenue's case is that rent is business income, it must give up the s. 22 route; it cannot take 30% under s. 24(a) and claim the same sum as business income.
As between assessees Not absolute. The same amount may be taxed in two hands at different stages — company and shareholder, firm and partner, HUF and member — because they are different persons and the second charge is on a different source. Bacha F. Guzdar (Topic 2) and N. Bagavathy Ammal (§7.8) illustrate this: the company's agricultural character does not travel to the shareholder, and the shareholder's receipt is a fresh taxable event.
As between years Not absolute. The same receipt may be taxed in a later year if the statute so directs (s. 45(1A) insurance; s. 25A arrears of rent; s. 45(5) compensation). These are statutory exceptions, not breaches of the rule; each is expressly enacted.

(c) Level three — the situs rule: the specific head prevails over the general

Where a receipt is capable of falling under two heads, the more specific head attracts it. The principle comes from the Privy Council in Fry v. Salisbury House Estate Ltd., LR (1930) AC 432 — a case about whether income from a building let out with a "water rate" and other services was property income or trade income — and from the Supreme Court in United Commercial Bank Ltd. v. CIT, 1958 SCR 79, both of which are discussed in East India Housing at reader p. 99. The practical hierarchies are:

Salariesprevails over business / other sources where the relation is master & servant
→
House propertyprevails over business where the income is from bare letting of a building, even if the building is used in a trade
→
Businessprevails where the letting is itself the business (composite rent, hiring of machinery, exploitation of an asset as stock-in-trade)

Other sourcesstrictly residuary — s. 56(1): "income of every kind which is not… chargeable under any of the heads A to E"
The trap in the phrase "mutually exclusive"

Mutual exclusivity operates within a single assessment, on a single receipt, and in a single capacity. It does not mean that an assessee can have only one head of income (Mr. JAAM in the Dec-2024 paper has five), nor that a receipt cannot change its head when the underlying facts change (a house let out becomes business income if the assessee's business is letting; a plot held as stock-in-trade never enters s. 22 at all). The question is always: what is the character of the income in the hands of this assessee, from this source, in this year?

◆1.4 Head B — the head that no longer exists

The syllabus expressly requires the student to state that Head B, "Interest on securities", was omitted by the Finance Act, 1988 with effect from 1 April 1989. The story is worth two paragraphs, because examiners ask "what happened to Head B and where did its income go?"

What Head B used to do

From 1922 until the late 1980s, interest on securities — primarily interest on Government securities, debentures and other securities — was a separate head with its own charging section (s. 18 of the 1961 Act), its own computation rule and its own history of exemptions. It was a head because, in the colonial and early-republican scheme, interest on Government paper was regarded as a distinct species of income, taxed at source and largely on a gross basis.

The Finance Act, 1986 first removed the substantive machinery: s. 18 was omitted with effect from 1 April 1987 (AY 1987-88) and interest on securities was made chargeable under the head "Income from other sources" (s. 56(2)(id)), and, where the securities were held as stock-in-trade, under "Profits and gains of business or profession".

What the Finance Act, 1988 did

The Finance Act, 1988 then deleted the head itself from s. 14 with effect from 1 April 1989, so that from AY 1989-90 the statute recognises five heads, not six. The result is purely presentational but practically important: the same receipt now enters through a different door, and with a different set of deductions.

Consequence today: interest on securities is business income under s. 28 if the securities are held as stock-in-trade by a dealer (banks, financial institutions, traders) and other-sources income under s. 56(2) if the securities are held as investments. Either way it is fully taxable; the difference is that a dealer claims the whole of ss. 30–37, while an investor claims only s. 57.

Older authorities still say "Head B"

Because the cases in this reader were decided between 1955 and 2007, you will see references to "interest on securities" as a separate head in judgments and commentaries. Modernise them in the answer: "…as the law stood before the head was omitted by the Finance Act, 1988 w.e.f. 1-4-1989; such interest is now taxable as business income or under other sources as the case may be." One clause, and the examiner knows you know the amendment.

◆1.5 Where the heads overlap in practice — and s. 14A

Mutual exclusivity is a rule about the same receipt. Real assessees hold several sources, and the statute handles the interface in four ways.

The four interfaces and how the Act resolves them
InterfaceThe problemHow the Act / courts resolve it
Property ⟷ Business Premises let out by a company whose business is letting; shops in a market; a factory building let to a sister concern; a mall developed and let. If the letting is exploitation of a commercial asset in the course of the assessee's business, the income is business income (Sultan Brothers line); if it is bare letting of a building as such, it is s. 22 income even for a company (East India Housing). Composite rent for amenities and services goes with the business head.
Salary ⟷ Business / Other sources A managing director's commission; a director's fee; a partner's remuneration; pension to a widow of a former employee. The employer–employee relation decides. Capital gains ⟷ Business Shares, land or debentures held sometimes as investment and sometimes as stock-in-trade. Section 14A: no deduction is allowed for expenditure incurred in relation to income that does not form part of total income. Rule 8D prescribes the mechanical computation. This is the price of having exempt income inside a taxable entity.

Section 14A — the anti-overlap rule

s. 14A(1): For the purposes of computing the total income under Chapter IV, no deduction shall be allowed in respect of expenditure incurred by the assessee in relation to income which does not form part of the total income under this Act.

s. 14A(2)–(3): If the Assessing Officer is not satisfied with the correctness of the claim of the assessee in respect of such expenditure, he shall determine the amount in accordance with the rules; Rule 8D prescribes a three-part formula (interest not directly attributable; 1% of the annual average of monthly averages of the value of investments yielding exempt income; and half a per cent in the current rule for cases where no expenditure is proved).

Why it belongs in this topic: s. 14A is one of the few places where the Act acknowledges that the heads are not hermetically sealed — expenses of a business may in fact be incurred for earning income under another head, or for earning no income at all. The provision is a reminder that the heads operate inside a single computation of total income, not as separate taxpayers. Section 14A is not dealt with in the DU reader; it is included here because it is the modern completion of the s. 14 scheme.

The one-line answer to "are the heads mutually exclusive?"

"Yes — as between receipts. Section 14 allots each item of income to a single head, each head has its own charging section and its own computation rule, and if a receipt falls squarely within a specific head the Revenue cannot tax it under another head merely because it might indirectly be covered by that other head (East India Housing). But the exclusivity is not a rule of persons or of years: the same amount may be taxed again in another hand, and express provisions such as ss. 25A, 45(1A) and 45(5) tax a receipt in a year later than the one in which it arose."

02Head ASalaries — sections 15 to 17

Salary is the first head and, in the statute, the simplest: a single charging section (s. 15), a small set of definitions (s. 17), and three deductions (s. 16). All the difficulty lies elsewhere — in deciding whether the relationship is one of employment at all (which is what Ram Pershad is about), and in deciding whether a particular benefit has become the employee's property or is still contingent (which is what L.W. Russel is about).

◆2.1 Chargeability — section 15

Section 15. The following income shall be chargeable to income-tax under the head "Salaries" —

(a)any salary due from an employer or a former employer to an assessee in the previous year, whether paid or not;
(b)any salary paid or allowed to him in the previous year by or on behalf of an employer or a former employer though not due or before it became due to him;
(c)any arrears of salary paid or allowed to him in the previous year by or on behalf of an employer or a former employer, if not charged to income-tax for any earlier previous year.

Explanation 1 — salary paid in advance, once taxed, is not taxed again when it falls due. Explanation 2 — salary, bonus, commission or remuneration received by a partner from his firm is not salary; s. 28(v) makes it business income.

Due or paid — whichever is earlier

The charge is on accrual, but advance receipts are caught too. The result is that the year of tax is the earlier of the year of accrual and the year of receipt, and the Explanation prevents a second charge. This "due or paid, whichever is earlier" formula is what makes Ram Pershad a salary case: the commission accrued when the accounts were passed, and the waiver came after accrual.

"Salary" from more than one employer

Salary from two employers in the same year is aggregated under the same head. There is one standard deduction under s. 16(ia) against the aggregate, not one per employer. Where the taxpayer has changed jobs, Form 12B is filed so that the second employer may deduct tax on the aggregate.

Former employer

Pension, gratuity, leave encashment, arrears and profits in lieu received after retirement are taxable under s. 15 because the section expressly covers a "former employer". The relationship survives the employment for this purpose — which is why s. 17(3)(i) and (ii) speak of "employer or former employer".

The two conditions precedent to any salary charge

(1) There must be an employer–employee relationship (master and servant) between the payer and the payee, subsisting at the time the remuneration was earned; and (2) the amount must be receivable qua employee — i.e. by reason of the employment and not in some independent capacity. Both conditions are examined at length in Ram Pershad (§3.1). If either fails, the receipt is not salary, however it is described in the contract or in the accounts.

◆2.3 Allowances — the three-way classification

An allowance is a fixed sum paid to meet a particular expense of the employee. Whether it is taxed depends entirely on whether the statute grants an exemption, and how much of the actual expenditure the statute is prepared to assume. Two standard traps in allowance questions

(i) HRA is computed on "salary", which for s. 10(13A) means basic + dearness allowance (if the terms of employment so provide) + commission as a fixed percentage of turnover — not the whole of the salary. (ii) Where the employee lives in his own house, no HRA is exempt — there is no rent paid, so the second limb of the "least of" test is zero. But if he pays rent to his wife or mother, the claim can succeed if the arrangement is genuine and the owner declares the rent; the Department examines the genuineness, not the relationship.

The two provisos that keep medical and other benefits out of the charge

Medical facilities

Under the proviso to s. 17(2): (i) the value of medical facilities provided to the employee or his family in a hospital maintained by the employer, or in a hospital maintained by the Government or a prescribed institution, is not a perquisite; (ii) reimbursement of medical expenses up to ₹15,000 in a year for treatment in a recognised hospital is excluded; (iii) treatment abroad carries a higher ceiling (₹2,00,000) restricted to the limits prescribed by the Reserve Bank; (iv) group medical insurance and the premium paid by the employer are excluded within limits.

This is why the standard proforma asks for "medical expenditure reimbursed — ₹15,000 exempt".

Section 17(2)(iii) and the "sweat equity" issue

"Perquisite" includes "the value of any other benefit or amenity, service, right or privilege provided by the employer, whether by way of a free or concessional benefit". The clause is deliberately open-ended — precisely the kind of open-ended language that failed in L.W. Russel, where the Court held that a contingent right to an annuity in the future could not be a perquisite at all because the assessee had nothing "fixed, taken into account, set apart or granted" in the relevant year.

Modern perquisite law has moved the other way by statute (the ESOP clauses of 1999 and 2009), but the principle of L.W. Russel survives: nothing is a perquisite until it vests, or is at least applied for the employee's benefit.

Ram Pershad v. Commissioner of Income-tax, New Delhi

(1972) 2 SCC 696 · Jaganmohan Reddy J. · appeal from Delhi (by certificate) · DU reader pp. 84–89
Case 13Salary v. businessManaging directorWaiver after accrual1922 Act s. 7
1 · Facts
  • The assessee and his wife held shares in a company carrying on the business of a hotel. Article 109 of the company's articles of association made him the first Managing Director of the company, and provided for his remuneration.
  • By an agreement dated 20 November 1955, the company appointed him Managing Director on terms: a salary of ₹2,000 per month, a car allowance of ₹500 per month, a commission of 10% of the gross profits of the company, and free board and lodging at the hotel. The agreement was to run for a fixed term and contained provisions for termination, including clause (k) (removal for negligence).
  • For the assessment year 1956-57 the assessee was assessed on the salary, the car allowance, the value of the free board and lodging and the commission of ₹53,913.
  • The commission had accrued because the company's accounts for the year had shown a gross profit. But before the annual general meeting passed those accounts, the assessee waived his right to the commission — his explanation being that after providing for the commission the company would show no net profit, and that he did not wish the company to be seen to have made a loss.
  • The assessee's case was that (i) he was not a servant of the company but an independent holder of an office — the remuneration was therefore business income, not salary; and (ii) in any case, having waived the commission before it was paid, he had never received it, so it could not be taxed. The Income-tax Officer, the Appellate Assistant Commissioner, the Tribunal and the High Court all rejected both contentions; the departmental authorities and the High Court held the remuneration to be salary and the waived commission to be taxable.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the remuneration received by the assessee as Managing Director was chargeable under the head Salaries (s. 7 of the 1922 Act) or under the head Business (s. 10).
  2. Whether, in determining that question, the "supervision and control" test is of universal application, and whether a Managing Director — who is both a director and an employee — can be a servant at all.
  3. Whether the waived commission of ₹53,913 could be included in the salary of the previous year, it having accrued before the waiver and having been given up before payment.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • A Managing Director is not a servant. He is the holder of an office under the articles, he controls the company's affairs, and he cannot be under the "supervision and control" of the company in the sense in which a servant is. Remuneration for an office is, like the remuneration of a professional, income from business.
  • The test of "control" is the accepted test of service, and on the facts the assessee controlled rather than was controlled. The Tribunal and the High Court erred in treating the label "Managing Director" and the presence of the articles as decisive.
  • The commission was never received. It was waived before the accounts were adopted and before any payment was made; there was therefore no income at all, or at best a receipt that never materialised. A man cannot be taxed on what he has given up.
  • Reliance was placed on the line of authority holding that the relationship of master and servant must be shown by the employer — Piyare Lal Adishwar Lal v. CIT, 40 ITR 17; Lakshminarayan Ram Gopal & Son Ltd. v. Government of Hyderabad, 25 ITR 449; Commissioner of Income-tax v. Armstrong Smith, 14 ITR 606 (Bom); and Commissioner of Income-tax v. Negi Reddy, 51 ITR 178 (Mad).
For the Revenue
  • The articles and the agreement together show a contract of service: fixed monthly salary, a fixed car allowance, free board and lodging, a term, and a power of removal for negligence under clause (k) of the agreement. Those are the marks of employment, not of an independent office.
  • The description "Managing Director" describes two capacities. A person may be a director (an office) and at the same time an employee (a servant); the question is in which capacity the particular sum was received. Here it was received as salary.
  • On the waiver: the commission accrued when the company's accounts disclosed a gross profit from which it was computable. Under s. 7 the charge attaches to salary "due", whether paid or not. A subsequent waiver cannot undo an accrual; it is at most an application of income already earned.
  • The authorities relied on by the assessee do not lay down a universal test; each was decided on its own facts, and none of them concerned a managing director with a service contract of this kind.
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed. The remuneration was salary, and the waived commission was taxable as salary of the previous year. Delivering the judgment, Jaganmohan Reddy J. held that no single test is universal:

"The greater the amount of direct control exercised over the person rendering the services by the person contracting for them, the stronger the conclusion in favour of his being a servant." Reader p. 87

And, on the dual capacity of a managing director, the Court adopted Lord Normand's formulation in Anderson v. James Sutherland (Peterhead) Ltd., that a managing director has

"two functions and two capacities … qua director his position is that of an office-bearer, but qua managing director he is a servant of the company." Reader p. 88

The Court examined Articles 139, 140 and 142 and clause (k) of the agreement — the power of removal for negligence — and concluded that they established control, and therefore service. On the waiver, the Court applied Morvi Industries Ltd. v. CIT for the proposition that income accrues when it becomes due, and held that the commission having accrued, the subsequent waiver did not prevent its taxation.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) The "control" test is helpful but not universal. The question in every case is the true construction of the articles of association and of the agreement, read as a whole, in the light of the surrounding circumstances — not the application of a mechanical formula. Reddy J. expressly declined to treat supervision and control as a "rough and ready test" of universal application, and illustrated the point with the position of a doctor or a chauffeur, whose degree of autonomy does not determine the character of their income.
  • (2) A managing director has two capacities. The same individual may hold an office (director) and be a servant (managing director). Which capacity governs a particular receipt depends on what the receipt is for.
  • (3) Accrual is decisive; waiver comes too late. Once salary or commission has accrued — that is, once it has become due — a subsequent waiver by the employee does not prevent it from being taxed. At most the waiver is an application of income. This is the salary-side counterpart of the diversion/application distinction the same Court developed in the diversion cases.
  • (4) Form is not conclusive either way. Neither the label "Managing Director" nor the label "salary" decides the question; the Court looks at the substantive terms — a fixed monthly sum, a fixed allowance, free board and lodging, a term, and a power of removal.
Why it matters in the exam. Ram Pershad is the answer to every question of the form "X is a director/manager/agent of a company; is his remuneration salary or business income?" Boil the case down to four propositions: (i) the control test is a guide, not a rule; (ii) the true test is the construction of the articles and the agreement; (iii) a managing director acts in two capacities and the receipt must be traced to one of them; (iv) salary "due" is taxable whether paid or not, and a later waiver does not help. Cite it also for the proposition that the head "Salaries" is not confined to the humble employee — it reaches the highest executive, provided the relation is one of service.

Commissioner of Income-tax, Kerala v. L. W. Russel

AIR 1965 SC 49 · K. Subba Rao J. · appeal from Kerala · DU reader pp. 90–94
Case 14PerquisiteContingent right"Paid", "due", "allowed"1922 Act s. 7(1) Expl. 1(v)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a European employee of the English and Scottish Joint Co-operative Wholesale Society Ltd., employed at Kozhikode. He was assessed as a resident for the assessment year 1957-58.
  • By a trust deed dated 27 July 1934 the employer had created a fund for the benefit of its European employees. Under the deed, the employer was to pay annual premia to the trustees, who were to invest them; on retirement or on the happening of specified events, the employee became entitled to an annuity or a lump sum, computed on the basis of his salary and length of service. The arrangement was, in substance, a deferred annuity scheme funded by the employer.
  • In the previous year relevant to AY 1957-58 the employer paid a premium of which ₹3,333 — one-third of the total premium — was referable to the assessee's own annuity. (The figure is the one-third share which the employer bore; the reader records it as ₹3,333 in 1956-57.)
  • The Income-tax Officer held that this sum was a perquisite of the assessee, "allowed" to him by the employer within the meaning of the Explanation to s. 7(1) of the 1922 Act, and added it to his salary. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Tribunal took a different view and deleted the addition; the Department's reference succeeded in the High Court, which held the amount taxable. The assessee appealed by certificate.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the employer's contribution to the annuity fund was a perquisite of the employee within s. 7(1) of the 1922 Act and its Explanation, in the year in which the premium was paid.
  2. What do the words "paid", "due" and "allowed" in s. 7(1) mean, and can they apply to a payment which gives the employee only a contingent right?
  3. Whether an employee who has no vested interest in the fund, and who may never receive anything if he leaves service or dies before superannuation, can be said to have received a benefit at all.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The premium was paid by the employer to the trustees, not to the assessee. It never entered his hands and never became his property.
  • All that the assessee had was a contingent right: he would get the annuity only if he continued in service until superannuation and survived; if he resigned, was dismissed, or died, he or his estate would get nothing (or only a discretionary benefit). A contingent right is not income.
  • The words of the statute are "paid", "due" and "allowed". A sum cannot be "allowed" to a man unless it is fixed, taken into account, set apart or granted for him. A premium paid to a trust for a fluctuating class of possible annuitants answers none of those descriptions.
  • The scheme is a provision for the future — a benefit that may or may not fructify — and the correct year of tax, if any, is the year in which the annuity is actually received, not the year in which the employer made its contribution.
For the Revenue
  • The premium was paid in respect of the assessee and on his account; the amount was measured by reference to his salary and service. It was therefore a benefit allowed to him by the employer by reason of the employment — the very definition of a perquisite.
  • That the benefit is deferred does not make it any the less a benefit. The statute taxes salary due as well as salary paid; the extension to "allowed" is designed precisely to catch payments which reach the employee indirectly.
  • If the Revenue's construction is rejected, an employer and employee may arrange all remuneration in the form of trust contributions and escape tax altogether.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed; the premium was not a perquisite. K. Subba Rao J. examined each of the three statutory words and held that none of them was satisfied:

"The word 'allowed' is of ordinary use and it may mean 'fixed, taken into account, set apart, granted'. … It cannot be said that a contingent payment is 'allowed' to the assessee." Reader p. 93 (paraphrase of the reasoning)

The Court held that on the terms of the trust deed the assessee had no vested right to the annuity until he reached superannuation: at best he had a contingent right, and in the meantime the fund vested in the trustees. A benefit that the employee may never receive is not "paid", is not "due" and is not "allowed" to him. The addition was therefore unsustainable.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) A perquisite requires vesting (or at least an accrual of a right). Nothing is taxable as a perquisite merely because the employer has spent money which may one day benefit the employee. The test is whether the employee has acquired a right in the relevant year.
  • (2) The three words of the charge are not synonyms. "Paid" means actually received; "due" means an obligation on the employer coupled with a right in the employee to claim; "allowed" means fixed, taken into account, set apart or granted. Analysing the words separately, as the Court did, is the technique to use in any salary-accrual problem.
  • (3) Contingency defeats the charge. Where entitlement depends on continuing in service or on surviving to a date, there is no income until the contingency is satisfied. The corollary is that the annuity, when received, is taxable — the postponement is of the year, not of the liability.
  • (4) Substance of the arrangement, not the route of the money. The Court did not say that employer-funded trusts are outside the Act; it said that this trust conferred no vested benefit. Modern provisions (ss. 17(1)(vi)–(vii), 17(2)(vii)–(viii)) tax employer contributions directly by statute, which is the legislative answer to the problem the case exposed.
Why it matters in the exam. L.W. Russel is the authority for the proposition that a contingent benefit is not a perquisite, and for the close reading of "paid / due / allowed". Use it (i) in any question about employer contributions to funds, ESOPs, or deferred compensation; and (ii) as the counterpoint to Ram Pershad: in Ram Pershad the right had accrued and a later waiver could not undo it; in L.W. Russel no right had accrued, so nothing could be taxed. The pair illustrates the twin principles of accrual and vesting which govern the timing of salary income.

(b) Salary or business? — the practical checklist

Salary

  • There is a master–servant relation and the payer can direct the manner of work.
  • Remuneration is fixed and periodic, or measured by a formula tied to the employer's turnover or profits but payable qua employee.
  • The employee bears no business risk, invests no capital, and cannot delegate.
  • The receipt is taxable on accrual; deductions are limited to s. 16; TDS under s. 192.
  • Waiver after accrual does not help (Ram Pershad).

  • There is a contract for services, not a contract of service; the payer directs the result, not the manner.
  • The assessee has his own establishment, bears his own risk, and may serve several principals.

04Head CIncome from house property — sections 22 to 27

House property is the only head in which the tax is levied on a notional income. You may receive no rent at all and still be taxed on the "annual value" of the property; and the annual value itself is a figure the statute invents — what the property "might reasonably be expected to let" for. Three of the prescribed cases are about this head, and each attacks a different element of it: the reality of the notional income (Biman Behari Shaw), the exclusivity of the head (East India Housing), and the identity of the owner (Jodhamal Kuthiala).

◆4.1 Section 22 — the four ingredients

Section 22. Income from house property. — The annual value of property consisting of any buildings or lands appurtenant thereto, of which the assessee is the owner, other than such portions, if any, as he has occupied for purposes of any business or profession carried on by him, the profits of which are chargeable to income-tax under this Act, shall be chargeable to income-tax under the head "Income from house property".

The section contains four ingredients, and each has generated litigation. An answer should always take them one by one.

Section 22 dissected — the four ingredients and their tests
IngredientWhat must be shownAuthorities and notes
1 · "Buildings or lands appurtenant thereto" There must be a building, and land which is appurtenant (attached for use with the building — a courtyard, garage site, compound). Vacant land is not within s. 22 at all; income from it is either business income (if held as stock-in-trade) or other-sources income. 2 · "Of which the assessee is the owner" Ownership, not possession, not beneficial enjoyment. 4 · Income from letting, not from a trade of letting If the income is from bare letting of the building as such, it falls under s. 22 even if the assessee is a company and the building forms part of its commercial activity — unless the letting is itself the business, or is inseparable from the provision of services and amenities, in which case it is business income under s. 28. East India Housing (§4.8) — shops and stalls in a market: s. 22, not s. 28; the Supreme Court applied Fry v. Salisbury House Estate and United Commercial Bank.
"Owner" — right, title and interest, not bare possession

The owner for s. 22 is the person entitled to the property in his own right and, generally, the person to whom the rent legally belongs. Registration of a sale deed is not in every case necessary: an assessee who has received possession under s. 53A of the Transfer of Property Act (part performance) is treated as owner under s. 27(v), and a person allotted a house by a co-operative society is treated as owner under s. 27(iii). But an executor, a receiver, an agent, a mortgagee in possession or a custodian holds for another and is not the "owner" in this sense — the point on which Jodhamal Kuthiala turns.

◆4.2 Section 23 — how the annual value is determined

Section 23 is the computational heart of the head. It proceeds in three steps: expected rent → annual value → net annual value after municipal taxes. The statutory words are "the sum for which the property might reasonably be expected to let from year to year".

Section 23: the four cases in which annual value is determined
SituationAnnual valueNotes
Let out Higher of (i) the expected rent (municipal value or fair rent, subject to the standard-rent ceiling) and (ii) the actual rent received or receivable — except that where the actual rent is lower than the expected rent only because of vacancy, the actual rent is taken. Post Finance Act 2001 simplification; the "one-sixth" and "10% of actual rent" choices of the old law are gone.
Let out and vacant part of the year The annual value is reduced in proportion to the period of vacancy — s. 23(1)(c) applies where the property was vacant and unlet for part of the year, and the expected rent is for the whole year. Only genuine vacancy counts; a property deliberately kept vacant does not escape.
Self-occupied or unoccupied Nil, provided the property is not actually let during any part of the year and no other benefit arises, and provided the assessee does not claim any other house as self-occupied. Then s. 24(b) interest alone produces a loss. s. 23(2). Post Finance Act 2019, two houses may be treated as self-occupied with nil annual value (s. 23(4)); before that, only one. The choice is made year by year.
Part self-occupied and part let The two portions are computed separately; the self-occupied portion takes nil, the let portion its annual value. Interest under s. 24(b) is apportioned between the portions, subject to the ₹2,00,000 ceiling on the self-occupied part.

The two adjustments made before s. 24 begins

(i) Municipal taxes

Under the proviso to s. 23(1), the annual value is reduced by the amount of municipal taxes actually paid by the owner during the previous year. Two conditions: the tax must be borne by the owner (not by the tenant), and it must actually have been paid (not merely demanded). The deduction is from annual value — before the 30% standard deduction, not after. So the sequence is: expected rent / actual rent → less municipal taxes = net annual value → less 30% → less interest.

(ii) Unrealised rent

Where the tenant defaults, the annual value is computed on the rent received or receivable. A deduction for unrealised rent is allowed where the prescribed conditions (Rule 4) are satisfied — the tenancy is bona fide, the default is proved, and the steps for recovery have been taken. When the amount is subsequently recovered, it is taxed under s. 25A in the year of receipt, with a 30% deduction.

◆4.3 Section 24 — the two deductions

Section 24: the only deductions from net annual value
DeductionQuantumConditions and traps
s. 24(a) — standard deduction 30% of the net annual value. Flat; no proof of expenditure required, and no account of actual expenditure. It is not available where the annual value is nil (there is nothing to take 30% of). Since the Finance Act 2001, repairs, insurance, ground rent and collection charges are all merged into this 30%; there is no separate deduction for any of them.
s. 24(b) — interest on borrowed capital Let out: the whole of the interest, with no ceiling. Self-occupied: up to ₹2,00,000 (₹2,50,000 where the loan is taken on or after 1-4-1999 for acquisition or construction completed within five years, and only for a self-occupied house). Pre-construction interest: the interest for the period before completion is allowed in five equal annual instalments beginning with the year of completion. Three conditions for the self-occupied ceiling: the loan is for acquisition or construction (not repairs or reconstruction); it is taken on or after 1 April 1999; and the acquisition or construction is completed within five years from the end of the year in which the loan was taken. Interest on a loan for repairs of a self-occupied house is capped at ₹30,000. Interest is deductible on due basis, not payment basis.
The three most-marked traps in s. 24(b)

(1) Housing-loan principal is not deductible here at all — it goes to s. 80C (old regime) or s. 80EEA, subject to its own ceilings. (2) The ₹2,00,000 ceiling applies to the aggregate interest on all self-occupied houses, not to each house. (3) Where the loan is taken by a co-owner set, each co-owner may claim the ceiling separately in proportion to his share, because each is a separate assessee under s. 26.

◆4.4 Sections 25A and 26 — receipts after the year, and co-owners

s. 25A — arrears and unrealised rent

Where the assessee recovers arrears of rent or unrealised rent which was not charged to tax in an earlier year because it had not been received, the amount is deemed to be income from house property of the year of receipt. A deduction of 30% of the amount is allowed, and the balance is taxable in that later year — irrespective of whether the assessee still owns the property. If the arrears were already taxed on accrual, s. 25A does not apply.

Exam use: s. 25A is the standard illustration that "the same income may be taxed in a later year" — the statutory exception to the mutual-exclusivity rule discussed in §1.3.

s. 26 — co-owners

Where property is owned by co-owners with definite and ascertainable shares, each is treated as the owner of his share, and the computation is made separately for each: his share of the annual value, his 30% deduction, his share of interest, and his own ₹2,00,000 ceiling on self-occupied interest. Neither can set off his loss against the other's income.

Where the shares are not definite (a HUF, a firm, an AOP), s. 26 does not apply and the property is assessed in the hands of the collective body. Joint ownership by spouses is the classic exam fact: two returns, two computations, two ceilings.

◆4.7 Case 15 — CIT, West Bengal v. Biman Behari Shaw Shebait

Commissioner of Income-tax, West Bengal v. Biman Behari Shaw Shebait

68 ITR 815 (Cal) · Banerjee J. · reference under s. 66(1) of the 1922 Act · DU reader pp. 95–97
Case 15Notional annual valueDebutter propertys. 9(2) of the 1922 Act"might reasonably be expected to let"
1 · Facts
  • One Banku Behari Saha, by his will dated 24 November 1925, created a debutter (an endowment for the worship of a deity) in respect of two properties in Calcutta: 12 Benode Behari Saha Lane (the Thakurbati, or temple premises) and 122A Manicktola Street, on which a temple was later built.
  • The properties were not let out at any time. They were used for the purposes of the endowment — for the residence and worship connected with the deity.
  • Clause (17) of the will imposed a restriction on letting (a restrictive covenant) which, the assessee contended, made it impossible for the shebait to let the properties at all.
  • The Income-tax Officer nevertheless assessed the shebait on the notional annual value of the two properties: for each, ₹4,000 as the expected rent, less ₹666 for repairs, giving ₹3,334 — on the footing that the properties "might reasonably be expected to let".
  • The Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Tribunal deleted the addition, relying on clause (17) of the will and holding that, the properties being debutter and the letting being restricted by the will, no annual value could be attributed. The Department obtained a reference to the High Court.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the income of the debutter properties was assessable under s. 9 of the 1922 Act (house property) at all, or whether the properties, being dedicated to a deity and held for worship, fell outside the head.
  2. Whether, on the true construction of s. 9(2), the annual value of properties which are never actually let can be assessed on the basis of what they "might reasonably be expected to let" for.
  3. Whether a restrictive covenant against letting contained in the founding instrument can reduce the annual value to nil, or only reduce it.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (shebait)
  • The properties are debutter — dedicated to a deity. Neither the shebait nor anyone else has a beneficial interest which could be taxed; the income belongs to the deity and is applied to worship.
  • Even if the properties are within s. 9, clause (17) of the will forbids letting. What cannot lawfully be let cannot be "reasonably expected to be let"; the annual value is therefore nil, which is what the Tribunal held.
  • The Tribunal's finding that the properties were never let and could not be let is a finding of fact, and the High Court should not interfere with it.
For the Revenue
  • Section 9(2) does not ask what the property is let for, or whether the owner chooses to let it. It asks what it "might reasonably be expected to let" for. The statute deliberately taxes a notional income, so that an owner cannot escape tax by leaving property idle.
  • A private covenant cannot take the property out of the charge. At most it may depress the figure at which the property might reasonably be expected to let; it cannot eliminate the annual value altogether.
  • The Tribunal erred in treating the covenant as conclusive; the question of annual value is one of law and estimate, not a simple finding of fact.
4 · Decision

Reference answered in favour of the Revenue: the notional annual value was assessable; the Tribunal was wrong to delete it. Banerjee J. held that s. 9(2) taxes an income which the statute artificially defines, and relied on D.M. Vakil v. CIT, 14 ITR 298, where Kania C.J. said:

"The annual value … is an artificially defined income … the word is 'might' and not 'can' or 'is'." D.M. Vakil, 14 ITR 298, quoted in the reader at p. 96

The Court also relied on Sir Currimbhoy Ebrahim Baronetcy Trust v. CIT, 48 ITR 507, and held that a restrictive covenant may reduce the value at which the property might reasonably be expected to let, but cannot eliminate it. Importantly, the Court expressly left open the question whether a temple wholly occupied by a deity, with no question of letting at all, falls within s. 9(2) — so the case is authority on the valuation question, not on the debutter question.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) House-property income is notional. The charge under s. 22 is on the annual value, which the statute fixes by reference to a hypothetical letting. Actual letting is irrelevant to chargeability; it matters only to quantum.
  • (2) "Might reasonably be expected to let" is an objective test. The words are "might", not "can" or "is"; the question is what a reasonable owner could obtain in the open market, not what this owner chooses or is contractually permitted to do.
  • (3) A private restriction reduces but does not destroy. A covenant against letting may be taken into account in estimating the hypothetical rent, because a property subject to such a covenant is less attractive; but it cannot be used to reduce the annual value to zero.
  • (4) The debutter point was left open. A well-answered question says so. The case is therefore a valuation authority, and its limits should be stated: it does not decide that a temple in the exclusive occupation of a deity is taxable property income.
Why it matters in the exam. It is the answer to any question about a property that is not actually let — kept vacant, occupied rent-free by a relative, used for a temple or a trust, or subject to a covenant. And it supplies the best one-line justification of the whole head: "the charge is on an artificially defined income, because otherwise an owner could escape tax by not letting" — which is also the reason the Dec-2024 Q7 property at Okhla (used only for dharmik pooja) needs careful analysis (§9.3).

◆4.8 Case 16 — East India Housing & Land Development Trust Ltd. v. CIT

East India Housing and Land Development Trust Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax, West Bengal

42 ITR 49 (SC) · Shah J. · appeal from Calcutta · DU reader pp. 98–99
Case 16Mutual exclusivitys. 9 v. s. 10Bare lettingCompany as owner
1 · Facts
  • The appellant was a company which, in 1946, purchased 10 bighas of land in Calcutta and constructed a market upon it, consisting of shops and stalls.
  • The company let out the shops and stalls to tenants. For the assessment year 1953-54 it received ₹53,145 from the shops and ₹29,721 from the stalls.
  • The company's objects included the acquisition of land and the erection of buildings, and it was assessed for several years on the footing that the income was business income under s. 10 of the 1922 Act. For AY 1953-54 the Income-tax Officer assessed the income under s. 9 (house property); the Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Tribunal restored the assessment to s. 10.
  • The Department took the matter to the High Court, which held that the income was properly assessable under s. 9. The company appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the income from the shops and stalls — a building constructed and let out by a company formed to acquire land and erect buildings — was chargeable under s. 9 (house property) or under s. 10 (business).
  2. Whether the assessee company, having let out the property as part of its business, could be said to have derived the income "from business", so that the business head would displace the property head.
  3. The general question: are the heads of income mutually exclusive, and what happens where a receipt could, on the facts, be described under more than one head?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee company
  • The company was formed with the object of acquiring land, developing it and letting out buildings. The letting of shops and stalls was its business, carried on systematically with a view to profit; the property was its stock-in-trade or its commercial asset.
  • Income derived from a business asset, by the exploitation of that asset, is business income. The maintenance of the market, the collection of rents, the provision of stalls and the management of a going market all show an activity, not bare ownership.
  • Since the income is business income, the deductions available under s. 10 must be allowed; being confined to s. 9 would deny the company the deductions it had enjoyed for years.
  • Reliance was placed on Commercial Properties Ltd. v. CIT, 3 ITC 23, where the income of a company which acquired land, built houses and let them out was held to be business income.
For the Revenue
  • Section 9 charges the income of a building to the person who owns it, and it does so regardless of the business of the owner. The source here is the ownership of the building and the receipt of rent from it — nothing more.
  • The heads are mutually exclusive and each has its own computation rule. A receipt which falls squarely in s. 9 cannot be pulled into s. 10 merely because the owner happens to be a company whose objects include property development.
  • Commercial Properties is distinguishable and has not been universally followed; the Privy Council in Fry v. Salisbury House Estate Ltd. and this Court in United Commercial Bank Ltd. support the exclusivity of the property head.
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed. The income was chargeable under s. 9 (house property), not s. 10. Shah J. laid down two propositions which are now the standard statement of the law:

"(1) that the several heads of income enumerated in s. 6 of the Income-tax Act are mutually exclusive, and each head of income has a definite rule provided for computation of the income; and (2) that if the income from a source falls within a specific head set out in s. 6, the fact that it may indirectly be covered by another head will not make the income taxable under the latter head." Reader p. 99

The Court explained the reason: the income from the shops and stalls arose from the ownership of the building and the letting of it; the source of the income was the property, not a business. Nor could the character of the letting change because the owner was a company — if the letting was not a business for an individual, it did not become one because it was done by a company; and if it were a business in the hands of a company, the company would have to show that the activity of letting was the business itself, which was not the case for a market let out as such.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) The heads are mutually exclusive. The case is the locus classicus for the proposition; it is the first authority to cite in any answer on the question the syllabus asks.
  • (2) The specific head prevails over the general. If the source falls within a specific head, the fact that the income could also be described under another head does not make it taxable under that head ("indirectly covered" is not enough).
  • (3) Bare letting of a building is property income, whatever the owner's status. The assessee's objects, its incorporation, and its systematic letting do not convert rent into business income unless letting is itself the business.
  • (4) The "exploitation of a commercial asset" doctrine is not displaced but confined. A business may exploit a building it owns; but if the exploitation amounts to nothing more than letting the building, s. 22 takes the income. The boundary was later worked out in Sultan Brothers (services and amenities; inseparable composite letting), which is discussed at §4.6.
Why it matters in the exam. Three uses. First, for the direct question "are the heads mutually exclusive?" — quote the two propositions verbatim. Second, for the problem "a company lets out shops/an office building/a mall; is it property or business income?" — answer by asking whether the letting is bare or whether it is inseparable from services, and note that the Dec-2024 Q7 problem (Mr. RAAM's business of "developing land into malls and further letting them on rent", accepted as business by the AO) is precisely the converse case where the letting is the business. Third, for any question on the interface between s. 22 and s. 28.

◆4.9 Case 17 — R. B. Jodhamal Kuthiala v. CIT

R. B. Jodhamal Kuthiala v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Punjab

AIR 1972 SC 126 · Hegde J. · appeals from Punjab & Haryana · DU reader pp. 100–106
Case 17"Owner" in s. 9Evacuee propertyCustodianBeneficial v. legal ownership
1 · Facts
  • A firm purchased Nedous Hotel, Lahore for ₹46 lakh — ₹30 lakh financed by a bank loan and ₹18 lakh advanced by the Raja of Jubbal, who in return took a half share in the hotel.
  • On partition the hotel became evacuee property and vested in the Custodian of Evacuee Property under the Administration of Evacuee Property Act and the Pakistan Evacuee Property Ordinance, 1949. The Custodian took over possession and management and had wide powers of sale, lease and management.
  • The assessee was assessed to tax in India in respect of interest said to be receivable on the investment in the hotel, under s. 9(1)(iv) of the 1922 Act, for the assessment years 1952-53, 1955-56 and 1956-57, on the footing that he was the owner of the property and that interest on the capital invested was part of the property income.
  • The assessee's case was that he had ceased to be the owner once the property vested in the Custodian: he had no right to receive rent or interest, no power to let or sell, and no effective enjoyment of the property. The Tribunal and the High Court held against him (the High Court holding that, the evacuee retaining the ultimate ownership, the assessee remained the owner for s. 9). The assessee appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the assessee was the "owner" of Nedous Hotel within the meaning of s. 9 of the 1922 Act during the relevant assessment years, the property having vested in the Custodian of Evacuee Property.
  2. Whether the retention of a residual or ultimate ownership by an evacuee (as recognised in Amur Singh v. Custodian, Evacuee Property, Punjab) makes him the owner for the purposes of s. 9.
  3. Whether a person who has a beneficial interest in property, but not the legal power to deal with it, can be taxed on its income.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • Ownership is the entirety of the powers of use and disposal allowed by law. Once those powers passed to the Custodian, the assessee retained nothing that could be called ownership for tax purposes.
  • The assessee could not let, sell, mortgage or even occupy the hotel; he could not receive a rupee of its income. To tax him on an income he cannot receive is to tax notional income twice removed from reality.
  • Amur Singh v. Custodian, Evacuee Property, Punjab, AIR 1957 SC 599, held that, although all evacuee property vests in the Custodian, "the evacuee has not lost his ownership in it": the law recognises his ultimate ownership subject to limitations. That reservation of ultimate ownership is not ownership within s. 9.
  • Reliance was also placed on Raja P. C. Lal Choudhary v. CIT, 16 ITR 123 (Pat), where a receiver appointed by the court was held not to be the owner because the property did not vest in him — a case, it was argued, showing that formal vesting alone does not make an "owner".
  • On Pollock on Jurisprudence: "We must look for the person having the residue of all such power when we have accounted for every detached and limited portion of it; and he will be the owner even if the immediate power of control and use is elsewhere." The residue of power was with the Custodian, not with the assessee.
For the Revenue
  • Section 9 taxes the income of property in the hands of the owner. The assessee remained the owner in law: the vesting in the Custodian was for purposes of management and administration, and did not extinguish his title. The evacuee could return and claim the property and an account of the management.
  • If a person's property is temporarily managed by another — a receiver, an official assignee, a custodian — the legal owner does not cease to be the owner for tax purposes; otherwise the income would escape tax altogether.
  • The decision in the Official Assignee for Bengal case, 5 ITR 233, shows that a person in whom property vests (there, the Official Assignee in insolvency) is the owner for s. 9. The Custodian's powers are "no less than that of the Official Assignee", so by parity the Custodian — or, if the vesting is incomplete, the assessee — must be the owner. Nawab Bahadur of Murshidabad v. CIT, 28 ITR 510, likewise held that restrictions on alienation do not prevent the holder from being the owner.
4 · Decision

Appeals allowed; the assessee was not the owner of Nedous Hotel for the relevant years, and the interest could not be assessed under s. 9. Hegde J. held:

"The word 'owner' … must be understood in its ordinary natural sense. … For the purpose of s. 9, the owner must be that person who can exercise the rights of the owner, not on behalf of the owner but in his own right." Reader pp. 104–105

The Court explained the test as being the person entitled to the income: "the focus of that section is on the receipt of the income". It distinguished Amur Singh on the ground that it was concerned with whether the evacuee's right was "property" for Art. 19(1)(f) of the Constitution, not with ownership for s. 9; observed that the evacuee's residual right, though real, was not ownership within the section; and warned that the meaning given to "owner" must not make the provision "an instrument of oppression". The Official Assignee case (5 ITR 233) was approved — because there the property vested in the assignee — and Raja P. C. Lal Choudhary (16 ITR 123) was held, if anything, to support the Revenue's general approach while remaining distinguishable. Nawab Bahadur of Murshidabad (28 ITR 510) was held not to help the assessee: there the Nawab remained owner notwithstanding restrictions on alienation, because he could still exercise the rights of an owner in his own right.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) "Owner" means the person who can exercise the rights of an owner in his own right. Not the person who merely holds for, or on behalf of, another; not the person with a bare beneficial or residual interest where the powers of enjoyment have passed elsewhere.
  • (2) The test is the right to receive the income. The section "seeks to bring to tax the income of the property in the hands of the owner", and its focus "is on the receipt of the income". A person who cannot receive the income is not the owner for this purpose.
  • (3) Restrictions on alienation do not destroy ownership. Restrictions which leave the owner the powers of enjoyment and receipt (as in Murshidabad) are consistent with ownership; a total transfer of the powers of enjoyment is not.
  • (4) Statutory vesting matters. Where property vests by operation of law in an Official Assignee or a Custodian, that person is the owner and is assessable; the original owner's residual right is not enough.
  • (5) A caution against oppression. The Court's warning that the meaning of "owner" must not make s. 9 "an instrument of oppression" is a policy argument worth quoting in any answer about literal construction in tax law.
Why it matters in the exam. It is the authority on the second ingredient of s. 22 — ownership. Use it whenever the problem involves a mortgagee in possession, a receiver, an executor, a guardian, a custodian, a benamidar, a lessee, or a purchaser without a registered deed. The four-step answer: (i) identify the person who can exercise the rights of an owner in his own right; (ii) ask who is entitled to the income; (iii) ask whether the property has vested in someone else by operation of law; (iv) check whether s. 27 deems someone to be the owner. The case is also the link between this head and the general principle of beneficial v. legal ownership, so it can be cited in Topic 1 answers on "assessee".

Two definitional anchors

"Business" (s. 2(13)) — "includes any trade, commerce or manufacture or any adventure or concern in the nature of trade, commerce or manufacture". The words "adventure in the nature of trade" are what enable the Department to tax isolated transactions as business — the very idea that Empire Jute and the capital/revenue cases test from the other side. "Profession" (s. 2(36)) — "includes vocation". The distinction between business and profession matters for ss. 44AA and 44AB (books and audit) and for the presumptive schemes, but not for the head itself.

◆5.3 Bad debts — s. 36(1)(vii) and s. 36(2)

s. 36(1)(vii): Subject to the provisions of sub-s. (2), the amount of any bad debt or part thereof which is written off as irrecoverable in the accounts of the assessee for the previous year shall be allowed as a deduction.

s. 36(2): No such deduction shall be allowed unless — (i) the debt or part of it has been taken into account in computing the income of the assessee of the previous year in which the amount was lent or in which the debt arose, or of an earlier previous year; or (ii) it represents money lent in the ordinary course of the business of banking or money-lending which is carried on by the assessee.

The two conditions in practice: (a) the debt must be made and written off in the assessee's accounts; and (b) it must be a debt of the business, i.e. it must have arisen from a transaction which produced, or would have produced, taxable business income. Note the substitution of "written off" for "established to have become bad" by the Finance Act, 1989 — the assessee no longer has to prove that the debtor is impossible; the writing-off is the statutory event, though the Assessing Officer may still test whether the debt had become irrecoverable.

◆5.4 Case 18 — B. D. Bharucha v. CIT

B. D. Bharucha v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Bombay

AIR 1967 SC 1505 / (1967) 3 SCR 238 · V. Ramaswami J. · appeal from Bombay · DU reader pp. 107–109
Case 18Bad debtMoneylending v. trading ventureCapital loss v. revenue losss. 10(2)(xi) of the 1922 Act
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was an individual with income from house property, Government securities, cinema exhibition, and financing film producers and distributors.
  • Between 3 March 1952 and 5 November 1952 he advanced ₹40,000 to a firm of film distributors, Tarachand Pictures.
  • By an agreement dated 5 January 1953 he advanced a further ₹60,000 in respect of the distribution, exploitation and exhibition of a film called "Shabab". By clause 2, the distributors were to pay a lump sum of ₹1,750 as interest on the first advance. Clause 3 provided that no further interest would run on the ₹40,000 or on the new advance, and that "in lieu of interest" the financier and the distributors would share the profit and loss of the distribution, exploitation and exhibition in the Bombay Circuit — two-thirds to the financier and one-third to the distributors.
  • By clause 4 the distributors were to submit monthly statements of account; by clause 5 proper accounts were to be kept and made available for inspection.
  • Clause 7 was the decisive provision: if the picture was not released in Bombay within 15 months of the agreement, the distributors were bound to return all moneys advanced, together with interest at 9% per annum. Clause 8 provided the same consequence on breach of any term.
  • The film was not released within the stipulated period (the date fixed being 4 April 1954); it was released only on 28 May 1954, so clause 7 came into operation. The venture failed commercially, the matter went to the City Civil Court, and ended in a consent decree in Suit No. 2061 of 1954. The assessee was left with a balance of ₹80,759 which proved irrecoverable, and wrote it off as a bad debt on 31 December 1955.
  • For AY 1956-57 he claimed the ₹80,759 as a bad debt under s. 10(2)(xi). The Income-tax Officer disallowed it: the advances were not a dealing in the course of the financing business but "a venture in the nature of a trade", so the loss was capital. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Tribunal agreed; the Tribunal added that the transaction was neither a joint venture nor a mere financing deal, but "an investment of capital for a return in the shape of a share of profits". The High Court answered the reference — "Whether the aforesaid loss of Rs 80,759 is deductible under any of the provisions of the Act?" — in the negative. The assessee appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the advances made by the assessee to the distributors were made in the course of his business of financing film producers and distributors, or whether they were a separate capital venture.
  2. Whether the loss of ₹80,759 was a bad debt deductible under s. 10(2)(xi), or a capital loss which no provision allows.
  3. Whether the stipulation in clause 3 that the financier would share the loss took the transaction outside the category of moneylending or of a financial deal, so as to convert it into a capital investment.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • He was, on the finding, carrying on the business of financing film producers and distributors. These advances were made in the ordinary course of that business; the debt therefore arose from the business and was a business debt.
  • Clause 3 does not change the character of the transaction. It is a common commercial arrangement that a financier shares in the fortunes of the venture he finances — a lender may take a share of profits and bear a share of loss without ceasing to be a lender.
  • Clause 7 is decisive: it expressly provides, in the event of non-release within 15 months, for the repayment of "all the moneys so far advanced" with interest at 9%. That is the language of loan, not of investment. The picture was not released in time; from 4 April 1954 the contract of loan became operative; the sum was repayable; it was not repaid; it was written off.
  • The statutory conditions of s. 10(2)(xi) are satisfied: the debt is in respect of the business, the accounts are kept on the mercantile basis, the debt became irrecoverable in the relevant year, and it was actually written off in the books.
For the Revenue
  • Under clause 3 the financier agreed to share the loss of the business. No moneylender shares the loss of the borrower's business; that feature takes the transaction out of the category of a loan or a financial deal and makes it a participation in a venture.
  • The return was not interest but a share of profits — "two-thirds of the profit or loss of the distribution, exploitation and exhibition" of the picture. Investment of capital for a share of profits is an investment, and the loss of it is a capital loss.
  • The Tribunal's finding that the transaction was neither a joint venture nor a mere financing deal but an investment of capital is a finding of fact, and the loss is therefore not a bad debt.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed. The ₹80,759 was a bad debt deductible under s. 10(2)(xi); the loss was a revenue loss, not a capital loss. Ramaswami J. held that clause 3 could not be read in isolation:

"If clause 3 of the agreement is taken in isolation there may be some force in the contention of the respondent … In the present case, however, clause 3 … cannot be read in isolation but it must be construed in the context of clause 7." Reader p. 108

Because clause 7 converted the arrangement into a contract of loan from 4 April 1954, with the principal repayable with 9% interest, the appellant was entitled to claim the balance as a bad debt. On the capital/revenue question the Court adopted the reasoning of Reid's Brewery Co. Ltd. v. Male, (1891) 3 Tax Cas 279, and of Pollock B.:

"[N]o person who is acquainted with the habits of business can doubt that this is not capital invested. What it is is this: it is capital used by the Appellants … it is used and it comes out of capital, but it is not an investment in the ordinary sense of the word." Reader p. 109

The Court held that all the conditions of s. 10(2)(xi) were satisfied — the debt was in respect of the business carried on in the relevant year, the accounts were mercantile, the debt was incidental to the business, it had become irrecoverable in the relevant year, and it had actually been written off.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Read the contract as a whole. A single clause (here, sharing of loss) cannot determine the character of a transaction; the agreement must be construed as an integrated whole, and a later clause (repayment with interest on default) may reveal the true nature of the earlier one.
  • (2) Money lent in the course of a moneylending or financing business is circulating capital, not capital invested. Its loss is a trading loss, deductible; the distinction is the same one drawn in Reid's Brewery and later in Mysore Sugar.
  • (3) The statutory conditions of s. 36(1)(vii) are cumulative. Business debt + mercantile accounts + irrecoverability in the year + actual writing off. Each must be shown.
  • (4) A debt can arise from a contingent arrangement. The advance was originally a profit-and-loss participation; but when the contingency in clause 7 occurred, the relationship crystallised into lender and borrower, and the debt arose at that moment.
Why it matters in the exam. It is the standard authority on the boundary between a bad debt under s. 36(1)(vii) and a capital loss. Use it whenever an assessee advances money and it becomes irrecoverable: ask (i) was the advance in the course of the business? (ii) did the arrangement contain a promise of repayment (with or without interest)? (iii) was it written off in the accounts? If yes to all three, the loss is revenue. Note that the same analysis now also has to reckon with s. 36(1)(vii) as amended in 1989 and with the Explanation that a mere provision for bad debt is not a write-off.

◆5.5 Case 19 — CIT v. Mysore Sugar Co. Ltd.

Commissioner of Income-tax, Mysore v. Mysore Sugar Co. Ltd., Bangalore

AIR 1967 SC 723 · M. Hidayatullah J. · appeal from Mysore · DU reader pp. 110–113
Case 19Advance to growersCapital v. revenue lossBad debtSs. 10(1), 10(2)(xi), 10(2)(xv)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee company purchased sugarcane from growers and crushed it to make sugar. As part of its business it entered into agreements with growers known locally as "Oppigedars", to whom it advanced sugarcane seedlings, fertilizers and cash.
  • The growers signed a written agreement called the "Oppige", by which they agreed to sell sugarcane exclusively to the assessee at current market rates, to have the advances adjusted against the price of the sugarcane, and to pay interest in the meantime. A separate account was opened for each grower. A sugarcane crop takes about 18 months to mature, and these agreements were made at each harvest season in preparation for the next crop.
  • In 1948-49, owing to drought, the company could not work its mills and the growers could not grow or deliver sugarcane. The advances of that year therefore remained unrecovered, since they could be recovered only by the supply of sugarcane.
  • The Mysore Government appointed a Committee, which reported on 27 July 1950 and recommended that the company should ex gratia forgo part of its dues. In the year of account ending 30 June 1952 the company waived its rights to the extent of ₹2,87,422 and claimed the amount under ss. 10(2)(xi) (bad debts) and 10(2)(xv) (business expenditure).
  • The Income-tax Officer refused: the amount was neither a trade debt nor a bad debt but "an ex-gratia payment almost like a gift". The Appellate Assistant Commissioner agreed. The Tribunal held that the payments were not made with an eye to commercial profit and could not be said to be dictated by commercial expediency, so s. 10(2)(xv) was not attracted; and that they were not bad debts because they were "advances, pure and simple, not arising out of sales". The Tribunal, however, referred the question "whether there are materials for the tribunal to hold that the sum of Rs. 2,87,422 represents a loss of capital"; the High Court held the expenditure was not capital and was deductible as revenue expenditure, relying on Badridas Daga v. CIT, AIR 1958 SC 783.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the amount forgone by the company represented a loss of capital or a revenue expenditure / trading loss.
  2. Whether the advances to the growers were made as an investment in the growers' agriculture (capital) or as advance payment of the price of sugarcane (revenue).
  3. Whether s. 10(2) is exhaustive of the deductions, or whether a loss may be allowed in computing the true profits under s. 10(1) even if it does not fall within any of the enumerated clauses.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee company
  • The advances were, in substance, payments of the price of the next crop in advance. The company was not investing in the growers' farms; it was securing its raw material and paying for it before delivery so that the crop should not suffer for want of funds.
  • The loss on those advances is therefore a loss on the revenue side — exactly as if the company had paid for a ready crop which was never delivered.
  • The waiver was not a gratuitous gift: it was forced by drought and by the recommendation of a Government committee, and it was dictated by commercial expediency — to keep the growers alive as a source of supply for future years.
  • The loss is deductible under s. 10(1) even if it fits no clause of s. 10(2): s. 10(2) does not deal exhaustively with deductions, as held in CIT v. S. M. Chitnavis, AIR 1932 PC 178.
For the Revenue
  • The advances were made to enable the growers to grow the crop — an investment in the source of supply, of enduring benefit, and therefore capital. The growers' lands, seedlings and fertilizers were financed by the company; it was, in effect, financing agriculture.
  • The Tribunal found that the payments were not dictated by commercial expediency and were not made with an eye to commercial profit, so s. 10(2)(xv) is not satisfied.
  • The sums were not "debts" at all: they were "advances, pure and simple, not arising out of sales". A bad debt presupposes a debt due from a sale or a loan in the course of business.
  • The waiver was ex gratia — a voluntary giving up, akin to a gift — and a gift is not a business loss.
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed. The loss was a revenue loss and was deductible. Hidayatullah J. laid down the governing test in words that are quoted in every textbook:

"The questions to consider in this connection are: for what was the money laid out? Was it to acquire an asset of an enduring nature for the benefit of the business, or was it an out-going in the doing of the business? If money be lost in the first circumstance, it is a loss of capital, but if lost in the second circumstance, it is a revenue loss." Reader p. 111

Applying it, the Court held that the amount was "an advance against price of one crop", that the growers were to get the assistance "not as an investment by the assessee Company in its agriculture, but only as an advance payment of price", and that "the resulting loss … was just as much a loss on the revenue side as would have been, if it had paid for the ready crop which was not delivered". The Court also affirmed that s. 10(2) is not exhaustive of the deductions, relying on Chitnavis, and analysed three English cases — English Crown Spelter Co. Ltd. v. Baker, (1908) 5 Tax Cas 327 (advance to a company formed to secure supplies: capital); Charles Marsden & Sons Ltd. v. IRC, (1919) 12 Tax Cas 217 (advance of £30,000 against ten years' future deliveries: capital, because "no one pays for goods ten years in advance"); and Reid's Brewery Co. Ltd. v. Male (loans by a brewery to its customers: revenue).

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) The test is the purpose of the outlay. Was the money laid out to acquire an asset of an enduring nature, or was it an outgoing in the doing of the business? Loss of the first is capital; loss of the second is revenue.
  • (2) An advance against the price of goods is revenue. If the advance is really a prepayment of the purchase price of circulating stock, its loss is a trading loss even though the goods were never delivered.
  • (3) An advance to create or secure a source of supply is capital. The contrast with English Crown Spelter and Charles Marsden is the operative distinction: there the money was adventured to establish a source; here it was paid against the price of one crop.
  • (4) s. 10(2) is not exhaustive. A loss which is genuinely a business loss may be deducted in computing the true profits under the charging provision, even if no clause covers it — the Chitnavis principle, which survives in the "real profits" doctrine under ss. 28–29 today.
  • (5) Commercial expediency can justify a waiver. A waiver forced by drought and recommended by a Government committee, to preserve the source of supply, is a business decision; but the Court did not need to rest on this, because the loss was revenue on the primary analysis.
Why it matters in the exam. Mysore Sugar is the classic answer to "advance to a supplier/grower written off — capital or revenue?" and it pairs with B.D. Bharucha (advance to a distributor). Read together they give you the two poles: money paid against the price of stock or lent in the course of a financing business is revenue; money paid to establish a source of supply is capital. The Dec-2024 Q7 problem (advance for raw material that could not be secured, written off) is almost exactly Mysore Sugar, and is answered on that footing at §9.3.

06Business expenditureSection 37(1) — the capital/revenue divide

Six of the fifteen prescribed cases sit in this block, and they are the most heavily examined in the whole topic. The reason is simple: the divide between capital and revenue is the one question in income-tax law on which there is no universal test, and every one of these cases says so in terms. What the cases give you is not a formula but a method: the tests, their limits, and the analogical reasoning by which a court moves from one to the next.

◆6.1 The section

Section 37(1). Any expenditure (not being expenditure of the nature described in ss. 30 to 36 and not being in the nature of capital expenditure or personal expenses of the assessee), laid out or expended wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the business or profession shall be allowed in computing the income chargeable under the head "Profits and gains of business or profession".

Explanation 1 — no allowance for expenditure incurred for any purpose which is an offence or which is prohibited by law. Explanation 2 — a provision for a liability which is not ascertained is not an expenditure. Explanation 3 (Finance Act, 2022) — expenditure incurred for a purpose which is an offence or prohibited by law includes expenditure in respect of which the assessee claims immunity from prosecution.

Four conditions. The expenditure must be (i) not covered by ss. 30–36; (ii) not capital; (iii) not personal; and (iv) laid out wholly and exclusively for the business. The capital/revenue question is condition (ii), and it is the one that has occupied the Supreme Court for seventy years.

Why "wholly and exclusively" is not the battleground

In almost every one of the six cases the Revenue conceded that the money had been spent for the purposes of the business. Empire Jute says so expressly: "it was not contended on behalf of the Revenue that the sum … was not laid out wholly and exclusively for the purpose of the assessee's business". The only question was whether the outlay was capital or revenue. That is the shape of the problem: business purpose is usually obvious; character is not.

1 · Enduring benefit (Lord Cave) "When an expenditure is made, not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, there is very good reason … for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital." 3 · Acquisition of a source of profit Disbursement for the acquisition of a source or "spinner" of income is capital; disbursement to operate an existing source is revenue. Commissioner of Income-tax, Kerala v. Travancore Sugars & Chemicals Ltd.

AIR 1973 SC 982 / (1973) 88 ITR 1 · P. Jaganmohan Reddy J. · with the earlier decision in Travancore Sugars & Chemicals Ltd. v. CIT, AIR 1967 SC 477 · outside the reader
Case 20Percentage of profitsPurchase considerationDiversion at source1922 Act s. 10(2)(xv)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee company was incorporated with the object of taking over certain industrial undertakings from the Government of the erstwhile State of Travancore — a sugar factory, a distillery, and the Government Tincture Factory at Trivandrum. The transfer was effected by agreements dated 18 June 1937 and 28 January 1947.
  • Apart from the cash consideration, the company agreed to pay the Government a percentage of its net profits every year. Clause 7 of the principal agreement originally entitled the Government to 20% of the net profits each year, subject to a maximum of ₹40,000 per annum; the clause was later amended so that the Government was entitled to 10% of the net profits, "net profits" being defined as the amount for which the company's audited profits were assessed to income-tax. The obligation was expressed in the recital to be "also in consideration" of the transfer — which is what led the Kerala High Court to treat it as part of the purchase price.
  • For the assessment year 1958-59 the amount payable was ₹42,480. The company claimed it as a deduction under s. 10(2)(xv) of the 1922 Act. The Income-tax Officer and the Appellate Assistant Commissioner disallowed it; the Tribunal allowed it, holding that the payment was "expenditure made in order to earn the profits of the business and not an expenditure out of the earned profits".
  • The High Court of Kerala held it was capital expenditure — part of the purchase price — and not allowable. The Supreme Court reversed in 1966 (AIR 1967 SC 477), holding that the payment was not capital, and remitted the matter for decision of the remaining questions. On remand the High Court decided those questions; the matter came back to the Supreme Court, which decided it in 1972–73 (AIR 1973 SC 982).
2 · Issues
  1. First round (1966): Whether the annual payment of a percentage of net profits to the vendor-Government was part of the purchase consideration (capital) or an item of revenue expenditure deductible under s. 10(2)(xv).
  2. Second round (1973): Whether the payment was a diversion of income at source by an overriding title — in which case the profits never became the company's income at all — or merely an application of profits after they had accrued; and whether the requirements of s. 10(2)(xv) were satisfied.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee company
  • The payment is not related to any part of the purchase price of the assets: there is no capital sum fixed anywhere in the agreement to which the percentage could be tied, and no reference to a capital sum in clause 7 at all.
  • The obligation is indefinite in duration ("in every year") and is measured by the annual profits flowing from the trading activities, with no relation to the capital value of the assets. A payment which varies with the annual trading result is the paradigm of a revenue payment.
  • The mere fact that a capital sum may be payable by instalments does not convert it into revenue — but conversely, a payment of instalments presupposes a fixed sum; here there is none.
  • In the alternative, even if it is expenditure, it is laid out for the purposes of the business within s. 10(2)(xv): the Government had undertaken obligations under the agreement, and the payment is in lieu of them. And in any event the payment is a diversion of profits by an overriding title, so that the amount never accrued to the company.
For the Revenue
  • The payment is part of the consideration for the purchase of the three undertakings: the recital says that the transfer is "also in consideration" of the 20% (later 10%) share of profits. Whatever its form, therefore, the payment is a payment for acquiring the business, and so capital.
  • A payment which is expressed to be consideration for the acquisition of a going concern cannot be converted into revenue expenditure merely because it is quantified by reference to future profits; that is the classic device of the "instalment" purchase price.
  • The obligation, being part of the price of acquisition, cannot also be described as a diversion of income by an overriding charge; and even if it were, s. 10(2)(xv) would not be satisfied because the payment is not an outgoing in earning the profits but the discharge of the company's own liability.
4 · Decision

In the assessee's favour on both rounds. In the 1966 decision the Supreme Court held that "no single test of universal application can be discovered" for the capital/revenue question, that "the name which the parties may give to the transaction … and the characterisation of the receipt by them are of little consequence", and that the court must ascertain the "true nature and character of the transaction from the covenants of the agreement tested in the light of surrounding circumstances". On that footing it held that the percentage of net profits was not capital: it was payable for an indefinite period, related to annual trading profits, and not tied to any fixed sum agreed as part of the purchase price.

In the 1973 decision the Court dealt with the diversion question. The obligation to pay the share of profits was created by, and was a condition of, the very transaction by which the company acquired the undertakings; it was therefore a charge on the source itself, and the amount was diverted before it reached the company. The Court stated the test in the standard form: where, by an obligation, income is diverted before it reaches the assessee, it is deductible (indeed it is never the assessee's income); where income is required to be applied to discharge an obligation after it reaches the assessee, it is a mere application of income and is not deductible.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Substance over nomenclature, but substance includes the covenants. The label "commission" or "share of profits" does not decide anything; nor does the label "part of the purchase price". The court construes the agreement and asks what the payment is for.
  • (2) An indefinite, profit-linked payment tied to no fixed capital sum is revenue. That is the operative test of the 1966 decision, and it is expressly limited: had a fixed sum been agreed and made payable by instalments, the character would have been capital.
  • (3) Diversion at source defeats the charge altogether. Where a profit-sharing obligation attaches to the source (here, the very acquisition under which the profits arise), the share is diverted before accrual and is not the assessee's income. This links the case to the diversion/application doctrine (Topic 1) and makes it, in effect, an alternative to the "revenue expenditure" route.
  • (4) The case is fact-specific. Its own later history proves the point: Jalan Trading (§6.7) held that the High Court there had "over-generalised" the Travancore Sugars reasoning, and L.B. Sugar (§6.6) held that Travancore-Cochin Chemicals — a superficially similar case — "must be confined to the peculiar facts of that case".
Why it matters in the exam. It is the authority on payments measured by a percentage of profits: of a managing agent, of a technical collaborator, of a vendor of a business, of a promoter. The answer structure is (i) is a fixed capital sum ascertainable from the agreement? (ii) is the payment indefinite in duration? (iii) is it measured by annual trading profits? (iv) does the obligation attach to the source (diversion) or arise after the income has accrued (application)? Applied to a modern fact pattern ("the company agreed to pay the seller 10% of its annual profits for ten years as consideration for the sale of the business"), the case yields revenue treatment where no capital sum is fixed — which is why modern drafting almost always fixes one.

◆6.5 Case 21 — Empire Jute Co. Ltd. v. CIT

Empire Jute Company Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax, West Bengal

AIR 1980 SC 1946 / (1980) 4 SCC 25 · P. N. Bhagwati J. · appeal by special leave · DU reader pp. 114–122
Case 21Loom hoursEnduring benefit testOperating costs. 10(2)(xv)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a limited company manufacturing jute, with a factory in West Bengal and a certain number of looms. It was a member of the Indian Jute Mills Association, whose objects included adjusting the production of member mills to world demand.
  • From 1939, the members entered into a series of working time agreements restricting the number of hours per week for which looms could be worked: the first on 9 January 1939 (five years), the second on 12 June 1944, the third on 25 November 1949, and the fourth on 9 December 1954, to remain in force for five years from 12 December 1954. The appeal concerned the fourth agreement.
  • Under clause 4 no signatory was to work more than forty-five hours per week, subject to clauses 11 and 12; clause 5 provided that the allotted hours could not be carried forward to the next week; clause 10 allowed members with registered complements of looms not exceeding 220 to work up to 72 hours.
  • Clause 6(b) was the operative provision: a signatory could transfer part or all of its weekly allotment of hours to one or more other signatories, subject to conditions — the transfer had to cover a period of not less than six months; the agreement had to be submitted to the committee, whose decision was final; and the transfer had to be registered and certified. This transaction was commonly called a "sale of loom hours".
  • The assessee purchased loom hours from four jute mills for an aggregate of ₹2,03,255 during the year 1 August 1958 to 31 July 1959 and claimed the amount as revenue expenditure, as part of "the cost of operating the looms which constituted the profit-making apparatus".
  • The Income-tax Officer disallowed it. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Tribunal allowed it. The High Court was inclined to agree, but felt compelled by CIT v. Maheshwari Devi Jute Mills Ltd., (1965) 57 ITR 36 — where the amount received for the sale of loom hours had been held to be a capital receipt — to hold the payment to be capital expenditure, and disallowed it. The assessee appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the ₹2,03,255 paid for the purchase of loom hours was capital or revenue expenditure.
  2. Whether the decision in Maheshwari Devi Jute Mills — that the receipt for sale of loom hours is a capital receipt — compels the conclusion that the payment for purchase of loom hours is capital expenditure.
  3. Whether the tests of "enduring benefit", "fixed v. circulating capital" and "acquisition of a source of profit" required the payment to be treated as capital.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • No new asset was acquired: the looms, the factory and the profit-making apparatus remained exactly as they were. What the purchase bought was the ability to work the existing looms for longer hours — part of the operating cost of production.
  • The advantage was not enduring: the transferred hours were available for a minimum of six months and, in any event, had to be used in the week in which they fell and could not be carried forward.
  • The payment is nothing more than the cost of relaxing a self-imposed restriction on production — the analogue of buying quota rights for raw material, or of paying for additional power.
  • Whatever the position on the receipt side, a payment may be revenue in the payer's hands while being capital in the payee's hands: Racecourse Betting Control Board v. Wild, (1938) 4 All ER 487 (Macnaghten J.).
For the Revenue
  • The assessee had purchased a right — a right to produce more than it was otherwise entitled to produce. A right to produce additional goods is an intangible source of profit, and its acquisition is capital expenditure.
  • The advantage is of an enduring character: it lasts for the period of the agreement (and, in practice, for six-month blocks), and it results in a permanent addition to the assessee's productive capacity for that period. The test in Atherton is satisfied.
  • Maheshwari Devi held loom hours to be an asset, and an asset which can be sold for a capital sum is capital; its purchase must therefore be capital expenditure. Consistency requires the same answer on both sides of the transaction.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed. The payment was revenue expenditure, allowable under s. 10(2)(xv). Bhagwati J. first disposed of Maheshwari Devi with two propositions:

"[I]t is not a universally true proposition that what may be capital receipt in the hands of the payee must necessarily be capital expenditure in relation to the payer … a 'payment may be a revenue payment from the point of view of the payer and a capital payment from the point of view of the receiver and vice versa'." Reader p. 116, citing Racecourse Betting Control Board v. Wild

and, more fundamentally, that Maheshwari Devi had proceeded on the "commonly accepted basis" that loom hours were an asset, an assumption which was never examined: "The question is res integra and we must proceed to examine it on first principle." On first principle, the allotment of loom hours was "merely a contractual restriction on the right of every mill under the general law to work its looms to full capacity" — and "this restriction could not possibly be regarded as an asset". Hence:

"The amount spent on purchase of loom hours thus represented consideration paid for being able to work the looms for a longer number of hours. It is difficult to see how such payment could possibly be regarded as expenditure on capital account." Reader p. 118

The Court then examined each of the accepted tests and found that none of them pointed to capital. On enduring benefit:

"It is not every advantage of enduring nature, acquired by an assessee that brings the case within the principle laid down in this test. What is material to consider is the nature of the advantage in a commercial sense and it is only where the advantage is in the capital field that the expenditure would be disallowable … If the advantage consists merely in facilitating the assessee's trading operations or enabling the management and conduct of the assessee's business to be carried on more efficiently or more profitably while leaving the fixed capital untouched, the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future." Reader p. 119

On fixed v. circulating capital: acquisition of additional loom hours "does not add at all to the fixed capital"; the permanent structure "remains the same; it is not enlarged" (p. 119–120). On source of profit: "The source of profit or income was the profit-making apparatus and this remained untouched and unaltered" (p. 120). The Court then used the quota analogy — a payment for a quota right to obtain more raw material, or for additional power, is plainly revenue — and concluded with the tests of Dixon J. in Hallstrom's and Lord Clyde in Addie's Collieries: the payment was "part of the process of profit-earning", not the acquisition of a right of a permanent character. The Privy Council's decision in Commissioner of Taxes v. Nchanga Consolidated Copper Mines Ltd., (1964) AC 948 — compensation paid to a sister company to cease production, held revenue — was found to be the closest analogy, and IRC v. Carron Co. (removal of restrictions in an antiquated charter) reinforced the conclusion.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) No test is paramount. "The decided cases have, from time to time, evolved various tests … but no test is paramount or conclusive. There is no all embracing formula … no touchstone has been devised. Every case has to be decided on its own facts keeping in mind the broad picture of the whole operation."
  • (2) The enduring-benefit test is not mechanical. It applies only where the advantage is in the capital field. An advantage which merely facilitates trading, or enables the business to be carried on more efficiently or profitably, is revenue even if it endures indefinitely.
  • (3) The asymmetry of payer and payee. A receipt may be capital to the recipient and revenue to the payer. Consistency across the two sides of a transaction is not a legal requirement.
  • (4) An operating cost is revenue. Expenditure which buys the right to operate the existing profit-making structure more (stock, power, quota, working hours) is revenue; expenditure which adds to or enlarges that structure is capital.
  • (5) A restriction on capacity is not an asset. A contractual limitation accepted by members of a trade association confers no right; the purchase of a relaxation of that limitation buys only the ability to work longer, and that ability is not an asset.
Why it matters in the exam. Empire Jute is the single most useful case in this block, because it supplies the modern formulation of the enduring-benefit test and because it contains the sentence that decides most problems: "if the advantage consists merely in facilitating the assessee's trading operations … the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future." That sentence answers the Dec-2024 Q1 problem (PIPER's road and water reservoir) and it is the sentence on which L.B. Sugar and Bikaner Gypsums were both decided.

◆6.6 Case 22 — L. B. Sugar Factory & Oil Mills (P) Ltd. v. CIT

L. B. Sugar Factory and Oil Mills (P) Ltd., Pilibhit v. Commissioner of Income-tax, U.P.

AIR 1981 SC 395 · P. N. Bhagwati J. · appeal by certificate from Allahabad · DU reader pp. 123–127
Case 22Contributions for roadsGood citizenship v. businessEnduring benefits. 10(2)(xv)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a private limited company manufacturing and selling crystal sugar at Pilibhit, Uttar Pradesh. The assessment year was 1956-57, the accounting year ending 30 September 1955.
  • In 1952-53 the State of Uttar Pradesh constructed the Deoni Dam and the Deoni Dam–Majhala road. The Collector requested the assessee to contribute towards these works, and the assessee paid ₹22,332.
  • The assessee also paid ₹50,000 to the State under a Sugarcane Development Scheme framed as part of the Second Five Year Plan, towards the cost of constructing roads in the area around its factory. Under the scheme, one-third of the cost was borne by the Central Government, one-third by the State Government, and the remaining one-third by sugar factories and sugarcane growers.
  • The assessee claimed both amounts under s. 10(2)(xv). The Income-tax Officer disallowed both as capital. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner agreed. Before the Tribunal there was a difference of opinion between the Judicial Member (who held both amounts to be revenue) and the Accountant Member (who held them to be capital); the matter was referred to a third member, who did not decide the capital/revenue question at all, but held that the contributions were made "as a good citizen just as any other person would" and were therefore not laid out wholly and exclusively for the purpose of the business. The Tribunal rejected the claim.
  • The High Court, going by the third member's finding and the Accountant Member's view, held that the expenditure was not related to the business activity and was not dictated by commercial expediency, and answered the reference against the assessee.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the two contributions were laid out wholly and exclusively for the purpose of the business.
  2. Whether they were capital or revenue expenditure, the roads not belonging to the assessee and the assessee bearing only part of the cost.
  3. Whether the enduring-benefit test in Atherton required the ₹50,000 contribution to be treated as capital, and how the apparently contrary decision in Travancore-Cochin Chemicals was to be dealt with.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The roads around the factory were essential to the business: sugarcane has to be brought in and sugar has to be carried out. Roads mean tractors and trucks can reach the cane fields and the factory, which directly facilitates the manufacturing activity.
  • The assessee acquired no asset: the roads belonged to the Government of Uttar Pradesh, not to the company, and the company paid only one-third of their cost.
  • The expenditure was incurred for running the business or working it with a view to producing profits, without the assessee getting any advantage of an enduring benefit to itself — the very formula used in Lakshmiji Sugar Mills Co. (P) Ltd. v. CIT, AIR 1972 SC 159, which is directly in point.
For the Revenue
  • The contribution of ₹22,332 was made long after the dam and the road had been built, with no legal obligation and nothing to show that either work was advantageous to the business: it was an act of good citizenship and not a business outlay.
  • The ₹50,000 brought into existence an advantage of an enduring character — motorable roads around the factory, which would last for years — and therefore satisfied Lord Cave's test in Atherton.
  • The decision in Travancore-Cochin Chemicals Ltd. v. CIT, AIR 1977 SC 991 — where a contribution towards roads and a water supply scheme was held capital — is comparable and should be followed; and if there is a conflict, the later decision should prevail over Lakshmiji Sugar Mills.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed in part. The ₹22,332 was rightly disallowed; the ₹50,000 was revenue expenditure, deductible under s. 10(2)(xv).

On the ₹22,332: the amount was contributed after the dam and road had been constructed, without legal obligation, and "purely as an act of good citizenship"; there was nothing to show that the works were advantageous to the assessee's business. It was therefore not laid out wholly and exclusively for the business.

On the ₹50,000: the roads were around the factory, they facilitated the transport of sugarcane to the factory and the flow of manufactured sugar out of it, and the expenditure was therefore "clearly and indubitably connected with the business activity". As to character, the Court adopted its own reasoning in Empire Jute:

"It is not every advantage of enduring nature acquired by an assessee that brings the case within the principle laid down in this test … If the advantage consists merely in facilitating the assessee's business operations or enabling management and conduct of the assessee's business to be carried on more efficiently or more profitably while leaving the fixed capital untouched the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future." Reader p. 125

The Court held that the assessee acquired no asset of an enduring nature — the roads belonged to the Government and only part of the cost was contributed — and that although the advantage would last as long as the roads remained motorable, "it was not an advantage in the capital field, because no tangible or intangible asset was acquired … nor was there any addition to or expansion of the profit making apparatus". On the conflict with Travancore-Cochin Chemicals, the Court held that each case turns on its own facts, that Lakshmiji Sugar Mills was closer on facts, and — importantly — that in Travancore-Cochin Chemicals the parenthetical clause of Lord Cave's test (which allows for special circumstances leading to a contrary conclusion) "was not brought to the attention of this Court", so that the test had been applied there as if it were absolute and universal.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Two distinct questions, in order. First, was the expenditure laid out wholly and exclusively for the business? (The ₹22,332 failed here.) Second, if yes, is it capital or revenue? (The ₹50,000 passed the first and failed the second.) An answer which jumps to the capital question without asking the first is incomplete.
  • (2) Good citizenship is not business. A voluntary contribution, made without obligation, after the works were complete, and with no demonstrated benefit to the business, is not deductible — even if it is admirable.
  • (3) Facilitation is revenue; acquisition is capital. This is the operational distinction the case establishes, on the authority of Empire Jute.
  • (4) No asset, no capital. Where the asset created belongs to the State and the assessee contributes only a fraction of the cost, the capital-advantage theory is at its weakest.
  • (5) Cases are decided on facts, and later cases confine earlier ones. The treatment of Travancore-Cochin Chemicals — "confined to the peculiar facts of that case" — is a warning about relying on apparently similar decisions without checking what the Court assumed.
Why it matters in the exam. This is the case to cite where a business contributes to public infrastructure — roads, water supply, power lines, drainage, a bus shelter — near its plant. The answer must separate the two limbs: (i) business nexus (was there an obligation? was there a benefit?); (ii) character (was an asset acquired by the assessee? did it add to the profit-making apparatus?). It is also the case that explains why the Dec-2024 Q1 problem is not straightforward: PIPER built the road and the reservoir itself and claimed them; the analysis must ask who owns them and whether they merely facilitated operations.

◆6.7 Case 23 — CIT v. Jalan Trading Co. (P) Ltd. outside the reader

Source note — read first

Case 23 is listed in the reader's table of contents at printed p. 128, but its judgment is not printed in the reader. The card below is built from the reported decision, (1985) 155 ITR 536 (SC), decided on 9 August 1985 by Tulzapurkar, Sabyasachi Mukharji and Ranganath Misra JJ., reversing the Bombay High Court in IT Ref. No. 112 of 1963. It is flagged outside the reader for that reason.

Commissioner of Income-tax (Central), Bombay v. Jalan Trading Co. (P) Ltd.

(1985) 155 ITR 536 (SC) · Tulzapurkar, Mukharji and Misra JJ. · Civil Appeal No. 1733 of 1973, decided 9-8-1985 · outside the reader
Case 23Sole selling agency75% of net profitsAsset of enduring natureSs. 10(1), 10(2)(xv)
1 · Facts
  • A manufacturing company (Bharat Barrel & Drum Manufacturing Co. Ltd.) gave its sole selling agency to a partnership firm by an agreement for a period of two years, with a right of renewal.
  • The assessee, Jalan Trading Co. (P) Ltd., was a private company newly incorporated. Under a deed of assignment the benefits of the agreement were assigned to the assessee, which then carried on the business of selling agent of the manufacturing company. On the basis of the option of renewal exercised by the assessee, a fresh agreement in respect of the sole selling agency, again with a renewal clause, was entered into.
  • As a term of the agreement, the assessee paid 75% of its net profits in the relevant assessment year to the manufacturing company, and claimed that amount as a business deduction.
  • The Income-tax Officer disallowed the claim, treating the payment as capital; the appellate authorities and the Tribunal agreed, holding — even though no specific sum had been fixed for the acquisition of the agency — that the expenditure was incurred for acquiring an enduring benefit, and was therefore capital expenditure.
  • The Bombay High Court, following the reasoning of Travancore Sugars, held that the payment represented business expenditure deductible under s. 10(2)(xv). The Revenue appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the payment of 75% of net profits to the manufacturing company was a deductible business expenditure or expenditure incurred for the acquisition of a capital asset.
  2. Whether the fact that the payment was made in instalments measured by profits, rather than as a lump sum, changed its character.
  3. Whether, on the principle that income-tax is levied on real income, the assessee could claim that only 25% of its profits were taxable.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The payment was measured entirely by the profits of the year and had no relation to any capital sum; on the reasoning of Travancore Sugars, a profit-linked, indefinite payment is revenue.
  • No capital sum was fixed anywhere in the arrangement, so it is impossible to say that the assessee "acquired" anything for a price; the payment is a sharing of the fruits of the business, not the purchase of its tree.
  • Income-tax is levied on real income; if 75% of the profits must be paid away as a condition of earning them, only the balance of 25% is real income and the rest is deductible.
For the Revenue
  • What the assessee obtained in return for the payment was the sole selling agency itself — an asset of an enduring nature, which generated the income. Expenditure incurred to acquire such an asset is capital, whatever its form or mode of payment.
  • The absence of a fixed sum is not decisive. A capital asset may be acquired for a price which is calculated by reference to future profits; the transaction is nonetheless an acquisition, as Assam Bengal Cement Co. Ltd. v. CIT, (1955) 1 SCR 972, shows ("elimination of competition" and "payment for obtaining rights" are capital).
  • Travancore Sugars was decided on its own facts, where the payment was held not to be tied to any capital sum in the purchase of the undertakings; it cannot be generalised into a rule that every profit-linked payment is revenue.
  • The "real income" argument proves too much: if accepted, any assessee could divert his profits into a payment for a capital asset and escape tax on them.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed; the payment was capital expenditure and not deductible. The Supreme Court held that the assessee had acquired an asset of an enduring nature — the sole selling agency — in return for the payment, and that the expenditure therefore related to the acquisition of a capital asset and was not admissible as a deduction under s. 10(2)(xv). The Court held further that, the amount having been spent to obtain a capital asset, the assessee could not claim it as a deduction under s. 10(1) either; and that on the principle that income-tax is levied on real income, an amount paid for obtaining a capital asset is not deductible. The Court emphasised that the nature of expenditure depends on the purpose for which it was incurred, and not on the source of the payment or the timing or manner of payment — and that the High Court had over-generalised the reasoning in Travancore Sugars, which was confined to its own facts.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) An agency right can be a capital asset. A sole selling agency, acquired for a consideration, is an asset of an enduring nature; the cost of acquiring it is capital.
  • (2) Mode of payment is irrelevant. Instalments, a percentage of profits, or a formula do not convert the acquisition of a capital asset into revenue expenditure. What matters is the purpose: acquisition versus operation.
  • (3) "Real income" is not a licence to deduct capital outlay. The doctrine of real income operates within the statute; it cannot be used to deduct what the statute forbids.
  • (4) Precedents must be confined to their facts. The case is the leading caution against citing Travancore Sugars for a general proposition about profit-linked payments.
Why it matters in the exam. Use it for any question about payments for acquiring a right, an agency, a route permit, a franchise, a licence or a non-compete covenant — a large family of modern transactions. The answer is that if what is bought is the right to earn (the source), the price is capital; if what is paid for is the earning itself (an operating cost), it is revenue. Pair it with Travancore Sugars and explain that the two cases appear similar but were separated by the purpose: there, the payment was part of the price of an undertaking with no capital sum fixed and an indefinite obligation; here, the payment bought the agency itself.

◆6.8 Case 24 — Bikaner Gypsums Ltd. v. CIT

Bikaner Gypsums Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Rajasthan

AIR 1991 SC 227 / (1991) 1 SCC 328 · K. N. Singh J. · appeal from Rajasthan · DU reader pp. 128–135
Case 24Shifting a railway stationRemoval of obstructionMining leases. 37 of the 1961 Act
1 · Facts
  • Natural Science (India) Ltd., predecessor of the assessee, acquired from the Maharaja of the erstwhile Bikaner State a lease dated 29 September 1948 for mining gypsum for twenty years over 4.27 square miles at Jamsar, renewable on expiry. By a deed of assignment dated 11 December 1948 the rights were assigned to Bikaner Gypsums Ltd., a company in which the State Government held 45% of the shares.
  • The lease conferred wide liberties: to search for, win, work, get, raise, convert and carry away gypsum "in the most economic, convenient and beneficial manner" (Part II, cl. 1); to sink, dig, drive, quarry, erect and maintain borings, pits, shafts, inclines, tunnels, trenches and waterways (cl. 2); to erect engines, machinery, plant, furnaces and kilns (cl. 3); to make and use roads (cl. 4); to stack and deposit produce on the surface (cl. 7); and to occupy surface land (cll. 8–9).
  • Part III, clause 3 imposed a restriction: "No mining operations or working shall be carried on … at or to any point within a distance of 100 yards from any railway, reservoir, canal or other public works … except with the previous permission in writing of the Minister". The clause was inserted to protect the railway track and station which stood inside the leased area.
  • The assessee carried on mining over the entire area. The railways then extended their area, laid fresh track, provided a siding and constructed quarters inside the lease area without the assessee's permission. The assessee sued for ejectment and lost.
  • The assessee approached the State Government (which held 45%) and the Railway Board. A survey showed that high-quality gypsum — the raw material required by Sindri Fertilizers, a Government of India undertaking with whom the assessee had a supply contract for gypsum of a minimum 83.5% quality — lay under the railway area. All four parties (Sindri Fertilizers, the Government of Rajasthan, the Railway Board and the assessee) negotiated, and the Railway Board agreed to shift the station, track and yards to an alternative site offered by the assessee, the total cost of ₹12 lakh being borne equally by the four parties.
  • The assessee paid ₹3 lakh to the Northern Railway as its share, and a further ₹7,300 as compensation for surface rights. It then mined the erstwhile railway area and raised 6,30,390 tonnes of gypsum, which it supplied to Sindri Fertilizers.
  • For AY 1964-65 the assessee claimed the ₹3 lakh as a deduction. The Income-tax Officer held it capital; the Appellate Assistant Commissioner agreed; the Tribunal held it revenue. On a reference under s. 256 the High Court held that on payment of ₹3 lakh the assessee had acquired "a new asset which was attributable to capital of enduring nature" and held the expenditure capital. The assessee appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the ₹3 lakh paid to the Northern Railway was revenue expenditure allowable as a deduction, or capital expenditure.
  2. Whether the payment was made to acquire a new right (a right to mine under the railway area, which clause 3 of Part III restricted) or merely to remove an obstruction to the exercise of a right the assessee already had.
  3. Whether the benefit obtained — the ability to mine 6.30 lakh tonnes of high-grade gypsum within two years — was an "enduring benefit" of a capital nature.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The entire 4.27 square miles, including the land under the railway, had been demised to the assessee; it had the right to win the minerals beneath it. The railway station, track, buildings and quarters merely obstructed the exercise of that right.
  • The payment was made for the removal of the obstruction, not for the grant of any new right. Permission to mine near the railway could be, and was, granted by the authorities under clause 3 itself; the payment was not made for that permission but for the physical shifting of the installations.
  • The assessee acquired no new asset: it already owned the mining rights and had already paid the tender money, licence fee and other charges for them. Nor did it acquire any right over the railway's new site beyond what the lease gave it.
  • The benefit was short-lived: the mining in that area was completed within two years. An advantage which is exhausted in two years is not "enduring".
For the Revenue
  • By clause 3 of Part III the assessee had no right to the surface of the land occupied by the railways; it could not mine there without permission. By paying ₹3 lakh it acquired that right, and thereby an enduring benefit.
  • The payment brought into existence an advantage of a capital nature — access to a high-grade gypsum deposit which the assessee's supply contract required — and the High Court so found.
  • The expenditure was a once-and-for-all outlay for the acquisition of a right, not a recurring cost of working the mine.
4 · Decision

Appeal allowed; the ₹3 lakh was revenue expenditure. K. N. Singh J., after surveying the authorities — Assam Bengal Cement Co., Abdul Kayoom, Gotan Lime Syndicate, M. A. Jabbar, Carron Co. and Empire Jute — distilled the governing principle:

"In our opinion where the assessee has an existing right to carry on a business, any expenditure made by it during the course of business for the purpose of removal of any restriction or obstruction or disability would be on revenue account, provided the expenditure does not acquire any capital asset. Payments made for removal of restriction, obstruction or disability may result in acquiring benefits to the business, but that by itself would not acquire any capital asset." Reader p. 134

Applying it, the Court held that under the lease the assessee "had acquired full right to carry on mining operations in the entire area including the Railway Area", that clause 3 was "only restrictive in nature" and "did not destroy the assessee's right to the minerals found under the Railway Area", and that the payment "was not made … for the grant of permission … instead the payment was made towards the cost of removing the construction which obstructed the mining operations". There was therefore "no acquisition of any capital asset"; and since the mining was completed within two years, the High Court's finding of an enduring benefit was unsustainable.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Removal of obstruction is revenue. Where the assessee already has the right, expenditure to remove a restriction, obstruction or disability which impedes the exercise of that right is revenue, provided no capital asset is acquired.
  • (2) Restriction ≠ destruction of the right. A clause which restricts the manner of exercise of a right does not take the right away; the payment to relax it does not "acquire" the right.
  • (3) Look at what the payment was actually for. Not for the permission, but for the physical shifting of the installations — a distinction the High Court missed.
  • (4) Duration matters as evidence. The exhaustion of the benefit within two years negatived any "enduring" character.
  • (5) The tests are guidelines, not a code. The Court expressly noted that "the tests so laid down are not exhaustive and it is not possible to reconcile the reasons given in all of them, as each decision is founded on its own facts".
Why it matters in the exam. It is the modern authority for the "removal of disability" principle, which is the most useful of all the tests for contemporary fact patterns: payment to a municipality to shift a drain, to a neighbour to give up an easement, to a landlord to permit a change of use, to a power utility to shift a transmission line, to a workers' union to give up a restrictive practice. The test to state: did the assessee already have the right, and did the payment merely clear an obstacle to its exercise?

◆6.9 Case 25 — CIT v. General Insurance Corporation

Commissioner of Income-tax v. General Insurance Corporation

2007 (1) SCJ 800 · Ashok Bhan J. · appeal from Bombay · DU reader pp. 136–140
Case 25Bonus sharesShare issue expensesCapital employeds. 37
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was an insurance company with four subsidiaries. For AY 1991-92 it filed a return of income of ₹58,52,80,850 along with the audit report.
  • During the accounting year the assessee incurred expenditure separately for (i) the increase of its authorised share capital from ₹75 crore to ₹250 crore, and (ii) the issue of bonus shares. The Assessing Officer disallowed ₹1,04,28,500 — stamp duty and registration fees — treating it as expenditure attributable to the acquisition of a capital asset of a durable nature.
  • The CIT (Appeals) bifurcated the disallowance: the portion relating to the increase in authorised capital was held not allowable, following Bombay Burmah Trading Corpn. Ltd. v. CIT, (1984) 145 ITR 793 (Bom), and Richardson Hindustan Ltd. v. CIT, (1988) 169 ITR 516 (Bom); the portion relating to the issue of bonus shares was allowed as revenue expenditure, being covered by Bombay Burmah Trading.
  • The Tribunal upheld the CIT (Appeals) on the bonus-share point, observing that the basis of Brooke Bond India Ltd. v. CIT, (1997) 10 SCC 362, was that the expenditure related to the expansion of the capital base, whereas in the case of bonus shares "there does not take place an expansion of the capital base of the Company but only reallocation of the existing funds".
  • The High Court of Bombay affirmed. The Revenue appealed, and the Supreme Court granted leave on the question "whether … the expenditure incurred on account of share issue is allowable expenditure".
2 · Issues
  1. Whether expenditure incurred in connection with the issue of bonus shares is capital or revenue expenditure.
  2. Whether such expenditure is distinguishable from expenditure incurred on the issue of fresh shares or on an increase in authorised capital, which is admittedly capital.
  3. Whether the issue of bonus shares confers an enduring benefit on the company (increased creditworthiness, an enlarged capital base).
3 · Arguments
For the assessee company
  • An issue of fresh shares brings new funds into the company and expands its capital employed; that is plainly capital. But the issue of bonus shares by capitalisation of reserves is "merely a reallocation of a company's funds". There is no inflow of fresh funds and no increase in the capital employed, which remains the same.
  • Because the capital employed is unchanged, the issue of bonus shares confers no enduring benefit; the expenditure is therefore revenue.
  • Reliance was placed on CIT v. Dalmia Investment Co. Ltd., AIR 1964 SC 1464, Bombay Burmah Trading Corpn. and Richardson Hindustan.
For the Revenue
  • The issue of bonus shares increases the issued and paid-up capital of the company and is directly connected with the capital structure; the expenditure confers an enduring benefit, including enhanced creditworthiness.
  • Bonus shares are an integral part of the permanent structure of the company and are not connected with working capital; they are, in substance, no different from rights shares.
  • Reliance was placed on Ahmedabad Mfg. & Calico (P) Ltd. v. CIT, (1986) 162 ITR 800 (Guj), CIT v. Mihir Textiles, Gujarat Steel Tubes, CIT v. Ajit Mills, Vazir Sultan Tobacco Co. v. CIT (AP), Punjab State Industrial Development Corpn. and Brooke Bond India.
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed. The expenditure on the issue of bonus shares was revenue expenditure; the expenditure on the increase of authorised capital remained capital. Ashok Bhan J. held that the capital base "prior to or after the issuance of bonus shares remains unchanged", that "issuance of bonus shares does not result in any inflow of fresh funds or increase in the capital employed", and that it "is merely a reallocation of the company's fund". The Court illustrated the point with a hypothetical tabulation showing capital employed of ₹600 both before and after the issue. It rejected the Gujarat High Court's reasoning (that a bonus is first paid to the shareholders who pay it back) as contrary to Dalmia Investment, where Hidayatullah J. had explained that

"what the shareholder held as a whole rupee coin is held by him, after the issue of bonus shares, in two 50 np. coins. The total value remains the same, but the evidence of that value is not in one certificate but in two." Dalmia Investment Co. Ltd., quoted in the reader at p. 138

and that "the floating capital used in the company which formerly consisted of subscribed capital and the reserves now becomes the subscribed capital" — the money "remains where it was, that is to say, employed in the business". The Court also rejected the "creditworthiness" argument: even if the paid-up capital increases and creditworthiness improves, that does not make the advantage a benefit of an enduring nature creating a capital asset. The fresh-share decisions (Brooke Bond, Punjab State Industrial Development Corpn.) were distinguished as cases of inflow of fresh funds.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Test: does the capital employed change? Expenditure which brings in fresh funds and expands the capital employed is capital; expenditure which merely re-arranges existing capital is revenue.
  • (2) Bonus shares are a reallocation, not an augmentation. Capitalisation of reserves converts reserves into share capital but adds nothing to the resources at the company's disposal.
  • (3) Creditworthiness is not an asset. A general improvement in the company's standing is not an "advantage of an enduring nature" in the capital field.
  • (4) The enduring-benefit test, restated. The Court expressly derived its approach from Empire Jute — the expenditure must be "once and for all" and directed at bringing into existence an asset or advantage of enduring benefit in the capital field.
  • (5) Bifurcation is permissible. A single lump of "share issue expenses" may have to be split between the capital part (increase in authorised capital) and the revenue part (bonus issue).
Why it matters in the exam. It is the modern, and simplest, illustration of the enduring-benefit test actually being applied — with the Empire Jute qualification doing the work. It is also the standard authority on share-issue expenses: public issue of fresh shares = capital; bonus issue = revenue; increase in authorised capital = capital. And it is a useful reminder of the technique of bifurcation: where a single head of expenditure serves two purposes, the court will divide it.

Capital gains is the odd head: alone among the five, it taxes a realised accretion to capital instead of a periodic income. It is therefore built not on "receipts" but on three definitions — what a capital asset is (s. 2(14)), what a transfer is (s. 2(47)), and whether the asset is short-term or long-term (ss. 2(42A), 2(29A)) — and one computational formula (s. 48 read with s. 55). The single prescribed case, N. Bagavathy Ammal, is about the boundary of s. 46(2) — whether the word "assets" in that provision means "capital assets" as defined.

◆7.1 Section 2(14) — the capital asset

Section 2(14). "Capital asset" means property of any kind held by an assessee, whether or not connected with his business or profession, but does not include —

(i)any stock-in-trade, consumable stores or raw materials held for the purposes of his business or profession;
(ii)personal effects, that is to say, movable property (including wearing apparel and furniture) held for personal use by the assessee or any member of his family dependent on him, but excluding jewellery, archaeological collections, drawings, paintings, sculptures or any work of art;
(iii)agricultural land in India, not being land situated in any area which is comprised within the jurisdiction of a municipality (whether known as a municipality, municipal corporation, notified area committee, town area committee, town committee or by any other name) or a cantonment board and which has a population of not less than ten thousand, or in any area within such distance (not exceeding two kilometres) from the local limits of any municipality or cantonment board as the Central Government may notify;
(iv)–(vii)specified gold bonds, special bearer bonds (1991), gold deposit bonds under the 1999 scheme, and such other assets as the Central Government may notify.

The definition is a negative one: everything is a capital asset unless excluded. That is why the burden of establishing an exclusion lies on the assessee.

The two exclusions that matter most

Stock-in-trade (cl. (i)) — the same shares, land or goods may be a capital asset in one hand and stock-in-trade in another. A dealer in land holds land as stock; an investor holds it as capital. The question is the same "fixed v. circulating capital" question discussed in Empire Jute (§6.5), and it decides whether the profit is business income or capital gain.

Personal effects (cl. (ii)) — a car, a refrigerator, a watch, furniture and wearing apparel are not capital assets, so their sale produces no taxable capital gain. Jewellery and works of art are the exceptions: they are capital assets, taxed at 12.5% (long-term) without indexation from 23-7-2024.

Agricultural land: the two-way gate

Rural agricultural land is outside the definition, so its sale produces neither capital gain nor business income — a link back to Topic 2. Urban agricultural land (within the jurisdiction of a municipality or cantonment board with a population of 10,000 or more, or within two kilometres) is a capital asset, and its transfer is taxable.

Note the date-sensitive provision in Bagavathy Ammal: s. 47(viii) exempted any transfer of agricultural land in India effected before 1 March 1970. That cut-off is the reason the case had to be decided the way it was (§7.8).

◆7.5 Sections 46 and 47 — when there is no transfer

s. 46 — liquidation

46(1): where the assets of a company are distributed to its shareholders on liquidation, such distribution shall not be regarded as a transfer by the company for the purposes of s. 45.

46(2): where a shareholder, on the liquidation of a company, receives any money or other assets from the company, he shall be chargeable under the head "Capital gains" in respect of the money so received or the market value of the other assets on the date of distribution, as reduced by the amount assessed as dividend under s. 2(22)(c); and the sum so arrived at is deemed to be the full value of the consideration for the purposes of s. 48. This is the provision construed in Bagavathy Ammal.

s. 47 — transactions not regarded as transfer

The list includes: a distribution of assets on the total partition of a HUF; transfer under a gift, will or irrevocable trust; transfer by a HUF to its member; transfer by an individual to a HUF wholly for consideration; transfer in a scheme of amalgamation or demerger (with the corresponding exemption for the shareholders); conversion of bonds or debentures into shares; transfer of agricultural land in India effected before 1 March 1970 — s. 47(viii); transfer of a capital asset to a subsidiary by the holding company and vice versa; and transfer of shares in a scheme of lending and borrowing of securities.

Section 47 is a list of exempt transfers, not of exempt assets: the transaction is outside s. 45, but the transferee takes the asset at the transferor's cost (s. 49) and the accumulated gain is taxed when the transferee eventually sells. Section 47(viii) is the pivot of Bagavathy Ammal: it exempted the transfer of agricultural land before 1 March 1970, but the shareholders who received agricultural land on the company's liquidation in 1969 were held to be outside that exemption, because their receipt was governed by s. 46(2) and not by s. 45.

◆7.8 Case 26 — N. Bagavathy Ammal v. CIT

N. Bagavathy Ammal v. Commissioner of Income-tax

JT 2003(1) SC 363 · Ruma Pal J. · civil appeals, assessment year 1970-71 · DU reader pp. 141–144
Case 26"Assets" in s. 46(2)LiquidationAgricultural landMadurai Mills
1 · Facts
  • The appellants, two sisters, were shareholders in M/s. Palkulam Estate (Private) Ltd., Nagercoil. The company went into liquidation in 1964.
  • Under a compromise decree dated 22 December 1969 in litigation between the assessees and their brother (also a shareholder) and the company represented by the liquidator, the assets of the company — which included agricultural lands — were distributed to the appellants and eight others. The decree recorded that "as far as liabilities of Palkulam Estate Private Limited is concerned, the immovable properties be and hereby are distributed as indicated in schedule 'A' of the compromise".
  • The appellants received 479.89 acres of agricultural land before the end of the relevant accounting year (31 March 1970). The original assessment for AY 1970-71 was completed on 27 February 1971 and was reopened under s. 148.
  • The Income-tax Officer included the value of the share of agricultural land received by each appellant as capital gains under s. 46(2). The assessee's contention was that "assets" in s. 46(2) must be read subject to the definition of "capital asset" in s. 2(14), and that since agricultural land was excluded from that definition, the value could not be taxed.
  • The CIT (Appeals) accepted that contention; the Tribunal agreed, reasoning from ss. 45, 46(2) and 48 and from s. 47(viii) that "it is difficult to interpret s. 46(2) as including agricultural lands which is outside the scope of the income-tax". The High Court, on a reference under s. 256(1), reversed and held for the Revenue. The assessees appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the word "assets" in s. 46(2) must be understood and construed according to the definition of "capital asset" in s. 2(14).
  2. Whether the fact that agricultural land was excluded from the definition of "capital asset" prevented the taxation, under s. 46(2), of the market value of agricultural land received by a shareholder on the liquidation of a company.
  3. Whether s. 46(2) is an independent charging section, so that the exemptions in s. 47 (in particular s. 47(viii), which exempted pre-1-3-1970 transfers of agricultural land) do not apply to the shareholder's receipt.
3 · Arguments
For the assessees
  • Section 46(2) uses the word "assets" in a fasciculus of provisions — ss. 45, 46, 47, 48 — which are all built on the concept of the "capital asset". The word must be read consistently with that concept, and therefore as "capital assets".
  • Agricultural land is expressly outside s. 2(14). It cannot be a capital asset for s. 45 and a taxable "asset" for s. 46(2) in the same breath.
  • Section 47(viii) exempted "any transfer of agricultural land in India effected before 1st day of March, 1970". The distribution took place in 1969, so the transfer was exempt; if the receipt is a "transfer" it falls within the exemption, and if it is not a transfer it cannot be taxed at all.
  • On the principle of Bacha F. Guzdar (Topic 2), the character of the underlying asset does not change merely because it passes through a company; agricultural land remains agricultural land in the shareholder's hands.
For the Revenue
  • Section 46(2) is drafted in different language from s. 45. Section 45 speaks of a "capital asset"; s. 46(2) speaks of "any money or other assets". Parliament is presumed to have used the wider word deliberately.
  • Section 46(2) is an independent charging section with its own method of computation: it charges the money received or the market value of the assets on the date of distribution, as reduced by the amount assessed as dividend under s. 2(22)(c), and deems that sum to be the full value of the consideration for s. 48. It "does not make any reference to capital assets either in connection with the imposition of capital gains tax nor its computation".
  • The object of s. 46(2) was to overcome Madurai Mills — which had held that a distribution on liquidation is not a "transfer" — and to make the shareholder's receipt a taxable event for the first time. As CIT v. R. M. Amin said, "but for that sub-section … it would have been difficult to levy tax on capital gains to the shareholders on distribution of assets by a company in liquidation".
  • Section 47(viii) operates on transfers chargeable under s. 45; it has nothing to do with receipts under s. 46(2). And in any event the transfer by the company — a distribution on liquidation — was not a transfer at all (s. 46(1)).
4 · Decision

Appeals dismissed. "Assets" in s. 46(2) is not confined to "capital assets" as defined in s. 2(14); the market value of the agricultural land received on distribution was chargeable as capital gains under s. 46(2). Ruma Pal J. reasoned:

  • Section 46(2) is "in terms of an independent charging section", with "a distinct method of calculation of capital gains"; "the section does not make any reference to capital assets either in connection with the imposition of capital gains tax nor its computation".
  • "Having referred to 'capital asset' in section 45(1), 47 and 48, parliament appears to have deliberately chosen to use the word 'asset' in section 46(1) and (2), the ostensible intention being to bring assets of all kinds within the scope of the charge. It is not necessary to refer to a dictionary to hold that capital assets are a species of the genus 'assets'."
  • "If the words 'capital assets' and 'assets' as used in sections 45(1) and 46 respectively did not overlap then there was no need to provide for a non obstante clause in section 46(1) with reference to section 45. As correctly held by the High Court, agricultural land would have been a 'capital asset' but for the exclusion from the definition of 'capital asset' and what is not a capital asset may yet be an asset for the purposes of section 46(2)."
  • "Therefore, to the extent that a shareholder assessee receives assets whether capital or any other from the company in liquidation, the assessee is liable to pay tax on the market value of the assets as on the date of the distribution as provided under section 46(2)."
5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) A charging provision is construed on its own words. Where Parliament uses "capital asset" in one section and "asset" in another within the same fasciculus, the difference is presumed deliberate; a definition elsewhere in the Act is not read into the charging section.
  • (2) s. 46(2) is an independent charge with its own computation. It does not borrow either the charge or the machinery of s. 45, and therefore does not borrow its exclusions either.
  • (3) "Capital assets are a species of the genus 'assets'." The genus includes things excluded from the species — such as agricultural land.
  • (4) Liquidation receipts are a distinct taxable event. The company is not taxed on the distribution (s. 46(1)); the shareholder is taxed on the market value of what he receives (s. 46(2)).
  • (5) The wider lesson about interpretation. The case is a clean demonstration that tax statutes are construed on their own language, that a non obstante clause is evidence of a distinct charge, and that an exclusion from a definition does not necessarily travel to a differently worded provision.
Why it matters in the exam. Two uses. First, as the pure interpretation case in this topic — the one to cite for "the words of the charging section prevail over general definitions". Second, and more practically, it decides the facts of the Dec-2024 Q4 problem (Ms. NEENA received "agricultural land worth Rs. 25 lakhs during the liquidation process of a company … under final settlement against her share holding"): that receipt is taxable as capital gains under s. 46(2) at the market value of the land on the date of distribution, notwithstanding that agricultural land is excluded from s. 2(14). Capital gains in one paragraph — for the last five minutes

Ask five questions. (1) Is there a capital asset? (s. 2(14) — everything except stock-in-trade, personal effects other than jewellery and art, rural agricultural land and specified bonds.) (2) Is there a transfer? 08Head FIncome from other sources — sections 56 to 59

"Other sources" is the residuary head: it catches every income which is not chargeable under Heads A to E. Because it is residuary it is also the head with the least generous computation — s. 57 allows fewer deductions than s. 37, and s. 58 expressly forbids some. The single prescribed case, Rajendra Prasad Moody, is about the meaning of "for the purpose of making or earning such income" in s. 57(iii), and it produces one of the cleanest statements in the Act's case law of the distinction between purpose and result.

◆8.1 Sections 56 to 59

Section 56(1). Income of every kind which is not chargeable to income-tax under any of the heads specified in s. 14, items A to E, shall be chargeable to income-tax under the head "Income from other sources".

The residuary character is statutory. The head applies only after the other heads have been ruled out. So a receipt which can be brought under s. 22 or s. 28 must be brought there, not here — a point which follows directly from the exclusivity rule of East India Housing (§4.8).

◆8.3 Case 27 — CIT v. Rajendra Prasad Moody

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Rajendra Prasad Moody

(1978) 115 ITR 519 (SC) · P. N. Bhagwati J. · two references under s. 256 · DU reader pp. 145–147
Case 27Interest on borrowings. 57(iii)Purpose v. resultExempt income
1 · Facts
  • Two references under s. 256 were made directly to the Supreme Court because of a conflict of High Court decisions. The assessees in the two references were brothers.
  • Each had borrowed monies for the purpose of making investments in shares of certain companies. During AY 1965-66 (the accounting year ending 10 April 1965) each paid interest on the monies borrowed, but did not receive any dividend on the shares purchased with those monies.
  • Each claimed a deduction of the interest under s. 57(iii). The Income-tax Officer and the Appellate Assistant Commissioner rejected the claim on the ground that, the shares having yielded no dividend in the relevant year, the interest could not be regarded as expenditure laid out or expended wholly and exclusively for the purpose of making or earning income chargeable under the head "Income from other sources".
  • The Tribunal disagreed and allowed the claim. The question referred was: "Whether, on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, interest on money borrowed for investment in shares which had not yielded any dividend is admissible under s. 57(iii)?"
2 · Issues
  1. Whether interest paid on money borrowed to invest in shares is deductible under s. 57(iii) in a year in which the shares yielded no dividend at all.
  2. Whether s. 57(iii) requires that the expenditure should actually result in income, or only that it should be laid out for an income-earning purpose.
  3. Whether the narrower language of s. 57(iii) ("for the purpose of making or earning such income") than that of s. 37(1) ("for the purposes of the business or profession") compels a different construction.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The statute speaks of the purpose of the expenditure, not of its success. The money was borrowed to buy shares; shares produce dividend, which is chargeable under this head; the interest is therefore laid out to make or earn income of that character.
  • The argument that the purpose must fructify in the same year produces an absurd result: Rs 1,000 of expenditure would be deductible if Re 1 of dividend were received and not deductible if nothing were received. That cannot be the intention of the legislature.
  • Commercial accounting requires that whatever is proper expenditure be debited in the profit and loss account of a source; the credit side of the account may be nil without affecting the debit.
  • Eastern Investments Ltd. v. CIT, (1951) 20 ITR 1 (SC) — interpreting the ipsissima verba of s. 12(2) of the 1922 Act — held that "it is not necessary to show that the expenditure was a profitable one or that in fact any profit was earned".
  • Hughes v. Bank of New Zealand, (1938) 6 ITR 636 (HL): "Expenditure in course of the trade which is unremunerative is none the less a proper deduction … It does not require the presence of a receipt on the credit side to justify the deduction of an expense."
For the Revenue
  • The words "for the purpose of making or earning such income" are narrower than "for the purposes of the business". Making or earning income is a sine qua non of the deduction: if no income is made or earned, there is nothing to which the expenditure can be related.
  • The legislature deliberately chose different language in the two sections; the court must give effect to the difference and not read s. 37(1) into s. 57(iii).
  • The position was supported by the Patna High Court in Maharajadhiraj Sir Kameshwar Singh v. CIT, (1957) 32 ITR 377, and the Calcutta High Court in Sohanlal v. CIT, (1963) 47 ITR 1.
4 · Decision

References answered in favour of the assessees. The interest was deductible under s. 57(iii) even though no dividend had been received. Bhagwati J. held:

"What s. 57(iii) requires is that the expenditure must be laid out or expended wholly and exclusively for the purpose of making or earning income. It is the purpose of the expenditure that is relevant in determining the applicability of s. 57(iii) and that purpose must be making or earning of income. … It does not say that the expenditure shall be deductible only if any income is made or earned." Reader p. 146

The Court described the Revenue's construction as producing "a strange and highly anomalous result" — that expenditure of ₹1,000 would be deductible if Re 1 of dividend were received and wholly lost if no dividend were received — and said "it is difficult to believe that the legislature could have ever intended to produce such illogicality". It pointed out that in a profit and loss account "whatever is the proper expenditure allowed by the statute would be debited … and it would make no difference whether there is any income and if so, what", so that the ultimate income or loss is found regardless. It relied on Eastern Investments and Hughes v. Bank of New Zealand, treated the narrower language of s. 57(iii) as immaterial because that language is "clear and unambiguous", and expressly held that the contrary decisions of the Patna High Court in Kameshwar Singh and the Calcutta High Court in Sohanlal were incorrect.

5 · Principle evolved
  • (1) Purpose, not result. A deduction under s. 57(iii) depends on the purpose for which the expenditure is incurred, not on whether the purpose is fulfilled in that year. This is the central holding and the sentence to quote.
  • (2) Expenditure may be deductible even if the source produces nothing. An investment may yield no dividend in a year; the interest on the borrowing which financed it is nonetheless a proper outgoing of that source.
  • (3) Accounting principle supports the construction. The statute allows proper expenditure to be debited; a nil credit balance does not convert a proper debit into an improper one.
  • (4) A difference in language is not decisive where the meaning is plain. The Court refused to construe s. 57(iii) narrowly merely because s. 37(1) uses wider words.
  • (5) Nexus with a taxable source is still required. The case does not dispense with the requirement of an income-earning purpose — it only holds that the purpose need not succeed. That is why s. 14A later had to be enacted to deal with expenditure relating to exempt income, which is not "such income" at all (§1.5).
Why it matters in the exam. It is the standard authority for (i) interest on borrowings to buy shares or other investments as a deduction under s. 57(iii); (ii) the general proposition that an unremunerative expenditure can still be a proper deduction; and (iii) the interpretation of "wholly and exclusively" as directed at purpose. It is also the neatest illustration in the whole topic of how the head determines the deduction: the same interest, if the shares were stock-in-trade, would be deductible under s. 36(1)(iii) without any controversy about dividends. The head decides the question.

DU Dec 2024 · Q8(a)TheoryHead Ass. 15–17

"Discuss income under the head 'salary'"

Question. "Discuss the following: (a) Income under the head 'salary'."
Answer plan

1 · The charge and its conditions → 2 · The meaning of salary (s. 17(1)) → 3 · Allowances → 4 · Perquisites (s. 17(2)) → 5 · Profits in lieu (s. 17(3)) → 6 · Deductions (s. 16) → 7 · Retirement benefits → 8 · Case law → 9 · Conclusion.

1 · The charge

Income from salary is chargeable under s. 15, which taxes (a) salary due from an employer or former employer, whether paid or not; (b) salary paid or allowed though not due or before it became due; and (c) arrears of salary not charged earlier. Two conditions must be satisfied before anything is taxed under this head. First, there must be an employer–employee relationship — a relation of master and servant — subsisting when the remuneration was earned. Second, the sum must be received qua employee and not in an independent capacity. Section 15 charges on the basis of "due or paid, whichever is earlier", so both accrual and receipt are taxable events, and Explanation 1 prevents a second charge on the same salary. Explanation 2 excludes a partner's remuneration from a firm, which is business income under s. 28(v).

2 · The meaning of "salary" — s. 17(1)

"Salary" is defined inclusively and covers wages; annuity or pension; gratuity; fees, commissions, perquisites or profits in lieu of, or in addition to, salary; advance salary; leave encashment during service; the employer's contribution to a recognised provident fund above 12% of salary; contributions to an approved superannuation fund above ₹1,50,000 and to a notified pension scheme above ₹7,50,000; and sums received under a life insurance policy within the limits of s. 10(10D). The width of the definition reflects the policy that every benefit flowing from the employment relation should be taxed, however it is described.

3 · Allowances

Allowances fall into three classes. Fully taxable — dearness allowance, city compensatory allowance, medical allowance unless against bills, servant allowance, deputation and non-practising allowances. Partly exempt — house rent allowance under s. 10(13A) with Rule 2A (the least of actual HRA, rent paid less 10% of salary, and 50% or 40% of salary according to the city); and the special allowances notified under s. 10(14) with Rule 2BB (hill and border areas, transport allowance for the disabled, education allowance of ₹100 per month per child up to two children, hostel allowance of ₹300 per month per child). Fully exempt — foreign allowances under s. 10(7), allowances of judges, and allowances of United Nations employees under s. 10(45).

4 · Perquisites — s. 17(2)

A perquisite is a benefit in addition to salary, received by reason of the employment. Section 17(2) lists rent-free or concessional accommodation; the motor car facility; the services of a domestic servant; gas, electricity and water; free or concessional education; transport for private purposes; "any other benefit or amenity, service, right or privilege"; the employer's contribution to a superannuation fund above the prescribed limit; and ESOPs and sweat equity. The valuation is prescribed by Rule 3. The provisos exclude medical facilities within limits (including reimbursement up to ₹15,000) and interest-free loans up to ₹20,000. The crucial legal point is that a perquisite must vest: in CIT v. L.W. Russel, AIR 1965 SC 49, an employer's contribution to an annuity trust was held not to be a perquisite because the employee had no vested right until superannuation — the amount was neither "paid", nor "due", nor "allowed" to him within s. 7(1) of the 1922 Act.

5 · Profits in lieu of salary — s. 17(3)

These include compensation received on the termination or modification of the terms of employment; sums received under a keyman insurance policy; and the residuary category of amounts received from an employer or former employer, or from a provident or other fund, to the extent not taxable under any other provision.

6 · Deductions — s. 16

Only three deductions are allowed, which is the distinguishing feature of this head: the standard deduction under s. 16(ia) of ₹50,000 (₹75,000 under the new regime of s. 115BAC); the entertainment allowance for Government employees under s. 16(ii), being the least of the actual allowance, ₹5,000 and 20% of salary; and the tax on employment (professional tax) actually paid under s. 16(iii). Nothing else — no conveyance, books, or insurance — is deductible here.

7 · Retirement benefits

Gratuity is exempt under s. 10(10) up to ₹20 lakh on the prescribed formula; commuted pension under s. 10(10A); leave encashment on retirement under s. 10(10AA) up to ₹25 lakh; provident fund receipts under ss. 10(11) and 10(12); superannuation under s. 10(13); voluntary retirement compensation up to ₹5 lakh under s. 10(10C) and retrenchment compensation under s. 10(10B).

8 · Case law

Ram Pershad v. CIT, (1972) 2 SCC 696 — a managing director's salary, car allowance, free board and lodging, and commission of 10% of gross profits were salary, not business income. The Court held that the "supervision and control" test is not universal, that the real question is the construction of the articles and the agreement, that a managing director has "two functions and two capacities" (quoting Anderson v. James Sutherland (Peterhead) Ltd.), and that a commission waived after it had accrued remained taxable as salary. CIT v. L.W. Russel supplies the other boundary: a contingent benefit is not a perquisite until it vests.

9 · Conclusion

Salary is the most rule-bound of the heads: a wide inclusive definition, an exhaustive list of perquisites valued by rule, and a closed list of three deductions. Its two litigated frontiers are the status of the recipient (employment or independent capacity) and the timing of the receipt (accrual, payment, allowance or vesting). Both frontiers are governed by Ram Pershad and L.W. Russel respectively.

Marking notes. Provision-by-provision coverage earns the bulk; but the two cases are what separate a first-class answer. Cite Ram Pershad for the employment test and accrual, and L.W. Russel for vesting, and you have covered every sub-head of the question.

Step 2 — The framework: how the courts test the distinction

Section 37(1) allows any expenditure which is not covered by ss. 30–36, is not capital and not personal, and is laid out wholly and exclusively for the business. The character question is governed by the following tests, in the order in which they should be applied:

  1. The enduring-benefit test (Lord Cave in Atherton v. British Insulated & Helsby Cables Ltd., 10 Tax Cas 155) — expenditure made "once and for all" with a view to bringing into existence an asset or advantage for the enduring benefit of the trade is capital. But Empire Jute (reader p. 119) holds that the test applies only "where the advantage is in the capital field"; if the advantage "consists merely in facilitating the assessee's trading operations or enabling the management and conduct of the assessee's business to be carried on more efficiently or more profitably while leaving the fixed capital untouched, the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future".
  2. The fixed/circulating capital test (Lord Haldane in John Smith & Son v. Moore) — applied in Empire Jute, and inconclusive where the outlay falls into neither category.
  3. The "source of profit" test — expenditure to acquire a source of income is capital; to operate an existing source is revenue (Empire Jute, p. 120).
  4. The business point of view test (Dixon J. in Hallstrom's, adopted at reader p. 121) — "what the expenditure is calculated to effect from a practical and business point of view".
  5. The "integral part of the profit-earning process" test (Lord Clyde in Addie's Collieries).
  6. The removal-of-disability test (IRC v. Carron Co.; adopted as the ratio in Bikaner Gypsums, reader p. 134).
  7. The capital-employed test (General Insurance Corporation, reader p. 139).
Step 3 — Application to the road (₹50 lakh)

Two features must be investigated before a conclusion is possible, and a good answer says so.

Factual featureConsequence
Who owns the road and the land beneath it? If the road is on land owned by PIPER and remains its asset, it is a capital asset (a building/structure within s. 32) and the expenditure is capital, recovered through depreciation. If the road belongs to the State or to the project partnership, PIPER has acquired no asset at all — the case falls close to L.B. Sugar Factory.
Does the road merely facilitate the transport of men and material, or does it constitute the business itself? For a construction company, road-building is the business; a road built for its own use to reach a warehouse is an internal facility. It "facilitates the business operations" — the language of L.B. Sugar, where the Supreme Court held a ₹50,000 contribution towards roads around a sugar factory to be revenue because the roads belonged to the Government, only part of the cost was contributed, and no asset was acquired.
Is the advantage co-extensive with the contract or indefinite? The warehouse is built under a contract with the State. If access to the warehouse is needed only for the duration of that contract, the advantage is not of an enduring character in the capital field.

Reasoned conclusion on the road: the better view — the one consistent with L.B. Sugar, Lakshmiji Sugar Mills and the qualification of the Atherton test in Empire Jute — is that the road expenditure is revenue if PIPER has not acquired an asset and the road merely facilitates the movement of materials to the warehouse; but it is capital (with depreciation) if PIPER owns the road and the land and the road is an enduring structure of its own profit-making apparatus. The determinative fact is ownership, exactly as it was in L.B. Sugar and, on the property side, in Jodhamal Kuthiala.

Step 4 — Application to the water reservoir (₹20 lakh)

Here the analysis tilts the other way. A reservoir near the warehouse built "in order to ease its business operation" is, on its face, a permanent structure which will serve the business for years, and it is normally an asset of the enterprise — a capital asset (land and building, or plant) on which depreciation is admissible under s. 32. The contrast with Empire Jute is instructive: loom hours could not be carried forward from one week to the next and were exhausted in their use, whereas a reservoir is consumed only in the sense that any fixed installation is. The case which the Revenue would rely on is Travancore-Cochin Chemicals Ltd. v. CIT, AIR 1977 SC 991 (contribution towards roads and a water supply scheme held capital) — but note that L.B. Sugar confined that decision "to the peculiar facts of that case" and observed that the parenthetical clause of Lord Cave's test had not been brought to the court's attention there. Conclusion: the reservoir is more likely to be capital, unless the facts show that PIPER built it for the State or the partnership and retains no ownership or enduring advantage.

Step 5 — The two further points that earn marks

(a) Capital does not mean lost. Where the expenditure is capital, the assessee is not without relief: the asset enters the block of assets and depreciation is allowed under s. 32 (and, for buildings and plant, additional depreciation where applicable). The AO's disallowance of the deduction is therefore only half an answer.

(b) Do not confuse a capital outlay with an operating cost. The distinction is what the money was laid out for: to acquire an asset of an enduring nature (capital) or as an outgoing in the doing of the business (revenue) — Mysore Sugar, reader p. 111. An outlay may facilitate the business and last for years and still be revenue, as Empire Jute and L.B. Sugar both demonstrate.

Marking notes. The examiner is asking for a method, not a verdict: "suggest how to test and differentiate" is the operative phrase. Give the seven tests, apply them item by item, and state that no test is conclusive (Empire Jute). Then take a position, but say what fact would change it — that is what distinguishes an answer on this topic.

Ms. NEENA — a s. 54 house sold within three years, a dividend, and land received on liquidation

Question. "Ms. NEENA earned a capital gain of Rs. 20 lakhs by selling her residential house property on 30th Nov., 2018-19. She acquired that property on 10th May 2016-17 under the exemption scheme of reinvestment u/s 54 of the Income Tax Act, 1961. In the year 2018-19, she received a dividend income of Rs. 1 lakh from a company MMR Ltd., engaged in agriculture business, out of which Rs. 60,000 was claimed as exemption under agriculture income. She also received an agricultural land worth Rs. 25 lakhs during the liquidation process of a company (KKS Ltd.) under final settlement against her share holding. In year 2020-21, the Income Tax Officer (ITO) reopened the assessment and issued notice to Ms. NEENA u/s 148 with a view that whole amount shall be charged to tax under the head capital gain. ITO also rejected the exemption of Rs. 60,000 claimed as agricultural income received as dividend from MMR Ltd. As an income tax law practitioner, discuss the arguments citing landmark cases."
Step 1 — Issues
  1. Whether the ₹20 lakh gain on the sale of the house on 30-11-2018 is short-term or long-term, and what is the effect of the transfer within three years of acquisition on the s. 54 exemption claimed in AY 2017-18.
  2. Whether the dividend of ₹1 lakh from an agricultural company can be claimed as agricultural income to the extent of ₹60,000.
  3. Whether the agricultural land worth ₹25 lakh received on the liquidation of KKS Ltd. is taxable as capital gains, notwithstanding that agricultural land is excluded from "capital asset".
  4. Whether the s. 148 notice issued in 2020-21 is sustainable, and whether the ITO is right in saying that the "whole amount" is chargeable under the head capital gains.
Step 2 — The house and s. 54(3)

Two consequences follow from the dates. First, the holding period was 10 May 2016 to 30 November 2018 — two years and six months. Under the law as it stood in AY 2019-20, immovable property had to be held for more than thirty-six months to be a long-term capital asset (the period was reduced to twenty-four months only by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, with effect from 23-7-2024). The ₹20 lakh was therefore a short-term capital gain, taxable at slab rates and not at the long-term rate.

Second, and more importantly, the property had been acquired in 2016 in order to claim exemption under s. 54 on an earlier sale. Section 54(3) provides that where the new house is transferred within three years of its acquisition, the capital gain exempted earlier is withdrawn: the amount exempted is deemed to be the capital gain of the previous year in which the new asset is transferred. The sale on 30 November 2018 is within three years of 10 May 2016. Hence, in AY 2019-20, Ms. NEENA is taxable on (i) the ₹20 lakh short-term gain on the sale of the new house, and (ii) the gain which was exempted in 2016-17. The exemption is not a permanent benefit; it is conditional on three years' retention.

Step 3 — The dividend from the agricultural company

The claim to treat ₹60,000 of the dividend as agricultural income is unsustainable. A dividend is not rent or revenue derived from land; it is derived from the shareholding, which is a contractual relation between the company and the shareholder. The company is a juristic person distinct from its shareholders; it owns the land and earns the agricultural income, and the shareholder owns only a right to participate in declared profits. This is the precise holding of Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 74, where it was held that even a dividend paid out of profits which are 60% agricultural in the company's hands is wholly taxable in the shareholder's hands. The dividend is chargeable under the head "Income from other sources" under s. 56(2)(i) (Topic 2 and §8.1).

Step 4 — The agricultural land received on liquidation

This is the point of the question, and it is governed squarely by N. Bagavathy Ammal v. CIT, JT 2003(1) SC 363 (§7.8). Under s. 46(2), a shareholder who receives any money or other assets from a company on its liquidation is chargeable under the head "Capital gains" in respect of the money received or the market value of the other assets on the date of distribution, reduced by the amount assessed as dividend under s. 2(22)(c); the resulting sum is deemed to be the full value of the consideration for s. 48. The Supreme Court held that the word "assets" in s. 46(2) is not controlled by the definition of "capital asset" in s. 2(14), because s. 46(2) is an independent charging section with its own computation, and because "having referred to 'capital asset' in section 45(1), 47 and 48, parliament appears to have deliberately chosen to use the word 'asset' in section 46(1) and (2)"; "capital assets are a species of the genus 'assets'" and "what is not a capital asset may yet be an asset for the purposes of section 46(2)".

Accordingly, the ₹25 lakh of agricultural land is taxable as capital gains in the year of distribution. The exemption in s. 47(viii) does not assist: it applied only to a transfer of agricultural land effected before 1 March 1970 and, in any event, operates on transfers chargeable under s. 45, not on receipts under s. 46(2).

Step 5 — The reassessment

The notice under s. 148 was issued in 2020-21 in respect of AY 2019-20, i.e. within four years of the end of the assessment year, which is the ordinary period. The validity of the notice will turn on whether the Assessing Officer had reason to believe that income had escaped assessment on the basis of tangible material, and whether the material was already disclosed in the return — in which case the reassessment may be vulnerable as a mere change of opinion. The assessee's arguments are: (i) the sale of the house and the s. 46(2) receipt were disclosed; (ii) the only issue is one of characterisation, which was considered at the original assessment; and (iii) the sanction and the reasons recorded must satisfy the statutory requirements.

On the merits, the ITO is partly right and partly wrong. Right: the ₹20 lakh gain, the revived s. 54 gain, and the ₹25 lakh of land are all capital gains (the land by force of s. 46(2)). Wrong: the dividend of ₹1 lakh is not a capital gain at all — it is income from other sources, taxable at slab rates. A notice which proposes to tax the "whole amount" under the head capital gains is therefore based on an erroneous application of s. 14, and the assessee may resist to that extent.

Conclusion
  • ₹20 lakh: short-term capital gain (holding 30 months), taxable at slab rates in AY 2019-20.
  • The s. 54 gain of 2016-17 stands withdrawn under s. 54(3) and is taxable in the year of the transfer of the new house.
  • ₹1 lakh dividend: fully taxable under "other sources"; the ₹60,000 claim fails — Bacha F. Guzdar.
  • ₹25 lakh land on liquidation: taxable under s. 46(2) — N. Bagavathy Ammal.
  • The reassessment is competent if the reasons satisfy s. 148, but cannot succeed to the extent that it characterises the dividend as a capital gain.
Marking notes. This question is a composite one and the marks are distributed: holding-period computation, s. 54(3), dividend characterisation, s. 46(2), and reassessment. The two "landmark" cases the examiner is looking for are Bacha F. Guzdar and Bagavathy Ammal — both of which appear in the DU material (the first in the agricultural income chapter, the second in this one), which is why this question can be answered wholly from the reader.

Q7. Supreme Court tests differentiating Revenue vs. Capital Expenditure under PGBP (s. 37(1))

Question (as printed): "Discuss in detail, the various tests applied by the apex court to differentiate between the treatment of revenue and capital expenditures under the head profits and gains of business and profession."

Statutory Gateway — s. 37(1) ITA, 1961: A residuary business expenditure is deductible only if (i) NOT of capital nature, (ii) NOT personal, and (iii) laid out wholly and exclusively for business. Since “capital expenditure” is undefined, courts apply cumulative commercial tests:

  • 1. Once-for-all Payment vs. Recurring Outlay: Vallambrosa Rubber Co. v. Farmer (weeding/tending immature rubber trees = recurring revenue). Not conclusive alone (Atherton).
  • 2. Enduring Benefit Test (Viscount Cave): British Insulated & Helsby Cables v. Atherton [1926] AC 205 — expenditure made “once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade” is capital.
  • 3. Qualification in Empire Jute Co. Ltd. v. CIT (1980) 124 ITR 1 (SC): Even an enduring advantage is REVENUE if it leaves the fixed capital / profit-making apparatus untouched and merely enables existing business to operate more profitably or efficiently (e.g., purchase of loom hours!).
  • 4. Profit-Earning Apparatus vs. Profit-Earning Process: Assam Bengal Cement Co. v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 89 (Bhagwati J. — fixed capital vs circulating capital: securing enduring monopoly protection = capital); contrasted with L.B. Sugar Factory & Oil Mills v. CIT (1980) 125 ITR 293 (SC) (contributing to State-owned cane-supply roads facilitates movement of raw material without acquiring a capital asset = Revenue!) and Bikaner Gypsums Ltd. v. CIT (1991) 187 ITR 39 (SC) (payment to shift railway station/structures obstructing existing mining rights = Revenue!).
  • 5. Nature of Business & Commercial Expediency: Judge from a practical businessman's standpoint (Alembic Chemical Works v. CIT, 177 ITR 377 SC — acquiring know-how to improve existing penicillin strain = revenue).

Q8(A). Short Note — Section 14 of the Income Tax Act, 1961 (Heads of Income)

Question (as printed): "Write short notes on the following : (A) Section 14 of the Income Tax Act, 1961"

1. Text & Scope: Save as otherwise provided, all income shall, for the purposes of charge of income-tax and computation of total income, be classified under five mutually exclusive heads: (A) Salaries (ss. 15–17); (C) Income from House Property (ss. 22–27); (D) Profits & Gains of Business or Profession (ss. 28–44DB); (E) Capital Gains (ss. 45–55A); and (F) Income from Other Sources (ss. 56–59). 2. Cardinal Doctrines Laid Down by the Supreme Court:
• Mandatory Classification & Mutual Exclusivity (Nalinikant Ambalal Mody v. S.A.L. Narayan Row, 61 ITR 428 SC & United Commercial Bank v. CIT, 32 ITR 688 SC): Specific heads are watertight compartments. Once a receipt falls under its specific head, it cannot be taxed under the residuary head (s. 56) even if no computation machinery taxes it under the specific head!
• Rental Income Precedence (East India Housing & Land Dev. Trust v. CIT, AIR 1961 SC 1570 vs. Chennai Properties): Rental income from owning house property normally falls under s. 22 regardless of corporate objects, unless letting itself constitutes the sole business.
• Heads Are Only Computation Channels (CIT v. Chugandas & Co.): Tax is one single levy under s. 4; s. 14 does not impose separate taxes but supplies distinct computation deductions & exemptions per head.

◆10.3 Forty points for the last hour

Framework (§1)

  1. s. 14 classifies; ss. 15–59 compute. Both stages matter.
  2. "Save as otherwise provided" — exemptions and special rates override the heads.
  3. Five heads since 1-4-1989; six before.
  4. Head B (interest on securities) omitted by the Finance Act, 1988.
  5. Heads are mutually exclusive as between receipts, not as between persons or years.
  6. Specific head prevails over the general; "indirectly covered" is not enough (East India Housing).
  7. "Other sources" is residuary but is still a head with its own charge and its own deductions.
  8. Classification decides the deductions, the rates, the set-off and the person chargeable.

Salaries (§2–3)

  1. Two conditions: employer–employee relation, and receipt qua employee.
  2. s. 15 charges on "due or paid, whichever is earlier"; advance salary is taxed.
  3. A partner's remuneration is not salary (s. 15 Expl. 2; s. 28(v)).
  4. A perquisite must vest (L.W. Russel).
  5. "Paid", "due" and "allowed" are not synonyms.
  6. Only three deductions: s. 16(ia), (ii), (iii).
  7. Entertainment allowance is for Government employees only.
  8. HRA exemption is the least of three figures.
  9. Waiver after accrual does not avoid tax (Ram Pershad).
  10. The control test is a guide, not a rule; read the articles and the agreement.

House property (§4)

  1. Four ingredients: building; ownership; not own-business occupation; income from letting.
  2. Owner = rights in his own right + entitlement to income (Jodhamal).
  3. s. 27 deems five classes of owner — memorise them.
  4. Annual value is notional; "might", not "can" (Biman Behari).
  5. Let out: higher of expected and actual rent.
  6. Municipal taxes are deducted only if paid by the owner, and before the 30%.
  7. 30% under s. 24(a) is flat and absorbs repairs, insurance and collection.
  8. Interest: unlimited for let property; ₹2,00,000 for self-occupied; pre-construction in five instalments.
  9. Two houses may be self-occupied at nil value since FA 2019.
  10. Co-owners with definite shares are taxed separately (s. 26).

Business, capital gains, other sources (§5–8)

  1. s. 29: compute under ss. 30–43D; the deduction code is statutory.
  2. Bad debt: business debt + mercantile accounts + irrecoverable + written off.
  3. An advance against the price of stock is revenue (Mysore Sugar).
  4. No universal capital/revenue test; the enduring-benefit test is qualified.
  5. Facilitation is revenue; acquisition is capital (Empire Jute, L.B. Sugar).
  6. Removal of an obstruction to an existing right is revenue (Bikaner Gypsums).
  7. Bonus shares leave the capital employed unchanged (General Insurance).
  8. Capital asset excludes stock-in-trade, personal effects (except jewellery/art) and rural agricultural land.
  9. s. 46(2) is an independent charge; "assets" is wider than "capital assets" (Bagavathy Ammal).
  10. s. 57(iii) requires a purpose, not a result (Moody).

◆10.4 The ten commonest errors

ErrorCorrection
Treating "mutually exclusive" as meaning an assessee can have only one head.It means one head per receipt. An assessee may have all five.
Saying Head B is "suspended" or "merged".It was omitted by the Finance Act, 1988 w.e.f. 1-4-1989.
Citing only s. 15 for salary and forgetting that "due" is sufficient.s. 15(a) taxes salary due, whether paid or not — the point of Ram Pershad.
Allowing a perquisite because the employer paid something.Ask whether it vested. If not, no perquisite — L.W. Russel.
Deducting 30% under s. 24(a) from the rent rather than from net annual value.Municipal taxes come out first; 30% applies to the NAV.
Treating a house occupied by parents as self-occupied.It is not; only two houses may be taken at nil (s. 23(4)) and the choice must be exercised.
Saying "the heads are exclusive, so a capital receipt cannot be business income".Exclusivity operates on income, not on receipts; and s. 28 expressly taxes some capital receipts (e.g. s. 28(va)).
Citing Atherton as if it were conclusive.Quote the parenthetical clause and Empire Jute's qualification at reader p. 119.
Assuming a capital outlay is lost.It is recovered through depreciation (s. 32) or as cost of the asset.
Treating a dividend from an agricultural company as agricultural income.Bacha F. Guzdar: it is not — it is income from other sources.

◆10.5 Three answer templates

Template 1 — the classification problem

(a) List the receipts and identify the assessee's sources. (b) For each receipt, run the drill in §1.6: exempt? employee? property? business/transfer? residuary? (c) State the charging section and the computation provision for each head. (d) Cite the authority for the classification (East India Housing for property v. business, Ram Pershad for salary v. business, Bagavathy Ammal for the width of a charging section). (e) Compute head-wise, then aggregate; note the set-off consequences.

Template 2 — the capital/revenue problem

(a) s. 37(1) and its four conditions. (b) "No test is paramount" — Empire Jute. (c) Apply the seven tests in sequence (§6.2). (d) Analogy: which prescribed case is closest, and why the nearest contrary case does not govern. (e) Conclusion and consequence (allowance, or depreciation). (f) One line on what fact would have changed the answer.

Template 3 — the house-property problem

(a) Is there a building and an owner (including a deemed owner under s. 27)? (b) Is any part occupied for the owner's own business (outside s. 22)? (c) Bare letting or business letting (East India Housing v. Sultan Brothers)? (d) Annual value under s. 23: let, vacant, self-occupied, two-house rule. (e) Less municipal taxes paid by the owner; less 30% (s. 24(a)); less interest (s. 24(b), with the ceiling for self-occupied). (f) Co-owners separately (s. 26); arrears later (s. 25A).

The one paragraph to write if you have one paragraph

"Section 14 requires all income to be classified under five heads (six before the omission of Head B by the Finance Act, 1988 w.e.f. 1-4-1989), because the head determines the charging section, the deductions, the rate and the person chargeable. The heads are mutually exclusive as between receipts: a receipt which falls within a specific head cannot be taxed under another head merely because it is indirectly covered by it (East India Housing). Each head then supplies its own charge and its own computation: salary on a due-or-paid basis with three deductions (Ram Pershad, L.W. Russel); house property on a notional annual value with two deductions (Biman Behari Shaw, Jodhamal Kuthiala); business on real profits with the statutory deduction code, including the capital/revenue divide of s. 37(1) (Empire Jute, Mysore Sugar, Bikaner Gypsums); capital gains on realised gains from the transfer of a capital asset (Bagavathy Ammal); and other sources as the residuary head, whose s. 57(iii) turns on the purpose of the expenditure rather than on its success (Rajendra Prasad Moody)."
