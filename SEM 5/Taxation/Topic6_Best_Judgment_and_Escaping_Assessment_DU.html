Assessment:
the estimate and the reopening

How the Department quantifies the tax — from the return under s. 139, through the notice under s. 142 and the assessment under s. 143, to the two extraordinary powers: best judgment when the assessee has defaulted (s. 144) and reassessment when income has escaped (ss. 147–153). Five prescribed cases, all briefed in the full five-part format, and a single unifying question: what must the officer have before he may substitute his own figure for the assessee's?

Primary source: DU case material (VI Term reader, pp. 168–189) 5 prescribed cases (32–36) Every provision, 139 to 153 Dec-2024 Q5 + Q3 answered

00OrientationHow this document is built & what to read first

Every other topic in this paper tells you what is taxed. This topic tells you how the figure is arrived at, and what happens when the assessee and the Department disagree about it. It is the procedural heart of the Act, and it is where the Constitution enters income-tax: the power to estimate another man's income, and the power to reopen a completed assessment years later, are the two points at which the administration can most easily oppress, and therefore the two points at which the courts have insisted most firmly on jurisdictional conditions.

The one question that unifies the topic

Both extraordinary powers in this topic — s. 144 and s. 147 — let the Assessing Officer substitute his own figure for the assessee's, or for the figure at which he was earlier assessed. In both, therefore, the law asks the same question: what must he have before him? Under s. 144 he must have default by the assessee plus all relevant material gathered plus an opportunity of being heard. Under s. 147 he must have reason to believe — and after 2021, information which suggests — that income has escaped, and that belief must rest on material with a "live link" to the escapement. The five prescribed cases are all about that question: Velukutty on what a "best judgment" may rest on; Burlop, Gemini, Lakhmani and Srikrishna on when a belief is a belief and not a suspicion.

01Section 1The machinery of assessment — and the two extraordinary powers

An assessment is a determination. The Act does not expect the assessee's return to be the last word, nor the officer's first impression to be the judgment. It builds a graduated structure: the assessee assesses himself and pays; the Department checks the arithmetic; a small proportion of cases is taken up for scrutiny; where the assessee defaults, the officer judges as best he can; and where income has escaped, a completed assessment may be reopened. Each step has its own conditions, its own limits and its own remedy, and the examiner's favourite question — "distinguish best judgment assessment from income escaping assessment" — is really a question about which step you are on.

"Assessment" — the word itself

The Act does not define "assessment". Section 2(7) says an "assessee" is a person by whom tax or "any other sum of money" is payable, and includes a person deemed to be an assessee; "assessment" is used throughout the Act in three senses: (i) the process of determining income; (ii) the figure determined; and (iii) the order by which it is determined. Nothing in this topic turns on the distinction more sharply than Change of opinion under s. 147: a completed assessment, once made, is final subject to appeal and revision, and a mere change of view on the same facts is not "income escaping assessment". See §5.7.

The sentence that ties the two together

"Both powers permit the Assessing Officer to depart from the assessee's own figure, and both are therefore treated as jurisdictional in character: under s. 144 the jurisdiction arises from the assessee's default and is exercised through a judicial estimate which must have a reasonable nexus to the material gathered (Velukutty); under s. 147 it arises from the officer's belief, which must rest on material having a live link with the escapement and must not be a mere change of opinion (Lakhmani Mewal Das). The difference is one of occasion, not of discipline: in both cases the officer must show his work."

02Section 2s. 139 — the return of income: who files, by when, and what if he does not

The whole machinery begins with a voluntary act. Income-tax is a self-assessed tax, and s. 139 turns the assessee's own declaration into the foundation of every subsequent step: the summary intimation under s. 143(1), the scrutiny under s. 143(3), the best judgment under s. 144 where no return comes, and even the reopening under s. 147, because the words "omission or failure to make a return" are the oldest ground of escaped assessment. An examination question on assessment almost always begins here, and a failure to state which return, filed when, is fatal to the rest of the answer.

◆2.1 Who must furnish a return

The obligation in one sentence

Section 139(1) requires every person — being a company; a firm; or a person other than a company or firm whose total income (or the total income of any other person in respect of which he is assessable) during the previous year exceeded the maximum amount which is not chargeable to income-tax — to furnish a return of his income in the prescribed form and verified in the prescribed manner. The obligation is reinforced by several extended categories, and by a set of circumstances in which a return must be filed even when the income is below the exemption limit.

The classes of persons obliged to file — and the reason each is included
CategoryWhy the law places him under an obligation

Any other person, whose total income exceeds the maximum amount not chargeable to taxThe ordinary case. "Total income" is computed under the five heads after set-off and deductions, and includes income of another person clubbed with his (Topic 5).
A resident and ordinarily resident who (i) holds any asset (including a financial interest in any entity) located outside India, or (ii) has signing authority in any account located outside India, or (iii) has income from any source outside IndiaForeign assets and foreign income must be disclosed even where they produce no taxable income in India; the obligation is one of disclosure, not merely of tax payment.
A person in receipt of income derived from property held under trust or other legal obligation, wholly or partly for charitable or religious purposes (s. 139(4A)); a political party (s. 139(4B)); a scientific research association, news agency, etc. (s. 139(4C)); a university or college (s. 139(4D))Each claims an exemption or a special rate; the return is the price of the claim.

These "nil-return" categories exist so that high-value transactions leave a return behind, and they are the reason a person with income below the exemption limit may still be under an obligation to file.
Two drafting points worth a mark each
  • The obligation is to disclose, not merely to pay. This matters for reassessment: the failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts is the ground of escaped assessment under the pre-2021 law, and s. 139 is where that duty is born. Srikrishna (P) Ltd. says so in terms: "Section 139 places an obligation upon every person to furnish voluntarily a return of his total income … The obligation so placed involves the further obligation to disclose all material facts necessary for his assessment for that year fully and truly" (reader p. 183).
  • A return of loss is a return of income. Section 139(3) allows a person who has sustained a loss under "Profits and gains of business or profession" or "Capital gains" to furnish a return of loss within the time allowed, so that the loss may be carried forward. Filing within time is a condition of carry-forward, and s. 143(1)(a)(iii) expressly permits the summary machinery to disallow a loss claimed in a return filed late.

◆2.3 Belated, revised and defective returns

Belated — s. 139(4)

A person who has not furnished a return within the time allowed by s. 139(1), or in response to a notice under s. 142(1)(i), may furnish it at any time before three months prior to the end of the relevant assessment year, or before the completion of the assessment, whichever is earlier. A belated return attracts interest under s. 234A and the fee under s. 234F, and cannot be revised — the right under s. 139(5) is available only to a return furnished under sub-s. (1).

Revised — s. 139(5)

If a person discovers any omission or any wrong statement in a return furnished by him under sub-s. (1), he may furnish a revised return at any time before three months prior to the end of the relevant assessment year or before the completion of the assessment, whichever is earlier. A revised return substitutes the original; the officer must assess on the revised return, and a revision cannot be used to withdraw a claim that has already been allowed in a manner the assessee now regrets, nor to convert a belated return into a timely one.

Defective — s. 139(9)

Where the officer considers the return defective (for example, it is not signed or verified, the annexures are not attached, the tax and interest have not been paid, or the audit report is not accompanied), he may intimate the defect and give the assessee fifteen days (extendable on application) to rectify it. If the defect is not rectified, the return is treated as an invalid return — that is, as if no return had been furnished at all, which exposes the assessee to s. 144.

A point from the reader

In Srikrishna (P) Ltd. the Court describes s. 139 as the source of a dual obligation — to file, and to disclose fully and truly — and then explains why the safeguards of the reassessment chapter "avail only those assessees who disclose all material facts truly and fully" (reader p. 184). That single sentence is the bridge between §2 and §5 of these notes: the return is not merely the starting point of assessment; it is the document against which the legality of every later reopening is measured. §11.2 reduces the four reassessment cases to a matrix on exactly this point.

03Section 3ss. 142, 143 and 145(2) — inquiry before assessment, and the two kinds of assessment

Between the return and the order lies the inquiry. Section 142 gives the Assessing Officer his powers of investigation; s. 143 governs what he may do with what he finds — process the return and intimate the result, or take the case up for scrutiny and pass a written order. Section 145(2) belongs here because it is the provision that converts accounts from a private record into a statutory obligation: the Central Government may notify accounting standards, and an assessee who does not follow them, or whose accounts do not permit the income to be properly deduced, invites an assessment under s. 144.

◆3.1 Section 142(1) — the power to call for information and accounts

The three limbs of s. 142(1)

For the purpose of making an assessment, the Assessing Officer may serve a notice on any person who has made a return under s. 139 or in response to a notice under s. 142(1)(i) or s. 148 — and on any person who has not made a return — requiring him:

  1. to furnish a return of his income, where none has been furnished, within such time as may be specified in the notice;
  2. to produce, or cause to be produced, such accounts or documents as the officer may require; and
  3. to furnish information in such form as may be prescribed, on points bearing on the assessment.
QuestionAnswer, and where it comes from
Is the power unlimited?No. The notice must be for the purpose of making an assessment; a demand for material that has no bearing on the computation of income for that year is liable to be struck down. The words "for the purpose of" are a statutory limitation, not a formality.
Is the assessee bound to comply?Yes — and non-compliance has a specific consequence: s. 144(1)(b), which entitles the officer to make a best judgment assessment. The sanction for the notice is therefore the loss of the right to have one's own figure accepted.
What if the notice is vague?A notice which does not specify what is required, or which is impossible to comply with within the time given, may be challenged; but the ordinary rule is that the assessee must ask for time rather than ignore the notice.
What may the officer do with the material?Use it — but s. 142(3) requires that the assessee be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard before any material gathered (including any statement recorded) is used against him. This is the statutory form of the audi alteram partem rule.
Statements and evidences. 142(2) empowers the officer to make enquiries; s. 142(2A) deals with special audit; s. 142(3) with the hearing; and the material collected is "material gathered" for the purposes of the s. 144 estimate.

◆3.2 Section 142(2A) — special audit

The provision

Where, at any stage of the proceedings before him, the Assessing Officer has regard to (i) the nature and complexity of the accounts of the assessee and (ii) the interests of revenue, and is of the opinion that it is necessary to get the accounts audited, he may, with the previous approval of the Principal Chief Commissioner or Chief Commissioner or Principal Commissioner or Commissioner, direct the assessee to get his accounts audited by an accountant nominated by the Principal Chief Commissioner or Chief Commissioner or Principal Commissioner or Commissioner, and to furnish a report of such audit in the prescribed form.

Other features worth carrying into an answer: the expenses of the special audit are determined by the officer and are payable by the assessee; the report becomes part of the assessment record and may be used as evidence; the direction is subject to the requirement that the assessee be heard (the proviso requires that the assessee be given an opportunity of being heard before the direction is issued); and a direction which is made without application of mind to the two statutory conditions — the complexity of the accounts and the interests of the revenue — is vulnerable to challenge.

Why s. 142(2A) matters to a best-judgment question

Because it is the statutory answer to the situation that would otherwise lead straight to s. 144. Where the accounts are complex, the officer's remedy is a special audit, not an estimate: he is expected to use the investigative powers Parliament gave him before he falls back on his own judgment. The point is the same one the Supreme Court makes in a different context in Velukutty — that the officer "had every opportunity to base his judgment on relevant material" and it is no answer that the material was hard to get (reader p. 171). Section 142(2A) exists so that difficulty is not an excuse.

◆3.3 Section 143(1) — summary assessment by intimation

Every return is processed. That is the meaning of s. 143(1): without prejudice to the other provisions of the Act, the officer processes the return and issues an intimation of the sum payable by, or the refund due to, the assessee, after making only the adjustments the section expressly allows.

What the intimation is — and is not

  • It is deemed to be a notice of demand under s. 156, and where a refund is due, an intimation under s. 143(1) is deemed to be a notice of refund.
  • It is not a regular assessment: no hearing is contemplated, no evidence is taken, and no finding on a debatable issue can be made. An adjustment that requires the officer to decide a question of law or to weigh evidence is outside the section.
  • It carries a statutory outer limit: no intimation may be sent after nine months from the end of the financial year in which the return is furnished. Beyond that, the summary machinery is exhausted and the Department must use s. 143(2) or s. 147.
  • If the officer does not issue a s. 143(2) notice, the acknowledgement of the return is deemed to be an intimation under s. 143(1) — s. 143(4).

Remedies

  • Rectification under s. 154, for a mistake apparent from the record (including a mistake in the intimation itself).
  • Appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) under s. 246A, which lies against an intimation where the adjustment is one specified in s. 143(1) — the appeal is on the adjustment, not on the merits of the return.
  • The intimation does not bar a scrutiny assessment or a reopening; it is expressly "without prejudice" to the other provisions of the Act. This is why a processed return can still be reopened under s. 147 — a point that connects §3 with §5.

◆3.4 Sections 143(2), (3) and (4) — scrutiny

The distinction that decides many cases

No opinion is not the same as an opinion. Where a return was merely processed under s. 143(1) and never scrutinised, the Assessing Officer has formed no opinion on the issue; a reopening on that issue is therefore not a "change of opinion", and is permissible if the other conditions are met. Where, by contrast, the issue was considered and decided in a s. 143(3) assessment — or was the subject of a specific query answered by the assessee — a reopening on the same material is a change of opinion and is bad. The reader's cases illustrate the first proposition from the other side: in Burlop, Gemini and Lakhmani the officer had the material at the original assessment, and that is why the reopening failed.

◆3.5 Section 145(2) — the method of accounting and the notified standards

The section, in three sub-sections

145(1) — Income chargeable under the head "Profits and gains of business or profession" or "Income from other sources" shall, subject to sub-s. (2), be computed in accordance with either the cash or the mercantile system of accounting regularly employed by the assessee.

145(2) — The Central Government may notify accounting standards in the Official Gazette, to be followed by any class of assessees or in respect of any class of income.

145(3) — Where the Assessing Officer is not satisfied about the correctness or the completeness of the accounts of the assessee, or where no method of accounting has been regularly employed, or where the method employed is such that the income cannot properly be deduced therefrom, he may make the assessment in the manner provided in section 144.

What sub-s. (2) actually does

  • It is a delegated power: Parliament did not itself lay down the standards but authorised the Central Government to do so, in order to keep the rules abreast of accounting practice (and of the accounting standards issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India).
  • The standards notified under this power — the Income Computation and Disclosure Standards — have the force of law for the class of assessees to which they apply, and they are mandatory irrespective of the accounting treatment in the books. An assessee who follows a different treatment in his accounts must nevertheless compute his income under the notified standard.
  • The standards do not change the head of income or the charging provisions; they govern the computation of business income and income from other sources.
  • They matter to this topic because non-compliance, or accounts which do not reflect the standards, is precisely the situation in which the officer may invoke s. 145(3) and, through it, s. 144.

The link to best judgment — four steps

  1. s. 145(1) permits cash or mercantile accounting, regularly employed.
  2. s. 145(2) superimposes the notified standards on that choice.
  3. s. 145(3) turns a failure of the accounts — rejection of the books, absence of a regular method, or a method from which income cannot be properly deduced — into a jurisdictional ground for s. 144.
  4. But the rejection of the books is not a licence to estimate wildly: the estimate that follows must satisfy Velukutty. The rejection of accounts and the estimate of income are two different steps, and the second is reviewable even when the first is justified.
One-paragraph answer on s. 145(2)

"Section 145(2) empowers the Central Government to notify accounting standards for any class of assessees or class of income, and the standards so notified — the Income Computation and Disclosure Standards — are binding on the computation of income under the heads 'Profits and gains of business or profession' and 'Income from other sources', whatever the treatment in the books. The sub-section is therefore the statutory bridge between the accountant's records and the officer's assessment: it defines what a correct set of accounts looks like. Where the accounts do not meet that standard, or where no regular method has been employed, s. 145(3) entitles the officer to reject them and to proceed under s. 144 — but the best judgment that follows must still be an honest estimate having a reasonable nexus to the material on record, as State of Kerala v. C. Velukutty holds."

04Section 4s. 144 — best judgment assessment: the officer's own figure, and the law's limits on it

Section 144 is the Department's answer to the assessee who will not co-operate: if you will not tell me your income, I will determine it as best I can. The power is indispensable and dangerous, and the whole of the case-law in Velukutty exists to police the second half of that sentence. The principle, in one line: an estimate is permitted, but the estimate must be a judgment — honest, based on material, and disclosed in its basis — and not a caprice.

◆4.1 The six grounds on which a best judgment assessment may be made

The text, in substance

"144. Best judgment assessment.—The Assessing Officer, having regard to all the relevant material which he has gathered, shall, after giving an opportunity of being heard, make the assessment of the income or loss to the best of his judgment in the following cases…" The section then lists the defaults. Every word of the opening words has been litigated: all the relevant material (he must collect, not guess); an opportunity of being heard (the assessee must be confronted with the case against him); to the best of his judgment (the estimate must be a judgment, not a penalty).

The six grounds, with the practical situation each is aimed at
#DefaultWords of the section, and the situation

2Non-compliance with a s. 142(1) noticeFails to comply with all or any of the terms of a notice under s. 142(1) — accounts, documents or information not produced.

Two things the section does not say
  • It does not dispense with the assessment. The officer must still compute the income under the correct head, allow the deductions the law allows, and pass a written order showing the figure. A best judgment assessment is an assessment, not the abolition of one.
  • It does not authorise a punitive figure. The object is to determine the income that probably exists, not to punish the default. The penalty for the default is dealt with elsewhere (and requires positive proof of concealment) — the estimate must be an estimate of income. Confusing the two is the commonest error in the Department's orders and in students' answers alike.

◆4.2 "After giving an opportunity of being heard" — notice of intention and hearing

The December 2024 paper's Question 5 is built on precisely this sentence: "The Best Judgement assessment should be made by giving notice of intention and an opportunity to the assessee to file his reply and evidences…". The proposition is correct, and it is worth knowing why it is correct and how much it requires.

Element of the hearingContent, and source
A show-cause noticeThe officer must tell the assessee that he proposes to proceed under s. 144, and on what grounds. A best judgment order made without any intimation that the officer was minded to proceed ex parte is void, whatever the merits.
Disclosure of the materialThe assessee is entitled to see the material on which the proposed estimate is to rest — the third-party information, the seized records, the comparable cases. s. 142(3) puts this beyond doubt: material gathered cannot be used without a reasonable opportunity of being heard.
A reasonable time and a real opportunityThe time given must be sufficient to produce the evidence; an adjournment sought for good reason should ordinarily be granted. A hearing in form but not in substance is no hearing.
The consequences of breachThe order is void — not merely irregular — and is set aside by the appellate authority or quashed in writ proceedings. This is the strongest and simplest ground of appeal against a s. 144 order, and it should be pleaded before the quantum is argued.
The hearing is not a formality for the assessee eitherAn assessee who is given an opportunity and does not use it cannot later complain of the estimate unless he shows it had no nexus to the material. That is exactly the position of the assessee before the High Court in Velukutty's companion appeals, where the tribunal-level estimate survived challenge on the ground that the assessee had not displaced it.
The two-dimensional requirement

The requirement has a procedural dimension (notice, material, hearing) and a substantive one (the estimate must rest on material and have a reasonable nexus to it). An order can fail on either. In practice the procedural ground is the easier to argue on appeal and the substantive ground is the one on which Velukutty is cited — and a good answer takes them in that order.

◆4.3 What "best judgment" means — the doctrine from Badridas to Velukutty

(a) The Privy Council's formulation, as quoted by the Supreme Court in Velukutty

"He must not act dishonestly, or vindictively or capriciously because he must exercise judgment in the matter. He must make what he honestly believes to be a fair estimate of the proper figure of assessment, and for this purpose he must … be able to take into consideration local knowledge and repute in regard to the assessee's circumstances, and his own knowledge of previous returns by and assessments of the assessee, and all other matters which he thinks will assist him in arriving at a fair and proper estimate; and though there must necessarily be guess-work in the matter, it must be honest guess-work. In that sense, too, the assessment must be to some extent arbitrary." CIT v. Laxminarayan Badridas, (1937) 5 ITR 170, 180 (PC), quoted at reader p. 170

Four propositions are packed into that passage, and each is examinable: (i) the officer must exercise judgment, so dishonesty, vindictiveness and caprice are excluded; (ii) the standard is a fair estimate, honestly believed; (iii) the material he may use is wide — local knowledge, repute, his own knowledge of the assessee's previous returns and assessments, and "all other matters"; and (iv) guess-work is permitted but must be honest. The apparent tension between "arbitrary" and "judgment" is the subject of the Supreme Court's own gloss.

(b) The Supreme Court's gloss in Velukutty

"Under section 12(2)(b) of the Act, power is conferred on the assessing authority … to assess the dealer to the best of his judgment. The limits of the power are implicit in the expression 'best of his judgment'. Judgment is a faculty to decide matters with wisdom truly and legally. Judgment does not depend upon the arbitrary caprice of a judge, but on settled and invariable principles of justice. Though there is an element of guess-work in a 'best judgment assessment', it shall not be a wild one, but shall have a reasonable nexus to the available material and circumstances of each case. Though sub-section (2) of section 12 … provides for a summary method because of the default of the assessee, it does not enable the assessing authority to function capriciously without regard for the available material." reader p. 171

Three tests emerge, and they are the tests to apply to any s. 144 order: (1) Is the estimate based on material that the officer actually had? (2) Does it have a reasonable nexus with that material? (3) Is it the product of reasoning applied to the circumstances of this case, or of a formula imported from somewhere else? The third test is the one on which the assessee succeeded in Velukutty, and it is the one most often failed in practice.

The three-tier vocabulary to use in an answer

Permissible: an estimate; a percentage applied to a disclosed figure where the percentage is drawn from material relating to that business; reliance on comparable cases; reliance on the assessee's own past record; the rejection of books that cannot be relied upon. Impermissible: a figure with no material behind it; a percentage imported from a different business or a different year without a finding of comparability; a figure fixed because the assessee defaulted (punitive); an order that does not disclose its basis at all. Reviewable: both of the last two, and any order made without a hearing.

◆4.4 Estimate and concealment are different questions

An estimate under s. 144 is not a finding that the assessee concealed income, and the two must not be confused in an answer or in an order. The estimate answers the question "what is the income?"; concealment answers the question "did the assessee intend to hide it?" — and the second requires positive material, not the mere fact that the first was necessary. The reader's material does not decide this point directly, but it supplies the reasoning: Velukutty insists that the estimate be a judgment about income on the material, and the whole tenor of the case is that the officer's task is to find the true figure, not to sanction the default. Outside the reader the classic statement is CIT v. Anwar Ali, (1970) 76 ITR 696 (SC): the mere fact that an estimate has been made, or that additions have been sustained, does not justify a penalty for concealment; there must be positive evidence of a deliberate concealment, and a finding that the amount represents income is not the same as a finding that it was concealed. Use it, but badge it as outside the prescribed material.

◆4.5 The faceless dimension — s. 144B outside the reader

Since 2021 the best judgment power is in most cases exercised facelessly. Where the officer proposes to make a variation in the assessment which is prejudicial to the assessee and the variation exceeds the prescribed threshold (currently ₹5 lakh), s. 144B requires a draft order to be served on the assessee, a show-cause calling for objections, and a final order passed after considering the reply — all through the National Faceless Assessment Centre, with the assessee heard through video conferencing where he so requests. The statutory scheme is thus a codification, in elaborate form, of exactly the requirement §4.2 describes: notice, material, hearing, then the order. For examination purposes it is enough to know that (i) the requirement of an opportunity is nowhere relaxed by facelessness, and (ii) a final order which ignores the assessee's reply, or which travels beyond the draft order without a fresh show-cause, is vulnerable.

A short worked illustration

An assessee carrying on a retail business files no return despite a s. 142(1) notice. The officer obtains the VAT returns (turnover ₹80 lakh), the bank statements, and the electricity bills, issues a show-cause proposing to estimate income at 8% of turnover, and hears the assessee's accountant, who offers no accounts but disputes the percentage. Analysis: the default under ground 1 is established; the material is real and relates to this assessee; a net-profit estimate is a permissible method because there is material capable of supporting it; the order is valid if it shows why 8% was chosen (comparables, the assessee's own earlier returns, the trade's norm). If the officer had instead adopted 8% because a different assessee in a different trade was assessed at 8% — and said so — the estimate would be the very thing Velukutty condemns: "arbitrarily made by applying a ratio between disclosed and concealed turnover in one shop to another shop of the assessee" (reader p. 171).

05Section 5ss. 147–153 — income escaping assessment: the power to reopen

An assessment is meant to be final. But the Act also accepts that the Department will sometimes learn, after the event, that it assessed too little — and it therefore keeps a power to reopen. The whole of ss. 147 to 153 is an exercise in reconciling those two propositions: finality for the assessee who disclosed everything, and correction where income escaped. Four of the reader's five cases are about where that line falls, and the answer the courts have given is a single idea dressed in many phrases — the officer must have a reason, on material, with a live link to the escapement, and he must not merely have changed his mind.

Which text are you construing?

Every one of the four reassessment cases in the reader was decided under the pre-1989 text, and two of them under the 1922 Act. Their principles — the duty of full and true disclosure, the prohibition on change of opinion, the "live link" requirement, the limits of sanction — have been applied without interruption to every later version, including the 2021 scheme, because the underlying question (what must the officer know before he may reopen?) does not change with the drafting. But the words do change: "reason to believe" is gone from s. 147, the four-year/eight-year distinction has gone from s. 149, and the sanction has moved. An answer should say which statute it is construing, and then use the cases for the principle.

◆5.2 Section 147 — the substantive power

The text as the Court quotes it in Lakhmani Mewal Das (the pre-1989 form)

"147. Explanation 2 (as it stood) then lists the cases which are deemed to be cases of escapement: (a) where no return has been furnished although the income exceeded the maximum not chargeable to tax; (b) where a return has been furnished but no assessment has been made and the officer notices that the assessee has understated the income or claimed excessive loss, deduction, allowance or relief; (c) where an assessment has been made but (i) income has been under-assessed, or (ii) income has been assessed at too low a rate, or (iii) income has been made the subject of excessive relief, or (iv) excessive loss or depreciation or other allowance has been computed; and (d) where a person is found to have an asset located outside India.

◆5.3 Section 148 — the notice, and the reasons

The two requirements the cases read into the section

(1) A notice in the prescribed form, served on the assessee, requiring him to furnish a return of his income for the relevant assessment year within the time specified. The notice is the condition of jurisdiction; without it, nothing that follows is valid.

(2) Reasons recorded before the notice is issued — s. 148(2) in the pre-2021 text, and in Lakhmani the Court describes the recording of reasons as "another requirement" of the section. The reasons are not a formality: they are the record against which the existence of the belief is tested. Lakhmani puts the consequence in the sharpest terms when it holds that the existence of the belief can be challenged (though not the sufficiency of the reasons for it), and that it is "open to the court to examine whether the reasons for the formation of the belief have a rational connection with or a relevant bearing on the formation of the belief" (reader p. 179).

The practice that grew out of this — that the assessee may ask for the recorded reasons and object to them, and that the officer must dispose of the objections before proceeding — is a development after the reader's cases (the GKN Driveshafts line). It is worth knowing, because it is the practical route by which the Lakhmani principles are now argued in every reopening.

Limitation is jurisdictional, not procedural

A notice issued beyond s. 149, or an order passed beyond s. 153, is not merely irregular — it is void, because the officer had no power to issue it or to pass it. That is why the point can be taken in a writ petition, why it can be taken at any stage, and why the assessee's failure to object at the first opportunity does not cure it. The same logic explains Gemini Leather Stores: the notice there was quashed at the threshold, in writ proceedings, without waiting for the reassessment to be completed. An answer that treats limitation as a mere procedural objection has misunderstood the architecture of the chapter.

◆5.7 Change of opinion — the line the four cases draw

Three of the four reassessment cases in the reader end in the assessee's favour, and all three for the same reason: the officer had the material at the original assessment and drew (or failed to draw) an inference from it. The fourth, Srikrishna, ends the other way because the assessee's disclosure was false.

Fig. 4 — The disclosure fork, with the reader's four cases placed on it. The diagram makes the doctrinal shape of the topic visible: Burlop, Gemini and Lakhmani protect a truthful assessee from a second look; Srikrishna withdraws that protection from an untruthful one.

The single most useful distinction in the whole topic

Non-disclosure of a primary fact versus failure to draw an inference from disclosed facts. The first is the assessee's default and makes a reopening lawful; the second is the officer's default and makes it unlawful. A bogus creditor is a primary fact that was falsely stated (Srikrishna); a claim that a payment was a "share of profits" rather than a device is an inference the officer should have drawn (Burlop); a draft purchase found in the books and left un-inquired into is the officer's oversight (Gemini); a name-lender's name known but unexplained is not a live link (Lakhmani). Every problem question in this area can be answered by asking which of the two mistakes was made.

06Case 32 · reader pp. 168–171State of Kerala v. C. Velukutty — what a "best judgment" may rest on

Why this case is first in the topic

It is a sales-tax case, and that is precisely why it is worth studying first: it contains the fullest judicial account of the expression "to the best of his judgment", it traces that expression to s. 23(4) of the Income-tax Act, 1922 (the ancestor of s. 144), and it applies the Privy Council's income-tax authority to a concrete set of figures. Every principle a student needs for a s. 144 question is in these four pages: the estimate is permitted; it must be honest; it must have a reasonable nexus to material; and a ratio drawn from one business cannot be applied to another without a finding connecting them.

State of Kerala v. C. Velukutty

(1966) 60 ITR 239 (SC) — printed in the reader as "(1966) LX ITR 239 (SC)" · K. Subba Rao J. · two appeals by special leave from the High Court of Kerala (T.R.C. Nos. 52 and 53 of 1960) · DU reader pp. 168–171
Case 32Sales tax s. 12(2)(b)"Best of his judgment"Estimate v. capriceNo remand
1 · Facts
  • The respondent had two offices in Kozhikode: a head office at Court Road doing retail business and a branch office at Big Bazar doing wholesale business. They maintained separate accounts. Goods sent from the branch to the head office were entered in the accounts as transfers; the head office recorded the transferred goods and also the goods purchased locally; and the branch had dealings with other customers as well.
  • On 6 April 1957 the Deputy Commercial Tax Officer assessed the respondent on a net turnover of ₹9,30,565-10-5 for the assessment year 1955-56.
  • Later, on a surprise inspection of the head office by the Intelligence Officer, North Zone, Kozhikode, books of account and records were recovered — the "secret books". On 27 October 1958, on the basis of those books, the Sales Tax Officer issued a notice proposing to determine to the best of his judgment the turnover which had escaped assessment.
  • The respondent agreed that the head office turnover be assessed on the basis of the secret books, but objected to a fresh assessment of the branch office. The objection was rejected. The officer found that the head office's secret transactions were 135% of the recorded turnover, and applied the same 135% to the branch office, although no secret books had been found there. Total turnover of the two offices was fixed at ₹19,71,805-13-5 and tax at ₹16,269.37.
  • For the assessment year 1956-57 the same method was adopted with larger percentages: at the head office the officer found the escaped assessment to be 200% of the assessed turnover for general goods and 500% for sugar, and he added those percentages to the branch office turnover as well, "though no secret books were discovered in respect of that office". The total turnover was fixed at ₹39,66,377-2-6, made up of the head office at ₹2,21,251-14-5 and the branch office at ₹37,45,125-4-1 — a figure in which the branch, whose accounts had never been shown to be false, accounted for almost the whole of the turnover.
  • Appeals failed before the Appellate Assistant Commissioner and the Sales Tax Appellate Tribunal. In revision under s. 15B of the Travancore-Cochin General Sales Tax Act, 1125 M.E. (XI of 1125), the High Court of Kerala set aside both orders on the ground that the finding of escaped assessment so far as the branch office was concerned was based on conjecture and amounted to an error of law, and it refused the State's request for a remand. Hence the State's appeals to the Supreme Court.

The provisions — s. 12(2)(b) of the Act: "if no return is submitted by the dealer … or if the return submitted by him appears to the assessing authority to be incorrect or incomplete, the assessing authority shall assess the dealer to the best of his judgment", after giving him a reasonable opportunity of proving the correctness and completeness of his return. Section 15B confined the High Court's revision to the question whether the Tribunal had "either decided erroneously or failed to decide any question of law". (Reader pp. 169–170.)

2 · Issues
  1. What is the scope of the power to assess "to the best of his judgment"? What are the limits of a best judgment assessment, and what may the assessing authority take into account?
  2. Whether the Sales Tax Officer's estimate for the branch office — made by applying to the branch the very percentages of suppression discovered at the head office — satisfied those limits, or was capricious.
  3. Whether the High Court had jurisdiction under s. 15B to interfere with what the State called a finding of fact.
  4. Whether the High Court ought to have remanded the matter for a fresh assessment instead of setting the orders aside outright.
3 · Arguments
For the State of Kerala (appellant)
  • The High Court was wrong to call the estimate capricious. Once secret accounts were found at the head office, it was a reasonable inference that the branch office also maintained parallel secret accounts; the respondent had suppressed them; and the Sales Tax Officer therefore acted reasonably in working out the escaped turnover of the branch by reference to the ratio of suppression discovered in the assessee's own business.
  • The estimate is, by its nature, a matter of judgment, and a judgment based on the assessee's own record is the most reliable kind available. To require direct evidence. of the branch's suppression would make the power useless in exactly the cases for which it exists.
  • The High Court had no jurisdiction under s. 15B: whether an estimate is reasonable is a finding of fact for the Tribunal, and s. 15B permits interference only on a question of law.
  • In any event the proper course was a remand, so that the Department could support the estimate with material rather than lose the assessment altogether.
For the respondent (assessee)
  • There was no basis whatever for holding that the respondent maintained separate secret accounts for the branch office. The secret books recovered related to the head office, and to the head office alone.
  • There was absolutely no material before the Sales Tax Officer to sustain the best judgment assessment of the branch. The percentages of 135%, 200% and 500% were simply imported from one office to another.
  • The assessment was therefore capricious and arbitrary, and was rightly set aside by the High Court: an estimate which rests on nothing is not a judgment but a surmise, and a surmise is not what s. 12(2)(b) authorises.
  • No remand: the Department had every opportunity to gather material, and chose not to; it should not be given a second chance after having failed at four levels.
4 · Decision

The appeals were dismissed. The High Court was right: the branch office assessment was an arbitrary estimate and was properly set aside, and there was to be no remand. Six steps in the reasoning:

  1. The High Court did have jurisdiction. Section 15B is confined to whether the Tribunal "decided erroneously … any question of law" — and "the Sales Tax Officer acted capriciously and arbitrarily in assessing the respondent, which he could not do under section 12(2)(b) of the Act and the Tribunal confirmed that order. It is a clear case where the Tribunal decided erroneously on a question of law" (reader p. 170). The State's "finding of fact" objection therefore failed on the threshold.
  2. The expression is borrowed from the income-tax law. "The expression 'to the best of his judgment' in the said clause is presumably borrowed from section 23(4) of the Income-tax Act. The said expression in the Income-tax Act was the subject of judicial scrutiny" — and the Court then adopted the Privy Council's construction in CIT v. Laxminarayan Badridas, (1937) 5 ITR 170, 180 (reader p. 170).
  3. The Privy Council's standard, adopted in full: the officer "must not act dishonestly, or vindictively or capriciously because he must exercise judgment in the matter. He must make what he honestly believes to be a fair estimate of the proper figure of assessment, and for this purpose he must … be able to take into consideration local knowledge and repute in regard to the assessee's circumstances, and his own knowledge of previous returns by and assessments of the assessee, and all other matters which he thinks will assist him in arriving at a fair and proper estimate; and though there must necessarily be guess-work in the matter, it must be honest guess-work." The Supreme Court underlined the point: the Privy Council, "while recognizing that an assessment made by an officer to the best of his judgment involved some guess-work, emphasized that he must exercise his judgment after taking into consideration the relevant material". It noted that the same approach had been followed under sales-tax statutes by the Calcutta High Court in Jagadish Prosad Pannalal v. Member, Board of Revenue, (1951) 2 STC 21 (materials considered and mind applied), and by the Patna High Court in Doma Sahu Kishun Lal Sao v. State of Bihar, (1951) 2 STC 37 (situation of the shop, rush of customers, stock, and the Assistant Commissioner's earlier estimates all taken into account).
  4. The Court's own formulation — the sentence to memorise: "The limits of the power are implicit in the expression 'best of his judgment'. Judgment is a faculty to decide matters with wisdom truly and legally. Judgment does not depend upon the arbitrary caprice of a judge, but on settled and invariable principles of justice. Though there is an element of guess-work in a 'best judgment assessment', it shall not be a wild one, but shall have a reasonable nexus to the available material and circumstances of each case. Though sub-section (2) of section 12 of the Act provides for a summary method because of the default of the assessee, it does not enable the assessing authority to function capriciously without regard for the available material" (reader p. 171).
  5. Applied to the facts — three reasons why the estimate failed. (i) "From the discovery of secret accounts in the head office, it does not necessarily follow that a corresponding set of secret accounts were maintained in the branch office", and indeed "duplication of false accounts would facilitate discovery of fraud", so it was at least as likely that the assessee kept only one set; the maintenance of secret accounts at the branch "cannot be assumed in the circumstances of the case". (ii) Even if the branch had kept secret accounts, that "might lead to an inference that the accounts disclosed did not comprehend all the transactions of the branch office. But that does not establish or even probabilize the finding that 135% or 200% or 500% of the discovered turnover was suppressed. That could have been ascertained from other materials." (iii) Those other materials were available and unused: "The branch office had dealings with other customers. Their names [are] disclosed in the accounts. The accounts of those customers or their statements could have afforded a basis for the best judgment assessment. There must also have been other surrounding circumstances, such as those mentioned in the Privy Council's decision." Instead, "there was no material before the assessing authority relevant to the assessment and the impugned assessments were arbitrarily made by applying a ratio between disclosed and concealed turnover in one shop to another shop of the assessee. It was only a capricious surmise unsupported by any relevant material."
  6. No remand. "The sales tax authority had every opportunity to base its judgment on relevant material; but it did not do so. The department persisted all through the hierarchy of tribunals to sustain the impugned assessment. The High Court, having regard to the circumstances of the case, refused to give the department another opportunity. We do not think we are justified to take a different view."
5 · Principle evolved
  1. A best judgment assessment is a judicial act, not a licence. The opinion must be formed on relevant material, honestly, and as a fair estimate of the proper figure. Dishonesty, vindictiveness and caprice are the three vices the Privy Council names, and the Supreme Court adopts them.
  2. Guess-work is permitted; wild guess-work is not. The formula is one of degree but it is workable: the estimate must have a reasonable nexus to the available material and the circumstances of each case. The word "available" is important — the material must actually have been before the officer.
  3. A ratio cannot be transplanted. Suppression proved at one establishment of an assessee does not prove the same percentage of suppression at another; to carry the ratio across, the officer must find something that makes the two comparable. This is the most examinable holding in the case, because it is the commonest practical error.
  4. The power to estimate does not relieve the officer of the duty to investigate. The Court points to the customer names in the accounts, the customers' statements and the surrounding circumstances — all available, none used. A best judgment assessment is a power of last resort, and it may be set aside where ordinary investigative steps were open and were not taken.
  5. Judgment, defined: "a faculty to decide matters with wisdom truly and legally … not … arbitrary caprice … but … settled and invariable principles of justice." This is the sentence to quote in any answer on s. 144.
  6. No second chance after a fair failure. Where the Department had the opportunity at every stage to support its estimate and chose not to, a remand is not a matter of course. The same restraint appears in Lakhmani's refusal to allow reasons to be bolstered later.
  7. The decision is authority in income-tax by adoption. The Court expressly traces the sales-tax formula to s. 23(4) of the 1922 Act and applies the income-tax authority. That is what makes a sales-tax case part of an income-tax syllabus.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the leading authority on s. 144 in substance, and the only case in the reader on the quantum side of assessment. Cite it in every best-judgment answer, for the "reasonable nexus" test and for the prohibition on transplanted ratios.
  • It supplies the answer to Dec-2024 Q5: the requirement of notice and opportunity is the procedural half; Velukutty is the substantive half. See §12.2.
  • It explains why s. 144 orders are so often set aside on appeal: not because the officer had no power to estimate, but because the estimate was not connected to anything.
  • It corrects a common student error — the belief that an assessee who defaults has no answer to a best judgment assessment. He has two: that no notice or hearing was given, and that the estimate had no nexus to material. Velukutty is the second.

Mapping: the "best of his judgment" power in s. 12(2)(b) of the Travancore-Cochin General Sales Tax Act corresponds to s. 144 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and to s. 23(4) of the 1922 Act, from which the expression was borrowed); the privative/revisional provision in s. 15B corresponds to the appellate structure of ss. 246A and 253, and to the supervisory jurisdiction under Article 226.

07Case 33 · reader pp. 172–174CIT v. Burlop Dealers Ltd. — the duty stops at primary facts

The proposition in one line

An assessee must disclose the primary facts; he is under no obligation to tell the Income-tax Officer what inferences to draw from them. If the officer had the material and drew the wrong inference, the remedy is appeal or revision — not a reopening under the non-disclosure limb of s. 34(1)(a) (now s. 147).

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Burlop Dealers Ltd.

(1971) 79 ITR 609 (SC) · J.C. Shah, C.J.I. · appeal from the Calcutta High Court · AY 1949-50 · DU reader pp. 172–174
Case 331922 Act s. 34(1)(a)Primary facts v. inferencesNo duty to assist
1 · Facts
  • The assessee, Burlop Dealers Ltd., was a limited company. For the assessment year 1949-50 it submitted a profit and loss account disclosing ₹1,75,875 as profit from a joint venture with H. Manory Ltd., and claimed that ₹87,937 — being half the profit — had been paid to Ratiram Tansukhrai under a partnership agreement.
  • The arrangement as described was this: by an agreement of 5 June 1948 the assessee had agreed with H. Manory Ltd. to do business in plywood chests, and in consideration of financing the business was to receive 50% of its profits; and by a further agreement of 7 October 1948 the assessee had agreed with Ratiram Tansukhrai that the latter would finance the transactions of H. Manory Ltd. in the joint venture, in return for 50% of the profit earned by the assessee from that business.
  • The Income-tax Officer accepted the return and included only ₹87,937 as the profit earned on the joint venture.
  • In the next year, 1950-51, the assessee filed a return disclosing a total profit of ₹1,62,155 from H. Manory Ltd. and claimed that it had transferred ₹81,077 to Ratiram Tansukhrai as his share. This time the officer examined the transactions and brought the entire ₹1,62,155 to tax, holding that the agreement of October 1948 "had merely been got up as a device to reduce the profits received from H. Manory Ltd." That view was confirmed by the Appellate Assistant Commissioner, by the Tribunal and by the Calcutta High Court.
  • Meanwhile, on 13 May 1955, the Income-tax Officer issued a notice under s. 34 to reopen the 1949-50 assessment, so as to bring to tax the ₹87,937 which had been allowed as paid to Ratiram Tansukhrai. The assessee filed a return which did not include that amount; the officer reassessed under s. 34(1)(a) and added it. The Appellate Assistant Commissioner confirmed, observing that the assessee had misled the officer into believing that there was a genuine arrangement with Ratiram Tansukhrai.
  • The Tribunal reversed: the assessee "had produced all the relevant accounts and documents necessary for completing the assessment, and the assessee was under no obligation to inform the Income-tax Officer about the true nature of the transactions", and it directed that the amount be excluded. The Tribunal refused to state a case, and the High Court refused to direct it to do so. The Commissioner appealed to the Supreme Court.

The provision — s. 34(1) of the 1922 Act as it stood in 1949-50: the officer could act under clause (a) where he had reason to believe that, by reason of the omission or failure of the assessee to make a return or to disclose fully and truly all material facts, income had escaped assessment or been under-assessed — within eight years; and under clause (b) where, notwithstanding that there was no such omission or failure, he had, in consequence of information in his possession, reason to believe that income had escaped — within four years. (Reader p. 173.)

2 · Issues
  1. Whether, on the facts, the Income-tax Officer had jurisdiction to reopen the 1949-50 assessment under s. 34(1)(a) — that is, whether income had escaped assessment by reason of the assessee's omission or failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts.
  2. What is the content of the assessee's duty of disclosure: does it extend to pointing out the inferences which the officer might draw from the primary facts he has disclosed?
  3. Whether the production of books of account and documents amounts, without more, to a disclosure of the material facts contained in them.
  4. Which clause of s. 34 the case really fell under — (a) or (b) — and the effect of the answer on the availability of the power (since the period for clause (b) had expired).
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue (appellant Commissioner)
  • The assessee had misled the officer. It presented the payment to Ratiram Tansukhrai as a genuine share of profits under a partnership arrangement, whereas — as the very next year's assessment established — the "agreement" was a device to reduce profits. A statement which is untrue in substance is not a disclosure of the material facts; it is a suppression of them.
  • What the officer had before him was therefore not the true facts but the assessee's false version of them. The duty under s. 34(1)(a) is to disclose "fully and truly"; a disclosure that is coloured by a false characterisation cannot be "true".
  • The amount of ₹87,937 was, in truth, the assessee's own income, and it escaped assessment in 1949-50. The reopening was therefore within s. 34(1)(a), and the Tribunal erred in holding otherwise.
For the assessee (respondent)
  • Everything material was before the officer: the profit and loss account disclosed the joint venture and the receipt of ₹1,75,875; the payment of ₹87,937 to Ratiram Tansukhrai was shown; the agreements were produced; the books and documents were available. The primary facts were fully disclosed.
  • The question whether the payment was a genuine share of profits or a device is a matter of inference. The officer could have asked about it, examined the agreements, called for evidence of the financing, or disallowed the payment in the original assessment. He did none of these things.
  • The assessee is under no obligation to instruct the officer about the inference he should draw. To hold otherwise would convert every assessment into a contest in which the assessee must argue the Department's case against himself, and would make every original assessment provisional.
  • The Tribunal's finding was one of fact, and its refusal to refer a question of law was right.
4 · Decision

The appeal was dismissed with costs. The reopening was bad, and the ₹87,937 could not be brought to tax under s. 34(1)(a). Five steps in the reasoning:

  1. The case "would appropriately fall under section 34(1)(b)", because the officer acted "in consequence of information in his possession that the agreement with Ratiram Tansukhrai was a share transaction" — but "the period prescribed for serving a notice under section 34(1)(b) had elapsed". The Department was therefore driven to clause (a), and had to establish the assessee's non-disclosure.
  2. The Calcutta Discount principle was adopted: the words "omission or failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts necessary for his assessment for that year" postulate a duty to disclose all material facts; but the materiality of facts "will differ from case to case", and from the primary facts in his possession "whether on disclosure by the assessee, or discovered by him on the basis of the facts disclosed, or otherwise, the assessing authority has to draw inferences as regards certain other facts; and ultimately … the proper legal inferences".
  3. The rule: "We are of the view that under section 34(1)(a) if the assessee has disclosed primary facts relevant to the assessment, he is under no obligation to instruct the Income-tax Officer about the inference which the Income-tax Officer may raise from those facts. The terms of the Explanation to section 34(1) also do not impose a more onerous obligation."
  4. The books point, stated with care: "Mere production of the books of account or other evidence from which material facts could with due diligence have been discovered does not necessarily amount to disclosure within the meaning of section 34(1), but where on the evidence and the materials produced the Income-tax Officer could have reached a conclusion other than the one which he has reached, a proceeding under section 34(1)(a) will not lie merely on the ground that the Income-tax Officer has raised an inference which he may later regard as erroneous."
  5. Applied: "The assessee had disclosed his books of account and evidence from which material facts could be discovered; it was under no obligation to inform the Income-tax Officer about the possible inferences which may be raised against him. It was for the Income-tax Officer to raise such an inference and if he did not do so the income which has escaped assessment cannot be brought to tax under section 34(1)(a)."
5 · Principle evolved
  1. The primary-facts / inference divide. The statutory duty is to disclose primary facts fully and truly. It does not extend to the drawing of factual or legal inferences, which is the officer's function. This is the single most important proposition in the whole of the reassessment law.
  2. A wrong inference is not an escaped assessment. Where the material before the officer was sufficient to support a different conclusion, a reopening on the ground that he drew the wrong conclusion is a change of opinion, or a correction of his own error — and neither is within s. 34(1)(a)/s. 147.
  3. Mere production of books is not automatic disclosure — but the qualification is not a licence for the Department: the test is whether the officer could have reached the other conclusion on the material produced. If he could, the assessee has done enough.
  4. An untrue statement is different, and Burlop should not be read as protecting it. The Court's reasoning assumes a disclosure of the facts as they were; a false statement of a primary fact is not a disclosure at all — which is precisely the distinction Srikrishna (P) Ltd. draws twenty-five years later, and on which Srikrishna wins for the Revenue.
  5. Finality has weight. The scheme of the section presumes that an assessment, once made, will not be reopened merely because a later officer takes a different view; otherwise no assessment would ever be final.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the assessee's first line of defence in any reopening beyond the shorter period: "all the primary facts were before the officer; what he says he has now discovered is an inference he failed to draw."
  • Its limit is equally important — and examiners test it. Srikrishna explains that Burlop "must be confined to the particular fact-situation of that case and … cannot be construed to be of universal application irrespective of the facts and circumstances" (reader p. 188). The fact-situation was a truthful disclosure of a transaction whose character was arguable.
  • It links directly to the s. 143(1)/143(3) distinction (§3.4): where the officer never formed an opinion, there is nothing to have "changed" and Burlop does not assist; where he did form one on disclosed facts, Burlop is decisive.

Mapping: s. 34(1)(a) of the 1922 Act → the non-disclosure limb of s. 147 as it stood before 1989, and the proviso to s. 147 as it stood from 1989; the principle now surfaces in the requirement of "information which suggests" escapement in s. 148 (post-2021).

08Case 34 · reader pp. 175–176Gemini Leather Stores v. ITO — the officer's own oversight

The proposition in one line

Where the officer discovered the relevant transactions himself, questioned the assessee about them, recorded the answers, and then failed to draw the obvious conclusion, the escapement is the result of his own oversight — and s. 147(a) cannot be used to remedy it. The case is Burlop applied with even greater force, because here the assessee had not even disclosed the transactions; the officer had found them.

Gemini Leather Stores v. The Income-tax Officer, 'B' Ward, Agra

AIR 1975 SC 1268 · A.C. Gupta J. · appeal by certificate from the Allahabad High Court · AY 1956-57 · DU reader pp. 175–176
Case 341961 Act s. 147(a)Drafts outside the booksOversight is not non-disclosure
1 · Facts
  • The appellant was a partnership firm, assessed for the assessment year 1956-57 on a turnover of ₹15 lakh by order dated 22 January 1958.
  • The Income-tax Officer did not accept the return or the books of account and made a best judgment assessment. The turnover so assessed was reduced by the Appellate Assistant Commissioner and further reduced by the Appellate Tribunal.
  • On 31 March 1965 — more than seven years later — the Income-tax Officer issued a notice under s. 148 of the 1961 Act, stating that he had reason to believe that income chargeable in respect of AY 1956-57 had escaped assessment within the meaning of s. 147, and calling for a return.
  • The assessee filed a writ petition in the Allahabad High Court challenging the notice as without jurisdiction. A learned single Judge dismissed it, and a Division Bench affirmed. The appeal to the Supreme Court was by certificate.
  • The Department's justification, as stated by the Division Bench: "The firm utilised certain drafts for making purchases at Madras and Calcutta. These drafts represented undisclosed income of the firm. This aspect of the matter was not considered at the time of the original assessment … The amounts, for which drafts were purchased by the firm, were not recorded in the disclosed account of the firm. It is, therefore, proposed to tackle that income for purposes of reassessment."
  • The flaw in that justification, exposed by the Court from the officer's own original order: in making the best judgment assessment, the officer had written "a detailed order"; he had "found out all about the drafts which were not mentioned in the assessee's books of account"; he had given the partners an opportunity to explain the drafts; and he had recorded the statement of one partner, Shri Om Prakash, in these words: "He has said that the drafts which were sent by him relating to Messrs Gemini Leather Stores were entered in the books of the firm while other drafts which he has made would be of others whose name he does not remember. As he is unable to tell to whom other drafts sent by him relate in spite of specific opportunities given to him, the obvious inference is that moneys of the drafts are that of the firm with which he is connected." The officer had added: "Since these drafts have been sent or received in such circumstances and by such persons connected with the firm the conclusion is obvious that these drafts relate to the firm."
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the Income-tax Officer had jurisdiction under s. 147(a) to issue the notice — i.e. whether he had reason to believe that income had escaped assessment by reason of the omission or failure of the assessee to disclose fully and truly all material facts.
  2. Whether the assessee's failure to disclose the transactions evidenced by the drafts could be said to have caused the escapement, where the officer had himself discovered those transactions and questioned the partners about them at the original assessment.
  3. Whether the material before the officer at the original assessment was sufficient, in law, to require him to draw the inference — and, if it was, whether his failure to draw it can be cured by a reopening.
  4. Whether the High Court's dismissal of the writ was right, having regard to the Calcutta Discount line of authority.
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue
  • The amounts invested in the drafts were never recorded in the disclosed accounts, and the assessee never explained their source. That is a failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts. The primary fact that was missing — the source of the funds — was within the assessee's knowledge alone.
  • The original officer may have come across the drafts, but he did not apply his mind to the question whether the amounts invested in them could be treated as part of the total income. The learned single Judge had so held, and the Division Bench agreed: because the assessee did not disclose the source of the amounts, all the conditions for invoking s. 147(a) were present.
  • The reopening is therefore based on an omission of the assessee, not on a change of view, and the Department is entitled to bring the escaped income to tax.
For the assessee (appellant)
  • The Income-tax Officer had every primary fact before him: he discovered the drafts, he questioned the partners, he recorded their statements, and he recorded his own conclusion that "the obvious inference is that moneys of the drafts are that of the firm". Nothing was hidden from him.
  • What he did not do was add the amounts to the income. That is an error of judgment in the making of the assessment, not a failure of disclosure by the assessee. Calcutta Discount is decisive: "Once all the primary facts are before the assessing authority, he requires no further assistance by way of disclosure. It is for him to decide what inferences of facts can be reasonably drawn…"
  • The notice was issued in 1965 in respect of the assessment year 1956-57 — after the period for the "information" limb had long expired, which is why the Department was trying to dress an oversight as a non-disclosure.
  • The assessee is entitled to have the notice quashed in writ proceedings, because the defect goes to jurisdiction and not merely to the regularity of the assessment.
4 · Decision

The appeal was allowed; the notice of 31 March 1965 and the proceedings in consequence were quashed (reader p. 176). Four steps in the reasoning:

  1. The question was confined to s. 147(a). "It is not disputed that the case falls under clause (a) of section 147. The question is whether the Income-tax Officer had reason to believe that income chargeable to tax had escaped assessment … by reason of the omission or failure on the part of the assessee to disclose fully and truly all material facts." Everything therefore turned on the causation of the escapement.
  2. Calcutta Discount restated: "Once all the primary facts are before the assessing authority, he requires no further assistance by way of disclosure. It is for him to decide what inferences of facts can be reasonably drawn and what legal inferences have ultimately to be drawn. It is not for somebody else — far less the assessee — to tell the assessing authority what inferences, whether of facts or of law, should be drawn."
  3. Applied to the drafts: "In the case before us the assessee did not disclose the transactions evidenced by the drafts which the Income-tax Officer discovered. After this discovery the Income-tax Officer had in his possession all the primary facts, and it was for him to make necessary enquiries and draw proper inferences as to whether the amounts invested in the purchase of the drafts could be treated as part of the total income of the assessee during the relevant year. This the Income-tax Officer did not do. It was plainly a case of oversight, and it cannot be said that the income chargeable to tax for the relevant assessment year had escaped assessment by reason of the omission or failure on the part of the assessee to disclose fully and truly all material facts."
  4. The conclusion, in a sentence worth quoting: "The Income-tax Officer had all the material facts before him when he made the original assessment. He cannot now take recourse to Section 147(a) to remedy the error resulting from his own oversight."
5 · Principle evolved
  1. "Oversight" is a jurisdictional answer. Where the escapement is attributable to the officer's failure to draw an inference from material he already had, the statutory condition — that the escapement be "by reason of" the assessee's non-disclosure — is not satisfied, and the notice is void. The reasoning is causal: the section requires the non-disclosure to have caused the escapement.
  2. A reopening is not a review of one's own order. The officer cannot use s. 147 to correct a mistake in his own assessment. The Act provides appeal, revision and rectification for that; s. 147 is for escaped income, not for regretted conclusions.
  3. Investigation and assessment are distinct. The officer investigated the drafts thoroughly and then failed to assess them. Because the investigation was complete, the primary facts were before him; because the assessment was incomplete, the income escaped. Only the first limb matters for jurisdiction.
  4. Writ jurisdiction is the appropriate remedy where the defect is jurisdictional — the Court quashed the notice itself, seven years after it was issued, without requiring the assessee to await the reassessment and appeal against it.
  5. A limit, supplied by Srikrishna: the reasoning assumes that the assessee's disclosure was otherwise honest. Where the transactions are fabricated, a later discovery that the lender was a name-lender is a discovery of a false primary fact — a case outside Gemini's protection.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It gives the assessee the strongest form of the argument: not "I disclosed", but "you knew". Where the assessment order itself records the very transaction now said to have escaped, the reopening is unsustainable.
  • It pairs with Burlop as the two "assessee wins" authorities on the disclosure limb: Burlop where the facts were disclosed and the inference missed; Gemini where the facts were discovered and the inference missed.
  • It is a reminder about limitation: the notice came seven years after the assessment, and the Department's inability to use the "information" limb (because the period had expired) is what forced it into the non-disclosure limb — and what made the case lose.
  • It illustrates the practical value of the original assessment record. The Court's decision turned on what the officer had written in 1958. In an examination problem, always look for what the original order says the officer considered.

Mapping: s. 147(a) of the 1961 Act (pre-1989) → the non-disclosure limb of s. 147 before 1989 and the proviso to s. 147 from 1989; the principle survives in the post-2021 requirement that the information must suggest escapement, which excludes information already absorbed in the original assessment.

09Case 35 · reader pp. 177–182ITO v. Lakhmani Mewal Das — "reason to believe", not "reason to suspect"

Why this is the central case of the topic

It is the fullest judicial statement of the jurisdictional conditions for reopening: the two conditions precedent, the requirement of recorded reasons, the Commissioner's sanction, the assessee's duty of disclosure, and — above all — the requirement of a "live link" or "rational connection" between the material and the belief. Almost every proposition in §5.6 of these notes comes from these six pages, and the case supplies the language ("reason to believe", not "reason to suspect") that examiners look for.

Income-tax Officer v. Lakhmani Mewal Das

(1976) 3 SCC 757 · H.R. Khanna J. · appeal from the Calcutta High Court (Full Bench) · AY 1958-59 · DU reader pp. 177–182
Case 35ss. 147(a)/148Live linkName-lender creditsSanctionStrict view
1 · Facts
  • The respondent was assessed for the assessment year 1958-59 under s. 23(3) of the 1922 Act on 14 June 1960, his total income being assessed at ₹37,872. In making the assessment the officer allowed a deduction of ₹15,991 by way of expenses claimed, including ₹10,494 by way of interest.
  • According to the assessee, he had produced, through his authorised representative, all books of account, bank statements and other necessary documents in connection with the return.
  • On 14 March 1967 the assessee received a notice dated 8 March 1967 issued under s. 148, stating that the officer had reason to believe that his income for 1958-59 had escaped assessment within the meaning of s. 147, and that the notice was being issued after obtaining the necessary satisfaction of the Commissioner of Income-tax. He was called upon to file a return within 30 days.
  • On 2 May 1967 the assessee, through his lawyer, replied that there was no material on which the officer could have reason to believe that income had escaped; that the condition precedent to jurisdiction was therefore not satisfied; that the assessment could not be reopened on a mere change of opinion; and he called upon the officer to furnish the material. Receiving no satisfactory response, he filed a petition under Article 226 to quash the notice.
  • The Department's affidavit denied that all relevant material had been produced at the original assessment, and stated that "subsequent to the assessment for 1958-59, it was discovered … that some of the loans shown to have been taken and interest alleged to have been paid thereon by the petitioner during the relevant assessment year were not genuine".
  • During the proceedings the High Court directed the Department to produce the report made to the Commissioner for sanction. It read: "There are hundi loan credits in the name of Narayansingh Nandalal, D.K. Naraindas, Bhagwandas Srichand, etc., who are known name-lenders, and also hundi loan credit in the name of Mohansingh Kanayalal, who has since confessed he was doing only name-lending. In the original assessment these credits were not investigated in detail. As the information regarding the bogus nature of these credits is since known, action under Section 147(a) is called for … The assessee is still claiming that the credits are genuine in the assessment proceedings for 1962-63. Commissioner's sanction is solicited."
  • A Full Bench of three Judges of the Calcutta High Court held, unanimously, that the assessee was not being charged with an omission to disclose but with having made an untrue disclosure (he had said he received loans from certain persons when in fact he had received nothing from them). On that footing the duty was not merely to disclose, but to disclose truly and not to mislead. But by a majority (A.K. Mukherjea and S.K. Mukherjea JJ., Sabyasachi Mukherji J. dissenting) the Court held that the conditions precedent were not fulfilled because the report to the Commissioner was defective in the manner pointed out in Chhugamal Rajpal v. S.P. Chaliha, (1971) 1 SCC 453 — the Commissioner had acted mechanically, not stating that he was satisfied that it was a fit case. The notice was accordingly quashed by majority, and the Revenue appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. The jurisdictional question: what conditions must be satisfied before an officer acquires jurisdiction to issue a notice under s. 148 in respect of an assessment beyond four years but within eight years of the end of the relevant year?
  2. The "reason to believe" question: what is the content of that expression — is it a purely subjective satisfaction, and how far may a court examine it?
  3. The rational-connection question: whether the material relied on by the officer (a confession of name-lending by one creditor, and the reputation of others as name-lenders) had a rational connection with the belief that income had escaped by reason of the assessee's non-disclosure.
  4. The sanction question: whether the Commissioner's satisfaction was vitiated because the report placed before him was defective (the point on which the majority in the High Court had proceeded).
  5. The disclosure question: whether the assessee's production of books and documents, and his positive assertion that the loans were genuine, amounted to a full and true disclosure of primary facts.
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue (appellant)
  • The majority of the High Court erred on the sanction. The Commissioner had before him the officer's report and gave his sanction; the absence of a recital in the report that "this is a fit case" cannot invalidate a sanction which was in fact applied for and granted. The Chhugamal Rajpal point was, on the facts, a technicality.
  • The material was substantial. One creditor had confessed that he was doing only name-lending; others were known name-lenders. The credits were hundi credits of the classic bogus kind. There was therefore ample reason to believe that income had escaped.
  • The assessee had not disclosed truly. He asserted that he had taken loans from persons from whom, on the later information, he had taken nothing, and that he had paid them interest. A false assertion of a material fact is not a disclosure; it attracts the jurisdiction of the officer.
  • The court cannot go into the sufficiency of the grounds; it can only ask whether the officer held the belief at all.
For the assessee (respondent) — Dr Pal
  • The report was defective, as the majority held, and the Commissioner acted mechanically; the sanction is therefore no sanction, and the notice fails on that ground alone.
  • The finding that the assessee was charged with an untrue disclosure is itself wrong. He had disclosed the credits and the interest; what the Department says it has since learnt is that the creditors were not genuine. That is a matter of inference from subsequent investigation, not a primary fact concealed by the assessee. The duty to disclose primary facts was discharged in full.
  • Above all, the material had no rational connection with the formation of the belief. The reference to "known name-lenders" says nothing about how or when that became known, and therefore nothing about this assessee's assessment year; and the confession of Mohansingh Kanayalal is undated and is not shown to relate to the loan of ₹2,500 recorded in the assessee's books, much less to the period 1 April 1957 to 31 March 1958. To infer that it does would be "far-fetched".
  • The words of the statute are "reason to believe", not "reason to suspect"; the deletion of "definite information" in 1948 did not lower the standard.
4 · Decision

The appeal was dismissed with costs — the notice remained quashed — but the Supreme Court reached that result by a different route from the High Court's majority, resting on the absence of a rational connection rather than on the defect in the sanction. Eight steps:

  1. The statutory framework. Sections 147 to 153 correspond to s. 34 of the 1922 Act. On the section as it stood, "two conditions have to be satisfied before an Income-tax Officer acquires jurisdiction to issue notice under Section 148 in respect of an assessment beyond the period of four years but within a period of eight years from the end of the relevant year, viz. (1) the Income-tax Officer must have reason to believe that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment, and (2) he must have reason to believe that such income has escaped assessment by reason of the omission or failure on the part of the assessee (a) to make a return … or (b) to disclose fully and truly material facts necessary for his assessment for that year. Both these conditions must coexist in order to confer jurisdiction" (reader p. 179).
  2. Two further requirements: "It is also imperative for the Income-tax Officer to record his reasons before initiating proceedings as required by Section 148(2). Another requirement is that before notice is issued after the expiry of four years … the Commissioner should be satisfied on the reasons recorded by the Income-tax Officer that it is a fit case for the issue of such notice."
  3. The assessee's duty, defined: "the duty which is cast upon the assessee is to make a true and full disclosure of the primary facts at the time of the original assessment. Production before the Income-tax Officer of the account books or other evidence from which material evidence could with due diligence have been discovered … will not necessarily amount to disclosure contemplated by law. The duty of the assessee in any case does not extend beyond making a true and full disclosure of primary facts. Once he has done that his duty ends. It is for the Income-tax Officer to draw the correct inference from the primary facts. It is no responsibility of the assessee to advise the Income-tax Officer with regard to the inference which he should draw … If an Income-tax Officer draws an inference which appears subsequently to be erroneous, mere change of opinion with regard to that inference would not justify initiation of action for reopening assessment."
  4. "Reason to believe" defined: "The grounds or reasons which lead to the formation of the belief … must have a material bearing on the question of escapement of income … Once there exist reasonable grounds for the Income-tax Officer to form the above belief, that would be sufficient to clothe him with jurisdiction … Whether the grounds are adequate or not is not a matter for the court to investigate. The sufficiency of grounds … is, therefore, not a justiciable issue. It is, of course, open to the assessee to contend that the Income-tax Officer did not hold the belief … The existence of the belief can be challenged by the assessee but not the sufficiency of reasons for the belief. The expression 'reason to believe' does not mean a purely subjective satisfaction … The reason must be held in good faith. It cannot be merely a pretence. It is open to the court to examine whether the reasons for the formation of the belief have a rational connection with or a relevant bearing on the formation of the belief and are not extraneous or irrelevant for the purpose of the section" (reader pp. 179–180).
  5. The two grounds in the report, taken separately. (i) The reference to Narayansingh Nandalal, D.K. Naraindas, Bhagwandas Srichand, etc. as "known name-lenders" does not "stand on a better footing than the reference to the three names in the report made by the Income-tax Officer in the case of Chhugamal Rajpal", and could not have led to the formation of the belief. (ii) The confession of Mohansingh Kanayalal fares no better: "There is nothing to show that the above confession related to a loan to the assessee and not to someone else, much less to the loan of ₹2,500 which was shown to have been advanced by that person … There is also no indication as to when that confession was made and whether it relates to the period from April 1, 1957 to March 31, 1958." The report was made on 13 February 1967; in the absence of the date of the confession "it would not be unreasonable to assume that the confession was made a few weeks or months before the report", and to infer from it that it related to the earlier year and to this loan "would be rather far-fetched".
  6. The rational-connection test, stated in full: "Rational connection postulates that there must be a direct nexus or live link between the material coming to the notice of the Income-tax Officer and the formation of his belief that there has been escapement of the income of the assessee from assessment in the particular year because of his failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts. It is no doubt true that the court cannot go into the sufficiency or adequacy of the material … At the same time we have to bear in mind that it is not any and every material, howsoever vague and indefinite or distant, remote and far-fetched, which would warrant the formation of the belief. The fact that the words 'definite information' which were there in Section 34 … before its amendment in 1948 are not there in Section 147 of the Act of 1961 would not lead to the conclusion that action can now be taken … even if the information is wholly vague, indefinite, far-fetched and remote. The reason for the formation of the belief must be held in good faith and should not be a mere pretence" (reader p. 181).
  7. The two sentences to memorise: "The powers of the Income-tax Officer to reopen assessment though wide are not plenary. The words of the statute are 'reason to believe' and not 'reason to suspect'. The reopening of the assessment after the lapse of many years is a serious matter … It is, therefore, essential that before such action is taken the requirements of the law should be satisfied." And, on the facts: "The live link or close nexus which should be there between the material before the Income-tax Officer … and the belief which he was to form … was missing in the case. In any event, the link was too tenuous to provide a legally sound basis for reopening the assessment."
  8. The result: "We would, therefore, uphold the view of the majority and dismiss the appeal with costs." Note that the Court did not rest on the defective-sanction point; it upheld the result on the absence of a rational connection, which was the third ground argued by Dr Pal.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Two conditions precedent, both mandatory — (i) reason to believe that income has escaped; and (ii) reason to believe that it escaped by reason of the assessee's failure to make a return or to disclose fully and truly. They must coexist. Beyond the shorter period, the second is the gateway.
  2. Two further requirements — recorded reasons (s. 148(2)) and the Commissioner's satisfaction (s. 151). These are the "checks" on the power, and the Court describes the whole scheme as designed to prevent mechanical reopening.
  3. The duty of disclosure ends with primary facts. The assessee need not draw inferences for the officer; a reopening because the officer now draws a different inference is a change of opinion.
  4. "Reason to believe" is not a purely subjective satisfaction. It must be held in good faith and must not be a pretence, and the court may examine whether the reasons have a rational connection with, or relevant bearing on, the belief. The court may not examine the sufficiency or adequacy of the material.
  5. The live-link test. The material must be connected to (i) this assessee, (ii) this assessment year, and (iii) this escapement. Material which could relate to anyone, or to any period, is "vague, indefinite, far-fetched and remote" and cannot support jurisdiction.
  6. The standard was not lowered in 1948 or 1961. The deletion of "definite information" from the statute does not mean that vague information will do.
  7. "Reason to believe", not "reason to suspect". The most quoted six words in the topic, and a complete answer to a problem in which the officer has only a hunch.
  8. Reopening after many years is a serious matter — the Court's own words, and the policy reason behind the insistence on strict compliance: the Act departs from the "normal rule" of finality, and so must be strictly complied with.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the case to cite on jurisdiction. For any problem in which the assessee challenges a s. 148 notice, the answer is: (i) was there a reason to believe, (ii) held in good faith, (iii) on material with a live link to this year and this assessee, (iv) with recorded reasons, and (v) with a valid sanction?
  • It explains the standard of "information" now in s. 148 (post-2021): Parliament's substitution of "information which suggests" for "reason to believe" is a legislative attempt to codify what Lakhmani required — specific, relevant, connected information rather than a suspicion.
  • It supplies the contrast for Srikrishna: five years later, the same Court upheld a notice on materially similar facts (bogus hundi loans revealed in a later year) because there the link was proved — ten named lenders, ₹3,80,000, common to both years, all established as bogus. The difference between the two cases is the difference between a name and a link (§11.3).
  • Its dissenting-and-majority structure is a lesson in advocacy: the High Court majority quashed the notice on the sanction; the Supreme Court quashed it on the live link. Both grounds were available, and a good answer pleads both.

Mapping: decided under the pre-1989 ss. 147(a)/148 of the 1961 Act. The two conditions precedent survive as the proviso to s. 147 (1989–2021) and, since 2021, as the requirement of information in s. 148 read with s. 148A; the live-link test survives untouched and is applied to the post-2021 "information" in every writ petition on the subject.

10Case 36 · reader pp. 183–189Srikrishna (P) Ltd. v. ITO — a false disclosure is no disclosure

Why this case closes the topic

It is the last and, doctrinally, the most modern of the five. It accepts everything Burlop, Gemini and Lakhmani say — and then explains why none of them helps an assessee who invented the primary facts. The line between the first three cases and this one is the line the whole chapter turns on: truthful disclosure, however unhelpful to the Department, protects finality; a false disclosure forfeits it. And the case is also a model of how the court's enquiry is conducted: the Supreme Court called for the record and asked a specific question — were the lenders the same persons? — before deciding.

Srikrishna (P) Ltd. v. Income-tax Officer

(1996) 9 SCC 534 · B.P. Jeevan Reddy J. · appeal from the Calcutta High Court · AY 1959-60 · DU reader pp. 183–189
Case 36ss. 147(a)/148Bogus hundi loansFalse disclosurePhool Chand followedBurlop confined
1 · Facts
  • In the return for the assessment year 1959-60 the assessee-company had shown unsecured hundi loans of ₹8,53,298 said to have been taken from a number of persons. The Income-tax Officer accepted the averment and made the assessment.
  • In the assessment proceedings for the succeeding year, 1960-61, the assessee again showed hundi loans of more than ₹17 lakh. This time the officer enquired into the truth of the averment and found that many of them were bogus, and that some of the alleged lenders were near relations of directors or principal shareholders of the company. He held that out of the loans claimed, loans totalling ₹11,15,275 were not established to be genuine and added that amount as income from undisclosed sources.
  • "Having regard to the similarity of the claims and the persons who are said to have advanced the said unsecured hundi loans" in the earlier year, the Income-tax Officer issued a notice under s. 148 calling upon the assessee to file a revised return for 1959-60.
  • The assessee immediately filed a writ petition in the Calcutta High Court, contending that the officer had no reasonable ground to believe that income had escaped on account of any omission or failure on its part to make a full and true disclosure. A learned Single Judge allowed the petition; a Division Bench reversed; and the Supreme Court granted special leave on 26 July 1977 but declined to stay the proceedings, directing instead that the officer might complete the reassessment but should not issue a demand notice. The reassessment was accordingly completed, and the appeal survived as a challenge to the notice.
  • The reasons recorded by the officer (as required by s. 148(2)) were set out in full: in the 1960-61 proceedings investigations into unsecured loans of ₹17,32,298 "disclosed that a large number of them were bogus hundi loans or loans from near relations of the Directors or principal shareholders", and amounts credited to some of those accounts were assessed as undisclosed income to the extent of ₹11,51,275; "similar loans are noticed for the Assessment Year 1959-60 and they stand at ₹8,53,298 as per Balance-Sheet as on 16-4-1959"; "I have, therefore reasons to believe that by reason of omission or failure on the part of the assessee company to disclose fully and truly all material facts necessary for its assessment of 1959-60 in regard to these accounts, income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment. I, therefore, propose action under Section 147(a)."
  • The Court's own enquiry. After hearing the appeal for some time the Court found it appropriate to look into the record and passed an order on 10 October 1995 asking whether, at the time of issuing the notice, the officer had material showing that the persons who had lent ₹8,53,298 in the 1959-60 year were the very same persons who were said to have lent ₹11,51,275 (the bogus loans) in the 1960-61 year. In response the officer filed a chart showing that, of the ₹8,53,298, ten persons said to have lent a total of ₹3,80,000 were common to both years, and that all ten had been found to be bogus lenders in the 1960-61 proceedings.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether, in the facts stated, it could be said that the officer had no reason to believe that, on account of the assessee's omission or failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts, income had escaped assessment for 1959-60 — i.e. whether the notice under s. 148 was warranted at all.
  2. What is the content of the obligation to disclose "fully and truly" — and, in particular, whether a false or partial disclosure satisfies it.
  3. Whether a transaction which the assessee has positively asserted (a loan) but which is later found to be fictitious is a material fact or an inference — the distinction on which Burlop and Gemini turned.
  4. Whether the earlier authorities — Chhugamal Rajpal, Lakhmani Mewal Das and Burlop Dealers — laid down anything contrary, and whether Burlop is of universal application.
  5. At what stage the enquiry is conducted, and what the assessee must show at the stage of the notice (as opposed to the stage of the completed reassessment).
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue
  • The obligation to disclose is not satisfied by a false statement. The assessee asserted loans from named persons; in truth those persons were name-lenders or non-existent. Such an assertion is not a disclosure at all — it is a positive design to mislead.
  • The material is specific and connected: the officer had found, in the very next year's proceedings, that a large number of the loans were bogus and that many of the lenders were near relations of the directors; and the loans for 1959-60 were similar loans, from the same persons. Ten of those lenders, ₹3,80,000 of the ₹8,53,298, had already been established to be bogus.
  • At the stage of the notice the question is only whether there were reasonable grounds for the belief — not whether the escapement is established. The sufficiency of the reasons is not for the court.
  • Burlop and its companions were cases of truthful disclosure and a missed inference; they have no application where the primary fact itself was fabricated.
For the assessee (appellant) — Shri Gupta
  • The assessee had disclosed the loans in its balance sheet and the officer had accepted them at the original assessment; all primary facts were before him. What the officer has since learnt is that the lenders were not genuine — an inference drawn from a later investigation, not a fact concealed from him. On Burlop and Gemini, a reopening on that basis is a change of opinion or the correction of the officer's own oversight.
  • Chhugamal Rajpal and Lakhmani Mewal Das are directly in point: a mere reference to names of alleged name-lenders, without anything to connect them to the assessee's year of assessment, cannot support the belief.
  • The reasons recorded are vague: "similar loans" is not a finding; the officer does not say that the same persons lent in both years, nor that their loans in the earlier year were fictitious.
  • If the notice is bad in respect of some items it should be quashed altogether, because the officer's belief was a single composite belief.
4 · Decision

The appeal was dismissed: the notice was valid and the reopening was lawful. The reasoning develops in ten steps, and it is the most complete statement of the modern position:

  1. The obligation. "Section 139 places an obligation upon every person to furnish voluntarily a return of his total income … The obligation so placed involves the further obligation to disclose all material facts necessary for his assessment for that year fully and truly. If at any subsequent point of time, it is found that either on account of an omission or failure of the assessee to file the return or on account of his omission or failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts … income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment … the Income Tax Officer is entitled to reopen the assessment" (reader p. 183).
  2. The conditions as checks. "The existence of the reason(s) to believe is supposed to be the check, a limitation, upon his power to reopen the assessment." Section 148(2) imposes "a further check … viz., the requirement of recording of reasons", and s. 151 "yet another check … the Commissioner or the Board, as the case may be, has to be satisfied … that it is a fit case". "The power conferred … is thus not an unbridled one. It is hedged in with several safeguards conceived in the interest of eliminating room for abuse of this power … The idea was to save the assessees from harassment resulting from mechanical reopening of assessment, but this protection avails only those assessees who disclose all material facts truly and fully" (reader p. 184).
  3. The chart, and the presumption. Because the assessee had not alleged that the officer failed to tally the names of the lenders for the two years, "we must presume that the Income Tax Officer did find that a large number of alleged lenders who were found to be bogus during the Assessment Year 1960-61 were also put forward as lenders during the Assessment Year 1959-60 as well. Evidently, this is what he meant … when he spoke of 'similar loans'".
  4. The central proposition: "Every disclosure is not and cannot be treated to be a true and full disclosure. A disclosure may be a false one or true one. It may be a full disclosure or it may not be. A partial disclosure may very often be a misleading one. What is required is a full and true disclosure of all material facts necessary for making assessment for that year" (reader p. 185).
  5. Calcutta Discount restated — and its limit identified. The Constitution Bench had held that the duty is to disclose primary facts, and that the true nature of a transaction "being a matter capable of different opinions, is not a material or primary fact but a matter of inference". The Court in Srikrishna accepts that, and distinguishes it: "the obligation on the assessee to disclose the material facts … is not a mere disclosure but a disclosure which is full and true. A false disclosure is not a true disclosure. … A false assertion, or statement, of material fact, therefore, attracts the jurisdiction of the Income Tax Officer under Sections 34/147" (reader p. 186).
  6. The fact/inference distinction, applied: "Indubitably, whether a loan, alleged to have been taken by the assessee, is true or false, is a material fact — and not an inference, factual or legal, to be drawn from given facts." And, memorably: "Having created and recorded bogus entries of loans, with what face can the assessee say that he had truly and fully disclosed all material facts necessary for his assessment?" (reader p. 187).
  7. Why the officer's failure to investigate does not help. "True it is that Income Tax Officer could have investigated the truth of the said assertion — which he actually did in the subsequent assessment year — but that does not relieve the assessee of his obligation, placed upon him by the statute, to disclose fully and truly all material facts." This is the exact point on which Gemini is distinguished: there the officer had found the facts and failed to assess; here the assessee had falsified the facts, and the later discovery merely revealed the falsity.
  8. The stage of the enquiry: "It is necessary to reiterate that we are now at the stage of the validity of the notice under sections 148/147. The enquiry at this stage is only to see whether there are reasonable grounds for the Income Tax Officer to believe and not whether the omission/failure and the escapement of income is established. It is necessary to keep this distinction in mind."
  9. Phool Chand Bajrang Lal adopted, and the earlier cases confined. The Court follows Phool Chand Bajrang Lal v. ITO, (1993) 4 SCC 77 and quotes its holding that the sufficiency of the reasons is not for the court, but that the assessee may establish that there was no belief, or that it was not bona fide or was based on "vague, irrelevant and non-specific information", and that the court may examine whether the material had "any rational connection or a live link" with the belief. It then deals with Chhugamal Rajpal, Lakhmani Mewal Das and Burlop Dealers: "We cannot agree" that they lay down contrary propositions. "Calcutta Discount emphasises repeatedly the assessee's obligation to disclose all material facts … fully and truly … This obligation can neither be ignored nor watered down. Nor can anyone suggest that a false disclosure satisfies the requirement of full and true disclosure." And, specifically on Burlop: the Court agrees with Phool Chand "for holding that the decision of this Court in Burlop Dealers must be confined to the particular fact-situation of that case and that it cannot be construed to be of universal application irrespective of the facts and circumstances of the case before the Court" (reader p. 188).
  10. The supporting authorities, and the severability point. The Court also relies on Inspecting Assistant Commissioner v. V.I.P. Industries Ltd., (1991) 191 ITR 661 (SC) (subsequent facts showing the return was not a true declaration), and on Central Provinces Manganese Ore Co. Ltd. v. ITO, (1991) 4 SCC 166 (a Customs finding of under-invoicing is a valid reason to believe, even though not binding on the income-tax authorities; and non-production of the head-office accounts and original contracts satisfied the non-disclosure condition). Finally, on severability, it cites ITO v. Mewalal Dwarka Prasad, (1989) 176 ITR 529 (SC): if a notice is good in respect of one item, it cannot be quashed merely because it may not be valid in respect of other items — though the Court adds that no such argument had been urged before it.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. "Fully and truly" is a conjunctive requirement. A disclosure may be full but false, or true but partial; only a disclosure that is both satisfies s. 139 and protects against reopening. A partial disclosure may be a misleading one.
  2. A false assertion of a primary fact attracts s. 147. Whether a loan is real or fictitious is a material fact, not an inference; accordingly the Burlop/Gemini line — which protects an assessee who disclosed truthfully and lost only because the officer failed to draw an inference — does not apply.
  3. The safeguards are for the honest assessee only. The checks of s. 147 (reason to believe), s. 148(2) (recorded reasons) and s. 151 (sanction) exist "to save the assessees from harassment resulting from mechanical reopening", but "this protection avails only those assessees who disclose all material facts truly and fully".
  4. At the notice stage the court asks only whether there were reasonable grounds. It does not ask whether the escapement is established; that is for the reassessment. This is the standard against which a writ petition against a s. 148 notice is tested.
  5. Material must be specific and linked. The case shows what a live link looks like: named lenders, a stated amount (₹3,80,000 of ₹8,53,298), common to both years, each established as bogus. Compare Lakhmani, where the names were unconnected and the confession undated.
  6. Burlop is confined to its facts. It is not a general charter for assessees who file false returns; it is authority for the proposition that a truthful discloser need not argue the Department's case.
  7. Severability: a notice good for one item survives even if bad for another — though an assessee may argue that a composite belief cannot be severed.
  8. The court will look at the record, and may ask its own question. The order of 10 October 1995 and the chart filed in response are a reminder that the "reasons recorded" are tested against what the officer actually had, not merely against the language he used.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the modern authority, decided after all the others in the reader, and it aligns Indian law with Phool Chand Bajrang Lal. Where a problem involves invented credits, bogus loans, or a return that conceals the true character of a transaction, Srikrishna is the case to cite.
  • It supplies the counter-argument to every Burlop defence — and examiners love the pairing. The question "does Burlop help an assessee whose books contain fictitious entries?" is answered by quoting Srikrishna's confinement of Burlop to its facts.
  • It explains the shape of the present law. The post-2021 requirement of "information which suggests" escapement, and the s. 148A show-cause, are legislative descendants of the twin ideas in this case: the information must be specific, and the assessee must have a chance to answer it.
  • It shows how much turns on the framing of the assessee's case. The Single Judge had allowed the writ; the Division Bench reversed; the Supreme Court affirmed the Division Bench. The difference was not the facts but the legal characterisation of a false entry as a fact or an inference.

Mapping: decided under ss. 147(a)/148 as they stood before 1989; the reasoning is expressly built on s. 139 (obligation to disclose fully and truly), which is unchanged, and on the checks in ss. 148(2) and 151, whose modern equivalents are s. 148's "information" requirement and s. 151's "specified authority".

Read separately, the five cases look inconsistent: notices quashed in four and upheld in one; Burlop and Srikrishna apparently at war; Gemini and Srikrishna turning on nearly the same fact of a missed investigation. Read together, they are one rule applied to five different qualities of disclosure. The single rule behind all five

Escapement jurisdiction beyond the shorter period is built on the assessee's duty to disclose fully and truly all material facts. Every protection the chapter gives — finality of assessment, the bar on change of opinion, the requirement of a live link — is given because that duty is presumed performed. Where the duty is performed, the assessee is safe even if the officer blundered (Burlop, Gemini). Where the material has no connection with the assessee's year, the officer has no jurisdiction even though he suspects a great deal (Lakhmani). Where the duty is breached, no protection survives (Srikrishna). And where the very power to assess on an estimate is exercised, the estimate must still be a judgment (Velukutty).

◆11.3 The shift in doctrine

Phase 1 — the wide power and the estimate (1966). Velukutty belongs to the best-judgment strand rather than the reassessment strand, and it is placed first for a reason: it establishes that even the widest estimate is a judicial act. The officer may guess, but the guess must have "a reasonable nexus to the available material"; a ratio discovered in one shop cannot travel to another; and where the appellate court has all the material, it should decide rather than remand. The lesson is carried into the reassessment cases: the Department's powers are wide, but they are powers to be exercised on material and by reasoning.

Phase 2 — the protection of the assessee (1971–1975). Burlop and Gemini are the high water mark of finality. The assessee's duty is defined narrowly and the Department's capacity to correct itself by reopening is correspondingly confined: no reopening on a reappraisal of the same facts, and none to repair the officer's own oversight. In Burlop the officer had actually found the "device" in the later year and tried to reopen the earlier one; the Court said the assessee had done all that the statute asked, and that a disclosure is not undone by the officer's later change of mind.

Phase 3 — the discipline of jurisdiction (1976). Lakhmani does not reverse Phase 2 but surrounds it with conditions: the two conditions precedent must coexist; reasons must be recorded; the Commissioner must be satisfied; and the material must have a live link. The judicial technique is notable — the Court accepts that it cannot ask whether the grounds were sufficient, but insists that it may ask whether they were relevant. That distinction is the whole of Indian administrative law on subjective satisfaction compressed into one paragraph.

Phase 4 — the return of substance (1996). Srikrishna realigns the balance in the Department's favour, but on principle rather than on power: the protections of the chapter are "conceived in the interest of eliminating room for abuse", and they "avail only those assessees who disclose all material facts truly and fully". The Court openly confines Burlop to its facts and adopts Phool Chand Bajrang Lal. The doctrinal move is the reclassification of a false statement about a loan as a material fact rather than an inference — which removes such cases from the protection entirely, without disturbing the protection for the honest assessee.

Question 5 · LB-505, December 2024 · 20 marks

Best judgments. 144ss. 147–153ss. 139/142/143Case law

"The Best Judgement assessment should be made by giving notice of intention and an opportunity to the assessee to file his reply and evidences against Best Judgement assessment. In light of the above statement, discuss the concept of Best Judgement assessment comparing it with the concept of Income escaping assessment with the support of relevant provisions of the Income Tax Act and case law."

Model answer

1 · Reading the statement. The proposition to be examined contains three assertions and one comparison, and the answer should be organised around them: (a) that a best judgment assessment is preceded by a notice of intention; (b) that the assessee is given an opportunity to file a reply and evidence; and (c) that these features are what distinguish, or fail to distinguish, best judgment assessment from income escaping assessment. My submission will be that (a) and (b) are substantially correct as a statement of the section and of the case law, but that the contrast with escapement assessment must be drawn along four axes — trigger, stage, material and effect — and not along the notice-and-hearing axis, because the requirement of hearing attaches to both.

2 · The statutory setting. The Act provides four ways in which a total income may be determined: summary processing under s. 143(1); scrutiny assessment under s. 143(3); best judgment assessment under s. 144; and reassessment under ss. 147 to 153. Sections 139 (return), 142 (inquiry), 143 (assessment), 145(2) (accounting standards and the rejection of books under s. 145(3)) supply the material on which either kind of assessment is founded, and the two are linked at one crucial point: a best judgment assessment is itself an assessment, and may therefore itself be reopened if it was made on a non-disclosure or on information suggesting escapement.

Part A — Best judgment assessment: s. 144

3 · When it is made. Section 144(1) directs that the Assessing Officer shall, after taking into account all relevant material which he has gathered and after giving the assessee an opportunity of being heard, make the assessment of the total income or loss to the best of his judgment, where any of these conditions exists: (i) no return has been furnished within the time allowed under s. 139 and the officer is not satisfied that there was reasonable cause for the default; (ii) the assessee has failed to comply with the terms of a notice under s. 142(1); (iii) he has failed to comply with directions issued for the production of accounts or documents; (iv) he has failed to comply with a direction for special audit under s. 142(2A); (v) he has failed to comply with the procedure and directions of the faceless scheme under s. 144B; or (vi) the books of account have been rejected under s. 145(3) because they are incorrect or incomplete, or because the assessee has not maintained any accounts though required to do so by s. 44AA. The section is thus penal in form and remedial in purpose: it exists so that a defaulting assessee cannot escape assessment altogether.

4 · The notice of intention and the opportunity. The statement in the question is accurate so far as it goes. Section 144 itself requires an "opportunity of being heard"; the section does not, however, use the words "show-cause notice", and it is the general law of natural justice — audi alteram partem — which supplies the content of that opportunity. In practice, and as a matter of valid procedure, the officer must therefore: (i) put the assessee on notice that he proposes to proceed ex parte and on which of the statutory grounds; (ii) indicate the material he proposes to use, so that the reply is meaningful; (iii) give a reasonable time to reply; and (iv) consider the reply, and any evidence tendered, before passing the order. An order which records that the assessee "was given opportunity" but discloses no material and no application of mind is vulnerable, because the opportunity must be an opportunity against the proposed assessment, not a formality. Because s. 144 requires the officer to take into account all relevant material which he has gathered, the record must show what that material was.

5 · The content of "best judgment" — Velukutty. The leading authority is State of Kerala v. C. Velukutty, (1966) 60 ITR 239 (SC) (reader pp. 168–171, case 32), decided under s. 12(2)(b) of the Kerala General Sales Tax Act but accepted as the classic statement of the principle for all best-judgment provisions. The assessee there kept secret books disclosing a far larger turnover than the books produced; the officer estimated the retail turnover at disclosed + 135%, and then applied a higher ratio to the wholesale branch at another place. The Supreme Court set the estimate aside, holding that "Judgment is a faculty to decide matters with wisdom truly and legally" and is not "arbitrary caprice"; that the officer's guess must not be "a wild one" but must have "a reasonable nexus to the available material"; that the ratio of concealed to disclosed turnover in one shop could not be transplanted to a different shop; and, importantly for procedure, that the High Court, having all the material before it, should have decided the matter rather than remanding it. 6 · Section 145(2) and the rejection of books. Before any estimate can be made the accounts must be rejected under s. 145(3), which becomes available where the Assessing Officer is not satisfied as to the correctness or completeness of the accounts, or where the accounting standards notified under s. 145(2) (the Income Computation and Disclosure Standards) have not been followed. The rejection must be supported by reasons; the officer cannot reject the books merely because he would have drawn the accounts differently. Once the books are rejected, the estimate under s. 144 must still satisfy Velukutty: a nexus with such material as survives.

7 · Faceless machinery. Since 2021 assessments under s. 143(3) and s. 144 are made through the faceless assessment scheme and s. 144B prescribes its procedure (notice under s. 143(2), a draft assessment order, a show-cause, a draft penalty notice, and a final order with the approval of the National Faceless Assessment Centre). Non-compliance with these directions is itself a ground of best judgment assessment under s. 144(1), and the right to be heard is enforced through the draft-order stage. outside reader

Part B — Income escaping assessment: ss. 147 to 153

8 · The trigger. Section 147 permits the Assessing Officer to assess income which has escaped assessment where he has reason to believe (as the section stood until 2021) or, since the Finance Act 2021, information which suggests that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment, and — for a reopening beyond four years — where that escapement is attributable to the assessee's failure to make a return or to disclose fully and truly all material facts. Section 148 requires a notice calling for a return, with the reasons recorded; s. 148A (2021) requires an inquiry, a show-cause notice with the material relied on, a consideration of the reply and a reasoned order; s. 149 fixes the outer limits (three years, or ten years where the escaped income represented by an asset is ₹50 lakh or more); s. 151 requires the sanction of a specified authority; and s. 153 prescribes the time limit for completing the reassessment.

9 · The jurisdictional conditions — Lakhmani Mewal Das. The controlling authority in the reader is ITO v. Lakhmani Mewal Das, (1976) 3 SCC 757 (reader pp. 177–182, case 35). Two conditions must coexist: a reason to believe that income has escaped, and a reason to believe that it escaped by reason of the assessee's omission or failure to disclose. Reasons must be recorded, and the Commissioner must be satisfied. Most importantly, the material must have "a direct nexus or live link" with the belief: "it is not any and every material, howsoever vague and indefinite or distant, remote and far-fetched, which would warrant the formation of the belief"; and "the words of the statute are 'reason to believe' and not 'reason to suspect'". The existence of the belief is justiciable; its sufficiency is not.

10 · The other four cases, in one line each. The answer should deploy all five, because the question asks for "case law" and the five cases in the syllabus are precisely the five propositions:

  • CIT v. Burlop Dealers Ltd., (1971) 79 ITR 609 (case 33) — the assessee disclosed the primary facts (the joint-venture agreement dividing the profit); the officer's later view that the arrangement was a "device" is a change of opinion. There is no duty "to instruct the Income-tax Officer about the inference which he may draw".
  • Gemini Leather Stores v. ITO, AIR 1975 SC 1268 (case 34) — the purchase drafts were discussed in the officer's own best judgment order; the reopening was "plainly a case of oversight", and s. 147(a) cannot be used "to remedy the error resulting from his own oversight".
  • Lakhmani Mewal Das (case 35) — no live link; names of "known name-lenders" and an undated confession are too tenuous.
  • Srikrishna (P) Ltd. v. ITO, (1996) 9 SCC 534 (case 36) — "A false disclosure is not a true disclosure … A partial disclosure may very often be a misleading one"; whether a loan is genuine "is a material fact — and not an inference"; so an assessee who manufactured bogus hundi credits cannot plead Burlop, which is "confined to the particular fact-situation of that case".
  • Velukutty (case 32) — the estimate standard, applied where the reassessment culminates in a best judgment assessment.

11 · The procedural sequence after 2021. The answer should set out the four gates: information (s. 148) → inquiry, show cause and order (s. 148A) → sanction of the specified authority (s. 151) → time limits (ss. 149, 153); then the notice, the return in response, the assessment, and the appeal. The 2021 scheme replaced the earlier practice of issuing a notice and leaving the assessee to challenge it, by requiring the Department to disclose its material before the notice — a legislative adoption of the principles in the cases.

12 · Where the statement is right, and where it needs qualification. The statement is right that a best judgment assessment must be preceded by an intimation of intention and an opportunity to file a reply and evidence: that is what "after giving the assessee an opportunity of being heard" means, and it is the reason why an ex parte estimate made without notice is unsustainable. It needs three qualifications, and it is these that lift an answer from the average to the good: (i) the "notice of intention" is not a statutory notice of the s. 148 kind, and its content is judge-made — drawn from natural justice and from the requirement that the officer take into account all relevant material; (ii) the same requirement of hearing exists in escapement assessment, and in a more elaborate statutory form after 2021, so notice and opportunity cannot be the point of distinction between the two concepts; and (iii) the true distinctions are trigger, stage, jurisdictional precondition and effect on finality, as Table 12.2 shows. To that must be added the point of connection: the two are not alternatives but sequential — a s. 144 order may be reopened under s. 147, and a s. 147 proceeding may end in a s. 144 order if the assessee defaults again.

13 · Illustration of the interplay (worth two marks). A fails to file a return; the officer makes a best judgment assessment under s. 144 estimating income at ₹12 lakh on the basis of a bank-deposit summary. If A later produces the books and shows a disclosed turnover of ₹30 lakh on which no tax was assessed because the officer overlooked a limb of income, the Department's remedy is not s. 147(a) — that is the officer's own oversight (Gemini) and a change of opinion (Burlop), and in any case A never had an opportunity to disclose what was never asked. But if A had, in a return filed for a later year, admitted that ₹20 lakh of receipts were suppressed in the earlier year, and the same receipts are identifiable, the case falls within Srikrishna: a specific, linked material showing a false disclosure, on which a s. 148 notice is valid. And whichever way it goes, any fresh estimate must still satisfy Velukutty.

14 · Conclusion. Best judgment assessment under s. 144 is the Act's answer to the non-cooperating assessee; income escaping assessment under ss. 147 to 153 is its answer to the incomplete or untrue return of a cooperating one. Both confer wide powers, and both are controlled by the same two ideas — hearing and nexus: the assessee must be heard before an estimate is made against him, and the estimate or the belief must rest on material actually connected with him. The five cases in the syllabus trace that control from Velukutty (1966) to Srikrishna (1996): the powers are "wide but not plenary", and the words of the statute are "reason to believe", not "reason to suspect".

◆12.3 December 2024 — Question 3 (Mr ATEESH / Ms TANTO): the reassessment dimension

Question 3 · LB-505, December 2024 · 20 marks · the escapement half only

Notice of escapementDisclosure in books150% estimates. 147

"… The Assessing Officer (AO) clubbed the income from salary of both, with each other. The AO issued a notice of income escaping assessment with evidences where the transfer transaction between both the businesses are shown under the head of sales in books of accounts. The AO computed the income of Mr. ATEESH as 150% and the same was applied to Ms. TANTO as well. Mr. ATEESH and Ms. TANTO have planned to challenge the actions of Assessing Officer. As a tax lawyer, advise them how to proceed following various legal provisions and landmark cases under Income Tax Act, 1961."

Model answer on the escapement and estimate limbs

The clubbing limb of this question belongs to Topic 5 and is answered in the Topic-5 notes; what follows deals only with the s. 147 notice and the 150% computation, which are Topic 6 points.

1 · The notice is challenged on disclosure, not on suspicion. The facts state that the transfer transactions between the two businesses are shown in the books of account under the head "sales". If that is so, the assessee has done what Burlop Dealers (case 33) requires — disclosed the primary facts — and what the Department now says is that the inference drawn from those facts (that these were genuine sales rather than an arrangement between spouses) was wrong. On Burlop and on the principle in Lakhmani (case 35), a different inference from the same disclosed material is a change of opinion, and beyond four years it is barred by the words of the proviso to s. 147. The counter-argument, and it must be met, is Srikrishna (case 36): if what is shown in the books is not the truth but a false description of the transaction, the disclosure is not a true disclosure at all, and the notice is valid. The advice therefore turns on evidence: do the books record the transactions in a manner that discloses their real character, or do they disguise them?

2 · The live link must be shown. Even accepting the Department's case, a notice under s. 148 must disclose information with "a direct nexus or live link" to the escapement alleged. A general assertion that "transactions between the two businesses are under scrutiny" is the very kind of "vague and indefinite" material that Lakhmani rejects; the notice must identify the amounts, the entries and the year. After 2021, s. 148A(b) obliges the officer to supply that material before the notice, and s. 148A(d) obliges him to pass a reasoned order — the assessee's first step is therefore to demand and answer that show-cause, and to challenge the s. 148A(d) order if it is unreasoned. 2021 scheme — outside reader

3 · The 150% has no statutory basis — and Velukutty is directly in point. There is no provision in the Act which permits the Assessing Officer to compute income at 150% of the returned figure, or to apply one assessee's enhancement to the other. Even where an estimate is permissible — under s. 144, or in a reassessment where books are rejected under s. 145(3) — the estimate must be a judgment, made "with wisdom truly and legally", on "a reasonable nexus to the available material", and a ratio applied to one business cannot be transplanted to another (Velukutty, reader pp. 169–170). Applying one spouse's 150% to the other spouse's separate business is precisely the error the Supreme Court condemned. The clients should therefore challenge the 150% as (i) without jurisdiction, (ii) unrelated to any material, and (iii) a mechanical transplant.

4 · How to proceed, in order. (i) Obtain the reasons recorded and (after 2021) the s. 148A(b) material and the s. 148A(d) order; (ii) reply on merits, putting the books and the entries in issue and asserting full disclosure; (iii) if the notice survives, move a writ petition under Article 226 to quash it for want of jurisdiction — the absence of a live link and the absence of any failure to disclose being jurisdictional facts, not matters for the appellate authority (Lakhmani); (iv) in the alternative, contest the assessment on merits, with Velukutty against the 150%, and only then appeal under s. 246A; (v) keep the clubbing and the escapement challenges separate, because they rest on different provisions and different cases.

5 · Conclusion. The clubbing is unlawful (Topic 5); the notice is vulnerable to the extent it rests on material already disclosed in the books and on no more than a change of opinion (Burlop, Gemini), unless the Department can show a false or partial disclosure within Srikrishna; and the 150% enhancement is unsustainable on Velukutty in any event.

◆12.4 December 2024 — Question 4 (Ms NEENA): the s. 148 reopening limb

Question 4 · LB-505, December 2024 · 20 marks · reopening limb only

s. 148Change of opinionReasons recordedWrit

"Ms. NEENA earned a capital gain of Rs. 20 lakhs by selling her residential house property … She acquired that property … under the exemption scheme of reinvestment u/s 54 of the Income Tax Act, 1961. … In year 2020-21, the Income Tax Officer (ITO) reopened the assessment and issued notice to Ms. NEENA u/s 148 with a view that whole amount shall be charged to tax under the head capital gain. ITO also rejected the exemption of Rs. 60,000 claimed as agricultural income received as dividend from MMR Ltd. As an income tax law practitioner, discuss the arguments citing landmark cases."

Answer skeleton on the reopening
  • Threshold question — is there any failure to disclose? The claim under s. 54 and the claim of ₹60,000 as agricultural income were made in the return. A claim made openly, however wrong in law, is not a concealment of a primary fact: it is a disclosed claim which the officer either accepted or ought to have examined. On Burlop (case 33) and Lakhmani (case 35) that is a change of opinion, and s. 147's proviso bars a reopening beyond four years on such a ground. The only escape for the Department is Srikrishna (case 36) — proof that the facts stated were false (e.g. that the sale consideration or the reinvestment was misstated), not merely mischaracterised.
  • The reasons must be specific. A notice which asserts that "the whole amount shall be charged under the head capital gain" without stating what material suggests escapement, or which year's which fact was suppressed, is bad for want of a live link and for absence of application of mind. Ask for the recorded reasons; they are not privileged from disclosure at the stage of the challenge. 2021 scheme — outside reader
  • Sanction and time. The reopening in 2020-21 for the year 2018-19 is within three years, so the proviso to s. 147 (failure to disclose) is not even required — but s. 148A's predecessor requirement of recorded reasons and s. 151 sanction still had to be satisfied, and the notice must show which limb of s. 147 is invoked. Post-2021, s. 148A would have required a show-cause before any notice. outside reader
  • Remedy. A writ petition under Article 226 is the standard remedy, because want of jurisdiction is a collateral issue; alternatively, participate, obtain a reasoned assessment, and appeal. The safest advice is to participate without prejudice and reserve the jurisdictional objection.
  • Note (b) — a distinction the examiner will like. The rejection, in the original assessment, of a claim which was made in the return is not a reopening at all — in Question 2 (Mr FERN) the officer "rejected his claims" in the assessment itself. That is an ordinary assessment on merits, appealable under s. 246A. Only the post-assessment assumption of a fresh power is a s. 147 proceeding, and only that attracts Lakhmani.

◆13.1 The whole topic in twelve propositions

  1. Four ways of determining income: summary processing (s. 143(1)), scrutiny (s. 143(3)), best judgment (s. 144) and reassessment (ss. 147–153). Sections 139, 142, 143 and 145(2)–(3) supply the return, the inquiry, the assessment and the accounts — the material on which any of the four operates.
  2. Section 139 imposes the obligation to furnish a return voluntarily; the obligation carries within it the further obligation to disclose all material facts necessary for the assessment fully and truly — the sentence on which Srikrishna builds the whole chapter.
  3. Section 144 is the answer to a non-cooperating assessee: it applies on six grounds (no return; non-compliance with a s. 142(1) notice; non-compliance with production directions; failure of special audit under s. 142(2A); failure of the s. 144B faceless procedure; and rejection of books under s. 145(3)), and it requires that the officer take into account all relevant material gathered and give an opportunity of being heard.
  4. "Best judgment" is a judicial faculty, not a caprice (Velukutty): the estimate must have "a reasonable nexus to the available material"; a ratio found in one business cannot be transplanted to another; and an appellate court with the material should decide rather than remand.
  5. An estimate is not a finding of concealment. Rejection of books under s. 145(3) and an estimate under s. 144 do not by themselves establish concealment for the purposes of a penalty.
  6. Section 147 is the exception to finality: income which has escaped assessment may be assessed where the officer has reason to believe (pre-2021) / information which suggests (post-2021) escapement, and — beyond the shorter period — where it escaped because of the assessee's failure to make a return or to disclose fully and truly.
  7. Two conditions must coexist, plus two further requirements: recorded reasons (s. 148(2)) and the satisfaction of the specified authority (s. 151) — Lakhmani Mewal Das, reader p. 179.
  8. "Reason to believe" is not "reason to suspect", and the material must have a "direct nexus or live link" with the belief; the court will not ask whether the grounds were sufficient, but it will ask whether they were relevant.
  9. The duty to disclose ends with primary facts (Burlop, Lakhmani): no reopening on a change of opinion, and none to repair the officer's own oversight (Gemini: "plainly a case of oversight").
  10. But a false or partial disclosure is no disclosure at all (Srikrishna): "a false disclosure is not a true disclosure"; "a partial disclosure may very often be a misleading one"; whether a loan is genuine "is a material fact — and not an inference"; and Burlop is "confined to the particular fact-situation of that case".
  11. The machinery after 2021 — information (s. 148) → inquiry, show cause and reasoned order (s. 148A) → sanction (s. 151) → time (ss. 149 3/10 years; 153) — is Parliament's codification of the case law: specific information, disclosed to the assessee, with reasons on the record. outside reader
  12. The remedy against a notice is a writ under Article 226, because jurisdiction is a collateral issue; the remedy against an order is an appeal under s. 246A. And the remedy for a bad estimate is not a fresh estimate by the court but a direction that the estimate be made on relevant material — which is why Velukutty refused a remand.

◆13.4 Mistakes that cost marks

  • Saying that a s. 144 assessment requires a "notice". The statute requires an opportunity of being heard; the notice is the means of giving it, and its content comes from natural justice. Say that precisely.
  • Treating notice-and-hearing as the distinction between ss. 144 and 147–153. Both require it — more elaborately in the escapement chapter. The distinctions are trigger, stage, precondition and effect on finality (Table 12.2).
  • Confusing the rejection of books with the estimate. Section 145(3) permits rejection; s. 144 governs the estimate; Velukutty governs the quality of the estimate. Three steps, three provisions.
  • Asserting that an estimate proves concealment. It does not; concealment needs positive material.
  • Treating Lakhmani as having quashed the notice for a defective sanction. The High Court's majority did; the Supreme Court upheld the result on the absence of a live link. Get this right — it is the commonest error on the case.
  • Treating Srikrishna as overruling Burlop. It confines it "to the particular fact-situation of that case", and follows Phool Chand Bajrang Lal.
  • Forgetting that Velukutty is a sales tax case. It is; and that is precisely why it is authority for the principle of best judgment across statutes. Say so, and you turn a weakness into a point.
  • Quoting cases not in the reader without flagging them. The DU material ends at Srikrishna (1996). Nothing later is in the reader; use it as illustration only (§11.3).
  • Writing a s. 148 notice when the facts describe an assessment on merits. Where the officer rejects a claim in the assessment itself (as in Dec-2024 Q2), there is no reopening — only an appealable order.

◆13.7 The last word

This topic is a study of two powers and two limits. The powers are to assess on an estimate when the assessee will not cooperate (s. 144) and to assess a second time when income has escaped (ss. 147–153). The limits are hearing and nexus: the assessee must be heard before an estimate is made against him, and both the estimate and the belief must rest on material actually connected with him, his year and his transaction. Velukutty states the first limit in the language of "wisdom truly and legally"; Lakhmani states the second in the language of the "live link" and the contrast between "reason to believe" and "reason to suspect"; Burlop and Gemini protect the assessee who told the truth; and Srikrishna withdraws that protection from the assessee who did not. Every problem you will be set is answered by asking which of the two powers is engaged, and then whether either limit has been crossed.
