00OrientationHow this document is built & what to read first

Agricultural income is the one head of income that the Union Parliament cannot tax. That single constitutional fact explains almost every doctrinal oddity in this topic: why the definition in section 2(1A) is drawn so narrowly, why the courts speak of a direct and immediate relation with land, why a 60:40 formula exists for tea, and why an exempt income still has to be reported and can still raise your tax bill through partial integration. This document treats the topic in the order in which an examiner expects it: constitutional foundation → definition → element by element tests → case law → composite income → computation → capital-gains interface → past paper questions.

The single most important sentence in the topic

Agricultural income is exempt not because Parliament decided to be generous, but because Parliament had no power to tax it. The exemption in section 10(1) is therefore declaratory of a constitutional division of powers, and the definition in section 2(1A) is the gate that decides which receipts fall on the State side of the line. Every argument you will read in the seven prescribed cases is, at bottom, an argument about which side of that line the receipt falls.

01Constitutional foundationWhy agricultural income is exempt — and what that exemption really means

1.1The division of taxing powers

The Constitution distributes the power to tax income between the Union and the States by reference to the source of the income, not the identity of the recipient. Three provisions do the work:

The constitutional architecture of agricultural income
ProvisionText / substanceConsequence
Art. 246(1) + Entry 82, List I"Taxes on income other than agricultural income."Parliament's income-tax power stops at the agricultural boundary; the exclusion is written into the entry itself, not left to the statute.
Art. 246(3) + Entry 46, List II"Taxes on agricultural income."The State Legislatures have exclusive power over agricultural income — several States (e.g., Assam, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, West Bengal) actually levy an agricultural income-tax.
Art. 366(1)"'Agricultural income' means agricultural income as defined for the purposes of the enactments relating to Indian income-tax."The constitutional expression borrows its meaning from the income-tax statute — so the definition in s. 2(1A) operates as a constitutional boundary-marker, not a mere exemption clause.
s. 10(1), Income-tax Act, 1961"In computing the total income of a previous year of any person, any income falling within any of the following clauses shall not be included — (1) agricultural income."The statutory mechanism of exclusion. Under the 2025 Act: s. 11 read with Schedule II, Sl. No. 1.

Fig. 1 — The exemption is a boundary, not a bounty. Section 2(1A) decides which side of the constitutional line a receipt falls on.

Consequence 1 — the Union cannot tax it "indirectly" either

Because the power is exclusive, Parliament cannot achieve indirectly what it cannot do directly — a levy that is in substance a tax on agricultural income is ultra vires however it is labelled. The leading illustration is the challenge to the dividend distribution tax: a tea company whose income was 60% agricultural argued that s. 115-O, by taxing the whole of the distributed profit, taxed agricultural income, and that the levy had therefore to be confined to the 40% business slice (the rider the Calcutta High Court had imposed). In Union of India v. Tata Tea Co. Ltd. (2017) 398 ITR 260 the Supreme Court upheld the levy in full: s. 115-O falls under Entry 82 List I, and "when the dividend is declared to be distributed and paid … it is not impressed with the character of the source of its income." Note how the two ideas interlock — the same reasoning that defeats the tea company's challenge (the dividend has no agricultural character) is the reasoning of Bacha that the shareholder cannot claim exemption. And note the consequence that follows: because every receipt that slips into clause (a), (b) or (c) moves out of Parliament's reach altogether, the definition must be drawn — and is drawn — with precision.

Consequence 2 — exempt in Union law, but taxable under State law

As Bhagwati J. put it in Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy (reader p. 32): once it is determined that the income is agricultural income, "the assessee would not be liable to assessment under the Indian Income-tax Act but he would have to pay the Agricultural Income-tax which would be levied upon him under the relative Agricultural Income-tax Acts". Exemption under s. 10(1) is therefore not a statement that the income is untaxable in India — only that the Union is not the taxing authority.

Consequence 3 — a definition, not a computation provision

Section 2(1A) tells you what agricultural income is. It says nothing about how to compute it. That is why Rules 7, 7A, 7B, 7C and 8 exist (now Rules 270–271 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026): where a single business is partly agricultural and partly not, the statute has to split the income, because the exempt slice must be identified with arithmetic, not with adjectives. See §6.

1.2The policy of the exemption — who was it meant for?

The Supreme Court in Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT (reader p. 18) stated the policy in terms that recur throughout the topic:

"The policy of the Act as gathered from the various sub-clauses of Section 2(1) appears to be to exempt agricultural income from the purview of Income Tax Act. The object appears to be not to subject to tax either the actual tiller of the soil or any other person getting land cultivated by others for deriving benefit therefrom, but to say that the benefit intended to be conferred upon this class of persons should extend to those into whosoever hands that revenue falls, however remote the receiver of such revenue may be is hardly warranted." Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, Bombay, AIR 1955 SC 74, at reader p. 18

Three ideas follow, and they are the analytical spine of the whole topic:

  • The class protected. The "actual tiller of the soil" and "any other person getting land cultivated by others for deriving benefit therefrom" — i.e. the cultivator and the receiver of rent, including rent in kind. This is why clause (b)(ii) speaks of "a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind", and why the courts insist on a derivative interest in the land (see Maddi Venkatasubbayya, §4.4).
  • The nexus required. "Revenue derived from land" is confined to revenue received "by direct association with the land" — not extended "to cases where that revenue or part thereof changes hands either by way of distribution of dividends or otherwise". Directness is the test, remoteness the disqualification.
  • The method excluded. A receipt that is not itself agricultural does not become agricultural because of its source or its mode of calculation — the Premier Construction dictum (§4.3).
Common exam error

Do not write that agricultural income is exempt "to encourage agriculture" or "because farmers are poor". Those are policy rationalisations, not the legal reason. The legal reason is the constitutional division of powers; the tiller-protection idea is the interpretive policy that the Supreme Court used in Bacha to keep the definition from expanding. Say both, but say them in the right order.

1.3Rural v. urban: the same line, drawn twice

The Constitution draws the line between agricultural and non-agricultural income. The Act draws a second, parallel line — between rural and urban agricultural land — because Parliament can tax capital gains on land, and it has chosen to tax gains only on land that is not truly rural. The same distance-and-population formula is used in two places: the proviso to s. 2(1A)(c) (farm buildings) and s. 2(14)(iii) (definition of "capital asset").

Fig. 2 — The 2 / 6 / 8 km bands from the lecture notes, applied in the proviso to s. 2(1A)(c) and in s. 2(14)(iii).

Why this matters for Topic 2

A farm building in an urban area does not produce agricultural income (proviso (ii) to s. 2(1A)(c)). And under Explanation 1 to s. 2(1A), "revenue derived from land" never included income from the transfer of urban agricultural land — so the capital gain on such a transfer is taxable. Both rules are the same rural/urban distinction doing two different jobs. Worked through in §8.

02The definitionSection 2(1A): anatomy of "agricultural income"

Section 2(1A) defines agricultural income through four routes — three substantive (clauses (a), (b) and (c)) and one made-up route (Explanation 3). The whole of the case law is about drawing the perimeter of these four routes. Read the definition as a set of cumulative gateways: a receipt qualifies only if it passes through one gateway completely.

2.1The statute, clause by clause

Section 2(1A), Income-tax Act, 1961 — text (as it stands)

"agricultural income" means—

(a) any rent or revenue derived from land which is situated in India and is used for agricultural purposes;

(b) any income derived from such land by—

(i) agriculture; or

(ii) the performance by a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind of any process ordinarily employed by a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind to render the produce raised or received by him fit to be taken to market; or

(iii) the sale by a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind of the produce raised or received by him, in respect of which no process has been performed other than a process of the nature described in sub-clause (ii);

(c) any income derived from any building owned and occupied by the receiver of the rent or revenue of any such land, or occupied by the cultivator or the receiver of rent-in-kind, of any land with respect to which, or the produce of which, any process mentioned in paragraphs (ii) and (iii) of sub-clause (b) is carried on:

Provided that—

(i) the building is on or in the immediate vicinity of the land, and is a building which the receiver of the rent or revenue or the cultivator, or the receiver of rent-in-kind, by reason of his connection with the land, requires as a dwelling house, or as a store-house, or other out-building, and

(ii) the land is either assessed to land revenue in India or is subject to a local rate assessed and collected by officers of the Government as such, or where the land is not so assessed or subject to a local rate, it is not situated—

(A) in any area comprised within the jurisdiction of a municipality (by whatever name called) or a cantonment board having a population of not less than ten thousand according to the last preceding census of which the relevant figures have been published before the first day of the previous year; or

(B) in any area within such distance, not exceeding eight kilometres from the local limits of such municipality or cantonment board, as the Central Government may specify by notification, having regard to the extent of, and scope for, urbanisation;

Explanation 1.—For the removal of doubts, it is hereby declared that revenue derived from land shall not include and shall be deemed never to have included any income arising from the transfer of any land referred to in item (a) or item (b) of sub-clause (iii) of clause (14) of this section [i.e., urban agricultural land].

Explanation 2.—For the removal of doubts, it is hereby declared that income derived from any building or land referred to in sub-clause (c) arising from the use of such building or land for any purpose (including letting for residential purpose or for the purpose of any business or profession) other than agriculture falling under sub-clause (a) or sub-clause (b) shall not be agricultural income.

Explanation 3.—For the purposes of this clause, any income derived from saplings or seedlings grown in a nursery shall be deemed to be agricultural income.

The numbering you must know in the 2025 Act

From AY 2026-27 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaces the 1961 Act. Section 2(5) carries the definition of agricultural income (same four routes); the exemption is s. 11 read with Schedule II, Sl. No. 1; the apportionment rules are Rules 270 and 271 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026; and "previous year" plus "assessment year" are replaced by a single "tax year" (s. 3). Cite the 1961 Act in the answer, then add the 2025 mapping in one line — examiners in 2026 reward that.

2.2Clause (a): rent or revenue derived from land

Clause (a) is the route of the landholder — the person who does not necessarily cultivate but receives from the land. It has three cumulative requirements, and each has generated litigation.

Clause (a) — the three essentials, with the test and the case
EssentialWhat it meansAuthority / note
1. There must be rent or revenue Rent = consideration paid by a tenant to the landlord for the right to use agricultural land (may be cash or kind — "rent in kind"). Revenue = the wider residual term: income which the owner or person having an interest in the land derives from it in cash or kind, otherwise than as rent; typically the produce or its sale proceeds where the owner cultivates. The Act does not define either word; the courts give them their ordinary commercial meaning as "income from land". Explanation 1 excludes transfer consideration (capital) from "revenue".
2. Derived from land A real, direct nexus between the receipt and the land. Not enough that land is somewhere in the history of the money. CIT v. Raja Bahadur Kamakshya Narain Singh, AIR 1949 PC 1 — interest on arrears of rent is not agricultural income: "the enquiry should stop as soon as the effective source is discovered … rent is not land" (quoted at reader p. 20). Bacha; Maddi.
3. Land must be (i) situated in India and (ii) used for agricultural purposes Foreign land kills the claim, however agricultural the use. "Used for agricultural purposes" requires actual use — some measure of cultivation, some expenditure of skill and labour upon the land; mere potential for agriculture is not enough. CIT v. Mustafa Ali Khan, (1948) 16 ITR 330 (PC): "unless there is some measure of cultivation of the land, some expenditure of skill and labour upon it, it cannot be said to be used for agricultural purposes" (quoted in Benoy, reader p. 31).
A subtlety most answers miss — "such land" in clause (b)

Clause (b) does not say "any land"; it says "any income derived from such land" — that is, land which satisfies clause (a): situated in India and used for agricultural purposes. So the ingredients of clause (a) are imported into clause (b). A process carried on in a factory on non-agricultural land, or on produce grown abroad, is outside the definition no matter how agricultural the process. Benoy makes the point in terms: the two conditions are (i) that the land be used for agricultural purposes, and (ii) that the income be derived from such land by agriculture or by the operations in sub-clauses (ii) and (iii) (reader p. 32).

Land revenue: required, or not?

Under the 1922 Act, clause (a) required the land to be "assessed to land revenue in British India or subject to a local rate". The 1961 Act dropped that requirement from clauses (a) and (b) — so since 1961 an assessee need not show assessment to land revenue to claim agricultural income under those clauses. The requirement survives only in proviso (ii) to clause (c) (farm buildings), where the land must either be so assessed or be outside the urban bands. Benoy, at reader p. 32, still states the old two-condition formulation because it was dealing with the 1922 Act — a classic trap in the exam.

Rent in kind and the "receiver of rent-in-kind"

Clause (b)(ii) and (iii) extend the marketable-process and sale routes to "a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind". A landlord paid in grain (batai / share-cropping) is therefore within the definition when he performs the ordinary process on the share he receives, or sells it. This is the statutory echo of the Bacha policy passage — the protection covers the person "getting land cultivated by others".

2.3Clause (b)(i): "agriculture" — basic and subsequent operations

The Act nowhere defines "agriculture" or "agricultural purpose". That silence is the reason Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy is the foundational case of the topic. Its doctrine, in one line: agriculture is one integrated activity; the subsequent operations qualify only because, and so long as, they are attached to basic operations performed on the land.

Fig. 3 — The integrated-activity doctrine of Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy (reader pp. 33–35).

"…there is present all throughout the basic idea that there must be at the bottom of it cultivation of land in the sense of tilling of the land, sowing of the seeds, planting, and similar work done on the land itself. This basic conception is the essential sine qua non of any operation performed on the land constituting agricultural operation. If the basic operations are there, the rest of the operations found themselves upon the same. But if these basic operations are wanting the subsequent operations do not acquire the characteristic of the agricultural operations." Bhagwati J., CIT v. Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy, AIR 1957 SC 768, at reader p. 34

Two further consequences of the doctrine:

  • Products of nature are nobody's agriculture. "Products which grow wild on the land or are of spontaneous growth not involving any human labour or skill upon the land are not products of agriculture and the income derived therefrom is not agricultural income" (reader p. 34). The only work done is "collecting the produce and consuming and marketing the same".
  • What counts as "land" is generous, what counts as "agriculture" is not. The products may be "grain or vegetables or fruits … plantations and groves, or grass or pasture …, or articles of luxury such as betel, coffee, tea, spices, tobacco, etc. or commercial crops like cotton, flax, jute, hemp, indigo" (reader p. 34). But the process must be cultivation. Grassy land, fisheries, poultry farms and salt pans are therefore not automatically agricultural.
Did Benoy adopt the wide dictionary meaning of "agriculture"?

No — and this is the single most misreported point in the topic. Bhagwati J. noticed the dictionary amplitude (Webster, Murray, Corpus Juris: "including … husbandry, farming … forestry, butter and cheese-making etc.", reader p. 23), but held that for the Act the basic idea of cultivation of land must be present. That is why dairying, poultry farming, fisheries and salt manufacture are treated as business income and not as agricultural income, notwithstanding the dictionary. If a question asks "does agriculture include forestry?", the correct answer is: forestry operations can be agricultural if they comprise basic operations (planting, nursing trees planted by man) — as the Court itself accepted for the re-planted portions of the forest in Benoy's own case; but not if the growth is spontaneous.

2.4Clause (b)(ii): the process to render produce fit to be taken to market

This is the most heavily litigated clause in the topic and the subject of four of the seven prescribed cases (Sakarlal Naranlal, H. G. Date, K. Lakshmanan, and — negatively — Maddi). Learn it as two statutory conditions plus one judicial condition.

Clause (b)(ii) — the three conditions, their source and their failure mode
#ConditionContent and testIf not satisfied
1The process must be necessary to render the produce fit to be taken to market Necessarily implies that there is no market for the produce in its raw state. If the produce is already marketable, no process can be "necessary" to make it marketable. The market to be looked at is a ready and available market for that commodity, in the area where it is grown. Income from the process is business income. Sheolal (ginning of cotton); Bhikanpur Sugar Concern (sugar); Killing Valley Tea (manufactured tea); Lakshmanan (cocoons).
2The process must be one ordinarily employed by a cultivator (or receiver of rent-in-kind) "Ordinarily employed" postulates the conditions of the locality where the produce is grown — the customary process of cultivators there. A special or unusual process is outside the clause. Where the assessee is the only cultivator, his own process may supply the generalisation (J. M. Casey). The process is a manufacturing or trading operation, and its income is taxable. Sakarlal Naranlal (loofahs).
3The produce must retain its original character The judicial condition, settled by Dooars Tea Co. (1962) 44 ITR 6 and applied in Sakarlal and Lakshmanan: the process may change the condition of the produce to make it saleable, but must not alter its character. What is ultimately marketed must be that produce. The commodity sold is a different commodity. K. Lakshmanan (mulberry leaves → cocoons); Dooars Tea ratio; contrast the older, now overruled, Brihan Maharashtra suggestion that character could change where no market existed.
"It will be clear from this discussion that there are two conditions which are required to be fulfilled before a process performed by the assessee can be said to be a process within the meaning of section 2(1)(b)(ii). The first condition is that the process must be necessary to render the produce fit to be taken to market and that involves the proposition that there must be no market for the produce in its raw state … The second condition is that the process must be one which is ordinarily employed by a cultivator of the produce to render it marketable. But even if these two conditions are satisfied, it is not sufficient … the produce must not change its original character." Bhagwati J., Sakarlal Naranlal v. CIT, AIR 1965 Guj. 165, at reader p. 43

Why the clause exists at all — the statutory reason

Sakarlal supplies the best explanation in the reader, and it is worth reproducing because it converts a list of conditions into a single idea:

"A cultivator raises produce from the land with a view to selling it. If there is a market for the produce as grown, there is no difficulty; the cultivator can in such a case sell the produce without anything more … But if there is no market for the produce as grown and it can be sold only by performing some process on it, the cultivator would have to perform such process in order to be able to sell the produce; otherwise the produce would not be marketable and the raising of it would be futile. Where such is the case, the legislature says that, though strictly the agricultural operations cease when the produce is raised and removed from the soil, the performance of the process should be regarded as a continuation of the agricultural operations since the process has to be performed by the cultivator for the purpose of enabling him to sell the produce." Reader p. 43

Once you have that reason in mind, each condition follows: if a market exists, the process is not necessary; if the process is unusual, it is not a continuation of his agricultural operations; if the identity of the produce changes, what is sold is not the produce he raised.

What counts as a "market"?

  • The market must be ready and available — "a market at which it could be absorbed" (J. M. Casey, reader p. 45), not a theoretical one.
  • It must be a market for the produce as a commodity, ordinarily grown by cultivators, so that a cultivator "would ordinarily sell the produce as such and not perform a process upon it" (Sakarlal, reader p. 49).
  • Where must the market exist? Sakarlal refines the Tribunal's "Indian market" theory: the question is whether there was a market anywhere — India or abroad. If galkas could be sold raw in Japan, the loofah process was not necessary, even though there was no Indian market. The reference was answered on that footing by remanding for a finding.
  • Whether a market exists is a question of fact in each case (Brihan Maharashtra Sugar Syndicate (1946) 14 ITR 611, cited at reader p. 42).

2.5Clause (b)(iii): sale of produce on which no process was performed

The simplest route, and the most often forgotten. Where the cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind simply sells the produce, having performed no process on it other than a clause (b)(ii) process, the sale proceeds are agricultural income. Consequences:

  • Sale of grain, vegetables, fruit, milk-adjacent produce, green leaf, standing trees planted by the assessee — within clause (b)(iii) provided the produce was raised by agriculture on such land by that person.
  • The clause does not apply to a dealer who buys produce and resells it. He is not the cultivator and derived nothing "from such land" — the very point of Maddi Venkatasubbayya (§4.4), where a firm bought a standing crop of tobacco and made a profit of ₹7,500: business income, not agricultural.
  • Sale of trees cut from a forest of spontaneous growth is not within it, because clause (b) presupposes income "derived from such land by agriculture".

2.6Clause (c): the farm building

Clause (c) exempts income from a building — typically a dwelling house or a store-house — which is so intimately connected with the agricultural land that its income shares the land's character. Five conditions must be satisfied, and the provisos are cumulative.

Clause (c) — conditions for the building's income to be agricultural income
ConditionSource / content
1. The building must be owned and occupied by the receiver of rent or revenue, or occupied by the cultivator / receiver of rent-in-kindMain limb of clause (c). Letting the building out to a third party is not enough; the recipient must be the land-connected person.
2. It must be on or in the immediate vicinity of the landProviso (i). Both limbs of proviso (i) are joined by "and", so both must be met.

Explanation 2 — the anti-avoidance backstop

Even if the building satisfies clause (c), income from its use for any purpose other than agriculture — "including letting for residential purpose or for the purpose of any business or profession" — is not agricultural income by force of Explanation 2. So a farmhouse let out to holiday makers, or used as an office, produces taxable income under "income from house property". The clause protects the store-house, not the holiday home.

2.7Explanations 1 to 3 — three statutory glosses

Explanation 1

Transfer of urban agricultural land is not "revenue derived from land".

Converted into a deeming rule "never to have included" such income, with retrospective effect. The gain on transfer of land within s. 2(14)(iii)(a)/(b) is taxable under "capital gains" — see §8.

Explanation 2

Non-agricultural use of a clause (c) building or land is not agricultural income.

Removes doubts created by the words "any income derived from any building". Letting for residence or business is expressly excluded.

Explanation 3

Saplings and seedlings grown in a nursery are deemed agricultural income.

Inserted by the Finance Act, 2008 with effect from AY 2009-10, to settle the controversy whether plants raised in pots involve "land". Being a deeming fiction, it is in addition to, and cannot be stretched beyond, nursery saplings/seedlings.

The nursery story — worth a paragraph in any answer on "agricultural income"

In CIT v. Soundarya Nursery (2000) 241 ITR 530 (Mad.) the assessee raised plants in pots and sold seeds; the High Court held the income agricultural, because the operations on the land (preparing beds, sowing) plus the subsequent operations in pots together formed an integrated activity. The contrary view was that pots are not "land" and there is no cultivation of land. Parliament resolved the dispute by Explanation 3: nursery income is deemed agricultural income whether or not basic operations were carried out on land. Note the logic — a deeming provision implies that but for it the income would not be agricultural; therefore Explanation 3 cannot be extended to, say, mushroom cultivation in trays or floriculture in greenhouses, which must still satisfy the ordinary Benoy tests. The DU lecture notes list "income from nursery" in the agricultural column — that is now correct by statute, and you should cite Explanation 3 rather than the case alone.

2.8Section 10(1): the exemption provision and how it operates

Section 10 opens: "In computing the total income of a previous year of any person, any income falling within any of the following clauses shall not be included — (1) agricultural income". Three practical consequences:

  1. It is an exclusion, not a deduction. Agricultural income is never brought into the computation of total income at all — neither as a head of income nor as a deduction under Chapter VI-A. (Under the 2025 Act, the same result through s. 11 and Schedule II, Sl. No. 1.)
  2. It must still be disclosed. Agricultural income is reported in the return (Schedule EI; where it exceeds ₹5,000, Schedule AI with land particulars), and large agricultural income is a standard scrutiny trigger. Filing ITR-1 is not permitted where agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000.
  3. It can still increase your tax. Partial integration (§7) brings the exempt income back for rate purposes where the conditions are met.

4.1Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, Bombay

Bacha F. Guzdar v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Bombay

AIR 1955 SC 74 · Gulam Hasan J. · appeal from Bombay (Chagla C.J. and Tendolkar J.) · DU reader pp. 17–20
DividendDirect & immediate nexusEffective source1922 Act s. 4(3)(viii)Rule 24
1 · Facts
  • In the accounting year 1949-50 the appellant, Mrs Bacha F. Guzdar, held shares in two companies — Patrakola Tea Company Ltd. and Bishnauth Tea Company Ltd. — which carried on the business of growing and manufacturing tea. She received dividends aggregating ₹2,750.
  • Under Rule 24 of the Indian Income-tax Rules, 1922 (framed under s. 59), income from the sale of tea grown and manufactured by the seller was computed as if it were income derived from business, and 40% of it was deemed taxable; the remaining 60% was treated as agricultural income exempt in the companies' hands.
  • The appellant claimed that 60% of her dividend was likewise agricultural income, exempt under s. 4(3)(viii) of the 1922 Act. The ITO, the AAC, the Tribunal and the High Court all rejected the claim (the High Court answered the reference in the negative). She appealed to the Supreme Court.
  • Question referred: "Whether 60% of the dividend amounting to Rs 2750 received by the assessee from the two Tea companies is agricultural income and as such exempt under Section 4(3)(viii) of the Act."
2 · Issues
  1. Does a dividend received by a shareholder out of profits which are, to the extent of 60%, agricultural income in the company's hands, retain that character in the shareholder's hands?
  2. In other words, is the dividend "rent or revenue derived from land which is used for agricultural purposes" within s. 2(1)(a) of the 1922 Act — and does the answer change because the land belongs to a company in which the shareholder holds shares?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (Mr Kolah)
  • 60% of the profits out of which the dividend is paid is referable to agricultural operations on the company's land; the fund out of which the dividend is declared has therefore an agricultural character.
  • The dividend does not change its character merely because it changes hands — the character travels with the money.
  • On investing in shares the shareholder buys a proportionate interest in the assets of the concern and a right to participate in its future profits (relying on Lord Anderson in Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Forrest). He is, economically, a part-owner.
  • Reliance was also placed on CIT v. Sir Kameshwar Singh, where Lord Macmillan said the Act excludes agricultural income "howsoever or by whomsoever it may be received".
  • The etymological meaning of "dividend" (dividendum, the divisible sum) shows the shareholder owns a share of the divisible sum (Buckley on Companies).
For the Revenue (Attorney-General)
  • The statutory words are "revenue derived from land"; a dividend is derived from the shareholding — a contractual relation between company and shareholder — not from the land.
  • The company is a juristic person distinct from its shareholders; it owns the land and it earns the agricultural income. The shareholder owns only a right to participate in declared profits.
  • On the assessee's logic, a creditor who lends to an agriculturist and is repaid out of the produce would also be exempt — a reductio ad absurdum.
  • Rule 24 operates only for computing the company's income; it says nothing about the character of a dividend in the shareholder's hands.
  • The A-G expressly declined to support the High Court's reason that the declaration of dividend is the effective source of the income.
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed. The whole of the dividend is taxable; it is not agricultural income in the shareholder's hands. The Court added, however, that it could not accept the High Court's proposition that the declaration of dividend is the source of the profit:

"The declaration of dividend is certainly not the source of the profit. The right to participation in the profits exists independently of any declaration by the company with the only difference that the enjoyment of profits is postponed until dividends are declared." Reader p. 19
5 · Principle evolved
  1. The direct-and-immediate-nexus rule. "Revenue derived from land" is confined to revenue received "by direct association with the land which is used for agricultural purposes and not by indirectly extending it to cases where that revenue or part thereof changes hands either by way of distribution of dividends or otherwise" (reader p. 18).
  2. The class-protection rule. The exemption is for "the actual tiller of the soil or any other person getting land cultivated by others", not for "those into whosoever hands that revenue falls, however remote the receiver of such revenue may be" (reader p. 18).
  3. No interest in company property. A shareholder has "no interest in the property of the company though he has undoubtedly a right to participate in the profits if and when the company decides to divide them" (reader p. 18, applying the Sholapur Mills case, (1950) SCR 869).
  4. The source of a dividend is the investment, not the company's activity. "Dividend is derived from the investment made in the shares of the company and the foundation of it rests on the contractual relations between the company and the shareholder."
  5. Rule 24 is a computation rule, not a character rule. It splits the company's tea income; it does not impress a character on the dividend.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • The most quotable authority for the nexus requirement, and the standard answer to "dividend from a company engaged in agriculture" — including the "60% of dividend from a tea company" claim, which is wrong in law.
  • Its four supporting authorities are examinable in their own right: Kamakshya Narain Singh (interest on arrears of rent — the "genealogy"/effective-source test), Premier Construction (source and method of calculation do not confer character), Gopal Saran Narain Singh (annuity charged on land = personal obligation) and Kameshwar Singh (usufructuary mortgagee in possession — agricultural, because the profits came directly from the land). All four are digested in §5.
  • Directly tested in DU Dec-2024 Q4 (₹60,000 of dividend from MMR Ltd., an agriculture company, claimed as agricultural income) — see §9.3.

4.2CIT v. Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy

AIR 1957 SC 768 · Bhagwati J. · appeal from Calcutta by certificate under s. 66A(2) · DU reader pp. 21–35
Meaning of "agriculture"Basic v. subsequent operationsForest / spontaneous growth1922 Act s. 2(1), s. 4(3)(viii)
1 · Facts
  • The respondent owned 6,000 acres of forest land, assessed to land revenue, grown with Sal and Piyasal trees. The forest was of spontaneous growth, "not grown by the aid of human skill and labour", and about 150 years old.
  • Considerable income came from sales of trees. The forest income had last been taxed in 1923-24 and was thereafter left out; for AY 1944-45 the assessment was reopened under s. 34. Gross receipts were ₹51,798; the ITO allowed ₹17,548 as expenditure and added ₹34,430 as taxable.
  • The assessee claimed exemption under s. 4(3)(viii). The Tribunal found that considerable human labour and care were applied year after year "for keeping the forest alive as also for reviving the portions that get denuded", through a staff performing: (a) pruning (b) weeding (c) felling (d) clearing (e) cutting of channels to help the flow of rain water (f) guarding the trees against pests and other destructive elements (g) sowing of seeds after digging of the soil in denuded areas.
  • The Tribunal found that operations (a) to (f) were "necessary for the maintenance and upkeep of any forest of spontaneous growth", but that (g) was performed only occasionally and over a small fraction of the area; normally, when a tree was cut a stump of about 6 inches was left, which sent forth off-shoots.
  • The High Court held that actual cultivation was not required and that since human labour and skill were spent on the growth of the forest, the income was agricultural. The Revenue appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. What is the connotation of "agriculture" and "agricultural purpose" where the Act defines neither?
  2. Is income from the sale of trees in a forest of spontaneous growth, on which forestry operations involving human labour and skill are carried on, agricultural income within s. 2(1) and exempt under s. 4(3)(viii)?
  3. Can subsequent operations alone (pruning, weeding, guarding, harvesting) make land "agricultural", even where no basic operation was ever performed on it?
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue (appellant)
  • The forest is of spontaneous growth "not grown by the aid of human skill and labour"; nothing was ever done to the land to raise it.
  • Entries 14 (agriculture) and 19 (forests) of List II of the Seventh Schedule draw a clear line of demarcation: forestry is not agriculture, so income from forestry cannot be agricultural income.
  • Pruning, weeding, clearing and guarding are operations on the trees, not on the land; the "expenditure of skill and labour must be upon the land and not merely on the trees which are already growing on it" — the submission founded on the Privy Council's language in Mustafa Ali Khan.
  • Reliance on the Allahabad High Court in Pratap Singh v. CIT (1952) 22 ITR 1: planned and scientific exploitation of a forest of spontaneous growth is not agriculture, however regular the income.
For the assessee (respondent)
  • The Tribunal has found as a fact that a considerable amount of human labour and care is applied year after year to keep the forest alive and to revive denuded portions; that is cultivation in substance.
  • Actual "cultivation" in the narrow sense of ploughing and sowing is not essential; the High Court so held and the dictionary meanings of "agriculture" support a wide connotation.
  • The inference from the List II entries is unsound: the entries are heads of legislation to be construed liberally and are not mutually exclusive; and Art. 366(1) defines agricultural income by reference to the income-tax enactments, so the statutory definition governs, not the constitutional division.
  • Reliance on cases where tending operations were held to be cultivation — e.g. Moolji Sicka (tendu leaves: pruning and tending amount to cultivation) and Benoy Ratan Banerji (All.).
  • At the least, the trees planted by the proprietor in denuded areas are his own agricultural produce.
4 · Decision
  • The List II argument was rejected. The entries are "heads of legislation which are to be interpreted in a liberal manner … They are not mutually exclusive". If a proprietor plants trees on a vacant site with a view that they should grow into a forest — e.g. casuarina plantations — "the income from such trees would clearly be agricultural produce" (reader p. 23). Taxes on agricultural income under Entry 46 would comprise income from forestry operations provided it falls within the definition.
  • But the doctrine of basic operations was laid down. Agriculture requires the cultivation of land: tilling, sowing, planting, and similar work on the land itself. Subsequent operations qualify only when they are "in conjunction with and a continuation of" those basic operations.
  • Applied to the facts: spontaneous growth alone is not agriculture — "if there were no other facts found, that would entail the conclusion that the income is not agricultural income". But trees planted by the proprietor on the denuded areas are agricultural produce, and on a 150-year old forest the re-planted area "cannot be considered to be negligible".
  • Outcome: appeal dismissed with costs — not because the whole income was agricultural, but because the Department had made no attempt to apportion the income between spontaneous forest and planted trees, and after the lapse of time the Court declined to direct such an enquiry. The expenditure of about ₹17,000 against ₹51,000 of receipts suggested that "a substantial portion of the income must have been derived from trees planted by the proprietors themselves".
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Agriculture = integrated activity. "The cultivation of the land does not comprise merely of raising the products of the land in the narrower sense … but also includes the subsequent operations … all of which operations, basic as well as subsequent, form one integrated activity of the agriculturist" (reader p. 34).
  2. Basic operations are the sine qua non. "If the basic operations are wanting the subsequent operations do not acquire the characteristic of the agricultural operations" (reader p. 34).
  3. Products of wild or spontaneous growth are not agricultural produce — the only "work" is collecting, consuming and marketing (reader p. 34).
  4. Human labour on the land, not merely on the product. "The human labour and skill spent in the performance of subsequent operations cannot be said to have been spent on the land itself, though it may have the effect of preserving, fostering and regenerating the products of the land" (reader p. 34).
  5. Burden and two conditions. Two conditions must be satisfied (land used for agricultural purposes; income derived from such land by agriculture or by the clause (b)(ii)/(iii) operations — reader p. 32), and the burden of proving that a receipt is exempt lies on the assessee (Mustafa Ali Khan, applied).
  6. Forestry is not excluded by the Constitution. Planting and nursing trees can be agriculture; spontaneous forests are not.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • This is the case for defining agriculture; almost every later judgment in the topic begins by reciting its basic/subsequent distinction.
  • It is the standard answer for: bamboo or grass growing spontaneously and sold (DU Dec-2024 Q2!); forest produce; whether tending operations alone qualify; and whether dairy/poultry/fishery is agriculture.
  • The apportionment point — the revenue's failure to split the income between spontaneous and planted portions — links this case to Rules 7 to 8 (§6): where income is composite, the statute and the rules, not adjectives, must divide it.
  • Note the Delphic final order: the doctrine is against the assessee but the appeal was dismissed. In an exam, state both — examiners test whether you know that the Supreme Court refused to disturb the result for want of an enquiry.

Premier Construction Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Bombay City

(1948) XVI ITR 380 (PC) / AIR 1949 PC 20 · Sir John Beaumont · digested in the DU reader at p. 20 (inside Bacha)
Managing agency commissionSource v. characterMethod of calculationPrivy Council
1 · Facts
  • The assessee-company was the managing agent of a company, a part of whose income was agricultural income.
  • Under its agreement the assessee received remuneration at 10 per cent of the profits of the managed company.
  • Because a slice of those profits was itself agricultural income (the managed company held agricultural land), the assessee claimed that the corresponding slice of its commission was agricultural income and exempt.

In the reader the case is stated at exactly this level of generality — the point of law, not the balance sheet. The fuller facts in XVI ITR 380 show a managing agency of a company with agricultural and non-agricultural income, but the reader's account is sufficient for the exam, and is what should be cited.

2 · Issues
  1. Does remuneration under a contract of personal service, calculated by reference to the profits of an employer — part of whose income is agricultural — itself become agricultural income?
  2. Is the character of a receipt determined by its source or by its method of calculation?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee
  • The commission is 10% of the profits; 10% of an agricultural profit must itself be agricultural.
  • The measure of the receipt is the agricultural income, so the receipt partakes of its nature; to tax it is to tax agricultural income by a side wind.
For the Revenue
  • The commission is consideration for personal service under a contract; it is payable out of any moneys of the employer available for the purpose, and not in specie out of any particular item of profits.
  • Nothing in the contract traces the payment to the land; the "source" is the agency agreement.
4 · Decision

Held (as stated in the reader, p. 20): the assessee "received no agricultural income as defined by the Act but … received a remuneration under a contract for personal service calculated on the amount of profits earned by the employer, payable not in specie out of any item of such profits, but out of any moneys of the employer available for the purpose, and … the remuneration therefore was not agricultural income and was not exempt from tax."

"…where an assessee receives income, not itself of a character to fall within the definition of agricultural income contained in the Act, such income does not assume the character of agricultural income by reason of the source from which it is derived, or the method by which it is calculated." Sir John Beaumont, quoted in Bacha F. Guzdar, reader p. 20
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Source does not confer character. A receipt which is not itself agricultural does not become agricultural because it is derived from an agricultural source.
  2. Calculation does not confer character. Nor because it is measured by reference to agricultural profits.
  3. The "in specie" test. A payment is not agricultural unless it is paid out of, and referable to, the agricultural produce itself — a test that reappears in Maddi (a trader buying a crop in specie still fails, because he has no interest in the land) and in Kamakshya Narain Singh (rent vs interest on arrears).
  4. Its corollary is a warning. The dictum is double-edged: it cuts down the assessee's claim, but it also tells you that the receipt is taxed in the hands in which it arises as what it is — the very reasoning that makes a cultivator's sale of his own produce exempt.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • Pair it with Bacha: Bacha says the shareholder's dividend is not derived from the land; Premier says a fortiori that a contractual payment measured by agricultural profits is not derived from the land either. Together they form the "remote receiver" doctrine.
  • The dictum is also the standard authority against "look-through" arguments — that a receipt should be traced through intermediaries to its ultimate agricultural origin. Use it whenever a question invites you to re-characterise a payment by looking at what funded it.

4.4CIT v. Maddi Venkatasubbayya

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Maddi Venkatasubbayya

(1951) XX ITR 151 (Mad.) · Viswanatha Sastri J. · DU reader pp. 36–39
Standing cropInterest in landLicenceEffective sourceBurden of proof
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a firm of merchants. In January 1943 it purchased a standing crop of tobacco on 93 acres 12 cents for ₹13,833 from the person who had raised the tobacco on the land.
  • The tobacco was harvested, cured and sold by the assessee before 21 March 1943 for ₹33,498. The plucking of the ripe leaves, the pruning and the flue-curing were all done by the firm; there was "some sort of ploughing" on the land as well.
  • The firm was not a landholder, ryot or lessee of the land. The plants had already reached a maturity that made them saleable as standing crops.
  • The ITO and AAC held ₹7,500 of the profit to be non-agricultural; the Tribunal held the entire ₹12,000 to be agricultural income exempt under s. 4(3)(viii). Reference at the instance of the Commissioner.
2 · Issues
  1. Is the profit of a merchant who buys a standing crop, harvests, cures and resells it "income derived from such land by agriculture" under s. 2(1)(b)(i) of the 1922 Act?
  2. Does it matter that the curing process performed by him is one "ordinarily employed by a cultivator" to render tobacco fit for the market?
  3. Who, in law, can "derive" income from land for the purposes of the definition?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (firm)
  • The exemption attaches to a kind of income, not to the character of the recipient; agricultural income is exempt even if it could be brought under one of the heads in s. 6.
  • The firm performed agricultural operations — ploughing, plucking, pruning — and performed the curing that a cultivator ordinarily performs to make tobacco marketable; the income is therefore "derived from land by agriculture".
  • Reliance on the usufructuary-mortgagee cases (Kameswar Singh) to show that a person who is not the cultivating owner may still receive agricultural income.
For the Revenue
  • The burden of proving exemption lies on the assessee (Mustafa Ali Khan).
  • Only the owner, landlord, ryot, lessee, occupancy tenant, usufructuary mortgagee, sub-lessee — a person with a derivative interest in the land — can derive income from the land.
  • The merchant has no interest in the land and no right to exclusive possession; at best he is a licensee (s. 52, Indian Easements Act) entitled to enter to attend to and cut the crop.
  • Growing crops are movable property (s. 3, Transfer of Property Act; s. 2(6), Registration Act); a sale of them is a sale of chattels.
  • The direct source of the profit is the purchase at an advantageous price and resale at a higher price — a trading profit.
4 · Decision

The Tribunal was wrong. The ₹7,500 in question is not agricultural income; it is a business profit. The Court reasoned through the effective-source test:

"Once the standing crop passed from the ownership of the cultivating tenant to that of the trader who purchased it, it lost the quality of agricultural income at that point and any profit made by the trader thereafter by a sale of the produce at a higher price than his cost price would … be a business profit. The direct source of the assessee's income was the purchase and sale of the produce at an advantageous price." Reader p. 38; applying Kamakshya Narain Singh's "genealogy" test

Nor did the pruning and ploughing save the claim: they were "ancillary operations of an unsubstantial character … conducted under an arrangement with the person who raised the crop".

5 · Principle evolved
  1. The interest-in-land rule. The persons entitled to exemption are the owner who lets the land, the owner in occupation, the occupancy tenant, the lessee, the usufructuary mortgagee, the sub-lessee and "persons occupying a similar position" — a list drawn from the structure of clauses (a), (b)(ii), (b)(iii) and (c) themselves (reader p. 37).
  2. Passing of the produce does not pass the exemption. "Agricultural income cannot be said to accrue to every person into whose hands the produce of the land passes."
  3. The trader's source is the contract. A purchaser of a standing crop is in the position of the holder of a licence; a purchaser of harvested produce is a fortiori a trader. Even a fruit merchant buying an orchard's produce, or a timber merchant buying planted trees, earns business profit.
  4. The usufructuary-mortgagee cases are distinguished, not doubted. The mortgagee gets agricultural income "not because he is a usufructuary mortgagee but because … he has gone into possession of the land and received rent as such" — he has an interest in the land.
  5. Burden. He who claims the exemption must prove that the income answers the definition.
  6. Yagappa Nadar approved. A licensee who taps toddy from another's trees and sells it earns non-agricultural income, "though if the same income was earned by the owner or the lessee … it would be agricultural income" — character follows interest, not activity.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • The cleanest authority for the proposition that the exemption is personal to the land-connected assessee — use it whenever a trader, contractor, mill, co-operative or processor claims agricultural income on produce it bought.
  • It also separates the two questions examiners love to conflate: is the activity agricultural? (Benoy) and is this assessee the person who derives income from the land? (Maddi). A merchant may perform agricultural operations and still fail.
  • The contrast with Lakshmanan is instructive: there the assessee was the cultivator (it grew the mulberry), and still failed — because the thing sold (cocoons) was not the produce raised. Maddi fails on the person; Lakshmanan fails on the product.

4.5Sakarlal Naranlal v. CIT

Sakarlal Naranlal v. Commissioner of Income-tax

AIR 1965 Guj. 165 · N. H. Bhagwati J. · DU reader pp. 40–51
Galkas → loofahsThe three conditionsMeaning of "market"1922 Act s. 2(1)(b)(ii)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was an individual holding agricultural land. In 1952, on a friend's suggestion, he imported seeds of galka (luffa pentendra) — a kind grown widely in Formosa, Japan and elsewhere, not indigenous — prepared the land for cultivation and raised galka on his lands.
  • After the galkas were removed from the plants, he subjected them to a seven-stage process to make loofahs: (1) tapping dry galkas to take out seeds; (2) deskinning; (3) an acetic-acid bath; (4) holding in salicylic acid; (5) drying in the sun; (6) soaking in cold water for two days; (7) pressing for packing. The loofah is a fibrous pad used in the manufacture of shoes.
  • The foreign loofahs were about 16″ × 4″; the assessee's were 5″ × 2½″. He sent them to England on consignment but they could not be sold — even at the lowest rate, purchase tax would have caused a loss — and they were reshipped to India. He claimed losses of ₹1,85,932-8-0 for AY 1954-55, and similar losses for 1955-56 and 1956-57.
  • Book-keeping: cultivation of galkas was entered in the books of Sakarlal Sons and Company; the galkas were then transferred at a value fixed by the assessee to the books of Minaxi Trading Company, which processed and exported them. The losses were those of Minaxi Trading Company.
  • The assessee claimed them as business losses; the Revenue said they were agricultural losses (and so could not be set off against business income) because the processing was within s. 2(1)(b)(ii). The roles are reversed from the usual pattern — and the Court opens by noticing exactly that (reader p. 40).
  • The ITO, the AAC and the Tribunal all held the process to be one within s. 2(1)(b)(ii), so that the losses were agricultural. The Tribunal recorded that the "existence of a theoretical market in a place like Japan" was irrelevant and that there was no ready market in India. The question referred assumed only that there was no market in India.
2 · Issues
  1. What are the conditions for a process to fall within s. 2(1)(b)(ii)?
  2. Does the process of converting galkas into loofahs satisfy them?
  3. For the purpose of deciding whether a market exists, is it enough that there is no market in India, or must the Tribunal also find that there is no market abroad?
  4. Must the produce retain its original character even where there is no market for it in its raw state?
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue (supporting the Tribunal's finding of agricultural loss)
  • The item marketed was "a stranger to the Indian market"; there was therefore no ready market in India, and the loofah process was the customary process for rendering it marketable.
  • "Ordinarily employed" postulates the conditions of the locality where the produce is grown; a market in a distant country such as Japan is "theoretical". Analogy: produce grown in Kerala without a local market does not become non-agricultural merely because it could be sold raw in Punjab.
  • The Tribunal's finding was that there was no market at all for galkas, so the process was necessary.
  • If the assessee was the only cultivator of galkas, his own process supplies the generalisation of what cultivators ordinarily do (J. M. Casey).
For the assessee (Mr Kaji)
  • The process altered the character of the produce: loofahs are goods of a different character from galkas, so s. 2(1)(b)(ii) is not attracted at all.
  • There was a market for galkas as such — evidenced by the letters from Japanese buyers (offering 12s and 15½s a dozen in 1952) — so the process was not necessary to make them marketable.
  • The conduct of the assessee as a cultivator shows his intention was to sell galkas raw: the very bifurcation of his books into a cultivating concern and a trading concern demonstrates that an ordinary cultivator would not make loofahs.
  • At the least, the Tribunal never found that there was no market outside India, so the question referred does not answer the real controversy.
4 · Decision
  • Three conditions were laid down (two statutory, one judicial) and explained with the "reason of the enactment" (see §2.4 above and reader pp. 43–44).
  • Mr Kaji's identity argument was shut out on the facts. The Court accepted that if the galkas did not retain their character the process would fall outside the clause — indeed it held that the Brihan Maharashtra suggestion (that the character may change where there is no market) "can no longer be regarded as good law" after Dooars Tea — but the Tribunal had found to the contrary, and that finding was not challenged by the assessee. Having taken the benefit of a finding, he could not resile from it.
  • The book-keeping argument failed. "It cannot be overlooked that both the concerns belonged to the assessee … The determining factor must be whether there was a market for galkas as a commodity."
  • The market must be tested in India and abroad. If galkas could be sold raw anywhere — including Japan — the loofah process was not necessary. Since both loofahs and galkas could be sold only outside India, "it was not enough for the Tribunal to find that there was no market for galkas in India".
  • The question was reframed and the case remanded: "Whether, on the facts and circumstances of the case, the process employed on galkas for purposes of exporting and selling them abroad satisfies the requirements of section 2(1)(b)(ii) of the Act?" — with a direction to the Tribunal to find whether there was a market for galkas as a commodity in India or abroad. The Court expressly left open the question of burden of proof.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Condition 1 — necessity/marketability: there must be no market for the produce in its raw state. "That which is already marketable does not need any process to render it marketable."
  2. Condition 2 — ordinary process: the process must be one ordinarily employed by cultivators of that produce; a special or unusual process fails, because the process is "assimilated to agricultural operations" and must share their ordinariness.
  3. Condition 3 — identity: "the produce must not change its original character. The cultivator is permitted to subject the produce to a process in order to make it marketable and what is ultimately marketed must, therefore, be that produce."
  4. The market test is objective and commodity-based. What matters is not whether this assessee's crop is saleable but "whether there is a market where the produce ordinarily grown by a cultivator is bought or sold as a commodity" (reader p. 49), judged by reference to the area of growth and the customary process there — but not confined to India.
  5. Statutory reason governs interpretation. The clause is a continuation-of-agriculture fiction, available because the cultivator has no choice but to process in order to sell.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • The framework case. Every later decision in this branch — H. G. Date, Lakshmanan, and the Dooars Tea line — is best organised around Sakarlal's three conditions.
  • Note the reversal of parties. Here the Revenue argued for agricultural character (to defeat a business loss). That is a favourite exam twist: the same definition can be invoked by either side, and the court decides the character of the income, not the fortunes of the taxpayer.
  • Its doctrinal correction matters: after Dooars Tea, the identity condition cannot be waived even where no market exists. This is precisely the difficulty that surrounds H. G. Date (§4.6) and K. Lakshmanan (§4.7).

Commissioner of Income-tax, Poona v. H. G. Date

(1971) 82 ITR 71 (Bom.) · High Court of Bombay · assessment years 1952-53 and 1954-55
Sugarcane → jaggeryNo market for raw produceFinding of fact1922 Act s. 2(1)(b)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee, H. G. Date, cultivated sugarcane on his agricultural land and converted it into jaggery (gur) for sale. He had earlier been assessed on the jaggery income without claiming exemption.
  • For AYs 1952-53 and 1954-55 (accounting years S.Y. 2007 and 2008) he claimed that the jaggery income was agricultural income exempt under s. 2(1)(b) read with s. 4(3)(viii) of the 1922 Act. Exemption was refused by the ITO.
  • The Tribunal, by order dated 10 August 1956, held that no proper attempt had been made to ascertain whether there was in fact a market for the sugarcane, and remanded the case to the ITO on eleven formulated points, giving the assessee an opportunity to lead further evidence.
  • On remand, evidence was taken at length. The ITO served questionnaires on the Phaltan Sugar Works (about 7 miles from the assessee's fields), the Walchandnagar factory and the Brihan Maharashtra Sugar Factory, and reported that the Phaltan factory could absorb at least 35,000 tons of outside sugarcane each year, that the crushing season was about 120 days, and that yields exceeded 45 tons an acre. The Revenue's case was that there was a market within a 15-mile radius, so the sugarcane was marketable in its natural condition.
  • The assessee's case was that his variety was not fit for chewing, that sugarcane is perishable and must be crushed within a short period, that the only alternative open to a cultivator was to convert it into jaggery, and that the single mill was not in a position to absorb the crop of the plantations on the banks of the Nira.
  • The Tribunal, on the remand evidence, found that there was no market for the assessee's sugarcane in its natural condition, that conversion into jaggery was "a process ordinarily employed by a cultivator to render sugarcane fit to be taken to market", and that the income was therefore agricultural.
2 · Issues
  1. Was there evidence to justify the Tribunal's finding that there was no market for the sugarcane produced by the assessee?
  2. If so, is the income from the sale of jaggery agricultural income within s. 2(1)(b)?
  3. In a reference, how far may the High Court review a Tribunal's finding of fact on marketability?
3 · Arguments
For the Revenue
  • The Phaltan factory, 7 miles away, buys sugarcane from within a 15-mile radius and needs to buy about 35,000 tons a year from outsiders; therefore a ready market existed and the sugarcane was marketable.
  • The Tribunal's finding is based on a misconception of law and is so unreasonable that no person with reason could make it; the High Court is therefore entitled to interfere.
  • Conversion into jaggery changes the character of the produce and is a manufacturing activity; following Brihan Maharashtra Sugar Syndicate, where a market for sugarcane exists the conversion cannot be a process to render it marketable.
For the assessee
  • On the evidence on record — including the Revenue's own material — the sugarcane crop was not marketable; the single mill could not absorb the crop of the area.
  • Sugarcane cannot be stored like wheat or rice; it must be crushed quickly. Conversion into gur was the only alternative, and it is the process ordinarily employed by cultivators in the locality.
  • The Tribunal's finding is a pure finding of fact supported by evidence; a reference is not an appeal and the Court does not re-appreciate evidence.
4 · Decision
  • The High Court affirmed the Tribunal. It is not for the Court in a reference to scrutinise the details of the evidence so as to ascertain whether it justified the finding that there was no market for the sugarcane in its natural state.
  • The Tribunal had applied its mind to the basic fact — that the only purchaser available was a single mill — and had given reasons connected with that mill's capacity, the perishability of cane, and the absence of any alternative outlet. The finding was neither perverse nor based on a misconception of law.
  • Held: the income received by the assessee from the sale of jaggery was agricultural income, exempt from income-tax.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Where there is no market for the raw produce, the customary process of conversion performed by the cultivator is a s. 2(1)(b)(ii) process, and the income from the converted product is agricultural income.
  2. Marketability is a question of fact, and a finding of fact supported by evidence — even conflicting evidence — will not be disturbed in a reference unless perverse.
  3. The two familiar conditions still govern: the process must be one ordinarily employed by a cultivator, and it must be employed to render the produce fit to be taken to market.
  4. Practical test of "no market": number and capacity of available buyers, perishability of the crop, distance and season, and the customary practice of cultivators in that area.
6 · Critical note — how to use the case without being caught out
  • The tension. Converting sugarcane into gur does change the character of the produce. Read literally, that collides with condition 3 (Dooars Tea; Sakarlal says the Brihan Maharashtra concession on which Date's reasoning rests is no longer good law). The case survives on the footing that where the raw produce has no market at all, the converted article is treated as the cultivator's marketable produce.
  • The subsequent history. Seth Banarsi Das Gupta v. CIT (1977) 106 ITR 804 (All.) took the contrary view — conversion of sugarcane into gur is not a process ordinarily employed to render sugarcane fit for the market. Later decisions (including the Madras High Court in E. Palaniappan) confine Date to its own facts — a cane variety unusable in its natural form and a single mill refusing purchase — and the Bombay High Court has itself applied Date in CIT v. Kirloskar Brothers Ltd. for the broader proposition that "an agricultural operation extends to all activities which make the agricultural produce marketable".
  • So the safe exam formulation is: "H. G. Date is authority for the proposition that where a fact-finding body records, on evidence, that the raw produce has no market, the customary conversion is a s. 2(1A)(b)(ii) process; but the case must be confined to such facts, because the identity condition in Dooars Tea and Sakarlal Naranlal has not been overruled."
  • It is the direct authority for DU Dec-2024 Q2, where Mr. FERN could not sell his sugarcane because nearby factories refused to purchase it and made jaggery instead (§9.2).

4.7K. Lakshmanan & Co. v. CIT

K. Lakshmanan & Co. v. Commissioner of Income-tax

(1998) 9 SCC 537 · (1999) 239 ITR 597 · B. N. Kirpal and S. P. Kurdukar JJ. · DU reader pp. 52–53
Mulberry leaves → silk cocoonsIdentity of produces. 2(1)(b)(ii), 1961 ActDooars Tea applied
1 · Facts
  • The appellant was a partnership firm constituted for carrying on agricultural activities. In the course of its business it grew mulberry leaves and reared silkworms.
  • It purchased silkworm eggs; when the worms hatched they were fed principally on mulberry leaves. The leaves were plucked from trees grown by the appellant and cut into strips for feeding. The worms wound saliva around themselves and the hardened saliva formed the cocoons, which were sold in the market.
  • The appellant claimed that the entire income, from growing the leaves to selling the cocoons, was exempt as agricultural income under s. 2(1) of the 1961 Act.
  • The ITO accepted the claim only as to the growing of mulberry leaves; he estimated the income attributable to rearing the worms and selling the cocoons at 25% of the total and taxed it. The AAC decided in the appellant's favour; the Tribunal reversed the AAC, holding that even though mulberry leaves had no market, "the agricultural produce, viz., the mulberry leaves, was not what was sold in the market and what in fact was sold were cocoons which were not the agricultural produce of the appellant".
  • The question referred was whether the Tribunal was justified in holding that income from the process, i.e. the rearing of silkworms, was not entitled to exemption under s. 2(1)(b)(ii). The High Court answered in favour of the Revenue; the firm appealed.
2 · Issues
  1. Does the rearing of silkworms on mulberry leaves grown by the assessee, followed by the sale of cocoons, fall within s. 2(1)(b)(ii)?
  2. Where the assessee's own agricultural produce (mulberry leaves) is used as an input to produce something else (cocoons) and only that something else is sold, is the resulting income agricultural income?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (appellant)
  • Mulberry leaves have no market; they cannot be sold and can only be fed to silkworms. Therefore the rearing of worms and sale of cocoons is the process ordinarily employed by a grower of mulberry to render his produce fit to be taken to market.
  • The whole activity — from growing the leaves to selling the cocoons — is one integrated agricultural activity of the firm (an application of the Benoy "integrated activity" idea).
  • The firm is a cultivator; it performed the operation itself; and but for the process the crop would have had no market at all — the precise rationale of s. 2(1)(b)(ii).
For the Revenue
  • Mulberry leaves are the agricultural produce; cocoons are not. The worms are not grown from the land, and the cocoons are not the produce of the trees.
  • Feeding leaves to worms is not a process employed by a cultivator "to render the produce raised by him fit to be taken to market" — it is the manufacture of a different commodity.
  • Dooars Tea Co. requires that the produce retain its original character; the sale here is of "an item or a commodity which is different from what is cultivated and processed".
4 · Decision

Appeal dismissed; the High Court was right. The Court compressed the definition and applied it:

"It is clear from the reading of the aforesaid statutory provision that what is taken to the market and sold must be the produce which is raised by the cultivator. Even though for the purpose of making it marketable or fit for sale, some process may have to be undertaken, the section does not contemplate the sale of an item or a commodity which is different from what is cultivated and processed. Had mulberry leaves been subjected to some process and sold in the market as such then certainly the income derived therefrom would be regarded as agricultural income … The agricultural produce of the cultivator will be mulberry leaves and by no stretch of imagination can the silkworms, and certainly not the silk cocoons, be regarded as the agricultural produce of the cultivator." Reader p. 53

The Court expressly adopted the observations in Dooars Tea Co. Ltd. v. CIT (1962) 44 ITR 6, 12: the process must be one "usually employed by the cultivator"; it must be employed "with the object of making the produce marketable"; and "the employment of the process … must not alter the character of the produce … The produce must retain its original character and the only change that may have been brought about in the produce is to make it marketable."

5 · Principle evolved
  1. The produce sold must be the produce raised. Section 2(1)(b)(ii) presupposes that what reaches the market is the cultivator's own agricultural produce, merely processed to make it saleable.
  2. Absence of a market is necessary but not sufficient. Even though the leaves had no market, the claim failed because the identity condition was not met — the decisive rejection of the "no market → anything goes" reading of Brihan Maharashtra / H. G. Date.
  3. The character of the produce is a question of fact and substance, not of the assessee's book-keeping or integration of activities.
  4. Dooars Tea is the governing statement of the identity condition in the 1961 Act context; Lakshmanan gives it the authority of the Supreme Court in a modern setting.
6 · Significance & use in an answer
  • The controlling authority for "value-added products" claims: cocoons, honey, milk, cheese, jaggery, sugar, tea, rice, oil — each must be tested against identity, not merely against marketability.
  • It completes the doctrinal arc: Benoy (what is agriculture) → Sakarlal (three conditions) → Date (marketability as fact) → Lakshmanan (identity restored as the decisive condition). Write the arc in your answer and you have answered at the level the examiner wants.
  • Note the practical dividing line the Court itself draws: if the leaves had been processed and sold as leaves — dried, graded, packed — the income would have been agricultural.

05The authorities inside the readerNineteen supporting decisions every answer should be able to cite

These cases are not separately prescribed, but each is discussed inside the seven judgments in the DU reader — so they are case material in the strict sense, and quoting them lifts an answer from "read the textbook" to "read the judgment". They are grouped by the proposition they establish.

5.1The "effective source" group — what is not derived from land

CIT v. Raja Bahadur Kamakshya Narain Singh reader p. 20

Citation: AIR 1949 PC 1 (also reported (1948) 16 ITR 325). Bench: Privy Council, Lord Uthwatt.

Facts: a landlord was entitled to interest on arrears of rent payable in respect of land used for agricultural purposes. Was the interest agricultural income?

Held: no — it was neither rent nor revenue "derived from land" within s. 2(1).

The dictum everyone quotes (reader p. 20): "The word 'derived' is not a term of art. Its use in the definition indeed demands an enquiry into the genealogy of the product. But the enquiry should stop as soon as the effective source is discovered. In the genealogical tree of the interest land indeed appears in the second degree, but the immediate and effective source is rent, which has suffered the accident of non-payment. And rent is not land within the meaning of the definition."

Maharajkumar Gopal Saran Narain Singh v. CIT reader p. 20

Citation: AIR 1935 PC 143.

Facts: an annual payment for life arose out of a transfer by the assessee of a portion of his estate, made to discharge his debts and to secure an adequate income for life; the payment was secured by a charge on land.

Held: not rent or revenue derived from land, but money paid under a contract imposing a personal liability on the covenantor, the discharge of which was merely secured by a charge on land — hence not agricultural income.

Use: the authority against "it is charged on land, therefore it is agricultural" — a charge is security, not source. Pair with Premier (source and calculation) and Kamakshya (effective source).

CIT v. Sir Kameshwar Singh reader pp. 20, 37

Citation: (1935) 3 ITR 305 (PC). Bench: Privy Council, Lord Macmillan.

Facts: a usufructuary mortgagee in possession received the rents and profits of agricultural land in lieu of interest on the loan he had advanced; he was assessed as a money-lender on business profits.

Held: the receipts were agricultural income and exempt — "the result of those sections is to exclude agricultural income altogether from the scope of the Act howsoever or by whomsoever it may be received".

But read it narrowly. Bacha confines the observation "to the facts of that particular case": the implication is only that "whosoever receives profit from the land directly is entitled to the exemption". And Maddi explains why the mortgagee succeeds — "not because he is a usufructuary mortgagee but because … he has gone into possession of the land and received rent as such". That distinction is the difference between Kameshwar Singh and the trader in Maddi.

Seth Banarsi Das Gupta v. CIT outside the reader

Citation: (1977) 106 ITR 804 (All.).

Held: conversion of sugarcane into gur produces a different commodity; where sugarcane generally has a market, the conversion is not a process ordinarily employed by a cultivator to render sugarcane fit to be taken to market — so the income is not agricultural.

Use: the counterweight to H. G. Date. Cite both, then reconcile: Date depends on a recorded finding that the raw produce had no market; absent that finding, Banarsi Das Gupta applies.

5.2The "meaning of agriculture" group

CIT v. Mustafa Ali Khan reader pp. 31, 36

Citation: (1948) 16 ITR 330 (PC) — cited as Raja Mustafa Ali Khan.

Held: "unless there is some measure of cultivation of the land, some expenditure of skill and labour upon it, it cannot be said to be used for agricultural purposes within the meaning of the Act"; the land must actually be used for agriculture, not merely capable of it. The Court was "definite" that arboriculture was not decided, but the cultivation requirement was absolute.

Use:(i) the source of Benoy's basic-operations test; (ii) the burden of proof — the assessee who claims exemption must prove that the income answers the definition (applied in Maddi, reader p. 36).

Moolji Sicka & Co. cited in Benoy

Citation: (1939) 7 ITR 493.

Held: in the case of tendu leaves, the operations of pruning and tending amounted to cultivation of the land, so the income was agricultural — a decision on the "subsequent operations" side of the line.

Use: how to argue that tending operations qualify; then explain why it does not help where the growth is spontaneous (Benoy) — in Moolji Sicka there was cultivation; in a 150-year-old Sal forest there was none.

Pratap Singh v. CIT reader p. 31

Citation: (1952) 22 ITR 1 (All.).

Facts: income from the sale of forest trees growing naturally and spontaneously, but with forestry operations worked on scientific lines under a regular working plan, spending money to increase profit.

Held: not agricultural income — "mere weeding, care and preservation of forest trees which grew spontaneously" are not operations on the land; the expenditure of skill and labour must be upon the land, not merely on trees already growing on it.

Use: the high-water mark of the strict view, approved in substance by Benoy's basic-operations doctrine. Note the irony: Benoy rejected Pratap Singh's reasoning about the Constitution but reached a compatible result for spontaneous forests.

Benoy Ratan Banerji v. CIT; Deen Mohammad Mian; Beokar Singh cited in Benoy

Citation: (1949) 15 ITR 98 (All.) and the Nagpur/Patna decisions reviewed in Benoy at reader pp. 29–32.

Held: a range of views on whether tending and preservation of forests amount to agriculture — the very "divergence of opinion among the various Courts" (reader p. 32) which made the Supreme Court's intervention necessary.

Use: to show, in one sentence, why Benoy had to settle the law. Do not cite them for a proposition; cite them as the state of authority before Benoy.

5.3The "marketable process" group

Dooars Tea Co. Ltd. v. CIT through Sakarlal & Lakshmanan

Citation: (1962) 44 ITR 6 (SC), at p. 12 (quoted at reader pp. 44, 53).

Held (the classic three limbs): (i) the process must be one "usually employed by the cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind", simple or mechanical; (ii) it must be employed "with the object of making the produce marketable"; and (iii) "the employment of the process … must not alter the character of the produce. The produce must retain its original character and the only change … is to make it marketable."

Use: the source of condition 3. Whenever a question asks whether processing is exempt, quote Dooars Tea and then apply Sakarlal and Lakshmanan.

In re Bhikanpur Sugar Concern reader p. 44

Citation: Patna High Court, Full Bench (Dawson-Miller C.J.).

Facts: income from the sale of sugar manufactured from sugarcane grown by the assessee on its own lands.

Held: not agricultural income — the manufacture of sugar is not a process ordinarily employed by cultivators of sugarcane, because "the market of the vast majority of cultivators of sugarcane was the sugar factory or the country mill", and they do not manufacture sugar to make it marketable.

Use: the classic answer to "sugar" and the origin of condition 2. Note the technique: the Court defined the ordinary cultivator's market and asked whether the process was needed to reach that market.

Killing Valley Tea Co. Ltd. v. Secretary of State reader p. 44

Citation: AIR 1921 Cal. 40.

Facts: the company grew green leaf and manufactured tea from it; it claimed the whole of the income from sale of manufactured tea as agricultural income.

Held: the green leaf, though not a marketable article of food, was "a marketable commodity to be manufactured by people who possess the requisite machinery into tea fit for human consumption"; the manufacturing process was therefore not a process to render the leaves fit to be taken to market.

Use: the reason why tea needs a special statutory formula (Rule 8 / Rule 271) — without it, tea would be wholly business income.

J. M. Casey v. CIT reader pp. 44–45

Citation: AIR 1930 Pat. 44 (Courtney-Terrell C.J.).

Facts: the assessee cultivated aloe plants and, by machinery, prepared sisal fibre, which he sold.

Held: the whole income exempt — aloe leaves had no market; the process was one ordinarily employed to render them fit to be taken to market. Two propositions were drawn by Sakarlal: (1) the produce must not have "a ready and available market where goods of that kind are bought and sold"; (2) even if the assessee is the only cultivator, a generalisation can be made from his single instance.

Use: the leading case on "no market", and the answer to the objection "but nobody else in India grows this". Note also the Chief Justice's caveat that changed economic conditions might require reconsideration — judicial humility worth quoting.

Sheolal v. CIT reader p. 45

Citation: AIR 1932 Nag. 6.

Held: the process of ginning cotton was not a process ordinarily employed by cultivators to render cotton fit to be taken to market, since unginned cotton was sold by cultivators and ginning was not essential to marketability.

Use: the short illustration of conditions 1 and 2 failing together. Also the answer to a common exam question ("is ginning/rice-hulling/drying exempt?").

Brihan Maharashtra Sugar Syndicate Ltd. v. CIT reader pp. 42, 46

Citation: (1946) 14 ITR 611 (Bom.), Kania J.

Held: although there was evidence that converting sugarcane into gul was a process ordinarily employed by a cultivator, the finding that it was employed to render the produce fit to be taken to market was erroneous because there was a market for the sugarcane before it was turned into gul. Kania J.: "the produce must retain its original character in spite of the process unless there is no market for selling it in that condition."

Use — and caution: the case is the origin of the "no market → character may change" concession. Sakarlal holds that this part of it "can no longer be regarded as good law" after Dooars Tea, while what it establishes — "if there is a market for the produce, no process performed on it can be said to be a process necessary for rendering it fit to be taken to market" — stands. It is also authority that marketability is a question of fact (reader p. 42).

Boggavarapu Peda Ammaiah v. CIT reader p. 47

Citation: (1964) 1 ITJ 197 (A.P.).

Facts: the assessee exported tobacco grown on his lands. The Revenue treated income up to flue-curing as agricultural, but the later operations of re-drying, stripping and grading as non-agricultural.

Held: the Revenue's approach was right — after flue-curing the tobacco had a large market in the country, so re-drying, stripping and grading were not essential to make it marketable, and were not a process ordinarily employed by cultivators for that purpose. Income splitting was therefore required.

Use: the model for "split the activity at the point where marketability is achieved". Compare the stadium-grass case, where growing was agricultural but lifting, rolling and relaying was not.

Yagappa Nadar v. CIT reader p. 39

Citation: (1927) ILR 50 Mad. 923.

Held: income of a person holding a licence to tap toddy from another's trees and selling the toddy at a profit was non-agricultural — "though if the same income was earned by the owner or the lessee of the land on which the trees grew, it would be agricultural income".

Use: the "same activity, different person" demonstration — the perfect companion to Maddi, and the reason the answer to "who earns it" matters as much as "what was done".

CIT v. Kirloskar Brothers Ltd. outside the reader

Citation: Bombay High Court, 26 September 1989.

Held: applying H. G. Date, "an agricultural operation extends to all activities which make the agricultural produce marketable"; and the court will not, in a reference, scrutinise the evidence to see whether it justified a Tribunal's finding on the absence of a market.

Use: shows the continuing life of Date in the Bombay High Court — useful for the "critically examine" version of the question.

Sholapur Spinning & Weaving Co. reader p. 18

Citation: (1950) SCR 869, 904. Cited in Bacha for the nature of a shareholder's interest: a shareholder has no right in the property of the company, only a right to participate in profits. That single proposition is what makes the tea-company dividend non-agricultural.

Citation: JT 2003(1) SC 363 (Ruma Pal J.).

Facts: on the liquidation of Palkulam Estate (P) Ltd. the appellants, shareholders, received 479.89 acres of agricultural land; they claimed the value was outside s. 46(2) because agricultural land is excluded from "capital asset" in s. 2(14).

Use: the exemption of agricultural income says nothing about receipt of agricultural land. Directly relevant to DU Dec-2024 Q4 (§9.3).

06Composite incomePartly agricultural, partly business: Rules 7, 7A, 7B, 7C and 8 — and Rules 270–271 of 2026

A planter who grows tea and manufactures it, a miller who grows sugarcane and crushes it, a coffee estate that cures and roasts — each earns from a single indivisible business, one slice of which is agricultural and the rest is not. Clause (b)(ii) cannot decide that case, because the process alters the produce. Parliament therefore legislated the split in the Rules, and the lecture notes rightly give it a separate heading: "marketable process → agricultural income; profitable process → non-agricultural income".

The four routes under the 1962 Rules — and their 2026 equivalents
  • Rule 7 — income partly agricultural and partly from business. The market value of the agricultural produce raised by the assessee and used as raw material in his own business (or whose sale receipts are credited in the business accounts) is deducted from the business income and treated as agricultural income.
  • Rule 7A — rubber: 65% of the income from the sale of rubber, latex, crepe etc. manufactured from plants grown by the assessee is agricultural.
  • Rule 7B — coffee: 75% of income from coffee grown and cured by the seller is agricultural; 60% where the coffee is grown, cured, roasted and ground, with or without chicory.
  • Rules 7C and 8 — tea: 60% of the income from the sale of tea grown and manufactured by the seller is agricultural, 40% is business income deemed liable to tax.
  • 2026 Rules: Rule 270 reproduces Rule 7 (market-value method) and Rule 271 consolidates the commodity table (rubber 65:35, coffee 75:25, coffee with chicory 60:40, tea 60:40) — exactly as tabulated in the class notes.

6.1Rule 7 — the market-value method

Fig. 7 — Rule 7 (Rule 270 of the 2026 Rules): the produce is notionally sold to the factory at market value.

How "market value" is found — Rule 7(3) / Rule 270(3)

(a) Where the produce is ordinarily sold in the market in its raw state, or after the ordinary process

The value calculated according to the average price at which it has been so sold during the relevant previous year / tax year.

(b) Where it is not ordinarily so sold

The aggregate of — (i) the expenses of cultivation; (ii) the land revenue or rent paid for the area in which it was grown; and (iii) such amount as the Assessing Officer finds, having regard to all the circumstances, to represent a reasonable profit.

Worked example — a sugar mill that grows its own cane

A company grows sugarcane on its own farms and crushes it in its own mill. Its books show a composite profit of ₹60,00,000. The average market price of the cane so used is ₹40,00,000.

Composite income from growing + manufacturing + sale₹60,00,000

Two points examiners look for: (i) no separate deduction is allowed for the cost of cultivation once the market value is deducted (that would be double counting — Rule 7(2)/270(2)); and (ii) the exempt ₹40,00,000 still enters the partial-integration computation if the assessee is an individual/HUF (§7), but a company simply excludes it.

Use the commodity formulae (rubber, coffee, tea) where the produce is one of the listed commodities grown and processed by the assessee. Use Rule 7 for everything else — sugarcane, tobacco, paddy, oilseeds — where the agricultural produce is consumed in the assessee's own business. If the business is not the assessee's own (he merely sells the produce to someone else's factory), no rule is needed at all: the sale is clause (b)(iii) agricultural income in full.

07ComputationPartial integration: how an exempt income raises the tax on the rest

Agricultural income is never taxed. But the legislature has ensured that a person with large exempt farm income cannot use it to shelter the rest of his income behind the full slab exemption. The mechanism — partial integration — adds the agricultural income back for the purpose of fixing the rate. It is a compromise between two constitutional positions: the Union cannot tax the farm income, but it may take it into account in taxing what it can tax.

7.1The three conditions

When partial integration applies
#ConditionExplanation
1The assessee must be a "specified assessee" Individual, HUF, Association of Persons, Body of Individuals or Artificial Juridical Person. It does not apply to companies, firms or LLPs — they are taxed at flat rates, so there is no slab to manipulate.
2Net agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000 "Net" means gross agricultural receipts less revenue expenses incurred to earn them (and less admissible deductions). The ₹5,000 figure is statutory and has remained unchanged for decades.
3Non-agricultural income exceeds the basic exemption limit The limit of the relevant year and regime (old regime: ₹2,50,000 / ₹3,00,000 / ₹5,00,000 by age; new regime for FY 2025-26: ₹4,00,000). If the non-agricultural income is at or below the limit, the agricultural income is simply ignored.
Condition 3 is cumulative, not alternative

Both condition 2 and condition 3 must be satisfied. So a taxpayer with ₹10,00,000 of agricultural income but only ₹2,00,000 of salary gets no aggregation at all — his non-agricultural income does not exceed the exemption limit. Students routinely write "aggregation applies if agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000" and lose the mark.

Why the increase looks disproportionate: because step 2 permits the assessee to keep only one exemption limit of ₹2,50,000, while step 1 gives the exemption limit to the combined figure. The whole of the slab structure below (A + L) is thus used twice — once in step 1 and once in step 2 — and the benefit is neutralised. That is exactly the design.

7.3Traps, and the questions that test them

Trap 1 — "aggregation = taxation"

Never write that agricultural income is "taxed at slab rates" or "taxed above ₹5,000". It is exempt; it is only included for rate purposes. The income-tax itself is levied on the non-agricultural income.

Trap 2 — entities

A company or firm with ₹1 crore of agricultural income suffers no aggregation at all. Aggregation is a slab-rate device, and only for the specified assessees of §7.1.

Trap 3 — losses

A business loss cannot be set off against exempt agricultural income, and a loss from agricultural operations cannot be set off against business income (the head itself is outside the charge). Do not compute "net" agricultural income by setting off business losses.

Trap 4 — special-rate income

Income taxed at special rates (e.g. long-term capital gains under s. 112, winnings under s. 115BB) is computed separately; the aggregation exercise concerns the income taxable at slab rates. State your assumption, then compute.

Trap 5 — the rebate

The s. 87A rebate and the aggregation interact awkwardly (the rebate threshold is tested against total income, and agricultural income is not part of total income yet lifts the rate). Where a problem falls near the rebate threshold, show the workings and say which view you are taking.

Trap 6 — disclosure, not just computation

Agricultural income must be reported (Schedule EI; Schedule AI and land/district particulars where it exceeds ₹5,000), and ITR-1 cannot be used beyond ₹5,000. An exempt income that must be disclosed is the classic "exempt but not ignored" point.

DU Dec 2024 · Q2Full problem10 + 5 + 5 + 3 marksAgricultural income

Mr. FERN's five receipts — bamboo, farm house, produce, sugarcane and jaggery

Question. "Mr. FERN, a farmer having agriculture lands in village and cities. There was a spontaneous growth of bamboo on one of his agriculture lands and he sold it out for Rs. 5 lakh. He also sold out one of his farm house for Rs. 5 lakh which was in the municipal limits of Udaipur District. He derived Rs. 10 lakh by sale of agricultural produce from another agricultural land. Mr. FERN was not able to sell his sugarcane produce due to purchase denial by nearby factories. Mr. FERN made jaggery out of the sugarcane and sold it out for Rs. 3 lakh. Mr. FERN has filed his IT return and claimed all the income as agriculture income. The Income Tax Officer rejected his claims. Decide the case with the help of landmark judgements."
Step 1 — Frame the issues
  1. Is ₹5,00,000 from the sale of bamboo of spontaneous growth agricultural income?
  2. Is ₹5,00,000 from the sale of a farm house within municipal limits agricultural income?
  3. Is ₹10,00,000 from the sale of agricultural produce exempt?
  4. Is ₹3,00,000 from the sale of jaggery made from his own sugarcane agricultural income, where the sugarcane could not be sold because nearby factories refused to purchase it?
  5. What are the consequences of the Income-tax Officer's rejection, and how must the total income be computed?
Step 2 — The governing law

Section 2(1A) read with section 10(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (and, for AY 2026-27 onwards, s. 2(5) read with s. 11 and Schedule II, Sl. No. 1 of the Income-tax Act, 2025). The four routes are clauses (a), (b)(i)–(iii) and (c); the judge-made conditions are the direct and immediate nexus requirement (Bacha F. Guzdar), the basic operations requirement (Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy) and the three conditions of clause (b)(ii) (Sakarlal Naranlal, applying Dooars Tea).

Step 3 — Receipt-wise analysis

(a) Bamboo of spontaneous growth — ₹5,00,000

Not agricultural income. The decisive fact is the word "spontaneous". In CIT v. Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy the Supreme Court held that "products which grow wild on the land or are of spontaneous growth not involving any human labour or skill upon the land are not products of agriculture and the income derived therefrom is not agricultural income"; the only work done in such a case is "collecting the produce and consuming and marketing the same" (reader p. 34). The test is the presence of basic operations — tilling, sowing, planting, "similar work done on the land itself" — of which the subsequent operations (weeding, pruning, cutting, harvesting) must be "in conjunction with and a continuation". Cutting bamboo that nature grew is a subsequent operation divorced from any basic operation, and therefore does not qualify. The same conclusion follows from Pratap Singh v. CIT (1952) 22 ITR 1 (All.) approved in substance in Benoy, and from CIT v. Mustafa Ali Khan (1948) 16 ITR 330 (PC): unless there is "some measure of cultivation of the land, some expenditure of skill and labour upon it", the land cannot be said to be used for agricultural purposes.

Heads of income: the receipt is a revenue receipt (the bamboo is the produce of the land, cut and sold as part of a recurring activity), assessable as business income or as income from other sources according to whether the sale is a business. Contrast: had Mr. FERN planted the bamboo, the income would have been agricultural — precisely the distinction the Supreme Court drew for the re-planted portions of the forest in Benoy.

(b) Farm house within the municipal limits of Udaipur — ₹5,00,000

Not agricultural income. Three independent reasons:

  1. Clause (c) protects income from a building only inter alia where the land is assessed to land revenue or, if not, is not situated within a municipality or cantonment board with a population of 10,000 or more (proviso (ii), limbs (A) and (B)). A farm house inside municipal limits is therefore outside the clause.
  2. Even within clause (c), Explanation 2 excludes income from the use of such building "for any purpose (including letting for residential purpose or for the purpose of any business or profession) other than agriculture".
  3. The question is not about income from the building at all, but about the sale of the building. Land and buildings are separate capital assets; the sale is governed by sections 2(14) and 45, and Explanation 1 to s. 2(1A) makes it plain that "revenue derived from land" never included income from a transfer. The ₹5,00,000 is therefore a capital gain (short-term or long-term by the period of holding, with the rate and indexation position of the year of transfer), and in any event taxable.

Note the drafting trick: two ₹5,00,000 figures, one exempt-test and one taxable-test, with the same number to see whether the student is reading facts or adding figures.

(c) Sale of agricultural produce from another agricultural land — ₹10,00,000

Agricultural income; wholly exempt. This is the paradigm case under s. 2(1A)(b)(iii) — "the sale by a cultivator … of the produce raised … by him, in respect of which no process has been performed other than a process of the nature described in sub-clause (ii)". Mr. FERN is the cultivator (so Maddi Venkatasubbayya's objection does not apply), the land is in India and used for agricultural purposes, and the receipt is not excluded by anything in s. 10(1). The ITO's rejection is wrong to this extent.

(d) Sugarcane converted into jaggery — ₹3,00,000

Exempt on the facts stated, following H. G. Date — but the assessee must prove the premise.

  • Clause (b)(ii) requires (i) that the process be necessary to render the produce fit to be taken to market — which presupposes that there is no market for the raw produce; (ii) that it be a process ordinarily employed by cultivators; and (iii) that the produce retain its identity (Sakarlal Naranlal, AIR 1965 Guj. 165, reader p. 43; Dooars Tea Co. (1962) 44 ITR 6; K. Lakshmanan & Co. v. CIT, reader p. 53).
  • In CIT v. H. G. Date (1971) 82 ITR 71 (Bom.) the assessee's sugarcane could not be sold to the single available mill; the Tribunal found, on evidence, that there was no market for the cane in its natural condition, and the High Court declined to disturb that finding of fact, holding the jaggery income to be agricultural income exempt from tax. The present facts — "not able to sell his sugarcane produce due to purchase denial by nearby factories" — are materially identical.
  • But the burden lies on the assessee (Mustafa Ali Khan; Maddi), and he must produce the refusals, price correspondence and local material to establish the absence of a market. If the Revenue proves that the factories in fact bought cane of his variety, or that cane generally had a ready market, the case falls on condition (i) and, because jaggery is a different commodity from cane, also on condition (iii): see Seth Banarsi Das Gupta v. CIT (1977) 106 ITR 804 (All.) and the later Madras decision in E. Palaniappan, which confine Date to a cane variety unusable in natural form.

Advice to Mr. FERN: claim the ₹3,00,000 as exempt, but only with the documentary proof of refusal; and keep the jaggery-making accounts separate, because the Revenue will argue that the activity was a manufacturing business.

She also received an agricultural land worth Rs. 25 lakhs during the liquidation process of a company (KKS Ltd.) under final settlement against her share holding … In year 2020-21, the Income Tax Officer (ITO) reopened the assessment and issued notice to Ms. NEENA u/s 148 with a view that whole amount shall be charged to tax under the head capital gain. ITO also rejected the exemption of Rs. 60,000 claimed as agricultural income received as dividend from MMR Ltd. As an income tax law practitioner, discuss the arguments citing landmark cases."
(a) The ₹60,000 claim on the dividend — the assessee must fail

Provision and principle. Dividend is income under s. 2(24)(ii) and is taxable in the shareholder's hands. It is not "rent or revenue derived from land" under s. 2(1A)(a) because the shareholder has no interest in the company's land. The governing authority is Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, Bombay, AIR 1955 SC 74 (DU reader pp. 17–20), where precisely this claim was made — 60% of a dividend from two tea companies, on the strength of the 60:40 rule — and rejected:

  • "Agricultural income as defined in the Act is obviously intended to refer to the revenue received by direct association with the land which is used for agricultural purposes and not by indirectly extending it to cases where that revenue or part thereof changes hands either by way of distribution of dividends or otherwise" (reader p. 18).
  • A shareholder "has got no interest in the property of the company though he has undoubtedly a right to participate in the profits if and when the company decides to divide them" (applying the Sholapur Mills case). "Dividend is derived from the investment made in the shares of the company."
  • The 60:40 formula (Rule 24 of the 1922 Rules; now Rule 8 / Rule 271) operates only in computing the company's income. It does not impress an agricultural character on the dividend.
  • To the same effect, on method of calculation: Premier Construction Co. v. CIT, AIR 1949 PC 20 — income not itself agricultural "does not assume the character of agricultural income by reason of the source from which it is derived, or the method by which it is calculated."

Conclusion: the ITO is right to reject the ₹60,000; the whole ₹1,00,000 of dividend is taxable. Ms. NEENA's only surviving argument is procedural, not substantive.

(b) The ₹25 lakh of agricultural land received on liquidation — taxable under s. 46(2)

Provision. Section 46(2): where a shareholder, on the liquidation of a company, receives any money or other assets from the company, he is chargeable under the head "capital gains" in respect of the money so received or the market value of the other assets on the date of distribution, as reduced by the amount assessed as dividend.

The tempting but wrong argument is that agricultural land is excluded from "capital asset" under s. 2(14)(iii), so its receipt cannot be a capital gain. That argument was decisively rejected in N. Bagavathy Ammal v. CIT, JT 2003(1) SC 363 (DU reader pp. 141–144), on facts almost identical — distribution of 479.89 acres of agricultural land to shareholders of a company in liquidation:

  • Section 46 deliberately uses the word "assets", not "capital assets"; the non-obstante clause in s. 46(1) shows the draughtsman intended "assets of all kinds" to be within the charge.
  • "Agricultural land would have been a 'capital asset' but for the exclusion from the definition of 'capital asset' and what is not a capital asset may yet be an asset for the purposes of section 46(2)".
  • The market value of the land as on the date of distribution is taxable; s. 2(14) is "unnecessary for the purpose of construing section 46(2)".

Conclusion: the ₹25,00,000 is chargeable as capital gains — but only at the market value on the date of distribution, not at some later or assumed figure. The ITO's "whole amount" approach must be tested against the valuation date and against the deduction for any part assessed as dividend.

(c) The s. 54 / s. 148 limbs — note them, then stop
  • The ₹20,00,000 gain on the residential house: acquired on 10 May 2016 under a s. 54 reinvestment and sold on 30 November 2018 — within three years. Section 54(2) withdraws the earlier exemption where the new asset is transferred within three years, and the gain is taxed in the year of transfer; held for less than 24 months it is a short-term gain, taxed at slab rates. The ITO is therefore right that it is taxable.
  • The s. 148 notice is nonetheless open to challenge on jurisdiction: reopening beyond four years requires a failure to disclose fully and truly; and after the 2021 amendments a notice must comply with s. 148A (information, opportunity, reasoned order) and the specified monetary thresholds. Advise Ms. NEENA to attack the reasons to believe and the sanction, while conceding the dividend point.
Overall advice

Concede the dividend (₹60,000) — Bacha is conclusive against her. Contest the quantum and the valuation date of the agricultural land under s. 46(2) — Bagavathy Ammal is conclusive against exemption but not against the figure. Contest the validity of the reopening. This mixture of a "lost point" and a "defensible point" is what the examiner rewards.

9.5Theory question bank — fourteen answers in exam form

These are the questions this topic has traditionally generated (and the forms in which the case material is tested). Each answer is deliberately compressed to the marks it would earn in a 10-mark question; expand the authorities from §§4–5 as required.

Q1. Define "agricultural income" under s. 2(1A) and explain its clauses with illustrations.

Agricultural income means (a) any rent or revenue derived from land situated in India and used for agricultural purposes; (b) any income derived from such land by (i) agriculture — including the basic and subsequent operations of an integrated activity (Benoy); (ii) the performance by a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind of any process ordinarily employed to render the produce fit to be taken to market (Sakarlal: no market, ordinary process, identity preserved); or (iii) the sale of produce on which no other process has been performed; and (c) income from a farm building on or in the immediate vicinity of the land, required by the land-connected person as a dwelling house, store-house or out-building, subject to the provisos and to Explanation 2. Explanation 3 deems nursery income to be agricultural. Illustration: rent of a field → exempt (a); a cultivator's sale of wheat → exempt (b)(iii); drying and winnowing of that wheat by him → exempt (b)(ii); a trader's profit on buying and reselling the same wheat → taxable (Maddi).

Q2. "The exemption of agricultural income is constitutional, not concessional." Discuss.

Entry 82 of List I empowers Parliament to tax "income other than agricultural income", while Entry 46 of List II gives the States exclusive power over "taxes on agricultural income"; Art. 366(1) adopts the statutory definition. Section 10(1) is therefore the municipal-law machinery of a division of powers, not a bounty: the Union cannot tax the income even indirectly, and the same income may be taxed by a State under its Agricultural Income-tax Act (Benoy, reader p. 32). The consequence is a definition-driven jurisprudence — because every receipt that enters s. 2(1A) leaves Parliament's reach, the courts construe the definition strictly and confine it to revenue having a direct and immediate relation with the land (Bacha), declining to extend it to "those into whosoever hands that revenue falls". Two riders: (i) Parliament may still use the income for rate purposes (partial integration), since it is not taxing it; and (ii) capital gains on urban agricultural land are taxable, because the entry protects agricultural income, not all land.

Q3. State the conditions which a process must satisfy to fall within s. 2(1A)(b)(ii).

Per Sakarlal Naranlal v. CIT, AIR 1965 Guj. 165 (reader p. 43): (1) the process must be necessary to render the produce fit to be taken to market, which presupposes that there is no market for the produce in its raw state — "that which is already marketable does not need any process to render it marketable"; (2) it must be a process ordinarily employed by a cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind — a special or unusual process is excluded, though where the assessee is the only cultivator his own practice may supply the standard (J. M. Casey); and (3) the produce must retain its original character — the judicial condition, from Dooars Tea Co. (1962) 44 ITR 6 and applied in K. Lakshmanan: "what is ultimately marketed must be that produce". The reason for the clause is that where the cultivator has no choice but to process in order to sell, the process is treated as a continuation of his agricultural operations.

Q4. Distinguish basic and subsequent agricultural operations. Why is the distinction decisive?

Benoy Kumar Sahas Roy: basic operations — tilling of the land, sowing of seeds, planting and "similar work done on the land itself" — involve human skill and labour spent upon the land; subsequent operations — weeding, digging around the growth, removing under-growth, tending, pruning, cutting, harvesting, guarding against pests and rendering the produce fit for the market — are spent on the product. Subsequent operations are agricultural only when they are "in conjunction with and a continuation of" basic operations, because agriculture is "one integrated activity". The distinction is decisive where the product is of wild or spontaneous growth: there, subsequent operations are divorced from any basic operation and "do not acquire the characteristic of the agricultural operations", so the income is not agricultural (reader p. 34). It also explains why planted trees yield agricultural income while a 150-year-old spontaneous forest does not.

Q5. Is income from forest produce agricultural income? Discuss.

It depends on the origin of the forest. (i) A forest of spontaneous growth, even if tended, weeded, pruned and guarded, produces non-agricultural income — Benoy; Pratap Singh v. CIT (1952) 22 ITR 1 (All.). (ii) Trees planted and nursed by the assessee are agricultural produce, because basic operations are present — Benoy itself held that the re-planted portions of the forest yielded agricultural income and that on a 150-year-old forest such areas were not negligible. (iii) Forestry is not excluded by the Constitution: Entries 14 and 19 of List II are "not mutually exclusive", and if a proprietor plants trees so that they grow into a forest (e.g. casuarina), the income is agricultural (Benoy, reader p. 23). In practice the dispute becomes one of apportionment between planted and spontaneous portions, and the burden lies on the assessee (Mustafa Ali Khan).

Q6. Is a dividend received from a tea company agricultural income to the extent of 60%?

No — Bacha F. Guzdar v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 74. The 60:40 rule (Rule 24, 1922 Rules; Rule 8 / Rule 271 today) operates in computing the company's income; it does not travel with the dividend. The shareholder has no interest in the company's property, only a right to participate in profits when declared; the source of the dividend is the shareholding, and the declaration of dividend is not the source of the profit. "Revenue derived from land" requires a direct and immediate relation with the land, and the policy of the Act protects the tiller and the receiver of rent, not "those into whosoever hands that revenue falls, however remote the receiver". The whole dividend is taxable. Same result for a managing agent's commission calculated on profits — Premier Construction.

Q7. Write a note on Premier Construction Co. v. CIT.

(1948) XVI ITR 380 (PC) / AIR 1949 PC 20. The managing agent of a company, part of whose income was agricultural, claimed exemption for his remuneration at 10% of the company's profits. Held: he received no agricultural income, but remuneration under a contract for personal service, calculated on profits and payable out of any available moneys and not in specie out of any item of profits. Sir John Beaumont: "where an assessee receives income, not itself of a character to fall within the definition of agricultural income …, such income does not assume the character of agricultural income by reason of the source from which it is derived, or the method by which it is calculated." The dictum is the general anti-look-through rule and is quoted in Bacha (reader p. 20).

Q8. Who can claim agricultural income? Discuss with reference to Maddi Venkatasubbayya.

Only a person having an interest in the land: the owner who lets it, the owner-cultivator, the occupancy tenant, the lessee, the usufructuary mortgagee in possession, the sub-lessee and persons similarly placed (CIT v. Maddi Venkatasubbayya, (1951) XX ITR 151 (Mad.), reader p. 37). A merchant who buys a standing crop and harvests, cures and resells it is at best a licensee (s. 52, Indian Easements Act); growing crops are movable property (s. 3, T.P. Act; s. 2(6), Registration Act); and "once the standing crop passed from the ownership of the cultivating tenant to that of the trader … it lost the quality of agricultural income at that point" (reader p. 38). His profit is a trading profit. Compare Yagappa Nadar: a toddy-tapping licensee earns non-agricultural income, while the owner or lessee earning the same income would be exempt — character follows interest, not activity.

Q9. Explain the "market" test, comparing Sakarlal Naranlal with H. G. Date.

Sakarlal holds that the market to be considered is a market for the produce as a commodity, where "the produce ordinarily grown by a cultivator is bought or sold", and that it need not be an Indian market: if galkas could be sold raw anywhere, including Japan, the loofah process was not necessary. The enquiry is objective, judged by the area of growth and the customary process, and is a question of fact. H. G. Date (1971) 82 ITR 71 (Bom.) applies the test to jaggery: the Tribunal found, on evidence about the capacity of the only mill within reach and the perishability of cane, that there was no market for the sugarcane, and the High Court refused to disturb that finding of fact, holding the jaggery income agricultural. The tension: the identity condition (Dooars Tea; Sakarlal) is not satisfied when a new commodity is sold, and Seth Banarsi Das Gupta (All.) has taken the contrary view. Date must therefore be confined, as Lakshmanan confirms, to cases where the raw produce has no market at all.

Q10. Mulberry leaves are grown and fed to silkworms; the cocoons are sold. Discuss the claim to exemption.

K. Lakshmanan & Co. v. CIT, (1998) 9 SCC 537 / (1999) 239 ITR 597 (reader p. 52): the claim fails. Section 2(1)(b)(ii) requires that "what is taken to the market and sold must be the produce which is raised by the cultivator"; the section "does not contemplate the sale of an item or a commodity which is different from what is cultivated and processed". The agricultural produce is the mulberry leaf; the silkworm and the cocoon are not the produce of the cultivator. Although the leaves had no market — satisfying condition (1) — the identity condition failed. The Court added the counterfactual that marks the line: had the leaves been processed and sold as leaves, the income would have been agricultural.

Q11. When is income from a farm building agricultural income?

Under s. 2(1A)(c), five conditions: (i) the building is owned and occupied by the receiver of rent or revenue of the land, or occupied by the cultivator or receiver of rent-in-kind; (ii) it is on or in the immediate vicinity of the land (proviso (i)); (iii) by reason of his connection with the land, that person requires it as a dwelling house, store-house or other out-building (proviso (i)); (iv) the land is assessed to land revenue in India or subject to a local rate, or — if not — (v) the land is not situated in an area within a municipality or cantonment board of 10,000+ population, or within the notified distance up to 8 km (proviso (ii)(A)–(B)). Even then, Explanation 2 excludes income from the use of the building for any purpose other than agriculture, including letting for residence or business. So a store-house beside the fields is protected; a holiday home and a factory are not.

Q12. Write a note on the tax treatment of nursery income.

Income from saplings or seedlings grown in a nursery is deemed to be agricultural income by Explanation 3 to s. 2(1A) (inserted by the Finance Act, 2008 w.e.f. AY 2009-10), whether or not basic operations were carried out on land. The provision followed CIT v. Soundarya Nursery (2000) 241 ITR 530 (Mad.), which had held plants raised in pots to be the product of primary and subsequent operations; the contrary argument was that pots are not "land". Because it is a deeming fiction, it cannot be extended — mushroom cultivation in trays, floriculture and other controlled-environment activities must satisfy the ordinary Benoy tests, and a deeming provision implies that but for it the income would not be agricultural.

Q13. Explain partial integration with an illustration.

Where the assessee is an individual, HUF, AOP, BOI or artificial juridical person; his net agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000; and his non-agricultural income exceeds the basic exemption limit, the agricultural income is aggregated for rate purposes: (1) tax on (non-agricultural income + net agricultural income); (2) tax on (net agricultural income + basic exemption limit); (3) tax payable = (1) − (2), plus surcharge and cess. Illustration: non-agricultural ₹8,00,000, agricultural ₹1,00,000, old-regime slabs — (1) tax on ₹9,00,000 = ₹92,500; (2) tax on ₹3,50,000 = ₹5,000; tax = ₹87,500 (+ 4% cess). The farm income is never taxed; the increase results from the slab structure being used only once. Companies and firms are outside the scheme.

Q14. How is composite income apportioned? Explain Rules 7, 7A, 7B, 7C and 8.

Where one business is partly agricultural and partly not, the Rules fix the split. Rule 7: the market value of the agricultural produce raised by the assessee and used as raw material in his own business (or whose sale receipts are credited in the business accounts) is deducted from business income and treated as agricultural income — market value being the average sale price if the produce is ordinarily sold, and otherwise cultivation expenses + land revenue/rent + a reasonable profit. Rule 7A (rubber) 65% agricultural; Rule 7B (coffee grown and cured) 75%, (coffee roasted and ground) 60%; Rules 7C and 8 (tea) 60% agricultural and 40% taxable. The Income-tax Rules, 2026 consolidate these as Rule 270 (market-value method) and Rule 271 (the commodity table). Illustration: tea income ₹25,00,000 → ₹10,00,000 taxable, ₹15,00,000 exempt.

Q15. "Exempt income which must still be disclosed." Explain with reference to agricultural income.

Under s. 10(1), agricultural income is excluded from total income — but it must be reported in the return (Schedule EI; and Schedule AI with land and district particulars where it exceeds ₹5,000), ITR-1 is unavailable beyond ₹5,000, and the figure is a standard scrutiny trigger. It also affects tax through partial integration (§7) and disqualifies nothing: it remains relevant to the return, to rate, and to the capital-gains treatment of agricultural land (§8). This is the practical content of the phrase "exempt but not ignored".

Q16. Short notes: (a) rent in kind; (b) salami/premium on a lease of agricultural land; (c) interest on arrears of rent; (d) usufructuary mortgagee.

(a) Rent in kind: produce paid as rent; clause (a) covers "any rent or revenue" in cash or kind, and clauses (b)(ii) and (iii) expressly extend the process and sale routes to a "receiver of rent-in-kind" — the statutory protection of the person "getting land cultivated by others" (Bacha).

(b) Salami/premium: a single, non-recurring payment for the creation of a tenancy is a capital receipt — it is the price for parting with a slice of the lessor's interest, not rent — Sindhurani Chaudhurani, AIR 1957 SC 729; CIT v. Panbari Tea Co. (1965) 57 ITR 422 (instalments do not change the character); Durga Das Khanna v. CIT (1969) 72 ITR 796 ("prima facie premium or salami is not income; it is for the Revenue to show facts making it revenue"). Being capital, it is outside s. 2(1A) altogether.

(c) Interest on arrears of rent: not agricultural income — Kamakshya Narain Singh, AIR 1949 PC 1: the effective source is the unpaid rent, and "rent is not land".

(d) Usufructuary mortgagee in possession: his receipts are agricultural income, but because he has gone into possession and receives rent as a person interested in the land — Kameshwar Singh (1935) 3 ITR 305 (PC), as explained in Maddi; confirmed and confined in Bacha.

10.1One-page revision

The topic in ten propositions

  1. Agricultural income is exempt because of Entry 82 List I / Entry 46 List II; s. 10(1) is the machinery, Art. 366(1) the definitional bridge. It is exempt in Union law but taxable by States.
  2. Section 2(1A) has four routes: (a) rent or revenue; (b)(i) agriculture; (b)(ii) marketable process; (b)(iii) sale of produce; (c) farm building — plus the deemed nursery route (Explanation 3).
  3. Everything turns on a direct and immediate relation with land (Bacha); the enquiry stops at the effective source (Kamakshya Narain Singh).
  4. "Agriculture" is an integrated activity: basic operations on the land are the sine qua non; subsequent operations count only as their continuation (Benoy).
  5. Wild or spontaneous growth = no agricultural income, however much labour is spent tending it; planted and nursed trees = agricultural.
  6. Clause (b)(ii) has three conditions: no market for the raw produce; an ordinary process of cultivators; identity of the produce preserved (Sakarlal; Dooars Tea; Lakshmanan).
  7. The assessee must have an interest in the land; a purchaser of a standing crop is a licensee, and a shareholder or managing agent is a stranger (Maddi; Bacha; Premier).
  8. Where one business is partly agricultural, the split is statutory: Rule 7 (market value) and Rules 7A/7B/7C/8 → Rules 270–271 (2026): rubber 65:35, coffee 75:25, coffee with chicory 60:40, tea 60:40.
  9. Exempt does not mean irrelevant: partial integration applies to individuals/HUFs where agricultural income exceeds ₹5,000 and non-agricultural income exceeds the exemption limit — three-step method.
  10. Rural agricultural land is not a capital asset; urban land is, and gains on it are taxable (Explanation 1 to s. 2(1A); s. 10(37) for compulsory acquisition; s. 54B rollover; s. 46(2) for land received on liquidation — Bagavathy Ammal).

10.4Twelve errors that cost marks

1. Treating the exemption as a deduction

Agricultural income is excluded by s. 10(1); it is not claimed under Chapter VI-A.

2. Saying "60% of the dividend is exempt"

Bacha is directly against it.

3. Citing s. 2(1A) for a 1922-Act case

State the mapping (s. 2(1)(b) → s. 2(1A)(b); s. 4(3)(viii) → s. 10(1)) and then apply it.

4. Forgetting "such land"

Clause (b) imports the requirements of clause (a): land in India, used for agriculture.

5. Confusing the two questions

Is the activity agricultural? (Benoy) v. is this the person who derives from the land? (Maddi).

6. Treating absence of a market as sufficient

Identity must also be preserved — Lakshmanan says so expressly.

7. Asserting "forest income is exempt"

Only planted and nursed trees; spontaneous growth is taxable (Benoy; Pratap Singh).

8. Aggregating without condition 3

Non-agricultural income must exceed the exemption limit, not merely exist.

9. Applying partial integration to a company

Only individuals/HUFs/AOPs/BOIs/AJPs.

10. Taxing the farm income in the computation

It is added for rate only; do not carry it into total income.

11. Treating the farm house as exempt wherever it stands

Proviso (ii) to clause (c) and Explanation 2 exclude urban buildings and non-agricultural use.

12. Forgetting the 2025 Act mapping

s. 2(5); s. 11 + Schedule II, Sl. 1; Rules 270–271 of 2026 — one line, but it dates your answer.
