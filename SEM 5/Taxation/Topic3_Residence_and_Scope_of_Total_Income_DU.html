Topic 3 — Residence & scope of total income
LL.B. · VI Term · LB-604 Topic 3 of 6

Residence:
the reach of the charge

Every other topic tells you what is taxed. This one decides how far the tax reaches. Section 4 imposes the charge; section 5 fixes its scope by reference to two variables — where the assessee resides, and where the income arises; section 6 defines residence; section 9 deems income to arise in India when it does not. The whole topic is therefore one question asked twice: who are you, and where did the money come from? The three prescribed cases answer it — two on residence (Subbayya Chettiar, Narottam and Parekh) and one on the outer limit of the charge (Vodafone).

00OrientationHow this document is built & what to read first

This is the topic that makes the rest of the Act work. A resident of India is taxed on income arising anywhere in the world; a non-resident is taxed only on what arises, or is deemed to arise, in India. Nothing else in the statute changes the tax bill of two identical transactions so completely as the answer to the question "where does the assessee reside?" — which is why the two leading cases on the question are among the most cited in Indian tax law, and why the third case in this topic, Vodafone, is the most discussed tax judgment of the modern era.

The one question that unifies the topic

Section 4 charges the total income of the previous year of every person. To know what "total income" means for this person you must know two things, and only two: (1) his residential status for that year (s. 6), and (2) the source of each item — whether it is received or accrues in India, or is deemed to accrue in India (ss. 9 and 5). Section 5 then combines them into a single table: resident and ordinarily resident → worldwide income; resident but not ordinarily resident → Indian income plus foreign income from a business controlled in, or profession set up in, India; non-resident → Indian income alone. Every problem in this topic is that table applied to facts, and every case in this topic is about how one of the two variables is proved.

§

01Section 1The charge of tax, and the scheme that gives it scope

The Income-tax Act does not tax people. It taxes income, in the hands of a person, for a previous year, at the rate fixed by Parliament. Every one of those four elements is a separate question, and this topic is about the second and the third: whose income, and how much of it. Sections 4, 5, 6 and 9 answer them in a fixed order, and the order matters: get the sequence wrong and the whole computation is wrong.

◆1.1 Why residence decides the tax

Two assessees earn the same ₹10,00,000 from a business carried on in London. One is a resident Indian; the other is a German national who has never set foot in India. The first is taxed in India on the whole ₹10,00,000. The second is taxed in India on nothing at all. Now reverse the facts: the same two assessees earn ₹10,00,000 from a business carried on in Delhi. Both are taxed in India on the whole of it — the resident because his total income is chargeable under s. 5(1), the non-resident because the income is deemed to accrue in India under s. 9(1)(i) and is thus within s. 5(2).

The proposition in one line

The incidence of tax = residential status × source of income. Neither variable is sufficient by itself. A resident is taxed on foreign income because of his status; a non-resident is taxed on Indian income because of the situs of the source. The entire topic is the elaboration of that multiplication table — which is why the syllabus asks, as its final question, "does the incidence of tax vary with the residential status of an assessee?" The answer is yes, and §5 sets out exactly how.

The two variables are proved in very different ways, and that difference is what the three cases are about. Residence is a question of fact measured by days, or by the location of control and management, or by the place of incorporation (Subbayya Chettiar, Narottam and Parekh). Source is largely a question of law, because Parliament has enacted fictions in s. 9 that deem income to accrue in India when, on ordinary principles, it accrues nowhere near India; the reach of those fictions is the subject of Vodafone.

◆1.2 The chain: s. 4 → s. 5 → ss. 6 and 9 → computation

Section 4 — the charging section. It reads, in substance: "Where any Central Act enacts that income-tax shall be charged for any assessment year at any rate or rates, income-tax at that rate or those rates shall be charged for that year in accordance with, and subject to the provisions of, this Act in respect of the total income of the previous year of every person." ◆1.3 The constitutional frame: why India may tax a foreign source at all

The power to levy income-tax comes from Entry 82 of List I of the Seventh Schedule — "taxes on income other than agricultural income" — read with Article 246. Two consequences follow, and both appear in this topic:

  • Agricultural income is outside the Union's income-tax power (Entry 82 expressly excludes it; Entry 46 of List II gives it to the States). That is why s. 10(1) exempts it and why the Finance Act uses it only to fix the rate. This is the bridge to Topic 2.
  • A Union law may operate extra-territorially, but only on a real territorial nexus. The Explanation to Article 245 provides that a law made by Parliament shall not be invalid on the ground that it has extra-territorial operation; yet the courts have insisted that there must be a connection between the assessee or the income and India before the charge can reach it. Sections 5 and 9 are drafted on precisely that footing: s. 5(1)(c) reaches foreign income only of a resident, and s. 9 creates fictions only where there is a business connection, property, asset, source or capital asset in India. outside the reader The classic authority is A.H. Wadia v. CIT, (1949) 17 ITR 63 (FC), upholding the charge on a company resident in India in respect of income arising in Indian States, on the doctrine of territorial nexus.

The constitutional dimension is not decoration: it is the argumentative frame of Vodafone. When the Revenue contended that s. 9(1)(i) should be read as a "look through" so as to tax a transfer of shares effected in the Cayman Islands, the Supreme Court's answer was in substance a constitutional one — a legal fiction cannot be expanded by interpretation, and the legislature had not said that indirect transfers were taxable (§8.5, para 10 of the judgment). The Court then added that "look through" and limitation-of-benefits clauses are "a matter of policy" for Parliament and for treaties, not for the court.

02Section 2s. 5 — the scope of total income

Section 4 says the tax is charged on "total income". Section 5 says what "total income" includes — and it does so, remarkably, in two short subsections and a proviso. Every problem in this topic is answered by applying them. Read the text before you read any commentary on it: almost all of the syllabus's questions can be answered by pointing to a particular clause.

◆2.1 The text and its structure

Income-tax Act, 1961 · s. 5

"5. Scope of total income.—(1) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the total income of any previous year of a person who is a resident includes all income from whatever source derived which—
(a) is received or is deemed to be received in India in such year by or on behalf of such person; or
(b) accrues or arises or is deemed to accrue or arise to him in India during such year; or
(c) accrues or arises to him outside India during such year:
Provided that, in the case of a person not ordinarily resident in India within the meaning of sub-section (6) of section 6, the income which accrues or arises to him outside India shall not be so included unless it is derived from a business controlled in or a profession set up in India.

(2) Subject to the provisions of this Act, the total income of any previous year of a person who is a non-resident includes all income from whatever source derived which—
(a) is received or is deemed to be received in India in such year by or on behalf of such person; or
(b) accrues or arises or is deemed to accrue or arise to him in India during such year."

Reproduced in substance; the reader prints s. 5(2) verbatim at p. 67 in the judgment in Vodafone.

Four structural points must be noticed at once, because each is worth marks:

  1. Section 5(1) has three clauses; s. 5(2) has two. The missing clause is clause (c) — income accruing outside India. That single omission is the whole difference between a resident and a non-resident: the non-resident is not charged on foreign income at all, however large, however near, however much of it is later remitted to India.
  2. "Received" and "accrued" are separate and cumulative tests. Clause (a) catches income by reference to its receipt, clause (b) by reference to its origin. A resident is caught if either applies; so a resident who receives in India income which accrued in London is taxed twice over in principle — under (a) and under (c) — but of course only once in fact, because the same income cannot be assessed twice. The draftsman's point is that no route of escape is left open for a resident.
  3. "Deemed" does the heavy lifting. Deemed receipt (s. 7) and deemed accrual (s. 9) are the two fictions through which the Act reaches income that, in fact, was neither received nor earned in India. Section 9 is treated in §4, and it is the section that Vodafone construed.
  4. "Subject to the provisions of this Act" makes Chapter III (ss. 10–13A) operate as an exception: income included by s. 5 may still be excluded by s. 10 (agricultural income, share of a partner in a firm, gratuity, and so on). Section 5 fixes the scope; the exemptions fix the quantum.

"Received … by or on behalf of such person". The words "on behalf of" prevent the easy evasion of clause (a) by the device of having the income paid to an agent, a nominee or a bank account in India. Receipt by a person on behalf of the assessee is receipt by the assessee. Conversely, receipt by the assessee of money which he holds for someone else is not his income at all — which is the diversion-by-overriding-title idea from Topic 1, and the reason the two topics are examined together.

"Accrues or arises". The two words are not synonyms, though they are usually read together. Income accrues when the assessee acquires a right to receive it, even if payment is deferred; income arises from the source that produces it. The practical consequence is the rule of accrual for a mercantile assessee: the right to receive is taxed, not the cash. For an assessee keeping accounts on a cash basis, the position under the heads of income may differ, but the scope provision continues to speak of accrual.

◆2.3 The proviso: resident but not ordinarily resident

The proviso to s. 5(1) creates the intermediate category. For an RNOR, foreign income is taxed only if it is "derived from a business controlled in or a profession set up in India". Three points follow:

  • Two tests, both Indian. The RNOR is protected in respect of passive foreign income (dividends, interest, rent) but not in respect of foreign income produced by an Indian-controlled business or an Indian-based profession. The rationale is that a person who controls a business from India should not be able to defer Indian tax by leaving the profits abroad.
  • The words "controlled in" are the same words as the residence test. There is therefore a pleasing symmetry: the test that makes an HUF resident (control and management in India) is the test that deprives an RNOR of the benefit of the proviso in respect of a foreign business. Both trace back to Subbayya Chettiar (§6).
  • Who is an RNOR is defined by s. 6(6): an individual who fails the "9 out of 10 years and 729 days" test; an HUF whose manager fails it; the 120-day visitor with income above ₹15 lakh; and — since 2020 — a person deemed resident under s. 6(1A). Section 3.4 sets out the definition.

◆2.4 Receipt, accrual and the abolition of the remittance basis

Students often carry a rule of thumb from older books — "foreign income is taxed when it is remitted to India". That rule belonged to the 1922 Act, under which certain foreign income of a person not ordinarily resident was charged on a remittance basis. The 1961 Act abolished it. The position now is:

Three traps

(1) Remittance is not a test any more. A resident who earns interest in London and leaves it there is taxed on it. A non-resident who earned interest in London and later brings it to India is taxed on the receipt, in the year of receipt, under s. 5(2)(a) — because the receipt, not the accrual, is the taxable event for him. The two rules look similar and are completely different: for the resident the source is taxed; for the non-resident the receipt in India is taxed.

(2) A resident is taxed on foreign income even if it is not remitted, and is not taxed twice if it is. Section 5(1)(a) and (c) both apply to the same amount; the Act taxes it once, in the year of accrual (or of receipt if earlier), and provides relief for foreign tax under s. 90 (treaty) or s. 91 (unilateral credit).

(3) An RNOR is not a non-resident. Indian-source income is fully taxable in his hands; the proviso affects only foreign income, and only passive foreign income at that. A question that asks "what is the incidence of tax on an RNOR?" is answered by the proviso, not by s. 5(2).

03Section 3s. 6 — the tests of residential status

Section 6 is the shortest section in the topic and the one that decides the most. It contains four different tests for four different kinds of assessee — days for individuals, control and management for HUFs and firms, incorporation or place of effective management for companies, and a residual test for everyone else — and then, in sub-section (6), a second layer that splits residents into ordinary and not ordinary residents. Learn the architecture first, then the numbers.

◆3.1 Individuals — the two basic tests

Income-tax Act, 1961 · s. 6(1)

"An individual is said to be resident in India in any previous year, if he—
(a) is in India in that year for a period or periods amounting in all to one hundred and eighty-two days or more; or
(b) having within the four years preceding that year been in India for a period or periods amounting in all to three hundred and sixty-five days or more, is in India for a period or periods amounting in all to sixty days or more in that year; or
(c) having within the four years preceding that year been in India for a period or periods amounting in all to three hundred and sixty-five days or more, and is in India for a period or periods amounting in all to one hundred and twenty days or more in that year, and is a citizen of India, who is not liable to tax in any other country or territory by reason of his domicile or residence or any other criteria of similar nature."

Clause (c) was inserted by the Finance Act, 2020, with effect from AY 2021-22. The rest of the sub-section reproduces the pre-2020 structure, which is what the textbook editions and the older case law work with.

The structure is best remembered as a basic rule and two qualifications:

  • The basic rule — 182 days. Stay in India for 182 days or more in the previous year, and you are resident, whatever your nationality, domicile or intention. The count is of days of presence, aggregated over several visits ("period or periods amounting in all").
  • The first qualification — 365 + 60. Even if you stay less than 182 days, you are resident if you were in India for 365 days or more in the four years preceding the previous year and for 60 days or more in the previous year. This catches the habitual visitor who comes for a couple of months each year.
  • The second qualification — 120 days for certain citizens. Since AY 2021-22, a citizen of India who is not liable to tax in any other country becomes resident on 120 days' stay (subject to the 365-day condition over four years). The purpose is to reach "stateless" persons who, being taxable nowhere, would otherwise escape both India and the world.

Explanation 1: the substitutions. Two categories of person are treated leniently, and the 60-day figure in clause (b) is replaced by 182 days for them. They are: (i) a citizen of India who leaves India in the previous year for the purpose of employment outside India, or as a member of the crew of an Indian ship; and (ii) a citizen of India or a person of Indian origin who, being outside India, comes on a visit to India. The logic is that a person whose home is abroad and who comes to India on a visit should not become resident merely by staying between 60 and 181 days; he must cross the full 182-day line. The same Explanation supplies the definitions of "person of Indian origin" and of "income from foreign sources".

Both the day of arrival and the day of departure are counted as days in India; a stay that spans 12 noon does not have to be a night. Residence is determined afresh for every previous year: a person may be resident in year 1, non-resident in year 2 and ROR in year 3. That is why the last sentence of Subbayya Chettiar — that the decision was confined to the year of assessment before the Court — is a statement of the general principle and not a courtesy (§6.5).

◆3.2 The 182/120-day variants explained

Students lose marks by treating the Explanation as an exception to the 182-day rule. It is not. The structure is:

  1. Clause (a) — 182 days — is universal. Nobody escapes it. It applies to the visiting PIO, to the seaman, to the diplomat's spouse, to everybody.
  2. Clause (b) — 60 days — applies to everyone except the two categories in the Explanation, for whom 60 becomes 182. The effect is that for those two categories, clause (b) collapses into clause (a): only 182 days will do.
  3. Clause (c) — 120 days — applies only to a citizen of India who is not liable to tax in any other country. For that person, the 60-day threshold becomes 120. For every other individual it remains 60.

The 2020 package must be understood as a whole, because its four pieces were introduced together and are usually examined together: (i) the 120-day rule in s. 6(1)(c); (ii) deemed residence in s. 6(1A); (iii) the new RNOR category in s. 6(6)(c); and (iv) the rule in s. 6(6)(d) that a person deemed resident under s. 6(1A) is always RNOR. The legislative design is deliberate: a "nowhere resident" Indian who retains Indian personal or economic ties is brought within the net, but only as an RNOR, so that his passive foreign income remains outside the charge. outside the reader

◆3.3 Deemed residence — s. 6(1A)

Income-tax Act, 1961 · s. 6(1A)

"Notwithstanding anything contained in clause (1), a citizen of India, having total income, other than the income from foreign sources, exceeding fifteen lakh rupees during the previous year, shall be deemed to be resident in India in that previous year, if such person is not liable to tax in any other country or territory by reason of his domicile or residence or any other criteria of similar nature."

Inserted by the Finance Act, 2020, w.e.f. AY 2021-22. "Income from foreign sources" means income accruing or arising outside India, but does not include income from a business controlled in India or a profession set up in India.

Notice what s. 6(1A) does not do. It does not tax foreign income; it merely makes the person resident, and s. 6(6)(d) immediately makes him RNOR, so that s. 5(1)'s proviso applies and his passive foreign income stays out. The target of the provision is the Indian citizen who has arranged his affairs so as to be resident nowhere — a "deemed resident" is a person who is taxed in India on Indian-source income and on foreign income from an Indian business, but not on his foreign investments. The threshold is computed on total income excluding foreign-source income, so that a person cannot avoid deemed residence by having a large foreign salary.

◆3.6 Worked computations

Problem 1 — an individual

Mr A, a citizen of India, is in India for 95 days in the previous year 2024-25. In the four preceding years he was in India for 400 days in all. He was resident in India in 8 of the 10 preceding years and was present in India for 800 days in the 7 preceding years. He is liable to tax in the UAE, where he works.

Answer. Gate 1 (182 days) fails. Gate 2 (365 + 60) is satisfied — 400 days in four years and 95 days this year — and he is not within the Explanation because he is not a visitor (he works abroad, but the Explanation's first category covers a citizen leaving India for employment abroad; here he is already abroad and not "coming on a visit"), nor is he covered by clause (c) since he is liable to tax in the UAE. He is therefore resident under s. 6(1)(b). Stage 2: 8 out of 10 is less than 9, so he fails s. 6(6)(a) and is RNOR. Consequence: Indian income fully taxable; foreign salary taxable only if it is from a business controlled in India or a profession set up in India (it is not), so his UAE salary is outside the charge.

Problem 2 — an HUF

The K family HUF carries on a business in Kanpur. The karta, who lives in Kanpur, spent 200 days in Singapore in the previous year supervising a branch, and the branch's accounts are kept in Singapore. Is the HUF resident?

Answer. Yes. Under s. 6(2) the family is resident unless control and management is situated wholly outside India. The branch is supervised by the karta; the family's affairs as a whole are directed from Kanpur; the seat of control is therefore in India, and the "wholly outside" exception is not made out. The onus is on the assessee (Subbayya Chettiar). Whether the family is ROR or RNOR then depends on the karta's history under s. 6(6)(b).

Problem 3 — a company

P Ltd is incorporated in Singapore, listed in Singapore, and keeps its board in Singapore; its Indian subsidiary's business is directed from Singapore, but its entire operations, employees, plant and customers are in India, and its board meets twice a year in Mumbai where all substantive decisions are taken. Is it resident?

Answer. Under s. 6(3) a foreign company is resident if its place of effective management is in India. If the substantive decisions are in fact taken in Mumbai, the POEM is in India and the company is resident (and, unless the history under s. 6(6) is made out — which the section applies only to individuals and HUFs — it would be ROR, so that its worldwide income is taxable). The question is one of fact, and it is the modern descendant of the de facto control test applied in Narottam and Parekh. outside the reader

04Section 4s. 9 — income deemed to accrue or arise in India

Section 9 is the section that gives the charge its extraterritorial reach over non-residents. It does not itself levy any tax: it supplies, for the purposes of s. 5(2)(b), the fiction that certain income "accrues or arises in India" so that the non-resident may be taxed on it. Because it is a legal fiction, it is construed strictly, and it cannot be expanded by interpretation to catch income that Parliament has not described. That single proposition — stated by the Supreme Court in Vodafone in para 10 of the judgment — is the key to the whole of §8.

◆4.1 The architecture of s. 9(1)

Income-tax Act, 1961 · s. 9(1) — the opening words, and clause (i)

"9. Income deemed to accrue or arise in India.—(1) The following incomes shall be deemed to accrue or arise in India—
(i) all income accruing or arising, whether directly or indirectly, through or from any business connection in India, or through or from any property in India, or through or from any asset or source of income in India, or through the transfer of a capital asset situate in India;"
… and then clauses (ii) salary; (iii) dividend; (iv) interest; (v) [omitted]; (vi) royalty; (vii) fees for technical services.

Three structural points, all of which carry marks:

  1. Section 9(1)(i) is five sources in one sub-clause. The reader prints the sub-clause in full at p. 67, and the Supreme Court reads it as containing four or five distinct categories — business connection, property in India, asset or source of income in India, and transfer of a capital asset situate in India. Its ruling is that "the income dealt with in each sub-clause is distinct and independent of the other, and the requirements to bring income within each sub-clause are separately noted"; income falling in one category need not satisfy the requirements of another (Vodafone, para 10).
  2. The last category has three elements, all of which must exist. "The said sub-clause consists of three elements, namely, transfer, existence of a capital asset, and situation of such asset in India. All three elements should exist in order to make the last sub-clause applicable. Therefore, if such a transfer does not exist in the previous year no charge is attracted" (Vodafone, para 10). Memorise this sentence; it is the ratio's engine.
  3. A fiction is construed strictly and cannot be given a purposive expansion. "A legal fiction has a limited scope. A legal fiction cannot be expanded by giving purposive interpretation particularly if the result of such interpretation is to transform the concept of chargeability" (Vodafone, para 10). Compare the general principle: the fiction is to be carried to its logical conclusion, but no further than the purpose for which it was created.

◆4.3 The Explanations to s. 9(1)(i)

The Explanations do the detailed work, and their numbering has changed repeatedly; the substance is what matters.

  • Explanation 1 — business connection. Defines the expression to include a business activity carried out through a person who, acting on behalf of the non-resident, has and habitually exercises an authority to conclude contracts on behalf of the non-resident, and certain other activities; and then excludes a broker, a general commission agent, or an agent of independent status acting in the ordinary course of business, provided the agent's operations are not confined to buying goods for export. The proviso preserves the position that purchases of goods for export do not by themselves constitute a business connection.
  • Explanation 2 — significant economic presence (SEP). Substituted in 2021, this limb treats a non-resident as having a business connection if it transacts business in respect of any goods, services or property carried through digital or physical means, or systematically and continuously solicits business or users in India, and its income exceeds the prescribed thresholds. outside the reader — the thresholds are in Rule 11UD and have been revised since.
  • Explanations 3 to 5 — assets and indirect transfers. These carry the story of Vodafone after the judgment. Explanation 4 and Explanation 5 were inserted by the Finance Act, 2012 with retrospective effect from 1 April 1962 to make a transfer of shares of a foreign company, deriving substantial value from Indian assets, deemedly a transfer of an asset in India; Explanation 5 has since been amended by the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 so that certain small, non-controlling holdings of a non-resident are excluded. ◆4.5 Section 9(2), and the interaction with tax treaties

Section 9(2) empowers the Central Government either to classify income as income accruing in India, or to declare that no income of a specified class shall be deemed to accrue, or to compute the income of a non-resident in the manner prescribed, having regard to the proper proportion attributable to India. It is an administrative valve, not a source of charge.

The real limit on s. 9 comes from the treaty. Section 90 provides for agreements with foreign countries; and s. 90(2) gives the assessee the option to apply either the provisions of the Act or the provisions of the treaty, whichever is more beneficial. The reasoning is that the Act itself has made the treaty applicable, so the earlier drafters' fears of a clash between "source" and "residence" jurisdiction are resolved by the treaty. This is the architecture that Azadi Bachao Andolan approved and that Vodafone expressly declined to disturb (§8.5). Where there is no treaty, relief for foreign tax paid is given unilaterally by s. 91.

The trap in the phrase "deemed to accrue"

Students often write that s. 9 "taxes" the non-resident. It does not: it deems the income to accrue in India, and it is s. 5(2)(b) that brings it into total income and s. 4 that charges it. ◆5.2 Four worked illustrations

Illustration 1 — the resident who works abroad

Mr S, a resident and ordinarily resident for 2024-25, has: (i) salary from an Indian employer for services rendered in India ₹8,00,000; (ii) rent from a flat in Dubai ₹3,00,000, received in Dubai and kept there; (iii) dividend from a US company ₹1,00,000, credited to his US account; (iv) interest on an Indian fixed deposit ₹50,000.

Answer. All four are taxable. (i) and (iv) under s. 5(1)(a)/(b); (ii) and (iii) under s. 5(1)(c), notwithstanding that they were neither received nor kept in India and were never remitted. Relief for any foreign tax actually paid may be available under s. 90 or s. 91. Total ₹12,50,000. The remittance question is simply irrelevant for an ROR.

Illustration 2 — the non-resident with Indian investments

Ms T, a non-resident for 2024-25, has: (i) rent from a Mumbai flat ₹6,00,000, received in London into her UK account; (ii) capital gains of ₹10,00,000 on the sale of shares of a listed Indian company; (iii) interest on a London bank deposit ₹2,00,000, later brought to India in 2025-26.

Answer. (i) Taxable: the income accrues in India from property in India — s. 5(2)(b), read with s. 9(1)(i) ("through or from any property in India"). The place of receipt is immaterial. (ii) Taxable: transfer of a capital asset situate in India — s. 9(1)(i), last limb. (iii) Not taxable in 2024-25 — a non-resident has no charge on foreign income; but when the money is received in India in 2025-26, s. 5(2)(a) taxes the receipt in that year. That is the one situation in which the old "remittance" intuition is correct, and it is correct for a reason unconnected with remittance as a doctrine.

Illustration 3 — the RNOR

Mr R, RNOR for 2024-25, has: (i) profits of ₹20,00,000 from a business carried on in Singapore but controlled from India; (ii) rent from a Singapore flat ₹5,00,000; (iii) interest from an Indian company ₹1,00,000.

Answer. (i) Taxable — the proviso to s. 5(1) excludes foreign income only if it is not derived from a business controlled in, or a profession set up in, India; this business is controlled from India, so it is taxed. (ii) Not taxable — passive foreign income is exactly what the proviso protects. (iii) Taxable under s. 5(1)(b). Total ₹21,00,000.

Illustration 4 — an HUF, and a company

(a) The K HUF, whose karta has been resident in 9 of the last 10 years and present in India for 800 days in the last 7, is ROR: its foreign income is fully taxable. If the karta had been in India for only 600 days in the last 7, the family would still be resident under s. 6(2) — because its control and management is in India — but would be RNOR under s. 6(6)(b), and its foreign passive income would fall outside the charge. (b) A company incorporated in India is resident under s. 6(3) in every year; being a company, it cannot claim RNOR status under s. 6(6), which speaks only of individuals and HUFs — so a resident company is taxed on its worldwide income, and the RNOR category is simply unavailable to it.

◆5.3 The finer points that earn the extra marks

  • Residence is a question of fact, but it decides a question of law. The days, the meetings, the place of the board are facts; the consequence — worldwide or Indian-only taxation — is a legal consequence. This is why both residence cases end with a statement on onus: the assessee who claims to be outside the Indian charge must prove the facts that put him outside it (Subbayya Chettiar, §6.5).
  • The status of the payer is irrelevant; the status of the payee is everything. A payment by an Indian company to a non-resident is taxable in India if it falls within s. 9(1); a payment by a foreign company to a resident is taxable in India under s. 5(1)(c) even though nothing Indian was involved except the payee.
  • Withholding follows incidence. Section 195 obliges the payer to deduct tax from payments to a non-resident only where the payment is chargeable to tax in India. That is the proposition on which the final part of Vodafone turns, and it is the practical link between this topic and Topic 6's assessment machinery: if the income is not taxable, there is nothing to withhold, and no liability under s. 201.
  • Treaties redistribute, they do not create. Section 90(2) lets an assessee choose the treaty where it is more beneficial; the result is that a non-resident's income which is within s. 9 may still not be taxed, or may be taxed at a reduced rate, under the treaty. The Supreme Court in Vodafone refused to read a "limitation of benefits" clause into a treaty because such clauses are "a matter of policy" and must be expressly provided for.
  • Residence determines more than the tax bill. It determines the obligation to file a return (s. 139), the liability to deduct tax, the applicability of the presumptive and transfer-pricing regimes, the benefit of the exemptions in s. 10, and the availability of treaty benefits. That is why the topic is placed before the heads of income in the syllabus.
Answering the syllabus question in three sentences

"The incidence of tax varies with residential status because s. 5 defines total income differently for each status: an ROR is taxed on worldwide income under s. 5(1)(a)–(c); an RNOR is taxed on Indian income and on foreign income from an Indian business or profession, the rest being excluded by the proviso; and a non-resident is taxed only on income received or accruing in India under s. 5(2)(a)–(b), for which purpose s. 9 deems income to accrue in India. For Indian-source income, however, the incidence is the same for all three, and the source rules in s. 9 — whose outer limit was examined in Vodafone — are therefore as important as the residence rules in s. 6."

06Case 11 · reader pp. 54–57V.V.R.N.M. Subbayya Chettiar v. C.I.T. — where is the "head and brain" of a family?

Why this case is the foundation of the topic

Every proposition now used to decide the residence of an HUF, a firm or an association of persons comes from these four pages: that an HUF is presumed resident unless control and management is situated wholly outside India; that "control and management" means the controlling and directive power — the "head and brain" — functioning at a place with some degree of permanence; that "affairs" means affairs relevant to income-tax; that mere activity in India is not residence; and that the onus of displacing the presumption lies on the assessee. It is also the case that quietly settles the commonest student error in this topic: the residence of an HUF is not the residence of its karta.

V.V.R.N.M. Subbayya Chettiar v. Commissioner of Income-tax

AIR 1951 SC 101 · Fazl Ali J. · appeal from the Madras High Court · AY 1942-43 · DU reader pp. 54–57
Case 11s. 4A(b), 1922 ActHUFControl & managementOnusNow s. 6(2)
1 · Facts
  • The appellant was the karta of a Hindu undivided family. He had been living in Ceylon with his wife, son and three daughters, and the family was stated to be domiciled in that country.
  • He carried on business in Colombo under the name and style of the General Trading Corporation, and he owned a house, some immovable property and investments in British India. He also held shares in two firms situated at Vijayapuram and Nagapatnam in British India.
  • In the year of account 1941-42 (assessment year 1942-43) he visited British India on seven occasions, and the total period of his stay was 101 days. During those stays he: (i) personally attended to litigation relating to the family lands, both in the trial court and in the court of appeal; (ii) attended income-tax proceedings relating to the assessment of the family income, appearing before the income-tax authorities at Karaikudi and Madras; and (iii) on one occasion obtained an extension of time for payment of tax after interviewing the authority concerned.
  • The Department also relied on two further facts: that he had not produced the file of correspondence with the business in Colombo, which would have helped in determining whether the management and control of the business was situated in Colombo; and that he had started two partnership businesses in India on 25 February 1942 and remained in India for some time after their commencement.
  • Assessment history. The Income-tax Officer and the Appellate Assistant Commissioner held that he was resident within s. 4-A(b) and was therefore liable to be assessed in respect of his foreign income. The Tribunal took a different view, holding that no act of management or control had been exercised by the appellant during his stay in British India, so that he was not liable in respect of his income outside British India. The High Court (the Chief Justice and Patanjali Sastri J.) reversed the Tribunal: the Tribunal had misdirected itself, and on the facts proved the control and management of the family's affairs could not be held to have been wholly situated outside British India, so that the family was resident. The karta appealed.
  • The provision. Section 4-A(b) of the Indian Income-tax Act, 1922: "A Hindu undivided family, firm or other association of persons is resident in British India unless the control and management of its affairs is situated wholly without British India." This is, word for word, the text of s. 6(2) of the 1961 Act today.
2 · Issues
  1. The question referred was: "Whether in the circumstances of the case, the assessee (a Hindu undivided family) is 'resident' in British India under Section 4-A(b) of the Income Tax Act?"
  2. Which resolves into: was the control and management of the affairs of the family situated wholly without British India during the previous year, so as to displace the statutory presumption of residence?
  3. Subsidiarily: what does "control and management" mean; whose control is relevant (the karta's residence, or the family's affairs); what are the "affairs" of which the section speaks; and on whom does the burden of proof lie?
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (appellant)
  • The head and karta of the family lives permanently in Colombo; the family is domiciled in Ceylon; its only business is carried on there. The seat of direction is therefore Ceylon.
  • The visits to India were for litigation and tax proceedings — acts of attending to affairs, not of managing them. "Mere activity by the company in a place does not create residence" (Swedish Central Railway, principle (2)); the same applies to a family.
  • The house at Kanadukathan, where his mother lives, cannot constitute that place the seat of control and management of the family's affairs.
  • Starting two partnership businesses in India is again "activity"; it does not convert a Ceylon-resident family into an Indian-resident family.
  • The High Court took "rather a narrow view" of s. 4-A(b) in assuming that attending to some affairs during a visit brought the assessee within the rule.
For the Revenue
  • The presumption is residence: an HUF is resident unless control and management is situated wholly without British India. The onus of bringing the case within that exception lies squarely on the assessee.
  • The family undoubtedly has affairs in India — immovable property, shares in two firms, litigation, tax proceedings, and two partnership businesses started in February 1942. The question is not whether the family has Indian affairs but where those affairs are controlled.
  • The assessee produced no correspondence to show that instructions were issued from Colombo for the management of the Indian affairs; the material that could have established a single Ceylon centre was withheld.
  • Those Indian affairs were being looked after by an unauthorised clerk, from which it follows that whenever the karta came to India he was looking after them himself and exercising control by issuing instructions — the reasoning of the Appellate Assistant Commissioner, which the Supreme Court approved.
4 · Decision

The appeal was dismissed with costs: the family was resident in British India and liable to be assessed on its foreign income. Fazl Ali J. reached that conclusion in six steps.

  1. The English analogy accepted. Section 4-A(b) is based on the rule applied in England to corporations, stated by Lord Loreburn in De Beers Consolidated Mines v. Howe: "A company cannot eat or sleep, but it can keep house and do business. We ought, therefore, to see where it really keeps house and does business… the real business is carried on where the central management and control actually abides." "It is clear that what is said in Section 4-A(b) … is what Lord Loreburn intended to convey by the words 'where the central management and control actually abides'."
  2. The four principles of Swedish Central Railway v. Thompson adopted: (1) residence of an artificial person is determined by analogy — "where is the head and seat and directing power of the affairs"; (2) "mere activity" in a place does not create residence, so an entity may reside in one place and do a great deal of business in another; (3) central management and control may be divided, and there may be more than one residence; (4) in case of dual residence, it must be shown that the entity performs "some of the vital organic functions incidental to its existence" in both places, so that there are two centres of management.
  3. The definition of the key words — adopting the passage of Patanjali Sastri J.: "'Control and management' signifies, in the present context, the controlling and directive power, 'the head and brain' as it is sometimes called, and 'situated' implies the functioning of such power at a particular place with some degree of permanence, while 'wholly' would seem to recognize the possibility of the seat of such power being divided between two distinct and separated places." And: "As a general rule, the control and management of a business remains in the hand of a person or a group of persons, and the question to be asked is wherefrom the person or group of persons controls or directs the business."
  4. The four propositions of construction which the Court then applies: (i) normally an HUF will be taken to be resident, and the presumption does not apply only if the case is brought within the second part; (ii) "the word 'affairs' must mean affairs which are relevant for the purpose of the Income Tax Act and which have some relation to income"; (iii) in order to bring the case under the exception, ask whether the seat of direction and control of the affairs is inside or outside India; and (iv) the word "wholly" shows that an HUF may have more than one residence, as a corporation may.
  5. Application to the facts. The mere fact that the assessee has a house at Kanadukathan, where his mother lives, cannot constitute that place the seat of control; nor does the 101-day stay carry much weight — he was interested in the litigation and the tax proceedings, but "by merely coming out to India to take part in them, he cannot be said to have shifted the seat of management and control of the affairs of his family, or to have started a second centre for such control and management"; the same applies to the two partnership businesses, because "mere 'activity' cannot be the test of residence". The High Court's approach was "rather a narrow view"; the more correct approach was that of the Appellate Assistant Commissioner, whose finding was that it had not been established that the control and management of the Indian affairs was only in Colombo.
  6. Onus and result. "There can be no doubt that the onus of proving facts which would bring his case within the exception, which is provided by the latter part of Section 4-A(b), was on the assessee." The correspondence and other evidence which might have shown that the Indian affairs were normally controlled from Colombo were not produced; in their absence, the finding that the onus had not been discharged, and that "the normal presumption must be given effect to", was a legitimate conclusion. The appeal was therefore dismissed — but the Court added that, as the case had to be decided mainly on onus, "the decision in this appeal must be confined to the year of assessment to which this case relates", and it would be open to the appellant to show in future years, by proper evidence, that the seat of control and management was wholly outside British India.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Residence of an HUF is a presumption. The family is resident unless the assessee proves that control and management is situated wholly outside India. The burden is his (now s. 6(2)).
  2. Control and management = the head and brain: the controlling and directive power, not the day-to-day carrying on of business, and not the mere doing of acts in a place.
  3. "Affairs" are tax-relevant affairs. The section is concerned with the affairs that have some relation to income, not with every activity of the family (its charitable work, its social life or its litigation).
  4. "Wholly" permits a division. An HUF (like a company) may have two centres of control — but the assessee must prove that a second centre exists, and must show "vital organic functions" performed at each place.
  5. Mere activity is not residence. Attending litigation, appearing in assessment proceedings, obtaining an extension of time to pay tax, and even starting new businesses in India do not, by themselves, shift the seat of control.
  6. The residence of the karta is not the residence of the family. The section tests the location of the control and management of the family's affairs; a karta may live abroad and the family still be resident, or live in India and the family still be non-resident, depending on where its affairs are directed from. (Contrast s. 6(6)(b), where the manager's personal history is expressly made the test — but only for RNOR status, not for residence.)
  7. Residence is determined year by year. The Court expressly confined its decision to the assessment year before it, leaving the assessee free to prove a different position in later years on better evidence.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the text of s. 6(2), and the leading authority on it. December 2024's Question 8(b) — "Residential Status of a HUF under the Income Tax Act, 1961" — cannot be answered without it (§10.2).
  • It supplies the vocabulary of the whole subject: "head and brain", "controlling and directive power", "degree of permanence", "vital organic functions". Those phrases recur in every later case, including Narottam and Parekh (§7) and the discussion of holding companies in Vodafone (§8).
  • It is the ancestor of the POEM test. The "head and brain" idea, developed for HUFs and companies in these years, became the statutory "place of effective management" test in s. 6(3) from AY 2017-18 outside the reader.
  • It is a lesson in the burden of proof. The assessee lost not because the facts were necessarily against him but because he did not produce the evidence — the correspondence with Colombo — that would have proved his case. In an examination, this is the difference between "the family was resident because the karta lived in Ceylon" (wrong) and "the family was held resident because the assessee failed to discharge the onus" (right).

Mapping: decided under s. 4-A(b) of the 1922 Act; the same words now appear in s. 6(2) of the 1961 Act, and the reasoning applies unchanged to HUFs, firms, associations of persons and bodies of individuals.

07Case 12 · reader pp. 58–62Narottam and Parekh Ltd. v. C.I.T. — doing business is not control and management

Why this case matters

It is the Indian ancestor of the doctrine of place of effective management. Its central proposition — that a company resides where its head and brain functions, which is where its board directs the business, and not where the business is actually carried on — is the proposition now embodied in s. 6(3) and applied every day to foreign companies with Indian operations. It also supplies the topic's sharpest contrast with Vodafone: here the Court asked where the company controls its business; there the Court asked where a share is situated. Both are questions about "the seat of power", answered by reference to different facts.

Narottam and Parekh Ltd. v. Commissioner of Income-tax, Bombay City

AIR 1954 Bom 67 · Chagla C.J. · reference under s. 66(1), 1922 Act · AYs 1944-45 and 1945-46 · DU reader pp. 58–62
Case 12s. 4A(c), 1922 ActCompanyCentral controlDe facto v. de jureNow POEM, s. 6(3)
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a company and a subsidiary of the Scindia Steam Navigation Co. Ltd. Its business was stevedoring in Ceylon.
  • It was registered in Bombay and its registered office was in Bombay. The meetings of its Board of Directors were held in Bombay, as were the meetings of its shareholders.
  • The assessment years were 1944-45 and 1945-46. Its income in India was ₹3,791, while its total world income was ₹3,28,108 — "the bulk of which was earned in Ceylon by the business which it did". The second limb of the definition (Indian income exceeding foreign income) was therefore not in issue; the question was solely whether the company's control and management was situated wholly in the taxable territories.
  • Two managers acting under two powers of attorney looked after all the affairs of the company in Ceylon, and the powers conferred on them were, as the Court accepted, "the widest possible power and authority".
  • But the minutes of the Board of Directors — which were before the Court — showed that the central management and control had been kept in Bombay and exercised by the directors in Bombay. The minutes dealt with matters delegated to the two managers, and the directors, "from a proper sense of responsibility to the company", had retained complete control and had from time to time given directions to the managers as to how things should be done, and the correspondence between the Bombay office and the Colombo office confirmed the position.
  • The provision. Section 4A(c) of the 1922 Act: a company is resident in the taxable territories if (i) the control and management of its affairs is situated wholly in the taxable territories, or (ii) its income earned in the taxable territories exceeds its income outside — the second limb being immaterial here.
2 · Issues
  1. Whether the assessee company was a resident company within the meaning of s. 4A(c) of the 1922 Act — i.e. whether the control and management of its affairs was situated wholly in the taxable territories.
  2. What is meant by "control and management" — is it the carrying on of the business day to day, or the central direction of it; and is it de facto or de jure control that the section requires?
  3. Whether the conferment of the widest powers of attorney on managers who conduct the whole business at a place outside India makes that place the seat of control and management.
  4. Whether the fact that the entire business is done and the entire income earned outside India is relevant at all.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (Mr Kolah)
  • The whole business is done in Ceylon and the whole income is earned there. A company must reside where its business is carried on.
  • The two managers hold the widest possible powers of attorney; whatever may be the legal or juridical control, the actual (de facto) management is exercised in Ceylon. The section is concerned with actual, not theoretical, control.
  • A company which appoints agents with large powers to run its business at a place must be treated as managing its affairs at that place; the de jure right of the board to control is not the test.
  • Reliance was placed on the English line of authority, including De Beers and Swedish Central Railway, and on Indian cases such as Bhimji Naik and Talipatigala Estate, to show that the question is one of fact — where the control was actually exercised.
For the Revenue (Mr Setalvad)
  • The board of directors sits in Bombay, the shareholders meet in Bombay, and the minutes show that the directors kept control and issued directions from Bombay.
  • The managers are employees of the company, acting under revocable powers of attorney; they must carry out orders from Bombay and explain what they have done. The seat of power is therefore Bombay.
  • "Control and management" means central control and management, not the day-to-day conduct of business by servants, employees or agents.
  • The fact that the business and the income are abroad is irrelevant to the section: what matters is from where the business is controlled.
4 · Decision

The question was answered in the affirmative — the company was resident. Chagla C.J.'s reasoning runs in five movements:

  1. The structure of the section. Section 4A deals with residence as a single idea — of individuals, HUFs, firms and other associations of persons, and companies — and sub-clause (c) tells us what residence means for a company. "In order that a company's income should be subjected to tax as a resident, it has got to be established that the control and management of its affairs is situated wholly in the taxable territories."
  2. The asymmetry between the two formulae — the decisive point. "In this connection, it is pertinent to look at the converse definition of a Hindu undivided family, firm or other association of persons. In their case they are resident unless the control and management of its affairs is situated wholly without the taxable territories. Therefore, whereas in the case of a Hindu undivided family or firm or association of persons any measure of control and management within the taxable territories would make them resident, in the case of a company any measure of control and management of its affairs outside the taxable territories would make it non-resident."
  3. What "control and management" means. "In construing the expression 'control and management' it is necessary to bear in mind the distinction between doing of business and the control and management of business. Business and the whole of it may be done outside India and yet the control and management of that business may be wholly within India." And further: "The control and management contemplated by this sub-section is not the carrying on of day to day business by servants, employees or agents. The real test to be applied is, where is the controlling and directing power, or rather, where does the controlling and directing power function … there is always a seat of power or the head and brain, and what has got to be ascertained is, where is this seat of power, or the head and brain?" The passage then explains that it is not the servants and agents but "that authority to which the servants, employees and agents are subject, … the central authority", at whose place of functioning the company resides.
  4. De facto, not de jure — but the distinction does not help this assessee. Accepting Mr Kolah's submission that what matters is "not the 'de jure' control and management but the 'de facto' control and management", the Court held that on the minutes and correspondence there was both: the directors had retained complete control and had exercised it from Bombay. The two managers carried on the business; they did not control it. "Their power-of-attorney can be cancelled at any moment, they must carry out any orders given to them from Bombay, they must submit to Bombay an explanation of what they have been doing, and throughout the time that they are working in Ceylon a vigilant eye is kept over their work from the directors' board room in Bombay."
  5. The earlier authorities distinguished, and "affairs" confined. Bhimji Naik (the right to control vested in a partner resident in India) and Talipatigala Estate (control actually exercised from India through an agent holding a power of attorney) were followed for the proposition that the test is one of fact. Subbayya Chettiar was followed for two propositions: the De Beers/Swedish Central Railway principles, and the ruling that "the word 'affairs' must mean affairs which are relevant for the purpose of the Income-tax Act and which have some relation to income". Applying that, the Court confined itself to "the stevedoring business in Ceylon which has yielded the income", and still found that the head and brain of the company in relation to that business was in Bombay. Finally, the Court noted that a company may, like an individual, have more than one residence, and that a company "may have a dozen local branches at different places outside India … and yet it may retain the central management and control in Bombay".
5 · Principle evolved
  1. A company resides where its central management and control — its head and brain — functions, and that is where its board of directors directs the business.
  2. Doing business is not controlling it. The place where the operations are carried out, the income earned, the employees engaged and the powers of attorney exercised is not, by itself, the place of residence. Conversely, "business and the whole of it may be done outside India and yet the control and management of that business may be wholly within India".
  3. The test is de facto control, but de facto and de jure control will usually coincide where the directors in fact decide. A revocable power of attorney, exercised by an employee who reports back, does not transfer the seat of power.
  4. Asymmetry of the statutory formulae. For an HUF or firm, any measure of control in India makes it resident; for a company (under the 1922 Act) any measure of control outside India made it non-resident. The word "wholly" therefore produces opposite results in the two provisions, and the case turns on that.
  5. Dual residence is possible for a company as for an individual, but it must be shown by evidence that vital organic functions are performed at both places.
  6. "Affairs" are income-related affairs (following Subbayya Chettiar), so the enquiry is directed to the business that produced the income in question.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the source of the "place of effective management" test. Section 6(3) replaced the "control and management wholly in India" formula with POEM from AY 2017-18, and POEM is nothing but the "head and brain" of this case applied to a modern group. outside the reader
  • It is the answer to the commonest company-residence question in practice and in examinations: a foreign company with substantial Indian operations is not resident merely because it does business here; it is resident if the decisions are taken here.
  • It is the link to Vodafone. The 1922 Act asked where the company's control is; the 1961 Act asks where the income arises. Narottam shows that a parent's residence does not automatically make the subsidiary resident (a subsidiary is not a puppet), and Vodafone takes the same idea into the corporate group: "the legal position of any company incorporated abroad is that its powers, functions and responsibilities are governed by the law of its incorporation" (§8.5).
  • It illustrates the burden and the evidence. The case was won on the minutes of the board meetings and the correspondence. In a residence dispute, the documents are the case.

Mapping: decided under s. 4A(c) of the 1922 Act. Section 6(3) of the 1961 Act now provides that a company is resident if it is an Indian company, or if its place of effective management in that year is in India; the second limb carries this case's reasoning forward.

08Case 13 · reader pp. 63–83Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India — the reach of s. 9(1)(i)

Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India

(2012) 6 SCC 613 · Kapadia C.J.I. and K.S. Radhakrishnan J. · civil appeal by special leave from the Bombay High Court · DU reader pp. 63–83
Case 13s. 9(1)(i)Situs of sharesNo look-throughs. 195US$ 11.08 bn

This is the only Indian judgment that decides, in one place, how far the charge in this topic reaches: whether the transfer of a share in a foreign company can be treated as the transfer of the Indian business beneath it; what "situate in India" means for a share; whether a group of companies can be looked through because they are a group; whether the absence of an express anti-abuse rule permits a court to invent one; and what happens to the withholding obligation (s. 195) when the underlying income is not chargeable. It is also the cleanest demonstration of the difference between s. 6 (who is taxed) and s. 9 (on what), which is why the reader places it at the end of the topic rather than in the chapter on capital gains.

Structure of this section: §8.1 the transaction · §8.2 the questions and the proceedings · §8.3 the Revenue's arguments · §8.4 the assessee's arguments · §8.5 the Court on precedent · §8.6 the core holding · §8.7 s. 195 and the order · §8.8 the principle, and what Parliament did next.

◆8.1 The transaction

Facts
  • Vodafone International Holdings B.V. ("VIH"), a company incorporated in the Netherlands, acquired from Hutchison Telecommunications International Ltd. the entire share capital of CGP Investments (Holdings) Ltd. ("CGP"), a company incorporated in the Cayman Islands, under a Share Purchase Agreement dated 11 February 2007; completion took place on 8 May 2007. The consideration was US$ 11.08 billion.
  • CGP indirectly held 52% of the shareholding of Hutchison Essar Ltd. ("HEL"), an Indian company carrying on the telecom business in India, together with options to acquire a further 15% — so that, as the Revenue put it, the transaction gave VIH a 67% controlling interest in the Indian telecom business.
  • No part of the price was allocated in the agreement between the share of CGP and "the other rights" (including the options, the shareholder agreements, the non-compete covenants and the brand rights). That absence of apportionment became a central plank of the Revenue's case on valuation and on "transfer of a controlling interest".
  • VIH, through the Vodafone group's Indian subsidiaries, held participative investment in India: the group had paid taxes of about ₹20,242 crore in India and infused equity of about ₹18,000 crore; the Indian company had paid dividends of ₹3–250 crore per annum between 2002-03 and 2006-07, rising to ₹394–962 crore per annum thereafter (para 12). These facts were used to answer the "sham" argument.
  • CGP had been incorporated in 1998, long before the transaction, and had a genuine commercial role in the group's structure; the structure had existed since 1994 (paras 11–16).
  • On 31 May 2010 the Revenue issued a show-cause notice to VIH demanding capital gains tax on the transaction, together with interest, and treating VIH as an assessee in default for failure to withhold tax under s. 195. VIH filed a writ petition in the Bombay High Court.
Fig. 5 — The transaction. The whole case is the vertical distance between the top box and the bottom box: whether a sale of the share at the top can be taxed as a sale of the business at the bottom.

◆8.2 The questions, and the proceedings below

Issues
  1. Whether the sale of the entire share capital of CGP, a Cayman company, to VIH attracted capital gains tax in India under s. 9(1)(i) read with s. 5(2)(b) — i.e. whether there was a transfer of a capital asset situate in India.
  2. Whether the situs of the shares of a foreign company is in India because the company's underlying assets and business are in India, and whether s. 9(1)(i), which uses the words "directly or indirectly", permits the Court to look through the foreign companies to the Indian business.
  3. Whether the transaction involved the transfer of the telecom licences and other Indian assets — the "controlling interest" argument — and whether, the price not having been apportioned, the whole consideration could be treated as consideration for the Indian assets.
  4. Whether VIH was liable to deduct tax at source under s. 195 and was therefore an assessee in default under s. 201.
  5. Whether the Bombay High Court was right in declining to interfere at the notice stage and in requiring a deposit of ₹2,500 crore and a bank guarantee of ₹8,500 crore as a condition of interim relief.

The proceedings below. The Bombay High Court, by its judgment dated 8 September 2010 (Writ Petition No. 1325 of 2010), declined to interfere and prima facie accepted the Revenue's position that the transaction was taxable, at the same time requiring VIH to deposit ₹2,500 crore and to furnish a bank guarantee of ₹8,500 crore within three weeks. VIH appealed by special leave. The Supreme Court set aside the High Court's judgment and held that the transaction was not chargeable to tax in India; the deposit of ₹2,500 crore was directed to be returned with interest at 4% per annum and the bank guarantee was discharged and directed to be released within four weeks (para 39).

◆8.3 The Revenue's arguments

Arguments
For the Revenue
  • The transaction was in substance the acquisition of a 67% controlling interest in HEL, an Indian company. The substance over form doctrine, and the Supreme Court's decisions in McDowell and Azadi Bachao Andolan, required the Court to look at what was really bought.
  • Section 9(1)(i) uses the words "directly or indirectly". Income arising indirectly through an Indian source is therefore deemed to accrue in India, even where the immediate transfer is of a foreign share. The legislature's use of "indirectly" shows that the look-through is authorised.
  • The situs of the shares is not merely the place of incorporation. Shares in substance represent the assets and business of the company; the "underlying asset" being in India, the shares are situate in India for the purposes of s. 9(1)(i).
  • The absence of apportionment of the price between the share and the other rights showed that the parties themselves regarded the transaction as one composite deal whose real object was the Indian business; the whole of the consideration could be brought to tax.
  • The structure was a device: the "controlling interest" was an independent capital asset capable of being transferred, and the tax was attracted because what passed was the control over an Indian company, not merely a piece of paper in the Cayman Islands.
  • Since the consideration was chargeable, s. 195 obliged VIH to withhold; VIH was therefore an assessee in default, and the High Court was right to require security.

◆8.4 The assessee's arguments

Arguments
For VIH
  • The only asset transferred was a share in a Cayman company. A share is a bundle of rights against the company; it is not the company's assets. The situs of a share is the place of incorporation and where the register of members is kept — here, the Cayman Islands (para 16).
  • Section 9(1)(i) requires three elements — transfer, capital asset, and situs in India — and all three must exist. Here the capital asset was a foreign share and the transfer took place outside India; the third element failed, and the income could not be deemed to accrue in India (para 10).
  • The words "directly or indirectly" qualify the income, not the transfer of the asset. They describe the connection between the income and the Indian source; they do not convert a foreign asset into an Indian one.
  • Section 9 is a legal fiction and a fiction is construed strictly and cannot be expanded by "purposive interpretation" so as to transform the concept of chargeability. Reading in a look-through, an "underlying asset" test, or a "limitation of benefits" rule is legislation, not interpretation: such provisions exist in other jurisdictions and in the OECD Model, and their deliberate absence here is a matter of policy for Parliament (para 10).
  • Substance over form is not a licence to tax; McDowell itself is confined to colourable devices and subterfuges. The structure had existed since 1994, CGP was incorporated in 1998 with a genuine commercial role, and the group had made a participative investment in India of very large dimensions. This was not a sham (paras 11–16).
  • The valuation argument cannot create a charge: the difference between 52% and 67% is a matter of accounting standards and the treatment of the options; and "control" is not a separate capital asset — it is an incident of share ownership, part of the "congeries of rights and liabilities" that a share is (IRC v. Crossman, paras 17–19).
  • The transaction was one single consolidated bargain; it cannot be dissected into a sale of shares here and a sale of controlling interest there, and the absence of apportionment in the agreement is neutral, not incriminating (para 19).
  • If the transfer is not chargeable, s. 195 does not apply: there is no obligation to withhold tax on a sum which is not chargeable to tax at all. No default, and no liability under s. 201 (para 20).

The "puppet" point. The Court dealt separately with the argument that the Vodafone group had acquired control of an Indian company and that the group's structure should therefore be ignored. It held that the influence exercised by a parent over its subsidiary's shareholders does not make the subsidiary resident in the parent's jurisdiction unless it can be shown that the subsidiary's directors are "puppets" of the parent — that the parent dictates every decision. That is a high threshold, and it is a proposition of general application in this topic: the residence and the identity of a company are not altered by the identity of its shareholders (paras 5–6). Holding structures and special purpose vehicles are legitimate commercial forms; the onus of establishing abuse lies on the Revenue at the threshold, and it cannot be discharged by pointing to the existence of the structure alone.

◆8.6 The core holding — a fiction is not a policy

The heart of the judgment is in para 10, and it is best learned as a series of steps rather than as a sentence:

  1. The section is exhaustive of the charge. Section 9(1)(i) lists its categories; "the income dealt with in each sub-clause is distinct and independent of the other, and the requirements to bring income within each sub-clause are separately noted."
  2. The last category has three elements. "The said sub-clause consists of three elements, namely transfer, existence of a capital asset, and situation of such asset in India. All three elements should exist in order to make the last sub-clause applicable."
  3. The words "directly or indirectly" attach to the income. They describe the manner in which the income is derived from an Indian source (through a business connection, property, asset or source). They do not describe the manner in which the asset is transferred. Hence the phrase "indirect transfer" is not in the section and cannot be supplied by construction.
  4. "Underlying asset" is not a statutory concept for this purpose. A share is a bundle of rights against the company (IRC v. Crossman — a "congeries of rights and liabilities"); the assets of the company belong to the company, not to the shareholder. To treat the Indian business as the asset transferred requires a look-through which the words do not permit.
  5. Situs of shares. The situs of shares is the place of incorporation of the company and the place where its register of members is kept. CGP was a Cayman company with its register in the Cayman Islands; the shares were therefore not situate in India (para 16).
  6. Control is not a separate capital asset. "Control" is an incident of share ownership. The transfer of shares does not become the transfer of a distinct "controlling interest" asset. The transaction was one single consolidated bargain, and the absence of apportionment of the price does not convert part of the price into consideration for Indian assets (paras 17–19).
  7. Valuation cannot found a charge. The 52% / 67% difference is explicable by accounting standards and the treatment of the options; a dispute about how much an asset is worth cannot make it an asset situate in India.
  8. Conclusion. The capital asset transferred was situate outside India; no income could be deemed to accrue or arise in India; and the transaction was outside s. 9(1)(i). Consequently there was no liability to deduct tax under s. 195 and no default under s. 201.

◆8.7 Section 195, and the order of the Court

Two opinions, one result

On s. 195 the Court's reasoning is a corollary of the main holding, and it is worth stating as a rule of practice: the obligation to deduct tax at source arises only in respect of a sum which is chargeable to tax in India. Where the transfer of a foreign company's shares is outside s. 9(1)(i), the consideration is not income chargeable under the Act; the payer therefore has no obligation to deduct, and cannot be treated as an assessee in default under s. 201. closely related provisions — s. 195(2) and the 2012 amendments to ss. 195 and 201 — are outside the reader

◆8.8 The principle evolved — and what Parliament did next

Principle evolved
  1. Section 9(1)(i) is not a look-through provision. It taxes the transfer of a capital asset situate in India; it does not tax the transfer of a foreign company's shares because the company's business is in India. The words "directly or indirectly" qualify the income and not the transfer.
  2. The three-element test is cumulative. Transfer, capital asset and situs in India must all exist in the previous year under consideration; if any one fails, no charge arises.
  3. A legal fiction is construed strictly. It must be applied to the purpose for which it was created and cannot be extended by "purposive interpretation", still less so as to "transform the concept of chargeability".
  4. Substance over form has a statutory, not a judicial, door. Westminster survives; McDowell is confined to colourable devices and subterfuges; Ramsay's "look at" test assists construction but does not license the court to supply a missing provision; Azadi Bachao Andolan is reaffirmed; abus de droit is not part of Indian law.
  5. A share is a bundle of rights, and its situs is where the company is incorporated and its register of members is kept. "Control" is an incident of ownership, not a separate capital asset; a share sale is not an asset sale; and a composite transaction is a single consolidated bargain.
  6. Group structures are legitimate. Parental influence does not make a subsidiary resident in the parent's country unless the subsidiary's directors are "puppets". The onus of proving abuse lies on the Revenue, and it must be discharged at the threshold, not after a roving enquiry.
  7. Withholding follows chargeability. No charge on the income means no obligation under s. 195 and no default under s. 201.
Significance — and the legislative sequel
  • It is the ceiling on s. 9. Read with Azadi Bachao, it settles the architecture of the topic: residence under s. 6 fixes who is taxed; source under s. 5 and s. 9 fixes what; and where the statutory words run out, the gap can be closed only by treaty (s. 90), by specific anti-avoidance provisions, or by Parliament.
  • Parliament's answer was retrospective legislation. By the Finance Act, 2012, Explanations 4 and 5 were inserted into s. 9(1)(i) with effect from 1 April 1962, deeming the shares of a foreign company deriving substantial value from Indian assets to be a capital asset situate in India — precisely the fiction the Court had refused to infer. outside the reader
  • And then the answer was withdrawn. The Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 withdrew the retrospective levy for transfers completed before 28 May 2012, subject to conditions (withdrawal of pending claims and no set-off of the demand), and carved out small non-controlling holdings of non-residents from the forward-looking provision. outside the reader — see §4.6.
  • The case is therefore doubly instructive. It shows (i) what "situate in India" means for a share, and (ii) that a tax which the Court holds to be outside the statute can still be imposed by Parliament — but only by legislation, and the cost of doing so retrospectively was a decade of litigation and an amendment reversing it.
  • Its practical afterlife. The framework survives in the treaty network and in the general anti-avoidance provisions: as s. 9 read with s. 90(2) still permits an assessee to choose the more beneficial provision, and as the question of "situs" continues to govern the taxability of offshore share transfers, the reasoning of Vodafone on the construction of a fiction remains the starting point of every such dispute. ◆9.2 The shared method — four propositions from all three cases

  1. Residence and source are different questions and must not be mixed. Cases 11 and 12 decide status; case 13 decides charge. An examiner's favourite trick is to ask whether Vodafone makes VIH resident in India. It does not: acquiring the shares of a foreign company gives a person no residence, and acquiring the shares of an Indian company does not make the shareholder resident either. Residence depends on s. 6 alone. A subsidiary is not resident where its parent is resident unless the parent controls it as a "puppet" (§8.5).
  2. The test is substance, but substance is proved by evidence, not by assertion. In Subbayya Chettiar the assessee lost on documents he did not produce; in Narottam the Revenue won on board minutes and correspondence; in Vodafone the assessee won on the share purchase agreement and the corporate record. In every case the outcome turned on what the paper showed.
  3. The location of power decides. "Head and brain", "seat of power", "central management and control", "place of effective management" — these are successive names for one idea, and Vodafone's "situs" is the same idea applied to an asset rather than to a person.
  4. Strict construction cuts both ways. Section 6(2) is construed so that the assessee must prove he is outside it (a presumption of residence); s. 9(1)(i) is construed so that the Revenue must bring the income within its words (a fiction, strictly read). Both are applications of the same rule — that the burden lies on the party asserting the exception — which is why a good answer on this topic always mentions onus.

◆10.2 Model answer — Q8(b): Residential status of a HUF

1. The two questions to be answered. The residential status of a HUF is not a single question but two. Under s. 6(2) one asks whether the family is resident at all; under s. 6(6)(b) one asks, if it is resident, whether it is resident and ordinarily resident or resident but not ordinarily resident. The first is answered by the location of the control and management of the family's affairs; the second by the personal history of its manager.

2. Residence — s. 6(2). "A Hindu undivided family, firm or other association of persons is said to be resident in India in any previous year in every case except where during that year the control and management of its affairs is situated wholly outside India." Three features of the text must be noticed: (i) it is a presumption of residence — the family is resident unless the negative is proved; (ii) the negative requires that control and management be wholly outside India, so that any part of it in India keeps the family resident; and (iii) the test is not where the family does business but where its affairs are controlled.

3. What "control and management" means — V.V.R.N.M. Subbayya Chettiar v. CIT, AIR 1951 SC 101. The Supreme Court, dealing with the identical words in s. 4-A(b) of the 1922 Act, adopted Patanjali Sastri J.'s formula: "'Control and management' signifies … the controlling and directive power, 'the head and brain' as it is sometimes called, and 'situated' implies the functioning of such power at a particular place with some degree of permanence, while 'wholly' would seem to recognize the possibility of the seat of such power being divided between two distinct and separated places." The Court laid down four propositions: the presumption of residence operates unless the exception is made out; the word "affairs" means affairs relevant for the purposes of the Income-tax Act and having some relation to income; the question is where the seat of direction and control of those affairs is situated; and "wholly" shows that an HUF may have more than one residence.

4. The facts and the holding. The karta lived in Ceylon with his family, was domiciled there and carried on business in Colombo; the family owned a house, immovable property and investments in British India and shares in two Indian firms. He visited India on seven occasions for a total of 101 days, attending litigation, appearing in income-tax proceedings and obtaining an extension of time to pay tax, and started two partnership businesses in India. The Court held the family resident. Mere "activity" is not the test of residence; coming to India to attend to litigation and tax proceedings does not shift the seat of management or establish a second centre; and the onus of proving that control and management was wholly outside India lay on the assessee, who had failed to produce the correspondence with the Colombo business that might have discharged it. The Court added that, as the case turned largely on onus, the decision was confined to the year of assessment before it, leaving the assessee free to prove a different position in later years.

5. Not ordinarily resident — s. 6(6)(b). A resident HUF is RNOR if its manager (i) has been a non-resident in India in nine out of the ten previous years preceding the previous year, or (ii) has, during the seven previous years preceding that year, been in India for a period or periods amounting in all to 729 days or less. The family is ROR only if both limbs are satisfied in the opposite sense — resident in 9 out of 10 and present for 730 days or more in 7 years. Note the deliberate asymmetry: the family's control decides whether it is resident, but the manager's personal presence decides whether it is ROR.

6. Consequences. An ROR HUF is taxed on its worldwide income under s. 5(1)(a)–(c). An RNOR HUF is taxed on Indian income and on foreign income derived from a business controlled in India or a profession set up in India; other foreign income is outside its total income by virtue of the proviso to s. 5(1). A non-resident HUF is taxed only under s. 5(2).

7. Illustration. A family's affairs are directed from Kanpur, where the karta lives and where the family business is managed; a branch in Singapore is supervised by the karta through managers. The family is resident under s. 6(2): control and management is not wholly outside India, and the onus of showing that it is would be on the family. If the karta was resident in India in 7 of the last 10 years and present for 600 days in the last 7, the family is RNOR under s. 6(6)(b) — it has failed both limbs. Its income from the Singapore branch is nonetheless taxable, because it comes from a business controlled in India; income from its Singapore investments is not.

8. Conclusion. The HUF provisions are a compromise between the desire to tax a family entity by reference to the place of its real direction and the practical impossibility of proving where a family "thinks". The presumption of residence and the onus on the assessee are the statutory devices that make the test workable; Subbayya Chettiar is the authority for both, and it remains good law because the words of s. 6(2) are the words of s. 4-A(b). The test is, however, formal: an HUF whose karta is a non-resident abroad may still be resident because its affairs are directed from India, and vice versa — a result which explains why the modern statute has moved towards "place of effective management" for companies, though not, so far, for families. the POEM comparison is outside the reader

  15. Section 9(1)(i) has four or five sources; the last requires transfer + capital asset + situs in India, all in the previous year.
  16. "Directly or indirectly" qualifies the income, not the transfer (Vodafone, para 10).
  17. Situs of shares = place of incorporation and of the register of members (Vodafone, para 16).
  18. Control is not a separate capital asset; a share is a congeries of rights; a composite deal is a single consolidated bargain.
  19. No charge, no withholding — s. 195 is not attracted where the income is not chargeable; no default under s. 201.
  20. Section 90(2) lets the assessee choose the treaty where it is more beneficial; and a limitation of benefits clause cannot be read into a treaty by the court.
