The 60-second orientation

Topic 1 answers five questions that the whole Act depends on:

  1. What is being levied? — a tax (not a fee, not a colourable cess), under a constitutional entry, by authority of law (Art. 265).
  2. On what is it levied? — "income", a word of the widest amplitude, inclusively defined in s. 2(24) (Karthikeyan).
  3. On whom? — the "assessee" (s. 2(7)), on his real income, arrived at after excluding income diverted by an overriding title (Sitaldas, Kinariwala).
  4. Receipt or capital? — if the receipt is on capital account it falls outside the charging sections altogether (Salami line of cases).
  5. For which year, and when does it become taxable? — previous year (s. 3), assessment year (s. 2(9)), and the receipt / accrual / arisal trichotomy in ss. 4–5 read with s. 9.

01Syllabus item 1Concept of Tax, Cess and Surcharge

Taxation is the most pervasive exercise of sovereign power that a citizen meets in ordinary life, and it is the one power the Constitution is most anxious to discipline. The syllabus begins here because every later question — is this a tax or a fee? is this a cess or a surcharge? is this income? — is decided by reference to the nature of the levy and to Article 265.

1.1Tax — definition, essentials and juristic character

Working definition (use this in the exam)

A tax is a compulsory exaction of money by a public authority for public purposes, imposed by authority of law (Art. 265), without any quid pro quo between the payer and the State, and not imposed as a penalty for an offence. It is a compulsory contribution, levied on persons or property, to defray the general expenses of government, and the taxpayer receives no direct and measurable benefit in return.

The five essentials

  1. Compulsion — levied under the coercive power of the State; non-payment attracts recovery, interest and penalty. Consent is irrelevant.
  2. Public authority — imposed by Parliament / State Legislature (or a body to which the power is validly delegated).
  3. Public purpose — the proceeds go toward general public expenditure (a road, defence, administration), not a special service to the payer.
  4. No quid pro quo — the payer gets no specific, equivalent return. This is the single most decisive feature for distinguishing tax from fee.
  5. By authority of law — Art. 265: "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law."

Constitutional location

  • Art. 265 — the "golden rule": no tax without law.
  • Art. 245 + Seventh Schedule — competence: List I (Union), List II (State), List III (Concurrent — practically no taxing entries), Art. 248 + Entry 97 (residuary).
  • Art. 246A — simultaneous power of Union and States over GST (101st Amendment).
  • Art. 266 — all tax proceeds are credited to the Consolidated Fund; withdrawal needs appropriation.
  • Art. 270 / 271 — distribution between Union and States; surcharges excluded from sharing.
  • Art. 276 — profession tax capped at ₹2,500 p.a.
  • Art. 285 / 289 — inter-governmental immunities.
The authoritative descriptions — quote these
  • Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt, AIR 1954 SC 282 (7-Judge Bench): a tax is "a compulsory exaction of money by public authority for public purposes enforceable by law and is not payment for services rendered". This is the classical definition; the Court stressed that the element of compulsion — not the mode of assessment — is the distinguishing mark of a tax.
  • Mathews v. Dandu Payandi / Upper Canada line and the Indian adoption in Shirur Mutt — the "four elements" of a tax: (i) compulsory; (ii) by a public authority; (iii) for public purpose; (iv) not a penalty for an offence.
  • Union of India v. Sanyal and Kunnathat Thathunni Moopil Nair v. State of Kerala, AIR 1961 SC 552 — a tax that vests in the legislature arbitrary power, or is confiscatory, offends Art. 14 and is void even if formally authorised by law.

1.3Article 265 — "No tax shall be levied or collected except by authority of law"

Article 265 is short, but it contains three separate disciplines, each of which is a distinct ground of challenge:

1. "Levied"There must be a charge: a charging section fixing liability on a defined class
→
2. "Collected"Even collection requires authority — no retention of an unconstitutional levy
→
3. "Authority of law"Valid statute by a competent legislature under Arts. 245–246, Seventh Schedule

What Article 265 does not mean

  • It does not require reasonableness of rates — that is left to legislative policy (but see Moopil Nair where the levy was confiscatory).
  • It does not bar retrospective taxation — a law may operate retrospectively if the legislature is competent and the language of the statute supports it (Rai Ramkrishna v. State of Bihar, AIR 1963 SC 1667; the Vodafone–Cairn episode).
  • It does not bar delegation of ancillary matters (rates, assessment machinery) to the executive, so long as the legislature lays down the policy.

Consequence of breach

  • The levy is void — not merely irregular — and the amount collected is refundable (subject to statutory limitation and unjust-enrichment rules).
  • Unconstitutionality is a jurisdictional defect; the assessment need not be challenged within the assessment hierarchy.
  • "Collection without levy" is equally hit: cf. State of Kerala v. P.J. Joseph on executive levies.

1.4Cess — what it is, and why the label matters

Definition

A cess is a special-purpose levy, usually imposed as a percentage of an existing tax (hence "a tax on a tax"), the proceeds of which are earmarked for a specific object — education, health, sanitation, infrastructure, compensation to States. Its distinguishing feature is therefore not quid pro quo with the payer (there is none) but earmarking with the object.

Why the Centre prefers cess and surcharge — the federal-revenue point

Taxes under Article 270 are shared with the States on the recommendation of the Finance Commission (Art. 280). Cesses and surcharges are, by the express words of the Constitution, excluded from the divisible pool (Art. 270(1) proviso; Art. 271). Hence a rupee raised as Health & Education Cess reaches the Union in full, whereas a rupee of income-tax does not. This is a first-rate point for a long answer on federal finance, and it is why the Finance Commission has repeatedly flagged the "cess and surcharge" issue. Note also that the States cannot levy a surcharge on a Union tax, and that a State cess must still be traceable to a State entry.

1.5Surcharge — Article 271 and the Income-tax Act

Constitutional dimension

  • Article 271: "Notwithstanding anything in Articles 269 and 270, Parliament may at any time increase any of the duties or taxes referred to in those articles by a surcharge for purposes of the Union…" — and such surcharge is excluded from distribution to the States.
  • It is a levy on a levy: it takes the same base as the tax and adds a percentage to it.
  • Because it does not enter the divisible pool, it has become the Union's principal fiscal-space instrument — a standard critique of Indian fiscal federalism.

Statutory dimension (Income-tax Act, 1961)

  • Charged by the annual Finance Act (not by the Income-tax Act itself), usually as a percentage of income-tax on high-income bands.
  • Illustrative bands: 10% where total income exceeds ₹50 lakh; 15% above ₹1 crore; 25% above ₹2 crore; 37% above ₹5 crore — the 37% band is available only under the old (optional) regime; the default regime under s. 115BAC caps the surcharge at 25%.
  • Marginal relief ensures that the surcharge does not exceed the income above the threshold.
  • The Health & Education Cess (4%) is computed on (income-tax + surcharge) — the clearest everyday illustration of a cess.
  • Surcharge is not deductible in computing income; it is a charge on tax.
Three exam traps on levies
  1. Do not call every "cess" a fee. A cess has purpose-nexus; a fee has service-nexus. Dewan Chand Builders is the authority.
  2. Surcharge ≠ additional tax. Surcharge is levied by the Finance Act for Union purposes; "additional income-tax" (e.g. s. 115-O DDT, s. 115QA buy-back tax) is a separate charge with a different incidence.
  3. A valid tax may still be unconstitutional — Art. 265 is about authority; Arts. 14, 19(1)(g) and 300A are about content. Cite Moopil Nair.

02Syllabus item 2Types of Taxes — Direct and Indirect

The classification into direct and indirect taxes is not a matter of constitutional competence (both are "taxes" under Art. 265) but of incidence — of who ultimately bears the burden. That single idea generates all the practical differences: progressivity, inflation, collection cost, evasion, and the presence or absence of a shift.

The distinction in one line each

Direct tax: the burden is borne by the person on whom it is levied — the incidence and the impact fall on the same person; it cannot be shifted. Illustration: income-tax, corporation tax, capital gains tax, wealth-type levies. Indirect tax: the burden is shifted — the person who pays it to the Government (the manufacturer, seller, service provider) recovers it from another (the consumer); impact and incidence fall on different persons. Illustration: GST, customs duty, erstwhile excise and service tax, VAT/CST.

Fig. 2 — Impact versus incidence. Examiners expect the words "impact" (first burden) and "incidence" (final burden) to be used precisely.

Indian and comparative context — one paragraph you can lift

India's tax-to-GDP ratio relies relatively more on indirect taxes than the OECD average. The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 replaced a fragmented indirect-tax regime (excise on manufacture, service tax on services, VAT/CST on sales, entry tax on movement) with a destination-based dual GST: CGST + SGST on intra-State supply, IGST on inter-State supply (Arts. 246A, 269A, 279A). On the direct side, the shift has been toward base-widening with lower rates — the corporate rate cut of 2019 (s. 115BAA) and the default new personal regime under s. 115BAC (2019/2020, revamped in 2023 and 2025) — and toward administration-intensive anti-avoidance (GAAR, Chapter X-A; general anti-abuse rules for treaties, s. 90(2A); equalisation levy).

Short-note command word

"Distinguish between direct and indirect taxes" (5–10 marks) → give the definition, the shifting test, a 6-row table, 2 examples each, one merit and one demerit of each, and close with the sentence: "the distinction turns on incidence, not on the identity of the collecting government".

03Syllabus item 3 — the heart of the topicDefinition of "Income" — Section 2(24)

"Income" is the subject-matter of the charge. The Act does not define it exhaustively — it includes. The strategic significance of that drafting choice, and the judicial refusal to read the included clauses as exhaustive, is precisely what the Supreme Court decided in CIT v. G.R. Karthikeyan, the first prescribed case in the DU reader.

3.1Why an inclusive definition, and what it does

Statutory text (opening words)

Section 2(24), Income-tax Act, 1961 (as amended):

"'income' includes — (i) profits and gains; (ii) dividend; …"

The word is "includes", not "means". Parliament has been adding sub-clauses from time to time (Finance Act, 1972 added clause (ix); 1988, 1999, 2002, 2003, 2012, 2015, 2017 and 2018 added others).

What "includes" achieves

  • It widens the net; it does not limit the meaning.
  • A receipt that fits no sub-clause may still be income if it partakes of the nature of income (Karthikeyan, para 10).
  • Conversely, a receipt placed in a sub-clause is not thereby protected from other tests (e.g. exemption, capital-receipt character, real-income doctrine).
  • Its ambit is co-extensive with Entry 82, List I of the Seventh Schedule (taxes on income other than agricultural income) — Karthikeyan, para 8, applying Navinchandra Mafatlal v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 58.
The five definitional authorities you must know before Karthikeyan
  • Kamakshya Narain Singh v. CIT, (1943) 11 ITR 513 (PC) — "income … is a word difficult and perhaps impossible to define in any precise general formula. It is a word of the broadest connotation."
  • Gopal Saran Narain Singh v. CIT, (1935) 3 ITR 237 (PC) — "anything that can properly be described as income is taxable under the Act unless expressly exempted."
  • Navinchandra Mafatlal v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 58 — 'income' means "a thing that comes in"; its natural meaning "embraces any profit or gain which is actually received"; it includes capital gains, and the legislative entries must receive the most liberal construction.
  • Bhagwan Dass Jain v. Union of India, (1981) 2 SCC 135 — income is of the widest amplitude; it includes not merely what is received by exploiting property but "that which can be converted into income" (upholding the taxation of notional income from self-occupied property under s. 23(2)).
  • State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala, AIR 1957 SC 699 and State of A.P. v. K. Satyanarayana, AIR 1968 SC 825 — not all crossword puzzles, and not all card games, are gambling; games of skill are distinct. (Used in Karthikeyan to show that clause (ix) is not confined to gambling.)

3.2The general features of "income" Class notes

Your lecture notes set out the concept of income as a tree with three branches — general meaning and features, the statutory definition in s. 2(24), and the computation (the five heads of income under s. 13 of the 2025 Act ≡ s. 14 of the 1961 Act, leading to gross total income under s. 80B(5)). The "general rules" in the notes are the three propositions on which almost every problem is built:

Rule 1 — No double taxation

The same income cannot be taxed twice in the same hands for the same year. This is why there are specific provisions preventing overlap between heads and between persons (ss. 60–64 on clubbing; s. 10(2A) exempting a partner's share).

Rule 2 — Income may be positive or negative

A loss is negative income: it is computed under the same heads and set off (ss. 70–80) subject to restrictions (speculation, capital losses, house-property loss cap of ₹2 lakh).

Rule 3 — The source need not exist in the tax year

Income may be received in a year in which the source has ceased — salary arrears, dividend declared earlier, interest on a repaid deposit. Taxability follows the receipt or accrual of income, not the life of the source.

What is treated as income

FeatureExplanation & illustration
Cash or kindIncome need not be money. Perquisites, free accommodation, motor-car facility, ESOPs, gifts of property — all income (valued under the Rules).
Permanent or temporaryA recurring receipt is income; so is a single, non-recurring one (a prize, a one-time commission). Duration affects head and rate, not the character of income.
Accrual or receipt basisIncome is taxed when it accrues (the right to receive arises) even if not yet received, or when it is received even if it accrued earlier — see §7.
Legal or illegalIllegality is irrelevant to taxability. CIT v. S.C. Kothari, (1971) 82 ITR 794 (SC) (forward transactions in violation of a statutory ban); CIT v. Piara Singh, (1980) 124 ITR 40 (SC) (smuggling profits are business income; confiscation is a business loss). The principle is that the tax law is not a penal statute and does not refuse to tax what it forbids.
Disputed title / receipt by one on behalf of anotherIncome is taxed in the hands of the person who actually receives it, even if the title is in dispute; the dispute is resolved inter se later. This is why the "conduit" reasoning of Sitaldas matters: the collector for another is not the earner.
Real incomeOnly income that has really resulted can be taxed (see §7.2, Shoorji Vallabhdas).
Tax-free incomeAn income which is exempt in the recipient's hands (e.g. agricultural income, s. 10(1)) may still be income for the payer (deduction claims) and for rate purposes (partial integration).

3.4Prescribed Case 1 — CIT v. G.R. Karthikeyan

Commissioner of Income-tax v. G.R. Karthikeyan

1993 Supp (3) SCC 222 · Supreme Court of India · B.P. Jeevan Reddy, J. · Assessment year 1974-75
DU Reader p. 1s. 2(24)(ix)s. 10(3)Income — widest amplitudePrize / rally
Facts
  • The assessee, G.R. Karthikeyan, an individual assessed to salary and business income, participated in the All India Highway Motor Rally during the accounting year relevant to AY 1974-75.
  • The rally was organised jointly by the Automobile Association of Eastern India and the Indian Oil Corporation, supported by regional automobile associations, the Federation of Indian Motor Sports Clubs and the Federation of Indian Automobile Associations. It was restricted to private motor cars; the route was approximately 6,956 km; a competitor could start from Delhi, Calcutta, Madras or Bombay, proceed anti-clockwise and return to the starting point.
  • It was "designed to test endurance driving and the reliability of the automobiles", to be driven observing traffic regulations and the Rally Committee's regulations. Prizes were awarded on overall classification computed by a system of penalty points for violations; the competitor with the least penalty points won the first prize.
  • The assessee won the first prize: ₹20,000 from the Indian Oil Corporation and ₹2,000 from the All India Highway Motor Rally — a total of ₹22,000.
  • The ITO included the ₹22,000 in income, relying on the definition of "income" in s. 2(24). The AAC held that the rally was not a race and therefore the amount was not income under s. 2(24)(ix). The Tribunal dismissed the Revenue's appeal, recording: (a) the rally was not a race, but predominantly a test of skill and endurance and of the reliability of the vehicle; (b) it was not a "game" within s. 2(24)(ix); (c) the receipt was casual in nature, but was nevertheless not an income receipt and fell outside s. 10(3).
  • The Madras High Court decided in favour of the assessee, reasoning that the expression "winnings" at the inception of clause (ix) has acquired the connotation of money won by gambling or betting and controls the other expressions in the sub-clause; that the memorandum to the Finance Bill, 1972 showed the object was to rope in windfalls from lotteries, races and card games; and that s. 74A (which restricts set-off of losses from the sources listed in clause (ix)) supported that reading.

The question referred under s. 256(1): "Whether, on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the Appellate Tribunal was right in holding that the total sum of Rs 22,000 received by the assessee from the Indian Oil Corporation and All India Highway Motor Rally should not be brought to tax?"

Issues
  1. Does a prize won in a motor rally — a contest of skill, endurance and vehicle reliability, and not a race or game of gambling — constitute "income" within the meaning of s. 2(24)?
  2. Can a receipt which does not fall within any of the sub-clauses of s. 2(24) still be taxed as income?
  3. Does the fact that the receipt is casual in nature take it outside the charge, having regard to s. 10(3)?
Arguments
Revenue (appellant)
  • The definition of income in s. 2(24) is inclusive; clause (ix) covers winnings from "races … card games and other games of any sort or from gambling or betting of any form or nature whatsoever" — words of the widest amplitude.
  • The rally was a contest, at the least; the assessee entered to win and won; what he received was a "return" for his skill and endurance.
  • The sub-clause is not confined to gambling: crossword puzzles and card games may be pure games of skill (R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala; K. Satyanarayana).
  • Even a casual receipt is income; s. 10(3) exempts casual receipts only up to a limit, which presupposes that such receipts are income.
Assessee (respondent)
  • The word "winnings" controls the whole sub-clause and has "acquired a connotation of its own", meaning money won by gambling or betting.
  • A motor rally is not a race, and not a "game"; it is a test of driving skill, endurance and mechanical reliability.
  • The Finance Bill, 1972 memorandum shows the legislative intent was to catch windfalls from lotteries, races and card games — not prizes for skill.
  • Section 74A (losses from the enumerated sources set off only inter se) confirms that clause (ix) is a self-contained gambling-related category.
  • The receipt being casual and non-recurring, it fell outside s. 10(3) — as the Tribunal had found — and therefore was not income at all.
Decision — Supreme Court (Revenue's appeal allowed)
  • The Supreme Court reversed the High Court and held that the ₹22,000 did constitute income under s. 2(24); the question referred was answered in the negative, in favour of the Revenue and against the assessee.
  • The Court began from the definitional authorities: income is "a word of the broadest connotation" (Kamakshya Narain Singh); "anything that can properly be described as income is taxable unless expressly exempted" (Gopal Saran Narain Singh); its natural meaning "embraces any profit or gain which is actually received" and includes capital gain (Navinchandra Mafatlal); and it includes notional income which can be converted into income (Bhagwan Dass Jain).
  • Because s. 2(24) is inclusive, its ambit is the same as that of "income" in Entry 82, List I.
  • The Court accepted, arguendo, that "winnings" may connote gambling — but asked: "does it follow that merely because winnings from gambling/betting activities are included within the ambit of income, the monies received from non-gambling and non-betting activities are not so included?" It answered: no. "If the monies which are not earned — in the true sense of the word — constitute income, why do monies earned by skill and toil not constitute income? Would it not look odd, if one is to say that monies received from games and races of gambling nature represent income but not those received from games and races of non-gambling nature?"
  • "The rally in question was a contest, if not a race. The respondent-assessee entered the contest to win it and to win the first prize. What he got was a 'return' for his skill and endurance. Then why is it not income — which expression must be construed in its widest sense?"
  • On casual receipts: "May be it is casual in nature but it is income nevertheless. That even the casual income is 'income' is evident from Section 10(3)", which exempts casual receipts up to ₹1,000 — a provision that presupposes taxability. The Court found it "difficult to follow the logic" of the Tribunal's finding that the receipt was casual yet not an income receipt.
Principle evolved / ratio decidendi
  1. The definition of income is inclusive, not exhaustive. The several clauses of s. 2(24) are not a code: "It would be a wrong approach to try to place a given receipt under one or the other sub-clauses … and if it does not fall under any of the sub-clauses, to say that it does not constitute income."
  2. The "widest amplitude" rule. "Income" must receive its natural and grammatical meaning; it is co-extensive with Entry 82, List I.
  3. Receipts for skill, endurance or contest are income. A prize that is a return for the application of skill and toil is a fortiori income, because even unearned windfalls are taxed.
  4. Casual receipts are income; the character of being casual affects only the exemption under s. 10(3), not the character of the receipt. (The exemption limit is now ₹5,000, and it does not apply to winnings from lotteries, card games, races, etc.)
  5. Interpretive technique: a sub-clause inserted to catch windfalls cannot be read as confining the general definition; a noscitur a sociis reading ("winnings" colouring all the words) was rejected because the parent definition is inclusive and independent of the sub-clause.
Significance, criticism and connections
  • Doctrinal significance: the case is the standard cite for the proposition that "income" is not limited to what is earned in a recognised business or employment, and that the absence of a specific clause is fatal neither to the charge nor to the assessee's argument (it cuts both ways).
  • Statutory development: after Karthikeyan, winnings of the clause (ix) type are taxed at a flat rate under s. 115BB without any deduction (s. 58(4)) and with restricted loss set-off (s. 74A); the casual-income exemption under s. 10(3) has been raised to ₹5,000.
  • The two-sided use: assessees cite it for "only real income is taxable"; the Revenue cites it for "labelling a receipt as casual/unspecified does not take it out of the charge".
  • Link to Topic 3 (residence and scope): once a receipt is "income", ss. 4–5 and s. 9 decide whether the particular assessee is chargeable — a point the syllabus expects you to carry forward.
  • Link to Topic 4 (heads): a prize of this kind, if not business income, is taxed under the residuary head "Income from other sources" (s. 56), because it is not salary, not property, not business, not capital gain.
Advanced: three criticisms and a counter-argument (for a 20-mark answer)

(1) Circularity. The Court says a receipt is income if it "partakes of the nature of income" — but the nature of income is precisely what is in dispute. The answer is that the Court is using a conceptual, not a statutory, test: does the receipt represent a gain that comes in, in the nature of a return for an activity or an accession to wealth? That is a workable, if open-textured, standard.

(2) Legislative intent. The High Court's use of the Finance Bill memorandum was orthodox (K.P. Varghese permits recourse to the memorandum for the object of a provision). The Supreme Court's response is that a memorandum explains why a clause was inserted; it cannot cut down the operation of an inclusive parent definition.

(3) Two-way street. If the definition catches every gain, why are gifts, inheritance and capital receipts outside it? Because the categories that are outside are outside for structural reasons — a capital receipt is not a "coming in" of income but a change in the form of the asset (see §5), and gifts/inheritance are excluded by the scheme of s. 56(2)(x). This is the distinction the Court leaves implicit and which you should make explicit.

04Syllabus item 4Application of Income v. Diversion of Income by Overriding Title

Two of the three prescribed cases in this topic — Sitaldas Tirathdas and Sunil J. Kinariwala — turn on a single question of fact with huge legal consequences: did the income ever become the assessee's income, or did it merely pass through his hands? If it never became his, he is a collector for another and is taxed on nothing. If it became his and was then paid away, he is taxed on the whole and gets no deduction for the payment.

The two concepts

Diversion of income by overriding title: a third person becomes entitled to receive the income, under an obligation of the assessee, before the assessee could lay a claim to receive it as his income. The income is diverted at source; the assessee is at most a conduit. Tax consequence: not included in the assessee's total income.

Application of income: income accrues to the assessee first, and is thereafter applied by him in discharging an obligation (legal, contractual, personal or moral). Tax consequence: fully included in the assessee's total income, with no deduction for the payment (unless a specific provision allows it, e.g. s. 24(b) interest, or s. 37(1) business expenditure).

The one-sentence test (from Kinariwala, para 8)

"The determinative factor … is the nature and effect of the assessee's obligation in regard to the amount in question. When a third person becomes entitled to receive the amount under an obligation of an assessee even before he could lay a claim to receive it as his income, there would be diversion of income by an overriding title; but when after receipt of the income by the assessee, the same is passed on to a third person in discharge of the obligation of the assessee, it will be a case of application of income."

4.1The two Privy Council poles around which the doctrine is built

Pole A — Bejoy Singh Dudhuria v. CIT, (1933) 1 ITR 135 (PC) → DIVERSION

The Raja's stepmother sued for maintenance; a compromise decree bound him to pay ₹1,100 p.m. and the Court declared the amount a charge upon his properties. He claimed a deduction; the Calcutta High Court refused; the Privy Council allowed it.

"When the Act by Section 3 subjects to charge 'all income' of an individual, it is what reaches the individual as income which it is intended to charge. … The decree of the court by charging the appellant's whole resources with a specific payment to his step-mother has to that extent diverted his income from him … to that extent what he receives for her is not his income. It is not a case of the application by the appellant of part of his income in a particular way, it is rather the allocation of a sum out of his revenue before it becomes income in his hands."Lord Macmillan, PC

Why it went the assessee's way: the charge operated on the source (the properties). The income could not reach him free of the charge; he collected for the stepmother pro tanto.

Pole B — P.C. Mullick v. CIT, (1938) 6 ITR 206 (PC) → APPLICATION

A testator directed his executors to pay ₹10,000 out of the income of the estate on the occasion of his addya sradh. The executors paid ₹5,537 and claimed a deduction. The Privy Council refused: the payments were made out of the income of the estate coming to the hands of the executors and in pursuance of an obligation imposed by the testator. It was "not a case in which a portion of the income had been diverted by an overriding title from the person who would have received it otherwise".

Why it went the Revenue's way: the obligation was imposed on the recipient of the income, not on the source. The income reached the executors and was then spent.

4.2Prescribed Case 2 — CIT v. Sitaldas Tirathdas

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Sitaldas Tirathdas

(1961) 2 SCR 634 · (1961) 41 ITR 367 · Supreme Court of India · Hidayatullah, J. (3-Judge Bench) · AYs 1953-54 & 1954-55
DU Reader p. 6Maintenance decreeNo charge on propertyOverriding title tests. 9(1)(iv) distinguished
Facts
  • The assessee, Sitaldas Tirathdas of Bombay, had many sources of income — property, stocks and shares, bank deposits, and a share in the firm Messrs Sitaldas Tirathdas. He followed the financial year as his accounting year.
  • For AYs 1953-54 and 1954-55 his total income was computed at ₹50,375 and ₹55,160 respectively. The computation was not disputed.
  • He claimed to deduct ₹1,350 (AY 1953-54) and ₹18,000 (AY 1954-55) on the ground that under a decree he was required to pay these sums as maintenance to his wife, Bai Deviben, and his children.
  • The suit (Suit No. 102 of 1951, Bombay High Court) was for maintenance allowance, separate residence and marriage expenses for the daughters, and arrears. A decree by consent was passed on 11 March 1953 awarding maintenance of ₹1,500 per month. For the year ended 31 March 1953 only one payment was made; deducting ₹150 p.m. as rent for the flat occupied by the wife and children, the amount paid came to ₹1,350; for the next year the maintenance at ₹1,500 p.m. came to ₹18,000.
  • No charge on the property was created by the decree, and therefore the matter did "not fall to be considered under Section 9(1)(iv)" (the then provision for interest on borrowed capital/deductions from property income). The assessee rested his claim on Bejoy Singh Dudhuria.
  • The ITO disallowed the claim; the AAC affirmed. The Tribunal observed: "This is a case, pure and simple, where an assessee is compelled to apply a portion of his income for the maintenance of persons whom he is under a personal and legal obligation to maintain. The Income Tax Act does not permit of any deduction from the total income in such circumstances." Counsel's formulation before the Tribunal was: "I claim a deduction of this amount from my total income because my real total income is whatever that is computed, which I do not dispute, less the maintenance amount paid under the decree."
  • The Bombay High Court allowed the assessee, following two earlier Bombay decisions — Seth Motilal Manekchand v. CIT, (1957) 31 ITR 735 and Prince Khanderao Gaekwar v. CIT, (1948) 16 ITR 294 — holding that the test was the existence of an obligation enforceable in a court of law, even without a specific charge on property; the income to the extent of the decree must be taken to have been diverted to the wife and children and never became the assessee's income.
Issues
  1. Whether maintenance payable under a consent decree, where no charge is created on the assessee's property, is a case of diversion of income by an overriding title (deductible) or of application of income (not deductible).
  2. Whether the enforceability of the obligation in a court of law is by itself the true test — as the Bombay High Court had held.
  3. What is the reconciling principle between Bejoy Singh Dudhuria (diversion) and P.C. Mullick (application)?
Arguments
Assessee / respondent
  • The payment is obligatory, decreed by a competent court, and enforceable; it is not a voluntary disposition, so Bejoy Singh Dudhuria applies on principle even without a formal charge.
  • The "real income" of the assessee is what remains after the decree — the decree fixes what "reaches" him.
  • The earlier Bombay decisions (Seth Motilal Manekchand — a managing-agency commission split under a partition deed; Prince Khanderao Gaekwar — a family trust/charge in favour of a mother) establish that enforceability is the test.
  • Supporting line: Diwan Kishen Kishore v. CIT, (1933) 1 ITR 143 (Lahore) (impartible estate; allowance to junior member held a charge diverting income); CIT v. D.R. Naik, (1939) 7 ITR 362 (maintenance to widows, where there was a charge); Hira Lal, In re, (1945) 13 ITR 512 (arbitration awards made a rule of court, made a charge on the property).
Department / appellant
  • What the decree creates is a money obligation — a personal obligation of the husband/father, owed because of the Hindu-law duty to maintain — not a charge on the income-yielding asset.
  • The income accrues and reaches the assessee; the payment is made out of it. That is the P.C. Mullick situation.
  • Supporting line: P.C. Mullick (1938) 6 ITR 206 (PC); P.C. Mullick & D.C. Aich, In re, (1940) 8 ITR 236 (payments to be made "out of the income of my property" — assessable in the executors' hands); V.M. Raghavalu Naidu & Sons v. CIT, (1950) 18 ITR 787 (maintenance allowances to mother and widow of the testator — no deduction; a personal obligation of the testator would not have been deductible in his own hands, and the executor is no better); CIT v. Makanji Lalji, (1937) 5 ITR 539 (maintenance to a widow of a coparcener, even though decreed and charged — the Court doubted this case).
  • Enforceability alone proves nothing: every obligation may be enforced; if enforceability were the test, every debt would reduce income.
Decision — Supreme Court (Revenue's appeal allowed; High Court's view set aside)
  • The Court reviewed the entire line of authority — Bejoy Singh Dudhuria, P.C. Mullick, Diwan Kishen Kishore, Makanji Lalji, D.R. Naik, P.C. Mullick & D.C. Aich, Hira Lal, Prince Khanderao Gaekwar, Raghavalu Naidu and Seth Motilal Manekchand — and observed that they had "diversely applied" the principle, "some of them … correctly and some, not".
  • It then laid down the governing test in the passage that has been quoted in every later case (see below), and applied it: "the present case is one in which the wife and children of the assessee who continued to be members of the family received a portion of the income of the assessee, after the assessee had received the income as his own. The case is one of application of a portion of the income to discharge an obligation and not a case in which by an overriding charge the assessee became only a collector of another's income."
  • "The matter in the present case would have been different if such an overriding charge had existed either upon the property or upon its income, which is not the case."
  • The case therefore fell "outside the rule in Bejoy Singh Dudhuria" and "within the rule stated by the Judicial Committee in P.C. Mullick".
  • Order: the answer given by the High Court was discharged and the question answered in the negative — the assessee was not entitled to the deduction; appeal allowed with costs here and below.
Principle evolved — the classic formulation
"In our opinion, the true test is whether the amount sought to be deducted, in truth, never reached the assessee as his income. Obligations, no doubt, there are in every case, but it is the nature of the obligation which is the decisive fact. There is a difference between an amount which a person is obliged to apply out of his income and an amount which by the nature of the obligation cannot be said to be a part of the income of the assessee. Where by the obligation income is diverted before it reaches the assessee, it is deductible; but where the income is required to be applied to discharge an obligation after such income reaches the assessee, the same consequence, in law, does not follow. It is the first kind of payment which can truly be excused and not the second. The second payment is merely an obligation to pay another a portion of one's own income, which has been received and is since applied. The first is a case in which the income never reaches the assessee, who even if he were to collect it, does so, not as part of his income, but for and on behalf of the person to whom it is payable."Hidayatullah, J., CIT v. Sitaldas Tirathdas (1961) 41 ITR 367, 375
  1. The "never reached" test — a question of fact about the flow of income, not about the enforceability of the obligation.
  2. The "nature of the obligation" is decisive: an obligation on the source (a charge) diverts; an obligation on the recipient applies.
  3. The "collector" (conduit) metaphor — where diversion occurs, the assessee collects "for and on behalf of the person to whom it is payable", and is not taxed.
  4. A personal obligation to maintain — even when decreed — is an application of income. The absence of a charge is what defeated the assessee.
Significance & later application
  • Sitaldas is the test for the entire family of "diversion" problems: maintenance decrees, partnership assignments, trust settlements, statutory levies on a source, and the s. 24(b) charge cases.
  • It is applied and explained at length in Kinariwala (next case) and in Moti Lal Chhadami Lal Jain v. CIT, (1991) 190 ITR 1 (a company required to pay ₹10,000 of the ₹21,000 annual rent directly to a college, with a first charge in the college's favour — still held to be a mere mode of application of the landlord's income; the stipulation that the college could sue and the creation of a charge made no difference).
  • Note the practical corollary for maintenance: under s. 24/other provisions there is no general deduction for maintenance paid. Relief comes only through s. 64(1)(iv)-type clubbing analysis, through the charge route (if income from property is charged, the charge-holder's right may be enforced under the property head), or through a statutory provision such as s. 80C/80D for specified payments — never through the diversion doctrine absent a charge.
  • Relationship with s. 9(1)(iv) [now s. 24(b)]: the old s. 9(1)(iv) allowed interest payable on capital borrowed "where the property is charged or mortgaged" — the Court expressly noted that its inapplicability here (no charge) is why the assessee had to fall back on the diversion principle.

  • The assessee was a partner in the Ahmedabad firm Messrs Kinariwala R.J.K. Industries, holding a 10% share.
  • On 27 December 1973 he created a trust — the "Sunil Jivanlal Kinariwala Trust" — by a deed of settlement, assigning 50% out of his 10% right, title and interest (excluding capital) as a partner, together with ₹5,000 out of his capital in the firm, in favour of the Trust.
  • The Trust had three beneficiaries: the assessee's brother's wife, his niece and his mother.
  • In AY 1974-75 he claimed that, as 50% of the income attributable to his share from the firm stood transferred to the Trust, there was a diversion of income at source and the amount (₹20,141) could not be included in his total income.
  • The ITO rejected the claim — it was a case of application of income, not diversion at source; he also held that s. 60 was attracted, since only income (not the asset) had been settled.
  • The AAC allowed the appeal and directed exclusion of ₹20,141. The Tribunal, on the Revenue's appeal, reversed the AAC.
  • The Gujarat High Court, relying on CIT v. Bagyalakshmi & Co., (1965) 55 ITR 660 and Murlidhar Himatsingka v. CIT, (1966) 62 ITR 323, held that on assignment of 50% of his share the income became the Trust's income by overriding title and could not be added to the assessee's total income — the questions were answered in the assessee's favour.
Issues (as reframed by the Supreme Court on 3 December 2002)
  1. Whether the assignment of 50% out of the assessee's 10% share in right, title and interest (excluding capital) in the firm in favour of the Trust under the deed of 27-12-1973 creates an overriding title in favour of the Trust, and whether the income accruing to the Trust can be treated as the income of the assessee.
  2. Whether ₹20,141, being the profits referable to 50% of his 10% share, is not the real income of the assessee but of the Trust and is therefore assessable only in the hands of the Trust.
Arguments
Revenue (appellant) — Mr Preetesh Kapur
  • Having regard to the terms of the settlement, what was assigned was merely the right to receive profits to the extent of 50% of the assessee's share.
  • There was therefore no overriding title in the Trust so as to divert the income at source; the High Court erred in treating the assignment as diversion.
  • Section 60 was urged as an alternative contention (transfer of income without transfer of the asset) but was "not seriously pursued".
Assessee (respondent) — Mr U.U. Lalit
  • Under s. 29(1) of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, the Trust became entitled to receive 50% of the assessee's share of income from the firm by assignment under the settlement deed.
  • The Trust therefore got the income "by virtue of the overriding title", and the High Court had correctly answered the questions.
  • Reliance on Bagyalakshmi & Co. and Murlidhar Himatsingka: an assignee/sub-partner has an enforceable right in the profits, which changes the character of the receipt before it reaches the partner.
Decision — Supreme Court (Revenue's appeal allowed; High Court reversed)
  • The Court restated the scheme: "under the scheme of the Act, it is the total income of an assessee, computed under the provisions of the Act, that is assessable to income tax. So much of the income which an assessee is not entitled to receive by virtue of an overriding title created in favour of a third party would get diverted at source and the same cannot be added in computing the total income of the assessee." It then framed the "determinative factor" test quoted above (§4).
  • Applying it, the Court held that the assessee had not shown that the Trust became entitled to the amount before he could lay a claim to it. An assignment of a share of income is not the same thing as an overriding charge on the source.
  • The decisive distinction — sub-partnership v. assignment: "there is a clear distinction between a case where a partner of a firm assigns his share in favour of a third person and a case where a partner constitutes a sub-partnership with his share in the main partnership. Whereas in the former case, in view of Section 29(1) of the Indian Partnership Act, the assignee gets no right or interest in the main partnership, except … to receive that part of the profits of the firm referable to the assignment and to the assets in the event of dissolution of the firm, in the latter case the sub-partnership acquires a special interest in the main partnership."
  • Murlidhar Himatsingka was distinguished: a sub-partnership "creates a superior title and results in diversion of the income from the main firm to the sub-partnership before the same becomes the income of the partner concerned … even if the partner receives the income from the main partnership, he does so not on his behalf but on behalf of the sub-partnership." The present case could not "be treated as one of a sub-partnership": the Trust, "as an assignee, becomes entitled to receive the assigned share in the profits from the firm not as a sub-partner because no sub-partnership came into existence but as an assignee of the share of income of the assigner-partner."
  • Bagyalakshmi & Co. was held irrelevant: it concerned the registration of a firm and the correctness of the shares stated in the deed; it did "not deal with the principle of diversion of income by overriding title". K.A. Ramachar (assignment of profits by a partner to his wife, married daughter and minor daughter) was a "pure case of assignment of profits" — an application, not a diversion.
  • Order: "the share of the income of the assessee assigned in favour of the Trust has to be included in the total income of the assessee. The questions are … answered in favour of the Revenue and against the assessee."
Principle evolved
  1. Assignment ≠ diversion. A mere assignment of the right to receive income does not create an overriding title; the assignor remains taxable on it (his obligation to pass it on is an application).
  2. Look for a superior title in the source, not merely a claim on the fruit. A sub-partnership, a charge, a trust operating on the asset, or a statutory diversion creates a superior title. A deed that assigns only the income while expressly excluding capital does not.
  3. Section 29(1) of the Partnership Act is a double-edged sword. It gives the assignee a right to receive profits, but "no right or interest in the main partnership" — which is precisely why the assignment cannot divert at source.
  4. The drafting of the deed matters. The words "excluding capital" in the settlement were fatal: they showed that what was transferred was an income stream, not an interest in the firm.
  5. s. 60 remains available as an alternative Revenue argument (transfer of income without transfer of the asset → taxed in the transferor's hands), though the Court decided the case on the diversion principle itself.
Significance & exam use
  • Kinariwala is the modern, authoritative limit on Sitaldas: it tells you that the diversion doctrine is narrow, and that the "overriding title" must exist in the source.
  • It is the standard authority for the proposition that an assignee of a partner's share is taxable directly on that share (cf. s. 29(1) of the Partnership Act) while the assignor remains taxable in his own hands — an apparent tension with the assessee-friendly reading of Murlidhar Himatsingka, resolved by the sub-partnership/assignment distinction.
  • It links directly to Topic 5 (clubbing, ss. 60–64): where a settlement of income without the asset is involved, s. 60 taxes the transferor regardless of the diversion analysis; and to Topic 4 (PGBP): a partner's share is exempt under s. 10(2A) only in the hands of the partner entitled to it.
  • Practical planning lesson: a family settlement/trust intended to shift tax must transfer the asset (or an interest in it) and must not be a bare assignment of income.

The five errors students make on this topic
  1. Confusing "obligation" with "diversion". Every taxpayer has obligations; Sitaldas says the nature of the obligation decides.
  2. Assuming a decree equals diversion. It does not: Sitaldas itself was a consent decree and the payment was held non-deductible.
  3. Assuming a charge always equals diversion. Moti Lal Chhadami shows a charge in favour of a college did not help the landlord, because the rent had already accrued to him.
  4. Forgetting s. 60. Even a genuine settlement of income (without the asset) is taxed in the transferor's hands under Chapter V.
  5. Not giving the Revenue's side. In problem questions, always argue both: diversion for the assessee, application for the Revenue, then decide. Marks are given for the structure.

05Syllabus item 5Capital Receipt v. Revenue Receipt — Tests, with special reference to Salami

This is the most examined distinction in the whole paper, because it decides three separate questions at once: (i) whether a receipt is chargeable at all; (ii) whether an expenditure is deductible under s. 37(1); and (iii) if chargeable, under which head and at what rate. The syllabus links it to the definition of income (§3) because s. 2(24)(vi) brings in only capital gains chargeable under s. 45 — not capital receipts.

The organising idea

A revenue receipt is a receipt in the nature of income — it comes in from the exploitation of an existing source: rent, interest, profit, salary, commission. It is a "fruit".

A capital receipt is a receipt which represents a change in the form of the taxpayer's capital — the price of parting with the source itself, or compensation for its destruction, sterilisation or loss. It is a "tree", not a fruit.

The classic metaphor (from CIR v. Shaw Wallace & Co., (1932) 59 IA 206): income is "the produce of the land, the fruit of the tree"; a capital receipt is what you get when you sell the tree.

The reader's own authority on the receipt side — CIT v. Maheshwari Devi Jute Mills Ltd., (1965) 57 ITR 36 (SC)

Sale of "loom hours" (an allotment of working hours under a working-time agreement) by one mill to another was held to yield a capital receipt: "though loom hours were an asset, they could not from their very nature be let out while retaining property in them and there could be no grant of temporary right to use them … this was really a case of sale of loom hours and not of exploitation of loom hours by permitting user while retaining ownership" (as summarised in Empire Jute, DU Reader p. 117).

5.2Salami — the syllabus's special reference

What is salami?

Salami (also called nazrana, premium, or pagdi in different regions) is a lump-sum consideration paid by an incoming tenant/lessee to the landlord for being let into possession — i.e. for the grant of the lease or tenancy right. It is a payment made before the tenancy is created, once and for all, and it is the price of a right in land, not a payment for the use of land. 2025: same concept Section 105, Transfer of Property Act, 1882 supplies the statutory distinction between "premium" and "rent".

The three salami authorities

Member for the Board of Agricultural Income-tax, Assam v. Smt. Sindhurani Chaudhurani

AIR 1957 SC 729 · (1957) SCR 1019 · Kapur, Bhagwati and Venkatarama Aiyar, JJ. · 24 April 1957
The leading salami caseIndicia of salamiCapital receipt
Facts
  • Zamindar assessees in Assam received payments described as salami from tenants as consideration for granting agricultural leases which were "by no means of a precarious nature".
  • The payments were made prior to the grants, were non-recurring, but were calculated at rates varying with the nature of the land and were chargeable on every subsequent eviction and re-letting.
  • The question was whether salami was "agricultural income" within s. 2(a)(1) of the Assam Agricultural Income-tax Act, 1939, which covered "any rent or revenue derived from land" used for agricultural purposes.
Issue

Is salami "rent or revenue derived from land" — and therefore agricultural income — or a capital receipt outside the definition?

Arguments
Revenue (Board of Agricultural Income-tax)
  • Salami is a payment derived from land, made on every re-letting, computed by reference to the quality of the land; it is in substance a mode of realising the land's income-producing capacity, and the frequency of grants shows it is part of the landlord's business of letting.
  • Decisions of the Calcutta and Assam High Courts had treated such receipts as agricultural income.
Assessees (Sindhurani Chaudhurani and others)
  • Salami is a single, non-recurring payment made before the tenancy comes into existence; it lacks periodicity and does not arise from agricultural operations.
  • It is the price paid for the acquisition of a right in land — a capital asset — and cannot be "rent or revenue". Volume or frequency cannot convert a capital receipt into revenue.
Decision
  • The Supreme Court held that the payments were properly described as salami and were neither rent nor revenue within the definition of agricultural income; they could not be assessed under the Act. The State's appeals were dismissed.
  • "The salami has been, rightly, in their Lordships' opinion, treated as a capital receipt. It is a single payment made for the acquisition of the right of the lessees to enjoy the benefits granted to them by the lease. That general right may properly be regarded as a capital asset, and the money paid to purchase it may properly be held to be a payment on capital account."
  • The Court identified the two indicia of salami: "(1) its single non-recurring character and (2) payment prior to the creation of the tenancy. It is the consideration paid by the tenant for being let into possession and can be neither rent nor revenue but is a capital receipt in the hands of the landlord."
Principle
  1. Salami is a capital receipt, not income.
  2. The two indicia are non-recurrence and payment before the tenancy is created.
  3. The character is decided by the nature of the right acquired, not by the arithmetic of the rate or the frequency of letting; "the assumption of salami being part of the 'business activity' of letting land does not transform it into revenue".
  4. Consequence: no agricultural-income exemption is needed — the receipt never enters the charge at all (and therefore escapes both the State agricultural income-tax and the Union income-tax, save for capital gains where a "transfer" is involved).

CIT v. Panbari Tea Co. Ltd., (1965) 57 ITR 422 (SC)

The assessee took two tea estates on a registered lease for 10 years and paid a large premium. The Supreme Court, relying on s. 105 of the Transfer of Property Act, held that the premium was a capital receipt in the lessor's hands and the rent a revenue receipt. The key passage used in every later case:

"When the interest of the lessor is parted with for a price, the price paid is premium or salami. But the periodical payments made for the continuous enjoyment of the benefits under the lease are in the nature of rent. The former is a capital income and the latter a revenue receipt."

Use it for: the statutory foundation of the salami doctrine, and the proposition that a premium paid in instalments remains capital.

Durga Das Khanna v. CIT, (1969) 72 ITR 796 (SC)

The assessee took premises on a 99-year lease; the Revenue treated the premium (salami) as advance rent and hence a revenue receipt. The Supreme Court held otherwise, laying down the burden of proof:

"If premium represents whole or part of the price of land it cannot be income. … Prima facie premium or salami is not income and it would be for the income-tax authorities to show that facts exist which would make it a revenue receipt."

Use it for: the presumption and the onus — a superb point to make in a problem answer where the Revenue alleges that salami is disguised rent.

Salami — the complete answer in six moves (10–20 marks)
  1. Define salami as the lump-sum premium paid by an incoming tenant to the landlord for the grant of the lease — the price of a right in land, not of its use.
  2. Statutory anchor: s. 105, TP Act — "premium" (price for transfer of the right to enjoy) v. "rent" (periodical payment for use).
  3. Indicia: single/non-recurring; paid before the tenancy is created (Sindhurani Chaudhurani).
  4. Character: capital receipt — not rent, not revenue, not agricultural income; hence outside the charging provisions.
  5. Burden: prima facie not income; the Revenue must prove that the "salami" is really advance rent or deferred rent (Durga Das Khanna; Panbari Tea).
  6. Consequence: no tax as income; capital gains only if the transaction amounts to a transfer of a capital asset (s. 2(47) r/w s. 45). Add one line on why the rule exists: otherwise a landlord could be taxed on the whole capital value of his land merely because he chose to let it rather than sell it.

5.3The expenditure side — s. 37(1) and the Atherton test

Section 37(1)

"Any expenditure (not being expenditure of the nature described in sections 30 to 36 and not being in the nature of capital expenditure or personal expenses of the assessee) laid out or expended wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the business or profession shall be allowed in computing the income chargeable under the head 'Profits and gains of business or profession'." Explanation 1 disallows expenditure incurred for any purpose which is an offence or which is prohibited by law.

So s. 37(1) contains three filters: (i) not covered by ss. 30–36; (ii) not capital, not personal; (iii) laid out wholly and exclusively for the business.

"When an expenditure is made, not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, there is very good reason for treating such expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital." Viscount Cave, L.C., British Insulated & Helsby Cables Ltd. v. Atherton, [1926] AC 205 — quoted in Empire Jute, DU Reader p. 118

5.4From the DU reader — CIT v. Empire Jute Co. Ltd.

Commissioner of Income-tax v. Empire Jute Co. Ltd.

AIR 1980 SC 1946 · (1980) 124 ITR 1 · Supreme Court of India · DU Reader pp. 118–120 (under Topic 4)
Purchase of loom hourss. 37(1)Enduring benefit test reconsideredFixed v. circulating capital
Facts & statutory setting (as recorded in the reader)
  • Jute mills, members of an association, entered a working time agreement restricting the number of hours per week for which each mill could work its looms (e.g. members with up to 220 looms could work up to 72 hours per week). The restriction was "voluntarily accepted by each mill with a view to adjusting the production to the demand in the world market" and "could not possibly be regarded as an asset of such mill" (para 7).
  • Clause 6(b) permitted a signatory to transfer, in part or wholly, its allotment of hours of work per week to another signatory, subject to committee approval and registration — commonly called "sale of loom hours". The transferor lost those hours; the transferee could use them in addition to its own.
  • The assessee purchased loom hours from four jute manufacturing concerns for ₹2,03,255 during the previous year 1 August 1958 to 31 July 1959 (AY 1960-61) and claimed the amount as a revenue deduction under s. 10(2)(xv) of the 1922 Act (the predecessor of s. 37(1)).
  • The ITO and the AAC disallowed; the Tribunal allowed the claim, holding the expenditure revenue in nature. The High Court, though inclined to agree with the Tribunal, felt compelled by CIT v. Maheshwari Devi Jute Mills Ltd., (1965) 57 ITR 36 (where the sale of loom hours was held a capital receipt) to decide in favour of the Revenue, and held the payment to be capital expenditure. The assessee appealed by special leave.
Issue

Whether the sum of ₹2,03,255 paid for the purchase of loom hours was capital or revenue expenditure; and, in particular, whether the fact that the receipt on sale of loom hours was capital in the seller's hands necessarily made the payment capital in the buyer's hands.

Arguments
Revenue
  • Symmetry: the sale of loom hours was held a capital receipt in Maheshwari Devi; therefore the purchase must be capital expenditure.
  • By buying loom hours the assessee acquired a right to produce more than it otherwise could — an addition to / augmentation of its profit-making structure, i.e. acquisition of a source of profit, which is capital.
  • The advantage was of an enduring nature, satisfying the Atherton test.
Assessee
  • The payment was made wholly and exclusively for the business and was part of the cost of performing the income-earning operations.
  • No new asset was created: "the income-earning machine remains what it was … the assessee is merely enabled to operate the profit-making structure for a longer number of hours".
  • The advantage was not enduring — it was limited to six months and the extra hours lapsed if not used within the week.
Decision — Supreme Court (assessee's appeal allowed)
  • The "double fallacy": "in the first place it is not a universally true proposition that what may be a capital receipt in the hands of the payee must necessarily be capital expenditure in relation to the payer"; and secondly, Maheshwari Devi proceeded on the premise that loom hours were an asset — a hypothesis which, examined on principle, the Court doubted, since the allotment was a restriction voluntarily accepted, not a right conferred.
  • No single test: "The decided cases have, from time to time, evolved various tests for distinguishing between capital and revenue expenditure but no test is paramount or conclusive. There is no all-embracing formula … no touchstone has been devised. Every case has to be decided on its own facts keeping in mind the broad picture of the whole operation."
  • Enduring benefit refined: "It is not every advantage of enduring nature acquired by an assessee that brings the case within the principle … What is material to consider is the nature of the advantage in a commercial sense and it is only where the advantage is in the capital field that the expenditure would be disallowable. If the advantage consists merely in facilitating the assessee's trading operations or enabling the management and conduct of the business to be carried on more efficiently or more profitably while leaving the fixed capital untouched, the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future."
  • Fixed v. circulating capital (Lord Haldane in John Smith & Son v. Moore) was considered but held not decisive: the payment was not an addition to fixed capital.
  • The quota analogy: "if payment has to be made for securing additional power every week, such payment would also be part of the cost of operating the profit-making structure … payment made for purchase of loom hours … would also be part of the cost of performing the income-earning operations and hence revenue in character."
  • Held: the expenditure was revenue expenditure and deductible.
Principle evolved
  1. No single conclusive test — the "enduring benefit" test is a guide, not a formula; Atherton must not be applied "blindly and mechanically".
  2. Enduring benefit must be in the capital field. An advantage which merely facilitates trading, or makes it more efficient/profitable, is revenue even if it lasts indefinitely.
  3. Asymmetry between payer and payee. A capital receipt in the seller's hands does not entail capital expenditure in the buyer's hands.
  4. Test of the profit-making apparatus: did the outlay add to or alter the apparatus (capital) or merely operate it (revenue)?
  5. Look at the whole operation and the commercial nature of the advantage.
Why this case is worth more than its marks
  • It supplies the vocabulary examiners look for on any capital-v-revenue problem: "profit-making apparatus", "capital field", "facilitating trading operations", "whole operation", "no touchstone".
  • It is the authority to use against a mechanical application of Atherton — which is exactly what a good answer on the Dec 2024 Q1 (PIPER) needs, in both directions.
  • It also connects to the reader's other Topic-4 cases on s. 37(1) — CIT v. Travancore Sugar & Chemicals Ltd., L.B. Sugar Factory & Oil Mills (P) Ltd., CIT v. Jalan Trading Co. (P) Ltd., Bikaner Gypsums Ltd. and General Insurance Corporation — which you will study under "Business Expenditure".

06Syllabus items 6–8"Assessee", Previous Year and Assessment Year

Before the charge can operate, the statute must identify who is chargeable and for which period. These three definitions look mechanical, but they carry several exam marks because they contain the exceptions that trip students up.

6.1"Assessee" — section 2(7) 2025: s. 2(11)

Statutory definition

"Assessee" means a person by whom any tax or any other sum of money is payable under this Act, and includes —

  1. every person in respect of whom any proceeding under this Act has been taken for the assessment of his income, or of the loss sustained by him, or of the refund due to him;
  2. every person in respect of whom any proceeding has been taken for the assessment of the income of any other person in respect of which he is assessable, or of the loss sustained by, or refund due to, such other person;
  3. every person who is deemed to be an assessee under any provision of this Act; and
  4. every person who is deemed to be an assessee in default under any provision of this Act.

Ordinary assessee

A person by whom tax or another sum (interest, penalty, fee) is payable: an individual, HUF, company, firm, AOP/BOI, local authority or artificial juridical person — the seven categories of "person" in s. 2(31) 2025: s. 2(77).

Deemed assessee

A person made assessable on another's income: the legal representative of a deceased assessee (s. 159), an executor/administrator, a guardian of a minor, an agent of a non-resident (s. 163), a representative assessee (ss. 160–161), a receiver. These are the representative assessees in the class notes.

Assessee in default

A person who has failed to perform a statutory duty — typically a deductor who has not deposited TDS (s. 201), or a person who has not paid tax demanded. He is treated as an assessee for recovery purposes, though no income is assessed on him.

Why this matters for Topic 1

The "assessee" concept links directly to the diversion discussion: it is the person whose income it is who is the assessee. Sitaldas and Kinariwala are, at bottom, disputes about whose income the receipt is; the "collector" is not an assessee in respect of what he collects for another. Similarly, under s. 2(7)(b) the person assessed on another's income is a deemed assessee — which is how a Trust or an executor is brought in.

6.2Previous Year — section 3 2025: replaced by "tax year", s. 3

Definition and the "previous year" rule

  • s. 3(1): "previous year" means the financial year immediately preceding the assessment year — i.e. 1 April to 31 March.
  • Proviso to s. 3(1): in the case of a business or profession newly set up, or a source of income newly coming into existence, the previous year is the period beginning with the date of setting up (or the date the source comes into existence) and ending with 31 March — i.e. the first previous year may be shorter than twelve months.
  • Uniform previous year: from AY 1989-90 onwards every assessee must adopt the financial year as the previous year, and one previous year must be used for all sources of income. The old option to adopt one's own accounting year (the pre-1989 s. 3(1)(b)–(f)) was abolished by the Direct Tax Laws (Amendment) Act, 1987 w.e.f. 1-4-1989; ss. 3(2) and 3(3) are merely transitional provisions for the changeover (Tenth Schedule).
  • Consequence: income of FY 2025-26 is assessed in AY 2026-27.

The exceptions — where the previous year of a person is not the preceding financial year

  • s. 172 — shipping business of a non-resident: assessment on the first arrival of the ship, on a presumptive 7.5% of freight earnings.
  • s. 174 — persons leaving India (or likely to leave) with no intention to return: assessment on income accrued up to the probable date of departure.
  • s. 174A — AOP/BOI formed for a single event or purpose: assessment on income up to the date of discontinuance/dissolution.
  • s. 175 — persons likely to transfer property to avoid tax: assessment on income up to the date of the notice.
  • s. 176 — discontinued business: assessment of the income of the period up to discontinuance.

6.3Assessment Year — section 2(9) 2025: abolished; merged into "tax year"

Definition

"Assessment year" means the period of twelve months commencing on the 1st day of April every year, which is the year in which the income of the previous year is assessed. (The phrase "previous year" is defined by reference to the assessment year, and vice versa — a deliberate circularity that works because s. 3 fixes the terminus a quo.)

Fig. 5 — Previous year v. assessment year. Under the 2025 Act the two are unified, which is why your class notes say "tax year (s. 3)".

The classic trick question

"Mr X's accounting year ends on 30 June. What is his previous year for AY 2026-27?" → 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026. An assessee may keep any books he likes, but the previous year under the Act is the financial year and it must be the same for all sources (post-1989 position). The only statutory relaxation is the proviso to s. 3(1), which shortens the first previous year of a newly set-up business or a newly created source; the separate exceptions in ss. 172, 174, 174A, 175 and 176 concern the year of assessment of particular persons, not the definition of previous year.

7.2The real income doctrine — accrual has limits

"No income, no tax" — the corrective to a mechanical accrual rule
  • CIT v. Shoorji Vallabhdas & Co., (1962) 46 ITR 144 (SC) — where income does not result at all, a mere book entry or a mere claim to receive it cannot be taxed: "Income-tax is a levy on income. No doubt, the Income-tax Act takes into account two points of time at which the liability to tax is attracted, viz. the accrual of the income or its receipt; but the substance of the matter is the income; if the income does not result at all, there cannot be a tax."
  • Godhra Electricity Co. Ltd. v. CIT, (1997) 225 ITR 746 (SC) — a dispute over enhanced electricity charges; the claim was not real income because it was in litigation and uncertain. Accrual must be real, not hypothetical.
  • Dhakeshwari Cotton Mills Ltd. v. CIT, (1954) 26 ITR 775 (SC) — the assessee's own accounts are not conclusive; the question is whether the right to receive is real and settled.
  • State Bank of Travancore v. CIT, (1986) 158 ITR 102 (SC) — the counterweight: a mere provision for bad debts or a hypothetical liability does not reduce income; the real-income doctrine cannot be used to defeat a statutory scheme.
How to use the doctrine in an answer

Real income is the assessee's best friend and the examiner's favourite test of depth. Use it (i) where the amount has been waived or reduced before receipt; (ii) where the payment is under litigation and no settled right exists; (iii) where income is diverted before it reaches the assessee (Sitaldas is a real-income case in disguise); and (iv) where the entry is fictitious. But concede that a statutory accrual (e.g. under s. 45) is not displaced merely because the assessee would prefer to defer it.

DU · Dec 2024 · LB-505Q. 120 marksCapital v. Revenue

Q1. PIPER Pvt. Ltd. — road and water reservoir: capital or revenue expenditure?

Question (as printed): "PIPER Pvt. Ltd. is a company having objective of building, construction and mining operations like extraction of mica and limestone. Government of Uttarakhand made a contract with the company to build a mica warehouse in partnership near PIPER's head office. PIPER Pvt. Ltd. constructed a road with a cost of Rs. 50 Lakhs, from its head office to the new warehouse and also spent an amount of Rs. 20 Lakhs for the project of water reservoir near to its new warehouse in order to ease its business operation. PIPER Corporation claimed these expenses as deductions in their books of accounts. The Assessing Officer however rejected these claimed deductions contending these expenditures as capital in nature. Discuss issues in the light of landmark judgements and suggest how to test and differentiate the expenses incurred, as capital or revenue."
Step 1 — Identify the issue precisely

The deductibility of ₹70 lakh turns on s. 37(1), which allows an expenditure only if it is (i) not covered by ss. 30–36, (ii) not capital and not personal, and (iii) laid out wholly and exclusively for the business. The Revenue does not dispute purpose; it disputes character. So the single issue is: are the road and the reservoir capital or revenue expenditure? Two sub-issues follow: (a) does the road create an asset of enduring benefit in the capital field? (b) does the reservoir merely facilitate operations (revenue) or add to the profit-making apparatus (capital)?

Step 2 — State the tests (this is what the question really asks for)
  1. The enduring-benefit test — British Insulated & Helsby Cables Ltd. v. Atherton, [1926] AC 205: expenditure made "not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade" is capital.
  2. The "capital field" refinement — CIT v. Empire Jute Co. Ltd., AIR 1980 SC 1946 (DU Reader p. 118–120): "It is not every advantage of enduring nature … What is material to consider is the nature of the advantage in a commercial sense and it is only where the advantage is in the capital field that the expenditure would be disallowable. If the advantage consists merely in facilitating the assessee's trading operations … while leaving the fixed capital untouched, the expenditure would be on revenue account, even though the advantage may endure for an indefinite future."
  3. Fixed v. circulating capital — John Smith & Son v. Moore, (1921) 2 AC 13 (quoted in Empire Jute): fixed capital is what the owner turns to profit by keeping; circulating capital by parting with it.
  4. Profit-making apparatus test — Empire Jute: does the outlay add to or alter the apparatus (capital), or merely operate it (revenue)?
  5. Source test — expenditure which creates or extends the source of income is capital; expenditure to earn from an existing source is revenue.
  6. Whole-operation / commercial sense — Empire Jute: "no test is paramount or conclusive … every case has to be decided on its own facts keeping in mind the broad picture of the whole operation."
Step 3 — Apply to the road (₹50 lakh)
FactorApplication
Ownership of the landThe road runs from PIPER's head office to its new warehouse — the facts indicate it is constructed on the company's own land and remains its asset.
Nature of the advantagePermanent access between two fixed installations; the road is an asset of an enduring nature in the capital field, not a facility for trading.
Effect on the apparatusIt adds to the profit-making structure (infrastructure connecting the head office and the warehouse), rather than merely operating it.
Once and for allA single lump-sum outlay, not a recurring operating cost; ordinary repairs to the road would be revenue, but its construction is not.
ConclusionCapital expenditure — not deductible under s. 37(1). Depreciation may be available if the road qualifies as a "building" forming part of the plant/building used for business (s. 32), a point worth flagging in the answer.

Counter-argument to acknowledge (and then answer): the assessee may cite the "approach road" line of cases in which construction of an access road was held to be revenue because it conferred no asset or enduring advantage on the assessee — e.g. CIT v. Associated Fibre & Rubber Industries (P) Ltd., (1999) 236 ITR 471 (Bom.), and the "leasehold" principle of CIT v. Madras Auto Service (P) Ltd., (1998) 233 ITR 468 (SC), where expenditure on a building on leasehold premises was held revenue because the enduring advantage accrued to the lessor and not to the assessee. The answer: those cases turn on the absence of any enduring advantage in the assessee's own capital field; here the road is on the company's own land and services its own new warehouse, so the advantage is the assessee's. This is precisely the Empire Jute method: ask whose capital field is enriched.

Step 4 — Apply to the water reservoir (₹20 lakh)

For the Revenue (capital): the reservoir is a structure — a new, permanent installation constructed near the warehouse for the company's mining/construction operations. It creates an asset of enduring benefit which is not consumed in a single year; the company retains it. On the Atherton test that is capital expenditure, and it is not saved by the words "in order to ease its business operation", because every capital asset is acquired to ease operations.

For the assessee (revenue): the phrase "in order to ease its business operation" brings the outlay within the Empire Jute principle — an advantage which merely facilitates trading, leaving the fixed capital untouched, is revenue. If the reservoir is a replaceable operating facility (like additional power or additional water supply needed to run the plant) rather than a new installation forming part of the capital structure, the analogy in Empire Jute (payment for additional power every week) makes it revenue.

Better view: a reservoir is a constructed asset, not a supply of a consumable. Unless the facts show that it is a temporary or replaceable facility, it satisfies both the Atherton and Empire Jute tests for capital: it is a new asset, retained, and it enlarges the profit-making apparatus. Hence capital expenditure. (Where the assessee can show that the reservoir is merely part of routine water-management replacing an existing facility, or is required by statute and handed over to a public authority, the conclusion would be revenue — a distinction worth stating in one line.)

Step 5 — Conclude, with the "test-differentiate" framework the question demands
The four-question drill you should write out
  1. What did the money buy? An asset/advantage, or the running of the business?
  2. Where does the advantage sit? In the capital field (fixed capital, source) or in the revenue field (trading facility, working cost)?
  3. Does it alter the profit-making apparatus or merely operate it?
  4. Does it endure, and on whose side? An enduring advantage to a third party (lessor/Government) does not make the payer's outlay capital.

Final answer: both items are capital expenditure; the ₹70 lakh is not deductible under s. 37(1); the AO is right. Add: (i) capital expenditure may still yield depreciation under s. 32 if the asset falls within "building", "plant" or "furniture"; (ii) the assessee's remedy is therefore not s. 37(1) but s. 32, and the claim should be recast accordingly; (iii) if the road had been surrendered to the Government as a condition of the contract, the answer would change to revenue.

Argument for the Revenue (fails to that extent)
  • Maintenance is a personal obligation of a son/step-son; a decree does not convert it into a charge on income unless the decree actually creates one.

Essay · 10–20 marksIncome · s. 2(24)Karthikeyan

Q. "The definition of income in section 2(24) is inclusive and not exhaustive." Discuss, and examine whether a receipt which falls within no sub-clause of section 2(24) can still be taxed as income.

1. Introduction

Income-tax is a levy on "income", yet the Act nowhere defines the word exhaustively. Section 2(24) says "income includes", and Parliament has added sub-clauses from time to time (clause (ix) in 1972; (x), (xi) later; (xiii)–(xviii) with the gift provisions). The drafting is deliberate: an inclusive definition widens the net without freezing its content, leaving the courts to apply the natural meaning of the word.

2. The meaning of "income" in the cases
  • Kamakshya Narain Singh v. CIT, (1943) 11 ITR 513 (PC) — "a word difficult and perhaps impossible to define in any precise general formula … of the broadest connotation".
  • Gopal Saran Narain Singh v. CIT, (1935) 3 ITR 237 (PC) — anything properly described as income is taxable unless expressly exempted.
  • Navinchandra Mafatlal v. CIT, AIR 1955 SC 58 — income means "a thing that comes in"; it includes capital gain; the legislative entries receive the most liberal construction; and the ambit of s. 2(24) is co-extensive with Entry 82, List I.
  • Bhagwan Dass Jain v. Union of India, (1981) 2 SCC 135 — income includes not merely what is received but "that which can be converted into income" (notional income from self-occupied property upheld).
3. The core authority — CIT v. G.R. Karthikeyan, 1993 Supp (3) SCC 222

Facts: the assessee won the first prize of ₹22,000 in the All India Highway Motor Rally, a contest of driving skill, endurance and vehicle reliability. The ITO taxed it under s. 2(24); the AAC and the Tribunal held the rally was not a race or game; the High Court held that "winnings" in clause (ix) connotes money won by gambling or betting and that the receipt was therefore outside the clause.

Held (Revenue's appeal allowed): the receipt was income. The Court held that it would be a "wrong approach to try to place a given receipt under one or the other sub-clauses … and if it does not fall under any of the sub-clauses, to say that it does not constitute income"; "even if a receipt does not fall within the ambit of any of the sub-clauses in Section 2(24), it may still be income if it partakes of the nature of the income. The idea behind providing an inclusive definition in Section 2(24) is not to limit its meaning but to widen its net." The rally was a contest; the prize was a "'return' for his skill and endurance"; and casual receipts are income, because s. 10(3) exempts them only up to a limit.

4. Answer to the second limb

Yes — a receipt outside every sub-clause can still be taxed, provided it partakes of the nature of income. Conversely, a receipt which falls within a sub-clause is not thereby immunised: it may still be excluded because it is a capital receipt (unless it is a capital gain under s. 45), because it is exempt under Chapter III, or because, on the real-income doctrine, no income has resulted (CIT v. Shoorji Vallabhdas & Co., (1962) 46 ITR 144 (SC)).

5. Features and limits

Income may be received in cash or kind; on accrual or receipt; it may be legal or illegal (CIT v. S.C. Kothari, (1971) 82 ITR 794 (SC); CIT v. Piara Singh, (1980) 124 ITR 40 (SC)); it may be negative (a loss); and the source need not exist in the year of receipt. What it cannot be is fictitious, a mere book entry, pin money, a mutual-concern surplus, or a pure capital receipt.

6. Conclusion

Section 2(24) is a net, not a list. Karthikeyan settles that the definitions are illustrative and that the widest amplitude must be given to "income", subject always to the exemption provisions and the capital/revenue divide. The only discipline on this breadth is the requirement that the receipt be a real gain "coming in" to the assessee.

Essay · 20 marksSitaldasKinariwalaBejoy Singh

Q. Distinguish between "diversion of income by overriding title" and "application of income". Examine the test laid down in CIT v. Sitaldas Tirathdas and its application in CIT v. Sunil J. Kinariwala.

1. Why the distinction matters

The charge is on the total income of the assessee (ss. 4–5). If a third person is entitled to the income before it becomes the assessee's, it never enters the total income at all; if the assessee receives it first and then pays it away, it is taxed in full and the payment is (subject to specific provisions) not deductible. The difference between the two characterisations can therefore be the difference between nil and full liability.

2. The two Privy Council poles
  • Bejoy Singh Dudhuria v. CIT, (1933) 1 ITR 135 (PC) — diversion: a compromise decree charged the assessee's properties with his step-mother's maintenance; "the decree … has to that extent diverted his income from him … It is rather the allocation of a sum out of his revenue before it becomes income in his hands."
  • P.C. Mullick v. CIT, (1938) 6 ITR 206 (PC) — application: executors directed to pay ₹10,000 "out of the income" of the estate for the testator's sradh; the payments were assessable in their hands, there being no overriding title.
3. The test — CIT v. Sitaldas Tirathdas, (1961) 41 ITR 367 (SC)

Facts: the assessee claimed to deduct ₹1,350 and ₹18,000 paid as maintenance to his wife and children under a consent decree of the Bombay High Court. No charge was created on his property. The Tribunal held that a person compelled to apply part of his income to maintain persons whom he is under a personal and legal obligation to maintain could claim no deduction; the Bombay High Court, following earlier decisions, allowed the claim on the ground that the obligation was enforceable in a court of law.

Held (Revenue's appeal allowed): "The true test is whether the amount sought to be deducted, in truth, never reached the assessee as his income. Obligations, no doubt, there are in every case, but it is the nature of the obligation which is the decisive fact. … Where by the obligation income is diverted before it reaches the assessee, it is deductible; but where the income is required to be applied to discharge an obligation after such income reaches the assessee, the same consequence, in law, does not follow. … The first is a case in which the income never reaches the assessee, who even if he were to collect it, does so, not as part of his income, but for and on behalf of the person to whom it is payable." The Court added that the result would have been different "if such an overriding charge had existed either upon the property or upon its income".

Two propositions follow: (i) enforceability of the obligation is not the test — otherwise every debt would reduce income; (ii) the test is the timing and target of the obligation — does it bite on the source before accrual, or on the recipient after receipt?

4. The modern limit — CIT v. Sunil J. Kinariwala, (2003) 1 SCC 660

Facts: a partner holding 10% in a firm settled 50% of his right, title and interest (excluding capital) in favour of a trust and claimed that the ₹20,141 attributable to that half was diverted at source. The Gujarat High Court, relying on Bagyalakshmi & Co. and Murlidhar Himatsingka, accepted the claim.

Held (Revenue's appeal allowed): "The determinative factor … is the nature and effect of the assessee's obligation … When a third person becomes entitled to receive the amount under an obligation of an assessee even before he could lay a claim to receive it as his income, there would be diversion … but when after receipt of the income by the assessee, the same is passed on to a third person in discharge of the obligation …, it will be a case of application." The Court distinguished a sub-partnership (which "creates a superior title and results in diversion of the income … before the same becomes the income of the partner") from a bare assignment (under s. 29(1) of the Partnership Act the assignee gets no interest in the firm), and held that the assigned share had to be included in the assessee's total income.

The doctrine is real but narrow. Sitaldas supplies the test; Kinariwala supplies its limit — the overriding title must exist in the source and must be anterior to accrual. Essay · 10–20 marksSalamiCapital v. revenue receipt

Q. What is salami? Discuss the tests to determine whether a receipt is capital or revenue, with special reference to salami.

1. Meaning

Salami (premium, nazrana, pagdi) is a lump-sum payment made by an incoming tenant to the landlord for being let into possession — the price of the right to enjoy the property, as distinct from rent, which is the periodical payment for its use. Section 105 of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 draws precisely this line between "premium" and "rent".

2. The tests for capital v. revenue receipts
  1. Source and fruit (CIT v. Shaw Wallace & Co.) — a receipt from the exploitation of a source is revenue; the price of the source is capital.
  2. Fixed v. circulating capital (John Smith & Son v. Moore, quoted in Empire Jute).
  3. Substitution / sterilisation — compensation for the destruction or sterilisation of a capital asset is capital (Glenboig Union Fireclay v. CIT); compensation for loss of profits is revenue (Burmah Steam Ship).
  4. Loss of source v. loss of profit (Karanpura Development Co. v. CIT, (1962) 44 ITR 362 (SC) — compensation for loss of a managing agency is capital).
  5. Once and for all v. recurring — but substance prevails over the number of instalments (Panbari Tea).
  6. Enduring benefit in the capital field (Atherton, refined by Empire Jute).
  7. Selling an asset v. exploiting it — CIT v. Maheshwari Devi Jute Mills, (1965) 57 ITR 36 (SC) (sale of loom hours = capital receipt).
3. Salami is a capital receipt — the authorities
  • Member for the Board of Agricultural Income-tax, Assam v. Sindhurani Chaudhurani, AIR 1957 SC 729 — the two indicia of salami are "(1) its single non-recurring character and (2) payment prior to the creation of the tenancy"; it "can be neither rent nor revenue but is a capital receipt in the hands of the landlord"; it is a "single payment made for the acquisition of the right of the lessees to enjoy the benefits granted … That general right may properly be regarded as a capital asset." It is therefore not agricultural income under a State agricultural income-tax Act.
  • CIT v. Panbari Tea Co. Ltd., (1965) 57 ITR 422 (SC) — the premium is capital, the rent revenue; "in some cases the so-called premium is in fact advance rent and in others rent is deferred price" — hence substance over form; a premium paid in instalments remains capital.
  • Durga Das Khanna v. CIT, (1969) 72 ITR 796 (SC) — "prima facie premium or salami is not income and it would be for the income-tax authorities to show that facts exist which would make it a revenue receipt."
4. Consequences of the characterisation
  • Not taxable as income: no charge under "Income from house property" (ss. 22–24) or "Other sources" (s. 56).
  • Not agricultural income even if the land is agricultural — so the s. 10(1) exemption is irrelevant; the receipt is simply outside the charge.
  • Capital gains arise only if the transaction amounts to a "transfer" of a capital asset (s. 2(47) r/w s. 45) — a lease for a term ordinarily parts with an interest but not with the asset itself, so the usual result is no capital gain either. Where the lease is in substance a transfer of the land, s. 45 applies.
  • Where salami is disguised rent (e.g. paid in instalments coterminous with the lease, or where the "rent" is artificially depressed), the Revenue may re-characterise it as revenue — the burden lies on the Revenue (Durga Das Khanna).
5. Conclusion

Salami is the paradigm capital receipt: it is paid once, before the tenancy, for a right in land. The decisions in Sindhurani, Panbari Tea and Durga Das Khanna together establish both the character and the burden of proof. The general lesson is that the character of the right transferred, not the label or the arithmetic, determines the answer — exactly the method of Empire Jute on the expenditure side.

  7. Only real income is taxed — Shoorji Vallabhdas, Godhra Electricity.
  8. Diversion (before receipt; on the source) excludes; application (after receipt; personal obligation) does not — Sitaldas, Bejoy Singh, Kinariwala.
  9. Capital receipts are outside the charge (salami — Sindhurani, Panbari Tea); capital expenditure is barred by s. 37(1) (Atherton, Empire Jute).
  10. Income is taxed for the previous year in the assessment year, on receipt or accrual or arisal — ss. 3, 2(9), 4, 5, 9.

10.3Common errors — a checklist to read before you enter the hall

Substantive errors

  • Treating diversion as a deduction. It is an exclusion — the income was never the assessee's.
  • Assuming a court decree = diversion. Sitaldas is the opposite.
  • Assuming a charge always = diversion. Moti Lal Chhadami says no.
  • Forgetting that winnings are taxed at a flat rate under s. 115BB and are not "business income".
  • Calling a dividend from an agricultural company agricultural income (Bacha F. Guzdar).
  • Saying salami is exempt as agricultural income. It is a capital receipt — a different route to non-taxability.
  • Applying Atherton mechanically — Empire Jute requires the advantage to be in the capital field.
  • Saying the Tax Act defines income "exhaustively" — the single fastest way to lose marks on Q. 1.

Presentation errors

  • Not framing issues in a problem question. The examiner awards marks for the issue statement.
  • Giving only the assessee's argument. Both sides, then a decision.
  • Citing a case without its proposition. "As held in Sitaldas" earns nothing; the test does.
  • Writing facts of cases at length in a 10-mark answer. In a 20-mark answer, three lines of facts are enough unless the question says "discuss the case".
  • Ignoring the 2025 Act when the question uses its numbering; ignoring the 1961 Act when the syllabus prescribes it. Bridge both in one sentence.
  • No conclusion. Every answer must end with a determinate answer to the question asked.
  • Illegible structure: use headings (Issue / Rule / Application / Conclusion), tables and numbered propositions.
