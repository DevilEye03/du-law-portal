00OrientationHow this document is built & what to read first

Income-tax is, in principle, a tax on the person who earns. Chapter V (ss. 60 to 64) is the systematic exception. It says that in four situations the income which legally belongs to A shall be computed and taxed as the income of B: where A has transferred income but not the assets producing it (s. 60); where A has made a revocable transfer (ss. 61–63); where A is related in a specified way to the earner — spouse, son's wife, son's minor child, minor child (s. 64(1)); and where A, being a member of a HUF, has impressed his separate property with the character of family property (s. 64(2)). The topic is therefore best understood not as four unrelated rules but as one idea — anti-avoidance through the family — expressed through one chapter.

The single most important idea in the topic

Clubbing does not transfer ownership and does not make the transferee an agent. It creates what the Supreme Court has repeatedly called an "artificial income" — a statutory deeming by which one person's income is deemed to be another's for the purpose of computation and charge. Two consequences follow, and they are the two propositions on which almost every answer turns. First, because the provision is artificial and penal in effect, it is strictly construed: the statutory words must be exactly satisfied (Bhogilal Laherchand, 25 ITR 523; Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel, 55 ITR 637, both approved in the DU material). Second, there must be a real and proximate nexus between the transfer and the income clubbed: a remote connection is not enough (Mohini Thapar, reader p. 167; CIT v. Prem Bhai Parakh, distinguished there).

01Section 1The concept of clubbing, and why it is justified

Before a single sub-clause is read, the topic must be located. Sections 60 to 64 are not a code of liability of relatives; they are the income-tax analogue of the law of fraudulent preferences. The statute assumes that a person who can direct where income shall go will direct it to the person taxed least, and it neutralises that assumption by declaring that, in defined cases, the income shall be computed in the hands of the person who created the source rather than the person who holds it.

◆1.1 What "clubbing" means

The word

"Clubbing" is not a statutory expression. It is the practitioners' shorthand for the operation of ss. 60 to 64, in which income arising to one person is included in, i.e. added to or "clubbed with", the total income of another. The statutory verbs are "shall be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor" (ss. 60, 61), "shall be included in his total income" (s. 60, s. 61 proviso language, s. 64(1)), "shall be deemed to arise to the individual" (s. 64(2)).

Because the words differ, the consequences differ: a deeming that income "arises to" A is a charge on A and not on B; an "inclusion" leaves B taxable only in the residual cases. An answer that uses the words interchangeably will lose marks on precisely the point the four cases turn on.

The three defining features

  1. It is a rule of computation, not of ownership. Title to the asset is untouched. The transferee remains the owner in civil law; only the tax computation is re-pointed.
  2. It is an artificial income. The words are the Supreme Court's: s. 16(3) of the 1922 Act "creates an artificial income and must be strictly construed" (Philip John Plasket Thomas, reader p. 151, approving Bhogilal Laherchand, 25 ITR 523 and Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel).
  3. It is anti-avoidance, not penal. There is no penalty and no assessment of the transferee; the provision merely reattributes an existing income so that the transferor gains nothing by the split.

◆1.2 The architecture of Chapter V — a map

The chapter is best carried in the memory as three mechanisms and one enforcement provision. The divisions matter for analysis as well as for memory: ss. 60–63 look at the transaction (what did the transferor give up?); s. 64 looks at the relationship (who is the earner?); s. 65 supplies the machinery of recovery when the clubbed income belongs in law to someone else.

◆1.3 Justifiability — why clubbing is defensible

The syllabus uses the word "justifiability", which is an invitation to argue. The argument has a statutory side, a judicial side and a policy side; a complete answer gives all three, and then concedes the counter-argument.

(a) The statutory design is a closed list, not a suspicion

The legislature has not said "any income which the assessee procures for another shall be his". It has named four relationships (spouse, son's wife, son's minor child, minor child) and one transactional pattern (revocable transfer). That is the first answer to the charge of arbitrariness: clubbing is a rule of fixed categories, and outside the categories the Revenue must fall back on general anti-avoidance or on the diversion-of-income doctrine of Topic 1. It is in this sense a legislative rather than an administrative anti-avoidance device — the assessee knows in advance where he stands.

(b) The Supreme Court's own statement of purpose

In Philip John Plasket Thomas (reader p. 151) the Court says of s. 16(3) of the 1922 Act — the ancestor of s. 64(1)(iv) — that it

"clearly aims at foiling an individual's attempt to avoid or reduce the incidence of tax by transferring his assets to his wife or minor children", reader p. 151

and in Mohini Thapar (reader p. 167) the words "as arises directly or indirectly" are read as deliberately extending the reach beyond the first generation of income, so that the object of the section is not defeated by the simple expedient of selling the transferred asset and re-investing the proceeds.

The five policy arguments for clubbing

ArgumentContentJudicial or statutory echo
1 · The family is an economic unitIncome-tax is imposed on individuals, but in a Hindu or a middle-class household the earning member effectively controls the family's resources. If the law taxed only the legal earner, the family could spread one income over four returns and four basic-exemption thresholds, while enjoyment remained undivided.This is the assumption behind both limbs of the chapter — s. 64(1) (spouse, child) and s. 64(2) (HUF). The Supreme Court's "artificial income" language in Thomas is a candid admission that the unit of taxation is being enlarged.
2 · The source, not the hand, is taxedThe transferor built the source. Where he gives the income but keeps the source (s. 60), or keeps a power over the source (s. 61), or gives the source to a person whom he is presumed to control (s. 64(1)), the income is still, economically, his.The device is not new: s. 60 is the codification of the House of Lords' Ramsay-era learning on assignment of income; Lord Vestey's Executors and IRC v. Gaunt are discussed and distinguished in Thomas (reader pp. 151–153) precisely because English law treats husband and wife as one person for tax, while Indian law does not.
3 · Defeasance of progressionProgressive rates are the main distributive instrument of the Act. A transfer to a spouse or a minor with no other income converts a 30% slice into a nil-rated slice without any change in real command over resources. Clubbing removes the arbitrage; it is, in effect, the price of progression.Reflected in the greater-income rule of the Explanation to s. 64(1)(ii): where both spouses are within the clause, the income is clubbed in the hands of the spouse with the higher income — a rule that can only be explained by progression, since it deliberately picks the costlier hand.
4 · Administrative necessityProving that the transferor continues to enjoy the income is, in the nature of family life, almost impossible. The statute substitutes an irrebuttable presumption for an unprovable enquiry. The presumption is crude but cheap, and crude-but-certain rules are a legitimate legislative choice in tax.The Explanation defining "substantial interest" and the deeming in s. 64(2) ("notwithstanding anything contained in any other provision of this Act or in any other law") are both exercises in that substitution.
5 · Symmetry with the general lawThe chapter is the tax counterpart of the Hindu law doctrines of the joint family and of the karta's power over family property, and of the trust law of revocable settlements. It prevents the tax system from being used to produce results the general law would not permit.s. 64(2) expressly invokes Hindu law's "throwing into the common stock" and then overrides its effects for tax purposes — the strongest example in the chapter of the legislature refusing to follow civil law.
The statutory limits that make the scheme "justifiable" and not merely "draconian"

Three internal restraints are worth spelling out because they show the legislature's own calibration, and because an answer that mentions them reads as understanding rather than recitation:

  • Adequate consideration is a complete answer under s. 64(1)(iv), (vi), (vii) and (viii). A transfer at market value is outside the chapter, however close the relationship.
  • An agreement to live apart is a complete answer under s. 64(1)(iv). The legislature has thus recognised that in a broken marriage the spouses are separate economic units; Thomas shows the Supreme Court supplying a parallel restraint by construction where the marriage itself had not yet begun.
  • Genuine qualifications are rewarded under the proviso to s. 64(1)(ii). If the spouse is paid for what she knows rather than for whom she married, the salary escapes — the subject of Batta Kalyani and J.M. Mokashi.

◆1.4 The critique — and the answer to it

A "justifiability" answer that only defends the chapter is half an answer. The standard criticisms, each with the judicial material that supports it:

CriticismWhy it bitesAuthority / answer
1 · It taxes income the assessee never receivedThe most fundamental objection: ability-to-pay is measured by what a person can command. Clubbing charges A on money that B has already spent, and A may have to borrow to pay it.Accepted on all sides — hence s. 65, which gives the assessed transferee a right to recover the tax from the transferor, and hence the judicial insistence that the provision be strictly construed (Thomas, reader p. 151).
2 · It creates an "artificial income"Because the income is fictitious so far as A is concerned, courts cannot extend it by analogy. Every extension must be found in the words.Thomas (reader p. 151), approving Bhogilal Laherchand, 25 ITR 523 and Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel, 55 ITR 637; harmonised with CIT v. Sodra Devi, 32 ITR 615 (a beneficial provision must not be cut down by the strict-construction rule).
3 · Over-inclusion — the innocent case is caughtThomas is the perfect example: a man settled shares on the woman he was about to marry, three days before the wedding, and the Revenue clubbed four years of dividends. Nobody suggested the gift was a sham. The provision has no mens rea requirement and no "purpose" test, unlike a general anti-avoidance rule.Met (partly) by construction: the Court held that the relationship must subsist both when the transfer is made and when the income accrues, thereby reading a temporal limit into the section that is not in the words (reader pp. 153–155).
4 · Under-inclusion — the obvious case escapesWhy the son's wife but not the daughter's husband? Why the minor child but not the major unemployed child? Why not a parent, a brother, a live-in partner, a friend? The list is a historical accretion, not a principle.Frankly conceded: the categories reflect the family forms Parliament had in mind in 1937 (when s. 16(3) was inserted) and in 1961. The gap is filled, imperfectly, by the diversion-of-income doctrine (Topic 1) and by the general anti-avoidance provisions.
5 · The "nexus" problemOnce income is clubbed "directly or indirectly", how far does the chain run? Cash → shares → dividends → bonus shares → sale proceeds → new deposits.Cut off by a requirement of a proximate connection: Mohini Thapar (reader p. 167) holds substituted assets to be within the section, but the Court in that very case distinguishes Prem Bhai Parakh on the ground that the connection there was too remote. The line is one of degree, which is honest but unpredictable.
6 · Rate distortionBecause the clubbed income is added to the transferor's total income, it can push him into a higher slab while the transferee's own exemption and deductions go unused. The family may pay more in total than if nothing had been transferred at all.This is deliberate: clubbing is meant to make the transfer tax-neutral at best, so that a transfer motivated purely by tax is pointless. It also explains why the greater-income rule exists.
7 · Double-taxation anxietyIf both A and B are assessed, the same income is taxed twice.Answered by the structure: the deeming is to the exclusion of the transferee ("shall be deemed to arise to the individual and not to the family" — s. 64(2)(b)); the transferee is taxed only on income which the section does not reach, or on income which the section expressly leaves to her.
Model paragraph — "Discuss the justifiability of the clubbing provisions"

"Sections 60 to 64 rest on the legislative judgment that the family, and not the individual, is the real unit of economic enjoyment, and that a progressive rate scale can be defeated by the simple device of spreading one person's income among several returns. The provisions are therefore defences of progression rather than encroachments on property. Their justifiability is, however, conditional on three things, each of which the judiciary has supplied. First, because they create an artificial income, they are strictly construed: the case must fall within the words (Philip John Plasket Thomas, approving Bhogilal Laherchand). Secondly, the connection between the transfer and the income must be proximate, not remote (Mohini Thapar). Thirdly, the statutory exceptions — adequate consideration, an agreement to live apart, and genuine technical or professional qualification — must be given their full effect, and the phrase 'technical or professional qualifications' must not be read down to a formal degree (Batta Kalyani). Read with s. 65, which gives the person assessed a right of recovery, the scheme is a proportionate anti-avoidance measure and not an arbitrary attribution of another's income; but Thomas shows that where the words do not reach the case, the section fails even against strong suspicion."

◆1.5 The canons of construction the cases actually apply

All four prescribed cases are, at bottom, exercises in statutory construction. Reduced to canons, they are easy to hold in the memory and easy to deploy in an answer:

CanonStatementWhere it comes from
Strict constructionThe section creates an artificial liability; it must not be extended beyond its words, and the taxpayer is entitled to the benefit of a genuine ambiguity.Thomas, reader p. 151 (Bhogilal Laherchand; Keshavlal Lallubhai Patel).
Purpose, but only within the wordsThe object is to foil avoidance, and the words "directly or indirectly" must be given effect so that the object is not defeated — but a purpose cannot supply a condition the legislature omitted.Mohini Thapar, reader p. 167; contrasted with the refusal to extend in Thomas.
Temporal coherenceWhere the section uses a relationship as its trigger, the relationship must exist at the material time — and by a parity of reasoning at both material times.Thomas, reader pp. 153–155 (rejecting both the "date of computation" and the "date of accrual" tests of the High Court).
Purposive reading of an exceptionAn exception inserted to protect genuine earning capacity must be read so as to protect it; a formal reading that defeats the exception is wrong.Batta Kalyani, reader pp. 155–157 ("technical or professional qualification" need not be a degree).
Cumulative conditions are cumulativeWhere a proviso requires two things joined by "and", both must be shown; a court cannot convert them into alternatives to make the exception easier.J.M. Mokashi, reader pp. 161–164 (the two limbs of the proviso).
Substance over form — but only where the statute asks for it"Substantial interest" is measured at any time in the year, alone or with relatives, beneficially: a floor, not a ceiling.J.M. Mokashi, reader pp. 159–160 (100% holding qualifies; "substantial" does not mean "not overwhelming").

02Section 2ss. 60–63 — income transferred, ownership retained

Sections 60 to 63 answer the oldest avoidance device in the book: keep the tree, give away the fruit. If a man can assign the rents of his house to his son while remaining the owner of the house, he has parted with nothing but the tax. The 1961 Act meets the device twice over — once by a rule of source (s. 60: you cannot assign income while retaining the asset), and once by a rule of control (ss. 61–63: you cannot transfer the asset while keeping the power to take it back). Between them the two rules close the circle; what remains outside them is reached by s. 64.

◆2.1 Section 60 — transfer of income where there is no transfer of assets

The text

"60. Transfer of income where there is no transfer of assets.—All income arising to any person by virtue of a transfer whether revocable or not and whether effected before or after the commencement of this Act shall, where there is no transfer of the assets from which the income arises, be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor and shall be included in his total income."

Four features of the drafting should be noticed at once, because each is a hold on the marks. (i) The section applies whether the transfer is revocable or not — revocability, which is the whole subject of ss. 61–63, is irrelevant here. (ii) It applies whether the transfer was effected before or after the commencement of the Act — a deliberate (and, unusually, retrospective) reach back to assignments made under the 1922 Act. (iii) Its condition is negative: "where there is no transfer of the assets from which the income arises". (iv) Its effect is chargeability, not mere inclusion: the income "shall be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor" and "shall be included in his total income".

Elements — what the Revenue must establish

#ElementWhat it means, and where the argument usually is
1There is a "transfer"Given the widest meaning by s. 63(a): "any settlement, trust, covenant, agreement or arrangement". No instrument and no registration is needed. A letter, a book entry or an oral arrangement will do — the question is whether the transferor has bound himself.
2What is transferred is incomeThe subject-matter must be income, or a right to receive income, and not the source. Assignment of "the rent of my flat for five years" is income; assignment of "my flat" is not.
3The assets producing the income are not transferredThe decisive element. If the asset goes with the income, s. 60 is out of the picture and the enquiry shifts to ss. 61–63 (revocability) and, if the transferee is a spouse or minor, to s. 64(1)(iv).
4Income in fact arises to another person "by virtue of" the transferCausation. If the other person's receipt is not traceable to the transfer, s. 60 has nothing to operate on.
5The consequenceThe income is charged as the transferor's and included in his total income — notwithstanding that in civil law it is the transferee's.

Illustrations that make the section concrete

  1. The classic assignment. A, who owns ₹5,00,000 of Government securities, executes a deed assigning to his nephew "all interest which shall accrue on the said securities during the next seven years". The securities are not transferred. Every year's interest is A's income under s. 60, and the nephew is not taxed on it at all.
  2. The salary direction. An employee directs his employer, in writing, to pay one-half of his salary to his mother. There is no transfer of any asset (salary is income, not an asset); the whole salary remains taxable in his hands — which is also the answer at common law, because salary is taxable on the person who earned it.
  3. The firm's profits. A partner assigns to his son a half share of the profits of the firm without transferring any part of his interest in the firm. s. 60 applies: the son is not a partner, holds no asset, and receives only income. (Had he transferred a share of his interest in the firm, the asset would have gone with the income and s. 60 would be excluded.)
  4. Rent of a house. A, the owner of a house, executes a document entitling his brother to receive the rent for ten years while A continues to be the owner. s. 60 applies to the rent; and by Topic 4, the notional income of the house continues to be computed in A's hands under s. 22.

Doctrinal points worth a separate paragraph in an answer

  • s. 60 is the statutory version of the diversion/application distinction of Topic 1. A diversion of income at source — by an overriding obligation arising before the income accrues — is not the assessee's income at all; the mere application of income after it accrues is. Section 60 short-circuits the debate for voluntary transfers: it does not ask whether the assignment operated before or after accrual, but simply makes the income the transferor's whenever the assets stayed with him.
  • It is not limited to relatives. Unlike s. 64, s. 60 applies to a transfer to any person. This is why it is the broadest of the attribution rules and why it needs no "substantial interest" test.
  • Because the assets are not transferred, there is nothing for the transferee to be taxed on. The section is thus the only one in the chapter which wholly displaces the transferee rather than merely adding the income to the transferor's total.
  • The heading is part of the enactment. "Transfer of income where there is no transfer of assets" — courts use headings to resolve the very ambiguity that arises when the asset and the income are given in the same instrument but at different times.
The examination trap

Students routinely answer a s. 60 problem by discussing revocability. The section says in terms "whether revocable or not". If the question adds "the transfer was revocable", the correct method is to say: revocability is immaterial under s. 60 because the assets were not transferred; s. 61 would have been the relevant section had the assets been transferred along with a power to revoke. One sentence, and the examiner knows the difference between the two rules.

◆2.2 Section 61 — revocable transfer of assets

The text

"61. Revocable transfer of assets.—All income arising to any person by virtue of a revocable transfer of assets shall be chargeable to income-tax as the income of the transferor and shall be included in his total income."

Proviso.—Nothing contained in this section shall apply to any income arising to any person by virtue of a transfer —

  1. by way of trust which is not revocable during the lifetime of the beneficiary, and, in the case of any other transfer, which is not revocable during the lifetime of the transferee; or
  2. made before the 1st day of April, 1961, which is not revocable for a period exceeding six years.

Provided further—that the transferor derives no direct or indirect benefit from such income. The second proviso is a condition governing both limbs: an otherwise protected settlement loses the protection the moment the settlor takes a benefit out of it.

Structurally, s. 61 is the mirror image of s. 60. In s. 60 the assets stay and the income goes; in s. 61 both go, but the transferor keeps a power of recall. The legislature treats the retained power as proof that the transfer was not in truth a disposition at all — the "transferor" has merely parked the property. The provision is therefore not about the ownership of the asset (which is genuinely transferred) but about the quality of the disposition: a transfer that can be undone has not definitively altered the taxpayer's command over resources.

"Revocable" is defined — and defined very widely

s. 63(b) does not ask whether the transferor intends to revoke, or even whether he can revoke today in every circumstance. It asks whether the transfer contains (i) any provision for the re-transfer of the whole or any part of the income or assets to the transferor, or (ii) anything which in any way gives the transferor a right to assume, directly or indirectly, control over the whole or any part of the income or assets. Either is enough. And a transfer which is revocable only after the expiry of a specified period is deemed to be a transfer containing a provision for revocation. The result is that very few professionally drafted settlements escape: the drafter must ensure that the settlor retains neither a power over the corpus nor a power over the income, and takes no benefit.

◆2.3 Section 62 — fixing the date from which clubbing runs

Sections 61 and 62 divide the labour: s. 61 decides whether a transfer is caught; s. 62 decides from when. The section has two limbs, both concerned with timing, and both built on the proviso to s. 61.

(i) A power of revocation acquired later

Where the income of a transfer is not chargeable to tax in the transferor's hands because the transfer falls within the proviso to s. 61, but the transferor later acquires the power to revoke the transfer, the income arising from the date on which that power is acquired becomes chargeable as his income. The reasoning is that the moment the power exists, the "irrevocable" character of the settlement has gone; the position is tested year by year, not once for all. An important practical consequence: the clubbing is prospective — it does not reopen the years during which the transfer was genuinely irrevocable.

(ii) A revocation exercisable at a fixed future time

The second limb deals with the transfer that is revocable only after a stated period, or upon a future contingency. Its function is to prevent the transferor from arguing that, because the power is not exercisable today, the transfer is "irrevocable" and the clubbing must wait indefinitely. Read with s. 63(b) (a power exercisable after a period is a provision for revocation), the scheme of ss. 61–62 is: the existence of any provision for re-transfer puts the income in the transferor's hands, and s. 62 tells you the year in which the inclusion begins.

  • s. 61 — assets with a string attached: the transferor pays, unless the proviso protects the transfer.
  • s. 62 — the string appears later: the transferor pays from the date the power arises.
  • s. 63 — the dictionary: "transfer" is very wide; "revocable" is very wide; the two definitions together explain why so few settlements survive.

03Section 3s. 64(1)(ii) — the spouse's remuneration from a "concern"

This is the most heavily litigated clause in the chapter, and the only one on which the reader prints two full High Court judgments (Batta Kalyani for the assessee-spouse's qualification, J.M. Mokashi against him). It is also the clause that produces the commonest examination problem: a family business pays a salary to the wife (or the husband), and the question is whether that salary is hers or the proprietor's. Everything turns on three statutory expressions — "concern", "substantial interest" and "technical or professional qualifications … solely attributable to … technical or professional knowledge and experience".

◆3.1 The provision and its conditions

The text (as the Court states it in J.M. Mokashi, reader p. 159)

"64. ◆3.2 "Concern" and "substantial interest"

(a) "Concern" — wide, and not confined to business

In J.M. Mokashi the assessee's first argument was that "concern" means a business establishment and does not include a profession, so that a practising physician's payments to his wife were outside the clause. The argument failed. Saraf J. held that "concern" is "a word of wide import" (reader p. 159), drawing on the dictionary meanings quoted from Black's Law Dictionary (6th edn) and holding that in the setting of s. 64(1)(ii) read with Explanation 2:

"it is clear that 'concern' includes any company, firm, individual or any other entity carrying on business or professional activity. It cannot be given any restricted meaning to take out of its ambit professional organisations or organisations run as proprietary establishments." Mokashi, reader p. 159

The Court reinforced the point from Barendra Prasad Ray v. ITO, (1981) 129 ITR 295 (SC), where "business" was held to include "professions, vocations and callings" — so that even if "concern" were limited to business, a profession would still be a business. The conclusion (reader p. 160): "concern" takes within its sweep all organisations or establishments engaged in business or profession, "whether owned by a company, partnership or individual or any other entity".

(b) "Substantial interest" — a floor, not a ceiling

The assessee's second argument was ingenious and failed: "substantial" implies a proportion of a whole, so a man who owns 100% of his own proprietary concern has the whole interest and not a "substantial interest", and s. 64(1)(ii) — which speaks of a substantial interest — cannot apply to him.

The Court rejected this (reader p. 160). Explanation 2 is "a deeming provision" whose "object is to create a legal fiction to extend the application of section 64(1)(ii) to concerns in which the interest of individual concerned exceeds the limits specified therein. It sets out the lowest limit … Its object is to widen the net of the section — not to restrict it. No outer limit of interest of the individual has, therefore, been specified."

Hence: 100% is "substantial interest" — indeed, no resort to the fiction is needed at all, because the words bear their ordinary meaning. The Batta Kalyani case (reader p. 154) never even raised the point: the assessee there was the sole proprietrix of a hardware and paint shop, and the dispute was only about the qualification of the salaried husband.

The single most examinable proposition in this section

The two words "and" in the proviso are conjunctive. The assessee in Mokashi argued that "and" should be read as "or" so that the exception would apply if either limb was satisfied; the Tribunal held against him (reader p. 158, holding (vi): "the word 'and' appearing twice in the proviso … means 'and' and not 'or'"), and the High Court did not disturb that holding. This limb is the one on which both cases were actually lost, and it is the one students forget. It is a finding of fact, not a question of law: the court asks what the spouse was paid for. Two consequences follow, and they are worth memorising as an answer-opener:

  • Evidence is needed. Assertions in the assessment order that the wife "assisted in the business" do not discharge the burden. In Batta Kalyani the salary to the husband as "manager" of a hardware and paint shop was held not attributable to technical or professional knowledge; in Mokashi, the Tribunal recorded that "there was no material on record to show that Mrs. Mokashi had any technical or professional qualification or that the salary paid to her was attributable to any technical or professional knowledge and experience of hers" (reader p. 158, para 5) — and that finding of fact was fatal.
  • The word "solely" is a high threshold. A mixed remuneration — partly for skill, partly for the relationship — fails entirely, because the clause excludes only income solely attributable to skill. There is no apportionment under the proviso; apportionment, where it exists at all, happens elsewhere in the section (see the "portion" language of s. 64(2)(c) and the "extent" language of s. 64(1)(vii)/(viii)).

◆3.4 Where both spouses are within the clause — the greater-income rule

The problem the rule solves

Suppose H and W are both employed by a company in which each has a substantial interest, and each draws a salary. Literally applied, clause (ii) would club W's salary with H's income and H's salary with W's income — a circular result, and a double inclusion. The Act resolves it by a rule (carried in the Explanation to s. 64(1)(ii)): where both spouses have a substantial interest in the concern and both receive remuneration from it, the remuneration of both is included in the total income of that spouse whose total income, excluding such remuneration, is greater. And once the income has been included in the hands of one spouse, it continues to be included there in later years and cannot be shifted to the other spouse unless the Assessing Officer, after giving that spouse an opportunity of being heard, is satisfied that it is necessary to do so.

The rule is a small masterpiece of anti-avoidance design: it deliberately picks the costlier of the two hands, so that a family cannot choose the lower-rate spouse; and its "once-and-for-all" feature prevents year-to-year shopping. It also illustrates the point made in §1.3 about progression.

Three refinements that separate a good answer from a complete one
  • The clause clubs the income, not the asset.   • Substituted assets are within the clause; reinvested income is (on the authorities) outside it.   • Cross-transfers. Where H transfers assets to W and W transfers assets to H, the income from each set of assets is clubbed with the respective transferor. There is no netting: each transfer is examined on its own. The purpose of the rule is to prevent the two transfers from cancelling out.

◆4.2 The interface with s. 27(i) — a house is different

This is the one place where Topic 4 and Topic 5 meet, and it is a favourite of question-setters because it requires the student to see that two provisions could otherwise tax the same income twice.

The rule

Section 27 contains deemed owner provisions. Clause (i) provides, in substance, that a person who transfers a house property (otherwise than in connection with an agreement to live apart) to his spouse, or to his minor child not being a married daughter, without adequate consideration, is deemed to be the owner of the house. The income is then computed in his hands under s. 22, under the head "Income from house property", with all the incidents of that head — the standard deduction under s. 24(a), interest under s. 24(b), and so on.

Section 64(1)(iv) opens with the words "subject to the provisions of clause (i) of section 27" precisely because, without that opening, a transferred house would be caught twice: once by s. 27(i) and again by s. 64(1)(iv).

How to write it in an answer

"Where the asset transferred is a house property, the case is taken out of s. 64(1)(iv) by its own opening words and is governed by s. 27(i): the transferor is deemed to be the owner and is taxed under s. 22 on the notional income from house property, not under s. 64 on the transferee's income. The distinction is not merely one of drafting: the quantum differs, because s. 22 taxes a notional income computed with the s. 24 deductions, whereas s. 64(1)(iv) would tax the actual income in the transferee's hands. Where the asset is anything other than a house — shares, cash, a business — s. 64(1)(iv) applies in terms." For everything on s. 27(i), ss. 22–27 and the deemed-owner rules, see the Topic 4 notes.

Clauses (vii) and (viii) clubbing is proportionate, because a third party holds the asset and only part of the income may be destined for the spouse. The words "to the extent to which" are the tell-tale. A good answer tabulates the four clauses along this axis — transferee, consideration, date, and proportion.

◆4.4 Five worked problems on the asset-transfer clauses

Problem 1 — the timing point (Thomas in textbook form)

H transfers 500 shares worth ₹5,00,000 to W on 1 March 2024, ten days before their marriage. Dividends for AY 2025-26 are ₹18,000. Answer: not clubbed under s. 64(1)(iv). The relationship of husband and wife did not subsist when the transfer was made. That is the precise holding of Philip John Plasket Thomas (§7), read with the statute's use of the present relationship "spouse". Note that the result is the same whether one asks the question at the date of transfer, the date of accrual, or the date of computation — the Court insisted on both ends of the transaction, and the marriage came after the transfer.

Problem 2 — the substituted-asset point (Mohini Thapar in textbook form)

H gives W ₹2,00,000 in 2019. W buys shares with it; the shares yield ₹9,000 dividend in 2024 and she also places ₹50,000 on a fixed deposit yielding ₹3,500 interest. Answer: both the dividend and the interest are clubbed in H's hands. They arise "indirectly" from the transfer of the cash; the cash was the asset, and the shares and deposit are its substitutes. This is exactly the fact-pattern of Mohini Thapar (§10), where the clubbed figure was ₹21,225 of dividends and interest.

Problem 3 — the house property point

H transfers his only house to W in 2021 without consideration. The house is let and yields ₹2,40,000 a year. Answer: s. 64(1)(iv) is expressly subject to s. 27(i); H is deemed to be the owner and is taxed under s. 22 on the notional income from house property (after the s. 24(a) standard deduction of 30% and interest under s. 24(b)), computed in his own hands. The clause "otherwise than in connection with an agreement to live apart" in s. 27(i) mirrors the exception in s. 64(1)(iv).

Problem 4 — the routed benefit

H transfers ₹10,00,000 to a trust, directing the trustees to pay half the income to his wife and to accumulate the rest for his nephew. Income for the year: ₹80,000. Answer: s. 64(1)(vii) applies "to the extent to which the income from such assets is for the immediate or deferred benefit of the spouse" — so ₹40,000 is clubbed with H and ₹40,000 is assessed in the trust's hands (at the maximum marginal rate, under the trust provisions). If the trustees had discretion to apply the whole income for the wife's benefit, the whole ₹80,000 would be clubbed even if in fact they applied nothing to her, because the clause speaks of the benefit, immediate or deferred.

Problem 5 — the double-transfer

H transfers shares worth ₹4,00,000 to W; in the same year W transfers a fixed deposit worth ₹4,00,000 to H. Dividends ₹20,000 and interest ₹32,000. Answer: the dividend is clubbed with H (under (iv)) and the interest is clubbed with W (under (iv) as applied to her transfer). No set-off between the two. The device of mutual transfers produces mutual clubbing, which is precisely the design of the clause.

05Section 5s. 64(1A) — the minor child, and s. 65 — enforcement

The minor child's income was originally clubbed through the awkward device of treating the transfer to the minor as a transfer to the spouse; since 1992 the Act deals with it directly, in s. 64(1A), and the provision has become one of the most litigated in practice because it catches not only gifts but every investment made in a child's name. It belongs to this topic for completeness, and it is frequently examined as a short-note.

◆5.1 The provision

The scheme of s. 64(1A)

Main rule. In computing the total income of any individual, there shall be included all such income as arises or accrues to his minor child, not being a minor child suffering from a disability of the nature specified in s. 80U.

Which parent? The Explanation to the sub-section fixes the parent: the income of the minor child is included in the total income of that parent whose total income (excluding the minor's income) is greater. Where the marriage of the parents does not subsist, the income is included in the total income of the parent who maintains the minor child.

Anti-double-counting proviso. Nothing in the sub-section applies to income of the minor child which is relatable to income in respect of which the individual is already assessable under s. 64(1)(ii), (iv), (vi) or (vii) — i.e. the child is not to be taxed twice over on the same stream of income. The statutory words "relatable to" are doing the work that Mohini Thapar's "proximate nexus" test does elsewhere.

Relief. Section 10(32) allows a deduction of ₹1,500 in respect of the clubbed income of each minor child, so that the parent's total income is increased by the clubbed income less ₹1,500 per child.

◆5.2 The exceptions, and the computation

If the parents were divorced and W maintained the children, A's ₹40,000 would be clubbed with W notwithstanding her lower income.

◆5.3 Section 65 — enforcement, and the reason it matters to the "justifiability" debate

The text and its function

"65. Liability of person in respect of income included in the income of another person.—Where, by virtue of any provision of this Act, the income of a person is included in the total income of another person, then, notwithstanding anything contained in this Act or in any other law for the time being in force, the person whose income is so included shall be liable to pay that portion of the tax levied on such other person which is attributable to the income so included."

Section 65 is the answer to the strongest objection to the whole chapter (see §1.4, criticism 1). The transferor is assessed; the transferee is the person who has the money. The section therefore makes the transferee liable for the slice of the transferor's tax attributable to the clubbed income — a statutory indemnity in the Revenue's favour — and the transferee may in turn recover that amount from the transferor under the general law. Three points follow:

  • The liability arises only where income is included "by virtue of any provision of this Act" — so it covers all of ss. 60 to 64, and also the analogous provisions elsewhere (e.g. income of a minor under s. 64(1A), and clubbing under the deemed-owner rules).
  • It is a liability to pay the tax attributable to that income, not the whole of the transferor's tax; the proportion has to be worked out.
  • "Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act or in any other law" makes it independent of any contractual arrangement between the spouses; a private agreement that the husband will bear all taxes does not bind the department.
One-paragraph answer on s. 65

"Section 65 does not club anything; it is a machinery provision which makes the scheme of ss. 60–64 workable. Since the clubbed income is taxed in the hands of a person who has not received it, the section imposes on the person who did receive it a liability for that portion of the tax which is attributable to that income, notwithstanding any other law or agreement. Its importance in the present topic is doctrinal: it shows that the legislature was conscious that clubbing taxes an artificial income, and it is the strongest single answer to the objection that the chapter taxes income which the assessee never enjoyed."

06Section 6s. 64(2) — throwing separate property into the common stock, and its partition

The syllabus singles this out in words: "throwing of separate property into the common stock of Joint Hindu Family and subsequent partition of the same — section 64(2)". It deserves its own section, because it is the one provision in the chapter that engages with Hindu law, that overrides Hindu law for tax purposes, and that operates in two distinct stages — before partition, and after it.

◆6.1 The mischief — how a Hindu family used to save tax

The Hindu law power being exploited

Under Hindu law a coparcener may impress his self-acquired (separate) property with the character of joint family property. The act is called throwing the property into the common stock of the family or blending. Its requirements, as the section presupposes them, are: (i) the property must be the separate property of the coparcener; (ii) there must be an unequivocal declaration or an unambiguous act evincing the intention to abandon the separate character of the property; (iii) the intention must be to benefit the family as such, and (iv) it must be unconditional. Once done, the property is joint family property; it is held by the coparceners for the family, and on partition each coparcener takes his share.

In Hindu law this is a perfectly lawful and often pious act — usually performed to maintain the family and to bring the ancestral and the acquired together.

The two-stage tax plan the Act had to meet

Stage 1 — throw it in. The individual converts his separate investments into HUF property. The income from them now belongs to the family, which is a separate assessable entity with its own basic exemption and its own (until 1997, more favourable) rate schedule. One income becomes two; one exemption becomes two.

Stage 2 — divide it up. The family then partitions the property, and shares come into the hands of the coparceners and, in the cases where Hindu law gives her one, the wife. Now the same income is spread over three or four assessees, each with a nil or low rate.

The device is the Hindu-law counterpart of the transfer-to-spouse device: the assessee has given away nothing in substance, and has gained everything in tax. Section 64(2) is Parliament's answer, inserted by the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 1970 with effect from 1 April 1971, and reaching back to conversions made after 31 December 1969.

◆6.2 The text, clause by clause

s. 64(2) — material language

"64(2) Where, in the case of an individual being a member of a Hindu undivided family, any property having been the separate property of the individual has, at any time after the 31st day of December, 1969, been converted by the individual into property belonging to the family through the act of impressing such separate property with the character of property belonging to the family or throwing it into the common stock of the family or has been transferred by the individual, directly or indirectly, to the family otherwise than for adequate consideration (such property being hereinafter referred to as the converted property), then, notwithstanding anything contained in any other provision of this Act or in any other law for the time being in force, for the purpose of computation of the total income of the individual for any assessment year commencing on or after the 1st day of April, 1971 —

(a) the individual shall be deemed to have transferred the converted property, through the family, to the members of the family for being held by them jointly;

(b) the income derived from the converted property or any part thereof shall be deemed to arise to the individual and not to the family;

(c) where the converted property has been the subject-matter of a partition (whether partial or total) amongst the members of the family, the income derived from such converted property as is received by the spouse on partition shall be deemed to arise to the spouse of the individual and shall accordingly be included in the total income of the spouse of the individual."

The two most commonly misstated points about s. 64(2)
  • It is not a provision that taxes the family. Students write "the income is clubbed with the income of the HUF". The opposite is true: clause (b) says the income is the individual's and not the family's. The family is the device; the member is the taxpayer.
  • Partition does not "restore" the income to the individual. Clause (c) shifts the tax on the spouse's share to the spouse. What the individual avoids after partition, he avoids because the statute says so — not because the partition undid the conversion. And the portion received by members other than the spouse (a son, a daughter, the mother) is left to the ordinary rules: the section's expressed target is the spouse's share.

◆6.3 The partition stage in detail

(a) What "partition" means here

The section speaks of a partition "whether partial or total" among the members of the family. Both are covered, and the words matter: the classic avoidance device was a partial partition which carved out just enough property to give the wife an income of her own without disturbing the rest of the family arrangement. Because the section covers partial partitions, the device fails. A partition may be effected under Hindu law by a registered deed, by a decree of court, or by a severance of status evidenced by conduct; the taxing statute does not prescribe a form, and the question is one of fact — whether the members have in truth separated their rights.

(b) Why the statute singles out the spouse

The whole purpose of the two-stage plan was to move income to the wife (whose own income is usually nil or small) while keeping it within the household. Clause (c) therefore follows the income to the wife's hands and taxes it there — which has two effects. First, the individual can no longer claim the benefit of the family's lower rate for that portion and cannot claim that it is his; and secondly, the wife cannot be used as a tax-free conduit, because she now has a return of her own and her own slab. In substance the provision trades the husband's clubbing for the wife's separate assessment — a compromise characteristic of the whole chapter, which prefers a once-and-for-all attribution to a year-by-year enquiry.

07Case 28 · reader pp. 148–153Philip John Plasket Thomas v. CIT — when must the marriage exist?

Why this case is first

It is the only Supreme Court judgment in the topic on the construction of the clubbing provision as a whole, and it decides a question of pure statutory time: at what moment must the relationship of husband and wife exist for the section to bite? The answer it gives — both at the time of the transfer and at the time the income accrues — is the anchor for every "timing" problem under s. 64(1)(iv), and its statement that the provision "creates an artificial income and must be strictly construed" is the single most quoted sentence in this branch of the law.

Philip John Plasket Thomas v. Commissioner of Income-tax

(1964) 2 SCR 480 · AIR 1964 SC 587 · S.K. Das J. · four appeals by certificate under s. 66-A(2) of the 1922 Act from the Calcutta High Court · DU reader pp. 148–153
Case 281922 Act s. 16(3)(a)(iii)Pre-nuptial transferStrict constructionArtificial income
1 · Facts
  • The assessee, P.J.P. Thomas, held 750 'A' shares in J. Thomas & Co. Ltd. of 8 Mission Row, Calcutta. He became engaged to Mrs Judith Knight, described as a divorcee, and the engagement was announced in the newspapers on 3 September 1947.
  • On 10 December 1947 the assessee and Mrs Knight presented to the company an application to transfer the 750 'A' shares to her. The transfer deed of that date recited: "I, Philip John Plasket Thomas … in consideration of my forthcoming marriage with Judith Knight … do hereby transfer to the said transferee the 750 'A' shares … to hold to the said transferee … Executors, administrators and assigns, subject to the several conditions on which I hold the same at the time of the execution thereof."
  • On 15 December 1947 the company transferred the shares and registered Mrs Judith Knight as their owner. On 18 December 1947 the marriage was solemnised. On 26 January 1948 the fact of the marriage was communicated to the company and the name of the shareholder was changed in its books to Mrs Judith Thomas.
  • The four accounting years were those ending 30 April 1948, 1949, 1950 and 1951; the four assessment years were 1949-50 to 1952-53. The dividends received by Mrs Thomas, as grossed up, were ₹97,091, ₹78,272, ₹1,00,000 and ₹16,385.
  • For 1949-50 and 1950-51 assessments already completed were reopened under s. 34 of the 1922 Act and the dividends were reassessed in the assessee's hands; for 1951-52 and 1952-53 the Income-tax Officer included the dividends under s. 16(3)(b). The Appellate Assistant Commissioner confirmed, holding that both s. 16(3)(b) and s. 16(3)(a)(iii) applied.
  • Before the Tribunal the assessee argued (i) that he had transferred the shares when Mrs Knight was not yet his wife, (ii) that the transfer was absolute and unconditional when made, and (iii) that it was for adequate consideration. The Tribunal rejected s. 16(3)(b) but held that the case fell within s. 16(3)(a)(iii) because the transfer became effective only after the marriage, and further that the transfer could be construed as a revocable transfer within s. 16(1)(c).
  • Two questions were referred to the Calcutta High Court: whether the dividends could be included under s. 16(3)(a)(iii), and whether they could be included under s. 16(1)(c). By its judgment of 28 February 1961 the High Court answered the first against the assessee and the second in his favour. The Department did not appeal against the second answer; the appeals to the Supreme Court concerned only the first.

The provision construed — s. 16(3)(a)(iii) of the Indian Income-tax Act, 1922 (inserted in 1937): "In computing the total income of any individual for the purpose of assessment, there shall be included … (a) so much of the income of a wife or minor child of such individual as arises directly or indirectly … (iii) from assets transferred directly or indirectly to the wife by the husband otherwise than for adequate consideration or in connection with an agreement to live apart." (Reader pp. 149–150.)

2 · Issues
  1. The principal issue: on a true construction of s. 16(3)(a)(iii), at what point of time must the relationship of husband and wife subsist — at the date of the transfer, or at the date of the accrual of the income, or at the date of computation of the total income?
  2. The subordinate issue: whether the transfer of 10 December 1947 took effect immediately, or only from the date of the marriage on 18 December 1947 — i.e. whether "in consideration of my forthcoming marriage" made the gift conditional upon the marriage, and if so whether it was a condition precedent or a condition subsequent.
  3. The third issue, not decided: whether the transfer was for adequate consideration. The Court expressly left it open once the first two points were decided.
  4. Issue carried from the High Court: whether the words "husband" and "wife" in clause (a)(iii) mean legal husband and wife, or are merely descriptive of the persons referred to in clause (a).
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (appellant)
  • At the time of the transfer Mrs Knight was not his wife. Clause (a)(iii) speaks of assets "transferred … to the wife by the husband". There was no husband and no wife on 10 December 1947, so the clause is not attracted at all — quite apart from any question of consideration.
  • The transfer was absolute when made and contained no words of postponement; on the contrary its words show that it took effect at once. The Tribunal's reason — that the transfer "became effective only after the marriage" — reverses the document.
  • The recital "in consideration of my forthcoming marriage" is at most a statement of motive. The mutual promise to marry had already been made before 10 December 1947 (the engagement had been announced on 3 September 1947), so the marriage could not be consideration for the deed. If anything, the gift was an ordinary gift in contemplation of marriage, subject to a condition subsequent — and a gift subject to a condition subsequent takes effect when made and is divested only if the condition fails.
  • On the "revocable transfer" question the assessee had succeeded in the High Court, a conclusion the Department did not challenge.
For the Revenue (respondent)
  • The object of s. 16(3) is the principle of aggregation: the income of the wife is included in the husband's because of the influence which the husband exercises over the wife. What matters is therefore the relationship at the time the income is taxed, not the date of the transfer.
  • Sub-clause (i) demonstrates the point: it speaks of the wife's membership of a firm in which her husband is a partner, and it says nothing at all about any assets being brought into the firm by the wife. The sub-clauses must be read harmoniously (CIT v. Sodra Devi, 32 ITR 615), and harmony requires that clause (a)(iii) be read as a clause of aggregation rather than as a clause about transfers.
  • In clause (a) the word "wife" is merely descriptive of the woman referred to in clause (a), and "husband" refers merely to the individual whose total income is to be computed — hence the phrase "such individual".
  • The English authorities support a wide reading: in Lord Vestey's Executors and Vestey v. IRC, 31 TC 1, the House of Lords had to decide whether "wife" included "widow" (overruling IRC v. Gaunt, 24 TC 69, which had said that one word included the other), and Lord Morton observed that "the treatment of husband and wife by the legislature for income tax purposes rests on the view that any income enjoyed by one spouse is a benefit to the other spouse". Since income enjoyed by the wife is treated as a benefit to the husband, the exact date of the marriage is immaterial.
  • (The Revenue also succeeded on the "condition subsequent" analysis: even if the gift was subject to the condition of marriage, the condition was fulfilled, so the gift was effective — but effective from 10 December 1947, which was before the marriage. That concession, made "rightly" as the Court put it, destroyed the Revenue's own case before the Court.)
4 · Decision

The appeals were allowed and the question referred to the High Court was answered in favour of the assessee. The dividends were not includible in his total income under s. 16(3)(a)(iii).

  1. The relationship must subsist at both ends. The Court first accepted that "at the time when the income accrues, it must be the income of the wife of that individual whose total income is to be computed … the relationship of husband and wife must subsist at the time of the accrual of the income; otherwise the income will not be the income of the wife, for the word 'wife' predicates a marital relationship" (reader p. 151). But that was not the end: sub-clause (iii) adds a further condition, namely that the income must arise from assets "transferred directly or indirectly to the wife by the husband". Since "all income of the wife from all her assets is not includible in the income of the husband", the relationship must also subsist when the transfer is made (reader p. 151).
  2. Both views of the High Court were rejected. Mukharji J. had held the relevant date to be the date of computation; Bose J. had held it to be the date of accrual. The Supreme Court held that neither test was sufficient, because both addressed only the first of the two conditions in the sub-clause. It recorded, in fairness to Mukharji J., that he had not treated "husband" and "wife" as including prospective spouses — he insisted on legal husband and legal wife — but held that he had nonetheless read the clause without giving effect to its adjectival condition.
  3. "Husband" and "wife" bear their natural meaning. Reading the sub-clauses harmoniously (as Sodra Devi requires), the Court saw no disharmony: sub-clause (i) speaks only of membership of a firm and "has no reference to assets at all", whereas sub-clause (iii) "talks of assets and qualifies the word 'assets' by the adjectival clause 'transferred directly or indirectly to the wife by the husband'". There was no reason to take "husband" and "wife" "in the archaic sense" contended for by the Revenue (reader p. 152).
  4. The object is anti-avoidance, not aggregation. The Court held that "sub-section (3) of section 16 clearly aims at foiling an individual's attempt to avoid or reduce the incidence of tax by transferring his assets to the wife or minor child"; that object "does not require that the word 'wife' or the word 'husband' should be interpreted in an archaic or secondary sense" (reader p. 152).
  5. The English cases were distinguished. Lord Vestey's Executors and Gaunt proceeded on the English principle, embodied in Rule 16 of the General Rules, that for income-tax purposes husband and wife living together are one. "It is quite clear to us that the treatment of husband and wife in the Indian Income Tax Act, 1922 does not rest on the view that any income enjoyed by one spouse is a benefit to the other spouse; for sub-section (3) of section 16 makes it quite clear that all income enjoyed by the wife is not to be included in the income of the husband". Hence "the English decisions are not in point" (reader p. 152).
  6. The gift took effect on 10 December 1947. The transfer deed "contained no words of postponement. On the contrary, it contained words which indicated that the transfer took effect immediately." As to the condition: "A condition precedent is one to be performed before the gift takes effect; a condition subsequent is one to be performed after the gift had taken effect, and, if the condition is unfulfilled, will put an end to the gift. But if the gift had already taken effect on December 10, 1947 and the condition subsequent has been later fulfilled, then the gift is effective as from December 10, 1947 when the assessee and Mrs Knight were not husband and wife" (reader p. 153). The question whether a gift to an intended spouse is recoverable when the marriage does not take place was expressly left open.
5 · Principle evolved
  1. The double-subsistence rule. For clubbing under what is now s. 64(1)(iv), the relationship of husband and wife must subsist (i) when the assets are transferred and (ii) when the income accrues. A transfer before marriage — however plainly made in contemplation of it — is outside the clause; so, by parity of reasoning, is a transfer after the marriage has been dissolved.
  2. "Artificial income" and strict construction. Section 16(3) "creates an artificial income and must be strictly construed" (Bhogilal Laherchand v. CIT, 25 ITR 523, approved at reader p. 151). The rule is not an excuse for a narrow reading that defeats the section — the Court harmonises it with CIT v. Sodra Devi, 32 ITR 615 (provisions beneficial to the assessee must be liberally construed) — but it does mean that the Revenue must bring the case within the words, and cannot supply by purpose a condition Parliament omitted.
  3. The purpose of the chapter is anti-avoidance, and only anti-avoidance. The Court identifies the object as "foiling an individual's attempt to avoid or reduce the incidence of tax" — an object, it held, which is fully achieved by reading the section according to its terms and does not need to be enlarged.
  4. Indian law does not treat spouses as a single taxable person. Because s. 16(3) includes only some of the wife's income, the English premise that "any income enjoyed by one spouse is a benefit to the other" has no application. This is a foundational proposition of Indian tax law and worth remembering for its own sake.
  5. The law of conditions applies to gifts. A gift subject to a condition subsequent is effective from the date of the gift, and the subsequent fulfilment of the condition does not postpone its operation. The same reasoning would apply to a settlement expressed to be "in consideration of an intended marriage".
  6. The Revenue must plead the right provision. The officer had invoked s. 16(3)(b); the Appellate Assistant Commissioner added s. 16(3)(a)(iii); the Tribunal dropped (b) and rested on (a)(iii) plus s. 16(1)(c); the High Court upheld (a)(iii) but rejected (1)(c); the Supreme Court rejected (a)(iii). Four different theories in one case is a useful caution against assuming that the provision cited by the officer is the operative one — and it explains why the "revocable transfer" answer failed here even though a settlement in contemplation of marriage looks revocable in substance.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the first thing to cite on any timing problem under s. 64(1)(iv), (vi) or (vii). A transfer to a fiancée, to a woman who later becomes the wife, or to a person after divorce is outside the clause.
  • It supplies the topic's constructional creed. "Artificial income … strictly construed" is the sentence to open an answer with, and Mohini Thapar (§10) brings the same creed to the opposite conclusion on a different set of facts — a pairing worth pointing out in the exam.
  • It shows the limits of purpose. The Court accepted the anti-avoidance purpose in full and still refused to club. That is the classic demonstration that in a charging provision, purpose operates within words.
  • It is a reminder that facts about dates decide cases. Three documents — the deed of 10 December, the registration of 15 December and the marriage of 18 December — and the entire outcome depends on their order. In a problem question, draw the chronology before you argue.

Mapping: s. 16(3)(a)(iii) of the 1922 Act → s. 64(1)(iv) of the 1961 Act. The wording is materially identical ("assets transferred directly or indirectly to the spouse … otherwise than for adequate consideration or in connection with an agreement to live apart"), and the ratio applies without qualification. The 1922 Act's s. 16(3)(a)(iv) (minor child) corresponds to s. 64(1)(iv) read with s. 64(1)(viii) and, since 1992, to s. 64(1A).

08Case 29 · reader pp. 154–156Batta Kalyani v. CIT — what is a "technical or professional qualification"?

Why this case is a trap in the exam

The assessee's argument succeeded in principle and the assessee still lost. Batta Kalyani is the leading authority for the proposition that "technical or professional qualification" in the proviso to s. 64(1)(ii) need not be a degree or diploma from a recognised body — a proposition that is now standard — and yet the salary was clubbed, because the second limb of the proviso was not established. Students who cite the case for the first limb and stop there get half marks; students who explain why the assessee lost get all of them.

Batta Kalyani v. Commissioner of Income-tax

(1985) 154 ITR 59 (AP) · Anjaneyulu J. · reference under s. 256(1) of the 1961 Act · AY 1976-77 · DU reader pp. 154–156
Case 291961 Act s. 64(1)(ii)Proviso, limb (i)Expertise v. degreeHusband paid by wife
1 · Facts
  • The assessee, Smt Batta Kalyani, ran hardware and paint shops. There was no dispute that the business was carried on by her as a sole proprietrix.
  • She employed her husband, Shri B. Venkataramaiah, "to manage the business", and paid him salary for services rendered. The reference related to the assessment year 1976-77.
  • The Income-tax Officer included the salary in the assessee-wife's total income under s. 64(1)(ii). He held that the husband "did not possess any technical or professional qualification" and that the income was "not solely attributable to the application of the technical or professional knowledge and experience" of the husband; accordingly the proviso had no application.
  • The Appellate Assistant Commissioner allowed the wife's appeal, holding that the salary was governed by the proviso and was therefore not includible. The Department appealed to the Tribunal.
  • The Tribunal allowed the Department's appeal on two grounds: (a) that the proviso can have no application unless the "technical or professional qualifications" relate to a qualification awarded by a recognised body; and (b) that there was no evidence to show that the income earned by the husband was solely attributable to the application of technical or professional knowledge and experience.
  • The question referred was: "Whether, on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the Appellate Tribunal is justified in holding that the income of the assessee's husband is includible in the assessment of the assessee under s. 64(1)(ii) of the Act?"
  • It is important to note what the case was not about: the Revenue never disputed that the wife had a "substantial interest" — she owned 100% of the concern, and the wide reading of Explanation 2 in J.M. Mokashi (§9) was to settle that question later. Nor was the "concern" question raised.
2 · Issues
  1. The question of law: whether the words "technical or professional qualification" in the first part of the proviso to s. 64(1)(ii) are confined to a degree, diploma or certificate conferred by a recognised body, or whether they extend to expertise — technical or professional knowledge and experience — acquired otherwise.
  2. Whether the two parts of the proviso must be read harmoniously, so that the expression "knowledge and experience" in the latter part informs the meaning of "qualification" in the former.
  3. The question of fact: whether, on the material, the salary paid to the husband was solely attributable to the application of his technical or professional knowledge and experience.
  4. The question of appealability: whether the Tribunal's finding on the second limb was a finding of fact which the High Court could not disturb in a reference.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (the wife)
  • The proviso contains no requirement of a recognised qualification. On its plain words it asks only whether the spouse "possesses technical or professional qualifications"; it says nothing about a degree, a diploma or a certificate from a university or institute. "If a person possesses technical or professional knowledge, that itself is an attribute of qualification."
  • The internal evidence of the statute supports this. The latter part of the proviso speaks not of "qualifications" but of "knowledge and experience". Had the legislature intended a formal qualification, "the latter part of the proviso could surely have proceeded to state that the income should be solely attributable to the application of his or her technical or professional qualifications". The deliberate change of expression shows that substance, not form, was intended.
  • The word "qualification" must therefore be read with "knowledge and experience" in mind — a harmonious construction of the two parts of one proviso.
  • On the facts, the husband "had rich experience in paint business and he used his skill and knowledge in the paint business and helped the assessee to manage the business who was otherwise incapable of carrying on the business". The requirements of the proviso were therefore satisfied, and the salary should not have been clubbed.
For the Revenue
  • "Qualification" in the first part refers to a certificate, diploma or degree conferred by a recognised body. That is the ordinary meaning of the word in the context of "technical or professional" qualification; it denotes a formal standing, not a personal faculty.
  • The second part is parasitic on the first. The "technical or professional knowledge and experience" in the latter part "must also originate from the qualification referred to in the first part". Technical or professional knowledge and experience simpliciter, without a qualification, do not satisfy the second part.
  • Hence a dichotomy: "in a case where there is no recognised technical or professional qualification as such, mere possession of technical knowledge and experience does not bring into application the proviso".
  • The Tribunal's view was therefore correct on both limbs, and its finding on the absence of evidence for the second limb was a finding of fact which the High Court ought not to disturb in a reference under s. 256(1).
4 · Decision

The question was answered in the affirmative — in favour of the Revenue and against the assessee (reader p. 156). The salary paid to the husband was includible in the wife's total income under s. 64(1)(ii). But the route by which the Court reached that result is what matters, and it splits into two parts.

  1. The assessee won on the law. "We find considerable force in the submission of the learned counsel for the assessee that the words 'technical or professional qualification' occurring in the first part of the proviso do not necessarily relate to the technical or professional qualifications acquired by obtaining a certificate, diploma or a degree or in any other form from a recognised body like a university or an institute" (reader p. 155). The reason is the legislature's own change of language: "That this was not the intention of the Legislature is clear from the use of the expression 'knowledge and experience' in the latter part of the proviso, as otherwise it would have been perfectly permissible for the Legislature to use the same expression as occurring in the first part."
  2. The rule of harmonious construction. "The harmonious construction of the two parts of the proviso … would be that if a person possesses technical or professional knowledge and the income is solely attributable to the application of such technical or professional knowledge and experience, the requirement for the application of the proviso is satisfied, although the person concerned may not possess any qualification issued by a recognised body."
  3. The test stated. "It is enough … if the recipient of the salary possesses the attributes of technical or professional qualification, in the sense that he has got expertise in such profession or technique. If by the use of that expertise in the profession or technique, the person concerned earns salary, then the [latter] part of the proviso is also satisfied." The Tribunal therefore "erred in coming to the conclusion that unless a recognised body conferred a qualification, it should not be considered that a person possessed technical or professional qualification".
  4. The assessee lost on the facts. "Coming, however, to the facts of the present case, we are not satisfied that the second part of the proviso is complied with. The finding of the Tribunal is that there was no evidence to prove that the income earned by the assessee's husband was solely attributable to the application of technical or professional knowledge and experience."
  5. And the finding could not be disturbed. "This is essentially a finding of fact and it is not challenged before the lower authorities." The Court was therefore "unable to accept the submission … that in the present case both the requirements of the proviso are satisfied".
  6. The conclusion. "We consider that the Tribunal was justified in coming to the conclusion that the salary paid by the assessee's wife to her husband is includible in her total income under s. 64(1)(ii)."
5 · Principle evolved
  1. Limb (i) is satisfied by expertise. A "technical or professional qualification" within the proviso means the attributes of qualification — the expertise which makes a person fit to do the technical or professional work — and not necessarily a degree, diploma or certificate of a recognised body.
  2. The two limbs must be read harmoniously, because the legislature used "qualification" in the first limb and "knowledge and experience" in the second. The change of expression is deliberate and cannot be ignored.
  3. But the proviso as a whole is not satisfied by expertise alone. Limb (ii) — that the income be "solely attributable to the application of such technical or professional knowledge and experience" — remains a separate requirement, and it is a question of fact on which the burden lies on the assessee. A bare assertion that the spouse "managed the business" or "had rich experience" is not evidence.
  4. Findings of fact are final in a reference. Where the Tribunal records that there is no evidence on limb (ii) and that finding is not challenged, the High Court will not substitute its own view; the reference under s. 256(1) is on a question of law only. This procedural point is a good deal of the value of Batta Kalyani as an authority.
  5. Gender neutrality in operation. The case is a neat illustration that s. 64(1)(ii) works in both directions: here the wife was the proprietor and the husband the salaried spouse, and the clause was applied against her. The section protects the revenue, not the husband.
6 · Why it matters today — and how Mokashi treats it
  • It is the authority for a liberal limb (i), and in that respect it survives: even J.M. Mokashi, which refuses to follow it, accepts the Revenue's own concession that "the words 'technical or professional qualifications' [cannot] be equated to educational qualifications" (reader p. 158).
  • It is expressly disapproved on the reasoning. Saraf J. in Mokashi says of this very case: "In our opinion for the reasons set out by us in this decision, the interpretation of the proviso by the Andhra Pradesh High Court is not correct. It goes counter to the express language of the proviso. We, therefore, express our inability to agree with the same" (reader p. 164). The error, on the Bombay view, is using "knowledge and experience" in the second limb to dilute "qualification" in the first.
  • The disagreement is narrow and should be stated precisely. Both courts accept that the limbs are two; both accept that limb (ii) must be separately proved. They differ on whether expertise alone can constitute qualification under limb (i). See §11.2 for the resolution, and §12.2 for how to use the conflict in an answer.
  • Practical lesson for a problem question: argue limb (i) generously (cite Batta Kalyani), then prove limb (ii) with facts — the qualifications, the nature of the duties, comparability of the remuneration with what an outsider would be paid, and the absence of any other explanation for the payment. That is precisely what was missing here.

Mapping: decided under s. 64(1)(ii) of the 1961 Act, i.e. the provision still in force and examinable; no 1922 Act counterpart exists, the clause having been newly inserted in 1961.

09Case 30 · reader pp. 157–165J.M. Mokashi v. CIT — the doctor, his wife, and the whole of s. 64(1)(ii)

Why this is the most important case in the topic

It is the only judgment in the reader which decides every disputed expression in s. 64(1)(ii) — "concern", "substantial interest", "technical or professional qualification", "solely attributable" and the conjunctive "and" — and it is the only one which dissents from a decision printed alongside it. If a question is set on s. 64(1)(ii), Mokashi is the spine of the answer; Batta Kalyani is the counterpoint. It runs to nine printed pages (157–165) and repays close reading.

J.M. Mokashi v. Commissioner of Income-tax

(1994) 207 ITR 252 (Bom) · B.P. Saraf J. · reference under s. 256(1) of the 1961 Act · AY 1978-79 · DU reader pp. 157–165
Case 30s. 64(1)(ii)"Concern" includes a profession100% is substantialCumulative limbsDissents from Batta Kalyani
1 · Facts
  • The assessee was a practising physician and cardiologist. His wife, Smt Jayashree J. Mokashi, "had passed first year Arts of the Bombay University" — and that was her only qualification.
  • She was employed by him as a receptionist-cum-accountant. During the accounting period relevant to the assessment year 1978-79, the assessee paid her ₹8,100 by way of salary.
  • The Income-tax Officer included that amount in the assessee's income by applying s. 64(1)(ii). The Appellate Assistant Commissioner rejected the assessee's appeal and affirmed the order.
  • On second appeal, because "there were some conflicting decisions of the various Benches of the Tribunal on the point of issue", the Tribunal by order dated 15 October 1980 referred the matter to a Special Bench.
  • Before the Special Bench the assessee raised four contentions (reader p. 157): (1) "concern" does not include "profession" as distinguished from "business"; (2) "substantial interest" in Explanation 2(ii) refers only to a proportion of the whole interest, not the whole interest, so that the clause cannot apply to a proprietary concern in which the assessee has 100 per cent interest; (3) "technical or professional qualifications" does not mean a degree of a competent authority or university — sufficient if the spouse possesses the necessary knowledge and experience enabling her to perform her duties; and (4) the word "and" appearing twice in the proviso should be read as "or", so that the proviso applies if either requirement is met.
  • The Special Bench rejected all four contentions and recorded seven holdings (reader pp. 157–158) — reproduced below, because they are the framework of the whole judgment.
  • The Tribunal further found on the facts that "there was no material on record to show that Mrs Mokashi had any technical or professional qualification or that the salary paid to her was attributable to any technical or professional knowledge and experience of hers" (reader p. 158), and confirmed the addition. The question referred was: "Whether … the Tribunal has rightly held that the income of the assessee's wife is includible in the income of the assessee under section 64(1)(ii)?"
2 · Issues
  1. "Concern": whether the word in s. 64(1)(ii) includes a professional concern, and a concern run as a proprietary establishment — or is confined to business establishments.
  2. "Substantial interest": whether a person entitled to 100% of the profits of his own proprietary concern is a person having a "substantial interest" within the meaning of Explanation 2, or whether "substantial" implies a part only of a whole.
  3. "Technical or professional qualifications": what the expression means, and in particular whether it means a formal qualification necessary for the particular profession, or any expertise which makes a person suitable for the job.
  4. The structure of the proviso: whether its two requirements are cumulative or alternative, and whether "and" should be read as "or".
  5. Limb (ii) in operation: whether, and to what extent, the benefit of the proviso can be restricted to that portion of the remuneration which is reasonably attributable to the spouse's knowledge and experience.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (Dr Mokashi)
  • "Concern" means a business establishment. Professional organisations depend on the personal skill and knowledge of the person concerned, and establishments of professionals like doctors do not fall within the ambit of "concern". s. 64(1)(ii) therefore has no application to payments made by an individual who is a professional.
  • "Substantial interest" presupposes a fraction. If a man owns 100% of his proprietary concern, he owns the whole interest; he does not hold a "substantial interest" in it. Explanation 2 being a deeming provision, it cannot be stretched to cover a case where the individual owns everything.
  • "Qualification" must be liberally interpreted "to mean and include any qualification which makes a person suitable for a job". It should not be given a narrow or restrictive meaning, and it certainly does not require a university degree.
  • The two conditions are alternative, not cumulative. They must be read harmoniously; and the use of the words "knowledge and experience" in the second part shows that the proviso applies even where the spouse does not possess a technical or professional qualification but has the requisite technical or professional knowledge or experience. It follows that "and" must be read as "or".
  • On the facts, Mrs Mokashi had worked as receptionist-cum-accountant; the remuneration was modest (₹8,100 for the year); the presumption should therefore be in favour of its genuineness.
  • Authorities relied on: Batta Kalyani v. CIT (1985) 154 ITR 59 (AP); CIT v. Sorabji Dorabji (1987) 168 ITR 598 (Ker); Dr K. Thomas Varghese v. CIT (1986) 161 ITR 21 (Ker); CIT v. Dr K.K. Shah (1982) 135 ITR 146 (Guj); and CIT v. Madhubala Shrenik Kumar (1990) 181 ITR 180 (MP).
For the Revenue
  • "Concern" cannot be equated to "business establishment", and "technical or professional qualifications" cannot be equated to educational qualifications. These words "have their special meaning and they have to be interpreted accordingly" (reader p. 158).
  • Explanation 2 specifies the lowest limit of the interest which attracts s. 64(1)(ii). "It cannot be interpreted to mean that interest higher than the lowest limit specified in the definition will not amount to 'substantial interest'. Such an interpretation will be most unnatural and will go counter to the very object and scheme of section 64(1)(ii)."
  • The word "experience" in the latter part restricts rather than enlarges the exception: it ties the excluded income to the qualification, so that knowledge without qualification cannot qualify.
  • The limbs are cumulative. The proviso protects only income which satisfies both; a construction that makes them alternative renders one part of the proviso redundant.
  • The Tribunal's findings of fact were correct and the answer must be in the Revenue's favour.
4 · Decision

The question was answered in the affirmative — in favour of the Revenue and against the assessee (reader p. 165). The salary of ₹8,100 paid to the wife was includible in the assessee's total income. The Court reached that result by deciding each of the four contentions against the assessee, and the reasoning is best set out contention by contention.

  1. "Concern" includes a profession and a proprietary concern. The word "is a word of wide import. It has various shades of meanings." After referring to the dictionaries and to Black's Law Dictionary (6th edn), the Court held (reader p. 159): "in the context of section 64(1)(ii) of the Act read with Explanation 2 thereto, it is clear that 'concern' includes any company, firm, individual or any other entity carrying on business or professional activity. It cannot be given any restricted meaning to take out of its ambit professional organisations or organisations run as proprietary establishments." It reinforced the point from Barendra Prasad Ray v. ITO (1981) 129 ITR 295 (SC), that "business" is wide enough to include "professions, vocations and callings", and concluded (reader p. 160) that "concern" takes in "all organisations or establishments engaged in business or profession, whether owned by a company, partnership or individual or any other entity".
  2. "Substantial interest" — a floor, not a ceiling. "Explanation 2 is a deeming provision … The object of this Explanation is to create a legal fiction to extend the application of section 64(1)(ii) to concerns in which the interest of individual concerned exceeds the limits specified therein. It sets out the lowest limit of interest of the individual in the concern for the purpose of applicability of section 64(1)(ii). Its object is to widen the net of the section — not to restrict it. No outer limit of interest of the individual has, therefore, been specified." It would be "a most unreasonable and unnatural interpretation" to hold that while a person with 20% has substantial interest, a person with 100% does not. The Court therefore held "that an individual entitled to cent per cent of the profits of a concern is a person having substantial interest within the ordinary meaning of the expression itself. No resort to the deeming provision contained in Explanation 2 is necessary in such a case, though even on application thereof, the same result will be achieved" (reader p. 160).
  3. "Technical or professional qualification" means the qualification necessary for that profession or job. This is the heart of the judgment and it is developed over three pages (reader pp. 161–163). The Court began from the dictionary meanings — "qualification" is a word of very wide import, meaning "any quality which makes a man fit for any job", but it is here qualified by "technical or professional"; "technical" means "belonging or peculiar to an art or profession"; "profession" is a vocation requiring special, usually advanced, education, knowledge and skill (Black's; Halsbury's, 4th edn, Vol. 23; Corpus Juris Secundum, Vol. 72). From these it concluded: "the 'qualification' mentioned therein must be such which makes a person eligible for technical or professional work … professional qualification must mean qualification which is necessary for carrying on the particular profession." Hence: "Take, for example, the legal profession. The requisite qualifications for carrying on the legal profession have been laid down by the statute. In such a case, a person possessing such qualification alone can be said to be in possession of professional qualification, because such qualification is a must for carrying on the profession. Knowledge of law or experience is not relevant for that purpose." Similarly a person cannot carry on the medical profession without the requisite degree; and while some technical jobs require degrees and diplomas, "there are a few others where university degree or diploma is not necessary. Adequate training and evidence thereof might be sufficient." The Court added the decisive qualification: "it is not any and every qualification, academic or otherwise, which can bring a spouse within the scope and ambit of the proviso … It is the possession of only technical or professional qualification necessary for undertaking the particular technical job or carrying on the profession to which the income is attributed that will meet the requirement of the first part of the proviso" (reader pp. 162–163).
  4. The two limbs are cumulative, and "and" means "and". "In order to claim the benefit of the proviso … both the conditions specified in the proviso must be satisfied. The first condition relates to the spouse … who must possess 'technical or professional qualifications'. If this condition is not satisfied, the proviso will not apply and reference to the second requirement will be unnecessary." The Court rejected the argument that the second limb's "knowledge and experience" dilutes the first: "two different expressions have been used by Parliament in the very same proviso, not inadvertently, but with a deliberate purpose" (reader p. 161). And: "the two conditions mentioned in the proviso are cumulative and not alternative. They deal with two different aspects — one pertains to the eligibility of the spouse to claim benefit of the proviso, the other to the income which would qualify for exclusion from clubbing … Any attempt to [mix them] will go counter to the clear language, scheme and object of the proviso and the well-accepted rule of interpretation that one part of a section or clause should not be construed in such a manner as to render the other part redundant" (reader pp. 163–164).
  5. The function of limb (ii): reasonableness of the remuneration. The Court explained that even a qualified spouse's benefit is "hedged in with certain conditions and is limited to the extent indicated in the proviso". Its illustration is the LL.B. wife who has practised for only a year or two: the assessee "will be required to satisfy that the remuneration so paid to her for her legal services was 'solely attributable to the application of her professional knowledge and experience' as a lawyer. If the taxing authorities find that the remuneration paid for the legal services was excessive or high having regard to her limited professional knowledge and experience, he may determine the amount of remuneration which can be solely attributed to the application of her professional knowledge and experience and exclude only that part of her income from the clubbing provision." The object of the second limb is therefore "to restrict the benefit of the proviso only to reasonable payments for professional services and to put a check on diversion of income to the spouses possessing technical or professional qualifications in the guise of salary, fees, etc." (reader p. 163).
  6. Authorities considered. The Court followed CIT v. D. Rajagopal (1985) 154 ITR 375 (Kar) (both conditions must be satisfied); distinguished Kamlabai Gujri v. CIT (1986) (MP) on the footing that it presupposes the first condition is fulfilled and only casts the burden of the second on the assessee; and disagreed with CIT v. Madhubala Shrenik Kumar (1990) 181 ITR 180 (MP) so far as it held that "technical or professional qualifications" cannot mean a degree or diploma, and with Batta Kalyani (AP) in terms: "In our opinion for the reasons set out by us in this decision, the interpretation of the proviso by the Andhra Pradesh High Court is not correct. It goes counter to the express language of the proviso. We, therefore, express our inability to agree with the same" (reader p. 164).
  7. Applied to the facts. "In the instant case, the spouse of the assessee neither possessed any technical or professional qualification nor was she paid for any technical or professional services rendered by her. Admittedly, she had passed first year Arts of the Bombay University and that was her only qualification. She was employed … as receptionist-cum-accountant and paid a salary for that employment. In such a case, it is not only difficult but impossible to hold that she possessed any 'technical or professional qualification' … That being so, the proviso … is not applicable to her" (reader pp. 164–165).
5 · Principle evolved
  1. "Concern" is a word of wide import. It covers every entity — company, firm, individual or other — carrying on business or professional activity, including a sole proprietorship. A doctor's practice is a concern; a professional is within s. 64(1)(ii).
  2. "Substantial interest" is a statutory minimum, not a description of size. Twenty per cent is the threshold; 100% is inside the clause. Explanation 2 "widens the net … not restricts it".
  3. "Technical or professional qualification" means the qualification that is necessary for carrying on that particular profession or for doing that particular technical job. What is necessary varies from profession to profession: a statutory degree for law and medicine; a degree or diploma for some technical jobs; "adequate training and evidence thereof" for others. Knowledge and experience, without the qualification the profession requires, are not enough.
  4. The two limbs of the proviso are cumulative. Limb (i) is a condition precedent to invoking the proviso at all; limb (ii) then limits the relief to the amount which can be solely attributed to the application of the knowledge and experience. "And" is not to be read as "or", because that would render part of the proviso redundant.
  5. Limb (ii) embodies a test of reasonableness and permits apportionment. Excessive remuneration — judged against the spouse's actual knowledge and experience — is clubbed, and only the reasonable part is excluded. This is a distinct and important holding, easily missed: the proviso is not all-or-nothing on the second limb.
  6. Dissensus is itself part of the ratio's value. On the precise question whether expertise without formal qualification satisfies limb (i), Batta Kalyani (AP) and Madhubala Shrenik Kumar (MP) say yes and Mokashi (Bom) says no. The Madras, Kerala and Gujarat decisions cited by the assessee take the liberal view. The conflict is real and unresolved by the Supreme Court (§11.2).
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the complete digest of s. 64(1)(ii). Every expression in the clause is construed once, in one judgment, with reasons. A well-answered question on the clause will read like a summary of this case.
  • It supplies the argumentative structure for both sides on the qualification issue — and it is the case which shows why a family salary is scrutinised: not because it is a sham, but because the second limb requires the department to ask whether it is reasonable, and the first limb requires the assessee to show that it is necessary to the job.
  • Its wide reading of "concern" makes the clause practically important. Before Mokashi it was arguable that a professional's payments to a spouse escaped altogether; after it, the doctor, the lawyer and the architect are all within the clause.
  • Its reading of "substantial interest" makes the proprietary concern the paradigm case — which is precisely the fact-pattern of the most common examination problem, and of Batta Kalyani itself.

Mapping: decided under s. 64(1)(ii) of the 1961 Act — the same text which is in force and examinable. The judgments with which it disagrees are, with one exception, decisions of other High Courts; only the Supreme Court can settle the conflict, and it has not done so.

10Case 31 · reader pp. 166–167Mohini Thapar v. CIT — how far does "indirectly" reach?

Why this case is the second pillar

It is short — two printed pages — and it decides two things at once: that income from a substituted asset is clubbed under s. 16(3)(a)(iii) (now s. 64(1)(iv)), and that clubbing nevertheless requires a proximate nexus between the transfer and the income, so that the case where the connection is remote is outside the section. It is the only Supreme Court authority in the reader on the extent of "indirectly", and it is the natural companion to Thomas: the same creed of strict construction producing opposite results because the facts fell on different sides of the nexus line.

Mohini Thapar (dead) by L.Rs. v. Commissioner of Income-tax (Central), Calcutta

(1972) 4 SCC 493 · Hegde J. · appeals by certificate from the Calcutta High Court · AYs 1949-50 to 1953-54 · DU reader pp. 166–167
Case 311922 Act s. 16(3)(a)(iii)Cash gifts to wifeSubstituted assetsProximate nexus
1 · Facts
  • The assessee, Late Karam Chand Thapar, "made certain cash gifts to his wife, Smt Mohini Thapar". The transfers were direct transfers of cash.
  • "From out of those gifts, she purchased certain shares and the balance amount she invested." The shares earned dividends and the investments yielded interest.
  • The interest realised and the dividends earned were included in the income of Karam Chand Thapar for the assessment years 1949-50, 1950-51, 1951-52, 1952-53 and 1953-54. The amount in issue in the reference for 1949-50 was ₹21,225, "derived from deposits and shares held by the assessee's wife".
  • The assessee objected. The Income-tax Officer held the amounts includible; the Appellate Assistant Commissioner upheld him; the Tribunal upheld the Assistant Commissioner.
  • At the assessee's instance the question was referred under s. 66(1) of the 1922 Act: "Whether on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the income of ₹21,225 derived from deposits and shares held by the assessee's wife, Smt Mohini Devi Thapar, was income from assets directly or indirectly transferred by the assessee to his wife within the meaning of Section 16(3) of the Income-tax Act". Similar questions were referred for the other assessment years. The High Court answered in favour of the Revenue; hence the appeals.
  • The assessee died after the assessments were made, and the appeals were prosecuted by his legal representatives.
2 · Issues
  1. The principal issue: whether income earned by the wife from shares purchased, and deposits made, out of cash gifted by the husband is income "from assets transferred directly or indirectly to the wife by the husband" within s. 16(3)(a)(iii).
  2. The constructional issue: whether the words "as arises directly or indirectly" require the income to spring directly from the transferred asset, or are satisfied by an indirect derivation.
  3. The nexus issue: whether there is a sufficient connection between the transfer and the income, and how proximate that connection must be.
  4. The precedent issue: whether the decision in CIT v. Prem Bhat Parakh (1970) 1 SCC 784 — where minors' income from a firm, the capital of which came from their father's gifts, was held not clubbable — governed the case.
3 · Arguments
For the assessee (Dr Pal)
  • There is no nexus between the income earned and the transfer of the assets. The assets transferred were cash; the income clubbed was dividend and interest from shares and deposits. Different assets, different income.
  • The section requires income arising directly from the assets transferred. "Only such income which can be said to have directly sprung from the assets transferred can come within the scope of Section 16(3)(a)(iii)."
  • Prem Bhat Parakh is authority for a strict reading. There the capital invested by the minors in the firm came from gifts made by the assessee-father, and this Court held that "the connection between the gifts made by the assessee and the income of the minors from the firm was a remote one"; the income arose as a result of the minors' admission to the benefits of partnership and was not clubbable. The same reasoning applies with even greater force here, where the wife's income arose from shares and deposits she chose to buy.
  • The section creates an artificial income and must be strictly construed (CIT v. Keshavlal Lallubhai Patt, 55 ITR 637) — so the benefit of any doubt must go to the assessee.
For the Revenue
  • The transfers here were direct. Cash was given by the husband to the wife; the only question is whether the income from the assets into which the cash was converted is derived "indirectly" from the transfer.
  • It plainly is. "The incomes realised either as dividends from shares or as interest from deposits are income indirectly received in respect of the transfer of cash directly made."
  • That position "clearly emerges from the plain language of the section" — the expression "as arises directly or indirectly" would be robbed of all content if only directly-derived income were clubbed.
  • The nexus here is real and traceable: it is possible to point to the very rupees gifted and the very shares and deposits bought with them. Prem Bhat Parakh is different, because there the income arose from a new source — the firm's profits, arrived at through a partnership admission — and the connection was therefore genuinely remote.
4 · Decision

The appeals were dismissed. The dividends and interest were income from assets "indirectly" transferred by the assessee to his wife, and were properly included in his total income.

  1. The Revenue's contention was accepted as sound. "The assets transferred in this case is the gift of cash amounts made by the assessee to his wife. The transfers in question are direct transfers. But those assets … were invested either in shares or otherwise. Hence it was urged on behalf of the revenue that the incomes realised either as dividends from shares or as interest from deposits are income indirectly received in respect of the transfer of cash directly made. This contention of the revenue appears to be sound. That position clearly emerges from the plain language of the section." (reader p. 166.)
  2. Dr Pal's "direct only" argument was rejected as making the statute redundant. "We are unable to accept this contention as sound. Otherwise the expression 'as arises directly or indirectly' in Section 16(3)(a) would become redundant. The net cast by Section 16(3)(a)(iii) includes not merely the income that arises directly from the assets transferred but also that arises indirectly from the assets transferred." (reader p. 167.)
  3. But a nexus is required — and it existed here. "We are in agreement with the contention of Dr Pal that the income that can be brought to tax under Section 16(3)(a)(iii) must have a nexus with the assets transferred directly or indirectly. But in this case the income with which we are concerned has a nexus with the assets transferred." (reader p. 167.)
  4. Prem Bhat Parakh was distinguished on its facts. The Court set out that case in full — the assessee, a partner with a 7-anna share, retired from the firm on 1 July 1954; he then gifted ₹75,000 to each of his four sons, three of whom were minors; the firm was reconstituted from 2 July 1954 with the major son as a partner and the minors admitted to the benefits of partnership; the Tribunal found the minors' capital came from the gifts. The Supreme Court there held the connection "a remote one": "The income of the minors arose as a result of their admission to the benefits of the partnership. It is true that they were admitted to the benefits of the partnership because of the contribution made by them. But there is no nexus between the transfer of the assets and the income in question … The connection between the transfer of assets and the income must be proximate. The income in question must arise as a result of the transfer and not in some manner connected with it." Having quoted that passage, the Court in Mohini Thapar concluded: "The ratio of that decision is inapplicable to the facts of the present case."
  5. Result. "In the result, these appeals fail and they are dismissed" (reader p. 167).
5 · Principle evolved
  1. "Indirectly" is not surplusage. The words "as arises directly or indirectly" in s. 16(3)(a) — and, correspondingly, the words "as arises directly or indirectly" in s. 64(1) — extend the clause to income arising indirectly from the assets transferred. A construction which confines the clause to income springing directly from the transferred asset is wrong because it renders those words redundant.
  2. Income from a substituted asset is clubbed. Where cash is gifted and the transferee buys shares or makes deposits with it, the dividend and interest are income arising indirectly from the transfer. The identity of the asset may change; the nexus survives the change.
  3. But the nexus must be proximate. The income "must arise as a result of the transfer and not in some manner connected with it" (adopting Prem Bhat Parakh). The line is between a substituted asset (which is within the section) and a new source of income created by the transferee's independent act (which is not). In Prem Bhat Parakh the minors' income arose from a partnership into which they had been admitted — a new source; in Mohini Thapar it arose from the very money gifted.
  4. Strict construction and the anti-avoidance purpose are reconciled. The case expressly relies on Keshavlal Lallubhai Patt for strict construction (through the passage quoted from Prem Bhat Parakh) and yet finds for the Revenue, because on these facts the words were satisfied. Read with Thomas, the pair establishes the method: identify the words, give them their full content, and then test the facts against the required nexus.
  5. The "relatable to" idea of s. 64(1A) is the same idea. The later statutory formula in s. 64(1A) — income "relatable to" income already clubbed — is Parliament's attempt to codify exactly this proximity requirement.
6 · Why it matters today
  • It is the authority for the everyday proposition that a gift of money to a spouse does not achieve anything: whatever she buys with it, the income follows the gift back to the husband. In practice this is the most frequently applied holding of the four cases.
  • It supplies the limiting principle as well: the chain cannot be followed indefinitely. Once the transferee has created a genuinely new source of income, the nexus fails. That is the answer to argue where the department tries to follow the money through several independent transactions.
  • It is the best case in the reader for the "justifiability" discussion. It shows a court willing to give the anti-avoidance purpose full effect where the words support it, and equally willing to insist that the purpose cannot substitute for the words.
  • Together with Thomas, it forms a matched pair on the method of Chapter V: timing (Thomas) and nexus (Thapar) are the two questions the chapter always asks; both cases are Supreme Court; both are under the 1922 Act; both map directly onto s. 64(1)(iv).

Mapping: s. 16(3)(a)(iii) of the 1922 Act → s. 64(1)(iv) of the 1961 Act; the words "as arises directly or indirectly" survive in the opening words of s. 64(1), so the ratio is unaffected by the re-enactment.

"Batta Kalyani and Mokashi are not in conflict on the architecture of the proviso: both hold that it contains two cumulative requirements and that the assessee must establish both. ◆11.3 The synthesis — the two nexuses

Read together, the four cases reduce the whole of Chapter V to two questions. This is the intellectual spine of the topic and the best possible conclusion to an essay-type answer.

Nexus 1 — the legal nexus: is the case within the words?

Does the statutory description fit the facts? Was there a transfer? Was the transferee a spouse at both material times? Was the transfer for adequate consideration? Does the individual hold a substantial interest in the concern? Are both limbs of the proviso satisfied?

The governing authority is Thomas: the section "creates an artificial income and must be strictly construed", so the Revenue must bring the case within the words and cannot supply a missing condition by reference to the purpose. Applied in Thomas itself to defeat the Revenue.

The legal nexus is a question of law, and it is where the marks are most easily earned, because it requires only the statute and one case.

Nexus 2 — the factual nexus: is the connection proximate?

Is the income really derived from the thing transferred? Here the words "directly or indirectly" pull one way and the requirement of proximity pulls the other.

The governing authority is Mohini Thapar: substituted assets are within the clause, because otherwise "directly or indirectly" would be redundant; but "the connection between the transfer of assets and the income must be proximate", and Prem Bhat Parakh shows where the line falls — a new source created by the transferee's own act is too remote.

The factual nexus is a question of fact, and it is where Batta Kalyani and Mokashi were both decided — on the absence of material to prove the second limb of the proviso.

Model conclusion for an essay answer on the topic

"The four cases disclose a single method. The court first asks the legal question — whether the words of the section reach the facts — and answers it strictly, because the income with which the section deals is artificial (Thomas). It then asks the factual question — whether the income in truth flows from the transfer — and answers it purposively, giving full weight to 'directly or indirectly' while insisting that the connection be proximate (Thapar). Where the statutory language contains an exception designed to protect genuine earning capacity, the court reads that exception so as to make it work, but refuses to relax the conditions the legislature attached to it (Kalyani and Mokashi). The result is a chapter which defeats the tax planner without catching the ordinary family transaction — which is precisely the balance that the word 'justifiability' in the syllabus invites the student to assess."

◆12.2 December 2024 — Question 3 (Mr ATEESH / Ms TANTO)

Question 3 · LB-505, December 2024 · 20 marks

Clubbings. 64(1)(ii)ReassessmentRemedies

"Mr. ATEESH started an e-business to sell groceries online using domain ATEESH.COM. His wife Ms. TANTO also carried an e-business of selling fruits and vegetables. Place of business were common for both husband and wife. In this arrangement, both of them employed each other for rendering their services. The annual income of husband and wife was Rs. 7,80,000 and Rs. 8,60,000 respectively. The Assessing Officer (AO), clubbed the income from salary of both, with each other. The AO issued a notice of income escaping assessment with evidences where the transfer transaction between both the businesses are shown under the head of sales in books of accounts. The AO computed the income of Mr. ATEESH as 150% and the same was applied to Ms. TANTO as well. Mr. ATEESH and Ms. TANTO have planned to challenge the actions of Assessing Officer. As a tax lawyer, advise them how to proceed following various legal provisions and landmark cases under Income Tax Act, 1961."

Model answer

1 · What is really in issue. Four distinct questions are bundled in this problem, and a good answer separates them at the outset: (a) whether the salaries paid by each spouse to the other are liable to be clubbed at all; (b) if so, in whose hands they are to be clubbed — because the Assessing Officer has clubbed each salary with the other, which is exactly what the section forbids; (c) whether the Assessing Officer could lawfully reopen the assessments under the escapement provisions; and (d) whether the 150% enhancement of income has any legal basis. The advice must deal with all four, because a client who wins on (b) and (d) but loses on (a) still needs to know what to do.

2 · The governing provision. Section 64(1)(ii) provides that in computing the total income of any individual there shall be included all such income as arises directly or indirectly to the spouse of such individual by way of salary, commission, fees or any other form of remuneration, whether in cash or in kind, from a concern in which such individual has a substantial interest. Its proviso excludes remuneration which is solely attributable to the technical or professional qualifications and knowledge of the spouse. And where both spouses have a substantial interest in the concern and both receive remuneration from it, the Explanation to the clause requires the remuneration of both to be included in the total income of that spouse whose total income, excluding such remuneration, is greater; once so included, it continues in that spouse's hands in later years unless the Assessing Officer, after hearing the spouse, directs otherwise.

3 · Applying the five conditions (see §3.1 for the full checklist):

ConditionApplication to Mr ATEESH and Ms TANTO
Individual assesseeSatisfied — both are individuals.
"Concern"Satisfied. Each e-business is a concern: per J.M. Mokashi (reader pp. 159–160) the word covers every entity carrying on business or profession, "whether owned by a company, partnership or individual", and expressly includes a proprietary establishment. ATEESH.COM and TANTO's business each qualify.
"Substantial interest"Satisfied on both sides. Each spouse owns 100% of his or her own business, and per Mokashi (reader p. 160) Explanation 2 sets only the lowest limit of 20%; "an individual entitled to cent per cent of the profits of a concern is a person having substantial interest within the ordinary meaning of the expression itself."
Income to the spouse, by way of salary / commission / fees / other remunerationSatisfied on the facts stated — "both of them employed each other for rendering their services", and the payments are salary. Note the limitation: only remuneration is clubbed. If, on examination of the books, the payments turn out to be shares of profit or transfers of stock at cost, they are not "remuneration" and clause (ii) does not reach them at all.
Proviso not attractedOn the stated facts, not attracted. Neither spouse is described as possessing any technical or professional qualification, and there is no finding that the salary was solely attributable to the application of such knowledge. Applying Batta Kalyani (reader pp. 155–156) the qualification need not be a degree — expertise would do — but applying Mokashi (reader pp. 161–164) it must be the qualification necessary for the work actually done, and in either case limb (ii) is a question of fact on which the assessee bears the burden. Selling groceries and vegetables online does not supply it.

4 · The fatal error in the Assessing Officer's order: mutual clubbing. The Assessing Officer "clubbed the income from salary of both, with each other". That is a double inclusion of the same two sums, and it is precisely the situation the Explanation to s. 64(1)(ii) was inserted to prevent. Where both spouses have a substantial interest and both draw remuneration, the remuneration of both must be included in the total income of the one spouse whose total income excluding such remuneration is greater. On the figures — ₹7,80,000 for Mr ATEESH and ₹8,60,000 for Ms TANTO — the comparison (excluding the salaries in question) must be made, and on the stated figures it is Ms TANTO whose income is greater; the whole of both salaries must therefore be clubbed in her hands, and Mr ATEESH's assessment must be relieved of the clubbing altogether. That is the first and strongest plank of the advice, and it is worth stating plainly: the client's best point is not that the department was wrong to club, but that it clubbed in the wrong hands and twice over.

5 · The 150% enhancement has no legal basis. Nothing in the Act permits an Assessing Officer to compute a person's income at "150%" of the returned figure by applying a flat percentage. Income is computed under the five heads on the basis of accounts and evidence, and an assessment must be of the income which the officer finds, not of a penal multiple of the income returned. Even a best-judgment assessment under s. 144 must rest on a basis disclosed to the assessee and applied with material; an arbitrary percentage applied uniformly to both spouses, and applied to a business for which the only "evidence" consists of entries in the other spouse's books, is the very kind of conjecture that appellate authorities strike down. Two further points sharpen the attack: (i) if the officer has already clubbed both salaries, an enhancement of each spouse's income by 150% and clubbing amounts to taxing the same rupees three times; and (ii) the "transfer transactions between both the businesses … shown under the head of sales" is a matter for verification of the business income — it cannot justify inflating salary income, which is a fixed contractual figure.

6 · The reassessment. The notice of "income escaping assessment" is issued under ss. 147/148. It can be sustained only if the Assessing Officer has reasons to believe, recorded in writing, that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment, and the belief must rest on tangible material and not on a mere change of opinion on the same facts already disclosed (the settled position under the Kelvinator line of cases). Where the assessee has disclosed the two businesses, the common premises, the cross-employments and the salaries in the returns, a notice founded on those very facts is vulnerable: the officer is seeking to take a different view of disclosed material, not to bring to tax something concealed. Section 148A's procedure — information, show cause, and a reasoned order — must be complied with, and the assessee is entitled to test the reasons on a writ petition, since jurisdiction is in issue.

8 · The advice, in the order a lawyer would give it.

  1. File the return of income under protest and respond to the notice within the time allowed, seeking the recorded reasons and a copy of the s. 148A order. Do not allow the deadline to pass: the remedy of appeal is lost by default, and a writ petition is the harder route.
  2. Appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) under s. 246A against the assessment, taking three grounds: (i) mutual clubbing is impermissible — the Explanation requires clubbing in the hands of the spouse with the greater income, here Ms TANTO, and only there; (ii) the 150% enhancement is without jurisdiction and in any event double counts the same income; (iii) the reopening is founded on a change of opinion on fully disclosed facts and is bad for want of jurisdiction.
  3. Keep the second appeal to the Tribunal (s. 253) in reserve, and consider a writ petition under Art. 226 of the Constitution if the s. 148A order is plainly without jurisdiction, since a jurisdictional defect can be challenged without waiting for the appellate chain.
  4. On the merits of the clubbing itself, canvass the proviso but do not build the case on it. If Ms TANTO can show a genuine qualification — for instance a degree or diploma in the relevant field, or demonstrated expertise — and that the remuneration is comparable to what an outsider would be paid for the same work (the reasonableness test of Mokashi, reader p. 163), the exception may be available. On the facts given, that is a long shot; candour with the client is part of the advice.
  5. Restructure the arrangement prospectively. The lesson of s. 64(1)(ii) is that the salary flowing from a concern in which the other spouse has a substantial interest is taxable in the hands of the spouse with the greater income. If the payments are genuinely commercial and each business is separately owned and staffed by outsiders, clubbing does not arise. If the cross-employment is retained, both spouses should budget for tax in the higher-income spouse's hands, and remember s. 65: the spouse whose income is clubbed is liable to pay the portion of the other's tax attributable to it, and may recover it from the other.

9 · Conclusion. The Assessing Officer is right in principle that s. 64(1)(ii) applies — the businesses are "concerns", each spouse's 100% interest is a substantial interest, the payments are remuneration, and the proviso is not made out — but wrong in method on two counts: the clubbing must be in the hands of the spouse with the greater income and not mutually, and income cannot be computed at 150% of the returned figure. The correct order would be: club both salaries in Ms TANTO's total income, deleting the clubbing from Mr ATEESH's assessment; assess each spouse's business income on the basis of the accounts, after due enquiry into the cross-entries; and abandon the enhancement. That is also the order the appellate authority should be asked to make.

Also he claimed dividend of Rs. 40,000 and Agriculture income Rs. 2,00,000 as exemptions from tax. The Assessing Officer rejected all the claims except an exemption for agricultural income. Being a professional tax practitioner, suggest what shall be included in total Income of Mr. JAAM ? Prepare the arguments and further justify them in the light of relevant judgments."

Model answer (condensed — the question spans several topics)

(a) The maintenance payments are not deductible. The claim of ₹4,80,000 to each of the two women rests on the doctrine of diversion of income at source, and it fails on that doctrine. Where an obligation is created by the assessee himself — or arises from a decree passed on the basis of a pre-existing obligation to maintain — the payment is an application of income after it has accrued and not a diversion of it before accrual; the income is therefore first computed in the assessee's hands and the payment is not deducted. A statutory or decree-based obligation to maintain a wife or a parent does not carry the income away from the assessee: it discharges his personal obligation out of his own income. The fact that the step-mother's maintenance is charged on the properties does not alter the character of the payment — a charge secures the payment, it does not create a new earner. This is also why the Act contains no head of deduction for maintenance, the only statutory reliefs of that character being those Parliament has expressly created.

(b) The agricultural income of ₹2,00,000 is exempt under s. 10(1) — as the Assessing Officer accepted — but, where the assessee has non-agricultural income above the basic exemption, it must be aggregated for rate purposes under the partial-integration scheme. Exemption from tax is not the same as exclusion from the computation of the rate.

(c) The dividend of ₹40,000 is fully taxable at the slab rate; the exemption formerly available has been withdrawn, and the only relief is the deduction of interest actually incurred to earn the dividend, subject to the statutory ceiling of 20% of the dividend income. The assessee's claim of the whole ₹40,000 as exempt fails.

(d) The card-game income of ₹2,00,000 is winnings from card games and is not "speculative income" in the technical sense. Winnings of this character — lotteries, crossword puzzles, races including horse races, card games, gambling or betting of any form — are taxed under s. 115BB at the flat rate prescribed by that section (30%), without any deduction, without any Chapter VI-A relief, and without set-off of any loss, and without the benefit of the basic exemption. This is a classic examination trap and it must be stated affirmatively.

(e) The joint property. Mr JAAM and his step-mother are successors in respect of a property yielding ₹8,00,000 a year. Where a property is owned by two or more persons with definite and ascertainable shares, each is assessed on his own share of the income — the rule of s. 26 for house property — and the share must be determined on the facts (in the absence of evidence of unequal shares, half each). The step-mother's half is her income, not his, and is certainly not to be clubbed with his: clubbing requires a provision of Chapter V, and a step-mother is not within any clause of s. 64(1). This is where the question touches Topic 5: the reflex is to club the co-owner's share, and the reflex is wrong. If the property were the house property of the assessee, the notional income would be computed under s. 22 and the standard deduction of 30% under s. 24(a) allowed; if it were let, actual rent would be taken.

(f) The separation decree and Topic 5. One point in the problem is directly relevant to clubbing. There is a "decree of separation" between Mr JAAM and his wife. That fact matters for the exception in s. 64(1)(iv) and in s. 27(i): a transfer of assets to a spouse "in connection with an agreement to live apart" is outside the clubbing provision, and a house transferred in such circumstances does not make the transferor a deemed owner. So if any assets were transferred to the wife, the decree is the assessee's protection. Conversely, if a mere decree of judicial separation does not dissolve the marriage, the relationship still subsists for the purpose of Philip John Plasket Thomas's double-subsistence rule — and an answer should note both possibilities rather than assume one.

◆13.1 The whole topic in ten propositions

  1. Chapter V (ss. 60–64) attributes income to a person who did not earn it, in four defined situations, and s. 65 makes the earner liable for the tax attributable to it. It is a rule of computation, not of ownership.
  2. Three mechanisms: income transferred without the assets (s. 60); transfer of assets with a power reserved (ss. 61–63); relationship-based attribution (s. 64(1), 64(1A), 64(2)).
  3. s. 60 applies "whether revocable or not and whether effected before or after the commencement of this Act", where there is no transfer of the assets. s. 61 applies to a transfer of assets which keeps a string attached; the proviso excludes transfers irrevocable during the life of the beneficiary or transferee (or pre-1961 transfers irrevocable for more than six years), provided the transferor takes no direct or indirect benefit. s. 62 fixes the date; s. 63 defines "transfer" and "revocable" very widely.
  4. s. 64(1)(ii) clubbing requires: an individual; a concern; substantial interest (≥20%, a floor); income to the spouse by way of remuneration; and failure of the proviso, whose two limbs are cumulative. Where both spouses qualify, both salaries are clubbed in the hands of the spouse with the greater total income.
  5. "Concern" includes a profession and a proprietary concern; 100% is a substantial interest — J.M. Mokashi, reader pp. 159–160.
  6. "Technical or professional qualification" need not be a degree from a recognised body (Batta Kalyani), but on the stricter and fuller view it must be the qualification necessary for the particular profession or job, and knowledge and experience alone are not enough (Mokashi). On either view, the income must be solely attributable to it — a question of fact on which the assessee bears the burden.
  7. s. 64(1)(iv) clubs the income from assets transferred to the spouse otherwise than for adequate consideration or in connection with an agreement to live apart; the relationship must subsist at both material times (Thomas); "indirectly" reaches substituted assets provided the nexus is proximate (Thapar); and the clause is subject to s. 27(i) for house property.
  8. s. 64(1)(vi)–(viii) extend the same idea to the son's wife (transfers on or after 1-6-1973) and to transfers to any person for the benefit of the spouse or the son's wife, proportionately. s. 64(1A) clubs a minor child's income with the higher-income parent, subject to the s. 80U, manual-work and talent exceptions, and ₹1,500 per child under s. 10(32).
  9. s. 64(2) deems the conversion of separate property into HUF property to be a transfer through the family; the income is the individual's "and not the family's"; and after a partition, partial or total, the income from the spouse's share is taxed on the spouse.
  10. The judicial method is two-stage: a strict (legal) nexus question — is the case within the words, the income being "artificial"? — and a purposive (factual) nexus question — is the connection proximate? Thomas and Thapar are the two Supreme Court halves of that method; Kalyani and Mokashi are its application to a proviso.

Held: "technical or professional qualification" need not come from a recognised body — expertise will do; but the second limb (income "solely attributable") is a question of fact, the assessee had no evidence, so the salary was clubbed. Revenue wins.
Case 30 · J.M. Mokashi (Bom, 1994)
Practising cardiologist pays ₹8,100 to his wife (first-year Arts) as receptionist-cum-accountant. Held: "concern" includes a profession and a proprietary concern; 100% is a substantial interest (Explanation 2 is a floor); "qualification" means the qualification necessary for the particular profession, and knowledge and experience alone are not enough; the limbs are cumulative; the second limb permits apportionment to the reasonable part. Expressly dissents from Batta Kalyani; follows D. Rajagopal. Revenue wins.
Case 31 · Mohini Thapar (SC, 1972)
Cash gifts to the wife, who buys shares and makes deposits; dividends and interest of ₹21,225 clubbed for five years. Held: "as arises directly or indirectly" reaches income from substituted assets — otherwise the words would be redundant — but the connection between transfer and income must be proximate; Prem Bhat Parakh (minors' firm income) distinguished as remote. Revenue wins.
Final instruction

If you remember nothing else: the topic is one idea in five sections — the law refuses to let a person separate the tax from the income he controls — and it is applied with two tests — is the case within the words, and is the connection proximate? Every case in the reader is an application of one or other of those tests, and every problem you will be set is answered by walking the conditions of the relevant clause in order.
