🎯 THE ONE IDEA THAT CARRIES THIS ENTIRE UNIT

A paper signature does four jobs at once: it proves identity (who signed), approval (they agreed), integrity (the document has not changed since), and non‑repudiation (they cannot later deny signing). A screen cannot do this by itself. Every provision in this Unit is a piece of legal engineering that rebuilds one of those four functions in an electronic environment — and every case you will read is a dispute about which of the four broke down.

If you remember one sentence for the exam: the IT Act does not say "an electronic record is as good as paper"; it says an electronic record shall not be denied legal effect merely because it is electronic (s.4), and that an electronic signature shall not be denied legal effect merely because it is electronic (s.5) — a technology‑neutral, functional‑equivalence model borrowed from UNCITRAL, operationalised through s.3 (digital signature), s.3A (reliable electronic signature), and proven in court through the s.65B / s.63 BSA certificate.

1The mischief: why e‑commerce needed functional equivalence

Foundational concept  Read this before any section. It explains why ss.3–15 exist at all, and it is the opening paragraph of almost every "critically examine" answer.

1.1 The problem: four functions of a handwritten signature

A handwritten signature on paper is not legally special because of the ink. It is special because it simultaneously performs four functions, each of which can be independently challenged in court. When commerce moved to networks, all four broke down:

FunctionHow paper achieves itWhy electronic form broke itStatutory fix in the IT Act
1. Identity
who signed?		 The handwriting is a unique, personally verifiable attribute of a specific human being. A message on a wire is just bits. Anyone with the device can generate it. Nothing on the face of the record identifies a person. s.2(1)(f) asymmetric cryptosystem + key pair (s.2(1)(x)); s.3(4) private key unique to the subscriber; s.35 issue of the Electronic Signature Certificate.
2. Approval
did they consent?		 The act of signing is an outward act of assent. A click, an OTP reply or an automated system output does not visibly manifest assent. s.5 (legal recognition of electronic signatures) read with the Explanation defining "signed" and "signature"; s.11 (attribution); s.10A (validity of e‑contracts).
3. Integrity
is it unchanged?		 Erasures, overwriting and insertions are visible to the eye. A digital file can be altered silently, perfectly and undetectably — no forensic trace remains. s.3(2) hash function; s.14 secure electronic record; s.3A(2)(c)–(d) detectability of alteration; s.16 security procedure.
4. Non‑repudiation
can they deny it later?		 The signatory cannot credibly disown their own handwriting. "That was not my email" / "my password was stolen" / "an auto‑reply sent it" become cheap, complete defences. s.42 control of private key; s.3A(2)(a)–(b) linkage and control; s.11 attribution; s.85B / s.87 BSA presumptions; s.65B / s.63 BSA certificate.

1.2 The two Model Laws and the Indian answer

The international community answered this problem with functional equivalence: the law should not ask "does this look like paper?" but "does it do what paper did?" India, unlike many countries, did not merely copy a statute — it enacted a new kind of statute, one that regulates both the conduct (ss.3–15) and the trusted third parties who vouch for identity (ss.17–42).

11Offences against the authentication infrastructure — ss.71, 73 and 74

Syllabus item 11  The syllabus expressly requires that "publishing false digital signatures and publication of digital signatures for fraudulent purposes are offences under the Act (ss.73, 74)".

s.73 · PENALTY FOR PUBLISHING ELECTRONIC SIGNATURE CERTIFICATE FALSE IN CERTAIN PARTICULARS · VERBATIM

Penalty for publishing Electronic Signature Certificate false in certain particulars

(1) No person shall publish an Electronic Signature Certificate or otherwise make it available to any other person with the knowledge that—
  (a) the Certifying Authority listed in the certificate has not issued it; or
  (b) the subscriber listed in the certificate has not accepted it; or
  (c) the certificate has been revoked or suspended,
unless such publication is for the purpose of verifying an electronic signature created prior to such suspension or revocation.
(2) Any person who contravenes the provisions of sub‑section (1) shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, or with both.
Note the pre‑2008 wording: the original section referred to a "Digital Signature Certificate" and to "verifying a digital signature created prior to such suspension or revocation". The 2008 Amendment substituted "electronic signature" throughout. The structure and the penalty are unchanged.
s.74 · PUBLICATION FOR FRAUDULENT PURPOSE · VERBATIM

Publication for fraudulent purpose

Whoever knowingly creates, publishes or otherwise makes available an Electronic Signature Certificate for any fraudulent or unlawful purpose shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, or with both.
Note: s.74 has no sub‑sections — it is a single‑sentence offence. It is broader than s.73 in one respect (it covers creation, not merely publication) and narrower in another (it requires a fraudulent or unlawful purpose, which s.73 does not).
📜 s.71 · PENALTY FOR MISREPRESENTATION — the companion offence

Whoever makes any misrepresentation to, or suppresses any material fact from, the Controller or the Certifying Authority for obtaining any licence or Electronic Signature Certificate, as the case may be, shall be punished with imprisonment for a term which may extend to two years, or with fine which may extend to one lakh rupees, or with both. Cite s.71 alongside ss.73 and 74 whenever the question is about offences against the authentication infrastructure — it is the upstream offence (obtaining the certificate by deceit) of which ss.73–74 are the downstream counterparts (misusing it).

11.2 Real examples of the two offences

s.73 scenarios
  • A person takes a suspended ESC belonging to a former colleague and, knowing it has been suspended, publishes it on a vendor portal to continue bidding in the former colleague's name. This is s.73(1)(c) — the publication is not for the purpose of verifying a signature created before the suspension.
  • A broker forges an ESC purporting to have been issued by a licensed CA, prints it and circulates it to banks, knowing the CA never issued it. This is s.73(1)(a).
  • An IT department generates an ESC for an employee who has never applied for or accepted it, and e‑mails it to clients knowing the employee has not accepted it. This is s.73(1)(b).
s.74 scenarios
  • A person creates a fake ESC in the name of a company director and uses it to sign a fraudulent share transfer — creation + fraudulent purpose.
  • An employee publishes his employer's ESC to a third party in order to bind the employer to a loan the employer never authorised.
  • A service provider makes available an expired ESC so that a back‑dated e‑tender appears timely.
✅ What these offences are really protecting

Sections 71, 73 and 74 protect the integrity of the trust chain, not the individual document. The whole PKI edifice rests on three propositions: that the CA is licensed and has issued the certificate (s.73(1)(a)); that the subscriber accepted it (s.73(1)(b), reinforced by s.36(c) and s.41); and that the certificate's current status is published and reliable (s.73(1)(c), reinforced by s.39). If any of those propositions can be defeated by a private person, the relying party's reliance — and the presumptions under BSA ss.87 and 89 — collapse. These are the criminal law's backstop for the civil law of electronic signature.

12Originator, addressee, attribution, acknowledgment, and despatch & receipt — ss.11–13

Syllabus item 12  These three sections are, in substance, Articles 13, 14 and 15 of the UNCITRAL MLEC 1996. Together they decide who sent an electronic record and when and where it was sent and received.

s.11 · ATTRIBUTION OF ELECTRONIC RECORDS · VERBATIM

Attribution of electronic records

An electronic record shall be attributed to the originator—
  (a) if it was sent by the originator himself;
  (b) by a person who had the authority to act on behalf of the originator in respect of that electronic record; or
  (c) by an information system programmed by or on behalf of the originator to operate automatically.
Two linked definitions: s.2(1)(za) "originator" means a person who sends, generates, receives or stores an electronic record, or provides any service with respect to it, but does not include an intermediary. s.2(1)(b) "addressee" means a person who is intended by the originator to receive the electronic record but does not include any intermediary.
🧩 The three limbs of s.11 — read them as three different kinds of agency
  • Limb (a) — direct act. The originator sends it himself. The simplest case: you type and send your own e‑mail.
  • Limb (b) — human agency. A person with authority to act on the originator's behalf sends it. This imports ordinary agency law: a secretary sending on instructions, a bank officer authorised to send a demand notice, an agent within actual or apparent authority. Without authority, the record is not attributed.
  • Limb (c) — automatic act. An information system programmed by or on behalf of the originator to operate automatically. This is the provision that makes auto‑replies, auto‑debits, e‑tender submissions, algorithmic trading messages and system‑generated confirmations attributable to the person who set the system up. Note the statutory phrase: the system must be programmed "by or on behalf of" the originator.
✅ Real examples of each limb
  • (a) A customer types her own transfer instruction in her net‑banking session and clicks send.
  • (b) A company's authorised signatory sends a purchase order from the company's shared mailbox; or a chartered accountant e‑files a client's return with the client's authority.
  • (c) A bank's auto‑debit for a loan EMI; an auto‑reply "your application has been received"; a standing instruction that transfers a fixed sum monthly; an e‑commerce order confirmation generated automatically by the seller's server.

12.1 Section 12 — acknowledgment of receipt

s.12 · ACKNOWLEDGMENT OF RECEIPT · VERBATIM (THREE SUB-SECTIONS)

Acknowledgment of receipt

(1) Where the originator has not agreed with the addressee that the acknowledgment of receipt of electronic record be given in a particular form or by a particular method, an acknowledgment may be given by—
  (a) any communication by the addressee, automated or otherwise; or
  (b) any conduct of the addressee, sufficient to indicate to the originator that the electronic record has been received.
(2) Where the originator has stipulated that the electronic record shall be binding only on receipt of an acknowledgment, then the electronic record shall be deemed not to have been sent, unless the acknowledgment has been received by the originator.
(3) Where the originator has not so stipulated and the acknowledgment has not been received by the originator within the time specified or agreed or, if no time has been specified or agreed, within a reasonable time, then the originator may give notice to the addressee stating that no acknowledgment has been received by him and specifying a reasonable time by which the acknowledgment must be received by him; and if the acknowledgment is not received within the aforesaid reasonable time, the originator may treat the electronic record as though it has never been sent, after giving notice to the addressee.
The three rules in one line each: (1) form‑free acknowledgment if nothing was agreed; (2) deemed never sent if the originator made the record binding only on acknowledgment and none is received; (3) a notice‑and‑cure mechanism where the originator did not so stipulate.

12.2 Section 13 — time and place of despatch and receipt

s.13 · TIME AND PLACE OF DESPATCH AND RECEIPT OF ELECTRONIC RECORD · VERBATIM (FIVE SUB-SECTIONS)

Time and place of despatch and receipt of electronic record

(1) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, the despatch of an electronic record occurs when it enters a computer resource outside the control of the originator.
(2) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, the receipt of an electronic record occurs when—
  (a) if the addressee has designated a computer resource for the purpose of receiving such electronic record, receipt occurs—
    (i) when the electronic record enters the designated computer resource; or
    (ii) if the electronic record is sent to a computer resource of the addressee that is not the designated computer resource, when the electronic record is retrieved by the addressee; or
  (b) if the addressee has not designated a computer resource for the purpose of receiving such electronic record, receipt occurs when the electronic record enters any computer resource of the addressee.
(3) Save as otherwise agreed between the originator and the addressee, an electronic record is deemed to be despatched at the place where the originator has his place of business, and is deemed to be received at the place where the addressee has his place of business.
(4) The provisions of sub‑section (2) shall apply notwithstanding the fact that the place where the computer resource is located may be different from the place where the electronic record is deemed to have been received under sub‑section (3).
(5) For the purposes of this section—
  (a) if the originator or the addressee has more than one place of business, the place of business is that which has the closest relationship to the underlying transaction or, where there is no underlying transaction, the principal place of business;
  (b) if the originator or the addressee does not have a place of business, reference is to be made to the usual place of residence; and
  (c) if the originator or the addressee is a body corporate, its usual place of residence is the place of registration.
The headline: s.13(1) and (2) displace the postal rule. Under the postal rule (the general Contract Act rule), acceptance is complete on posting. Under s.13, receipt is complete only on entry into the designated resource (or retrieval, or entry into any resource of the addressee). For electronic contracts, the mailbox rule is statutorily displaced.
⚠️ Two points on the wording of s.13(2)

The three receipt rules, in the order you should state them:
(i) Where the addressee has designated a computer resource and the record goes to that resource — receipt on entry into the designated resource.
(ii) Where a resource has been designated but the record is sent to a different, non‑designated resource of the addressee — receipt on retrieval by the addressee. This is the rule that catches the record dumped into a spam folder or a personal inbox when the designated address was a corporate one.
(iii) Where no resource has been designated — receipt when the record enters any computer resource of the addressee.
Every sub‑section of s.13 opens with "Save as otherwise agreed", so the parties may always contract out of the whole scheme. This mirrors Article 15(2) of the MLEC.

12.3 The postal rule and Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia — the statutory analogue

⚖️ Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas, AIR 1966 SC 543
Facts (brief)
The plaintiff offered, by telephone from Ahmedabad, to sell certain goods to the defendant at Delhi. The defendant accepted the offer by telephone from Delhi. The question was where the contract was made — which determined which court had jurisdiction.
Issue
Whether the general rule for contracts by post (that the contract is made where the acceptance is posted) applies to contracts made by telephone or other instantaneous communication.
Held
The Supreme Court distinguished instantaneous communication from post. Where the parties are in direct, instantaneous communication (telephone), the contract is made where the acceptance is received, not where it is spoken. The postal rule is confined to non‑instantaneous modes.
Principle
The instantaneous‑communication doctrine: for communications where the offeror can know at once whether the acceptance has been received, the place of contract is the place of receipt of the acceptance.

Why it matters for s.13: Kedia laid down the common‑law analogue for electronic communications; s.13 now codifies it for electronic records. Where Kedia decided that an electronic/telephonic contract is made where acceptance is received, s.13(2)–(3) achieves the same result by fixing receipt at entry into the designated computer resource and deeming the record received at the addressee's place of business. The postal rule is statutorily displaced for electronic contracts. The Delhi High Court revisited the doctrine in World Wrestling Entertainment v. Reshma Collections (Unit IX), applying the same reasoning to internet‑based communications and the situs of the cause of action.

🎓 Model answer — "Short note: Time and place of despatch and receipt of electronic record" (Nov–Dec 2025, Q.8(b))

Structure (this is a short note — aim for 250–350 words):

  1. Source. s.13, IT Act, 2000 (substantially Article 15 of the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce, 1996).
  2. Time of despatch — s.13(1). When the record enters a computer resource outside the control of the originator.
  3. Time of receipt — s.13(2). If the addressee designated a resource: on entry into it. If not designated (or sent to a non‑designated resource): on retrieval by the addressee, or on entry into any computer resource of the addressee.
  4. Place — s.13(3). Despatched at the originator's place of business; received at the addressee's place of business. Under s.13(5): more than one place → the one with the closest relationship to the underlying transaction, failing which the principal place of business; no place of business → usual place of residence; body corporate → place of registration.
  5. s.13(4). Applies notwithstanding that the computer resource is physically located elsewhere — so a server in Singapore does not make the receipt occur in Singapore.
  6. Effect. The postal rule is displaced; this codifies the instantaneous‑communication doctrine of Bhagwan Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas, AIR 1966 SC 543 (revisited in World Wrestling Entertainment v. Reshma Collections).
  7. Consequence. Jurisdiction in an e‑contract dispute ordinarily lies where the addressee has his place of business — subject always to the parties' agreement, since every sub‑section of s.13 opens "Save as otherwise agreed".

13Authentication revisited: Syed Asifuddin and Diebold Systems

Supporting authorities  Both cases are fully briefed in Unit I of this companion set. They are reproduced here in short form because the DU Unit II case material directs that they be read alongside this Unit's authentication provisions — Syed Asifuddin defines the technological environment in which authentication happens, and Diebold supplies the limiting principle on how far the IT Act's definitions travel.

CASE‑MAT · SUPPORTING AUTHORITY 1

Syed Asifuddin v. State of Andhra Pradesh

2006 (1) ALD (Cri) 96 2005 CriLJ 4314 Andhra Pradesh High Court 29 July 2005

1Facts

Reliance Infocomm launched a subsidised handset scheme — the Dhirubhai Ambani Pioneer Offer — with a three‑year network lock‑in. Employees of TATA Indicom allegedly re‑programmed the Electronic Serial Number (ESN) of Reliance‑locked Samsung / LG handsets so that subscribers could migrate networks. An FIR was registered under ss.409, 420 and 120‑B of the IPC, s.65 of the IT Act, and s.63 of the Copyright Act, 1957.

2Issues

  1. Is a cell phone a "computer" within s.2(1)(i) of the IT Act?
  2. Does altering the ESN amount to "tampering with computer source code" under s.65?
  3. Does it infringe copyright in the embedded computer programme?

3Reasoning

The Court traced the technical architecture of a cell phone — the circuit board and the microprocessor converting analogue ↔ digital signals — and held that this satisfies the definition of "computer": "a high‑speed data processing device… performing logical, arithmetic and memory functions by manipulation of electronic, magnetic or optical impulses". The ESN, MIN and SID were held to fall within "computer source code" — defined in the Explanation to s.65 as the "listing of programmes, computer commands, design and layout and programme analysis of computer resource in any form" — because they are permanently or semi‑permanently programmed into the handset. In reaching the "source code" point the Court relied on Rowland & MacDonald, Information Technology Law (Cavendish, 1997).

4Holding

A cell phone is a "computer" within s.2(1)(i); reprogramming the ESN prima facie attracts s.65, since the second respondent was found to be maintaining the source code, satisfying the disjunctive "required to be kept or maintained by law" test; and the copyright charge under s.63 of the Copyright Act was also held not liable to be quashed at the FIR stage.

Principle evolved
  1. An expansive, purposive reading of "computer" — the device need not look like a desktop; what matters is the data‑processing function.
  2. Identifiers embedded in hardware (ESN, MIN, SID) can be "computer source code".
  3. Statutory definitions are context‑bound — the very point on which Diebold distinguishes it.

5Relevance to Unit II

The authentication infrastructure operates inside computer resources. Before you can ask whether an electronic record was authenticated, you must know that the device holding it is a "computer" — Syed Asifuddin is the authority that makes the everyday mobile phone part of that legal universe. It is also the leading authority on s.65, the tampering offence, which is the criminal counterpart of the integrity function of a signature.

CASE‑MAT · SUPPORTING AUTHORITY 2

Diebold Systems Pvt. Ltd. v. The Commissioner

ILR 2005 KAR 2210 Karnataka High Court 31 January 2005 Tier 3

1Facts & Issue

The question was the sales‑tax classification of an ATM — in substance, whether an ATM is a "computer" for the purposes of the relevant fiscal Entry. The Revenue relied on the IT Act's enlarged definition of "computer" to argue that an ATM fell within the higher‑rated category.

2Holding

The Karnataka High Court held that the enlarged definition of "computer" under the IT Act cannot be transplanted to interpret an Entry under a fiscal statute; an ATM was accordingly not treated as a "computer" for the purposes of the sales‑tax classification in issue.

3Arguments — both sides

  • Revenue: the IT Act definition is broad and purposive; Syed Asifuddin had already read "computer" expansively; an ATM performs logical, arithmetic and memory functions.
  • Assessee: a fiscal Entry must be read on its own terms and in the context of the taxing statute; definitions in one enactment cannot be imported into another with a different object; Syed Asifuddin was decided in the context of the IT Act's own penal scheme.
Principle evolved
  1. IT Act definitions are not automatically read into unrelated legislation. There is a limiting principle on functional equivalence: the technology‑neutral definitions of the IT Act operate within the four corners of that Act.
  2. Statutory definitions are context‑bound: a word defined for a cyber‑penal statute does not travel into a fiscal statute.
  3. Diebold distinguishes rather than overrules Syed Asifuddin — both remain good law, in their respective contexts.

4Relevance to Unit II

This is the cautionary authority for any argument that begins "the IT Act's definitions apply generally". When you argue that an electronic record or signature has a certain legal character, you must show that the statute in question permits it. Diebold is the answer to over‑enthusiastic functional‑equivalence arguments, and it is a favourite examiner trap precisely because students assume the IT Act's definitions are universal.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Contrast the two in one sentence: "Syed Asifuddin reads 'computer' purposively and expansively within the IT Act, so that a mobile phone is a computer and ESN reprogramming attracts s.65; Diebold Systems holds that the same enlarged definition cannot be imported into a fiscal statute, so that an ATM is not a 'computer' for sales‑tax classification. Together they establish that the Act's definitions are expansive but context‑bound."

14.2 The central critique — Shah & Srivastava, "Legislative Chaos"

📚 Hemali Shah & Aashish Srivastava, "Signature Provisions in the Amended Indian Information Technology Act 2000: Legislative Chaos", 43 Common Law World Review 208 (2014)

The thesis, in their words (as summarised in the DU reading list): India's shift from a rigid digital‑signature (PKI‑only) model to a technology‑neutral electronic‑signature model via the 2008 Amendment was implemented without adequate consequential clean‑up of the Act, producing a definitional inconsistency between "digital signature" (retained for CA / PKI purposes) and "electronic signature" (the broader umbrella term).

The four consequences they identify:

  1. Technological rigidity in the original design. The 2000 Act hard‑wired a single cryptographic method into the statutory definition of "digital signature" — a legislative miscalculation that "froze the law to a particular vintage of cryptography just as global commerce was moving toward biometric, mobile‑OTP and cloud‑based authentication."
  2. Two parallel regimes with no clean interface. s.3A cured the rigidity but left ss.3 and 3A as two parallel tracks "whose interplay with the CA‑licensing architecture (built exclusively around PKI) remains under‑theorised".
  3. Structural mismatch between statute and practice. Aadhaar e‑Sign and OTP authentication are today the dominant methods of "signing" in India — income tax filings, bank KYC, DigiLocker — yet the statutory scaffolding (ss.17–42) still speaks the language of Certifying Authorities issuing PKI‑based certificates. Vakul Sharma's later editions flag this; Parliament has not resolved it.
  4. Sub‑delegation risk. s.5's "manner prescribed by the Central Government" requirement means that the operative authentication framework lives in executive Rules (the CA Rules 2000 and the ESEATPR 2015) rather than in the Act — raising an argument of unconstitutional sub‑delegation and of instability, since Rules can be changed without Parliament.
📚 Christopher Reed, "Legally Binding Electronic Documents: Digital Signatures and Authentication", 35(1) International Lawyer 89 (2001)

The foundational comparative analysis that underlies the whole functional‑equivalence framing used in this Unit. Reed's core distinction: the authentication function — identifying the signer and indicating the intention to be bound — is separate from the technical mechanism chosen to achieve it. Once that separation is made, it follows that the law should regulate the function and remain indifferent to the mechanism. That is precisely the move s.3A makes and the one the original 2000 Act failed to make. Cite Reed in any answer that asks "is a digital signature legally necessary?"

14.4 Recent developments (through mid‑2026)

🆕 Five developments to cite
  1. Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (Act 47 of 2023), in force from 1 July 2024, replacing the Indian Evidence Act, 1872. s.63 BSA (successor to s.65B IEA) now requires, under s.63(4), a certificate signed by both the person in charge of the device or system and an "expert" — a materially heavier dual‑signature requirement than the old single‑certificate regime — in the prescribed Schedule format including a hash value. This directly modernises the s.87 / s.89 presumption framework.
  2. Section 61 BSA 2023 has been read by some commentators as restoring a degree of pre‑Anvar flexibility by recognising alternative modes of proof — an emerging, unsettled debate as of mid‑2026 ("Does s.61 BSA override Arjun Panditrao Khotkar?").
  3. Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — enacted 11 August 2023; the DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025, together with the constitution of the Data Protection Board of India and a phased 12–18‑month compliance timeline, with full commencement of the remaining provisions by 13 May 2027. This is the successor regime to the 2011 SPDI Rules (framed under s.43A IT Act) and originates definitionally from the "electronic record" / "digital form" architecture built in Unit II.
  4. The Aadhaar Act, 2016 and the e‑KYC / e‑Sign ecosystem continue to operate as the dominant practical authentication mechanism in India, illustrating the s.3A technology‑neutral pathway in real‑world use far more than the s.3 PKI/CA pathway envisaged in 2000.
  5. Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, as amended in 2026, continue to affect authentication / attribution obligations of "significant social media intermediaries", particularly traceability requirements — to be read alongside s.11 (attribution of electronic records).
🎓 Model exam conclusion (one paragraph — reuse it verbatim in any essay)
"The IT Act's authentication regime reflects a deliberate legislative choice — functional equivalence over formal identity — but its implementation has been uneven: the 2000 Act's PKI‑exclusive design (s.3) was technologically premature and had to be supplemented by the technology‑neutral s.3A in 2008, producing a dual‑track regime whose CA‑licensing infrastructure (ss.17–42) has not kept pace with the practical dominance of Aadhaar‑based and biometric authentication. The evidentiary counterpart of this framework — first s.65B of the Evidence Act, now s.63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 — has been judicially stabilised through Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer and Arjun Panditrao Khotkar, establishing certification as a mandatory condition precedent for secondary electronic evidence, now reinforced by the BSA's dual‑signature and hash‑value requirements. The doctrinal architecture is sound; what remains unresolved is institutional modernisation of the CA hierarchy and legislative clarification of the s.61 / s.63 BSA interface."

MAY–JUNE 2025Q.3 · Essay Explain the functions of public and private keys and describe the authentication process under the IT Act 2000. Are there shortcomings?
Structure

  2. Definitions. s.2(1)(f) asymmetric cryptosystem; s.2(1)(x) key pair; s.2(1)(zc) private key; s.2(1)(zd) public key; s.2(1)(p) digital signature.
  3. Functions. The public key verifies; the private key creates. The four functions — confidentiality, authentication, integrity, non‑repudiation — with the express caveat that confidentiality is not a function of the signature but of encryption.
  4. The authentication process. The eight steps of Fig. 6, citing s.3(1)–(4) and the Explanation to s.3(2).
  5. Diagram. Redraw Fig. 6 (or a simplified version: M → hash → private key → signature → transmit → public key → compare).
  6. Shortcomings. Choose four: technological rigidity; dual‑track mismatch; sub‑delegation; private‑key custody; institutional mismatch; First Schedule gap; the proof bottleneck.
  7. Conclusion. Use the model conclusion at §14.4.
Key argument to hit

Build the definitions → the s.3 mechanism → contrast with s.3A → conclude with the Shah & Srivastava "legislative chaos" critique. The examiner is testing whether you can distinguish s.3 from s.3A and whether you know that s.2(1)(zc) is the private key.

NOV–DEC 2024Q.1 · Essay What are the ways to authenticate an electronic record? Can biometrics be used? On what grounds can an Electronic Signature Certificate be suspended?
Part A — the ways to authenticate
  1. s.3 — digital signature. Asymmetric cryptosystem + hash function, via a DSC from a licensed CA (ss.17–42).
  2. s.3A — electronic signature. Any technique that is reliable under s.3A(2) and specified in the Second Schedule.
  3. s.2(1)(d) — "affixing electronic signature" = "adoption of any methodology or procedure".
  4. s.14 / s.15 — secure electronic record and secure electronic signature.
Part B — can biometrics be used?

Yes — because s.2(1)(d) is technology‑neutral and the Second Schedule's entry 1 (e‑authentication using Aadhaar or other e‑KYC services) expressly contemplates OTP, fingerprint minutiae and iris authentication. But it must satisfy all four conditions of s.3A(2)(a)–(d): linked to the signatory alone; under the signatory's control at the time of signing; alteration to the signature detectable; alteration to the information detectable. A biometric captured without consent, or an authentication factor shared with another, fails limb (b).

Part C — grounds for suspension

s.37(1)(a)(i) request of the subscriber; s.37(1)(a)(ii) request of a person duly authorised to act on his behalf; s.37(1)(b) the CA's opinion that the certificate should be suspended in the public interest. Add the safeguards: s.37(2) no suspension exceeding fifteen days unless the subscriber is heard; s.37(3) forthwith communication to the subscriber; s.39 publication in the repository. Then, for completeness, add revocation under s.38(1)(a)–(c) and s.38(2)(a)–(d), with s.38(3) hearing and s.38(4) communication, and state the suspension/revocation difference.

NOV–DEC 2025Q.1 · Problem Examine the validity of an e-contract where signatures are affixed via an online signing platform and one party denies validity.
Framework
  1. Is there a contract at all? s.10A: where proposals, acceptances and their revocation are expressed in electronic form or by means of an electronic record, the contract shall not be deemed unenforceable solely on that ground. Apply the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (offer, acceptance, consideration, free consent, capacity, lawful object) to the e‑mail/click exchange.
  2. Is the record a document? s.4 — in electronic form and accessible for subsequent reference. An online signing platform stores a signed PDF in the cloud: both conditions met.
  3. Is the signature legally effective? s.5 — an electronic signature affixed in the manner prescribed. If the platform uses Aadhaar e‑Sign or an OTP‑based e‑KYC, that is an s.3A signature under Second Schedule entry 1. If it uses a DSC, it is an s.3 signature. Either is recognised.
  4. Apply the s.3A(2) four‑fold reliability test to the platform's audit trail: (a) was the authentication data linked to the denying party alone (her own Aadhaar / her own mobile OTP)? (b) was it under her control at the time of signing? (c) and (d) is any alteration to the signature or to the document detectable? If the platform's audit trail shows the signing link was opened, the OTP was delivered to her registered number and entered, and the document hash was sealed — all four limbs are satisfied.
  5. Attribution. s.11(b) — was the person who clicked the link authorised by the denying party? s.11(c) — if the platform's system acted automatically on her behalf, the record is still hers.
  6. Presumptions. s.14 makes the record secure; IEA s.85B(2)(a) → BSA s.87 presumes the integrity of a secure electronic record; IEA s.85C → BSA s.89 presumes the ESC was issued by the CA and accepted by the subscriber. The denying party must disprove, not merely assert.
  7. Estoppel. A party who has affixed a signature by a method she chose, and who has enjoyed the benefit of the contract, is estopped from denying its validity — unless she proves compromise of her authentication factor and that she complied with s.42 (reasonable care to retain control of the private key) and s.40A(c) (prompt notification of compromise).
  8. Conclusion. The contract is valid. The later denial does not invalidate it if the four‑fold reliability test is satisfied and the presumptions are not displaced.
Trap to avoid

Do not say "an e‑contract is invalid because it is not on paper" or "because there is no wet‑ink signature". Both propositions are expressly negated by ss.4, 5 and 10A. Equally, do not assume that every online signing platform produces a legally valid signature — the answer turns on the audit trail and the four s.3A(2) conditions, not on the platform's branding.

LIKELY / REPEATEDEssay Discuss the interplay between s.65B of the Evidence Act (now s.63 BSA) and the IT Act's authentication provisions, referencing Anvar P.V. and Arjun Panditrao Khotkar.

(ss.65A/65B a complete code; Navjot Sandhu overruled; certification a mandatory condition precedent, except for primary evidence); (3) state the refinement from Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (reaffirmed; certificate ideally with the chargesheet but may be furnished later on a genuine attempt or court direction; inapplicable to primary evidence); (4) bring in the IT Act layer — IEA s.73A / BSA s.73 (court may direct the subscriber, the Controller or the CA to produce the DSC and may direct any person to apply the public key and verify), and IEA s.47A / BSA s.47A (the opinion of the issuing CA is a relevant fact), plus s.45A (Examiner of Electronic Evidence); (5) conclude with BSA s.63(4)'s dual‑signature + hash value requirement and the unsettled s.61 BSA debate.

LIKELY / CRITICALEssay Critically examine whether India's PKI-centric CA hierarchy remains fit for purpose given the dominance of Aadhaar e-Sign and OTP authentication.
Structure
  1. Describe the hierarchy (Fig. 8): Controller (s.17) → repository/publication functions (s.18(ca)–(cb)) → licensed CAs (ss.21–27) → subscribers (ss.35–42) → suspension/revocation (ss.37–39) → relying parties.
  2. The case that it remains fit: even Aadhaar e‑Sign routes through a CA and a trusted third party (Second Schedule entries 1(b) and 2); the CA still generates the DSC, still verifies identity under Form C, and still operates under the Controller's e‑authentication guidelines. The hierarchy survives by absorption, not replacement.
  3.   4. Conclusion: the regime is doctrinally sound but institutionally stale. Cite Shah & Srivastava and Vakul Sharma. Recommend: express statutory recognition of technology‑neutral tiers (the eIDAS model), a repository that works in practice, and reform of the First Schedule.

Syllabus item 1  This is the first named item on the Unit II syllabus. It is a "book‑law" topic — you must be able to list the Articles and pair each with its Indian counterpart.

📜 The two Model Laws — what they are
MLEC 1996
UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce, adopted by the United Nations Commission on International Trade Law on 16 December 1996 (85th plenary meeting), alongside the Model Law's accompanying Guide to Enactment. 17 Articles (Arts. 1–17). It is the substantive contract and records law — recognition, writing, signature, originality, admissibility, retention, formation, attribution, acknowledgment, and timing of despatch/receipt.
MLES 2001
UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures (2001). It is the technology‑neutral signature layer. Its Article 6 — "Compliance of a signature" — is the direct source of India's s.3A.
UN Convention 2005
The United Nations Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts (New York, 2005) — MLEC updated and made binding on states that ratify. Useful to mention as the modern successor in a "recent developments" paragraph.
🧠 The single most quotable sentence from the MLEC

The Model Law does not create equivalence by asserting it. It does so by removing the grounds of denial. Article 5(1): "Information shall not be denied legal effectiveness, validity or enforceability solely on the ground that it is in the form of a data message." The word "solely" is the whole model: the law removes the medium as an objection; it does not remove the need to prove the content.

  2. Body — part 1: list the Articles by theme, not by number: (a) recognition & writing (Arts. 5, 5 bis, 6); (b) signature & originality (Arts. 7, 8); (c) evidence & retention (Arts. 9, 10); (d) contract formation (Arts. 11, 12); (e) communications (Arts. 13, 14, 15).
  3. Body — part 2: the Indian transposition — for each theme give the corresponding IT Act section. State expressly: "The Indian IT Act, 2000 is substantially modelled on the MLEC; ss.11, 12 and 13 are, in substance, Articles 13, 14 and 15 of the Model Law."

2.2 The Model Law on Electronic Signatures, 2001 (MLES) and its Indian footprint

The 1996 MLEC answered "what is a signature?" functionally but not technologically. It did not say which techniques were good enough. The 2001 MLES closed that gap with a three‑tier, technology‑neutral structure:

BaselineAny electronic signatureNo requirementMust not be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic (non‑discrimination)
MiddleElectronic signature (Art. 6(3) reliability test)Four conditions: (a) signature creation data linked to the signatory and to no other; (b) under the signatory's sole control at the time of signing; (c) any alteration to the signature after affixing is detectable; (d) any alteration to the information after authentication is detectablePresumed to satisfy the legal requirement of a signature unless proved otherwise

🔗 India's s.3A is Article 6(3) of the MLES — almost word for word

Compare the MLES Art. 6(3) conditions with s.3A(2)(a)–(d): they are the same four conditions in the same order. The 2008 Amendment inserted s.3A precisely to bring India into line with the MLES' technology‑neutral tiering. This is the single best "linkage" point to score in any answer on s.3A.

Where India differs from the MLES: the MLES leaves the "middle tier" to be proved by the party relying on the signature, and reserves automatic equivalence for the qualified tier. India's structure is different: s.3 digital signatures sit inside a licensed PKI and are treated as automatically recognised (s.5 + s.3), whereas s.3A electronic signatures must be shown to be reliable. In other words, India collapses the MLES' middle and top tiers into two parallel tracks (s.3 and s.3A) rather than three ascending tiers.

✅ Real example — where the MLES architecture actually matters commercially

An Aadhaar e‑Sign on a loan agreement is an s.3A electronic signature (Aadhaar e‑KYC is entry 1 of the Second Schedule), not an s.3 digital signature. A DSC on a Class‑III token used for an Income Tax portal return is an s.3 digital signature. Both are legally effective under s.5 — but they fail in different ways. The Aadhaar e‑Sign record proves identity through UIDAI's audit trail; the DSC proves identity through the CA's certificate and the private key. An examiner who asks "can biometrics be used?" is really asking whether OTP/iris/fingerprint authentication satisfies the s.3A(2) test — which it does.

3Legal recognition of electronic records — s.4

Syllabus item 2  The first of the two "recognition" provisions. Together with s.5 this is the heart of the Unit.

s.4 · LEGAL RECOGNITION OF ELECTRONIC RECORDS · VERBATIM

Legal recognition of electronic records

Where any law provides that information or any other matter shall be in writing or in the typewritten or printed form, then, notwithstanding anything contained in such law, such requirement shall be deemed to be satisfied if such information or matter is—
   (a) rendered or made available in an electronic form; and
   (b) accessible so as to be usable for a subsequent reference.
Structure: a single sentence, two cumulative conditions joined by "and", preceded by a non obstante clause ("notwithstanding anything contained in such law"). The non obstante clause is what lets s.4 override any other statute that demands paper.

3.1 The four elements you must break out separately

ElementTextWhat it means in practiceHow it fails
E1 — Trigger"Where any law provides that information or any other matter shall be in writing or in the typewritten or printed form"s.4 is a substitution rule: it only operates when some other law imposes a writing requirement. E2 — Rendering"(a) rendered or made available in an electronic form"The information must exist in electronic form — i.e. as data, text, image, sound, code, etc. held in a computer resource (s.2(1)(v) "information" + s.2(1)(k) "computer resource").A draft, an oral statement, or a record held only on a volatile display and never stored will not satisfy this. E3 — Accessibility"(b) accessible so as to be usable for a subsequent reference"The key words are "subsequent reference". ✅ Real examples that satisfy s.4
  • An e‑invoice raised on the GST portal (e‑invoice with an IRN and signed QR code) — rendered in electronic form on the taxpayer's server and retrievable at any time from the portal. Satisfies the writing requirement of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017.
  • An IRCTC SMS or e‑mail ticket — the information is in electronic form on the telecom provider's server and remains retrievable for later presentation to the TTE.
  • An RTI application filed by e‑mail to a public authority's CPIO — satisfies the "in writing" requirement of s.6(1) of the Right to Information Act, 2005.
  • A salary slip in an HR portal, a DSC‑signed share certificate, an insurance policy PDF delivered to a DigiLocker, a NEFT/NECS transaction advice in internet banking.
  • A contract concluded entirely by e‑mail exchange with no paper original at all — satisfies the writing requirement for a contract not required to be in writing by any statute, and is valid under s.10A.

5Authentication of electronic records — s.3

Syllabus items 3–4  This section covers the definitional scaffold of s.2(1), the four functions of a signature, and the mechanics of s.3. It is the longest and most‑examined part of the Unit.

s.3 · AUTHENTICATION OF ELECTRONIC RECORDS · VERBATIM

Authentication of electronic records

(1) Subject to the provisions of this section, any subscriber may authenticate an electronic record by affixing his digital signature.
(2) The authentication of the electronic record shall be effected by the use of asymmetric cryptosystem and hash function which envelop and transform the initial electronic record into another electronic record.
Explanation: For the purposes of this sub‑section, "hash function" means an algorithm mapping or translating one sequence of bits into another, generally smaller set known as "hash result" such that an electronic record yields the same hash result every time the algorithm is executed using the same algorithm, and it is computationally infeasible—
  (a) to derive or reconstruct the original electronic record from the hash result produced by the algorithm;
  (b) that two electronic records produce the same hash result using the algorithm.
(3) Any person by the use of a public key of the subscriber can verify the electronic record.
(4) The private key and the public key are unique to the subscriber and constitute a functioning key pair.
Note the opening words of sub‑section (1): "Subject to the provisions of this section" — this is the textual hook on which s.3A hangs, since s.3A begins "Notwithstanding anything contained in section 3…".

The syllabus expressly lists four functions: confidentiality, authentication, integrity and non‑repudiation. Be careful — the first is not, strictly, a function of the signature at all. This is a favourite examiner trap.

✅ Real example — a DSC‑signed income tax return

When a taxpayer signs a return on the Income Tax portal with a Class‑III DSC: (i) authentication — the portal trusts the DSC issued by a licensed CA, which binds the taxpayer's PAN to her public key; (ii) integrity — the portal computes the hash of the filed return and verifies the signature against it, so a single changed digit in a figure would invalidate the signature; (iii) non‑repudiation — the taxpayer cannot later say "I did not file that", because s.42 obliged her to keep her private key secure and the presumptions operate against her; (iv) confidentiality — comes from the HTTPS/TLS channel and, for the underlying data, from the portal's access controls, not from the signature.

5.5 Shortcomings of the s.3 / PKI model

The May–June 2025 Q.3 ends with "Are there shortcomings?" Do not skip it — the critique carries a third of the marks.

⚠️ Eight shortcomings — with the authority for each
  1. Technological rigidity. The 2000 Act hard‑wired a single cryptographic method into the statutory definition of "digital signature" (s.2(1)(p) → s.3). Shah & Srivastava's central thesis: this "froze the law to a particular vintage of cryptography just as global commerce was moving toward biometric, mobile‑OTP and cloud‑based authentication."
  2. Two parallel regimes with no clean interface. s.3A (2008) cured the rigidity but left ss.3 and 3A as two parallel tracks whose interplay with the CA‑licensing architecture — built exclusively around PKI — "remains under‑theorised" (Shah & Srivastava).
  3. Sub‑delegation risk. s.5 makes validity depend on the signature being affixed "in such manner as may be prescribed by the Central Government". The operative authentication framework therefore lives in executive Rules (CA Rules 2000; ESEATPR 2015) rather than the Act — raising an argument of excessive sub‑delegation and of instability, since Rules can be changed without Parliament.
  4. Private‑key custody is the weakest link. s.42 places the burden of controlling the private key on the subscriber, but a shared office computer, a stolen token, or malware defeats it. Non‑repudiation is only as strong as the key's custody.
  5. Institutional mismatch. Aadhaar e‑Sign and OTP are now the dominant methods of signing in India (income tax filings, bank KYC, DigiLocker), yet the statutory scaffolding (ss.17–42) still speaks the language of CAs issuing PKI‑based certificates. Vakul Sharma flags this structural mismatch; Parliament has not resolved it.
  6. No certification of the signature itself. The DSC certifies the key‑to‑identity binding, not the signature. A valid signature on a fraudulent document is still valid — s.73/s.74 deal only with false certificates, not false documents.
  7. First Schedule gap. Wills, powers of attorney, trusts and immovable‑property contracts are outside the Act, so the entire s.3 apparatus is unavailable for them (Apar Gupta's critique).
  8. Proof is a separate bottleneck. A perfectly valid digital signature still fails in court without the s.65B / s.63 BSA certificate — see §8. Authentication and admissibility are two different problems.

6Electronic signatures — s.3A and the Second Schedule

Syllabus items 4–5  The technology‑neutral track, inserted by the 2008 Amendment. This is the most‑asked short question in the Unit.

s.3A · ELECTRONIC SIGNATURE · VERBATIM

Electronic signature

(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in section 3, but subject to the provisions of sub‑section (2), a subscriber may authenticate any electronic record by such electronic signature or electronic authentication technique which—
  (a) is considered reliable; and
  (b) may be specified in the Second Schedule.
(2) For the purposes of sub‑section (1), any electronic signature or electronic authentication technique shall be considered reliable if—
  (a) the signature creation data or the authentication data are, within the context in which they are used, linked to the signatory and to no other person;
  (b) the signature creation data or the authentication data were, at the time of signing, under the control of the signatory and of no other person;
  (c) any alteration to the electronic signature made after affixing such signature is detectable;
  (d) any alteration to the information made after its authentication by the electronic signature is detectable; and
  (e) it fulfils such other conditions as may be prescribed.
(3) The Central Government may prescribe the procedure for the purpose of ascertaining whether the electronic signature is that of a person by whom it is purported to have been affixed.
(4) The Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, add to or omit from the Second Schedule any electronic signature or electronic authentication technique: Provided that no such notification shall be issued unless the technique is considered reliable.
(5) Every notification made under sub‑section (4) shall be laid, as soon as may be after it is made, before each House of Parliament.
Why it exists: s.3A is the Indian transposition of Article 6 of the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures, 2001. It makes the law technology‑neutral: it asks whether a technique is reliable, not whether it uses a particular cryptography.

Yes. 7Secure electronic record (s.14), secure electronic signature (s.15) and s.16

Syllabus item 6  These three provisions are what convert a signature into a legally presumed document. Note the pre‑/post‑2008 wording difference in s.15 — a classic exam trap.

s.14 · SECURE ELECTRONIC RECORD · VERBATIM

Secure electronic record

Where any security procedure has been applied to an electronic record at a specific point of time, then such record shall be deemed to be a secure electronic record from such point of time to the time of verification.
Structure: a single conditional sentence with a deeming consequence. The window of security runs from the point of time of application to the point of time of verification. Outside that window, the record is not a "secure electronic record" for s.14 purposes.
s.15 · SECURE ELECTRONIC SIGNATURE · POST-2008 (substituted)

Secure electronic signature

If, by application of a security procedure agreed to by the parties concerned, it can be verified that an electronic signature, at the time it was affixed, was—
(a) unique to the subscriber affixing it;
(b) capable of identifying such subscriber;
(c) created in a manner or using a means under the exclusive control of the subscriber and linked to the electronic record to which it relates in such a manner that if the record was altered the electronic signature would be invalidated,
then such electronic signature shall be deemed to be a secure electronic signature.
Pre‑2008 wording (for reference only, do not quote as current law): the original section read "Secure digital signature" and spoke of a "digital signature", with the security procedure agreed to by the parties concerned being one specified in the Third Schedule by the Central Government. The Third Schedule was omitted by the 2008 Amendment, and "digital" was replaced by "electronic" throughout.

7.1 Reading ss.14 and 15 together

Questions.14 (secure electronic RECORD)s.15 (secure electronic SIGNATURE)
What is secured?The record (the document).The signature (the act of authentication).
What is the test?"Any security procedure has been applied… at a specific point of time".Three conditions, verifiable at the time the signature was affixed: (a) uniqueness, (b) capability of identifying the subscriber, (c) exclusive control + linkage such that alteration invalidates.
Whose agreement matters?None stated.The security procedure must be "agreed to by the parties concerned".
What is the legal effect?The record is deemed secure from the point of application to the point of verification.The signature is deemed secure.
Why does it matter in court?Because of the presumptions. IEA s.85B(2)(a) (now BSA s.87) presumes the integrity of a secure electronic record from the point of time of securing, unless the contrary is proved. And IEA s.85C (now BSA s.89) presumes that a secure electronic signature affixed to a secure electronic record is the signature of the subscriber, unless the contrary is proved. Sections 14 and 15 are therefore the gateways to the presumptions.
🔗 The doctrinal chain — say this in any answer on ss.14–15

s.14/s.15 → s.85B(2)(a) / s.85C (IEA) → s.87 / s.89 (BSA 2023). A record is deemed secure by the security procedure; the court then presumes its integrity; and the person denying it must disprove — not merely doubt — the presumption. That is the practical payoff of the whole Chapter II architecture.

7.2 Section 16 — Security procedure

s.16 · SECURITY PROCEDURE · SUBSTANCE

Security procedure

The Central Government may, by rules in the Official Gazette, prescribe the security procedure having regard to the nature of the transaction and the level of sophistication of the parties with respect to their technological capacity, the volume of similar transactions engaged in by other parties, the availability and cost of alternative procedures and other relevant factors, so as to satisfy the requirements of sections 14 and 15.
Function: s.16 supplies the factors the Government must weigh when prescribing the "security procedure" referred to in ss.14 and 15. It is the source of the graded, proportionate approach: a high‑value transaction justifies a stronger procedure than a low‑value one.
✅ Real example of the s.16 proportionality logic

A Rs 50 lakh wire transfer initiated through corporate internet banking typically requires a Class‑III DSC on a hardware token plus a second factor — a strong "security procedure" appropriate to the volume and nature of the transaction. A Rs 20 UPI payment requires only a PIN. Both are legitimate; neither is "the" security procedure. Section 16 is the statutory recognition that security is proportionate, not absolute.

2Admission of Facts

In the general election to the Kerala Legislative Assembly held on 13 April 2011, the first respondent, P.K. Basheer, was declared elected to the 034 Eranad Legislative Assembly Constituency. He was a candidate supported by the United Democratic Front (UDF). The appellant, Anvar P.V., contested the election as an independent candidate, allegedly supported by the Left Democratic Front (LDF). The sixth respondent was the chief election agent of the first respondent.

There were five candidates. The appellant came second in terms of votes; the others secured only marginal votes. The appellant sought to set aside the election under s.100(1)(b) read with ss.123(2)(ii) and (4) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 (the RP Act), and also sought a declaration in his own favour.

By order dated 16 November 2011, the High Court held that the election petition to set aside the election on the ground under s.123(2)(a)(ii) was not maintainable; that ground was not pursued before the Supreme Court. Issues (1) and (2) were on maintainability and were answered as preliminary, in favour of the appellant.

The evidence fell into three parts: (i) electronic records; (ii) documentary evidence other than electronic records; and (iii) oral evidence. As the major thrust in the arguments was on electronic records, the Court dealt with those first.

The electronic evidence consisted of CDs — Exhibits P4, P8, P9, P10, P12, P13, P15, P20 and P22 — containing recordings of songs, announcements and speeches used in the campaign. These recordings had been made using other instruments and then fed into a computer, from which CDs were made. The appellant admittedly produced no certificate under s.65B(4) of the Evidence Act in respect of any of them.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether secondary electronic evidence (the CDs) is admissible without the certificate required by s.65B(4) of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872.
  2. Whether the general secondary‑evidence provisions — ss.63 and 65 — can be independently invoked to bypass the special procedure of s.65B.
  3. Whether the admissibility of an electronic record is governed by the special provisions (ss.59, 65A, 65B) or by the general law of evidence.
  4. Whether the position in State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu (2005) 11 SCC 600, which had permitted the use of electronic evidence without a s.65B certificate, lays down the correct law.
  5. Whether an electronic record produced as primary evidence under s.62 requires compliance with s.65B.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments of the Appellant (election petitioner)
  • The CDs were relevant and had been produced in court; the failure to file a s.65B(4) certificate was at most an irregularity that could be cured, and not a defect going to admissibility.
  • Reliance was placed on Navjot Sandhu: a two‑Judge Bench had held that "irrespective of the compliance with the requirements of Section 65‑B… there is no bar to adducing secondary evidence under the other provisions of the Evidence Act, namely, Sections 63 and 65."
  • Section 63 defines secondary evidence to include "copies made from the original by mechanical processes which in themselves insure the accuracy of the copy, and copies compared with such copies" — a CD made from a recording is precisely such a mechanical copy.
  • Section 65 permits secondary evidence where the original is of such a nature as not to be easily movable; the recordings were held on servers/equipment that could not be produced in court.
  • In Navjot Sandhu a responsible official had duly certified the document at the time of production and the signatures in the certificate had been identified — so, the argument ran, oral evidence and general secondary evidence could fill the gap in any event.
Arguments of the Respondents (returned candidate & others)
  • The CDs were wholly inadmissible because no s.65B(4) certificate existed; the defect was fundamental, not curable.
  • Section 65B is a special provision introduced by the Information Technology Act, 2000 and is therefore the exclusive route for electronic records; ss.63 and 65 are general provisions that must yield to it.
  • The appellant had admittedly produced no certificate — there was nothing to cure.
  • Electronic records are more susceptible to tampering, alteration, transposition and excision than paper; admitting them without the statutory safeguards would allow a trial to become a travesty of justice.
  • A charge of corrupt practice is substantially akin to a criminal charge and must be proved to the criminal standard; unreliable evidence cannot found it.

5The Court's reasoning

Kurian, J. began with first principles: "Construction by plaintiff, destruction by defendant. Construction by pleadings, proof by evidence; proof only by relevant and admissible evidence. Genuineness, veracity or reliability of the evidence is seen by the court only after the stage of relevancy and admissibility."

Step 1 — the electronic record is a document. An electronic record produced for the inspection of the court is documentary evidence under s.3 of the Evidence Act.

Step 2 — the amendatory scheme. The Court set out the amendments made by Act 21 of 2000 (the IT Act): s.22A (oral admissions as to contents of electronic records are not relevant unless genuineness is in question); s.45A (opinion of the Examiner of Electronic Evidence referred to in s.79A IT Act is a relevant fact, and such Examiner is an expert); s.59 (all facts except the contents of documents or electronic records may be proved by oral evidence); s.65A ("The contents of electronic records may be proved in accordance with the provisions of section 65B"); and s.65B in full (all five sub‑sections, with the four conditions in sub‑s.(2) and the certificate in sub‑s.(4)).

Step 3 — the Statement of Objects and Reasons. The Court quoted the SOR to the IT Act: "New communication systems and digital technology have made drastic changes in the way we live. A revolution is occurring in the way people transact business. In fact, there is a revolution in the way the evidence is produced before the court. Properly guided, it makes the systems function faster and more effective."

Step 4 — s.65B is a complete code. "Any documentary evidence by way of an electronic record under the Evidence Act, in view of Sections 59 and 65A, can be proved only in accordance with the procedure prescribed under Section 65B. Section 65B deals with the admissibility of the electronic record. The purpose of these provisions is to sanctify secondary evidence in electronic form, generated by a computer. It may be noted that the Section starts with a non obstante clause."

Step 5 — the maxim. "Proof of electronic record is a special provision introduced by the IT Act… The very caption of Section 65A of the Evidence Act, read with Sections 59 and 65B is sufficient to hold that the special provisions on evidence relating to electronic record shall be governed by the procedure prescribed under Section 65B of the Evidence Act. That is a complete code in itself. Being a special law, the general law under Sections 63 and 65 has to yield." The maxim applied: generalia specialibus non derogant — the special law will always prevail over the general law.

Step 6 — Navjot Sandhu overruled. The Court held that in Navjot Sandhu the court "omitted to take note of Sections 59 and 65A dealing with the admissibility of electronic record. Sections 63 and 65 have no application in the case of secondary evidence by way of electronic record; the same is wholly governed by Sections 65A and 65B. To that extent, the statement of law on admissibility of secondary evidence pertaining to electronic record, as stated by this court in Navjot Sandhu case (supra), does not lay down the correct legal position. It requires to be overruled and we do so."

Step 7 — the five requirements of the certificate. The Court enumerated what the certificate must do: (a) identify the electronic record containing the statement and describe the manner in which it was produced; (b) furnish the particulars of any device involved in the production of that record; (c) deal with the applicable conditions mentioned under s.65B(2); (d) deal with any of the matters to which the conditions relate; and (e) be signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device or the management of the relevant activities. The person need only state the matter to the best of his knowledge and belief.

Step 8 — the timing and the purpose. "Most importantly, such a certificate must accompany the electronic record like computer printout, Compact Disc (CD), Video Compact Disc (VCD), pen drive, etc., pertaining to which a statement is sought to be given in evidence, when the same is produced in evidence. All these safeguards are taken to ensure the source and authenticity, which are the two hallmarks pertaining to electronic record sought to be used as evidence. Electronic records being more susceptible to tampering, alteration, transposition, excision, etc. without such safeguards, the whole trial based on proof of electronic records can lead to travesty of justice."

Step 9 — the primary‑evidence exception. "It is clarified that notwithstanding what we have stated herein in the preceding paragraphs on the secondary evidence on electronic record with reference to Sections 59, 65A and 65B of the Evidence Act, if an electronic record as such is used as primary evidence under Section 62 of the Evidence Act, the same is admissible in evidence, without compliance of the conditions in Section 65B of the Evidence Act." And: "The situation would have been different had the appellant adduced primary evidence, by making available in evidence, the CDs used for announcement and songs. Had those CDs… been duly got seized through the police or Election Commission and had the same been used as primary evidence, the High Court could have played the same in court to see whether the allegations were true."

Step 10 — comparative note. The Court observed that s.69 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act, 1984 (PACE) in the UK, dealing with evidence on computer records, was repealed by s.60 of the Youth Justice and Criminal Evidence Act, 1999; computer evidence in the UK therefore follows the common‑law rule, under which a presumption exists that the computer producing the evidential output was recording properly at the material time, rebuttable by evidence to the contrary. In the United States, under the Federal Rules of Evidence, reliability of records normally goes to the weight of evidence and not to admissibility.

Step 11 — the standard of proof. The Court relied on Razik Ram v. Jaswant Singh Chouhan and P.C. Thomas v. P.M. Ismail for the proposition that a charge of corrupt practice is substantially akin to a criminal charge, and that proof beyond reasonable doubt is required, though the doctrine is not to be stretched to make proof well‑nigh impossible.

6Matrix of Holding

  • ss.65A and 65B of the Evidence Act constitute a complete code for the proof of the contents of electronic records.
  • Being a special law, s.65B prevails over the general secondary‑evidence provisions of ss.63 and 65 (generalia specialibus non derogant).
  • State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu (2005) 11 SCC 600 is overruled to the extent it held that secondary evidence of an electronic record could be adduced under ss.63 and 65 irrespective of compliance with s.65B.
  • An electronic record by way of secondary evidence shall not be admitted in evidence unless the requirements under s.65B are satisfied.
  • In the case of a CD, VCD, chip, etc., the record shall be accompanied by the certificate in terms of s.65B obtained at the time of taking the document, without which the secondary evidence pertaining to that electronic record is inadmissible.
  • The requirement does not apply where the electronic record is used as primary evidence under s.62.
  • Result on the facts: the appellant had admittedly not produced any s.65B certificate in respect of the CDs (Exhibits P4, P8, P9, P10, P12, P13, P15, P20 and P22). They could not be admitted in evidence, and "the whole case set up regarding the corrupt practice using songs, announcements and speeches fall to the ground." The grounds based on Exhibit‑P10‑CD and on the speeches of PW‑4 Palliparamban Aboobakar and PW‑30 Mullan Sulaiman failed with it.
  • Since there was no reliable evidence to reach the irresistible inference that Exhibit‑P1 (the leaflet) was published with the consent of the first respondent or his election agent, the election could not be set aside on the ground of corrupt practice under s.123(4) of the RP Act. The ground of undue influence under s.123(2) was given up, as was the ground on publication of flex boards.
Principle evolved — ratio decidendi
  1. Section 65B is a complete and exclusive code. The contents of an electronic record may be proved only in accordance with s.65A read with s.65B. The general law on secondary evidence (ss.63 and 65) has no application to secondary evidence by way of an electronic record and must yield to the special law.
  2. Certification is a mandatory condition precedent, not a curable irregularity. The certificate under s.65B(4) must accompany the electronic record when it is produced in evidence. Without it, the record is inadmissible — the defect is not curable by oral evidence or by later filing.
  3. The safeguards exist to secure source and authenticity. Electronic records are more susceptible to tampering, alteration, transposition and excision; the statutory certificate is the mechanism by which the court is satisfied as to source and authenticity. Without it, a trial based on electronic records can become a travesty of justice.
  4. Primary evidence is outside the regime. Where the electronic record itself is produced as primary evidence under s.62, s.65B's conditions do not apply.
  5. Genuineness, veracity and reliability are questions for a later stage. The court examines them only after the stages of relevancy and admissibility. A record that is inadmissible never reaches the reliability stage at all.

7Application & Significance

Anvar P.V. is the single most important decision on the proof of electronic records in India. It settled, for a decade, that the s.65B(4) certificate is a condition precedent. It produced a wave of acquittals and quashing of charges where prosecution agencies had tendered call records, CCTV footage, pen drives or CDs without a certificate — and it made the certificate a routine, non‑negotiable part of any chargesheet involving electronic evidence.

Its consequence was in turn refined by the Constitution Bench in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal, (2020) 7 SCC 1, which is Case‑MAT 2 below. And the entire IEA architecture has now been renumbered by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, where the corresponding provision is s.63.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Learn the case as four sentences: (1) Anvar P.V., (2014) 10 SCC 473, 3‑Judge Bench, Kurian J.; (2) ss.65A/65B are a complete code; (3) the certificate is a condition precedent, not a curable irregularity; (4) Navjot Sandhu is overruled and primary evidence under s.62 is the only exception. Then add one line on the BSA: "the position is now governed by s.63, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which in s.63(4) additionally requires a dual‑signature certificate with a hash value."

CASE‑MAT 2 · TIER 1 · CONSTITUTION BENCH

Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal

(2020) 7 SCC 1 2020 SCC Online SC 571 Constitution Bench Supreme Court of India

1Citation & Bench

Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal, (2020) 7 SCC 1; 2020 SCC Online SC 571; Supreme Court of India, Constitution Bench. Printed in the DU case material as a bullet under Anvar P.V. at page 79 et seq.

2Admission of Facts

The case arose in the context of corruption prosecutions in which the prosecution relied on electronic records — in the leading matter, recorded conversations and call records — tendered as secondary electronic evidence. Following Anvar P.V., High Courts had divided on whether the s.65B(4) certificate had to be filed along with the chargesheet or whether it could be supplied later. Several High Courts had taken the strict view that a certificate not filed with the chargesheet rendered the evidence inadmissible for good, leading to acquittals purely on that technical ground.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the certificate required by s.65B(4) of the Evidence Act must be produced along with the chargesheet / at the time the electronic record is tendered, or whether it may be furnished later.
  2. Whether Anvar P.V. lays down the correct law.
  3. Whether the requirement applies to electronic records produced as primary evidence.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments for the accused / petitioners
  • Anvar P.V. held that the certificate must be obtained "at the time of taking the document" and must accompany the record when produced in evidence. A certificate produced later cannot cure the defect.
  • Certification is a mandatory condition precedent; a condition precedent cannot be satisfied ex post facto.
  • Permitting later production would allow the prosecution to fabricate or tailor a certificate after seeing the defence case.
Arguments for the prosecution / respondents
  • Anvar P.V. was reaffirmed and remains the correct law; but its object was to prevent the bypassing of s.65B, not to create a technical trap.
  • The certificate is procedural: what s.65B(4) requires is that the certificate be signed by a person in a responsible official position and deal with the matters in s.65B(2). It does not prescribe a deadline for filing.
  • Where the prosecution has made a genuine attempt to obtain the certificate, or where the court directs its production, refusing to receive it would defeat the ends of justice.
  • The requirement has no application at all to primary evidence.

5Matrix of Holding

  • Anvar P.V. is reaffirmed as the correct law. Sections 65A and 65B constitute a complete code; the general secondary‑evidence provisions yield to them.
  • The certificate should ideally accompany the chargesheet or the electronic record when it is produced in evidence.
  • However, the certificate may be furnished later — including by way of court‑directed production — where the party seeking to rely on the record shows a genuine attempt to obtain it.
  • The requirement is inapplicable where the electronic record is produced as primary evidence under s.62.
  • The Court deprecated the practice of High Courts acquitting purely on the technicality of the certificate's timing where a genuine attempt had been made, and directed that in such cases the court should, in exercise of its inherent / procedural powers, call for the certificate.
Principle evolved
  1. The substantive rule of Anvar P.V. survives: the s.65B(4) certificate is a condition precedent to the admission of secondary electronic evidence.
  2. But timing is procedural, not jurisdictional: the certificate may be supplied later, or obtained on the court's direction, where a genuine attempt to procure it is demonstrated.
  3. The requirement is confined to secondary evidence. Primary evidence — the original device or record produced from proper custody with the custodian's testimony — is outside it.
  4. The two decisions together produce a workable rule: prove the system, not just the file.

6Application & Significance

Arjun Panditrao Khotkar is the answer to the practical chaos that Anvar P.V. caused in the trial courts. It preserves the rigour of Anvar while refusing to let it become an instrument of acquittal by technicality. Under the BSA, 2023, the corresponding provision is s.63, and s.63(4) now adds the dual‑signature (person in charge + expert) and hash value requirements, in the form set out in the Schedule.

🎓 Exam takeaway

Cite Arjun Panditrao Khotkar whenever a question asks about the timing or curability of the s.65B / s.63 BSA certificate. One sentence suffices: "A Constitution Bench in Arjun Panditrao Khotkar (2020) 7 SCC 1 reaffirmed Anvar P.V. but held that the certificate, while ideally accompanying the chargesheet, may be furnished later or on the court's direction where a genuine attempt to obtain it is shown, and is inapplicable to primary evidence."

CASE‑MAT 3 · TIER 2 · AUTHENTICATION CREDENTIALS

Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi

W.P.(C) No. 160/2012 Delhi High Court Order dated 27 January 2012 Vipin Sanghi, J.

1Citation & Bench

Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi, W.P.(C) No. 160/2012, Delhi High Court, order dated 27 January 2012, Vipin Sanghi, J. Printed in the DU case material at page 11 et seq.

2Admission of Facts

The petition was filed under Article 226 of the Constitution to assail an order passed by Sh. Rajesh Aggarwal, the Adjudicating Officer under the Information Technology Act, 2000, Government of Maharashtra, in Complaint No. 2/2010 preferred by the petitioners before him. The reason for approaching the High Court was that the Cyber Appellate Tribunal, New Delhi — to which an appeal against the impugned order lay — was bereft of its Chairman and was not in a position to function.

The petitioners had preferred a complaint against, inter alia, Ms. Madhvika Joshi, Respondent No. 1. Petitioner No. 2 was married to Ms. Madhvika Joshi, whereas petitioner No. 1 was her father‑in‑law. Ms. Joshi accessed the e‑mail accounts of both the petitioners and printed chat sessions and e‑mails from those accounts, which she then used to pursue her case filed against the petitioners under s.498A of the Indian Penal Code.

3Statutory Issues

  1. Whether the Adjudicating Officer was right in holding the respondent technically guilty of breaching s.43 of the IT Act (unauthorised access, downloading, copying or extracting data from a computer / computer system / computer network) while denying compensation.
  2. Whether a claim for compensation under s.43 arises merely on account of the breach, or whether damage must be independently pleaded and proved.
  3. Whether the Adjudicating Officer had jurisdiction to impose a token fine of Rs.100 under s.66C (fraudulent or dishonest use of another person's electronic signature, password or unique identification).
  4. Whether the respondent's use of the retrieved material in a s.498A IPC proceeding could give rise to a cause of action for damages.

4Examination of Reasoning (with the arguments of both sides)

Arguments of the petitioners (husband and father‑in‑law)
  • The respondent had hacked their e‑mail accounts and thereby committed an enumerated act under s.43 (downloading, copying or extracting data without permission of the owner or the person in charge).
  • The claim for compensation was framed at Rs.50 lakhs, comprising: (a) Rs.1.03 lakh towards court fee / application money; (b) Rs.25,000 towards expenses on lodging and travel to Pune and Mumbai for pursuing the case before the Cyber Crime Cell Pune and the Adjudicating Officer at Mantralaya Mumbai and attending hearing dates; (c) Rs.50,000 towards litigation charges paid to counsel; (d) Rs.5,000 towards documentation expenses.
  • The alleged heads of loss included: being dragged into unwanted litigation by registration of a false s.498A case; keeping the complainant's son in eight days' police custody by waving copies of "illegally hacked, fabricated and created" chat sessions; delaying an overseas business assignment to Hong Kong; blocking a client‑site posting to the USA by unauthorised seizure of a passport through the police; blocking business opportunities by deleting business e‑mails regularly for more than 15 months; and damaging reputation in society.
  • An exemplary penalty and costs were sought, with rigorous imprisonment, so that "economically well placed employees and companies" would not repeat such malpractices — a deterrent for one and all.
  • The Adjudicating Officer had no jurisdiction to levy a fine under s.66C, since he functions like a civil court, whereas a fine could be imposed only by a criminal court dealing with offences under the Act.
Arguments of the respondent (wife)
  • The right to pursue legal remedies — including under s.498A IPC — cannot be questioned. The invocation of a right available to a person to ventilate grievances and to take action against perceived illegalities and criminalities does not give a cause of action to claim damages to those against whom action may be initiated.
  • The information retrieved was used only for the purpose of pursuing her case, and for no other purpose. It was not used to malign the petitioners or to cause them any loss; it was given only to the police authorities or the court.
  • The consequences flowing from the registration of the s.498A case are remote and indirect.
  • The petitioners had led no evidence of any damage suffered on account of the retrieval of their e‑mails and chat sessions.

5Matrix of Holding

  • The Adjudicating Officer was right in holding the respondent technically guilty of breaching s.43 — she had accessed the e‑mail accounts without permission and downloaded, copied and extracted data from them, squarely within the enumerated acts of s.43.
  • But the claim for compensation would not arise merely on account of the breach of s.43. "The claim would have to be established like in Civil Court, by making requisite averments with regard to the damage suffered, and thereafter by leading evidence in support thereof, to show that one or the other prohibited activities enumerated in Section 43 of the IT Act have resulted in the sufferance of damages by the person concerned."
  • The petitioners had evidently not led any evidence to show what damage they had suffered on account of the retrieval of their e‑mails and chat sessions.
  • The information was used only in the s.498A IPC case and was not made public to malign the petitioners or to hurt their business or reputation; it was provided only to the police authorities or the court.
  • The right to pursue legal remedies under s.498A IPC cannot be questioned. The invocation of such a right does not give a cause of action to claim damages against those against whom action may be initiated, unless it is eventually found that such complaint or action was filed maliciously.
  • All the consequences flowing from the registration of the s.498A case are remote and indirect consequences; in any event it was too premature to conclude that the respondent was accountable for the so‑called damages.
  • The token fine of Rs.100 under s.66C was upheld. The Court found no merit in the submission that the Adjudicating Officer could not levy it: "The fine has been imposed under Section 66C against the respondent No.1. In case respondent No.1 is aggrieved by the said token fine on the ground that the adjudicating authority did not have jurisdiction to levy the same under Section 66C, it shall be open to her to take appropriate action."
  • Petition dismissed. However, the Court left the door open: "In case it is held by the concerned Court that the proceedings under Section 498A have been maliciously instituted, it shall be open to the petitioners, at that stage, to take appropriate steps to claim damages against the respondent No.1 for loses or damages directly arising from such conduct of respondent No.1."
Principle evolved
  1. s.43 does not create automatic liability. A breach of s.43 establishes the wrong, but compensation requires independent proof of loss — pleaded with particularity and proved by evidence, exactly as in a civil court. s.43 is not a strict‑liability damages provision in practice.
  2. Authentication credentials are the key to the whole regime. The respondent could access, copy and print from the e‑mail accounts only because she had the passwords / login credentials. That is precisely the point of contact between Unit II and Unit III: the security of an electronic record depends on the security of the authentication factor, and s.42's obligation to control one's private key has its everyday analogue in the obligation to control one's password.
  3. Remoteness and indirectness defeat the claim. Consequences flowing from litigation that the wrongdoer was entitled to initiate are remote and indirect; liability under s.43 is confined to directly arising damage.
  4. Exercise of a legal right is not a wrong. Using material in judicial proceedings, where the material is not published to malign or injure, does not give rise to a damages claim, unless the proceedings are ultimately found to be malicious.

6Application & Significance

Vinod Kaushik is the leading authority on the civil liability side of the Act and is relied on in Unit III for the proposition that s.43 ≠ automatic damages. For Unit II its value is different: it demonstrates, on real facts, that the integrity of an electronic record and the attribution of an electronic record are both ultimately credential‑dependent. An e‑mail account's security is only as strong as its password; an s.3 signature's security is only as strong as the private key's custody (s.42); an s.3A Aadhaar e‑Sign's security is only as strong as the control the subscriber had over the authentication factor at the time of signing (s.3A(2)(b)).

🎓 Exam takeaway

Use Vinod Kaushik in an authentication answer for one sentence: "The entire attribution analysis in s.11 presupposes that the originator controlled the means of authentication; Vinod Kaushik v. Madhvika Joshi (Delhi HC, 27.01.2012) is a practical illustration — the respondent's access to the e‑mail accounts turned on the credentials she held, and the Court's treatment of her conduct under s.43 shows how credential control determines who is treated as the originator of a record."

✅ Real example — when a suspension actually happens

A chartered accountant's DSC on a USB token is stolen. She telephones the CA and, on her request (or that of her authorised partner), the CA suspends the certificate immediately under s.37(1)(a)(i) and notifies her under s.37(3). The suspension can run for up to fifteen days without a hearing (s.37(2)); if the CA wishes to suspend for longer, it must hear her. Meanwhile the CA publishes the suspension in the repository under s.39, so that any relying party who checks the certificate's status will see it is no longer valid. If the token is never recovered, the CA revokes under s.38(1)(a) and issues a fresh certificate with a new key pair (s.40).

🔗 Why s.42 is the doctrinal core of non‑repudiation

Non‑repudiation works in law because of a risk allocation: the subscriber is the person best placed to protect the private key, so the law puts the loss on the subscriber if the key is not protected. s.42 imposes the duty of reasonable care; s.40A(c) imposes the duty to notify compromise; s.3(4) states that the private key is unique to the subscriber; and the presumptions (IEA s.85B / BSA s.87; IEA s.85C / BSA s.89) then complete the chain. If the subscriber fails in any of these duties, the presumption operates against her.
